# Augustus

## Overview

Augustus (born Gaius Octavius; 23 September 63 BC – 19 August AD 14), also known as Octavian, was the founder of the Roman Empire and its first emperor from 27 BC until his death. His reign initiated the Pax Romana, an era of imperial peace largely free of armed conflict, and established the principate—a system where the emperor showed nominal deference to the Senate—which lasted until the Crisis of the Third Century. Augustus dramatically enlarged the Empire, reformed its taxation, currency, military, and city of Rome, and his legacy shaped Roman and later European governance for centuries.

## Body

### Rise to power

Octavian was born into an equestrian branch of the gens Octavia; his mother Atia was a niece of Julius Caesar. After his father's death (59 or 58 BC), he was educated by tutors in Greek, rhetoric, and philosophy. Caesar named him principal heir in a will deposited in 45 BC. When Caesar was assassinated on 15 March 44 BC, Octavian was in Apollonia, Illyria. He accepted the will on 8 May 44 BC, purporting that Caesar adopted him as son, and assumed the name Gaius Julius Caesar. He recruited Caesar's veterans with generous bonuses and marched on Rome.

Octavian clashed with Mark Antony, Caesar's former colleague, who refused him funds from Caesar's estate. Cicero became Octavian's ally, delivering speeches against Antony. After Antony besieged Decimus Brutus at Mutina, Octavian was made senator and given imperium pro praetore. Both consuls died in battle, leaving Octavian in sole command. When the Senate refused him the consulship, he marched on Rome and became consul on 19 August 43 BC at age 19.

### Second Triumvirate and civil wars

In October 43 BC, Octavian, Antony, and Lepidus formed the triumvirate, legitimized by the lex Titia on 27 November. They initiated proscriptions targeting some 300 senators and equestrians as outlaws, partly to raise money for troops and partly to eliminate political enemies. After defeating Brutus and Cassius at Philippi in 42 BC, the triumvirs divided the provinces. Octavian settled veterans in Italy by confiscating land from citizens, causing widespread discontent. Antony's brother Lucius Antonius and wife Fulvia raised an army against Octavian, but were forced to surrender at Perusia in 40 BC; Octavian executed 300 senators and equestrians for allying with them.

Octavian married Scribonia in 40 BC, then divorced her to marry Livia Drusilla, who was already pregnant with her second son. After war with Sextus Pompey, Agrippa defeated Sextus at Naulochus in 36 BC. When Lepidus attempted to claim Sicily, his troops deserted him; Octavian forced him into retirement but allowed him to remain pontifex maximus. Antony's Parthian campaign failed, and his alliance with Cleopatra alienated Roman opinion. Octavian obtained Antony's will, which promised Roman territories to his sons and designated Alexandria as burial site. The Senate declared war on Cleopatra in late 32 BC. Agrippa cut off Antony's supply lines, and Octavian defeated Antony at Actium on 2 September 31 BC. Antony and Cleopatra died by suicide in 30 BC; Octavian ordered the death of Caesarion, Cleopatra's son by Caesar.

### Principate and settlements

After conquering Egypt, Octavian controlled the Republic under an unofficial principate, with himself as princeps ('leading citizen'). On 13 January 27 BC, he made a show of returning power to the Senate. In the first settlement, he accepted a ten-year responsibility for chaotic provinces—Hispania, Gaul, Syria, Cilicia, Cyprus, and Egypt—which gave him control over most of Rome's legions. On 16 January 27 BC, the Senate granted him the title augustus ('revered'), a religious rather than political honor indicating he approached divinity. He styled himself Imperator Caesar divi filius ('Commander Caesar son of the deified one').

By 23 BC, his continuous consulships drew attention to his dominance. After a severe illness, Augustus resigned the consulship on 1 July 23 BC. In the second settlement, the Senate granted him imperium proconsulare maius ('greater proconsular power') applicable throughout the empire, superior to all other proconsuls. He also received tribunicia potestas (tribune's power) for life, allowing him to convene the Senate, veto decisions, and preside over elections, plus censorial powers to supervise public morals and hold censuses. The Senate granted him sole imperium within Rome itself. After 19 BC, he received credit for every Roman military victory, and only his family members could receive triumphs.

### War and expansion

Augustus's armies conquered northern Hispania, the Alpine regions of Raetia and Noricum, Illyricum and Pannonia, and extended Africa Proconsularis. Galatia became a province in 25 BC. In 20 BC, Tiberius restored Tigranes V to the Armenian throne, and Augustus negotiated with Phraates IV of Parthia for the return of battle standards lost by Crassus at Carrhae—a symbolic victory used as propaganda. The Battle of the Teutoburg Forest in AD 9 was a major setback: Arminius destroyed three legions under Varus. Augustus advised against further conquests beyond the Rhine, ending expansion into Germania. A campaign against the Sabaeans in Arabia Felix failed due to water shortages, but a peace treaty with the Kingdom of Kush after a counterattack established a new border and guaranteed trade.

### Succession and death

Augustus needed to designate an heir subtly to avoid fears of monarchy. After his nephew Marcellus died in 23 BC, he married his daughter Julia to Agrippa. He adopted his grandsons Gaius and Lucius, but both died. In AD 4, he adopted Tiberius on condition that Tiberius adopt his nephew Germanicus. Augustus exiled his youngest grandson Agrippa Postumus, who was likely killed after Augustus's death. Augustus died at Nola on 19 August AD 14; Tacitus and Cassius Dio claimed Livia poisoned him, but many historians dismiss this as fabrication. The Senate deified him on 17 September AD 14.

### Legacy and reforms

Augustus created a regime of relative peace lasting two centuries, laying foundations for universal monarchy in the Byzantine and Holy Roman Empires. His Res Gestae Divi Augusti, inscribed in bronze before his mausoleum, is his only surviving major work. He established a standing army of 28 legions (about 170,000 soldiers), the Praetorian Guard in 27 BC, a permanent police and firefighting force (the vigiles), and the aerarium militare military treasury. His tax reforms abolished tax farming, replacing private publicani with salaried collectors, and established censuses with fixed quotas. He renovated Rome extensively, boasting he found it brick and left it marble, building the Ara Pacis, Forum of Augustus, and Temple of Mars Ultor. The month Sextilis was renamed August in 8 BC.

## Terms

- **Principate**: System of government where the emperor showed nominal deference to the Senate; lasted until the Crisis of the Third Century
- **Imperium proconsulare maius**: "Greater proconsular power" giving Augustus constitutional authority superior to all other proconsuls throughout the empire
- **Tribunicia potestas**: Tribune's power granted for life, allowing Augustus to convene the Senate, veto decisions, and preside over elections
- **Pontifex maximus**: "Supreme pontiff," high priest of the College of Pontiffs; Augustus assumed this title in 12 BC
- **Pax Romana**: "Roman peace," era of relative peace and prosperity initiated by Augustus's reign
- **Proscriptions**: Legal declarations outlawing political enemies, used by the triumvirs to eliminate rivals and raise money
- **Res Gestae Divi Augusti**: Augustus's account of his achievements, inscribed in bronze before his mausoleum
- **Praetorian Guard**: Imperial bodyguard established in 27 BC, evolving into an important political force
- **Aerarium militare**: Military treasury established in AD 6, funded by 170 million sesterces from Augustus

## Debates and open questions

Ancient and modern views of Augustus vary widely. Tacitus wrote that Augustus cunningly subverted Republican Rome into slavery; Cassius Dio viewed him as a benign autocrat. Modern scholars debate the extent of his censorship—he destroyed public records from 44–36 BC but allowed poets like Virgil and Horace to write freely, while exiling Ovid in AD 8. Ronald Syme's The Roman Revolution (1939) portrayed Octavian as a "sickly and sinister youth" and his faction as akin to a crime syndicate, rejecting fascist appropriations of Augustus. Adrian Goldsworthy (2014) argues Augustus was essentially a military dictator but no more ruthless than other warlords, and that comparing him to Mussolini is anachronistic. Scholars also debate whether Augustus attempted to distance himself from Caesar the dictator while embracing the deified Caesar, and whether the alleged poisoning by Livia has any basis.
