# September 11 attacks

## Overview
The September 11 attacks were coordinated suicide hijackings by al-Qaeda against the United States on September 11, 2001. They killed 2,977 people, caused massive infrastructure damage, and triggered the global "war on terror," including long-term U.S. military interventions in Afghanistan and Iraq. They remain the deadliest terrorist attack in history, fundamentally reshaping U.S. foreign policy, domestic security, and global geopolitics.

## Timeline
- **1996** — Osama bin Laden issues his first fatwa declaring jihad against the U.S.
- **1998** — Al-Qaeda bombs U.S. embassies in Kenya and Tanzania; bin Laden issues a second fatwa.
- **Late 1999** — The "Hamburg cell" (including Mohamed Atta) meets al-Qaeda in Afghanistan.
- **Jan 2000** — Hijackers Nawaf al-Hazmi and Khalid al-Mihdhar enter the U.S.
- **2000** — Key hijackers, including Atta, arrive in the U.S. and begin flight training.
- **Aug 6, 2001** — President Bush receives a Presidential Daily Brief titled "Bin Ladin Determined To Strike in US."
- **Sep 11, 2001** — Four hijacked planes are crashed into the World Trade Center, the Pentagon, and a Pennsylvania field.
- **Oct 7, 2001** — U.S. and coalition forces invade Afghanistan, beginning the War in Afghanistan.
- **May 2, 2011** — Osama bin Laden is killed by U.S. forces in Abbottabad, Pakistan.
- **Aug 30, 2021** — The last U.S. troops withdraw from Afghanistan, ending the 20-year war.

## Body

### Background and Planning
Al-Qaeda, led by Osama bin Laden, declared war on the U.S. due to its support of Israel, military presence in Saudi Arabia, and sanctions against Iraq. Khalid Sheikh Mohammed, the plot's principal architect, proposed the idea to bin Laden in 1996. Bin Laden approved a revised plan in late 1998 or early 1999. The hijackers were selected for their skills; the "Hamburg cell" members were chosen for being educated, English-speaking, and Westernized. They entered the U.S. in 2000 and took flight training. Intelligence agencies had fragments of information about potential attacks but failed to share them effectively, a failure attributed to policies limiting intelligence-sharing between criminal and intelligence investigators.

### The Attacks
On the morning of September 11, 2001, 19 hijackers took control of four commercial airliners. American Airlines Flight 11 struck the World Trade Center's North Tower at 8:46 a.m., followed by United Airlines Flight 175 into the South Tower at 9:03 a.m. Both towers collapsed. American Airlines Flight 77 hit the Pentagon at 9:37 a.m. United Airlines Flight 93, intended for a Washington, D.C. target, crashed in Pennsylvania at 10:03 a.m. after passengers revolted. The FAA grounded all U.S. civilian air traffic. The attacks killed 2,977 people (including the hijackers), injured thousands, and caused at least $10 billion in infrastructure damage. World Trade Center site cleanup was completed in May 2002.

### Immediate and Long-Term Aftermath
The U.S. launched the "war on terror." NATO invoked its collective defense clause (Article 5) for the first time. The U.S. invaded Afghanistan in October 2001, overthrowing the Taliban regime that harbored al-Qaeda. Domestically, the U.S. enacted the Homeland Security Act of 2002, creating the Department of Homeland Security, and passed the USA PATRIOT Act, expanding surveillance powers. The attacks also led to a surge in hate crimes against Muslims, Sikhs, and others perceived to be of Middle Eastern origin. Long-term health consequences for first responders and residents exposed to toxic dust at Ground Zero have been significant, with thousands developing illnesses. The economic impact included a sharp stock market decline, airline industry losses, and an estimated $5 trillion in war and security spending.

### Investigations and Legal Proceedings
The FBI's investigation, PENTTBOM, was the largest criminal inquiry in U.S. history. The 9/11 Commission, formed in 2002, concluded in its 2004 report that the attacks were planned and executed by al-Qaeda and identified failures in imagination, policy, capabilities, and management by U.S. agencies. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) determined the World Trade Center towers collapsed due to fire-induced structural failure after the impacts. Osama bin Laden evaded capture for nearly a decade before being killed in 2011. The main trial of alleged co-conspirators, including Khalid Sheikh Mohammed, has been prolonged and unresolved. In 2023, a potential plea deal was revoked by the Defense Secretary.

### Rebuilding and Memorials
The World Trade Center site was rebuilt, with One World Trade Center opening in 2014. Memorials include the National September 11 Memorial & Museum in New York, the Pentagon Memorial in Virginia, and the Flight 93 National Memorial in Pennsylvania. The names of the victims are read annually on the anniversary.

## Terms
- **Fatwa**: A ruling on a point of Islamic law given by a recognized authority.
- **Jihad**: An Arabic term meaning "struggle" or "striving," often interpreted in a military context as holy war.
- **Hamburg cell**: A group of Islamist terrorists based in Hamburg, Germany, who were key operatives in the 9/11 plot.
- **War on terror**: A global military, political, and ideological campaign launched by the U.S. and allies after 9/11 to combat terrorist organizations.
- **Article 5**: The collective defense clause of the NATO treaty, stating an attack on one member is an attack on all.
- **USA PATRIOT Act**: A U.S. law passed after 9/11 that expanded law enforcement's surveillance and investigative powers.
- **Ground Zero**: The nickname for the World Trade Center site after the attacks.
- **Enhanced interrogation**: A euphemism for interrogation techniques widely considered to be torture, used by the CIA during the war on terror.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent of any involvement or foreknowledge by the Saudi Arabian government remains a subject of investigation and legal action, with some documents still classified.
- The effectiveness and morality of the "war on terror," including the use of torture, drone strikes, and the human and financial costs of the wars in Afghanistan and Iraq, are heavily debated.
- The 9/11 Commission noted that several of its recommendations to prevent future attacks had not been implemented years later.
- The long-term health and economic impacts on first responders, survivors, and the surrounding community continue to unfold.
