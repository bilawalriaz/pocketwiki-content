# Temperature

## Overview

Temperature is a fundamental physical quantity that measures the average kinetic energy of particles in a system, reflecting how energy is distributed among microscopic degrees of freedom. It is central to classical thermodynamics and kinetic theory, governing heat transfer, phase transitions, chemical reaction rates, and material properties. Temperature is measured using thermometers calibrated to scales—Celsius, Fahrenheit, and the absolute Kelvin scale—with the kelvin as the SI base unit. Absolute zero (0 K) represents the theoretical limit where classical particle motion ceases, though quantum zero-point energy persists.

## Timeline

- **c. 1700s** — Fahrenheit adapts Rømer's thermometer and scale
- **1848** — Kelvin publishes "On an Absolute Thermometric Scale"
- **1851–1853** — Kelvin defines thermodynamic temperature via Carnot cycle
- **1954** — Kelvin scale redefined using triple point of water (273.16 K)
- **1995** — Lowest macroscopic temperature achieved: 20 nK at NIST
- **May 2019** — Kelvin redefined via fixed Boltzmann constant value

## Body

### Temperature Scales and Definitions

Temperature scales require two defining points: a zero reference and the size of the unit increment. The Celsius scale sets 0 °C as water's freezing point and 100 °C as its boiling point at sea level pressure. The Fahrenheit scale uses 32 °F and 212 °F for these same points. The Kelvin scale is absolute, with 0 K (absolute zero) as its zero point, equivalent to −273.15 °C. Since 2019, the kelvin is defined through the Boltzmann constant, linking temperature to microscopic particle motion rather than macroscopic reference states.

### Absolute Zero and the Third Law

Absolute zero is the lowest possible temperature, where no energy can be extracted from matter as heat, as stated by the third law of thermodynamics. Experimentally, it can only be approached; the lowest recorded temperature is 38 pK. At absolute zero, classical particle motion ceases, but quantum zero-point energy remains. Negative absolute temperatures exist and are hotter than any positive temperature, arising in systems with bounded energy states like spin subsystems.

### Empirical vs. Theoretical Scales

Empirical scales rely on measurable macroscopic properties, such as mercury column length in glass thermometers. These are practical but limited in range and substance-dependent. Theoretical scales, developed in the mid-19th century, are based on kinetic theory and thermodynamics. The Kelvin scale, grounded in the Boltzmann constant and Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, is independent of specific materials and serves as the international standard.

### Kinetic Theory Approach

Kinetic theory relates temperature to the average kinetic energy of microscopic particles. The equipartition theorem states each classical degree of freedom contributes kBT/2 to the average energy. For translational motion (three degrees of freedom), the average kinetic energy is 3kBT/2. Diatomic molecules have additional rotational and vibrational modes, increasing their heat capacity. The speed of sound in gases is proportional to the square root of absolute temperature, enabling precise thermometry via acoustic methods.

### Thermodynamic Approach

Thermodynamically, temperature is defined through the Carnot engine cycle, relating heat transfer between reservoirs to their absolute temperatures. The zeroth law establishes thermal equilibrium and enables temperature measurement. The second law connects temperature to entropy: in a reversible cycle, the ratio of heat transfer to temperature is path-independent, defining entropy as a state function. Temperature emerges as the partial derivative of internal energy with respect to entropy at constant volume.

### Heat Capacity and Measurement

Heat capacity is the ratio of heat transferred to the resulting temperature change. Specific heat normalizes this per unit mass. Modern thermometry detects properties varying predictably with thermal energy: electrical resistance, thermoelectric voltage, infrared radiation, and acoustic speed. The ideal gas law (pV = nRT) provides a theoretical basis for gas thermometers, though practical limitations prevent reaching absolute zero directly.

### Non-Equilibrium and Local Temperature

In non-equilibrium systems, temperature may vary spatially. Local thermodynamic equilibrium allows defining temperature at points within a medium. However, at interfaces between materials, temperature discontinuities can occur due to abrupt changes in thermal properties. These microscale variations are often smoothed out in macroscopic measurements but are critical for understanding heat transfer at small scales.

## Terms

- **Absolute zero**: The lowest temperature (0 K), where classical particle motion ceases; equivalent to −273.15 °C.
- **Boltzmann constant (kB)**: Proportionality factor relating temperature to average microscopic kinetic energy; fixed value since 2019 defines the kelvin.
- **Carnot engine**: Idealized heat engine operating reversibly between two temperatures, used to define thermodynamic temperature.
- **Equipartition theorem**: States each classical degree of freedom contributes kBT/2 to average energy.
- **Kinetic theory**: Microscopic model explaining temperature as average kinetic energy of particles in motion.
- **Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution**: Probability distribution of particle speeds in an ideal gas, used for temperature measurement.
- **Thermodynamic equilibrium**: State where macroscopic properties are uniform and unchanging; required for well-defined temperature.
- **Triple point**: Unique temperature and pressure where solid, liquid, and gas phases coexist in equilibrium.
- **Zeroth law of thermodynamics**: If two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third, they are in equilibrium with each other; basis for temperature measurement.

## Debates and Open Questions

The transition from macroscopic reference-point definitions (triple point of water) to microscopic definitions (Boltzmann constant) in 2019 resolved long-standing metrological debates but introduced new challenges in precision measurement. Whether temperature remains continuous at material interfaces at the atomic scale is debated, with evidence of discontinuities up to 7.8 K at evaporating water droplet surfaces. The applicability of kinetic theory to non-equilibrium systems and the extension of temperature concepts to quantum systems with few particles remain active research areas. The use of Fermi–Dirac statistics for thermometry, unlike Maxwell–Boltzmann, has not yet been experimentally realized despite theoretical predictions.
