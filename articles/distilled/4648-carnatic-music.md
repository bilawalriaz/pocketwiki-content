# Carnatic music

## Overview
Carnatic music is one of the two main sub-genres of Indian classical music, primarily associated with South India. It is a highly structured system built on ancient Hindu traditions, with a strong emphasis on vocal performance and compositions. Its core elements—melodic modes (rāga) and rhythmic cycles (tāḷa)—form the basis for both improvisation and composed works, making it a living tradition of profound cultural and spiritual significance.

## Timeline
- **Ancient** — Foundations laid in Vedic texts like the Samaveda; concepts detailed in treatises like the Natya Shastra.
- **12th century onwards** — Persian and Islamic influences in North India cause Indian classical music to diverge into Hindustani and Carnatic styles.
- **14th–20th centuries** — Development of the kriti composition form by composers like Purandara Dasa and the Trinity.
- **16th–17th centuries** — Clear demarcation between Carnatic and Hindustani music; Carnatic music flourishes in the Vijayanagara Empire.
- **1660 AD** — Venkatamakhin authors the Chaturdandi Prakasika, establishing the melakarta raga classification system.
- **18th–19th centuries** — Patronage by kingdoms of Mysore, Travancore, and Tanjore; emergence of court musicians.
- **Late 19th century** — Chennai (Madras) emerges as the primary locus for Carnatic music.
- **1927** — The Madras Music Season festival is inaugurated.
- **1947 onwards** — Post-independence, patronage shifts from royalty to public sabhās (private institutions) and ticketed performances.

## Body

### Origin and Historical Development
Carnatic music originated in the Karnataka region and is rooted in ancient Hindu texts, with the Samaveda cited as a key foundation. The system diverged from Hindustani music from the 12th century onwards due to Persian and Islamic influences in North India, while remaining relatively unaffected in the South. By the 16th and 17th centuries, the styles were distinct. The system of teaching was formulated by Purandara Dasa (1484–1564), known as the "father of Carnatic music." The melakarta system for classifying ragas was invented by Venkatamakhin in 1660 and later expanded by Govindacharya. Patronage shifted from royal courts in the 18th and 19th centuries to public institutions in the 20th century, with Chennai becoming its modern center.

### Core Musical Elements
The foundation of Carnatic music consists of four elements: **śruti** (the relative musical pitch or tonic), **svara** (a single note, with seven basic solfege syllables: sa-ri-ga-ma-pa-da-ni), **rāga** (a melodic formula or mode with specific rules for movement and ornamentation), and **tāḷa** (a fixed rhythmic cycle). The raga system is highly structured, with 72 parent ragas (melakarta) classified by their scale, from which countless descendant ragas (janya) are derived. The tala system is built from groupings of beats, with over 108 possible cycles formed from basic components.

### Compositions and Improvisation
Unlike Hindustani music, Carnatic music is primarily taught and performed through compositions, which encode intricate musical details and provide scope for improvisation. The most significant forms are the **varnam** (a technical exercise and concert opener) and the **kriti** (a structured song with pallavi, anupallavi, and charanam sections). Improvisation is essential and includes forms like **alapana** (free-rhythm exploration of a raga), **niraval** (melodic variation on a line of text), and **kalpanaswaram** (improvisation using solfege syllables). The principal long-form improvisation is **Ragam Tanam Pallavi**.

### Performance and Learning
A typical concert (kutcheri) is a three-hour event featuring a small ensemble: a principal vocalist, a melodic accompaniment (usually violin), a rhythm accompaniment (usually mridangam), and a drone (tambura or electronic substitute). The concert follows a structured progression from an opening varnam through main kritis, featuring extensive improvisation and a percussion solo (tani avartanam). Carnatic music is traditionally learned through a graded system of exercises, starting with basic solfege and progressing to complex compositions, historically via the guru-student (gurukula) system.

## Terms
- **Śruti**: The relative musical pitch or tonic from which other notes are derived.
- **Svara**: A single musical note; the solfege syllables (sa-ri-ga-ma-pa-da-ni).
- **Rāga**: A melodic mode or formula with specific rules for scale, movement, and ornamentation.
- **Tāḷa**: A fixed rhythmic cycle or metre built from groupings of beats.
- **Melakarta**: The system of 72 parent ragas classified by their scale.
- **Kriti**: A structured Carnatic composition, typically with three sections (pallavi, anupallavi, charanam).
- **Varnam**: A technical composition that encapsulates the features of a raga, often used as a concert opener.
- **Alapana**: A slow, free-rhythm improvisation that explores the nuances of a raga.
- **Kalpanaswaram**: Improvisation using solfege syllables (swaras) within a raga and tala.
- **Tani Avartanam**: An extended solo performance by the percussionist(s) in a concert.
