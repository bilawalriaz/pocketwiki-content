# Antoine Lavoisier

## Overview

Antoine-Laurent de Lavoisier (1743–1794) was a French nobleman and chemist who led the 18th-century chemical revolution, transforming chemistry from a qualitative to a quantitative science. His central achievements include discovering oxygen's role in combustion, overturning the phlogiston theory, establishing the law of conservation of mass through precise measurement, reforming chemical nomenclature, and helping construct the metric system. His work laid the foundation for modern chemistry and influenced biology through his demonstration that respiration is a form of slow combustion. A wealthy administrator of the hated Ferme générale tax farm, he was guillotined during the French Revolution on charges of tax fraud and tobacco adulteration, then exonerated posthumously.

## Body

### Early life and education
Lavoisier was born 26 August 1743 in Paris to an attorney's family and inherited a large fortune at age five when his mother died. At the Collège des Quatre-Nations he studied chemistry, botany, astronomy, and mathematics, and came under Abbé Nicolas Louis de Lacaille, who instilled a lasting interest in meteorological observation. He earned a law degree and was admitted to the bar but never practiced, continuing scientific education in his spare time. His passion for chemistry was influenced by Étienne Condillac, and his first chemical publication appeared in 1764. From 1763 to 1767 he studied geology under Jean-Étienne Guettard, collaborating on a geological survey of Alsace–Lorraine in 1767 and working on the first geological map of France in 1769.

### Social reformer and public service
Lavoisier devoted significant fortune and work to public benefit. In 1765 he submitted an essay on improving urban street lighting; in 1768 he designed an aqueduct to bring clean water from the river Yvette to Paris, and when construction never commenced, turned to purifying water from the Seine, sparking his interest in water chemistry and sanitation. He studied health risks of gunpowder's effect on air, worked on reconstructing the Hôtel-Dieu hospital with proper ventilation in 1772, and investigated prison hygiene in 1780 and 1791, though his suggestions were largely ignored. He used his wealth from the Ferme générale to open an expensive, sophisticated laboratory so aspiring scientists could study without funding barriers, and founded two educational organizations, Lycée and Musée des Arts et Métiers, the former offering public courses from 1793.

### Ferme générale, marriage, and tobacco
At age 26 Lavoisier bought a share in the Ferme générale, a tax farming company that advanced estimated tax revenue to the royal government in return for collection rights. He commissioned a wall around Paris to collect customs duties, which damaged his reputation during the Revolution. In 1771 he married Marie-Anne Pierrette Paulze, who translated English documents (including Richard Kirwan's *Essay on Phlogiston* and Joseph Priestley's research), assisted in the laboratory, created sketches and engravings of instruments, edited and published his memoirs, and hosted scientific salons. The Farmers General held a monopoly on tobacco; Lavoisier devised a method to detect ash adulteration using acid (which caused effervescence), found small ash amounts improved flavour, and recommended adding a consistent 6.3% water by volume. His rigorous enforcement systems were deeply unpopular with retailers, with consequences during the Revolution.

### Combustion and oxygen theory
In late 1772 Lavoisier turned to combustion. He reported that burning phosphorus combined with air and increased in weight, and extended this to sulfur, persuading himself that all substances gaining weight by combustion or calcination did so from the same cause. Reviewing literature on "fixed air" (carbon dioxide), he recognized Joseph Black's discovery that mild alkalies like chalk contained fixed air as a distinct chemical species. In 1774 he confirmed that metals gain weight in sealed vessels by combining with air. After Joseph Priestley visited Paris in October 1774 and described air produced by heating mercury calx that supported combustion vigorously, Lavoisier conducted his own research, presented in the Easter Memoir of April 1775. He initially concluded this was pure common air, but by the 1778 revised version recognized it as "the eminently respirable part of the air," naming it oxygen from Greek words meaning "acid former," believing all acids contained oxygen.

### Stoichiometry and conservation of mass
Lavoisier performed some of the first truly quantitative chemical experiments, carefully weighing reactants and products in sealed glass vessels so no gases escaped. In 1774 he showed that total mass remains the same at the end as at the beginning of every chemical change, supporting the law of conservation of mass—taught in France as Lavoisier's Law, paraphrased from his *Traité*: "Nothing is lost, nothing is created, everything is transformed." Mikhail Lomonosov had expressed similar ideas in 1748, and Jean Rey, Joseph Black, and Henry Cavendish also pre-dated his work.

### Chemical nomenclature and revolution
In 1787 Lavoisier, with Louis-Bernard Guyton de Morveau, Claude-Louis Berthollet, and Antoine François de Fourcroy, published *Méthode de nomenclature chimique*, discarding the classical elements of earth, air, fire, and water. Some 33 substances not decomposable by known means were provisionally listed as elements, including light, caloric, oxygen, hydrogen, azote (nitrogen), carbon, sulfur, phosphorus, metals, earths, and alkalies. Acids were named by element and oxygenation degree ("ic" vs. "ous" endings), and their salts by "ate" vs. "ite" suffixes—replacing terms like "vitriol of Venus" with "copper sulfate." The new nomenclature spread throughout Europe and the United States. Lavoisier faced opposition, especially from British phlogistic scientists like Priestley, Richard Kirwan, James Keir, and William Nicholson, who argued that precise quantification did not imply conservation of mass and that his results to five to eight decimal places were a "parade" beyond experimental accuracy.

### Water, phlogiston, and the Traité
In 1783 Lavoisier attacked phlogiston theory in *Réflexions sur le phlogistique* and, learning of Henry Cavendish's production of water by burning hydrogen in oxygen, recognized water as a compound. With Laplace he synthesized water by burning hydrogen and oxygen in a bell jar over mercury, overturning the 2,000-year-old idea that water was an element. Working with Jean-Baptiste Meusnier, he passed water through a red-hot iron gun barrel to decompose it, and later demonstrated decomposition and synthesis before thirty savants using carefully calibrated apparatus, convincing many attendees. His *Traité élémentaire de chimie* (1789), the first modern chemistry textbook, presented the oxygen theory, stated conservation of mass, denied phlogiston, and defined elements as substances not breakable by known analysis; it was translated into English within about a year.

### Physiological work and legacy
With Laplace in 1782–1783, Lavoisier used an ice calorimeter to measure heat from combustion and respiration. By comparing heat and carbon dioxide from a live guinea pig with carbon burned to produce the same CO₂, they concluded respiration is a slow combustion—"la respiration est donc une combustion"—explaining animal heat. He continued with Armand Seguin in 1789–1790, though the Revolution disrupted completion. Lavoisier established consistent use of the chemical balance, developed the radical theory (radicals combine with oxygen), discovered diamond is a crystalline form of carbon (suggesting allotropy), and built the gasometer. His erroneous belief that all acids contain oxygen was later disproven. After his death, his manuscripts and instruments were kept at the Château de la Canière in Puy-de-Dôme; Mount Lavoisier in New Zealand was named for him in 1970.

## Terms

- **Phlogiston theory**: Pre-Lavoisier theory that combustible substances release a fire-like element called phlogiston during burning; Lavoisier overturned it with the oxygen theory.
- **Ferme générale**: Tax farming company that advanced tax revenue to the royal government in exchange for collection rights; Lavoisier's involvement led to his execution.
- **Fixed air**: Joseph Black's term for carbon dioxide (CO₂), a distinct chemical species in the atmosphere.
- **Calx**: Metal oxide formed by heating a metal in air; Lavoisier showed the weight gain came from combining with air.
- **Dephlogisticated air**: Priestley's term for oxygen, thought to be common air deprived of phlogiston.
- **Caloric**: Matter of heat, which Lavoisier listed as an element in his new nomenclature.
- **Ice calorimeter**: Apparatus designed by Lavoisier and Laplace to measure heat produced during combustion or respiration.
- **Radical theory**: Lavoisier's idea that radicals, groups functioning as a single unit in chemical processes, combine with oxygen in reactions.
- **Azote**: Lavoisier's term for nitrogen, from Greek meaning "lifeless."

## Debates and open questions

Lavoisier's antiphlogistic approach was long unaccepted by many chemists, especially British phlogistic scientists who argued that quantification of substances did not imply conservation of mass and that his precision to five to eight decimal places exceeded experimental accuracy. The priority of conservation of mass is debated: Mikhail Lomonosov expressed similar ideas in 1748, and Jean Rey, Joseph Black, and Henry Cavendish pre-dated Lavoisier's work. His claim that all acids contain oxygen later proved erroneous. The story that Lavoisier blinked after decapitation to test consciousness is apocryphal—not recorded in contemporary accounts and likely originating from a 1990s Discovery Channel documentary.
