# Quebec

## Overview
Quebec is Canada's largest province by area and its only Francophone-majority region, home to a distinct society shaped by its history as a French colony, British conquest, and modern cultural evolution. It matters as a unique North American jurisdiction with its own civil law system, language laws, and ongoing debates about sovereignty within Canada.

## Timeline
- **1534** — Jacques Cartier lands in Gaspé, claiming the region for France.
- **1608** — Samuel de Champlain founds Quebec City, the capital of New France.
- **1763** — Treaty of Paris ends the Seven Years' War; France cedes Canada to Britain.
- **1774** — Quebec Act restores French civil law and Catholic rights.
- **1791** — Constitutional Act divides Quebec into Upper and Lower Canada.
- **1837-38** — Lower Canada Rebellion led by Louis-Joseph Papineau.
- **1867** — Canadian Confederation; Quebec becomes a founding province.
- **1960s-80s** — Quiet Revolution modernizes Quebec society and state institutions.
- **1977** — Charter of the French Language (Bill 101) makes French the official language.
- **1995** — Sovereignty referendum fails with 50.6% voting "No".

## Body

### History
Quebec's history begins with Indigenous peoples, including the Inuit and ten First Nations, who inhabited the land for millennia. European exploration started with Jacques Cartier in 1534, followed by Samuel de Champlain's founding of Quebec City in 1608, which became the administrative heart of New France. The colony grew through the fur trade and alliances with Indigenous nations, but faced conflicts with the British and their Iroquois allies. After the Seven Years' War, the 1763 Treaty of Paris transferred Canada to Britain, beginning a new era. British rule led to political tensions, culminating in the 1837-38 Lower Canada Rebellion. Following its suppression, the 1840 Act of Union merged Upper and Lower Canada, a union marked by political instability until Confederation in 1867.

### Modern Era and Quiet Revolution
The early 20th century saw Quebec's society dominated by the Catholic Church and conservative nationalism, a period sometimes called the "Grande Noirceur." The Quiet Revolution of the 1960s-1980s was a transformative period of secularization, state modernization, and economic development. It fostered a new Québécois identity and nationalism, leading to the 1977 Charter of the French Language. Sovereignty debates became central to politics, with referendums in 1980 and 1995 narrowly rejecting independence. The 1982 Constitution Act was patriated without Quebec's consent, a point of ongoing contention.

### Government and Law
Quebec operates as a constitutional monarchy and liberal democracy within a Westminster system. Its legal system is unique in Canada, mixing civil law for private matters (based on the Civil Code of Quebec) and common law for public law. The province has its own Charter of Human Rights and Freedoms and is the only Canadian province with French as its sole official language, protected by the Charter of the French Language.

### Economy and Demographics
Quebec's economy is diverse, with major sectors in services, aerospace, hydroelectricity, mining, and pharmaceuticals. It is the world's second-largest hydroelectricity producer. The population of about 8.5 million is predominantly French-speaking, with French as the mother tongue of 74.8% of residents. Montreal is the largest city and a major economic and cultural hub. The province has a distinct culture, celebrated in its literature, music, comedy, and cuisine, including famous products like maple syrup and poutine.

## Terms
- **Quiet Revolution**: A period of intense social, political, and economic modernization in Quebec during the 1960s and 1970s, marked by secularization and state intervention.
- **Sovereignty-Association**: A political concept proposed by René Lévesque, advocating for Quebec's political independence while maintaining an economic partnership with Canada.
- **Charter of the French Language (Bill 101)**: A 1977 law that made French the official language of Quebec, regulating its use in commerce, education, and government.
- **Civil Code of Quebec**: The body of private law governing relationships between individuals in Quebec, based on civil law tradition.
- **CEGEP**: Collège d'enseignement général et professionnel; a publicly funded college providing pre-university and technical education unique to Quebec's education system.
- **Seigneurial System**: A historical land tenure system in New France where landlords (seigneurs) granted land to tenants (habitants) in exchange for duties.
- **Grande Hémorragie**: The mass emigration of approximately 900,000 French Canadians from Quebec to New England between the mid-19th century and the Great Depression.
- **Gérin-Lajoie Doctrine**: A 1965 doctrine forming the basis of Quebec's international policy, asserting the province's right to conduct its own international relations.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Quebec Sovereignty**: The question of whether Quebec should become an independent country remains a central and divisive political issue, as evidenced by the narrow 1995 referendum result.
- **Constitutional Accord**: Quebec has never formally consented to the 1982 Constitution Act. Efforts to amend the constitution to gain Quebec's recognition, such as the Meech Lake and Charlottetown Accords, have failed.
- **Language and Identity**: Debates continue over the balance between protecting the French language and accommodating linguistic minorities, particularly anglophones and allophones.
- **Indigenous Relations**: The nature and scope of Indigenous self-governance and land rights within Quebec, especially following agreements like the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement, remain active areas of negotiation and discussion.
