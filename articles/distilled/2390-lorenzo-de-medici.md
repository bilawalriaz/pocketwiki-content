# Lorenzo de' Medici

## Overview
Lorenzo de' Medici (1449–1492), known as "the Magnificent," was the de facto ruler of the Florentine Republic and the most powerful patron of the Italian Renaissance. He maintained the balance of power in Italy through the Italic League, and his life coincided with Florence's golden age. His death led to the collapse of the Peace of Lodi (1454) and the eventual end of Medici rule in Florence.

## Timeline
- **1449** — Lorenzo born to Piero and Lucrezia de' Medici.
- **1469** — Assumes leadership of Florence after his father's death; marries Clarice Orsini.
- **1478** — Survives the Pazzi conspiracy; his brother Giuliano is killed.
- **1478–79** — Excommunicated by Pope Sixtus IV; resolves war with Naples via personal diplomacy.
- **1479** — Becomes a permanent member of the committee supervising Florence's rebuilding.
- **1492** — Dies at Careggi; the Peace of Lodi collapses.
- **1494** — His son Piero loses Florence, ending Medici rule.
- **1512** — His son Giovanni (Pope Leo X) restores Medici power.
- **1531** — His nephew Giulio (Pope Clement VII) makes Florence a hereditary duchy.

## Body

### Youth and Education
Lorenzo was the grandson of Cosimo de' Medici, who first led Florence and ran the Medici Bank. His father, Piero, was an art patron, and his mother, Lucrezia Tornabuoni, was a poet and advisor. Lorenzo was tutored by humanists like Marsilio Ficino and trained in Greek. He was considered plain in appearance but was groomed for power, undertaking diplomatic missions as a youth.

### Politics and Foreign Policy
Lorenzo ruled Florence indirectly through surrogates, payoffs, and strategic marriages. His tenure was marked by the Pazzi conspiracy (1478), a plot backed by Pope Sixtus IV to seize control, which resulted in his brother Giuliano's death. Lorenzo's subsequent war with the Pope and Naples was resolved by his personal diplomacy in Naples. He then pursued a policy of balancing power among Italian states and keeping foreign powers like France and the Holy Roman Empire out of Italy. He maintained trade relations with the Ottoman Empire. A conflict over alum mining in Volterra, which he suppressed by force, marred his reputation.

### Patronage and Humanism
Lorenzo's court included artists like Botticelli, Leonardo da Vinci, and Michelangelo, who lived with his family. He expanded the Medici Library and supported humanists like Ficino and Pico della Mirandola, who merged Platonic philosophy with Christianity. He used art for diplomacy, such as commissioning Florentine artists for the Sistine Chapel. He calculated his family had spent 663,000 florins on public good since 1434.

### Family and Legacy
Lorenzo married Clarice Orsini in 1469; they had eight surviving children, including Piero ("the Unfortunate") and Giovanni (future Pope Leo X). He also adopted his nephew Giulio (future Pope Clement VII). Lorenzo died in 1492; his death was followed by the rise of the friar Girolamo Savonarola and the temporary fall of the Medici. His heir, Piero, lost Florence in 1494. Medici rule was later restored and formalized by his descendants.

## Terms
- **Italic League:** A 1454 alliance of Italian states that Lorenzo helped maintain to stabilize the peninsula.
- **Pazzi conspiracy:** A 1478 plot to overthrow the Medici, led by the Pazzi family with papal backing.
- **Humanism:** A Renaissance intellectual movement that studied classical Greek and Roman texts.
- **Medici Bank:** The powerful financial institution run by the Medici family.
- **Interdict:** A papal sanction suspending religious services in a territory.
- **Alum:** A mineral crucial for industries like glassmaking, whose mining in Volterra caused conflict.
- **Signoria:** The governing council of the Florentine Republic.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact nature of Lorenzo's rule—whether he was a tyrant or a benevolent leader—is debated.
- The cause of his death has been speculated to involve acromegaly, a disorder of excessive growth hormone.
- The rumour that Savonarola damned him on his deathbed has been refuted by historical research.
