# Cinderella

## Overview
Cinderella is a globally widespread folk tale about a mistreated young woman who, through magical aid and a unique identifying item, rises from obscurity to marry royalty. Its enduring power lies in its adaptable core narrative of injustice, transformation, and recognition, which has been retold in countless cultures and media for centuries, making it a foundational story in world folklore and popular culture.

## Timeline
- **c. 7 BC – 23 AD** — Greek geographer Strabo records the story of Rhodopis, the earliest known version of the Cinderella tale.
- **c. 175–235** — Roman orator Aelian reports the Rhodopis story, adding the pharaoh's name as Psammetichus.
- **c. 860** — Duan Chengshi writes the Chinese tale of Ye Xian in *Miscellaneous Morsels from Youyang*.
- **12th century** — Marie de France writes the lai *Le Fresne*, a variant featuring a twin sister.
- **1634** — Giambattista Basile publishes *La gatta Cenerentola*, the first literary Western version, in his *Pentamerone*.
- **1697** — Charles Perrault publishes *Cendrillon* in France, introducing the fairy godmother and glass slipper.
- **1812** — The Brothers Grimm publish *Aschenputtel* in *Grimms' Fairy Tales*, a more violent version with a magical tree.
- **1819** — The Grimms add a coda to *Aschenputtel* in the second edition, in which the stepsisters are blinded.
- **1893** — Marian Roalfe Cox publishes *Cinderella: Three Hundred and Forty-Five Variants*, a major scholarly study.
- **1950** — Walt Disney's animated film *Cinderella* is released, becoming the most widely known adaptation.

## Body

### Ancient and Non-European Roots
The tale's oldest recorded form is the Greek story of Rhodopis, a courtesan whose sandal is snatched by an eagle and delivered to the Pharaoh, leading to their marriage. Similar ancient variants include the story of Aspasia of Phocaea. Outside Europe, early versions appear in China (*Ye Xian*, c. 860), Vietnam (*Tấm and Cám*), and Korea (*Kongjwi and Patjwi*), often featuring a magical animal helper (like a fish) that is the reincarnation of the heroine's mother. These versions frequently include extended plots with reincarnation and violent revenge, contrasting with later Western adaptations.

### Major Literary Versions
The first European prose version was Giambattista Basile's *La gatta Cenerentola* (1634), set in Naples. Charles Perrault's French *Cendrillon* (1697) popularized the tale internationally, adding the fairy godmother, the pumpkin carriage, and the iconic glass slipper. The Brothers Grimm's German *Aschenputtel* (1812) is darker; help comes from a magical tree on her mother's grave, and the stepsisters mutilate their own feet to fit the golden slipper, later being blinded as punishment.

### Core Motifs and Variations
Folklorists classify Cinderella as tale type 510A, "Persecuted Heroine." Key motifs vary across cultures: the magical helper is often the heroine's dead mother (as an animal or tree), not a fairy godmother. The identifying item is usually a slipper (glass, gold, or other material), but can be a ring, anklet, or bracelet. The number of balls and the midnight curfew are not universal. In many non-Western versions, the story continues after marriage with the heroine being murdered and reborn, ultimately avenging herself on her stepfamily.

### Modern Adaptations and Legacy
The story has been adapted into countless operas, ballets, musicals (e.g., Rodgers and Hammerstein's *Cinderella*), and films. Disney's 1950 animated classic cemented the Perrault version in global consciousness. The term "Cinderella" has entered the language to describe an underdog who achieves unexpected success, particularly in sports. The narrative's themes of resilience, transformation, and justice ensure its continued relevance in literature, theatre, and film worldwide.

## Terms
- **Folk tale**: A traditional story passed down through oral tradition, often with anonymous authorship.
- **Variant**: A different version of a folk tale, sharing a core plot but differing in details, characters, or setting.
- **Motif**: A recurring element, theme, or device in a story (e.g., the glass slipper, the magical helper).
- **Tale type**: A classification system for folk tales based on shared plot elements; Cinderella is type 510A.
- **Fairy godmother**: A magical benefactor who aids the heroine, a key addition in Perrault's version.
- **Persecuted heroine**: A folk tale archetype (type 510) where a young woman is mistreated, often by family, before achieving a happy ending.
- **Patten**: A type of overshoe or sandal, used in Basile's version instead of a slipper.
- **Lai**: A short narrative poem in Old French, like Marie de France's *Le Fresne*.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Origin and diffusion**: Scholars debate whether the tale originated in a single culture (e.g., China or Greece) and spread, or arose independently in multiple regions.
- **The glass slipper**: Some suggest Perrault's "glass slipper" (*pantoufle de verre*) may be a mishearing of "squirrel fur slipper" (*pantoufle de vair*), though most believe it was a deliberate poetic invention.
- **Symbolism of the slipper**: The slipper's material (glass, gold, etc.) and its role as a unique identifier are analyzed for cultural and symbolic meaning.
- **Stepfamily hostility**: Folklorists interpret the stepmother's jealousy as competition for resources (e.g., dowries), though the tale often leaves this implicit.
- **East vs. West endings**: The violent revenge in many East Asian versions versus the forgiveness in Perrault's version reflects differing cultural values and narrative traditions.
