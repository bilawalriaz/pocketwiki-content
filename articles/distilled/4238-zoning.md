# Zoning

## Overview
Zoning is a regulatory method used by governments to divide land into districts with specific rules for development, such as permitted uses, building size, and density. It is the most common urban planning tool in developed countries, used to guide growth, separate incompatible activities, and shape the physical form of cities. Its implementation varies widely, from strict single-use separation to flexible, form-based codes, and it is often debated for its impacts on housing, equity, and urban sprawl.

## Timeline
- **c. 1046–256 BC** — Land use classification practiced in Chinese cities during the Zhou Dynasty.
- **1836** — Colonel William Light designs Adelaide, South Australia, with parklands to separate the city center from suburbs.
- **1902** — Ebenezer Howard publishes *Garden Cities of To-morrow*, proposing concentric rings of land uses.
- **1904** — Los Angeles establishes the first modern land-use zoning ordinances in the United States.
- **1916** — New York City adopts the first city-wide zoning resolution in the U.S.
- **1926** — U.S. Supreme Court upholds the constitutionality of zoning in *Village of Euclid, Ohio v. Ambler Realty Co.*
- **1973** — Lane Kendig first advocates for performance zoning.
- **2019** — Miami replaces its conventional zoning code with a smart, form-based code.

## Body

### History and Origins
The concept of separating land uses dates to antiquity, with ancient cities in China, India, and the Roman Empire classifying areas by function. The rapid urbanization of the Industrial Revolution led to new urban regulations. Planned cities like Adelaide and Ebenezer Howard's garden city concept envisioned separated land uses but were static masterplans. The first modern, regulatory zoning systems emerged in the early 20th century in the United States, with Los Angeles (1904) and New York City (1916) leading the way.

### Main Zoning Approaches
Zoning systems are broadly categorized by their primary regulatory focus.

**Use-based zoning** separates land by function. **Single-use zoning** (or Euclidean zoning, named for the 1926 court case) designates zones for one primary use (e.g., residential, commercial, industrial). It has been dominant in North America but is criticized for causing sprawl and car dependency. **Mixed-use zoning** allows compatible activities like residential and commercial to coexist, a concept advocated by Jane Jacobs and central to New Urbanism.

**Form-based zoning** regulates the physical form of buildings (height, setbacks, density) rather than their specific use, aiming to create walkable environments. The French system is largely form-based.

**Performance zoning** uses goal-oriented criteria (e.g., environmental impact) to review projects, offering flexibility but complexity. **Incentive zoning** allows developers greater density in exchange for providing public benefits like affordable housing.

### Additional Provisions and Variations
Many jurisdictions use hybrid or supplementary techniques. **Smart zoning** incorporates technology for flexibility. **Floating zones** define a district's rules but not its location until conditions are met. **Cluster zoning** groups buildings to preserve open space. **Planned unit developments (PUDs)** allow negotiated, unified projects. **Pattern zoning** offers pre-approved building designs to streamline permitting. **Overlay zones** add specific regulations (e.g., for historic districts) atop existing zones. **Transferable development rights** allow the sale of development potential from one parcel to another.

### Criticisms and Social Impacts
Zoning is criticized for increasing traffic and car use by separating daily needs, reducing economic efficiency, and limiting housing supply, which can raise costs. In the United States, single-use and density zoning are linked to racial and economic segregation, as restrictive rules can limit affordable housing options. The first U.S. zoning ordinance in Modesto, California (1885), was explicitly aimed at racial segregation. Corruption and "fiscal zoning" (using zoning to attract high-tax revenue uses) are also concerns.

### International Variations
Zoning practices differ globally. The **United Kingdom** does not use zoning; its discretionary system requires planning permission for most changes. **Houston, Texas**, has no zoning ordinances but uses private deed restrictions. **France** uses a national, form-based Code of Urbanism. **Japan** employs a 12-zone system based on intensity of use. **Australia** and **Canada** delegate zoning authority to states/provinces and municipalities, respectively.

## Terms
- **Zoning:** A method of land-use regulation where a government divides land into districts with specific rules for development.
- **Single-use (Euclidean) Zoning:** A system where each zone is designated for one primary type of use (e.g., residential, commercial).
- **Mixed-use Zoning:** A system that allows multiple compatible activities, such as residential and commercial, within the same zone or building.
- **Form-based Zoning:** A regulatory approach that focuses on the physical form and scale of buildings rather than their specific use.
- **Performance Zoning:** A flexible system that uses performance criteria (e.g., environmental impact) to evaluate proposed developments.
- **Variance:** An official exception to zoning rules, typically granted due to a property-specific hardship.
- **Overlay Zone:** A zoning district that overlaps existing zones to address a specific issue, like historic preservation or transit access.
- **Planned Unit Development (PUD):** A regulatory process or development type that groups multiple uses in a unified project, often negotiated with the local government.
- **Spot Zoning:** The controversial practice of rezoning a small parcel in a way inconsistent with the broader community plan.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Effectiveness vs. Efficiency:** Does zoning effectively guide orderly growth, or does it hinder economic efficiency and housing production?
- **Equity and Segregation:** To what extent do zoning laws, particularly single-family and density restrictions, contribute to racial and economic segregation?
- **Sprawl vs. Walkability:** How can zoning balance the need for development with the goals of reducing car dependency and creating walkable communities?
- **Reform:** Is the traditional use-based zoning model obsolete, and should it be replaced or supplemented by form-based, performance, or smart zoning approaches?
