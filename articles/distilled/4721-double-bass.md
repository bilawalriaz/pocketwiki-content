# Double bass

## Overview
The double bass is the largest and lowest-pitched bowed string instrument in the modern symphony orchestra. It is a foundational instrument in Western classical music and is also essential to jazz, blues, rockabilly, and bluegrass. Its exact lineage is debated, with features from both the older viol family and the violin family, making it a unique hybrid instrument.

## Timeline
- **15th century** — The violone, a large viol, originates in Europe.
- **c. 1763** — Joseph Haydn composes the earliest known double bass concerto (now lost).
- **18th century** — The "Viennese tuning" (A–D–F♯–A) is popular for solo bass.
- **Late 18th century** — Bassist Johann Hindle pioneers tuning the bass in fourths (E–A–D–G).
- **1821–1889** — Giovanni Bottesini, the "Paganini of the double bass," popularizes virtuoso solo playing.
- **1951** — Leo Fender releases the first commercially successful electric bass guitar.
- **1960s–2000s** — Gary Karr becomes a leading proponent of the double bass as a solo instrument.

## Body

### Terminology and Identity
The instrument has many names, reflecting its diverse roles. In classical music, it is called the **contrabass** or **string bass**. In jazz and popular music, it is commonly the **upright bass** or **acoustic bass**. The names "contrabass" and "double bass" refer to its range and its historical role of playing the cello part an octave lower. Its construction is a hybrid, with features from both the violin family (internal construction, f-holes) and the older viol family (sloped shoulders, tuning in fourths).

### Construction and Design
A typical double bass stands around 180 cm (6 feet) tall, with the 3/4 size being most common even in professional orchestras. It is usually made from maple (back, neck), spruce (top), and ebony (fingerboard). Key design features include sloped shoulders for easier access to high positions, a curved bridge and fingerboard to allow bowing of individual strings, and machine tuners instead of the friction pegs used on smaller string instruments. The instrument is supported by a sturdy metal endpin.

### Tuning and Pitch
The standard double bass is tuned in fourths: E1, A1, D2, G2 (lowest to highest). This is the same as a bass guitar and one octave lower than the lowest four strings of a standard guitar. It is a transposing instrument, notated one octave higher than it sounds. The lowest note is typically E1 (~41 Hz), but a **C extension** or a five-string bass can extend the range down to C1 (~33 Hz) or B0 (~31 Hz). The upper range can extend to D5 or higher using harmonics.

### Playing Techniques
The bass is played either with a bow (**arco**) or by plucking the strings (**pizzicato**). In orchestral and tango music, both techniques are used. In jazz, blues, and rockabilly, pizzicato is the norm. A percussive **slap style**, where strings are struck against the fingerboard, is used in rockabilly and some bluegrass. Classical players use vibrato for expression and learn various bow articulations. Jazz bassists develop virtuoso pizzicato techniques for walking basslines and solos.

### History and Evolution
The double bass is generally regarded as a modern descendant of the 15th-century violone. Its exact lineage is debated; scholar Paul Brun argues its internal construction aligns with the violin family, while professor Larry Hurst suggests it is not a true member of either family. The instrument evolved to meet orchestral demands for lower notes and greater volume. The shift from gut to steel strings in the 20th century improved pitch stability and volume, and allowed for new playing techniques.

### Use in Music Genres
In the **orchestra**, the bass section (typically 4–8 players) provides the harmonic foundation. In **jazz**, the upright bass replaced the tuba in the 1920s and is central to the rhythm section, playing walking basslines. In **bluegrass** and **country**, it is the standard bass instrument, usually plucked. In **rock and roll** and **rockabilly**, it was the original bass instrument before being largely supplanted by the electric bass guitar in the 1960s, though it has seen a revival in roots music.

## Terms
- **Arco**: Playing the instrument with a bow.
- **Pizzicato**: Plucking the strings with the fingers.
- **Transposing instrument**: An instrument whose written notes sound at a different pitch than notated; the double bass sounds an octave lower than written.
- **C extension**: An add-on that extends the fingerboard under the lowest string, allowing it to be fretted down to C1 or B0.
- **Endpin**: The adjustable metal rod with a spike or rubber tip that supports the instrument on the floor.
- **Sound post**: A small wooden dowel inside the body that transmits vibrations from the top to the back.
- **Wolf tone**: An unwanted, dissonant sympathetic vibration that can occur with certain notes.
- **Slap style**: A percussive playing technique where strings are pulled and released to strike the fingerboard.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Lineage**: Scholars are divided on whether the double bass is derived from the viol family (like the violone) or the violin family. Evidence supports both connections.
- **Amplification in Classical Music**: The use of microphones and amplifiers for double bass solos in classical settings is debated between purists who favor natural acoustics and those who support acoustic enhancement.
- **Slap Bass in Bluegrass**: The use of slap-style bass is a subject of minor controversy in the bluegrass scene, with some players considering it stylistically inappropriate for certain contexts.
