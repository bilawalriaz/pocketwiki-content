# Divan-i Shams-i Tabrizi

## Overview
The *Divan-i Kabir* (also known as *Divan-i Shams-i Tabrizi*) is a vast collection of lyric poems by the 13th-century Persian poet and Sufi mystic Rumi. Comprising over 40,000 verses, it is a cornerstone of Sufi literature, written in the aftermath of Rumi's transformative and painful relationship with his spiritual teacher, Shams-i Tabrizi. The work is celebrated for its ecstatic, trance-like style and its profound exploration of divine love and longing.

## Timeline
- **1244 C.E.** — Rumi, a jurist in Konya, meets the wandering dervish Shams-i Tabrizi.
- **1246 C.E.** — Shams abruptly leaves Konya.
- **c. 1247 C.E.** — Shams returns, then vanishes again in 1248 C.E., possibly murdered.
- **Post-1248** — Rumi writes poems praising and lamenting Shams, forming the core of the *Divan*.
- **December 1258** — Death of Salah al-Din Zarkub, to whom Rumi dedicated later poems.
- **16th Century** — Editors standardize the *Divan*'s arrangement, typically alphabetically by line's last letter.
- **1838** — First printed copy in Europe by Vincenz von Rosenzweig-Schwannau.
- **1957** — Badi al-Zaman Foruzanfar publishes a critical edition based on early manuscripts.
- **2020** — Completion of Jeffrey R. Osborne's twenty-volume English translation from Persian.

## Body

### Content and Form
The *Divan* is a compilation of lyric poems in various Eastern-Islamic styles, including 3,229 ghazals (short lyric poems), quatrains, and elegies. While predominantly in Persian, it also contains poems in Arabic and some bilingual pieces in Turkish, Arabic, and Greek. Most poems follow the ghazal form, a traditional vehicle for expressing love and mystical themes. Rumi often signed his ghazals with the pen names *Khâmush* (Silence) or *Shams-i Tabrizi*.

### Origins and Style
The collection originated from Rumi's intense bond with Shams-i Tabrizi. After Shams' final disappearance, Rumi's grief and spiritual ecstasy fueled a prolific outpouring of poetry. His style is noted for its extemporaneous, trance-like quality, which sometimes chafed against the strict metrical conventions of the ghazal form. The poems were collected by his students after his death.

### Themes and Interpretation
Though not a didactic work like Rumi's *Masnavi*, the *Divan* is deeply philosophical, centered on themes of divine love and longing. Scholarly interpretation of this theme varies. Rokus de Groot argues that Rumi ultimately rejects longing in favor of *tawhid* (divine unity), seeing longing as creating a duality that contradicts oneness with God. In this view, Rumi's use of Shams' name as a pen name signifies this unity. Conversely, Mostafa Vaziri proposes a non-Islamic "Religion of Love," interpreting Rumi's references to Shams as symbolizing a universal, incorporeal reality of love rather than a personal relationship.

### Legacy and Transmission
The *Divan* has had a wide-reaching influence. It inspired American Transcendentalists like Emerson and Whitman and later English poets through translations. Modern interpreters, such as Coleman Barks, have popularized Rumi in English, though sometimes controversially regarding accuracy. The work remains a central text for understanding Rumi's mystical philosophy and poetic innovation.

## Terms
- **Ghazal**: A form of lyric poem, often about love or mystical subjects, composed of rhyming couplets.
- **Sufi**: A mystic within Islam who seeks direct experience of God.
- **Takhallos**: The pen name a poet adopts and invokes at the end of a ghazal.
- **Tawhid**: The Islamic concept of the oneness of God.
- **Mevlevi Order**: The Sufi order founded by Rumi's followers, known for the "whirling dervish" ceremony.
- **Masnavi**: A long, didactic poem in rhyming couplets; Rumi's most famous work of this type is the *Masnavi-ye Ma'navi*.
- **Samas**: Sufi devotional ceremonies involving music, poetry, and dance.
- **Foruzanfar's edition**: The critical scholarly edition of the *Divan* published in 1957, based on early manuscripts.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Interpretation of Love**: A key debate centers on whether Rumi's poetry advocates for a personal, longing love (for Shams as a divine mirror) or a universal, impersonal "Religion of Love" that transcends Islamic theology.
- **Authenticity and Translation**: The accuracy and fidelity of popular English translations, particularly those by Coleman Barks, are contested by scholars. The process of selecting and interpreting poems for translation raises questions about preserving Rumi's original intent and context.
- **Arrangement of the Text**: The historical shift in how the poems are organized—alphabetically in Persian editions versus by metre in Turkish editions—reflects different editorial and cultural priorities in presenting Rumi's work.
