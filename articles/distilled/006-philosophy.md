# Philosophy

## Overview

Philosophy (from Ancient Greek *philosophía*, 'love of wisdom') is the systematic, rational, and critical study of fundamental questions about existence, knowledge, mind, reason, language, and value. Historically, the individual sciences (physics, psychology) were part of philosophy; they became separate disciplines in the modern era. Major traditions include Western, Arabic–Persian, Indian, and Chinese philosophy, and core branches are epistemology, ethics, logic, and metaphysics. Philosophers use diverse methods—conceptual analysis, thought experiments, and phenomenological description—and the discipline informs fields from law to politics.

## Timeline

- **6th century BCE** — Pre-Socratic philosophers (e.g., Thales) seek rational explanations of the cosmos in Ancient Greece.
- **6th century BCE** — Confucianism (Confucius) and Daoism (Laozi) emerge in China amid political turbulence.
- **563–483 BCE** — Buddhism founded by Gautama Siddhartha in India, challenging the Vedic idea of a permanent self.
- **469–322 BCE** — Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle expand philosophy to ethics, knowledge, and reality.
- **5th century CE** — Medieval Western period begins, focusing on religious topics using ancient philosophy.
- **9th century CE** — Arabic–Persian philosophy arises; Al-Kindi (801–873) is the first philosopher of this tradition.
- **11th century CE** — Neo-Confucianism systematizes Confucian teachings in China; Al-Ghazali critiques reason's reach.
- **17th century CE** — Modern Western period starts; central concern is how philosophical and scientific knowledge are created.
- **c. 1800 CE** — Modern Indian period begins, shaped by encounters with Western thought.
- **Early 20th century** — Modern Chinese philosophy begins; New Confucianism and Chinese Marxism emerge.
- **20th century** — Rapid expansion of academic philosophy; formal logic, pragmatism, and continental movements rise.

## Body

### Etymology and Conceptions

The word *philosophy* derives from the Greek *philosophía* ('love of wisdom'), from *phílos* ('loving') and *sophía* ('wisdom'). Some sources attribute the coinage to Pythagoras, but this is uncertain. The term entered English from Old French and Anglo-Norman around 1175 CE. Before the modern age, philosophy included most rational inquiry, such as natural philosophy (physics, chemistry, biology); Isaac Newton's 1687 *Principia* used this sense. The meaning narrowed toward the end of the modern period to metaphysics, epistemology, and ethics.

Precise definitions of philosophy are debated in metaphilosophy. Some define it by method (pure reasoning), others by topic (the "big questions"). Immanuel Kant united philosophy under four questions: "What can I know?", "What should I do?", "What may I hope?", and "What is the human being?" Some see philosophy as an immature science ("the midwife of the sciences"), while others emphasize conceptual analysis, thinking about thinking, or linguistic therapy (Ludwig Wittgenstein). Phenomenologists like Edmund Husserl call it a "rigorous science" investigating essences. Ancient Greek and Roman thinkers saw it as a spiritual practice of developing rational capacities, as with the Stoics aiming for *eudaimonia* (flourishing).

### History

**Western** philosophy began in Ancient Greece in the 6th century BCE with pre-Socratics like Thales of Miletus (c. 626–c. 545 BCE). Socrates (469–399 BCE), Plato (427–347 BCE), and Aristotle (384–322 BCE) expanded its scope. Later ancient movements included Epicureanism, Stoicism, Skepticism, and Neoplatonism. The medieval period (from the 5th century CE) focused on religious topics. The Renaissance (from the 14th century) revived Platonism and humanism. The modern period (17th century) examined reason and sensory experience; the Enlightenment used these to challenge traditional authorities. The 19th century saw German idealism and Marxism; the 20th century brought formal logic, pragmatism, phenomenology, existentialism, and post-structuralism, plus a rapid expansion of academic philosophy.

**Arabic–Persian** philosophy arose in the early 9th century CE, influenced by ancient Greek thinkers, to interpret the Quran. Al-Kindi (801–873) sought harmony between reason and faith; Avicenna (980–1037) built a comprehensive system; Al-Ghazali (1058–1111) critiqued reason's limits. After this classical period, influence waned until Mulla Sadra (1571–1636). Islamic modernism emerged in the 19th–20th centuries.

**Indian** philosophy integrates reality, knowledge, and enlightenment. It began in the second and first millennia BCE with the Vedas and Upanishads. Buddhism (Gautama Siddhartha, 563–483 BCE) and Jainism (Mahavira, 599–527 BCE) arose as non-Vedic teachings. The classical period (from c. 200 BCE) saw six orthodox schools of Hinduism; Advaita Vedanta (Adi Shankara, c. 700–750 CE) held that multiplicity is illusion, while Ramanuja (1017–1137) argued individual entities are real parts of unity. The modern period (from c. 1800 CE) harmonized diverse teachings, as with Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902).

**Chinese** philosophy focuses on practical social conduct, government, and self-cultivation. Confucianism (Confucius, 551–479 BCE) emphasized moral virtues; Daoism (Laozi, 6th century BCE) taught harmony with nature. Mohism developed altruistic consequentialism; Legalism stressed strong state and laws. Buddhism entered in the 1st century CE; Xuanxue (3rd century CE) added metaphysical interpretations; Neo-Confucianism (11th century) sought a metaphysical foundation for ethics. The modern period (early 20th century) saw Chinese Marxism and New Confucianism.

**Other traditions**: Japanese philosophy synthesized Shinto, Confucianism, and Buddhism; the Kyoto School (20th century) integrated Eastern spirituality with Western philosophy. Latin American philosophy moved from indigenous questions to colonial scholasticism (from c. 1550) to positivism and liberation philosophy. African philosophy was oral, focusing on community and Ubuntu; systematic African philosophy emerged in the 20th century, addressing ethnophilosophy, négritude, and postcolonialism.

### Core Branches

**Epistemology** studies knowledge: its nature, sources, limits, and value. It examines declarative knowledge (knowing facts), practical knowledge (knowing how), and knowledge by acquaintance. The influential tripartite theory—knowledge as justified true belief—faces the Gettier problem. Sources of knowledge include perception, introspection, memory, inference, and testimony; empiricists ground knowledge in experience, rationalists allow innate knowledge. The regress problem asks how justification terminates; foundationalists and coherentists offer different solutions. Philosophical skepticism doubts some or all knowledge claims.

**Ethics** (moral philosophy) studies right conduct, moral character, and institutions. Its branches are meta-ethics (nature of morality), normative ethics (theories of right and wrong), and applied ethics (specific situations). Influential schools include consequentialism (judging actions by consequences, e.g., utilitarianism), deontology (judging by adherence to duties), and virtue ethics (judging by the agent's character).

**Logic** studies correct reasoning, divided into formal logic (using artificial symbolic languages) and informal logic (using content and context). Deductive arguments guarantee their conclusions if valid (e.g., modus ponens); inductive arguments generalize from cases; abductive arguments infer the best explanation. Fallacies are incorrect forms of reasoning, formal or informal.

**Metaphysics** studies the most general features of reality: existence, objects, properties, space, time, and causation. It divides into general metaphysics (being as such) and special metaphysics (kinds of being). Ontology investigates categories of being; philosophical cosmology examines the universe as a whole. Key topics include whether reality is only physical, the problem of identity (essential vs. accidental features), and the distinction between particulars and universals.

**Other branches**: Aesthetics studies beauty and aesthetic properties. Philosophy of language examines meaning, reference, and truth. Philosophy of mind addresses the mind-body problem, with responses including materialism, idealism, dualism, and functionalism. Philosophy of religion critically reflects on religious concepts and arguments. Philosophy of science examines scientific methods and assumptions. Political philosophy investigates government, power, justice, and liberty.

### Methods and Relations

Methods of philosophy include conceptual analysis (clarifying concepts by analyzing their components), reliance on common sense and ordinary language, use of intuitions and thought experiments, reflective equilibrium (forming a coherent position), pragmatism (assessing theories by practical consequences), and phenomenology (bracketing assumptions to describe experience). Methodological naturalism emphasizes empirical approaches from the natural sciences.

Philosophy is a meta-discipline that clarifies the nature and limits of other fields. It informs bioethics (e.g., abortion), business ethics, law (evidence standards), journalism (truth and objectivity), and theology (rational belief in revelation). Logic influenced mathematics and computer science. Philosophical ideas shaped political developments: Enlightenment ideals underpinned constitutional democracy and the American and French Revolutions; Marxist philosophy influenced the Russian and Chinese Communist Revolutions; Gandhi's non-violence shaped Indian independence. Feminist philosophy (Mary Wollstonecraft, Simone de Beauvoir, Judith Butler) shaped understandings of gender and identity. Some academics reject philosophy's practical utility, holding that it is undertaken for its own sake.

## Terms

- **Epistemology**: The branch of philosophy studying knowledge, its sources, limits, and value.
- **Metaphysics**: The study of the most general features of reality, such as existence, objects, and causation.
- **Ontology**: A subfield of metaphysics investigating categories of being and what exists fundamentally.
- **Gettier problem**: Difficulties with the theory that knowledge is justified true belief, suggesting additional components are needed.
- **Consequentialism**: An ethical school judging actions by their consequences, including utilitarianism.
- **Deontology**: An ethical school judging actions by adherence to moral duties regardless of consequences.
- **Modus ponens**: A deductive rule of inference: "p; if p then q; therefore q."
- **Epoché**: The phenomenological technique of bracketing or suspending judgments about the external world.
- **Eudaimonia**: Flourishing or well-being, the goal of Stoic philosophical practice.

## Debates and open questions

- Whether philosophy has essential features or is merely an empty blanket term (studied in metaphilosophy).
- Whether definitions of philosophy are too wide (including non-philosophy) or too narrow (excluding sub-disciplines).
- Whether philosophy is a proper science, an immature science, or fundamentally distinct from science.
- Whether knowledge requires absolute certainty, as philosophical skepticism assumes.
- Whether beauty is objective or subjective; whether reality is only physical or includes mental and abstract entities.
- Whether empirical observations are neutral or theory-laden in science.
