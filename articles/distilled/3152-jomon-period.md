# Jōmon period

## Overview
The Jōmon period (c. 14,000–300 BC) was a long era in Japanese prehistory when the archipelago was inhabited by diverse hunter-gatherer and early agriculturalist populations. It is significant for producing some of the world's earliest pottery and for establishing a complex, sedentary culture that later admixed with the Yayoi people, forming the genetic and cultural foundations of modern Japan.

## Timeline
- **c. 14,500 BC** — Earliest known pottery fragments found at Odai Yamamoto I site.
- **c. 13,750 BC** — Start of the Incipient Jōmon phase.
- **c. 8,500 BC** — Start of the Initial Jōmon phase.
- **c. 5,000 BC** — Start of the Early Jōmon; population explosion and warmer climate.
- **c. 3,520 BC** — Start of the Middle Jōmon; peak of ornate pottery and cultural complexity.
- **c. 2,470 BC** — Start of the Late Jōmon; climate cooling begins.
- **c. 1,250 BC** — Start of the Final Jōmon; population contraction.
- **c. 900 BC** — Korean-type settlements with new technologies appear in western Kyushu.
- **c. 300 BC** — End of the Jōmon period; succeeded by the Yayoi culture in most of Japan.

## Body

### Origins and Genetic Legacy
The Jōmon people descended from diverse ancestors who migrated from Northeast Asia, the Korean Peninsula, China, and Southeast Asia. Genetic studies show they contributed approximately 30% of paternal ancestry and about 10-15% of maternal and autosomal ancestry to modern Japanese populations, an imbalance known as the "admixture paradox." Their genetic makeup was heterogeneous, with lineages linked to both Northeast Asia and southern regions like the Tibetan Plateau. In Hokkaido, Jōmon populations later merged to form the proto-Ainu.

### Chronology and Regional Variation
The ~14,000-year period is divided into six phases: Incipient, Initial, Early, Middle, Late, and Final. Chronology is based primarily on ceramic typology. The period's application varies regionally: it does not apply to the Ryukyu Islands, where the Shellmidden Period is used instead. In Hokkaido, the Jōmon were succeeded not by the Yayoi but by the Zoku-Jōmon (post-Jōmon) culture.

### Subsistence and Early Agriculture
Jōmon people were primarily hunter-gatherers who exploited rich marine and forest resources, including shellfish, salmon, nuts, and acorns. They achieved a high degree of sedentism, supported by abundant food. The extent of their agriculture is debated, but evidence points to the management of nut-bearing trees and the cultivation of plants like adzuki beans, which were domesticated in Japan by c. 3000–5000 BP. A domesticated peach variety, similar to modern forms, appeared by 4700–4400 BC.

### Pottery and Material Culture
Jōmon culture is defined by its cord-marked pottery, among the oldest in the world. Early vessels were small, round-bottomed bowls for boiling food. Over time, pottery became larger, more ornate (e.g., "flame style" vessels), and developed flat bottoms for stability, reflecting increasingly settled life. They also created lacquerware, figurines (*dogū*), and tools from bone, stone, and shell.

### Decline and Transition
After 1500 BC, a cooling climate led to population decline. During the Final Jōmon, increasing contact with Korea introduced new technologies like wet rice farming and metallurgy. By c. 300 BC, the Yayoi culture, named for an archaeological site near Tokyo, succeeded the Jōmon in most of Japan, leading to cultural and genetic admixture.

## Terms
- **Jōmon**: Means "cord-marked," referring to the decorative technique on pottery.
- **Sedentism**: A non-nomadic lifestyle of settled residence in one place.
- **Horticulture**: Small-scale gardening or cultivation of plants, distinct from large-scale agriculture.
- **Dogū**: Clay figurines with distinctive shapes, often with large eyes.
- **Magatama**: Curved, comma-shaped beads, often made of stone or jade.
- **Yayoi**: The culture that succeeded the Jōmon, bringing wet-rice agriculture from the Korean Peninsula.
- **Zoku-Jōmon**: "Post-Jōmon" culture in Hokkaido, distinct from the Yayoi succession.
- **Admixture paradox**: The imbalance between higher paternal Jōmon ancestry and lower maternal/autosomal ancestry in modern Japanese.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The degree to which Jōmon people practiced horticulture versus being purely hunter-gatherers is debated.
- The exact origin and migration routes of the Jōmon ancestors, and the nature of their admixture with the Yayoi, remain under study.
- Whether similarities between Jōmon culture and distant cultures (e.g., Valdivia in Ecuador) resulted from independent development or ancient contact is an open question.
