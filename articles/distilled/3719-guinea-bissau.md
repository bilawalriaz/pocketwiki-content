# Guinea-Bissau

## Overview
Guinea-Bissau is a West African nation with a complex history of pre-colonial kingdoms, prolonged Portuguese colonization, and a turbulent post-independence era marked by frequent political instability and coups. Its strategic coastal location and involvement in the Atlantic slave trade shaped its early interactions with Europe, while its modern identity is defined by a struggle for stable governance and economic development.

## Timeline
- **13th century** — Kaabu established as a province of the Mali Empire.
- **1455-1456** — First European contact by Venetian and Portuguese explorers.
- **15th-18th centuries** — Kingdom of Kaabu becomes an independent federation; slave trade dominates the economy.
- **1867** — Battle of Kansala; Fuladu forces defeat Kaabu.
- **1956** — African Party for the Independence of Guinea and Cape Verde (PAIGC) founded.
- **1963** — Guinea-Bissau War of Independence begins.
- **24 September 1973** — Independence unilaterally declared.
- **10 September 1974** — Independence formally recognized by Portugal.
- **14 November 1980** — João Bernardo Vieira overthrows President Luís Cabral in a coup.
- **12 April 2012** — Military stages a coup d'état, arresting interim president and candidates.
- **26 November 2025** — Soldiers seize power following the general election, suspending the constitution.

## Body

### Pre-Colonial Kingdoms and Societies
The region was inhabited by diverse ethnic groups like the Jola, Papel, and Balanta, with later migrations of the Mandinka and Fulani. Societies varied from decentralized village structures to kingdoms with social stratification. The Kingdom of Kaabu, initially a Mali Empire province, became a powerful independent federation ruled by a warrior elite (Nyancho) until its defeat by Fulani forces in the 19th century. The warlike Bijagos Islanders raided the mainland for captives, who were often sold into the Atlantic slave trade.

### European Contact and the Slave Trade
Portuguese explorers arrived in the mid-15th century, but control remained limited to coastal forts and trading posts. Unlicensed traders (lançados) assimilated into local societies, challenging Portugal's monopoly. The region was a significant source of enslaved people for the Atlantic trade, with wars increasingly fought to capture slaves for European purchase. This trade enriched local nobles but brought widespread insecurity. Portugal abolished slavery in 1869, replacing it with a contract labor system.

### Colonialism and the Struggle for Independence
Formal colonization as Portuguese Guinea occurred in the late 19th century after territorial disputes with other European powers. The PAIGC, led by Amílcar Cabral, launched an armed independence war in 1963. By 1973, the PAIGC controlled much of the territory, despite Cabral's assassination that year. Independence was declared in 1973 and recognized in 1974.

### Post-Independence Political Instability
Since independence, Guinea-Bissau has experienced chronic instability. The first president, Luís Cabral, was overthrown in 1980. The country transitioned to multi-party rule in the 1990s but has suffered multiple coups, civil war (1998-1999), and assassinations. No president completed a full term until 2019. The 2012 coup led to a transitional government, and the 2025 post-election coup resulted in the military suspending the constitution and arresting political leaders.

### Economy and Society
Guinea-Bissau has one of the world's lowest GDP per capita figures, with an economy dependent on agriculture, particularly cashew nut exports. Political instability has severely hampered development. The population is ethnically diverse, with Fula and Mandinka as the largest groups. Portuguese is the official language, but Guinea-Bissau Creole is the widely spoken lingua franca. Islam is the largest religion, followed by folk religions and Christianity.

## Terms
- **Lançados**: Unlicensed European traders who settled in West Africa, often assimilating into local cultures and defying colonial trade monopolies.
- **Nyancho (Ñaanco)**: The elite warrior class of the Kaabu federation, known for their cavalry skills and patrilineal lineage.
- **PAIGC**: The African Party for the Independence of Guinea and Cape Verde, the liberation movement that led the war for independence.
- **CFA franc**: A currency used by several West African countries, which Guinea-Bissau joined in 1997 for monetary stability.
- **Creole (Kriol)**: A Portuguese-based creole language that serves as the national language and primary lingua franca.
- **Harmattan**: A dry, dusty wind that blows from the Sahara, affecting the region's climate from December to April.
- **Bijagos Archipelago**: An 88-island chain off the coast, historically home to a distinct, warlike society.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source highlights ongoing scholarly and practical debates about Guinea-Bissau's future. A key question is whether its political system can achieve stability, given the military's repeated interventions and the pattern of coups. The country's economic vulnerability is compounded by its status as a suspected transshipment point for narcotics, raising concerns about it becoming a "narco-state." The long-term impact of the 2025 coup and the suspension of constitutional order on international relations and internal governance remains an open issue.
