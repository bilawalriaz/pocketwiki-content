# Communication

## Overview

Communication is commonly defined as the transmission of information, though its precise definition is disputed. Models of communication are simplified overviews of its main components and their interactions, often involving a source using a coding system to express a message, which is sent through a channel to a receiver who decodes it. The main field of inquiry is communication studies, and the topic spans human, animal, plant, and computer communication. Human communication has evolved dramatically over time, driven by new technologies from writing to the internet.

## Timeline

- **c. 40,000 years ago** — Language develops (some estimates; others consider it much older)
- **c. 9000 BCE** — Basic pictographic symbols used to represent things like farming produce
- **c. 3500 BCE** — Sumerians develop cuneiform, the first complex writing system
- **c. 2500 BCE** — Egyptians invent papyrus, improving portability of texts
- **Mid-15th century** — Johann Gutenberg popularizes mass printing, increasing circulation and literacy
- **19th–20th centuries** — Telegraphs and telephones enable fast transmission without transporting documents
- **Later 19th–20th centuries** — Wireless radio, photography, cinema, and television develop
- **20th century** — Satellites enable global broadcasting of radio and television
- **Late 20th century** — The internet becomes a milestone, enabling websites, e-mail, and social media

## Body

### Definitions

The word communication derives from the Latin verb *communicare*, meaning 'to share' or 'to make common'. Communication is usually understood as conveying a message from a sender to a receiver using a medium such as sound, written signs, bodily movements, or electricity. Sender and receiver can be individuals, groups, organizations, or nations, and an individual can also communicate with themselves.

The precise characterization is disputed. Some definitions are broad, encompassing unconscious and non-human behavior; others restrict communication to conscious human interactions. Some stress the communicator's intent, as in philosopher Paul Grice's view that communication aims to make the recipient aware of the communicator's intention. Questions include whether only successful transmissions count as communication and whether deliberate deception constitutes it. Literary critic I. A. Richards defined communication as one mind acting upon its environment to transmit its experience to another mind. Communication theorists Claude Shannon and Warren Weaver characterized it as transmission involving components like source, message, encoder, channel, decoder, and receiver. Transactional and constitutive views reject this transmission-only picture, holding that communication also creates meaning. Researchers of animal and plant communication often define communicative behavior by criteria like beneficial roles in survival and reproduction or observable responses.

### Models of communication

Models of communication are conceptual representations providing a simplified overview of the process. They are usually visual diagrams and are categorized by intended application and how they conceptualize communication. A key classification distinguishes linear transmission, interaction, and transaction models. Linear models show information flowing one way; interaction models add a feedback loop; transaction models allow sending and responding simultaneously, as with non-verbal feedback during face-to-face conversation, and hold that meaning is produced during communication.

Early 20th-century models were linear. Lasswell's model asks five questions: "Who?", "Says what?", "In which channel?", "To whom?", and "With what effect?"—identifying sender, message, channel, receiver, and effect. The Shannon–Weaver model describes a source creating a message, a transmitter translating it into a signal, noise potentially distorting it, and a receiver translating it back. It discusses making messages partially redundant to overcome noise. Other linear models include Gerbner's and Berlo's. The earliest interaction model was Wilbur Schramm's, which introduced encoding (expressing an idea using a code), decoding, feedback, and the need for overlapping fields of experience. The first transactional model was Dean Barnlund's (1970), understanding communication as "the production of meaning, rather than the production of messages."

### Human communication

Human communication is studied in anthroposemiotics. A central distinction is between verbal and non-verbal communication, and between interpersonal and intrapersonal communication.

**Verbal** communication is the exchange of messages in linguistic form. In academic discourse, it encompasses speech, writing, and sign language. Language is a conventional system of symbols and rules, with grammar governing how units combine into sentences. Human language is distinguished by its complexity and expressive power, able to refer to abstract and distant ideas. Natural languages like English developed unplanned, while artificial languages like Esperanto and C++ are purposefully designed. Verbal communication functions to exchange information, express feelings, maintain social relations, coordinate behavior, and help conceptualize the world.

**Non-verbal** communication exchanges information through non-linguistic modes like facial expressions, gestures, and postures. It often happens unintentionally and unconsciously, and frequently accompanies verbal communication. Forms include kinesics (body language, including facial expressions and oculesics), proxemics (personal space), haptics (touch), paralanguage (vocal elements like pitch and loudness), chronemics (use of time), and physical appearance. Research interest increased in the 1950s; some theorists like Ray Birdwhistell claim the majority of ideas are conveyed non-verbally. The earliest human communication, like crying and babbling, is non-verbal.

**Interpersonal** communication is between distinct people, typically dyadic. It can be synchronous (both parties send at once) or asynchronous (taking turns). Some theorists distinguish content messages (feelings about the topic) from relational messages (feelings about the relationship). Theories of its function include making sense of the world, understanding others' behavior, reducing uncertainty, and satisfying needs like belonging and being liked.

**Intrapersonal** communication is communication with oneself, manifesting externally (monologues, diaries, shopping lists) or internally (thinking, daydreaming). It helps regulate mental activity, internalize cultural norms, aid memory, and solve problems. Some theorists suggest it is more basic than interpersonal communication, as when young children use egocentric speech to direct their behavior.

**Channels** are the technical means by which messages travel, often understood in terms of the senses or technological means like books and radio waves. Physical characteristics of a channel affect what codes can be used; face-to-face communication often combines auditory and visual channels. The receiver's ability to understand may vary by channel.

**Communicative competence** is the ability to communicate effectively and appropriately. Effectiveness is achieving desired outcomes; appropriateness means meeting social standards and expectations. Components include empathy, control, flexibility, sensitivity, and knowledge, with skills like reading, writing, listening, and speaking. Barriers to effective communication include poorly expressed messages, distraction, selective perception, noise, ambiguous expressions, and cultural differences.

### Other species

Non-human communication is studied in biocommunication and biosemiotics. Researchers often use altered definitions, such as biologists Rumsaïs Blatrix and Veronika Mayer's: "the exchange of information between individuals, wherein both the signaller and receiver may expect to benefit from the exchange." A common pragmatic constraint is requiring an observable response by the receiver.

**Animals** — Animal communication is studied in zoosemiotics. It is restricted to non-verbal communication, though some theorists debate whether animals have a referential function; warning signals for different predators in vervet monkeys, Gunnison's prairie dogs, and red squirrels contradict the claim that they lack it. Forms include visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory, and gustatory communication. Functions include courtship, parent-offspring relations, social relations, navigation, self-defense, and territoriality. Two competing theories are nature theory (communication programmed into genes) and nurture theory (learned through conditioning, often via imprinting).

**Plants, fungi, and bacteria** — Plant communication is studied in phytosemiotics. Plants lack a central nervous system and have rigid cell walls, making chemical communication more prominent than visual or auditory. Biologist Richard Karban distinguishes three steps: emission of a cue, perception by a receiver, and the receiver's response. Forms include airborne communication through volatile organic compounds (VOCs), as when maple trees warn neighboring plants of herbivore attack, and communication through mycorrhizal fungi networks (the "Wood-Wide Web"). Fungi communicate via pheromones; bacteria use quorum sensing, releasing hormone-like molecules to coordinate population-wide responses like bioluminescence and biofilm formation.

**Interspecies** — Most communication is intraspecies because its purpose is usually cooperation. Interspecies communication occurs especially in symbiotic relations. Flowers use colors and symmetrical shapes to signal nectar to insects; fruit-bearing plants use color to signal ripeness to seed-dispersing animals. Humans engage in interspecies communication with pets and working animals; dogs can learn commands and react to pitch and frequency of the human voice.

### Computer communication

Computer communication concerns data exchange between devices connected through a transmission system. Channels are analog or digital, characterized by bandwidth and latency. Networks include LANs (local area networks, under one kilometer) and WANs (wide area networks like the internet), plus PANs, CANs, and MANs. Devices must follow a communication protocol governing message format, error response, and synchronization. Systems are simplex (one direction), half-duplex (one direction at a time), or full-duplex (both directions simultaneously). Cryptography can secure connections. Human-computer communication occurs through user interfaces, from early command-line interfaces to modern graphical user interfaces, often using skeuomorphism to mimic real-world objects.

### Communication studies

Communication studies is the academic discipline studying communication, distinct from semiotics in focusing on technical questions of sending and receiving rather than abstract questions of meaning. It overlaps with biology, anthropology, psychology, sociology, linguistics, media studies, and journalism. Communication was studied in Ancient Greece by Plato and Aristotle, who stressed rhetoric; Aristotle held that the goal is to persuade. It became a separate discipline in the 20th century, especially from the 1940s. Subfields include organizational, marketing, political, intercultural, development, and health communication.

### History

Communication history studies how communicative processes evolved with society, culture, and technology. It is usually divided into ages for speaking, writing, print, electronic mass communication, and the internet. Language developed around 40,000 years ago (some estimates) from grunts, cries, gestures, and facial expressions. Writing was triggered by the need for stable records of ownership and transactions in agricultural communities. Proto-writing included decorations on pottery and knots in cords. Pictograms began around 9000 BCE; cuneiform developed around 3500 BCE. Alphabetic systems solved the problem of needing many symbols. Papyrus (c. 2500 BCE), parchment, and paper improved portability. Mass printing in the mid-15th century increased circulation and literacy. Telegraphs, telephones, radio, cinema, television, satellites, and the internet each marked further milestones.

## Terms

- **Encoding/Decoding**: Expressing an idea into a message using a code (encoding) and interpreting it (decoding).
- **Channel**: The technical means by which a message travels from sender to receiver.
- **Feedback**: The receiver's response to a message, enabling two-way communication.
- **Noise**: Influences that interfere with and distort a message on its way to the receiver.
- **Kinesics**: The study of bodily behavior (body language) in conveying information.
- **Paralanguage**: Non-verbal vocal elements in speech, like pitch and loudness.
- **Quorum sensing**: Bacterial communication via hormone-like molecules to coordinate population-wide responses.
- **Volatile organic compounds (VOCs)**: Airborne chemicals plants release to warn other plants of threats.
- **Communication protocol**: A common set of conventions governing data exchange between devices.
- **Skeuomorphism**: A design concept mimicking real-world objects to make new interfaces feel familiar.

## Debates and open questions

- Whether unintentional or failed transmissions count as communication, and whether communication transmits meaning or also creates it.
- Whether only successful transmissions should be regarded as communication, and whether deliberate deception constitutes communication.
- Whether animal communication has a referential function or lacks the complexity of human language, including recursion.
- Whether communicative competence is best defined by effectiveness, appropriateness, or both.
- The precise periodization of communication history into ages is disputed.
- The extent to which animal communication is programmed into genes (nature) versus learned (nurture).
