# Atmosphere of Earth

## Overview

Earth's atmosphere is the layer of mixed gas retained by gravity around the planet's surface. It shields the surface from most meteoroids and ultraviolet solar radiation, reduces temperature extremes between day and night, keeps the planet warm via the greenhouse effect, redistributes heat and moisture through air currents, and provides the chemical and climate conditions that allow life to exist. By mole fraction, dry air contains 78.08% nitrogen, 20.95% oxygen, 0.93% argon, 0.04% carbon dioxide, and small amounts of trace gases, plus variable water vapor (about 1% at sea level). The atmosphere has a mass of about 5.15×10¹⁸ kg, three-quarters within about 11 km of the surface, with no definite boundary to space; the Kármán line at 100 km is the conventional edge of space.

## Timeline

- **Hadean eon** — First atmosphere forms from solar nebula gases; Moon-forming collision with Theia drives off volatiles
- **End of Hadean** — Crust solidification cools atmosphere; water vapor condenses into a superocean
- **~3.8 billion years ago** — Carbon dioxide dissolves and forms carbonate sediments
- **~3.5 billion years ago** — Earliest life forms appear; nitrogen becomes the major component
- **~2.4 billion years ago** — Great Oxygenation Event: free oxygen accumulates; banded iron formations end
- **2.15–2.08 billion years ago** — Gabon sediments record dynamic oxygenation fluctuations (Lomagundi-Jatuli Excursion)
- **End of Cryogenian** — Eukaryotic photoautotrophs (green and red algae) inject further oxygenation
- **Ediacaran period** — Avalon explosion: complex metazoan life proliferates
- **539 million years ago** — Phanerozoic eon begins; Cambrian explosion diversifies animal life
- **~280 million years ago** — Oxygen peaks at about 35% during the Carboniferous
- **Since 1750** — Industrial Revolution increases greenhouse gases; global temperatures rise 1.1 °C by 2011–2020 vs. 1850

## Body

### Composition

The three major constituents are nitrogen, oxygen, and argon. Water vapor accounts for roughly 0.25% of the atmosphere by mass, varying from about 10 ppm by mole fraction in the coldest regions to 5% in hot, humid air. Trace gases include greenhouse gases (carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, ozone) and noble gases (neon, helium, krypton, xenon). Xenon's concentration is much lower than other noble gases—a mystery called "the missing xenon problem," with proposals including Xe–Fe oxides in the lower mantle, xenon dioxide in silica, and reactions with Fe/Ni in the core. Aerosols from natural and industrial sources include dust, pollen, sea spray, volcanic ash, chlorine, fluorine compounds, and sulfur compounds. The average molecular weight of dry air is about 28.946 or 28.964 g/mol. Up to ~100 km, turbulence mixes gases so relative concentrations stay constant; from roughly 80–120 km, turbulent mixing yields to molecular diffusion, forming the heterosphere where lighter gases increase with altitude.

### Stratification

Air pressure and density decrease with altitude, but temperature has a more complicated profile. The temperature/altitude profile (lapse rate) provides a metric to distinguish five main layers: troposphere (0–12 km), stratosphere (12–50 km), mesosphere (50–80 km), thermosphere (80–700 km), and exosphere (700–10,000 km).

The **troposphere** is the lowest layer, extending to an average height of about 12 km (9 km at the poles, 17 km at the Equator). Temperature usually declines with altitude because the layer is mostly heated from the surface, promoting vertical mixing (from Greek *tropos*, "turn"). It contains roughly 80% of the atmosphere's mass and nearly all its water vapor, so most weather occurs here. The **stratosphere** extends from the tropopause to the stratopause at 50–55 km and contains the ozone layer. Temperatures rise with altitude due to ozone absorbing ultraviolet radiation, which restricts turbulence and mixing. This layer is unique to Earth—neither Mars nor Venus have one due to low oxygen abundances. The **mesosphere** extends from the stratopause to the mesopause at 80–85 km. It is the coldest place on Earth, averaging about −85 °C. Most meteors burn up here, and transient luminous events (lightning-induced discharges) occasionally form above thunderclouds. The **thermosphere** extends from the mesopause to the thermopause at 500–1000 km. Temperature rises with height, up to 1500 °C, due to absorption of ionizing UV and X-ray radiation, though the air is so rarefied it would not feel hot to human skin. The International Space Station orbits here (370–460 km). The **exosphere** is the outermost layer, so tenuous some scientists consider it interplanetary space. It extends from the exobase (500–1000 km) to a poorly defined boundary, with estimates from 10,000 km to about 190,000 km (halfway to the Moon). Particles follow ballistic trajectories and constantly escape; Earth loses about 3 kg of hydrogen and 50 g of helium per second.

Secondary layers include the **ozone layer** (within the stratosphere, peak concentration of 15 ppm at 32 km, about 90% of atmospheric ozone), the **ionosphere** (ionized by solar radiation, 50–1000 km during daytime, influencing radio propagation), the **homosphere/heterosphere** (divided at the turbopause at ~100 km), the **planetary boundary layer** (the troposphere's lowest part, directly affected by the surface), and the **barosphere** (where the barometric law applies, from ground to thermopause).

### Physical properties

Average sea-level pressure is defined as 101325 pascals (1 atm). Total atmospheric mass is 5.1480×10¹⁸ kg. Air pressure decreases exponentially with altitude at a rate determined by the scale height (typically 5.5–6 km up to ~80 km); each increase by this height reduces pressure by a factor of e (~2.718). Fifty percent of the atmosphere's mass is below 5.6 km, 90% below 16 km, and 99.99997% below the Kármán line. Air density at sea level is about 1.29 kg/m³, calculated from temperature, pressure, and humidity using the ideal gas law. The average surface temperature is 14 °C or 15 °C, depending on the reference.

### Optical properties

Solar radiation is subject to absorption, emission, and scattering as it propagates through the atmosphere. Clouds reflect about 20% of incoming energy globally, contributing two-thirds of the planet's total albedo. **Rayleigh scattering** causes shorter (blue) wavelengths to scatter more easily than longer (red) wavelengths, making the sky appear blue and sunsets appear red. Different molecules absorb different wavelengths: O₂ and O₃ absorb almost all radiation shorter than 300 nm; water absorbs at many wavelengths above 700 nm. The atmosphere leaves "windows" of low opacity—the optical window runs from ~300 nm to ~1100 nm, with infrared and radio windows at longer wavelengths. The atmosphere emits infrared radiation; without greenhouse gases (CO₂ and H₂O), Earth's average surface temperature would be −18 °C rather than 15 °C. The refractive index of air is close to but greater than 1, causing light bending over long paths (e.g., seeing ships just over the horizon) and mirages.

### Circulation

Atmospheric circulation is the large-scale movement of air through the troposphere, distributing heat around Earth. Its basic structure is determined by Earth's rotation rate and the difference in solar radiation between equator and poles. The flow is divided into three main convection cells: the Hadley cell (rising air at the equator, flowing poleward aloft), the Ferrel cell (mid-latitudes, ground air flowing toward the poles), and the Polar cell (high latitudes). The interfaces between cells produce jet streams—narrow, fast-moving bands flowing west to east at ~9,100 m elevation, strongest in winter. Instabilities in jet streams move weather systems. The atmosphere is also subject to waves (gravity waves, Rossby waves) and atmospheric tides triggered by non-uniform solar heating.

### Evolution of Earth's atmosphere

The **first atmosphere** (Hadean eon) consisted of solar nebula gases—primarily hydrogen and simple hydrides like water vapor, methane, and ammonia. The Moon-forming collision with Theia melted and ejected large portions of the mantle and crust, outgassing steam that eventually condensed to contribute to ocean water.

The **second atmosphere** formed as crust solidification cooled the atmosphere, condensing water vapor into a superocean. Further outgassing from volcanism and the Late Heavy Bombardment created an Archean atmosphere of nitrogen, carbon dioxide, methane, and inert gases. Carbon dioxide dissolved in water and reacted with metals during weathering to form carbonate sediments. About 3.4 billion years ago, nitrogen became the major component. The "faint young Sun paradox" asks how Earth maintained a warm climate when the early Sun put out 30% lower radiance. The Great Oxygenation Event (~2.4 billion years ago) resulted from a billion years of cyanobacterial photosynthesis; free oxygen accumulated only when production exceeded the availability of reducing substances (ferrous iron, sulfur, methane).

The **third atmosphere** reflects the influence of plate tectonics and life. Oxygen showed major variations during the Proterozoic, including a billion-year period of euxinia, until reaching a steady state of more than 15% by the end of the Precambrian. The rise of eukaryotic photoautotrophs (green and red algae) injected further oxygenation, followed by the Avalon explosion (Ediacaran) and the Cambrian explosion (539 million years ago). Oxygen fluctuated over the last 600 million years, peaking at about 35% around 280 million years ago. The cause of oxygen variation is not precisely understood; periods with more oxygen were often associated with more rapid animal development.

### Air pollution

Air pollution is the introduction of airborne chemicals, particulate matter, or biological materials that harm organisms. Population growth, industrialization, and motorization have increased airborne pollutants, causing smogs, acid rains, and pollution-related diseases. Stratospheric ozone depletion is caused chiefly by chlorofluorocarbons and other ozone-depleting substances. Since 1750, human activity has increased greenhouse gas concentrations (carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide); coupled with deforestation, this has caused global average surface temperatures to be 1.1 °C higher in 2011–2020 than in 1850, raising concerns about sea level rise, ocean acidification, glacial retreat, extreme weather, and ecological collapse.

## Terms

- **Kármán line**: The 100 km altitude conventionally marking the edge of space
- **Lapse rate**: The rate at which temperature changes with altitude, used to distinguish atmospheric layers
- **Scale height**: The altitude increase over which pressure decreases by a factor of e (~2.718)
- **Rayleigh scattering**: Preferential scattering of shorter (blue) wavelengths, explaining the blue sky and red sunsets
- **Greenhouse effect**: Heat retention caused by gases (CO₂, H₂O) that absorb and emit infrared radiation but not visible sunlight
- **Great Oxygenation Event**: The accumulation of free oxygen ~2.4 billion years ago, caused by cyanobacterial photosynthesis
- **Euxinia**: A billion-year period of anoxic, sulfidic ocean conditions during the Proterozoic
- **Albedo**: The fraction of incoming solar radiation reflected by a surface or body
- **Jet streams**: Narrow, fast-moving west-to-east air bands at ~9,100 m, formed at boundaries between convection cells
- **Heterosphere**: The region above ~100 km where gases stratify by molecular weight rather than being turbulently mixed

## Debates and open questions

- **Missing xenon problem**: Xenon's concentration is much lower than other noble gases; proposed explanations include Xe–Fe oxides in the lower mantle, xenon dioxide in silica, and reactions with Fe/Ni in the core, but no consensus exists.
- **Faint young Sun paradox**: How Earth maintained liquid water and life when the early Sun put out 30% lower radiance than today remains unresolved.
- **Oxygen variation**: The cause of atmospheric oxygen fluctuations over the last 600 million years (peaking at ~35% around 280 million years ago) is not precisely understood, though it correlates with plant carbon fixation and animal oxygen consumption.
