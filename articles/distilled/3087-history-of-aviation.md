# History of aviation

## Overview
Aviation is the science and practice of flight, encompassing the design, development, and operation of aircraft. Its history spans from ancient myths and early experiments to modern supersonic travel and space exploration, fundamentally transforming global transportation, warfare, and human connectivity.

## Timeline
- **c. 5th century BC** — Kites invented in China, the earliest man-made heavier-than-air aircraft.
- **1783** — Montgolfier brothers launch first unmanned hot-air balloon; first manned free flights follow.
- **1804** — George Cayley builds first modern model glider, establishing fixed-wing aircraft configuration.
- **1891-1896** — Otto Lilienthal makes over 2,000 controlled glider flights, publishing foundational aerodynamic research.
- **17 Dec 1903** — Wright brothers achieve first sustained, controlled, powered heavier-than-air manned flight.
- **1914-1918** — World War I sees major military use of aircraft for reconnaissance, bombing, and dogfighting.
- **1927** — Charles Lindbergh completes first solo non-stop transatlantic flight.
- **1939** — First jet aircraft, the Heinkel He 178, flies in Germany.
- **1947** — Chuck Yeager becomes first person to fly faster than the speed of sound in the Bell X-1.
- **1952** — De Havilland Comet enters service as first commercial jet airliner.
- **1969** — Boeing 747 "Jumbo Jet" and Concorde supersonic airliner have maiden flights.
- **19 Apr 2021** — NASA's Ingenuity helicopter achieves first powered flight on Mars.

## Body

### Early Concepts and Lighter-Than-Air Flight
Early human flight attempts, like tower jumping, were based on imitating birds and lacked understanding of aerodynamics. The invention of the kite in China (c. 5th century BC) was the first successful heavier-than-air flight. The modern era began in 1783 with the Montgolfier brothers' hot-air balloon and the subsequent hydrogen balloon, which provided the first human aerial platforms. These led to the development of steerable airships, or dirigibles, pioneered by Ferdinand von Zeppelin in the early 20th century. Airships dominated long-distance flight until the 1930s, when their era ended following disasters like the Hindenburg fire in 1937.

### Foundations of Aerodynamics and Gliding
Scientific study of heavier-than-air flight began in earnest with Sir George Cayley in the early 19th century. He identified the four forces of flight (lift, thrust, drag, weight) and defined the modern airplane configuration: fixed wings, fuselage, and tail. His work was advanced by researchers like Horatio Phillips (aerodynamics) and Otto Lilienthal, whose extensive glider flights in the 1890s provided crucial data on control and stability. Lilienthal's death in a crash highlighted the need for better control systems, a problem later solved by the Wright brothers.

### The Wright Brothers and the First Powered Flight
The Wright brothers' success in 1903 was due to their methodical approach. They built a wind tunnel to correct flawed aerodynamic data from predecessors and developed a three-axis control system: wing warping for roll, a movable rudder for yaw, and a forward elevator for pitch. Their first flight at Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, was short but sustained, controlled, and powered. They continued to refine their designs, with the 1905 Flyer III becoming the first practical aircraft, capable of sustained, controlled flight.

### World Wars and the Rise of the Airplane
Aircraft were first used militarily by Italy in 1911. World War I (1914-1918) accelerated development, leading to specialized fighters, bombers, and reconnaissance planes. The interwar period saw the transition from wood-and-fabric biplanes to all-metal monoplanes, driven by designers like Hugo Junkers. Commercial aviation began, and long-distance flights (e.g., Lindbergh's 1927 transatlantic crossing) captured public imagination. World War II (1939-1945) saw the introduction of jet engines, radar, and large-scale strategic bombing, cementing the airplane's central role in warfare.

### The Jet Age and Modern Aviation
Post-WWII, jet engines revolutionized both military and civilian aviation. The de Havilland Comet (1952) was the first commercial jetliner, though early models suffered fatal structural failures. The Boeing 747 (1969) made mass air travel affordable. Supersonic flight was achieved in 1947, leading to passenger services like the Concorde (1976). The late 20th century saw the rise of digital avionics, fly-by-wire controls, and composite materials. The 21st century is characterized by the widespread use of unmanned aerial vehicles (drones), advances in fuel efficiency, and the first extraterrestrial flight with NASA's Ingenuity helicopter on Mars (2021).

## Terms
- **Aerodynamics**: The study of the properties of moving air and the interaction between the air and solid bodies moving through it.
- **Dirigible**: A steerable airship, as opposed to a free-floating balloon.
- **Lift**: The force, generated by the motion of air over a wing, that acts perpendicular to the direction of motion and opposes weight.
- **Thrust**: The forward force produced by a propulsion system (e.g., propeller, jet engine).
- **Drag**: The aerodynamic force that opposes an aircraft's motion through the air.
- **Wing warping**: An early method of lateral control where the wings were twisted to change their angle of attack, used by the Wright brothers.
- **Aileron**: A hinged flight control surface on the trailing edge of a wing, used to control roll.
- **Jet engine**: A reaction engine that produces thrust by ejecting a high-speed jet of gas.
- **Fly-by-wire**: An electronic flight control system where pilot inputs are transmitted via electrical signals to computers, which then actuate control surfaces.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Claims to first flight**: The Wright brothers are officially recognized, but claims persist for earlier flights by figures like Gustave Whitehead (1901) and Richard Pearse (1903), though evidence is disputed.
- **Impact of the Wright patent war**: Historians disagree on whether the Wright brothers' aggressive patent litigation significantly impeded the development of the American aviation industry compared to Europe.
- **Future of supersonic travel**: After the Concorde's retirement, there is renewed interest in developing new, economically viable supersonic passenger aircraft.
