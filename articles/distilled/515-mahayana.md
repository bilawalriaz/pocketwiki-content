# Mahayana

## Overview

Mahāyāna ("Great Vehicle") is the largest branch of Buddhism, encompassing diverse traditions, texts, and philosophies that emerged in India around the 1st century BCE. It accepts early Buddhist scriptures but adds a vast corpus of Mahāyāna sūtras—such as the *Lotus Sutra* and *Prajñāpāramitā* texts—which promote the **bodhisattva path**: the vow to attain full Buddhahood for the benefit of all sentient beings, motivated by **bodhicitta** (the awakening mind). Mahāyāna reinterprets the Buddha as a transcendent, eternal being (described by the **Trikāya** or three-body doctrine) and introduces new figures like Amitābha and Avalokiteśvara. Philosophically, it develops the Madhyamaka theory of **emptiness (śūnyatā)**—the lack of inherent existence in all phenomena—and the Yogācāra "consciousness-only" doctrine. Though initially a minority movement in India, it flourished at monastic universities like Nalanda (5th–12th centuries) and spread via the Silk Road to become dominant in East Asia (China, Korea, Japan, Vietnam) and the Himalayas (Tibet, Mongolia, Bhutan), where the tantric **Vajrayāna** subset prevails.

## Timeline

- **c. 1st century BCE – 1st century CE** — Composition of earliest Mahāyāna sūtras (e.g., *Lotus Sutra*, *Prajñāpāramitā*) in India.
- **2nd century CE** — Lokakṣema translates early Mahāyāna texts into Chinese from Gandhāra.
- **c. 150–250 CE** — Nāgārjuna founds Madhyamaka philosophy; possible activity in Āndhra (south India).
- **c. 180 CE** — Earliest Mahāyāna inscription naming Amitābha found at Mathura (Kushan era).
- **4th–5th century CE** — Significant growth in Mahāyāna manuscript production and epigraphic evidence.
- **5th century CE** — Establishment of Nalanda monastic university under Gupta emperor Kumaragupta I.
- **c. 400–500 CE** — Translation peak in China (Kumārajīva, Dharmakṣema); rise of distinct Chinese schools (Tiantai, Pure Land).
- **7th century CE** — Xuanzang and Yijing travel to India; document coexistence of Mahāyāna and non-Mahāyāna monks.
- **8th–12th century CE** — Pala Empire patronage; Vajrayāna/tantric Buddhism dominates Indian Buddhism; Vikramashila founded.
- **c. 7th–9th century CE** — Transmission to Tibet (Padmasambhava, Śāntarakṣita); establishment of Vajrayāna as state religion.
- **12th–13th century CE** — Decline of institutional Buddhism in India; Mahāyāna thrives in East Asia and Tibet.
- **2010** — Demographic estimate: 53% East Asian Mahāyāna, 6% Vajrayāna, 36% Theravāda of global Buddhists.

## Body

### Origins and Early Development
The origins of Mahāyāna remain debated. Early Western scholarship posited a schismatic "Hīnayāna vs. Mahāyāna" split, but modern research rejects this. Theories include: **Mahāsāṃghika origins** (doctrine of the Buddha’s supramundane nature as precursor, possibly in Āndhra); **forest ascetic hypothesis** (elite monastics imitating the Buddha’s wilderness dwelling, per *Ugraparipṛcchā*); **cult of the book** (groups venerating specific sūtras); and **textual movement theory** (David Drewes: Mahāyāna was a pan-Buddhist movement focused on revealing/disseminating sūtras by *dharmabhāṇakas* (preachers), never leaving mainstream monastic structures). Crucially, Mahāyāna never had a separate **Vinaya** (monastic code); adherents belonged to early *nikāyas* (e.g., Dharmaguptaka in East Asia, Mūlasarvāstivāda in Tibet) and often shared monasteries with non-Mahāyānists. Archaeological evidence (Gregory Schopen) shows Mahāyāna was a "limited minority" with "virtual invisibility" until the 5th century.

### Growth and Institutionalization (5th–12th Centuries)
A watershed occurred in the 5th–6th centuries: manuscript production surged, epigraphic evidence appears (royal support at Shan shan, Bamiyan, Mathura), and massive monastic universities arose. **Nalanda** (founded 5th century) and **Vikramashila** (c. 783–800) became hubs for the "Golden Age of Indian Buddhist Philosophy" (1st millennium–7th century), hosting Madhyamaka, Yogācāra, Buddha-nature, and Buddhist logic (Dignāga, Dharmakīrti). Yet even at its height, Xuanzang (7th century) found fewer than 50% of Indian monks were Mahāyānists.

### Expansion Beyond India
Mahāyāna spread via Silk Road trade routes. Central Asian translators (Lokakṣema, Kumārajīva, Xuanzang) rendered texts into Chinese. From China, it radiated to Korea, Vietnam, and Japan (6th–8th centuries), spawning indigenous schools: **Tiantai**, **Huayan**, **Pure Land**, **Chan (Zen)**. Simultaneously, it moved from India to Southeast Asia (Myanmar, Sumatra, Java, Philippines). By the 4th century, Chinese pilgrims (Faxian) traveled to India for texts. In the Himalayas, the 8th-century adoption of **Vajrayāna** (tantric Mahāyāna) in Tibet created a distinct "Northern Buddhism" that later dominated Mongolia, Bhutan, and Himalayan regions.

### Doctrinal Worldview
Mahāyāna is a "loosely bound collection" unified by the **bodhisattva ideal** and the vision that Buddhas remain accessible. It accepts early doctrines (Four Noble Truths, Dependent Origination, *anātman*) but elevates Mahāyāna sūtras as definitive.

**Buddhas and the Trikāya:** Mahāyāna posits countless Buddhas (Amitābha, Akṣobhya, Bhaiṣajyaguru, Vairocana) inhabiting **buddha-fields (buddhakṣetra)**. The Buddha is **lokottara** (supramundane), with immeasurable lifespan (*Lotus Sutra*). The **Trikāya** (three bodies) resolves this: *Dharmakāya* (ultimate reality/emptiness), *Saṃbhogakāya* (enjoyment body for advanced bodhisattvas), and *Nirmāṇakāya* (emanation bodies like Śākyamuni). Buddhas are active saviors; devotion enables rebirth in pure lands (e.g., Sukhāvatī).

**Bodhisattvas and Bodhicitta:** The bodhisattva path is superior to *Śrāvakayāna* (arhat path), deemed "Hīnayāna" (inferior vehicle) for seeking only personal nirvāṇa. **Bodhicitta**—the vow to attain Buddhahood for all beings—defines the bodhisattva. High-level bodhisattvas (Avalokiteśvara, Mañjuśrī, Tārā, Maitreya) are objects of devotion. Two models exist: (1) postpone nirvāṇa until Buddhahood; (2) attain *apratiṣṭhita* (non-abiding) nirvāṇa, remaining engaged forever.

**The Bodhisattva Path:** Traditionally spans three/four *asaṃkheyyas* (incalculable eons). It begins with *bodhicittotpāda* (arising of awakening mind), cultivated via meditations on equality/exchange of self/others (Śāntideva) or past-life connections (Atisha). Practitioners take **bodhisattva vows** (*praṇidhāna*) and practice **pāramitās** (perfections): usually six (generosity, ethics, patience, effort, meditation, wisdom), sometimes ten (adding skillful means, vow, power, knowledge). **Prajñā** (wisdom realizing emptiness) is paramount.

**Bhūmis (Stages):** Texts map progress via *bhūmis*: *Daśabhūmika* gives ten (linked to ten pāramitās); *Bodhisattvabhūmi* gives seven/thirteen; *Avataṃsaka* gives forty. In later scholasticism (Kamalaśīla, Atiśa), the **five paths** (accumulation, preparation, seeing, meditation, no-more-learning) merge with ten *bhūmis*. The first *bhūmi* (path of seeing) requires direct, non-conceptual insight into emptiness, making one an *ārya* (noble being).

**Upāya and Ekayāna:** **Upāya** (skillful means) explains the Buddha’s adapted teachings—even contradictory ones—as compassionate expedients. The **Ekayāna** (One Vehicle) doctrine (*Lotus Sutra*) asserts the three vehicles are ultimately one, all leading to Buddhahood. **Yogācāra** dissented, arguing not all beings have the "seed" for Buddhahood.

### Core Philosophical Schools

**Prajñāpāramitā (Perfection of Wisdom):** Earliest Mahāyāna texts. Teach **śūnyatā** (emptiness): all *dharmas* lack *svabhāva* (inherent nature), are unarisen (*anutpāda*), illusory (*māyā*), dream-like. Famous metaphors: star, bubble, lightning (*Diamond Sutra*). Wisdom entails **non-grasping** (*aparigṛhīta*)—not clinging even to Buddhist concepts. *Kṣānti* (receptivity/patient acceptance of non-arising) is a key virtue.

**Madhyamaka (Centrism/Emptiness Theory):** Founded by **Nāgārjuna** (c. 150–250 CE). Refutes all *svabhāva* using **dependent origination**: inherent existence contradicts dependent arising. Employs **two truths**: conventional (*saṃvṛti*) existence vs. ultimate (*paramārtha*) emptiness. Emptiness itself is empty (*śūnyatā śūnyatā*); it is a therapeutic tool, not an absolute. All is conceptual construction (*prajñapti-mātra*); concepts must be relinquished.

**Yogācāra / Vijñānavāda (Consciousness-Only):** Systematized by **Asaṅga** and **Vasubandhu** (4th–5th century). *Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra* claims to be the "third turning." Ultimate truth: all is *citta-mātra* (mind-only) or *vijñapti-mātra* (perceptions-only); no external objects exist apart from the dependently originated mental flow. Key models: **eight consciousnesses** (including *ālayavijñāna*, storehouse consciousness) and **three natures** (imagined, dependent, perfected). Realizing non-dual *tathatā* (Thusness) is nirvāṇa. Critiqued Madhyamaka as veering toward nihilism.

**Buddha-nature (Tathāgatagarbha):** *Tathāgatagarbha Sūtra*, *Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra*. All beings possess an innate "Buddha-womb/embryo"—eternal, pure, replete with virtues—obscured by defilements. Interpreted variously: as positive expression of emptiness/potential (Williams); as skillful means using *ātman*-language to counter nihilism (*Laṅkāvatāra*); or as an affirmative eternal Self (Zimmermann, Hookham). Central to all modern Mahāyāna traditions.

### Authenticity and Self-Definition
Mahāyāna faced critiques that its sūtras were not the Buddha’s word (*buddhavacana*). Defenses included: (1) teachings were too profound for the Buddha’s era, hidden by *nāgas*/bodhisattvas until ready; (2) revealed via visions/dreams to later masters; (3) "well-spoken" (*subhāsita*) = Buddha-word if it accords with Dharma (Śāntideva, D.T. Suzuki). Mahāyāna claimed superiority via "greatness" of support, practice, understanding, energy, resourcefulness, attainment, and deeds (*Abhidharmasamuccaya* commentary). Later sūtras grew sectarian, denigrating *śrāvakas* as "Hīnayāna"; early ones (*Ugraparipṛcchā*) did not.

### Practice
**Merit (*puṇya*)** is the "universal currency" for Buddhahood. Key practices:
- **Devotion/Ritual:** *Pūjā* (offerings), circumambulation, chanting (Heart Sutra, *dhāraṇīs*), **Seven-part worship** (vandana, pūjā, refuge, confession, rejoicing, prayer, dedication/transfer of merit).
- **Text-centered:** Reciting, copying, venerating, teaching sūtras.
- **Bodhisattva Vows & Precepts:** Formal commitment.
- **Pāramitās:** Cultivation of the six/ten perfections.
- **Meditation:** Shared early methods (mindfulness, *śamatha-vipaśyanā*, four foundations) plus distinct Mahāyāna forms: **Buddha visualization/mindfulness** (*buddhānusmṛti*) for pure land access or visionary encounters; **Pure Land *nianfo/nembutsu*** (Amitābha name-recitation); **Chan/Zen** (*huatou*, *kōan*, silent illumination/*shikantaza*); **Tibetan** *tonglen*, *lojong*, *samatha-vipaśyanā*.
- **Vajrayāna/Tantra:** Separate category requiring initiation (*abhiṣeka*). Deity Yoga, *Dzogchen*, *Mahāmudrā*, mantras, *mudrās*, *maṇḍalas*. Dominant in Tibet; present in East Asian Esoteric Buddhism (Shingon, Tendai).

### Scripture and Hermeneutics
Mahāyāna accepts early **Āgamas/Nikāyas** but prioritizes **Mahāyāna sūtras** (called *vaipulya* "extensive" or *gambhira* "profound" initially). Key texts: *Prajñāpāramitā* corpus, *Lotus*, *Vimalakīrti*, *Avataṃsaka*, *Saṃdhinirmocana*, *Tathāgatagarbha*, Pure Land sūtras. Asaṅga (*Abhidharmasamuccaya*, 4th century) classifies early canon as *Śrāvakapiṭaka* and Mahāyāna sūtras as *Bodhisattvapiṭaka*. Massive commentarial literature (*śāstra*, *vṛtti*, *kārikā*) includes Nāgārjuna’s *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*, Vasubandhu’s *Abhidharmakośa* (non-Mahāyāna but foundational) and Yogācāra works, Dignāga/Dharmakīrti’s logic/epistemology.

**Three Turnings of the Dharma Wheel** (*Saṃdhinirmocana*):
1. **First:** Four Noble Truths at Vārāṇasī (Śrāvaka vehicle); requires interpretation.
2. **Second:** *Prajñāpāramitā* (emptiness, no-arising) for bodhisattvas; requires interpretation. Exemplar: Madhyamaka.
3. **Third:** *Saṃdhinirmocana* (Yogācāra, Buddha-nature); explicit, no interpretation needed. (Tibetan Gelug often ranks second turning highest; some tantric traditions claim Vajrayāna as third/fourth turning).

**Continuity with Early Texts:** Nāgārjuna cites *Kātyāyana Sūtra*; Yogācāra cites *Lesser Discourse on Emptiness*. Both schools claimed to preserve the Middle Way against nihilism (Madhyamaka critique of Yogācāra) and substantialism (Yogācāra critique of Madhyamaka). *Bodhicitta* and Buddha-nature language echoes early "luminous mind" passages.

### Contemporary Forms
- **East Asian Mahāyāna ("Eastern Buddhism"):** ~360 million. China (Chan, Pure Land, Tiantai, Huayan, Humanistic Buddhism; 228–239M), Japan (Pure Land, Zen, Nichiren, Shingon, new religions; 52–84M), Vietnam (Thiền, Tịnh Độ, Plum Village; 43M), Korea (Seon/Zen, 10.9M), plus diaspora in Taiwan, Malaysia, Singapore, Philippines, Indonesia.
- **Indo-Tibetan/Vajrayāna ("Northern Buddhism"):** ~18.2 million. Tibet (5.4M), Mongolia (2.7M), Inner Mongolia (5M), Nepal (2.9M), Bhutan (0.49M), Himalayan India/Russia. Revived post-1980s after communist suppression.
- **Minor/New Movements:** Newar (Nepal), Azhali (Yunnan), Won Buddhism, Triratna, Sōka Gakkai.
- **Syncretic Traditions:** Bon, Shugendō, Shinbutsu-shūgō (Shinto-Buddhist syncretism), Chinese salvationist religions.

### Relationship to Theravāda
**Bodhisattva Role:** Theravāda views Buddhahood as a rare, long-path aspiration for few; Mahāyāna universalizes it. Some modern Thai Theravāda masters are popularly regarded as bodhisattvas.
**Hīnayāna Label:** Xuanzang (7th c.) labeled Sri Lankan *Mahāvihāra* monks "Hīnayāna Sthaviras." Modern scholars debate applicability: Theravāda rejects *Sarvāstivāda* substantialism (the main Madhyamaka target) and upholds non-substantialism (*Kathāvatthu*). Some contemporary Theravādins express sympathy for *Heart Sutra* and Nāgārjuna.

## Terms

- ****Bodhicitta**** — The altruistic "awakening mind": the vow to attain full Buddhahood for the benefit of all sentient beings; the defining motivation of the bodhisattva path.
- ****Bodhisattva**** — A being (*sattva*) intent on awakening (*bodhi*); in Mahāyāna, one who has generated bodhicitta and vows to remain in *saṃsāra* to liberate all beings.
- ****Buddha-nature (Tathāgatagarbha)**** — The innate potential or "embryo" of Buddhahood present in all sentient beings; described as eternal, pure, and replete with virtues, though interpretations vary (potentiality vs. affirmative Self).
- ****Dharmakāya**** — The "Truth Body" or "Reality Body": the ultimate nature of a Buddha, identified with emptiness (*śūnyatā*) or Thusness (*tathatā*); the ground of the other two bodies.
- ****Ekayāna**** — "One Vehicle": the doctrine (*Lotus Sutra*) that the three vehicles (Śrāvaka, Pratyekabuddha, Bodhisattva) are ultimately skillful means (*upāya*) converging on the single goal of Buddhahood.
- ****Madhyamaka**** — "Centrism": the philosophical school founded by Nāgārjuna that deconstructs all views of inherent existence (*svabhāva*) using the logic of dependent origination and the two truths.
- ****Prajñāpāramitā**** — "Perfection of Wisdom": a genre of early Mahāyāna sūtras and the transcendent, non-conceptual wisdom realizing the emptiness (*śūnyatā*) and illusory nature
