# Ingrid Bergman

## Overview
Ingrid Bergman (1915–1982) was a Swedish actress whose five-decade career established her as one of cinema’s most influential figures. She is one of only four actresses to win three competitive Academy Awards for acting (two Best Actress, one Best Supporting Actress) and the second to achieve the Triple Crown of Acting (Oscar, Emmy, Tony). Bergman rose to fame in Swedish and German films before David O. Selznick brought her to Hollywood for *Intermezzo* (1939). Her natural, luminous style—minimal makeup, emotional restraint, and a polyglot fluency in five languages—defined roles in *Casablanca* (1942), *Gaslight* (1944), and three Alfred Hitchcock thrillers. In 1950, her affair with Italian director Roberto Rossellini during *Stromboli* caused a massive U.S. scandal, including Senate denunciation, exiling her to Europe for seven years. There, she made psychologically complex, commercially unsuccessful but critically seminal neorealist films with Rossellini (*Europa '51*, *Journey to Italy*). Her Hollywood return with *Anastasia* (1956) won a second Oscar. Later highlights include *Murder on the Orient Express* (1974, third Oscar) and Ingmar Bergman’s *Autumn Sonata* (1978, final nomination). She worked until months before dying of breast cancer on her 67th birthday.

## Body

### Early Life and Swedish Beginnings
Born in Stockholm, Bergman lost her mother at age two and her father at 14, a trauma she later called "living with an ache." Her father, a photographer, documented her childhood extensively. She attended the prestigious Palmgrenska Samskolan but was an indifferent student. A scholarship to the Royal Dramatic Training Academy (where Greta Garbo had studied) led to a professional stage role after only months—against school procedure—and a contract with Svensk Filmindustri. Between 1935 and 1938 she made eleven Swedish films under director Gustaf Molander, notably *Intermezzo* (1936) and *A Woman's Face* (1938), the latter requiring heavy burns makeup and earning a Venice Festival Special Recommendation. A brief UFA contract in Germany produced one film, *The Four Companions* (1938), before she returned to Sweden to give birth to daughter Pia. Critics noted her characters’ uncertainty and anxiety, and her work with Swedish luminaries like Gösta Ekman and Victor Sjöström.

### Hollywood Breakthrough and Wartime Stardom (1939–1949)
David O. Selznick signed Bergman for the English remake *Intermezzo: A Love Story* (1939), protecting her natural look from studio makeup artists. The film’s success launched her U.S. career. Loaned to other studios, she appeared in *Adam Had Four Sons*, *Rage in Heaven*, and *Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde* (all 1941), insisting on the "bad girl" role in the last. *Casablanca* (1942) cemented her stardom, though she later considered it overrated. *For Whom the Bell Tolls* (1943) brought her first Oscar nomination; Hemingway had insisted she play Maria. *Gaslight* (1944) won her first Academy Award for a restrained portrayal of a wife driven toward madness. *The Bells of St. Mary's* (1945) opposite Bing Crosby became 1945’s top grosser and earned a third consecutive nomination. She began a three-film collaboration with Alfred Hitchcock: *Spellbound* (1945), *Notorious* (1946)—widely considered a masterpiece—and *Under Capricorn* (1949). Simultaneously, she triumphed on Broadway in Maxwell Anderson’s *Joan of Lorraine* (1946–47), winning a Tony Award. The 1948 film *Joan of Arc*, based on the play, flopped amid the emerging Rossellini scandal.

### The Rossellini Scandal and Italian Neorealist Period (1950–1955)
Admiring Rossellini’s *Rome, Open City* and *Paisan*, Bergman wrote to him in 1949. Cast in *Stromboli* (1950), she began an affair and became pregnant. The U.S. reaction was vitriolic: Senator Edwin C. Johnson denounced her on the Senate floor as "a powerful influence for evil"; Ed Sullivan banned her; hate mail arrived by the bag. She divorced Lindström (who had refused a divorce for years, citing her income), married Rossellini, and remained in Italy. *Stromboli* lost money for RKO and was savaged by U.S. critics (Bosley Crowther called it "incredibly feeble"), though it won Italy’s Rome Prize. Later reassessments (Criterion, *Sight & Sound*) rank it a pioneering modernist work. Rossellini directed her in *Europa '51* (1952), the documentary segment *Siamo donne* (1953), *Journey to Italy* (1954)—credited by Godard and Scorsese as launching the French New Wave—and *Fear* (1954). Rossellini’s neorealist methods (no script, minimal comforts, psychological introspection) clashed with her star status; critics accused him of abandoning neorealism and her of ruining her career. The marriage deteriorated; Rossellini had an affair in India (1957), and they divorced.

### Hollywood Return and Middle Period (1956–1972)
*Anastasia* (1956), filmed in Europe for Fox, won her a second Best Actress Oscar (accepted by Cary Grant) and signaled full U.S. rehabilitation—confirmed by a standing ovation presenting at the 1959 Oscars. She starred in Stanley Donen’s *Indiscreet* (1958) with Grant, *The Inn of the Sixth Happiness* (1958), and won an Emmy for the TV drama *The Turn of the Screw* (1960). Stage work continued: *Hedda Gabler* (BBC/CBS, 1962; Paris stage), *The Visit* (1964), *A Month in the Country* (West End, 1965), and Jean Cocteau’s *The Human Voice* (TV, 1966). *Cactus Flower* (1969) with Walter Matthau and Goldie Hawn showcased comic timing. *A Walk in the Spring Rain* (1970) and Shaw’s *Captain Brassbound's Conversion* (London, 1971) followed. In 1971, Senator Charles Percy entered a formal Congressional apology for the 1950 Senate attack.

### Final Years and Late Masterpieces (1973–1982)
Bergman served as 1973 Cannes jury president. *Murder on the Orient Express* (1974) brought a third Oscar (Best Supporting Actress) for a five-minute, single-take scene as missionary Greta Ohlsson—she had refused the larger Princess Dragomiroff role. She accepted a Lifetime Achievement Award for Jean Renoir at the 1975 Oscars. France awarded her the first Honorary César (1976). *Autumn Sonata* (1978), written for her by Ingmar Bergman (no relation), paired her with Liv Ullmann as a pianist confronting her neglected daughter. Filming accommodated her breast cancer surgeries. Critics called it a career peak: "a tour-de-force such as the cinema rarely sees." Her final role, *A Woman Called Golda* (1982), required her to perform with a mastectomy-swollen arm; she won a posthumous Emmy. She died on her 67th birthday in London; ashes were scattered off Fjällbacka, Sweden, and interred beside her parents in Stockholm.

### Acting Style, Persona, and Public Image
Bergman’s hallmark was "naturalness"—minimal makeup, emotional restraint, and a face that, in close-up, conveyed micro-shifts of thought. Hitchcock coached her toward "less is more," quelling gestures to concentrate expression in the eyes. Critics noted her "downcast eyes" (*Casablanca*, *Gaslight*) and ability to "peer into" a co-star’s face, reflecting his emotions. Her polyglot fluency (Swedish, German, English, Italian, French) added vocal texture, though some felt she was less effective in French/German. On-screen she often played "fallen women" (*Intermezzo*, *Casablanca*, *Dr. Jekyll*), yet her saintly roles (*Joan of Arc*, *The Bells of St. Mary's*) created a public "Saint Ingrid" image—deliberately crafted by Selznick—that made the Rossellini scandal feel like betrayal. Bergman herself rejected the saint label: "I'm just a woman, another human being." Ingmar Bergman (director) found her stubborn, resisting direction until convinced; she prepared intensely (mental hospital visit for *Gaslight*, tapes for *Golda*).

### Activism and Personal Life
In 1946, Bergman protested racial segregation at Washington’s Lisner Auditorium during *Joan of Lorraine*, drawing hate mail; a bust now honors her there. She entertained troops in Alaska and Europe (1945), but refused to visit a concentration camp, a guilt she carried lifelong. She felt early guilt for dismissing Nazism as a "temporary aberration" while filming in Germany (1938). Marriages: Petter Lindström (1937–1950, daughter Pia); Roberto Rossellini (1950–1957, son Renato, twins Isotta and Isabella); Lars Schmidt (1958–1975, no children). Affairs included Spencer Tracy, Gary Cooper, Robert Capa (photographer, dramatized in *Rear Window* and novel *Seducing Ingrid Bergman*), and Gregory Peck. She was Lutheran, 5'9" (taller than Bogart, Rains; blocks/ramps used in scenes).

### Legacy and Cultural Impact
At death, front-page obituaries worldwide praised her "innocent yet provocative beauty" and courage. Co-stars (Grant, Peck, Ullmann, Cotten) lauded her tenacity. The 1983 Venice Festival honored her; Wesleyan University holds her archive. Centennial celebrations (2015) included MoMA/AFI retrospectives, a Cannes-premiered documentary (*Ingrid Bergman: In Her Own Words*), joint US/Swedish stamps, and global screenings. She inspired the *Mission: Impossible* character Ilsa Faust (Rebecca Ferguson studied *Notorious*), appears in *La La Land* posters/soundtrack, and has a rose, a plane, a square (Fjällbacka), and murals named for her. Woody Guthrie’s 1950 song "Ingrid Bergman" (covered by Billy Bragg/Wilco) and Hitchcock’s *Rear Window* (based on her Capa affair) attest to cultural penetration. Biographer Donald Spoto called her "arguably the most international star in the history of entertainment."

## Terms
- **Triple Crown of Acting**: Competitive wins of an Academy Award, Emmy Award, and Tony Award in acting categories. Bergman was the second actress (1960) and youngest performer to achieve it.
- **Neorealism**: Italian post-war film movement using non-professional actors, location shooting, and social themes. Rossellini applied its methods to Bergman, a star, creating "psychological introspection" films.
- **Volpi Cup**: Venice Film Festival Best Actress award. Bergman won for *Europa '51* (1952) but was denied it because her voice was dubbed; awarded posthumously in 1992.
- **Chiaroscuro**: Lighting technique using strong contrasts to model form and mood. Used in Bergman’s films (*Casablanca*, *Gaslight*) to externalize inner turmoil through her face.
- **Saint Ingrid**: Media/public persona of moral purity built by Selznick around Bergman’s saintly roles and "idyllic home life," which intensified the 1950 scandal’s backlash.
- **Polyglot**: Speaker of multiple languages. Bergman acted in five: Swedish, German, English, Italian, French.
- **Method acting (implied contrast)**: Bergman’s preparation was external/observational (mental hospital, tapes) rather than internal/psychological immersion associated with the Method.
- **Honorary César**: French national film award created in 1976; Bergman was its first recipient.
- **David di Donatello**: Italian film academy award; Bergman received its Golden Medal (1982).
- **Illis Quorum**: Swedish royal medal for cultural significance; awarded to Bergman (1979).

## Debates and open questions
- **Rossellini collaboration: career suicide or artistic breakthrough?** Contemporary U.S. critics saw commercial failure and neorealist betrayal; modern critics (Kehr, Cronk, *Sight & Sound*) rank *Stromboli* and *Journey to Italy* as foundational to modern European cinema and the French New Wave.
- **Hitchcock’s "resistant blonde":** Scholars debate whether Bergman fits the "Hitchcock blonde" archetype. Susan White argues she is a "resistant and defiant blonde" (vs. Grace Kelly’s malleability), complicating auteur readings of *Notorious* and *Spellbound*.
- **Scandal’s xenophobic/gendered dimensions:** The 1950 Senate denunciation and press vitriol ("Dirty whore," "Latin lover" tropes) reflect post-war conservatism, anti-immigrant sentiment, and punishment of female sexuality clashing with a manufactured saintly image.
- **Language and performance:** Critics (Callahan, Bastién) note Bergman’s voice/accent as a "secret weapon" in English/Swedish but detect diminished creative energy in French/German roles—raising questions about linguistic embodiment in acting.
- **Autumn Sonata as swan song:** Whether Bergman’s final film represents her greatest work (Kaufmann, *Newsweek*, *The Times*) or a fitting capstone to a career already defined by *Gaslight* and *Notorious* remains a critical touchstone.
- **Selznick’s responsibility:** Selznick later admitted, "I'm afraid I'm responsible for the public's image of her as Saint Ingrid... I guess that backfired later," framing the scandal as a studio-created trap.
