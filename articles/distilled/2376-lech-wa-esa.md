# Lech Wałęsa

## Overview
Lech Wałęsa is a Polish statesman and Nobel Peace Prize laureate who led the Solidarity movement, the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc. His leadership was instrumental in ending Communist rule in Poland in 1989, a pivotal event that helped conclude the Cold War. He served as Poland's first democratically elected president from 1990 to 1995, guiding the country's transition to a market democracy.

## Timeline
- **29 September 1943** — Born in Popowo, Poland, under Nazi occupation.
- **14 August 1980** — Instigates and leads a strike at the Lenin Shipyard in Gdańsk.
- **31 August 1980** — Signs the Gdańsk Agreement, granting workers the right to an independent union.
- **1980** — Co-founds the Solidarity trade union, which grows to over 10 million members.
- **13 December 1981** — Arrested after General Jaruzelski imposes martial law and outlaws Solidarity.
- **1983** — Awarded the Nobel Peace Prize; his wife accepts on his behalf.
- **February–April 1989** — Leads Solidarity in the Round Table Negotiations with the Communist government.
- **June 1989** — Solidarity wins a landslide victory in semi-free parliamentary elections.
- **December 1990** — Wins Poland's first direct presidential election, becoming head of state.
- **1995** — Narrowly loses the presidential election to Aleksander Kwaśniewski.
- **2000** — Officially cleared of secret police collaboration by a lustration court.
- **16 February 2016** — Institute of National Remembrance seizes documents from a deceased minister alleging his collaboration.

## Body

### Early Life and Activism
Born to a carpenter who died from exhaustion after Nazi internment, Wałęsa trained as an electrician. He began work at the Lenin Shipyard in 1967, becoming a trade-union activist. His involvement in illegal protests and commemoration of 1970 shipyard massacre victims led to his 1976 dismissal and repeated arrests. He worked with the underground Workers' Defence Committee (KOR).

### The Solidarity Movement
On 14 August 1980, a price hike sparked a strike at the Lenin Shipyard. Wałęsa climbed the fence to join and quickly became a leader. The strike spread nationwide, and he headed the Inter-Enterprise Strike Committee. The resulting Gdańsk Agreement, signed on 31 August, legalized independent trade unions. Solidarity's membership surged to over ten million. After martial law was declared in 1981, Wałęsa was arrested for 11 months. Following his release, he continued underground activism. In 1988, he instigated new strikes leading to the Round Table Negotiations (1989), which established Solidarity's legal status and paved the way for semi-free elections. Solidarity's June 1989 victory led to the first non-Communist government in the Soviet bloc.

### Presidency and Transition
Wałęsa won the 1990 presidential election, becoming Poland's first freely elected head of state in 63 years. His presidency oversaw the successful Balcerowicz Plan for economic transition, the withdrawal of Soviet troops, and Poland's initial steps toward NATO and EU membership. However, his confrontational style led to political infighting and isolation. He was criticized for an authoritarian approach and for surrounding himself with unpopular advisors. His popularity waned during the difficult economic transition, and he narrowly lost the 1995 election.

### Post-Presidency and Legacy
After his presidency, Wałęsa founded the Lech Wałęsa Institute (1995) and embarked on a global lecture circuit. His political influence diminished after a poor showing in the 2000 election. He remained a vocal international figure, commenting on global politics, though some statements on LGBT rights and migration drew controversy. He later expressed support for same-sex marriage and Ukraine's defense against Russian invasion.

### Secret Police Allegations
A major controversy concerns allegations that Wałęsa was an informant for the Communist Security Service (SB) under the codename "Bolek" from 1970 to 1976. A 2008 book by historians Sławomir Cenckiewicz and Piotr Gontarczyk presented archival documents purporting to prove this. In 2016, the Institute of National Remembrance seized a dossier from a former interior minister's widow containing documents, including a handwritten commitment to cooperate. A 2017 handwriting study confirmed the signatures were his. However, a 2018 INR investigation was discontinued, concluding no crime had been committed. Wałęsa vehemently denies being a paid informant, calling the documents forgeries, though he has made conflicting statements about signing papers under duress. A 2000 lustration court had previously cleared him due to insufficient evidence.

## Terms
*   **Solidarity (Solidarność):** An independent trade union and social movement founded in 1980, the first of its kind in the Soviet bloc, which played a key role in ending Communist rule.
*   **Gdańsk Agreement:** A 1980 accord between striking workers and the Polish government that granted the right to form independent trade unions.
*   **Martial Law:** Imposed in Poland on 13 December 1981 by General Wojciech Jaruzelski, it outlawed Solidarity and led to mass arrests.
*   **Round Table Negotiations:** Talks held from February to April 1989 between the Communist government and Solidarity, which led to semi-free elections and a power-sharing agreement.
*   **Lustration:** The process of vetting public officials for past collaboration with the Communist secret police, often involving access to secret police files.
*   **Balcerowicz Plan:** The economic stabilization and transformation program implemented in Poland in the early 1990s to transition from a planned to a market economy.
*   **Institute of National Remembrance (INR/IPN):** A Polish state institution responsible for researching and documenting crimes of Nazi and Communist regimes, including managing secret police archives.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Nature of Collaboration:** The central debate is whether Wałęsa was a willing, paid informant for the Security Service from 1970-1976, as alleged by historians and some documents, or if any signatures were forged or signed under duress without subsequent action.
*   **Destruction of Evidence:** Allegations persist that during his presidency, Wałęsa used his office to remove incriminating documents from archives, a claim he denies.
*   **Impact on Legacy:** Scholars and the public continue to debate how the collaboration allegations, if proven true, should affect the historical assessment of his role in Poland's liberation from Communism.
