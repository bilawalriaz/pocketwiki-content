# History of physics

## Overview
Physics is the branch of science studying matter and energy. Its history traces the shift from philosophical speculation to a mathematical, experimental science, culminating in the 20th-century revolutions of relativity and quantum mechanics, which redefined our understanding of space, time, and the fundamental constituents of the universe.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BCE** — Early civilizations (Sumerian, Egyptian) develop predictive astronomy.
- **c. 310–230 BCE** — Aristarchus of Samos proposes a heliocentric model of the Solar System.
- **c. 287–212 BCE** — Archimedes lays foundations of hydrostatics, statics, and the mathematics of the lever.
- **1543** — Nicolaus Copernicus publishes *De revolutionibus*, advocating a heliocentric model.
- **1609–1610** — Galileo Galilei makes key telescopic discoveries and formulates laws of motion.
- **1687** — Isaac Newton publishes *Principia Mathematica*, establishing classical mechanics and universal gravitation.
- **1865** — James Clerk Maxwell publishes *A Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism*, unifying electromagnetism.
- **1900** — Max Planck introduces the quantum hypothesis to explain blackbody radiation.
- **1905** — Albert Einstein publishes papers on special relativity and the photoelectric effect.
- **1915** — Einstein completes the general theory of relativity.
- **1925–26** — Werner Heisenberg and Erwin Schrödinger formulate matrix and wave mechanics, respectively.
- **2012** — The Higgs boson is discovered at CERN's Large Hadron Collider.

## Body

### Ancient Foundations
Early physics was intertwined with astronomy, optics, and mechanics, studied through geometry. Ancient Babylonian and Egyptian cultures tracked celestial bodies for predictive purposes. In Greece, the move toward rational explanation began with Pre-Socratic philosophers like Thales. Aristotle (384–322 BCE) founded a system of "physics" based on the four elements and qualitative explanations for motion, which dominated Western thought for centuries. Key advances came from Archimedes, who mathematically described principles of buoyancy and the lever, and from Aristarchus, who proposed a heliocentric model. Similar traditions of atomism and astronomy developed in ancient India and China.

### The Scientific Revolution (16th–17th Centuries)
A major shift began with Nicolaus Copernicus's 1543 heliocentric model, which was refined by Johannes Kepler's laws of planetary motion. Galileo Galilei championed the experimental method and mathematical description of motion, challenging Aristotelian physics. The revolution culminated with Isaac Newton, whose *Principia Mathematica* (1687) formulated the three laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation, creating a unified system for terrestrial and celestial mechanics. This period also saw foundational work in fluid dynamics (Evangelista Torricelli) and optics (Christiaan Huygens).

### 18th and 19th Century Developments
The 18th century saw the elaboration of Newtonian mechanics into classical mechanics by mathematicians like Leonhard Euler and Joseph-Louis Lagrange. The study of heat and energy evolved from the caloric theory to the mechanical theory, with experiments by Count Rumford suggesting heat's relation to motion. The 19th century was defined by the unification of electricity, magnetism, and optics into electromagnetism by James Clerk Maxwell. The laws of thermodynamics were formulated, and the kinetic theory of gases linked heat to molecular motion, leading to statistical mechanics. The discovery of the electron (J.J. Thomson, 1897) and radioactivity revealed the atom was not indivisible.

### 20th Century: Modern Physics
At the turn of the century, classical physics failed to explain phenomena like blackbody radiation and the photoelectric effect. This led to two revolutions. Albert Einstein's theories of special (1905) and general (1915) relativity redefined space, time, and gravity. Quantum mechanics, developed by Planck, Bohr, Heisenberg, Schrödinger, and others, described the behavior of atoms and subatomic particles, introducing concepts like wave-particle duality and the uncertainty principle. These theories split physics into "classical" and "modern" domains.

### Contemporary Physics
Post-WWII physics focused on particle physics, leading to the Standard Model, which classifies fundamental particles and forces (except gravity). The discovery of the Higgs boson in 2012 confirmed a key part of this model. Cosmology advanced with the Big Bang theory, supported by the discovery of cosmic microwave background radiation. Major open questions include the nature of dark matter and dark energy, and the unification of general relativity with quantum mechanics.

## Terms
- **Classical Physics**: The physics of macroscopic phenomena, based on Newtonian mechanics, thermodynamics, and Maxwell's electromagnetism, valid at everyday scales and speeds.
- **Modern Physics**: Physics based on the theories of relativity and quantum mechanics, describing phenomena at atomic and subatomic scales or at speeds approaching light.
- **Quantum Mechanics**: The theory describing the behavior of matter and energy at the atomic and subatomic levels, where particles exhibit wave-like properties and outcomes are probabilistic.
- **General Relativity**: Einstein's theory describing gravity not as a force, but as a curvature of spacetime caused by mass and energy.
- **Standard Model**: The theoretical framework describing the fundamental particles and three of the four known fundamental forces (electromagnetic, weak, and strong).
- **Entropy**: A measure of the disorder or randomness of a system; the second law of thermodynamics states that entropy in an isolated system tends to increase.
- **Electromagnetic Induction**: The production of an electric current by a changing magnetic field, discovered by Michael Faraday.
- **Higgs Boson**: A fundamental particle associated with the Higgs field, which gives mass to other elementary particles.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The **interpretation of quantum mechanics** (e.g., Copenhagen vs. many-worlds) remains philosophically debated, concerning the nature of reality and measurement.
- The **unification of general relativity and quantum mechanics** into a theory of quantum gravity is a central unsolved problem.
- The nature of **dark matter** and **dark energy**, which together constitute most of the universe's mass-energy content, is unknown.
- The **arrow of time** and the ultimate fate of the universe (e.g., heat death) are active areas of cosmological research.
