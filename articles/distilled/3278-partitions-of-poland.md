# Partitions of Poland

## Overview
The Partitions of Poland were three successive territorial seizures (1772, 1793, 1795) by the Habsburg monarchy, Kingdom of Prussia, and Russian Empire that dissolved the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, eliminating sovereign Poland and Lithuania for 123 years. They resulted from the Commonwealth's internal political paralysis and the geopolitical ambitions of its powerful neighbors.

## Timeline
- **1730** — Prussia, Austria, and Russia sign secret treaty to maintain the Commonwealth's laws (status quo).
- **1767–68** — Russian ambassador Repnin forces a constitution on the Commonwealth at the "Repnin Sejm."
- **1768–72** — Bar Confederation war against Russian intervention; ends in defeat.
- **5 August 1772** — First Partition treaty signed; Russian, Prussian, and Austrian troops occupy agreed territories.
- **1791** — Constitution of 3 May adopted, enacting major reforms.
- **1792** — Russian forces invade to crush the reformist government; pro-Russian Targowica Confederation forms.
- **23 January 1793** — Second Partition treaty signed at the Grodno Sejm (Prussia and Russia only).
- **1794** — Kościuszko Uprising begins against the partitioning powers.
- **24 October 1795** — Third Partition treaty signed; the Commonwealth is formally dissolved.
- **25 November 1795** — King Stanisław August Poniatowski abdicates under Russian escort.
- **1815** — Congress of Vienna redraws borders; creates Congress Poland under Russia and Galicia under Austria.
- **1918** — Full Polish independence restored after World War I.

## Body

### Causes and Prelude
The Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth was weakened by the *liberum veto*, a parliamentary rule requiring unanimous consent for legislation, which paralyzed governance and invited foreign bribery. By the mid-18th century, its kings were effectively chosen by foreign powers. Russia, Prussia, and Austria signed a 1730 secret treaty to preserve this dysfunctional status quo. After Russia imposed a constitution in 1767 guaranteeing the *liberum veto*, the Bar Confederation (1768–72) rose against Russian domination but was defeated. The ensuing chaos, including a Cossack rebellion and Ottoman intervention, provided the pretext for the first partition.

### The Three Partitions
The **First Partition (1772)** was agreed in Vienna in February and formalized in August. Prussia gained Royal Prussia (excluding Gdańsk), connecting its territories. Austria received Galicia, including rich salt mines. Russia took lands east of the Dvina and Dnieper rivers. The Commonwealth lost about 30% of its territory and half its population (4 million people).

The **Second Partition (1793)** followed the Commonwealth's reformist Constitution of 3 May 1791, which alarmed its neighbors. Russia invaded in 1792, and pro-Russian magnates formed the Targowica Confederation. After Polish reformist forces were defeated, Prussia and Russia annexed more land at the Grodno Sejm. Only one-third of the 1772 population remained in a rump Poland.

The **Third Partition (1795)** was triggered by the failed Kościuszko Uprising (1794). The three powers signed a final treaty, erasing the Polish state. Russia received the largest share (including Vilnius), Prussia gained Warsaw, and Austria took Lublin and Kraków. King Stanisław August Poniatowski abdicated.

### Aftermath and Legacy
The partitions created three distinct administrative zones: the Austrian Partition (Galicia), the Prussian Partition, and the Russian Partition. After the Napoleonic Wars, the Congress of Vienna (1815) redrawn borders, establishing the Russian-controlled Congress Poland and Austrian Galicia. Poles faced varying degrees of repression and cultural suppression, leading to the "Great Emigration" and numerous 19th-century uprisings. Full independence was restored in 1918 after World War I.

The term "Fourth Partition" is used in historiography to refer to subsequent annexations of Polish lands (e.g., 1815, 1832, 1846, 1939) or, in a temporal sense, to the Polish diaspora (*Polonia*) that maintained the independence movement abroad.

## Terms
- **Liberum veto**: A parliamentary procedure in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth allowing any single member to veto legislation, leading to political paralysis.
- **Bar Confederation (1768–72)**: An association of Polish nobles formed to resist Russian domination and defend the Commonwealth's sovereignty.
- **Targowica Confederation (1792)**: A pro-Russian group of Polish magnates who opposed the Constitution of 3 May and invited Russian intervention.
- **Kościuszko Uprising (1794)**: A national insurrection led by Tadeusz Kościuszko against Russian and Prussian forces, which failed and precipitated the final partition.
- **Congress Poland**: An autonomous polity created by the Congress of Vienna (1815) within the Russian Partition, later losing autonomy after uprisings.
- **Galicia**: A region in the Austrian Partition, where Poles experienced relatively greater cultural and political freedoms.
- **Great Emigration**: The mass emigration of Polish intellectuals, politicians, and artists in the 19th century after failed uprisings, who became revolutionaries abroad.
- **Fourth Partition**: A term for later annexations of Polish territory (e.g., 1939) or for the political role of the Polish diaspora.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Justification vs. Violation**: Historians from partitioning states (e.g., Russian scholar Sergey Solovyov) argued the partitions were justified due to the Commonwealth's degeneration. Critics like British jurist Sir Robert Phillimore and historian Edmund Burke condemned them as illegal and immoral.
- **Economic Motives**: Some scholars emphasize Prussia's goal of controlling Baltic grain trade via Gdańsk and Russia's concern over peasants fleeing to the relatively better conditions in the Commonwealth.
- **Ethnic and Cultural Claims**: Russian historians stressed annexing "Eastern Slavic" (Ukrainian, Belarusian) lands as a reintegration of historic Kievan Rus', while ignoring the annexation of ethnically Polish and Lithuanian areas.
- **Impact of Reforms**: More recent studies suggest the last two partitions were a direct reaction to the Commonwealth's strengthening reforms (e.g., Constitution of 3 May 1791), which threatened the neighbors' power.
