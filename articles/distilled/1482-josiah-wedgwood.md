# Josiah Wedgwood

## Overview
Josiah Wedgwood (1730–1795) was an English potter, entrepreneur, and abolitionist who industrialised European pottery manufacturing through systematic experimentation and modern marketing. Rising from a Staffordshire potter family, he founded the Wedgwood company in 1759, developing innovative ceramic bodies—creamware, black basalt, and jasperware—that imitated expensive porcelain at lower costs. He leveraged royal patronage (Queen Charlotte’s "Queen’s Ware") and classical revival fashions to capture aristocratic demand, then used emulation effects to sell cheaper versions to the middle classes. Wedgwood pioneered direct mail, money-back guarantees, illustrated catalogues, free delivery, and showrooms, becoming a model for modern retail. A Unitarian and member of the Lunar Society, he invented a pyrometric device for kiln control, backed the Trent and Mersey Canal, and built the model village of Etruria. His anti-slavery medallion ("Am I Not a Man and a Brother?") became the era’s most famous abolitionist image. He was the grandfather of Charles Darwin.

## Body

### Early Life and Apprenticeship
Wedgwood was born into the fifth generation of a Burslem potting family. His mother, Mary Stringer, daughter of a dissenting minister, instilled values of "knowledge based on reason, experience, and experiment" over dogma. After his father’s death in 1739, Josiah began an informal apprenticeship, learning to "throw" pots on the wheel. A severe smallpox attack at age twelve damaged his right knee, a disability that recurred throughout his life. He received a formal indenture in 1744 under his brother Thomas, but knee pain forced him to switch from wheel-throwing to moulded ware and ornaments. When Thomas rejected his improvement ideas and a partnership, Josiah left in 1751–52 to manage a works near Stoke.

### Partnership with Whieldon and Independent Foundation
Around 1754, Wedgwood partnered with Thomas Whieldon, a maker of high-value items like snuff boxes. After six months of research, he developed an exceptionally brilliant green glaze that generated immediate demand. A subsequent knee injury forced months of convalescence, during which he read widely in science and literature, aided by minister William Willet (his brother-in-law). In 1759, he launched his own business at Ivy House Works, financed partly by his cousin Richard Wedgwood, a wealthy cheesemonger whose daughter Sarah became Josiah’s wife after prolonged settlement negotiations.

### Scientific Innovation and Ceramic Bodies
Wedgwood applied scientific method to pottery, systematically experimenting with clay bodies and glazes. His major innovations were:
- **Creamware** (c. 1762): a refined earthenware with a creamy glaze, later branded "Queen's Ware" after Queen Charlotte’s patronage.
- **Black basalt** (c. 1768): a fine black stoneware imitating excavated Etruscan/Greek vases.
- **Jasperware** (perfected c. 1773–75): an unglazed, coloured stoneware (typically pale blue) with applied white relief decoration, his most famous product.
He never made true porcelain, but his earthenwares and stonewares matched its qualities at lower cost. He also adopted transfer printing early, achieving hand-painted effects cheaply.

### Marketing, Distribution, and Fashion Leadership
Wedgwood recognised that aristocratic adoption drove mass demand. He secured Queen Charlotte’s permission to call his creamware "Queen's Ware" (1765), exhibited royal pieces before delivery, and opened fashionable London showrooms (Charles Street, then Grosvenor Square). He divided decorative wares with partner Thomas Bentley (from 1767) and expanded showrooms to Bath, Liverpool, Dublin, and Westminster. He timed new releases for maximum effect, limited jasperware display to create scarcity, and named patterns after aristocratic patrons (Bedford, Oxford, Chetwynd vases). The 1773 Frog Service for Catherine the Great—952 pieces with over a thousand original paintings—drew crowds for a month, providing immense advertising value despite being "strictly uneconomical." In 1773 he published the first illustrated Ornamental Catalogue. His marketing toolkit included direct mail, money-back guarantees, travelling salesmen with pattern boxes, self-service, free delivery, "buy one get one free," and multilingual clerks for foreign buyers.

### Etruria Works, Canal, and Industrial Organisation
A major backer of the Trent and Mersey Canal, Wedgwood built the Etruria Works (opened June 1769) beside it, naming it after the Italian district where black Etruscan pottery was excavated. The factory ran for 180 years. He constructed a worker village at Etruria, inscribed with the motto *Sic fortis Etruria crevit* ("Thus Etruria grew strong"). After Bentley’s death in 1780, Erasmus Darwin helped manage the business, cementing the Wedgwood-Darwin family alliance (Josiah’s daughter Susannah married Robert Darwin; their son Charles married Emma Wedgwood, Josiah’s granddaughter). By 1784, nearly 80% of production was exported; by 1790, Wedgwood sold in every European city.

### Scientific Recognition and the Lunar Society
Wedgwood was elected to the Royal Society in 1783 for his **pyrometric device** (a pyrometer based on clay contraction at high temperatures, known as the Wedgwood scale), solving the problem of measuring kiln heat accurately. He was an active member of the Lunar Society of Birmingham, meeting at Erasmus Darwin House, and is commemorated on Birmingham’s Moonstones.

### Abolitionism
A prominent abolitionist, Wedgwood mass-produced the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade’s seal as a cameo medallion ("Am I Not a Man and a Brother?"), probably modelled by William Hackwood or Henry Webber. The medallion became, per the BBC, "the most famous image of a black person in all of 18th-century art." Thomas Clarkson noted ladies wore them as bracelets and hair pins, turning fashion into a vehicle for justice. The design was reproduced on wall paintings and clay tobacco pipes. His daughter Sarah was a founding member of the Birmingham Ladies Society for the Relief of Negro Slaves, the first women’s anti-slavery society.

### The Portland Vase and Final Years
To prove his technical mastery, Wedgwood spent three years duplicating the Portland Vase (a 1st-century BC blue-and-white glass vase), achieving a satisfactory jasperware copy in 1789. He died in 1795, leaving the company to his children. A marble memorial was installed in Stoke parish church in 1802.

### Legacy
Wedgwood is credited as a pioneer of modern marketing and managerial accounting. Historians Judith Flanders and Neil McKendrick rank him among the greatest innovative retailers; Tristram Hunt calls him a "difficult, brilliant, creative entrepreneur whose personal drive and extraordinary gifts changed the way we work and live." The Adam Smith Institute likens him to Steve Jobs and Elon Musk. The Wedgwood company survives (now part of Fiskars); "Wedgwood China" commonly denotes jasperware. The Wedgwood Museum’s archive is inscribed on UNESCO’s UK Memory of the World Register (2011).

## Terms
- ****Creamware**** — A refined earthenware with a creamy-coloured lead glaze, Wedgwood’s first major commercial body, later branded "Queen's Ware."
- ****Black basalt**** — A fine-grained, unglazed black stoneware developed c. 1768 to imitate excavated Etruscan and Greek vases.
- ****Jasperware**** — An unglazed, vitrified stoneware (usually pale blue) with applied white relief decoration, perfected c. 1773–75; Wedgwood’s most iconic product.
- ****Transfer printing**** — A decoration method where an engraved copper plate prints a design onto tissue paper, transferred to the ceramic, giving hand-painted effects at lower cost.
- ****Pyrometric device / Wedgwood scale**** — A high-temperature measuring instrument invented by Wedgwood, using the predictable contraction of clay cylinders to gauge kiln heat; basis for his Royal Society election (1783).
- ****Lunar Society of Birmingham**** — A dining club of Midlands industrialists, scientists, and intellectuals (including Erasmus Darwin, Joseph Priestley, James Watt) meeting monthly near the full moon; Wedgwood was an active member.
- ****Trent and Mersey Canal**** — A canal linking the River Trent to the River Mersey, backed by Wedgwood; its route determined the site of his Etruria Works.
- ****Etruria Works**** — Wedgwood’s purpose-built factory and village (opened 1769), named after the Italian Etruscan pottery that inspired his black basalt.
- ****Portland Vase**** — A 1st-century BC Roman cameo-glass vase (blue with white figures) that Wedgwood spent three years replicating in jasperware (success 1789).
- ****Anti-slavery medallion**** — A cameo produced by Wedgwood from 1787 bearing the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade’s seal: a kneeling enslaved man with the motto "Am I Not a Man and a Brother?"

## Debates and open questions
- **Attribution of the anti-slavery medallion design**: The source states the actual cameo modelling was "probably done by either William Hackwood or Henry Webber," modellers at the factory, leaving the precise authorship unresolved.
- **Wedgwood’s role in managerial accounting**: Anthony Hopwood (LSE) identifies him as an "early adopter/founder of managerial accounting principles," but the extent of his systematic innovation versus ad hoc practice is debated in accounting history (McKendrick 1970).
- **Relationship with John Turner the elder**: The source notes they were "friend, and commercial rival" and that "their works have sometimes been misattributed," implying ongoing scholarly difficulty in distinguishing their outputs.
- **Cause of death**: The source gives "probably cancer of the jaw" but does not confirm a diagnosis, reflecting the limits of 18th-century medicine.
