# Guitar

## Overview
The guitar is a fretted, stringed musical instrument classified as a chordophone, meaning sound is produced by vibrating strings. It is a primary instrument in numerous global music genres, from blues and rock to folk and classical. Its modern form was developed in the 19th century, and the invention of the electric guitar in the 20th century profoundly influenced popular music and culture.

## Timeline
- **c. 1200** — Two types of "guitar" (guitarra latina and guitarra morisca) are documented in Spain.
- **15th–16th C.** — The Spanish vihuela, a key ancestor, is popular in Spain and Italy.
- **Mid-16th C.** — The five-course baroque guitar is established.
- **c. 1850** — Spanish makers, notably Antonio Torres Jurado, develop the modern guitar's form and fan-bracing pattern.
- **1931** — The first successful magnetic pickup is incorporated into the Rickenbacker "Frying Pan" lap steel.
- **1937** — The electric guitar is first patented.
- **Post-WWII** — Nylon and steel strings become mainstream; the solid-body electric guitar is popularized by Gibson (Les Paul) and Fender.
- **1960s–70s** — Solid-body electric guitars dominate the market.

## Body

### History and Ancestors
The modern guitar's origins are debated, but its ancestors include the gittern, vihuela, and the four- and five-course baroque guitars. The word "guitar" derives from the Spanish *guitarra*, which has roots in the Andalusian Arabic *qīthārah* and Latin *cithara*. The Spanish vihuela (15th–16th centuries) is widely considered the most important influence on the baroque guitar. The modern six-string form and structure were established around 1850 by Spanish luthiers, most importantly Antonio Torres Jurado, who increased the body size and invented the fan-braced pattern, greatly improving volume and tone.

### Types and Construction
Guitars are broadly divided into acoustic and electric. **Acoustic guitars** produce sound via a hollow body that acts as a resonant chamber. Key subtypes include the **classical (Spanish) guitar** (nylon strings, played with fingers), the **flat-top steel-string guitar** (using X-bracing developed by C.F. Martin), and the **archtop guitar** (with a carved, violin-like top). **Electric guitars** use electromagnetic pickups to convert string vibrations into electrical signals, which are amplified. They can have solid, semi-hollow, or hollow bodies. Solid-body designs, less prone to feedback, became dominant in the 1960s.

A guitar's core components are the **headstock** (with tuning machines), the **neck** (containing the **fretboard** with metal **frets**), and the **body**. The **truss rod** inside the neck counteracts string tension. The **bridge** and **saddle** transfer string vibration to the body (acoustic) or hold the strings for pickup (electric). **Pickups** are transducers; common types are **single-coil** (brighter sound) and **humbucker** (double-coil, quieter, heavier sound).

### Tuning and Playing
The standard tuning for a six-string guitar is EADGBE (from low to high), a series of ascending fourths with one major third. This tuning facilitates common chord shapes and scale patterns. Alternative tunings include **open tunings** (strumming open strings produces a chord, common in blues and slide guitar) and **regular tunings** (equal intervals between strings, favored by some jazz players). A **capo** is a clamp used on the fretboard to raise the pitch of open strings, allowing players to use familiar chord shapes in different keys.

### Role in Music
The electric guitar's amplified sound and capacity for effects like distortion and reverb were central to the development of blues, rock, and heavy metal. It is a primary instrument in genres including jazz, country, folk, punk, and pop. The acoustic guitar remains fundamental to classical, flamenco, folk, and singer-songwriter traditions.

## Terms
1.  **Chordophone**: A musical instrument that produces sound via a vibrating string stretched between two fixed points.
2.  **Fret**: A raised ridge on the neck's fingerboard that divides the scale length, allowing the player to shorten the vibrating string length and raise its pitch.
3.  **Pickup**: A transducer on an electric guitar that converts string vibrations into an electrical signal for amplification.
4.  **Humbucker**: A type of electromagnetic guitar pickup using two coils to cancel (or "buck") electromagnetic interference, producing a quieter, often heavier tone.
5.  **Truss Rod**: A metal rod inside the guitar neck used to adjust its curvature and counteract string tension.
6.  **Action**: The height of the strings above the fretboard.
7.  **Capo**: A device clamped onto the fretboard to raise the pitch of the open strings.
8.  **Scordatura**: An alternative tuning of the strings of a stringed instrument.
9.  **Plectrum**: A small, flat tool (guitar pick) used to strum or pluck the strings.
10. **Bracing**: The internal pattern of wood reinforcements inside an acoustic guitar's body that strengthens the top and shapes its resonant sound.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The exact origins of the modern guitar are "not known," and scholars debate the extent of influence from instruments like the lute or ancient kithara.
*   The claim that the poet Vicente Espinel invented the five-course guitar is contested, as his birth year (1550) may make this chronologically impossible.
