# Zara Yaqob

## Overview
Zara Yaqob (r. 1434–1468) was a powerful and influential Emperor of Ethiopia from the Solomonic dynasty. He is remembered for centralizing religious and political authority, engaging in significant military campaigns, and fostering a golden age of Ge'ez literature. His reign was marked by both profound cultural achievements and a descent into despotic rule, making him a pivotal yet controversial figure in Ethiopian history.

## Body

### Early Life and Accession
Zara Yaqob was the youngest son of Emperor Dawit I. After his father's death, he was confined on the royal prison mountain of Amba Geshen for about 20 years by his older brothers. This seclusion left him inexperienced in statecraft. He became emperor in 1434 upon the death of his last older brother, inheriting a kingdom facing internal plots, religious division, and external threats.

### Religious Policy and Church Affairs
Zara Yaqob launched a severe campaign against paganism and heresy, using spies and executing dissenters, including some of his own children. He enforced the wearing of inscribed fillets as a sign of Christian faith. His most significant religious achievement was resolving a century-long schism in the Ethiopian Church over Sabbath observance. He brokered a compromise between the traditional Sunday-only faction and the Ewostathians, who observed both Saturday and Sunday. This was formally accepted by the Egyptian church authorities at the Council of Debre Mitmaq in 1450.

### Military Campaigns and Foreign Relations
Zara Yaqob conducted successful wars against Muslim neighbors. He invaded the Hadiya Kingdom, married its princess Eleni, and later defeated the Adal Sultanate at the Battle of Gomit in 1445, consolidating Ethiopian control in the south. In the north, he settled Shewan warriors in Hamassien and, near the end of his reign, extracted tribute from the Sultanate of Dahlak. He also asserted Ethiopia's power diplomatically, protesting the destruction of Coptic churches in Egypt and warning he could divert the Nile. He sent delegates to the Council of Florence in 1441 and a mission to Europe in 1450 to request artisans, establishing contact with the Holy See and the Kingdom of Aragon.

### Later Reign and Literary Works
In his later years, Zara Yaqob became increasingly despotic and paranoid, punishing critics and abusing his family. This led to a break with his son and heir, Baeda Maryam, though they later reconciled. Despite his tyranny, his reign was a high point for Ge'ez literature. He was himself a prolific author, writing theological works such as *Mashafa Berhan* ("The Book of Light") and *Mashafa Milad* ("The Book of Nativity"). He also founded the capital of Debre Birhan after observing Halley's Comet in 1456, which he interpreted as a divine sign.

## Terms
- **Solomonic dynasty:** The royal line of Ethiopia that claimed descent from King Solomon and the Queen of Sheba.
- **Ge'ez:** An ancient Semitic language of Ethiopia, used as the liturgical language of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church and for scholarly literature.
- **Ewostathians:** A Christian faction in Ethiopia that insisted on observing both the Saturday (Sabbath) and Sunday, following the teachings of their founder, Ewostatewos.
- **Abuna:** The head of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church, historically appointed by the Coptic Pope of Alexandria.
- **Amba Geshen:** A mountain fortress near Axum used as a royal prison for princes of the Solomonic dynasty.
- **Hadiya Kingdom:** A medieval Muslim kingdom in the southern Ethiopian highlands.
- **Adal Sultanate:** A medieval Muslim state in the Horn of Africa, often in conflict with the Ethiopian Empire.
- **Bahr Negash:** A title for the ruler of the coastal regions of the Ethiopian Empire, first recorded during Zara Yaqob's reign.
- **Prester John:** A legendary Christian patriarch and king believed by medieval Europeans to rule a lost Christian kingdom in the East, often identified with the Ethiopian emperor.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The details of Zara Yaqob's early life are disputed. While tradition holds he was raised secretly in Tigray, the scholar Taddesse Tamrat argues this is improbable, noting Zara Yaqob's own writings state he was imprisoned on Mount Gishen until just before his accession.
- The extent of his literary output is debated. Richard Pankhurst attributes three major works to him, while Edward Ullendorff attributes only two.
- The long-term success of his appointments of daughters and nieces as provincial governors is noted as unsuccessful in the source, but the specific reasons and consequences are not detailed.
