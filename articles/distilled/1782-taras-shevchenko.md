# Taras Shevchenko

## Overview

Taras Hryhorovych Shevchenko (1814–1861) was a Ukrainian poet, writer, artist, public and political figure, folklorist and ethnographer. Born a serf in Russian-ruled Ukraine, he became a fellow of the Imperial Academy of Arts in Saint Petersburg before joining the emerging Ukrainian national movement. His poetry collection *Kobzar* is regarded as the foundation of modern Ukrainian literature and, to some degree, the modern Ukrainian language. His significance for Ukrainian and wider Slavic culture has led some to compare him to Robert Burns.

## Body

### Childhood and serfdom

Shevchenko was the third of six children born to Kateryna (née Boiko) and Hryhoriy Ivanovych Shevchenko, former subjects of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth who became serfs. According to biographer Oleksandr Konysky, the original family surname was Hrushivskyi; "Shevchenko" (denoting descent from a shoemaker) came from an ancestor in that trade. The family moved to Kyrylivka in 1816. After his mother died in 1823 and his father in 1825, Shevchenko endured a cruel stepmother until his grandfather expelled her. He worked as a swineherd, groom's assistant, and servant to violent village precentors, reading psalms over the dead. In 1828 he became a kitchen servant and kozachok (court servant) to Pavel Engelhardt, seeing the luxuries of the Russian nobility for the first time.

### Path to freedom

In 1829, Engelhardt caught Shevchenko painting a portrait of Cossack general Matvei Platov at night and had him whipped, but later apprenticed him to painters in Warsaw, including the Polish artist Franciszek Ksawery Lampi. In Warsaw, Shevchenko fell in love with a Polish noble girl, Dunia Gusikowska, which he later said first awoke in him the idea of equality between social classes. After the November Uprising of 1830 forced the household to leave for St. Petersburg, Shevchenko studied under the cruel painter Vasiliy Shiriayev. In the Summer Garden, he met Ukrainian artist Ivan Soshenko, who recognized his talent and introduced him to Karl Briullov, the most fashionable painter of the day. Briullov's request for Shevchenko's freedom was refused, so he painted a portrait of poet Vasily Zhukovsky as a lottery prize for the imperial family; the tsarina drew the winning ticket, and Engelhardt signed the release papers on 5 May 1838.

### Initial success

At the Academy, Shevchenko studied under Briullov, won three silver medals (1839, 1839, 1841), and produced book illustrations to earn income. His poetry collection *Kobzar* (1840), submitted to censors by Yevhen Hrebinka, sold out; it protested social injustice without openly calling for revolution. His epic poem *Haidamaky* (1841) drew sharp criticism from critic Vissarion Belinsky for "romantic pompous ingenuity." He also wrote the drama *Nazar Stodolia* (1843) and part of the tragedy *Mykyta Haidai* (1842). His 1843–1846 trips to Ukraine exposed him to the difficult conditions of his still-enserfed siblings, and he met intellectuals including Panteleimon Kulish and Mykhaylo Maksymovych. His planned album of etchings, *Picturesque Ukraine*, was meant to buy his family's freedom, but poor planning meant only six etchings were printed.

### Exile

In 1845, Shevchenko was appointed teacher of drawing at Kiev University and met historian Mykola Kostomarov and other members of the Brotherhood of Saints Cyril and Methodius, a clandestine society dedicated to political liberalization and a federation of Slavic nations. His 1844 poem *Dream*, mocking Tsar Nicholas I and his wife, became a scandal when copies were confiscated. The tsar laughed at the section about himself but turned to "bitter hatred" at the part describing his wife. Shevchenko was arrested on 5 April 1847 and exiled as a private to Orsk fortress near the Ural Mountains, with the tsar's personal addition: "Under the strictest surveillance, without the right to write or paint." He suffered from rheumatism and scurvy. In 1848–49 he served on the Aral Sea expedition under Lieutenant Butakov, sketching landscapes and Kazakh people. In April 1850 he was re-arrested for wearing civilian clothes and violating the drawing ban, and transported to Novopetrovsk fortress on the Caspian Sea, where he endured strict drills and abuse. He worked on clay sculptures in 1853 to bypass the bans.

### Release and final years

Amnestied in 1857, Shevchenko was forced to stay in Nizhniy Novgorod before being permitted to return to St. Petersburg in 1858. Received as a celebrity, he met Ivan Turgenev, Mikhail Mikeshin, and the African-American Shakespearean actor Ira Aldridge, whose portrait he drew. He lobbied for his family's liberation from serfdom. His last visit to Ukraine in June 1859 ended with arrest on accusations of blasphemy, though he was released. Two failed marriage plans—with Kharytyna Dovhopolenko and Lykera Polusmakova—delivered a heavy blow; after the break with Polusmakova, he began consuming large amounts of rum. Despite illness, he issued *Bukvar Yuzhnorussky*, a grammar book for Sunday schools. Diagnosed with dropsy, he died on 10 March 1861 at age 47. He was initially buried in St. Petersburg, then reburied on Chernecha hora (Monk's Hill) in Kaniv per his poem *Testament* ("Zapovit").

### Literary and artistic legacy

Shevchenko wrote 237 poems, but only 28 were published in the Russian Empire during his lifetime; six others appeared in the Austrian Empire. His early works continued the tradition of Ivan Kotliarevsky and Hryhorii Kvitka-Osnovianenko, the first authors to write in common Ukrainian speech, and drew on Ukrainian folklore and history, especially the image of Zaporozhian Cossacks as defenders of Ukraine. His later period, after 1845, abandoned romantic idealization of the Cossack past, recognizing Russian Tsarism—particularly Peter I and Catherine II—as the chief enemy of his people. His prose, about 20 novellas in Russian, was never published during his lifetime and contains autobiographical elements. His diary (1857–58) combines features of a literary work, a diary, and a travel book. Of his known paintings and drawings, 835 works survive; he was awarded the title of Academician in 1860 specifically for achievements in etching, which he may have pioneered in the Russian Empire.

## Terms

- **Kobzar**: Shevchenko's 1840 poetry collection, regarded as the foundation of modern Ukrainian literature; the most widely read book in Ukraine, frequently compared to a national Gospel.
- **Kozachok**: A court servant, the role Shevchenko filled for Pavel Engelhardt.
- **Brotherhood of Saints Cyril and Methodius**: A clandestine society dedicated to political liberalization of the Russian Empire and its transformation into a federation of Slavic nations.
- **Chumaks**: Travelling merchants who once found the young Shevchenko lost and returned him to his village.
- **Serfdom**: The feudal system in which peasants worked land owned by nobles; Shevchenko was born a serf and freed in 1838.
- **Zaporozhian Cossacks**: Ukrainian Cossack defenders whose idealized image appears in Shevchenko's early poems.
- **Testament (Zapovit)**: Shevchenko's 1845 poem, translated into more than 150 languages, expressing his wish to be buried in Ukraine.
- **Etching**: An art technique for which Shevchenko was awarded the title of Academician in 1860; he may have pioneered it in the Russian Empire.

## Debates and open questions

During the Soviet era, official sources represented Shevchenko as "the founder of the revolutionary democratic trend in the history of Ukrainian social thought" and a utopian socialist, attributing his worldview to Russian revolutionary democrats such as Herzen, Belinsky, Dobrolyubov, and Chernyshevsky. Soviet editors and commentators went so far as to falsify the texts of his poems, removing mentions of God and religion. However, historian Dmytro Doroshenko argued that while Shevchenko foresaw a future social revolution, he hoped to influence the ruling classes through his word so they would peacefully renounce their privileges and prevent bloodshed. Shevchenko himself rejected the label "materialism," understanding by that word the vulgar materialism of contemporary thinkers such as Büchner, Moleschott, and Vogt.
