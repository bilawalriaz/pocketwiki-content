# Battle of the Atlantic

## Overview
The Battle of the Atlantic (1939–1945) was the longest continuous military campaign of World War II, pitting Allied naval and air forces against German U-boats and surface raiders. Its core was a "tonnage war": the Allies struggled to supply Britain, which required over a million tons of imports weekly, while Germany sought to sever these supply lines. The campaign's outcome was a strategic Allied victory, essential for the buildup of forces for the D-Day landings, but achieved at a cost of 3,500 Allied merchant ships and 175 warships sunk.

## Timeline
- **3 Sep 1939** — War declared; U-30 sinks SS *Athenia*.
- **17 Dec 1939** — German pocket battleship *Admiral Graf Spee* scuttled after Battle of the River Plate.
- **14 Sep 1940** — U-boat "Happy Time" begins; wolf pack tactics first used successfully against convoys.
- **10 Jun 1940** — Italy enters war; Italian submarines later deploy to Atlantic.
- **24 May 1941** — German battleship *Bismarck* sinks HMS *Hood*; *Bismarck* sunk 27 May.
- **9 May 1941** — Capture of Enigma materials from U-110 aids codebreaking.
- **13 Jan 1942** — U-boats begin "Second Happy Time" off unprepared US East Coast.
- **1 Feb 1942** — Kriegsmarine switches to four-rotor Enigma (TRITON), blinding Allied codebreakers.
- **30 Oct 1942** — Enigma materials salvaged from U-559, helping break TRITON.
- **19 Nov 1942** — Admiral Max Horton takes command of Western Approaches, introduces "support groups."
- **12 May 1943** — "Black May": 43 U-boats sunk; Dönitz halts North Atlantic wolf pack operations.
- **8 May 1945** — German surrender; last U-boat actions occur hours before.

## Body

### Background and Early Campaign (1939–1940)
Germany's naval strategy relied on commerce raiding. U-boat commander Karl Dönitz believed 300 medium U-boats using coordinated "wolf pack" (*Rudeltaktik*) tactics could sink a million tons monthly, strangling Britain. Early Allied anti-submarine warfare was hampered by overconfidence in ASDIC (sonar), which had significant flaws, and a shortage of convoy escorts. The German conquest of France in 1940 provided U-boats with Atlantic bases, doubling their effective force, while the loss of the French Navy stretched British resources.

### The Happy Time and Allied Adaptation (1940–1941)
From mid-1940, U-boats enjoyed a "Happy Time," sinking over 270 ships by October. The Allies countered by forming permanent escort groups and improving training. A critical breakthrough came from breaking the German Enigma cipher, which, combined with HF/DF (Huff-Duff) radio direction-finding, allowed convoys to be routed around wolf packs. However, the growing number of U-boats and the introduction of new German codes in 1942 temporarily reversed Allied gains.

### The Tipping Point (1942–1943)
The US entry into the war initially led to catastrophic losses off its unprepared coast. After the US instituted convoys, U-boats shifted back to the North Atlantic's "air gap." A convergence of Allied technologies and resources turned the tide: long-range B-24 Liberator bombers closed the air gap; centimetric radar (undetectable by early German Metox receivers) and ahead-throwing weapons like Hedgehog improved attacks; and escort carriers provided continuous air cover. In May 1943, devastating U-boat losses ("Black May") forced Dönitz to withdraw from the North Atlantic.

### Final Phase (1943–1945)
Germany developed new technologies like the snorkel (allowing submerged diesel operation) and acoustic torpedoes, but could not regain the initiative. Allied air and naval superiority, based in part on the captured Azores, made the Atlantic lethal for U-boats. The focus shifted to protecting the D-Day invasion fleet, which crossed with minimal losses. By war's end, the Allies had built over 38 million tons of new shipping, far outpacing sinkings.

## Terms
*   **Wolf Pack (Rudeltaktik):** A German tactic where multiple U-boats coordinated via radio to attack a convoy simultaneously, typically at night on the surface.
*   **ASDIC/Sonar:** An underwater detection system using sound pulses to locate submarines. Its effectiveness was limited by depth, speed, and environmental conditions.
*   **HF/DF (Huff-Duff):** High-frequency direction-finding equipment used on ships and shore stations to locate U-boats by their radio transmissions.
*   **Enigma:** A cipher machine used by Germany to encrypt communications. Breaking its naval variants was crucial for Allied intelligence.
*   **Tonnage War:** A strategy of attrition where the belligerents sought to sink enemy merchant shipping faster than it could be replaced.
*   **Escort Carrier:** A small, slow aircraft carrier built on a merchant ship hull, used to provide air cover for convoys.
*   **Leigh Light:** A powerful searchlight fitted to aircraft, allowing them to attack surfaced U-boats at night after detecting them with radar.
*   **Schnorkel:** A retractable air intake that allowed U-boats to run diesel engines while submerged, recharging batteries without surfacing.

## Debates and Open Questions
Historians debate the primary cause of the German defeat. Some, like Max Hastings, argue that breaking the Enigma code ("Ultra") was decisive, saving millions of tons of shipping. Others, citing German archives, contend that the development of centimetric radar and the massive increase in Allied escorts and aircraft were more critical. A related debate concerns whether Germany ever came close to winning. While some historians suggest the U-boat threat nearly starved Britain, others, like Alan Levine, argue this is a misperception, noting that over 99% of ships sailing to Britain arrived safely and that Allied shipbuilding consistently outpaced sinkings after 1941.
