# Medicine

## Overview

Medicine is the science and practice of caring for patients, managing the diagnosis, prognosis, prevention, treatment, and palliation of injury or disease while promoting health. It encompasses health care practices evolved to maintain and restore health, applying biomedical sciences, research, genetics, and technology through pharmaceuticals, surgery, and therapies. Practiced since prehistoric times, medicine was long an art tied to religious and philosophical beliefs; since modern science, it has become a combination of art and science, with prescientific forms now termed traditional or alternative medicine.

## Body

### Etymology and Nature

"Medicine" derives from Latin *medicus*, meaning "a physician." "Physic," source of "physician," was the old word for medicine. For most of history, medicine was an art linked to religious and philosophical beliefs—a medicine man applied herbs and prayers, or a philosopher-physician used bloodletting per humorism (the four humors theory). Recently, most medicine blends art and science: stitching is an art learned through practice, while science explains cellular and molecular processes in the tissues stitched.

### Clinical Practice

Medical availability varies by culture and technology. Modern scientific medicine dominates the Western world, while low-resource regions—parts of Africa, Oceania, Southeast Asia, and Latin America—often rely on traditional medicine, which may lack regulation or robust evidence. Evidence-based medicine (EBM) is not universally applied; a 2007 survey of literature reviews found that about 49% of medical interventions lacked sufficient evidence to support either benefit or harm. Applying uncertain interventions without justification may violate non-maleficence, the "first, do no harm" principle from the Hippocratic Oath.

A medical encounter begins with reviewing the patient's history and record, then an interview and physical exam. The interview covers chief complaint, current activity, history of present illness, past medical history, medications, family history, social history, and review of systems. The exam uses inspection, palpation (feel), percussion (tap for resonance), and auscultation (listen), covering cardiovascular, respiratory, neurological, and musculoskeletal systems. The physician develops differential diagnoses (possible conditions to rule out), orders tests or treatments, and documents everything in the medical record, a legal document in many jurisdictions.

### Institutions and Delivery

Contemporary medicine operates within government-established health care systems. Christian emphasis on practical charity gave rise to systematic nursing and hospitals; the Catholic Church today remains the largest non-government provider of medical services in the world. Advanced industrial countries (except the U.S.) and many developing countries provide medical services through a system of universal health care that aims to guarantee care for all through a single-payer health care system or compulsory private or cooperative health insurance. Most tribal societies provide no guarantee of healthcare—it is available to those who can afford to pay for it, have self-insured it, or are covered by care financed directly by the government or tribe.

Care falls into three categories. Primary care involves first-contact providers (physicians, nurse practitioners) handling about 90% of visits. Secondary care is specialist care for referred patients in community hospitals, including emergency departments and intensive care. Tertiary care occurs in larger specialized hospitals with facilities for complex conditions like burns, organ transplants, and high-risk pregnancy. Separation of prescribing and dispensing—physician prescribes, pharmacist dispenses—is traditional in the West but not in many Asian countries where physicians deliver drugs directly.

### Branches

Basic sciences include anatomy (structure), biochemistry (chemistry of organisms), physiology (normal function), pharmacology (drugs), pathology (disease study), microbiology (microorganisms), immunology (immune system), genetics (genes and inheritance), and epidemiology (disease demographics). Specialties divide into "Medicine" (non-operative, requiring Internal Medicine training) and "Surgery" (operative, requiring General Surgery training), though some—radiology, pathology, anesthesia—fit neither easily. U.S. surgical training requires minimum five years of residency, often seven or more with fellowships. Internal medicine deals with adult disease prevention, diagnosis, and treatment; internists often subspecialize by organ system (e.g., gastroenterology, nephrology). Diagnostic specialties include clinical laboratory sciences, radiology (x-rays, CT, ultrasound, MRI), nuclear medicine (radiolabelled substances imaged by gamma camera or PET), and pathology (basis of modern scientific medical knowledge). Other major specialties include anesthesiology, emergency medicine, family medicine, neurology, obstetrics and gynecology, pediatrics, psychiatry, and preventive medicine. Interdisciplinary fields include aerospace medicine, biomedical engineering, forensic medicine, health informatics, sports medicine, and tropical medicine.

### Education and Legal Controls

Medical education typically involves university medical school, supervised internship or residency, and postgraduate vocational training. In Canada and the U.S., a Doctor of Medicine (M.D.) or Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine (D.O.) degree is required. Regulatory authorities require continuing medical education as knowledge evolves. Most countries legally require licensing or registration—a medical degree plus accreditation by a medical board—to restrict authority to qualified physicians and guard against charlatans. In the European Union, the profession of doctor of medicine is regulated; the regulated professions database contains a list for EU member states, EEA countries and Switzerland, covered by Directive 2005/36/EC. Negligent or harmful doctors face malpractice charges with civil, criminal, or professional sanctions.

### Medical Ethics

Medical ethics applies moral principles to practice, encompassing clinical application plus history, philosophy, theology, and sociology. Six core values guide discussions: autonomy (patient's right to refuse or choose treatment), beneficence (acting in the patient's best interest), justice (fair distribution of scarce health resources), non-maleficence ("first, do no harm"), respect for persons (dignity), and truthfulness and honesty (informed consent, emphasized since the Doctors' Trial of the Nuremberg trials and Tuskegee syphilis experiment). These values frame conflicts but not answers; when values clash, dilemmas arise, as when autonomy and beneficence conflict over blood transfusions.

### History

Prehistoric medicine used plants, animal parts, and minerals, often ritually by priests or shamans, within systems like animism (inanimate objects having spirits) and shamanism (individuals with mystic powers). The earliest known medical texts in the world were found in the ancient Syrian city of Ebla and date back to 2500 BCE. Egyptian medicine featured Imhotep (first physician known by name), the Kahun Gynaecological Papyrus (c. 2000 BCE), Edwin Smith Papyrus (1600 BCE, surgery), and Ebers Papyrus (1500 BCE). Chinese medicine dates to the Shang dynasty; the Huangdi Neijing, the progenitor of Chinese medicine, is a medical text written beginning in the 2nd century BCE and compiled in the 3rd century. In India, the Sushruta Samhita described early plastic surgery and sterilization; earliest dedicated hospitals appear at Mihintale, Sri Lanka. Greek physician Hippocrates, "father of modern medicine," introduced the Hippocratic Oath and categorized illnesses; Galen performed brain and eye surgeries. After the Western Roman Empire fell, Greek medicine declined in Western Europe but continued in the Byzantine Empire.

In the Middle Ages, the Byzantine Empire developed the concept of hospital as an institution to offer medical care and the possibility of a cure due to the ideals of Christian charity, rather than just a place to die. After 750 CE, Muslim scholars translated Hippocrates, Galen, and Sushruta into Arabic; Avicenna wrote The Canon of Medicine, a standard text at medieval European universities. Rhazes questioned Greek humorism, recognized the pupil's reaction to light, and has been described as a doctor's doctor and the father of pediatrics. Charlemagne decreed hospitals attached to cathedrals and monasteries; the Catholic Church ran hospitals, orphanages, and hospices, funded by taxes and farmlands. The Schola Medica Salernitana in Salerno became medieval Europe's finest medical school. The Black Death of the 14th–15th centuries devastated both Middle East and Europe, prompting gradual rejection of "traditional authority" approaches—the notion that past authorities' statements were definitive. Vesalius improved upon or disproved past theories in De humani corporis fabrica. Van Leeuwenhoek's 1676 microscope observation initiated microbiology.

The modern era began with Jenner's smallpox vaccine (late 18th century, inspired by ancient Chinese variolation), Koch's bacterial transmission discoveries (c. 1880), and antibiotics (c. 1900). Veterinary medicine was, for the first time, truly separated from human medicine in 1761 with Bourgelat's Lyon school. The first antibiotic was arsphenamine (Salvarsan) discovered by Paul Ehrlich in 1908 after he observed that bacteria took up toxic dyes that human cells did not; the first major class of antibiotics was the sulfa drugs, derived by German chemists originally from azo dyes. Modern biotechnology enables targeted drugs, and genomics increasingly influences medicine as causative genes for most monogenic disorders are identified. Evidence-based medicine, facilitated by the Cochrane Collaboration, uses systematic reviews and meta-analysis to establish effective practice algorithms; a 2001 review of 160 Cochrane systematic reviews revealed that, according to two readers, 21.3% concluded insufficient evidence, 20% concluded evidence of no effect, and 22.5% concluded positive effect.

### Quality and Telemedicine

Evidence-based medicine, prevention of medical error (iatrogenesis), and avoidance of unnecessary care are priorities, particularly in the U.S. where healthcare is regarded as excessively costly but population health metrics lag similar nations. Health spending varies by country, affecting access; most developed countries provide health care to all citizens except the U.S., where restrictions on health insurance coverage may limit affordability. Telemedicine (or telehealth) delivers preventive, promotive, and curative care remotely, including telenursing, telepalliative care, telepharmacy, telepsychiatry, telepsychology, teleneurotherapy, telenutrition, and telerehabilitation.

## Terms

- **Evidence-based medicine (EBM)**: Practice using systematic reviews and meta-analysis to establish effective treatment algorithms
- **Non-maleficence**: The ethical principle "first, do no harm" (Latin: *primum non nocere*)
- **Differential diagnosis**: The list of possible conditions to rule out based on gathered information
- **Humorism**: Ancient theory that health depends on balance of four bodily humors
- **Iatrogenesis**: Harm caused by medical treatment or error
- **Primary care**: First-contact medical services handling about 90% of visits
- **Auscultation**: Listening to body sounds, part of physical examination
- **Informed consent**: Patient's right to understand and agree to treatment, emphasized since Nuremberg trials
- **Telemedicine**: Remote delivery of clinical services via telecommunications
- **Monogenic disorders**: Diseases caused by mutations in a single gene

## Debates and Open Questions

The source notes that evidence-based medicine is not universally applied—about 49% of medical interventions lacked sufficient evidence to support either benefit or harm. There is a perceived tension between transparency in healthcare (which helps patients make informed decisions) and concerns over patient confidentiality and commercial misuse of medical data; critics warn insurers, employers, or marketers could exploit sensitive records. Ethical dilemmas arise when medical community values conflict with patient, family, or community values—for example, when autonomy and beneficence clash over blood transfusions, or when truth-telling was not emphasized before the HIV era. The source also notes that alternative treatments outside scientific medicine with ethical, safety, and efficacy concerns are termed quackery or being based on fringe science, implying ongoing debate about their legitimacy.
