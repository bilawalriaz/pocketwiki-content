# Little Ice Age

## Overview
The Little Ice Age (LIA) was a period of regional cooling, most pronounced in the North Atlantic region, from roughly the 14th to the 19th centuries. It was not a true global ice age but a significant climatic event that caused widespread social, economic, and political upheaval, particularly in Europe, by shortening growing seasons, increasing famine, and exacerbating conflicts.

## Timeline
- **c. 1250** — Atlantic pack ice begins to grow, possibly triggered by the 1257 eruption of Mount Samalas.
- **1275–1300** — Radiocarbon dating shows abrupt cold and ice growth on Baffin Island and Iceland.
- **1315–1317** — The Great Famine strikes Northern Europe.
- **1560–1630** — The "Grindelwald Fluctuation" sees rapid glacial advance in the Alps.
- **1645–1715** — The Maunder Minimum, a period of very low solar activity, occurs.
- **1650** — The First Climatic Minimum, one of the coldest intervals, begins.
- **1770** — The Second Climatic Minimum begins.
- **1783** — The Laki volcano erupts in Iceland, causing widespread hardship.
- **1815** — The eruption of Mount Tambora leads to the "Year Without a Summer" (1816).
- **1850** — The Third Climatic Minimum begins; the LIA ends in the latter half of the 19th century.

## Body

### Definition and Extent
The term "Little Ice Age" was coined by François E. Matthes in 1939. Its conventional timeframe is the 16th to 19th centuries, though some experts define it as c. 1300 to c. 1850. The IPCC notes that the cooling was not globally synchronous; at most, it was a modest cooling of the Northern Hemisphere of less than 1°C relative to late 20th-century levels. Evidence shows increased glaciation in regions like Alaska, New Zealand, and Patagonia, but the timing of maximum advances differed, suggesting largely independent regional climate changes.

### Regional Impacts: Europe
Europe experienced severe consequences. Winters became colder and longer, with the 15th, 17th, and 19th centuries particularly affected. This led to the freezing of rivers like the Thames, enabling frost fairs, and the expansion of glaciers that destroyed alpine farms. The shortened growing season caused repeated famines, such as those in France (1693–94) and Scandinavia (1695–97), which killed up to a third of the population in some areas. Social responses included violent scapegoating, with witchcraft trials increasing during cold, dry periods, and antisemitic violence, as Jews were blamed for plagues. The "General Crisis of the seventeenth century" saw widespread conflict, including the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648), linked to climate-induced instability.

### Regional Impacts: The Americas and Asia
In North America, the LIA coincided with European colonization, bringing colder winters and droughts that contributed to the collapse of early settlements like Jamestown and Roanoke. In Asia, the cooling disrupted agriculture; in China, it is linked to the fall of the Ming dynasty due to droughts and famines. The Ottoman Empire faced food shortages and the Celali Rebellions (c. 1596–1610) during its most intense phase.

### Possible Causes
Scientists propose several interconnected causes:
1.  **Solar Activity:** Periods of low solar output, like the Spörer Minimum (1400–1550) and Maunder Minimum (1645–1715), correlate with cooler temperatures, though the relationship is complex.
2.  **Volcanic Activity:** A series of large eruptions, such as Samalas (1257) and Tambora (1815), injected sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere, reflecting sunlight and causing short-term cooling. This is considered a major driver.
3.  **Ocean Circulation:** A slowdown of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) has been proposed, though recent research suggests any link is uncertain.
4.  **Human Population Decrease:** Massive depopulation from events like the Black Death and the post-Columbian collapse in the Americas may have led to reforestation, increasing carbon dioxide uptake and contributing to cooling.
5.  **Orbital Cycles:** A long-term, slow cooling trend in the Northern Hemisphere from orbital forcing provided a background condition.

## Terms
- **Little Ice Age (LIA):** A period of regional cooling, particularly in the North Atlantic region, from roughly the 14th to 19th centuries.
- **Maunder Minimum:** A period of very low sunspot activity from 1645 to 1715, coinciding with some of the coldest years of the LIA.
- **Thermohaline Circulation:** A global ocean circulation pattern driven by differences in water temperature and salinity; a slowdown in the Atlantic branch (AMOC) has been hypothesized as a cause of LIA cooling.
- **Orbital Forcing:** Long-term changes in Earth's orbit and axial tilt that influence climate over millennia.
- **Bond Event:** A hypothesized cycle of North Atlantic cooling events occurring roughly every 1,500 years.
- **Grindelwald Fluctuation:** A phase of rapid Alpine glacial advance from 1560 to 1630.
- **General Crisis of the 17th Century:** A period of widespread conflict, economic hardship, and political instability in Europe, linked to LIA climate stress.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Timing and Definition:** There is no consensus on the exact start and end dates of the LIA, as they vary by region and dataset.
- **Global Synchronicity:** The IPCC states the LIA was not a globally synchronous cold period, but rather a series of independent regional climate changes.
- **Primary Cause:** The relative importance of solar minima, volcanic eruptions, ocean circulation changes, and human depopulation remains debated. Volcanic activity is often cited as a key driver of the coldest intervals.
- **Southern Hemisphere Onset:** The beginning of the LIA in the Southern Hemisphere is thought to have been delayed by about two centuries compared to the Northern Hemisphere.
