# Miriam Makeba

## Overview
Zenzile Miriam Makeba (1932–2008), known as "Mama Africa," was a South African singer, songwriter, actress, and civil rights activist who became the first African musician to achieve global stardom. She popularized Afropop and world music genres while using her platform to expose the brutality of apartheid, testifying at the United Nations and enduring three decades of exile after the South African government revoked her passport in 1960. Her marriage to Black Power leader Stokely Carmichael in 1968 triggered a U.S. government backlash that forced her relocation to Guinea, where she continued performing and advocating for African liberation. She returned to South Africa in 1990 after Nelson Mandela’s release, continuing her humanitarian work until her death on stage in Italy in 2008.

## Timeline
- **4 March 1932** — Born in Prospect township near Johannesburg to a Xhosa father and Swazi mother.
- **1949–1950** — Married James Kubay; gave birth to daughter Sibongile "Bongi" Makeba; diagnosed with breast cancer; husband left her.
- **1953** — Recorded first hit "Lakutshn, Ilanga" with the Manhattan Brothers.

- **1962, 1964** — Testified before UN Special Committee against Apartheid; South Africa revoked her citizenship; became stateless (held nine passports total).
- **15 March 1966** — Won Grammy Award for Best Folk Recording with Harry Belafonte for *An Evening with Belafonte/Makeba*.
- **1967** — "Pata Pata" released as a single in the US, becoming a worldwide hit.
- **March 1968** — Married Stokely Carmichael (later Kwame Touré); US popularity collapsed; visa revoked while abroad; relocated to Guinea.
- **1975** — Appointed Guinea's official delegate to the UN; addressed General Assembly on apartheid.
- **1976** — Hugh Masekela wrote "Soweto Blues" for her following the Soweto uprising; became staple of her performances.
- **1985** — Daughter Bongi died in childbirth; Makeba moved to Brussels, Belgium.
- **1987** — Joined Paul Simon's Graceland Tour (filmed as *Graceland: The African Concert*); returned to US for first time since 1968.
- **10 June 1990** — Returned to South Africa on French passport after Mandela's release and unbanning of ANC.
- **1991** — Released *Eyes on Tomorrow* with Dizzy Gillespie, Nina Simone, and Hugh Masekela; starred in film *Sarafina!* (1992).
- **16 Oct 1999** — Named FAO Goodwill Ambassador.
- **9 Nov 2008** — Died of heart attack during concert in Castel Volturno, Italy.

## Body

### Early Life and Musical Foundations
Makeba was born in Prospect township, the only child of her father Caswell (a Xhosa teacher) and sixth child of her mother Christina (a Swazi domestic worker). Her father died when she was six; at eighteen days old, she spent six months in prison with her mother, who was jailed for selling *umqombothi* (traditional beer) because the family could not afford the fine. She sang in the choir at Kilnerton Training Institution, a Methodist primary school, performing in English, Xhosa, Sotho, and Zulu—learning to sing in English before speaking it. Family music shaped her: her mother played traditional instruments, her brother collected Duke Ellington and Ella Fitzgerald records, and her father sang in a group called The Mississippi 12. After leaving school to work as a nanny and launderer, she married policeman-in-training James Kubay in 1949, had daughter Bongi in 1950, survived breast cancer, and endured an allegedly abusive two-year marriage before Kubay left her.

### Professional Breakthrough in South Africa
Makeba began singing professionally in the 1950s with the Cuban Brothers, then the Manhattan Brothers (as the only woman), recording her first hit "Lakutshn, Ilanga" in 1953. In 1956 she joined the Skylarks, an all-woman group formed by Gallotone Records, blending jazz and traditional melodies; historian Rob Allingham called them "real trendsetters" with unprecedented harmonization. She received no royalties from Gallotone. Her 1956 solo single "Lovely Lies" (an English adaptation of "Lakutshona Ilanga") became the first South African record on the US Billboard Top 100. In 1959 she starred in the jazz opera *King Kong* (performed for integrated audiences) and made a four-minute cameo in Lionel Rogosin’s clandestine anti-apartheid film *Come Back, Africa*, which won the Critics' Choice Award at the Venice Film Festival. Her performance—singing in Zulu, embodying cosmopolitan Black identity—launched her international career.

### Exile in the United States (1959–1968)
Belafonte mentored Makeba after meeting in London, helping her move to New York in late 1959. Her US debut on *The Steve Allen Show* reached 60 million viewers; her Village Vanguard concert drew Miles Davis and Duke Ellington. After the 1960 Sharpeville massacre killed two uncles and her mother, the South African government cancelled her passport when she sought to attend the funeral, beginning permanent exile (her daughter Bongi joined her in the US months later). She signed with RCA Victor, releasing *Miriam Makeba* (1960) and *The World of Miriam Makeba* (1964), an early world music album peaking at #86 on Billboard 200. She testified at the UN in 1962 and 1964, demanding sanctions and an arms embargo against South Africa; in retaliation, the regime banned her music, revoked her citizenship, and rendered her stateless (Algeria, Guinea, Belgium, and Ghana issued her passports). Emperor Haile Selassie invited her to sing at the 1963 Organisation of African Unity inauguration as the sole performer. She engaged deeply with civil rights, Black Power, and Black Consciousness movements, fundraising for Martin Luther King Jr.'s SCLC and protesting Jim Crow segregation in Atlanta. Her 1965 Grammy-winning album with Belafonte, *An Evening with Belafonte/Makeba*, included explicitly political songs like "Ndodemnyama we Verwoerd" ("Watch out Verwoerd") and "Nongqongqo" naming Mandela and Robert Sobukwe as political prisoners. "Pata Pata," recorded years earlier, became a global hit in 1967.

### Guinea Years (1968–1985)
Marrying Stokely Carmichael in March 1968 caused Makeba's US support to collapse: white audiences abandoned her, the CIA and FBI surveilled her, and the State Department revoked her visa while she was abroad, forcing relocation to Guinea. President Ahmed Sékou Touré welcomed artists, creating the Syliphone label with guaranteed wages; Makeba recorded there and praised Touré's support for musicians. She wrote songs criticizing US racial policies ("Lumumba" 1970, "Malcolm X" 1974) and performed across Africa at independence celebrations (Kenya, Angola, Zambia, Mozambique). She served as Ghana's diplomat and Guinea's UN delegate (1975), addressing the General Assembly. The 1976 Soweto uprising inspired Hugh Masekela's "Soweto Blues," which she performed for years; *Musician* magazine called its lyrics "searingly righteous." She divorced Carmichael in 1978, married airline executive Bageot Bah in 1981, and lived with daughter Bongi and grandchildren until Bongi's 1985 death in childbirth strained their relationship.

### Belgium, Graceland Tour, and Return to South Africa (1985–1990)
After Bongi's death and a military coup in Guinea following Touré's 1984 death, Makeba moved to Brussels with her two grandchildren. Hugh Masekela introduced her to Paul Simon; she joined his massively successful Graceland Tour (1987), performing from a wheelchair after fracturing her leg. Her participation sparked controversy because Graceland was recorded in South Africa, violating the cultural boycott she endorsed. Warner Bros. signed her; she released *Sangoma* (healing chants honoring her sangoma mother) and co-wrote autobiography *Makeba: My Story* (1987), criticizing US consumerism. She performed at the 1988 Nelson Mandela 70th Birthday Tribute at Wembley Stadium (broadcast to 600 million), which a survey showed made 75% of viewers aged 16–24 aware of Mandela and supportive of his release.

### Final Years in South Africa (1990–2008)
After President F.W. de Klerk unbanned the ANC and freed Mandela in February 1990, Mandela persuaded Makeba to return. She entered on a French passport on 10 June 1990 to national fanfare. She recorded *Eyes on Tomorrow* (1991) with Gillespie, Simone, and Masekela, toured globally, appeared on *The Cosby Show*, performed her first South African concerts since 1960 (Johannesburg, 1992), and starred in *Sarafina!* as Angelina, the mother of a Soweto uprising student—*The New York Times* praised her "immense dignity." She became FAO Goodwill Ambassador (1999), advocated for children with HIV/AIDS and child soldiers with Graça Machel-Mandela, founded the Makeba Centre for Girls (orphanage), and appeared in documentary *Amandla!* (2002). Despite osteoarthritis, she performed until her death on 9 November 2008, suffering a heart attack after singing "Pata Pata" at a benefit concert in Italy for writer Roberto Saviano.

### Musical Style and Innovation
Makeba's early groups performed *mbube*, a vocal harmony style blending American jazz, ragtime, Anglican hymns, and indigenous forms. Historian David Coplan described her "African jazz" as "inherently hybridized... Americanized African music, not Africanized American music." Robin Denselow called it a "unique blend of rousing township styles and jazz-influenced balladry." Over 30+ albums, her style shifted from African jazz to Belafonte-influenced crooning to traditional South African forms, incorporating Latin American rhythms. She sang in English, Xhosa, Zulu, Swahili, Sotho—but never Afrikaans ("When Afrikaaners sing in my language, then I will sing hers"). Her dynamic range included operatic soaring, whispers, growls, and Xhosa epiglottal clicks. Songs in African languages reaffirmed Black pride; English was the language of political resistance because the Manhattan Brothers had been prohibited from recording in it under apartheid.

### Politics, Perception, and Image
Makeba insisted she sang "not politics, but the truth" of her life under apartheid. Initially avoiding explicit politics to protect family in South Africa, she became increasingly vocal after exile and her daughter's death. Even apolitical songs were subversive because banned in South Africa; records circulated underground. She became a symbol of apartheid's cruelty and a spokesperson for oppressed Africans. She linked civil rights, anti-colonialism, Black Power, and Pan-Africanism: "Africans who live everywhere should fight everywhere... The Black people are the victims of capitalism, racism and oppression, period." Her marriage to Carmichael amplified his "Black is beautiful" message. Alongside Simone, Lena Horne, and Abbey Lincoln, she highlighted inseparable race and gender liberation. Scholars note Western audiences exoticized her—calling her "African tribeswoman," fixating on Xhosa clicks, ignoring her cosmopolitan background—while she shaped Pan-African identity during decolonization. She rejected white beauty standards: no makeup, natural hair (establishing the "Afro look"), African jewelry, refusing skin-lightener ads. Scholar Tanisha Ford called her hairstyle a "liberated African beauty aesthetic." US media language often "sexualized, infantalized, and animalized" her, per scholars.

### Legacy and Honors
Makeba was the first African recording artist to win a Grammy (1966). Mandela called her "South Africa's first lady of song" whose "music inspired a powerful sense of hope." She brought African music to Western audiences, popularizing world music (though she disliked the term as marginalizing). Collaborators Youssou N'Dour, Salif Keita, Ali Farka Touré, Baaba Maal, and Angélique Kidjo share credit for the genre. Her work with Belafonte created world music conceptually before the term existed. In South Africa, she influenced kwaito artist Thandiswa Mazwai (Bongo Maffin's "De Makeba" modifies "Pata Pata"), Simphiwe Dana ("the new Miriam Makeba"), and Lira (performed "Pata Pata" at 2010 World Cup opening). Kidjo dedicated a 2011 concert to her; scholar Lara Allen called her "arguably South Africa's most famous musical export." Awards: 1996 Kora Awards lifetime achievement; five South African Music Awards (2001–2004); 2001 Polar Music Prize (shared with Sofia Gubaidulina, presented by King Carl XVI Gustaf); 1986 Dag Hammarskjöld Peace Prize; 2001 Otto Hahn Peace Medal (UN Association of Germany); Order for Meritorious Service (2003); voted 38th in "100 Great South Africans" (2004); Rolling Stone #53 "200 Greatest Singers" (2023). Posthumous tributes: University of Fort Hare Miriam Makeba Centre of Performing Arts (~2003); *Hommage à Miriam Makeba* (Paris 2009, London 2009); documentary *Mama Africa* (2011); Google Doodles (2013, 2017); Ghent's "Miriam Makebaplein" square (2014); musical *Mama Africa* (2016); Somi's play *Dreaming Zenzile* (2021) and album *Zenzile: The Reimagination of Miriam Makeba* (2022); Jain's viral TikTok tribute "Makeba" (2023).

## Terms
- **Afropop**: Genre blending African musical traditions with Western pop, which Makeba helped popularize globally.
- **Apartheid**: South Africa's system of institutionalized racial segregation and white-minority rule (1948–1994) that Makeba opposed.
- **Mbube**: South African vocal harmony style drawing on American jazz, ragtime, Anglican hymns, and indigenous music; Makeba's early foundation.
- **Sangoma**: Traditional healer/diviner in Southern African cultures; Makeba's mother became one, inspiring album *Sangoma*.
- **Umqombothi**: Traditional Xhosa beer brewed from malt and cornmeal; Makeba's mother was jailed for selling it.
- **World music**: Marketing category for non-Western traditional and contemporary music; Makeba pioneered it but disliked the label as marginalizing.
- **Black Consciousness Movement**: Anti-apartheid ideology emphasizing Black pride and self-reliance, led by Steve Biko; Makeba engaged with it in the 1960s–70s.
- **Sharpeville massacre**: 21 March 1960 police killing of 69 Black protesters; triggered Makeba's passport cancellation and political radicalization.
- **Soweto uprising**: 1976 student protests against Afrikaans-medium education; police killed hundreds; inspired "Soweto Blues."
- **Cultural boycott**: International campaign isolating apartheid South Africa culturally; Makeba endorsed it but controversially joined Paul Simon's Graceland Tour recorded there.

## Debates and open questions
- **Graceland Tour controversy**: Makeba's participation in Paul Simon's tour (recorded in South Africa) was seen as violating the cultural boycott she supported; scholars debate whether her presence mitigated or legitimized the breach.
- **World music label**: Makeba rejected the term as a "third world" marginalization; scholars debate whether the category helped or ghettoized African artists.
- **Western exoticization vs. agency**: Audiences viewed Makeba as "embodiment of Africa," ignoring her cosmopolitanism; scholars debate how much she controlled versus was constrained by this framing.
- **Feminist identification**: Makeba critiqued second-wave feminism as a "product of luxury"; scholars debate whether her intersectional activism constitutes a distinct Black feminism.
- **Political vs. personal art**: Makeba insisted her songs were personal truth, not politics; scholars examine how this distinction functioned under apartheid where the personal was structurally political.
