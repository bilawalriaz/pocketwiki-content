# National park

## Overview
A national park is a protected area of natural or cultural significance, owned and managed by a government, primarily for conservation and public enjoyment. The concept, pioneered in the 19th century, has become a global model for preserving wilderness and national heritage, though definitions and management standards vary internationally.

## Timeline
- **1776** — Tobago Main Ridge Forest Reserve established, one of the oldest legally protected areas.
- **1832** — U.S. federal government sets aside Hot Springs, Arkansas, for future disposal.
- **1864** — Yosemite Valley and Mariposa Grove ceded to California by federal act.
- **1872** — Yellowstone National Park established as the world's first national park.
- **1879** — Royal National Park (Australia) established, the world's second official national park.
- **1885** — Banff National Park becomes Canada's first national park.
- **1909** — Sweden establishes its first national parks, the first in Europe.
- **1911** — Parks Canada created, the world's first national park service.
- **1916** — U.S. National Park Service established by the Organic Act.
- **1925** — Albert National Park (now Virunga) established in Congo, Africa's first.
- **1951** — Peak District National Park designated, the first in the United Kingdom.
- **1974** — Northeast Greenland National Park established, now the world's largest.

## Body

### Definition and Standards
The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) defines a national park as a Category II protected area: a relatively large, ecologically intact area with ecosystems not materially altered by human exploitation, where highest authority prevents exploitation to protect natural features, and visitors are allowed for inspirational and recreational purposes. Key benchmarks include a minimum size of 1,000 hectares and statutory legal protection. However, many protected areas called "national parks" do not meet this IUCN definition, and some areas that do meet it are not called national parks. The term is used loosely in some countries, like the UK, to describe scenic, undeveloped areas with planning restrictions.

### History and Early Development
Early protected areas included royal hunting reserves, like the Forest of Fontainebleau (1861). The modern concept emerged from early 19th-century advocacy, such as William Wordsworth's 1810 description of the Lake District as "national property." In the U.S., the first federal set-aside was Hot Springs (1832), followed by the ceding of Yosemite to California (1864). The establishment of Yellowstone in 1872, under federal control because it was in a territory, set the precedent for national parks as federally managed public lands. The movement was driven by conservationists like John Muir and Theodore Roosevelt.

### Global Expansion
The model spread internationally after Yellowstone. Australia's Royal National Park (1879) and Canada's Banff (1885) followed. The first European national parks were established in Sweden (1909). Africa's first was Albert National Park (1925, now Virunga). After World War II, designation accelerated, with the UK's first park in 1951. Notable later parks include France's Vanoise (1963) and China's Qomolangma National Nature Preserve (1989), which covers 3.381 million hectares and is managed by local authorities for lower cost.

### Management and Services
The world's first national park service was Parks Canada (1911). The U.S. National Park Service followed in 1916. Park rangers' duties have evolved from simple resource custodianship to include law enforcement, traffic control, and search and rescue. Tourism is a major economic driver; for example, Costa Rica saw a 400% increase in park tourism from 1985 to 1999. The "national park" brand is associated with high-quality natural environments and tourist infrastructure.

### Concerns and Criticisms
National parks, especially in former colonies, are criticized for perpetuating colonialism by displacing indigenous communities to create "pristine" wilderness, reinforcing a nature-culture divide. This is sometimes termed eco-land grabbing. Additionally, high tourism levels can cause habitat degradation, pollution, soil erosion, and wildlife disturbance, potentially undermining conservation goals.

## Terms
- **National Park**: A protected area of natural or cultural significance, owned by a government, primarily for conservation and public enjoyment.
- **IUCN (International Union for Conservation of Nature)**: An organization that defines and categorizes protected areas, including national parks as Category II.
- **Category II Protected Area**: The IUCN classification for national parks, emphasizing ecosystem protection and public recreation.
- **Parks Canada**: The world's first national park service, established in 1911.
- **National Park Service (NPS)**: The U.S. federal agency created in 1916 to manage national parks and other sites.
- **Park Ranger**: A professional responsible for the conservation, management, and public service within a park.
- **Eco-land Grabbing**: A criticism that park creation displaces local communities to preserve "pristine" nature for others.
- **Nature-Culture Divide**: The perceived dichotomy between untouched nature and human society, which park creation can reinforce.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The IUCN continues to discuss the precise parameters for defining a national park, as many existing parks do not meet its criteria.
- There is ongoing debate about the colonial legacy of national parks and the ethics of displacing indigenous peoples to create protected areas.
- Scholars and managers question how to balance tourism benefits with the potential for environmental degradation from high visitation.
