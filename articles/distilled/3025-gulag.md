# Gulag

## Overview
The Gulag was the system of forced labor camps in the Soviet Union, primarily active from the 1930s to the early 1950s under Joseph Stalin. It served as a major instrument of political repression and a source of cheap labor for state projects, housing both ordinary criminals and political prisoners. Its legacy is a central part of the history of Soviet repression and state terror.

## Timeline
- **1918–1920s** — Bolsheviks establish early "special" prison camps under the Cheka, using forced labor for political repression.
- **1929** — Politburo decree creates a system of self-supporting camps, laying the legal foundation for the Gulag.
- **25 April 1930** — The Gulag (Main Directorate of Correctional Labour Camps) is officially established by OGPU order.
- **1930–1933** — Rapid expansion begins, fueled by dekulakization and collectivization, sending millions to camps and exile.
- **1937–1938** — The Great Purge leads to mass arrests and a sharp increase in the Gulag population.
- **1941–1945** — World War II causes a spike in mortality due to harsh conditions; the Gulag also produces arms and supplies.
- **1945–1946** — Millions of Soviet repatriates are "filtered"; many are sent to Gulag camps or special settlements.
- **1948** — A system of "special camps" is created exclusively for political prisoners.
- **March 1953** — Following Stalin's death, a mass amnesty is granted to non-political and short-sentence political prisoners.
- **25 January 1960** — The Gulag system is officially abolished by Khrushchev's decree, though forced labor colonies persist.

## Body

### Origins and Expansion
The Gulag's roots lie in the tsarist practice of katorga (penal servitude) and exile to Siberia. The Bolsheviks, many of whom had experienced exile, created a separate camp system during the Civil War to isolate "class-alien" elements. The system was formalized in 1929–1930 to provide a cheap, forced labor force for Stalin's rapid industrialization and colonization of remote regions. It expanded dramatically during dekulakization and the Great Purge, with political prisoners convicted under Article 58 of the Criminal Code.

### Economic and Political Functions
The Gulag had dual punitive and economic roles. It was a tool for eliminating perceived enemies of the state while also being an independent branch of the national economy. Prisoners built canals, railways, and cities; mined minerals; and logged timber. However, forced labor was inefficient, and the system was never economically profitable. Its primary economic purpose was often financing its own supervision.

### Conditions and Mortality
Conditions varied but were generally brutal, especially in remote camps like Kolyma and Norilsk. Mortality was high due to starvation, overwork, disease, and executions. The consensus from archival data is that of the 18 million people who passed through the Gulag from 1930 to 1953, between 1.5 and 1.7 million perished. A significant portion of deaths occurred during World War II. The practice of releasing terminally ill prisoners complicates mortality estimates.

### Decline and Legacy
After Stalin's death in 1953, the system began to dismantle. Khrushchev's de-Stalinization led to mass releases and rehabilitations. The Gulag was formally abolished in 1960. Its legacy includes the colonization of Siberia and the Far East, long-term psychological trauma, and a lasting impact on Russian society. Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn's *The Gulag Archipelago* (1973) gave the term its international notoriety.

## Terms
- **Gulag (ГУЛАГ):** Acronym for "Main Directorate of Correctional Labour Camps," the Soviet agency administering the forced labor camp system.
- **Article 58:** A section of the Soviet Criminal Code defining "counterrevolutionary activities," used to sentence millions to the Gulag.
- **NKVD:** People's Commissariat for Internal Affairs, the Soviet secret police that administered the Gulag from 1934 to 1946.
- **Dekulakization:** Stalin's policy of forcibly dispossessing and deporting wealthy peasants (kulaks) in the late 1920s and early 1930s.
- **Katorga:** A tsarist category of penal servitude involving hard labor, a precursor to the Gulag system.
- **Special camps:** Camps established in 1948 exclusively for political prisoners convicted under severe articles of the Criminal Code.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Death Toll:** While archival studies suggest 1.5–1.7 million deaths, some historians argue for higher figures (e.g., 6 million) by including those whose lives were shortened by camp conditions or who died shortly after release.
- **Economic vs. Political Purpose:** Scholars debate whether the Gulag was primarily an economic enterprise for state development or a political tool for repression and social control.
- **Archival Reliability:** The completeness and accuracy of Soviet archival data are debated, with some researchers arguing it may understate the true scale of repression and mortality.
- **Historiography:** Interpretations range from viewing the Gulag as a product of totalitarian ideology to seeing it as a pragmatic, if brutal, instrument of state policy.
