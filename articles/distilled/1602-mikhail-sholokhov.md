# Mikhail Sholokhov

## Overview
Mikhail Sholokhov (1905–1984) was a Soviet novelist awarded the 1965 Nobel Prize in Literature for his epic depiction of the Don Cossacks during the revolutionary upheavals of the early 20th century. Born an *inogorodnye* (outsider) in the Cossack heartland, he fought for the Bolsheviks at age 13 before becoming a writer. His masterpiece, *And Quiet Flows the Don* (1928–1940), and the collectivization novel *Virgin Soil Upturned* (1932–1960) defined the socialist realist canon. Sholokhov occupied a unique, perilous position: he enjoyed Stalin’s personal protection—intervening successfully for locals during the Great Terror—yet faced persistent, unresolved allegations that he plagiarized *And Quiet Flows the Don* from a dead White officer, Fyodor Kryukov. Manuscript discoveries in the 1980s and 1999 largely affirmed his authorship, though dissenting linguistic analyses persist.

## Timeline
- **24 May 1905** — Born in Kruzhilin hamlet, stanitsa Vyoshenskaya, Don Cossack Host region, to a Russian father and Ukrainian mother; family classified as *inogorodnye* (non-Cossack outsiders).
- **1918** — Joins Bolshevik forces in Russian Civil War at age 13; *inogorodnye* generally supported Reds, Cossacks generally supported Whites.
- **1923** — First publication: satirical article "The Test" in *Pravda*.
- **1926** — Publishes *Tales from the Don*, stories based on WWI and Civil War experiences. Begins *And Quiet Flows the Don*.
- **1928–1929** — Plagiarism rumors surface after first volumes of *And Quiet Flows the Don*; special commission validates Sholokhov's authorship.
- **1930–1933** — Corresponds directly with Stalin, exposing catastrophic conditions in Don *kolkhozes* and *sovkhozes* and OGPU torture; Stalin intervenes, sending aid and punishing officers.
- **1932** — Joins Communist Party (CPSU).
- **1937** — Elected to Supreme Soviet; refuses to attend international writers' conference until Stalin releases arrested friend P.K. Lugovoi.
- **1939** — Elected Academician of USSR Academy of Sciences; completes *And Quiet Flows the Don* (14 years). Awarded 1941 Stalin Prize.
- **1942** — Mother killed in bombing of Veshenskaya; writes war journalism and unfinished novel *They Fought for Their Country*.
- **1957** — Publishes short story "The Fate of a Man" (filmed 1959).
- **1960** — Completes second volume of *Virgin Soil Upturned* (28 years total); awarded Lenin Prize.
- **1965** — Awarded Nobel Prize in Literature.
- **1969** — Effectively stops publishing.
- **1972** — Criticizes Alexander Yakovlev’s "Against Antihistoricism," leading to Yakovlev’s Politburo censure and removal.
- **1984** — Manuscript drafts (605 pages in Sholokhov's hand) discovered; Russian Academy of Sciences later confirms authorship.
- **21 Feb 1984** — Dies of laryngeal cancer in Vyoshenskaya; buried on estate grounds.

## Body

### Early Life and Civil War
Sholokhov was born in the "land of the Cossacks" but belonged to the *inogorodnye*—non-Cossack settlers excluded from Host governance and generally poorer than the Cossack elite. His father was a Russian farmer and trader; his mother, a Ukrainian peasant widow, learned to read only to write to her son. This marginal status shaped his perspective on the Cossack world he later immortalized. In 1918, at 13, he joined the Bolshevik (Red) side in the Civil War, a conflict where class and ethnicity aligned: *inogorodnye* largely backed the Reds, while Don Cossacks largely backed the Whites. He fought for several years before turning to writing at 17.

### Literary Career and Major Works
Sholokhov’s debut, *Tales from the Don* (1926), drew on his wartime youth. He then embarked on *And Quiet Flows the Don* (1928–1940), a four-volume epic tracing the Melekhov family through WWI, Revolution, and Civil War. It won the 1941 Stalin Prize and became the cornerstone of his Nobel citation. *Virgin Soil Upturned* (Part I: *Seeds of Tomorrow*, 1932; Part II: *Harvest on the Don*, 1960) chronicled the brutal collectivization of the Don region over 28 years, hailed as a model of socialist realism and influential in China. His WWII journalism and unfinished novel *They Fought for Their Country* documented the "Great Patriotic War," during which his mother was killed in a 1942 bombing. "The Fate of a Man" (1957), a concise story of a soldier’s endurance, became a celebrated film.

### The Authorship Controversy
Doubts about *And Quiet Flows the Don* erupted in 1928: critics alleged Sholokhov, then 23, had stolen the manuscript from a dead White officer. A 1929 *Pravda* commission, comparing manuscripts to *Tales from the Don*, upheld his authorship. The debate reignited in the 1960s, championed by Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn (possibly retaliating for Sholokhov’s harsh review of *One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich*). The leading candidate for "true author" became Fyodor Kryukov, a Cossack anti-Bolshevik who died in 1920. The case was complicated by the wartime destruction of Sholokhov’s archive. In 1984, Geir Kjetsaa’s statistical analysis of sentence lengths supported Sholokhov. The discovery of 143 manuscript pages (1975) and, crucially, 890 pages of drafts and notes in 1987 (605 in Sholokhov’s hand, 285 transcribed by his wife and sisters) provided physical evidence of the creative process. The Russian Academy of Sciences’ 1999 analysis concluded Sholokhov wrote the novel. However, linguist Zeev Bar-Sella (2000s) maintains the manuscripts post-date 1929 and attributes authorship to Viktor Sevsky (Veniamin Krasnushkin).

### Political Activity and Relations with Power
Sholokhov met Stalin in 1930 and became a rare truth-teller who survived. Throughout the 1930s, he wrote detailed letters exposing famine in *kolkhozes* (collective farms), *sovkhozes* (state farms), and OGPU (secret police) torture in his district. Stalin responded by dispatching investigators (Matvei Shkiryatov, Vladimir Stavsky), executing then commuting sentences of named torturers, and sending food aid. In 1937, Sholokhov refused foreign travel until Stalin released his arrested friend, P.K. Lugovoi, succeeding after a Kremlin meeting. A 1938 entanglement with Yevgenia Yezhova (wife of NKVD chief Nikolai Yezhov) in a bugged hotel room triggered surveillance; Sholokhov confronted Stalin directly, who feigned ignorance alongside Yezhov. Sholokhov joined the CPSU in 1932, entered the Supreme Soviet in 1937, and rose to the Central Committee (1961) and vice-presidency of the Union of Soviet Writers. In 1966, he denounced the lenient sentences in the Sinyavsky–Daniel trial (dissident writers), alienating the intelligentsia and prompting open letters from Lydia Chukovskaya and Yuri Galanskov. In 1972, his attack on Alexander Yakovlev’s critique of "antihistoricism" (Russian nationalism) forced a Politburo session and Yakovlev’s ouster as propaganda chief.

### Later Life and Legacy
After 1969, Sholokhov published little, residing in Vyoshenskaya. He funded a local school with his Order of Lenin stipend and toured Europe and Japan with his Nobel prize money. He died of laryngeal cancer in 1984. Posthumous honors include an asteroid (2448 Sholokhov), the Sholokhov Moscow State University for Humanities, the National Sholokhov Museum-Reserve at his estate, and monuments in Moscow and Rostov-on-Don.

## Terms
- **inogorodnye** ("outlanders"): Non-Cossack settlers in the Don Host territory, denied voting rights in Host elections and generally poorer than the Cossack estate.
- **stanitsa**: A Cossack village or administrative unit; Vyoshenskaya was Sholokhov’s lifelong base.
- **kolkhoz / sovkhoz**: Collective farm / state farm; the targets of Sholokhov’s 1930s letters to Stalin regarding catastrophic conditions.
- **OGPU** (Unified State Political Directorate): The Soviet secret police (1922–1934), predecessor to the NKVD; Sholokhov accused its officers of torture.
- **Socialist Realism**: The official Soviet aesthetic doctrine requiring art to depict reality in its revolutionary development; *Virgin Soil Upturned* was upheld as a paradigm.
- **Great Patriotic War**: The Soviet/Russian term for the Eastern Front of WWII (1941–1945); subject of Sholokhov’s journalism and *They Fought for Their Country*.
- **Sinyavsky–Daniel Trial** (1966): Prosecution of writers Andrei Sinyavsky and Yuli Daniel for "anti-Soviet agitation"; Sholokhov publicly supported harsher punishment.
- **Fyodor Kryukov** (1870–1920): Cossack writer and White Army officer, the primary candidate in plagiarism theories regarding *And Quiet Flows the Don*.
- **Geir Kjetsaa** (1937–2008): Norwegian Slavist whose 1984 stylometric analysis (sentence-length statistics) supported Sholokhov’s authorship.
- **Zeev Bar-Sella**: Russian-Israeli linguist who, in the 2000s, argued manuscripts date post-1929 and identified Viktor Sevsky as the true author.

## Debates and open questions
- **Authorship of *And Quiet Flows the Don***: Despite the 1929 commission, Kjetsaa’s stylometrics, the 1987 manuscript trove, and the 1999 Academy verdict, a minority (notably Bar-Sella) contests Sholokhov’s authorship, proposing alternative writers (Kryukov, Sevsky) and arguing the drafts are not definitive proof of original composition.
- **Motivation for Solzhenitsyn’s accusations**: Whether Solzhenitsyn’s 1960s plagiarism campaign was purely literary or retaliatory for Sholokhov’s 1962 condemnation of *One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich* remains debated.
- **Sholokhov’s political agency**: Historians dispute whether his interventions with Stalin represent genuine moral courage within a totalitarian system or a privileged collaboration that legitimized the regime while securing personal safety and status.
- **Extent of censorship/self-censorship**: The degree to which *Virgin Soil Upturned*’s 28-year gestation and the truncation of *They Fought for Their Country* reflect state pressure versus artistic struggle is unclear from the source.
