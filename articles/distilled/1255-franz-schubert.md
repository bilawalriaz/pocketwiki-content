# Franz Schubert

## Overview

Franz Peter Schubert (1797–1828) was an Austrian composer bridging the Classical and Romantic eras. Despite dying at 31, he produced over 1,500 works, including more than 600 Lieder (German art songs), seven complete symphonies, chamber music, piano sonatas, and sacred music. Born in Vienna, he trained at the Stadtkonvikt under Antonio Salieri but worked briefly as a schoolteacher before relying on a circle of friends for support. His music, characterized by lyrical melody, harmonic daring, and structural experimentation, was largely unpublished and unperformed publicly during his lifetime. Posthumous advocacy by composers such as Mendelssohn, Schumann, Liszt, and Brahms secured his reputation as a central figure in Western classical music.

## Timeline

- **31 Jan 1797** — Born in Himmelpfortgrund, Vienna.
- **1808** — Enters Stadtkonvikt on choir scholarship; studies with Salieri.
- **1813** — Leaves Stadtkonvikt; begins teacher training.
- **1814–1816** — Teaches at father's school; composes prolifically (e.g., *Gretchen am Spinnrade*, *Erlkönig*, Mass No. 1).
- **1816** — Moves in with Franz von Schober; abandons teaching for composition.
- **1817** — Meets baritone Johann Michael Vogl, key advocate for his songs.
- **1820** — Arrested with friends for suspected revolutionary activity; Johann Senn imprisoned.
- **1821** — Admitted to Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde; *Erlkönig* published (Op. 1).
- **1822** — Begins "Unfinished" Symphony (D 759); meets Weber and Beethoven.
- **1823** — Writes first song cycle *Die schöne Müllerin*; syphilis symptoms appear.
- **1825** — Holiday in Upper Austria; composes "Ave Maria" (D 839) and begins "Great" C major Symphony (D 944).
- **1827** — Writes *Winterreise*, Impromptus, piano trios; Beethoven dies.
- **26 Mar 1828** — Only public concert of his own works; critical and financial success.
- **19 Nov 1828** — Dies in Vienna, aged 31; official cause typhoid fever (syphilis debated).
- **1839** — Mendelssohn conducts premiere of "Great" C major Symphony.
- **1867** — Grove and Sullivan recover manuscripts of symphonies and operas in Vienna.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Schubert was the twelfth child of Franz Theodor and Elisabeth's fourteen children (one illegitimate, born in 1783), of whom only five survived to adulthood. His father was a schoolmaster in Vienna. He received early violin and piano lessons from his father and brother Ignaz, quickly surpassing them. At eight, he studied with parish organist Michael Holzer, who claimed the boy already knew everything he tried to teach. In 1804, he came to the attention of Antonio Salieri, Vienna's leading musical authority. A choir scholarship placed him at the Stadtkonvikt (1808–1813), where he absorbed the orchestral works of Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven, and encountered the Lieder of Johann Rudolf Zumsteeg, which he sought to "modernize." His mother’s death in 1812 prompted Salieri to take him as a private student in thoroughbass and composition; musicologist Lorraine Byrne Bodley views the subsequent compositional surge as a refuge from grief.

### Teaching Years and the Circle of Friends
From 1814 to 1816, Schubert taught at his father's school. In 1815, he composed over 20,000 bars of music, more than half of which were for orchestra, including nine church works, a symphony, and about 140 Lieder. He met soprano Therese Grob but could not marry due to a 1815 law requiring proof of financial stability. A failed application for a Kapellmeister post in Laibach (Ljubljana) in 1816 coincided with an invitation from the affluent student Franz von Schober to share lodgings. Schubert abandoned teaching, declaring, "I compose every morning." Schober introduced him to baritone Johann Michael Vogl in 1817; Vogl became the primary interpreter of Schubert's songs in Viennese circles. The composer also participated in the *Unsinnsgesellschaft* (Nonsenses Society) and the social gatherings later termed *Schubertiads*. In 1820, he and four friends were arrested for suspicious political activity; one, Johann Senn, was imprisoned and exiled, an event that may have triggered a falling out with roommate Johann Mayrhofer.

### Musical Maturity and Operatic Failure
Schubert's style advanced markedly in 1819–1820, producing the *Wanderer Fantasy*, the *Quartettsatz*, and the oratorio *Lazarus*. Two operas, *Die Zwillingsbrüder* and *Die Zauberharfe*, were staged in 1820, but publishers remained reluctant; Anton Diabelli printed early songs only on commission. Admission to the Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde as a performing member in 1821 boosted his profile, leading to performances of his overtures and songs. He met Weber and Beethoven in 1822; Beethoven reportedly recognized his "divine genius" on his deathbed. However, Schubert's twenty stage projects uniformly failed: *Alfonso und Estrella* (1822) was rejected for its libretto; *Fierrabras* (1823) fell victim to the vogue for Rossini; *Die Verschworenen* was banned by the censor; and *Rosamunde* closed after two nights due to the play's poor quality.

### Last Years and Masterworks
Despite operatic disappointment, Schubert produced his most profound instrumental and vocal works. The "Unfinished" Symphony (D 759, 1822) broke new ground but was never mentioned to friends. The song cycles *Die schöne Müllerin* (1823) and *Winterreise* (1827), setting Wilhelm Müller, established the genre's dramatic potential. A summer at Zselíz (1818, 1824) with the Esterházy family yielded Hungarian-influenced works and the "Trout" Quintet (D 667), scored unusually for piano, violin, viola, cello, and double bass. The "Great" C major Symphony (D 944), largely written 1825–1826, was rehearsed by the Gesellschaft orchestra but never publicly performed; the reasons remain unknown, although the difficulty of the symphony is a possible explanation. In his final year (1828), Schubert completed the C major String Quintet (D 956), the three final piano sonatas (D 958–960), the *Schwanengesang* collection (posthumously assembled), and the Mass in E-flat (D 950). He sketched a D major Symphony (D 936A) anticipating Mahler and arranged counterpoint lessons with Simon Sechter.

### Final Illness and Death
Schubert's health declined in the late 1820s. In late summer 1828, physician Ernst Rinna confirmed a terminal prognosis. Symptoms—headaches, fever, vomiting, inability to retain food—matched mercury poisoning (a standard syphilis treatment), though the official cause was typhoid fever. The syphilis diagnosis, originating with biographer Otto Erich Deutsch (1907), is debated: scholar Eva M. Cybulska argues symptoms could indicate leukaemia, anaemia, or Hashimoto's thyroiditis, noting the absence of classic syphilitic signs. Robert L. Rold contends Schubert had syphilis but died of a gastrointestinal infection. Five days before death, violinist Karl Holz played Beethoven's String Quartet No. 14 for him. Schubert died 19 November 1828 at his brother Ferdinand's apartment. Per his request, he was buried near Beethoven in Währing; both were moved to Vienna's Zentralfriedhof in 1888. His epitaph by Franz Grillparzer reads: "The art of music has here interred a precious treasure, but yet far fairer hopes."

### Music: Style and Output
Schubert wrote over 1,500 works. Ernst Krenek (1947) revised his early dismissal of Schubert as a mere tunesmith, citing the piano sonatas' "technical finesse" and "appetite for experimentation." Harold C. Schonberg noted the "piercingly melodic" quality and "Schubertian" melancholy, exemplified by remote modulations (e.g., A-flat to E major in the B-flat Trio). While using Classical sonata forms, Schubert prioritized melodic development over harmonic drama, leading Robert Schumann to describe the Ninth Symphony's "heavenly lengths." His instrumental adventurousness appears in unusual modulations (String Quintet D 956, second movement: E major to F minor) and instrumentation (Arpeggione Sonata D 821; Trout Quintet D 667).

### Lieder and Art Songs
Schubert transformed the Lied from strophic, folksong-like settings into a vehicle for dramatic narrative and harmonic innovation. Leon Plantinga credits him with exploring the genre's potential "as no composer before him." Antonín Dvořák (1894) gave Schubert "chief credit" for originating the short Romantic piano piece and creating "a new epoch with the Lied." His Goethe settings—*Gretchen am Spinnrade* (D 118, spinning-wheel figuration) and *Erlkönig* (D 328, galloping accompaniment)—are landmarks of tone-painting. The cycles *Die schöne Müllerin* and *Winterreise* achieved near-operatic dramatic cohesion. The posthumous *Schwanengesang* (D 957) collects settings of Rellstab, Heine, and Seidl; Alex Ross highlights "Der Doppelgänger" for its "acutely unnerving progression in B minor in which each chord has been lobotomized by the surgical removal of one note."

### Publication and Catalogue
Only ~100 opus numbers appeared in Schubert's lifetime (mostly songs and small pieces). Manuscripts of major works languished in private cabinets until George Grove and Arthur Sullivan recovered six symphonies, *Rosamunde* music, and several operas in 1867. Breitkopf & Härtel issued a collected edition (1884–1897) with Brahms editing the symphonies. The *Neue Schubert-Ausgabe* (Bärenreiter) began mid-20th century. Otto Erich Deutsch's thematic catalogue (1951, revised 1978) assigns "D" numbers, essential because opus numbers are non-chronological and sparse. Symphony numbering remains confused: the "Unfinished" (D 759) has been numbered 7, 8, or 9; the "Great" C major (D 944) has been 7, 8, 9, or 10. English sources typically number them: No. 7 (E major, D 729), No. 8 "Unfinished" (B minor, D 759), No. 9 "Great" (C major, D 944), No. 10 (D major, D 936A).

### Recognition and Legacy
Schubert's early death inspired regret (Grillparzer's epitaph; Radu Lupu) but also counter-arguments: Robert Schumann insisted "He did enough," and András Schiff called his 31 years "very concentrated... he lived more than other people would live in 100 years." The Wiener Schubertbund (1863) became a middle-class cultural bulwark. A 1872 monument in Vienna's Stadtpark honored him. In 2008, ABC Classic FM listeners ranked his chamber works dominant: Trout Quintet #1, C major String Quintet #2, Notturno #3. Anthony Tommasini (*New York Times*) ranked him fourth greatest (after Bach, Beethoven, Mozart), citing the "Unfinished" and Ninth Symphonies as paving the way for Bruckner and Mahler. Liszt transcribed songs, calling Schubert "the most poetic musician who ever lived"; Mendelssohn premiered the "Great" Symphony (1839, abridged). 20th-century homages came from Strauss, Webern, Britten, Crumb, Zender, and even Kraftwerk. Centenary festivals in 1897 and 1928 (with a $10,000 symphony competition won by Kurt Atterberg) cemented his public stature.

## Terms

- ****Lied (pl. Lieder)**** — German art song for solo voice and piano, elevated by Schubert to a high dramatic and poetic form.
- ****Song cycle**** — A group of songs designed to be performed in sequence as a single entity, often with a unifying narrative or poetic theme (e.g., *Die schöne Müllerin*, *Winterreise*).
- ****Stadtkonvikt**** — Imperial seminary in Vienna where Schubert received his general and musical education (1808–1813).
- ****Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde**** — Society of Friends of Music in Vienna; admitted Schubert as a performing member in 1821, providing crucial performance opportunities.
- ****Schubertiads**** — Informal social gatherings of Schubert's circle featuring performances of his music, often at Ignaz von Sonnleithner's apartment.
- ****Unfinished Symphony**** — Symphony No. 8 in B minor (D 759), comprising two complete movements and sketches for a third; left incomplete for unknown reasons.
- ****Great C major Symphony**** — Symphony No. 9 in C major (D 944), Schubert's last completed symphony, noted for its expansive "heavenly lengths" and influence on later Romantics.
- ****Trout Quintet**** — Piano Quintet in A major (D 667), scored for piano, violin, viola, cello, and double bass (rather than the standard string quartet), named for its variations on the song "Die Forelle."
- ****Arpeggione**** — A short-lived, six-stringed, fretted bowed instrument (guitar-violoncello hybrid) for which Schubert wrote the Sonata in A minor (D 821).
- ****Deutsch (D) catalogue**** — Thematic catalogue of Schubert's works compiled by Otto Erich Deutsch (1951/1978), providing chronological "D" numbers used as standard identifiers.

## Debates and open questions

*   **Cause of death:** Official diagnosis was typhoid fever. The syphilis hypothesis (based on mercury-treatment symptoms and friend accounts) is contested by scholars who cite absence of typical tertiary signs, a 68 km walk weeks before death (ruling out musculoskeletal syphilis), and compositional activity until the end (ruling out neurosyphilis). Alternative theories include salmonella, leukaemia, anaemia, or Hashimoto's thyroiditis.
*   **Sexuality:** Maynard Solomon (1989) proposed homoerotic attraction; Rita Steblin and Lorraine Byrne Bodley argue for "homosocial" 19th-century friendships and heterosexual interest. The debate influences modern biography but lacks definitive evidence.
*   **"Unfinished" Symphony:** Why Schubert abandoned the B minor Symphony after two movements (and sketches for a third) remains unknown; he never mentioned it to friends.
*   **Symphony numbering:** Confusion persists over the numbering of the late symphonies (particularly the "Unfinished" and "Great") due to posthumous publication order and the status of fragments (D 729, D 936A).
*   **Operatic failure:** Despite 20 attempts, Schubert never achieved stage success. Causes cited include weak libretti (often by friends), the dominance of Rossini, censorship, and a mismatch between his lyrical style and theatrical demands.
*   **Schwanengesang status:** Published posthumously as a cycle, it combines poems by Rellstab, Heine, and Seidl. Scholars debate whether Schubert intended it as a unified cycle like *Winterreise* or if it is a publisher's anthology.
