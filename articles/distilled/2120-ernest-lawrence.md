# Ernest Lawrence

## Overview
Ernest Orlando Lawrence (1901–1958) was an American physicist who invented the cyclotron, a particle accelerator that enabled high-energy nuclear research. He received the 1939 Nobel Prize in Physics for this invention. Lawrence was a central figure in "Big Science," leading major wartime projects for the Manhattan Project and founding two national laboratories that bear his name.

## Timeline
- **August 8, 1901** — Born in Canton, South Dakota.
- **1925** — Earns Ph.D. in physics from Yale University.
- **1928** — Hired as associate professor at University of California, Berkeley.
- **1929** — Conceives the cyclotron after seeing a diagram in a journal.
- **1931** — First successful cyclotron (11-inch) produces 80,000-electron volt protons.
- **1936** — Radiation Laboratory becomes an official UC department; Lawrence is director.
- **November 1939** — Awarded Nobel Prize in Physics for the cyclotron.
- **1941** — Berkeley team discovers plutonium-239, a fissile isotope for bombs.
- **1943** — Leads development of electromagnetic isotope separation (calutrons) for Manhattan Project.
- **July 16, 1945** — Observes the Trinity nuclear test.
- **1952** — Helps establish Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory.
- **August 27, 1958** — Dies in Palo Alto, California.

## Body

### Invention and Development of the Cyclotron
In 1929, Lawrence conceived a compact particle accelerator after seeing a diagram of a linear accelerator. He proposed using a magnetic field to spiral charged particles between two semicircular electrodes. The first working model, built with graduate students, was only 4 inches in diameter. Lawrence then pursued a pattern of building progressively larger cyclotrons (27-inch, 37-inch, 60-inch) to achieve higher energies. His Radiation Laboratory at UC Berkeley became a world-leading center for nuclear physics research in the 1930s.

### Nobel Prize and Medical Applications
Lawrence received the 1939 Nobel Prize "for the invention and development of the cyclotron and for results obtained with it, especially with regard to artificial radioactive elements." He actively promoted the cyclotron's use for medical research, particularly in producing radioisotopes for cancer therapy. Working with his brother John, a physician, they used phosphorus-32 from the cyclotron to treat blood diseases and leukemia, laying groundwork for nuclear medicine.

### Manhattan Project and Electromagnetic Separation
During World War II, Lawrence led the development of electromagnetic isotope separation for the Manhattan Project. This process used "calutrons," a hybrid of mass spectrometers and cyclotrons, to separate fissile uranium-235 from uranium-238. A massive plant, Y-12, was built at Oak Ridge, Tennessee. The process was inefficient but based on proven technology and was built in stages. It produced the first enriched uranium for the atomic bomb.

### Post-War "Big Science" and Livermore
After the war, Lawrence became a forceful advocate for "Big Science," which required large-scale government funding for massive machines and research teams. He campaigned for and helped establish the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in 1952, initially to pursue thermonuclear (hydrogen) bomb development. He also oversaw the completion of the 184-inch synchrocyclotron and planned the bevatron, a gigaelectronvolt synchrotron.

## Terms
- **Cyclotron**: A circular particle accelerator invented by Lawrence that uses a magnetic field to spiral charged particles between two electrodes, accelerating them to high energies.
- **Calutron**: A device used for electromagnetic isotope separation, combining features of a mass spectrometer and a cyclotron. The name derives from "California university cyclotron."
- **Big Science**: A term for large-scale scientific research projects that require massive funding, equipment, and teams, a model Lawrence championed post-WWII.
- **Uranium enrichment**: The process of increasing the concentration of the fissile isotope uranium-235 from natural uranium, necessary for atomic bombs.
- **Synchrocyclotron**: An improved cyclotron design that varies the frequency of the accelerating voltage to account for relativistic effects at high energies.
- **Bevatron**: A large proton synchrotron at Berkeley designed to reach energies in the billion-electron-volt (GeV) range.
- **Radioisotope**: A radioactive form of an element, used in medicine for diagnosis and therapy, and in research as tracers.
- **Lawrencium (Lr)**: Chemical element 103, named in Lawrence's honor after its discovery at Berkeley in 1961.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Initial Scientific Misinterpretation**: Lawrence's team initially postulated a "new particle" as the source of energy from deuterium bombardment, but this was later shown by others to be contamination from nuclear fusion reactions.
- **Efficiency vs. Necessity**: The electromagnetic separation process at Oak Ridge was acknowledged to be inefficient and costly compared to other methods, but was chosen for its lower technical risk and ability to be built in stages.
- **Political Tensions**: Lawrence's laboratory was embroiled in post-war political tensions, including loyalty oath campaigns and security investigations of staff like Robert Oppenheimer, which created ill-feeling and damaged collaborations.
- **Legacy of Livermore**: Lawrence's widow later petitioned to remove his name from the Livermore lab due to its focus on nuclear weapons, highlighting an ongoing debate about the ethical legacy of his work.
