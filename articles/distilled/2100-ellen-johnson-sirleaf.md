# Ellen Johnson Sirleaf

## Overview
Ellen Johnson Sirleaf is a Liberian economist and politician who served as the 24th president of Liberia from 2006 to 2018. She is internationally significant as the first elected female head of state in Africa, a Nobel Peace Prize laureate, and a key figure in Liberia's recovery from civil wars. Her presidency focused on debt relief, anti-corruption efforts, and national reconciliation, though it faced challenges including criticism from a Truth and Reconciliation Commission.

## Timeline
- **29 October 1938** — Born in Monrovia, Liberia.
- **1979–1980** — Serves as Minister of Finance under President William Tolbert.
- **12 April 1980** — Samuel Doe seizes power in a coup; Tolbert is executed. Sirleaf flees the country.
- **1985** — Returns to contest elections; arrested for sedition, sentenced to 10 years, later pardoned.
- **1997** — Finishes second in presidential election won by Charles Taylor.
- **16 January 2006** — Inaugurated as President of Liberia after winning the 2005 election.
- **2007** — U.S. waives Liberia's $391 million debt; G-8 provides $324.5 million for IMF debt.
- **2009** — Truth and Reconciliation Commission report recommends barring Sirleaf from office for 30 years.
- **2010** — Liberia reaches completion point for HIPC debt relief; external debt is largely written off.
- **2011** — Wins re-election; jointly awarded the Nobel Peace Prize.
- **2016** — Elected Chair of ECOWAS, the first woman to hold the position.
- **2018** — Presidency ends; she founds the Ellen Johnson Sirleaf Presidential Center for Women and Development.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Born to a Gola father and a Kru-German mother, Sirleaf was educated in Liberia and the United States, earning a Master of Public Administration from Harvard University. She began her career in Liberia's Treasury Department and served as Assistant Minister and later Minister of Finance under President William Tolbert. After the 1980 coup by Samuel Doe, she initially accepted a post but fled the country after publicly criticizing the new regime. She worked for Citibank and the World Bank abroad before returning to Liberia for the 1985 elections, where her criticism of the government led to her arrest and imprisonment.

### Political Rise and Civil Wars
Sirleaf's political path was shaped by Liberia's civil conflicts. She initially supported Charles Taylor's rebellion against Doe in the First Liberian Civil War but later opposed him. She ran for president in 1997, placing second to Taylor. Following the Second Liberian Civil War, she won the 2005 presidential election, defeating George Weah in a run-off. She was re-elected in 2011, a campaign marked by controversy when her Nobel Peace Prize award was criticized as political interference.

### Presidency: Domestic Policy
Sirleaf's presidency prioritized national recovery. A major achievement was securing massive debt relief, including the cancellation of over $4.9 billion in external debt through initiatives like the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) program. Her administration established the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) to investigate the civil wars. The TRC's 2009 report controversially recommended barring Sirleaf from public office for 30 years due to her early support for Taylor; she apologized, and the Supreme Court later ruled the recommendation unconstitutional. Her "zero tolerance" anti-corruption policy faced criticism as corruption remained a challenge, though she established new oversight bodies. She also signed West Africa's first Freedom of Information law.

### Presidency: Foreign Policy and International Image
Sirleaf strengthened ties with the United States and China. She offered to host the U.S. Africa Command (AFRICOM) and addressed the U.S. Congress in 2006. Her government severed ties with Libya's Gaddafi regime during the 2011 civil war. Internationally, she was lauded as a transformative leader; *The Economist* called her "arguably the best president the country has ever had." She received numerous honors, including the U.S. Presidential Medal of Freedom (2007) and the Nobel Peace Prize (2011, jointly with Leymah Gbowee and Tawakkol Karman) for her work for women's rights and peace.

### Post-Presidency
After leaving office, Sirleaf founded a center for women's development. She served as WHO Goodwill Ambassador and co-chaired the Independent Panel for Pandemic Preparedness and Response during the COVID-19 pandemic. Her family's involvement in government drew scrutiny, including a 2019 investigation into her son Charles Sirleaf for economic sabotage.

## Terms
- **Americo-Liberian:** A term for descendants of free-born and formerly enslaved African Americans who settled in Liberia in the 19th century, forming a distinct cultural and political elite.
- **Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC):** A body established to investigate human rights abuses and civil conflicts in Liberia from 1979 to 2003, aiming to promote national healing.
- **Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative:** A program led by the IMF and World Bank to provide debt relief to qualifying low-income countries.
- **Paris Club:** An informal group of official creditors that negotiates debt-restructuring agreements for debtor countries.
- **Sedition:** Speech or conduct inciting rebellion against the authority of a state.
- **Ex post facto law:** A law that retroactively changes the legal consequences of actions committed before the law was passed.
- **ECOWAS:** The Economic Community of West African States, a regional political and economic union of 15 countries.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **TRC Recommendation:** The TRC's recommendation to bar Sirleaf from office for 30 years sparked debate over reconciliation versus political accountability, ultimately ruled unconstitutional.
- **Corruption Allegations:** Critics argued her administration failed to fully deliver on its "zero tolerance" corruption pledge, while Sirleaf pointed to institutional reforms and the need for political compromise.
- **Gay Rights Stance:** Sirleaf's position—refusing to repeal the existing anti-sodomy law while also vetoing proposed harsher penalties—was seen by some as defending discrimination and by others as a pragmatic stance to avoid political suicide in a conservative society.
- **Family in Government:** The appointment of her sons and stepson to government positions raised concerns about nepotism and conflicts of interest.
