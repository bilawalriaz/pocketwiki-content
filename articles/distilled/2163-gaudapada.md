# Gaudapada

## Overview
Gauḍapāda (fl. c. 6th century CE) was an early medieval Hindu philosopher and foundational teacher of the Advaita Vedanta school. His primary work, the *Māṇḍūkya Kārikā*, is the earliest extant systematic treatise on Advaita, presenting rational arguments for nonduality. His ideas profoundly influenced later philosophers, most notably Adi Shankara, who revered him as a "highest teacher" (*Paramaguru*).

## Body

### Authorship and Dating
Gauḍapāda is the author or compiler of the *Māṇḍūkya Kārikā* (also called *Gauḍapāda Kārikā* or *Āgama Śāstra*), a 215-verse explanation of the short *Mandukya Upanishad*. His exact dates are uncertain, with estimates ranging from the early 6th to 7th century CE. One estimate places him around 500 CE, based on citations of his work by the Buddhist scholar Bhavaviveka. Another, based on the traditional lineage to Adi Shankara, places him in the 7th century. He is certain to have lived after the 4th century, as he cites Buddhist views from that period.

### The Māṇḍūkya Kārikā: Structure and Core Teachings
The *Kārikā* is divided into four chapters. The first three chapters are Vedantic in doctrine, while the fourth uses Buddhist terminology, indicating influence.

*   **Chapter One (Agama):** Presents the traditional doctrine of the Self (*Ātman*) in three states: waking (*Vaiśvānara*), dreaming (*Taijasa*), and deep sleep (*Prajñā*). It introduces a fourth, transcendent state, *Turiya*, which is nondual, unchanging, and without sorrow. The syllables of *Om* (A, U, M) are symbolically linked to the first three states, with *Turiya* beyond them.
*   **Chapter Two (Vaitathya):** Argues that both dream and waking experiences are unreal (*māyā*), as objects perceived in them are later found to be nonexistent. The true reality is the nondual *Ātman*, which is imagined as various dualities.
*   **Chapter Three (Advaita):** Critiques dualistic worship and argues that the individual soul (*jīva*) is identical with the universal *Brahman*, like space enclosed in a jar is identical with infinite space. It describes *Asparśa Yoga* ("non-contact" meditation) as the practice to realize this nonduality by resting the mind free from subject-object duality.
*   **Chapter Four (Alatasanti):** Uses the metaphor of a spinning firebrand to explain consciousness. It cross-examines the causal theories of the Samkhya school and the non-origination (*Ajativada*) theory of Buddhists. It asserts that only consciousness (*vijñāna*) is real and that origination is impossible.

### Soteriology and Relationship to Buddhism
For Gauḍapāda, liberation (*mokṣa*) is the realization that the Self is unborn and has never been in bondage. Bondage is a mental projection (*vikalpa*), and liberation follows the transcendence of ignorance and error to realize the *Turiya* state.

The influence of Buddhist doctrines on Gauḍapāda is a major scholarly debate. He adopted concepts like *vijñapti-mātra* ("representation-only") from Yogācāra Buddhism and the four-cornered negation from Madhyamaka Buddhism. However, his work is fundamentally Vedantic, grounded in the Upanishads and the authority of the *Ātman*/*Brahman*, which is the opposite of the Buddhist doctrine of no-self (*anātman*). Some scholars suggest Chapter Four may have a different author due to its distinct style and lack of citation by later Vedantins.

## Terms
*   **Advaita Vedanta:** A school of Hindu philosophy teaching that the individual self (*Ātman*) and the ultimate reality (*Brahman*) are one and the same, and the perceived world is an illusion.
*   **Ātman:** The inner self, soul, or consciousness in living beings.
*   **Brahman:** The ultimate, unchanging, infinite reality; the ground of all being.
*   **Māyā:** The illusory or apparent nature of the empirical world, which conceals the true nondual reality.
*   **Turiya:** The "fourth" state of consciousness, beyond waking, dreaming, and deep sleep; the true nature of the Self.
*   **Ajativada:** The doctrine of "non-origination," asserting that nothing is ever truly born or created.
*   **Asparśa Yoga:** A meditative practice of "non-contact" where the mind is stilled and freed from grasping dualistic appearances.
*   **Jīva:** The individual soul or living being, seemingly separate from Brahman due to ignorance.
*   **Vikalpa:** Mental construction, imagination, or conceptual differentiation that creates the illusion of duality.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Buddhist Influence:** The extent to which Gauḍapāda's philosophy was influenced by Buddhism is heavily debated. While most scholars acknowledge the use of Buddhist terminology and concepts, they maintain his core doctrine is Vedantic. Some, like Swami Nikhilananda, deny significant influence, while others, like Dasgupta, state it is undeniable.
*   **Authorship of Chapter Four:** Due to its distinct style, use of Buddhist terminology, and the fact that later Vedantins primarily quoted the first three chapters, some scholars (e.g., Murti, King) suggest Chapter Four may have been authored by someone other than Gauḍapāda.
*   **Dating:** Gauḍapāda's exact dates are uncertain, with estimates varying by over a century (early 6th to 7th century CE) based on different historical and textual evidence.
