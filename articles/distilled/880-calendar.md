# Calendar

## Overview
A calendar is a system for organizing days by assigning them to named periods—typically days, weeks, months, and years—and can also refer to the physical record of that system or a list of planned events. Calendars synchronize these periods with astronomical cycles, most commonly the solar year and the lunar month, and serve practical functions in agriculture, civil governance, religious observance, and commerce. The evolution from early lunar and lunisolar systems to today’s globally dominant Gregorian calendar reflects millennia of astronomical observation, political reform, and cultural adaptation.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Earliest recorded physical calendars emerge in Egypt and Sumer
- **c. 1500–500 BC** — Vedic India develops sophisticated astronomical calendars for rituals
- **Iron Age** — Babylonian calendar influences Persian, Zoroastrian, and Hebrew systems
- **Classical Greece** — Multiple Hellenic calendars developed
- **46 BC** — Julius Caesar reforms the Roman calendar into the Julian calendar
- **2nd century AD** — Fragmentary Coligny calendar shows possible algorithmic intercalation
- **632 AD** — Islamic calendar established, prohibiting intercalation
- **1582** — Gregorian calendar introduced to correct Julian drift
- **1923** — Greece becomes the last European country to adopt the Gregorian calendar

## Body

### Etymology and Early Development
The word *calendar* derives from the Latin *kalendae*, the first day of each Roman month, itself from *calare* (“to call out”), referencing the announcement of the new moon. The Latin *calendarium* meant “account book,” since debts were settled on the calends. Old French *calendrier* entered Middle English as *calender* by the 13th century; the modern spelling *calendar* is early modern.

Early calendars relied on the most predictable natural cycles: the solar year and the lunar month. Pre-modern societies worldwide used lunation and the year as primary time units. The Roman calendar retained remnants of a pre-Etruscan 10-month solar year. The first recorded physical calendars, dependent on writing, appeared in Bronze Age Egypt and Sumer.

### Major Calendar Systems
Most ancient calendars were lunisolar, adding intercalary months to align lunar months with the solar year. The Roman calendar was reformed by Julius Caesar in 46 BC into the Julian calendar, which replaced lunar observation with a leap-day algorithm every four years, decoupling months from lunar phases. The Gregorian calendar, introduced in 1582, corrected the Julian calendar’s residual error against the solar year.

The Islamic calendar, based on Muhammad’s prohibition of intercalation (dated to 632 AD), is purely lunar and shifts relative to the seasons. Hindu, Chinese, Hebrew, and Buddhist calendars remain predominantly lunisolar, often incorporating complex cycles like the Hebrew 19-year cycle.

### Calendar Types and Structures
Calendars can be astronomical (based on observation) or arithmetic (rule-based). Astronomical calendars, like the traditional Islamic and Second Temple-era Jewish calendars, are perpetually accurate but hard to predict. Arithmetic calendars, like the current Jewish calendar, are easy to compute but slowly lose accuracy due to changes in Earth’s rotation.

Solar calendars assign dates to solar days; lunar calendars number days within lunar phases; lunisolar calendars add months to reconcile both. The week cycle is not tied to any astronomical phenomenon. Most calendars combine multiple cycle types and include non-cyclical elements.

### Modern Usage and Formats
The Gregorian calendar is the de facto international standard for civil purposes, using a 400-year leap-day cycle to average 365.2425 days per year. Its epoch originates from the medieval convention of Dionysius Exiguus, with years labeled AD or CE. Religious calendars persist alongside it: the Hebrew calendar for Jewish observance, the Islamic Hijri calendar for Muslim festivals, and various Hindu and Buddhist calendars in South and Southeast Asia.

Fiscal calendars structure budgeting and taxation, such as the U.S. fiscal year (October 1–September 30) or India’s (April 1–March 31). Software calendars, found in PDAs, smartphones, and computers, offer electronic scheduling, event management, and data sharing via formats like iCalendar (.ics) and protocols like CalDAV and iTIP.

## Terms
- **Epoch**: A reference starting point from which calendar dates are counted.
- **Intercalation**: The insertion of extra days or months to align a calendar with astronomical cycles.
- **Leap year**: A year containing an extra day (or month) to compensate for the mismatch between calendar and astronomical years.
- **Lunisolar calendar**: A calendar combining lunar months with solar-year adjustments via intercalary months.
- **Gregorian calendar**: The modern solar calendar introduced in 1582, correcting the Julian calendar’s drift.
- **Julian calendar**: The solar calendar reformed by Julius Caesar in 46 BC, using a four-year leap cycle.
- **Hijri calendar**: The Islamic lunar calendar beginning with the Hijra (622 AD), used for religious observances.
- **ISO week date**: A week-based calendar system where weeks begin on Monday and Week 1 contains the first Thursday of the year.
- **iCalendar**: A standard file format (.ics) for exchanging calendar data across platforms.
- **CalDAV**: A protocol enabling networked access and sharing of calendar data over WebDAV.

## Debates and Open Questions
Several modern calendar reform proposals—such as the World Calendar, International Fixed Calendar, Holocene calendar, and Hanke–Henry Permanent Calendar—have been promoted but failed to gain widespread adoption due to concerns over continuity, upheaval, and disruption of religious cycles. The interpretation of prehistoric artifacts as calendars remains debated: Alexander Marshack’s reading of a 25,000-year-old bone baton as a lunar calendar and Michael Rappenglueck’s analysis of a 15,000-year-old cave painting as lunar notation are both controversial. Additionally, the extent to which early calendars were observation-based versus algorithmic continues to be studied, particularly regarding fragmentary sources like the Coligny calendar.
