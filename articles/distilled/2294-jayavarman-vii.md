# Jayavarman VII

## Overview
Jayavarman VII (c. 1122–1218) was the most powerful king of the Khmer Empire, ruling from 1181 to c. 1218. A devout Mahayana Buddhist, he is renowned for repelling a Cham invasion, centralizing the state, and launching a vast public works and temple-building program that included the iconic Bayon and Angkor Thom. His reign is considered a high point of Khmer power and is marked by the introduction of a welfare state motivated by Buddhist compassion.

## Body

### Military Victories and Consolidation of Power
Jayavarman VII came to prominence in his mid-50s by leading the resistance against the Cham occupation of Cambodia, which began with the 1177 sacking of Yasodharapura. After a successful campaign, including a naval battle, he recaptured the capital and was crowned king in 1181. Early in his reign, he repelled another Cham attack and quelled a rebellion in the vassal Kingdom of Malyang. With the help of the military commander Prince Sri Vidyanandana, he later conquered Champa (c. 1190–1191), making it a dependency for 30 years and expanding Khmer control along the Mekong Valley.

### Public Works and Buddhist Welfare State
Motivated by Mahayana Buddhist principles to alleviate suffering, Jayavarman VII initiated a grand construction program. This included practical infrastructure like 102 hospitals, 121 rest houses ("houses with fire") every 15 kilometers along raised highways, and reservoirs. He also erected Buddha stone images in 23 towns across the empire. This program represented an early form of welfare state, though it required massive labor and coincided with greater state centralization.

### Monumental Temple Construction
Jayavarman VII's building program culminated in major religious monuments. He dedicated Ta Prohm (1186) to his mother and Preah Khan to his father. His greatest achievement was the Bayon, a multifaceted temple at the center of his new capital city, Angkor Thom ("Grand Angkor"). The Bayon's walls feature bas-reliefs depicting warfare, daily life, and a naval battle on the Tonlé Sap. He also built the smaller, beautiful temple of Neak Pean on an island in an artificial lake.

### Legacy and Succession
Jayavarman VII died c. 1218 and was succeeded by his son Indravarman II. Despite having many sons, only Indravarman II inherited the throne, illustrating that Khmer succession was not strictly patrilineal. His Buddhist works were later targeted for destruction by his successor, the Shaivite king Jayavarman VIII, who had Buddha images removed or converted into linga. His bust remains a popular icon in Khmer households.

## Terms
- **Mahayana Buddhism**: A major branch of Buddhism emphasizing the path of the bodhisattva and compassion for all sentient beings, which motivated Jayavarman VII's public works.
- **Champa**: A kingdom in what is now central and southern Vietnam, a frequent rival and enemy of the Khmer Empire.
- **Yasodharapura**: The capital city of the Khmer Empire, later known as Angkor.
- **Bas-relief**: A type of sculpture where figures are carved to project slightly from a background, used extensively on temple walls.
- **Angkor Thom**: A large walled city complex built by Jayavarman VII, meaning "Grand Angkor."
- **Bayon**: The state temple of Jayavarman VII at the center of Angkor Thom, known for its many towers and bas-reliefs.
- **Linga**: A symbolic representation of the Hindu deity Shiva, often worshipped as a phallic emblem.
- **Bhikku**: A fully ordained male Buddhist monk.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact date and even the occurrence of the 1177 Cham invasion and sacking of Angkor have been questioned by historian Michael Vickery, who doubts the reliability of the Chinese sources for this period.
- The scale of Jayavarman VII's building program and the centralization of the state it required raise questions about the labor and social costs, despite its stated aim of alleviating suffering.
