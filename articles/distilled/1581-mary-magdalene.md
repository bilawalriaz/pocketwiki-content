# Mary Magdalene

## Overview
Mary Magdalene was a historical Jewish woman from Magdala who followed Jesus of Nazareth, supported his ministry financially, and witnessed his crucifixion and burial. She is distinguished in the canonical gospels as the first person to discover the empty tomb and encounter the risen Jesus, earning the title "Apostle to the Apostles." Despite this biblical prominence, Western Christianity conflated her with Mary of Bethany and the unnamed "sinful woman" of Luke 7 beginning in 591 CE, creating a centuries-long tradition portraying her as a repentant prostitute—a portrayal with no scriptural basis. The Eastern Orthodox Church never accepted this conflation, venerating her instead as a virtuous Myrrhbearer and "Equal to the Apostles." Gnostic texts from the second century onward elevate her as Jesus's most insightful disciple and recipient of secret revelation, often depicting tension between her and Peter. Modern scholarship affirms her historical existence and leadership among female disciples while rejecting later legends of her marriage to Jesus or travel to France as unfounded.

## Timeline
- **c. 30 CE** — Mary Magdalene follows Jesus; Luke 8:2–3 records Jesus exorcised "seven demons" from her; she supports his ministry financially.
- **c. 30 CE** — Witnesses Jesus's crucifixion (Mark 15:40, Matt 27:55–56, John 19:25) and burial by Joseph of Arimathea (Mark 15:47, Matt 27:61).
- **c. 30 CE** — Discovers empty tomb and encounters risen Jesus (all four gospels; John 20:11–18 gives most detailed account).
- **c. 55 CE** — Paul writes 1 Corinthians 15:3–8; pre-Pauline creed names Peter (Cephas) as first resurrection witness, omitting Mary Magdalene.
- **c. 170–180 CE** — Pagan critic Celsus dismisses Mary as a "hysterical female"; Origen defends her in *Against Celsus*.
- **c. 200 CE** — Tertullian references the "sinful woman" of Luke 7 to prove Jesus's physicality; early conflation begins.
- **591 CE** — Pope Gregory I delivers Easter sermon identifying Mary Magdalene, Mary of Bethany, and Luke's sinful woman as one person; explicitly calls her a repentant prostitute.
- **c. 1260** — Jacobus de Voragine compiles *The Golden Legend*, codifying elaborate medieval legends of Mary's wealth, journey to France, and desert penance.
- **1279** — Charles II of Naples orders excavation at Saint-Maximin-la-Sainte-Baume; "discovers" relics, sparking major pilgrimage site.
- **1517** — Jacques Lefèvre d'Étaples publishes argument against the composite Magdalene; Sorbonne condemns his view as heretical (1521).
- **1969** — Pope Paul VI revises General Roman Calendar, separating Mary Magdalene from Mary of Bethany and the sinful woman; feast day (July 22) focuses solely on her as first resurrection witness.
- **2016** — Catholic Congregation for Divine Worship elevates her liturgical commemoration to a Feast, adding preface naming her "Apostle to the Apostles."

## Body

### Historical Figure and Gospel Portrayal
Scholars universally accept Mary Magdalene as a historical figure. Her epithet *Magdalene* likely denotes origin from Magdala, a Galilean fishing village, though some scholars argue it may be an honorific meaning "Tower." The Synoptic Gospels (Mark, Matthew, Luke) are the earliest sources; Paul’s epistles never mention her. Luke 8:2–3 provides the fullest ministry summary: Jesus expelled "seven demons" from her (interpreted as severe illness or symbolic of total affliction), after which she joined a group of women—including Joanna and Susanna—who funded Jesus’s mission from their own resources. In every Synoptic list of female followers, Mary appears first, signaling leadership analogous to Peter’s among the male apostles.

### Crucifixion, Burial, and Resurrection Witness
All four gospels place Mary at the cross, alongside other women (names vary). Historians regard this as probable fact: women were less liable to arrest, and the criterion of embarrassment makes invention unlikely. The burial accounts differ: Mark and Matthew name Mary and another Mary as witnesses to Joseph of Arimathea’s interment; Luke mentions "the women from Galilee"; John omits women at the burial but includes Nicodemus. Scholars debate the historicity of an honorable tomb burial, given Roman practice of denying burial to crucified criminals.

The resurrection narratives diverge sharply. Mark 16:1–8 (original ending) has Mary, Mary mother of James, and Salome find the stone rolled away, hear an angelic announcement, and flee in silence. Matthew adds an earthquake, an angel rolling the stone, and Jesus appearing to the women. Luke has a larger group (including Joanna) find the tomb open, meet two men, and report to disbelieving apostles. John 20 elevates Mary dramatically: she comes alone, finds the tomb empty, summons Peter and the Beloved Disciple, then encounters Jesus herself—mistaking him for the gardener until he speaks her name. He commissions her to announce the resurrection to the disciples, making her the *apostola apostolorum*. The "longer ending" of Mark (second century) harmonizes these traditions by adding appearances to Mary, the Emmaus disciples, and the Eleven.

Scholarly debate centers on the empty tomb’s historicity. Ehrman initially argued the story must be early because women’s testimony was legally invalid, so Christians would not invent them as primary witnesses. He later reversed this, arguing the tomb burial itself is improbable, making the empty tomb a later legend. Casey contends the women are literary heroines, not legal witnesses, and the empty tomb narrative was crafted by Mark from the historical fact of their presence at the cross and burial.

### Patristic Era and the Prostitute Conflation
Early Church Fathers barely mention Mary. Celsus (c. 170–180) attacks her credibility; Origen counters by citing Matthew’s two-woman witness. Hippolytus conflates her with Mary of Bethany. Ephrem the Syrian (c. 306–373) first explicitly calls her a redeemed sinner, possibly influenced by Magdala’s reputation for vice. Gregory of Nyssa links her seven demons to transgression; Ambrose bizarrely suggests two distinct Mary Magdalens; Augustine praises her ardent love but does not conflate her with the sinful woman.

The definitive Western shift occurred in 591 when Pope Gregory I identified three figures as one: Mary Magdalene (Luke 8:2), Mary of Bethany (Luke 10:39, John 12:1–8), and the unnamed sinful woman who anoints Jesus’s feet (Luke 7:36–50). In his homily, Gregory allegorized the seven demons as the seven deadly sins and the anointing oil as perfume once used for "forbidden acts." This composite Magdalene—penitent prostitute—dominated Western theology, art, and liturgy for 1,400 years. The Eastern Orthodox Church rejected this conflation entirely, maintaining her as a virtuous Myrrhbearer and "Equal to the Apostles."

### Medieval Legend Expansion
From the tenth century, Western writers fabricated aristocratic biographies. Odo of Cluny made her a royal noblewoman; Honorius Augustodunensis added adultery and flight to Jerusalem. Under influence of the *vita* of Mary of Egypt (a fourth-century penitent hermit), artists depicted Mary Magdalene as a desert ascetic, hair covering her naked body. The title *apostolorum apostola* became commonplace by the twelfth century (Hugh of Semur, Peter Abelard, Geoffrey of Vendôme).

Competing French shrines claimed her relics. Vézelay Abbey announced discovery of her skeleton c. 1050; in 1279 Charles II "found" a second burial at Saint-Maximin-la-Sainte-Baume with an explanatory inscription, building a basilica there. *The Golden Legend* (c. 1260) synthesized these traditions: Mary, Martha, and Lazarus (conflated as siblings) sail to Marseille, convert the governor, and Mary spends thirty years in a Provençal cave, lifted by angels at canonical hours. Domenico Cavalca speculated she was betrothed to John the Evangelist; Voragine dismissed this.

Cathar dualists allegedly taught Jesus had a sexual relationship with Mary (concubine or wife), a claim recorded by orthodox chroniclers to discredit them. Late medieval piety intensified the penitent motif: a fourteenth-century Dominican described her brutal self-mutilation, inspiring Donatello’s gaunt *Penitent Magdalene* (c. 1454).

### Reformation and Counter-Reformation
Lefèvre d'Étaples (1517) challenged the composite Magdalene; the Sorbonne condemned him. Luther and Zwingli accepted the composite figure; Luther jested about Jesus’s adultery with her. Zwingli demanded abolition of her cult; Calvin rejected the conflation as Catholic ignorance. The Counter-Reformation doubled down on the penitent Magdalene: Baroque art emphasized eroticized nudity; "Magdalene cells" (hermitages) dotted noble estates in southern Germany. The 1969 calendar reform finally separated her liturgy from the sinful woman, assigning John 20 as the Gospel reading.

### Gnostic Portrayals
Nag Hammadi texts (second–third century) portray Mary as Jesus’s favored confidante and sole true understander of his teaching.
- **Dialogue of the Savior**: Mary speaks three sayings attributed to Jesus in the New Testament; narrator praises her as "a woman who understood everything."
- **Pistis Sophia**: Mary asks 39 of 64 questions; Jesus calls her "blessed above all women" and "the fullness of fullness." Peter complains she dominates; Jesus defends her authority.
- **Gospel of Thomas** (Saying 114): Peter demands Mary leave because "women are not worthy of the life." Jesus replies he will "make her male" so she can enter the kingdom—reflecting the ancient view that women were "imperfect men."
- **Gospel of Philip**: Calls Mary Jesus’s *koinônos* (partner/companion); says he loved her more than the disciples and kissed her often. The kiss symbolizes transmission of gnosis, not romance.
- **Gospel of Mary**: Only Gnostic gospel named for a woman. Mary comforts frightened disciples, reveals a vision of the soul’s ascent through planetary archons, and is challenged by Andrew and Peter. Matthew defends her: "If the Savior made her worthy, who are you to reject her?"
- **Borborite texts** (lost, summarized by Epiphanius): Alleged ritual sexual practices and consumption of semen/menstrual blood as Eucharist; likely polemical distortion.

Scholars (Sanders, Ehrman, King) agree these texts reflect late Gnostic theology, not historical memory, though they attest to early contests over female authority.

### Relics and Modern Veneration
Major relic sites: skull and *noli me tangere* (forehead flesh) at Saint-Maximin-la-Sainte-Baume (annual tibia procession); left hand at Simonopetra Monastery, Mount Athos; left foot (first to enter Holy Sepulchre) in Rome’s San Giovanni dei Fiorentini, reliquary by Cellini.

Catholicism: 2016 decree elevated her July 22 memorial to a Feast (rank of Apostles), with preface "Apostle to the Apostles." Eastern Orthodoxy: venerates as Myrrhbearer and Equal-to-the-Apostles; red-egg miracle tradition with Tiberius. Protestantism: Anglican/Episcopal Festival/Major Feast; ELCA honors as apostle (lesser festival); Presbyterians call her "apostle to the apostles." Baháʼí Faith: exalts her as "archetypal woman of all cycles," channel of confirmation to the disciples, equal or superior to men in holiness.

### Speculations and Popular Culture
- **Beloved Disciple theory** (Jusino, 1998): Argues John’s Beloved Disciple was originally Mary Magdalene; no manuscript evidence.
- **The Da Vinci Code** (2003): Popularized false claims—Benjaminite lineage, marriage to Jesus, child/bloodline, disguised presence in *Last Supper*. No ancient evidence; art historians identify the figure as John.
- **Gospel of Jesus’s Wife** (2012): Coptic fragment "My wife... she will be my disciple"; consensus: modern forgery; King retracted in 2016.
- **Scholarly consensus** (Ehrman, Casey): No evidence for marriage or sexual relationship. Jesus’s teaching on marriage in the coming kingdom (Mark 12:25) implies celibacy; gospels name all other family members but never a wife. Sources are "too contradictory and simultaneously too silent" for certainty (Kripal).

## Terms
- ****Myrrhbearer**** — Eastern Orthodox title for the women who brought myrrh to Jesus’s tomb; equivalent to Western "Three Marys."
- ****Apostle to the Apostles** (*apostola apostolorum*)** — Title for Mary Magdalene as first witness commissioned to announce the resurrection to the Twelve.
- ****Composite Magdalene**** — Conflation of Mary Magdalene, Mary of Bethany, and Luke’s sinful woman into a single penitent prostitute figure.
- ****Seven demons**** — Luke 8:2; Mark 16:9. Interpreted as literal exorcisms, severe illness, or symbolic of total affliction (seven = completion).
- ****Koinônos**** — Greek in *Gospel of Philip* for Mary’s relationship to Jesus; translated "partner, companion, associate"; denotes spiritual partnership.
- ****Noli me tangere**** — Latin "Touch me not" (John 20:17); Jesus’s words to Mary at the tomb; also a relic (forehead flesh) at Saint-Maximin.
- ****Gnostic writings**** — Second–third century texts (Nag Hammadi) emphasizing secret knowledge; portray Mary as visionary leader superior to male apostles.
- ****Criterion of embarrassment**** — Historical argument: early Christians would not invent women as primary resurrection witnesses because women’s testimony was legally invalid.
- ****Magdalene asylums**** — Eighteenth-century institutions for "wayward women," named for Mary as patroness of penitent prostitutes.
- ****Equal to the Apostles** (*isapostolos*)** — Eastern Orthodox honorific for Mary Magdalene, denoting status equivalent to the Twelve.

## Debates and open questions
- **Origin of "Magdalene"**: Geographic (Magdala) vs. honorific ("Tower")—Schrader Polczer and Taylor argue for the latter.
- **Historicity of the empty tomb**: Ehrman (2006) argued criterion of embarrassment supports it; Ehrman (2014) reversed, citing implausibility of tomb burial. Casey argues it is a Markan literary creation.
- **Nature of the "seven demons"**: Literal possession, multiple exorcisms, symbolic totality, or severe psychological trauma?
- **Relationship to the Beloved Disciple**: Jusino’s theory (Mary as Beloved Disciple) lacks textual support; remains a minority hypothesis.
- **Gnostic Mary vs. historical Mary**: To what extent do Gnostic texts preserve authentic tradition vs. projecting later theological debates onto her figure?
- **Relic authenticity**: Competing French sites (Vézelay, Saint-Maximin) and Mount Athos claim bodily remains; no scientific verification possible.
- **Jesus’s marital status**: *Da Vinci Code* claims and *Gospel of Jesus’s Wife* fragment are universally rejected by scholars; silence of canonical gospels and Paul suggests celibacy, but absolute certainty is impossible.
