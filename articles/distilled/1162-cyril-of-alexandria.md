# Cyril of Alexandria

## Overview
Cyril of Alexandria (c. 376–444) was Patriarch of Alexandria from 412 to 444 and a dominant figure in the Christological controversies of the late 4th and 5th centuries. His theological stance—emphasizing the unity of Christ’s divine and human natures and defending the title Theotokos (“God-bearer”) for Mary—shaped the Council of Ephesus (431) and left a lasting legacy in both Eastern and Western Christianity.

## Timeline
- **c. 376** — Born in Didouseya, Egypt
- **412** — Becomes Patriarch of Alexandria
- **415** — Expels Jews from Alexandria; Hypatia murdered
- **425** — Adopts Metonic 19-year lunar cycle in Paschal table
- **429** — Writes letters defending Theotokos
- **430** — Issues Twelve Anathemas against Nestorius
- **431** — Council of Ephesus condemns Nestorius
- **444** — Dies in Alexandria

## Body

### Early Life and Rise to Power
Born circa 376 in Didouseya (modern El-Mahalla El-Kubra), Cyril was the nephew of Theophilus, Patriarch of Alexandria, who arranged his education in theology and rhetoric. He accompanied Theophilus to Constantinople in 403 for the Synod of the Oak, which deposed John Chrysostom. Upon Theophilus’s death in 412, Cyril became Patriarch after a violent rivalry with Archdeacon Timotheus. As Patriarch, he wielded significant ecclesiastical and civic authority, closing Novatian churches and asserting control over Alexandria’s diverse population.

### Conflict with Orestes and the Jews
Cyril clashed with Orestes, the imperial prefect, over secular jurisdiction. In 415, Orestes punished the Christian provocateur Hierax for inflaming a crowd during an edict reading. In response to the harassment of Christians, Cyril threatened the Jews with "the utmost severities." The Jews then grew furious and plotted to flush Christians out at night by running through the streets claiming that the Church of Alexander was on fire. When Christians responded to what they believed was the burning church, the Jews fell upon and slew them. Cyril responded by banishing the Jews from Alexandria and seizing their property. The conflict escalated when monks from the desert attacked Orestes; a monk named Ammonius threw a stone hitting Orestes in the head. Orestes had Ammonius tortured to death, but Cyril later honored him as a martyr.

### The Murder of Hypatia
Hypatia, a renowned philosopher and advisor to Orestes, was murdered in March 415 by a Christian mob led by Peter the Lector. She was stripped, killed with ostraka (roof tiles or oyster shells), and her body burned outside the city. While Socrates Scholasticus did not name Cyril directly, later sources like Damascius blamed him for inciting the violence. Modern historians debate his direct involvement, though he certainly fostered an environment hostile to pagan intellectuals.

### Theological War with Nestorius
The dispute with Nestorius began in 429 when Cyril wrote letters to the Egyptian monks warning them of Nestorius's views on the Theotokos. Nestorius argued that Mary was Christotokos (“Christ-bearer”), distinguishing between Christ’s divine Logos and human nature. Cyril countered that the Word of God truly suffered in the flesh and that Mary gave birth to the incarnate Son. Their exchange of letters grew increasingly polemical. In 430, Cyril wrote his famous 12 Anathemas, condemning anyone who refused to call Mary Theotokos. Theodosius II convened the Council of Ephesus in 431, where Cyril’s allies secured Nestorius’s deposition. However, Nestorius’s supporters then condemned Cyril as a heretic. Cyril eventually prevailed through political maneuvering, securing Nestorius’s exile.

### Christology and Mariology
Cyril taught that the Logos Incarnate suffered and died, maintaining the continuity of one subject—God the Word—from pre-incarnate to incarnate state. His phrase “one physis of the Word of God made flesh” influenced later miaphysite theology. Central to his Mariology was the belief that Mary’s role as Theotokos affirmed Christ’s full divinity. His Homily IV at Ephesus praised Mary as “container of the uncontained,” establishing devotional language that became standard in Orthodox tradition.

### Legacy and Death
Cyril died in 444, but the theological disputes continued through the Robber Synod (449) and the Council of Chalcedon (451). His writings remained authoritative in both Eastern and Western churches. The Second Council of Constantinople (553) praised him as one who “announced the right faith of Christians.”

## Terms
- **Theotokos**: Greek term meaning “God-bearer,” affirming Mary as the mother of the incarnate Word.
- **Christotokos**: Alternative title meaning “Christ-bearer,” used by Nestorius to distinguish Christ’s natures.
- **Physis**: Nature or essence, particularly in reference to Christ’s divine and human natures.
- **Hypostasis**: Individual subsistence or person; in Trinitarian theology, refers to the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.
- **Parabolani**: Volunteer Christian associations in Alexandria, often involved in charitable and sometimes violent activities.
- **Miaphysite**: Christian tradition emphasizing one incarnate nature of Christ, associated with Cyril’s theology.
- **Ousia**: Essence or being, especially of God or Christ.
- **Synaxarium**: Collection of lives of saints and accounts of their feast days.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Cyril’s role in Hypatia’s murder**: While Damascius directly implicated Cyril, Socrates Scholasticus avoided explicit attribution. Modern scholars debate whether Cyril incited the mob or merely created a climate of hostility.
- **Extent of Jewish expulsion**: Socrates claims all Jews were banished, while John of Nikiû suggests only those involved in the massacre were exiled.
- **Nature of Cyril’s Christology**: His formula “one physis” is interpreted variously as monophysite, miaphysite, or a unique expression of Chalcedonian orthodoxy.
- **Responsibility for posthumous controversies**: Whether Cyril’s theology led inevitably to the Monophysite schism remains debated among historians and theologians.
