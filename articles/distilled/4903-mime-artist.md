# Mime artist

## Overview
Mime is a theatrical art form where performers tell stories and convey emotions solely through body movements and gestures, without speech. It has ancient roots and has evolved through various cultural traditions, influencing modern theatre, film, and street performance. Its development highlights the universal human capacity for non-verbal communication and artistic expression.

## Timeline
- **c. 5th century BC** — First recorded mime performer, Telestēs, appears in Aeschylus's play *Seven Against Thebes* in Ancient Greece.
- **2nd century BC** — Mime becomes the sole dramatic event at the Roman festival of Floralia.
- **2nd century AD** — Roman Emperor Trajan banishes mime artists.
- **Early 19th century** — Jean-Gaspard Deburau in Paris solidifies the modern silent, white-faced mime figure.
- **20th century** — Étienne Decroux develops corporeal mime into a highly sculptural form.
- **20th century** — Jacques Lecoq contributes significantly to mime and physical theatre training methods.
- **2017** — Mime practice is included in France's list of Intangible Cultural Heritage elements.

## Body

### History and Origins
The performance of mime originates in Ancient Greece, where the name comes from *Pantomimus* ("imitator"). Early Greek mime was not necessarily silent. In Rome, mime (*mimus*) became a major form of theatre, eventually replacing the Atellan farce. It was distinguished by its lack of masks and inclusion of female performers. Roman mime featured stock characters like the *archymimus* (lead) and the *stupidus* (stooge). Its popularity fluctuated under different emperors; for example, Emperor Nero himself acted as a mime.

In medieval Europe, early forms like mummer plays evolved. The modern silent, white-faced mime figure was solidified in 19th-century Paris by Jean-Gaspard Deburau. Parallel traditions exist in other cultures, such as Indian classical theatre (e.g., Kathakali), which uses stylized gesture and mime illusions, and Japanese Noh theatre, which uses masks and physical performance. These Asian traditions later influenced key European mime practitioners.

### Modern Development and Forms
In the 20th century, mime was formalized as a distinct art. Jacques Copeau, influenced by commedia dell'arte and Japanese Noh, used masks in actor training. His pupil, Étienne Decroux, developed corporeal mime into a sculptural form outside naturalism. Jacques Lecoq further contributed with influential training methods.

Mime appears in various formats. In early silent film, its stylized physical acting was essential for storytelling, used effectively in German Expressionist film and by comedians like Charlie Chaplin and Buster Keaton. On stage, Marcel Marceau and his character "Bip" are the most famous examples. Mime is also popular in street theatre and busking. While traditionally silent and performed in black and white with whiteface, contemporary mimes may use vocal sounds and forgo whiteface. Mime acts can be comical or serious.

### Mime in Literature
The art form has been featured in literature, such as in Michael Jacot's novel *The Last Butterfly*, about a mime in Nazi-occupied Europe, and Heinrich Böll's *The Clown*, which relates the downfall of a mime artist.

## Terms
- **Mime / Pantomime**: The acting out of a story through body motions without speech.
- **Corporeal Mime**: A highly sculptural form of mime developed by Étienne Decroux, focusing on the entire body as an expressive instrument.
- **Mummer**: An earlier English term for a mime performer.
- **Stock Characters**: Recurring character types in a theatrical tradition, such as the Roman mime's *stupidus* (stooge).
- **Intangible Cultural Heritage**: Cultural traditions or living expressions inherited from ancestors and passed on to descendants, as recognized by UNESCO or national bodies.
- **Commedia dell'arte**: An Italian theatrical form of improvisation using masked characters, which influenced modern mime.
- **Noh**: A major form of Japanese classical musical drama that has influenced modern physical theatre and mime.
- **Busking**: Performing in public places for gratuities.

## Debates and Open Questions
The article notes that any recreation of mime as performed before the 20th century is "largely conjecture," based on interpretation of diverse sources, as there was no major treatise on the art before Étienne Decroux. The precise boundaries between mime and other forms of physical theatre or dance (like Butoh) are also fluid, with various practitioners adopting techniques across disciplines.
