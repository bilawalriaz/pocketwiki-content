# Gustave Flaubert

## Overview
Gustave Flaubert (1821–1880) was a French novelist regarded as the leading exponent of literary realism. His painstaking pursuit of *le mot juste* ("the right word") and his ideal of an author "present everywhere and visible nowhere" established the conventions of modern realist narration—in which precise, visual detail replaces authorial commentary. Though his output was small, works like *Madame Bovary* (1857) and *Sentimental Education* (1869) bridged Romanticism and Realism, influencing contemporaries such as Guy de Maupassant and Émile Zola, and later writers including Franz Kafka and J. M. Coetzee.

## Timeline
- **12 Dec 1821** — Born in Rouen, France, to Achille-Cléophas Flaubert, a senior surgeon, and Anne Justine Caroline Fleuriot.
- **1840** — Left Lycée Pierre-Corneille for Paris to study law; found studies indifferent and city distasteful.
- **1846** — Suffered an epileptic attack; abandoned law studies and returned to family estate at Croisset.
- **1849–1850** — Traveled to the Middle East (Greece, Egypt, Beirut, Istanbul); contracted syphilis in Beirut.
- **1850–1856** — Wrote *Madame Bovary*; serialized in *Revue de Paris* (1856); tried and acquitted for immorality (1857).
- **1858** — Traveled to Carthage to research *Salammbô*.
- **1862** — Published *Salammbô*.
- **1869** — Published *Sentimental Education* (*L'Éducation sentimentale*), his last complete novel.
- **1870–1871** — Prussian soldiers occupied his house during the Franco-Prussian War; mother died in 1872.
- **1875–1877** — Wrote *Three Tales* (*Trois Contes*).
- **8 May 1880** — Died at Croisset of a cerebral hemorrhage, aged 58; unfinished *Bouvard et Pécuchet* published posthumously (1881).

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Flaubert was born in Rouen, Upper Normandy, the second son of a hospital director. He began writing in childhood and attended the Lycée Pierre-Corneille until 1840. He moved to Paris to study law but was an indifferent student. After an epileptic attack in 1846, he abandoned legal studies permanently and returned to Croisset, near Rouen, where he lived for the rest of his life.

### Major Works and Composition
Flaubert’s first finished work was the novella *November* (1842). He completed the first version of *The Temptation of Saint Anthony* in 1849; friends Louis Bouilhet and Maxime Du Camp urged him to burn it and write about contemporary life instead. Heeding this advice, he spent five years (1850–1856) writing *Madame Bovary*, a portrait of provincial adultery that led to a prosecution for immorality (resulting in acquittal). He then spent four years researching and writing *Salammbô* (1862), set in ancient Carthage, followed by seven years on *Sentimental Education* (1869), which intertwines a young man’s romantic life with the 1848 Revolution and the Second Empire’s founding. In the 1870s, he published a revised *Temptation of Saint Anthony* and *Three Tales* (1877), comprising "A Simple Heart," "The Legend of St. Julian the Hospitaller," and "Herodias." His final years were consumed by *Bouvard et Pécuchet*, a satire on the futility of knowledge, left unfinished at his death.

### Perfectionist Style
Flaubert pursued *le mot juste* with extreme rigor, sometimes spending a week on a single page. He avoided clichés, abstractions, and assonances, aiming for a style "rhythmic as verse, precise as the language of the sciences." He famously declared the author should be "like God in the universe, present everywhere and visible nowhere." This discipline made him far less prolific than peers like Balzac or Zola; Walter Pater called him the "martyr of style." His correspondence reveals that correct prose never flowed naturally but was achieved only through relentless revision.

### Personal Life and Health
From 1846 to 1854, Flaubert had a relationship with poet Louise Colet, his only serious romance per biographer Émile Faguet. He never married or had children, writing in 1852 that he would "transmit to no one the aggravations and the disgrace of existence." He traveled extensively with Maxime Du Camp (Brittany, 1846; Middle East, 1849–50) and later to Carthage (1858). He was open in his letters about sexual encounters with male and female prostitutes during these travels. He contracted syphilis in Beirut, suffering venereal disease for life. The 1870s brought Prussian occupation of his home, his mother’s death (1872), and financial ruin caused by his niece’s husband’s business failures. He died of a cerebral hemorrhage in 1880.

### Legacy and Influence
Critic James Wood argues Flaubert created modern realist narration: "a time before Flaubert and a time after him." His fusion of Romantic and Realist impulses allowed realists, formalists, and later modernists to claim him. Vladimir Nabokov identified Flaubert as the greatest influence on Franz Kafka, noting their shared use of legal/scientific language for ironic precision and authorial impersonality. Flaubert influenced contemporaries Maupassant, Zola, Daudet, and the Goncourts, and 20th-century figures including Jean-Paul Sartre (who wrote *The Family Idiot*), Michel Foucault, Roland Barthes, Mario Vargas Llosa, and Georges Perec. His *Œuvres Complètes* appeared in 1885; his correspondence with George Sand was published in 1884.

## Terms
- **Literary Realism**: A 19th-century movement depicting everyday life and society without idealization; Flaubert is considered its leading French exponent.
- **Le mot juste** (French): "The right word"; Flaubert’s principle that precise diction is the key to literary quality.
- **Impersonal Narration**: Flaubert’s ideal that the author be "present everywhere and visible nowhere," removing overt commentary.
- **Free Indirect Discourse**: A narrative technique (perfected by Flaubert) blending a character’s thoughts with the narrator’s voice.
- **Salammbô** (1862): Flaubert’s historical novel set in ancient Carthage, noted for archaeological research and exoticism.
- **Sentimental Education** (*L'Éducation sentimentale*, 1869): Novel following Frédéric Moreau through the 1848 Revolution; Flaubert’s last complete novel.
- **Three Tales** (*Trois Contes*, 1877): Collection including "A Simple Heart," "The Legend of St. Julian the Hospitaller," and "Herodias."
- **Bouvard et Pécuchet** (1881): Unfinished posthumous satire on two copy-clerks attempting every branch of knowledge, exposing human mediocrity.
- **Guy de Maupassant** (1850–1893): French short-story writer and novelist, Flaubert’s protégé and literary heir.
- **The Family Idiot** (1971–72): Jean-Paul Sartre’s multi-volume psychoanalytic biography of Flaubert.

## Debates and open questions
- **Genre of *The Temptation of Saint Anthony***: Classified variously as a novel, prose poem, or drama; Flaubert revised it across three decades (1849, 1856, 1872, 1874).
- **Flaubert’s Political Stance**: He described himself variously as a "romantic and liberal old dunce," an "enraged liberal," and a hater of despotism, yet withdrew from active politics; scholars debate the coherence of his views.
- **Autobiographical Elements in *Sentimental Education***: The novel draws on Flaubert’s youth and the 1848 Revolution, but the extent to which Frédéric Moreau represents the author remains contested.
- **Cause of Epilepsy/Nervous Attacks**: The 1846 attack ended his legal career; the precise diagnosis (epilepsy vs. other neurological condition) is debated by biographers.
- **Status of *Bouvard et Pécuchet***: Flaubert considered it his masterpiece, but its unfinished state and satirical target (knowledge itself vs. bourgeois mediocrity) invite divergent readings.
