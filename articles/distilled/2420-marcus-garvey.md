# Marcus Garvey

## Overview
Marcus Garvey (1887–1940) was a Jamaican political activist and founder of the Universal Negro Improvement Association (UNIA), the largest mass movement in Black history. A black nationalist and Pan-Africanist, he advocated for racial separatism, economic independence, and African unification—ideas known as Garveyism. His work influenced later movements like Rastafari, the Nation of Islam, and Black Power, though collaborations with white supremacists and controversial views made him a polarizing figure.

## Timeline
- **17 Aug 1887** — Born in Saint Ann's Bay, Jamaica.
- **1914** — Founds the Universal Negro Improvement Association (UNIA) in Jamaica.
- **1916** — Moves to New York City, establishing a UNIA branch in Harlem.
- **1919** — Launches the *Negro World* newspaper and the Black Star Line shipping company.
- **Oct 1919** — Survives an assassination attempt in Harlem.
- **Aug 1920** — UNIA's First International Convention declares Garvey "Provisional President of Africa."
- **Jun 1922** — Meets with Ku Klux Klan (KKK) leadership, causing widespread controversy.
- **1923** — Convicted of mail fraud related to Black Star Line stock sales.
- **1925-1927** — Imprisoned in Atlanta Federal Penitentiary.
- **Dec 1927** — Deported to Jamaica after his sentence is commuted.
- **1929** — Founds Jamaica's first political party, the People's Political Party (PPP).
- **1935** — Relocates to London, England.
- **10 Jun 1940** — Dies in London.
- **1964** — Body returned to Jamaica and reburied as a National Hero.
- **19 Jan 2025** — Posthumously pardoned by U.S. President Joe Biden.

## Body

### Early Life and Travels
Born to a moderately prosperous Afro-Jamaican family, Garvey was apprenticed to a printer as a teenager. After working in Kingston and becoming involved in trade unionism, he traveled extensively through Costa Rica, Panama, and England between 1910 and 1914. In London, he worked for the *African Times and Orient Review* and was deeply influenced by Booker T. Washington's *Up from Slavery*. These experiences exposed him to global colonialism and racial inequality, shaping his vision for a unified Black political movement.

### Founding and Growth of UNIA
Returning to Jamaica in 1914, Garvey founded UNIA with the motto "One Aim. One God. One Destiny." After limited local success, he moved to the U.S. in 1916. UNIA grew rapidly in Harlem, emphasizing Black self-reliance, economic independence, and racial pride. Garvey launched businesses like the Negro Factories Corporation and the *Negro World* newspaper. The movement peaked in 1920 when UNIA's convention in Harlem declared Garvey the "Provisional President of Africa" and adopted the "Declaration of the Rights of the Negro Peoples of the World."

### Ideology and Controversies
Garveyism blended black nationalism and Pan-Africanism. Garvey advocated for racial separatism, arguing integration was impossible and that Black people should build their own institutions. He supported the Back-to-Africa movement, envisioning a unified, independent Africa. His ideology was controversial: he denounced miscegenation, clashed with integrationist leaders like W.E.B. Du Bois, and infamously collaborated with the KKK, who shared his separatist views. He also promoted a Black-centric Christianity and economic capitalism for the diaspora.

### Legal Troubles and Decline
The Black Star Line, Garvey's flagship shipping venture, was plagued by mismanagement and financial failure. In 1923, Garvey was convicted of mail fraud for selling stock in a ship the company did not yet own. He blamed a Jewish conspiracy for his conviction. After his appeal failed, he was imprisoned (1925–1927) and then deported to Jamaica. UNIA's membership and finances declined sharply during and after his imprisonment.

### Later Years and Legacy
In Jamaica, Garvey founded the People's Political Party but faced legal and financial difficulties. He moved to London in 1935, where he remained until his death in 1940. Despite controversies, Garvey is celebrated for instilling racial pride and self-worth among Black people globally. He was declared Jamaica's first National Hero in 1969. His ideas directly influenced the Rastafari movement, the Nation of Islam, and Pan-African leaders like Kwame Nkrumah of Ghana. In 2025, he received a posthumous presidential pardon in the United States.

## Terms
- **Garveyism**: The political and social ideology of Marcus Garvey, emphasizing black nationalism, Pan-Africanism, racial separatism, and economic self-reliance.
- **Universal Negro Improvement Association (UNIA)**: The mass movement founded by Garvey in 1914 to promote Black unity, pride, and economic independence.
- **Pan-Africanism**: A worldwide movement that aims to encourage and strengthen bonds of solidarity between all indigenous and diaspora people of African descent.
- **Black Star Line**: A shipping company established by Garvey in 1919 to facilitate trade and travel between Africa and the African diaspora, and to promote economic independence.
- **Back-to-Africa movement**: A movement encouraging African Americans to emigrate to Africa, which Garvey supported as a means to achieve racial self-determination.
- **Provisional President of Africa**: A title Garvey assumed in 1920, declaring himself the leader of a government-in-exile for a future unified African state.
- **Negro World**: The weekly newspaper published by UNIA from 1919, which served as a key organ for spreading Garvey's ideas.
- **Mail fraud**: The federal crime for which Garvey was convicted in 1923, related to the sale of stock in the Black Star Line.
- **Racial separatism**: The belief that different racial groups should live and develop separately, a core tenet of Garvey's ideology.
- **National Hero (Jamaica)**: A title posthumously conferred on Garvey in 1969, recognizing his contribution to Jamaican and Black history.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Collaboration with the KKK**: Garvey's meeting with the Ku Klux Klan remains highly controversial. He argued they were honest about their separatism, while critics saw it as a betrayal that undermined the fight for civil rights.
- **Antisemitism**: Garvey's antisemitic remarks during his trial, blaming a "Jewish cabal" for his conviction, are a major point of criticism, though he later denounced Nazi racial intolerance.
- **Effectiveness of the Black Star Line**: Scholars debate whether the shipping line was a visionary enterprise doomed by external sabotage and poor management, or a fundamentally flawed venture that defrauded investors.
- **Views on Africa and Africans**: Garvey's vision for Africa has been criticized as paternalistic and ignorant of the continent's diverse cultures, as he never visited and promoted a model based on Western imperialism.
- **Relationship with W.E.B. Du Bois**: The bitter rivalry between Garvey and Du Bois symbolizes the historical tension between black nationalist separatism and integrationist civil rights strategies.
- **Pardon and Historical Legacy**: The 2025 posthumous pardon by President Biden reignites debate about the validity of his original conviction and seeks to formally rectify a historical injustice against a civil rights leader.
