# Troy

## Overview
Troy, also known as Ilion, was an ancient city in northwestern Anatolia (modern Turkey) that was occupied for over 4,000 years. It is most famous as the legendary setting of the Trojan War in Greek mythology. Archaeological excavations have revealed a sequence of nine major settlement layers, with the Late Bronze Age city (Troy VI–VII) showing some correspondence to Hittite records and potentially providing a historical kernel for the myths.

## Timeline
- **c. 3600 BC** — First settlement (Troy 0) at the site.
- **c. 3000 BC** — Troy I founded as a small fortified city.
- **c. 2550 BC** — Troy II built, noted for its wealth and massive architecture.
- **c. 1750 BC** — Troy VI founded, becoming a major fortified city with a lower town.
- **c. 1300 BC** — Troy VIh destroyed, possibly by earthquake.
- **c. 1180 BC** — Troy VIIa destroyed by fire, showing evidence of attack.
- **c. 700 BC** — Greek immigrants begin rebuilding the site (Troy VIII).
- **334 BC** — Alexander the Great visits and sacrifices at the site.
- **85 BC** — City destroyed by the Roman general Fimbria.
- **20 BC** — Emperor Augustus finances restoration of the sanctuary of Athena.
- **1998** — Archaeological site added to UNESCO World Heritage List.

## Body

### Archaeological Layers and Excavation
The site of Hisarlık is a tell (artificial mound) containing nine primary archaeological layers, designated Troy I to IX, with a pre-Bronze Age layer (Troy 0). The city was repeatedly destroyed and rebuilt. Early excavations by Heinrich Schliemann (from 1871) initially misidentified the wealthy Troy II as Homeric Troy. Later work by Wilhelm Dörpfeld and Carl Blegen established the sequence and identified the Late Bronze Age layers (Troy VI–VII) as the most likely candidates for the historical city behind the myths. A major reinterpretation came in the 1980s with Manfred Korfmann's discovery of a large lower town for Troy VI, showing it was a significant settlement, not just a citadel.

### Troy I–V (Early and Middle Bronze Age)
Troy I (c. 3000–2550 BC) was a small fortified settlement with stone and mudbrick houses. Troy II (c. 2550–2300 BC) was larger and richer, with impressive walls and imported luxury goods, but it predates the Mycenaean era. Troy III–V (c. 2300–1750 BC) are less well-understood due to excavation gaps, but show continued occupation and some cultural influences from the Aegean.

### Troy VI–VII (Late Bronze Age)
Troy VI (c. 1750–1300 BC) was a major city with a massive "sawtooth" fortification wall, a lower town, and foreign contacts, including with Mycenaean Greece. It was likely the city known as **Wilusa** in Hittite texts. Troy VI was destroyed c. 1300 BC, possibly by an earthquake. Troy VIIa (c. 1300–1180 BC) was rebuilt by its survivors in a more crowded style and was destroyed by fire around 1180 BC, contemporary with the Late Bronze Age collapse. Troy VIIb shows cultural changes and a decline in population.

### Troy VIII–IX (Greek and Roman Periods)
After a period of reduced activity, the site was redeveloped as a Greek city (Troy VIII) from c. 700 BC, centered on the sanctuary of Athena Ilias. It became a tourist and pilgrimage site due to its Homeric associations. After destruction in 85 BC, it was rebuilt as the Roman city Ilium (Troy IX), favored by Roman leaders like Julius Caesar and Augustus who claimed descent from Troy. It flourished until the Gothic sack of 267 AD.

### Historical Troy and the Trojan War
Hittite records from the 14th–13th centuries BC refer to a vassal state named **Wilusa** in northwest Anatolia, generally identified with Troy. These texts mention conflicts and treaties involving Wilusa and the **Ahhiyawa** (likely Mycenaean Greeks), but they do not describe a specific war matching the Iliad. Archaeological evidence shows destruction layers at Troy (VIh and VIIa) but no definitive proof of a Greek attack. The exact relationship between the archaeological site and the Homeric legend remains a subject of scholarly debate.

## Terms
- **Tell**: An artificial mound formed by the accumulated remains of successive human settlements.
- **Stratigraphy**: The study of rock or soil layers (strata) to understand chronological sequence.
- **Megaron**: A rectangular hall with a central hearth, a common architectural form in Mycenaean and some Anatolian cultures.
- **Ashlar**: Precisely cut and squared stone used in masonry.
- **Pithos (pl. pithoi)**: A large storage jar, often buried in the ground.
- **Koinon**: A league or association of cities, in this context for organizing the festival of Athena Ilias.
- **Wilusa**: The name for Troy in Hittite texts, likely corresponding to the Greek "Ilion."
- **Ahhiyawa**: The Hittite term for a major power in western Anatolia, widely identified with Mycenaean Greece.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Which layer is Homeric Troy?** Troy VI and VIIa are the primary candidates due to their size, fortifications, and destruction layers, but neither is a perfect match.
- **Historicity of the Trojan War:** While Hittite texts confirm political turmoil in the region involving Wilusa and Ahhiyawa, there is no direct evidence for a decade-long Greek siege as described in the Iliad.
- **Language of Troy:** The language spoken in Bronze Age Troy is unknown; candidates include Luwian, Greek, or others, based on limited evidence like seals.
- **Cause of Troy VI's destruction:** The collapse of its walls is often attributed to an earthquake, but internal revolt or attack cannot be ruled out.
