# David Hume

## Overview
David Hume (1711–1776) was a Scottish philosopher, historian, and essayist whose empiricist system challenged fundamental assumptions about knowledge, causation, the self, and religion. His work, beginning with *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40), sought to ground human understanding in experience and observation, while his skeptical arguments—particularly the problem of induction and the is–ought distinction—profoundly shaped modern philosophy, science, and ethics.

## Body

### Empiricism and the Mind
Hume’s philosophy begins with the distinction between **impressions** (vivid sensory experiences) and **ideas** (fainter mental copies). All ideas derive from impressions, rejecting innate concepts. He further divides perceptions into simple (indivisible) and complex (composite), arguing that complex ideas like the self are merely bundles of perceptions linked by association.

### Hume’s Fork and Causation
Hume’s fork separates **relations of ideas** (a priori truths like mathematics) from **matters of fact** (empirical knowledge). Causal claims fall under the latter but cannot be logically deduced from their causes. Instead, causation arises from **constant conjunction**—repeated observation of events occurring together—which breeds expectation through **custom** or habit. This leads to the **problem of induction**: we cannot rationally justify believing the future will resemble the past.

### Self and Personal Identity
Rejecting substantialist views of the self, Hume argued that introspection reveals only particular perceptions—heat, cold, love, pain—never a persistent self. The self is thus a “bundle of perceptions” held together by relations of resemblance and causation. He later expressed dissatisfaction with this account in the *Treatise*’s Appendix.

### Morality and Sentiment
Hume held that **morality stems from sentiment**, not reason. Passions, not reason, drive action; reason is “the slave of the passions.” He introduced the **is–ought problem**, noting that normative conclusions cannot be derived from factual premises without additional normative premises. His moral sentimentalism influenced later thinkers like Adam Smith and modern metaethics.

### Religion and Miracles
Hume critiqued religious arguments, especially the **design argument**, by noting that we have no experience of world-making and thus cannot infer a designer. In his essay “Of Miracles,” he argued that testimony for miracles is always outweighed by the regularity of natural laws, since miracles are violations of those laws. He also proposed that religions evolved from polytheism to monotheism via fear of the unknown.

### Free Will and Determinism
A **compatibilist**, Hume reconciled freedom with determinism by defining liberty as the ability to act according to one’s will, and necessity as the consistent conjunction of causes and effects. Moral responsibility requires causation—if actions were random, praise or blame would be meaningless.

### Political Theory and History
Hume advocated for **limited government**, **constitutionalism**, and **rule of law**, emphasizing moderation and skepticism toward radical change. His *History of England* (1754–1762) became a bestseller, interpreting English liberty as the pinnacle of political progress. Though labeled a Tory historian, he described himself as a “skeptical Whig.”

### Economic Thought
In *Political Discourses*, Hume contributed early theories on money, interest, and trade. He anticipated **monetarism**, arguing that changes in money supply affect prices and that there is no fixed amount of money needed for prosperity. He rejected Locke’s view of property as natural right, instead grounding it in scarcity.

## Terms
- **Impressions**: Vivid sensory experiences from which all ideas originate.
- **Ideas**: Fainter mental copies of impressions.
- **Hume’s Fork**: Division between relations of ideas (a priori) and matters of fact (empirical).
- **Problem of Induction**: The inability to rationally justify belief that the future resembles the past.
- **Constant Conjunction**: Repeated pairing of events that creates the impression of causation.
- **Bundle Theory**: View that the self is a collection of perceptions without underlying substance.
- **Compatibilism**: Doctrine that free will is compatible with causal determinism.
- **Is–Ought Problem**: The logical gap between descriptive statements and prescriptive conclusions.
- **Custom/Habit**: Psychological mechanism replacing reason in forming expectations about the future.
- **Design Argument**: Theological argument inferring God’s existence from apparent order in nature.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Religious Views**: Was Hume an atheist, deist, or agnostic? His writings oscillate between critique and cautious theism, leaving his true stance debated.
- **Causation Interpretations**: Logical positivists see causation as mere regularity; realists argue Hume acknowledged necessary connection; quasi-realists claim he occupied a middle ground.
- **Self and No-Self**: Is Hume’s bundle theory eliminativist (denying the self) or reductionist (redefining it)? Some link it to Buddhist anattā doctrine.
- **Slave of the Passions**: Does Hume fully reject practical reason, or allow limited rational influence on moral judgment?
- **Slave Trade Involvement**: Recent scholarship debates whether Hume actively promoted or merely conveyed proposals for slave plantation investment.
- **Historical Bias**: Critics argue Hume’s *History of England* reflects anti-Whig, pro-royalist bias, while others see it as a nuanced political philosophy text.
