# Number

## Overview

A number is a mathematical object used to count, measure, and label. The most basic examples are the natural numbers: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and so forth. Individual numbers can be represented in spoken or written language with number words, or with dedicated symbols called numerals; for example, "eleven" is a number word and "11" is the corresponding numeral. The most common representation is the Hindu–Arabic numeral system, a decimal system which can display any non-negative integer using a combination of ten Arabic numeral symbols called digits. In mathematics, the notion of a number has been extended over the centuries to include zero, negative numbers, rational numbers, real numbers, and complex numbers, with calculations done via arithmetical operations like addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and exponentiation. The concept of a number has been further extended through algebraic structures, some of which are explicitly referred to as numbers (such as p-adic numbers and hypercomplex numbers), though this is more a matter of convention than mathematical distinction.

## Body

### First use of numbers

Bones and other artifacts have been discovered with marks cut into them that many believe are tally marks. Some historians suggest that the Lebombo bone (dated about 43,000 years ago) and the Ishango bone (dated about 22,000 to 30,000 years ago) are the oldest arithmetic artifacts, but this interpretation is disputed. These tally marks may have been used for counting elapsed time, such as numbers of days or lunar cycles, or keeping records of quantities. A tallying system has no concept of place value, which limits its representation of large numbers, but tallying systems are considered the first kind of abstract numeral system. The earliest unambiguous numbers in the archaeological record are the Mesopotamian base 60 system (c. 3400 BC); place value emerged in the 3rd millennium BCE. A Babylonian clay tablet dated to 1900–1600 BC provides an estimate of π as 3.125, possibly the oldest approximation of π.

### Numerals and zero

Numbers should be distinguished from numerals, the symbols used to represent numbers. The Egyptians invented the first ciphered numeral system, and the Greeks followed by mapping their counting numbers onto alphabets. In 300 BC, Archimedes first demonstrated the use of a positional numeral system to display extremely large numbers in The Sand Reckoner. Roman numerals remained dominant in Europe until the spread of the Hindu–Arabic numeral system around the late 14th century. The key to the effectiveness of this system was the symbol for zero, developed by ancient Indian mathematicians around 500 AD. The first known recorded use of zero as an integer dates to AD 628 in Brahmagupta's Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta, where he treated 0 as a number and discussed operations involving it, including division by zero. Earlier uses of zero were as a placeholder numeral in place-value systems, as done by the Babylonians. The late Olmec people of south-central Mexico began to use a placeholder symbol for zero by 38 BC, and the Maya developed zero as a cardinal number. By 130 AD, Ptolemy was using a symbol for 0 within a sexagesimal numeral system, the first documented use of a true zero in the Old World. The Ancient Greeks seemed unsure about the status of 0 as a number, asking "How can 'nothing' be something?"

### Negative and rational numbers

The abstract concept of negative numbers was recognized as early as 100–50 BC in China, where red rods denoted positive coefficients and black for negative. The first reference in a Western work was in the 3rd century AD in Greece, when Diophantus referred to an equation with a negative solution as giving an "absurd result." During the 600s, negative numbers were in use in India to represent debts, and Brahmagupta used them to produce the general form quadratic formula. European mathematicians resisted the concept of negative numbers until the 17th century, though Fibonacci allowed negative solutions in financial problems where they could be interpreted as debts. René Descartes called them "false roots," and Nicolas Chuquet referred to them as "absurd numbers." As recently as the 18th century, it was common practice to ignore negative results on the assumption that they were meaningless.

The concept of fractional numbers likely dates to prehistoric times. The Ancient Egyptians used their Egyptian fraction notation for rational numbers in texts such as the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus. Classical Greek and Indian mathematicians made studies of the theory of rational numbers, with Euclid's Elements (c. 300 BC) being particularly influential. The 16th century brought final European acceptance of negative integers and fractional numbers. The concept of decimal fractions is closely linked with decimal place-value notation; the two seem to have developed in tandem.

### Real, irrational, and transcendental numbers

The Babylonians, as early as 1800 BCE, demonstrated numerical approximations of irrational quantities such as √2 on clay tablets with accuracy analogous to six decimal places. The first existence proofs of irrational numbers are usually attributed to the Pythagorean Hippasus, who produced a proof of the irrationality of the square root of 2. According to legend, Pythagoras sentenced Hippasus to death by drowning to impede the spread of this unsettling news. The concept of real numbers was introduced in the 17th century by René Descartes. While studying compound interest, in 1683 Jacob Bernoulli found that as the compounding intervals grew ever shorter, the rate of exponential growth converged to a base of 2.71828...; this key mathematical constant would later be named Euler's number (e). Irrational numbers began to be studied systematically in the 18th century, with Leonhard Euler proving that Euler's number (e) is irrational, and Johann Lambert proving the irrationality of π in 1761. Real numbers were rigorously defined in the second half of the 19th century with the work of Cauchy, Méray, Weierstrass, Heine, Cantor, and Dedekind.

A transcendental number is a numerical value that is not the root of a polynomial with integer coefficients. The existence of transcendental numbers was first established by Liouville (1844, 1851). Hermite proved in 1873 that e is transcendental, and Lindemann proved in 1882 that π is transcendental. Cantor showed that the set of all real numbers is uncountably infinite but the set of all algebraic numbers is countably infinite, so there is an uncountably infinite number of transcendental numbers.

### Infinity and complex numbers

In mathematics, infinity is considered an abstract concept rather than a number; instead of being "greater than any number," infinite is the property of having no end. Aristotle distinguished between actual infinity and potential infinity, with the general consensus being that only the latter had true value. Georg Cantor introduced transfinite numbers and formulated the continuum hypothesis in 1895. In the 1960s, Abraham Robinson showed how infinitely large and infinitesimal numbers can be rigorously defined and used to develop the field of nonstandard analysis.

The earliest fleeting reference to square roots of negative numbers occurred in the work of Heron of Alexandria in the 1st century AD. They became more prominent in the 16th century when closed formulas for the roots of third and fourth degree polynomials were discovered by Italian mathematicians such as Tartaglia and Cardano. René Descartes coined the term "imaginary" for these quantities in 1637, intending it as derogatory. The 18th century saw the work of Abraham de Moivre and Leonhard Euler, with Euler's formula giving us e^(iθ) = cos θ + i sin θ, and Euler's identity e^(iπ) + 1 = 0 showing a profound connection between the most fundamental numbers in mathematics. The existence of complex numbers was not completely accepted until Caspar Wessel described the geometrical interpretation in 1799. In the same year, Gauss provided the first generally accepted proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra, showing that every polynomial over the complex numbers has a full set of solutions in that realm.

### Prime numbers and classification

Prime numbers are natural numbers that are not a product of two smaller natural numbers. The formal study of prime numbers is first documented by the ancient Greeks; Euclid proved the infinitude of the primes and the fundamental theorem of arithmetic. In 240 BC, Eratosthenes used the Sieve of Eratosthenes to quickly isolate prime numbers. In 1796, Adrien-Marie Legendre conjectured the prime number theorem, which was finally proved by Jacques Hadamard and Charles de la Vallée-Poussin in 1896. The Riemann hypothesis, formulated by Bernhard Riemann in 1859, and the Goldbach conjecture remain unproven and unrefuted.

Numbers can be classified into sets, called number systems, such as the natural numbers and the real numbers. Each of these number systems extends the preceding one: N ⊂ Z ⊂ Q ⊂ R ⊂ C. The natural numbers are the most familiar, with the mathematical symbol N. The integers, Z (from German Zahl 'number'), combine negative numbers with natural numbers. A rational number can be expressed as a fraction with an integer numerator and a positive integer denominator, with the symbol Q (for quotient). The real numbers, R, include all the measuring numbers, with every real number corresponding to a point on the number line. The complex numbers, C, consist of all values of the form a + bi where a and b are real numbers and i is the imaginary unit, the square root of −1.

## Terms

- **Numeral**: A symbol used to represent a number, such as "11" for eleven
- **Place value**: The value of a digit based on its position in a number; the rightmost digit has place value 1, each digit to the left has ten times the value
- **Rational number**: A number expressible as a fraction with an integer numerator and a positive integer denominator
- **Irrational number**: A real number that cannot be expressed as a fraction; its decimal representation has an infinite sequence of varying digits
- **Transcendental number**: A number that is not the root of a polynomial with integer coefficients
- **Imaginary unit (i)**: The square root of −1, a symbol assigned by Leonhard Euler
- **Prime number**: An integer greater than 1 that is not the product of two smaller positive integers
- **Fundamental theorem of arithmetic**: The proven claim that every integer greater than one is a product of primes in only one way, except for rearrangement
- **Hypercomplex numbers**: Higher dimensional number systems constructed from the real numbers, including quaternions, octonions, and sedenions
- **Transfinite numbers**: Generalizations of natural numbers for dealing with infinite sets, including ordinal numbers (ordering) and cardinal numbers (size)

## Debates and open questions

The interpretation of tally marks on bones as arithmetic artifacts is disputed. The status of zero as a number raised philosophical and religious arguments in Ancient Greece and the Medieval period, with the Greeks questioning whether even 1 was a number. The Goldbach conjecture (whether every even number is the sum of two primes) and the Riemann hypothesis remain unproven and unrefuted. Whether certain algebraic structures are "numbers" is more a matter of convention than mathematical distinction.
