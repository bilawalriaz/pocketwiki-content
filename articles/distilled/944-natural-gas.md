# Natural gas

## Overview

Natural gas is a fossil fuel composed primarily of methane (90–95%), formed over millions of years from the thermal decomposition of organic matter under high pressure and heat. It is the second-largest greenhouse gas contributor after carbon dioxide, with methane having 84 times the global warming potential of CO₂ over a 20-year period and 28 times over a 100-year period. While burning natural gas emits fewer toxic pollutants and less CO₂ than oil or coal, fugitive emissions and venting throughout the supply chain can negate these benefits. The industry has grown rapidly since the early 20th century, driven by pipeline development and hydraulic fracturing, but faces increasing scrutiny due to climate impacts.

## Body

### Formation and Composition

Natural gas forms when organic matter—mostly marine microorganisms—is buried under sediment and subjected to intense heat and pressure over millions of years. The resulting mixture contains mostly methane (95%), along with smaller amounts of ethane, propane, butanes, pentanes, and traces of CO₂, nitrogen, hydrogen sulfide, and helium. Because raw gas is odorless, an odorant like methanethiol is added commercially to detect leaks.

### Extraction and Sources

Most natural gas comes from underground geological formations and is often found alongside oil and coal. It can be “associated” (from oil fields) or “non-associated” (from dedicated gas reservoirs). Unconventional sources include shale gas, tight gas, coalbed methane, and sour gas. Extraction methods range from conventional drilling to hydraulic fracturing ("fracking"), which involves injecting water, sand, and chemicals at high pressure to release trapped gas.

### Processing and Byproducts

Before transport or use, raw natural gas must be processed to remove impurities such as water, CO₂, H₂S, and heavier hydrocarbons. Valuable byproducts include ethane, propane, butanes, pentanes, sulfur, and natural gas liquids (NGLs). Processing also yields condensate, which forms during pressure drops in non-associated gas fields.

### Transport and Storage

Natural gas is transported via pipelines where feasible, but long-distance or international transport requires liquefaction into LNG (liquefied natural gas) or compression into CNG (compressed natural gas). Underground storage in depleted reservoirs, salt domes, or aquifers helps balance supply and demand. FLNG (floating liquefied natural gas) enables offshore development without shore-based infrastructure.

### Uses

Natural gas serves multiple roles: residential heating and cooking, industrial processes, electricity generation, and as a chemical feedstock. It is critical in ammonia production via the Haber process for fertilizers. Additionally, it powers engines, fuels vehicles (CNG/LNG), and supports synthetic fuel production through gas-to-liquids (GTL) technologies like Fischer-Tropsch.

### Demand Trends

U.S. production peaked three times—1973, 2001, and 2019—with the third peak reaching 40.7 trillion cubic feet annually. The first peak reached 24.1 trillion cubic feet per year in 1973, followed by a decline, and the second peak reached 24.5 trillion cubic feet in 2001. After a brief drop, withdrawals increased nearly every year since 2006 (owing to the shale gas boom), with 2017 production at 33.4 trillion cubic feet and 2019 production at 40.7 trillion cubic feet. Post-2019 declines were linked to reduced demand during the COVID-19 pandemic. Globally, demand surged after 2020, contributing to the 2021 energy crisis.

### Environmental and Health Impacts

Burning natural gas releases about 40–45% less CO₂ than coal and 25–30% less than oil per unit of energy. However, methane leaks throughout the supply chain significantly increase its overall climate impact. Methane has a half-life of ~7 years but traps 84 times more heat than CO₂ over 20 years. Indoor air pollution from gas stoves contributes to asthma and other respiratory issues. Radionuclides like radon and polonium-210 are also released during extraction.

### Safety Concerns

Gas leaks pose explosion risks, especially indoors. Odorants help detect leaks, though masking effects can reduce effectiveness. Carbon monoxide poisoning remains a risk from poorly ventilated appliances. Hydraulic fracturing raises concerns about groundwater contamination, seismic activity, and chemical exposure.

## Terms

- **Associated gas**: Natural gas produced alongside crude oil.
- **Non-associated gas**: Gas extracted independently from oil.
- **Fugitive emissions**: Unintended releases of methane during extraction, processing, and transport.
- **Hydraulic fracturing (fracking)**: Technique using pressurized fluid to fracture rock and release gas.
- **LNG (Liquefied Natural Gas)**: Gas cooled to liquid state (-162°C) for easier transport.
- **CNG (Compressed Natural Gas)**: Gas stored under high pressure (>200 bar) for vehicle fuel.
- **NGL (Natural Gas Liquid)**: Heavier hydrocarbons separated from raw gas, including ethane, propane, and butane.
- **Sour gas**: Natural gas containing significant amounts of hydrogen sulfide.
- **Coalbed methane**: Methane adsorbed in coal seams.
- **Retrograde condensation**: Condensation of heavier hydrocarbons when reservoir pressure drops below dew point.

## Debates and Open Questions

- **Climate impact vs. transition role**: Is natural gas a “bridge fuel” or a lock-in to continued fossil fuel dependence? The IPCC warns new gas infrastructure risks becoming stranded assets unless paired with carbon capture and leak prevention.
- **Reserve estimates**: Disagreement persists over which country holds the largest proven reserves—Russia, Iran, or others—depending on methodology and data sources.
- **Naming controversy**: Advocates argue “fossil gas” better reflects climate risks than “natural gas,” citing studies showing improved public risk perception with alternative terms.
- **Fracking regulation**: Ongoing debates over environmental safety, water contamination, and seismic risks versus economic benefits of shale gas development.
- **Future demand**: Will global reliance on natural gas decline with renewable energy growth, or will emerging markets sustain demand?
