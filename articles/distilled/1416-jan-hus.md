# Jan Hus

## Overview
Jan Hus (c. 1369–1415) was a Czech theologian, philosopher, and rector of Charles University in Prague who became the primary inspiration for Hussitism, a major precursor to Protestantism. Influenced by John Wycliffe, Hus criticized clerical corruption, simony, and the sale of indulgences, arguing that the true Church comprised the predestined elect headed by Christ, not the papal hierarchy. Summoned to the Council of Constance (1414–1418) under a promise of safe conduct from King Sigismund, Hus was arrested, tried for heresy, and burned at the stake on 6 July 1415. His execution triggered the Hussite Wars (1420–1434), in which Bohemian forces defeated five papal crusades, eventually securing the Basel Compacts (1436), which granted limited religious autonomy. Hus also reformed Czech orthography, introducing diacritics (háček, acute accent) to represent phonemes with single symbols.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Hus was born in Husinec (southern Bohemia) around 1369–1372 to peasant parents; his surname derives from his birthplace and means "goose" in Czech. After early schooling at a monastery, he entered the University of Prague, earning a Bachelor of Arts in 1393 and a Master’s in 1396. The strongly anti-papal views of many professors there shaped his later theology. He supported himself as a choir boy and through other employment, gaining access to the university library.

### Academic Career and Wycliffite Influence
Hus began teaching at the university in 1398 and publicly defended Wycliffe’s propositions in 1399. Ordained in 1400, he became dean of the philosophy faculty (1401) and rector (1402). That year he was appointed preacher at Bethlehem Chapel, a venue for Czech-language sermons. Despite a 1403 ban on Wycliffe’s works, Hus translated the *Trialogus* into Czech and circulated it. Archbishop Zbyněk Zajíc initially tolerated Hus’s criticisms of clerical morals, even appointing him to preach at a synod, but Pope Innocent VII (1405) ordered action against Wycliffe’s doctrine of impanation in the Eucharist.

### The Western Schism and Kutná Hora Decree
The Western Schism (rival popes Gregory XII in Rome and Benedict XIII in Avignon) split Charles University in 1408. King Wenceslaus IV, seeking neutrality to protect his imperial ambitions, ordered the university to remain neutral. Only the Bohemian "nation" (one of four governing sections), led by Hus, complied; the German nations remained loyal to Gregory. In January 1409, Wenceslaus issued the Kutná Hora Decree, granting the Czech nation three votes and the German nation only one. German masters and over a thousand students departed, founding universities at Heidelberg and Leipzig and spreading accusations of Bohemian heresy.

### Conflict with Antipopes Alexander V and John XXIII
The Council of Pisa (1409) elected Alexander V, whom Hus, his followers, and Wenceslaus recognized. Under royal pressure, Archbishop Zajíc also transferred allegiance. Zajíc then accused Prague Wycliffites of "ecclesiastical disturbances." Alexander V’s bull of 20 December 1409 empowered the archbishop to suppress Wycliffism: books were burned, free preaching banned, and Hus excommunicated (1410). Alexander died in 1410; his successor, John XXIII, proclaimed a crusade against King Ladislaus of Naples (1411) and authorized indulgences to fund it. Hus condemned the traffic in indulgences, asserting forgiveness comes through true repentance, not money. Three laymen who called indulgences a fraud were beheaded, becoming the first Hussite martyrs.

### Exile, Appeal to Christ, and *De Ecclesia*
After Archbishop Zajíc’s death (1411), an interdict was placed on Prague. To spare the city, Hus withdrew to the countryside (1412), where he wrote Czech-language tracts for poorly educated priests. On 18 October 1412, he formally appealed to Jesus Christ as supreme judge, bypassing papal and conciliar authority—a step the source compares to Luther’s Ninety-five Theses. In 1413, at Kozí Hrádek, he completed *De Ecclesia*; its first ten chapters epitomize Wycliffe’s *De Ecclesia*, the remainder abstracts Wycliffe’s *De potentate papae*. Hus argued the Church is the body of the predestined, headed by Christ, not the pope, and that obedience to the pope is not necessary for salvation.

### Council of Constance: Imprisonment and Trial
Sigismund of Hungary, heir to the Bohemian crown, convened the Council of Constance (1414–1418) to end the schism and reform the Church. Hus attended under Sigismund’s safe conduct, arriving 3 November 1414. On 28 November, he was imprisoned on a rumor of flight. Sigismund protested but was told he could not be bound by promises to a heretic. After John XXIII fled, Hus was transferred to the bishop of Constance’s castle at Gottlieben, where he spent 73 days chained, poorly fed, and ill.

At trial (5–8 June 1415), extracts from *De Ecclesia* and treatises against Stanislav ze Znojma and Štěpán Páleč were read. Hus refused formulae of submission, offering to recant only if convinced from Scripture. He denied defending Wycliffe’s view of the Lord’s Supper or the forty-five articles, stating he had opposed only their summary condemnation. On 8 June, thirty-nine sentences were presented; Hus again declared willingness to submit if proven wrong, but refused to recant teachings he never held or to act against conscience.

### Condemnation and Execution
On 6 July 1415, in the cathedral, the Bishop of Lodi preached on eradicating heresy. An Italian prelate pronounced sentence. Hus knelt, prayed for his enemies, and was degraded: vestments removed, tonsure destroyed, a paper hat inscribed "Haeresiarcha" placed on his head. The council relinquished him to the secular arm. At the stake, he declined a final recantation, declaring readiness to die for the Gospel truth he had taught. Anecdotes record his "O Sancta Simplicitas!" when an old woman added brushwood, and a final cry, "Christ, son of the Living God, have mercy on us!" His ashes were cast into the Rhine.

### Aftermath: Bohemian Protest and Hussite Wars
On 2 September 1415, 100 Bohemian and Moravian nobles signed the "Bohemian Protest" condemning Hus’s execution. Pope Martin V authorized a crusade against Hussites (1 March 1420). King Wenceslaus IV died (1419); Sigismund failed to control Bohemia. Under Jan Žižka (d. 1424) and later Prokop the Great (d. 1434), Hussites defeated five crusades (1419–1434). The Basel Compacts (1436) granted the Catholic Church’s official recognition of Utraquist Hussitism in Bohemia. By the late 15th century, up to 90% of Czech Crown lands inhabitants followed Hussite teachings. The 1620 Battle of White Mountain ended this autonomy, imposing Habsburg rule and forced re-Catholicization for 300 years.

### Scholarship, Teachings, and Language Reform
Hus’s reform aimed at ethical abuses: simony, paid sacraments, and indulgences. He adopted Wycliffe’s view of the Lord’s Supper (consubstantiation), which the Taborites made central. His ecclesiology defined the Church as the predestined elect, not the clerical hierarchy. Hus also reformed Czech orthography: the *Orthographia Bohemica* (1406, attributed to him) introduced the háček (hook) for ⟨č⟩, ⟨ě⟩, ⟨š⟩, ⟨ř⟩, ⟨ž⟩; the acute accent for long vowels ⟨á⟩, ⟨é⟩, ⟨í⟩, ⟨ó⟩, ⟨ú⟩; and a dot for strong accent, assigning one symbol per phoneme.

### Legacy and Memory
Hus is a Czech national hero (voted greatest in a 2015 survey). The Unitas Fratrum (Moravian Church) claims direct descent. His influence on Luther is acknowledged; Luther adapted Hus’s "goose/swan" prophecy. The Catholic Church expressed regret in 1999 (Pope John Paul II). Some Eastern Orthodox jurisdictions venerate him as a martyr saint. Jan Hus Day (6 July) is a Czech public holiday. Memorials stand in Prague, New York, and elsewhere. His writings remain foundational to Czech literature and Protestant history.

## Terms
- **Hussitism**: Bohemian reform movement inspired by Hus; precursor to Protestantism; emphasized Scripture, communion in both kinds (utraquism), and a Church of the predestined elect.
- **Utraquism**: Moderate Hussite faction advocating communion *sub utraque specie* (bread and wine for laity); achieved legal recognition via Basel Compacts (1436).
- **Taborites**: Radical Hussite group centered at Tábor; adopted Wycliffe’s consubstantiation; communal, millenarian, militarily effective under Žižka.
- **Western Schism (1378–1417)**: Split in Catholic Church with rival popes in Rome and Avignon (later Pisa); context for Hus’s university politics and conciliar summons.
- **Antipope**: Claimant to papacy not canonically recognized; Alexander V (Pisa, 1409) and John XXIII (1410) were later declared antipopes by Council of Constance (1418).
- **Simony**: Buying or selling ecclesiastical offices or spiritual things; a primary target of Hus’s preaching.
- **Indulgence**: Remission of temporal punishment for sin, often sold for money; Hus condemned the 1411 crusade indulgence as corruption.
- **Safe conduct (salvus conductus)**: Legal guarantee of safe passage; Sigismund granted Hus one for Constance, but council prelates argued it did not bind them toward a heretic.
- **Basel Compacts (1436)**: Agreement between Utraquist Hussites and Council of Basel granting Bohemia the right to practice its own Christianity (communion in both kinds).
- **Háček (hook)**: Diacritic (ˇ) introduced by Hus’s orthographic reform to denote palatalized consonants (č, š, ž, ř, ě), replacing digraphs.

## Debates and open questions
- **Birth date**: Sources dispute c. 1369 vs. 1372–1375; recent scholarship favors c. 1372.
- **Authorship of *Orthographia Bohemica***: Attributed to Hus by František Palacký, but may have been compiled by another Charles University scholar.
- **Theological alignment**: While traditionally viewed as proto-Protestant, some Eastern Orthodox argue Hus’s theology (e.g., ecclesiology, liturgy) aligns closer to Orthodoxy; several Orthodox jurisdictions now venerate him as a saint.
- **Extent of Wycliffe dependence**: *De Ecclesia*’s first ten chapters are an epitome of Wycliffe; scholars debate how much Hus innovated versus transmitted.
- **Safe conduct violation**: Whether Sigismund bears moral/legal responsibility for Hus’s arrest despite the safe conduct remains a point of historical contention.
