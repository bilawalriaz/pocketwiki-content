# Louis Braille

## Overview
Louis Braille (1809–1852) was a French educator who invented braille, a tactile reading and writing system using six-dot cells that remains the global standard for visually impaired people. Blinded by a childhood accident, he developed his method as a student at the Royal Institute for Blind Youth in Paris, improving upon Charles Barbier’s twelve-dot "night writing" code to create a compact, efficient system readable with a single fingertip. Though resisted by sighted educators during his lifetime, braille was adopted by his institute in 1854 and spread internationally by the 1880s, revolutionizing literacy and communication for the blind.

## Body

### Early Life and Blindness
Louis Braille was born in Coupvray, near Paris, to Simon-René, a leatherer and harness maker, and Monique. At age three, he stabbed his eye with a stitching awl in his father’s workshop. The wound became severely infected; despite treatment by a Paris surgeon, the eye was lost, and sympathetic ophthalmia spread the blindness to the other eye. By age five he was totally blind. His parents, unusually for the era, raised him to navigate independently using canes his father made. Local teachers and priests recognized his intelligence, securing him higher education.

### Education at the Royal Institute
In February 1819, ten-year-old Braille entered the Royal Institute for Blind Youth, the world’s first school for blind children. Founded by philanthropist Valentin Haüy, the Institute taught reading via the **Haüy system**: heavy paper embossed with raised Latin letters. While groundbreaking in recognizing touch as a reading strategy, Haüy books were few (only three at opening), fragile, expensive, and slow to read. Crucially, students could not write them independently. Braille excelled academically, exhausted the curriculum, and became a teacher’s aide, reaching a full professorship by 1833. He also became an accomplished cellist and organist, serving at Paris churches including Saint-Nicolas-des-Champs (1834–1839) and Saint-Vincent-de-Paul.

### Invention of the Braille System
#### Origins and Design
In 1821, Charles Barbier, a former artillery officer, introduced his "night writing" to the Institute: a phonetic code of up to twelve dots in two columns, impressed on thick paper with a slate and stylus. Braille saw its potential but recognized its flaws—its phonetic basis ignored spelling, and its large cells required multiple finger touches. Determined to bridge the communication gap between sighted and blind, Braille reworked the system by 1824. He reduced the cell to six dots (two columns of three), assigned fixed patterns to letters (not sounds), and added punctuation and numbers. His first 1829 version used both dots and dashes; the 1837 revision discarded dashes as tactually difficult. The six-dot cell’s genius was that it fit under a single fingertip, enabling rapid reading.

#### Musical Notation and Publications
Braille extended his code to music, publishing the first braille book in 1829 (ironically printed via Haüy’s raised letters). He meticulously designed the musical notation to be flexible for any instrument. He also wrote *Little Synopsis of Arithmetic for Beginners* (1838) and *New Method for Representing by Dots...* (1839), the latter proposing **decapoint**: a dot-matrix system using a specialized grill and number table to form standard printed letters readable by sighted people. His friend Pierre Foucault then invented the **Raphigraphe**, a piston-driven machine to emboss decapoint rapidly, exhibited at the 1855 Paris World’s Fair.

### Professional Struggles and Later Life
Braille’s system was supported by Principal Alexandre-René Pignier but opposed by his successor, Pierre-Armand Dufau, who banned it in 1840. It was restored in 1844 by teacher Joseph Guadet. Braille suffered from a persistent respiratory illness, likely tuberculosis, for 16 years. By 40 he had to stop teaching; he died in the Institute infirmary on 6 January 1852, two days after his 43rd birthday.

### Legacy and Adoption
Blind pupils’ insistence forced the Institute to adopt braille in 1854. It spread through the French-speaking world, but international adoption was slower. Dr. Thomas Rhodes Armitage championed it at the 1873 European conference, accelerating global use. By 1882, Armitage noted braille was used in nearly every civilized institution except some in North America. US schools adopted it in 1916; a unified English code followed in 1932. Modern extensions include braille computer terminals, RoboBraille email, and **Nemeth Braille** for math and science. Braille’s childhood home is a museum; his remains were moved to the Panthéon in 1952 (hands left in Coupvray). He is honored on World Braille Day (4 January, since 2019), postage stamps, coins, asteroid 9969 Braille, and in the *Encyclopædia Britannica*’s "100 Most Influential Inventors."

## Terms
- ****Haüy system**** — The first embossed reading method for the blind, devised by Valentin Haüy, using raised impressions of standard Latin letters on heavy paper; readable by touch but too large for single-finger recognition and impossible for students to write themselves.
- ****Night writing**** — Charles Barbier’s twelve-dot, two-column tactile code, originally designed for silent military communication at night; phonetic rather than alphabetic, and too large for a single fingertip.
- ****Braille cell**** — The fundamental unit of the braille system: a rectangle of six dots arranged in two columns of three, allowing a letter or symbol to be recognized with one fingertip touch.
- ****Decapoint**** — Braille’s 1839 system combining dot-punching with a specialized grill and memorized number table to produce standard printed letters readable by sighted people.
- ****Raphigraphe**** — A machine invented by Pierre Foucault (c. 1840s) that used pistons to rapidly emboss decapoint letters; exhibited at the 1855 Paris World’s Fair.
- ****Sympathetic ophthalmia**** — A rare inflammatory condition where trauma to one eye triggers an autoimmune response that destroys the other eye; the likely cause of Braille’s total blindness.
- ****Nemeth Braille**** — A specialized braille code for mathematical and scientific notation, developed later (mid-20th century) as an extension of Braille’s original system.
- ****Slate and stylus**** — The basic writing tools for braille: a hinged metal slate with windows for the six-dot cell, and a pointed stylus to punch dots from the back of the paper.

## Debates and open questions
- **Priority of adoption**: The Institute banned braille under Dufau (1840–1844) despite pupil demand; the source notes this was driven by sighted educators’ preference for systems resembling print (Haüy) over tactile efficiency.
- **International resistance**: North American institutions resisted braille until 1916, favoring competing tactile codes (e.g., New York Point, American Modified Braille); the "War of the Dots" is implied but not detailed in the source.
- **Cause of death**: The source states a "persistent respiratory illness, long believed to be tuberculosis" but does not confirm a definitive diagnosis.
- **Decapoint vs. braille**: Braille invested significant effort in decapoint (writing for sighted readers), yet it was his six-dot braille that endured; the source does not analyze why decapoint failed to catch on.
