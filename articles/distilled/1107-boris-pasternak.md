# Boris Pasternak

## Overview
Boris Leonidovich Pasternak (1890–1960) was a Russian poet, novelist, and translator whose life encapsulates the tragedy of the creative intelligentsia under Soviet totalitarianism. Born into a prominent artistic family in Moscow, he gained early acclaim for revolutionary poetry collections like *My Sister, Life* (1922) but increasingly clashed with state demands for socialist realism. His masterpiece, *Doctor Zhivago* (1957), a sweeping novel prioritizing individual conscience over historical determinism, was banned in the USSR, smuggled to Italy for publication—partly via a CIA operation—and became a global sensation. Awarded the 1958 Nobel Prize in Literature, Pasternak was subjected to a vicious state smear campaign, expelled from the Writers' Union, and forced to decline the prize to avoid exile. He died of lung cancer in 1960; his funeral became a spontaneous act of dissent. Posthumously rehabilitated during *perestroika*, *Doctor Zhivago* entered the Russian school curriculum in 2003.

## Body

### Early Life and Formation
Pasternak was born into a wealthy, assimilated Jewish family in Moscow. His father, Leonid, was a post-Impressionist painter and professor; his mother, Rosa Kaufman, a concert pianist. The household was a cultural salon visited by Tolstoy, Rachmaninoff, Scriabin, and Rilke. Pasternak initially pursued music at the Moscow Conservatory, inspired by Scriabin, but abandoned it for philosophy, studying at the University of Marburg (1910–1912) under neo-Kantian philosophers Hermann Cohen, Nicolai Hartmann, and Paul Natorp. This philosophical training deeply informed his early poetry's structure. A formative romantic attachment to his cousin Olga Freidenberg (1910) evolved into a 40-year intellectual correspondence. A failed proposal to Ida Wissotzkaya (1912) and the family's Tolstoyan Christian leanings—despite Jewish heritage—shaped his spiritual distinctiveness, which he later called the root of his "way of seeing things."

### Poetry, Futurism, and the 1920s
Pasternak debuted with the Futurist group *Centrifuge*, publishing early poems in *Lirika*. His first major collection, *My Sister, Life* (composed 1917, published 1922), revolutionized Russian verse with its Kantian allusions, wild rhythms, and quotidian vocabulary, influencing Mandelstam and Tsvetaeva. Unlike many peers, Pasternak stayed in Russia after the 1917 Revolution, enduring Civil War privation (selling books for bread) and a publishing freeze. He married Evgeniya Lurye in 1922; their son Yevgeny was born in 1923. By the late 1920s, feeling his "colourful style" alienated a mass readership, he simplified his language in *The Second Birth* (1932), alienating émigré critics. He also turned to prose (*Safe Conduct*, *The Childhood of Luvers*) and translation to survive.

### The Stalin Era: Terror and Compromise
The 1930s brought mortal danger. In 1934, Osip Mandelstam recited his "Stalin Epigram" to Pasternak, who begged him to deny it. After Mandelstam's arrest, Pasternak appealed to Nikolai Bukharin and received a terrifying phone call from Stalin, who mocked his inability to "stick up for a comrade." During the Great Purge (1937), Pasternak refused to sign a Writers' Union petition demanding the death penalty for Marshal Tukhachevsky and General Yakir. He appealed directly to Stalin, citing Tolstoyan pacifism; Stalin reportedly crossed his name off an execution list, calling him a "cloud dweller" or "holy fool." Close friend Titsian Tabidze was executed; Tsvetaeva and Paolo Iashvili committed suicide. Pasternak survived but entered a "silent duel" with the regime. In 1932, he fell in love with Zinaida Neuhaus, divorcing Evgeniya and marrying Zinaida in 1934.

### War, Translation, and Olga Ivinskaya
During WWII, Pasternak served as a fire warden in Moscow and visited the front in 1943. He privately condemned Stalin as a "murderer" and read Orwell's *Animal Farm*, recognizing Napoleon as Khrushchev. In 1946, he met Olga Ivinskaya, a *Novy Mir* editor, beginning an affair that lasted until his death. She became the muse for Lara in *Doctor Zhivago*. In 1949, the KGB arrested Ivinskaya to pressure Pasternak; she was sentenced to 10 years in the Gulag, suffering a miscarriage. Pasternak credited her silence with saving his life. She was released in 1953 after Stalin's death. Throughout the 1940s–50s, Pasternak translated Goethe, Shakespeare, Calderón, and Petőfi to earn a living, developing a theory of translation as "insight of a higher kind" rather than literal philology. His *Faust* translation drew official criticism for retaining "irrational" supernatural elements.

### *Doctor Zhivago* and the Nobel Crisis
Completed in 1955, *Doctor Zhivago* was rejected by *Novy Mir* for rejecting socialist realism and criticizing Stalinism, collectivization, and the Gulag. Italian Communist journalist Sergio D'Angelo smuggled the manuscript to publisher Giangiacomo Feltrinelli in Milan. Despite Soviet demands to withdraw it, Feltrinelli published in November 1957. The CIA covertly purchased and distributed copies globally. The novel became a bestseller (26 weeks atop *The New York Times* list), though criticized in Israel for assimilationist themes. In October 1958, Pasternak won the Nobel Prize. The Soviet state unleashed a Stalinist hate campaign: *Pravda* denunciations, forced colleague condemnations (poet Boris Slutsky went insane), and a Komsomol rally where Vladimir Semichastny—scripted by Khrushchev—called Pasternak a "mangy sheep" and a pig. Threatened with permanent exile if he traveled to Stockholm, Pasternak renounced the prize on October 29. The Writers' Union expelled him, petitioning for citizenship revocation. Indian PM Nehru reportedly intervened with Khrushchev to prevent his exile.

### Final Years and Death
Pasternak spent his last years in Peredelkino, writing the poetry collection *When the Weather Clears* (1959) and beginning a trilogy, *The Blind Beauty*, cut short by terminal lung cancer. He maintained a strict writing schedule and continued translating Calderón with quiet Party support from Nikolai Liubimov. He died on 30 May 1960. His funeral, attended by thousands despite KGB surveillance, became a landmark dissent event: a young worker publicly swore to preserve Pasternak's works, and banned poems were recited over Party officials' objections. Bells of the local church tolled as the crowd chanted "Glory to Pasternak!"

### Posthumous Legacy
Ivinskaya was re-arrested in 1960 (released 1964); her memoirs published abroad in 1978. *Doctor Zhivago* circulated in *samizdat* until serialized in *Novy Mir* in 1988. In 1989, Pasternak's son Yevgeny accepted the Nobel Medal in Stockholm. Since 2003, the novel has been mandatory reading in Russian 11th grade. Monuments, museums, and commemorative stamps now honor him across the former USSR.

## Terms
- **Socialist Realism**: The official Soviet aesthetic doctrine (1932–1991) requiring art to depict reality in its "revolutionary development" to educate citizens in the spirit of socialism; *Doctor Zhivago* was rejected for violating this.
- **Samizdat**: (Self-publishing) The clandestine copying and circulation of banned literature in the Soviet Union; *Doctor Zhivago* spread this way before official publication.
- **Gulag**: The Soviet forced-labor camp system; Ivinskaya served a ten-year sentence there (1949–1954).
- **Neo-Kantianism**: A late 19th/early 20th-century revival of Immanuel Kant's philosophy emphasizing epistemology and ethics; Pasternak studied under its leading figures (Cohen, Hartmann, Natorp) at Marburg, shaping his early poetic theory.
- **Centrifuge (Tsentrifuga)**: A Russian Futurist literary group (c. 1914) where Pasternak debuted as a poet/pianist; published the journal *Lirika*.
- **Stalin Epigram**: Osip Mandelstam's 1934 satirical poem attacking Stalin ("the Kremlin mountaineer"), recited to Pasternak, leading to Mandelstam's arrest and Pasternak's confrontation with Stalin.
- **Cloud Dweller / Holy Fool (Yurodivy)**: Stalin's reported epithet for Pasternak during the 1937 Purge, implying a detached, sacred madness that placed him beyond ordinary judgment—and execution.
- **Khrushchev Thaw**: The period (mid-1950s to mid-1960s) of reduced repression under Nikita Khrushchev; Pasternak's Nobel ordeal occurred during its early, contradictory phase.
- **Peredelkino**: A writers' colony near Moscow where Pasternak lived his final years; site of his dacha, death, burial, and later house-museum.
- **CIA Zhivago Operation**: The U.S. Central Intelligence Agency's covert program (1957–58) to purchase, print, and distribute *Doctor Zhivago* globally to undermine Soviet cultural authority.

## Debates and open questions
- **Stalin's 1934 phone call**: The exact transcript and tone remain reconstructed from Ivinskaya's memoir and Pasternak's accounts; scholars debate whether Stalin's mockery ("you aren't able to stick up for a comrade") was a calculated warning or a capricious test.
- **Pasternak's 1937 survival**: Why Stalin spared him—personal whim, respect for his father's art, fear of international scandal, or the "holy fool" calculus—is unresolved.
- **CIA role in publication**: The source confirms CIA purchase/distribution of the novel; the extent to which this *caused* the Nobel Prize versus merely amplifying an existing literary consensus is debated.
- **Pasternak's agency in declining the Nobel**: Ivinskaya blamed herself for pressuring him; Solzhenitsyn condemned the capitulation. The source presents Pasternak's telegram as coerced by exile threats, but his letter to Khrushchev frames it as a voluntary act of loyalty to Russia.
- **Jewish identity and assimilation**: The novel's Israeli critics attacked its "assimilationist" Christianity; Pasternak defended it as written pre-1948 and reflecting his Orthodox practice. The tension between his Jewish lineage, Christian spirituality, and Soviet identity remains a critical flashpoint.
- **Ivinskaya as Lara**: The source identifies her as the model, but the degree to which Lara is a composite (like Strelnikov/Minchakievich) versus a portrait is a literary question.
