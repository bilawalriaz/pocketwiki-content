# Amur

## Overview
The Amur River (Heilong River) is a major perennial river in Northeast Asia, forming the natural border between the Russian Far East and Northeast China. It is the world's tenth longest river when including its main tributary, the Argun, and is a critical geopolitical and ecological feature of the region.

## Timeline
- **13th–14th c.** — Mongol Yuan dynasty establishes a tenuous military presence on the lower Amur.
- **1411–early 1430s** — Ming dynasty expeditions reach the lower Amur, rebuilding the Yongning Temple and securing nominal tribal allegiance.
- **1643–1651** — Russian Cossack expeditions under Poyarkov and Khabarov explore the Amur and its tributaries.
- **c. 1683–1684** — Qing dynasty re-establishes the town of Aigun and campaigns against Russian settlers at Albazin.
- **1689** — Treaty of Nerchinsk ends hostilities, leaving the entire Amur valley in Chinese hands.
- **1858** — Treaty of Aigun forces the Qing to cede all lands north of the Amur to the Russian Empire.
- **1860** — Convention of Peking grants Russia lands east of the Ussuri and the lower Amur.
- **1916** — The first permanent bridge across the Amur is completed at Khabarovsk.
- **2021** — The Tongjiang-Nizhneleninskoye railway bridge is completed.
- **2022** — The Blagoveshchensk–Heihe Bridge opens for freight traffic.

## Body

### Name and Geography
The etymology of "Amur" is unknown, with theories linking it to Tungusic or Mongolic words for "river" or "big river." Its ancient Chinese name, Heishui ("black water"), is the basis for the modern Chinese name Heilongjiang ("Black Dragon River"). The river rises at the confluence of the Shilka and Argun rivers in Northeast China, flows east and southeast forming the China-Russia border, then arcs northeast past Khabarovsk before turning east to its estuary on the Sea of Okhotsk. Its drainage basin covers 1,855,000 km².

### History and Geopolitics
The Amur region was historically inhabited by Tungusic, Mongol, and other indigenous groups, for whom fishing was a primary livelihood. The river became a focal point of Chinese-Russian relations. After a period of Chinese control formalized by the 1689 Treaty of Nerchinsk, Russian expansion in the 19th century led to the Qing dynasty ceding the northern bank (1858) and later the eastern bank (1860) to Russia. The river was a prominent symbol during the Sino-Soviet split of 1956–1966.

### Infrastructure and Modern Crossings
For centuries, crossing the Amur was difficult. The first permanent bridge, the Khabarovsk Bridge, was completed in 1916 for the Trans-Siberian Railway. Later major crossings include the Komsomolsk-on-Amur bridge (1975) and the modern Blagoveshchensk–Heihe Bridge (2022), which facilitates freight and passenger traffic between Russia and China.

### Wildlife and Ecology
The Amur basin is ecologically significant, hosting at least 123 fish species. It is home to large predatory fish like the Amur pike and taimen, and the kaluga, a sturgeon reaching up to 5.6 m in length. The river also supports the northernmost populations of the Amur softshell turtle and Indian lotus. Notable terrestrial wildlife in the region includes the Amur leopard and Amur tiger.

## Terms
- **Perennial river**: A river that flows continuously throughout the year.
- **Drainage basin**: The area of land where all water drains into a common outlet, such as a river.
- **Tributary**: A river or stream that flows into a larger river.
- **Anadromous**: Fish that migrate from saltwater to freshwater to spawn (e.g., salmon).
- **Endemic**: A species native and restricted to a certain place.
- **Estuary**: The tidal mouth of a large river, where the tide meets the stream.
- **Braided character**: A river pattern with multiple, interweaving channels separated by small islands.
- **Endorheic**: A closed drainage basin that retains water and allows no outflow to other external bodies of water.

## Debates and Open Questions
The etymology of the river's name "Amur" remains unknown, with competing theories from Tungusic and Mongolic languages. The exact nature and permanence of early Chinese (Ming dynasty) presence on the middle Amur are described as "short-lived" and "tenuous" in historical sources.
