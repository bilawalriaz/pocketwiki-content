# Islamic art

## Overview
Islamic art encompasses the visual arts produced since the 7th century CE by people in territories inhabited or ruled by Muslim populations. It is a concept first used by Western art historians in the late 19th century to describe characteristic traditions across a vast range of lands, periods, and genres. While public religious art is often non-representational, emphasizing calligraphy, geometric patterns, and abstract floral forms, figurative art has a rich history in secular and courtly contexts. The art reflects a synthesis of diverse cultural influences and is deeply rooted in the written word of the Quran.

## Timeline
- **7th century CE** — Islamic art begins with the rise of Islam.
- **c. 660–750** — Umayyad Desert Castles feature early figurative art.
- **8th century** — First Islamic opaque glazes appear in Basra; lustre painting develops in Egypt.
- **9th century** — Stonepaste ceramics originate in Iraq; distinctive glazed tiling tradition develops.
- **9th–11th centuries** — East Persian "epigraphic ware" pottery flourishes.
- **13th century** — Persian miniature tradition becomes dominant.
- **16th–early 17th centuries** — Peak of Persian carpet production and Ottoman İznik pottery.
- **16th–17th centuries** — Persian miniature tradition reaches a climax.
- **2001** — Taliban destroys figurative depictions in Afghanistan.

## Body

### Terminology and Scope
The term "Islamic art" is a modern construct, but it remains useful due to shared artistic traits across the Muslim world, especially during the Islamic Golden Age. It broadly includes all arts produced by Muslim peoples, whether religious or secular, covering over 40 nations. The field encompasses visual arts, literature, performing arts, and music.

### Calligraphy and Painting
Calligraphy is omnipresent in Islamic art, reflecting the primacy of the Quran. Scripts like kufic and naskh adorn buildings, objects, and manuscripts. While religious art often avoids figures, a continuous tradition of figurative painting exists, from Umayyad castles to Persian, Ottoman, and Mughal miniatures. These miniatures, often illustrating historical or poetic stories, developed a distinctive style with bird's-eye views and bright colors, influenced by Chinese art. Aniconism (avoidance of images of living beings) stems from prohibitions on idolatry but was not universally applied.

### Ceramics, Glass, and Metalwork
Islamic potters innovated with tin-opacified glazes, stonepaste, and lustreware, often emulating Chinese ceramics. Centers like Raqqa, Damascus, and later İznik produced notable wares. Tiling became a major architectural feature, using geometric patterns and sometimes figurative elements. Islamic glass, especially from Egypt and Syria, was highly sophisticated, using techniques like lustre painting and enameling. Metalwork, primarily in brass and bronze, featured dense decoration with inlays of precious metals, avoiding figural representation on vessels due to religious prohibitions.

### Rugs, Textiles, and Other Arts
The pile carpet (Oriental rug) is the most widely recognized Islamic art form, used for floors, walls, and religious purposes. Designs evolved from geometric to complex arabesques, with major centers in Persia, Egypt, and Turkey. Textiles like silk, despite hadith prohibitions, continued Byzantine and Sassanian traditions with figural designs. Other achievements include ivory carving (especially Spanish caskets), wood carving (including muqarnas vaulting), and Indonesian batik, whose abstract designs were refined under Islamic influence.

### Architecture
Islamic architecture is consistently prominent, drawing on late antique traditions and developing regional styles. It heavily features applied decoration—arabesques, calligraphy, and geometric patterns—in materials like tile, stone, and wood. Innovations spread widely, and buildings like mosques and palaces are key repositories of Islamic artistic traditions.

## Terms
- **Arabesque**: A form of artistic decoration consisting of rhythmic, spiralling linear patterns of interlacing foliage or geometric motifs.
- **Aniconism**: The practice of avoiding the representation of human or animal figures, often rooted in religious prohibitions against idolatry.
- **Calligraphy**: The art of beautiful writing, central to Islamic art as it represents the word of God in the Quran.
- **Muqarnas**: A form of ornamented vaulting in Islamic architecture, resembling honeycombs or stalactites.
- **Lustreware**: Pottery or glass with a metallic, iridescent glaze, developed in the Islamic world from the 8th century.
- **Stonepaste**: A ceramic material developed in the Islamic world, made from ground quartz, allowing for finer decoration.
- **Epigraphic ware**: Pottery decorated primarily with stylized inscriptions, prominent in early Islamic Persia.
- **Muraqqa**: A bound album containing miniatures and calligraphy sheets, a common format for preserving art.
- **Pietra dura (Parchin kari)**: A technique of inlaying semi-precious stones into marble or other stone, used notably by the Mughals.

## Debates and Open Questions
The very concept of "Islamic art" is debated by some scholars as a Western construct that may oversimplify the diversity of artistic production across Muslim societies. The extent and nature of aniconism are also debated; while some interpretations strictly ban depictions of living beings, historical evidence shows figurative art flourished in many periods and contexts, including under rulers who were religious zealots. The origins and precise dating of many carpet designs remain unclear, as traditional labels often derive from design rather than documented provenance.
