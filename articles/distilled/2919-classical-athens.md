# Classical Athens

## Overview
Athens was a prominent city-state (polis) in ancient Greece, located in the Attica peninsula. It is most famous for establishing the world's first known democracy in 508 BC and for its cultural and political achievements during the Classical period, which are widely considered a foundation of Western civilization.

## Timeline
- **c. 508 BC** — Cleisthenes establishes Athenian democracy.
- **490 BC** — Athenians defeat the Persians at the Battle of Marathon.
- **480 BC** — Persians sack Athens; Greeks later win the naval Battle of Salamis.
- **470s–440s BC** — Athens leads the Delian League, reaching its peak under Pericles.
- **431–404 BC** — Peloponnesian War ends in Athens' defeat by Sparta.
- **403 BC** — Democracy is restored after the rule of the Thirty Tyrants.
- **378 BC** — Athens establishes the Second Athenian League.
- **338 BC** — Philip II of Macedon defeats Athens and Thebes at Chaeronea.
- **322 BC** — Macedonian general Antipater dissolves the Athenian government.

## Body

### Archaic Period and the Rise of Democracy
Early Athens was a fortified Mycenaean center. By the 8th century BC, it was a prominent polis. The 7th and 6th centuries BC saw the creation of legal codes by Draco and Solon, and the rule of the tyrant Peisistratus. After the overthrow of Peisistratus's son Hippias by Sparta, the statesman Cleisthenes used popular support to gain power and enact democratic reforms. These reforms replaced the four traditional tribes with ten new ones based on geography, which formed the basis for the new governing council (Boule) and the popular assembly (Ecclesia).

### Classical Period: Hegemony and War
Athens' power grew after victories in the Persian Wars (490 and 480–479 BC). It then led the Delian League, an alliance that became an Athenian-dominated empire. Under the statesman Pericles, Athens entered a "Golden Age," using league funds to build monuments like the Parthenon. This hegemony led to the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC) against Sparta and its allies. Athens was defeated, its democracy briefly overthrown, and its empire dissolved. It later formed the Second Athenian League but was ultimately subjugated by Philip II of Macedon in 338 BC.

### The City of Athens
Athens was located in Attica, about 30 stadia from the sea. The walled city was about 1.5 km in diameter, with the Acropolis at its center. It was connected by the Long Walls to its port city, Piraeus. The city was rebuilt and adorned with public buildings after its destruction by the Persians in 480 BC. Key areas included the Acropolis, crowned by temples like the Parthenon; the Agora (marketplace); and various districts and hills like the Pnyx, where assemblies were held. The city's population may have reached 120,000–180,000 at its height.

### Legacy and Notable Figures
The period from the Persian Wars to the Macedonian conquest was the zenith of Athenian culture. It was home to foundational figures in Western thought and arts, including the philosophers Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle; the historians Herodotus and Thucydides; and the dramatists Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides. Pericles was the leading statesman of its golden age. Athens is remembered as "the school of Hellas" for its enduring intellectual and artistic legacy.

## Terms
- **Polis**: A city-state, the fundamental political unit of ancient Greece.
- **Democracy**: A system of government by the whole population, typically through elected representatives. Athenian democracy was direct, with citizens voting on laws.
- **Boule**: The council of 500 that governed Athens on a day-to-day basis, with members selected by lot.
- **Ecclesia**: The assembly of all full citizens, which served as the legislature and supreme court.
- **Delian League**: An alliance of Greek city-states led by Athens, which evolved into an Athenian empire.
- **Hegemony**: Leadership or dominance, especially by one state over others.
- **Acropolis**: The fortified upper city of Athens, home to its most important temples.
- **Agora**: The central public space and marketplace of an ancient Greek city.
- **Strategos**: A general; one of the few offices in Athens filled by election rather than lot.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that Athenian democracy's creation by Cleisthenes may have been motivated by his personal ambition to gain power, suggesting its origins could be "tainted." The population of classical Athens is uncertain, with estimates ranging from 120,000 to 180,000 inhabitants.
