# History of Oceania

## Overview
The history of Oceania spans the settlement of its islands by humans over tens of thousands of years, the era of European exploration and colonization, and the modern age of independence and geopolitical conflict. It matters as a story of remarkable human migration and adaptation, followed by profound disruption from colonial powers and global wars, shaping the diverse nations and cultures of the Pacific today.

## Timeline
- **c. 50,000–30,000 BCE** — First human settlement of Australia, New Guinea, and nearby islands.
- **c. 3000–1000 BCE** — Austronesian expansion from Taiwan through Island Southeast Asia into Melanesia and Polynesia.
- **c. 1500 BCE** — Earliest archaeological traces of civilization in Micronesia (Saipan).
- **c. 1000 BCE** — Lapita culture reaches Samoa and Tonga, gateway to Polynesia.
- **1519–1522** — Magellan-Elcano expedition achieves first circumnavigation, crossing the Pacific.
- **1642** — Abel Tasman becomes first European to sight Tasmania and New Zealand.
- **1768–1779** — Captain James Cook's three voyages map New Zealand, Australia's east coast, and Hawaii.
- **1788** — British colonization of Australia begins with the First Fleet.
- **1840** — Treaty of Waitangi initiates British colonization of New Zealand.
- **1899** — Tripartite Convention partitions Samoa between the US and Germany.
- **1941** — Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor brings the US into World War II.
- **1962** — Samoa becomes the first independent Pacific nation.

## Body

### Prehistory and Settlement
Human arrival in Oceania occurred in distinct waves. The first settlers reached Australia and Near Oceania (including New Guinea) between 50,000 and 30,000 years ago. The peopling of Remote Oceania (Polynesia, Micronesia) began much later, with Austronesian-speaking peoples expanding from Taiwan through Island Southeast Asia and Melanesia between about 3000 and 1000 BCE. The Lapita culture, identified by its pottery, spread rapidly eastward, reaching Fiji, Tonga, and Samoa by around 1000 BCE. From this "Polynesian Triangle" gateway, settlers eventually reached Hawaii, Easter Island, and New Zealand. The origins of Polynesians are debated, with genetic studies showing strong links to East Asia (particularly Taiwan) and evidence of admixture with Melanesian populations.

### European Exploration and Colonization
European contact began in the 16th century with Spanish and Portuguese navigators. The Dutch later charted much of Australia's coast. British explorer James Cook's three voyages (1768–1779) were pivotal, mapping New Zealand and Australia's east coast and claiming them for Britain. Colonization followed: Britain established colonies in Australia (1788), New Zealand (1840), and Fiji (1872). France colonized Tahiti (1880) and New Caledonia (1853). The United States expanded into the Pacific, annexing Hawaii (1898) and, after the Spanish-American War, gaining control of Guam and the Philippines. Germany also established colonies in New Guinea and Samoa. By the early 20th century, most of Oceania was under European or American control.

### The World Wars and Nuclear Testing
World War I saw Allied forces, primarily Australian and New Zealand, seize German colonies in the Pacific. World War II brought massive conflict, with Japan attacking Pearl Harbor (1941) and invading many islands. Major campaigns included Guadalcanal in the Solomon Islands and the Kokoda Track in New Guinea, where Japanese expansion was halted. After the war, the region became a site for Cold War nuclear testing. The United States conducted 67 tests in the Marshall Islands (1946–1958), including the first hydrogen bomb. The UK tested in Australia, and France tested at Moruroa Atoll until 1996, causing significant environmental and health impacts and sparking regional protests.

### Decolonization and Modern Conflicts
From the 1960s onward, many Oceanian nations gained independence, starting with Samoa (1962). Political instability has marked some nations, notably Fiji, which experienced military coups in 1987, 2000, and 2006, rooted in ethnic tensions. The Bougainville Civil War (1988–c. 2005) in Papua New Guinea, fought over the Panguna mine and secession, resulted in an estimated 15,000–20,000 deaths. Regional cooperation developed through the Pacific Islands Forum (founded 1971). In the 21st century, issues include asylum-seeker policies (Australia's "Pacific Solution") and ongoing autonomy debates, such as in French Polynesia.

## Terms
*   **Austronesian peoples:** A large group of peoples speaking related languages who originated in Taiwan and spread across the Pacific and Indian Oceans.
*   **Lapita culture:** An ancient archaeological culture identified by distinctive pottery, considered ancestral to modern Polynesians.
*   **Melanesia:** A subregion of Oceania extending from New Guinea eastward to Fiji, characterized by great linguistic and genetic diversity.
*   **Polynesia:** A subregion of Oceania including Hawaii, New Zealand, Easter Island, and Samoa, settled by Austronesian voyagers.
*   **Terra Australis:** A hypothetical "great southern land" sought by European explorers, disproven by Cook's voyages.
*   **Manila galleons:** Spanish trading ships that sailed annually across the Pacific between Mexico and the Philippines from 1565 to 1815.
*   **Trust Territory of the Pacific Islands:** A United Nations trust territory administered by the US after WWII, covering most of Micronesia.
*   **Pacific Islands Forum:** An intergovernmental organization of Pacific Island nations, founded in 1971.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Polynesian Origins:** The exact path and timing of Polynesian settlement are debated, with models like the "Express Train" (rapid migration from Taiwan) and "Slow Boat" (longer admixture in Melanesia) competing.
*   **Easter Island Settlement Date:** Estimates for the first human settlement vary widely, from as early as 300–400 CE to as late as 1200 CE, based on different archaeological and genetic evidence.
*   **Captain Cook's Death:** Scholarly debate continues over whether Hawaiians initially perceived Cook as the god Lono, a theory challenged by some historians.
*   **Colonial Impact:** The long-term social, economic, and cultural consequences of colonialism and the legacy of nuclear testing remain critical areas of study and political discourse.
