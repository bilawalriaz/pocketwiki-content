# Boyar

## Overview
A boyar (or bolyar) was the highest rank of feudal nobility in many Eastern European states from the 10th to the 17th centuries, second only to the ruling prince or tsar. Comparable to Western dukes, their power evolved from military service and land ownership to being gradually absorbed into centralized state bureaucracies, particularly in Russia.

## Timeline
- **681–1018** — The title *boila* is used for high aristocrats in the First Bulgarian Empire.
- **9th–13th c.** — Boyars in Kievan Rus' wield power through military service and advise princes via the Boyars' Duma.
- **13th c.** — The Mongol invasion disrupts Kievan Rus'; western boyars are later incorporated into Lithuanian/Polish nobility.
- **14th–15th c.** — Boyars in Moscow retain influence, but it begins to wane under Ivan III's centralizing policies.
- **1547** — Ivan IV (the Terrible) becomes tsar; his reign sees the crushing of boyar opposition via the *oprichnina* terror.
- **1565** — Ivan IV divides Muscovy, intensifying purges against boyars.
- **Late 16th c.** — After Ivan IV, the Time of Troubles begins; boyar factions fight for control.
- **17th c.** — Under the Romanovs, boyars are merged into a state bureaucracy, losing independent power.
- **1697** — Peter the Great's westernizing reforms, including replacing the boyar council with a senate, end boyar political dominance.

## Body

### Etymology and Early Use
The term has multiple proposed origins, including Old Turkic *bai* ("noble") + *är* ("man"), or proto-Slavic *boj* ("battle"). The oldest Slavic form, *bolyarin*, appears in 10th-century Bulgaria, where it succeeded the earlier Bulgar title *boila*. The title was hereditary and divided into "great" and "minor" ranks.

### Boyars in Serbia
In medieval Serbia, boyars were equivalent to barons, meaning "free warriors." They owned land but were obligated to fight for the king. The term became archaic after Ottoman rule.

### Boyars in Rus' and Russia
In Kievan Rus', boyars were a powerful military and landowning class who advised princes. After the Mongol invasion, their power shifted toward state service. In the Tsardom of Russia, their influence peaked in the 14th–15th centuries but was systematically reduced by Ivan III and especially Ivan IV, who used terror and land reforms to centralize power. By the 17th century, they were absorbed into the bureaucracy. Peter the Great's 18th-century reforms, which created a senate and promoted Westernization, delivered the final blow to their political status.

### Boyars in Moldavia and Wallachia (Romania)
In the Carpathian regions, the boyar class emerged from local community leaders (*cneaz*, *jude*) whose judicial and administrative powers became hereditary. A boyar was defined by land ownership, possession of serfs, and a military or administrative function (*dregătorie*). They were organized into three states with distinct rights, such as the right to wear a beard. The prince, often a boyar himself, could appoint functions but could not arbitrarily seize hereditary land. During the Phanariot period, functions could be sold, and the prince became an Ottoman-appointed official.

## Terms
- **Boila/Bolyar**: The hereditary title for the highest feudal nobility in Eastern Europe, especially Bulgaria and Russia.
- **Boyars' Duma**: The council in Kievan Rus' and later Russia that advised the grand prince and served as a major legislative body.
- **Oprichnina**: The policy of territorial division and terror purges instituted by Ivan IV to crush boyar opposition and centralize power.
- **Dregătorie/Boierie**: The military or administrative functions that, combined with land and serfs, defined the boyar condition in Romania.
- **Mazil**: In the Romanian hierarchy, a landowner with serfs but no official function, still considered of noble origin.
- **Phanariot**: A period of Greek administration under Ottoman suzerainty in Wallachia and Moldavia, where princely appointments were often sold.
- **Szlachta**: The Polish nobility; after 1430, Galician boyars were equated with this class.

## Debates and Open Questions
The exact etymology of "boyar" is debated, with theories linking it to Turkic, Slavic, or Romanian roots. The origin of the Romanian nobility is also contested, with one theory suggesting a significant Cuman origin for the medieval nobility.
