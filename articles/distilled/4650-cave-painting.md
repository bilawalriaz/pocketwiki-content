# Cave painting

## Overview
Cave paintings are a form of prehistoric parietal art found on cave walls and ceilings, primarily dating to the Paleolithic era. They are crucial for understanding the development of human abstract thought, creativity, and spirituality. Recent discoveries have pushed back their origins, suggesting some may have been created by Neanderthals and Denisovans, not just *Homo sapiens*.

## Timeline
- **c. 73,000 years ago** — Oldest known rock painting (a hand-drawn line on a rock fragment) created in South Africa.
- **c. 67,800 years ago** — Oldest known cave painting (a hand stencil) created on Muna Island, Indonesia.
- **c. 64,000 years ago** — Non-figurative symbols in Spanish caves (Maltravieso, Ardales, La Pasiega) made by Neanderthals.
- **c. 51,200 years ago** — Oldest known figurative painting (a pig-hunting scene) created in Sulawesi, Indonesia.
- **c. 40,000 years ago** — Earliest known European figurative paintings created at the Cave of El Castillo, Spain.
- **c. 35,000–30,000 years ago** — Two major periods of painting activity at Chauvet Cave, France.
- **c. 17,000 years ago** — Magdalenian-style paintings created at Lascaux, France, and Altamira, Spain.
- **c. 10,000–5,500 years ago** — Rock art of the Iberian Mediterranean Basin created, featuring less naturalistic, grouped figures.

## Body

### Dating and Discovery
The age of cave paintings was initially contentious due to contamination risks with radiocarbon dating. Modern techniques now date pigment, torch marks, or carbonate deposits directly. The oldest confirmed cave painting is a hand stencil from Muna Island, Indonesia, dated to at least 67,800 years old. The oldest figurative painting is a hunting scene from Sulawesi, Indonesia, dated to at least 51,200 years old. In Europe, the Cave of El Castillo contains art dated to at least 40,000 years ago. The famous Chauvet Cave in France was painted in two phases, around 35,000 and 30,000 years ago. Some paintings, like those in Arnhem Land, Australia, may depict extinct megafauna, but their age is inferred from the animals' estimated extinction dates.

### Subjects, Themes, and Techniques
Common subjects are large wild animals (bison, horses, deer), hand stencils, and abstract patterns. Human figures are rare and schematic. Artists used techniques including finger tracing, engravings, and pigments like ochre and charcoal. Hand stencils, made by blowing pigment around a hand placed on the wall, are found globally. Some hands show missing fingers, with various explanations proposed. A 2012 study suggests artists depicted animal gaits with great accuracy, implying close observation for survival.

### Theories and Interpretations
Early 20th-century scholars interpreted paintings as "hunting magic" to ensure prey abundance. A later theory by David Lewis-Williams proposes they were shamanic visions painted in trance states. R. Dale Guthrie suggests many artists were adolescent males, while Dean Snow's analysis of handprints indicates a significant proportion were female. A 2022 study proposes that lines and dots on paintings may represent a proto-writing system tracking animal mating cycles via a lunar calendar.

### Regional Distribution
**Europe:** Famous sites include Chauvet (France), Lascaux (France), and Altamira (Spain). Art evolved from naturalistic animals to less detailed, grouped figures in the later Iberian Mediterranean Basin style.
**Asia:** Indonesia's Sulawesi and Borneo contain the world's oldest figurative art. Sites in Mongolia, India, and Thailand also feature Paleolithic paintings.
**Africa:** The oldest known rock painting (73,000 years old) is from Blombos Cave, South Africa. Holocene art includes the 5,000-year-old Laas Geel paintings in Somaliland and San rock art in South Africa.
**Australia:** Significant ochre paintings exist in Kimberley and Kakadu. The oldest reliably dated is a 17,300-year-old kangaroo.
**Americas:** Prehistoric art includes the 7,500-year-old Great Murals of Baja California and the 10,000-year-old hunting scenes at Cueva de las Manos, Argentina.

## Terms
- **Parietal art:** Art made on the walls, ceilings, or floors of caves and rock shelters.
- **Petroglyphs:** Images created by removing part of a rock surface by incising, picking, or carving.
- **Figurative art:** Art that represents real-world objects, people, or animals, as opposed to abstract shapes.
- **Hand stencil:** A negative image of a hand made by blowing or spattering pigment around a hand placed on a surface.
- **Upper Paleolithic:** The later part of the Old Stone Age, marked by advanced tool-making and the emergence of art, roughly 50,000 to 10,000 years ago.
- **Magdalenian:** A late Upper Paleolithic culture in Europe known for its sophisticated cave art and bone/ivory tools.
- **Ochre:** An earth pigment containing iron oxide, used in prehistoric paints.
- **Neolithic:** The later Stone Age period, characterized by the development of agriculture and settled communities.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Authorship:** While most art is attributed to *Homo sapiens*, some non-figurative art in Spain predates modern humans in Europe, strongly suggesting Neanderthal creation.
- **Purpose:** Theories range from practical hunting magic and shamanic rituals to educational tools or early symbolic communication systems.
- **Chronology and Style:** The relationship between the age of paintings and their stylistic complexity is debated, as some "advanced" paintings appear older than cruder ones, possibly due to repeated modifications over millennia.
- **Dating Challenges:** The age of some art, like Australian megafauna depictions, remains uncertain as it relies on estimates of species extinction rather than direct dating.
