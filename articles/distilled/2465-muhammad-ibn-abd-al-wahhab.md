# Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab

## Overview
Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab (1703–1792) was an 18th-century Arab scholar and reformer from Najd, Arabia, who founded the Wahhabi movement. He advocated a return to what he saw as the pure, foundational principles of Islam, emphasizing strict monotheism (tawhid) and rejecting practices he considered idolatrous innovations. His alliance with the political leader Muhammad bin Saud provided the military power to establish the first Saudi state, creating a dynastic and religious partnership that continues to shape Saudi Arabia.

## Timeline
- **1703** — Born in 'Uyayna, Najd, into a family of Hanbali jurists.
- **c. 1723** — Leaves 'Uyayna; performs pilgrimage to Mecca and studies in Medina.
- **1720s-1730s** — Travels to Basra, Baghdad, and other cities, studying under various scholars.
- **1740s** — Returns to Najd; begins preaching reform and writing *Kitab al-Tawhid*.
- **1744** — Expelled from 'Uyayna; forms a pact with Muhammad bin Saud in Diriyah.
- **1746-1773** — War with Riyadh and other towns; expansion of the Emirate of Diriyah.
- **1773** — Capture of Riyadh; Ibn Abd al-Wahhab retires from public life.
- **1792** — Dies in Diriyah; buried in an unmarked grave.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born into a family of Hanbali jurists, Ibn Abd al-Wahhab's early education was in standard Hanbali jurisprudence. His travels for study, particularly to Medina, were decisive. There, he was influenced by the scholar Muhammad Hayat al-Sindi, who encouraged him to reject the blind following of legal schools (*taqlid*) and to engage in independent scriptural research (*ijtihad*). Al-Sindi also instilled in him a strong opposition to the veneration of saints' tombs and graves, which he viewed as heretical innovation (*bid'ah*) or idolatry (*shirk*).

### Preaching and the Pact with Ibn Saud
After returning to Najd, Ibn Abd al-Wahhab began preaching a revivalist message based on the core doctrine of *tawhid* (the absolute oneness of God). He condemned widespread folk practices, such as seeking intercession from saints and venerating sacred trees, as forms of polytheism. His activism initially gained him the support of the ruler of 'Uyayna, but he was expelled after his actions, like leveling a revered tomb, provoked a powerful regional chief. In 1744, he formed a pivotal alliance with Muhammad bin Saud in Diriyah. This pact established a power-sharing arrangement: Ibn Saud would provide political and military leadership, while Ibn Abd al-Wahhab would provide religious authority and guidance.

### The First Saudi State and Military Expansion
The alliance formed the nucleus of the Emirate of Diriyah, the first Saudi state. Initially, the movement's preaching was non-violent, but conflict erupted in 1746 when a powerful chieftain attacked a town that had pledged allegiance to Diriyah. This sparked decades of warfare. Ibn Abd al-Wahhab justified military campaigns as defensive *jihad* against those who first excommunicated and attacked his followers. By the time of his retirement in 1773, after the capture of Riyadh, the Saudi-Wahhabi forces had conquered most of Najd.

### Core Teachings and Reforms
Ibn Abd al-Wahhab's central work, *Kitab al-Tawhid*, argued that the primary duty of a Muslim is to worship God alone, without intermediaries. He believed that many Muslims had lapsed into practices akin to the pre-Islamic era (*Jahiliyyah*). He advocated for every Muslim, male and female, to study the Quran directly and criticized the established clergy for promoting blind imitation. His social reforms included campaigns against usury, bribery, and practices that denied women their inheritance rights. He also condemned forced marriages and instant triple divorce, advocating for women's consent and rights within marriage.

## Terms
- **Tawhid**: The Islamic concept of the absolute oneness and uniqueness of God, which was the core doctrine of Ibn Abd al-Wahhab's teaching.
- **Taqlid**: The practice of blind adherence to the legal rulings of a particular school of Islamic jurisprudence, which Ibn Abd al-Wahhab strongly opposed.
- **Ijtihad**: Independent legal reasoning through direct research of Islamic scriptures (Quran and Hadith), which he advocated as an obligation for qualified scholars.
- **Bid'ah**: Religious innovation, considered heretical, especially in matters of worship. Ibn Abd al-Wahhab condemned practices like tomb veneration as *bid'ah*.
- **Shirk**: The sin of idolatry or polytheism, or associating partners with God. He labeled many popular folk practices as *shirk*.
- **Takfir**: The act of declaring a Muslim to be an apostate (*kafir*). His opponents accused him of indiscriminate *takfir*, a charge he denied.
- **Jihad**: In his context, primarily defined as a defensive struggle to protect the Muslim community and its faith, which could only be declared by a legitimate leader (Imam).
- **Madhhab**: A school of Islamic jurisprudence (e.g., Hanbali, Shafi'i). While trained in the Hanbali school, he criticized rigid partisanship to any *madhhab*.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **On *Takfir***: A central debate is whether Ibn Abd al-Wahhab advocated indiscriminate excommunication of other Muslims. He and his supporters maintained he only applied *takfir* to those who, after being clearly shown the evidence, persisted in acts of *shirk*. His critics, including his own brother Sulayman, accused him of declaring all who disagreed with him as unbelievers.
- **Defensive vs. Offensive Jihad**: Scholars debate whether the military campaigns of the early Saudi state were purely defensive, as Ibn Abd al-Wahhab claimed, or became offensive conquests to spread his doctrine. His later writings, like *Mufid al-mustafid*, are cited as justifying more aggressive action against rebels.
- **Relationship with Sufism**: While he condemned specific Sufi practices like saint veneration, he also praised the inner spiritual science of *Tasawwuf* (purification of the heart). The extent to which his movement was anti-Sufi in principle versus anti-certain practices is debated.
- **Modern Legacy**: There is significant scholarly debate about the connection between his 18th-century reformist teachings and the ideologies of modern Salafi-jihadist groups. Some analysts see a direct lineage, while others argue his movement was a distinct, context-specific reform and that modern extremism is a later synthesis influenced by other thinkers.
