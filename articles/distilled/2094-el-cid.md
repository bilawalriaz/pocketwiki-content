# El Cid

## Overview
Rodrigo Díaz de Vivar (c. 1043–1099), known as El Cid, was a Castilian knight who became a legendary figure of the Reconquista. He served both Christian and Muslim rulers, eventually conquering and ruling the city of Valencia. His life inspired Spain's most famous epic poem, making him a national hero and an enduring symbol of medieval chivalry.

## Timeline
- **c. 1043** — Born in Vivar, near Burgos, Castile.
- **1065** — Becomes commander and royal standard-bearer for King Sancho II of Castile.
- **1072** — Sancho is murdered; his brother Alfonso VI becomes king. El Cid loses his court position.
- **1081** — Exiled from Alfonso VI's court.
- **1081–1086** — Serves the Muslim Taifa of Zaragoza, defending it from Christian and Muslim enemies.
- **1086** — Almoravid invasion defeats Castile; Alfonso VI recalls El Cid.
- **1090** — Defeats and captures Berenguer Ramon II of Barcelona.
- **1092** — Begins siege of Valencia after an anti-Almoravid uprising.
- **1094** — Conquers Valencia, establishing an independent principality.
- **1097** — His only son, Diego Rodríguez, is killed at the Battle of Consuegra.
- **10 July 1099** — El Cid dies in Valencia.
- **1102** — His wife, Jimena Díaz, is forced to surrender Valencia to the Almoravids.

## Body

### Early Life and Service to Castile
Born to minor nobility, Rodrigo Díaz was raised at the court of Ferdinand the Great. He rose to prominence under Ferdinand's son, Sancho II, becoming his chief military commander. He led successful campaigns that expanded Castilian territory against both Sancho's brothers and Muslim kingdoms. After Sancho's assassination in 1072, the throne passed to Alfonso VI, whom El Cid had previously opposed. Though he continued to serve, El Cid lost his status and was eventually exiled in 1081, likely due to unauthorized military actions and court intrigue.

### Exile and Moorish Service
In exile, El Cid offered his services to the Taifa of Zaragoza, where he was given the honorific "El Cid" (The Master). He successfully defended Zaragoza against attacks from the Taifa of Lérida and the Kingdom of Aragon. His military reputation grew significantly during this period. In 1086, a major Almoravid invasion from North Africa defeated the Christian kingdoms, prompting Alfonso VI to recall El Cid. Though he returned briefly, El Cid soon resumed independent operations.

### Conquest and Rule of Valencia
El Cid gradually built a power base in the Mediterranean, defeating rivals like Berenguer Ramon II of Barcelona. He focused on the wealthy city of Valencia, then under the control of the Muslim ruler Yahya al-Qadir. After an Almoravid-inspired uprising in Valencia killed al-Qadir in 1092, El Cid besieged the city. He captured it in 1094 and established an independent principality. He ruled over a pluralistic society of Christians and Muslims, maintaining his own army and administration while nominally acknowledging Alfonso VI. His final years were spent defending Valencia against the Almoravids.

### Death and Legacy
El Cid died on July 10, 1099, undefeated in Valencia. His wife, Jimena Díaz, inherited rule but was forced to surrender the city to the Almoravids in 1102. A famous legend claims that after his death, his corpse was armored and mounted on his horse, Babieca, to lead a final, victorious charge. His life became the subject of the 12th-century epic poem *El Cantar de mio Cid*, which portrays him as the ideal medieval knight. He remains Spain's most celebrated national hero.

## Terms
- **Taifa**: An independent Muslim-ruled principality in the Iberian Peninsula, especially after the collapse of the Caliphate of Córdoba.
- **Almoravids**: A Berber Muslim dynasty from North Africa that invaded and controlled much of al-Andalus in the late 11th century.
- **Reconquista**: The centuries-long period of Christian reconquest of the Iberian Peninsula from Muslim rule.
- **Armiger regis**: Latin for "royal standard-bearer," a high military office in a medieval court.
- **Campeador**: A title derived from Latin *campi doctor*, meaning "master of the battlefield," used by El Cid's contemporaries.
- **Mozarabs**: Christians living under Muslim rule in al-Andalus, often retaining their language and customs.
- **Paria**: A tribute paid by a Muslim taifa to a Christian king in exchange for protection.
- **Tizona**: The name traditionally given to one of El Cid's swords, later confirmed to be of 11th-century Moorish manufacture.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise reasons for El Cid's exile from Alfonso VI's court in 1081 are debated, with theories ranging from unauthorized military campaigns to court jealousy.
- The historicity of the famous oath El Cid supposedly forced Alfonso to take at Santa Gadea is questioned, as it appears in the epic poem but not in the more reliable 12th-century chronicle *Historia Roderici*.
- The origin of his honorific "El Cid" is uncertain; it may have been earned during his service in Zaragoza or after his conquest of Valencia.
- The legend of his posthumous victory is considered a later myth, possibly derived from the manner of his funeral procession into Burgos.
