# Kraków

## Overview
Kraków is Poland's second-largest city and one of its oldest, situated on the Vistula River. It served as the nation's official capital until 1596 and remains a paramount center of Polish economic, academic, cultural, and artistic life. Its historic core is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and the city is a major European tourist destination and academic hub.

## Timeline
- **c. 8th century** — Fortified settlement established on Wawel Hill by the Vistulan tribe.
- **1038** — Becomes the seat of Polish monarchs from the Piast dynasty.
- **1257** — City rights granted under Magdeburg law by Duke Bolesław V the Chaste.
- **1364** — University of Kraków (later Jagiellonian University) founded by Casimir III the Great.
- **1596** — King Sigismund III Vasa moves the royal court to Warsaw, ending Kraków's status as capital.
- **1791** — Kazimierz district, historically separate, is incorporated into Kraków.
- **1815** — Congress of Vienna creates the Free City of Kraków.
- **1846** — Kraków Uprising is crushed; the Free City is annexed by the Austrian Empire.
- **1918** — Kraków becomes part of the re-established Second Polish Republic.
- **1939** — City becomes the capital of the Nazi German Distrikt Krakau within the General Government.
- **1978** — Kraków's Old Town becomes a UNESCO World Heritage Site; Archbishop Karol Wojtyła is elected Pope John Paul II.
- **2023** — Hosts the European Games.

## Body

### History and Significance
Kraków's origins as a fortified settlement on Wawel Hill date to the 8th century. It became the seat of Polish government in 1038 and grew into a leading trade center, though it was sacked by Mongols in 1241 and 1259. The city flourished under the Jagiellonian dynasty as the capital of the Kingdom of Poland, attracting craftsmen and scholars. Its political importance waned after King Sigismund III moved the court to Warsaw in 1596. Following the partitions of Poland, Kraków was annexed by Austria in 1846. It regained prominence as a Polish national symbol and cultural center under Austrian rule, known as the "Polish Athens." After World War I, it became part of the independent Second Polish Republic. During World War II, it was the capital of the Nazi General Government; its Jewish population was forced into the Kraków Ghetto and later sent to extermination camps, though the city's architectural core was largely spared destruction. In the postwar communist era, the industrial suburb of Nowa Huta was built. The election of Kraków's archbishop as Pope John Paul II in 1978 and the city's designation as a UNESCO site that same year marked a symbolic renaissance.

### Architecture and Cityscape
Kraków's historic center showcases Gothic, Renaissance, and Baroque architecture. Key landmarks include Wawel Royal Castle and Cathedral, St. Mary's Basilica with its Gothic altarpiece, and the vast medieval Main Market Square (Rynek Główny). The district of Kazimierz, founded in the 14th century, contains the historic Jewish quarter. The city's architecture reflects its layered history, from medieval fortifications like the Barbican to 19th-century Austro-Hungarian buildings and the socialist-realist district of Nowa Huta, constructed after World War II. The Old Town, encircled by Planty Park, was among the first sites inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1978.

### Culture and Education
Kraków is a major cultural capital, home to institutions like the National Museum, Juliusz Słowacki Theatre, and the Kraków Opera. It hosts numerous festivals, including the Jewish Culture Festival and Misteria Paschalia. The city is a center of learning, anchored by the Jagiellonian University, founded in 1364, one of Europe's oldest universities. Other significant institutions include the AGH University of Science and Technology and the Academy of Music. Kraków was named a UNESCO City of Literature in 2013 and was the European Capital of Culture in 2000.

### Economy and Demographics
Kraków is a key economic hub for southern Poland, with a strong private sector hosting many multinational companies, particularly in business process outsourcing (BPO). The city is the second-most visited in Poland. Its population has grown, reaching over 800,000 in the city proper and 1.4 million in the metropolitan area. Historically, the city was ethnically diverse, with a large Jewish community before World War II. Today, it is predominantly Polish, though the foreign-born population has increased significantly in recent decades.

## Terms
- **General Government**: The Nazi German administrative unit established in occupied central Poland during World War II, with its capital in Kraków.
- **Jagiellonian University**: Founded in 1364, it is one of the oldest universities in Europe and Poland's most prestigious academic institution.
- **Magdeburg law**: A legal charter granting municipal rights and autonomy, widely adopted by medieval Polish cities, including Kraków in 1257.
- **Nowa Huta**: A post-WWII planned socialist-realist district built east of the city center, originally intended as a model communist town.
- **Sonderaktion Krakau**: A Nazi operation in November 1939 in which over 180 Kraków university professors and academics were arrested and sent to concentration camps.
- **Vistulans**: A Lechitic tribe that inhabited the region around Kraków before the establishment of the Polish state.

## Debates and open questions
- The true origin of the city's name is disputed. While traditionally derived from the legendary founder Krakus, other theories, such as a link to the word for "crow" (*kruk*), exist, with no unanimous consensus among historians.
