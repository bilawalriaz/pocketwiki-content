# Kunlun Mountains

## Overview
The Kunlun Mountains are one of Asia's longest mountain chains, stretching over 3,000 km across Western China. They form the northern edge of the Tibetan Plateau and are deeply embedded in Chinese culture, known as the "Forefather of Mountains." The range's definition has evolved over time, and its name originates from a semi-mythical paradise in classical Chinese texts.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Name "Kunlun" originates as a semi-mythical location in the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*.
- **475–221 BCE** — Kunlun Mountains enter Chinese ideology during the Warring States period.
- **c. 126 BCE** — Emperor Wu of Han sends men to find the Yellow River's source and names the mountains at its source "Kunlun."
- **976–922 BCE** — Legend states King Mu of Zhou was the first to visit the Kunlun paradise.
- **Late Triassic** — The mountain range forms from the collision of the Cimmerian Plate with Siberia, closing the Paleo-Tethys Ocean.
- **May 27, 1951** — Last known eruption in the Kunlun Volcanic Group.

## Body

### Extent and Geography
The Kunlun chain runs east from the Pamirs of Tajikistan through southern Xinjiang to Qinghai province, bordering the Tarim Basin and Taklamakan Desert to the north and the sparsely populated Changtang region of the Tibetan Plateau to the south. Its highest peak is Kunlun Goddess Peak (7,167 m). Major northern ranges include the Altyn-Tagh and Qilian Shan, while the Bayan Har Mountains form the watershed between the Yangtze and Yellow Rivers. The range is remote, crossed by only two major roads: Highway 219 in the west and Highway 109 in the east.

### Geological Formation and Volcanism
The Kunlun range formed during the Late Triassic period as a result of the collision between the Cimmerian Plate and Siberia, which closed the Paleo-Tethys Ocean. The Kunlun Volcanic Group consists of over 70 volcanic cones, not volcanic mountains. If classified as volcanic mountains, their highest point (5,808 m) would make them the highest volcano in Asia and China. The last recorded eruption occurred in 1951.

### Mythology and Cultural Significance
Kunlun is originally the name of a mythical Taoist paradise, believed to be the capital of the emperor and the residence of gods. The mythology, which entered Chinese culture during the Warring States period, centers on figures like the Yellow Emperor and the Queen Mother of the West (Xi Wang Mu). Scholar Gu Jiegang divides Chinese mythology into the western Kunlun system and the eastern Penglai system. The mountains are considered the birthplace of the Chinese nation and the staircase to heaven in legend.

## Terms
- **Tibetan Plateau:** A vast, high-altitude region in Central Asia, the Kunlun Mountains form its northern edge.
- **Tarim Basin:** A large endorheic basin in Xinjiang, China, containing the Taklamakan Desert, bordered to the south by the Kunlun.
- **Cimmerian Plate:** A tectonic plate whose collision with Siberia formed the Kunlun range.
- **Paleo-Tethys Ocean:** An ancient ocean that closed due to the collision forming the Kunlun Mountains.
- **Kunlun Volcanic Group:** A cluster of over 70 volcanic cones in the Kunlun range, not classified as volcanic mountains.
- **Taoist paradise:** A mythical, sacred realm in Taoism, of which Kunlun is a prime example.
- **Queen Mother of the West (Xi Wang Mu):** A deity in Chinese mythology whose mythical abode is in the Kunlun Mountains.
- **Watershed:** A ridge of land that separates river basins; the Bayan Har Mountains form the watershed between the Yangtze and Yellow Rivers.

## Debates and Open Questions
The exact geographical definition of the Kunlun Mountains varies. Older sources used "Kunlun" to refer to a broader mountain belt across central China, including the Altyn Tagh, Qilian, and Qin Mountains. Modern sources define it more narrowly as the range south of the Tarim Basin. There is also debate about its northwestern extent, with some authorities including peaks like Kongur Tagh and Muztagh Ata, though these are physically more linked to the Pamir group.
