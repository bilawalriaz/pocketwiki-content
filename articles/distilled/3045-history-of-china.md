# History of China

## Overview
China's history spans millennia, originating in the Yellow and Yangtze river basins as one of the world's oldest continuous civilizations. Traditionally viewed through a dynastic cycle of unification, fragmentation, and reunification, it has profoundly shaped global culture, technology, and politics. The modern era marks a dramatic transformation into a major global economic power.

## Timeline
- **c. 1250 BC** — Shang dynasty begins, leaving oracle bone inscriptions.
- **1046–256 BC** — Zhou dynasty introduces Mandate of Heaven; foundations for Confucianism, Taoism, Legalism.
- **221 BC** — Qin Shi Huang unifies China, establishing first centralized imperial state.
- **202 BC – 220 AD** — Han dynasty institutionalizes Confucianism; advances like paper-making.
- **581–907** — Sui and Tang dynasties restore unity; Tang is a golden age.
- **960–1279** — Song dynasty advances printing, technology, imperial examinations.
- **1271–1368** — Mongol Yuan dynasty rules China as an ethnic minority.
- **1368–1644** — Ming dynasty notes for maritime exploration and Great Wall restoration.
- **1644–1912** — Qing dynasty expands territory to imperial peak before 19th-century defeats.
- **1912** — 1911 Revolution ends imperial rule, establishing Republic of China.
- **1949** — Mao Zedong proclaims People's Republic of China after civil war.
- **1978** — Deng Xiaoping initiates reform and opening up, transforming economy.

## Body

### Prehistory and Early Civilizations
Chinese civilization emerged from the Neolithic, with agriculture developing along the Yangtze (rice, c. 8,000 years ago) and Yellow (millet, c. 7000 BC) rivers. The Bronze Age saw cultures like Majiayao and Sanxingdui. Traditional historiography begins with the legendary Xia dynasty (c. 2070–c. 1600 BC), possibly linked to Erlitou. The first verified dynasty is the Shang (c. 1600–c. 1046 BC), known from oracle bones at Yinxu.

### The Zhou Dynasty and Philosophical Foundations
The Zhou dynasty (1046–256 BC), the longest-lasting, introduced the **Mandate of Heaven** to legitimize rule. Its later periods—the Spring and Autumn (722–476 BC) and Warring States (476–221 BC)—were times of fragmentation and warfare but also of the "Hundred Schools of Thought," producing foundational philosophies like Confucianism and Taoism.

### Imperial Unification and the Han Dynasty
In 221 BC, the Qin state unified China under Qin Shi Huang, establishing a centralized bureaucratic empire. The subsequent Han dynasty (202 BC – 220 AD) consolidated this order, made Confucianism orthodox, and expanded via the Silk Road. After a brief interregnum, the Eastern Han saw paper-making and the spread of Buddhism.

### Periods of Division and Reunification
Following the Han's collapse, China entered centuries of division, including the Three Kingdoms (220–280) and Northern and Southern dynasties (420–589), during which Buddhism spread widely. Unity was restored by the Sui (581–618) and Tang (618–907), a golden age of cosmopolitan trade and culture. After the Tang's fall, the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period (907–960) preceded the Song dynasty (960–1279), which achieved economic and technological brilliance but faced military pressure from northern powers.

### Mongol Rule and the Ming Dynasty
The Mongol Yuan dynasty (1271–1368), established by Kublai Khan, was the first conquest dynasty to rule all of China. It was followed by the native Ming dynasty (1368–1644), which restored Han rule, expanded maritime exploration under Zheng He, and built much of the existing Great Wall.

### The Qing Dynasty and the End of Empire
The Manchu-led Qing dynasty (1644–1912) expanded China's territory to its greatest extent. Internal rebellions and military defeats by Western powers in the 19th century weakened it. The 1911 Revolution ended imperial rule, leading to the Republic of China (ROC).

### Modern China: Republic and People's Republic
The ROC, led by the Kuomintang (KMT), faced warlordism and then the Chinese Civil War with the Chinese Communist Party (CCP). After the Second Sino-Japanese War (1937–1945), the CCP, under Mao Zedong, won the civil war. In 1949, Mao proclaimed the People's Republic of China (PRC) on the mainland, while the ROC government retreated to Taiwan. The PRC's early decades included the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution. After Mao's death, Deng Xiaoping's reforms from 1978 onward shifted China toward a market-oriented economy, leading to rapid growth.

## Terms
- **Mandate of Heaven**: A Zhou-era concept legitimizing rule, where a dynasty's right to govern is granted by a divine force and can be lost due to misrule.
- **Oracle Bones**: Inscribed animal bones and shells used for divination in the Shang dynasty, providing the earliest Chinese writing.
- **Confucianism**: A philosophical system emphasizing social harmony, filial piety, and ethical governance, founded by Confucius.
- **Legalism**: A philosophy advocating strict laws and centralized state control, used by the Qin dynasty.
- **Silk Road**: An ancient network of trade routes connecting China to the West, facilitating cultural and economic exchange.
- **Imperial Examination System**: A civil service recruitment system based on merit, perfected in the Song dynasty.
- **Neo-Confucianism**: A revival of Confucian thought incorporating metaphysical elements, dominant from the Song dynasty onward.
- **Long March**: The 1934–1935 military retreat of the CCP, a foundational event in its history.
- **Reform and Opening Up**: Deng Xiaoping's post-1978 policies transitioning China toward a market economy.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The existence and nature of the Xia dynasty are debated, as it is not confirmed by contemporary written records.
- The extent of Shang political control beyond its core at Anyang is uncertain, with evidence of diverse contemporary cultures like Sanxingdui.
- The causes of the significant population decline during the Mongol Yuan dynasty are debated, with theories ranging from administrative recording failures to plague and enserfment.
- The historical accuracy of certain events, such as the Qin dynasty's burning of books and burial of scholars, is questioned by contemporary scholars.
