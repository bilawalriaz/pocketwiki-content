# History of Brazil

## Overview
Brazil's history spans from ancient indigenous civilizations to a modern federal republic, marked by European colonization, a long period of slavery, cycles of economic booms, and political instability including dictatorships. It is the largest country in South America and the fifth-largest in the world, with a diverse population shaped by indigenous, European, and African roots. Its trajectory from colony to empire to republic reflects broader themes of colonialism, resource extraction, and the struggle for democracy.

## Timeline
- **c. 11,000 BC** — Earliest human remains (Luzia Woman) found in Minas Gerais.
- **1494** — Treaty of Tordesillas divides Portuguese and Spanish domains.
- **April 22, 1500** — Pedro Álvares Cabral claims Brazil for Portugal.
- **1549** — Establishment of the dual state government (Salvador and Rio de Janeiro).
- **1630–1654** — Dutch Brazil controls parts of the northeast coast.
- **1695** — Zumbi dos Palmares, leader of a maroon republic, is executed.
- **1720s** — Diamond deposits found near Diamantina; gold rush in Minas Gerais.
- **1808** — Portuguese royal court flees to Rio de Janeiro.
- **September 7, 1822** — Prince Pedro declares Brazil's independence.
- **November 15, 1889** — Military coup establishes the First Brazilian Republic.
- **1930–1945** — Getúlio Vargas rules in an authoritarian dictatorship.
- **1964–1985** — Military dictatorship rules Brazil.
- **1985** — Return to civilian governance.
- **January 1, 2023** — Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva begins his third presidential term.

## Body

### Pre-Colonial and Colonial Foundations
Before 1500, Brazil was inhabited by hundreds of indigenous tribes, with evidence of complex societies like the Marajoara culture, which built mounds and created sophisticated pottery. The Portuguese arrival in 1500 initiated colonization, initially focused on extracting brazilwood. The economy shifted to sugar plantations (*engenhos*) in the 16th century, relying on enslaved African labor after indigenous populations were decimated by European diseases. The Dutch and French attempted to seize parts of Brazil, but were expelled by Portuguese and their allies. In the 18th century, a gold and diamond rush in Minas Gerais shifted the colony's economic center southward and stimulated urban growth.

### Empire and Independence
In 1808, the Portuguese royal court, fleeing Napoleon, relocated to Rio de Janeiro, elevating Brazil's status. In 1815, it became a united kingdom with Portugal. After King João VI returned to Portugal in 1821, his son Pedro declared Brazil's independence on September 7, 1822, becoming Emperor Pedro I. The Empire of Brazil (1822–1889) was a constitutional monarchy. Pedro I abdicated in 1831, leaving his young son Pedro II to rule after a regency period marked by rebellions. Pedro II's long reign saw economic growth from coffee and rubber exports, but also internal conflicts and the costly Paraguayan War. The monarchy ended with a military coup in 1889.

### Republics and Political Instability
The First Republic (1889–1930) was a formal democracy but excluded most voters. Power alternated between coffee oligarchies from São Paulo and Minas Gerais (*política do café com leite*). Political instability led to a 1930 coup by Getúlio Vargas, who ruled as a dictator until 1945. After a brief democratic period (the Fourth Republic, 1945–1964), a U.S.-backed military coup established a dictatorship that lasted until 1985. This regime achieved economic growth (the "Brazilian Miracle") but also committed widespread human rights abuses.

### Modern Democracy and Challenges
Democracy was restored in 1985. Subsequent governments, including those of Fernando Henrique Cardoso and Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, focused on economic stabilization and social programs like *Bolsa Família* to address extreme inequality. Brazil hosted the 2014 World Cup and 2016 Olympics. Recent years have been marked by political polarization, corruption scandals, and the COVID-19 pandemic, which caused a severe economic downturn and over 670,000 deaths. Religious demographics have also shifted, with a decline in Catholicism and a rise in evangelical Protestantism.

## Terms
- **Hereditary Captaincies:** A 16th-century Portuguese system dividing Brazil into strips of land granted to noblemen for settlement and administration.
- **Treaty of Tordesillas (1494):** An agreement dividing newly discovered lands outside Europe between the Portuguese and Spanish empires along a meridian.
- **Quilombo:** A settlement founded by escaped enslaved Africans in colonial Brazil.
- **Bandeirantes:** 17th-century paramilitary adventurers who explored Brazil's interior in search of gold and native slaves.
- **Política do café com leite:** ("Coffee with milk" policy) The political arrangement in the First Republic where the presidency alternated between coffee-producing São Paulo and dairy-producing Minas Gerais.
- **Coronelismo:** A political system in which local bosses (*coronéis*) controlled votes in exchange for favors.
- **Brazilian Miracle:** The period of rapid economic growth in the early 1970s under the military dictatorship.
- **Plano Real:** A 1994 economic plan that successfully stabilized Brazil's currency and curbed hyperinflation.

## Debates and Open questions
- The extent and complexity of pre-Columbian societies, such as the Marajoara culture, is debated among archaeologists regarding their origins and scale.
- Historians debate the precise political power of coffee growers during the First Republic.
- The true number of deaths and disappearances during the military dictatorship (1964–1985) is contested, with official figures at 434 killed or missing, while activists suggest it could be much higher.
- The causes of the 2002 currency devaluation before Lula's first term are disputed, with some blaming his election and others pointing to low foreign reserves left by the previous administration.
