# Leonhard Euler

## Overview
Leonhard Euler (1707–1783) was a Swiss polymath whose work defined 18th-century mathematics and science. He founded graph theory and topology, established analytic number theory, and introduced much of modern mathematical notation—including the function notation *f*(*x*), the base of the natural logarithm *e*, the imaginary unit *i*, the summation symbol Σ, and the popularization of π. His prolific output (866 publications) spanned calculus, number theory, mechanics, fluid dynamics, optics, astronomy, and music theory. Euler spent his career at the academies in Saint Petersburg and Berlin, maintaining extraordinary productivity even after becoming almost totally blind.

## Timeline
- **15 April 1707** — Born in Basel, Switzerland.
- **1720** — Enrolls at University of Basel at age 13; studies under Johann Bernoulli.
- **1723** — Receives Master of Philosophy; begins theological studies.
- **1727** — Moves to Saint Petersburg; joins Imperial Russian Academy of Sciences.
- **1735** — Solves the Basel problem (Σ 1/*n*² = π²/6); solves Seven Bridges of Königsberg, founding graph theory.
- **1736** — Publishes *Mechanica*, reformulating Newton's laws for rigid bodies.
- **1741** — Moves to Berlin Academy under Frederick the Great.
- **1748** — Publishes *Introductio in analysin infinitorum*, systematizing analysis and introducing *f*(*x*) and *e*.
- **1755** — Publishes *Institutiones calculi differentialis*; elected to Royal Swedish and French Academies.
- **1757** — Publishes Euler equations for inviscid fluid flow.
- **1766** — Returns to Saint Petersburg Academy under Catherine the Great.
- **1768–1772** — Publishes *Letters to a German Princess*, a widely read exposition of science and philosophy.
- **1771** — Home destroyed by fire in Saint Petersburg.
- **1773** — Wife Katharina dies; marries her half-sister Salome Abigail Gsell.
- **18 September 1783** — Dies in Saint Petersburg of a brain hemorrhage.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Euler was born in Basel to a pastor father who had studied under Jacob Bernoulli. After early tutoring, he entered the University of Basel at 13, where Johann Bernoulli recognized his talent and mentored him via weekly consultations. Bernoulli persuaded Euler’s father to allow a mathematical career over the ministry. Euler earned a Master’s degree in 1723 comparing Descartes and Newton, then wrote a dissertation on sound propagation (*De Sono*, 1726). He entered the Paris Academy prize competition 15 times, winning 12, beginning with a second-place finish on ship mast placement in 1727.

### First Saint Petersburg Period (1727–1741)
Recruited by Daniel Bernoulli, Euler joined the Imperial Academy established by Peter the Great to close Russia’s scientific gap with Europe. He mastered Russian, served as a navy medic, and rose to professor of physics (1731) despite funding cuts under Peter II. After Anna of Russia assumed power, conditions improved. Euler headed the mathematics department after Daniel Bernoulli’s departure (1733), married Katharina Gsell (1734), and began a massive correspondence with academy secretary Johann Daniel Schumacher. Frederick the Great recruited him to Berlin in 1741; Euler cited failing eyesight to secure release.

### Berlin Period (1741–1766)
Euler spent 25 years at the Berlin Academy, producing 380 works (275 published). He published *Introductio in analysin infinitorum* (1748) and *Institutiones calculi differentialis* (1755), supervised the academy’s library, observatory, and botanical garden, and tutored Princess Friederike Charlotte, producing the popular *Letters to a German Princess*. Despite his prestige, Frederick II found Euler unsophisticated and named himself academy president. Euler clashed with Voltaire and failed to satisfy Frederick’s practical engineering demands (e.g., the Sanssouci fountains), though his calculations were likely correct. During the Seven Years’ War (1760), Russian troops sacked his farm; Empress Elizabeth compensated him generously. He maintained strong ties to Saint Petersburg, sending over 100 memoirs there.

### Second Saint Petersburg Period (1766–1783)
Catherine the Great’s accession stabilized Russia; Euler returned on generous terms (3,000 rubles, pensions, posts for his sons). Assisted by student Anders Johan Lexell, he continued prodigious output despite near-total blindness from a 1766 cataract and complications from couching. A 1771 fire destroyed his home. He died in 1783 discussing the orbit of Uranus with Lexell.

### Personal Life and Eyesight
Euler married Katharina Gsell in 1734; of 13 children, five survived. After her 1773 death, he married her half-sister Salome Abigail Gsell. He possessed legendary memory: he knew the *Aeneid* by heart, the first 100 primes and their powers to the sixth degree, and could recite historical dates effortlessly. His eyesight failed early: right eye blindness by 1738 (blamed on cartography), left eye cataract in 1766. He remarked, “Now I will have fewer distractions.” Productivity increased with scribes; in 1775 he averaged one paper per week.

### Mathematical Notation
Euler standardized modern notation through widely circulated textbooks. He introduced *f*(*x*) for function values, *e* for the natural logarithm base (Euler’s number), *i* for √−1, Σ for summation, Δ for finite differences, and popularized π for the circle ratio (originated by William Jones). He used lowercase for triangle sides and capitals for angles.

### Analysis
Euler made infinitesimal calculus his central focus, heavily using power series (e.g., *e*ˣ = Σ *x*ⁿ/*n*!). Though his proofs sometimes lacked modern rigor (relying on the “generality of algebra”), his methods yielded breakthroughs. He solved the Basel problem (1735), proving Σ 1/*n*² = π²/6. He introduced the Euler–Mascheroni constant γ ≈ 0.5772 (limit of harmonic series minus ln *n*). He defined logarithms for negative and complex numbers, discovered Euler’s formula *e*ⁱᵠ = cos φ + *i* sin φ, and its special case Euler’s identity *e*ⁱπ + 1 = 0. He introduced the gamma function, invented the calculus of variations (Euler–Lagrange equation), and pioneered analytic number theory, linking primes to the Riemann zeta function via the Euler product formula.

### Number Theory
Influenced by Christian Goldbach and Pierre de Fermat, Euler disproved Fermat’s conjecture that all numbers 2²ⁿ + 1 are prime. He proved the sum of reciprocal primes diverges, invented the totient function φ(*n*) (count of integers ≤ *n* coprime to *n*), and generalized Fermat’s little theorem to Euler’s theorem. He proved the Euclid–Euler theorem: a one-to-one correspondence between even perfect numbers and Mersenne primes. He conjectured the law of quadratic reciprocity (later proved by Gauss) and by 1772 proved 2³¹ − 1 = 2,147,483,647 is a Mersenne prime, possibly the largest known prime until 1867. He also advanced partition theory.

### Graph Theory and Topology
In 1735, Euler solved the Seven Bridges of Königsberg problem, proving no path crosses each bridge exactly once—the first theorem of graph theory. He discovered the polyhedron formula *V* − *E* + *F* = 2 (Euler characteristic) for convex polyhedra, later generalized by Cauchy and L’Huilier into the foundations of topology.

### Physics, Astronomy, and Engineering
Euler integrated Leibniz’s calculus with Newton’s fluxions, creating tools for physical problems. He invented numerical approximations (Euler’s method, Euler–Maclaurin formula). With the Bernoullis, he developed the Euler–Bernoulli beam equation, a cornerstone of structural engineering. In astronomy, he accurately determined comet orbits, calculated solar parallax, and contributed to longitude tables (winning multiple Paris Academy prizes). In optics, he opposed Newton’s corpuscular theory, advancing Huygens’ wave theory. In fluid dynamics, he predicted cavitation (1754), derived the Euler number for turbine efficiency, and published the Euler equations for inviscid flow (1757). He formulated Euler’s critical load for column buckling.

### Logic
Euler introduced closed-curve diagrams (Euler diagrams) in 1768 to illustrate syllogistic reasoning. These represent sets and their relationships (intersection, subset, disjointness) via overlapping curves. They became instructional tools in set theory (1960s “new math”) and remain widely used for visualizing logical combinations.

### Demography
In 1760, Euler modeled geometric population growth under constant fertility/mortality using difference equations, deriving relationships among demographic indices for estimation with missing data. Alfred J. Lotka (1907, 1911, 1922) later adapted this approach into the Stable Population Model, founding 20th-century formal demography.

### Music Theory
Euler’s *Tentamen novae theoriae musicae* (1739) sought to mathematize music. He defined 18 “genres” as octave divisions using primes 3 and 5 (e.g., fifth = 3, major third = 5), culminating in Genre 18 (2ᵐ·3³·5²), the “diatonico-chromatic” system used in composition. He devised the *Speculum musicum* graph (later the Tonnetz in Neo-Riemannian theory) to map intervals. He proposed a *gradus suavitatis* (degree of agreeableness) for intervals/chords derived from prime factors in just intonation.

### Personal Philosophy and Religious Beliefs
Euler was a devout Christian who believed in biblical inspiration, arguing against Leibniz’s monadism and Christian Wolff’s philosophy as “heathen and atheistic” for lacking precise quantitative laws. His *Letters to a German Princess* and *Rettung der Göttlichen Offenbahrung* articulate his views. A famous apocryphal legend claims he silenced atheist Denis Diderot at Catherine’s court with a nonsensical mathematical “proof” of God: “(*a* + *b*ⁿ)/*n* = *x*, hence God exists.”

## Terms
- **Function notation *f*(*x*)**: Euler’s notation for the value of a function *f* applied to argument *x*.
- **Euler’s number (*e*)**: The base of the natural logarithm, ≈ 2.71828, defined by Euler as limₙ→∞ (1 + 1/*n*)ⁿ.
- **Imaginary unit (*i*)**: Euler’s symbol for √−1, enabling complex analysis.
- **Euler’s formula**: *e*ⁱᵠ = cos φ + *i* sin φ, linking exponential and trigonometric functions.
- **Euler’s identity**: *e*ⁱπ + 1 = 0, a special case of Euler’s formula uniting five fundamental constants.
- **Euler–Mascheroni constant (γ)**: ≈ 0.5772, the limit of the harmonic series minus the natural logarithm.
- **Euler product formula**: ζ(*s*) = Π (1 − *p*⁻ˢ)⁻¹, linking the Riemann zeta function to prime numbers.
- **Totient function φ(*n*)**: The count of positive integers ≤ *n* coprime to *n*; used to generalize Fermat’s little theorem.
- **Euler characteristic**: *V* − *E* + *F* = 2 for convex polyhedra; a topological invariant generalized by genus.
- **Euler equations (fluid dynamics)**: Partial differential equations for inviscid flow published in 1757.
- **Euler diagrams**: Closed-curve diagrams representing set relationships (intersection, subset, disjointness).

## Debates and open questions
- **Cause of blindness**: Euler blamed cartography for his right-eye blindness (1738); the exact etiology remains speculative.
- **Rigor of proofs**: Euler’s reliance on the “principle of the generality of algebra” and manipulation of divergent series did not meet later standards of rigor (Cauchy, Weierstrass), though his results were overwhelmingly correct.
- **Diderot legend**: The story of Euler’s algebraic “proof of God” before Diderot is almost certainly apocryphal (Diderot knew mathematics), but it reflects contemporary perceptions of Euler’s piety and the role of mathematics in apologetics.
- **Priority of π notation**: William Jones used π for the circle ratio in 1706; Euler’s adoption in *Introductio* (1748) cemented its universal acceptance.
- **Sanssouci fountains**: Frederick the Great ridiculed Euler’s hydraulic calculations, but modern analysis suggests Euler’s mathematics was sound; the failure likely lay in execution or communication.
