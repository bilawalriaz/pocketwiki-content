# Peter Abelard

## Overview
Peter Abelard (1079–1142) was a French philosopher, logician, theologian, composer, and poet whose work bridged the early medieval reliance on Plato and the later Aristotelian scholasticism. He is celebrated for resolving the problem of universals through conceptualism—a middle ground between realism and nominalism—and for pioneering an ethics of pure intentionalism, arguing that moral culpability rests solely on consent, not external acts. In theology, he developed the moral influence theory of atonement and the concept of limbo for unbaptized infants, while his dialectical method in *Sic et Non* established "theology" as a rational discipline. His tumultuous affair with his brilliant student Héloïse d'Argenteuil, their secret marriage, his castration by her uncle Fulbert, and their subsequent monastic correspondence (*Historia Calamitatum* and the Letters) made them iconic figures of tragic love and intellectual partnership, influencing European literature from Chaucer to the Romantics.

## Body

### Early Life and Intellectual Formation
Abelard was born c. 1079 in Le Pallet, Brittany, the eldest son of a minor knight, Berenger. Encouraged to study the liberal arts, he excelled at dialectic (logic) and rejected a military career for the itinerant life of a scholar. He studied first under the nominalist Roscellinus of Compiègne in the Loire region, then c. 1100 moved to Paris to attend the cathedral school of Notre-Dame under William of Champeaux, a leading realist. Abelard quickly mastered and then publicly refuted William’s realism, triggering a feud that drove William from his post and established Abelard’s reputation. Despite his success, Abelard’s arrogance alienated both William and Roscellinus. He opened rival schools at Melun and Corbeil (1102–1104), but the strain caused a nervous breakdown, forcing a multi-year retreat to Brittany.

### The Problem of Universals and Logical Innovation
Returning to Paris after 1108, Abelard again defeated William in debate over universals—the question of whether general terms (e.g., "humanity") refer to real shared essences (realism) or merely names (nominalism). Abelard’s solution, **conceptualism**, held that universals exist as mental concepts (*sermones*) signifying a common nature, but not as independent realities. He argued that only nouns possess universality, grounding nominalism, yet insisted these names signify real similarities, distinguishing him from extreme nominalists. His philosophy of language distinguished the **force** (assertion, question, command) from the **content** of a proposition—a distinction later attributed to Gottlob Frege but articulated by Abelard seven centuries earlier. He analyzed conditionals as affirming a connection between antecedent and consequent without asserting either, and developed a theory of **entailment** distinguishing complete (formally valid under uniform substitution) from incomplete inferences. His logical works (*Logica Ingredientibus*, *Dialectica*, *Glosses on Porphyry*) made him the greatest logician between antiquity and William of Ockham.

### Ethics of Intent
Abelard’s mature ethics, in *Ethica (Scito te ipsum)* and the *Dialogus*, established **pure intentionalism**: moral evaluation depends exclusively on *consent* (intention), not on acts, desires, or outcomes. Sin is contempt of God; a person who resolves to sin but is prevented externally is equally culpable. He identified eight behavioral components, rejecting involuntary inclinations (e.g., arousal) as morally determining. His famous example: a man bound between two women who experiences arousal without consent has not sinned. Consequently, pleasure is not inherently sinful. Virtue lies in love of God and neighbor. A person acting on sincere but erroneous conscience is not blameworthy, though the act prevents full virtue. This led to his controversial claim in the *Dialogus* that the Jews who crucified Christ were blameless—they genuinely believed they were executing a false messiah. Abelard also analyzed **involuntary consent**: an agent may desire an act (first-order) while desiring not to desire it (second-order), rendering the consent irrational and involuntary, though the agent remains culpable. The external act itself is morally indifferent; a judge sentencing a murderer acts laudably if motivated by justice, reprehensibly if by hatred.

### Theology and the Limits of Reason
Abelard coined "theology" in its modern sense as the rational investigation of faith. In *Sic et Non* (Yes and No), he compiled contradictory patristic authorities to demonstrate that dialectic must resolve apparent contradictions, establishing the scholastic method. His *Theologia Summi Boni* (1120) applied logic to the Trinity, provoking charges of **Sabellianism** (modalism—the Father, Son, and Spirit as mere modes of one person) at the 1121 Council of Soissons. He was forced to burn the work and briefly confined. Later *Theologia* versions (1130s) praised pagan philosophers for discovering Christian truths by reason alone. On atonement, he rejected the ransom-to-Satan and satisfaction theories, proposing the **moral influence theory**: Christ’s incarnation and death demonstrate God’s love, moving humans to repentance and love in return. Regarding unbaptized infants, he interpreted Augustine’s "mildest punishment" as *carentia visionis Dei* (loss of the beatific vision) without positive suffering, shaping the later doctrine of **Limbo of Infants**. Pope Benedict XVI (2009) later critiqued Abelard’s Trinitarian logic as fragile and his ethics as dangerously subjectivist, but acknowledged his decisive contribution to scholastic theology and his insight that non-Christian traditions prepare for Christ.

### Conflict with Bernard of Clairvaux
After 1136, William of St Thierry and Thomas of Morigny extracted heretical propositions from Abelard’s writings and denounced him to Bernard of Clairvaux, the era’s dominant monastic theologian. Bernard objected that Abelard misapplied logic to mysteries of faith, risking intellectualism and relativizing truth. Abelard challenged Bernard to a public debate at the 1141 Council of Sens. Bernard circumvented this by securing private episcopal condemnations of the propositions before the council convened. Presented with a *fait accompli*, Abelard—ill and unable to respond—appealed to Rome. Pope Innocent II excommunicated him (16 July 1141), ordered perpetual silence, and mandated book burning. Peter the Venerable, abbot of Cluny, intercepted Abelard en route to Rome, sheltered him, negotiated a reconciliation with Bernard, and secured papal approval for Abelard to remain at Cluny under obedience.

### Relationship with Héloïse
Héloïse d'Argenteuil, niece of canon Fulbert, was renowned for her mastery of Latin, Greek, and Hebrew. In 1115–1116, Abelard entered Fulbert’s household to pursue her. His *Historia Calamitatum* frames it as seduction; her letters depict mutual intellectual and erotic partnership. They exchanged love poems (now lost) and used coded phrases in his lectures. Héloïse bore a son, **Astrolabe** (named for the astronomical instrument), sent to Brittany. To appease Fulbert, they married secretly—clerical celibacy was then hardening into law—but Héloïse denied the marriage publicly to protect Abelard’s career. Fulbert, enraged, hired men who castrated Abelard in 1117. Abelard became a monk at Saint-Denis; Héloïse entered Argenteuil as a nun (unveiled). In 1129, Abbot Suger expelled the Argenteuil nuns; Héloïse became abbess of the Paraclete, the community Abelard had founded as a hermit. He gave it a rule emphasizing literary study and composed a hymnary (only *O quanta qualia* survives). Their correspondence—*Historia Calamitatum*, three letters by Abelard, four by Héloïse—explores theology, monastic vocation, and their enduring bond. Héloïse’s letters reveal a fierce intellect struggling with the tension between erotic love and religious duty.

### Psychology and Mental Health
Abelard suffered at least two major collapses: c. 1104–1105 from overwork, and at the 1141 Council of Sens, where Geoffrey of Auxerre recorded his memory and reason failing. He developed an elementary cognitive theory in *Tractatus de Intellectibus* and argued that "human beings speak to God with their thoughts." He pioneered the **insanity defense**: children and the insane lack reason, so "nothing is counted as sin for them," rejecting demonic possession as the cause of mental illness. Modern scholars speculate his grandiosity, paranoia, shame, and cyclical breakdowns suggest bipolar disorder or narcissistic traits, though medieval sources attribute his temperament to Breton heritage and overwork.

### Poetry and Music
Abelard composed love songs for Héloïse (lost, save one fragment, "Dull is the Star") praised for charm and melodic sweetness. His hymnary for the Paraclete (post-1130) innovated by using homogeneous, metrically grouped material, allowing few melodies to serve many texts. He wrote six biblical *planctus* (laments) in diastematic neumes—*Dinae*, *Iacob*, *Virginum Israel*, *Samson*, *David super Abner*, *David super Saul et Jonatha*—influencing the later *lai* form. Surviving melodies show expressive flexibility and technical adroitness matching his poetic skill.

## Terms
- ****Conceptualism**** — Abelard’s solution to the problem of universals: general terms signify mental concepts (*sermones*) formed from perceived similarities, not independent real essences (realism) nor mere names (nominalism).
- ****Nominalism**** — The view that universals are only names (*nomina*); Abelard is a founder but holds a moderate form where names signify real resemblances.
- ****Intentionalism (Ethics)**** — The doctrine that moral worth depends solely on the agent’s consent or intention, not on the act, its consequences, or involuntary desires.
- ****Involuntary Consent**** — Abelard’s category for an agent who acts on a first-order desire while possessing a contrary second-order desire (not wanting to want), rendering the consent irrational and involuntary, though the agent remains culpable.
- ****Moral Influence Theory of Atonement**** — Abelard’s view that Christ’s life and death save by demonstrating God’s love, inspiring human repentance and love, rather than by paying a ransom or satisfying divine honor.
- ****Limbo of Infants**** — The theological hypothesis, shaped by Abelard, that unbaptized infants suffer only the pain of loss (*carentia visionis Dei*)—exclusion from the beatific vision—without positive punishment.
- ****Sic et Non**** — Abelard’s dialectical textbook juxtaposing contradictory patristic authorities to train students in resolving apparent contradictions through logic.
- ****Sabellianism**** — A Trinitarian heresy (modalism) charging that the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are merely modes of one divine person; Abelard was condemned for this at Soissons (1121).
- ****Planctus**** — A biblical lament genre; Abelard composed six, notated in early neumes, influencing the later *lai*.
- ****Historia Calamitatum**** — Abelard’s autobiographical letter ("History of My Calamities"), the first of the eight-letter collection with Héloïse, pioneering self-reflective autobiography.

## Debates and open questions
- **Authenticity of the Letters**: While most scholars (Gilson, Dronke, Mews) accept the *Historia Calamitatum* and the seven personal/directional letters as genuine, John Benton and others argue they are later literary fabrications, citing the absence of 12th-century manuscripts (earliest copies are late 13th century).
- **The *Epistolae Duorum Amantium***: An anonymous collection of love letters argued by Constant Mews (building on Ewad Könsgen) to be the lost early correspondence of Abelard and Héloïse. Attribution rests on circumstantial evidence (vocabulary, biographical details) and remains contested.
- **Resting Place**: The Oratory of the Paraclete claims the couple’s bones never left; Père Lachaise asserts they were transferred in 1817; some scholars hold Abelard is at Père Lachaise but Héloïse remains elsewhere.
- **Extent of Abelard’s Heresy**: Bernard of Clairvaux and the Council of Sens condemned specific propositions; Pope Innocent II ratified the condemnation. Peter the Venerable secured reconciliation, and Abelard died in communion. Historians debate whether his Trinitarian theology was genuinely heterodox or a victim of political-theological rivalry.
- **Mental Health Diagnosis**: Retrospective diagnoses (bipolar disorder, narcissistic personality) are speculative; the source notes medieval observers attributed his temperament to Breton heritage, overwork, and an "indomitable" personality.
