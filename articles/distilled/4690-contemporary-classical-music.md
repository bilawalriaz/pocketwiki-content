# Contemporary classical music

## Overview
Contemporary classical music refers to Western art music composed from the mid-20th century onward, primarily after 1945. It is characterized by a wide diversity of styles and techniques, moving beyond traditional tonality to explore new sound worlds, technologies, and compositional philosophies. This field remains a vital, evolving tradition, with new works constantly being created and performed.

## Timeline
- **Early 20th C.** — Composers experiment with dissonant pitch language and atonality.
- **Post-WWI** — Neoclassicism emerges as a reaction against late Romanticism.
- **Post-WWII** — Serialism and total serialism become dominant in Europe and America.
- **1945–75** — European and American compositional traditions diverge significantly.
- **1975–1990** — Personal computers become affordable, revolutionizing electronic music production.
- **1980s onward** — Minimalism and post-minimalism gain prominence, often incorporating historical styles.
- **Late 20th C.** — High modernism declines but retains an active core of composers.
- **21st C.** — Ambient, "neo-classical," and art-rock-influenced styles broaden the genre's audience.

## Body

### Background and Post-War Divergence
Early 20th-century composers pushed towards dissonance and atonality. After World War I, neoclassicism sought a return to balanced forms. The post-WWII era saw a major split: European composers like Pierre Boulez and Karlheinz Stockhausen advanced **serialism** (a method organizing all musical elements by fixed series), while American composers like John Cage explored **indeterminacy** (incorporating chance into composition). This period also saw the birth of electronic music and the beginnings of minimalism.

### Major Movements and Styles
**High modernism** and serialism dominated the post-war avant-garde. In reaction, **minimalism** emerged, using repetitive textures and consonant harmony. This led to **post-minimalism**, which often blends minimalist techniques with other influences, including historical styles (**historicism**). Other significant movements include **spectral music** (composing based on the physical properties of sound), **New Complexity** (characterized by extremely intricate notation and performance demands), and **polystylism** (eclectic mixing of styles).

### Technology and New Media
Advances in technology have been central to contemporary classical music. The development of **electronic music** moved from tape-based studios to personal computers, which now handle synthesis, processing, and composition. This has also influenced film and television scoring, with composers like Ligeti and Penderecki creating works used in major films. The genre has also seen crossovers with ambient and art rock, broadening its reach.

## Terms
*   **Serialism:** A compositional technique where musical elements (pitch, rhythm, dynamics) are ordered into fixed series or rows.
*   **Indeterminacy:** A compositional approach that incorporates chance or performer choice into the creation or performance of a piece.
*   **Minimalism:** A style using repetitive patterns, steady pulses, and consonant harmony, often with gradual transformation.
*   **Spectral Music:** A compositional technique that uses the acoustic properties of sound (the harmonic spectrum) as the basis for harmony and orchestration.
*   **New Complexity:** A contemporary style noted for its highly intricate and demanding musical notation.
*   **Historicism:** The use of historical materials, styles, or techniques from earlier musical periods in new compositions.
*   **Polystylism:** An eclectic compositional approach that combines multiple, often contrasting, musical styles.
*   **Post-minimalism:** A broad category of music that follows minimalism, often incorporating its techniques with other influences like tonality or world music.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes a debate over the definition of **polystylism**, with some authors equating it with eclecticism and others making a sharp distinction. The very definition and boundaries of "contemporary classical music" are fluid, as the field continually absorbs new influences and technologies, leading to ongoing discussions about its scope and future direction.
