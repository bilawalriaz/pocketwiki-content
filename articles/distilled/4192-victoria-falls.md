# Victoria Falls

## Overview
Victoria Falls is one of the world's largest waterfalls, located on the Zambezi River between Zambia and Zimbabwe. Known locally as Mosi-oa-Tunya ("the smoke that thunders"), it is a major tourist destination and a site of significant biodiversity, though its character is now threatened by climate change-induced variations in water flow.

## Timeline
- **c. 200 million years ago** — Basalt plateau forming the falls' foundation is created during the Jurassic period.
- **c. 2 million years ago** — Uplift blocks the Zambezi's southward drainage, leading to the formation of Lake Makgadikgadi.
- **c. 20,000 years BP** — Lake Makgadikgadi overflows eastward, beginning to cut the Batoka Gorge.
- **1715** — A map by Nicolas de Fer shows the falls in the correct position.
- **16 November 1855** — Scottish missionary David Livingstone becomes the first recorded European to view the falls.
- **1860** — Livingstone returns for a detailed study with John Kirk.
- **1905** — The Victoria Falls Bridge opens, initiating major tourism.
- **1964** — Northern Rhodesia becomes the independent state of Zambia.
- **1980** — Zimbabwe's internationally recognized independence brings peace and renewed tourism.
- **2019** — Unusually low rainfall reduces the falls to their lowest flow in a century.

## Body

### Name and Size
The falls are named after Queen Victoria by David Livingstone, but the Lozi name Mosi-oa-Tunya remains in common use. Classified as the largest waterfall by combined width (1,708 m) and height (108 m), it forms the world's largest sheet of falling water. The river plunges into a transverse chasm called the First Gorge, with the entire volume exiting through a 110 m wide gap.

### Formation and Gorges
The falls formed where the Zambezi River drops along a fracture zone in a basalt plateau. The river's course through a series of zigzagging gorges reflects structural control along joints and faults. The First Gorge is followed by the Second Gorge (spanned by the Victoria Falls Bridge), the Third Gorge (containing a power station), and several others, with the Songwe Gorge being the deepest at 140 m.

### History and Tourism
Local knowledge of the falls predates European contact, with early Stone Age tools found at nearby sites. European settlement began around 1900, driven by Cecil Rhodes' British South Africa Company. The 1905 railway bridge made the falls accessible, spurring tourism. Political instability during the Rhodesian Bush War (1972–1980) reduced visitors, but peace in Zimbabwe led to a resurgence. By the late 1990s, annual visitors approached 400,000. Tourism is a major industry, with activities like whitewater rafting and bungee jumping.

### Natural Environment
The area is rich in biodiversity. The spray from the falls nurtures a unique rainforest with rare plants. The surrounding national parks—Mosi-oa-Tunya in Zambia and Victoria Falls in Zimbabwe—support wildlife including elephants, buffalo, giraffes, and rhinos. The falls act as a barrier to fish migration, with 84 species above and 39 below. Climate change is a growing concern; rising temperatures and more frequent droughts are causing significant water flow variability, threatening the falls' aesthetics and ecosystem.

## Terms
- **Basalt**: A dark, fine-grained volcanic rock that forms the plateau over which the Zambezi flows.
- **Gorge**: A narrow, steep-sided valley formed by river erosion.
- **First Gorge**: The initial chasm into which the Zambezi River plunges at Victoria Falls.
- **Mosi-oa-Tunya**: The Lozi language name for Victoria Falls, meaning "the smoke that thunders."
- **Paleolake**: An ancient lake, such as Lake Makgadikgadi, that has since dried up or significantly reduced.
- **Riparian zone**: The interface between land and a river or stream, characterized by unique vegetation.
- **Sheet of falling water**: A waterfall where the water flows over a relatively uniform, wide ledge.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise timing of the falls' formation and the number of past fall positions are subjects of geological study.
- The impact of climate change on the falls' long-term viability and World Heritage status is a major concern, sparking debate between tourism industries and environmentalists.
- The extent to which historical figures like the Portuguese priest Gonçalo da Silveira may have seen the falls before Livingstone is debated.
