# Heart of Darkness

## Overview
*Heart of Darkness* (1899) is a novella by Joseph Conrad that critiques European colonialism through the journey of sailor Charles Marlow up the Congo River to find the enigmatic ivory trader Kurtz. It remains a foundational text for examining imperialism, morality, and the psychological effects of power, though its portrayal of Africa has sparked significant scholarly debate.

## Timeline
- **1890** — Joseph Conrad serves as a steamer captain on the Congo River for a Belgian trading company.
- **1899** — *Heart of Darkness* is first published as a three-part serial in *Blackwood's Magazine*.
- **1902** — Published as a single volume in the collection *Youth, A Narrative; and Two Other Stories*.
- **1925** — T. S. Eliot quotes the novella's final words of Kurtz as the epigraph for his poem "The Hollow Men."
- **1958** — Chinua Achebe publishes *Things Fall Apart*, a direct literary response to Conrad's work.
- **1975** — Chinua Achebe delivers his lecture "An Image of Africa," criticizing the novella as racist.
- **1979** — Francis Ford Coppola's film *Apocalypse Now*, inspired by the novella, is released.
- **1998** — The Modern Library ranks *Heart of Darkness* 67th on its list of the 100 best English-language novels of the 20th century.

## Body

### Composition and Publication
Conrad drew directly from his 1890 experience commanding a steamer on the Congo River, a journey that inspired Marlow's narrative. He began writing the novella eight years later, describing it as "a wild story." It was first serialized in 1899 for the 1000th edition of *Blackwood's Magazine* and later collected in 1902. The character of Kurtz is a composite, with literary critics proposing numerous real-life prototypes, including Belgian agent Georges-Antoine Klein and figures from the Emin Pasha Relief Expedition.

### Plot and Themes
The story is framed by Marlow recounting his voyage to his listeners on the Thames. Hired by a Belgian ivory trading company, Marlow travels upriver into the Congo Free State—a private colony of King Leopold II—to retrieve Kurtz, a brilliant but rogue agent. The journey exposes the brutal reality of colonial exploitation, symbolized by the "dismal and insipid" Outer Station and its dying African laborers. Marlow's fascination with Kurtz grows, culminating in their meeting at the Inner Station, where Kurtz has been deified by local people and rules through terror, evidenced by severed heads on posts. Kurtz's final words, "The horror! The horror!", encapsulate the moral abyss Marlow witnesses. The novella parallels the "darkness" of the African interior with the hypocrisy of "civilised" Europe, questioning the moral superiority of the colonizers.

### Critical Reception and Debate
The novella was not a major success during Conrad's lifetime but became a standard academic text by the 1960s. Its most famous critique came from Chinua Achebe, who argued it dehumanizes Africans and reduces Africa to a mere backdrop for European psychology. Defenders contend that Conrad, through Marlow's perspective, condemns colonial brutality and that the narrative voice should not be conflated with the author's own views. Scholars like Peter Firchow argue the text does not claim European biological superiority. The debate continues, with some, like Adam Hochschild, emphasizing the novella's accurate depiction of colonial horrors in the Congo Free State.

## Terms
- **Congo Free State**: The private colony of Belgium's King Leopold II, the setting for the novella's African interior.
- **Ivory trade**: The economic engine driving the colonial presence in the Congo, central to Kurtz's activities.
- **Steamer**: The riverboat Marlow commands, essential for travel and a symbol of technology in the wilderness.
- **"Going native"**: A colonial term for a European who adopts the customs of indigenous people, as Kurtz is accused of doing.
- **Frame narrative**: A story within a story; Marlow's tale is told to listeners aboard a ship on the Thames.
- **Imperialism**: The policy of extending a country's power and influence through colonization, the central target of Conrad's critique.
- **Postcolonial criticism**: A field of study examining the cultural legacy of colonialism, which includes Achebe's famous critique of the novella.

## Debates and Open Questions
The central debate concerns whether *Heart of Darkness* is a profound anti-colonial critique or a racist text. Chinua Achebe's 1975 lecture argued it dehumanizes Africans and uses Africa as a mere symbol for European decay. Critics of Achebe's view, such as Cedric Watts, contend that Conrad's narrative voice (Marlow) condemns colonial brutality and that the novella's ambiguity is its strength. A related question is whether the novella's psychological focus, as noted by Adam Hochschild, overshadows its accurate depiction of the historical atrocities committed in the Congo Free State under Leopold II.
