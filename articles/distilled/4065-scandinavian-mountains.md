# Scandinavian Mountains

## Overview
The Scandinavian Mountains, or Scandes, are a major mountain range running along the Scandinavian Peninsula. They are geologically young and high, forming the dramatic western edge of Norway with its fjords and acting as a climatic barrier between maritime Norway and continental Sweden. Their formation and current topography are the result of ancient mountain-building, more recent tectonic uplift, and extensive glacial erosion.

## Timeline
- **c. 53 million years ago** — Possible early uplift related to the Iceland plume during the rifting of Greenland and Scandinavia.
- **Silurian-Devonian periods** — The Caledonian orogeny stacks nappes of rock, forming the ancient Caledonian Mountains.
- **Mesozoic & Oligocene** — A two-stage model proposes initial uplift of the Scandinavian Mountains in South Norway.
- **Early Pliocene** — The Hardangervidda plateau uplifts from sea level to its present altitude.
- **Quaternary period (last 2.75 million years)** — Glaciations begin, likely starting in the mountains. Ice caps cover the range for ~50% of this time.
- **Last Glacial Maximum (c. 20,000 years ago)** — The entire range is covered by the Fennoscandian Ice Sheet.
- **22,000–17,000 years ago** — The ice sheet begins to recede, becoming concentrated in two main centres in the mountains.
- **c. 10,000 years ago** — The two ice centres become disconnected.
- **c. 9,700 years ago** — The last remnant of the ice sheet disappears from the Sarek Mountains.

## Body

### Orography and Topography
The highest summits are concentrated in South Norway between Stavanger and Trondheim, with many peaks over 1,300 m and some over 2,000 m. The highest peak in mainland Northern Europe is Galdhøpiggen (2,469 m) in Norway. The southern mountains are broader, featuring a series of stepped plateaux and undulating surfaces. To the east, the main range borders a lower, less dissected belt of mountains in Sweden called the *förfjäll* ('fore-fell'). Most of the range lacks classic "alpine topography"; features like cirques are found at various altitudes, not just the highest points.

### Climate, Permafrost, and Glaciers
The mountains create a sharp climate divide: a wet, maritime climate on the Norwegian coast and a drier, continental climate in the rain shadow of Sweden. This moisture, combined with a northerly location, has fostered the formation of many glaciers and ice fields. Mountain permafrost (permanently frozen ground) exists at high altitudes, with its lower limit decreasing from west to east due to increasing continentality. In contrast, the glaciation limit (snow line) increases from west to east because it is more dependent on precipitation.

### Geology and Origin
The bedrock is primarily composed of rocks from the Caledonian orogeny, which formed when the ancient continents of Laurentia and Baltica collided, creating a Himalayan-sized mountain range. These rocks were thrust over much older Precambrian rocks. However, the modern Scandinavian Mountains are not the eroded remnants of the Caledonides; they are a separate, younger topographic feature. Their origin is debated, but they are an elevated passive continental margin, uplifted mainly during the Cenozoic era by processes like lithospheric folding, isostatic rebound from erosion, or mantle dynamics.

### Quaternary Geology and Glaciation
The landscape has been profoundly shaped by repeated glaciations. Glacial erosion carved the fjords in the west and straightened valleys along tectonic fractures. The ice sheet that covered Fennoscandia during the Last Glacial Maximum grew from mountain-centred ice caps. As it retreated, it left behind extensive glacial deposits like till, moraines, and eskers. The final remnants of the ice sheet persisted in the Scandinavian Mountains until about 9,700 years ago.

## Terms
- **Caledonian orogeny:** A mountain-building event in the Paleozoic era caused by the collision of ancient continents.
- **Nappe (skollor):** A large, sheet-like body of rock that has been moved by thrust faulting.
- **Förfjäll:** The lower, less dissected belt of mountains east of the main Scandinavian range.
- **Paleic surface:** A pre-glacial, uplifted erosion surface in Norway.
- **Strandflat:** A coastal plain feature along Norway; its relationship to mountain uplift is debated.
- **Permafrost:** Ground that remains frozen for at least two consecutive years.
- **Glaciation limit (snow line):** The altitude above which snow accumulates more than it melts, allowing glaciers to form.
- **Isostatic rebound:** The rise of land masses after the removal of the weight of ice sheets or other loads.
- **Inselberg:** An isolated hill or mountain rising abruptly from a plain.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The primary mechanism for the Cenozoic uplift of the Scandinavian Mountains is not settled. Proposed causes include lithospheric folding, isostatic response to erosion, and mantle plume activity.
- The origin and relationship of the coastal *strandflat* to the uplift of the mountains are points of disagreement among geologists.
- The exact timing and details of the various uplift phases across the range (e.g., the relative timing in the north vs. south) are still researched.
