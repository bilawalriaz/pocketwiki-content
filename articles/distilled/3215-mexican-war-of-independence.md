# Mexican War of Independence

## Overview
The Mexican War of Independence (1810–1821) was a complex political and military process that ended Spanish colonial rule in New Spain. It was not a single event but a series of regional insurgencies and political shifts, culminating in the creation of the First Mexican Empire. The conflict was driven by a crisis of legitimacy following Napoleon's invasion of Spain, deep-seated social and racial inequalities, and the competing ambitions of different social groups.

## Timeline
- **16 September 1810** — Miguel Hidalgo issues the Cry of Dolores, launching the first major insurgency.
- **July 1811** — Hidalgo is captured and executed by royalist forces.
- **1813** — José María Morelos convenes the Congress of Chilpancingo, which formally declares independence.
- **5 November 1815** — Morelos is captured and executed; conventional warfare ends.
- **1815–1820** — Vicente Guerrero leads a guerrilla insurgency in southern Mexico during a period of military stalemate.
- **24 February 1821** — Agustín de Iturbide and Vicente Guerrero proclaim the Plan of Iguala, uniting royalists and insurgents.
- **24 August 1821** — The Treaty of Córdoba is signed, recognizing Mexican independence.
- **27 September 1821** — Iturbide's Army of the Three Guarantees enters Mexico City.
- **28 September 1821** — Independence of the Mexican Empire is officially proclaimed.
- **1822** — Iturbide is declared Emperor of Mexico.
- **1823** — Iturbide's empire is overthrown; a federal republic is declared.
- **28 December 1836** — Spain formally recognizes the independence of Mexico.

## Body

### Causes and Early Challenges
The independence movement was fueled by long-standing tensions. The Bourbon Reforms (mid-18th century) increased Spanish control and revenue extraction, alienating American-born elites (*criollos*). A rigid racial hierarchy placed Spanish-born *peninsulares* at the top, blocking *criollos* from the highest offices. Religious and cultural divides existed between the ruling elite and the broader population. While earlier revolts were local and isolated, Napoleon's 1808 invasion of Spain created a crisis of legitimacy. This led to the formation of governing juntas and, in New Spain, a coup against the viceroy, radicalizing both *criollos* and *peninsulares*.

### The Hidalgo Revolt (1810–1811)
The insurgency began with Miguel Hidalgo, a parish priest, who rallied a massive but undisciplined following of peasants and mixed-race Mexicans. His movement was characterized by its religious symbolism, notably the banner of the Virgin of Guadalupe. Despite early victories, Hidalgo's forces lacked organization and strategy. His retreat from Mexico City after the Battle of Monte de las Cruces is considered a critical error. He was captured in 1811, defrocked, and executed. The royalist response was brutal, aimed at crushing the insurgency and deterring future rebellion.

### The Morelos Insurgency (1811–1815)
Leadership passed to the mixed-race priest José María Morelos, who organized a more disciplined force and articulated clear political goals. In 1813, his Congress of Chilpancingo issued the *Sentimientos de la Nación*, which declared independence, called for the abolition of slavery and racial castes, and established Catholicism as the state religion. Morelos was captured and executed in 1815, after which the insurgency fragmented into guerrilla warfare.

### Stalemate and the Plan of Iguala (1815–1821)
Vicente Guerrero emerged as the key insurgent leader, maintaining a guerrilla campaign in southern Mexico. The conflict reached a stalemate. The decisive shift came in 1820 when Spanish liberals forced King Ferdinand VII to reinstate the liberal Constitution of 1812. Conservative *criollos* in New Spain, including royalist officer Agustín de Iturbide, saw independence as a way to preserve their privileges against the liberal Spanish regime. Iturbide negotiated with Guerrero, and together they issued the Plan of Iguala (1821). This plan guaranteed independence, the Catholic faith, and equality for all races, uniting former enemies into the Army of the Three Guarantees.

### Independence and Aftermath
The Plan of Iguala rapidly gained support, leading to the collapse of royal government. The Treaty of Córdoba (1821) was signed, and Iturbide's forces entered Mexico City. Iturbide was later declared Emperor of Mexico, but his First Mexican Empire was short-lived, collapsing in 1823. Mexico became a federal republic. Spain made several attempts to reconquer Mexico, the last major one in 1829, before finally recognizing its independence in 1836.

## Terms
- **Criollos**: American-born people of full Spanish descent. They resented their exclusion from the highest political offices held by *peninsulares*.
- **Peninsulares**: People born in the Iberian Peninsula (Spain/Portugal) who held the top positions in the colonial government and society.
- **Plan of Iguala**: The 1821 agreement between Iturbide and Guerrero that united royalists and insurgents, outlining the three guarantees of independence, Catholicism, and racial equality.
- **Army of the Three Guarantees**: The unified military force created under Iturbide to enforce the Plan of Iguala.
- **Sentimientos de la Nación**: The 1813 document drafted by Morelos, which formally declared independence and outlined a vision for a sovereign, egalitarian Mexico.
- **Grito de Dolores**: The "Cry of Dolores," Hidalgo's 1810 call to arms that initiated the war.
- **Fuero**: A set of special legal privileges granted to certain groups, such as the clergy or military, exempting them from the jurisdiction of ordinary courts.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The role of popular versus elite motivations: Was the conflict primarily a *criollo* power struggle or a genuine social revolution driven by the masses?
- The significance of the 1810–1815 phase: Historians debate whether Hidalgo and Morelos's uprisings were a "popular revolution" or a "civil war" within the colonial structure.
- The legacy of the Plan of Iguala: Some historians view it as a pragmatic compromise that secured independence, while others argue it betrayed the more radical social goals of earlier insurgents like Morelos by preserving elite privileges.
