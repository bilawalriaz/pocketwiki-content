# Bell

## Overview
A bell is a directly struck idiophone percussion instrument, typically a hollow cup that vibrates to produce a single strong tone. Bells have been used for millennia across cultures for religious, musical, timekeeping, and signaling purposes, evolving from simple pottery forms to complex, harmonically tuned bronze instruments. Their study is known as campanology.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BCE** — Earliest archaeological evidence of bells (pottery clapper-bells) from the Yangshao culture in Neolithic China.
- **c. 2000 BCE** — First metal bells appear in China (Taosi and Erlitou sites).
- **c. 1600–1050 BCE** — During the Shang dynasty, bells are relegated to subservient functions like horse gear.
- **13th century BCE** — Chinese bells weighing over 150 kg are cast.
- **1000 BCE** — First bells appear in West Asia.
- **7th century BCE** — Assyrian bells are about 4 inches high.
- **1st–2nd century CE** — Roman bells are about 8 inches high.
- **5th century CE** — Small handbells made from folded and riveted sheet iron appear in the Celtic Church of Britain.
- **1079 CE** — The earliest dated iron bell is manufactured in Hubei Province, China.
- **10th century CE** — European bells are no higher than 2 feet.
- **1484** — The Great Bell of Dhammazedi, possibly the largest ever made (c. 300 tonnes), is cast in Burma.
- **1497** — The Maria Gloriosa, the world's largest medieval free-swinging bell, is cast in Erfurt, Germany.
- **1520** — The Sigismund bell is cast in Kraków, Poland.
- **1680** — Great Tom is cast in Oxford, England.
- **1737** — The Tsar Bell, the largest bell still in existence (160 tonnes), breaks.
- **1922** — The St. Petersglocke is cast in Cologne, Germany.
- **1978** — A complete set of 65 ancient Chinese Zhong bells is found in the tomb of Marquis Yi.
- **1997** — Muffled bells are used at the funeral of Diana, Princess of Wales.
- **2006** — The Gotenba Bell, the largest functioning swinging bell (36,200 kg), is cast.
- **2012** — The Olympic Bell, the largest harmonically-tuned bell, is cast for the London Olympics.
- **2016** — The largest bell of the People's Salvation Cathedral is cast, becoming the largest free-swinging church bell.

## Body

### Construction and Tuning
Bells are most commonly cast from bell metal, a bronze alloy of about 80% copper and 20% tin, chosen for its resonant properties. The process, called bellfounding, involves creating a two-part mould from loam and pouring molten metal into it. The tone of a bell is primarily determined by its shape. A well-tuned bell has harmonics in harmony with its strike note, a process known as harmonic tuning. The main partials (harmonics) of a tuned bell include the hum note, strike tone, tierce (minor third), quint (fifth), and nominal (octave above). Tuning is achieved by removing metal from specific parts of the bell, historically with tuning forks and now with electronic tuners. An experimental "major third bell" design was introduced in the 1980s but found little favor.

### Styles of Ringing and Use
Bells are rung in various ways. They can be fixed ("hung dead") and struck by a hammer, or mounted to swing. In English full-circle ringing, a bell is swung through a complete circle by a ringer. A carillon is a musical instrument of at least 23 tuned bells played via a keyboard. In Eastern traditions, large temple bells are often struck from the outside with a beam. Bells are integral to clock towers, with sequences like the Westminster Quarters marking time. Historically, they have called communities to religious services, commemorated events, and symbolized peace and freedom.

### Musical and Cultural Roles
Beyond signaling, bells are musical instruments. Ancient Chinese *bianzhong* (chime bells) were polyphonic; a set from 433 BCE could produce a 12-tone scale. Other examples include the Kyrgyz *konguro'o*, the Lithuanian *skrabalai* (wooden bells), and tubular bells or chimes. Bells have diverse cultural roles, from farm bells calling workers to the Scottish "dead bell" rung at funerals. Notable bells include the Liberty Bell (USA), Big Ben (UK), and the Tsar Bell (Russia).

## Terms
- **Idiophone**: An instrument that creates sound primarily by the instrument as a whole vibrating, without the use of strings or membranes.
- **Bell metal**: A bronze alloy, typically about 80% copper and 20% tin, used for casting bells due to its resonant properties.
- **Campanology**: The study of bells.
- **Clapper**: An internal pendulum that strikes the inside of a bell to produce sound.
- **Harmonic tuning**: The process of shaping a bell so its strongest harmonics are in harmony with each other and the strike note.
- **Strike note**: The primary perceived pitch of a bell when struck.
- **Carillon**: A musical instrument consisting of at least 23 cast bronze bells, tuned to a common scale and played via a keyboard.
- **Full-circle ringing**: A style of ringing where a bell is swung through a complete circle, controlled by a ringer.
- **Sound bow**: The thickest part of a bell, where the clapper or hammer strikes.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that the etymological relationship between "bell" and the Old English word *bellan* ("to roar") is "popularly but not certainly" accepted. The concept of the "major third bell," proposed in the 1980s to better suit Western major-key music, "has found little favour," and most bells cast today remain minor third bells. The classification of the largest "free-swinging" bell is debated, as some large bells (like the Gotenba Bell) swing around their center of gravity, which some consider more akin to turning.
