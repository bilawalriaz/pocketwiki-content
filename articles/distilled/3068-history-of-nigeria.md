# History of Nigeria

## Overview
Nigeria's history spans from ancient prehistoric settlements to its current status as Africa's most populous nation and a major oil producer. Its trajectory is defined by the rise and fall of powerful pre-colonial kingdoms, the imposition of arbitrary colonial borders, a tumultuous post-independence struggle with ethnic and religious tensions, and a recent, fragile transition to democracy. Understanding Nigeria is key to understanding West African history, the legacy of colonialism, and the challenges of nation-building in a diverse, resource-rich state.

## Timeline
- **c. 1500 BC** — Nok culture emerges, known for terracotta sculptures and early iron smelting.
- **11th century** — Islam arrives in the north via the Kanem-Bornu Empire and Hausa states.
- **1464–1591** — Songhai Empire controls parts of northern Nigeria.
- **1804** — Usman dan Fodio leads a jihad, establishing the Sokoto Caliphate.
- **1861** — Britain annexes Lagos, beginning formal colonial expansion.
- **1885** — Berlin Conference formalizes European spheres of influence in Africa.
- **1914** — British colonies of Northern and Southern Nigeria are amalgamated.
- **1960** — Nigeria gains independence from the United Kingdom.
- **1967–1970** — Nigerian Civil War (Biafran War) results in massive humanitarian loss.
- **1999** — Transition to the Fourth Republic, ending decades of military rule.
- **2015** — Muhammadu Buhari wins presidency, marking the first democratic transfer of power from one party to another.
- **2023** — Bola Tinubu is inaugurated as president after a disputed election.

## Body

### Prehistory and Early Civilizations
Human settlement in Nigeria dates back to at least 9000 BC. The Nok culture (c. 1500 BC–1 BC) is notable for its terracotta art and possible independent development of iron smelting. Advanced societies followed, including the Ife Empire (c. 1200–1420), famed for its bronze sculptures and urban planning, and the Oyo Empire, which dominated the southwest with its cavalry. In the southeast, the Kingdom of Nri (from c. 10th century) was a theocratic state central to Igbo culture. Islam spread from the 11th century, influencing the Hausa states and the Kanem-Bornu Empire.

### The Slave Trade and Early European Contact
Portuguese traders arrived around 1472, initiating trade in ivory, pepper, and later, enslaved people. Coastal city-states like Calabar, Badagry, and Bonny became major slave-trading hubs. The transatlantic slave trade, which intensified from the 16th century, profoundly disrupted societies and economies. The Sokoto Caliphate, founded in 1804, also maintained a large internal slave population.

### British Colonial Rule
Britain's interest shifted to palm oil after abolishing the slave trade in 1807. Following the 1885 Berlin Conference, Britain expanded inland, defeating the Sokoto Caliphate by 1903. In 1914, Lord Lugard amalgamated the Northern and Southern Protectorates into a single colony, Nigeria, primarily for administrative efficiency. The British employed "indirect rule" in the north, preserving emirate structures, while imposing "warrant chiefs" in the southeast, which sparked the 1929 Women's War. Colonial policies created regional imbalances in education and infrastructure that would fuel post-independence tensions.

### Independence, Civil War, and Military Rule
Nigeria gained independence in 1960 as a federation of three regions. Political parties formed along ethnic lines, leading to instability. A 1966 coup and subsequent counter-coup triggered pogroms against the Igbo people, leading to the secession of the Eastern Region as the Republic of Biafra. The ensuing Biafran War (1967–1970) caused an estimated 1–3 million deaths, mostly from hunger. After the war, Nigeria experienced repeated military coups and dictatorships, including those of Gowon, Murtala Muhammed, and Babangida. The regime of Sani Abacha (1993–1998) was marked by extreme repression and corruption.

### The Fourth Republic and Contemporary Nigeria
Following Abacha's death, a transition to democracy led to the Fourth Republic in 1999. This era has seen peaceful transfers of power (2007, 2010, 2015, 2023) but also persistent challenges: corruption, ethnic and religious violence, and the Boko Haram insurgency (gaining global attention in 2014). The economy is heavily dependent on oil, discovered in 1956, which has fueled growth but also environmental damage in the Niger Delta and corruption. Recent years have seen growth in technology and cultural exports like music and film, but also severe economic hardship, high inflation, and a kidnapping crisis.

## Terms
*   **Amalgamation:** The 1914 merging of the Northern and Southern Protectorates by the British to form the single colony of Nigeria.
*   **Indirect Rule:** A British colonial policy of governing through existing traditional authorities (like emirs in the north) rather than replacing them with direct colonial administration.
*   **Sokoto Caliphate:** An Islamic state founded in 1804 by Usman dan Fodio after a jihad, which dominated much of northern Nigeria until its defeat by the British in 1903.
*   **Biafra:** The short-lived secessionist state in southeastern Nigeria whose declaration of independence in 1967 sparked the Nigerian Civil War.
*   **Fourth Republic:** The current republican government of Nigeria, established in 1999 after the end of military rule.
*   **Boko Haram:** An Islamist insurgent group active in northeastern Nigeria since 2009, known for its 2014 kidnapping of schoolgirls in Chibok.
*   **Nollywood:** Nigeria's prolific film industry, one of the largest in the world by volume of films produced.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Colonial Legacy:** Scholars debate whether British rule, with its creation of an artificial state and favoritism toward certain regions, is the primary cause of Nigeria's post-independence ethnic conflicts and institutional weaknesses.
*   **Ethnic Federalism:** The effectiveness of Nigeria's federal structure in managing its immense diversity is constantly questioned. Proposals for restructuring or devolution of power remain contentious.
*   **Resource Curse:** There is ongoing debate over whether Nigeria's vast oil wealth has been a blessing or a curse, fueling corruption, environmental degradation, and conflict rather than sustainable development.
*   **Democratic Consolidation:** Despite peaceful elections, concerns persist about electoral transparency, institutional corruption, and the military's lingering influence, questioning the depth of Nigeria's democratic consolidation.
