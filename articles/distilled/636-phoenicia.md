# Phoenicia

## Overview  
The Phoenicians were an ancient Semitic-speaking people who inhabited independent city-states along the Levantine coast (modern Lebanon) from roughly the 12th century BC onward. Renowned for seafaring and trade, they established a vast maritime network connecting Egypt, Mesopotamia, and the Mediterranean world, founding colonies such as Carthage. Their most enduring legacy is the alphabet—a simplified script derived from earlier writing systems—that became the ancestor of Greek, Latin, and other scripts.

## Timeline  
- **c. 1200 BC** — Late Bronze Age collapse; Phoenician city-states survive and begin expanding
- **c. 1050 BC** — Phoenician alphabet developed
- **c. 969–936 BC** — Reign of Hiram I of Tyre; height of Tyrian power
- **814 BC** — Traditional foundation date of Carthage
- **744 BC** — Assyrian annexation of northern Phoenicia begins
- **539 BC** — Persian conquest; Phoenicia becomes part of Achaemenid Empire
- **332 BC** — Alexander the Great conquers Phoenicia
- **146 BC** — Roman destruction of Carthage ends Punic dominance

## Body  

### Origins and Identity  
The Phoenicians emerged from the Canaanite culture of the Bronze Age Levant, with roots traceable to the Ghassulian chalcolithic culture. They were never a unified nation but rather a collection of independent city-states sharing language and customs. The term “Phoenician” is a Greek exonym; native inscriptions refer to them as Canaanites. Modern scholarship debates whether they were truly distinct from other Canaanite groups, with many viewing the distinction as artificial post-c. 1200 BC.

### Political Organization  
Phoenician society was organized into autonomous city-states like Tyre, Sidon, and Byblos. Kingship was common, though powerful merchant families often governed via oligarchies. By the 6th century BC, some cities adopted republican institutions, including elected judges called *sufetes*. There is no evidence of a shared national identity or centralized rule.

### Economy and Trade  
Lacking natural resources beyond cedar wood, the Phoenicians built an economy based on maritime trade and industry. They acted as intermediaries between major civilizations, exporting goods like purple dye, glassware, and metalwork. Their trade extended from Cyprus and Sardinia to the Iberian Peninsula and North Africa. Silver from Spain and tin from Britain were key imports. Colonies like Carthage grew into major powers, controlling western Mediterranean trade routes.

### Maritime Innovation  
Phoenician shipbuilders pioneered techniques such as the mortise-and-tenon joint (*Phoenician joint*) for hull construction, enhancing durability. They may have introduced the bireme and influenced early trireme design. The amphora, a standardized container for transporting liquids, originated in Phoenicia. They also advanced cartography and established artificial harbors (*cothons*).

### Culture and Religion  
Phoenician religion centered on a pantheon led by El and Baal, with local manifestations like Melqart and Astarte. Rituals included votive offerings and communal feasts through institutions called *marzeh*. Practices such as child sacrifice and temple prostitution are debated among scholars. Art was ornamental, blending Egyptian, Greek, and Assyrian influences. Women participated in public life and religious ceremonies, with some attaining political power.

### Language and Alphabet  
The Phoenician language belonged to the Canaanite branch of Northwest Semitic. Around 1050 BC, they developed an abjad of 22 consonants—the Phoenician alphabet—which spread through trade networks. The Greeks adapted it into the first true alphabet, leading to Latin, Cyrillic, and others. Punic, its descendant spoken in Carthage, persisted until late antiquity.

### Decline and Legacy  
Phoenician independence eroded under successive empires: Assyrian, Babylonian, and Persian. Alexander the Great’s conquest in 332 BC marked the end of native autonomy. Carthage’s destruction in 146 BC ended Punic influence. Yet their cultural impact endured—especially the alphabet, which revolutionized literacy and communication across Eurasia.

## Terms  
- **Abjad**: A writing system representing consonants only.  
- **Cothon**: An artificial harbor used by Phoenicians and Carthaginians.  
- **Punic**: The later stage of the Phoenician language spoken in Carthage.  
- **Sufetes**: Elected magistrates in some Phoenician city-states, akin to Roman consuls.  
- **Tophet**: A site associated with Phoenician child sacrifice, debated by scholars.  
- **Tyrian purple**: A rare dye extracted from murex snails, symbolizing wealth and status.  
- **Phoenician joint**: A shipbuilding technique using interlocking wooden pegs.  
- **Marzeh**: A religious institution fostering social cohesion through ritual meals.  
- **Exonym**: A name given by outsiders, e.g., “Phoenician” vs. self-designation “Canaanite.”  
- **Hiram I**: King of Tyre (c. 969–936 BC), noted for trade and building projects.

## Debates and Open Questions  
Scholars debate whether the Phoenicians constituted a distinct people or were simply Canaanites in specific coastal cities. The extent of practices like child sacrifice remains contested. The origins of the trireme and the precise route of alphabet transmission to Greece are also disputed. Genetic studies reveal continuity with Bronze Age populations but highlight admixture events during the Iron Age and later periods. The role of Phoenician women in politics and religion continues to be explored through limited textual and archaeological evidence.
