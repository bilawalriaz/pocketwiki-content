# Grand Principality of Moscow

## Overview
The Grand Principality of Moscow, or Muscovy, was a late medieval Russian monarchy that evolved from a small appanage principality in the 13th century into the centralized Russian state. Its rise was driven by strategic alliances, control of tribute collection, and the backing of the Russian Orthodox Church, culminating in the formation of the Tsardom of Russia in 1547.

## Timeline

- **1263** — Daniel, son of Alexander Nevsky, receives Moscow as an appanage.
- **c. 1300** — Moscow becomes a leading principality within Vladimir.
- **1325** — Ivan I becomes prince; later secures grand princely title and tribute control.
- **1326** — Russian Orthodox Church seat moves from Vladimir to Moscow.

- **1505** — Vasily III completes annexation of remaining appanages.
- **1547** — Ivan IV crowned first Russian tsar.

## Body

### Origins and Early Rise
Moscow grew in importance in the late 12th century as a fortified stronghold. It became a separate principality when Daniel (r. 1263–1303) received it as an appanage. By the late 13th century, Moscow was a leading principality alongside Tver. A power struggle began when Mikhail of Tver became grand prince in 1304. Daniel's son Yury (r. 1303–1325) contested this, eventually being made grand prince in 1318 by the Golden Horde khan.

### Consolidation under Ivan I and Successors
Ivan I (r. 1325–1340) regained the grand princely title and secured the right to collect tribute for the khan, greatly increasing Moscow's wealth. The relocation of the Orthodox Church's seat to Moscow in 1326 established it as the spiritual center. Ivan I defeated Tver and secured the title for his sons, Simeon (r. 1340–1353) and Ivan II (r. 1353–1359). After Ivan II's death, the title was temporarily lost until Dmitry (r. 1359–1389) permanently united the thrones of Vladimir and Moscow. His victory at Kulikovo in 1380 was a milestone against Tatar forces.

### Expansion and Independence
As the Golden Horde declined, its hegemony was challenged. Vasily I (r. 1389–1425) expanded Moscow's domain but resumed paying tribute after Tatar raids. Vasily II (r. 1425–1462) consolidated control after a civil war, and in 1448, the Russian Church declared autocephaly. Ivan III (r. 1462–1505) absorbed nearly all Russian states, annexing Novgorod in 1478 and adopting the title of sovereign of all Russia. His defeat of the Tatars in 1480 ended Mongol suzerainty. Vasily III (r. 1505–1533) completed the annexation of remaining appanages. His son, Ivan IV, was crowned the first tsar in 1547.

### Government and Society
The political system was dominated by the grand prince, who viewed the country as his personal patrimony. The boyar duma served as an advisory council. The Sudebnik of 1497 introduced uniform law. The state was administered through governors (namestniki) and a growing bureaucracy of state secretaries (dyaki). Taxation included levies on land, property, and commerce. Society was divided between peasants, loyal to the commune, and a warrior nobility serving in the military. The appanage system was gradually reduced by Ivan III and Vasily III.

### Foreign Relations and Religion
Moscow's relationship with the Golden Horde involved tribute payment and resistance. The Horde's decline allowed Moscow to assert independence. The fall of Constantinople in 1453 reinforced Moscow's claim as the Byzantine successor and center of Orthodox Christianity, leading to the "Third Rome" doctrine. Within the Russian Church, a major debate occurred between the Josephites, who supported monastic land ownership, and the non-possessors, who opposed it.

### Economy and Culture
The economy relied on agriculture, internal trade, and external trade in furs. After the Hanseatic League's decline, Ivan III closed its kontor in Novgorod in 1494 to promote direct trade. Culturally, Muscovite Russia was influenced by Slavic and Byzantine elements. A distinct Moscow school of icon painting emerged, led by Andrei Rublev. Architecture evolved, with Italian Renaissance masters like Aristotele Fioravanti contributing to the Moscow Kremlin in the late 15th century. Literature included heroic tales like *The Tale of the Battle with Mamai* and works promoting Moscow's imperial ideology.

## Terms
- **Appanage principality**: A territory granted to a younger son, governed semi-autonomously.
- **Suzerainty**: A relationship where a superior state has control over a subordinate state, which retains internal autonomy.
- **Grand Prince (veliky knyaz)**: The senior ruler among Russian princes, originally the prince of Vladimir.
- **Tribute (dan)**: A tax made by a subordinate state to a superior power.
- **Autocephaly**: The status of a self-governing church independent of external authority.
- **Boyar Duma**: A council of nobles advising the grand prince.
- **Sudebnik**: A legal code; the Sudebnik of 1497 was a key law code issued by Ivan III.
- **Votchina**: An estate held in absolute tenure by a noble family.
- **Pomestye**: A land grant given to a noble in exchange for military service, non-inheritable.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact size and boundaries of the original Moscow principality under Daniel are unknown.
- The role and power of the boyar duma versus the grand prince is debated; some historians argue for a "façade of autocracy" where consultation was significant.
- The extent to which the 1503 church council formally discussed monastic landholding is questioned due to lack of evidence.
- The traditional narrative that the "Judaizers" heresy originated from a Jew arriving in Novgorod in 1471 is rejected by most modern scholars.
