# Great Dividing Range

## Overview
The Great Dividing Range is a vast cordillera system running parallel to Australia's east coast, forming the continent's most significant topographic feature. It acts as the primary watershed for eastern Australia's river systems, separating coastal rivers flowing to the Pacific from inland rivers draining westward. Its formation and presence have profoundly influenced the continent's climate, ecology, and human history.

## Timeline
- **c. 300 million years ago** — Formed during the Carboniferous period when Australia collided with landmasses that are now parts of South America and New Zealand.
- **Pre-1788** — Home to various Aboriginal Australian peoples for tens of thousands of years.
- **1788** — British colonisation begins; the range becomes an obstacle to European exploration and settlement.
- **1813** — Blaxland, Lawson, and Wentworth cross the Blue Mountains using Aboriginal trails, opening inland New South Wales.
- **Late 1830s** — Most fertile adjacent rangelands (e.g., Gippsland, Riverina, Darling Downs) explored and settled.
- **1860s-1870s** — Major railway lines built across the range (e.g., Sydney–Lithgow, Brisbane–Toowoomba).
- **20th Century** — Major water supply projects (e.g., Snowy Mountains Scheme) and road networks developed.
- **2009** — Named a Q150 Icon of Queensland.

## Body

### Geography and Hydrology
The Great Dividing Range is not a single chain but a complex cordillera of ranges, plateaus, and escarpments. Its crest is defined by the watershed boundary between river systems draining east to the coast and those draining west inland. Rivers east of the divide flow into the Pacific Ocean, Tasman Sea, or Bass Strait. Major western drainage basins include the Murray–Darling Basin (flowing to the Great Australian Bight) and the Lake Eyre Basin (an endorheic, or closed, basin). The range is the source of virtually all eastern Australia's water supply, captured in dams and reservoirs like the Snowy Mountains Scheme.

### Climate and Ecology
The range's sharp rise from coastal lowlands significantly affects Australia's climate through orographic precipitation (rain caused by air being forced upward over mountains). It creates a rain shadow on its leeward (western) side and shields southeastern areas from cold Southern Ocean fronts in winter. The Föhn effect, a dry, warming wind, occurs on the leeward slopes in southern New South Wales, increasing fire danger. The range hosts diverse ecosystems, including World Heritage Areas like the Gondwana Rainforests and Wet Tropics of Queensland, and contains all of mainland Australia's alpine areas, including Mount Kosciuszko, the highest point on the continent.

### Human History and Settlement
For Aboriginal peoples, the range was a homeland with established trails. After 1788, it was a major barrier to European expansion. The discovery of a usable route across the Blue Mountains in 1813 was pivotal for inland settlement. Subsequent explorers sought agricultural land, leading to the appropriation of fertile regions like the Liverpool Plains and Darling Downs by the late 1830s. While many areas remain remote, major cities (e.g., Canberra, Toowoomba) and towns are located on the uplands, and numerous highways and railways traverse the range.

### Natural Features and Land Use
The range comprises hundreds of peaks and smaller named ranges (e.g., Blue Mountains, McPherson Ranges). While some areas are rugged, the age and erosion of the range mean most peaks are not extremely steep. Flatter, well-watered tablelands (e.g., Atherton Tableland, Northern Tablelands) are used for agriculture and pastoralism. Rugged areas are often used for forestry or protected within national parks. The eastern escarpment features spectacular waterfalls.

## Terms
- **Cordillera**: A system of parallel mountain ranges.
- **Watershed**: The line of high ground separating different drainage basins.
- **Orographic precipitation**: Rainfall caused when moist air is forced to rise over a mountain range.
- **Rain shadow**: A dry area on the leeward (downwind) side of a mountain range.
- **Föhn effect**: A dry, warm wind that descends the leeward slope of a mountain.
- **Endorheic basin**: A drainage basin that retains water and allows no outflow to other external bodies of water.
- **Tableland**: A large area of relatively level high ground; a plateau.
- **Escarpment**: A long, steep slope or cliff, especially one at the edge of a plateau.
