# West Lake

## Overview
West Lake (Xihu) is a freshwater lake in Hangzhou, China, renowned for its scenic beauty and profound cultural influence. As a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2011, it is recognized as an idealized fusion of human and natural landscapes, having inspired garden designs across East Asia for centuries. Its history of dredging, literary association, and evolution into a major tourist destination reflects broader patterns in Chinese urban culture and statecraft.

## Timeline
- **c. 111** — First known description of the waters (as Wulin River) in the *Book of Han*.
- **591** — Hangzhou's government seat relocated east of the lake; Grand Canal completion makes it a transport hub.
- **822** — Bai Juyi dredges the lake and builds a causeway.
- **1089** — Su Shi orders dredging and constructs the Su Causeway.
- **1129** — Song dynasty capital moves to Hangzhou, boosting the lake's status as a cultural and tourist landmark.
- **1275** — Military patrols appointed to maintain the lake; littering and unauthorized planting forbidden.
- **1508** — Governor Yang Mengying leads major dredging, repairing causeways and creating the Yanggong Causeway.
- **1689** — Qing Emperor Kangxi visits; imperial palace built on Gushan Island.
- **1929** — West Lake Exposition held to showcase Chinese culture and merchandise.
- **2002** — Park walls removed, making the scenic area free to enter.
- **2011** — Designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Body

### Natural Formation and Human Intervention
West Lake originated as a shallow bay of the Qiantang River. Over millennia, silt deposits and the construction of a seawall (c. 25–220 AD) isolated it, forming a freshwater lagoon. Continuous dredging from the Tang dynasty onward prevented it from reverting to a swamp. Key projects by officials like Bai Juyi and Su Shi used silt to build causeways, shaping the lake's iconic layout. This ongoing human management is central to its identity as a cultural landscape.

### From Literary Tradition to Tourism
The lake's fame grew through its association with poets like Bai Juyi and Su Shi, who compared it to the beauty Xizi. During the Southern Song dynasty (1127–1279), when Hangzhou became the capital, it evolved into a major tourist destination. Sightseeing emerged as a popular activity, with standardized routes, tour guides, and entertainment. The concept of the "Ten Scenes of West Lake" was codified, cementing its status as a cultural landmark. This tourism declined under Mongol rule but was revitalized in the Ming and Qing dynasties through imperial patronage and dredging.

### Modern Era and Management
After the 1911 revolution, imperial properties were nationalized and the lakefront integrated into the city as public parks. The 1929 West Lake Exposition promoted Chinese culture globally. Following the Communist takeover in 1949, the lake underwent socialist-era renovations, though some historical sites were damaged during the Cultural Revolution. Since the 1980s, restoration and tourism promotion have intensified. In 2002, the scenic area became free to enter, a policy that boosted the local economy. It is now managed by a dedicated municipal committee focused on preservation.

### Layout and Features
The lake's layout is summarized as "one hill, two pagodas, three islets, four causeways, and five lakes." Gushan is the only natural island. The two pagodas, Leifeng and Baochu, stand on opposite shores. Three artificial islets were created from dredged silt. The four causeways—Bai, Su, Yanggong, and Zhaogong—divide the water into five interconnected sections. The "Ten Scenes of West Lake," each with a poetic name, are iconic views marked by imperial calligraphy.

### Hydrology and Ecology
The lake has a catchment area of 21.22 km² and is maintained at a consistent water level through inflows from the Qiantang River and local streams. Its subtropical climate supports rich vegetation, including 1,369 species of spermatophytes. The four seasonal flowers—peach blossom, lotus, osmanthus, and plum blossom—are integral to its landscape design. The surrounding hills are famed for Longjing tea cultivation, which benefits from the area's unique microclimate.

### Economy and Gastronomy
Tourism is the primary economic driver. The free-access policy increased annual visitor numbers to over 30 million and dramatically grew the area's GDP. Agriculture, particularly Longjing tea production, remains culturally and economically significant. Hangzhou's local cuisine, a branch of Zhejiang cuisine, features dishes using lake fish and lotus, such as West Lake Fish in Vinegar Gravy.

## Terms
- **Causeway:** An embankment or road across water, built here from dredged lake silt.
- **Dredging:** The process of removing silt and sediment from the lake bed to maintain depth and water quality.
- **Ten Scenes of West Lake:** A traditional set of ten iconic views, each with a four-character poetic name, dating to the Song dynasty.
- **UNESCO World Heritage Site:** A location recognized for its outstanding universal cultural or natural value.
- **Longjing Tea:** A renowned variety of Chinese green tea grown in the hills west of the lake.
- **Zhejiang Cuisine:** One of China's eight major culinary traditions, known for its fresh, mellow flavors.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise location of the "Wulin River" mentioned in the *Book of Han* (111 AD) as the first description of West Lake is debated, though it is widely interpreted as referring to the lake.
- The origin of the "Three Ponds Mirroring the Moon" scenic spot is attributed to Su Shi's 11th-century designation of an area to prevent silting, but this is presented as a possible origin.
- The economic and social impact of the 2002 free-access policy is a subject of analysis, with studies noting its role in boosting the local economy but also contributing to extreme crowding during holidays.
