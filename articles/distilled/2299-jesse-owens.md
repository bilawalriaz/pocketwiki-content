# Jesse Owens

## Overview
Jesse Owens was an American track and field athlete whose four gold medals at the 1936 Berlin Olympics made him a global symbol of athletic excellence and a powerful rebuke to Nazi ideology. His career achievements, including setting five world records in a single hour, cemented his legacy as one of the greatest athletes in history, though his post-Olympic life highlighted the racial discrimination he faced in the United States.

## Timeline
- **September 12, 1913** — Born in Oakville, Alabama.
- **1922** — Family moves to Cleveland, Ohio, as part of the Great Migration.
- **May 25, 1935** — Sets five world records and ties another in 45 minutes at the Big Ten Championships.
- **1935-1936** — Wins eight individual NCAA championships while at Ohio State University.
- **August 3-9, 1936** — Wins four gold medals (100m, long jump, 200m, 4x100m relay) at the Berlin Olympics.
- **1936** — Is permanently suspended from amateur competition after refusing to continue a European exhibition tour.
- **1955** — Appointed "Ambassador of Sports" by President Dwight D. Eisenhower.
- **1976** — Awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom.
- **March 31, 1980** — Dies of lung cancer in Tucson, Arizona.
- **1990** — Posthumously awarded the Congressional Gold Medal.

## Body

### Early Life and Collegiate Career
Born the youngest of ten children, Owens moved with his family from Alabama to Cleveland, Ohio, in 1922. His athletic talent was nurtured by his junior high coach, Charles Riley. At Ohio State University, he became known as the "Buckeye Bullet," winning a record eight individual NCAA championships. Despite his athletic success, Owens faced segregation, living off-campus and eating at "blacks-only" establishments while traveling with the team. He did not receive an athletic scholarship and worked part-time jobs to support himself.

### The 1936 Berlin Olympics
Owens's international fame was secured at the 1936 Summer Olympics in Berlin. He won gold medals in the 100 meters, long jump, 200 meters, and 4 × 100-meter relay, setting an Olympic record in each event. His performance was widely seen as a symbolic defeat of Adolf Hitler's myth of Aryan supremacy. While a popular narrative claims Hitler snubbed Owens, historical accounts are mixed; Owens himself stated that Hitler waved to him, but he felt snubbed by U.S. President Franklin D. Roosevelt, who never invited him to the White House.

### Post-Olympic Life and Legacy
After the Olympics, Owens was stripped of his amateur status by the AAU for refusing to continue an exhausting exhibition tour. Despite his global acclaim, he struggled to find work in the segregated United States, taking jobs like a gas station attendant and racing against horses for prize money. He later worked as a sports promoter, public relations agent, and goodwill ambassador for the U.S. government. His legacy is honored through numerous awards, including the Presidential Medal of Freedom, and the Jesse Owens Award, given annually by USA Track & Field.

## Terms
- **Great Migration:** The movement of six million African Americans from the rural Southern United States to the urban Northeast, Midwest, and West between 1910 and 1970.
- **Amateur status:** A requirement, now largely historical, that athletes not accept payment for competition, which was strictly enforced in the early 20th century.
- **Jim Crow:** State and local laws that enforced racial segregation in the Southern United States from the late 19th century until 1965.
- **World record:** The best performance ever officially recorded in a specific athletic event.
- **Olympic record:** The best performance ever recorded in a specific event at an Olympic Games.
- **Congressional Gold Medal:** The highest civilian honor bestowed by the United States Congress.
- **Presidential Medal of Freedom:** The highest civilian honor bestowed by the President of the United States.

## Debates and open questions
The primary historical debate concerns Adolf Hitler's reaction to Owens's victories. While popular culture holds that Hitler deliberately snubbed him, accounts vary. Some contemporary reports and later testimonies suggest Hitler may have offered a congratulatory wave or salute, while others maintain he refused to acknowledge non-German winners. Owens himself gave conflicting statements, at times saying he felt snubbed by President Roosevelt more than by Hitler.
