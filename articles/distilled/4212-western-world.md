# Western world

## Overview
The Western world is a cultural, political, and economic concept referring primarily to nations in Europe, Northern America, and Australasia, with debate over the inclusion of others like Latin America and former Soviet states. Its identity evolved from ancient Greco-Roman roots and Latin Christendom, solidified through historical events like the East–West Schism and colonial expansion, and is defined more by shared influences than fixed geography.

## Timeline
- **c. 8th century BC – AD 476** — Ancient Roman civilization dominates the Mediterranean, contributing law, language, and governance.
- **AD 330** — Constantine establishes Constantinople as the "New Rome," reflecting an East-West divide within the Empire.
- **AD 476** — Traditional fall of the Western Roman Empire, leading to the rise of Germanic kingdoms.
- **AD 800** — Charlemagne crowned Emperor by the Pope, establishing the Holy Roman Empire and reinforcing a Western Latin Christian identity.
- **1054 CE** — The Great Schism formally divides the Western (Catholic) and Eastern (Orthodox) churches.
- **1202–1204** — The Fourth Crusade sacks Constantinople, deepening the schism and weakening the Byzantine Empire.
- **1492** — Christopher Columbus's voyage marks the beginning of widespread European colonization of the Americas.
- **1517** — Martin Luther's Reformation begins, fracturing Western Christendom.
- **1648** — The Peace of Westphalia ends the Thirty Years' War, establishing the modern nation-state system.
- **18th century** — The Enlightenment promotes reason and secularism, shaping modern Western political thought.
- **1947–1991** — The Cold War defines "the West" (First World) in opposition to the Soviet-led Eastern Bloc (Second World).

## Body

### Origins and Ancient Foundations
Western civilization traces its origins to ancient Greece and Rome, which provided foundational influences in philosophy, democracy, law, art, and engineering. The concept of a distinct "West" began to form in the 4th century CE with the division of the Roman Empire into Greek East and Latin West. The survival of the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire and the influence of the Catholic Church helped preserve classical culture through the Middle Ages, while also introducing theological differences with the East.

### Medieval Division and Christendom
The political and religious division between East and West culminated in the Great Schism of 1054, which split Christianity into Roman Catholicism and Eastern Orthodoxy. This division was solidified by the Crusades, which were initially aimed at aiding Byzantium but resulted in Western states being established in Eastern territories. The Holy Roman Empire, crowned by the Pope in AD 800, became the heartland of Western Christendom, a distinct cultural and political sphere that later faced internal fracture during the Protestant Reformation.

### Expansion and Colonialism
From the 15th century onward, the Age of Exploration and subsequent European colonialism spread Western institutions, culture, and Christianity globally. This expansion, driven by trade and later industrial demand, led to the colonization of the Americas, Africa, and parts of Asia, creating a "Western" world as an inheritor of Latin Christendom. The process was accompanied by the Atlantic slave trade and the imposition of nation-state models, which often created arbitrary borders. By the 19th century, industrialization fueled "New Imperialism," with Western powers exerting direct colonial control over vast territories.

### Modern Identity and the Cold War
In the 20th century, the concept of the West was redefined during the Cold War as the "First World" (NATO and U.S.-aligned nations) in opposition to the Soviet-led "Second World." The end of the Cold War and decolonization shifted the focus to economic and cultural influence. Today, the West is often associated with developed economies, democratic governance, and a legacy of secularism stemming from the Enlightenment, though Christianity remains the dominant religion. Its boundaries remain debated, with countries like Australia included due to cultural ties, and others like Russia sometimes included or juxtaposed against it.

## Terms
- **Occident/Orient**: Latin-derived terms for the Western and Eastern worlds, respectively.
- **Great Schism (1054)**: The formal division of Christianity into the Roman Catholic Church in the West and the Eastern Orthodox Church.
- **Holy Roman Empire**: A multi-ethnic complex in Western and Central Europe from the Middle Ages to 1806, seen as a revival of the Roman Empire under Germanic rule.
- **Westphalian sovereignty**: The principle of absolute national sovereignty in international law, established by the Peace of Westphalia in 1648.
- **First World**: A Cold War term for the U.S.-aligned, capitalist bloc; often used interchangeably with "the West" or developed nations.
- **Neocolonialism**: The use of economic, political, or cultural pressures to control or influence former colonies or developing countries, often via multinational corporations.

## Debates and Open questions
- The exact scope of the West is debated, with varying perspectives on whether to include Latin America, Orthodox Europe, or parts of the former Soviet Union.
- Scholars disagree on whether Western civilization was born around AD 500 after the fall of Rome or earlier, with its roots in classical antiquity.
- There is debate over whether the concept of "the West" is a useful or accurate framework, with some scholars opposing a categorical "West and the Rest" division.
