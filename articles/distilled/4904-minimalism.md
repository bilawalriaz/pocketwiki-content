# Minimalism

## Overview
Minimalism is an art and design movement that emphasizes reducing elements to their essentials, focusing on the object itself and the viewer's experience with minimal mediation from the artist. It emerged in the post-World War II era as a reaction against abstract expressionism and modernism, influencing visual arts, music, literature, architecture, and lifestyle.

## Timeline
- **1924** — The Rietveld Schroder House is commissioned, a precursor to minimalist architecture.
- **1949–1950** — Yves Klein produces monochrome paintings and holds his first private exhibition.
- **Early 1960s** — Minimalism emerges as a specific movement in New York's visual arts.
- **1964** — Donald Judd and Dan Flavin showcase works at Green Gallery in Manhattan.
- **1968** — Michael Nyman first uses the term "minimal music" in a review.
- **1970s** — The concept of a capsule wardrobe is credited to Susie Faux.
- **Late 1980s** — Minimalist architecture becomes popular in London and New York City.
- **1985** — Donna Karan popularizes the capsule wardrobe concept.
- **1990s** — Minimalist architecture gains traction in Japan due to urbanization.
- **2018** — Ed Hawkins develops warming stripes graphics for climate communication.

## Body

### Visual Arts and Literalist Art
Minimalism in the visual arts, also called "literalist art" or "ABC Art," emerged in New York in the early 1960s. It was a reaction against the painterly subjectivity of abstract expressionism, asserting that art should refer only to itself and omit extra-visual associations. Artists like Donald Judd, Dan Flavin, and Frank Stella used simple geometric forms and industrial materials to create works that provided a purely visual, instantaneous reaction. The movement was influenced by earlier European abstract movements like the Bauhaus, De Stijl, and Russian Constructivism. Critic Michael Fried pejoratively called these artists "literalists," arguing their work required a spectator to validate it as art.

### Design, Architecture, and Spaces
Minimalist design reduces subjects to necessary elements, emphasizing vacant space, neutral colors, and the elimination of decoration. It became popular in the late 1980s in London and New York. The movement draws from De Stijl's organization of basic elements and was heavily influenced by Japanese traditional design and Zen philosophy, which values simplicity, emptiness (Ma), and the innate character of materials (wabi-sabi). Architects like Ludwig Mies van der Rohe ("Less is more") and contemporary practitioners such as Tadao Ando and John Pawson focus on light, form, and material to create spaces that feel calm and essential.

### Literature
Literary minimalism is characterized by economy of words and a focus on surface description, allowing context to dictate meaning. Writers like Raymond Carver and Amy Hempel eschew adverbs, expecting readers to actively interpret oblique hints. The style has precursors in writers like Ernest Hemingway and arose partly in response to metafiction. Minimalist poetry, sometimes overlapping with visual poetry, includes works by poets like Aram Saroyan and Geof Huth, and can be associated with the haiku form.

### Music
Minimal music, a term derived around 1970, features methods like repetition and gradual variation. Its roots include Yves Klein's Monotone Symphony (conceived 1947–1949, premiered 1960). Prominent composers include La Monte Young, Steve Reich, and Philip Glass.

### Film and Cinema
Minimalist film is associated with directors like Robert Bresson and Yasujirō Ozu, who tell simple stories with straightforward camera work and minimal score. This style, termed "transcendental cinema" by Paul Schrader, influences later movements like Dogme 95.

### Lifestyle and Other Fields
Minimalism in lifestyle involves using only essential materials and quantities to reduce waste and focus on what is important. In fashion, the capsule wardrobe (popularized in the 1970s–80s) uses few staple pieces. In science communication, minimalist graphics like "warming stripes" use color alone to convey data clearly.

## Terms
- **Literalist Art**: A pejorative term for minimalism, coined by critic Michael Fried, describing art that presents itself as a literal object in a situation.
- **Hard Edge**: A painting style using sharp, clear edges between areas of color, characteristic of minimalist painting.
- **Ma**: A Japanese aesthetic concept referring to empty or open space, central to minimalist architecture.
- **Wabi-sabi**: A Japanese aesthetic that values simplicity, plainness, and the innate character of materials.
- **Capsule Wardrobe**: A minimalist fashion concept using a small collection of essential, interchangeable garments.
- **Transcendental Cinema**: A term for minimalist film characterized by simple stories and straightforward technique.
- **Pwoermd**: A term for a poem consisting of a single word, associated with minimalist poetry.
- **Tempestry**: A portmanteau of "temperature" and "tapestry," a craft using colored yarn to visually represent climate data.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source highlights a key debate: critic Michael Fried argued that minimalist "literalist" art is dependent on a spectator to be validated as art, unlike traditional sculpture which retains its status unseen. There is also a distinction noted between true minimalism and "modern minimalism," with the latter incorporating more decorative and trendy elements while the former emphasizes strict geometric simplicity and emptiness.
