# Mimar Sinan

## Overview
Mimar Sinan (c. 1488/1490 – 17 July 1588) was the chief Ottoman architect, engineer, and mathematician for sultans Suleiman the Magnificent, Selim II, and Murad III. Over nearly fifty years, he designed or supervised more than 300 major structures—including mosques, bridges, aqueducts, and civic complexes—transforming Ottoman architecture from a pragmatic, additive tradition into a unified, geometrically rational system. His empirical approach, honed as a military engineer, allowed him to resolve structural tensions and create vast, light-filled domed interiors. His masterpiece, the Selimiye Mosque in Edirne, represents the climax of classical Ottoman architecture; his most famous work is the Süleymaniye Mosque in Istanbul. He trained a generation of architects, including Sedefkar Mehmed Agha (Sultan Ahmed Mosque) and Mimar Hayruddin (Stari Most), though none surpassed his synthesis of space, structure, and light.

## Body

### Origins and Early Training
Sinan was born in the Orthodox Christian (Rum) village of Ağırnas. His birth name was Joseph; his father, Abdülmennan, was a stonemason. Scholarly debate surrounds his ethnicity—Armenian, Cappadocian Greek, Christian Turkish, Albanian, Bulgarian, or Jewish origins have been proposed. A 1573 decree by Selim II exempting Sinan’s relatives from the deportation of Kayseri’s Rum community to Cyprus supports a Christian background. In 1935, Turkish scholars exhumed his skull to claim "Turkish racial heritage," though the skull is now missing. At around age 22, Sinan entered Ottoman service through the *devshirme* (child levy), converted to Islam, and trained as a Janissary officer. Too old for the palace Enderun School, he attended an auxiliary school, possibly the Ibrahim Pasha School, where he may have received the Islamic name Sinan. He initially learned carpentry and mathematics but through his intellectual qualities soon assisted leading architects and trained as an architect. During the next six years he also trained to be a Janissary officer (*acemioğlan*). He possibly joined Selim I in his last military campaign, Rhodes according to some sources, but when the Sultan died, this project ended.

### Military Engineering and Rise to Chief Architect
Sinan’s architectural genius was forged on campaign. He served under Suleiman the Magnificent (Mohács, Baghdad, Corfu, Apulia, Moldavia), and later sultans. He mastered fortifications, roads, bridges, and aqueducts; converted churches to mosques; and built ships for the Lake Van crossing (1535). His ability to identify structural weak points while "gunning them down" informed his later seismic-resistant designs. When the Ottoman army captured Cairo, Sinan was promoted to chief architect and was given the privilege of tearing down any buildings in the captured city that were not according to the city plan. In 1539, Grand Vizier Çelebi Lütfi Pasha appointed him Architect of the Abode of Felicity, overseeing imperial infrastructure and supplies. Sinan expanded this office into the Architect of the Empire, heading a corps of architects, deputies, and pupils.

### Architectural Method: Empiricism over Theory
Unlike contemporary European theorists such as Alberti (who studied Vitruvius), Sinan adopted an empirical, engineer’s approach akin to Brunelleschi and Michelangelo. He rejected the Ottoman tradition of additive, repetitive plans with wasteful safety margins. Instead, he pursued geometric purity—using multiples of two, then ratios of two-to-three—to unify dome, semi-domes, piers, and galleries into a single spatial whole. He transformed the dome’s circle into rectangular, hexagonal, or octagonal support systems, flooding the central zone with light from myriad windows. His *külliye* (multi-functional complexes) integrated mosques with madrasas, hospitals, kitchens, baths, and caravanserais, serving as intellectual, social, and welfare centers.

### Apprenticeship Period (to mid-1550s)
Early works followed tradition but showed growing experimentation. The Hüsrev Pasha Mosque in Aleppo (1536–37) was hasty and coarse. The Haseki Sultan Complex for Hürrem Sultan (wife of Suleiman) adhered to inherited plans but displayed superior craftsmanship. The Mihrimah Sultan Mosque (Üsküdar, 1548) introduced hallmarks of his mature style: high-vaulted basement, slender minarets, single dome with three semi-domes, and double portico. The Şehzade Mosque (1543–48), commissioned by Suleiman for his deceased son, is considered his first masterpiece. Its central dome on four half-domes, supported by four free-standing octagonal fluted piers and four wall piers, with corner turrets as stabilizers, broke from additive planning into a coherent structural concept.

### Qualification Stage (mid-1550s to 1570)
The Süleymaniye Mosque (1550–57) crowned this phase. Built on a hill dominating the Golden Horn, its vast complex included four colleges, soup kitchen, hospital, asylum, hamam, caravanserai, and hospice. Sinan adapted the half-cubic roof form from Hagia Sophia but sought simplification over enrichment, maximizing volume under a single dome (31.28 m diameter, 42 m high). He employed subtle geometric ratios, reflecting a Renaissance concern for ideal harmony. Concurrently, he produced the Rüstem Pasha Mosque (octagonal, modeled on Saints Sergius and Bacchus), the Mihrimah Mosque at Edirnekapı (revolutionary light-filled design with lateral galleries and gigantic piers, approaching Gothic verticality), the Banya Bashi Mosque in Sofia, and the Kırkçeşme water system—an engineering feat spanning 55 km with 35 aqueduct bridges, four reaching 35 m height and 700 m length.

### Master Stage (1570–1588)
In his eighties, Sinan eliminated subsidiary spaces beyond the central dome’s supports, achieving completely unified interiors. The Sokollu Mehmed Pasha Mosque at Kadırga (1571–72) and the Selimiye Mosque in Edirne (1568–75) exemplify this. According to his autobiography *Tezkiretü'l-Bünyan*, his masterpiece is the Selimiye Mosque. Breaking free of traditional Ottoman architecture, his stated motivation was the saying: "You can never build a dome larger than the dome of Hagia Sophia and specially as Muslims." When completed, Sinan claimed it had the largest dome in the world, leaving Hagia Sophia behind. In fact, the dome height from ground level was lower and the diameter barely larger (~0.5 m) than Hagia Sophia, though measured from its base the dome of Selimiye is higher. The dome (31.28 m wide, 42 m high) rests on eight massive marble-and-granite piers without capitals, using squinches so arches appear to grow organically from the piers. Lateral galleries are pushed outward, enhancing three-dimensionality; screen walls of windows flood the interior. Buttressing semi-domes hide weight and tension, creating an "airy and elegant effect." The four 83 m minarets are the tallest in the Muslim world. He also designed the Sulaymaniyya Takiyya in Damascus (introducing Ottoman style there) and the Mehmed Paša Sokolović Bridge in Višegrad (now a UNESCO site).

### Legacy and Decline
At his death, classical Ottoman architecture had peaked. His successors, including Davud Agha, retreated to earlier models like the Şehzade Mosque; innovation faded. Sinan’s official list (*Tezkiretü'l Ebniye*) credits 476 buildings (196 surviving), though he delegated provincial works. He is commemorated by a Mercury crater, Mimar Sinan Fine Arts University, banknotes, coins, stamps, and literature (e.g., Elif Shafak’s *The Architect’s Apprentice*).

## Terms
- **Devshirme**: Ottoman levy of Christian boys for conversion to Islam and service in the Janissary corps or administration.
- **Janissary**: Elite Ottoman infantry corps, originally recruited via *devshirme*; Sinan rose to command an *orta* (battalion).
- **Külliye**: A multi-building charitable complex centered on a mosque, including schools, hospitals, kitchens, baths, and hostels.
- **Türbe**: A mausoleum, typically domed, for Ottoman royalty or dignitaries.
- **Mesçit**: A small neighborhood mosque without a minbar (pulpit) for Friday prayers.
- **Medrese / Darülkurra**: Islamic theological college / school for Koran reciters.
- **Imaret**: Public soup kitchen within a *külliye*.
- **Hamam**: Public bathhouse.
- **Squinche / Console**: Architectural element transitioning from a square base to a circular dome; Sinan used these at pier summits to make arches appear integral.
- **Haseki'i**: Title meaning Sergeant-at-Arms in the Sultan’s bodyguard, granted to Sinan in 1535.

## Debates and open questions
- **Ethnic origin**: Sources dispute whether Sinan was Armenian, Cappadocian Greek, Christian Turkish, Albanian, Bulgarian, or Jewish. The 1573 deportation exemption for his Rum relatives supports a Greek/Christian Turkish origin; Turkish scholars later used cranial measurements to claim Turkish ethnicity.
- **Birth date**: Variously placed between 1488–1491 or 1494–1499; the article uses c. 1488/1490.
- **Selimiye vs. Hagia Sophia dome**: Sinan claimed Selimiye’s dome was the world’s largest. In fact, its diameter exceeds Hagia Sophia’s by only ~0.5 m, and its height from ground level is lower, though measured from its base it is higher.
- **Attribution of works**: The official list counts 476 structures; Sinan supervised a large office and delegated extensively, so personal design authorship varies.
- **Western influence**: The article notes Sinan likely knew Alberti’s/Vitruvian ideals but pursued simplification rather than enrichment; the extent of direct Renaissance influence remains a scholarly question.
