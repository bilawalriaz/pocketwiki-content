# Moses

## Overview
Moses is the central prophet and lawgiver of Judaism, revered across Abrahamic faiths as the leader who liberated the Israelites from Egyptian slavery in the Exodus and mediated the Mosaic Law (Torah) at Mount Sinai. The biblical narrative presents him as a Hebrew raised in Pharaoh’s court who fled to Midian, encountered God (YHWH) in a burning bush, and returned to inflict the Ten Plagues before leading the people through the Red Sea and forty years of wilderness wandering. He died on Mount Nebo within sight of the Promised Land. Modern scholarship largely regards the biblical Moses as a legendary figure, though a historical "Moses-like" leader may have existed in the 13th century BCE; no extra-biblical Egyptian or archaeological evidence confirms the Exodus account. His legacy profoundly shaped Western law, political symbolism (especially in the United States), and religious theology, where he serves as a type for later deliverers and, in Christianity, a prefiguration of Jesus.

## Body

### Etymology and Name
The name "Moses" (Hebrew *Mōše*) carries competing etymologies. The biblical folk etymology (Exodus 2:10) derives it from the Hebrew root *m-š-h* ("to draw out"), explaining that Pharaoh’s daughter named him because she "drew him out of the water." Scholars note the grammatical oddity: she uses the active participle ("drawer-out") rather than the passive ("drawn-out"), interpreted by medieval commentator Isaac b. Asher haLevi as a prophecy that Moses would draw others out of Egypt.  
Egyptian etymologies are widely considered more plausible historically. The Egyptian *msy* or *mose* ("child of") appears in theophoric names like Thutmose ("born of Thoth") and Ramose ("born of Ra"). Linguist Abraham Yahuda proposed a Hebrew-Egyptian hybrid meaning "child of the Nile" (*mw-š*). Kenneth Kitchen argues the Hebrew sounds do not match the Egyptian pronunciation of *msy* in the relevant period, favoring the Hebrew etymology, though the Egyptian origin of the name itself remains the scholarly consensus.

### Biblical Narrative: Prophet, Deliverer, Lawgiver
The Torah (Exodus–Deuteronomy) structures Moses’ life in three forty-year acts: prince in Egypt, shepherd in Midian, leader in the wilderness.  
**Exodus and Revelation:** Born to Amram and Jochebed (Levites) during a genocidal decree against Hebrew males, Moses is hidden in an ark on the Nile, adopted by Pharaoh’s daughter, and raised as Egyptian. After killing an Egyptian taskmaster, he flees to Midian, marries Zipporah (daughter of Jethro/Reuel), and encounters YHWH in a burning bush on Mount Horeb/Sinai. God reveals the divine name (YHWH) and commissions Moses to free Israel. Aaron serves as his spokesman.  
**The Plagues and Sea Crossing:** Ten plagues compel Pharaoh to release the Israelites. At the Red Sea (Sea of Reeds), God parts the waters; the Egyptians are drowned.  
**Sinai and Covenant:** At Mount Sinai, Moses receives the Decalogue (Ten Commandments) and the Covenant Code (Exodus 20–23). During his prolonged absence, the people worship a golden calf; Moses smashes the tablets, orders the execution of the idolaters, and intercedes for the nation. God renews the tablets and gives instructions for the Tabernacle (mobile shrine) and the Aaronic priesthood.  
**Wilderness Wandering:** From Sinai, the Israelites reach Kadesh-barnea. Twelve spies scout Canaan; the negative report triggers rebellion. God condemns that generation to forty years of wandering. Rebellions (Korah, etc.) and battles (Amalek, Og, Sihon, Midian) punctuate the journey.  
**Transjordan and Death:** On the plains of Moab, Moses delivers the Deuteronomic law code, blesses the tribes, appoints Joshua as successor, ascends Mount Nebo, views the Promised Land, and dies at age 120. God buries him in an unknown grave (Deuteronomy 34).

**Lawgiving:** Tradition ascribes the entire Torah to Moses. Critical scholarship distinguishes three main legal corpora: the Covenant Code (Exodus 20:22–23:19), the Priestly/Holiness codes (Leviticus–Numbers), and the Deuteronomic Code (Deuteronomy 12–26), likely compiled over centuries.

### Historicity and Scholarly Debates
**Consensus:** The majority of scholars (e.g., William Dever, Israel Finkelstein) view the biblical Moses as largely mythical or legendary. No contemporary Egyptian texts mention Moses, the plagues, or a mass Exodus; no archaeological trace exists in the Sinai for a large population wandering forty years. The earliest extra-biblical references appear in Hellenistic Greek texts (4th century BCE), over a millennium after the putative events.  
**Minimalist/Maximalist Spectrum:**  
- *Mythical:* Jan Assmann argues Moses cannot be verified outside tradition; David Leeming classifies him as a mythic hero.  
- *Historical Kernel:* Dever allows a "Moses-like figure" in southern Transjordan (mid-late 13th century BCE). Rudolf Smend considers only the Egyptian name and Midianite marriage historically probable.  
- *Specific Identifications:* Manfred Görg and Rolf Krauss propose the historical vizier **Amenmose** (c. 1200 BCE), dismissed from office, whose name simplified to *msy*. Aidan Dodson calls this "intriguing, but beyond proof." Israel Knohl identifies Moses with **Irsu**, a "Shasu" (Levantine) usurper in Papyrus Harris I (c. 1152 BCE) who disrupted Egyptian cults and was expelled by Setnakhte.  
- *Literary Parallels:* The birth narrative follows the "exposed hero" motif (e.g., Sargon of Akkad, 23rd century BCE). Manetho’s **Osarseph** (via Josephus)—a renegade Egyptian priest who leads lepers, allies with Hyksos, and changes his name to Moses—offers a hostile Egyptian counter-memory.  
**Literary Development:** Jean-Louis Ska argues the Pentateuch uses Moses as a "narrative bracket" (Martin Noth) to weld independent traditions. Exilic texts (Isaiah 63, Ezekiel 33) elevate the Exodus under Moses as Israel’s true founding moment, rivaling Abrahamic claims of the land-based population.

### Hellenistic and Classical Reception
Greek and Roman writers (323 BCE – 2nd century CE) portray Moses as a foundational lawgiver and philosopher.  
- **Hecataeus of Abdera** (4th c. BCE): Calls Moses a wise, courageous leader who founded cities, established a cult, and gave written laws.  
- **Artapanus** (2nd c. BCE): Jewish apologist; depicts Moses as a cultural hero (Hermes/Thoth/Musaeus) who taught ibis worship, circumcision, and ox-cult (Apis), and divided Egypt into 36 districts.  
- **Strabo** (c. 24 CE): Presents Moses as an Egyptian priest who rejected idolatry, teaching a monotheism of "the universe/nature of things" accessible only through virtue and justice. Jan Assmann considers Strabo the closest ancient approximation of Mosaic monotheism.  
- **Tacitus** (c. 100 CE): Notes Jewish monotheism; describes a six-day desert march (vs. biblical forty years).  
- **Longinus** (*On the Sublime*): Quotes Genesis, calling Moses "the Lawgiver" (*thesmothetēs*), placing him alongside Lycurgus, Minos, and Homer.  
- **Josephus** (*Antiquities*): Emphasizes Moses’ cardinal virtues (wisdom, courage, temperance, justice, piety) and role as educator-king.  
- **Numenius & Justin Martyr** (2nd c. CE): Numenius calls Plato a "Greek Moses"; Justin argues Moses predates and surpasses Greek philosophers.

### Abrahamic Traditions
**Judaism:** Moses (*Moshe Rabbenu*, "Our Teacher") is the supreme prophet, yet fully human—never worshipped. Rabbinic tradition assigns him multiple names (Jekuthiel, Heber, Toviah, Levi, *Mechoqeiq*—lawgiver) and legends: he ascended the seven heavens, visited Paradise and Hell, and received both Written and Oral Torah (including hidden *hokhmat nistar*). The blessing "may you live to 120" derives from his age (Deut 34:7; Gen 6:3).  
**Christianity:** Moses appears more than any OT figure in the New Testament. He functions typologically: the bronze serpent (John 3) and manna (John 6) prefigure Christ; the Transfiguration (Matt 17, Mark 9, Luke 9) pairs him with Elijah as witnesses to Jesus. Jesus critiques Pharisees sitting "on the chair of Moses" (Matt 23). Commemorated as saint/prophet (Sept 4 in East/West; July 20 with Aaron/Elijah/Elisha).  
**Mormonism:** Accepts biblical account plus *Book of Moses* (Pearl of Great Price). Teaches Moses was "translated" (taken to heaven without death) and appeared to Joseph Smith and Oliver Cowdery in the Kirtland Temple (April 3, 1836) to restore priesthood keys.  
**Islam:** Moses (*Mūsā*) is the most-mentioned individual in the Quran, a *nabi* (prophet) and *rasūl* (messenger with a book/law). Key differences: Pharaoh’s wife **Asiya** (not daughter) finds him; the Quran emphasizes his mission to Pharaoh; Israelites refuse to enter Canaan, prompting 40-year wandering. A hadith recounts Moses bargaining with Muhammad in heaven to reduce daily prayers from fifty to five. Burial traditionally at Maqam El-Nabi Musa (near Jericho).  
**Baháʼí Faith:** Moses is a Manifestation of God ("One Who Conversed with God"). Baháʼu'lláh is identified as the voice from the burning bush. ʻAbdu'l-Bahá stresses Moses’ humble origins (shepherd, stammerer, murderer) transformed by divine assistance into a lawgiver who brought civilization; Socrates allegedly learned monotheism and soul immortality from Israelite teachings.  
**Druze:** Moses is among the seven major prophets of history.

### Legacy in Politics, Law, and Culture
**United States Symbolism:** The Puritans viewed themselves as a "New Israel" (John Carver = "Moses of the Pilgrims"; William Bradford invoked Moses’ meekness). The 1776 Great Seal committee (Adams, Jefferson, Franklin) chose Moses leading Israel. Two-thirds of Washington eulogies called him "America’s Moses." Franklin proposed governing by "the laws of Moses" pending a constitution; Adams preferred Mosaic law over Greek philosophy.  
**Abolition and Civil Rights:** Harriet Tubman was nicknamed "Moses." Lincoln’s assassination prompted Black Americans to mourn losing "their Moses." Martin Luther King Jr. was called a "modern Moses," framing the freedom struggle in Exodus terms.  
**Law and Government:** The Ten Commandments are cited as a foundation of Western law (William Barclay; Pope Francis, 2015). Moses appears in U.S. government art: forward-facing bas-relief in the House Chamber (c. 1350–1250 BCE); eight times in the Supreme Court Great Hall ceiling; holding tablets on the east pediment; tablets on courtroom doors, gates, and woodwork.  
**Art:** The Sistine Chapel (1481–82) cycles Moses opposite Christ. Jerome’s Vulgate mistranslated *qaran* ("shone/rayed") as *cornutam* ("horned"), yielding the horned Moses iconography (Michelangelo, 1513–15) common until the Renaissance; early Jewish art used rays.  
**Literature/Film:** Freud’s *Moses and Monotheism* (1939) posits an Egyptian nobleman murdered in the wilderness (pseudohistory per historians). Novels by Mann, Hardy, Card. Major films: DeMille’s *The Ten Commandments* (1923, 1956, Heston), *The Prince of Egypt* (1998), *Exodus: Gods and Kings* (2014, Bale).

### Criticism
**Thomas Paine** (*Age of Reason*, 1794–1807) called Moses a "detestable villain," citing Numbers 31 (killing Midianite males and non-virgin females, sparing virgins).  
**Rabbinic/Modern Apologetics:** Joel Grossman reads it as a fable condemning sexual idolatry (Baal Peor); Alan Levin as a caution against idolatry; Chasam Sofer emphasizes divine command, not Moses’ initiative.  
**Secular Ethics:** Keith Allan notes the conduct would constitute a war crime today.  
**Feminist/Womanist Critique:** Nyasha Junior argues Moses is a valid object of feminist inquiry regarding gender, power, and violence.

## Terms
- ****Torah**** — The first five books of the Hebrew Bible (Genesis–Deuteronomy), traditionally ascribed to Moses; contains the Mosaic Law.
- ****Decalogue**** — The Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:1–17; Deuteronomy 5:6–21), the core stipulations of the Sinai covenant.
- ****YHWH**** — The personal name of Israel’s God, revealed to Moses at the burning bush (Exodus 3:14); likely pronounced *Yahweh*.
- ****Midian**** — Desert region south of Judah (northwest Arabia) where Moses fled, married Zipporah, and encountered God at Horeb/Sinai.
- ****Typology**** — Christian interpretive method viewing OT figures/events (e.g., Moses, bronze serpent) as anticipatory "types" fulfilled in Christ ("antitypes").
- ****Manifestation of God**** — Baháʼí term for a divine messenger (e.g., Moses, Baháʼu'lláh) who reveals God’s will for an age.
- ****Nabi / Rasul**** — Quranic terms: *nabi* = prophet; *rasul* = messenger bearing a new scripture/law. Moses is both.
- ****Shasu**** — Egyptian term for semi-nomadic Levantine pastoralists; Irsu (identified by Knohl with Moses) is called a Shasu.
- ****Vizier Amenmose**** — Historical Egyptian official (c. 1200 BCE) proposed by Görg/Krauss as a possible kernel for the Moses legend.
- ****Osarseph**** — Figure in Manetho’s Egyptian history (via Josephus): a renegade priest who leads lepers, allies with Hyksos, and becomes "Moses."

## Debates and open questions
1. **Historical Existence:** Did a single historical Moses exist, or is the figure a composite of multiple traditions/leaders (e.g., a Transjordan conqueror, a lawgiver, a Midianite priest)?
2. **Date and Setting:** If historical, does he belong to the 13th century BCE (Ramesside period), the 12th century (Irsu/Sea Peoples era), or an earlier Hyksos expulsion (16th century)?
3. **Egyptian Core vs. Canaanite Origin:** The name is Egyptian, but the consensus holds most Israelites were indigenous to Canaan. How does an Egyptian-named leader fit an indigenous emergence?
4. **YHWH’s Origin:** Was YHWH originally a Midianite/Edomite deity (Kenite hypothesis, per Cornelis Tiele 1872) introduced by Moses via his father-in-law Jethro, or native to Israel?
5. **Pentateuch Composition:** To what extent does the Torah preserve genuine Mosaic tradition vs. Persian-period (538–332 BCE) editorial construction using Moses as a "narrative bracket"?
6. **Ethics of Conquest:** How should modern readers judge the biblical accounts of *herem* (total destruction) and the Midianite war (Numbers 31) attributed to Moses—divine command, symbolic fable, or historical atrocity?
