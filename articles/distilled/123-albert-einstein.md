# Albert Einstein

## Overview

Albert Einstein (1879–1955) was a German-born theoretical physicist who revolutionized physics with his theories of special and general relativity and made foundational contributions to quantum theory. His mass–energy equivalence formula E = mc² became "the world's most famous equation." He received the 1921 Nobel Prize in Physics (awarded 1922) "for his services to theoretical physics, and especially for his discovery of the law of the photoelectric effect." His work transformed scientific understanding of space, time, matter, and gravitation, and his public fame made him perhaps the first celebrity scientist.

## Body

### Early life and education

Einstein was born to secular Ashkenazi Jewish parents, Hermann Einstein (a salesman and engineer) and Pauline Koch. The family moved to Munich in 1880, where his father and uncle founded a company manufacturing electrical equipment based on direct current. Slow to learn to talk, his parents worried he had a learning disability. At age five, a compass his father gave him sparked a lifelong fascination with electromagnetism. By twelve, he was teaching himself algebra, calculus, and Euclidean geometry, and discovered an original proof of the Pythagorean theorem before his thirteenth birthday. A family tutor, Max Talmud, introduced him to Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* at thirteen.

In 1894, his father's company failed to secure a Munich lighting contract due to lack of capital to update from direct current to alternating current, forcing the family to move to Italy. Einstein stayed behind to finish school but found the Gymnasium's rote learning harmful to creativity. In 1895, he failed the general portion of the entrance exam for the Swiss federal polytechnic school but excelled in physics and mathematics. He completed secondary education at the Argovian cantonal school in Aarau, Switzerland, graduating in 1896. In January 1896, with his father's approval, he renounced his citizenship of the German Kingdom of Württemberg to avoid conscription into military service.

### Marriage and family

At the polytechnic, Einstein befriended fellow student Mileva Marić, a Serbian woman; they became lovers and married in January 1903. Their daughter Lieserl, born in early 1902, disappeared from the record—in a letter written in September 1903, Einstein suggested the girl was either given up for adoption or died of scarlet fever in infancy. Their sons Hans Albert (born 1904) and Eduard (born 1910) followed. In 1912, Einstein began a relationship with his cousin Elsa Löwenthal. When Marić learned of the infidelity in 1914, she returned to Zurich with the children. The couple divorced in 1919, with Einstein agreeing to give Marić his Nobel Prize money. He married Löwenthal in 1919. Eduard was later diagnosed with schizophrenia and spent his life in care. Einstein had several other romantic relationships, including with a secretary, Betty Neumann, and possibly Margarita Konenkova, thought by some to be a Russian spy.

### Patent office and early career

After graduating in 1900, Einstein failed to secure a teaching position. With help from Marcel Grossmann's father, he obtained a post at the Swiss Patent Office in Bern in 1902, becoming permanent in 1903. His work evaluating patent applications—including ideas for a gravel sorter and electric typewriter—may have influenced his thinking on signal transmission and clock synchronization, relevant to special relativity. In 1902, he formed the "Olympia Academy" discussion group with friends Conrad Habicht and Maurice Solovine, reflecting on Poincaré, Mach, and Hume.

### Annus Mirabilis (1905)

In 1905, Einstein published four papers in *Annalen der Physik*: on the photoelectric effect (postulating light consists of localized particles, later called photons), on Brownian motion (showing molecules exist), on special relativity (reconciling Maxwell's equations with mechanics, making the luminiferous aether superfluous), and on mass–energy equivalence (E = mc²). His PhD dissertation, "A New Determination of Molecular Dimensions," was completed 30 April 1905 and approved three months later. These works "deeply impressed" contemporaries and founded modern physics.

### Academic career and general relativity

Einstein secured a junior teaching position at the University of Bern in 1908, then moved through positions at Zurich and Prague before returning to ETH Zurich in 1912. In 1913, Max Planck and Walther Nernst persuaded him to move to Berlin with membership in the Prussian Academy of Sciences, the directorship of the planned Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Physics, and a chair at Humboldt University with no teaching duties. He moved to Berlin in April 1914.

In 1907, Einstein formulated his equivalence principle: an observer in free fall cannot detect a gravitational field. He predicted gravitational time dilation, redshift, and lensing. By fall 1915, he completed general relativity, modeling gravitation with the Riemann curvature tensor of non-Euclidean four-dimensional spacetime. He applied it to the precession of Mercury's perihelion. The 29 May 1919 total solar eclipse allowed Sir Arthur Eddington to confirm gravitational lensing, making Einstein world-famous. In 1916, Einstein predicted gravitational waves, first indirectly detected in the 1970s via pulsar PSR B1913+16 and directly by LIGO on 14 September 2015.

In 1917, Einstein applied general relativity to the universe as a whole. Finding the field equations predicted a dynamic universe, he introduced the cosmological constant to allow a static universe—the "Einstein World." After Edwin Hubble's 1929 discovery of galactic recession, Einstein abandoned the constant and proposed dynamic models. He is often quoted as calling the constant his "biggest blunder," though astrophysicist Mario Livio has cast doubt on this claim. In 1931, Einstein briefly explored a steady-state model with continuous matter creation, but abandoned it due to a fundamental flaw.

### Quantum theory contributions

Einstein's 1905 light quanta were initially rejected by nearly all physicists, including Planck and Bohr, only gaining acceptance after Millikan's experiments and Compton scattering measurements. In 1907, he proposed a model of quantized atomic vibrations (the Einstein model), later refined by Peter Debye. In 1917, he proposed stimulated emission, the mechanism behind masers and lasers. In 1924, he extended Satyendra Nath Bose's statistics to atoms, laying groundwork for Bose–Einstein statistics and predicting the Bose–Einstein condensate (first produced experimentally in 1995). He supported Louis de Broglie's matter waves, which inspired Schrödinger's 1926 work.

### Objections to quantum mechanics

Despite his foundational contributions, Einstein became displeased with quantum mechanics after 1925, skeptical that its randomness was fundamental rather than deterministic—"God does not play dice." The Bohr–Einstein debates became famous in the philosophy of science. The 1935 Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen (EPR) paper argued that quantum theory is incomplete, invoking the "EPR criterion of reality." In 1964, John Stewart Bell showed that hidden-variable explanations require nonlocality, resolving the paradox "in the way which Einstein would have liked least." The EPR paper became a centerpiece of quantum information theory.

### Unified field theory and later work

Encouraged by general relativity's success, Einstein sought a unified field theory treating gravitation and electromagnetism as aspects of a single entity. His model notably failed to accommodate the strong and weak nuclear forces, neither well understood until after his death. Most researchers now believe his approach was mistaken, though his goal of a theory of everything persists. He also collaborated with Nathan Rosen on wormholes (Einstein–Rosen bridges, 1935), with Wander Johannes de Haas on the Einstein–de Haas experiment (1915), and with Leó Szilárd on the Einstein refrigerator (patented 1930).

### Emigration and American years

In February 1933, while visiting the US, Einstein knew he could not return to Germany with the rise to power of the Nazis. The Gestapo raided his Berlin apartment; his cottage was raided by the Nazis and his personal sailboat confiscated. Upon landing in Antwerp, Belgium on 28 March, he went to the German consulate and surrendered his passport, formally renouncing his German citizenship. After brief stays in Belgium and England (where Oliver Locker-Lampson provided bodyguards), he accepted a position at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton in October 1933, becoming one of its four first selected scholars alongside John von Neumann, Kurt Gödel, and Hermann Weyl. He became a US citizen in 1940.

In July 1939, Szilárd and Wigner visited Einstein to explain the possibility of atomic bombs. Einstein signed a letter to President Roosevelt recommending US nuclear weapons research—believed to be "arguably the key stimulus" for the Manhattan Project. In 1954, he called signing the letter "one great mistake," though justified by the danger the Germans would make bombs. He campaigned for civil rights, joining the NAACP, considering racism America's "worst disease," and inviting black contralto Marian Anderson to stay at his house when the Nassau Inn refused her a room.

### Political and religious views

Einstein advocated a democratic global government and socialism, detailing the latter in "Why Socialism?" (1949). He was a pacifist, declaring he would "unconditionally refuse every direct or indirect war service." He corresponded with Gandhi, describing him as "a role model for the generations to come." He helped establish the Hebrew University of Jerusalem but opposed Jewish nationalism, initially favoring binationalism; by 1947 he supported a Jewish state in part of the land. In 1952, Prime Minister David Ben-Gurion offered him the largely ceremonial position of President of Israel; Einstein wrote that he was "deeply moved" but could not accept it. He described himself as an agnostic or "deeply religious nonbeliever," sympathetic to Spinoza's pantheism, and served on the advisory board of the First Humanist Society of New York.

### Death and legacy

On 17 April 1955, Einstein experienced internal bleeding from a ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm. He refused surgery, saying, "I want to go when I want... I will do it elegantly." He died in the Princeton Hospital early the next morning at the age of 76. Pathologist Thomas Stoltz Harvey removed his brain for preservation without the permission of his family. His ashes were scattered at an undisclosed location. He bequeathed his personal archives, library, and intellectual assets to the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. In 1999, he was named Time's Person of the Century. The element einsteinium was named in his honor in 1955. IUPAC declared 2005 the "World Year of Physics" in recognition of his miracle year.

## Terms

- **Special relativity**: Einstein's 1905 theory reconciling Maxwell's equations with mechanics; effects most apparent at high speeds; makes the luminiferous aether superfluous.
- **General relativity**: Theory of gravitation (1907–1915) where gravitational attraction results from the warping of spacetime by masses.
- **Equivalence principle**: Argument that free fall is inertial motion; for a free-falling observer, special relativity rules apply.
- **Photoelectric effect**: Emission of electrons from matter when light shines on it; explained by Einstein's light quanta postulate.
- **Bose–Einstein statistics**: Statistical model (1924) applying Bose's counting method to atoms as well as light particles; predicts the Bose–Einstein condensate.
- **Cosmological constant**: Term Einstein introduced into field equations (1917) to allow a static universe; later abandoned.
- **EPR paradox**: 1935 argument by Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen that quantum theory is incomplete, based on quantum entanglement.
- **Stimulated emission**: Process proposed by Einstein (1917) making masers and lasers possible.
- **Unified field theory**: Einstein's unsuccessful attempt to treat gravitation and electromagnetism as aspects of a single entity.

## Debates and open questions

Historians of physics are divided on the extent to which Mileva Marić contributed to Einstein's annus mirabilis insights; some evidence suggests influence, but scholars doubt its significance. Einstein's claim to have called the cosmological constant his "biggest blunder" is doubted by astrophysicist Mario Livio. The assertion that the geodesic equation can be derived from the field equations applied to gravitational singularities "remains disputed." Einstein's steady-state model of the universe (1931) contained a fundamental flaw and was quickly abandoned. The fate of his daughter Lieserl remains uncertain—either adoption or death from scarlet fever.
