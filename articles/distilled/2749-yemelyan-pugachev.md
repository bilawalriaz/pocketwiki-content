# Yemelyan Pugachev

## Overview
Yemelyan Pugachev (c. 1742–1775) was a Don Cossack who led a massive peasant and Cossack uprising against Catherine the Great's Russia from 1773 to 1774. Claiming to be the deposed Tsar Peter III, he promised an end to serfdom and rallied a large army that briefly controlled a vast territory. His rebellion was one of the most serious threats to the Russian state in the 18th century and had a lasting impact on imperial policy.

## Timeline
- **c. 1742** — Born in stanitsa Zimoveyskaya, Don Cossack territory.
- **1759** — Begins military service, later fighting in the Seven Years' War.
- **1770** — Deserts the army during the Russo-Turkish War, beginning years as a fugitive.
- **1773 (Sept)** — Arrives among the Yaik Cossacks and initiates his revolt, claiming to be Tsar Peter III.
- **1774 (March)** — Captures and burns the city of Kazan.
- **1774 (Aug)** — Suffers a crushing defeat at Tsaritsyn from General Michelsohn.
- **1774 (Sept)** — Betrayed and captured by his own Cossacks.
- **1775 (Jan)** — Publicly executed in Moscow by decapitation and quartering.

## Body
**Early Life and Desertion**
Pugachev was the son of a small Don Cossack landowner. He served in the Imperial Russian Army during the Seven Years' War and the Russo-Turkish War of 1768–1774. In 1770, he deserted the military and spent years as a fugitive, moving between various Cossack communities and border settlements. During this period, he gained experience in evasion and began to build a network of discontented contacts, including Old Believers (a dissenting Orthodox sect).

**The Impersonation and Revolt**
Pugachev conceived the idea of impersonating the late Emperor Peter III, who had been overthrown and murdered in 1762. This was a potent claim, as it offered a legitimate alternative to Catherine's rule. By promising to restore Cossack privileges and abolish serfdom, he amassed a large army of peasants, Cossacks, and Old Believers. His forces overran the region between the Volga and the Urals, culminating in the capture and burning of Kazan in 1774.

**Defeat and Execution**
The imperial government initially struggled to suppress the revolt due to logistical problems and indiscipline among its troops. The turning point came in August 1774, when General Johann von Michelsohnen inflicted a devastating defeat on the rebels near Tsaritsyn. Weakened and betrayed, Pugachev was captured by his own Cossacks and delivered to the authorities. He was transported to Moscow and executed publicly in January 1775.

**Legacy**
The rebellion's horrors caused Catherine the Great to abandon plans for emancipating the serfs and to tighten provincial administration. The uprising became a symbol of popular discontent in Russian culture. It was famously chronicled by Alexander Pushkin in *The History of Pugachev* and fictionalized in his novella *The Captain's Daughter*. The term "Pugachevshchina" has been used to describe a tendency toward rebellious discontent in Russian culture.

## Terms
- **Ataman:** A leader or commander of a Cossack community.
- **Cossack:** A member of a semi-autonomous, militaristic community in southern Russia and Ukraine, with distinct privileges and traditions.
- **Serfdom:** The system binding peasants to the land and the will of the landowner, prevalent in 18th-century Russia.
- **Old Believers:** A group of Russian Orthodox Christians who rejected the liturgical reforms of the 17th century and faced persecution.
- **Yaik Cossacks:** The Cossack community of the Yaik River (later Ural River) region, central to Pugachev's revolt.
- **Peter III:** Emperor of Russia for six months in 1762 before being overthrown and killed by supporters of his wife, the future Catherine the Great.
- **Khorunzhiy:** A Cossack military rank roughly equivalent to a company commander.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source material does not present specific scholarly debates, but it notes that the story of Pugachev's strong physical resemblance to Peter III originates from a later legend. The rebellion's long-term impact is clear, but its immediate success was due to a combination of genuine popular grievances and Pugachev's effective use of the powerful "pretender phenomenon" in Russian politics.
