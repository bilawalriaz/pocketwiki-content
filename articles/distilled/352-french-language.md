# French language

## Overview
French is a Romance language of the Indo-European family, descended from the Vulgar Latin spoken in Roman Gaul and shaped by native Celtic (Gaulish) and Germanic (Frankish) influences. It evolved through Old French (8th–14th centuries) and Middle French (14th–17th centuries) into Modern French, which became the dominant language of diplomacy and international relations from the 17th to the mid-20th century. Today, French is an official language in 26 countries and a working language of major international organizations (UN, EU, NATO, IOC). With approximately 310–396 million speakers globally (depending on methodology), it is the fifth most spoken language by total speakers and the fourth most used on the internet. Demographic growth in sub-Saharan Africa—where 65–70% of Francophones now reside—drives rapid expansion, projecting 500 million to over 1 billion speakers by 2050, even as the language faces political contestation in parts of the Sahel and declining influence in Southeast Asia.

## Timeline
- **1st–5th c. CE** — Roman rule spreads Latin in Gaul; Gaulish survives until c. 6th century, influencing Vulgar Latin (loanwords, sound changes, *oui*).
- **5th–8th c.** — Germanic Frankish invasions create north/south divide: *langues d'oïl* (north) vs. *langues d'oc* (south); Frankish contributes ~15% of modern vocabulary (e.g., *on*, *guerre*).
- **842** — Oaths of Strasbourg: earliest extant text in a Romance language (Old French).
- **11th–13th c.** — Old French literature flourishes (*Chanson de Roland*); Crusades establish French as Mediterranean *lingua franca*; Arabic loans enter (e.g., *alcool*, *zéro*, *algèbre*).
- **1250–1254** — Louis IX in Levant adopts Outremer French administrative model; professionalizes French chancery upon return.
- **1536** — Aosta Valley adopts Modern French as official language (first polity to do so).
- **1539** — Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts: Francis I mandates French for all legal acts in France, replacing Latin.
- **1635** — Richelieu establishes Académie française to regulate the language.
- **17th c.** — French replaces Latin as primary language of diplomacy; "Grand Siècle" cements prestige.
- **1794** — Grégoire's report initiates state policy to eradicate *patois* (regional languages) via compulsory French education.
- **1919** — Treaty of Versailles written in English and French; cited as first diplomatic blow to French dominance.
- **1992** — French enshrined as sole official language in Constitution of France.
- **2022–2025** — OIF reports growth from 321M to 348–396M speakers; Mali (2023), Burkina Faso (2024), Niger (2025) demote/remove French as official language.
- **2026** — 20th Sommet de la Francophonie scheduled in Cambodia; presidency transfers from France to Cambodia.

## Body

### Origins and Early Formation (Gaul to Old French)
Roman conquest introduced Latin to Gaul (1st c. BCE–5th c. CE). The resulting Gallo-Romance retained a distinct character: 90% of the population remained indigenous, and Latin spread unevenly from urban centers (e.g., Narbonensis) into rural areas. Gaulish survived until c. 6th century, contributing ~154–240 words (plant life, animals, rural measures), sound changes, and syntactic calques like *oui* (from *hoc ille*). The Crisis of the Third Century accelerated Latin adoption for communication with Germanic tribes and settlers; Gaulish writing vanished by the 5th century. Frankish invasions (5th c. onward) split the territory: the north spoke *langues d'oïl* (ancestor of French), the south *langues d'oc* (Occitan). Frankish contributed a superstrate of ~15% of modern vocabulary (e.g., *guerre*, *bleu*, *on* < *man*), V2 word order in elite speech, and the name *français* itself. Old French (late 8th–mid-14th c.) retained Latin's nominative/oblique case system longer than most Romance languages and featured heavy syllabic stress producing diphthongs (e.g., *-eau*).

### Middle French and State Standardization (14th–17th c.)
The Francien dialect (Paris basin) emerged as the basis of Modern French. Crucially, French became a language of administration first in the Crusader Kingdom of Jerusalem (mid-13th c.), where nobility replaced Latin with *Outremer* French (a koiné of Parisian, Norman, Picard) to codify the Assizes of Jerusalem for legal security. This model "rebounded" to Europe via Louis IX (r. 1226–1270), who professionalized the French chancery after his Levant stay. The Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts (1539) made French the exclusive language of law and administration in France, centralizing the state around the Parisian standard. Grammatically, noun declensions were lost; Robert Estienne published the first Latin-French dictionary with phonetic, etymological, and grammatical notes.

### Classical French and Colonial Expansion (17th–20th c.)
During the *Grand Siècle* (17th c.), under Richelieu and Louis XIV, French replaced Latin as the international *lingua franca* of diplomacy, science, and literature. The Académie française (1635) was founded to "protect" the language. The French Revolution and Third Republic weaponized education to eradicate regional languages (*patois*): the 1794 Grégoire report explicitly aimed to "annihilate the patois"; teachers in Brittany and Occitania were instructed to "kill the Breton language" and instill shame (*Vergonha*). Colonialism spread French to the Americas, Africa, and Asia, creating creoles (notably Haitian Creole). French retained diplomatic primacy until the mid-20th century; the bilingual Treaty of Versailles (1919) marked the symbolic rise of English.

### French in Africa: Demographic Engine and Political Contestation
Africa hosts the majority of Francophones (est. 167M in 2023 across 35 countries). French functions primarily as a second language/lingua franca, but is becoming a native language in urban hubs (Kinshasa, Abidjan, Douala, Yaoundé). Youth sociolects (Camfranglais, Nouchi) drive linguistic innovation influencing the global Francosphere. However, the Sahel has seen a political rupture: Mali (2023), Burkina Faso (2024), and Niger (2025) demoted or removed French as an official language, elevating indigenous tongues, framing the move as anti-colonial sovereignty assertion amid shifting alliances (e.g., toward Russia). Yet French persists *de facto* in higher education, media, and interethnic commerce in Bamako and Ouagadougou. In the Maghreb, trajectories diverge: Algeria mandates Arabic/English in education amid diplomatic tensions with France, yet French persists in justice and business; Morocco and Tunisia maintain prestigious bilingual curricula. Sub-Saharan demographic growth (youthful population, urbanization, rising school enrollment) drives global projections of 500M–1B+ speakers by 2050.

### French in the Americas, Asia, and Oceania
**Canada:** Federal official language with English; sole official language of Quebec (80% native speakers). Protected by Charter of the French Language (Bill 96, 2022). **USA:** 4th most spoken language (2011); historic dialects (Louisiana French, Missouri French, New England French) largely endangered; Louisiana immersion programs growing (4,300 pupils, 2024–25). **Caribbean:** Official in Haiti (with Creole) and French West Indies. **South America:** Official in French Guiana. **Asia:** Former official language of French Indochina. Vietnam: <1% fluent (2018), displaced by English; Laos/Cambodia: administrative remnants, growing educational programs (Cambodia: 400K learners, 2025). **Lebanon:** 40% Francophone; French used in education (math/science), currency, signage; English rising in business. **India:** Former official language of Puducherry (until 1965), now marginal. **Oceania:** Official in Vanuatu, New Caledonia (97% literacy), French Polynesia (95%), Wallis and Futuna (84%); French increasingly displaces Polynesian languages at home.

### Linguistic Structure: Phonology, Orthography, Grammar
**Phonology:** Up to 17 vowels (oral /a, ɑ, e, ɛ, i, o, ɔ, y, u, œ, ø/ + nasal /ɑ̃, ɛ̃, ɔ̃, œ̃/); mergers underway in France (/ɑ/→/a/, /ɛː/→/ɛ/), distinctions retained in Quebec/Belgium. Voiced stops fully voiced; voiceless stops unaspirated. Single variable rhotic (uvular fricative [ʁ] ~ trill [ʀ] ~ apical [r]). Liaison links word-final consonants to vowel-initial words (mandatory, optional, or forbidden contexts). **Orthography:** 26 Latin letters + 4 vowel diacritics (acute, grave, circumflex, diaeresis) + cedilla (ç); ligatures *œ*, *æ* often typed *oe*, *ae*. Morphophonemic: preserves etymology (e.g., *doigt* < *digitus*, *pied* < *pedem*) over phonology; 130 graphemes for 36 phonemes. 1990 reform (implemented in schools 2016) allows simplified spellings (e.g., *oignon*→*ognon*, *coût*→*cout*). **Grammar:** Moderately inflected. Nouns: binary gender (masc./fem.), number (plural often silent). Adjectives agree in gender/number. Verbs: 4 finite moods (indicative, subjunctive, imperative, conditional) + 3 non-finite; 8 indicative tense-aspect forms (spoken French favors *passé composé* over *passé simple*). Voice: active vs. passive (*être* + past participle); impersonal *on* often replaces passive. Word order: SVO baseline; pronoun objects precede verb; inversion for questions; VOS/OSV in formal/literary contexts.

### Vocabulary and Numerals
Majority from Vulgar Latin/Greek roots; doublets common: native/popular vs. learned (e.g., *frère/fraternel*, *doigt/digital*, *foi/fidèle*, *œil/oculaire*). ~12% of common words (4,200/35,000) are "foreign" (excluding Greco-Latin): 25% from English (recent), 707 Italian, 550 Germanic, 481 Gallo-Romance, 215 Arabic, 164 German, 160 Celtic, etc. Toubon Law (1994) mandates French neologisms for imports (e.g., *mercatique* for *marketing*, *bloc-notes* for *notepad*). Numerals: mixed decimal/vigesimal (70=*soixante-dix*, 80=*quatre-vingts*, 90=*quatre-vingt-dix*); Belgium/Switzerland/Aosta/DRC/Rwanda/Burundi use *septante/huitante/nonante*. Decimal comma (2,5); space for thousands.

### International Status and Future Trajectory
French is an official language of the UN (1 of 2 Secretariat working languages), EU (1 of 3 procedural languages; sole working language of Court of Justice), NATO, IOC, WTO, Red Cross, etc. Ranked 2nd most influential language (Weber 1997/2008) after English. In EU, lost ground to English post-1995/2004 enlargements; Brexit (2016) rekindled debates on French's role. UK businesses rank French most valuable foreign language (50% of managers); MIT study shows 2.3% wage premium. OIF projects 466M–666M speakers by 2050 (9 of 10 in Africa). Growth drivers: African population growth, youth bulge (150M young Francophones, 2025), urbanization (70% urban), educational expansion (170M pupils in 36 countries, 80% in Africa), institutional networks (AEFE: 600 schools, 397K students; Alliance Française: ~1,000 branches, 450K students; Campus France: 440K international students). Non-Francophone states (Rwanda, Ethiopia, Ghana, Moldova, UAE) joining OIF for diplomatic/economic access. Media (TV5Monde, RFI, France 24), research (AUF), and cultural events (Jeux de la Francophonie) sustain global presence.

## Terms
- ****Vulgar Latin**** — The colloquial, non-literary varieties of Latin spoken by common people in the Roman Empire, ancestor of all Romance languages.
- ****Gallo-Romance**** — The subgroup of Romance languages originating in Roman Gaul, including French, Franco-Provençal, and the *langues d'oïl*.
- ****Langues d'oïl / Langues d'oc**** — Medieval dialect groups named for their word for "yes" (*oïl* > *oui* in north, *oc* in south); ancestors of French and Occitan respectively.
- ****Koiné**** — A standardized dialect arising from contact between mutually intelligible varieties (e.g., *Outremer* French in the Crusader states).
- ****Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts (1539)**** — Royal decree by Francis I making French the sole language of legal and administrative acts in France, replacing Latin.
- ****Patois**** — Pejorative term used in France for non-standard regional languages (Occitan, Breton, Basque, etc.) targeted for eradication by state education policy from 1794 onward.
- ****Vergonha**** — "Shame" in Occitan; the psychological process of making speakers feel their ancestral language is inferior to French.
- ****Francophonie / OIF**** — The global community of French-speaking peoples/states; *Organisation internationale de la Francophonie* (54 members) promotes French language and cooperation.
- ****Liaison**** — Phonological rule where a normally silent word-final consonant is pronounced before a following vowel-initial word (e.g., *les amis* [le.z‿a.mi]).
- ****Creole**** — A stable natural language developed from a pidgin, with native speakers; French-based creoles (e.g., Haitian) derive most vocabulary from French but have distinct grammars.

## Debates and open questions
- **Sahel rupture vs. persistence:** Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger have constitutionally demoted French (2023–25), yet it remains the *de facto* lingua franca of higher education, media, and interethnic commerce. Will formal status loss erode actual usage, or will French stabilize as a neutral "working" language?
- **African demographic projections:** OIF methodologies yield divergent totals (348M vs. 396M in 2025; 466M–666M by 2050). Projections assume sustained school enrollment and literacy; political instability, funding gaps, or language policy shifts could alter trajectories.
- **Linguistic center of gravity:** Youth sociolects (Camfranglais, Nouchi) and urban vernaculars in Kinshasa, Abidjan, Yaoundé are generating innovations that flow back to Europe via music/social media. Will a pan-African "African French" norm emerge to rival the Parisian standard?
- **EU institutional role:** Post-Brexit, French advocates push for restored parity with English in EU bodies. However, English remains entrenched as the default vehicular language among officials; French retains sole working language status only in the Court of Justice.
- **Creole recognition:** Haitian Creole and other French-based creoles have millions of native speakers but limited official status. Debates continue on standardization, education in creole vs. French, and linguistic discrimination.
- **Digital divide:** Only ~65.5% of Francophones have internet access (2025). As French ranks 4th online, unequal access may skew the digital corpus toward Northern/urban varieties, marginalizing rural African speakers.
