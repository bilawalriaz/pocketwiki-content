# History of economic thought

## Overview
The history of economic thought traces the evolution of ideas about wealth, value, markets, and policy from ancient times to the present. It matters because these philosophies have shaped economic systems, government policies, and societal well-being, moving from moral and philosophical inquiries to a formalized, mathematical discipline. The field shows a progression from early discussions of household management and just prices to modern debates over market efficiency, government intervention, and global inequality.

## Timeline
- **c. 360 BC** — Xenophon's *Oeconomicus* discusses household management.
- **c. 350 BC** — Aristotle's *Politics* analyzes property and the "art of acquisition."
- **1273** — Thomas Aquinas's *Summa Theologica* argues for a "just price."
- **1517** — Nicolaus Copernicus publishes the first argument for the quantity theory of money.
- **1776** — Adam Smith publishes *An Inquiry Into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations*.
- **1817** — David Ricardo's *On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation* is published.
- **1867** — Karl Marx publishes *Das Kapital*.
- **1871** — The marginalist revolution begins with works by Jevons, Menger, and Walras.
- **1936** — John Maynard Keynes publishes *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*.
- **1947** — Paul Samuelson's *Foundations of Economic Analysis* mathematizes economics.
- **1962** — Milton Friedman's *Capitalism and Freedom* advocates monetarism.
- **2008** — The global financial crisis prompts a resurgence of Keynesian thought and debate over austerity.

## Body

### Ancient and Medieval Thought
Early economic ideas were embedded in philosophy and theology. Ancient Greek thinkers like Aristotle examined household management (*oikonomia*) and the ethics of wealth acquisition, debating private versus public property. In medieval Europe, Scholastic philosophers like Thomas Aquinas developed the concept of a "just price" based on cost and social need, integrating Aristotle's ideas with Christian morality. Thinkers like Ibn Khaldun offered theories on the lifecycle of civilizations and money, while the School of Salamanca linked value to scarcity and market estimation.

### Mercantilism and Its Critics (16th-18th Centuries)
Mercantilism dominated European policy, advocating state intervention to achieve a favorable balance of trade and accumulate bullion. This era saw the rise of national economies and colonial trade. Critics emerged, including the Physiocrats like François Quesnay, who argued that agricultural surplus was the true source of wealth and advocated *laissez-faire* policies. Pre-classical thinkers like John Locke and David Hume laid groundwork for classical economics by emphasizing property rights, labor theory of value, and the self-regulating nature of markets.

### Classical Economics (Late 18th-19th Century)
Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776) systematized the idea of a "system of natural liberty," where the "invisible hand" of self-interest guides markets to efficient outcomes through competition and the division of labor. David Ricardo later refined theories of value, rent, and comparative advantage. The classical school, including John Stuart Mill, generally supported free markets but debated issues like population growth and income distribution. This era was challenged by Karl Marx, who critiqued capitalism's inherent contradictions, arguing that the labor theory of value revealed exploitation and that crises would lead to revolution.

### The Marginalist Revolution and Neoclassical Economics (Late 19th Century)
In the 1870s, a shift occurred from labor-based value to subjective value based on marginal utility—the satisfaction from consuming one additional unit. This "marginalist revolution," led by William Stanley Jevons, Carl Menger, and Léon Walras, formed the basis of neoclassical economics. Alfred Marshall later synthesized these ideas, popularizing supply-and-demand analysis and the term "economics." The Lausanne School developed general equilibrium theory, while the Austrian School, following Menger, emphasized deductive logic and subjective value.

### Keynesianism and the 20th Century
The Great Depression discredited classical orthodoxy. John Maynard Keynes's *General Theory* (1936) argued that aggregate demand drives output and employment, and that government intervention through fiscal policy is necessary to combat unemployment and stabilize economies. This led to the Keynesian consensus and the neoclassical synthesis. Post-WWII, the Chicago School, led by Milton Friedman, revived classical ideas, advocating monetarism and criticizing government intervention. The late 20th century saw the rise of New Classical Macroeconomics, which incorporated rational expectations, and New Keynesian economics, which provided microfoundations for sticky prices and market failures.

### Late 20th and 21st Century Developments
Economics became increasingly mathematical and specialized. Fields like game theory, information economics (Akerlof, Stiglitz), and behavioral economics emerged. The 2008 financial crisis revived Keynesian policy debates and criticism of austerity measures. Globalization spurred new trade theories, while development economics, influenced by thinkers like Amartya Sen, focused on poverty, inequality, and human capabilities. Institutional economics, revived by scholars like Douglass North, emphasized the role of rules and organizations in economic performance.

## Terms
- **Marginal Utility**: The additional satisfaction gained from consuming one more unit of a good or service.
- **Invisible Hand**: Adam Smith's metaphor for how individuals' self-interested actions in a free market can lead to beneficial social and economic outcomes.
- **Just Price**: A medieval concept, developed by Thomas Aquinas, that a price should be fair, cover the seller's costs, and be acceptable to the community.
- **Mercantilism**: An economic theory dominant in Europe from the 16th to 18th centuries that advocated state regulation to achieve a favorable balance of trade and accumulate national wealth in bullion.
- **Labor Theory of Value**: A theory, used by classical economists like Adam Smith and David Ricardo and central to Karl Marx's critique, that the economic value of a good is determined by the total labor required to produce it.
- **Keynesianism**: An economic theory, founded by John Maynard Keynes, that advocates for active government intervention through fiscal and monetary policy to manage aggregate demand and stabilize economies.
- **Monetarism**: A school of thought, led by Milton Friedman, that emphasizes the role of governments in controlling the amount of money in circulation as the primary tool for managing economic growth and inflation.
- **Rational Expectations**: An economic theory stating that individuals make decisions based on their rational outlook, available information, and past experiences, which can limit the effectiveness of government policy.
- **General Equilibrium Theory**: A macroeconomic theory that describes how supply and demand interact in all markets simultaneously to reach an economic equilibrium.
- **Information Economics**: A field studying how information asymmetries (where one party has more or better information) affect market outcomes, leading to problems like adverse selection and moral hazard.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Role of Government**: A persistent debate exists between those advocating for free markets (e.g., Chicago School) and those arguing for government intervention to correct market failures, reduce inequality, or stabilize economies (e.g., Keynesians).
- **Value Theory**: The fundamental question of what determines value—labor, utility, scarcity, or social estimation—has evolved but remains a point of divergence between schools like Marxian, neoclassical, and Austrian economics.
- **Causes of Crises**: Economists debate whether financial crises and recessions stem primarily from internal market dynamics (e.g., debt cycles, asset bubbles), external shocks, or flawed government policies.
- **Measurement and Goals**: There is ongoing debate over whether economic success should be measured by GDP growth alone or by broader metrics of human development, sustainability, and inequality, as highlighted by thinkers like Amartya Sen.
- **Globalization's Impact**: The effects of global trade and capital flows on domestic employment, inequality, and sovereignty continue to be contested, with theories ranging from comparative advantage to new trade theory and critiques of neoliberalism.
