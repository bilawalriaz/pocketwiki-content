# John the Apostle

## Overview
John the Apostle (c. 6 AD – c. 100 AD), son of Zebedee and brother of James the Great, was one of the Twelve Apostles of Jesus. Traditionally identified as the "Beloved Disciple" and the author of the Johannine works (Gospel of John, three epistles, and Revelation), he is venerated as a saint across Christian denominations. Modern scholarship largely disputes his direct authorship of these texts, suggesting instead that the Gospel and epistles originated from a "Johannine community" influenced by his testimony, while Revelation was likely written by a separate figure, John of Patmos. Extrabiblical tradition holds that he led the church in Ephesus, was exiled to Patmos, and died of natural causes—the only apostle to do so—though alternative accounts claim martyrdom.

## Timeline
- **c. 6 AD** — Birth (approximate) in Galilee to Zebedee and Salome.
- **c. 27–30 AD** — Called by Jesus as a disciple; forms inner circle with Peter and James.
- **c. 30 AD** — Present at the Last Supper, Crucifixion (entrusted with care of Mary), and empty tomb.
- **c. 30–44 AD** — Active in Jerusalem church; imprisoned with Peter; recognized as a "Pillar" by Paul (Gal 2:9).
- **44 AD** — Brother James martyred by Herod Agrippa I.
- **c. 48–50 AD** — Remains in Judea while other apostles attend the Apostolic Council in Jerusalem.
- **c. 50–90 AD** — Traditional relocation to Ephesus; leadership of Asian churches; authorship of epistles (traditional view).
- **c. 90–95 AD** — Exile to Patmos under Domitian; composition of Revelation (traditional view).
- **c. 98–100 AD** — Death of natural causes at Ephesus during reign of Trajan (traditional view).
- **787 AD** — *Acts of John* condemned as heretical at Second Council of Nicaea.

## Body

### New Testament Portrait and Role Among the Apostles
John was a Galilean fisherman called early in Jesus' ministry alongside his brother James; Jesus nicknamed them *Boanerges* ("sons of thunder"). He belonged to an informal inner triad with Peter and James, privileged to witness the raising of Jairus' daughter, the Transfiguration, and the Agony in Gethsemane. In the Synoptic Gospels and Acts, he is consistently listed among the first four apostles. After the Resurrection, he and Peter were central to the early Jerusalem church: they healed a lame man at the Temple, were imprisoned together, and visited new believers in Samaria. Paul explicitly names John, Peter, and James the Just as the three "Pillars of the Church" who recognized his Gentile mission (Galatians 2:9).

### The "Beloved Disciple" Identity
The Gospel of John uses the phrase "the disciple whom Jesus loved" six times, describing a figure present at the Last Supper (leaning on Jesus), the Crucifixion (receiving care of Mary), the empty tomb (arriving first), and the post-Resurrection fishing miracle (John 21). John 21:24 explicitly claims the Gospel is based on this disciple's testimony. While church tradition identifies this figure as John the Apostle, the Gospel never names him. The Synoptic Gospels do not mention a "beloved disciple" in parallel scenes (e.g., Luke 24:12 has Peter alone at the tomb). Scholars debate whether the figure is a historical apostle (John son of Zebedee, Lazarus, or another), a symbolic literary device, or the leader of the community that produced the Gospel.

### Authorship of the Johannine Works
**Traditional View:** Church Fathers (Irenaeus, Eusebius) attribute the Gospel, three epistles, and Revelation to John the Apostle. Eusebius (4th c.) notes the Gospel and 1 John were universally accepted, but 2–3 John and Revelation were disputed.
**Modern Critical Consensus:** Most scholars reject apostolic authorship of all five books.
*   **Gospel of John:** Dated c. 90–110 AD (some argue stages from 65–85 AD). Written in sophisticated Greek; Acts 4:13 calls John "unlettered" (agrammatos). The author claims eyewitness authority (19:35) but omits key events John witnessed (Gethsemane, eschatological discourses). Many scholars posit a "Johannine community" where a disciple of John (the Beloved Disciple or an amanuensis) composed the Gospel, preserving his testimony.
*   **Epistles:** 1, 2, and 3 John share vocabulary and theology; most agree they share a single author (the "Elder" of 2–3 John), distinct from the Gospel's final editor.
*   **Revelation:** Author identifies as "John" on Patmos c. 95–100 AD. Justin Martyr (c. 150 AD) first equated him with the Apostle. Eusebius and Jerome distinguished "John the Presbyter" (Elder) as the seer. Style and theology differ sharply from the Gospel; most scholars view John of Patmos as a separate prophet.

### Extrabiblical Traditions and Death
Tradition holds John ministered in Judea for ~12 years, then moved to Ephesus after Mary's Assumption. Irenaeus places him there until Trajan's reign (98–117 AD). Tertullian recounts a legend (c. 200 AD) that John survived boiling oil in Rome under Nero or Domitian, leading to exile on Patmos. He reportedly trained Polycarp (Bishop of Smyrna) and Ignatius of Antioch, linking apostolic teaching to the post-apostolic fathers. Irenaeus preserves a story of John fleeing a bathhouse to avoid the heretic Cerinthus.

**Death:** The dominant tradition states he died of natural causes at Ephesus c. 100 AD, the only non-martyred apostle. His tomb at Selçuk (near Ephesus) yielded no bones under Constantine, fueling beliefs in bodily assumption. An alternative tradition via Papias (early 2nd c.) claims he was slain by Jews; scholars debate Papias's reliability. Medieval legends claimed he did not die but sleeps in his tomb, emitting healing "manna" (dust) annually on May 8—a phenomenon reported by Augustine and the 14th-century traveler Catalan Muntaner, and alluded to by Melville in *Moby-Dick*. These legends likely stem from a literal reading of John 21:22–23 ("If I want him to remain until I come...").

### Liturgical and Cross-Tradition Veneration
*   **Western Christianity:** Feast day December 27 (Roman Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran). Formerly an Octave (to Jan 3) abolished 1955. Pre-1960 feast "St. John Before the Latin Gate" (May 6) commemorated the boiling-oil miracle.
*   **Eastern Orthodoxy:** Multiple feasts: Feb 15, May 8 (manna miracle), June 30 (Synaxis of Twelve), July 10, Aug 2, Sep 26 (Repose). Title: "John the Theologian."
*   **Islam:** Disciples unnamed in Quran but identified in exegesis as the Twelve, including John.
*   **Druze:** Honored as a prophet; Gospel writers seen as "carriers of wisdom."
*   **Latter-day Saints:** Identify John the Apostle = Evangelist = Patmos = Beloved Disciple. Teach he was "translated" (did not die) and remains a ministering servant until the Second Coming; appeared with Peter and James to Joseph Smith in 1829 to restore priesthood.
*   **Freemasonry:** Patron saint (with John the Baptist); Feast of St. John the Evangelist opens the Masonic year.

### Artistic Depiction
John is depicted in two distinct modes: as a beardless youth (common in Western Medieval art, reflecting legends of his young call) or as an aged, bearded elder (Byzantine style, possibly influenced by Socrates iconography). Key attributes: **chalice with serpent** (legend of drinking poison unharmed, from *Acts of John*), **book/scroll** (writings), **eagle** (symbol of theological soaring). Medieval and Renaissance art often portrays him androgynously or feminized; historians argue this made him relatable to women and served as an "image of mixed gender" for men cultivating *affective piety* (emotional devotion). *The Da Vinci Code* (2003) popularized the speculative claim that the feminine figure in Leonardo's *Last Supper* is Mary Magdalene, not John.

## Terms
- ****Boanerges**** — Aramaic nickname given by Jesus to James and John, meaning "sons of thunder" (Mark 3:17).
- ****Beloved Disciple**** — Anonymous figure in the Gospel of John ("the disciple whom Jesus loved"), traditionally identified with John the Apostle; claimed as the Gospel's source (John 21:24).
- ****Johannine Works**** — Collective term for the Gospel of John, 1–3 John, and Revelation, traditionally ascribed to John the Apostle.
- ****Johannine Community**** — Hypothetical early Christian group, centered perhaps in Ephesus, which produced the Gospel and epistles; posited to explain their distinct theology and style.
- ****John the Elder / Presbyter**** — Obscure early figure (mentioned by Papias, Eusebius) distinguished from the Apostle; proposed as author of 2–3 John and/or Revelation.
- ****John of Patmos**** — Author of Revelation, identifying himself as "John" exiled on Patmos; most scholars consider him distinct from the Apostle and the Gospel's author.
- ****Affective Piety**** — Late-medieval devotional style emphasizing emotional identification with Christ's suffering; John's feminized iconography aided male devotees.
- ****Translated Being**** — LDS doctrine: a person changed to an immortal state without dying; applied to John and the "Three Nephites."
- ****Manna (of St. John)**** — Miraculous dust/sand said to emanate annually from John's tomb in Ephesus on May 8, believed to heal and calm storms.
- ****Acts of John**** — 2nd-century apocryphal text (attributed to Leucius Charinus) recounting John's miracles; condemned as heretical (docetic) at Nicaea II (787); source of chalice/serpent iconography.

## Debates and Open Questions
1.  **Historicity of the Beloved Disciple:** Is the "disciple whom Jesus loved" a historical person (John son of Zebedee, Lazarus, an unnamed disciple) or a literary/rhetorical construct of the Gospel's author?
2.  **Authorship of the Gospel:** Did the Apostle John compose, dictate, or merely stand behind the Gospel? Was it written in stages by a community over decades?
3.  **Relationship of the Five Books:** Did one author write all Johannine works? Consensus: Epistles share an author (the Elder); Gospel and Epistles share theology but differ in redaction; Revelation is distinct in style, theology, and Greek.
4.  **John's Literacy and Language:** Acts 4:13 calls him *agrammatos* (unlettered); the Gospel is high-literary Greek. How to reconcile? (Amanuensis? Later composition by disciples?)
5.  **Date and Place of Death:** Natural death at Ephesus c. 100 AD (majority tradition) vs. martyrdom by Jews (Papias tradition) vs. non-death/translation (LDS, medieval "sleeping" legends).
6.  **Identity of John the Elder:** Is he a distinct historical figure from the Apostle, the author of 2–3 John, the seer of Revelation, or a conflation of these?
7.  **Reliability of Extrabiblical Legends:** Boiling oil (Tertullian), Cerinthus bathhouse (Irenaeus), manna miracle (Augustine, Muntaner)—historical kernels or hagiographic embellishment?
