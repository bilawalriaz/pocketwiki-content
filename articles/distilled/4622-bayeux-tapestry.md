# Bayeux Tapestry

## Overview
The Bayeux Tapestry is a monumental 11th-century embroidered cloth, nearly 70 metres long, that narrates the Norman Conquest of England in 1066. It is a primary historical source for the events, arms, and culture of the period, and a rare surviving example of secular Romanesque art. Its creation, likely in England for William the Conqueror's half-brother, and its subsequent history make it a globally significant artifact.

## Timeline
- **1064-1066** — Events depicted: Harold's oath to William, Edward's death, Harold's coronation, Norman invasion, Battle of Hastings.
- **c. 1070s** — Tapestry likely commissioned by Bishop Odo of Bayeux and made in England.
- **1476** — First known written reference in an inventory of Bayeux Cathedral.
- **1562** — Survives the sack of Bayeux by Huguenots.
- **1729** — Rediscovered by scholars; Bernard de Montfaucon publishes detailed drawings.
- **1792** — Confiscated during French Revolution for use as wagon covering; later rescued.
- **1797** — Exhibited at the Louvre, causing a public sensation.
- **1842** — Displayed in a purpose-built room in Bayeux.
- **1944** — Seized by the Gestapo; nearly taken to Berlin before being recovered.
- **2007** — Admitted to UNESCO's Memory of the World Register.
- **2026-2027** — Loaned to the British Museum in London, its first return to England in ~900 years.

## Body

### Origins and Commission
The tapestry's origins are debated. While French legend credits Queen Matilda, scholarly consensus points to Bishop Odo of Bayeux, William's half-brother, as the likely commissioner. Evidence includes Odo's followers appearing in the work, its discovery in the cathedral he built, and its probable completion for the cathedral's dedication c. 1077. It was most likely designed and embroidered in England by Anglo-Saxon needleworkers, possibly for display in Odo's palace. Alternative theories suggest commissioners like Edith of Wessex or designers like Abbot Scolland or Archbishop Lanfranc.

### Construction and Design
Technically an embroidery, not a woven tapestry, it is worked in coloured wool yarn on a linen ground. It consists of nine panels sewn together, with a central narrative zone and decorative borders. The design uses outline and couching stitches. The borders contain animals, fables, and scenes that sometimes comment on the main action, including the first known depiction of Halley's Comet. The end of the tapestry is missing, and the final Latin inscription is likely a later addition.

### Narrative and Historical Content
The tapestry tells the story from a Norman perspective, likely as propaganda justifying the conquest. It depicts Edward the Confessor sending Harold to Normandy, Harold's oath to William (the nature of which is unclear), Harold's coronation after Edward's death, the Norman invasion, and the Battle of Hastings. Key figures include William, Harold, and Bishop Odo. The work is invaluable for its depictions of 11th-century arms, armour, and daily life, though it contains no hereditary coats of arms.

### Recorded History and Modern Significance
After its 1476 inventory listing, the tapestry survived wars and revolutions. It was rediscovered by scholars in 1729, confiscated during the French Revolution, and nearly lost during World War II. It is now a UNESCO-listed treasure. A major loan to the British Museum in 2026-2027, secured with a £800 million liability guarantee from the UK government, has generated immense public interest, with tickets selling out instantly.

## Terms
- **Opus Anglicanum:** A style of fine embroidery produced in England, famous across medieval Europe.
- **Crewel:** A type of wool yarn used for embroidery.
- **Tituli:** The Latin inscriptions embroidered on the tapestry to explain scenes and name people.
- **Motte and bailey:** A simple fortification consisting of a raised earth mound (motte) and an enclosed courtyard (bailey).
- **Witenagemot:** The council of nobles that advised the Anglo-Saxon king and, with him, decided on succession.
- **Apologia:** A written or spoken defense of one's opinions or conduct; the tapestry is seen as a visual apologia for the Norman Conquest.
- **Dux:** Latin for "leader" or "duke," used for both William and Harold before his coronation.
- **Rex:** Latin for "king," used for Harold after his coronation.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Harold's Death:** The traditional depiction of Harold killed by an arrow to the eye is disputed. Earlier engravings show a spear, and needle holes suggest the arrow may be a later modification. Some historians argue he was slain by a sword in the subsequent scene.
- **The Ælfgyva Scene:** The meaning of the panel showing a cleric and a woman named Ælfgyva is unknown. Speculation links it to a contemporary scandal or a marriage arrangement involving Harold's sister.
- **Missing Panels:** At least two panels are missing, likely depicting William's coronation. Their absence leaves the narrative incomplete.
- **Historical Accuracy:** While a Norman apologia, the tapestry's accuracy on specific events (e.g., who crowned Harold) is debated against other contemporary sources.
