# Historiography

## Overview
Historiography is the study of how history is written, encompassing the methods, sources, and theoretical approaches historians use. It matters because historical interpretation is not fixed; it evolves as scholars apply new techniques, uncover new sources, and re-examine the past through different cultural and intellectual lenses. Understanding historiography reveals how our knowledge of the past is constructed and debated.

## Timeline
- **5th century BC** — Herodotus establishes Greek historiography with *The Histories*.
- **2nd century BC** — Cato the Elder writes *Origines*, the first Roman historiography in Latin.
- **c. 100 BC** — Sima Qian and Sima Tan establish Chinese historiography with the *Shiji*.
- **1751-1756** — Voltaire publishes key Enlightenment histories, emphasizing social and cultural history.
- **1776** — Edward Gibbon publishes *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*.
- **1824** — Leopold von Ranke begins publishing, pioneering source-based, professional history.
- **1848** — Thomas Babington Macaulay publishes *The History of England*, a classic "Whig history."
- **1929** — The *Annales* journal is founded, launching a school focused on long-term social history.
- **1961** — E. H. Carr publishes *What Is History?*, sparking debate on historical objectivity.
- **1963** — E. P. Thompson's *The Making of the English Working Class* pioneers "history from below."
- **1980s** — The "cultural turn" shifts focus to language, symbols, and memory studies.

## Body

### Origins and Classical Foundations
The academic discipline began in 5th-century BC Greece with Herodotus, who researched and narrated past events. His successor, Thucydides, established a rationalistic precedent by eliminating divine causality. In Rome, Cato the Elder initiated Latin historiography. In China, Sima Qian and his father created the "annals-biography" format with the *Shiji*, which became the standard for Chinese historical writing.

### Medieval and Enlightenment Developments
Medieval historiography was dominated by chronicles and annals, often written by clergy. Christian historians like Eusebius and Bede incorporated theology and a linear view of time. In the Islamic world, historians developed rigorous methods to verify sources, culminating in the work of Ibn Khaldun. During the Enlightenment, Voltaire, David Hume, and Edward Gibbon transformed history into a modern discipline. They emphasized social history, critical use of primary sources, and secular analysis, moving away from theological and political narratives.

### 19th-Century Professionalization and Schools
The 19th century saw history become a professional academic discipline. Leopold von Ranke in Germany established the seminar method and insisted on writing history "as it essentially was" based on primary sources. This era also produced major schools of thought: the "Whig history" of Thomas Macaulay, which presented the past as inevitable progress toward liberal democracy; the Marxist historiography of Karl Marx and later scholars like E. P. Thompson, which focused on class struggle and economic forces; and the French *Annales* school, which pioneered the study of long-term social structures (*la longue durée*).

### 20th-Century Shifts and Debates
The 20th century was marked by a shift from political and diplomatic history toward social, cultural, and economic history. The *Annales* school, led by Fernand Braudel, emphasized geography and mentalities. Marxist historians analyzed history "from below." A major debate erupted between E. H. Carr, who argued history is a dialogue between past and present, and traditionalists like Geoffrey Elton, who defended objective, evidence-based history. The "cultural turn" of the 1980s further redirected focus to language, symbols, and memory studies.

## Terms
- **Historiography**: The study of historical writing; the history of how history has been researched and interpreted.
- **Primary Source**: Original documents or artifacts from the period under study (e.g., diaries, government records).
- **Annales School**: A French historical movement emphasizing long-term social history, geography, and interdisciplinary methods.
- **Whig History**: An interpretation of the past as an inevitable march of progress toward modern liberal institutions.
- **Historical Materialism**: A Marxist theory that economic conditions and class struggle are the primary drivers of historical change.
- **La longue durée**: A concept from the *Annales* school focusing on slow, long-term historical structures and processes.
- **History from below**: A historical approach focusing on the experiences and perspectives of ordinary people, not just elites.
- **Cultural Turn**: A late-20th-century shift in historiography toward analyzing culture, language, symbols, and representation.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Objectivity vs. Subjectivity**: A central debate, exemplified by E. H. Carr's argument that historians choose facts based on present concerns versus Geoffrey Elton's defense of objective, evidence-based analysis.
- **The Role of Theory**: Ongoing tension between historians who use social science theories (like Marxism or structuralism) and those who favor narrative and empirical description.
- **Whig History and Teleology**: The critique of "Whig history" for assuming a goal-directed, progressive past remains a key methodological concern.
- **The Influence of the Historian's Context**: The extent to which historians' own national, cultural, or political loyalties shape their interpretations is a perpetual question.
