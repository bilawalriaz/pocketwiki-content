# Mir Taqi Mir

## Overview
Mir Taqi Mir (1723–1810) was a foundational Urdu poet of 18th-century Mughal India whose work shaped the Urdu language itself. A principal figure of the Delhi School of the ghazal (a lyric form of rhyming couplets), he balanced indigenous Hindustani with Persian diction to create the elite poetic register known as Rekhta. His life traced the collapse of Mughal Delhi: after witnessing the city’s repeated sacking by Ahmad Shah Abdali (from 1748), he migrated to Lucknow in 1782, where he died in isolation. His poetry is defined by profound grief—over personal loss, the ruin of Delhi, and the human condition—earning him recognition as a master of pathos.

## Timeline
- **Feb 1723** — Born in Agra (Akbarabad) to Mir Abdullah; grandfather migrated from Hejaz via Hyderabad.
- **Teen years** — Father dies, leaving debt; Mir leaves Agra for Delhi to complete education and seek patronage.
- **1748 onward** — Ahmad Shah Abdali sacks Delhi annually; Mir witnesses the city’s devastation.
- **1782** — Migrates to Lucknow at the invitation of Nawab Asaf-ud-Daulah.
- **1782–1810** — Resides in Lucknow; relations with court sour; dismisses new Lucknow poetry as frivolous.
- **21 Sep 1810** — Dies in Lucknow from a purgative overdose; grave later lost to railway construction.
- **1970s** — Cenotaph erected near burial site by Maqbool Ahmed Lari, founder of Mir Academy.

## Body

### Early Life and Formation
Mir was born in Agra in February 1723. His grandfather had migrated from Hejaz to Hyderabad and then to Agra. His father, Mir Abdullah, was a religious figure with a large following whose emphasis on love and compassion profoundly shaped Mir’s worldview and poetry. After his father’s death in Mir’s teens—leaving behind debts—Mir relocated to Delhi to finish his education and secure patrons. He received a daily allowance from the Mughal Amir-ul-Umara and Mir Bakhshi, Khan-i Dauran, a fellow Agra native. The primary source for his early life is his Persian autobiography, *Zikr-e-Mir*, though scholars consider it unreliable, as it conceals more than it reveals and lacks chronological order. Two early *masnavis* (long narrative poems in rhyming couplets), *Mu'amlat-e-ishq* (The Stages of Love) and *Khwab o Khyal-e Mir*, are debated as autobiographical accounts of love affairs and madness; scholars contrast their austere tone with suggestions of eroticism in his other verse.

### The Collapse of Delhi and Migration
Mir lived for decades in Mughal Delhi, residing in Kuchha Chelan. The repeated invasions of Ahmad Shah Abdali beginning in 1748 devastated the city, a trauma Mir lamented in famous couplets mourning Delhi’s transformation from a chosen metropolis to a desolate ruin. In 1782, he accepted an invitation to the court of Asaf-ud-Daulah in Lucknow. Though welcomed, he found the Lucknow courtiers considered him old-fashioned; he reciprocated by dismissing the new Lucknow poetry—specifically the poet Jur’at’s work—as mere "kissing and cuddling." His relationship with his patron deteriorated until he severed ties, spending his final years in deep isolation, compounded by the deaths of his daughter, son, and wife.

### Literary Achievement and Language
Mir’s complete works (*Kulliyat*) comprise six *diwans* (volumes) totaling 13,585 couplets across forms including *ghazal*, *masnavi*, *qasida* (panegyric), *rubai* (quatrain), and satire. His reputation rests on the *ghazals*, many exploring love, and the *masnavi Mu'amlat-e-Ishq*, considered a pinnacle of Urdu love poetry. Writing when Urdu was still formative, Mir synthesized his native Hindustani with Persian imagery and idiom to forge *Rekhta* (or *Hindui*), a poetic language at once simple, natural, and elegant that guided later generations. He credited a Sayyid from Amroha, Syed Sadaat Ali, for urging him to write in Urdu, then gaining currency as the language of Hindustan by royal authority. Personal tragedies and the trauma of Delhi’s fall infused his work with a signature pathos and melancholy.

### Major Works
Beyond the Urdu *Kulliyat-e-Mir*, Mir authored several Persian works: *Zikr-e-Mir* (autobiography); *Nikat-us-Shura*, a biographical dictionary of contemporary Urdu poets; *Faiz-e-Mir*, five didactic stories on Sufis and *faqirs* (mendicants) written for his son Mir Faiz Ali; and *Kulliyat-e-Farsi*, a Persian poetry collection. He also composed *Mir Taqi Mir Ki Rubaiyat*, a collection of quatrains.

### Legacy and Reception
Mir was a contemporary of the noted poet Mirza Rafi Sauda. Later critics and poets frequently compare him to Mirza Ghalib (1797–1869); Ghalib himself acknowledged Mir’s genius in verse, as did Ghalib’s rival Zauq. Mir appears in modern fiction, notably Khushwant Singh’s novel *Delhi: A Novel*, and the 2016 Pakistani biographical film *Mah e Mir*.

## Terms
- **Ghazal**: A lyric poem composed of rhyming couplets (*sher*) with a refrain, traditionally expressing love, loss, or mystical longing; the primary form of Mir’s acclaim.
- **Masnavi**: A long narrative poem in rhyming couplets; Mir’s *Mu'amlat-e-ishq* is a celebrated example.
- **Rekhta / Hindui**: The early elite register of Urdu poetry, blending Hindustani grammar with Persian vocabulary and idiom; Mir was a principal architect of this synthesis.
- **Takhallus**: A poet’s pen name; Mir used "Mir" as his *takhallus*, often embedded in the final couplet of a *ghazal*.
- **Diwan**: A collected volume of a poet’s work; Mir’s *Kulliyat* consists of six *diwans*.
- **Qasida**: A formal panegyric or ode, often praising a patron; one of the forms in Mir’s *Kulliyat*.
- **Rubai**: A quatrain (four-line stanza); Mir compiled a separate collection, *Mir Taqi Mir Ki Rubaiyat*.
- **Faqir**: A Muslim ascetic or mendicant; subject of Mir’s didactic work *Faiz-e-Mir*.
- **Amir-ul-Umara / Mir Bakhshi**: High Mughal court titles; Khan-i Dauran held these posts and patronized Mir in Delhi.
- **Zikr-e-Mir**: Mir’s Persian autobiography, the main (though unreliable) source for his early life.

## Debates and open questions
- **Autobiographical reliability**: *Zikr-e-Mir* is undated and non-chronological; scholars debate how much of Mir’s early life—including the love affairs depicted in his *masnavis*—is factual versus literary convention.
- **Tone of early verse**: Critics contrast the spiritual austerity of *Mu'amlat-e-ishq* with evidence of erotic frankness in other poems, raising questions about Mir’s poetic persona.
- **Comparative supremacy**: The literary debate over whether Mir or the later Mirza Ghalib is the "greater" Urdu poet remains active, though Ghalib and his rival Zauq both deferred to Mir’s genius.
- **Grave location**: The exact burial site was lost when railway tracks were built over it; the 1970s cenotaph marks only the vicinity.
