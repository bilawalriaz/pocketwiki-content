# Outback

## Overview
The Outback is a vast, remote, and sparsely populated region of Australia, unified by low human population density and a largely intact natural environment. It is deeply ingrained in Australian heritage and history, and its global significance lies in being one of the largest remaining intact natural areas on Earth.

## Timeline
- **c. 50,000 years ago** — Aboriginal peoples have lived in the Outback, occupying all regions including deserts.
- **1813** — Gregory Blaxland's party first crosses the Blue Mountains.
- **1817–1821** — John Oxley explores inland rivers, seeking an "inland sea."
- **1829–1830** — Charles Sturt follows rivers, finding they flow into the Murray and Darling systems.
- **1858–1861** — John McDouall Stuart leads six expeditions, successfully crossing the continent.
- **1860–61** — The Burke and Wills expedition ends in disaster with three deaths.
- **1865** — Surveyor George Goyder maps a rainfall reliability line in South Australia.
- **1870s** — The Overland Telegraph line is built along Stuart's route.
- **1928** — The Royal Flying Doctor Service begins operations.
- **1950s** — Len Beadell explores and builds roads for nuclear and rocket testing.
- **2002** — Declared the "Year of the Outback."

## Body

### History and Exploration
Aboriginal peoples have been the Traditional Owners of the Outback for at least 50,000 years. European exploration initially focused on coasts, with inland journeys beginning in the 19th century. Early explorers like Oxley and Sturt sought an inland sea but found river systems flowing south. The "Afghan" cameleers were instrumental in opening up the region from 1858. Successful crossings, like Stuart's, contrasted with failed expeditions like Burke and Wills. Infrastructure followed, including the Overland Telegraph line in the 1870s. Exploration continued into the 20th century for military and mineral purposes.

### Environment and Ecosystems
The Outback is globally significant for its intact natural state, comparable to the Boreal forests and Amazon Basin. It contains major ecosystems including the savanna of the Top End (the world's largest intact savanna), the ten central deserts, and the Great Western Woodlands (the largest remaining temperate woodland). Wildlife is well-adapted but often hidden; prolific birdlife congregates at waterholes. Feral animals like camels and brumbies (wild horses) are present, alongside native species such as kangaroos, emus, and dingoes. The Dingo Fence was built to protect southern agricultural areas. Riversleigh in Queensland is a World Heritage fossil site.

### Industry and Economy
Pastoralism (livestock grazing) is the largest industry by area, enabled by bore water from aquifers like the Great Artesian Basin. Many properties are certified organic. Tourism is a major industry, marketing nature-based and Indigenous experiences, with attractions like Uluru and Kakadu National Park. Mining is the other primary economic activity, exploiting rich deposits of iron, aluminium, uranium, gold, and opals due to a lack of geological disturbance since the Permian period. Major mining areas include the Pilbara (iron ore, oil, gas) and Coober Pedy (opals).

### Population and Facilities
The total Outback population was estimated at 607,000, with 17% identifying as Indigenous. Population declined slightly from 1996 to 2006, with increases in the Kimberley and Pilbara. Essential services include the Royal Flying Doctor Service (established 1928) for medical care and the School of the Air for educating children in remote communities. Transport relies on major highways like the Stuart Highway, with air transport crucial for mail and medical services due to sparse settlement and seasonal road closures.

## Terms
- **Outback**: Remote, sparsely populated regions of Australia behind the cities and towns.
- **Pastoralism**: Livestock grazing (cattle, sheep) in natural ecosystems, reliant on the environment.
- **Traditional Owners**: Aboriginal peoples legally recognised as having native title to their traditional lands.
- **Great Artesian Basin**: One of the largest underground water resources in the world, enabling pastoralism in arid areas.
- **Native Title**: Commonwealth legislation recognising Aboriginal land rights.
- **Brumbies**: Feral horses descended from station horses.
- **School of the Air**: Educational service for remote children, originally via radio.
- **Royal Flying Doctor Service (RFDS)**: Aeromedical service providing healthcare to remote areas.
- **Palaeosols**: Ancient, infertile soils that dominate much of the Outback.
