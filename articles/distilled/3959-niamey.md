# Niamey

## Overview
Niamey is the capital and largest city of Niger, serving as the nation's primary economic, political, and cultural hub. Situated on the Niger River, its development from a cluster of pre-colonial villages into a modern metropolis reflects Niger's colonial history, post-independence economic cycles, and rapid, often unplanned, urban growth driven by migration and climate events.

## Timeline
- **1901** — Captain Henri Salaman establishes the first French military post at Niamey.
- **1903** — Niamey replaces Zinder as the capital of the Third Military Territory of Niger.
- **1926** — Niamey is permanently re-established as the capital of the Colony of Niger.
- **1930** — The first city plan is implemented, creating segregated European and indigenous quarters.
- **1956** — Niamey holds Niger's first municipal elections; Djibo Bakary becomes the first mayor.
- **1960** — Niger gains independence; Niamey's population is 33,816.
- **1970** — The Kennedy Bridge opens, connecting the left and right banks of the Niger River.
- **1970s-80s** — A uranium boom and severe droughts fuel rapid population growth and urban sprawl.
- **1990s** — Protests in Niamey contribute to national democratisation; an Islamic revival begins.
- **2024** — The military junta dissolves the municipal government, placing the city under military rule.

## Body

### Historical Development
Before French colonization, the Niamey area was a buffer zone between Sahelian kingdoms, settled by Zarma, Songhai, Hausa, and Fula peoples. The French established a military post in 1901 and made Niamey the capital in 1903, though it was briefly moved back to Zinder in 1911. It was permanently re-established as the capital in 1926, leading to large-scale development and the implementation of segregated urban plans. After independence in 1960, Niamey's growth was initially slow but accelerated dramatically in the 1970s and 1980s due to a uranium boom and drought-induced rural migration. This period saw significant infrastructure projects like the Kennedy Bridge but also the proliferation of informal settlements. The 1990s brought political protests, democratisation, and an Islamic revival. Recent years have been marked by urban redevelopment projects and, since 2023, military rule following a coup.

### Geography and Climate
Niamey is located on the Niger River, primarily on its left (east) bank. The river is the city's only permanent water source and is prone to seasonal flooding from two distinct floods: the local "red flood" and the distant "Guinean flood." The city has a hot semi-arid climate, making it one of the world's hottest major cities. Temperatures regularly exceed 40°C (104°F) in the hot dry season. The region experiences a pattern of droughts and floods, with climate change contributing to increased surface water levels despite reduced rainfall—a phenomenon known as the "Niamey paradox."

### Demographics and Society
Niamey is a highly diverse and cosmopolitan city, with residents from all of Niger's ethnic groups. The Hausa and Zarma languages are the primary lingua francas. The city's population is extremely young and has one of the highest growth rates in the world, driven by natural increase and constant rural-to-urban migration, particularly by Hausa men seeking work. This migration is often circular, swelling the population during the dry season. Most residents work in the informal economy, and wealth disparity is significant. An Islamic revival since the 1990s has increased religious activism and the influence of movements like Izala (Salafism) and Tariqa (Sufism).

### Economy and Infrastructure
Niamey's economy is dominated by commerce, largely within the informal sector, including extensive street markets and urban agriculture. Industrial activity is limited. The city's infrastructure struggles to keep pace with growth. While it has an international airport and major highways, public utilities are inconsistent. As of 2005, only about 54% of households had electricity and 38% had piped water. Access is particularly poor in informal settlements, which are often excluded from official grids. Water is supplied by the Niger River and distributed through a mix of pipes, standpipes, and a large network of informal vendors. Waste management is rudimentary, with no standardized collection system.

### Governance and Administration
Niamey constitutes the Niamey Urban Community (CUN), a first-level administrative division of Niger. It is divided into five municipal districts (communes). Governance has historically involved a mix of appointed officials (like the CUN Governor) and elected councils. However, in April 2024, the military junta dissolved the municipal government, replacing the mayor with an army official. Traditional chiefs still play a role in land allocation and community representation, particularly in the informal settlements that expand the city's boundaries semi-legally.

## Terms
- **CUN (Communauté Urbaine de Niamey):** The first-level administrative division that constitutes the capital district of Niamey.
- **Quartier non-loti:** An informal settlement or neighborhood constructed without official administrative approval, often allocated by traditional chiefs.
- **Ga'ruwa:** Water deliverers who purchase water from standpipes and sell it to households via pushcart, providing over half of the city's drinking water.
- **Akwaku:** Street vendors who sell generic pharmaceuticals in individual portions, operating without formal pharmaceutical expertise.
- **Fadas:** Social conversation groups for young men, often unemployed, which originated in Niamey during the 1990s.
- **Hira groups:** Hausa conversation groups that assemble at streetside spots, often based on hometown connections.
- **Izala:** A Salafi Islamic movement opposed to Sufism that gained traction in Niamey in the 1990s.
- **Tariqa:** A Sufi Islamic movement that is popular in Niamey.
- **Niamey paradox:** The phenomenon of increased surface water levels in the Niamey region following decreases in rainfall.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The etymology of the name "Niamey" is debated, with different oral histories from the Maouri, Zarma, and Songhai peoples offering distinct explanations.
- The exact chronology of Niamey's early settlement is uncertain, with oral histories differing on the sequence of village foundations.
- The effectiveness and equity of urban planning and utility provision are ongoing issues, particularly regarding the exclusion of informal settlements from public services and the impact of gentrification projects.
