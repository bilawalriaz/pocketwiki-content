# Persian Constitutional Revolution

## Overview
The Persian Constitutional Revolution (1905–1911) was the first successful movement in the Islamic world to establish a parliament and a constitution, fundamentally challenging the absolute monarchy of the Qajar dynasty. It was a complex struggle between reformers seeking modern, law-based governance and traditional forces defending royal and clerical authority, ultimately reshaping Iran's political landscape and setting a precedent for future revolutions.

## Timeline
- **1891–1892** — Tobacco Protest: First nationwide mass movement against a foreign concession, seen as a "dress rehearsal" for the revolution.
- **1905** — Protests erupt against tariff impositions to repay a Russian loan for the Shah's European tour.
- **Jan 1906** — Shah agrees to dismiss his prime minister and create a "house of justice" after protests.
- **Aug 1906** — Mozaffar ad-Din Shah agrees to a parliament (Majlis).
- **Dec 1906** — Shah signs the constitution, modeled on the Belgian constitution, shortly before his death.
- **1907** — Anglo-Russian Convention divides Iran into British and Russian spheres of influence.
- **1908** — Mohammad Ali Shah Qajar stages a coup, bombards the Majlis, and begins the "Minor Tyranny."
- **1909** — Constitutionalists march on Tehran, force the Shah's abdication, and re-establish the constitution.
- **Dec 1911** — Revolution ends when deputies of the Second Majlis are expelled with Russian military support.

## Body

### Causes and Background
The revolution arose from deep discontent with Qajar rule, marked by foreign economic domination, military weakness, and royal extravagance financed by crippling loans. The 1891–92 Tobacco Protest, where a nationwide boycott forced the Shah to cancel a British tobacco monopoly, demonstrated the power of a coalition between bazaaris (merchants), ulama (clerics), and intellectuals. This alliance, united by common enemies rather than goals, sought to curb royal power and foreign influence. The Russo-Japanese War (1904–05) and Russian Revolution further inspired opposition, as constitutions were seen as the "secret of strength" of Western powers.

### The Constitutional Struggle
Protests in 1905–06 led to the creation of the first Majlis and the 1906 constitution, which established a parliament and made royal power contingent on the law. The 1907 Fundamental Laws defined a bicameral legislature and declared Twelver Shi'ism the state religion, with a clerical council to ensure laws complied with Islam. However, Shah Mohammad Ali Qajar, with foreign backing, abolished the constitution in 1908. After a civil war, constitutionalist forces, led by figures like Sattar Khan, re-established the parliament in 1909. The revolution collapsed in 1911 when the Second Majlis was forcibly dissolved by the Shah's cabinet, backed by 12,000 Russian troops.

### The Religious Debate
The revolution sparked a major theological debate within Shi'a Islam. The highest-ranking clerics in Najaf (Akhund Khurasani, Mirza Husayn Tehrani, and Shaykh Abdullah Mazandarani) supported the constitution, arguing that democracy was a "lesser evil" that reduced tyranny during the occultation (absence) of the Hidden Imam. In opposition, cleric Fazlollah Nouri argued that Sharia was a complete code of life, making a secular constitution unnecessary and un-Islamic. This split revealed a fundamental conflict over the role of religion in governance, with the pro-constitutional clergy establishing a model of religious secularity in government.

## Terms
*   **Majlis:** The national consultative assembly or parliament established by the constitution.
*   **Qajar dynasty:** The ruling dynasty of Iran from 1789 to 1925.
*   **Ulama:** Islamic scholars and clergy.
*   **Bazaari:** The merchant class of the traditional bazaar.
*   **Marja'-e taqlid (Marja'):** A high-ranking Shi'a cleric considered a "source of emulation" for followers.
*   **Fatwa:** A ruling on a point of Islamic law given by a recognized authority.
*   **Occultation (Ghayba):** In Twelver Shi'ism, the period of absence of the Hidden Imam, the last divinely appointed leader.
*   **Minor Tyranny:** The period of Mohammad Ali Shah's rule (1908–1909) after his coup against the parliament.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Legacy and Interpretation:** The revolution's legacy is contested. Ayatollah Khomeini later dismissed the 1906 constitution as an imperialist plot, while the Islamic Republic paradoxically honored the anti-constitutionalist cleric Fazlollah Nouri.
*   **Clerical Role:** The debate between Nouri and the Najaf marjas over whether Sharia permits or forbids constitutional government remains a central question in Iranian political theology.
*   **Coalition Dynamics:** Scholars analyze why the fragile alliance of ulama, bazaaris, and intellectuals ultimately fractured, leading to the revolution's failure.
