# Martin Luther King Jr.

## Overview
Martin Luther King Jr. (1929–1968) was an American Baptist minister and the most prominent leader of the civil rights movement. He advanced racial equality through nonviolent resistance and civil disobedience, helping to secure landmark federal legislation and becoming a global symbol of the struggle for justice.

## Timeline

- **Dec 1, 1955** — Rosa Parks arrested, sparking the Montgomery bus boycott led by King.
- **1957** — Co-founds the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC).
- **Sep 20, 1958** — Survives a stabbing in Harlem.
- **Apr 1963** — Leads the Birmingham campaign; writes "Letter from Birmingham Jail."
- **Aug 28, 1963** — Delivers "I Have a Dream" speech at the March on Washington.
- **Oct 14, 1964** — Awarded the Nobel Peace Prize.
- **Mar 7, 1965** — "Bloody Sunday" in Selma helps galvanize support for voting rights.
- **Apr 4, 1967** — Delivers "Beyond Vietnam" speech opposing the war.
- **Apr 4, 1968** — Assassinated in Memphis, Tennessee.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born Michael King Jr., he was renamed Martin Luther King Jr. at age 5 after his father adopted the name of the Protestant Reformation leader. Raised in Atlanta, he experienced segregation firsthand, including a childhood friendship that ended due to racial prejudice. He entered Morehouse College at 15, graduated in 1948, and earned a Ph.D. in systematic theology from Boston University in 1955. A 1991 academic inquiry found his dissertation contained plagiarism, but his degree was not revoked.

### Rise to Leadership and the Montgomery Bus Boycott
King became pastor of Dexter Avenue Baptist Church in Montgomery, Alabama, in 1954. Following Rosa Parks' 1955 arrest, he led the 385-day Montgomery bus boycott, which ended with a court ruling desegregating public buses. This victory transformed him into a national figure. In 1957, he co-founded the SCLC to harness the moral authority of Black churches for nonviolent protest.

### Major Campaigns and Legislative Victories
The SCLC, under King's leadership, employed strategic, nonviolent confrontation. The 1963 Birmingham campaign, marked by violent police responses to child protesters, generated national outrage and helped spur the Civil Rights Act of 1964. King's "I Have a Dream" speech at the 1963 March on Washington became iconic. Later, the 1965 Selma voting rights marches, culminating in "Bloody Sunday," were pivotal in passing the Voting Rights Act of 1965. King's final years saw a shift toward economic justice with the Poor People's Campaign and vocal opposition to the Vietnam War, which cost him some political allies.

### Assassination and Aftermath
On April 4, 1968, King was assassinated in Memphis, where he was supporting striking sanitation workers. James Earl Ray was convicted of the murder, though conspiracy theories persist. King's death sparked riots in numerous cities and helped accelerate the passage of the Fair Housing Act of 1968. He was posthumously awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom (1977) and the Congressional Gold Medal (2003).

### FBI Surveillance and Personal Life
From 1963, the FBI, under J. Edgar Hoover, conducted intensive surveillance of King through its COINTELPRO program, seeking to discredit him by alleging communist ties and documenting his extramarital affairs. An anonymous 1964 FBI letter, accompanied by recordings, threatened to expose his private life and was interpreted by King as an attempt to make him commit suicide. Historians debate the reliability of later FBI allegations regarding his personal conduct.

## Terms
- **Nonviolent resistance:** A strategy for social change that avoids physical violence, using methods like marches, sit-ins, and civil disobedience to protest injustice.
- **Civil disobedience:** The nonviolent refusal to obey unjust laws as a form of peaceful protest.
- **Jim Crow laws:** State and local statutes that enforced racial segregation in the Southern United States.
- **Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC):** An organization founded in 1957 by King and others to coordinate nonviolent civil rights activism among Black churches.
- **COINTELPRO:** A series of covert and illegal FBI projects aimed at surveilling, infiltrating, and disrupting domestic political organizations, including the civil rights movement.
- **Poor People's Campaign:** A 1968 effort organized by King to address economic inequality through a multiracial coalition demanding federal anti-poverty measures.
- **"Letter from Birmingham Jail":** A 1963 open letter by King defending nonviolent civil disobedience against calls for patience and legal channels.
- **"I Have a Dream":** The famous speech delivered by King at the 1963 March on Washington, envisioning a future of racial harmony and equality.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Plagiarism in his dissertation:** A 1991 Boston University inquiry found King improperly included passages without citation, but concluded his work still made an "intelligent contribution to scholarship."
- **Conspiracy theories surrounding his assassination:** While James Earl Ray pleaded guilty, his later recantation and a 1999 civil trial verdict finding a conspiracy involving "other unknown co-conspirators" have fueled ongoing debate. A 2000 DOJ investigation found no evidence of a conspiracy.
- **Reliability of FBI allegations:** Historians dispute the credibility of late-added FBI summaries alleging King's complicity in or tolerance of sexual violence, citing the bureau's active campaign to discredit him and the lack of corroborating evidence on sealed recordings.
- **His evolving political views:** Scholars analyze the shift in his later years from focusing solely on legal segregation to critiquing economic inequality, capitalism, and the Vietnam War, and his private expressions of support for democratic socialism.
