# Theatre

## Overview
Theatre is the oldest form of drama, using live performers—actors, singers, dancers—to present real or imagined events before an audience in a designated space, typically a stage. It integrates gesture, speech, music, and movement with stagecraft like lighting and scenery to create immediacy and presence. Modern Western theatre derives primarily from ancient Greek traditions, borrowing its terminology, genres, themes, and structural conventions.

## Timeline
- **c. 534 BCE** — Tragic performances may have begun at the City Dionysia in Athens; official records begin from 501 BCE
- **472 BCE** — Aeschylus wins first prize for *The Persians*, the notable exception in surviving drama that dramatizes the Persian response to defeat at Salamis in 480 BCE
- **c. 335 BCE** — Aristotle composes *Poetics*, earliest surviving dramatic theory
- **240 BCE** — Regular Roman drama begins with Livius Andronicus; Gnaeus Naevius also began writing five years later
- **1642–1660** — Theatres closed in England during the Puritan Interregnum
- **1660** — Restoration of Charles II; two companies licensed: the Duke's Company and the King's Company; first West End theatre designed by Thomas Killigrew
- **1890s** — Edwardian musical comedy emerges
- **1943** — *Oklahoma!* shifts musical theatre toward greater dramatic integration, following Edwardian musical comedy, Princess Theatre musicals, and 1920s–30s comedies

## Body

### Origins in Ancient Greece
Western theatre originated in the city-state of Athens as part of a broader culture of performance tied to religious festivals honoring Dionysus. Citizens participated in the City Dionysia, where playwrights competed in tetralogies—three tragedies and one satyr play. Only fragments survive from the 6th century BCE, but complete works by Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides remain from the 5th century BCE. Tragedy dramatized mythological narratives, while comedy evolved through Old, Middle, and New phases. The satyr play blended tragic and comic elements, featuring choruses of satyrs accompanying Dionysian tales. Greek actors were always male, wore masks, and performed in semi-circular open-air auditoria seating up to 20,000.

### Roman Expansion
Roman theatre expanded dramatically after encountering Greek drama during the Republic's expansion (270–240 BCE). The first Roman dramatist was Livius Andronicus (from 240 BCE), followed by Gnaeus Naevius. No plays from either writer have survived. Surviving Roman comedies—all *fabula palliata*—come from Plautus and Terence, who adapted Greek originals, eliminated the chorus, and added musical accompaniment. Roman tragedy, though largely lost, included works by Seneca (*fabula crepidata*) and the anonymous *Octavia* (*fabula praetexta*). Unlike Greek theatre, Roman stages permitted female performers, some achieving fame and forming guilds like the Sociae Mimae.

### Non-Western Traditions
Sanskrit theatre emerged between the 2nd century BCE and the 1st century CE, flourishing until the 10th century CE under royal patronage. It began after the development of Greek and Roman theatre and before the development of theatre in other parts of Asia; earliest-surviving fragments date from the 1st century CE. Its foundational text, the *Nātyaśāstra* (attributed to Bharata Muni), is a compendium whose date of composition is uncertain (estimates range from 200 BCE to 200 CE). It codified acting styles (*abhinaya*), including realistic (*lokadharmi*) and conventional (*natyadharmi*) modes. Notable dramatists include Kālidāsa (*Abhijñānaśākuntala*) and Bhavabhuti. In East Asia, Chinese shadow puppetry developed during the Tang dynasty, with northern (Pekingese) and southern (Cantonese) styles distinguished by puppet construction and rod placement. Japanese Nō, Kabuki, and Kyōgen emerged in the 17th century CE. Indonesian wayang traditions—including *wayang kulit* (leather shadow puppets) and *wayang golek* (rod puppets)—drew from Hindu epics. Medieval Islamic theatre featured ta'ziyeh passion plays and puppet shows.

### Early Modern and Modern Developments
Between the 15th and 19th centuries, Western theatre diversified into forms like commedia dell'arte and melodrama, shifting from poetic to naturalistic prose. The English Civil War halted theatre from 1642 to 1660; the Restoration brought French influence, proscenium stages, and women performers. The 19th century saw Romanticism, Realism, and Naturalism, culminating in modern movements: Stanislavski's psychological realism, Brecht's political epic theatre, and postmodern experiments by directors like Peter Brook and Robert Wilson.

### Types and Organization
Theatre encompasses drama, musical theatre, comedy, tragedy, and improvisation. Productions require collaboration among playwrights, directors, actors, and technical crews. Organizations range from community groups to Broadway houses, with structures including repertory companies, touring ensembles, and resident theatres. Unions such as Actors' Equity Association and IATSE regulate labor standards.

## Terms
- **Theatron**: Greek term meaning "place for viewing"; origin of the word "theatre".
- **Tetralogy**: A set of four plays—typically three tragedies and one satyr play—presented together in ancient Greek competitions.
- **Fabula palliata**: Roman comedy adapted from Greek originals, named for the Greek cloak (*pallium*) worn by characters.
- **Abhinaya**: The art of acting in Sanskrit theatre, divided into realistic (*lokadharmi*) and conventional (*natyadharmi*) styles.
- **Stagecraft**: Technical aspects of production including lighting, sound, costumes, and set design.
- **Scenography**: Broader artistic vision encompassing all visual elements of a production, distinct from stagecraft.
- **Sutradhara**: Stage manager in Sanskrit theatre, metaphorically the "holder of strings" directing the performance.
- **Ta'ziyeh**: Shia Islamic passion play reenacting episodes from early Muslim history.
- **Gesamtkunstwerk**: German term meaning "total work of art," describing Wagner's ideal of unifying all artistic elements in opera.
- **Repertory system**: Performance model where multiple plays are rehearsed and performed alternately by the same company.

## Debates and Open Questions
Several key debates persist: the precise origins of Greek tragedy remain obscure despite Aristotle's attribution to Dionysian festivals; the authorship and dating of the *Nātyaśāstra* vary widely among scholars; the extent to which Roman adaptations departed from Greek originals is contested; and the relationship between historical theatrical practices and modern theoretical frameworks continues to evolve, particularly regarding cross-cultural influences and the role of theatre in social change.
