# Mount Pinatubo

## Overview
Mount Pinatubo is an active stratovolcano in the Philippines, most famous for its catastrophic 1991 eruption. This event was the second-largest terrestrial eruption of the 20th century, causing massive local destruction and significant global climate effects. The volcano's history and the 1991 disaster highlight the interplay between geological processes, human preparedness, and indigenous rights.

## Timeline
- **c. 1.1 million years ago** — Activity of "Ancestral Pinatubo" begins.
- **c. 79,000 BC** — "Modern Pinatubo" is born from a cataclysmic eruption of the ancestral volcano.
- **c. 1500 AD** — The most recent major eruptive period before 1991 (Buag Eruptive Period).
- **March–April 1991** — Pre-eruption activity begins with earthquakes and steam blasts.
- **June 12, 1991** — First explosive eruption phase; lava dome forms.
- **June 15, 1991** — Climactic VEI-6 eruption; summit collapses to form a caldera.
- **1992–1993** — Minor dome-forming eruptions occur inside the new caldera.
- **2001** — Controlled draining of Lake Pinatubo begins due to instability fears.
- **2009–2010** — Aeta communities are granted ancestral domain titles over Pinatubo lands.
- **2021** — Increased seismic activity leads to a temporary Alert Level 1.

## Body

### Geological History and Setting
Mount Pinatubo is a stratovolcano (a conical volcano built from layers of lava and ash) in the Zambales Mountains of Luzon, formed by the subduction of the Eurasian Plate beneath the Philippine Mobile Belt. Geologists recognize two phases: "Ancestral Pinatubo," active until about 1.1 million years ago, and "Modern Pinatubo," which began with a massive eruption c. 79,000 BC. Modern Pinatubo has had episodic, large explosive eruptions every few thousand years, each ejecting over 10 km³ of material. Its 1991 eruption was among the smallest in its documented geological record.

### The 1991 Eruption
In early 1991, rising magma triggered earthquakes and gas emissions. The climactic eruption on June 15, coinciding with Typhoon Yunya, ejected about 10 km³ of material and sent an ash cloud 35 km into the atmosphere. The eruption caused the summit to collapse into a 2.5 km-wide caldera. Pyroclastic surges and flows filled valleys with deposits up to 200 m thick. Early scientific monitoring and predictions allowed for the evacuation of tens of thousands, saving many lives. The eruption severely damaged infrastructure and altered river systems, with subsequent rain-triggered lahars (volcanic mudflows) causing destruction for years.

### Global Effects and Aftermath
The eruption released 20 million tonnes of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere, forming a global sulfuric acid haze. This caused global temperatures to drop by about 0.5 °C (0.9 °F) from 1991 to 1993 and contributed to temporary ozone depletion. The caldera later filled with water to form Lake Pinatubo. Minor eruptions continued until 1993. In 2009 and 2010, the indigenous Aeta people, who had inhabited the volcano's slopes for centuries, were granted legal ancestral domain titles over parts of Pinatubo.

## Terms
- **Stratovolcano:** A conical volcano built from alternating layers of lava flows, volcanic ash, and other debris.
- **VEI (Volcanic Explosivity Index):** A scale (0-8) that measures the explosiveness of volcanic eruptions based on volume of ejected material and eruption column height.
- **Pyroclastic surge:** A fast-moving, ground-hugging avalanche of hot ash, gas, and rock fragments.
- **Lahar:** A destructive mudflow or debris flow composed of a slurry of pyroclastic material, rocky debris, and water.
- **Caldera:** A large depression formed when a volcano erupts and collapses inward after emptying its magma chamber.
- **Subduction:** A geological process where one tectonic plate slides beneath another into the Earth's mantle.
- **Ancestral domain title:** A legal certificate in the Philippines recognizing an indigenous community's collective ownership and rights to their traditional lands.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact total volume of material ejected during the c. 79,000 BC eruption that created the modern volcano is unknown.
- The decreasing maximum size of eruptions over Pinatubo's 35,000-year history may be an artifact of erosion and burial of older deposits, rather than a true trend.
- The precise trigger for the 1991 eruption is understood in broad terms (magma ascent), but the specific timing and interaction with the typhoon's effects are subjects of ongoing study.
