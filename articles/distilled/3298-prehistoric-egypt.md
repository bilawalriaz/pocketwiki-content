# Prehistoric Egypt

## Overview
Prehistoric and Predynastic Egypt encompasses the period from the first human occupation of the Nile Valley to the unification of Egypt under the First Dynasty around 3100 BC. This era witnessed the gradual transition from nomadic hunter-gatherer societies to settled agricultural communities, culminating in the complex society that would produce pharaonic civilization. Understanding this period is crucial for explaining the origins of Egyptian culture, social structure, and state formation.

## Timeline
- **c. 42,000–32,000 BC** — Khormusan industry develops tools from stone, bone, and hematite.
- **c. 32,000 BC** — Late Paleolithic begins; Nazlet Khater skeleton dates to c. 33,000 years ago.
- **c. 13,000–9,000 BC** — Qadan culture practices wild grain harvesting and develops sickles and grinding stones.
- **c. 6000 BC** — Neolithic settlements with crop cultivation and pottery appear across Egypt.
- **c. 5600–4400 BC** — Faiyum A culture is the earliest farming culture in the Nile Valley.
- **c. 4400–4000 BC** — Badarian culture in Upper Egypt produces black-topped ware and early faience.
- **c. 4000–3500 BC** — Naqada I (Amratian) period sees increased trade and early mud-brick buildings.
- **c. 3500–3200 BC** — Naqada II (Gerzean) period features Mesopotamian artistic influences and larger cities.
- **c. 3200–3000 BC** — Naqada III (Protodynastic) period sees first hieroglyphs, irrigation, and royal cemeteries.
- **c. 3100 BC** — Unification of Upper and Lower Egypt under Pharaoh Narmer (or Menes).

## Body

### Paleolithic and Mesolithic Foundations
Human occupation in Egypt likely spans over a million years. Early stone tool industries include the Oldowan and Acheulean. By the Late Paleolithic (c. 32,000 BP), populations like the Khormusan and later Qadan cultures developed specialized hunting and fishing. The Qadan people were among the first to systematically harvest wild grains, creating sickles and grinding stones, though they did not practice agriculture. This period established a pattern of seasonal movement and adaptation to the Nile's inundation.

### The Neolithic Transition and Early Farming
Around 6000 BC, the Neolithic package—domesticated crops and animals from West Asia—arrived in Egypt, likely via the Sinai. This led to sedentary farming communities. The Faiyum A culture (c. 5600–4400 BC) is the earliest known farming culture in the Nile Valley. In Lower Egypt, cultures like Merimde (c. 5000–4200 BC) and Maadi (c. 4000–3500 BC) show evidence of settled villages, agriculture, and trade. The origins of these early farmers are debated; some evidence suggests migration from the Fertile Crescent, while other studies emphasize indigenous development with local continuity.

### Upper Egyptian Cultural Development
In Upper Egypt, the Tasian (c. 4500 BC) and Badarian (c. 4400–4000 BC) cultures are known for their distinctive black-topped pottery. The Badarian period is considered Chalcolithic (Copper-Stone Age) due to the use of copper. Anthropological studies indicate these populations had strong biological affinities with other Northeast African populations, particularly from the Sahara and Nubia. This cultural sequence laid the groundwork for the more complex Naqada culture.

### The Naqada Culture and Path to Unification
The Naqada culture (c. 4000–3000 BC) is divided into three phases. Naqada I (Amratian) saw increased trade and the first mud-brick buildings. Naqada II (Gerzean) was marked by significant Mesopotamian artistic influence, larger settlements, and the use of copper for tools and weapons. This period coincided with increased aridification, pushing populations toward the Nile. Naqada III (Protodynastic) is characterized by the first hieroglyphs, irrigation, and royal cemeteries, indicating the emergence of a stratified society and the political unification of Egypt. The process of unification, culminating c. 3100 BC, is believed to have been driven by the expansion of the Naqada culture from Upper Egypt into the Delta.

## Terms
*   **Predynastic Period:** The era in ancient Egyptian history before the unification of the country under the First Dynasty (c. 3100 BC), characterized by the development of agriculture and complex societies.
*   **Neolithic:** The New Stone Age, a period of transition to farming, sedentism, and pottery production, beginning in Egypt around 6000 BC.
*   **Chalcolithic:** The Copper-Stone Age, a period where copper tools were used alongside stone tools, beginning in Egypt with the Badarian culture.
*   **Sequence Dating (S.D.):** A relative dating system developed by Flinders Petrie for Predynastic Egypt based on the analysis of pottery styles.
*   **Black-topped ware:** A type of red and brown pottery with a blackened top and interior, characteristic of the Tasian and Badarian cultures.
*   **Serekh:** A rectangular frame representing a palace facade, used in early dynastic Egypt to enclose the name of the king.
*   **Protodynastic Period:** The final phase of the Predynastic period (Naqada III), marked by the emergence of state-level society and the first use of hieroglyphs.
*   **Faience:** A glazed ceramic material, with the earliest known examples produced in the Badarian period.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Origins of the Population:** Scholarly debate continues regarding the demographic formation of Predynastic Egyptians. Some evidence points to significant migration and cultural influence from the Near East (Levant and Mesopotamia) during the Neolithic, while other studies emphasize indigenous development and biological continuity with local Northeast African populations.
*   **End of the Predynastic Period:** The exact date and definition of the period's end are contested. The gradual development of state institutions makes the boundary between "Predynastic" and "Early Dynastic" blurry, leading to terms like "Protodynastic" or "Dynasty 0."
*   **Process of Unification:** Whether the unification of Egypt under the First Dynasty was achieved through military conquest, political infiltration, or a more gradual process of cultural and economic integration remains an open question.
