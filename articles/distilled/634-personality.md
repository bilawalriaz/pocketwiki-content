# Personality

## Overview  
Personality psychology studies the relatively stable behavioral, cognitive, and emotional patterns that make each person unique, while acknowledging that these patterns change over time through experience and development. The field integrates multiple approaches—including trait, biological, psychodynamic, cognitive-experiential, social-cultural, and developmental perspectives—to explain individual differences and their links to life outcomes such as mental health, relationships, and longevity.

## Timeline  
- **Medieval period** — Self understood through social roles (family, guilds, kinship)
- **Renaissance** — Shift toward individual personality shaped by environment
- **1960s** — Eysenck proposes two-factor model: extraversion and neuroticism
- **1980s–1990s** — Big Five (OCEAN) model gains dominant empirical support
- **1907** — William James lectures on pragmatism, linking temperament to philosophical outlook
- **1970s** — Ainsworth’s Strange Situation identifies attachment styles
- **1990s** — Judith Rich Harris proposes peer-group socialization theory
- **2000s** — Cross-cultural validation of Big Five via NEO-PI-R and BFI across nations

## Body  

### Measurement and Trait Models  
Personality is assessed using objective tests (e.g., Big Five Inventory, MMPI-2, EPQ-R) and projective measures (e.g., Rorschach Inkblot Test). Traits are often extracted statistically via factor analysis. The Big Five—openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism (OCEAN)—are the most supported framework, with each trait splitting into aspects and facets. About half the variance in these traits is genetic; the rest stems from environmental influences.

### Developmental and Biological Foundations  
Temperament, seen in early childhood, precedes personality. The Temperament and Character Inventory identifies four basic temperaments—harm avoidance, reward dependence, novelty-seeking, persistence—each tied to neural circuits and neurotransmitter systems (e.g., serotonin, dopamine, oxytocin). Attachment styles (secure, ambivalent, avoidant, disorganized), identified by Ainsworth, predict later personality traits. Identical twins share personality largely due to genetics, not shared environment.

### Environmental Influences  
Personality is more malleable than once assumed. Parenting styles shape early traits, but peer groups become primary influencers in adulthood (Harris). Daily experiences accumulate to drive personality change, moderated by individual susceptibility (e.g., attachment security). Personality also shapes environments through *situation selection* (choosing contexts) and *situation evocation* (eliciting responses that reinforce traits).

### Cross-Cultural Perspectives  
While many traits are universal, culture shapes expression and values. The Big Five has shown cross-cultural validity through translations of the NEO-PI-R and BFI across dozens of languages. However, cultural differences exist—Western cultures emphasize extraversion and individualism, while Eastern cultures value agreeableness and collectivism. Emic traits (culture-specific) contrast with etic traits (universal), and translation biases complicate cross-cultural comparisons.

### Historical and Philosophical Roots  
Modern personality emerged during the Renaissance, shifting from collective identity to individual self shaped by urbanization, education, and industrialization. William James argued that philosophers’ temperaments bias their reasoning—rationalists favor abstract principles, empiricists rely on sensory facts. Later thinkers like Locke, Hume, Descartes, and Spinoza debated personal identity, consciousness, and the role of mind versus body.

## Terms  
- **Factor Analysis**: Statistical method used to identify underlying personality dimensions from questionnaire data.  
- **Big Five (OCEAN)**: Dominant trait model comprising openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism.  
- **Temperament**: Early-appearing, biologically based behavioral style; precursor to personality.  
- **Etic vs. Emic**: Universal versus culture-specific personality traits.  
- **Situation Selection/Evocation**: Processes by which personality influences environmental exposure and social feedback.  
- **Attachment Styles**: Patterns of emotional bonding (secure, avoidant, ambivalent, disorganized) established in infancy.  
- **Neuroticism**: Tendency toward emotional instability and negative affect.  
- **Self-Efficacy**: Belief in one’s capacity to execute behaviors needed to produce desired outcomes.  
- **Personology**: Holistic study of personality as a multidimensional system.  
- **Ego Depletion**: Cognitive fatigue resulting from acting against one’s natural disposition.

## Debates and Open Questions  
- Is personality best explained by traits, biological mechanisms, or sociocultural forces?  
- Do the Big Five represent true universals or artifacts of Western lexical traditions?  
- How much personality change is driven by intentional effort versus accumulated life experiences?  
- Can acting extraverted benefit introverts long-term, or does it incur lasting cognitive costs?  
- Are cultural differences in personality real or methodological artifacts of translation and sampling?
