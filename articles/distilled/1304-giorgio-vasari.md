# Giorgio Vasari

## Overview
Giorgio Vasari (1511–1574) was an Italian Renaissance painter, architect, and art historian whose *Lives of the Most Excellent Painters, Sculptors, and Architects* (1550, expanded 1568) established the template for Western art-historical writing. Though his biographies contain factual errors—especially regarding pre-Renaissance artists—his work promoted the idea of Florentine artistic supremacy and introduced the term *rinascita* ("rebirth"), later rendered in French as "Renaissance."

## Timeline
- **1511** – Born prematurely in Arezzo, Tuscany  
- **1527** – Sent to Florence; joins circle of Andrea del Sarto  
- **1529** – Visits Rome; studies Raphael and High Renaissance works  
- **1547** – Completes frescoes in Palazzo della Cancelleria, Rome (*Sala dei Cento Giorni*)  
- **1550** – Publishes first edition of *Lives of the Artists*  
- **1555** – Begins work on *Sala di Cosimo I* frescoes in Palazzo Vecchio  
- **1563** – Helps found Accademia e Compagnia delle Arti del Disegno in Florence  
- **1568** – Publishes expanded second edition of *Lives* with artist portraits  
- **1572** – Begins *Last Judgement* fresco for Florence Cathedral dome  
- **1574** – Dies in Florence, aged 62  

## Body

### Early Life and Training
Born in Arezzo to a family of modest means, Vasari was recommended by his cousin Luca Signorelli and trained under Guglielmo da Marsiglia, a stained-glass painter. At sixteen, he moved to Florence under Cardinal Silvio Passerini’s patronage, joining the workshop of Andrea del Sarto and encountering Rosso Fiorentino and Pontormo. His humanist education flourished, and he formed a lasting bond with Michelangelo, whose style deeply influenced his own.

### Painting Career
Vasari’s Mannerist style was highly praised in his lifetime but fell from favor in later centuries. His major works include the *Sala dei Cento Giorni* in Rome (1547), the *Sala di Cosimo I* in the Palazzo Vecchio (1555), and frescoes in the Sala Regia. He worked extensively for the Medici in Florence and Rome, and also painted in Naples and Arezzo. His final project, the *Last Judgement* for the Florence Cathedral dome, was left unfinished at his death and completed by Federico Zuccari.

### Architectural Work
As an architect, Vasari contributed significantly to Florentine urban design. He designed the Uffizi loggia, creating a unified Renaissance streetscape along the Arno, and conceived the Vasari Corridor—a private passage linking the Uffizi to the Palazzo Pitti across the Ponte Vecchio. He renovated Santa Maria Novella and Santa Croce, removing medieval rood screens and applying Mannerist aesthetics. Other works include the octagonal dome of the Basilica of Our Lady of Humility in Pistoia (1562) and collaboration on Pope Julius III’s Villa Giulia in Rome.

### *The Lives of the Artists*
Vasari’s *Lives* revolutionized art writing by compiling biographical sketches of artists from Cimabue to his contemporaries. He coined "rinascita" to describe the cultural awakening sparked by Giotto, a term later adopted as "Renaissance." The work promoted Florentine primacy in art, often at the expense of Venetian and northern European contributions. While the 1568 edition expanded coverage of Venetian artists like Titian, it retained a pro-Florentine bias. Vasari’s anecdotes mix verified research with fictional embellishments, such as the tale of young Giotto painting a fly on Cimabue’s work. Despite inaccuracies, his accounts remain valuable for insights into contemporary artists and workshop practices.

## Terms
- **Mannerism**: An artistic style emphasizing artificiality, elongation, and complex composition, emerging after the High Renaissance.  
- **Rinascita**: Italian term meaning "rebirth," used by Vasari to describe the revival of classical art initiated by Giotto.  
- **Renaissance**: French term derived from *rinascita*, denoting the broader cultural rebirth in Europe from the 14th to 17th centuries.  
- **Gothic art**: Term Vasari used pejoratively to describe the pre-Renaissance style he viewed as barbaric.  
- **Accademia e Compagnia delle Arti del Disegno**: A Florentine academy founded in 1563 to elevate the status of artists through education and regulation.  
- **Vasari Corridor**: A covered passageway in Florence connecting the Uffizi Gallery to the Palazzo Pitti, designed by Vasari.  
- **Rood screen**: A medieval partition separating the nave from the chancel in churches; Vasari removed these in his renovations.  
- **Concorrenza**: Italian term for "competition," which Vasari identified as a driving force behind Florentine artistic excellence.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars continue to debate the reliability of Vasari’s *Lives*. His pro-Florentine bias leads him to attribute major innovations—such as the invention of engraving—to Florentine artists while marginalizing Venetian and northern European achievements. Many of his chronological claims and anecdotal stories have been disproven by modern archival research. However, his firsthand knowledge of contemporary artists and access to documents make his later biographies more credible. The extent to which his fictionalized tales influenced popular understanding of Renaissance art remains a subject of scholarly inquiry.
