# Democritus

## Overview
Democritus (c. 460 – c. 370 BC) was a pre-Socratic Greek philosopher from Abdera in Thrace, best known for developing the atomic theory of the universe alongside his mentor Leucippus. He argued that reality consists solely of infinite, indivisible atoms moving in a void, and that all sensory qualities (color, taste, temperature) are conventional rather than real, arising from atomic configurations. A polymath who wrote nearly eighty treatises on ethics, physics, mathematics, and anthropology, none of his original works survive; our knowledge derives from fragments preserved by later authors like Aristotle and Diogenes Laertius. He was called the "laughing philosopher" for his ethical emphasis on cheerfulness (*euthymia*) and his view that the wise person laughs at human folly rather than weeping at misfortune.

## Timeline
- **c. 460 BC** — Born in Abdera, Thrace (date per Apollodorus; Thrasyllus suggests c. 470 BC).
- **c. 430–400 BC** — Travels extensively in Asia, Egypt (possibly 5 years), and possibly India and Ethiopia, studying mathematics and natural phenomena.
- **c. 420 BC** — Returns to Abdera; devotes inheritance to study and writing; interacts with Leucippus, founder of atomism.
- **c. 370 BC** — Dies in Abdera (some sources claim age 109; death dated to 357 BC by Olympiad reckoning).
- **4th century BC** — Aristotle writes two books critiquing Democritus' problems; Plato allegedly wishes his books burned.
- **3rd century BC** — Callimachus catalogues works; Timon of Phlius praises his style.
- **1st century BC** — Diodorus Siculus records biographical details in *Bibliotheca historica*.
- **3rd century AD** — Diogenes Laertius preserves life, works list, and fragments in *Lives of the Eminent Philosophers*.
- **1749** — Samuel Johnson invokes Democritus in *The Vanity of Human Wishes*.
- **19th–20th century** — Modern atomic theory revives interest; Democritean atoms compared to quantum particles.

## Body

### Life and Travels
Democritus was born into a wealthy family in Abdera; his father (variously named Hegesistratus, Damasippus, or Athenocritus) hosted Xerxes during the Persian invasion. Using his inheritance, Democritus traveled widely to acquire knowledge, visiting Egypt (where he lived five years and praised the *harpedonaptai*, or "rope-stretchers"/mathematicians), Babylon, Meroe, and possibly India and Ethiopia. He claimed no contemporary had traveled further or met more scholars. Upon returning, he exhausted his wealth on research, dying in poverty but honored by fellow citizens for predicting natural phenomena. Ancient anecdotes claim he blinded himself to avoid distraction, though scholars consider this a later invention; he likely lost sight from intense study. He lived to an advanced age (some say 109), dying c. 370 BC.

### Atomism: Physics and Metaphysics
Democritus systematized Leucippus' atomism to solve Eleatic paradoxes (Parmenides/Zeno) denying motion and void. He posited:
- **Atoms** (*atomos*, "uncuttable"): infinite in number, eternal, homogeneous in quality but heterogeneous in shape, size, and arrangement; impenetrable and indivisible.
- **Void** (*kenon*): infinite empty space where atoms move; necessary for motion, rejected by Eleatics as "non-being."
- **Mechanics**: Atoms move eternally in a "whirling" vortex; similars attract, forming complexes (worlds, objects) separated by void. Necessity (cause-and-effect) governs collisions; "chance" (*tyche*) is merely human ignorance of causes.
- **Worlds**: Infinite worlds exist, some growing, some decaying, differing in suns/moons; worlds begin and end, possibly by collision.
- **Perception vs. Reality**: Only atoms and void are real. Sensory qualities (hot/cold, sweet/bitter, color) are "by convention" (*nomos*), not nature (*physis*), caused by atomic interactions with sense organs. The soul consists of fine, spherical fire-atoms permeating the body; thought arises from atomic impacts.

Democritus' atoms differ from modern ones: they are inert solids interacting mechanically, not quantum entities with electric/magnetic properties. Yet the Pauli exclusion principle (fermions excluding volume) echoes Democritean impenetrability.

### Epistemology
Democritus distinguished **obscure knowledge** (*skotiē krisis*) from senses—unreliable because perceptions depend on bodily conditions, organ differences, and atomic fluxes—from **genuine knowledge** of atoms and void. However, he held reason is not distinct from sensation; both are effects of atomic impressions. Thus, the mind is not a separate faculty but a configuration of soul-atoms. He acknowledged radical uncertainty: "either nothing is true or at least it is not evident to us." He proposed three criteria: phenomena (to discover hidden causes), thought (to investigate), and assertions (to judge desires). This skepticism about sensory access to reality anticipates later debates on primary/secondary qualities.

### Mathematics
Democritus pioneered geometry and infinitesimal reasoning. Archimedes credits him with asserting (without rigorous proof) that a cone’s volume is one-third its cylinder, and a pyramid’s one-third its prism—later proven by Eudoxus via exhaustion. Plutarch reports Democritus argued a cone’s cross-section must be "step-like," not smooth like a cylinder, suggesting an early intuition of limits or indivisibles (anticipating calculus). He also wrote *On Irrational Lines and Solids* and *On the Great Year* (astronomy/calendar).

### Anthropology and Ethics
**Anthropology**: Early humans lived anarchic, animal-like lives, foraging alone until fear of beasts drove them into societies. They lacked language, fire, clothing, agriculture; progress came by trial and error (caves, food storage, reason).

**Ethics**: The supreme good is *euthymia* (peace of mind/cheerfulness), achieved by freedom from fear (*phobos*, *deisidaimonia*—superstition) and passion. Means: moderation, knowing one’s limits, avoiding overcommitment. Virtue is not conquering passion but lacking the *desire* to do wrong ("A good person not only refrains from wrongdoing but does not even want to do wrong"). Happiness lies in soul-goods, not riches; "the hopes of the educated are better than the riches of the ignorant." He controversially advised against having children due to the pain of potential failure. His maxim "better to suffer wrong than do wrong" and "sin is ignorance of the better" influenced Socratic thought. Sixty pages of fragments (Diels–Kranz) cover moral counsel.

### Aesthetics and Works
Thrasyllus (1st century AD) identified six aesthetic works (on poetry, beauty of verses, harmony, etc.), making Democritus the earliest systematic aesthetician. Diogenes Laertius lists ~73 genuine works in tetralogies: Ethics (e.g., *On Contentment*, *On Virtue*), Natural Science (*The Great World-System*, *On Nature*, *On the Mind*), Mathematics (*On Geometry*, *On Irrational Lines*), Literature (*On Homer*, *On Poetry*), Technical (*On Farming*, *Tactics*, *Medical Judgment*), and Commentaries (*On Babylonian Writings*, *Circumnavigation of the Ocean*). None survive complete; fragments are mostly ethical.

### Legacy
- **Antiquity**: Aristotle respected him as a serious rival, writing two books on his problems; Plato allegedly wanted his books burned. Cicero praised his poetic style; Pyrrho and Timon imitated it.
- **Renaissance/Modern**: Samuel Johnson (1749) invoked him as a model of "cheerful wisdom." Modern science notes structural parallels (indivisible particles, void, mechanical interaction) but emphasizes different epistemic bases: Democritus reasoned a priori; modern atoms are empirically inferred and divisible (protons, electrons). Quantum particles share indivisibility (fermions) and exclusion principles, but bosons (photons) violate Democritean impenetrability.

## Terms
- ****Atom** (*atomos*)** — "Uncuttable"; the smallest, indivisible, eternal particle of matter, homogeneous in substance but varying in shape, size, and arrangement.
- ****Void** (*kenon*)** — Infinite empty space; a necessary condition for atomic motion, rejected by Eleatics as "non-being."
- ****Necessity** (*ananke*)** — The deterministic chain of cause-and-effect governing atomic collisions; what humans call "chance" is ignorance of necessity.
- ****Euthymia**** — "Cheerfulness" or "peace of mind"; the ethical *telos* (end), achieved by freedom from fear, superstition, and excessive passion.
- ****Nomos vs. Physis**** — "Convention" vs. "Nature"; sensory qualities (color, taste) exist by *nomos* (convention), while only atoms and void exist by *physis* (nature).
- ****Harpedonaptai**** — "Rope-stretchers"; Egyptian mathematicians/surveyors whose knowledge Democritus praised during his travels.
- ****Skotiē krisis**** — "Obscure judgment/knowledge"; Democritus' term for unreliable sensory perception, contrasted with genuine knowledge of atoms.
- ****Tyche**** — "Chance"; for Democritus, not randomness but a "cover for human ignorance" of atomic causes.
- ****Deisidaimonia**** — "Superstition" or "fear of the gods"; a primary disturbance of *euthymia* that philosophy cures.
- ****Diels–Kranz (DK)**** — Standard collection of pre-Socratic fragments; Democritus' ethical fragments span ~60 pages in this edition.

## Debates and open questions
- **Leucippus vs. Democritus**: Early sources (Aristotle, Theophrastus) credit Leucippus as atomism's founder and Democritus as developer; later sources attribute it solely to Democritus. Their individual contributions remain inseparable.
- **Atomic weight**: Democritus stated "the more any indivisible exceeds, the heavier it is," but whether he meant weight is intrinsic to size or arises from atomic interactions is disputed.
- **Blinding anecdote**: Did he deliberately blind himself (later moralizing tale) or lose sight from study? Scholars favor the latter; the story appears in Hellenistic biographical tropes.
- **Plato's silence**: Plato never mentions Democritus, despite being contemporaries. Diogenes Laertius claims Plato wanted his books burned; some scholars (e.g., Groen van Prinsterer) detect Democritean influence in Plato's language/style.
- **Empirical basis**: Democritus' atomism is often called "scientific," but it lacked empirical foundation; it was a metaphysical response to Eleatic logic. The resemblance to 19th-century atoms is structural, not methodological.
- **Ethical consistency**: His advice against procreation conflicts with his view that humans naturally form societies; whether this reflects a consistent anti-natalism or context-dependent maxims is debated.
- **Aesthetic fragments**: Only a small percentage of his six aesthetic works survive; the scope of his theory of art/poetry remains largely unrecoverable.
