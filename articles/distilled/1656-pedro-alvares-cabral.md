# Pedro Álvares Cabral

## Overview
Pedro Álvares Cabral (c. 1467/1468–1520) was a Portuguese nobleman and navigator credited as the European discoverer of Brazil. In 1500, he commanded a 13-ship fleet bound for India via Vasco da Gama’s route around Africa. Swinging far into the western Atlantic—whether by design or accident—he made landfall on the South American coast in April 1500, claimed it for Portugal under the Treaty of Tordesillas, and named it Ilha de Vera Cruz. After a brief stay, the fleet proceeded to India, where Cabral established a factory at Cochin but suffered a massacre at Calicut instigated by Arab merchants. He retaliated with bombardment and blockade, then returned to Portugal in 1501 with a cargo of spices yielding up to 800% profit. Despite this success, Cabral fell from royal favor, was replaced by Vasco da Gama for the next India armada, and retired to obscurity. His reputation was rehabilitated in the 19th century by Emperor Pedro II of Brazil to foster national identity. Historians debate whether the Brazilian landfall was intentional; no consensus exists, though Cabral’s voyage effectively launched the Portuguese seaborne empire from Africa to the Far East and laid the foundation for Brazil as the sole Portuguese-speaking nation in the Americas.

## Body

### Early Life and Court Career
Pedro Álvares Cabral was born in 1467 or 1468 at Belmonte, central Portugal, the son of Fernão Álvares Cabral and Isabel Gouveia. His family, descended from a Castilian clan (Cabreiras) and rewarded with the hereditary fief of Belmonte for loyalty to King João I during the 1383–85 war with Castile, belonged to the lower nobility. At age 12, Cabral entered the court of Afonso V, receiving a humanist education and military training. He was named *moço fidalgo* in 1484. Records are sparse, but he may have campaigned in North Africa. In 1497, Manuel I granted him a substantial annual allowance (30,000 *reais*), the title *Fidalgo* in the King's Council, and knighthood in the Order of Christ. Contemporaries described him as well-learned, prudent, and generous, but also vain and sensitive about his honor. He stood roughly 190 cm (6 ft 3 in) tall.

### Appointment and Fleet Composition
On 15 February 1500, Manuel I appointed Cabral *Capitão-mor* of the second India armada. The choice reflected court politics: the Cabral family’s loyalty, the influence of his brothers on the King's Council, and a desire to balance rival noble factions. Cabral lacked command experience; seasoned navigators Bartolomeu Dias, Diogo Dias, and Nicolau Coelho were seconded to assist. The fleet comprised 13 ships (nine *naus*/carracks, three round caravels) and 1,500 men, including 700 mostly untrained soldiers. The primary division (11 ships) aimed for Calicut to establish a *feitoria* (factory) and trade; a second division (two ships) targeted Sofala (Mozambique) for gold. Cabral stood to gain 10,000 *cruzados*, the right to buy 30 tonnes of pepper tax-free, and 10 boxes of other spices duty-free—a fortune if he returned.

### Discovery of Brazil
The fleet left Lisbon 9 March 1500. After stopping at Cape Verde (22 March), it executed the *volta do mar*—swinging far west into the Atlantic to catch favorable winds. On 22 April, land was sighted at Monte Pascoal. Cabral initially thought it an island. Contact with Tupiniquim inhabitants was peaceful; gifts were exchanged, and on 26 April the first Mass in Brazil was celebrated. Sailing north to Porto Seguro, Cabral confirmed the land lay east of the Tordesillas meridian (1494), placing it in the Portuguese sphere. On 1 May, a large wooden cross was erected and a second Mass held, formalizing the claim. He named the land Ilha de Vera Cruz. A supply ship was dispatched to notify Manuel I. Cabral soon realized the landmass was a continent, not an island.

### Voyage to India: Storms and African Coast
Resuming the voyage 2–3 May, the fleet turned east toward Africa. On 23–24 May, a violent storm in the South Atlantic high-pressure zone sank four ships, including Bartolomeu Dias’s caravel; 380 men perished. The six surviving ships regrouped at the Primeiras and Segundas Archipelago off East Africa. After repairs near Sofala, Cabral failed to negotiate a treaty at Kilwa Kisiwani (26 May). At Malindi (2 August), he secured pilots for the Indian Ocean crossing. At Anjadip Island, ships were careened and prepared for the final leg to Calicut.

### Calicut: Trade, Massacre, and Retaliation
Arriving 13 September, Cabral negotiated with the Zamorin (ruler of Calicut) and established a factory. Tensions rose as Arab merchants, fearing loss of their spice monopoly, incited a surprise attack on 16–17 December by 300–several thousand Muslims and Hindus. Over 50 Portuguese died; survivors fled to the ships. After the Zamorin failed to apologize, Cabral seized ten Arab merchant ships in the harbor, killed ~600 crewmen, confiscated cargoes, and burned the vessels. He then bombarded Calicut for a day. Historians attribute this severity to centuries of anti-Muslim conflict (the *Reconquista*), the Portuguese determination to dominate the spice trade by force, and a calculated "gunboat diplomacy" precedent to compensate for their numerical inferiority in future fleets.

### Cochin Alliance and Return
Heeding Vasco da Gama’s earlier advice, Cabral sailed to Cochin (24 December), a vassal of Calicut seeking independence. He allied with its ruler and other local chiefs, exploiting Indian disunity—a tactic that secured Portuguese hegemony. Factories were established at Cochin and Kannur, and ships loaded with pepper and spices. The return began 16 January 1501. One ship was lost on a sandbar; another (Pedro de Ataíde) separated. At Mozambique Island, Cabral met Nicolau Coelho (sent ahead) and Diogo Dias (lost since the storm). At Beseguiche (Dakar), Amerigo Vespucci, leading a follow-up Portuguese expedition, confirmed Brazil was a continent. Cabral reached Portugal 21 July 1501. Of 13 ships, six were lost, two returned empty, five full; the Crown reaped ~800% profit, covering all costs and losses.

### Later Years, Disgrace, and Death
Manuel I planned a "Revenge Fleet" for 1502 under Cabral, but he was relieved of command after eight months of preparation. The cause remains uncertain: a dispute over giving Vicente Sodré independent command, factional rivalry with da Gama’s supporters, and the King’s irritation. Vasco da Gama led the 1502 armada. Cabral left court permanently. In 1503 he married Dona Isabel de Castro, a wealthy noblewoman and niece of Afonso de Albuquerque; they had at least four children. Albuquerque’s 1514 plea for Cabral’s rehabilitation failed. From 1509, suffering recurrent fever and tremor (possibly malaria), Cabral lived in Santarém. He was elevated to knight in the King's Council in 1518 with an extra allowance. He died c. 1520 and was buried in the Convento da Graça, Santarém.

### Legacy and Historical Debates
Cabral’s tomb was forgotten until its 1839 rediscovery by Varnhagen. Emperor Pedro II (r. 1831–1889) promoted Cabral as a national hero to unify Brazil’s diverse citizenry around a shared Portuguese-speaking identity distinct from Hispanic America. Exhumations in 1882 and 1896 brought an urn of remains to Rio de Janeiro (1903). In Portugal, he remains overshadowed by Vasco da Gama.

**Intentionality of Discovery:** Scholars have debated since 1854 (prompted by Pedro II) whether Cabral’s landfall was accidental or deliberate. Early chroniclers (Castanheda, Barros, Góis) assumed accident. Proponents of intentionality (Varnhagen, Capistrano de Abreu) cite the fleet’s wide western swing and prior Portuguese suspicions of land. Opponents (Hélio Vianna, Thomas Skidmore) note signs but no irrefutable proof. Charles R. Boxer deems the debate "irrelevant"; Anthony Smith considers it unresolvable.

**Forerunners:** Cabral was not the first European near Brazil. Norsemen reached North America earlier. Columbus touched Venezuela (1498). Spaniards Vicente Yáñez Pinzón and Diego de Lepe sailed Brazil’s northern coast January–March 1500. However, these visits were cursory and left no lasting impact. Cabral’s claim rests on his voyage initiating the continuous Portuguese presence that created Brazil’s unique history, culture, and society.

## Terms
- ***Capitão-mor*** — Commander-in-chief of a Portuguese fleet; a noble appointed by the Crown regardless of naval experience.
- ***Volta do mar*** — Navigational technique of sailing far west into the Atlantic to catch prevailing westerlies for the return to Europe or the turn toward Africa/India.
- ***Feitoria*** — A fortified trading post/factory established by Portugal in Africa, India, and Brazil to control trade and collect spices.
- ***Zamorin*** — The hereditary title of the Hindu ruler of Calicut (Kozhikode), the major spice entrepôt on India’s Malabar Coast.
- **Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)** — Papal-mediated treaty dividing non-European world between Portugal (east) and Spain (west) along a meridian 370 leagues west of Cape Verde.
- ***Nau* (carrack)** — Large, ocean-going Portuguese sailing ship (3–4 masts) used for the India run; high cargo capacity but slow.
- ***Moço fidalgo*** — "Young nobleman"; a minor court title granted to adolescent nobles, marking entry into royal service.
- ***Cruzado*** — Portuguese gold coin; 10,000 *cruzados* ≈ 35 kg of gold, Cabral’s fixed fee for commanding the 1500 armada.
- ***Reconquista*** — Centuries-long Christian reconquest of the Iberian Peninsula from Muslim (Moorish) rule, shaping Portuguese anti-Muslim military culture.
- **Gunboat diplomacy** — Coercive use of naval artillery to impose political/commercial demands; Cabral’s 1500 bombardment of Calicut is an early prototype.

## Debates and open questions
1. **Intentional vs. accidental discovery of Brazil:** No contemporary document proves prior knowledge. The fleet’s trajectory (*volta do mar*) could explain the landfall, but the wide western swing and immediate claim suggest possible secret instructions. The debate remains unresolved.
2. **Reason for Cabral’s 1502 dismissal:** Sources cite a quarrel over Vicente Sodré’s independent command, factional rivalry with the da Gama clan, or the King’s pique. The exact cause is unrecorded.
3. **Scale of the Calicut massacre:** Portuguese accounts vary wildly (300 to several thousand attackers; 50–70 Portuguese dead). The Zamorin’s role—complicit or unable to control Arab merchants—is unclear.
4. **Cabral’s activities 1509–1520:** Only fragmentary royal letters survive (property dispute, military service privileges). His daily life in Santarém is largely unknown.
5. **Exact date and cause of death:** "c. 1520" from unspecified causes; the tomb inscription is lost.
