# Organic chemistry

## Overview
Organic chemistry is the scientific study of the structure, properties, and reactions of carbon-containing compounds. Carbon’s unique tetravalent bonding—forming single, double, triple, and delocalized bonds—creates a structurally diverse array of molecules that constitute the basis of all known life and the majority of known chemicals. The field underpins the synthesis of pharmaceuticals, polymers, petrochemicals, and agrochemicals, overlapping heavily with biochemistry, medicinal chemistry, and materials science. Historically defined by a "vital force" distinguishing living matter, the discipline was founded on the laboratory synthesis of organic molecules from inorganic precursors, establishing that the same chemical laws govern both animate and inanimate matter.

## Timeline
- **Pre-18th c.** — Vitalism dominates: organic compounds believed to require a "vital force" from living organisms.
- **1816** — Michel Chevreul demonstrates chemical changes in fats (soaps) without vital force.
- **1828** — Friedrich Wöhler synthesizes urea from inorganic salts (Wöhler synthesis), disproving vitalism.
- **1856** — William Henry Perkin accidentally discovers mauve dye, sparking commercial interest.
- **1858** — Kekulé and Couper independently propose tetravalent carbon linking into lattices (chemical structure theory).
- **1890s** — Bayer manufactures acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin), launching the pharmaceutical industry.
- **1910** — Paul Ehrlich develops arsphenamine (Salvarsan), the first chemotherapy agent (syphilis treatment).
- **1897–1914** — Adolf von Baeyer’s synthetic indigo replaces plant-derived production (19,000 → 1,000 tons).
- **1985** — Discovery of C60 buckminsterfullerene by Kroto, Smalley, and Curl (Nobel Prize 1996).

## Body

### Historical Foundations
Before the 19th century, **vitalism** held that organic compounds possessed a "vital force" absent in inorganic matter. Michel Chevreul’s 1816 studies on soap formation showed that fats could be chemically altered into new compounds without biological intervention. The definitive break came in 1828 when Friedrich Wöhler synthesized **urea** (a known constituent of urine) from inorganic **potassium cyanate** and **ammonium sulfate**. Though Wöhler was cautious, the **Wöhler synthesis** is now accepted as the experimental refutation of vitalism. Justus von Liebig subsequently systematized the field. Commercial relevance exploded after William Henry Perkin’s 1856 accidental discovery of **mauveine** (Perkin's mauve) while targeting quinine. The theoretical backbone arrived in 1858 when **Friedrich August Kekulé** and **Archibald Scott Couper** independently proposed that tetravalent carbon atoms link to form chains and rings, allowing structural formulas to be deduced from reactions.

### Rise of Industry and Synthesis
The pharmaceutical industry began in the 1890s with Bayer’s production of **acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin)**. By 1910, Paul Ehrlich introduced **arsphenamine (Salvarsan)**, the first effective syphilis treatment, founding **chemotherapy** and the "magic bullet" concept. Systematic synthesis replaced serendipity: Adolf von Baeyer’s synthetic indigo collapsed natural production from 19,000 tons (1897) to 1,000 tons (1914). **Total synthesis**—the multi-step construction of complex molecules—escalated in complexity through the 20th century, achieving glucose, terpineol, cholesterol derivatives (enabling hormone synthesis), lysergic acid, and vitamin B12. The discovery of petroleum and the **petrochemical industry** provided vast feedstocks, enabling plastics, synthetic rubber, adhesives, and catalysts. **Biochemistry**, essentially a branch of organic chemistry focusing on carbon compounds in organisms, coalesced as a distinct field around 1900, with research volume growing exponentially thereafter.

### Characterization and Analysis
Because organic compounds often exist as mixtures, **purity assessment** is critical. Separation techniques include **distillation, crystallization, evaporation, solvent extraction**, and **chromatography** (HPLC, gas chromatography). Structural elucidation has shifted from "wet" chemical tests to instrumental methods:
*   **Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) Spectroscopy**: The primary tool; exploits NMR-active isotopes **¹H** and **¹³C** to assign atom connectivity and **stereochemistry** (3D spatial arrangement).
*   **Mass Spectrometry (MS)**: Determines molecular weight and structure via fragmentation patterns; high-resolution MS yields exact molecular formulas. Modern ionization methods handle non-volatile compounds.
*   **Elemental Analysis**: Destructive determination of elemental composition; largely supplanted by high-res MS.
*   **X-ray Crystallography**: Provides precise molecular geometry from single crystals; now rapid due to automation.
*   **Infrared (IR), UV/Vis, Optical Rotation**: Provide supplementary functional group and chirality data.

### Physical Properties
Key quantitative properties include **melting point (m.p.)**, **boiling point (b.p.)**, **solubility**, and **refractive index**; qualitative traits include odor, color, and consistency. Unlike many inorganics, organics typically melt and boil (though they degrade above ~300 °C). M.p./b.p. correlate with **polarity** and **molecular weight**; symmetrical compounds may **sublime** (e.g., para-dichlorobenzene). **Solubility**: Neutral organics are generally **hydrophobic** (insoluble in water, soluble in organic solvents), except low-MW alcohols, amines, and carboxylic acids, which hydrogen-bond with water. **Solid-state properties** of conjugated polymers (piezoelectricity, electrical conductivity, non-linear optics) bridge into materials science.

### Nomenclature and Structure Representation
Names are **systematic** (IUPAC rules: parent structure + prefixes/suffixes/locants) or **nonsystematic** (trivial names like "aspirin" or "LSD"; common for complex natural products). IUPAC is rigorous but cumbersome for large molecules. Machine-readable formats include **SMILES** and **InChI**. Structures are primarily communicated via **line-angle formulas** (skeletal structures), where vertices/termini represent carbon atoms and hydrogen count is implied by tetravalency. The 1892 **Geneva Rules** were the first international standardization effort, prompted by chaotic naming.

### Classification of Organic Compounds

#### Functional Groups
A **functional group** is a specific atomic grouping (e.g., C–O–H in alcohols) that confers predictable reactivity and properties across different molecules. Classification by functional group (alcohols, carboxylic acids, amines, etc.) is central. Most contain **heteroatoms** (non-C/H). Groups modulate acidity/basicity (**pKa**): lower pKa increases **electrophilicity** (susceptibility to attack); higher pKa increases **nucleophilicity** (attacking ability). Reactivity hierarchies exist (e.g., acyl chlorides > carboxylic acids > esters > amines). **Dipole moments** and **steric hindrance** further modulate reactivity.

#### Hydrocarbon Frameworks
*   **Aliphatic**: Open-chain or non-aromatic cyclic. **Alkanes** (single bonds), **alkenes** (double bonds), **alkynes** (triple bonds). Branching affects properties (e.g., octane number). **Cycloalkanes** (saturated rings) are most stable at 5–6 members; **cycloalkenes/ynes** contain unsaturation.
*   **Aromatic**: Contain conjugated π-systems (sp² hybridized) with **4n+2 π electrons** (Hückel’s rule), conferring extra stability (e.g., benzene). **4n π electrons** cause **antiaromaticity** (instability).
*   **Heterocyclic**: Rings containing heteroatoms (O, S, N) as ring members (**endocyclic**). **Aromatic heterocycles** (pyridine, furan) and **alicyclic** (piperidine, tetrahydrofuran) are ubiquitous in dyes, drugs, alkaloids, vitamins, steroids, and nucleic acids (DNA/RNA). **Polycyclic** (fused rings, e.g., purines) and **spiro** (corner-sharing) systems are common in natural products.

#### Polymers and Biomolecules
**Polymerization** links **monomers** into long chains/networks via C–C bonds. **Synthetic polymers** (industrial plastics, rubbers) vs. **biopolymers** (natural). **Biomolecules** (studied in **biomolecular chemistry**) include:
*   **Biopolymers**: Proteins (peptides), nucleic acids (DNA, RNA), polysaccharides (starch, cellulose).
*   **Monomers/Metabolites**: Amino acids, carbohydrates, lipids, Krebs cycle intermediates.
*   **Isoprenoids**: Animal cholesterol/steroid hormones; plant terpenes, rubber (polyisoprene).
*   **Petroleum**: Fossilized biomolecules.
*   **Small molecules**: Biologically active non-polymers (< ~1000 g/mol), central to pharmacology.

#### Allotropes and Special Classes
**Fullerenes** (C60 "buckyballs," nanotubes) discovered 1985 (Nobel 1996) bridge to materials science. Other classes organized by heteroatom: **organometallic** (C–metal bonds), **organosulfur**, **organophosphorus**, **organosilicon**.

### Organic Reactions
Reactions involve organic substrates and are governed by **electron affinity**, **bond strengths**, and **steric hindrance**, which dictate the stability of **reactive intermediates** and thus the reaction path. Fundamental types: **addition, elimination, substitution, pericyclic, rearrangement, redox**. Mechanisms are depicted via **arrow pushing** (curved arrows tracking electron flow). Example: Nucleophilic substitution (Nu⁻ + C–X → C–Nu + X⁻). The number of possible reactions is infinite, but general patterns allow prediction. Many mechanisms remain debated.

### Organic Synthesis
**Synthetic organic chemistry** is an applied science bordering engineering. **Total synthesis** designs multi-step routes to a **target molecule** from simple starting materials, exploiting functional group reactivity (e.g., carbonyls as nucleophiles via **enolates** or electrophiles; combined in the **aldol reaction**). **Retrosynthetic analysis** (E.J. Corey) works backward from the target, cleaving bonds via known reactions to reach available precursors, generating a "synthetic tree" of alternative routes. Laboratory execution is mandatory to validate designs.

## Terms
- ****Vitalism**** — Historical doctrine that organic compounds require a "vital force" from living organisms and cannot be synthesized from inorganic matter.
- ****Wöhler Synthesis**** — The 1828 laboratory preparation of urea from inorganic ammonium cyanate, disproving vitalism.
- ****Functional Group**** — A specific group of atoms (e.g., –OH, –COOH) within a molecule that determines its characteristic chemical reactions and properties.
- ****Total Synthesis**** — The complete, multi-step chemical construction of a complex molecule (often a natural product) from simple, commercially available precursors.
- ****Retrosynthetic Analysis**** — A problem-solving technique (Corey) for planning synthesis by mentally deconstructing the target molecule into simpler precursors via known reactions.
- ****NMR Spectroscopy**** — An analytical technique exploiting the magnetic properties of nuclei (¹H, ¹³C) to determine atomic connectivity, conformation, and stereochemistry.
- ****Hückel’s Rule (4n+2)**** — Criterion for aromaticity: a planar, cyclic, conjugated π-system is aromatic if it contains 4n+2 π electrons (n = integer).
- ****Polymerization**** — The chemical process of linking monomer units via covalent bonds (typically C–C) to form macromolecular chains or networks (polymers).
- ****Biopolymer**** — A polymer produced by living organisms (e.g., proteins, DNA, polysaccharides), as opposed to synthetic industrial polymers.
- ****Small Molecule**** — In pharmacology, a low molecular weight (< ~1000 g/mol) organic compound that is biologically active but not a polymer.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Reaction Mechanisms**: The stepwise mechanisms for certain organic reactions remain subjects of ongoing debate and have not been fully elucidated.
*   **Nomenclature for Complexity**: Systematic IUPAC naming becomes impractical for highly complex molecules; tension exists between human-readable trivial names and machine-readable identifiers (SMILES/InChI).
*   **Total Synthesis Efficiency**: While complexity achievable in total synthesis has increased (vitamin B12, etc.), designing *practically useful* (efficient, scalable, economical) routes remains a distinct engineering challenge.
*   **Organometallic Boundaries**: The overlap between organic, organometallic, and materials chemistry (e.g., fullerenes, conductive polymers) creates fluid classification boundaries.
