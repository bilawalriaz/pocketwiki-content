# Robert the Bruce

## Overview
Robert I (1274–1329), known as Robert the Bruce, was King of Scots who led Scotland to independence from England during the First War of Scottish Independence. His reign restored Scottish sovereignty and established the Bruce dynasty, making him a national hero whose descendants include all subsequent Scottish and British monarchs.

## Timeline
- **11 July 1274** — Born, likely at Turnberry Castle, Ayrshire
- **1292** — "Great Cause" awards Scottish throne to John Balliol; Bruce family loses claim
- **1296** — Swears fealty to Edward I of England at Berwick
- **1297** — Joins Scottish rebellion against Edward I
- **1298** — Appointed joint Guardian of Scotland with John Comyn
- **1306** — Murders John Comyn; crowned King of Scots on 25 March
- **1307** — Defeats English at Battle of Loudoun Hill; begins guerrilla war
- **1308** — Defeats Comyns at Battle of Inverurie; "Harrying of Buchan" destroys Comyn power
- **1314** — Decisive victory over English at Battle of Bannockburn
- **1320** — Declaration of Arbroath asserts Scottish independence
- **1328** — Treaty of Edinburgh–Northampton recognizes Scottish independence
- **7 June 1329** — Dies at Cardross; buried at Dunfermline Abbey

## Body

### Early Life and Dynastic Claim
Robert was the eldest son of Robert de Brus, 6th Lord of Annandale, and Marjorie, Countess of Carrick. Through his father, he was a fourth-great-grandson of King David I, giving him a claim to the Scottish throne. His grandfather, Robert de Brus, 5th Lord of Annandale, had been a claimant during the "Great Cause" that awarded the throne to John Balliol in 1292. Robert inherited the Earldom of Carrick from his mother and was raised in both Anglo-Norman and Gaelic cultures, becoming trilingual in English, Gaelic, and Latin.

### Path to the Throne
After the "Great Cause," the Bruces initially sided with Edward I against Balliol. Robert joined the 1297 Scottish rebellion but submitted to Edward in 1302. In 1305, he made a secret pact with his rival John Comyn, who agreed to forfeit his throne claim in exchange for Bruce lands. When Comyn allegedly betrayed this agreement to Edward, Bruce killed him in a Dumfries chapel on 10 February 1306. This act led to Bruce's excommunication, though he received absolution from Bishop Robert Wishart. He was crowned king six weeks later.

### War for Independence (1306–1314)
After his coronation, Bruce was defeated at Methven and forced into hiding. He re-emerged in 1307, winning small victories at Glen Trool and Loudoun Hill. Following Edward I's death in July 1307, Bruce waged a guerrilla campaign, systematically destroying Comyn strongholds in the north. By 1309, he controlled Scotland north of the Tay. His first parliament met at St. Andrews in March 1309. Between 1310 and 1314, he captured key English-held castles including Linlithgow, Dumbarton, and Perth.

### Bannockburn and Aftermath
In 1314, Bruce's brother Edward besieged Stirling Castle, forcing Edward II to march north with 15,000–20,000 troops. At Bannockburn on 23–24 June, Bruce's 5,500–6,500 spearmen defeated the larger English army in cramped terrain. The victory confirmed Scottish independence and allowed Bruce to raid northern England and invade Ireland in 1315, where his brother Edward was crowned High King. The Irish campaign ultimately failed due to famine and resistance.

### Diplomatic Triumph
Despite military victories, English recognition came slowly. The 1320 Declaration of Arbroath, submitted to Pope John XXII, asserted Scotland's independence and Bruce's rightful kingship. The Pope recognized Bruce as king in 1324 and lifted his excommunication in 1328. The Treaty of Edinburgh–Northampton (1328) finally saw Edward III renounce all claims to Scottish sovereignty.

### Death and Burial
Bruce died on 7 June 1329 at Cardross, possibly from leprosy (though modern analysis of his skull shows no signs of the disease). His body was buried at Dunfermline Abbey in an elaborate Paris-made tomb. His heart, following his dying wish, was taken by Sir James Douglas on a crusade; Douglas was killed in Spain in 1330, and the heart was returned and buried at Melrose Abbey.

## Terms
- **Guardian of Scotland**: Regent ruling during a monarch's absence or minority; Bruce served jointly with John Comyn after Wallace's resignation.
- **Great Cause**: The 1292 legal proceeding where Edward I adjudicated competing claims to the Scottish throne, awarding it to John Balliol.
- **Hobelar**: Light cavalry using small, sturdy ponies for mounted raids—a tactic associated with Gaelic warfare.
- **Declaration of Arbroath**: 1320 letter from Scottish nobles to the Pope asserting independence and Bruce's legitimate kingship.
- **Harrying**: Systematic destruction of a region's resources and strongholds to eliminate opposition, as Bruce did in Buchan (1308).
- **Pan-Gaelic Greater Scotia**: Bruce's ideological vision of a united Gaelic realm spanning Scotland and Ireland.
- **Capitulation of Irvine**: 1297 treaty where rebellious Scots, including Bruce, submitted to Edward I while reserving rights against foreign service.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Cause of death**: Contemporary accounts mention "leprosy," but modern analysis of his skull shows no signs of the disease. Alternatives proposed include eczema, tuberculosis, syphilis, motor neuron disease, cancer, or strokes.
- **Winter 1306–1307 hiding place**: Uncertain whether Bruce sheltered in the Hebrides, Ireland, Orkney, or Norway after his defeat at Methven.
- **Role at Falkirk (1298)**: Some sources claim Bruce fought for Edward I, but he does not appear on the English army roll, and he soon attacked English-held Ayr Castle.
- **Comyn murder details**: Accounts vary—Barbour says Bruce stabbed Comyn, while the *Scotichronicon* claims Bruce's supporters finished him off after the initial attack.
