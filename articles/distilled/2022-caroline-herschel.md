# Caroline Herschel

## Overview
Caroline Herschel (1750–1848) was a German astronomer and the first woman to earn a salary as a scientist and hold a government position in England. Working alongside her brother William, she became a pioneering figure in astronomy, discovering several comets and creating foundational star catalogues that advanced the field. Her career broke significant gender barriers in science, earning her lasting recognition as a professional astronomer in her own right.

- **9 January 1848** — Dies in Hanover, Germany.

## Body

### Early Life and Move to England
Caroline Herschel received a limited education in Hanover, where childhood typhus stunted her growth and impaired her vision. Her father provided some tutoring, but her mother intended her for domestic service. In 1772, her brothers William and Alexander brought her to Bath, England, where she initially trained as a singer for William's musical performances. She managed his household and pursued lessons in singing, arithmetic, and the harpsichord, becoming a respected vocalist.

### Transition to Astronomy and Collaboration
As William shifted his focus from music to astronomy in the 1770s, Caroline supported him by polishing telescope mirrors and recording observations. She moved with him to Datchet in 1782, where she began "sweeping" the sky to search for nebulae and comets. Her role evolved from assistant to independent researcher; she learned to identify stars using John Flamsteed's catalogue and developed her own organizational system. In 1787, King George III granted her a salary of £50 annually for her work, making her the first woman in England to receive a government scientific position.

### Independent Discoveries and Catalogues
Between 1786 and 1797, Herschel discovered eight comets, achieving priority on five and rediscovering Comet Encke. Her first comet, found on 1 August 1786, was demonstrated to the royal family by William. She also created important catalogues: in 1798, she published an index to Flamsteed's observations, listing 560 omitted stars and correcting errors. In 1802, she presented a catalogue of approximately 500 new nebulae and clusters, which was published by the Royal Society under William's name. Later, she organized 2,500 nebulae into zones for her nephew John Herschel, forming the basis for the New General Catalogue (NGC).

### Later Life and Legacy
After William's death in 1822, Caroline returned to Hanover, where she verified his findings and compiled her catalogue work. She received numerous honors, including the Royal Astronomical Society's Gold Medal in 1828 and an honorary membership in 1835. The King of Prussia awarded her a gold medal for science in 1846. Her legacy includes the asteroid 281 Lucretia, the lunar crater C. Herschel, and the NGC objects she catalogued. She died in 1848, having contributed to the discovery of over 2,400 astronomical objects with her brother.

## Terms
- **Nebula**: A cloud of gas and dust in outer space, often appearing as a fuzzy patch of light through a telescope.
- **Periodic comet**: A comet that orbits the Sun at regular intervals, such as 35P/Herschel–Rigollet.
- **Sweeping**: The methodical scanning of the sky in strips to discover new celestial objects.
- **Reflecting telescope**: A telescope that uses mirrors to gather and focus light, as opposed to lenses.
- **New General Catalogue (NGC)**: A catalogue of deep-sky objects (nebulae and star clusters) compiled from Caroline Herschel's work and expanded by her nephew.
- **Honorary member**: A status granted by a learned society to recognize distinguished contributions, often without the usual membership requirements.
- **Gold Medal (Royal Astronomical Society)**: A prestigious award for outstanding contributions to astronomy, first awarded to a woman (Herschel) in 1828.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The source notes that while Herschel is often credited as the first woman to discover a comet, Maria Kirch discovered one in the early 1700s, though the discovery was attributed to her husband.
- The exact nature of the tension in the sibling relationship after William's 1788 marriage is complex; Caroline destroyed her journals from 1788 to 1798, leaving her feelings during that period partially unknown.
