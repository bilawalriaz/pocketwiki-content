# Great Game

## Overview
The Great Game was a 19th-century geopolitical rivalry between the British and Russian empires for influence and control in Central Asia, primarily in Afghanistan, Persia, and Tibet. It was marked by military interventions, diplomatic intrigue, and the creation of buffer states, but never escalated into direct war between the two powers. The rivalry shaped the political boundaries of modern Central and South Asia and ended with a formal alliance in 1907.

## Timeline
- **1801** — Tsar Paul I plans the "Indian March" to invade British India; he is assassinated before it begins.
- **1813** — Treaty of Gulistan ends the first Russo-Persian War, giving Russia rights to intervene in Persia.
- **1828** — Treaty of Turkmenchay ends the second Russo-Persian War, cementing Russian dominance in Persia.
- **1830** — Lord Ellenborough tasks Lord Bentinck with establishing a trade route to Bukhara, a traditional start date for the Game.
- **1838** — Britain invades Afghanistan, beginning the First Anglo-Afghan War.
- **1842** — British envoys Stoddart and Conolly are executed in Bukhara.
- **1856** — Britain fights the Anglo-Persian War over Herat.
- **1873** — Anglo-Russian Agreement defines spheres of influence, recognizing Afghan territory.
- **1878** — Russia sends an uninvited mission to Kabul, triggering the Second Anglo-Afghan War.
- **1885** — The Panjdeh incident sees Russia annex Afghan territory, leading to border protocols.
- **1895** — Pamir Boundary Commission protocols define the Afghan-Russian border, a traditional end date.
- **1907** — Anglo-Russian Convention formally ends the rivalry, delineating spheres in Persia, Afghanistan, and Tibet.

## Body

### Origins and Motivations
The rivalry stemmed from mutual fear: Britain feared Russia's southward expansion would threaten its "jewel in the crown," India, while Russia feared British commercial and political encroachment into Central Asia. Britain aimed to create a chain of buffer states (Ottoman Empire, Persia, Afghanistan) to protect India. Russia sought secure southern borders, access to warm-water ports, and to counter British influence. Both empires saw themselves as "civilizing powers" expanding into what they perceived as backward regions.

### Early Conflicts and Exploration (1800s–1840s)
Early signs included Tsar Paul I's planned invasion of India and Russian wars with Persia (1804–1813, 1826–1828). British explorers and intelligence officers, like Alexander Burnes and Arthur Conolly, mapped routes and assessed Russian intentions. The First Anglo-Afghan War (1838–1842) was a disastrous British attempt to install a friendly ruler, ending in a catastrophic retreat. The Anglo-Sikh Wars (1845–1849) expanded British territory in the Punjab, bringing it closer to Central Asia.

### Expansion and Diplomacy (1850s–1890s)
After the Crimean War, Russia aggressively expanded into Central Asia, conquering Tashkent (1865), Samarkand (1868), and Khiva (1873). Britain responded with the Second Anglo-Afghan War (1878–1880) to secure a buffer state. Diplomatic efforts, like the 1873 Agreement and the Afghan Boundary Commission, attempted to define borders. The 1885 Panjdeh incident, where Russia seized Afghan territory, nearly caused war but was resolved by protocol. Competition also extended to Tibet, with British and Russian expeditions and espionage.

### Conclusion and Legacy
The 1907 Anglo-Russian Convention formally ended the Great Game. The empires, now facing the rising threat of Germany, agreed on spheres of influence: Britain dominated southern Persia and Afghanistan, Russia dominated northern Persia and Central Asia, and Tibet was declared neutral. The Game's legacy includes the modern borders of Afghanistan, the Wakhan Corridor, and the political dynamics of the region. Historians debate its exact dates and whether it was a coherent strategy or a later legend, but its impact on imperial policy and regional geopolitics is undeniable.

## Terms
- **Buffer State**: A country placed between hostile powers to prevent conflict, like Afghanistan between Britain and Russia.
- **Forward Policy**: A British strategy of extending influence and military presence into frontier regions to pre-empt Russian advances.
- **Khanate**: A Central Asian state ruled by a khan, such as the Khanates of Khiva and Bukhara.
- **Spheres of Influence**: Areas where a foreign power has exclusive economic or political control, as defined in treaties like the 1907 Convention.
- **Pamir Boundary Commission**: A joint Anglo-Russian body that demarcated the border between Russian and British spheres in the Pamir Mountains in 1895.
- **Wakhan Corridor**: A narrow strip of Afghan territory created as a buffer between Russian and British India.
- **Anglo-Russian Convention (1907)**: The treaty that formally ended the Great Game by delineating spheres of influence in Persia, Afghanistan, and Tibet.
- **Cossack Brigade**: A Persian military unit led by Russian officers, used to exert Russian influence in Iran.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Dating**: Historians disagree on the start (1813, 1830, 1838) and end (1895, 1907, 1917) of the Great Game.
- **Reality vs. Legend**: Some scholars, like Gerald Morgan and Malcolm Yapp, argue the "Great Game" was a later legend and that British policy in India was primarily about internal control, not countering Russia.
- **Russian Intentions**: Debate continues over whether Russia ever seriously planned to invade India or used the threat as a bargaining chip in European diplomacy.
- **Impact on Partition**: Some research suggests the Great Game influenced the 1947 partition of India, as Britain sought to create a friendly state (Pakistan) to counter Soviet influence.
