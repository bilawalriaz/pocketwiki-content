# Equatorial Guinea

## Overview
Equatorial Guinea is a Central African nation and the only Spanish-speaking country on the continent. Its modern history is defined by a brutal post-independence dictatorship, followed by an oil boom that created immense national wealth but with extreme inequality, all under a long-standing authoritarian regime.

## Timeline
- **1472** — Portuguese explorer Fernando Pó sights the island of Bioko.
- **1778** — Treaty of El Pardo cedes Bioko and mainland rights from Portugal to Spain.
- **1900** — Treaty of Paris defines the mainland territory of Río Muni.
- **1926** — Bioko and Río Muni are united as the colony of Spanish Guinea.
- **12 Oct 1968** — Independence from Spain; Francisco Macías Nguema becomes president.
- **3 Aug 1979** — Teodoro Obiang Nguema Mbasogo overthrows Macías in a coup.
- **1995** — Major oil discovery by Mobil transforms the economy.
- **2011** — A new constitution is approved, limiting presidential terms.
- **2017** — The new capital city is renamed Ciudad de la Paz.
- **19 May 2025** — The ICJ grants Equatorial Guinea sovereignty over several disputed islands.

## Body

### History and Colonial Rule
The region was first settled by Pygmies, followed by Bantu migrations. Portuguese explorers arrived in 1472, and Spain gained control via the 1778 Treaty of El Pardo. The colony was economically marginal until the 20th century, relying on plantation agriculture and immigrant labor. The mainland territory of Río Muni was formally defined in 1900. Spanish Guinea was administered as a colony until growing nationalist pressure and UN resolutions led to independence in 1968.

### Post-Independence Dictatorship and Oil
Independence was followed by the bloody dictatorship of Francisco Macías Nguema, who declared himself president for life in 1972. His regime was characterized by mass killings and economic collapse, leading to his overthrow by his nephew, Teodoro Obiang, in 1979. Obiang has ruled since. The 1995 discovery of oil led to rapid economic growth, making Equatorial Guinea one of Africa's richest countries per capita. However, this wealth is extremely unevenly distributed, and the country ranks low on human development indices.

### Government and Politics
Equatorial Guinea is a unitary state under the long-standing authoritarian rule of President Obiang. The 1982 constitution grants him extensive powers. A 2011 constitution introduced term limits, but Obiang has stated they are not retroactive. The political system is dominated by Obiang's party, with opposition severely restricted and elections consistently criticized for fraud and violence. The country has one of the world's worst human rights records, with reports of torture, arbitrary detention, and severe press censorship.

### Economy and Demographics
The economy is overwhelmingly dependent on oil, which accounts for 97% of exports. Despite high GNI per capita, poverty is widespread due to corruption and inequality. The population is predominantly Bantu, with the Fang people as the largest ethnic group. Spanish is the main official language, with French and Portuguese also official. The country is divided into eight provinces, with a new capital, Ciudad de la Paz, established in 2017.

## Terms
- **Fang**: The largest ethnic group in Equatorial Guinea, indigenous to the mainland.
- **Bubi**: The second-largest ethnic group, indigenous to Bioko Island.
- **Río Muni**: The mainland region of Equatorial Guinea, bordered by Cameroon and Gabon.
- **Bioko**: The largest island, site of the former capital Malabo.
- **Spanish Guinea**: The colonial name for the territory before independence.
- **CFA franc**: The currency adopted by Equatorial Guinea in 1985.
- **Kleptocracy**: A government where leaders exploit national resources for personal gain.
- **Fernandinos**: A Creole community on Bioko Island with mixed African and European ancestry.

## Debates and Open questions
The primary debate concerns the sustainability of the current political system and the potential for a post-Obiang transition, especially given the role of his son, Teodorín, in government. Scholars also debate the effectiveness of international pressure and oil revenue management in promoting development and human rights. The extreme disparity between national wealth and public welfare remains a central issue for analysis.
