# Myocardial infarction

## Overview
Myocardial infarction (MI), commonly called a heart attack, is the death of heart muscle tissue caused by prolonged ischemia (lack of oxygen delivery) due to blocked coronary blood flow. It is the most dangerous form of acute coronary syndrome, distinguished from unstable angina by the presence of cell death detectable via cardiac biomarkers like troponin. The primary mechanism is the rupture of an atherosclerotic plaque triggering thrombus formation, though supply-demand mismatch or coronary spasm can also cause infarction. Rapid diagnosis via ECG and biomarkers, followed by immediate reperfusion (PCI or fibrinolysis) and antithrombotic therapy, is critical to limit infarct size. Long-term management focuses on secondary prevention through lifestyle modification, antiplatelet agents, beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and statins. Prognosis depends heavily on infarct extent, complications (e.g., heart failure, arrhythmia), and social determinants of health.

## Body

### Definition and Classification
Myocardial infarction is defined by a rise and/or fall of cardiac biomarkers (preferably troponin) with at least one of: ischemic symptoms, new ECG changes (ST/T changes, Q waves, LBBB), imaging evidence of new wall motion abnormality, or thrombus identification. It is a subset of acute coronary syndrome involving actual myocyte necrosis. The clinical classification distinguishes **ST-elevation MI (STEMI)** (25–40% of MIs), caused by complete coronary occlusion, from **non-ST-elevation MI (NSTEMI)**, caused by partial occlusion or transient blockage. The 2012 universal definition adds five etiological types: Type 1 (spontaneous plaque rupture/erosion); Type 2 (supply-demand mismatch, e.g., spasm, anemia, hypotension); Type 3 (sudden cardiac death with suggestive findings but no biomarkers); Type 4 (PCI/stent-related); and Type 5 (CABG-related).

### Pathophysiology
The dominant mechanism is **atherosclerosis**: decades-long buildup of lipid-laden foam cells and fibrous tissue in the arterial intima. Plaques with thin fibrous caps and large lipid cores are prone to rupture, exposing thrombogenic material and triggering platelet activation and thrombus formation, which can occlude the artery in minutes. If flow is not restored, the **ischemic cascade** begins: ATP depletion disables Na/K pumps, causing calcium overload, enzyme activation, and necrosis. Death begins in the **subendocardium** (most vulnerable to low flow) within 15–30 minutes, progressing as a "wavefront" toward the epicardium over 3–4 hours. Collagen scar replaces dead tissue. **Reperfusion injury** (calcium influx, oxygen free radicals) and the **no-reflow phenomenon** (microvascular obstruction despite epicardial patency) add further damage. Non-atherosclerotic causes include coronary spasm (Prinzmetal’s angina), embolism, dissection, and demand ischemia (tachycardia, anemia, hypotension).

### Clinical Presentation
Classic symptom is retrosternal pressure/tightness radiating to the left arm, jaw, or back, lasting >20 minutes, unrelieved by position. **Levine’s sign** (clenched fist over sternum) is classic but has poor predictive value. **Atypical presentations** are common: women, elderly, and diabetics more often report dyspnea, fatigue, nausea, jaw/neck pain, or syncope without chest pain. **Silent MIs** (no symptoms) constitute 22–64% of infarcts, detected later by ECG or troponin; they are prevalent in diabetics (autonomic neuropathy) and transplant recipients (denervated heart). Complications presenting acutely include ventricular fibrillation (sudden death), cardiogenic shock, heart block, papillary muscle rupture (mitral regurgitation), and free wall rupture.

### Diagnosis
**Biomarkers:** High-sensitivity cardiac troponin (hs-cTn) is the gold standard (rises 2–3h, peaks 1–2d). A single normal hs-cTn with a normal ECG can rule out MI. CK-MB and myoglobin are discouraged (lower specificity). **ECG:** Serial ECGs are essential. STEMI criteria: new ST elevation ≥2mm (men) or ≥1.5mm (women) in V2–V3, or ≥1mm in two other contiguous leads. NSTEMI shows ST depression, T-wave inversion, or non-specific changes. ECG localizes infarct territory (e.g., anterior = LAD, inferior = RCA/LCx). **Imaging:** Echocardiography assesses wall motion abnormalities and complications; nuclear/PET scans assess perfusion/viability but are not for acute diagnosis. **Differential** includes unstable angina, pericarditis, aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism, GERD, and musculoskeletal pain.

### Management
**Immediate (Pre-hospital/ED):** Aspirin (loading dose) reduces mortality ~50%. P2Y12 inhibitors (clopidogrel, ticagrelor, prasugrel) added for dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT). Anticoagulation (unfractionated heparin) recommended. Nitroglycerin/opioids for pain (no mortality benefit). Oxygen only if hypoxic (SpO2 <90%).
**Reperfusion (STEMI):** Primary **PCI** is preferred if achievable within 90–120 minutes of first medical contact. If PCI delayed >120 min, **fibrinolysis** (tPA, tenecteplase) within 30 min of arrival is indicated (contraindicated if high bleeding risk, >24h symptoms). Rescue PCI for failed lysis.
**NSTEMI:** Early invasive strategy (PCI within 1–3 days) for high-risk patients (GRACE/TIMI scores); ischemia-guided strategy for lower risk. 2017 review questioned benefit of *very* early PCI.
**Adjunctive:** Beta-blockers within 24h (if no shock/block) reduce reinfarction/arrhythmia but not mortality in modern era. ACE inhibitors within 24h (if no renal failure/hypotension) reduce mortality. High-intensity statins lower LDL and events. Aldosterone antagonists if LV dysfunction. ICD implantation considered if LVEF ≤35% after 40 days optimal medical therapy.
**Rehabilitation:** Early exercise-based cardiac rehab reduces readmissions, improves quality of life, and lowers cardiovascular mortality.

### Prevention
**Primary:** Smoking cessation (36% of CAD attributable), healthy diet (Mediterranean pattern strongest evidence; replace saturated with polyunsaturated fats; avoid trans fats), 150 min/week moderate exercise, weight control, moderate alcohol. Statins for elevated risk. Aspirin for primary prevention is controversial: bleeding risk often outweighs benefit except in very high-risk/low-bleeding-risk individuals. Influenza vaccine reduces MI risk 15–45%.
**Secondary:** Same lifestyle measures plus DAPT (aspirin + P2Y12 inhibitor) for 12 months post-MI/stent (longer if high ischemic/low bleeding risk). Beta-blockers, ACEi/ARB, statins, aldosterone antagonists indefinitely. Gradual return to exercise after 1–2 weeks.

### Prognosis and Complications
Untreated: ~25% die within minutes, ~40% within a month. Treated STEMI: 5–6% in-hospital mortality, 7–18% 1-year mortality. Poor prognostic markers: older age, anterior infarct, LV dysfunction, persistent VT/VF, heart block, cardiogenic shock, renal dysfunction, diabetes. **Early complications:** Arrhythmias (VF, heart block), cardiogenic shock (leading cause of in-hospital death), mechanical complications (free wall rupture, VSD, papillary muscle rupture). **Late complications:** Heart failure (remodeling, scar), LV aneurysm (~10%, risk for thrombus/arrhythmia), Dressler’s syndrome (post-MI pericarditis), stroke (cardioembolic or anticoagulation-related).

### Epidemiology and Social Determinants
Global burden shifting: declining in high-income nations but rising in developing world (e.g., India leading cause by 2004). 2030 projection: IHD leading cause of death globally. **Disparities:** Low SES areas have 2x MI rates. African Americans have higher burden due to untreated risk factors. South Asians have earlier onset. Uninsured/underinsured delay care, worsening outcomes. Lower education correlates with higher MI incidence and mortality. Immigration barriers (fear, language, eligibility) limit access.

### Society, Culture, and Law
Media depictions (sudden collapse) misrepresent typical symptoms, delaying care-seeking. **Legal:** At common law, MI is usually a "disease," not an "injury," affecting workers' compensation. Exceptions: unusual exertion/stress at work, or statutory presumptions for specific occupations (e.g., police). Some jurisdictions restrict driving/flying post-MI.

## Terms
- ****Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS)**** — Spectrum of conditions (STEMI, NSTEMI, unstable angina) caused by sudden reduced coronary blood flow.
- ****Troponin**** — Cardiac regulatory protein released into blood upon myocyte necrosis; gold-standard biomarker for MI diagnosis.
- ****STEMI**** — ST-elevation MI; complete coronary occlusion requiring emergent reperfusion (PCI/fibrinolysis).
- ****NSTEMI**** — Non-ST-elevation MI; partial/transient occlusion managed with antithrombotics ± early invasive strategy.
- ****PCI**** — Percutaneous coronary intervention; catheter-based balloon angioplasty ± stenting to open blocked artery.
- ****Fibrinolysis**** — Pharmacologic dissolution of thrombus (e.g., alteplase, tenecteplase); alternative if PCI unavailable timely.
- ****DAPT**** — Dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin + P2Y12 inhibitor); standard post-MI/stent for ~12 months.
- ****Ischemic Cascade**** — Sequence of cellular events (ATP loss → ion imbalance → calcium overload → necrosis) following coronary occlusion.
- ****No-Reflow Phenomenon**** — Failure of myocardial perfusion despite patent epicardial artery, due to microvascular obstruction/endothelial swelling.
- ****Cardiogenic Shock**** — Life-threatening hypotension and end-organ hypoperfusion due to pump failure; leading cause of in-hospital MI death.

## Debates and open questions
- **Aspirin for primary prevention:** Net benefit unclear; guidelines conflict on whether bleeding risk outweighs ischemic reduction in moderate-risk populations.
- **Saturated fat vs. polyunsaturated fat:** Evidence conflicting on whether reducing saturated fat intake lowers MI risk; Mediterranean diet has strongest support but specific macronutrient targets debated.
- **Beta-blockers post-MI in reperfusion era:** Modern trials question mortality benefit when added to contemporary therapy (PCI, statins, ACEi); may reduce reinfarction/arrhythmia but impair hemodynamics in some.
- **Very early invasive strategy for NSTEMI:** 2017 review found no mortality benefit for immediate (<24h) vs. delayed (1–3 days) PCI; optimal timing remains debated.
- **Duration of DAPT:** Optimal length beyond 12 months uncertain; trials test extended vs. shortened regimens balancing ischemic vs. bleeding risk.
- **Social determinants:** Mechanisms linking neighborhood disadvantage, race, and insurance status to biology (stress, access, treatment bias) need further elucidation to design effective interventions.
