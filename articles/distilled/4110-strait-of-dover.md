# Strait of Dover

## Overview
The Strait of Dover is the narrowest part of the English Channel, separating Great Britain from continental Europe. It is one of the world's busiest international shipping lanes and a critical geographic and historical boundary. Its formation is a result of catastrophic geological events, and it now hosts a unique marine ecosystem.

## Timeline
- **c. 425,000 years ago** — First major flood from an ice-dammed lake breaches the Weald-Artois chalk range, beginning the strait's formation.
- **c. 225,000 years ago** — Second major flood from ice-dammed lakes of the Meuse and Rhine rivers further scours the channel.
- **c. 6,500–6,200 BCE** — Rising sea levels submerge Doggerland, the land bridge connecting Britain to France.
- **Late 17th century** — Severe winter ice during the "Little Ice Age" nearly freezes the strait, with only a league of open water reported in 1684.
- **1994** — The Channel Tunnel opens, providing a fixed link beneath the strait.

## Body

### Geography and Shipping
The Strait of Dover is the narrowest point of the English Channel, with a shortest crossing distance of 20.6 miles (33.2 km) from South Foreland, UK, to Cap Gris-Nez, France. It marks the boundary between the English Channel and the North Sea. The strait is within UK and French territorial waters, but international law allows free transit for vessels. It is one of the world's busiest seaways, with over 400 commercial vessels passing through daily, necessitating strict traffic management by UK and French authorities. Major ferry routes connect Dover to Calais and Dunkirk, and the Channel Tunnel, bored through chalk at an average depth of 45 m (148 ft) below the seabed, provides an alternative crossing.

### Geological Formation
The strait was formed by catastrophic erosion, not gradual processes. For millennia, a chalk land bridge linked Britain to France. The formation began about 425,000 years ago when an ice-dammed lake in the southern North Sea overflowed, catastrophically breaching the chalk ridge. A second, similar flood occurred about 225,000 years ago. These events carved massive channels into the dry bed of what would become the English Channel. The final submersion of the land bridge, Doggerland, occurred around 6,500–6,200 BCE due to melting ice and rising sea levels. The seabed is predominantly chalk, which erodes to form the famous white cliffs on both coasts.

### Marine Ecology and Protection
The strait's depth varies from 20 m (66 ft) on sandbanks to 68 m (223 ft) in the deep Lobourg channel. Its seabed features rocky zones, sandy flats, and sub-aqueous dunes. Strong tidal currents create calmer "lee zones" around rocky masses, allowing clearer water and algae growth, which supports high biodiversity. This area is a transition zone for Atlantic and North Sea species, hosting some endemic species. A 682 km² (263 sq mi) area, including the Lobourg channel and several shoals, is a Natura 2000 protected zone named "Ridens et dunes hydrauliques du Pas de Calais."

### Historical and Cultural Notes
The strait is famous for the visibility of its opposite coastlines on clear days, notably the White Cliffs of Dover from France. It has been a site for numerous unusual crossing attempts, including by swimming, pedalo, and other unconventional means. Historically, during the "Little Ice Age" in the late 17th century, the strait experienced severe winter ice, with reports of it nearly freezing over in 1684.

## Terms
- **Strait:** A narrow passage of water connecting two larger bodies of water.
- **Interglacial:** A warm period between glacial (ice age) periods.
- **Chalk:** A soft, white, porous sedimentary rock formed from the mineral calcite, often from marine organisms.
- **Doggerland:** A now-submerged landmass that once connected Great Britain to continental Europe.
- **Natura 2000:** A network of nature protection areas in the European Union.
- **Maerl:** A collective term for several species of seaweed that form unattached, branching structures of calcium carbonate on the seabed.
- **Urstrom:** A German term for a glacial meltwater river.

## Debates and Open Questions
The precise mechanisms and relative impacts of the two major catastrophic floods that formed the strait are subjects of ongoing geological study. The 2007 study by Gupta and Collier proposed the two-flood model, which was updated in 2017 to explain specific underwater features like plunge pools. The exact timing and processes of the final submergence of Doggerland are also refined by ongoing research.
