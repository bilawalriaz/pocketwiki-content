# Mary Wollstonecraft

## Overview
Mary Wollstonecraft (1759–1797) was an English writer and philosopher regarded as a founding feminist thinker. Her seminal work, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792), argued that women’s apparent inferiority stemmed from a lack of education, not nature, and demanded they be treated as rational beings. Her life—marked by unconventional relationships, suicide attempts, and death from childbed fever at 38—overshadowed her writing for nearly a century after her widower William Godwin published a candid memoir. With the rise of the suffrage movement and modern feminism, her arguments for female education, legal rights, and rational companionship in marriage were reclaimed as foundational to the struggle for gender equality.

## Timeline

- **1787–1788** — Publishes *Thoughts on the Education of Daughters*, *Mary: A Fiction*, and *Original Stories from Real Life*.

- **1792** — Publishes *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*; travels to Paris to witness the Revolution.
- **14 May 1794** — Gives birth to daughter Fanny Imlay in Le Havre; Gilbert Imlay later abandons her.
- **Dec 1794** — Publishes *An Historical and Moral View of the French Revolution*.
- **1795** — Returns to London; attempts suicide twice after Imlay’s final rejection.
- **1796** — Publishes *Letters Written in Sweden, Norway, and Denmark*; renews acquaintance with William Godwin.

- **30 Aug 1797** — Gives birth to Mary Wollstonecraft Godwin (later Mary Shelley).
- **10 Sept 1797** — Dies of septicaemia (childbed fever) 11 days postpartum.
- **1798** — Godwin publishes *Memoirs of the Author of A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*, damaging her reputation for a century.

## Body

### Early Life and Formative Relationships
Wollstonecraft’s childhood was defined by financial instability caused by her father’s speculative failures and his violent drunkenness; she often shielded her mother and sisters. Two friendships proved pivotal: Jane Arden introduced her to an intellectual household, while Fanny Blood became her closest emotional anchor. In 1784, Wollstonecraft helped her sister Eliza flee a likely postpartum depression, an act of defiance that condemned Eliza to poverty. After a brief, miserable stint as a lady’s companion in Bath (1778), she returned to nurse her dying mother. She then lived with the Bloods, attempting to realize a "female utopia" of mutual support. Economic necessity forced Wollstonecraft, her sisters, and Blood to open a school at Newington Green, a Dissenting community. Blood’s marriage and move to Lisbon ended the plan; Wollstonecraft nursed her through a fatal pregnancy in 1785, abandoning the school, which subsequently failed. Blood’s death devastated Wollstonecraft and inspired her first novel, *Mary: A Fiction* (1788).

### Literary Career and the "Vindications"
Frustrated by the narrow options for poor gentlewomen—governess or companion—Wollstonecraft resolved to become "the first of a new genus": a professional female author. In London, publisher Joseph Johnson provided work translating and reviewing for the *Analytical Review*, and introduced her to radicals like Thomas Paine and William Godwin. In 1790, Edmund Burke’s *Reflections on the Revolution in France* provoked her *A Vindication of the Rights of Men*, published anonymously then under her name. She attacked Burke’s defense of aristocracy and his gendered rhetoric, redefining his aesthetic categories of the "sublime" (masculine/strong) and "beautiful" (feminine/weak) to argue his prose turned citizens into passive "women." This pamphlet launched her fame and led directly to *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792). There, she argued women are not naturally inferior but rendered so by denied education. She demanded co-educational "country day schools," insisted women be "companions" to husbands rather than ornaments, and critiqued Rousseau’s *Émile* and conduct books that prized "sensibility" over reason. While asserting equality in the eyes of God and moral nature, she ambiguously granted men superior physical strength, complicating later feminist classification.

### France, Revolution, and Gilbert Imlay
Wollstonecraft arrived in Paris in December 1792, weeks before Louis XVI’s execution. She aligned with the moderate Girondins, witnessing the Revolution’s descent into the Jacobin Terror. She fell in love with Gilbert Imlay, an American adventurer, and lived with him unmarried—practicing the sexual autonomy she theoretically questioned in *Rights of Woman*. Disillusioned by the Revolution’s "venal" government and the Jacobins’ denial of women’s rights (confining them to Rousseau’s domestic ideal), she nonetheless hoped for a "fairer day." Pregnant and vulnerable as a British subject during the Terror, she was protected when Imlay falsely claimed them married to the U.S. embassy, granting her American citizenship. Many friends were guillotined; she fainted upon hearing of the Girondin leaders’ executions in October 1793. She gave birth to Fanny in May 1794 at Le Havre, writing *An Historical and Moral View of the French Revolution* while nursing the infant. Imlay eventually abandoned her for commercial blockade-running; her desperate letters reveal profound depression amid revolutionary chaos.

### *An Historical and Moral View of the French Revolution*
Published in London in December 1794, this history aimed to counter British "hysterical" anti-revolutionary narratives that depicted the French as mad. Wollstonecraft argued the Revolution arose from structural social, economic, and political crises. She condemned the Jacobin Terror but defended the Revolution’s early achievements, ending her narrative in 1789 to avoid detailing the Terror. She rebutted Burke’s portrayal of the October 1789 march on Versailles—where he called women "furies from hell"—by depicting them as housewives desperate for bread. She analyzed Marie Antoinette as a product of aristocratic values that reduced women to reproductive vessels and encouraged manipulative "femme fatale" behavior. Historian Tom Furniss later called it her "best work," though it was neglected until a 1989 reprint.

### England, Godwin, and Death
Returning to London in April 1795, Wollstonecraft was rejected by Imlay. She attempted suicide with laudanum (May 1795), then undertook a hazardous business trip to Scandinavia for him with her infant and maid, producing the acclaimed *Letters Written in Sweden, Norway, and Denmark* (1796). Upon realizing Imlay was gone, she attempted suicide again by jumping into the Thames, viewing the act as a "calmest act of reason." Rescued, she rejoined Johnson’s circle and began a relationship with William Godwin. Godwin, initially disappointed by her in 1791, fell in love after reading her *Letters*. They became lovers; when she became pregnant, they married on 29 March 1797 to legitimize the child, despite Godwin’s philosophical opposition to marriage. They lived separately but adjacently at The Polygon, Somers Town, a happy arrangement. On 30 August 1797, she gave birth to Mary (future author of *Frankenstein*). The placenta fragmented, causing puerperal fever; she died of septicaemia on 10 September 1797.

### Posthumous Reputation and Legacy
Godwin’s 1798 *Memoirs*, detailing her affairs, illegitimate child, and suicide attempts, scandalized the public. Robert Southey accused Godwin of "stripping his dead wife naked." For nearly a century, she was pilloried in novels (e.g., Maria Edgeworth’s *Belinda*) as a cautionary tale. Yet readers like Elizabeth Barrett Browning (age 12), Lucretia Mott, and Elizabeth Cady Stanton found inspiration; Stanton and Mott’s reading catalyzed the 1848 Seneca Falls Convention. George Eliot wrote a comparative essay on her and Margaret Fuller in 1855. The suffrage movement rehabilitated her: Millicent Garrett Fawcett claimed her as the "foremother" of the vote in the 1892 centenary edition. Virginia Woolf (1929) declared her "immortal"—"alive and active... we hear her voice." Second-wave feminism (1960s–70s) cemented her academic status. Modern figures from Ayaan Hirsi Ali to Amartya Sen cite her. A 2020 sculpture at Newington Green sparked debate over symbolic versus lifelike representation.

### Major Works: Themes and Arguments
**Educational Works** (*Thoughts on the Education of Daughters*, 1787; *Original Stories*, 1788): Advocate middle-class virtues (self-discipline, frugality) and reason, influenced by John Locke but distinct in valuing religious faith and "sensibility." Argue educated women become better wives, mothers, and citizens.
**Vindication of the Rights of Men** (1790): First salvo in the "Revolution Controversy." Attacks hereditary privilege, monarchy, and Burke’s gendered aesthetics; links aristocratic vice to the perpetuation of slavery via tradition.
**Vindication of the Rights of Woman** (1792): Foundational feminist philosophy. Demands education commensurate with women’s social role as educators of children and rational companions. Critiques "sensibility" as irrationality that destroys civilization. Proposes national co-educational system; suggests separating poor children after age nine unless "brilliant." Ambiguously asserts equality of virtue but grants male physical superiority.
**Novels** (*Mary: A Fiction*, 1788; *Maria: or, The Wrongs of Woman*, 1798): Critique patriarchal marriage as economic trap. Heroines find fulfillment in passionate friendships (same-sex and cross-class) outside marriage. *Maria*’s friendship with servant Jemima hints at cross-class feminist solidarity. Both novels use and subvert the "novel of sensibility" genre to expose its danger to women.
**Letters Written in Sweden, Norway, and Denmark** (1796): Personal travel narrative blending sociological observation, sublime nature writing, and philosophical reflection on identity and commerce. Influenced by Rousseau’s *Reveries* but celebrates domestic industry. Godwin called it the book that made him love her; it influenced Wordsworth and Coleridge.

## Terms
- ****Sensibility**** — An 18th-century moral philosophy and aesthetic valuing acute emotional responsiveness; Wollstonecraft critiqued its "false" form in women as irrationality that undermines reason and civilization.
- ****Vindication**** — A formal defense or justification; used in her two major political tracts to rebut Burke (*Rights of Men*) and patriarchal education (*Rights of Woman*).
- ****Girondins / Jacobins**** — Rival factions in the French Revolution. Girondins: moderate republicans (Wollstonecraft’s circle). Jacobins: radical faction that instituted the Reign of Terror (1793–94) and suppressed women’s political clubs.
- ****Reign of Terror**** — Period (1793–94) under the Committee of Public Safety marked by mass executions of "enemies of the republic"; Wollstonecraft lived in Paris through its peak.
- ****Childbed fever (puerperal fever)**** — Post-partum infection, often fatal in the 18th century; cause of Wollstonecraft’s death 11 days after delivering Mary Shelley.
- ****Blue Stockings Society**** — Informal women’s intellectual and literary circle in 1750s–90s England; Wollstonecraft connected with members in London.
- ****Revolution Controversy**** — British pamphlet war (1790s) sparked by Burke’s *Reflections*; included Wollstonecraft’s *Rights of Men*, Paine’s *Rights of Man*, and debates on monarchy vs. republicanism.
- ****Co-education**** — Educating boys and girls together; Wollstonecraft proposed this in *Rights of Woman* (Ch. 12) as essential for marriage as "cement of society."
- ****Memoirs (Godwin, 1798)**** — William Godwin’s biography revealing Wollstonecraft’s unconventional life; destroyed her reputation for ~100 years but preserved her voice.
- ****Sublime / Beautiful**** — Aesthetic categories from Burke’s 1756 treatise: Sublime = vast, terrifying, masculine/strong; Beautiful = small, smooth, feminine/weak. Wollstonecraft inverted them to attack Burke’s rhetoric.

## Debates and open questions
- **Equality vs. Difference**: *Rights of Woman* asserts women are equal to men "in the eyes of God" and in moral nature ("virtue has only one eternal standard"), yet grants men superior physical strength and "valour." Scholars debate whether this constitutes a coherent feminist equality or a "bourgeois" feminism accepting gender complementarity.
- **Class Limitations**: Her national education plan separates poor children after age nine. Critics argue this reinforces class hierarchy; defenders note she exempts "brilliant" poor children and targets the middle class as the "most natural state."
- **Sensibility and Reason**: She rejects "false sensibility" but insists reason and feeling "should inform each other." The precise boundary remains contested: is she an Enlightenment rationalist or a precursor to Romantic subjectivity?
- **Marriage and Autonomy**: She theorizes marriage as rational companionship (*Rights of Woman*) but depicts it as a prison in her novels. Her own life—unmarried with Imlay, married to Godwin for legitimacy while living apart—embodies this tension.
- **Race and Slavery**: She condemns Burke’s system for perpetuating slavery "because it had been an ancestral tradition," but *Rights of Woman* focuses on middle-class white women. The extent of her anti-racist commitment is debated.
- **Godwin’s Memoir as Betrayal or Preservation**: Did Godwin’s candor destroy her legacy or save her from sanitization? The *Memoir* provided the primary source for her inner life but fueled a century of misogynistic dismissal.
- **Historical Method in *French Revolution***: She was not a trained historian; she used journals and letters to capture "ordinary people’s" reactions. Scholars debate its reliability versus its value as a contemporary, partisan counter-narrative.
