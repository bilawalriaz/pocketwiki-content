# Anarchism

## Overview

Anarchism is a political philosophy and movement that seeks to abolish all institutions perpetuating authority, coercion, or hierarchy, primarily targeting the state, capitalism, and organized religion. It advocates replacing these with stateless societies and voluntary free associations, and is described as part of the libertarian wing of the socialist movement (libertarian socialism). Modern anarchism emerged from the Enlightenment, flourished globally from the late 19th century through the Spanish Civil War (the "classical era"), and has been resurgent since the late 20th century within anti-capitalist, anti-war, and anti-globalisation movements.

## Timeline

- **224–651 CE** — In the Sasanian Empire, Mazdak calls for an egalitarian society and abolition of monarchy; he is executed by Emperor Kavad I.
- **1642** — "Anarchisme" appears in English; early usages emphasise a sense of disorder.
- **1840** — Proudhon publishes *What Is Property?*, characterising his goal as a "third form of society"; he is the first to call himself an anarchist.
- **1864** — The International Workingmen's Association (First International) forms; Bakunin's collectivist faction later clashes with Marx's.
- **1871** — The Paris Commune occurs; its suppression and the execution/exile of Communards favour individualist political expression and acts.
- **1872** — Bakuninists are expelled from the First International at the Hague Congress; anarchists form the St. Imier International.
- **1890s–1900s** — Propaganda of the deed (assassinations, terrorism) peaks; the Immigration Act of 1903 (Anarchist Exclusion Act) targets anarchists in the US.
- **1917–23** — Revolutionary wave sees anarchist participation, including in the Russian Revolution and Makhnovshchina; Bolsheviks later crush anarchists.
- **1936–39** — Spanish Civil War: anarchists (CNT and FAI) play a pivotal role; communists defeat them after the May Days.
- **1999** — Seattle WTO protests mark a significant event of the anti-globalisation movement, with anarchist black blocs prominent.

## Body

### Etymology, terminology, and definition

The term derives from Ancient Greek *anarkhia* ("without a ruler"), from *an-* ("without") and *arkhos* ("leader"). The first political philosopher to call himself an anarchist and give the word a positive meaning was Pierre-Joseph Proudhon (1809–1865). Since the 1890s, "libertarianism" has often been a synonym for anarchism, though in the US it more often refers to individualistic free-market philosophy. Scholars generally highlight anarchism's socialist credentials and reject anarcho-capitalism as a misunderstanding of anarchist principles. Major definitional elements include the will for a non-coercive society, rejection of the state apparatus, belief that human nature allows such a society, and a suggestion on how to pursue it.

### Pre-modern and modern history

Notable precursors existed in ancient China (Taoist philosophers Zhuang Zhou and Laozi) and Greece (Aeschylus, Sophocles, Socrates, Cynics, Stoics). Enlightenment challenges to intellectual authority and the revolutions of the 1790s and 1848 spurred classical anarchism's development. The first anarchist currents developed through the 19th century: Godwin's philosophical anarchism in England, Stirner's individualism, and Proudhon's mutualism in France. Bakunin founded collectivist anarchism and joined the First International; after bitter disputes, the Bakuninists were expelled in 1872. Kropotkin expanded anarcho-communism, which drew inspiration from the 1871 Paris Commune. A minority adopted revolutionary political violence ("propaganda of the deed"). By the turn of the 20th century, anarchism had spread worldwide, with Argentina a stronghold for anarcho-syndicalism. Anarchists participated in the Russian Revolution but were harshly suppressed by the Bolsheviks, including at Kronstadt. In the Spanish Civil War, anarchists collectivised land and controlled Barcelona, but were defeated by communists after the May Days. Post-WWII, anarchism revived in the 1960s, became associated with punk subculture, and grew in influence within anti-globalisation movements around the turn of the 21st century, including at the 1999 Seattle WTO protests. Contemporary anarchism often builds alternative social organisation (e.g., Food Not Bombs) and has influenced the Zapatistas and Rojava.

### Schools of thought

Anarchist schools are generally grouped into social anarchism and individualist anarchism. The individualist current emphasises negative liberty; the social current emphasises positive liberty through equality and social ownership. Classical currents include mutualism (Proudhon), collectivist anarchism (Bakunin), anarcho-communism (Kropotkin), and anarcho-syndicalism. Individualist anarchism draws on Godwin, Stirner, and Thoreau. Post-classical currents include anarcha-feminism, green anarchism, and post-anarchism. "Anarchism without adjectives," a call for toleration first adopted by Fernando Tarrida del Mármol in 1889, responded to sectarianism. Philosophical anarchism holds that the state lacks moral legitimacy without necessarily accepting revolution to eliminate it.

### Tactics

Anarchist tactics serve two goals: opposing the Establishment and promoting anarchist ethics. A broad categorisation distinguishes revolutionary aims (destroying oppressive states) from evolutionary aims (gradual, often nonviolent change). Classical-era anarchists engaged in strikes, militancy, and some terrorism. Contemporary anarchists are much less violent, engaging in direct action, horizontalism (equal say in decisions), prefigurative politics, squatting, and creating Temporary Autonomous Zones. They form small affinity groups (5–20 individuals) that interconnect into larger networks, and support wildcat strikes. Anarchists have also developed free software and online portals to spread their message.

### Key issues

Objection to the state is central to anarchism; many consider it illegitimate regardless of its political tendencies. Critics argue anarchism defies biological inclination to authority, is naive or unrealistic, and that humans cannot self-govern. Anarchists reject these, arguing the negatives of state organisation outweigh benefits. On gender and sexuality, classical anarchists rarely discussed sexuality, but a free love current flourished 1890–1920, opposing marriage as male authority. Anarcha-feminism returned with vigour during second-wave feminism. In education, anarchists consider proper education an act of mutual aid. Francisco Ferrer established the Escuela Moderna in Barcelona in 1901, a secular school fostering class consciousness; Leo Tolstoy and A. S. Neill founded similar free schools. Contemporary anarchist writers (Paul Goodman, Herbert Read, Colin Ward) critique state education as perpetuating socioeconomic inequality. In the arts, anarchism connected with futurists, surrealists, punk, and neo-impressionism; art served as critique, prefigurative tool, and direct action.

## Terms

- **Mutualism**: Proudhon's economic theory; aims at abolishing the state, reciprocity, free association, voluntary contract, and monetary reform.
- **Collectivist anarchism**: Bakunin's revolutionary socialism; advocates collective ownership of the means of production, achieved through violent revolution, with workers paid according to time worked.
- **Anarcho-communism**: Advocates common ownership of the means of production via a federal network of voluntary associations, distributing goods "according to one's needs."
- **Anarcho-syndicalism**: Views labour syndicates as a force for revolutionary change, replacing capitalism and the state with worker self-management.
- **Propaganda of the deed**: Revolutionary political violence, including assassinations, used by a minority of anarchists in the late 19th century.
- **Platformism**: A post-Russian-Revolution current seeking a coherent group to push for revolution; contrasted with the anarchist synthesis, which opposed anything resembling a political party.
- **Black bloc**: Ad hoc, leaderless, anonymous cadres engaged in rioting and property destruction during protests.
- **Horizontalism**: Anti-authoritarian decision-making where everyone has equal say in each decision.
- **Prefigurative politics**: Embodying the principles and organisation of the desired future society in present actions.
- **Temporary Autonomous Zones (TAZ)**: Squatted or reclaimed spaces during protests where art and poetry display the anarchist ideal.

## Debates and open questions

Scholars debate the definition of anarchism itself, as various currents perceive it differently. The compatibility of capitalism, nationalism, and religion with anarchism is widely disputed. Whether anarchism can succeed is questioned by Marxist critics, who argue it is utopian, cannot win, and lacks the ability to implement its ideas. Critics also argue anarchism defies biological inclination to authority and that humans cannot self-govern without a state. Anarchists counter that authority can be challenged without losing its advantages, and that state negatives outweigh benefits. Within anarchism, there is disagreement over violence versus nonviolence, and over whether contemporary individualism in sexual-identity critique drops the cause of social liberation.
