# Texas

## Overview
Texas is the second-largest U.S. state by area and population, known for diverse geography, a robust economy, and a distinct cultural identity. Its history as an independent republic and role in major U.S. developments make it pivotal in the American South and Southwest.

## Timeline
- **1519** — Spanish explorer Alonso Álvarez de Pineda maps the Texas Gulf Coast.
- **1685** — René-Robert Cavelier de La Salle accidentally establishes Fort Saint Louis in Texas.
- **1821** — Texas becomes part of Mexico after Mexican independence from Spain.
- **1836** — The Texas Revolution ends with the creation of the independent Republic of Texas.
- **1845** — Texas is annexed by the United States as the 28th state.
- **1846** — The Mexican–American War begins, partly due to the disputed Texas border.
- **1861** — Texas secedes from the Union and joins the Confederate States of America.
- **1901** — The Spindletop oil discovery near Beaumont initiates the Texas oil boom.
- **1945** — Texas's economy begins transforming from rural/agricultural to urban/industrial.
- **2021** — Winter Storm Uri causes a statewide power crisis, leaving millions without electricity.

## Body

### Etymology and Early History
The name "Texas" derives from the Caddo word *táy:shaʼ* ("friend"). Before European contact, the region hosted diverse Indigenous cultures, including the agricultural Caddo and various nomadic groups. Spanish colonization began in the 18th century, with missions like San Antonio (1718) established to counter French influence.

### Revolution, Republic, and Statehood
Tensions between Anglo-American settlers and the Mexican government led to the Texas Revolution (1835–1836), culminating in the Battle of the Alamo and the Republic of Texas. The Republic existed for nearly a decade before U.S. annexation in 1845, which triggered the Mexican–American War. The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848) established the Rio Grande as the border.

### Civil War, Reconstruction, and Economic Shifts
Texas seceded in 1861 to join the Confederacy. Post-Civil War Reconstruction brought economic stagnation. The late 19th-century economy relied on cattle, cotton, and lumber. The 1901 Spindletop oil discovery transformed Texas, making energy a dominant economic force.

### Modern Texas
From the mid-20th century, Texas urbanized and diversified into technology, aerospace, and commerce. Significant population growth occurred via domestic migration and immigration. Politically, the state shifted from Democratic to Republican dominance by the late 20th century. Texas ranks high for business growth and has the second-highest gross state product in the U.S.

### Geography and Climate
Texas is the second-largest U.S. state, with geography ranging from Gulf Coastal Plains and piney woods in the east to the Great Plains and desert mountains in the west. Its climate is highly variable, with 10 distinct regions. The state is prone to hurricanes and tornadoes and is the top U.S. greenhouse gas emitter.

### Demographics and Culture
Texas is a majority-minority state; the Hispanic/Latino population surpassed the non-Hispanic White population as the largest group in 2022. The state is predominantly Christian, with a significant Catholic population. Culture blends Southern, Western, and Southwestern influences, with strong cowboy and country music associations. Major urban centers like Houston, Dallas, and San Antonio are diverse and economically powerful.

### Economy and Infrastructure
Texas has a diversified economy led by energy, agriculture, technology, and commerce. It is a top producer of cattle, cotton, and wind power. The state has no income tax, relying on property and sales taxes. Its transportation network includes major ports, airports (like DFW), and highways. The Texas power grid operates independently from the rest of the U.S., a factor in crises like the 2021 winter storm.

### Government and Politics
Texas has a plural executive branch and a biennial bicameral legislature. The state is politically conservative, with Republican dominance since the 1990s. It is known for capital punishment and strict criminal laws. Political battles often involve redistricting, immigration, and social policy.

## Terms
- **Empresario**: A person granted rights to settle land in exchange for recruiting settlers.
- **Republic of Texas**: The independent nation (1836–1845) after winning independence from Mexico.
- **Spindletop**: Site of the first major Texas oil well (1901), sparking the oil boom.
- **Gross State Product (GSP)**: Total value of goods/services produced within a state; Texas's is second-highest in the U.S.
- **Plural Executive**: A system dividing executive power among independently elected officials, limiting the governor's authority.
- **Permanent University Fund**: A public endowment funding the University of Texas and Texas A&M systems, supported by oil revenues.
- **Texas Interconnection**: The electric grid serving most of Texas, operating independently from U.S. interconnections.
- **Colonias**: Unincorporated rural settlements along the Texas–Mexico border, often lacking basic infrastructure.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Political Realignment**: Scholars debate causes of Texas's shift from Democratic to Republican stronghold, citing national party liberalism, suburban growth, and strategic campaigns.
- **Economic Model**: The low-tax, low-regulation model is praised for growth but criticized for high poverty and inadequate public services like healthcare and education.
- **Energy and Environment**: Texas's role as a leading energy producer creates tension between economic interests and environmental concerns, especially regarding emissions and grid reliability.
- **Demographic Change**: The rapid growth of the Hispanic population and its potential political and cultural impact is subject to ongoing analysis.
