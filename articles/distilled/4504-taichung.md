# Taichung

## Overview
Taichung is a special municipality in central Taiwan and the island's second-largest city, with over 2.86 million residents. It serves as the economic and cultural core of Central Taiwan. The city's history spans from indigenous settlements through Qing dynasty development, Japanese colonial modernization, and post-war growth, culminating in its current status as a major metropolitan area.

## Timeline
- **1705** — Settlement named Toatun established
- **1721** — Qing garrison established near present-day Taichung Park
- **1887** — Taiwan Province declared; Toatun designated as capital (later moved to Taipei)
- **1895** — Taiwan ceded to Japan; city renamed Taichū
- **1920** — Taichū City officially declared by Japanese authorities
- **1945** — Handover to Republic of China; Taichung County established
- **2010** — Taichung City and Taichung County merged into special municipality
- **2017** — Taichung surpassed Kaohsiung as Taiwan's second-largest city
- **2021** — MRT Green Line opened
- **2024** — MRT Blue Line approved

## Body

### Early History and Qing Dynasty
The Taichung Basin was originally populated by Atayal aborigines and Taiwanese Plains Aboriginal tribes including the Taokas, Papora, Pazeh, Hoanya, and Babuzas. In the 17th century, some tribes established the Kingdom of Middag in western Taichung. The Qing dynasty established Zhuluo County in 1684, and the settlement Toatun was founded in 1705. A garrison was built in 1721, and several rebellions occurred, including an aboriginal revolt in 1731-1732 and the Lin Shuangwen rebellion in 1786. When Taiwan became an independent province in 1887, Toatun was designated as the capital, but the provincial capital was ultimately moved to Taipei.

### Japanese Colonial Period (1895-1945)
After the Treaty of Shimonoseki, Taiwan was ceded to Japan. The Japanese renamed the city Taichū and invested heavily in modern infrastructure, including roads, dams, railways, and public facilities. Taichū Park was completed in 1903, the railway station in 1917, and the city hall in 1924. The city became a center of Taiwanese cultural nationalism, particularly through institutions like Taichū Middle School (1915) and the Taiwanese Cultural Association (moved to Taichū in 1927).

### Republic of China Era (1945-Present)
After Japan's surrender, Taichung County was established. The city served as a temporary capital for the exiled Jiangxi provincial government in 1949. On December 25, 2010, the original provincial city merged with Taichung County to form a special municipality. The city has grown rapidly, becoming Taiwan's second-largest in 2017 due to natural growth and migration.

### Geography and Climate
Taichung sits in the Taichung Basin along Taiwan's western coastal plain, bordered by the Central Mountain Range to the east. It has a warm humid subtropical climate with average annual temperature of 23.3°C and rainfall of about 1,700 mm. The mountain protection makes it rarely affected by severe typhoons.

### Economy
Taichung is Central Taiwan's economic center with main industries in machinery, retail, and manufacturing. Key sectors include tool machines, mechanical components, photoelectric panels, bicycles, woodworking machinery, and aerospace. The Central Taiwan Science Park hosts semiconductor factories including TSMC. The city is famous for bicycle manufacturing (Giant Bicycles, SRAM) and invented bubble tea at Chun Shui Tang teahouse.

### Transportation
The city has two parallel railways, a high-speed rail station in Wuri District, and Taichung Port as Taiwan's second-largest cargo facility. Road networks include radial layouts from the city center and multiple freeways. Public transportation includes 18 bus companies with 275 routes, the MRT Green Line (opened 2021), and Taichung International Airport.

### Culture and Religion
Taichung hosts major cultural institutions including the National Museum of Natural Science and National Taiwan Museum of Fine Arts. The city has 1,012 registered temples, predominantly Taoist and Buddhist. The Jenn Lann Temple is the starting point of the annual Dajia Mazu Pilgrimage, one of Taiwan's largest religious processions. Night markets like Fengjia and Yizhong Street are popular cultural attractions.

## Terms
- **Special municipality**: A top-level administrative division in Taiwan, equivalent to a province
- **Plains Aboriginal tribes**: Indigenous peoples who inhabited Taiwan's western coastal plains
- **Kingdom of Middag**: A 17th-century indigenous polity in western Taichung
- **Hanyu Pinyin**: The official romanization system for Mandarin Chinese
- **Mazu**: A Chinese sea goddess widely worshipped in coastal regions
- **Pan-Blue/Pan-Green**: Taiwan's two major political camps, with Pan-Blue favoring closer ties with China and Pan-Green favoring Taiwanese independence
- **THSR**: Taiwan High Speed Rail, connecting major cities along the west coast
- **MRT**: Mass Rapid Transit, Taiwan's urban rail systems

## Debates and Open Questions
The article notes ongoing debates about romanization systems on road signs, with different systems (Hanyu Pinyin, Wade-Giles, Tongyong Pinyin) used inconsistently. Political balance between Pan-Blue and Pan-Green camps remains dynamic, with both major parties winning recent mayoral elections. The city's rapid growth raises questions about sustainable development and infrastructure capacity.
