# Atropatene

## Overview
Atropatene was an ancient Iranian kingdom established c. 323 BC in northwestern Iran, centered in the region now known as Azerbaijan. It was founded by the Persian satrap Atropates after the death of Alexander the Great and was ruled by his descendants until the early 1st century AD. The kingdom is historically significant as the only Iranian region to maintain continuous Zoroastrian authority from the Achaemenid period until the Arab conquest, serving as a stronghold of Iranian culture and religious tradition.

## Timeline
- **c. 323 BC** — Atropatene is established as an independent kingdom by the Persian satrap Atropates following the Partition of Babylon.
- **223 BCE** — Seleucid King Antiochus III attacks and defeats Atropatene, making it a dependent state.
- **190 BC** — The Roman Empire defeats the Seleucids at the Battle of Magnesia, prompting Atropatene and Parthia to ally against Rome.
- **36 BC** — Roman general Mark Antony attacks the city of Fraaspa in Atropatene but is forced to retreat after a long blockade.
- **20 BC** — King Ariobarzan II of Atropatene moves to Rome, signaling a shift toward closer ties with the Roman Empire.
- **134-135 AD** — Phrasmanes II of Iberia devastates Atropatene, weakening Parthian control over the region.
- **216-224 AD** — The people of Atropatene ally with the Sasanian prince Ardashir I against the Parthian king Artabanus IV.
- **226 AD** — Atropatene submits to Ardashir I after his victory at the Battle of Hormozdgan, becoming a Sasanian province.

## Body
### Foundation and Early Independence
Atropatene was created from the northern part of the former Achaemenid satrapy of Media. After Alexander the Great's death in 323 BC, the region was assigned to Atropates, a former Achaemenid governor who had become Alexander's father-in-law. He soon refused allegiance to the Seleucid Empire, establishing Atropatene as an independent kingdom. The kingdom's name derives directly from Atropates.

### Relations with Hellenistic and Roman Powers
Atropatene navigated complex geopolitics between the Seleucid Empire, the rising Parthian Empire, and Rome. After a Seleucid victory in 223 BCE, the kingdom accepted Seleucid suzerainty while preserving internal independence. Following Rome's defeat of the Seleucids in 190 BC, Atropatene allied with Parthia to resist Roman expansion. A major Roman attack in 36 BC failed, but later, Atropatene began drawing closer to Rome to counter Parthian annexation attempts.

### Sasanian Conquest and Zoroastrian Legacy
During the late Parthian era, Atropatene's nobility and peasantry allied with the Sasanian prince Ardashir I against the weakening Parthian monarchy. After Ardashir I defeated the last Parthian king in 226 AD, Atropatene submitted with little resistance. The region's elite likely supported the Sasanians for stability, and the Zoroastrian priesthood favored the new dynasty's religious associations. Atropatene was the only Iranian region to remain under continuous Zoroastrian authority from the Achaemenids to the Arab conquest, making it a key center for Zoroastrianism and Iranian culture.

## Terms
- **Satrap**: A provincial governor in the ancient Persian empires.
- **Diadochi**: The successors of Alexander the Great who fought for control of his empire after his death.
- **Zoroastrianism**: An ancient Iranian religion based on the teachings of the prophet Zoroaster, emphasizing the struggle between good and evil.
- **Marzban**: A military commander or governor of a frontier province in the Sasanian Empire.
- **Arsacid dynasty**: The ruling dynasty of the Parthian Empire.
- **Sasanian Empire**: The last Persian empire before the Muslim conquest, ruling from 224 to 651 AD.
- **Achaemenid Empire**: The first Persian Empire, founded by Cyrus the Great.
- **Ganzak**: The presumed capital of Atropatene, located near Lake Urmia.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact birthplace of the prophet Zoroaster is uncertain; while traditional accounts place it in Atropatene, scholarly consensus suggests he originated in the east.
- The extent of Iranianization among the general population of Atropatene by the 3rd century BC is unclear, though the capital area likely hosted a large Iranian population.
