# Pope Alexander VI

## Overview
Pope Alexander VI (born Rodrigo Borgia, c. 1431–1503) was a Spanish-born pontiff whose reign (1492–1503) epitomized the political ambition, nepotism, and scandal of the Renaissance papacy. His papacy was pivotal in shaping European colonial policy through the Treaty of Tordesillas and in consolidating papal power in Italy through complex alliances and family aggrandizement. He remains one of history's most controversial popes, known for openly fathering children and for the enduring notoriety of the Borgia name.

## Timeline
- **c. 1431** — Born Roderic de Borja in Xàtiva, Kingdom of Valencia.
- **1456** — Ordained deacon and made cardinal by his uncle, Pope Callixtus III.
- **1457** — Appointed vice-chancellor of the Holy Roman Church, a post he held for 35 years.
- **1492** — Elected Pope, taking the name Alexander VI.
- **1493** — Issued papal bulls (e.g., *Inter caetera*) dividing newly discovered lands between Spain and Portugal.
- **1494** — Charles VIII of France invades Italy; Alexander initially opposes, then allies with him.
- **1495** — Forms the Holy League to expel the French from Italy.
- **1498** — Annuls his daughter Lucrezia's marriage; allies with France to support his son Cesare's campaigns.
- **1500** — Institutes the tradition of opening a "holy door" for the Jubilee.
- **1502** — Cesare Borgia crushes a conspiracy, consolidating Borgia power in the Papal States.
- **1503** — Dies after a sudden fever; his rival Giuliano della Rovere succeeds him as Pope Julius II.

## Body

### Early Life and Ecclesiastical Career
Rodrigo Borgia was born into the prominent Borja family in Valencia. His career in the Church began in 1445 with a position secured by his uncle, who later became Pope Callixtus III. This pattern of nepotism defined his rise: he was made cardinal in 1456 and vice-chancellor in 1457. He served under four popes, accumulating wealth and influence while strategically positioning himself as a kingmaker in papal elections, ensuring his retention of the lucrative vice-chancellorship.

### Election and Papal Policy
Elected pope in 1492 at age 61, Alexander VI initially governed with strict justice but soon focused on advancing his family. His 1493 papal bulls confirmed Spanish rights in the New World, establishing the basis for the Treaty of Tordesillas. His foreign policy was fluid and opportunistic, shifting alliances between France, Spain, and Naples to secure advantages for his children, particularly Cesare and Lucrezia. This involved complex diplomacy during the Italian Wars, where he alternately opposed and allied with French invaders.

### Nepotism and the Borgia Family
Alexander's pontificate was dominated by nepotism. He openly acknowledged and legitimized his children, appointing them to powerful positions. His son Cesare was made a cardinal at 18 and later a military commander, while his daughter Lucrezia was married into influential families to forge political alliances. This aggressive family aggrandizement brought the Borgias into conflict with powerful Roman families like the Orsini and Colonna, whom Alexander eventually subjugated, strengthening direct papal control.

### Legacy and Controversy
Alexander's reign was marked by scandal, particularly due to his acknowledged illegitimate children, which made "Borgia" synonymous with corruption. His death in 1503, possibly from malaria, was followed by rapid decomposition, fueling rumors of poisoning. His successor, Julius II, condemned his legacy. However, Alexander was also a patron of the arts, commissioning works from Raphael and Michelangelo, and he founded King's College, Aberdeen. His treatment of Jews expelled from Spain was relatively tolerant, granting them refuge in the Papal States.

## Terms
- **Nepotism:** The practice among popes of appointing relatives to powerful Church positions, a common but controversial practice in the Renaissance.
- **Vice-Chancellor:** A powerful and lucrative administrative position in the Roman Curia, which Borgia held for 35 years before becoming pope.
- **Papal Bull:** An official public decree or charter issued by a pope, such as the *Inter caetera* bulls that divided the New World.
- **Condottiero:** A professional military commander for hire; Cesare Borgia served in this role for the French king.
- **College of Cardinals:** The body of senior cardinals who advise the pope and elect a new pope upon a vacancy.
- **Holy Door:** A special door in major basilicas, opened only during Jubilee years, a tradition formalized by Alexander VI in 1500.
- **Simony:** The buying or selling of ecclesiastical privileges, a charge often leveled against Renaissance popes.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **The Nature of His Children:** While Alexander openly acknowledged his children, some historians, like G. J. Meyer, argue that the birth dates and his whereabouts preclude him from being the biological father of all of them, suggesting his "acknowledgment" was political.
- **Responsibility for Slavery:** Scholars debate whether his 1493 bulls implicitly authorized the enslavement of indigenous peoples in the New World. Some historians assert they did, while Vatican scholars and others strongly disagree.
- **Cause of Death:** His death is attributed by contemporaries and historians to malaria or other pestilence common in Rome, though the dramatic decomposition of his body fueled contemporary rumors of poisoning.
- **Historical Reputation:** Some Catholic apologists argue his criticized behaviors were typical for the era, while rivals like Julius II and later historians have condemned him as uniquely corrupt. The extent to which his reputation was exaggerated by Italian rivals remains a point of discussion.
