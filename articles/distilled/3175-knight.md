# Knight

## Overview
A knight is a person granted an honorary title by a head of state for service to the monarch, church, or country, especially in a military capacity. The concept evolved from mounted warriors in the Early Middle Ages into a class of petty nobility associated with the chivalric code, a set of ideals for the perfect Christian warrior. While the military role of knights became obsolete with the rise of standing armies and gunpowder, the titles and orders persist today as prestigious honors for civic service and achievement.

## Timeline
- **c. 732** — Frankish forces, largely infantry with mounted elites, defeat the Umayyad invasion at the Battle of Tours.
- **8th century** — The first knights appear during the reign of Charlemagne.
- **864** — Charles the Bald issues the Edict of Pîtres, calling for mounted service to repel Viking attacks, marking a key step in the development of knighthood.
- **1099** — The First Crusade leads to the founding of the first military orders, the Knights of the Holy Sepulchre and the Knights Hospitaller.
- **12th century** — Knighthood becomes a distinct social rank; the Crusades influence the chivalric ideal.
- **1348** — Edward III of England founds the Order of the Garter.
- **1430** — Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, founds the Order of the Golden Fleece.
- **1477** — Swiss infantry defeat Charles the Bold's cavalry at the Battle of Nancy, demonstrating the vulnerability of knights to pike formations.
- **15th-16th centuries** — Plate armour reaches its peak, but new firearms and tactics begin to render knights obsolete.
- **1611** — The British Crown begins awarding hereditary baronetcies.
- **1901** — Queens consort are made Ladies of the Order of the Garter, reviving the practice of appointing women.

## Body

### Origins and Evolution
The concept of knighthood may have been inspired by ancient Greek *hippeis* and Roman *equites*. In the Early Middle Ages, any well-equipped horseman could be called a knight. The first true knights emerged under Charlemagne in the 8th century, with the stirrup enabling fighting from horseback. They were rewarded with land grants called benefices. By the 10th century, the institution was well-established, with knights serving as vassals who held land in exchange for military service, typically 40 days a year.

### The Chivalric Ideal and Military Orders
During the High Middle Ages, knighthood became a social rank. The Crusades were pivotal, leading to the creation of military orders like the Knights Templar and Teutonic Knights, who combined monastic discipline with martial prowess. These orders, along with literary works like the *Matter of Britain* (King Arthur legends), helped define chivalry—a code emphasizing bravery, loyalty, courtesy, and protection of the weak. Tournaments, especially the joust, became key venues for knights to display their skills and heraldry.

### Training and Equipment
A knight's training began at age seven as a page, learning hunting, academics, and basic combat. At fourteen, he became a squire, mastering advanced combat skills and serving a lord. The accolade, or knighting ceremony, involved a ritual bath, oath, and the dubbing with a sword. Knights used swords, maces, and lances. Their armour evolved from mail to full plate by the 15th century, and they used shields like the kite and heater shield. Horses were also armoured with caparisons and chanfrons.

### Decline and Modern Legacy
By the mid-16th century, standing armies equipped with firearms made knights obsolete. The feudal system that supported them also ended. However, the titles and orders survived. Today, orders like the Order of the Garter, the Order of the Thistle, and the Sovereign Military Order of Malta continue to exist. Modern knighthoods are honorific awards granted for distinguished service to society, not military prowess. The female equivalent is a dame.

## Terms
- **Knight**: A person granted an honorary title for service, especially military, to a monarch or state.
- **Chivalry**: A code of conduct for the ideal Christian warrior, emphasizing bravery, courtesy, and honor.
- **Vassal**: A person who holds land (a fief) from a lord in exchange for loyalty and military service.
- **Accolade**: The ceremony of conferring knighthood, involving an oath and the dubbing with a sword.
- **Heraldry**: The system of coats of arms and other symbols used to identify knights in battle and tournaments.
- **Man-at-arms**: A fully armoured soldier, a role any knight would fill in war, though not all men-at-arms were knights.
- **Fief**: An estate of land granted by a lord to a vassal in return for service.
- **Squire**: A young noble acting as an attendant to a knight while training for knighthood.
- **Tournament**: A medieval martial contest, often featuring jousts and melees, for knights to display skill.
- **Military Order**: A religious order whose members combined monastic life with military service, like the Knights Templar.

## Debates and Open Questions
The exact origins of knighthood are debated, with possible inspiration from ancient Greek and Roman mounted classes. The extent to which the Crusades, rather than internal European social and military developments, shaped the chivalric code is also a subject of scholarly discussion. The precise moment when the knight's military role became obsolete is not fixed, as iconic battles of military orders like the Knights Hospitaller occurred after the rule of Holy Roman Emperor Maximilian I (1459–1519), often called the "last knight."
