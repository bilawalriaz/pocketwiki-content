# History of Canada

## Overview
The history of Canada spans from the arrival of Paleo-Indians thousands of years ago to the present day. It is the story of diverse Indigenous societies, followed by centuries of European exploration and colonial rivalry between France and Britain, culminating in a self-governing federation that gradually achieved full sovereignty. This evolution has been shaped by immigration, conflicts with Indigenous peoples, and the ongoing negotiation of a multicultural, bilingual national identity.

## Timeline
- **c. 16,000 years ago** — People move south and east from Beringia into Canada as glacial ice recedes.
- **c. 1000 CE** — Norse build a settlement at L'Anse aux Meadows, Newfoundland.
- **1534** — Jacques Cartier claims the Gaspé Peninsula for France.
- **1608** — Samuel de Champlain founds Quebec City, the first permanent French settlement.
- **1763** — France cedes nearly all its North American possessions to Britain in the Treaty of Paris.
- **1867** — The British North America Act creates the Dominion of Canada through Confederation.
- **1931** — The Statute of Westminster recognizes Canada as co-equal with the United Kingdom.
- **1949** — Newfoundland and Labrador becomes Canada's tenth province.
- **1982** — The Constitution is patriated, ending legal dependence on the British Parliament.
- **2005** — The Civil Marriage Act legalizes same-sex marriage nationwide.

## Body

### Indigenous Peoples and Early Contact
Archaeological evidence indicates humans migrated to the Americas via the Bering land bridge during the last Ice Age. By 8000 BCE, distinct regional cultures like the Archaic and Woodland traditions had emerged. Complex societies developed, including the Algonquian and Iroquoian peoples of the east, the bison-hunting Cree of the Plains, and the cedar-dependent cultures of the Pacific Coast. The Norse arrived around 1000 CE, but sustained European contact began with John Cabot's 1497 voyage for England. This led to competing claims by England, France, Portugal, and Spain.

### French and British Colonial Rivalry
France established its colony of New France, with permanent settlements starting in 1608. The fur trade was its economic engine. Britain also established colonies and trading posts, leading to a series of colonial wars. The decisive conflict was the Seven Years' War (1756–1763), which ended with the Treaty of Paris (1763). France ceded its mainland North American territories to Britain. The British Royal Proclamation of 1763 organized the new empire and recognized Aboriginal title, while the Quebec Act of 1774 guaranteed French civil law and Catholic rights.

### The Path to Confederation and Expansion
The American Revolution (1775–1783) brought Loyalist refugees north, leading to the creation of New Brunswick (1784) and the division of Quebec into Upper (English) and Lower (French) Canada in 1791. Rebellions in 1837 prompted the Durham Report, which recommended uniting the Canadas and granting responsible government. The Province of Canada was formed in 1841, and responsible government was achieved in 1848. In 1867, the Province of Canada united with Nova Scotia and New Brunswick to form the Dominion of Canada. Over the next 82 years, Canada expanded westward and eastward, incorporating other British colonies, including British Columbia (1871) and finally Newfoundland (1949).

### World Wars and Sovereignty
Canada's contributions in World War I, including key battles like Vimy Ridge, fostered a distinct national identity and led to greater autonomy in foreign affairs. The Statute of Westminster (1931) formalized legislative independence from Britain. In World War II, Canada declared war independently and played major roles in the Battle of the Atlantic and the liberation of Europe. The post-war era saw economic growth, the development of social programs, and a foreign policy often aligned with the United States through NATO and NORAD. The Constitution was patriated in 1982, with the Charter of Rights and Freedoms, marking the final step to full sovereignty.

### Modern Canada
Recent decades have been defined by debates over national unity, particularly Quebec sovereignty, which was rejected in referendums in 1980 and 1995. Multiculturalism became official policy in the 1970s, and immigration patterns shifted from Europe to Asia and other regions. Canada has grappled with its colonial legacy, including a formal apology for the residential school system and ongoing reconciliation efforts with Indigenous peoples. The country has also navigated economic integration with the U.S. through free trade agreements and addressed social issues like the legalization of same-sex marriage and cannabis.

## Terms
*   **Confederation:** The 1867 process of uniting British North American colonies into the self-governing Dominion of Canada.
*   **Dominion:** The title given to Canada at its creation in 1867, indicating a self-governing polity within the British Empire.
*   **Patriation:** The 1982 process of bringing Canada's constitution fully under Canadian control, ending the British Parliament's authority to amend it.
*   **Responsible Government:** A system where the executive branch (the Prime Minister and Cabinet) is responsible to the elected legislature, not the monarch's representative. Achieved in Canada in 1848.
*   **Statute of Westminster (1931):** British law that recognized the Dominions, including Canada, as co-equal with the United Kingdom and granted them legislative autonomy.
*   **Treaty of Paris (1763):** The agreement that ended the Seven Years' War, resulting in France ceding nearly all its North American territories to Britain.
*   **Indian Act:** A Canadian federal law, first enacted in 1876, that governs matters relating to First Nations peoples, including status, land, and reserves.
*   **Quiet Revolution:** A period of intense socio-political and socio-cultural modernization in Quebec during the 1960s.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **The Conquest (1763):** Historians debate whether it was a disaster that destroyed French-Canadian society or a providential event that allowed for the preservation of its language and religion under British rule.
*   **The American Revolution's Impact:** Some historians argue it created a counter-revolutionary, loyalist English Canada, while others see it as setting Canada on a different path toward "modern liberty" focused on protecting rights from state infringement.
*   **National Unity:** The failure of constitutional accords like Meech Lake (1990) and Charlottetown (1992) to gain Quebec's acceptance of the 1982 Constitution remains a central, unresolved issue in Canadian federalism.
