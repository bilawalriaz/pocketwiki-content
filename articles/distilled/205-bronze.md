# Bronze

## Overview
Bronze is a copper-based alloy, historically defined by the addition of tin (typically 12–12.5%) but broadly encompassing alloys with aluminium, manganese, nickel, zinc, arsenic, or silicon. Its discovery initiated the Bronze Age (mid-4th millennium BCE in western Eurasia), the first period where metal tools and weapons surpassed stone and copper in hardness and durability. The transition to the Iron Age (c. 1300 BCE) was driven not by iron’s superiority—bronze is harder than wrought iron—but by a collapse in the tin trade that made bronze scarce and expensive. Bronze remains vital today for its corrosion resistance, low friction, acoustic properties, and non-sparking nature, serving in marine hardware, bearings, musical instruments, sculpture, and safety tools.

## Timeline
- **c. 4650 BCE** — Earliest known tin-copper artifact (Vinča culture, Pločnik, Serbia), likely smelted from natural stannite ore.
- **c. 3800–3400 BCE** — Earliest arsenic-copper artifacts (Yahya Culture, Tal-i-Iblis, Iranian plateau).
- **Late 3rd millennium BCE** — Tin becomes the primary alloying element, replacing arsenic due to better process control, strength, and non-toxicity.
- **Mid-4th millennium BCE** — Conventional start of Bronze Age in western Eurasia.
- **Early 2nd millennium BCE** — Bronze Age begins in China.
- **c. 1200–1100 BCE** — Population migrations disrupt Mediterranean and British tin trade, triggering transition to Iron Age.
- **c. 1300 BCE** — Iron Age begins; reaches most of Eurasia by 500 BCE.
- **704–681 BCE** — Assyrian king Sennacherib claims first monumental bronze statues cast via two-part moulds.
- **1904** — Bronze medals adopted for third place at Summer Olympics.
- **1952** — Largest ornamental bronze fountain cast (Andrew W. Mellon Memorial Fountain, Washington, DC).
- **1958** — Seagram Building completed, first fully bronze-sheathed skyscraper (3,200,000 lbs of bronze).

## Body

### Etymology
The word "bronze" entered English c. 1730–1740 from Middle French *bronze* (1511), from Italian *bronzo* (13th century, Medieval Latin *bronzium*). Two origins are proposed: a back-formation from Byzantine Greek *brontēsíon*, possibly linked to Brindisi (Brentḗsion), famed for its bronze; or from Old Persian *birinj* ('brass') and *piring* ('copper'), cognate with Georgian *brinǯi*, Turkish *pirinç*, and Armenian *brinj*. Early dictionaries (1746–1872) defined it variably as a copper-tin alloy, a copper-brass mix, or simply an alloy of copper and tin of a brown color.

### Early Metallurgy and the Bronze Age
The earliest tin-bronze artifact (c. 4650 BCE) from Pločnik was likely smelted from stannite, a natural tin-copper ore. Arsenical copper—copper-arsenic alloys from mixed ores—appeared earlier (c. 3800–3400 BCE) at Tal-i-Iblis, offering a silver hue, lower melting point, and easier workability than pure copper. By the late 3rd millennium BCE, tin bronze superseded arsenical copper because alloying was more controllable, the alloy was stronger and easier to cast, and tin lacked arsenic’s toxicity. Since copper and tin ores rarely co-occur (exceptions: Cornwall, one site in Thailand, one in Iran), bronze production necessitated long-distance trade. Cornish tin reached Phoenicia in the eastern Mediterranean. Large hoards of unworn bronze tools (e.g., socketed axes in Europe) and Chinese ritual vessels indicate bronze functioned as a store of value and status marker. In the Indus Valley Civilisation (2600–1900 BCE), 20% of 324 metal finds contained tin; 8% exceeded 10% tin, suitable for casting, with tin possibly sourced from Bukhara or Samarkand.

### Transition to Iron
Despite bronze’s Vickers hardness (60–258) exceeding wrought iron (30–80), the Bronze Age ended c. 1200–1100 BCE when population migrations disrupted tin shipments from the Mediterranean and Britain, spiking prices. Ironworking improved concurrently; iron became cheaper and, with the development of machine-forging (water-powered trip hammers) and steelmaking, ultimately surpassed bronze in strength and edge retention. Bronze persisted through the Iron Age and remains in use today.

### Composition and Alloys
Modern standard bronze is ~88% copper, 12% tin. **Alpha bronze** (4–5% tin, alpha solid solution of tin in copper) is used for coins, springs, turbines, and blades. Historical compositions vary wildly; the 12th-century Gloucester Candlestick contains copper, zinc, tin, lead, nickel, iron, antimony, arsenic, and 5.76–22.5% silver, suggesting it was made from melted coins. The "Benin Bronzes" (13th century) are actually brass (copper-zinc), as are modern "commercial bronze" (90% Cu, 10% Zn) and "architectural bronze" (57% Cu, 3% Pb, 40% Zn). During the Bronze Age, "classic bronze" (~10% tin) was cast for blades, while "mild bronze" (~6% tin) was hammered into sheets for armor. **Plastic bronze** (high lead) improves plasticity; **Silicon bronze** (2.8–3.8% Si, 0.5–1.3% Mn, balance Cu) is the primary contemporary statuary alloy. Other alloys: aluminium bronze, phosphor bronze, manganese bronze, bell metal, arsenical bronze, speculum metal, bismuth bronze, cymbal alloys.

### Properties
Bronze melts at ~950 °C (1,742 °F), lower than steel/iron, and is ~10% denser than steel (aluminium/silicon alloys are slightly less dense). It conducts heat and electricity better than most steels. Cost sits between carbon steels and nickel alloys. Bronzes are ductile and far less brittle than cast iron. They oxidize superficially; the copper oxide layer protects underlying metal (visible on Hellenistic statues). However, copper chlorides cause "bronze disease," a progressive, destructive corrosion. Bronze is typically nonmagnetic, though iron- or nickel-containing alloys may be magnetic.

### Applications

**Marine and Industrial**  
Bronze’s toughness and saltwater corrosion resistance made it standard for ship fittings before stainless steel; it remains common in propellers and submerged bearings. Its non-sparking property (unlike steel) mandates its use for tools (hammers, wrenches) in explosive or flammable atmospheres. **Phosphor bronze** (high lead) serves in precision bearings, springs, electrical contacts, and musical strings. **Aluminium bronze** provides hard, wear-resistant bearings and machine ways. Oil-impregnated bronze (e.g., Oilite) creates self-lubricating bearings.

**Architecture**  
The Seagram Building (1958, NYC) pioneered full bronze cladding (3.2M lbs, fabricated by General Bronze Corp.), chosen for color stability, corrosion resistance, and extrusion properties. General Bronze/Roman Bronze Works also cast the massive bronze doors of the US Supreme Court and the Andrew W. Mellon Memorial Fountain (1952, Washington, DC), using statuary bronze (Cu-Zn-Sn-Pb quaternary alloy).

**Coinage and Medals**  
Most "copper" coins are bronze (~4% Sn, 1% Zn). Bronze medals for third place originated at the 1904 Olympics, symbolizing the Bronze Age of heroes in Greek mythology (following Golden and Silver Ages). The term now denotes third place generically. Bronze plaquettes (relief artworks) serve a collectors' market.

**Mirrors**  
Before flat glass, bronze was the standard mirror material worldwide (independent discoveries). Surviving examples: Egyptian Middle Kingdom (2040–1750 BCE), China (c. 550 BCE), Etruscan (6th century BCE), Greek, Roman. Bronze mirrors persisted in Japan into the 18th century and are still made in Kerala, India.

**Musical Instruments**  
**Bell metal** (~23% Sn) is preferred for bells. Professional cymbals use **B20 bronze** (~20% Sn, 80% Cu, traces Ag) or tougher **B8 bronze** (8% Sn, 92% Cu); higher tin lowers timbre. Bronze windings on steel/nylon strings (double bass, piano, harpsichord, guitar) offer superior sustain for lower piano notes. Struck idiophones globally—bells, singing bowls, gongs, cymbals, Javanese gamelan—use bronze. Indonesian finds (1–2 BCE) include struck plates; Thai/Vietnamese drums date back 2,000 years; Thai/Cambodian bells to 3600 BCE. Modern saxophones use phosphor bronze (3.5–10% Sn, ≤1% P); banjo tone rings use bell bronze/B20 for crisp lows and clear highs.

**Sculpture**  
Bronze expands slightly before setting, filling mold details, then shrinks for easy removal. Sennacherib (704–681 BCE) claimed first monumental two-part mold castings (up to 30 tonnes). Greek bronze statues were the pinnacle of Classical art, but few survive due to melting for reuse; many are known via Roman marble copies. Indian bronze sculpture flourished in Kushana, Gupta (e.g., Sultanganj Buddha), and Chola periods (lost-wax method, ongoing tradition in Swamimalai/Chennai). Chinese ritual bronzes (Shang/Zhou dynasties) were primarily ceremonial vessels. The medieval Benin "Bronzes" (13th–19th century CE) are technically leaded brass.

## Terms
- ****Alpha bronze**** — Solid solution of tin in copper (typically 4–5% tin); used for coins, springs, turbines, blades.
- ****Arsenical copper**** — Copper-arsenic alloy from mixed ores; silver hue, lower melting point, easier working than pure copper, but toxic.
- ****Bell metal**** — High-tin bronze (~23% Sn) used for bells; resonant acoustic properties.
- ****B20 bronze**** — Cymbal alloy: ~20% tin, 80% copper, traces of silver; standard for professional cymbals.
- ****B8 bronze**** — Cymbal alloy: 8% tin, 92% copper; tougher, higher timbre than B20.
- ****Bronze disease**** — Destructive corrosion caused by copper chlorides; progresses until object is destroyed.
- ****Classic bronze**** — Bronze Age casting alloy: ~10% tin; used for bladed weapons.
- ****Mild bronze**** — Bronze Age wrought alloy: ~6% tin; hammered into sheets for armor/helmets.
- ****Phosphor bronze**** — Bronze with phosphorus (and often lead); used for precision bearings, springs, electrical contacts, strings.
- ****Silicon bronze**** — Modern statuary alloy: 2.8–3.8% Si, 0.5–1.3% Mn, balance Cu; enables color-matched brazing repairs.
- ****Speculum metal**** — High-tin bronze (often >30% Sn) historically used for mirrors and telescope mirrors.
- ****Stannite**** — Natural tin-copper ore (Cu₂FeSnS₄); source of earliest tin-bronze artifact (c. 4650 BCE).

## Debates and open questions
- **Etymology**: Whether *bronze* derives from Byzantine Greek *brontēsíon* (via Brindisi) or Old Persian *birinj*/*piring* remains unresolved.
- **Indus Valley tin sources**: The origin of tin in Indus Valley bronzes (20% of finds) is unknown; Bukhara and Samarkand (Uzbekistan) are speculative candidates.
- **Benin "Bronzes" composition**: The famous Benin court artworks (13th–19th century) are technically leaded brass (copper-zinc), not bronze (copper-tin), yet the misnomer persists in museology.
- **Gloucester Candlestick provenance**: The 12th-century candlestick’s anomalous composition (high silver, multiple impurities) suggests it was cast from a hoard of old coins, but this is inferred from metallurgical analysis, not documented.
- **Transition causality**: The source presents the tin-trade collapse (1200–1100 BCE) as the primary driver for the Iron Age transition, but notes iron’s initial inferiority to bronze; the relative weight of supply shock vs. technological pull is not quantified.
