# Prehistory

## Overview

Prehistory is the period of human history between the first known use of stone tools by hominins (c. 3.3 million years ago) and the beginning of recorded history with the invention of writing systems (c. 5,200 years ago). Because writing was adopted slowly across the globe, the end of prehistory occurred at different times in different places. Unlike history, prehistory deals with archaeological cultures rather than named nations or individuals, and is known only through material evidence—remains and artefacts—rather than written records. The term is less often used for societies where prehistory ended relatively recently; the period when a culture is written about by others but has not developed its own writing is called its protohistory.

## Body

### History of the term

The notion of "prehistory" emerged during the Enlightenment among antiquarians who used the word "primitive" for societies before written records. The word "prehistory" first appeared in English in 1836 in the *Foreign Quarterly Review*. During the nineteenth century, the geologic time scale and the three-age system were systematised by British, French, German, and Scandinavian scholars. An early conception held that without written records there could be no history; today, most accept that history draws on a wide range of evidence, and while the term has been questioned, it has not been discarded—archaeologists in particular view it as a meaningful and necessary distinction.

### Means of research

The main source of information for prehistory is the archaeological record, studied alongside other disciplines. Primary researchers are anthropologists (archaeologists and physical anthropologists) using excavation, geologic and geographic surveys, and scientific analysis. Human population geneticists, historical linguists, and cultural anthropologists also contribute. Data comes from anthropology, archaeology, archaeoastronomy, comparative linguistics, biology, geology, molecular genetics, paleontology, palynology, and more. The key step is dating: radiocarbon dating is the most common technique. Further evidence comes from reconstructing ancient spoken languages, forensic chemical analysis of materials, and genetic analysis of bones for kinship and physical traits.

### Beginning and end

The beginning of prehistory is marked by human-like beings appearing on Earth; its end is typically the advent of the contemporary written historical record. Both dates vary widely by region. African prehistory begins c. 2.5 million years ago; prehistoric Asia dates back 1.8 million years; European prehistory begins c. 1.3 million years ago. In Egypt, prehistory ended around 3100 BCE; in New Guinea, it ended in the 1870s when the Russian anthropologist Nicholai Miklukho-Maklai described native peoples' way of life. In Europe, classical Greek and Roman cultures had neighbours like the Celts and Etruscans with little writing; historians debate how much weight to give to biased Greek and Roman accounts of these protohistoric cultures.

### Time periods

Historians divide Eurasian prehistory using the three-age system: Stone Age, Bronze Age, and Iron Age, named for predominant tool-making technologies, with a transition period (Chalcolithic or Copper Age) in some areas. The early Bronze Age saw Sumer, the Indus Valley Civilisation, and ancient Egypt develop their own scripts; most other civilizations reached the end of prehistory during the Iron Age. The three-age system is not used where hard-metal working arrived abruptly from contact with Eurasian cultures—Oceania, Australasia, much of Sub-Saharan Africa, and parts of the Americas. For example, 1788 is usually taken as the end of Australian prehistory.

### Stone Age

The Stone Age lasted roughly 3.4 million years, ending between 4000 BC and 2000 BC with the advent of metalworking, and encompasses 99% of human history. In the Americas it is called the Lithic stage or Paleo-Indian.

**Palaeolithic** ("Old Stone Age") extends from the first stone tools (c. 3.3 million years ago) to the end of the Pleistocene (c. 11,650 BP). The Lower Palaeolithic begins with tools at Lomekwi, Kenya, which predate the genus *Homo* and were probably used by *Kenyanthropus*. Control of fire is uncertain until c. 790,000–690,000 BP at Bnot Ya'akov Bridge, Israel. Early *Homo sapiens* appeared c. 300,000 years ago, ushering in the Middle Palaeolithic, with the first definitive fire use (Zambia, 180,000 BP), burial of the dead, music, and prehistoric art. The Upper Palaeolithic (50,000–12,000 years ago) saw the first organized settlements and artistic blossoming. Humans generally lived as nomadic hunter-gatherers in small, egalitarian societies, though some developed sedentary lifestyles, chiefdoms, and social stratification; long-distance contacts existed, as with Indigenous Australian "songlines."

**Mesolithic** ("Middle Stone Age") began with glacier retreat at the end of the Pleistocene (c. 10,000 BP) and ended with agriculture's introduction, varying by region. In the Near East, agriculture was already underway, so the Mesolithic is short; in areas with limited glacial impact, "Epipalaeolithic" is preferred. Northern Europe had a more evident Mesolithic, with rich marshland food supplies producing distinctive cultures (Maglemosian, Azilian), delaying the Neolithic until c. 4000 BCE. Remains are often limited to middens (refuse heaps). The period is characterized by microliths and microburins (small composite flint tools); fishing tackle, stone adzes, canoes, and bows appear. These technologies first occur in Africa, spreading to Europe via the Iberomaurusian and Kebaran cultures, though independent discovery is not ruled out.

**Neolithic** ("New Stone Age") began c. 10,200 BCE in parts of the Middle East, ending between 4,500 and 2,000 BCE. Only *Homo sapiens sapiens* remained. The era commenced with farming—the "Neolithic Revolution"—and established domestication of crops and animals, permanent settlements, and early chiefdoms. Early farming included einkorn wheat, millet, and spelt, plus dogs, sheep, and goats; by c. 6,900–6,400 BCE, cattle, pigs, settlements, and pottery appeared. Settlements became permanent, with circular or rectangular mudbrick houses, sometimes walled; burial finds suggest an ancestor cult. The Vinča culture may have created the earliest writing system. Most Neolithic societies were relatively simple and egalitarian; clothing was mostly animal skins, with wool and linen possibly appearing later.

### Chalcolithic

The Chalcolithic, Eneolithic, or Copper Age is a transitional period where early copper metallurgy appeared alongside stone tools, still largely Neolithic in character. A Serbian site contains the oldest securely dated evidence of high-temperature copper making, from 7,500 years ago—extending the known record by about 800 years and suggesting independent invention in Asia and Europe. The Fertile Crescent is the traditional origin of metallurgy, but Vinča culture finds are now dated slightly earlier. Timna Valley shows copper mining 7,000 years ago. The transition is marked by a decline in high-quality raw material procurement.

### Bronze Age

The Bronze Age is the earliest period in which some civilizations reached the end of prehistory by introducing written records; it is only part of prehistory for regions without writing. The most advanced metalworking involved smelting copper and tin from ores and casting bronze; tin ores are rare, with no tin bronzes in Western Asia before 3000 BCE. Tin often had to be traded over long distances, stimulating extensive trading routes. Bronze was used for weapons but long not for agricultural tools, and much was hoarded by social elites. By the end of the Bronze Age, large literate empires had arisen in Egypt, China, Anatolia (the Hittites), and Mesopotamia.

### Iron Age

The Iron Age is not part of prehistory for civilizations that had writing in the Bronze Age; most remaining civilizations adopted writing during the Iron Age, often through conquest by expanding empires. In most of Europe, Roman conquest replaced the term Iron Age with "Roman" or "Gallo-Roman"; many areas had a protohistory before conquest, as with Ireland. The Iron Age refers to the advent of ferrous metallurgy, coinciding with changes in agriculture, religion, and art—the "Axial Age" in philosophy. Ironworking requires more heat than earlier metals, but once solved, iron's abundance meant armies could be armed much more easily.

## Terms

- **Protohistory**: The period when a culture is written about by others but has not developed its own writing system.
- **Three-age system**: Periodization of human prehistory into Stone, Bronze, and Iron Ages, named for predominant tool-making technologies.
- **Hominins**: Human-like beings and their ancestors; the first tool users.
- **Radiocarbon dating**: The most common dating technique for prehistoric evidence.
- **Microliths**: Small composite flint tools characteristic of the Mesolithic.
- **Neolithic Revolution**: The commencement of farming, which began the Neolithic era.
- **Chalcolithic**: The Copper Age, a transition period between Stone and Bronze Ages.
- **Middens**: Refuse heaps, often the main remains from the Mesolithic.
- **BP**: "Before Present (1950)"; **BCE**: "Before Common Era."
- **Axial Age**: A period in the history of philosophy coinciding with the archaeological Iron Age.

## Debates and open questions

- The beginning of prehistory is "normally taken" to be marked by human-like beings, but the exact date varies by region (Africa c. 2.5 million years ago; Asia 1.8 million; Europe c. 1.3 million).
- Control of fire by early hominins in the Lower Palaeolithic is "uncertain and has at best limited scholarly support."
- The Mesolithic is "short and poorly defined" where agriculture arrived early; the term "Epipalaeolithic" is preferred in areas with limited glacial impact.
- Copper smelting may have been invented independently in separate parts of Asia and Europe, rather than spreading from a single source; the Fertile Crescent is the traditional origin, but Vinča culture finds are dated slightly earlier.
- Historians debate how much weight to give to biased Greek and Roman accounts of protohistoric cultures like the Celts and Etruscans.
- The Vinča culture "may have created the earliest system of writing"—this is uncertain.
- All timeline dates are approximate and conjectural, subject to revision from new discoveries or improved calculations.
