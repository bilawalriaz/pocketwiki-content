# Language

## Overview

Language is a structured system of communication consisting of grammar and vocabulary, primarily used by humans to convey meaning through spoken, signed, or written forms. It is characterized by productivity (the ability to create an infinite number of sentences from finite elements) and displacement (the ability to refer to events not immediately present). Human language relies on social convention, is acquired through learning, and is modality-independent—any language can be encoded into writing, whistling, signing, or braille. The scientific study of language is linguistics, and debates about its nature and origin date back to ancient Greek philosophy, continuing through thinkers like Rousseau, Kant, Wittgenstein, Saussure, and Chomsky.

## Body

### Definitions and philosophical debates

The English word "language" derives from Proto-Indo-European *dn̥ǵʰwéh₂s ("tongue, speech") through Latin lingua and Old French language. As an object of study, "language" has two primary meanings: an abstract concept and a specific system (e.g., "French"). Saussure distinguished langage (language as concept), langue (a specific language system), and parole (concrete speech use).

Greek philosophers Gorgias and Plato debated whether language represents objective reality; Gorgias argued communication and truth were impossible, while Plato held that language represents ideas existing independently of it. During the Enlightenment, Rousseau and Herder argued language originated from emotions and was closer to music, while Kant and Descartes saw it as rational. Twentieth-century philosophers, especially Wittgenstein, argued philosophy is essentially the study of language—the "linguistic turn."

Three main definitions of language exist: as a mental faculty (innate, emphasized by Chomsky's universal grammar and Fodor's innatism), as a formal symbolic system (Saussure's structuralism, Chomsky's generative grammar), and as a tool for communication (functionalist theories, associated with pragmatics and sociolinguistics).

### Human versus animal language

Charles Hockett's design features distinguish human language from animal communication. Animal systems are closed with finite possible ideas; human language is open-ended and productive, based on a dual code where meaningless elements combine into infinite meaningful units. Human language employs grammatical categories (noun/verb, present/past) and recursivity (a clause containing another clause). It is the only known natural communication system that is modality-independent. Displacement—referring to abstract, imagined, or hypothetical events—is used to a unique degree in human language, though bees communicate nectar locations out of sight. Some animals (e.g., bonobo Kanzi) acquire many symbols, but none approach the grammar of a 4-year-old human.

### Origin

Continuity-based theories hold that language evolved from earlier pre-linguistic systems; discontinuity-based theories see it as appearing suddenly. Most scholars favor continuity, but differ on precursors: Pinker sees animal cognition, Tomasello sees primate communication for cooperation, and Mithen sees music (a view espoused by Rousseau, Herder, Humboldt, and Darwin). Stephen Anderson estimates spoken languages are 60,000–100,000 years old and suggests language was invented only once. Chomsky, a discontinuity proponent, proposes a "random mutation" implanted a language organ in a primate brain. Scholars debate whether proto-language emerged with Homo habilis (2.3 million years ago), Homo erectus (1.8 million), or Homo heidelbergensis (0.6 million), with language proper developing with anatomically modern humans less than 100,000 years ago. A 2024 study places the beginnings at about 1.6 million years ago.

### Study of language

Linguistics began with Pāṇini's Sanskrit grammar (5th century BC), though Sumerian scribes studied grammar around 1900 BC. The 17th-century Port-Royal Grammarians proposed universal grammar; William Jones's comparative method sparked comparative linguistics in the 18th century. Saussure introduced diachronic/synchronic distinctions and the syntagm/paradigm distinction. Chomsky's 1960s generative theory posits Universal Grammar as the primary object of linguistics. Functional theories analyze structures by their communicative functions; cognitive linguistics focuses on how the mind creates meaning. Subdisciplines include descriptive linguistics, theoretical linguistics, sociolinguistics, neurolinguistics, computational linguistics, and historical linguistics.

### Brain and speech anatomy

Language is processed especially in Wernicke's area (posterior superior temporal gyrus) and Broca's area (posterior inferior frontal gyrus). Lesions in Wernicke's area cause receptive aphasia (impaired comprehension, fluent but meaningless speech); lesions in Broca's area cause expressive aphasia (knowing what to say but inability to speak fluently). Both conditions affect sign language analogously, showing impairment is specific to language ability. The only gene definitively implicated in language is FOXP2. Speech relies on lungs, larynx, and upper vocal tract. Segmental elements (vowels, consonants) combine into syllables; suprasegmental phenomena include stress, tone, and intonation. Vowels vary by lip aperture and tongue position; consonants vary by place and manner of articulation, and voicing.

### Structure

Language consists of signs, meanings, and a code connecting them (semiotics). Key properties include arbitrariness, duality, discreteness, and productivity. Semantics studies meaning; pragmatics studies meaning in context. Phonemes are abstract sound units distinguishing minimal pairs (e.g., bat/pat); allophones are variants of a single phoneme. Languages range from 10 phonemes (Pirahã) to 141 (Taa). Writing systems include alphabetic, syllabic (Inuktitut), and logographic scripts; no purely logographic scripts exist. Grammar comprises morphology (word structure) and syntax (phrase/sentence structure). Morphemes are free (words) or bound (affixes). Languages range from isolating (Chinese) to fusional (Latin) to agglutinative (Turkish) to polysynthetic (Yupik). Word order typology (SVO, SOV) correlates with other syntactic parameters via implicational universals. Languages use nominative-accusative, ergative, or tripartite alignment.

### Social contexts

Children acquire language through exposure, not formal teaching—Darwin called it "an instinctive tendency to acquire an art." Acquisition proceeds from babbling (6 months) to holophrases (12–18 months) to two-word utterances to telegraphic speech, reaching adult-like proficiency by age 3–5. Language signals group identity and social stratification; communicative styles vary between communities. Deixis (I, now, here) and speech acts (naming, pronouncing marriage) show language's social functions. Writing emerged in the late 4th millennium BC with Sumerian cuneiform and Egyptian hieroglyphs. Languages constantly change—Grimm's law affected Germanic stop consonants, and the Great Vowel Shift explains English spelling-pronunciation mismatches. Language contact produces pidgins (no native speakers) and creoles (pidgins that become first languages), such as Tok Pisin and Haitian Kreyòl.

### Linguistic diversity

Ethnologue cataloged 7,170 living languages as of 2026. The distinction between language and dialect is politically and socially determined—Hakka, Cantonese, and Mandarin are called "dialects" of Chinese though more different than Swedish and Norwegian. The Indo-European family has the most speakers (46% of world population); Sino-Tibetan has 20%; Niger-Congo 6.9%; Afroasiatic a similar number; Austronesian 5.5%. Papua New Guinea has 830 living tongues (12% of world total). Between 50% and 90% of languages spoken in 2010 are expected to be extinct by 2100. UNESCO operates five endangerment levels from "safe" to "critically endangered." Many projects aim to revitalize endangered languages; the University of Waikato uses Welsh as a model for Māori revitalization.

## Terms

- **Phoneme**: The smallest unit in a language that can distinguish meaning between minimally different words (e.g., /b/ and /p/ in bat/pat).
- **Morpheme**: The smallest meaningful unit; free morphemes are words, bound morphemes are affixes.
- **Grammar**: The system of rules governing how morphemes combine into utterances; includes morphology (word structure) and syntax (sentence structure).
- **Langue/parole**: Saussure's distinction between language as an abstract system (langue) and its concrete manifestation in speech (parole).
- **Universal Grammar**: Chomsky's hypothesized innate set of syntactic rules underlying all human languages.
- **Displacement**: The ability to refer to events, objects, or ideas not immediately present in time or space.
- **Pidgin**: A simplified mixed language with no native speakers, arising from contact between adult speakers of different languages.
- **Creole**: A pidgin that becomes a community's first language and acquires greater structural complexity.
- **Language isolate**: A language demonstrated to have no living or non-living relationship with any other language (e.g., Basque).
- **Aphasia**: Language impairment from brain lesions; receptive aphasia affects comprehension, expressive aphasia affects production.

## Debates and open questions

Scholars debate whether language originated from emotions (Rousseau, Herder) or rational thought (Kant, Descartes), and whether it evolved continuously from primate systems or appeared suddenly (Chomsky's mutation hypothesis). The timing of proto-language emergence is contested, ranging from Homo habilis (2.3 million years ago) to Homo heidelbergensis (0.6 million years ago). Whether Egyptian and Chinese writing developed independently of Sumerian or through cultural diffusion remains unresolved. The language/dialect distinction is fundamentally contested, with political considerations often overriding linguistic criteria. Whether language loss harms cultural diversity or is a natural process not to be counteracted is debated among linguists.
