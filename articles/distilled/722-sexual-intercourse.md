# Sexual intercourse

## Overview  
Sexual intercourse, also called coitus or copulation, is an intimate act typically involving penile–vaginal penetration and thrusting, primarily for pleasure, reproduction, or bonding. While the term most narrowly denotes reproductive penetration, it is variously defined across cultures, laws, and disciplines to include other penetrative and non-penetrative acts. Its study intersects biology, public health, psychology, law, and religion, making definitional clarity essential for research, policy, and sexual health education.

## Timeline  
- **c. 3000 BC** — Religious texts begin codifying sexual ethics (e.g., Hindu Kama Sutra, early Judeo-Christian law)
- **1890s** — Karl Heinrich Ulrichs and Magnus Hirschfeld begin early scientific study of sexuality
- **1948** — Alfred Kinsey publishes *Sexual Behavior in the Human Female*, documenting diverse sexual practices
- **1960s–70s** — Sexual revolution challenges traditional norms; WHO recognizes cultural variation in sexual terminology
- **1980s** — HIV/AIDS epidemic prompts global focus on safer sex practices
- **1991** — Kinsey Institute notes variability in duration of intercourse
- **2005** — WHO estimates 448 million new STI cases annually among 15–49 year-olds
- **2010** — NSSHB reports 1 in 4 U.S. vaginal intercourse acts are condom-protected
- **2012** — FBI redefines rape to include all forms of penetration, not just penile–vaginal

## Body  

### Definitions  
Sexual intercourse is most precisely defined as penile–vaginal penetration (vaginal sex), though broader definitions include anal, oral, and other forms of penetration. The term *coitus* derives from Latin *coire*, meaning "to come together." Non-penetrative acts are sometimes labeled *outercourse*. Definitions vary widely: some cultures and individuals consider oral or anal sex as "sex," while others reserve the term for reproductive acts. Legal systems often define intercourse broadly under "sexual penetration." The age of first intercourse is termed *sexarche*.

### Stimulation  
Sexual activity involves physiological and psychological stimulation, including foreplay, varied sex positions (e.g., missionary), and use of sex toys. Clitoral stimulation is crucial for female orgasm—70–80% of women require direct or indirect clitoral contact. Techniques like the coital alignment method enhance stimulation. Anal and oral sex involve distinct anatomical targets. Unlike non-primate mammals, human females can conceive throughout their menstrual cycle, and pheromone detection is reduced in humans.

### Reproduction  
Natural reproduction requires ejaculation of sperm into the vagina, with fertilization occurring in the fallopian tubes. The fertile window spans roughly five days before to one day after ovulation. *Timed intercourse*, guided by ovulation tests, may aid conception but lacks consistent evidence for improving pregnancy rates. *Natural insemination* refers to intercourse for conception without medical assistance; *artificial insemination* and other assisted reproductive technologies serve those unable to conceive naturally.

### Safe Sex and Birth Control  
Safe sex practices—especially consistent condom use—reduce STI transmission by 85–99% for HIV. Abstinence is the most effective prevention. Cultural factors influence contraceptive choices: Catholic-majority nations favor fertility awareness methods, while IUDs are globally prevalent. Condoms are underutilized for oral and anal sex. Unintended pregnancies account for 70% of global pregnancies, with unsafe abortions causing significant mortality in developing regions.

### Prevalence  
Vaginal intercourse is the most common sexual behavior across demographics. Oral sex is widespread among adolescents and adults; anal sex is less common but practiced by 13–15% of U.S. men aged 25–49. Age at first intercourse varies cross-culturally: later in some European and Asian societies, earlier in Latin American and sub-Saharan African contexts. In the U.S., median age of first intercourse is 17.1 years.

### Health Effects  

#### Benefits  
Regular sexual activity correlates with lower blood pressure, increased antibody production, reduced prostate cancer risk, and elevated oxytocin levels promoting bonding. Orgasms release endorphins and may slow aging, per longitudinal studies.

#### Risks  
STIs affect 19 million Americans annually; untreated infections can lead to infertility, cancer, or enhance HIV transmission risk tenfold. Excessive sexual activity may trigger addictive behaviors linked to dopamine pathways. Coital death—rare cardiac events during exertion—is possible, especially in sedentary individuals.

#### Complications  
Gender disparities exist in sexual dysfunction: ~25% of women report orgasm difficulties, often due to inadequate clitoral stimulation or lack of sex education. *Vaginismus* (involuntary vaginal muscle spasms) and *dyspareunia* (painful intercourse) affect many women. Erectile dysfunction affects ~40% of men; premature ejaculation affects up to 30%. Treatments include PDE5 inhibitors (e.g., Viagra) and SSRIs like dapoxetine.

### Disabilities and Other Complications  
People with disabilities face barriers including pain, fatigue, anxiety, and societal stigma. Those with intellectual disabilities often lack access to sexual education and are at higher risk of abuse. Healthcare providers frequently overlook sexual health needs in disabled populations.

### Social Effects  

#### Adults  
Sexual satisfaction supports relationship stability, though mismatched libidos contribute to divorce. Cohabiting couples report more frequent sex than married ones, possibly due to novelty. Older adults engage less frequently but value emotional connection over orgasm.

#### Adolescents  
Sexual activity among teens can foster intimacy but increases risks of STIs and unintended pregnancy. Early puberty pressures youth into adult roles prematurely. Research shows little correlation between self-esteem and sexual activity, contradicting earlier assumptions. Positive romantic relationships during adolescence predict healthier adult relationships.

### Ethical, Religious, and Legal Views  

#### General  
Human societies regulate sex through moral codes and laws. Anal sex has historically been criminalized (*sodomy laws*). Consent and age of consent (typically 16–18) are central legal concerns.

#### Sexual Offenses  
Rape is non-consensual sexual penetration. Historically limited to penile–vaginal acts, definitions now include all penetration types. The FBI updated its definition in 2012. Incest laws protect family structures regardless of consent. Zoophilia is illegal in many jurisdictions under animal cruelty statutes.

#### Marriage and Relationships  
Marriage traditionally mandates consummation; non-consummation may permit annulment. Adultery remains criminalized in some countries. Same-sex marriage legality varies globally, with opposition often rooted in religious beliefs about heteronormative reproduction.

#### Religious Views  
Christianity emphasizes marital fidelity and procreation; Catholicism prohibits contraception. Judaism requires spousal intimacy (*onah*). Islam permits up to four wives. Hinduism embraces sensual pleasure via the Kama Sutra. Buddhism advocates celibacy for monastics and mindful conduct for laypeople. Wicca celebrates pleasure within ethical bounds. The Baháʼí Faith restricts sex to marriage. Unitarian Universalists emphasize consent and ethics.

### Other Animals  
Animal copulation typically serves reproduction, though bonobos, chimpanzees, and dolphins engage in sex for pleasure and social bonding, including same-sex interactions. Spiders transfer sperm via pedipalps; insects use specialized organs. Aquatic species often rely on external fertilization.

## Terms  
- **Coitus**: Penile–vaginal penetration; from Latin *coire* ("to come together").  
- **Outercourse**: Non-penetrative sexual activity.  
- **Sexarche**: First instance of sexual intercourse.  
- **Intromission**: Insertion of the penis into the vagina.  
- **Timed Intercourse**: Scheduling intercourse around ovulation to increase pregnancy chances.  
- **Natural Insemination**: Conception through intercourse without medical intervention.  
- **Vaginismus**: Involuntary tightening of vaginal muscles preventing penetration.  
- **Dyspareunia**: Painful sexual intercourse.  
- **Anorgasmia**: Inability to achieve orgasm despite adequate stimulation.  
- **Sodomy**: Historically, anal or non-procreative sex; used in legal contexts.

## Debates and Open Questions  
- Whether oral or anal sex qualifies as "real sex" affects adolescent risk perception and STI prevention messaging.  
- Researchers' failure to define "sex" consistently skews data collection, particularly regarding LGBTQ+ behaviors.  
- Condom desensitization beliefs persist in some African communities, undermining HIV prevention efforts.  
- The role of clitoral vs. vaginal stimulation in female orgasm remains debated, influencing therapeutic approaches.  
- Cross-cultural variations in age at first intercourse challenge universal sexual health guidelines.  
- Religious exemptions in rape law historically allowed marital immunity, now largely abolished but still influential in some regions.  
- The classification of hypersexuality as a behavioral addiction in psychiatric manuals remains contentious.
