# Henry II of England

## Overview
Henry II (1133–1189), the first Plantagenet king of England, ruled from 1154 until his death. He established the vast Angevin Empire, spanning England and much of France, and laid the foundations of English Common Law through legal reforms. His reign was marked by energetic governance, territorial expansion, and intense conflicts with Thomas Becket and his own sons.

## Timeline
- **5 March 1133** — Born in Le Mans, Maine, to Geoffrey Plantagenet and Empress Matilda.
- **1150** — Created Duke of Normandy by his father.
- **18 May 1152** — Married Eleanor of Aquitaine, gaining control of the Duchy of Aquitaine.
- **1153** — Treaty of Wallingford: King Stephen recognizes Henry as his heir.
- **19 December 1154** — Crowned King of England at Westminster Abbey.
- **1157–1158** — First royal expedition into Wales; defeated at the Battle of Ewloe.
- **1164** — Constitutions of Clarendon: Henry attempts to assert royal authority over the Church, sparking conflict with Thomas Becket.
- **29 December 1170** — Thomas Becket murdered in Canterbury Cathedral.
- **1171** — Henry invades Ireland, asserting his lordship.
- **1173–1174** — The Great Revolt: Henry's sons and rebellious barons, supported by France and Scotland, rise against him; Henry is victorious.
- **1175** — Treaty of Windsor: Henry establishes overlordship over Ireland.
- **1189** — Final rebellion by his son Richard and Philip II of France; Henry dies on 6 July at Chinon Castle.

## Body

### Early Life and Accession
Henry was the eldest son of Geoffrey Plantagenet and Empress Matilda, whose claim to the English throne was contested by King Stephen, leading to the civil war known as The Anarchy. Educated in Anjou and England, Henry first intervened militarily in England in 1147. He inherited Normandy, Anjou, and Maine from his father in 1151 and, through his marriage to Eleanor of Aquitaine, gained control of the vast Duchy of Aquitaine. This made him a more powerful French vassal than King Louis VII. In 1153, Henry invaded England, leading to the Treaty of Wallingford, which made him Stephen's heir. He became king upon Stephen's death in 1154.

### The Angevin Empire and Governance
Henry controlled the "Angevin Empire," a loose network of territories from Scotland to the Pyrenees. He ruled through a peripatetic court, constantly traveling to administer justice and maintain control. His government relied on a mix of traditional nobility and "new men"—loyal administrators promoted for their skills. In England, he restored royal authority after the civil war, demolishing unauthorized castles and reviving financial systems like the Exchequer. His legal reforms, which expanded royal justice through traveling judges and new legal procedures (assizes), are considered the foundation of English Common Law.

### Conflict with the Church and France
A major conflict arose with Thomas Becket, his Archbishop of Canterbury, over the jurisdiction of Church courts versus royal authority. This dispute culminated in Becket's murder in 1170, an event that horrified Europe and forced Henry into public penance. His relationship with Louis VII of France was a long-running "cold war" of alliances, sieges, and truces, fought over territories like the Vexin and Toulouse. Henry expanded his empire at Louis's expense, taking control of Brittany and pushing into central and southern France.

### Family Rebellions and Final Years
Henry's later reign was dominated by attempts to provide for his eight children with Eleanor, leading to destructive family conflicts. His eldest surviving son, Henry the Young King, rebelled in 1173–1174, supported by his brothers, Eleanor, and foreign allies. Henry crushed the revolt but faced ongoing tensions. After Young Henry's death in 1183, Henry favored his youngest son, John, creating friction with his heir, Richard. The new French king, Philip II, skillfully exploited these divisions. In 1189, Richard allied with Philip against Henry. Defeated and suffering from a bleeding ulcer, Henry was forced to submit at Ballan before dying at Chinon Castle.

## Terms
- **Angevin Empire**: The vast, loosely connected collection of territories ruled by Henry II, centered on the Plantagenet family lands of Anjou and including England, Normandy, Aquitaine, and other French regions.
- **The Anarchy**: The civil war in England (1135–1153) between supporters of King Stephen and Empress Matilda for control of the throne.
- **Constitutions of Clarendon**: A set of laws enacted by Henry II in 1164 to assert royal authority over the Church, particularly in the trial of clergy, which directly led to his conflict with Thomas Becket.
- **Assizes**: Legal procedures introduced by Henry II that expanded the reach of royal justice in England. Examples include *novel disseisin* (for wrongful dispossession of land) and *mort d'ancestor* (for inheritance disputes).
- **Exchequer**: The royal treasury and accounting body in England, which Henry reformed to improve revenue collection and financial administration.
- **New Men**: Administrators and officials promoted by Henry II from minor nobility or non-noble backgrounds, chosen for loyalty and competence rather than hereditary status.
- **Great Revolt (1173–1174)**: A major rebellion against Henry II led by his sons Henry, Richard, and Geoffrey, along with rebellious barons and foreign allies, which Henry ultimately defeated.
- **Treaty of Falaise (1174)**: The agreement imposed on William I of Scotland after his capture during the Great Revolt, making him a vassal of the English crown.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Nature of the Angevin Empire**: Historians debate whether it was a coherent political entity or merely a "family firm" of separate territories held together by personal loyalty to Henry. Its lack of a central administration and reliance on Henry's personal rule made it fragile.
- **Henry's Legal Reforms**: While credited with founding English Common Law, scholars question whether Henry had a grand vision or if the reforms were a pragmatic, incremental response to post-civil war disorder. His personal role in drafting specific laws is also uncertain.
- **Motives in the Becket Conflict**: The depth of Henry's personal animosity versus his political strategy in the dispute with Becket is debated. Some historians suggest his famous outburst leading to the murder may have been theatrical, not a literal command.
- **Collapse of the Empire**: The rapid loss of Angevin lands in France under King John is attributed to various factors, including long-term economic shifts, cultural divergence between England and Normandy, and the inherent instability of Henry's dynastic inheritance system.
