# North Pole

## Overview
The North Pole is the northernmost point on Earth, where the planet's axis of rotation meets the surface. It is located in the Arctic Ocean, amidst shifting sea ice, making it distinct from the land-based South Pole. Its unique geography, extreme environment, and strategic location have made it a focal point for scientific exploration, geopolitical claims, and cultural symbolism.

## Timeline
- **1827** — William Edward Parry reaches latitude 82°45′ N, a record at the time.
- **1909** — Robert Peary claims to have reached the Pole; his claim remains disputed.
- **1926** — Roald Amundsen's airship *Norge* makes the first undisputed flight over the Pole.
- **1937** — The first North Pole ice station, North Pole-1, is established by the Soviet Union.
- **1948** — A Soviet party becomes the first verified individuals to reach the Pole on foot.
- **1958** — The USS *Nautilus* becomes the first naval vessel to cross the North Pole underwater.
- **1968** — Ralph Plaisted's team completes the first confirmed surface journey to the Pole.
- **1977** — The Soviet icebreaker *Arktika* makes the first surface vessel journey to the Pole.
- **2007** — A Russian expedition makes the first manned descent to the seabed at the North Pole.

## Body

### Geography and Environment
The North Pole lies at 90° North latitude, where all lines of longitude converge. It is situated in the middle of the Arctic Ocean, with a sea depth measured at 4,261 m (13,980 ft) in 2007. The nearest land is Kaffeklubben Island, about 700 km (430 mi) away. The Pole experiences six months of continuous daylight in summer and six months of darkness in winter. Winter temperatures average around −31 °C (−24 °F), while summer averages near 0 °C (32 °F). The sea ice is typically 2 to 3 m (6 ft 7 in to 9 ft 10 in) thick, though its extent and thickness vary. Studies indicate the ice is thinning, and the Pole may become seasonally ice-free due to global warming.

### Exploration and Firsts
Early attempts to reach the Pole began in the 19th century. Frederick Cook (1908) and Robert Peary (1909) both claimed to have reached it on foot, but their claims are now widely disputed. The first verified attainment was by air, when Roald Amundsen's airship *Norge* flew over the area in 1926. The first confirmed surface journey was by Ralph Plaisted's team in 1968. The Soviet Union established the first ice station in 1937 and made the first verified on-foot arrival in 1948. The first submarine to cross was the USS *Nautilus* in 1958. In 2007, a Russian team made the first manned dive to the ocean floor at the Pole.

### Scientific Stations and Modern Access
Permanent construction at the Pole is impractical due to the shifting ice. The Soviet Union and Russia have operated manned drifting ice stations since 1937. Since 2002, the seasonal Russian camp Barneo has been established annually near the Pole for research and tourism. In the 21st century, access by air and icebreaker has become more routine, including for tourists. The 2007 Russian expedition to the seabed was part of research supporting Russia's continental shelf claim.

### Territorial Claims and Climate
Under international law, no country owns the North Pole. The five surrounding Arctic states (Russia, Canada, Norway, Denmark, and the United States) have 200-nautical-mile exclusive economic zones. Countries can claim extended continental shelves under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea. The retreat of Arctic sea ice, linked to global warming, has commercial implications for shipping and resource extraction, and may accelerate further warming by reducing solar reflection.

## Terms
- **Geodetic latitude 90° North**: The precise angular measurement defining the North Pole's position.
- **Antipodal**: Directly opposite; the North and South Poles are antipodes.
- **Chandler wobble**: A small, periodic wandering of the Earth's axis of rotation, discovered by Seth Carlo Chandler.
- **Instantaneous pole**: The exact point where the Earth's axis intersects the surface at any given moment, subject to wobble.
- **Polynya**: An area of open water surrounded by sea ice, once thought to exist at the Pole.
- **Exclusive economic zone (EEZ)**: A maritime zone extending 200 nautical miles from a coast, granting sovereign rights to resources.
- **Continental shelf**: The extended, submerged perimeter of a continent, relevant to territorial claims under international law.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The validity of early exploration claims, particularly those of Frederick Cook (1908) and Robert Peary (1909), remains highly disputed due to questionable navigational data and incredible claimed speeds.
- The precise timeline for when the North Pole may become seasonally ice-free is debated, with predictions ranging from the mid-21st century to later.
- The geopolitical future of the Arctic is uncertain, as retreating ice may intensify territorial and resource claims among the five surrounding nations.
