# Hanseatic League

## Overview
The Hanseatic League was a medieval commercial and defensive network of merchant guilds and market towns in Central and Northern Europe. It dominated maritime trade in the North and Baltic Seas from the 13th to 15th centuries, establishing a powerful economic system that influenced regional politics and development before gradually declining due to the rise of territorial states and changing trade patterns.

## Timeline
- **1159** — Rebuilding of Lübeck by Henry the Lion, a traditional origin point for the League.
- **1241** — Lübeck and Hamburg form a precursor alliance, controlling the salt-fish trade.
- **1267** — First documentary mention of the term "Hanse" for a German merchant group.
- **1356/1358** — First formal Hanseatic Diet (Hansetag) is held in Lübeck.
- **1361–1370** — Danish-Hanseatic War culminates in the Treaty of Stralsund (1370), marking the League's peak influence.
- **1398** — The Teutonic Order conquers Gotland, restoring its Hanseatic privileges.
- **1494** — Tsar Ivan III closes the Hanseatic Kontor in Novgorod, a major blow to eastern trade.
- **1597–1598** — Queen Elizabeth I expels the League from London, closing the Steelyard.
- **1669** — Last formal Hanseatic Diet is held; the League effectively disintegrates.

## Body

### Origins and Early Development
The League grew from loosely associated groups of German traders and towns in the late 12th century, seeking to expand commerce and protect against robbery. It evolved from a *Kaufmannshanse* (merchant guild network) into a *Städtehanse* (city confederation). Lübeck became a central node after its 1159 rebuilding, offering better access and legal protections than older ports like Schleswig. The League's expansion was aided by German colonization of the Baltic coast, with new cities adopting Lübeck law. A key early alliance was formed in 1241 between Lübeck and Hamburg, which gained control of the vital salt-fish trade.

### Commercial Expansion and Power
The League established major foreign trading posts called *Kontore* in Novgorod, Bruges, Bergen, and London (the Steelyard). These were extraterritorial enclaves with legal autonomy. Hanseatic trade was not exclusively maritime; an integrated network of river and land routes was crucial. Major goods included eastern commodities like wax, furs, and timber, exchanged for western cloth and manufactured goods. The League wielded significant economic power, using embargoes, blockades, and even military action to pressure rulers and protect its privileges. It also invested in infrastructure, erecting lighthouses and training pilots.

### Zenith and Organizational Structure
The League's zenith was marked by the Treaty of Stralsund (1370), which granted it significant influence over the Øresund strait and Scania. Organizationally, it remained a loose confederation without a permanent treasury or standing army. Decisions were made by consensus at irregular *Hansetagen* (Diets). The Grand Master of the Teutonic Order was often considered its head (*caput Hansae*). The League was internally divided into *Drittel* (thirds) and later *Quartiere* (quarters) to manage its diverse membership from the Netherlands to Russia.

### Decline and End
From the 15th century, the League faced rising competition from territorial states like England, the Netherlands, and Poland-Lithuania. The discovery of the Americas shifted trade routes away from the Baltic. Internal tensions grew between the Wendish cities (like Lübeck) and Prussian cities interested in bulk grain exports. Key blows included the closure of the Novgorod Kontor (1494), the expulsion from London (1598), and the destructive Thirty Years' War. The last Diet in 1669 was poorly attended, and the League silently disintegrated, though cities like Hamburg, Bremen, and Lübeck retained "Hanseatic" in their names.

## Terms
- **Kontor**: A major foreign trading post of the League, organized as a private corporation with legal autonomy, treasury, and court.
- **Hansetag**: The Hanseatic Diet, an irregular central meeting of member cities for deliberation and consensus-based decision-making.
- **Cog**: The most emblematic Hanseatic ship, a clinker-built, square-rigged vessel used for both trade and war.
- **Kaufmannshanse**: The early phase of the League, a network of merchant guilds.
- **Städtehanse**: The later phase of the League, a confederation of cities.
- **Drittel/Quartiere**: Internal administrative divisions (thirds, later quarters) of the League to organize its geographically dispersed members.
- **Lübeck law**: A legal code adopted by many Hanseatic cities, providing for appeals to Lübeck's city council.
- **Rezess**: A binding decision or recess recorded after a Hanseatic Diet.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact date of the League's "founding" is debated, as it was never formally established. Historians traditionally point to Lübeck's 1159 rebuilding, but modern scholarship views it as a gradual combination of regional trading systems.
- The role and influence of the Teutonic Order as the only territorial state member is a subject of study, particularly its economic and military interdependency with the League.
- The extent to which the League's governance structure, based on consensus and loose confederation, can be seen as a precursor to modern supranational models like the European Union is a point of popular and academic discussion.
