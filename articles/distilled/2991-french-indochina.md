# French Indochina

## Overview
French Indochina was a group of French dependent territories in Southeast Asia from 1887 to 1954, comprising Vietnam (divided into Cochinchina, Annam, and Tonkin), Cambodia, Laos, and the leased territory of Guangzhouwan. It was established as a federation of colonies and later reorganized as a confederation of associated states within the French Union. The colonial administration was characterized by economic exploitation, limited modernization, and persistent anti-colonial resistance, culminating in the First Indochina War and the territory's dissolution following the Geneva Accords.

## Timeline
- **1862** — Treaty of Saigon: France gains control of three provinces in southern Vietnam.
- **1863** — Cambodia becomes a French protectorate.
- **1887** — French Indochina is formed, uniting Cochinchina, Annam, Tonkin, and Cambodia.
- **1893** — Franco-Siamese crisis leads to Laos being added to the union.
- **1940** — Vichy France grants Japan military access to Tonkin.
- **1945** — Japan overthrows the French colonial regime; Ho Chi Minh declares Vietnamese independence.
- **1946** — Franco-Chinese and Ho–Sainteny Agreements allow French forces to return north of the 16th parallel.
- **1949** — Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia become associated states within the French Union.
- **1954** — French defeat at Điện Biên Phủ leads to the Geneva Accords, ending French Indochina.

## Body

### Establishment and Colonial Rule
French Indochina was consolidated in 1887 from territories acquired through military conquest and treaties, including the French colony of Cochinchina and the protectorates of Annam, Tonkin, and Cambodia. Laos was incorporated in 1893 following a crisis with Siam (Thailand). The French administration, headed by a governor-general, maintained direct rule in Cochinchina and key cities, while in protectorates, local rulers like the Nguyễn emperors and Cambodian and Laotian kings were kept as figureheads. The economy was structured for exploitation, with the colonial government deriving significant revenue from monopolies on opium, salt, and alcohol, and later from rubber plantations and mining. Infrastructure development, such as the Trans-Indochinois railway, primarily served French economic and strategic interests.

### Resistance and Nationalism
From its inception, French rule faced resistance, beginning with the Cần Vương monarchist movement in the 1880s. In the early 20th century, nationalist movements evolved, influenced by international events like the Russo-Japanese War and the Xinhai Revolution. Figures like Phan Bội Châu and Phan Châu Trinh advocated for modernization, while secret societies and later parties like the Việt Nam Quốc Dân Đảng (VNQDĐ) and the Indochinese Communist Party organized against colonial authority. World War I was a catalyst, as over 100,000 Indochinese served in Europe, exposing them to new political ideas and fueling post-war nationalist and communist movements. The French response involved both limited reforms, such as the collaboration Franco-annamite, and the expansion of a repressive security apparatus, the Sûreté générale indochinoise.

### World War II and the End of French Rule
During World War II, French Indochina was administered by Vichy France and occupied by Japan from 1940. A devastating famine in 1944–1945 killed an estimated one to two million people in northern Vietnam. In March 1945, Japan overthrew the French administration, establishing puppet states. Following Japan's surrender, the Viet Minh, led by Ho Chi Minh, launched the August Revolution and declared the independence of the Democratic Republic of Vietnam on September 2, 1945. France attempted to reassert control, leading to the First Indochina War (1946–1954). To counter the Viet Minh, France reorganized the territory in 1949, creating the State of Vietnam and granting associated statehood to Laos and Cambodia. The war ended with the French defeat at Điện Biên Phủ and the 1954 Geneva Accords, which recognized the independence of Laos, Cambodia, and a divided Vietnam.

## Terms
- **Protectorate**: A territory under indirect colonial rule where local rulers remained as figureheads, but real power was held by the French administration.
- **Cochinchina**: The southern region of Vietnam, directly administered as a French colony, unlike the protectorates of Annam and Tonkin.
- **Sûreté générale indochinoise**: The French colonial political police force responsible for surveillance and repression of anti-colonial activities.
- **Corvée**: A system of forced, unpaid labor imposed on the local population for public works projects.
- **Colonie d'exploitation économique**: A colony primarily valued for its economic resources and exploitation, as opposed to a settlement colony.
- **Associated State**: A status within the French Union granted to Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia in 1949, granting greater autonomy but not full independence.
- **Geneva Accords**: The 1954 agreements that ended the First Indochina War, recognized the independence of Laos and Cambodia, and temporarily divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The death toll of the 1944–1945 Vietnamese famine is debated, with estimates ranging from 200,000 to over 2 million. The figure of 2 million, cited by Ho Chi Minh and the Communist Party of Vietnam, is contested by some historians and Vietnamese sources.
- The motivations behind various anti-French revolts, such as the 1914 Yunnanese invasion of Laos, are disputed, with contemporary French officials attributing them to criminal activity while historians like Geoffrey C. Gunn argue for political motives.
- The nature of recruitment for World War I service is debated. While some historians describe it as forced, the source indicates the vast majority of Indochinese soldiers were volunteers motivated by economic and personal ambitions.
