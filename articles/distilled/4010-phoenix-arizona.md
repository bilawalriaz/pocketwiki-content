# Phoenix, Arizona

## Overview
Phoenix is the capital and most populous city of Arizona, and the fifth-most populous city in the United States. Its growth from a small agricultural settlement to a major metropolis was driven by irrigation, railroad access, post-WWII migration, and the advent of air conditioning, which made its extreme desert climate habitable for large-scale urban development.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC–1450 AD** — Hohokam people occupy the area, building extensive irrigation canals.
- **1867** — Jack Swilling founds an agricultural community, the first American settlement.
- **1881** — Phoenix is incorporated as a city.
- **1889** — Phoenix becomes the capital of Arizona Territory.
- **1912** — Arizona is admitted to the Union; Phoenix becomes the state capital.
- **1948** — Motorola opens a research and development center, marking the arrival of high-tech industry.
- **1968** — The Central Arizona Project is approved, securing future water supplies.
- **1981** — Sandra Day O'Connor, a Phoenix resident, is sworn in as the first female U.S. Supreme Court justice.
- **2008** — The Valley Metro Rail light rail system opens.
- **2023** — The state halts new groundwater-dependent housing development in the metro area due to a predicted water shortfall.

## Body

### Early History and Founding
The Phoenix area was home to the Hohokam civilization for 2,000 years, known for their canal systems. After their abandonment, groups like the Akimel O'odham (Pima) and Maricopa inhabited the region. Following the Mexican–American War, the area became part of the U.S. In 1867, Jack Swilling, noting the ruins of ancient canals, formed a company to dig new ones for farming. The settlement was named "Phoenix" by settler Lord Darrell Duppa, symbolizing a city born from the ruins of a former civilization. It was incorporated in 1881 and became the territorial capital in 1889.

### Growth and Economic Shifts
Phoenix's early economy was based on the "Five C's": cotton, cattle, citrus, climate, and copper. The arrival of railroads in the 1880s and 1890s made it a trade center. After Arizona achieved statehood in 1912, growth accelerated. The post-WWII era saw explosive expansion, fueled by returning military personnel, the arrival of high-tech companies like Motorola, and the widespread adoption of air conditioning. This transformed the city into a major industrial and population hub, though growth was initially segregated.

### Modern Metropolis and Challenges
From the 1960s onward, Phoenix continued rapid growth, becoming a center for tourism, electronics manufacturing, and finance. The city adopted a "Concept 2000" plan, dividing it into urban villages. However, its development also made it a textbook case of urban sprawl. The city was severely impacted by the 2008 subprime mortgage crisis but has since recovered. A critical ongoing challenge is water security, highlighted by the 2023 state decision to halt new groundwater-dependent development.

### Climate and Geography
Located in the Sonoran Desert, Phoenix has a hot desert climate with extremely hot summers and mild winters. It is one of the sunniest cities on Earth, with an average of 3,872 hours of sunshine annually. The city has recorded temperatures as high as 122 °F (50 °C). The urban heat island effect limits nighttime cooling. The region experiences a summer monsoon season that can bring heavy rain, dust storms (haboobs), and flash floods.

### Demographics and Culture
Phoenix is a diverse city, with a population that was 41.1% Hispanic or Latino as of the 2020 census. It has a large Native American population and is a major destination for immigrants. The city's cultural institutions include the Phoenix Art Museum, the Heard Museum (focused on Native American art), and the Musical Instrument Museum. It is known for its Mexican cuisine and hosts numerous annual events celebrating its heritage.

### Economy and Infrastructure
The economy is diverse, with major sectors including real estate, finance, manufacturing, and healthcare. Key employers include Banner Health, Intel, and the U.S. government. Phoenix Sky Harbor International Airport is one of the nation's busiest. The city has an extensive freeway system and a growing public transit network, the Valley Metro Rail. Water is supplied via a system of canals, primarily from the Colorado River through the Central Arizona Project.

## Terms
- **Hohokam**: A prehistoric Native American civilization that built extensive irrigation canals in the Salt River Valley.
- **Akimel O'odham (Pima)**: A Native American group, descendants of the Hohokam, who practiced irrigation-based agriculture in the region.
- **Five C's**: The traditional pillars of Phoenix's economy: cotton, cattle, citrus, climate, and copper.
- **Council-manager system**: A form of city government where an elected council sets policy and a hired city manager oversees daily operations.
- **Urban sprawl**: The rapid, often unplanned expansion of a city into low-density, car-dependent communities on its outskirts.
- **Urban heat island effect**: The phenomenon where urban areas experience higher temperatures than surrounding rural areas due to human activities and infrastructure.
- **Central Arizona Project (CAP)**: A major canal system that delivers water from the Colorado River to central and southern Arizona.
- **Haboob**: An intense dust storm or sandstorm carried by a strong wind, common in arid regions.
- **Miranda Warning**: A statement of rights required by U.S. law to be given to criminal suspects in police custody, stemming from a 1966 Supreme Court case originating in Phoenix.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Water Sustainability**: The long-term viability of Phoenix's growth is debated due to its reliance on the Colorado River and groundwater, with the 2023 state halt on new groundwater-dependent development highlighting the crisis.
- **Urban Sprawl vs. Density**: The city's low-density, sprawling development pattern is frequently analyzed as a case study, raising questions about its environmental and economic sustainability compared to more compact cities.
- **Historical Segregation**: The legacy of post-WWII segregation, where growth was concentrated in white neighborhoods on the north side, continues to shape the city's social and geographic landscape.
