# Madhvacharya

## Overview
Madhvacharya (1199–1278 CE or 1238–1317 CE) was a 13th-century Indian philosopher and theologian who founded the Dvaita (dualism) school of Vedanta. His philosophy, which he called Tattvavāda ("arguments from a realist viewpoint"), asserts a fundamental, eternal difference between the individual soul (Atman) and the ultimate reality, God (Brahman as Vishnu). He was a fierce critic of the monist schools of Adi Shankara (Advaita) and Ramanuja (Vishishtadvaita), and his teachings have significantly influenced Vaishnavism and the Bhakti movement.

## Timeline
- **c. 1199 or 1238 CE** — Born in Pajaka near Udupi, Karnataka, India.
- **Teenage years** — Became a Sannyasi (monk), joining the Advaita Vedanta monastery of Achyutapreksha.
- **Early career** — Left the monastery after disagreeing with Advaita's nondualism, founding his own Dvaita movement.
- **Throughout career** — Toured India, engaging in philosophical debates at centers like Badrinath, Varanasi, and Bengal.
- **1285 CE** — Established the Krishna Mutt (monastery) at Udupi with a murti (sacred image) from Dwarka.
- **c. 1278 or 1317 CE** — Death (year is debated).

## Body

### Early Life and Monastic Training
Madhvacharya was born into a Brahmin family and was named Vāsudeva. He began his education after his Upanayana (sacred thread ceremony) at age seven. As a teenager, he became a monk (Sannyasi) and joined an Advaita Vedanta monastery in Udupi, accepting Achyutapreksha as his guru. He studied the Upanishads and Advaita literature but became unconvinced by its philosophy of oneness between the soul and God. After frequent disagreements with his guru, he left the monastery to establish his own dualist philosophy, Tattvavāda.

### Philosophy and Core Teachings
Madhvacharya's Dvaita Vedanta is built on the premise of a fundamental difference between the individual soul (Atman) and Brahman (God Vishnu). He posited a pluralistic metaphysics with two primary categories of reality: the independent reality (God) and the dependent reality (souls and matter). He enumerated a fivefold difference (pancha-bheda) between God, souls, and material things, asserting that this difference is eternal and natural.

His epistemology (Anu pramana) accepts three valid means of knowledge: perception (Pratyaksha), inference (Anumāna), and testimony (Śabda), which includes the Vedas. He held that the Vedas are author-less and eternally valid. For Madhva, liberation (moksha) is achievable only through the grace of God, attained via loving devotion (Bhakti), not merely through intellectual knowledge.

### Critique of Other Schools
Madhvacharya was a prolific critic of competing philosophies. He directed his most ardent criticism at Shankara's Advaita Vedanta, accusing it of teaching Buddhism under the guise of Vedanta. He reinterpreted Advaitic statements like "Tat Tvam Asi" ("That thou art") to emphasize difference rather than identity. He also disagreed with Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita, rejecting its "qualified non-dualism" in favor of a "qualitative and quantitative pluralism of souls." Unlike the other schools, Madhva posited that some souls are eternally destined for liberation, while others are destined for rebirth or damnation.

### Works and Legacy
Thirty-seven texts are attributed to Madhvacharya, including commentaries (bhasya) on the Principal Upanishads, the Bhagavad Gita, and the Brahma Sutras. His masterpiece is the Anuvyakhyana, a poetic supplement to his Brahma Sutra commentary. He established eight monasteries (mathas) in Udupi, which continue to follow a rotating pontiff system (Paryaya). His lineage of students, including Jayatirtha and Vyasatirtha, further developed Dvaita thought. The Hari-Dasas, a later group of devotees, spread his teachings through vernacular songs and bhakti.

## Terms
- **Dvaita Vedanta**: The school of Hindu philosophy founded by Madhvacharya, asserting eternal dualism between the soul and God.
- **Tattvavāda**: Madhva's name for his philosophy, meaning "arguments from a realist viewpoint."
- **Atman**: The individual soul or self.
- **Brahman**: The ultimate reality; in Madhva's view, identified exclusively with God Vishnu.
- **Sannyasi**: A Hindu monk who has renounced worldly life.
- **Bhakti**: Loving devotion to God, which Madhva considered the essential path to liberation.
- **Pancha-bheda**: The fivefold eternal difference between God, souls, and matter.
- **Pramana**: A valid means of knowledge; Madhva accepted three.
- **Moksha**: Liberation from the cycle of rebirth, achieved only through God's grace.
- **Matha**: A Hindu monastery; Madhva established eight in Udupi.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Date of Birth**: Sources conflict, placing his life either in the 1199–1278 or 1238–1317 CE period.
- **Authenticity of Scriptural Citations**: A major scholarly debate concerns Madhva's use of scriptural quotations. Critics like Appayya Dīkṣita and modern scholar Roque Mesquita argue that many passages he cited from Puranas and the Mahabharata are untraceable and may have been composed by Madhva himself under claimed divine inspiration. Dvaita scholars contest this, suggesting some sources exist in unconsulted manuscript traditions.
- **Influence of Christianity**: Early colonial Indologists like G.A. Grierson suggested Madhva's ideas were influenced by Christianity due to parallels in dualism, grace, and miracles. Modern scholarship rejects this, finding no evidence of contact or influence.
- **Problem of Evil**: Madhva's tripartite classification of souls (destined for liberation, rebirth, or damnation) is seen by some, like David Buchta, as an insufficient answer to the problem of evil, arguing an omnipotent God could change the system. Dvaita scholars counter that changing a soul's intrinsic nature would destroy its identity, and God allows independent operation to foster free will.
