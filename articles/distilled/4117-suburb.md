# Suburb

## Overview
A suburb is a predominantly residential area within a metropolitan region, typically located within commuting distance of a central city. Suburbs emerged on a large scale in the 19th and 20th centuries due to improved transportation, and their development has profoundly shaped urban geography, social demographics, and cultural life worldwide. Their growth is often linked to economic expansion, housing policies, and transportation infrastructure, though their characteristics and governance vary significantly by country.

## Timeline
- **c. 1350** — First recorded use of the word "suburb" in English.
- **1860s** — Opening of London's Metropolitan Railway catalyzes early suburban development.
- **1904** — Establishment of the Hampstead Garden Suburb in London.
- **1915** — The term "Metro-land" is coined to promote suburban living near London.
- **1917** — Tudor Walters Committee report influences post-WWI suburban housing standards.
- **1919** — Housing, Town Planning, &c. Act (Addison Act) enables large suburban housing estates in the UK.
- **1930s** — Over 4 million new suburban houses built in England during the "suburban revolution."
- **1946-1955** — Post-WWII housing boom in North America; average of 1.45 million new units built annually.
- **1950** — First year that more people in the U.S. lived in suburbs than elsewhere.
- **1956** — U.S. Highway Act funds 64,000 km of highways, facilitating suburban expansion.
- **1957-1960** — Number of U.S. shopping centers more than doubles from 940 to meet suburban demand.
- **1970-1980** — Black suburbanization in the U.S. grows by 2.6%.

## Body

### Origins and Early Development
The earliest suburbs appeared alongside the first urban settlements, with smaller villages growing around walled towns. The term was first used by the Roman statesman Cicero for villas on Rome's outskirts. Modern suburbs began in the late 18th century as wealthy middle classes moved to estates on the edges of industrializing English cities like London. This trend accelerated in the 19th century with rail transport, allowing commuters to live outside crowded city centers. The Metropolitan Railway in London actively developed and marketed suburban estates, creating the "Metro-land" concept.

### 20th-Century Expansion and Policy
Suburban growth was heavily influenced by the garden city movement and post-WWI housing reforms. In the UK, the Tudor Walters Committee report (1917) set standards for suburban construction, leading to the proliferation of semi-detached houses. In North America, the post-WWII economic boom caused a suburban population explosion. Federal policies like the G.I. Bill and FHA loans, along with mass-produced housing like Levittown, made homeownership accessible. However, practices like redlining and discriminatory zoning fostered racial segregation and "white flight," concentrating minorities in inner cities.

### Global Variations and Modern Trends
Suburban forms differ globally. In Australia and South Africa, "suburb" is a formal administrative term for city neighborhoods. In the UK, suburbs are residential areas outside the city centre, often former absorbed towns. In many developing nations, suburbs range from affluent planned communities to impoverished peripheries. In the U.S., suburbs are characterized by low density, single-family homes, car-dependent infrastructure, and commercial shopping malls. Since 2010, U.S. suburbs have become more racially diverse, while some city centers experience renewed growth.

## Terms
- **Suburb:** A predominantly residential area within a metropolitan area, within commuting distance of a central city.
- **Exurb:** An area beyond the suburbs with less population density than suburbs but more than rural areas.
- **White flight:** The large-scale migration of white people from racially mixed urban regions to more racially homogeneous suburban regions.
- **Redlining:** The discriminatory practice of refusing to insure mortgages in and near African-American neighborhoods.
- **Garden city movement:** An urban planning movement promoting self-contained communities surrounded by "greenbelts," combining town and country living.
- **Metro-land:** A term coined in 1915 to promote suburban areas served by London's Metropolitan Railway.
- **Bedroom community:** A suburb where most daytime business activity occurs elsewhere, and residents commute out for work.
- **Zoning laws:** Regulations that separate residential, commercial, and industrial areas into designated zones.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise definition of a suburb is disputed, especially in the U.S., where the federal government has no formal definition.
- Scholars debate the extent to which early suburbs were exclusively middle-class white enclaves versus including working-class and minority residents.
- The impact of suburbs on social equity is debated, with criticism focusing on their role in fostering racial segregation, car dependency, and homogeneity, while others highlight their role in providing homeownership and a desired lifestyle.
