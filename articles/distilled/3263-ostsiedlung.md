# Ostsiedlung

## Overview
Ostsiedlung ("East settlement") refers to the medieval migration of ethnic Germans and the Germanization of areas in Central and Eastern Europe populated by Slavic, Baltic, and Uralic peoples. This process, occurring primarily from the 11th to 14th centuries, profoundly reshaped the legal, cultural, linguistic, and economic landscape of the region from the Baltic Sea to the Carpathians, with effects lasting until the 20th century.

## Timeline
- **8th-9th c.** — Charlemagne creates border marches (Marken) in Eastern Francia.
- **843** — Carolingian Empire partitions; Louis the German inherits East Francia.
- **983** — Slavic revolt ends Holy Roman Empire control and Christianization east of the Elbe for ~200 years.
- **c. 1124** — First Flemish/Dutch settlers arrive in Holstein.
- **1147** — Wendish Crusade devastates Slavic territories, creating conditions for settlement.
- **1157** — Albert the Bear establishes the Margraviate of Brandenburg.
- **1207** — Teutonic State founded in Prussia.
- **1224** — Andrew II of Hungary signs charter defining rights of German settlers in Transylvania.
- **1300s** — Migration slows due to climate deterioration, agricultural crisis, and the Black Death.
- **1945** — Post-WWII expulsions reverse Ostsiedlung east of the Oder-Neisse line.

## Body

### Causes and Early Framework
The migration was driven by a massive population increase in Germany (from ~4 to 12 million between the 11th-13th centuries), a surplus of landless nobility, and the need for agricultural expansion. Local Slavic and Polish rulers, seeking economic development, actively invited German settlers by offering land, legal privileges, and tax exemptions. The process was organized by *Lokators* (land allocators) who recruited settlers and established new villages and towns under German law.

### Regional Settlement Patterns
Settlement occurred in distinct phases and regions. After the 983 Slavic revolt, major colonization began in the mid-12th century in Northeastern Germany (Mecklenburg, Pomerania, Brandenburg). In Bohemia, German settlers—traders, miners, and farmers—arrived from the late 12th century, settling the Sudetenland but not fully Germanizing the region. The Teutonic Order encouraged German settlement in Prussia and the Baltics from the early 13th century. In Hungary, German settlers (later called Transylvanian Saxons) were invited from the 11th century, playing key roles in mining and trade.

### Social, Economic, and Cultural Impact
Settlers introduced advanced agricultural techniques, including the three-field system and heavy mouldboard ploughs, which increased cultivated land. They founded planned towns with geometric layouts and granted them German city law (e.g., Lübeck, Magdeburg), fostering urbanization. New crafts, like hard-fired pottery and half-timbered architecture, spread. Christianization was renewed through parish churches and monasteries, often built on pagan sites. The influx created a mixed population, leading to mutual assimilation; for example, ~25% of German family names are of Slavic origin.

### Reversal and Legacy
The Ostsiedlung was reversed in the 20th century. After World War I, Germans in new Polish territories faced pressure. During World War II, the Nazis exploited the history for expansionist claims (Drang nach Osten) and initiated Generalplan Ost. After 1945, the Potsdam Conference sanctioned the expulsion of nearly all Germans from Eastern Europe east of the Oder-Neisse line, radically altering the region's ethnic composition. Only areas settled earliest (e.g., Mecklenburg, Brandenburg) remained part of Germany.

## Terms
- **Ostsiedlung**: The medieval migration and Germanization of Eastern Europe.
- **Marches (Marken)**: Border territories established by the Carolingian and Holy Roman Empires for defense and control.
- **Wends/Elbe Slavs**: Collective term for Slavic tribes living east of the Elbe and Saale rivers.
- **Lokator**: An agent who organized and recruited settlers for new colonies.
- **German Town Law**: Legal charters (e.g., Lübeck, Magdeburg) granting towns administrative autonomy, market rights, and specific judicial systems.
- **Three-field System**: A medieval agricultural method dividing land into three rotating fields for crop cultivation, increasing productivity.
- **Mouldboard Plough**: A heavy plough with a curved blade that turns soil, suitable for dense, clay-rich earth.
- **Transylvanian Saxons**: German settlers invited to Hungary (Transylvania) from the 11th century, known for mining and trade.
- **Drang nach Osten**: A 19th-century nationalist slogan ("Drive to the East") framing the Ostsiedlung as a precursor to German expansionism.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise demographic impact is debated: scholars question whether population growth in Eastern Europe was primarily due to migration or increased agricultural productivity and urbanization.
- The nature of Slavic-German interaction is contested; while often framed as colonization, evidence shows significant cooperation, intermarriage, and mutual assimilation in many regions.
- The end of the Ostsiedlung is not clearly defined; it is linked to the 14th-century crisis (climate change, Black Death) but may have continued in some areas into the 15th century.
