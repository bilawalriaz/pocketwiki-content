# Abraham

## Overview

Abraham (originally Abram) is a foundational legendary figure revered in the Abrahamic religions—Judaism, Christianity, and Islam—as well as in the Baháʼí Faith and Druze faith. He is regarded as the common forefather of both the Arab people through his son Ishmael and the Jewish people through his son Isaac. In Judaism, he is the first Hebrew patriarch and ethnic ancestor of the Jewish people; in Christianity, the forebear of Jesus and spiritual ancestor of all believers; in Islam, a link in the chain of prophets from Adam to Muhammad. His story, told in the Book of Genesis, centers on God's promise of progeny and land, which shapes his life's crises and covenants. Most scholars view the patriarchal age as a late literary construct, not a historical era, with traditions developed orally over centuries before being written into Genesis during the Persian period.

## Timeline

- **c. 2nd millennium BCE** — Traditional "patriarchal age" once proposed by scholars; later rejected as historical

- **Genesis 13** — Abram and Lot separate; Lot chooses the Jordan plain, Abram settles in Hebron
- **Genesis 14** — Abram rescues Lot from Elamite king Chedorlaomer; meets Melchizedek
- **Genesis 15** — Covenant of the pieces; God promises Abram a son and the land
- **Genesis 16** — Hagar bears Ishmael to Abram, age 86
- **Genesis 17** — God renames Abram "Abraham" and Sarai "Sarah"; covenant of circumcision
- **Genesis 21** — Isaac born to Abraham, age 100; Ishmael and Hagar sent away
- **Genesis 22** — Binding of Isaac; Abraham's obedience earns renewed promises
- **Genesis 25** — Abraham dies at 175, buried in the Cave of the Patriarchs beside Sarah

## Body

### The Abraham cycle: structure and calling

The Abraham cycle (Genesis 11:27–25:11) is not a unified plot but a series of loosely linked episodes using the "obstacle story" device, where God's promise of progeny and land is threatened by successive crises. The narrative programs are set in Genesis 11:27–31 (Sarah's sterility) and Genesis 12:1–3 (Abraham's call to leave his birthplace). Terah, ninth from Noah, fathered Abram, Nahor, and Haran; Haran's son Lot was Abram's nephew. Abram married Sarai, but they were childless. After Terah died in Haran at 205, God told Abram, then 75, to leave for Canaan, promising to make him a great nation. Some exegetes, like Nahmanides, argue Abram was actually born in Haran and later moved to Ur.

### Crises and covenants

A famine drove Abram to Egypt, where he told Sarai to claim she was his sister; Pharaoh took her, but God afflicted his household with plagues, and Pharaoh expelled them. After returning, Abram and Lot's herdsmen conflicted, so Lot chose the well-watered Jordan plain toward Sodom, while Abram settled in Hebron. When Elamite forces captured Lot, Abram assembled 318 trained servants, launched a night raid at Dan, and defeated King Chedorlaomer at Hobah. On return, Melchizedek, king of Salem and priest of El Elyon, blessed Abram, who gave him a tenth of everything. God then made a covenant, promising Abram a son and the land of ten named peoples, and foretelling bondage in Egypt. Sarai, childless after ten years, gave her slave Hagar to Abram; Hagar conceived, despised Sarai, fled, and was told by an angel to return and name her son Ishmael—"a wild ass of a man." Abram was 86.

### Renaming and the covenant of circumcision

When Abram was 99, God appeared, commanding him to be "blameless," and renamed him Abraham—"father of a multitude"—and Sarai "Sarah," promising a son. Abraham laughed at the prospect of a child at age 100 with Sarah at 90. He immediately circumcised his entire household, including himself and Ishmael (13). Three visitors at Mamre announced Sarah would bear a son; she laughed, and the visitor asked why, noting nothing is too hard for God. Abraham then pleaded with God to spare Sodom if ten righteous were found; God agreed. The visitors went to Sodom, where Lot protected them from a mob, offering his virgin daughters instead; the city's wickedness was confirmed, and it was destroyed. Abraham then moved to Gerar, again claiming Sarah was his sister; King Abimelech took her, but God warned him in a dream. Abimelech returned Sarah, gave gifts and silver, and later made agreements with Abraham over honest dealings and a well, named Beersheba.

### Isaac, Ishmael, and the binding

Sarah bore Isaac when Abraham was 100, and he was circumcised at eight days. At Isaac's weaning feast, Sarah saw Ishmael mocking and demanded he and Hagar be sent away; God reassured Abraham that Isaac would carry the seed, but Ishmael would become a nation. Hagar and Ishmael wandered in Beersheba until an angel showed a well; Ishmael became an archer in Paran. God then commanded Abraham to sacrifice Isaac in Moriah; Abraham traveled three days, and as he was about to kill his son, an angel stopped him, providing a ram caught in a thicket. The place was named Jehovah-jireh, and Abraham received renewed promises. Sarah died, and Abraham purchased the Cave of the Patriarchs at Hebron for her burial. He later married Keturah, with whom he had six sons, but Isaac received "all Abraham's goods." Abraham died at 175, buried by Isaac and Ishmael.

### Historicity and origins of the narrative

In the early and mid-20th century, scholars like William F. Albright and John Bright believed the patriarchs were real or composite figures of the 2nd millennium BCE. In the 1970s, Thomas L. Thompson's *The Historicity of the Patriarchal Narratives* (1974) and John Van Seters' *Abraham in History and Tradition* (1975) argued that the texts reflect first-millennium conditions and Iron Age creations, shifting scholarship to reject patriarchal historicity. By the 21st century, archaeologists stopped seeking a historical Abraham. The story likely had a substantial oral prehistory; Abraham's name is ancient, with its original meaning likely "father is exalted," while "father of a multitude" is a folk etymology. Most scholars date the written stage to the Persian period (c. 520–320 BCE), with hypotheses including Persian Imperial authorization or criteria for post-Exilic community membership. Ezekiel and Isaiah testify to tension over land claims, with Abraham serving to support the claims of Judah's great landowners against returning exiles. Some scholars propose an Amorite origin, noting parallels with 2nd-millennium Amorite culture and the name Abram attested at Mari; others propose a Canaanite origin, citing a possible 10th-century BCE reference and a 13th-century BCE tribe called Raham.

### Religious traditions

In Judaism, Abraham is *Avraham Avinu*, "our father Abraham," the first Jew, whose story is read in the weekly Torah reading portions, predominantly in the parashot: Lech-Lecha, Vayeira, Chayei Sarah, and Toledot. Rabbinic tradition gives his mother's name as Amatlai bat Karnevo and says he worked in Terah's idol shop, was saved from Nimrod's fiery furnace, and taught hospitality and knowledge of God. In Mandaeism, Abraham is mentioned in the Right Ginza as the patriarch of the Jewish people, considered originally a Mandaean priest, but circumcision is forbidden as bodily mutilation. In Christianity, Paul taught that Abraham's faith, preceding the Mosaic law, made him the prototype of all believers; Augustine, Ambrose, and Luther emphasized him as the spiritual father of Christians. The Roman Catholic Church calls Abraham "our father in Faith" in the Eucharistic prayer of the Roman Canon. He is commemorated on 20 August by the Maronite Church, 28 August in the Coptic Church and the Assyrian Church of the East, and on 9 October by the Roman Catholic Church and the Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod. The Eastern Orthodox Church commemorates him as the "Righteous Forefather Abraham," with two feast days in its liturgical calendar. He is the patron saint of those in the hospitality industry. A popular hymn sung in many English-speaking Sunday Schools by children is known as "Father Abraham." In Islam, Abraham (ʾIbrāhīm) is mentioned in 35 chapters of the Quran, more often than any other biblical personage apart from Moses. He is called both a *hanif* (monotheist) and *muslim* (one who submits), the archetype of the perfect Muslim and reformer of the Kaaba, referred to as *Khalīlullāh*, "Friend of God." Islamic tradition considers Abraham the first "pioneer of Islam" (which is also called *millat ʾIbrāhīm*, the "religion of Abraham"). The Druze regard him as the third spokesman (*natiq*) after Adam and Noah, and he is among the seven prophets who appeared in different periods of history according to the Druze faith. Baháʼís consider him a Manifestation of God and founder of monotheism; ʻAbdu'l-Bahá states he was born in Mesopotamia and opposed his polytheistic people, with only Lot supporting him. Bahá'u'lláh states that the language which Abraham spoke, when "he crossed the Jordan," is Hebrew ('Ibrání), so "the language of the crossing."

### Artistic depictions

Paintings on Abraham focus on a few incidents: the sacrifice of Isaac, meeting Melchizedek, entertaining the three angels, and Hagar in the desert. Artists inspired by his life include Albrecht Dürer, Caravaggio, Rembrandt (at least seven works), Rubens, Marc Chagall (at least five), Gustave Doré (six), and James Tissot (over twenty). The Sarcophagus of Junius Bassus (d. 359) depicts Abraham about to sacrifice Isaac and is described as "probably the single most famous piece of early Christian relief sculpture." George Segal's 1987 sculpture *Abraham's Farewell to Ishmael* depicts the family's emotional dilemma. In Christian iconography, the sacrifice of Isaac was a typological foreshadowing of Christ's crucifixion from the 3rd century; some early writers interpreted the three visitors as the Trinity, as in Andrei Rublev's *Trinity*. In literature, Søren Kierkegaard's *Fear and Trembling* (1843) explored Abraham's anxiety; W. G. Hardy's *Father Abraham* (1935) fictionalized his life. In music, Marc-Antoine Charpentier composed *Sacrificium Abrahae* (1681), and Steve Reich's opera *The Cave* (1994) is based on Abraham's story.

## Terms

- **Abrahamic religions**: Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, which revere Abraham as a foundational figure.
- **Covenant**: A divine promise or agreement, central to Abraham's story, involving progeny and land.
- **Circumcision**: The physical sign of God's covenant with Abraham, commanded in Genesis 17.
- **Hanif**: An Islamic term for a monotheist, applied to Abraham.
- **Khalīlullāh**: "Friend of God," an Islamic title for Abraham.
- **Manifestation of God**: A Baháʼí term for a prophet or founder of a religion, applied to Abraham.
- **Natiq**: A Druze term for a spokesman prophet; Abraham is the third after Adam and Noah.
- **Obstacle story**: A literary device where a promised outcome is threatened by successive crises.
- **Patriarchal age**: The proposed 2nd-millennium BCE era of the patriarchs, now rejected by most scholars.
- **Typology**: Christian interpretation where Old Testament events prefigure New Testament ones, as Isaac's sacrifice prefigures Christ's.

## Debates and open questions

Scholars debate whether Abraham's story retains any historical memory: some argue for an Amorite origin based on parallels with 2nd-millennium Mari texts and the name Abram, while others propose a Canaanite origin, citing a possible 10th-century BCE reference and a 13th-century BCE tribe named Raham. The mechanisms by which oral traditions became written text in the Persian period remain unknown, with competing hypotheses of Persian Imperial authorization versus community-defining criteria. The original meaning of Abraham's name is uncertain, with "father is exalted" proposed as more likely than the biblical folk etymology "father of a multitude."
