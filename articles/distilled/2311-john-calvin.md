# John Calvin

## Overview
John Calvin (1509–1564) was a French theologian, pastor, and reformer in Geneva whose teachings became the foundation of Calvinism, a major branch of Protestantism. His systematic theology, particularly the doctrines of predestination and God's absolute sovereignty, profoundly shaped the Reformed churches and influenced global Christian thought.

## Timeline
- **10 July 1509** — Born Jehan Cauvin in Noyon, France.
- **c. 1529–1530** — Breaks from the Roman Catholic Church; conversion likely occurs.
- **1534** — Flees France during the Affair of the Placards.
- **1536** — Publishes first edition of *Institutes of the Christian Religion*; recruited by William Farel to Geneva.
- **1538** — Expelled from Geneva with Farel; moves to Strasbourg.
- **1541** — Returns to Geneva to lead the Reformation.
- **1541** — Geneva's council passes the Ecclesiastical Ordinances, establishing church government.
- **1553** — Michael Servetus is tried and executed for heresy in Geneva.
- **1555** — Calvin's political opponents (the "libertines") are defeated in elections.
- **1559** — Opens the Collège and Académie in Geneva (later University of Geneva).
- **1559** — Publishes final, expanded edition of the *Institutes*.
- **27 May 1564** — Dies in Geneva.

## Body

### Early Life and Conversion
Calvin was trained as a humanist lawyer, studying at Orléans and Bourges. He broke from the Catholic Church around 1530, an event he described both as a sudden divine conversion and a process of inner turmoil. After implicating him in the 1533 reform speech of Nicolas Cop, French authorities forced Calvin into hiding. He fled France in 1534 following violent anti-Protestant backlash to the Affair of the Placards.

### First Geneva Period and Strasbourg
In 1536, Calvin published the first *Institutes* and was recruited by William Farel to help reform Geneva. Their strict proposals, including mandatory confession of faith, clashed with the city council. After a dispute over church ceremonies with the city of Bern, they were expelled in 1538. Calvin then served as minister to French refugees in Strasbourg (1538–1541), where he refined his theology, married Idelette de Bure, and produced influential biblical commentaries.

### Reform in Geneva
Recalled to Geneva in 1541, Calvin implemented the Ecclesiastical Ordinances, which defined four ministerial offices (pastors, doctors, elders, deacons) and created the Consistory, an ecclesiastical court. He authored liturgical books and a catechism, and preached over two thousand sermons. His authority was challenged by powerful local families known as the "libertines," who opposed the Consistory's moral discipline.

### The Servetus Affair and Triumph over Opposition
Calvin's fortunes turned with the 1553 arrival of Michael Servetus, a fugitive who denied the Trinity. Calvin had previously alerted the Inquisition about Servetus's writings. After Servetus was arrested in Geneva, Calvin's secretary compiled accusations against him. The city council, seeking external opinions, condemned Servetus and burned him at the stake. This event bolstered Calvin's standing as a defender of orthodoxy. The final defeat of the libertines came in 1555 when pro-Calvin candidates won city elections, ending organized opposition.

### Theology and Legacy
Calvin's theology, systematized in the *Institutes*, emphasized human depravity, salvation by grace alone through faith, and God's sovereign predestination of the elect. He defined the true church as the body of believers under Christ's headship, rejecting papal authority. He accepted only two sacraments: baptism and the Lord's Supper. His writings, including commentaries on most of the Bible, shaped Reformed confessions worldwide. He founded Geneva's academy, which trained ministers who spread Calvinism to France, the Netherlands, Scotland, England, Hungary, and beyond.

## Terms
- **Calvinism**: The system of Christian theology and church polity developed by John Calvin, emphasizing God's sovereignty and predestination.
- **Predestination**: The doctrine that God has eternally chosen those whom he intends to save (the elect) and those whom he intends to damn.
- **Consistory**: The ecclesiastical court in Geneva composed of ministers and elders, tasked with church discipline.
- **Ecclesiastical Ordinances**: The 1541 laws passed in Geneva that established the structure and ministry of the Reformed church.
- **Institutes of the Christian Religion**: Calvin's magnum opus, a systematic exposition of Protestant theology first published in 1536 and expanded until 1559.
- **Libertines**: The political and religious opposition in Geneva who resisted the Consistory's moral authority.
- **Justification by faith**: The Protestant doctrine that sinners are declared righteous by God through faith in Christ, not by works.
- **Sacrament**: An earthly sign associated with a divine promise; Calvin recognized only baptism and the Lord's Supper.
- **Transubstantiation**: The Catholic doctrine that the bread and wine of the Eucharist become the actual body and blood of Christ, which Calvin rejected.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Theocracy in Geneva**: Historians debate the extent to which Geneva under Calvin was a theocracy, given the separation of church and state in his theology versus the clerics' significant political power.
- **Calvin's view of Jews**: Scholars disagree on whether Calvin was less antisemitic than other reformers or firmly within the antisemitic camp of his era, noting his distinction between biblical and contemporary Jews.
- **Interpretation of his conversion**: Calvin's own accounts of his conversion differ—one describes a sudden change, the other a prolonged struggle—leading to scholarly debate about its nature and timing.
- **Role in Servetus's execution**: While Calvin and ministers sought a less brutal execution (beheading), the council insisted on burning, raising questions about Calvin's direct responsibility versus the council's legal authority.
