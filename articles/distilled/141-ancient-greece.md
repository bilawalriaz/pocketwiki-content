# Ancient Greece

## Overview

Ancient Greece (Ancient Greek: Ἑλλάς, Hellás) was a civilization existing from the Greek Dark Ages (12th–9th centuries BC) to the end of classical antiquity (c. 600 AD), comprising a loose collection of culturally and linguistically related city-states and regions centered in the northeastern Mediterranean. Prior to the Roman period, the area was officially unified only once, from 338 to 323 BC under the Kingdom of Macedon. Classical Greek culture, especially philosophy, had a powerful influence on Ancient Rome, which propagated it throughout the Mediterranean and Europe; for this reason, Classical Greece is generally considered the cradle of Western civilization.

- **490 BC** — Athenian victory over Persia at Battle of Marathon
- **480–479 BC** — Second Persian invasion; Greek victories at Salamis and Plataea
- **431–404 BC** — Peloponnesian War between Athens and Sparta; Athens surrenders

- **334–323 BC** — Alexander the Great conquers the Achaemenid Empire; dies 323 BC

- **146 BC–330 AD** — Roman Greece; province of Achaea established 27 BC
- **529 AD** — Closure of the Academy of Athens by Justinian I; end of Late Antiquity

## Body

### Chronology and historiography

Classical antiquity in Greece is commonly considered to have begun in the 8th century BC (around the time of Homer's earliest recorded poetry) and ended in the 6th century AD. The Archaic period (c. 8th century BC) followed the Dark Ages and led to the Classical period (from the Persian invasion of 480 BC to Alexander's death in 323 BC), characterized by a "classical" style considered exemplary by later observers, most famously the Parthenon. The Hellenistic period (323–146 BC) saw Greek culture expand into the Near East. Roman Greece is usually counted from the Battle of Corinth (146 BC) to Byzantium's establishment as Roman capital (330 AD). Late Antiquity refers to Christianization in the later 4th to early 6th centuries AD, consummated by the closure of the Academy of Athens in 529.

Ancient Greece is unique as the first period attested directly in comprehensive narrative historiography. Herodotus, the "father of history," wrote his Histories between the 450s and 420s BC, reaching about a century into the past; his accuracy is debated. He was succeeded by Thucydides, Xenophon, Demosthenes, Plato, and Aristotle, most of whom were Athenian or pro-Athenian, so far more is known about Athens than other cities. Their scope is limited by a focus on political, military, and diplomatic history, ignoring economic and social history.

### Archaic period (c. 800–500 BC)

The polis (city-state) became the most important political unit. The absence of powerful states after the Mycenaean collapse, and Greece's mountainous geography, encouraged small independent city-states. Tyrants rose to power, most famously at Corinth from 657 BC. Greek colonies were founded around the Mediterranean, with Euboean settlements at Al-Mina in the east as early as 800 BC and Ischia in the west by 775. Contact with non-Greek peoples inspired developments in art, architecture, coinage, and the Greek alphabet.

Athens developed democracy gradually: the right of all citizen men to attend the assembly existed by the 7th century; Draco established a law code in 621; Solon enacted reforms in 594 balancing rich and poor; Pisistratus established a tyranny in the mid-6th century; after his son Hippias was overthrown, Cleisthenes carried out further democratizing reforms. Sparta developed a system with two kings, a council of elders, and five ephors, traditionally established by the legendary lawgiver Lycurgus. Through the Messenian Wars, Sparta subjugated Messenia, enserfing the population. In the 6th century, city-states began forming formal alliances, including Sparta's Peloponnesian League with Corinth, Elis, and Megara.

### Classical Greece (480–323 BC)

In 499 BC, Ionian city-states under Persian rule rebelled, aided by Athens and Eretria; the revolt failed by 494. Darius retaliated in 490 but was defeated at Marathon. Ten years later, Xerxes launched a second invasion; a coalition of 31 Greek city-states resisted. At Thermopylae (480 BC), a small rearguard led by three hundred Spartans held a crucial pass; at the same time, Gelon of Syracuse defeated a Carthaginian invasion at Himera. The Persians were defeated at sea at Salamis and on land at Plataea (479 BC). The Delian League, led by Athens from 477, gradually transformed into an Athenian empire. Athens ended campaigns against Persia in 450 after defeats in Egypt (454) and the death of Cimon on Cyprus (450).

Conflict grew between Athens and Sparta. The Thirty Years' Peace (446/445) ended the first phase, but the Peloponnesian War began in 431 BC. After Athenian victories at Pylos and Sphakteria, Sparta sued for peace, but Athens rejected the proposal; a peace was negotiated in 421 by Nicias. The peace failed: Athens' Sicilian expedition ended in disaster at Syracuse (415–413), and after the Spartan victory at Aegospotami (405), Athens surrendered, joining the Peloponnesian League. Sparta installed the Thirty Tyrants in Athens, overthrown after a year. In the 4th century, Sparta, then Thebes (victorious at Leuctra in 371, leading to Messenia's independence), and then Macedon under Philip II filled the power vacuum. Philip defeated a Greek alliance at Chaeronea (338 BC) and formed the League of Corinth. After Philip's murder (336 BC), Alexander the Great conquered Persia, defeating Darius III at Issus (333) and Gaugamela (331), proclaiming himself king of Asia; he died in 323 BC.

### Hellenistic and Roman Greece

After Alexander's death, his generals took power: Antigonus I and his son Demetrius in Macedonia, Ptolemy in Egypt, and Seleucus I in Syria and the former Persian empire. Antigonus was killed at Ipsus (301 BC). The Seleucid kingdom gave territory to the Indian king Chandragupta Maurya in exchange for war elephants and later lost parts of Persia to the Parthian Empire. The great capitals were Alexandria and Antioch. Many Greeks emigrated to new Hellenistic cities as far as Afghanistan and Pakistan. Greek city-states formed the Achaean and Aetolian Leagues, often at war. The Antigonid Kingdom fought Rome in the Macedonian Wars, ending with absorption by 149 BC; the Seleucid Empire survived until 64 BC; the Ptolemaic Kingdom fell to Rome in 30 BC. The Achaean League was defeated in 146 BC, ending Greek independence. Greece became the Roman province of Achaea in 27 BC; the Greek language served as a lingua franca in the East and in Italy.

### Politics, society, and economy

Ancient Greece consisted of hundreds of relatively independent poleis, a situation unlike contemporary tribal or kingdom-based societies. The Greeks shared religion, culture, and language and were aware of tribal origins, but independence was fiercely defended. Smaller poleis grouped into leagues, later dominated by Athens, Sparta, or Thebes. Initially petty kingdoms, most city-states became aristocratic oligarchies by the Archaic period. Athens founded the world's first democracy after its tyranny ended; the assembly (Ecclesia) became the de jure government, though non-citizens (metics, slaves) had no political rights. Sparta was ruled by two hereditary kings (a diarchy) from the Agiads and Eurypontids, checked by the Gerousia and Ephors.

Only free, land-owning, native-born men could be full citizens. In Athens, the population was divided into four wealth-based classes. Women primarily performed domestic tasks. Slaves had no political rights; by the 5th century BC they made up one-third of the population in some city-states, and 40–80% of Classical Athens. Most families owned slaves; owners could not beat or kill them. Freedmen became metics, not citizens. Sparta's helots were Messenians enslaved en masse; they won freedom in 370/369 BC after Epaminondas liberated Messenia. Education was private except in Sparta; boys learned reading, writing, music, and athletics, and wealthy families used a paidagogos (household slave) to accompany boys. At its economic height in the 5th–4th centuries BC, Classical Greece was perhaps the most prosperous society in the ancient world, with urban wages reaching 7–12 kg of wheat daily for unskilled workers in Athens.

### Culture

Ancient Greek philosophy focused on reason and inquiry, influencing modern philosophy and science. The pre-Socratics attempted naturalistic descriptions of the world; Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle laid the basis of Western philosophy. Hellenistic philosophy included cynicism (Antisthenes), stoicism (Zeno of Citium), and Neoplatonism (Plotinus). The earliest Greek literature was oral poetry; Homer was the earliest known poet, while Archilochus (mid-7th century BC) was the first to compose in writing. Tragedy developed at the end of the archaic period; comedy became official at the City Dionysia in 486 BC. Greek prose emerged in the 6th century BC and matured in the classical era. The Hellenistic period moved the literary center to Alexandria, where the Library of Alexandria aimed to collect all known Greek authors. Music was ever-present and primarily vocal, accompanied by stringed instruments (lyres, harps) and wind instruments (notably the aulos).

Greek mathematics contributed geometry, formal proof, and number theory; Pythagoras, Euclid, and Archimedes are still taught today. The Greeks developed astronomy as a branch of mathematics: Eudoxus and Callippus created geometrical models of planetary motion; Heraclides Ponticus proposed Earth's rotation; Aristarchus of Samos first suggested a heliocentric system; Eratosthenes estimated Earth's circumference; Hipparchus measured precession and compiled the first star catalog. The Antikythera mechanism (c. 80 BC), a device for calculating planetary movements, used a differential gear previously believed invented in the 16th century. Hippocrates, the "father of medicine," founded the Hippocratic school, establishing medicine as a profession distinct from philosophy and theurgy. Greek art and architecture, particularly sculpture, influenced the Roman Empire, Greco-Buddhist art, and later European art through the Renaissance and into the 19th century. Religion was central; the Greeks were polytheistic, and a pantheon of twelve Olympians began developing by the 6th century BC. The most important religious act was animal sacrifice, accompanied by public prayer and hymns.

## Terms

- **Polis**: Greek city-state, the most important unit of political organization
- **Tyrant**: A ruler who seized control, often with a populist agenda (not the modern sense of repressive autocracy)
- **Delian League**: Alliance led by Athens that transformed into an Athenian empire
- **Helots**: Messenians enslaved en masse by Sparta, assigned to families to raise food and do chores
- **Metics**: Foreigners living in Athens without political rights
- **Ephors**: Spartan magistrates appointed to watch over the kings
- **Trireme**: Greek warship powered by 170 oarsmen seated in 3 rows per side
- **Aulos**: Reed wind instrument, the most important wind instrument in Greek music
- **Paideia**: The education system of the wealthy ancient Greeks
- **Hellenistic period**: Era from Alexander's death (323 BC) to Cleopatra's death, marked by successor kingdoms

## Debates and open questions

- The accuracy of Herodotus' Histories is debated.
- It is unclear exactly how Greek city-states transitioned from petty kingdoms to aristocratic oligarchies.
- The population estimate for Greek settlement expansion (800–400 BC) is given as "according to one estimate," roughly ten-fold from 800,000 to 7½–10 million.
- Economic historians debate whether Classical Greece was one of the most advanced pre-industrial economies; estimates drawn from urban incomes may overstate overall living standards given slavery's role.
