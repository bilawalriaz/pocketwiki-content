# Eduard Bernstein

## Overview
Eduard Bernstein (1850–1932) was a German social democratic politician and socialist theorist whose reformist challenge to orthodox Marxism—known as evolutionary socialism or revisionism—fundamentally reshaped the international socialist movement. Born into a secular Jewish family in Berlin, Bernstein became active in socialist politics during the 1870s, later spending over two decades in exile in Switzerland and London under Bismarck’s Anti-Socialist Laws. There, his exposure to the stability of late Victorian capitalism and the reformist Fabian Society led him to reject Marx’s predictions of capitalism’s inevitable collapse, advocating instead for gradual, democratic change. His ideas sparked fierce debates within the SPD and broader socialist movement, influencing the development of modern social democracy.

- **1901**: Returns to Germany; elected to Reichstag in 1902.

- **1917**: Co-founds anti-war Independent Social Democratic Party (USPD).
- **1918**: Rejoins SPD during German Revolution; serves in provisional government.
- **1932**: Dies in Berlin weeks before Nazi seizure of power.

## Body

### Early Life and Political Beginnings
Bernstein was born in 1850 into a secular, lower-middle-class Jewish family in Berlin. His father was a railway engine driver, and the family observed Christmas as a cultural rather than religious holiday. Financial constraints prevented him from completing Gymnasium, leading to a banking apprenticeship. His political consciousness emerged during the Franco-Prussian War, influenced by socialist leaders like August Bebel and Wilhelm Liebknecht. In 1872, he joined the SDAP ("Eisenachers"), quickly becoming a prominent speaker and organizer. The 1875 Gotha Congress unified the Eisenachers with the Lassalleans to form the SPD, though Bernstein later admitted his early understanding of Marxism was limited.

### Exile (1878–1901)
Following Bismarck’s Anti-Socialist Laws, Bernstein moved to Zurich in 1878 as private secretary to socialist sympathizer Karl Höchberg. There, he encountered Engels’s *Anti-Dühring*, which he credited with converting him to Marxism. He became involved with the SPD’s illegal newspaper *Der Sozialdemokrat*, eventually becoming its editor in 1881 under Bebel’s appointment. A controversial article in 1879 briefly strained relations with Marx and Engels, but Bernstein reconciled with them during a 1880 London visit, forming a lasting bond with Engels. In 1888, Swiss expulsion forced his move to London, where he remained for thirteen years. During this period, he edited the paper until German laws relaxed in 1890, then worked as a freelance writer and correspondent for *Vorwärts* and *Die Neue Zeit*. His intellectual growth accelerated through engagement with British liberalism, evolutionary theory, and the Fabian Society, all shaping his skepticism toward revolutionary Marxism.

### Evolutionary Socialism
Bernstein’s revisionism crystallized in a series of articles titled *Probleme des Sozialismus* (1896–1898), culminating in *Die Voraussetzungen des Sozialismus und die Aufgaben der Sozialdemokratie* (1899). He rejected the Hegelian dialectic as a "snare," favoring Kantian ethics and Darwinian evolution as frameworks for understanding societal change. Economically, he disputed Marx’s predictions: capital concentration was not eliminating small enterprises; the middle class was evolving rather than disappearing; and the proletariat’s condition was improving, not deteriorating. Politically, he argued that since capitalism was stabilizing and democracy expanding, socialism should pursue gradual reform through parliamentary means. His famous dictum—"the goal is nothing, the movement everything"—emphasized process over dogma. He opposed the dictatorship of the proletariat and advocated for trade unions and cooperatives as vehicles for democratic transformation.

### International Relations and War
Bernstein increasingly focused on international law, peace, and self-determination. He promoted a "politics of peoples" over imperialist statecraft, supporting open diplomacy, free trade, and a "league of peoples." At the 1907 Stuttgart Congress, he defended colonial policy as potentially civilizing under socialist governance. During WWI, he initially supported war credits but later opposed the conflict, viewing it as imperialist. In 1915, he co-authored a manifesto condemning the war and helped found the USPD in 1917. His pacifism and Anglophilia distinguished him from pro-war SPD right-wingers.

### Return to Germany and Political Career
Returning to Germany in 1901, Bernstein became a leading voice in the SPD’s reformist faction. Elected to the Reichstag in 1902, he specialized in taxation, trade, and constitutional law. Despite official condemnation of revisionism at party congresses (Hanover 1899, Dresden 1903), the SPD’s practical policies remained reformist. Bernstein advocated alliances with progressive bourgeois parties to advance franchise reform. During the 1918 revolution, he served in the Treasury under the Council of the People's Deputies and symbolically rejoined the SPD while still in the USPD, promoting unity. He formally left the USPD in 1919 after it banned dual membership.

### Weimar Republic and Final Years
In the Weimar era, Bernstein defended parliamentary democracy and opposed both Bolshevism and right-wing reaction. He supported Germany’s acceptance of the Treaty of Versailles and opposed nationalist sentiment within the SPD. His historical works, including *Die deutsche Revolution* (1921), analyzed the revolution’s failures. He retired from the Reichstag in 1928, issuing a final manifesto warning against threats from conservatives and Communists. He died in 1932, aged 82, just weeks before Hitler’s appointment as Chancellor.

## Terms
- **Evolutionary Socialism**: Bernstein’s theory that socialism should develop gradually through democratic reform rather than revolutionary upheaval.
- **Revisionism**: The ideological current within Marxism that questions orthodox predictions about capitalism’s collapse and advocates adaptive strategies.
- **Anti-Socialist Laws**: Bismarck’s legislation (1878–1890) banning socialist organizations, meetings, and publications in Germany.
- **Fabian Society**: A British socialist group advocating gradual, non-revolutionary reform, influential in shaping Bernstein’s thought.
- **Erfurt Program**: The SPD’s 1891 Marxist program, officially maintained despite Bernstein’s critiques.
- **Dictatorship of the Proletariat**: Marxist concept of transitional rule by the working class; Bernstein rejected it as barbaric.
- **USPD (Independent Social Democratic Party)**: Anti-war split from the SPD formed in 1917, later reunited with SPD in 1921–1922.
- **Gotha Program**: The 1875 foundational document of the unified SPD, blending Marxist and Lassallean elements.

## Debates and Open Questions
Bernstein’s revisionism ignited enduring debates about the nature of socialist strategy. Orthodox Marxists like Karl Kautsky and Rosa Luxemburg accused him of abandoning revolutionary principles for bourgeois reform. Luxemburg’s *Social Reform or Revolution?* directly challenged his emphasis on gradualism. Some scholars argue Bernstein’s views were shaped more by his British experience than rigorous analysis, while others see him as a principled advocate for democratic ethics over deterministic theory. His nuanced stance on colonialism—condemning exploitation yet endorsing civilizing missions—remains contested. Additionally, his role in the SPD’s wartime split and post-war reunification efforts reflect tensions between idealism and pragmatism that continue to influence social democratic thought. The extent to which his revisionism prefigured later social democratic shifts, such as the SPD’s 1959 Godesberg Program, remains a subject of scholarly discussion.
