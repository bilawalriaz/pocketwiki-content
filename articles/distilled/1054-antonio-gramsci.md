# Antonio Gramsci

## Overview

Antonio Gramsci (1891–1937) was an Italian Marxist philosopher, journalist, and politician who co-founded the Italian Communist Party and became one of the most influential thinkers in 20th-century Western Marxism. His *Prison Notebooks*, written during 11 years of fascist imprisonment, introduced the concept of cultural hegemony—the idea that ruling classes maintain power not just through force but through ideology and consent. His work reshaped Marxist theory by emphasizing culture, education, and civil society as central to political struggle, influencing fields from critical theory to postcolonial studies.

## Timeline

- **1891** — Born in Ales, Sardinia, to a family of mixed Sardinian and Arbëreshë (Italo-Albanian) descent
- **1911** — Wins scholarship to University of Turin; studies literature and linguistics
- **1913** — Joins the Italian Socialist Party (PSI)
- **1919** — Co-founds the revolutionary socialist journal *L'Ordine Nuovo*
- **1921** — Helps establish the Communist Party of Italy (PCd'I)
- **1926** — Arrested by the Fascist regime and imprisoned; begins writing the *Prison Notebooks*
- **1937** — Dies in prison on April 27, aged 46

## Body

### Early Life and Intellectual Formation

Gramsci was born into a family marked by financial instability and regional marginality. His father, Francesco, was of Arbëreshë descent, and his mother came from a Sardinian landowning family. Growing up in Sardinia, Gramsci witnessed the economic neglect and cultural subordination of the island by northern Italy, an experience that deeply shaped his later critique of nationalism and class domination. His early exposure to Sardinian autonomism and peasant grievances laid the groundwork for his interest in subaltern voices and grassroots resistance.

### Political Activism and the Birth of Italian Communism

In Turin, Gramsci engaged with socialist circles and was influenced by thinkers like Benedetto Croce and Antonio Labriola, whose “philosophy of praxis” emphasized action over abstract theory. He became a prominent journalist and organizer, advocating for workers’ councils as organs of proletarian self-governance. His alignment with Leninist principles led to the founding of the Communist Party of Italy in 1921. However, tensions with Amadeo Bordiga over party discipline and strategy highlighted Gramsci’s more flexible, culturally attuned approach to revolution.

### Imprisonment and the Prison Notebooks

Gramsci’s arrest in 1926 under Mussolini’s emergency laws marked the beginning of over a decade of incarceration. Despite deteriorating health, he produced over 30 notebooks and 3,000 pages of analysis. These writings, smuggled out and later published, became foundational texts in Marxist theory. They explored themes such as the role of civil society, the nature of the state, and the importance of counter-hegemonic struggle. His imprisonment ended only months before his death, which was hastened by poor conditions and inadequate medical care.

### Cultural Hegemony and the State

Gramsci’s most enduring contribution is his theory of cultural hegemony. He argued that the bourgeoisie secures dominance not merely through coercion but by shaping the beliefs, values, and institutions of society so thoroughly that subordinated classes accept the existing order as natural. This process occurs primarily in civil society—through schools, churches, media, and other non-state institutions. Gramsci distinguished between political society (the realm of force) and civil society (the realm of consent), arguing that revolutionary change requires a “war of position” to build counter-hegemonic alliances before any “war of manoeuvre” (direct confrontation).

### Intellectuals, Education, and Counter-Hegemony

Gramsci redefined the role of intellectuals, distinguishing between traditional elites who claim detachment from society and “organic intellectuals” who emerge from and serve specific classes. He believed that working-class intellectuals must develop alternative ideologies rooted in the lived experiences of the oppressed. Education, for Gramsci, was not about transmitting knowledge but about fostering critical consciousness. His ideas prefigured later movements in critical pedagogy, particularly those of Paulo Freire, and remain central to debates on popular education and social transformation.

### Historicism and Philosophical Critique

Gramsci embraced historicism, rejecting timeless truths in favor of context-specific understandings of reality. Influenced by Croce, he viewed Marxism as a philosophy of praxis—grounded in human agency and historical struggle rather than deterministic laws. He critiqued economic determinism, arguing that cultural and ideological factors are equally vital in shaping history. His critique of materialism challenged orthodox Marxist views, proposing instead that truth emerges from the dynamic interplay between human activity and historical conditions.

## Terms

- **Cultural Hegemony**: The process by which a ruling class uses cultural institutions and ideology to legitimize its dominance, securing consent rather than relying solely on coercion.
- **Civil Society**: The sphere of social life—including family, education, religion, and media—where norms, values, and beliefs are constructed and contested; distinct from the coercive apparatus of the state.
- **Organic Intellectuals**: Thinkers who arise from within a class to articulate its interests and worldview, as opposed to traditional intellectuals who claim neutrality or superiority.
- **War of Position**: A prolonged cultural and ideological struggle to build counter-hegemonic consensus before attempting revolutionary action.
- **War of Manoeuvre**: Direct, armed revolutionary action aimed at seizing state power; contrasted with the war of position.
- **Historicism**: The view that all ideas, truths, and social phenomena must be understood within their specific historical and cultural contexts.
- **Praxis**: Reflective, purposeful human action aimed at transforming society; central to Gramsci’s understanding of Marxism.
- **Passive Revolution**: A process where the ruling class adapts to preserve power, incorporating demands from below without genuine structural change.
- **Subaltern**: Marginalized groups excluded from dominant power structures; a term Gramsci used to describe those outside hegemonic discourse.
- **Senso Comune**: Common sense or folk wisdom; the everyday beliefs that reflect deeper ideological structures and can be sites of both conformity and resistance.

## Debates and Open Questions

Scholars continue to debate the extent to which Gramsci’s ideas align with orthodox Marxism or represent a departure toward idealism. Some critics argue that his emphasis on culture risks downplaying material conditions and class struggle. Others question whether his concept of hegemony adequately accounts for systemic violence and structural inequality. Additionally, there is ongoing discussion about how Gramsci’s thought should be applied today—particularly in contexts far removed from early 20th-century Europe. His legacy also raises questions about the relationship between intellectual work and political activism, especially in authoritarian regimes. Finally, debates persist regarding the authenticity of certain attributed quotes and anecdotes, such as his alleged support for Juventus, underscoring the mythologization of his figure in both academic and popular discourse.
