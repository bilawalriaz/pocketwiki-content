# French Revolutionary Wars

## Overview
The French Revolutionary Wars (1792–1802) were a series of global conflicts sparked by the French Revolution, pitting revolutionary France against shifting European coalitions. These wars transformed European politics and warfare, spreading revolutionary principles through conquest and establishing France as a dominant military power under leaders like Napoleon Bonaparte. They are divided into the War of the First Coalition (1792–1797) and the War of the Second Coalition (1798–1802).

## Timeline
- **27 Aug 1791** — Declaration of Pillnitz issued by Austria and Prussia, threatening intervention over King Louis XVI.
- **20 Apr 1792** — France declares war on Austria and Prussia, beginning the War of the First Coalition.
- **20 Sep 1792** — French victory at the Battle of Valmy turns back the Prussian invasion.
- **1 Feb 1793** — France declares war on Great Britain and the Dutch Republic.
- **23 Aug 1793** — The *levée en masse* (mass conscription) is decreed by the French National Convention.
- **6 Apr 1795** — Peace of Basel: Prussia leaves the coalition, ceding the west bank of the Rhine to France.
- **17 Oct 1797** — Treaty of Campo Formio ends the War of the First Coalition.
- **1 Jul 1798** — Napoleon's French army lands in Egypt, beginning the War of the Second Coalition.
- **1 Aug 1798** — British Admiral Nelson destroys the French fleet at the Battle of the Nile.
- **3 Dec 1800** — French victory at the Battle of Hohenlinden forces Austria to sue for peace.
- **9 Feb 1801** — Treaty of Lunéville ends hostilities with Austria.
- **25 Mar 1802** — Treaty of Amiens is signed, ending the French Revolutionary Wars.

## Body

### Origins and the First Coalition (1791–1797)
European monarchies, horrified by the French Revolution, formed the First Coalition to contain its spread. After the ambiguous Declaration of Pillnitz (1791) and French fears of invasion, France declared war in April 1792. Initial French defeats were reversed by the victory at Valmy (September 1792), which emboldened the abolition of the monarchy. The war expanded as Britain and Spain joined the coalition in 1793. France responded with the *levée en masse*, creating massive citizen armies. After early setbacks, French victories in 1794 (Fleurus, Black Mountain) led to the capture of the Austrian Netherlands and the Dutch Republic by 1795. Napoleon Bonaparte's brilliant Italian campaign (1796–97) destroyed Austrian forces in Italy, leading to the Treaty of Campo Formio (1797), which ended the First Coalition.

### The Second Coalition and Napoleon's Rise (1798–1802)
A new coalition formed in 1798, with Britain and Austria joined by Russia. While Napoleon's Egyptian campaign (1798–99) achieved tactical victories like the Battle of the Pyramids but was ultimately stranded by Nelson's naval victory at the Nile, his allies fought in Europe. The allies initially succeeded in Italy and Switzerland but were checked by French victories, notably at Zurich (1799). Napoleon returned to France, seized power in the Coup of 18 Brumaire (November 1799), and became First Consul. He reorganized the army and, in 1800, crossed the Alps to win the decisive Battle of Marengo in Italy. In Germany, General Moreau defeated the Austrians at Hohenlinden (December 1800). Austria signed the Treaty of Lunéville (1801), leaving Britain isolated. The Treaty of Amiens (1802) ended the wars, though tensions soon led to the Napoleonic Wars.

### Influence and Military Transformation
The wars revolutionized warfare. The French *levée en masse* (1793) mobilized the entire nation for war on an unprecedented scale, creating large, enthusiastic citizen armies. This contrasted with the smaller, professional armies of the Ancien Régime. New tactical doctrines, like the "Drill-Book" legislation of 1791, emphasized the bravery of the ordinary soldier. The Revolution also broke aristocratic monopoly on officer ranks, promoting talent. These changes—mass armies, nationalist fervor, and merit-based command—created the model for "modern war," influencing later theorists like Clausewitz.

## Terms
- **Coalition**: A military alliance of European states formed to counter French expansion.
- **Levée en masse**: The mass conscription decree of 23 August 1793, mobilizing the entire French population for war.
- **Sister Republic**: A republic established by France in conquered territories (e.g., the Batavian Republic in the Netherlands), modeled on French revolutionary principles.
- **First Consul**: The title assumed by Napoleon Bonaparte after his coup of 18 Brumaire (1799), making him head of the French government.
- **Treaty of Campo Formio**: The 1797 agreement ending the First Coalition, where Austria ceded the Austrian Netherlands and recognized French control of the Rhine's west bank.
- **Treaty of Amiens**: The 1802 agreement that ended the French Revolutionary Wars between France and Britain.
- **Battle of Valmy**: The September 1792 battle where French forces halted the Prussian invasion, a crucial morale-boosting victory for the Revolution.
- **Battle of the Nile**: The 1798 naval battle where the British fleet under Nelson destroyed Napoleon's fleet in Egypt, stranding his army.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise military objective of Napoleon's 1798 Egyptian expedition remains unclear; it may have been to threaten British dominance in India.
- The role of the *levée en masse* in French success is debated: was it the decisive factor, or were tactical and leadership innovations equally critical?
- Scholars debate the appropriate endpoint for the "Revolutionary Wars," with the Treaty of Amiens (1802) being the most common, though Napoleon was not crowned emperor until 1804.
