# Great Sphinx of Giza

## Overview
The Great Sphinx of Giza is a colossal limestone statue of a reclining sphinx—a mythical creature with a human head and a lion's body—carved from the bedrock of the Giza Plateau in Egypt. As the oldest known monumental sculpture in Egypt, it is a central feature of the Memphite Necropolis and a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Its age, original purpose, and the identity of the pharaoh it represents remain subjects of significant scholarly debate.

## Timeline
- **c. 2590–2532 BC** — Construction during the Old Kingdom reign of Pharaohs Khufu or Khafre.
- **c. 1400 BC** — Pharaoh Thutmose IV clears sand from the Sphinx and erects the Dream Stele.
- **1st century AD** — Roman Emperor Nero orders the Sphinx cleared of sand; a monumental stairway is built.
- **1378 AD** — Muhammad Sa'im al-Dahr, a Sufi Muslim, reportedly damages the Sphinx's nose in an act of iconoclasm.
- **1817** — First modern archaeological dig, led by Giovanni Battista Caviglia, uncovers the chest.
- **1887** — The chest, paws, and altar are fully exposed; accurate measurements are taken.
- **1926** — Émile Baraize conducts major clearing and restoration, sealing fissures and closing holes.
- **1931** — Engineers repair the head, adding a concrete collar that alters its profile.
- **1980s–1990s** — Multiple renovations are performed on the stone base and raw rock body.

## Body

### Construction and Identity
The Sphinx was sculpted from the limestone bedrock of the Eocene-aged Mokattam Formation. Archaeological evidence places its creation in the Old Kingdom, during the reign of either Khufu (c. 2590–2566 BC) or his son Khafre (c. 2558–2532 BC). The face is believed to represent one of these pharaohs, or possibly Khufu's son Djedefre, but no contemporary inscription definitively links the statue to a specific ruler. The monument is 73 m long, 20 m high, and 19 m wide. It was carved from the same quarry that supplied stone for the Giza pyramids, and the stones removed from its enclosure were used to build a temple in front of it, which was never completed.

### Names and Religious Significance
The original Old Kingdom name is unknown. In the New Kingdom, the Sphinx was revered as the solar deity Hor-em-akhet ("Horus of the Horizon"). The name "Sphinx" was applied in classical antiquity by reference to a Greek mythological beast. Medieval Arab writers called it *Abū il-Hawl* ("father of terror") or *Belhib*, linking it to the Canaanite god Hauron.

### History of Exposure and Restoration
The Sphinx was buried in sand by the First Intermediate Period. Thutmose IV's excavation (c. 1400 BC) is the first documented attempt. It was cleared again in the 1st century AD for Roman Emperor Nero and in the 14th century, possibly by Ramesses II. In the Graeco-Roman period, it was a tourist attraction. After the fall of Rome, it was re-buried. Modern archaeological work began in 1817. Major restorations occurred in 1926, 1931, and in the 1980s and 1990s, which involved replacing layers of limestone blocks around the base.

### Degradation and Damage
The body's uneven degradation is due to the varying erosion resistance of its limestone layers; the head, carved from harder limestone, is better preserved. The circumstances of the nose's destruction are unknown, but evidence shows it was deliberately pried off with rods or chisels. Contrary to popular myth, it was not destroyed by Napoleon's troops, as drawings from 1737 show it already missing. The Sphinx may have had a ceremonial beard, added after its original construction. Traces of pigment suggest it was once brightly painted.

### Holes and Tunnels
The Sphinx contains several man-made openings. A hole in the head, possibly for anchoring a crown, was sealed in 1926. A tunnel drilled in 1837 behind the head became stuck and caused damage. A major natural fissure runs through its waist. A passage at the rump, rediscovered in 1980, may date to ancient explorations or 19th-century boring. Other features include a niche in the northern flank, a space behind the Dream Stele, and the "Keyhole Shaft" near the northern hind paw.

## Terms
- **Sphinx**: A mythical creature with the head of a human and the body of a lion.
- **Monolith**: A large single piece of stone or rock, such as the bedrock from which the Sphinx was carved.
- **Mokattam Formation**: The Eocene-aged limestone bedrock of the Giza Plateau.
- **Memphite Necropolis**: The ancient burial ground near Memphis, which includes the Giza pyramid complex and Sphinx.
- **Dream Stele**: An inscribed granite slab erected by Pharaoh Thutmose IV between the Sphinx's paws.
- **Hor-em-akhet**: The New Kingdom name for the Sphinx, meaning "Horus of the Horizon."
- **Nemes**: The striped headcloth worn by pharaohs, depicted on the Sphinx.
- **Iconoclasm**: The deliberate destruction of religious images or monuments.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Identity of the Pharaoh**: The primary debate is whether the Sphinx represents Khufu, Khafre, or Djedefre. Evidence is circumstantial, with no contemporary inscription providing a definitive link.
- **Age and Erosion**: Mainstream archaeology dates it to the 4th Dynasty (c. 2600–2500 BC). Dissenting hypotheses, such as the water erosion hypothesis, argue for a much earlier origin (e.g., late Predynastic), but these are considered pseudoarchaeology by mainstream scholarship due to contradictory evidence.
- **Cause of Damage**: The exact cause and perpetrator of the nose's destruction are unknown, though medieval accounts attribute it to a Sufi Muslim in 1378.
- **Purpose of Tunnels**: The origin and purpose of various shafts and passages within the Sphinx (e.g., the rump passage) are debated, with theories ranging from ancient tomb exploration to 19th-century excavation attempts.
