# Bicycle

## Overview

The bicycle is a human-powered, pedal-driven, single-track vehicle with two wheels mounted one behind the other on a frame. Invented in 19th-century Europe, it became one of the most widely produced machines in history, with over 1 billion in use by the early 21st century. Beyond transportation, the bicycle catalyzed industrial innovation—many components later essential to automobiles, such as ball bearings, pneumatic tires, and chain-driven sprockets, were first developed for bicycles—and reshaped society by enabling personal mobility, particularly for women, and influencing urban planning, economic models, and environmental outcomes.

## Body

### History and Etymology

The term "bicycle" first appeared in French in 1828, referring to a two-wheeled carriage, and entered English print in 1868. The design evolved from earlier velocipedes, with the modern safety bicycle—featuring a chain-driven mechanism and equal-sized wheels—emerging around 1885. This configuration remains largely unchanged today, though materials and design refinements have expanded the range of specialized bicycles.

### Uses

Bicycles serve multiple roles: transportation, commuting, recreation, fitness, military and police operations, courier services, and competitive sports. They are highly energy-efficient, requiring roughly 20% of the energy needed to walk the same distance. Professional uses include mail delivery, emergency response, and urban patrol. Recreational activities span touring, mountain biking, racing, BMX, and artistic cycling.

### Technical Aspects

#### Types

Bicycles are categorized by function, construction, gearing, and propulsion. Common types include utility, mountain, racing, touring, hybrid, cruiser, BMX, and electric bicycles. Others include tandems, recumbents, folding models, cargo bikes, and low riders. Unicycles, tricycles, and quadracycles are not strictly bicycles but fall under the broader category of cycles.

#### Dynamics

A moving bicycle remains upright through steering that keeps its center of mass aligned with the resultant force of gravity and centrifugal force. Countersteering—leaning or turning handlebars—induces the necessary lean for turns. Short-wheelbase or tall bicycles can generate enough front-wheel braking force to lift the rear wheel, a maneuver known as a stoppie or front wheelie.

#### Performance

The bicycle is the most efficient human-powered vehicle at low to medium speeds (16–24 km/h), with up to 99% of pedal energy transmitted to the wheels. Air drag becomes the dominant resistance factor at higher speeds, increasing with the square of velocity. Aerodynamic positioning and fairings significantly reduce drag; the fastest unpaced flat-surface speed recorded was over 92 km/h in 2016.

#### Frame

Most modern bicycles use a diamond frame—a truss of two triangles (front and rear)—which distributes rider weight efficiently. Women’s frames historically used a step-through design for easier mounting, though this compromises structural integrity. Recumbent bicycles offer superior aerodynamics but were banned from competition in 1934. Frame materials have evolved from steel to aluminum, titanium, and carbon fiber, with carbon fiber now standard in professional racing due to its strength-to-weight ratio.

#### Drivetrain and Gearing

Power is transmitted from pedals through cranks and a chain to the rear wheel. Variable gear ratios help maintain optimal pedaling cadence across terrain. Derailleur systems, with front and rear mechs, are most common, though hub gears (3–14 speeds) and belt drives offer alternatives. Gear selection allows cyclists to trade speed for torque, reducing fatigue on inclines or under load.

#### Steering, Seating, Brakes, Suspension, Wheels, Accessories

Handlebar styles include upright, drop, and riser bars, each suited to different riding positions. Saddles vary by riding style, with wider, cushioned options for upright bikes and narrower ones for racing. Brakes include rim, hub, and disc types; track bikes lack brakes and rely on fixed-gear resistance. Suspension systems, common on mountain and hybrid bikes, improve control and comfort. Tires range from narrow, high-pressure road tires to wide, treaded off-road variants. Accessories like fenders, lights, racks, and locks enhance utility and safety.

### Social and Historical Aspects

#### Daily Life and Urban Planning

Around 1900, bicycles enabled suburban commuting, reduced urban crowding, and decreased reliance on horses. Today, cities worldwide integrate cycling infrastructure to cut congestion and pollution. Bicycle-sharing systems, starting with Amsterdam’s White Bicycle plan in 1965, complement public transit. In the Netherlands, train stations provide free or low-cost bike parking, sometimes exceeding capacity.

#### Female Emancipation

The safety bicycle granted women unprecedented mobility, symbolizing independence and contributing to the "New Woman" movement. It also sparked the rational dress movement, replacing corsets and long skirts with bloomers. Susan B. Anthony called the bicycle a "freedom machine," and Frances Willard celebrated its health and political benefits. In 1985, Georgena Terry launched the first women-specific bicycle company, addressing fit disparities.

#### Economic Implications

Bicycle manufacturing pioneered mass production techniques later adopted by Ford and GM, including vertical integration, aggressive advertising, and annual model changes. The Wright brothers and companies like Rover and Morris began in the bicycle industry. Today, over 60% of bicycles are made in China, though production has largely shifted offshore, with most brands designing but not manufacturing frames.

#### Environmental Impact

Bicycle production emits approximately 70–250 kg CO₂eq per unit, depending on location and type. Materials—steel, aluminum, carbon fiber—are largely recyclable. Tire wear contributes to rubber pollution, though less than cars. Electric bicycles have higher production footprints but remain environmentally favorable compared to motor vehicles.

#### Legal Requirements and Theft

The 1968 Vienna Convention classifies bicycles as vehicles, requiring roadworthiness and lights in many jurisdictions. Bicycle theft is widespread; serial numbers aid recovery, but reporting rates remain low.

## Terms

- **Safety bicycle**: A bicycle with a chain-driven mechanism and equal-sized wheels, developed around 1885, forming the basis of modern bicycle design.
- **Countersteering**: The technique of initiating a turn by leaning or briefly turning the handlebars in the opposite direction to induce a lean.
- **Derailleur**: A mechanism that moves the chain between different gears (chainrings and sprockets) to vary mechanical advantage.
- **Diamond frame**: A bicycle frame design consisting of two triangles (front and rear) formed by the top tube, down tube, seat tube, chain stays, and seat stays.
- **Freewheel**: A mechanism that allows the rear wheel to rotate independently of the pedals, enabling coasting.
- **Hub gear**: A multi-speed gearing system enclosed within the rear wheel hub, offering between 3 and 14 gear ratios.
- **Recumbent bicycle**: A bicycle where the rider sits in a reclined position, offering better aerodynamics than upright designs.
- **Stoppie**: A trick where the front brake is used to lift the rear wheel off the ground while balancing on the front wheel.
- **Track bicycle**: A fixed-gear bicycle without brakes, designed for racing on velodromes.
- **Wheelset**: A pair of wheels, typically referring to pre-assembled performance-oriented wheels sold as a unit.

## Debates and Open Questions

- **Recumbent vs. upright design**: While recumbent bicycles are faster and more comfortable, their ban from competition since 1934 by the Union Cycliste Internationale remains controversial among enthusiasts.
- **Women’s frame geometry**: The traditional step-through design for women’s bikes is debated for its structural trade-offs versus accessibility.
- **Material preferences**: Carbon fiber dominates professional racing for its strength-to-weight ratio, but aluminum and steel remain popular for durability and cost.
- **Production centralization**: Over 60% of global bicycle production is concentrated in China, raising questions about sustainability, labor conditions, and local industry viability.
- **Environmental trade-offs**: While bicycles are eco-friendly in use, the carbon footprint of e-bikes and the pollution from tire wear are growing concerns.
