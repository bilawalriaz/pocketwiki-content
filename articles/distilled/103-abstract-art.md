# Abstract art

## Overview

Abstract art uses visual language of shape, form, color, and line to create compositions that may exist with a degree of independence from visual references in the world. Closely related terms include non-figurative art, non-objective art, and non-representational art—similar but not identical in meaning. Abstraction exists along a continuum, from partial abstraction (altering color or form conspicuously) to total abstraction (no trace of anything recognizable). Western art had been underpinned by perspective and the illusion of visible reality from the Renaissance to the mid-19th century, but by the end of that century, artists sought new forms reflecting changes in technology, science, and philosophy. This shift produced major movements—Fauvism, Cubism, Suprematism, Neo-Plasticism, and Abstract Expressionism—that redefined art's purpose and social role.

## Timeline

- **1871–1872** — Georgiana Houghton exhibits "spirit" drawings with abstract shapes; Whistler paints *Nocturne in Black and Gold: The Falling Rocket*, emphasizing visual sensation over depiction
- **c. 1905** — Fauvism emerges in Paris; Matisse and others use "wild," multi-colored expressive painting
- **1909–1913** — Experimental abstract works by Picabia, Kandinsky, Kupka, Delaunay, Survage, and Mondrian
- **1912** — Kupka exhibits *Amorpha, Fugue en deux couleurs*; Apollinaire names this approach "Orphism"
- **1915** — Malevich completes *Black Square*, his first entirely abstract Suprematist work
- **1915–1919** — Mondrian evolves Neo-Plasticism—horizontal/vertical lines with rectangles of color—with De Stijl group
- **1919** — Bauhaus founded at Weimar by Walter Gropius; closed 1932 under Nazi pressure
- **1931** — Abstraction-Création founded in Paris as a reference point for abstract artists
- **1935–1936** — First British abstract art exhibition (1935); Abstract and Concrete exhibition includes Mondrian, Miró, Hepworth, Nicholson
- **1937** — Nazi "Entartete Kunst" (degenerate art) exhibition; exodus of artists from Europe begins
- **1940s–1950s** — Abstract Expressionism and New York School emerge; New York becomes center of the art world

## Body

### 19th Century Foundations

In 19th-century Europe, church patronage diminished while private patronage grew, advancing artistic independence. Romanticism, Impressionism, and Expressionism contributed to abstract art's development. An objective interest in what is seen runs from Constable, Turner, and Corot through the Impressionists, who continued the plein air (outdoor) painting of the Barbizon school. Expressionists explored bold paint surfaces, distortions, exaggerations, and intense color, shifting emphasis from subject matter to psychological states; Edvard Munch and James Ensor were instrumental to 20th-century abstraction. Paul Cézanne, beginning as an Impressionist, aimed at "a logical construction of reality based on a view from a single point, with modulated color in flat areas"—the basis later developed into Cubism. Late 19th-century Eastern European mysticism and theosophy (Mme. Blavatsky) profoundly impacted geometric pioneers Hilma af Klint and Wassily Kandinsky; Gurdjieff and Ouspensky influenced Mondrian; spiritualism also inspired Malevich and Kupka.

### Early 20th Century

At the century's start, Matisse and young artists including Braque, Derain, Dufy, and Metzinger revolutionized Paris with "wild," multi-colored works critics called Fauvism. The Fauves' raw color language directly influenced Kandinsky. Cubism, based on Cézanne's idea that nature can be reduced to cube, sphere, and cone, opened the door to abstraction. At the 1912 Salon de la Section d'Or, Apollinaire named Kupka's and Delaunay's work "Orphism"—"the art of painting new structures out of elements...created entirely by the artist...it is a pure art." From 1909 to 1913, experimental works multiplied: Picabia's *Caoutchouc* (c. 1909), Kandinsky's *Untitled (First Abstract Watercolor)* (1913), Kupka's *Discs of Newton* (1912), Delaunay's *Simultaneous Windows* series, Survage's *Colored Rhythm* (1913), and Mondrian's *Tableau No. 1* (1913). Matisse came close to pure abstraction in *French Window at Collioure* (1914) and *View of Notre-Dame* (1914). Goncharova and Larionov's Rayist drawings used lines like rays of light; Malevich completed *Black Square* in 1915; Popova created Architectonic Constructions (1916–1921).

### Russian Avant-Garde and the Bauhaus

Russian abstract artists split: Constructivists (Tatlin, Stepanova, Exter) believed art was life itself—the artist must become a technician using modern production tools ("Art into life!"). Opposing them, Malevich, Pevsner, and Gabo argued art was essentially spiritual. Many hostile to materialist production left Russia: Pevsner to France, Gabo to Berlin/England/America, Kandinsky to the Bauhaus. By the mid-1920s the revolutionary experimental period ended; by the 1930s only socialist realism was allowed. The Bauhaus, founded 1919 by Walter Gropius, taught unity of all visual and plastic arts; teachers included Klee, Kandinsky, Itten, and the Alberses. After the 1937 "degenerate art" exhibition, the exodus from Europe began—many Bauhaus artists went to America.

### Paris, London, and the Late 20th Century

In 1930s Paris, artists fleeing totalitarianism created proximity that spurred analysis of aesthetic groupings. The Cercle et Carré exhibition (organized by Torres-García and Seuphor) included Neo-Plasticists, Kandinsky, Pevsner, and Schwitters; Van Doesburg criticized it as too indefinite and published the journal *Art Concret* defining abstraction where line, color, and surface are the only concrete reality. Abstraction-Création (1931) provided a reference point; many artists regrouped in London, where the first British abstract exhibition was held in 1935. During the Nazi rise, exiled Europeans—Duchamp, Léger, Mondrian, Ernst, Breton—arrived in New York; local painters distilled these influences. Abstract Expressionism and the New York School emerged; Graham and Hofmann bridged European Modernists and younger Americans. Rothko's surrealist imagery dissolved into color compositions; Pollock, Motherwell, and Kline emphasized the gesture and act of painting; Gorky and de Kooning evolved from figurative work to abstraction. New York became the world's art center.

### 21st Century

Directions relating to abstraction in the second half of the 20th century include digital art, hard-edge painting, geometric abstraction, minimalism, lyrical abstraction, op art, abstract expressionism, color field painting, monochrome painting, assemblage, neo-Dada, and shaped canvas painting. In the US, Minimalist sculpture (Donald Judd) and painting (Frank Stella) are newer permutations; other examples include Lyrical Abstraction and the sensuous color work of Motherwell, Heron, Noland, Francis, Twombly, Diebenkorn, Frankenthaler, Mitchell, and Ruiz de Velasco.

## Terms

- **Abstract art**: Art using shape, form, color, and line with independence from visual references in the world
- **Fauvism**: Early 20th-century movement using "wild," multi-colored expressive painting
- **Cubism**: Movement based on Cézanne's idea that nature reduces to cube, sphere, and cone
- **Orphism**: Apollinaire's term for art built from elements created entirely by the artist
- **Suprematism**: Malevich's movement; *Black Square* (1915) was its first entirely abstract work
- **Neo-Plasticism**: Mondrian's aesthetic of horizontal/vertical lines with rectangles of color
- **Constructivism**: Russian belief that art is life itself; artist as technician of modern production
- **Bauhaus**: German school (1919–1932) teaching unity of all visual and plastic arts
- **Abstract Expressionism**: Post-WWII American movement emphasizing gesture and the act of painting
- **Plein air**: Outdoor painting, continued by Impressionists from the Barbizon school

## Debates and Open Questions

Theodor W. Adorno's socio-historical explanation holds that abstraction in modern art responds to and reflects the growing abstraction of social relations in industrial society. Frederic Jameson similarly sees modernist abstraction as a function of money's abstract power, equating all things as exchange-values; abstract art's social content is then the abstract nature of social existence—legal formalities, bureaucratic impersonalization, information/power—in late modernity. By contrast, Post-Jungians see quantum theories, with their disintegration of conventional ideas of form and matter, as underlying the divorce of the concrete and abstract in modern art.
