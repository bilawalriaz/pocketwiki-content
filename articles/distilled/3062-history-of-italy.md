# History of Italy

## Overview
Italy's history spans from prehistoric settlements to its current status as a modern republic and EU founding member. It was the cradle of the Roman Empire, which profoundly shaped Western civilization, and later experienced fragmentation, foreign domination, and the Renaissance. The modern nation-state unified in the 19th century, endured a fascist dictatorship and World War II, and has since developed into a democratic republic and major economic power.

## Timeline
- **c. 753 BC** — Traditional founding of Rome as a kingdom.
- **509 BC** — Establishment of the Roman Republic.
- **27 BC** — Augustus becomes first emperor, beginning the Roman Empire.
- **476 AD** — Deposition of the last Western Roman Emperor, Romulus Augustulus.
- **1130** — Roger II founds the Norman Kingdom of Sicily.
- **1494** — French invasion begins the Italian Wars, ending city-state independence.
- **1861** — Most of the peninsula unified as the Kingdom of Italy under Victor Emmanuel II.
- **1922** — Benito Mussolini's March on Rome leads to the fascist regime.
- **1946** — Institutional referendum abolishes the monarchy, establishing the Italian Republic.
- **1957** — Italy is a founding signatory of the Treaty of Rome, creating the European Economic Community.
- **2022** — Giorgia Meloni becomes Italy's first female prime minister.

## Body

### Prehistory and Ancient Civilizations
Human presence in Italy dates to 850,000 years ago. The Copper Age (c. 3400–3100 BC) is exemplified by Ötzi the Iceman. From the mid-3rd millennium BC, Indo-European migrations brought metalworking and new cultures, culminating in the Iron Age Proto-Villanovan culture. The Nuragic civilization flourished in Sardinia from the 18th century BC. By 800 BC, the Etruscan civilization dominated central Italy, speaking a non-Indo-European language and engaging in extensive trade. Their influence waned after defeats by Greeks (474 BC) and Roman expansion. Concurrently, Italic peoples like the Latins and Samnites inhabited the peninsula, and Greek colonies in the south formed Magna Graecia, transplanting Hellenic culture and the alphabet.

### Roman Kingdom, Republic, and Empire
According to legend, Rome was founded in 753 BC and ruled by kings until the Republic was established in 509 BC. The Republic, governed by elected magistrates and a senate, gradually unified Italy and defeated Carthage in the Punic Wars (3rd–2nd centuries BC), becoming the Mediterranean's dominant power. Internal crises in the 1st century BC led to Julius Caesar's rise and, after his assassination, the transition to the Empire under Augustus in 27 BC. The Empire, marked by the Pax Romana, expanded to its greatest extent under Trajan (2nd century AD). It was divided into Eastern and Western halves in 395 AD; the Western Empire fell in 476 AD.

### Middle Ages and Renaissance
After Rome's fall, Italy was ruled successively by Ostrogoths, Byzantines, and Lombards. The Papal States were established in central Italy in 756. From the 11th century, maritime republics like Venice and Genoa prospered through trade. The 12th–13th centuries saw the rise of independent city-states, which became centers of commerce and finance. The Renaissance, a "rebirth" of arts and learning fueled by classical rediscovery, began in Tuscany in the 14th century and spread across Europe. Italian explorers, including Columbus and Vespucci, played key roles in the Age of Discovery. However, the Italian Wars (1494–1559) ended the independence of many city-states, leaving the peninsula divided and under foreign influence.

### Unification and the Liberal Period
The Risorgimento, the movement for unification, began after the Congress of Vienna (1815). Key figures included Giuseppe Mazzini, Giuseppe Garibaldi, and Camillo Benso, Count of Cavour, who served the Kingdom of Sardinia. Through wars and diplomacy, the Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed in 1861. Unification was completed in 1870 with the capture of Rome. The new state faced the "Southern question"—a deep economic divide with the impoverished south—which triggered a massive Italian diaspora from the 1880s onward. Politically, the liberal parliamentary system was marked by instability and *trasformismo* (political maneuvering).

### Fascist Regime and World War II
Discontent after World War I, where Italy suffered heavy losses for limited territorial gains, fueled the rise of Benito Mussolini's Fascist movement. Mussolini became prime minister in 1922 and gradually established a dictatorship. The Lateran Treaty (1929) resolved the "Roman question" with the Vatican. Fascist foreign policy pursued imperial ambitions, invading Ethiopia (1935) and allying with Nazi Germany. Italy entered World War II in 1940 but suffered major defeats. After the Allied invasion of Sicily (1943), Mussolini was deposed, and Italy signed an armistice. The country was then divided, with the Allies advancing in the south and a German-backed fascist state in the north, until liberation in 1945.

### Republican Era
A 1946 referendum abolished the monarchy, establishing the Italian Republic. The new constitution created a parliamentary democracy. Italy joined NATO (1949) and the European Economic Community (1957). The post-war "economic miracle" (1950s–60s) transformed the country into a major industrial power. The 1970s, known as the "Years of Lead," were marked by political violence and terrorism. The 1990s saw the "Mani pulite" corruption investigations, which dismantled the traditional party system. Italy has since experienced political instability, economic challenges including the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic, and the rise of populist movements.

## Terms
- **Risorgimento**: The 19th-century political and social movement for the unification of Italy.
- **Magna Graecia**: The coastal areas of Southern Italy colonized by Greek settlers from the 8th century BC.
- **Trasformismo**: A political practice in post-unification Italy where governments were formed by creating a broad, non-partisan coalition of moderates to isolate extremists.
- **Southern question (Questione meridionale)**: The political and social separation between the industrialized north and the impoverished, agrarian south after unification.
- **Lateran Treaty**: A 1929 agreement between Italy and the Holy See that established Vatican City as an independent state and resolved the "Roman question."
- **Years of Lead (Anni di piombo)**: A period of socio-political turmoil and widespread violence in Italy during the 1970s and early 1980s.
- **Mani pulite**: A nationwide judicial investigation into political corruption in Italy in the early 1990s, which led to the collapse of the traditional party system.
- **Tangentopoli**: Literally "Bribesville," a term referring to the widespread corruption uncovered by the Mani pulite investigations.

## Debates and open questions
- The origins of the Etruscan civilization are debated, with hypotheses suggesting indigenous development, migration from the Near East, or northern European origins. Genetic studies have provided conflicting evidence.
- Historians debate the effectiveness of Italy's military performance in World War II, with arguments ranging from it being a "hollow shell" to reassessments highlighting instances of effective action hampered by logistical and strategic problems.
- The "mutilated victory" sentiment after World War I is considered by historians to be a "political myth" used by fascists, though its impact on public opinion and political radicalization is analyzed.
