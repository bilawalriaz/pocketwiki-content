# Samuel Morse

## Overview
Samuel F.B. Morse (1791–1872) was an American inventor and painter who, after establishing a reputation in art, co-developed the single-wire telegraph and the associated Morse code in the 1830s. His work was pivotal in establishing the commercial telegraph, which revolutionized long-distance communication. His life also encompassed controversial political views and significant legal battles over his invention's patent.

## Body

### Early Life and Painting Career
Born to a Calvinist pastor, Morse was educated at Yale and initially pursued painting. He studied in England (1811–1815), where he created works like *Dying Hercules* and *Judgment of Jupiter*, which reflected his evolving political and religious views. Upon returning to the U.S., he became a prominent portraitist, painting figures like John Adams and the Marquis de Lafayette. His 1821 painting *The House of Representatives* aimed to glorify American democracy but was not a commercial success. He co-founded the National Academy of Design in 1826 and later became a professor of painting at New York University.

### Invention of the Telegraph
While returning from Europe in 1832, Morse conceived the idea for a single-wire telegraph after witnessing experiments with electromagnetism. He partnered with chemist Leonard Gale and mechanic Alfred Vail to overcome technical hurdles, particularly the problem of signal decay over distance. Their key innovation was the use of electromagnetic relays to boost the signal. After a successful public demonstration in 1838, Morse struggled to secure funding and faced patent priority challenges from English inventors William Cooke and Charles Wheatstone.

### Federal Support and Commercial Success
Morse finally secured U.S. government funding in 1843, leading to the construction of an experimental line between Washington, D.C., and Baltimore. The line's official opening on May 24, 1844, with the message "What hath God wrought," marked the birth of the commercial telegraph. The Magnetic Telegraph Company was formed in 1845, and by 1850, 12,000 miles of telegraph wire were laid in the U.S. Morse's system, using his code, became the global standard, though Cooke and Wheatstone's needle telegraph persisted in the British Empire.

### Later Years and Legacy
Morse engaged in prolonged litigation to defend his patent, culminating in the 1853 Supreme Court case *O'Reilly v. Morse*. The court upheld his patent for the repeater system but denied his broader claim over all use of electromagnetic force for signaling. He received significant financial awards from European governments in 1858 and supported the first transatlantic cable project. Morse was also a vocal anti-Catholic nativist and a defender of slavery. He died in 1872, having been honored with numerous foreign decorations but limited recognition in the U.S. during his lifetime.

## Terms
- **Telegraph**: A system for transmitting messages over long distances using wire and electrical signals.
- **Morse code**: A character encoding scheme using dots and dashes to represent letters and numbers, developed for telegraphy.
- **Electromagnetic relay**: A switch that uses an electromagnet to control a separate circuit, used to boost and repeat telegraph signals over long distances.
- **Single-wire telegraph**: A telegraph system that uses one wire to complete an electrical circuit, often with the earth as a return path.
- **Daguerreotype**: The first practical process of photography, which Morse helped publicize in the U.S.
- **Nativist**: A political ideology favoring native-born inhabitants over immigrants, often with anti-Catholic sentiment in the 19th century U.S.
- **Repeater**: In telegraphy, an apparatus that automatically retransmits a signal to extend its range.

- **Priority of Invention**: The article notes that William Cooke and Charles Wheatstone launched a commercial telegraph in England before Morse, despite starting later, leading to disputes over who was the true inventor.
- **Role of Collaborators**: The development of Morse code is noted to have involved significant contributions from Alfred Vail, challenging Morse's claim as the sole inventor.
- **Patent Scope**: The Supreme Court's rejection of Morse's broad "claim 8" in *O'Reilly v. Morse* established a key precedent limiting patents on abstract natural laws, a debate that continues in modern patent law for software and other technologies.
