# Government

## Overview

A government is the system or group of people that leads a state, responsible for directing the only entity whose use of force is socially acceptable. Governments have existed in many forms—dominated by one person (autocracy), a small ruling class (oligarchy), or the masses (democracy). The main modern political systems are democracies, totalitarian regimes, and, between these, authoritarian regimes with hybrid variants; monarchies are sometimes classified as a standalone or hybrid system. Understanding government is essential because it shapes how societies organize power, create laws, and manage public life.

## Timeline

- **c. 5,000 years ago** — First small city-states appear.
- **3rd–2nd millennia BC** — Larger governed areas develop: Sumer, ancient Egypt, Indus Valley, Yellow River civilizations.
- **375 BC** — Plato writes *The Republic*, classifying five basic government types.
- **17th century end** — Republican forms of government grow in prevalence.
- **17th–18th centuries** — English Civil War, Glorious Revolution, American Revolution, and French Revolution boost representative government.
- **19th–20th centuries** — Significant increase in size and scale of national governments; regulation of corporations and welfare state development.
- **20th century** — Soviet Union becomes the first large country with a Communist government.
- **Post–Berlin Wall fall** — Liberal democracy becomes even more prevalent.
- **2021** — 97 of 167 nations are democracies; world is becoming more authoritarian with a quarter of the population under democratically backsliding governments.

## Body

### Definitions and etymology

A government is the system to govern a state or community. The Cambridge Dictionary defines it as "the system used for controlling a country, city, or group of people," or "an organization that officially manages and controls a country or region, creating laws, collecting taxes, providing public services." While all organizations have governance, "government" often specifically refers to the approximately 200 independent national governments and their subsidiary bodies (state, provincial, and local governments). The word derives from the Greek verb *kubernáo* (to steer with a rudder), a metaphorical sense attested in classical antiquity, including Plato's Ship of State. In British English, "government" can also mean a "ministry" or "administration"—the policies and officials of a particular executive or governing coalition.

### History

The moment and place human government developed is lost in time, but history records early formations. About 5,000 years ago, the first small city-states appeared; by the third to second millennia BC, some grew into larger areas: Sumer, ancient Egypt, the Indus Valley civilization, and the Yellow River civilization. One explanation for government's emergence is agriculture: since the Neolithic Revolution, farming created food surpluses, enabling specialization in non-agricultural activities, including ruling others and social experimentation with governance models. Governments grew more complex as agriculture supported larger, denser populations, creating new interactions and social pressures needing control. Another explanation is the need to manage infrastructure projects like water systems, which required centralized administration—though archaeological evidence also shows successful egalitarian, decentralized complex societies.

Starting at the end of the 17th century, republican forms of government grew. The English Civil War, Glorious Revolution, American Revolution, and French Revolution contributed to representative government's growth. The Soviet Union was the first large Communist country. Since the Berlin Wall's fall, liberal democracy has become more prevalent. In the 19th and 20th centuries, national governments significantly expanded in size and scale, including corporate regulation and welfare state development.

### Political science and classification

Political scientists have long sought a typology of polities, but classification is not obvious; any complete taxonomy must include all forms of monopoly on legitimate force. Superficially, all governments have an official *de jure* (ideal) form—the United States is a federal constitutional republic, the former Soviet Union a federal socialist republic—but self-identification is not objective. As Kopstein and Lichbach argue, defining regimes is tricky, especially *de facto* (in practice), when government and economy deviate. Voltaire noted the Holy Roman Empire was "neither Holy, nor Roman, nor an Empire"; the Soviet Union was in practice a centralized autocratic one-party state under Stalin. Complications include parties naming themselves after ideologies, causing movements to be mistaken for forms of government, and non-consensus or deliberate bias in defining political ideologies. For example, "conservatism" in the United States differs from its use elsewhere: "what Americans now call conservatism much of the world calls liberalism or neoliberalism," and a Finnish "conservative" would be labeled a "socialist" in the US.

Opinions vary on government types; "shades of gray" are common. Even the most liberal democracies limit rival political activity, while the most tyrannical dictatorships must organize broad support, complicating narrow categorization. Some American voters claim the US is a plutocracy (rule by wealth) rather than a democracy, believing elections are manipulated by wealthy Super PACs. Some argue government should be reconceptualized so that in times of climatic change, individual needs are reshaped to generate sufficiency for all.

### Forms of government

Plato's *The Republic* (375 BC) divided governments into five types: aristocracy (rule by law and order), timocracy (rule by honor and duty, e.g., Sparta), oligarchy (rule by wealth), democracy (rule by liberty and equality), and tyranny (rule by fear). These progressively degenerate from aristocracy to tyranny. Aristotle's *Politics* elaborated on these, classifying by who rules: one person (autocracy, e.g., monarchy), a select group (aristocracy), or the people (democracy, e.g., republic). Thomas Hobbes held there can be only three kinds of Commonwealth: monarchy (one representative), democracy (assembly of all), or aristocracy (assembly of a part).

According to Yale professor Juan José Linz, three main political systems exist today: democracies, totalitarian regimes, and authoritarian regimes with hybrid regimes between them. Another system includes monarchies as standalone or hybrid. Scholars generally refer to a dictatorship as a form of authoritarianism or totalitarianism. Autocracy concentrates supreme power in one person, subject to neither external legal restraints nor regularized popular control (except implicit threats of coup or insurrection). Absolute monarchy is a historically prevalent form; most are hereditary, though some (e.g., the Holy See) are elected. Aristocracy places power in a small elite class, often as landed timocracy, plutocracy, or oligarchy. Democracies may combine direct and indirect governance—citizens select representatives but reserve rights to initiatives, referendums, and recall. A republic treats the country as a "public matter" (*res publica*), not the rulers' private property, with offices elected or appointed rather than inherited; Montesquieu included both democracies and aristocracies as republican forms. Federalism divides sovereignty constitutionally between a central authority and constituent units (states or provinces), creating a federation.

### Institutions and public administration

Governments are often organized into three branches—legislature, executive, judiciary (the *trias politica* model)—though parliamentary and semi-presidential systems often have intersecting branches; some have fewer or additional branches (e.g., electoral commissions). Party systems include majority governments (one or more parties holding an absolute majority of parliamentary seats), minority governments (plurality only, often relying on confidence-and-supply arrangements), coalition governments (multiple parties cooperating), and single-party governments. Governmental property, state-owned enterprises, public services, and civil servants compose the public sector. In modern developed countries, public services include courts, education, emergency services, health care, military, and policing. The United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 5 aims to influence public service provision for marginalized demographics. Public policy is the sum of a government's direct and indirect activities, covering education, health care, employment, finance, economics, and transportation. Government spending includes social protection, health care, education, economic affairs, public security, defense, and recreation; the government budget balance affects government bond yields.

## Terms

- **Autocracy**: System where supreme power is concentrated in one person, without external legal restraints or popular control mechanisms.
- **Oligarchy**: Rule by a small, elite class, often based on wealth.
- **Democracy**: Rule by the people, either directly or through elected representatives.
- **Totalitarian regime**: A political system where the state controls nearly all aspects of public and private life.
- **Authoritarian regime**: A system between democracy and totalitarianism, with limited political pluralism.
- **Republic**: A state considered a "public matter," where offices are elected or appointed, not inherited.
- **Federalism**: System where sovereignty is constitutionally divided between a central authority and constituent political units.
- **Trias politica**: The model of separating government into legislative, executive, and judicial branches.
- **Coalition government**: Multiple parties cooperating to form a government via a coalition agreement.
- **De jure / de facto**: Legally prescribed form versus actual practice.

## Debates and open questions

Scholars debate how to classify governments because self-identification is not objective and practice often deviates from ideal forms. Defining regimes is tricky when both government and economy deviate in practice, as with the Soviet Union under Stalin. Political ideologies are subject to non-consensus or deliberate "distortion or bias," making terms like "conservatism" mean different things across countries. There is also debate over whether governments can be "pigeonholed" into narrow categories, given that liberal democracies limit rival political activity and dictatorships must organize broad support. Some question whether the United States is a plutocracy rather than a democracy, and others argue government should be reconceptualized to address climate change by reshaping individual needs for sufficiency.
