# Léopold Sédar Senghor

## Overview
Léopold Sédar Senghor (1906–2001) was a Senegalese poet, cultural theorist, and the first president of independent Senegal (1960–1980). He is a central figure in 20th-century African intellectual history, primarily as a co-founder of the Négritude movement, which celebrated African culture and identity. His political career was marked by the establishment of a one-party state, a later, limited political opening, and a rare voluntary retirement from power.

## Timeline
- **9 October 1906** — Born in Joal, Senegal.
- **1928** — Moves to France for higher education.
- **1935** — Receives the Agrégation in French Grammar.
- **1940–1942** — Serves in the French army; captured and imprisoned by Germans.
- **1948** — Founds the Senegalese Democratic Bloc (BDS) with Mamadou Dia.
- **1960** — Becomes the first President of Senegal.
- **1962** — Arrests Prime Minister Mamadou Dia, consolidating power.
- **1966** — Senegal becomes a de facto one-party state.
- **1976** — Permits limited multi-party competition.
- **31 December 1980** — Resigns the presidency, handing power to Abdou Diouf.
- **2 June 1983** — Elected to the Académie française, the first African member.
- **20 December 2001** — Dies in Verson, France.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Senghor was born to a wealthy Serer peanut merchant and a Muslim mother of Fula origin. He began his education in a Catholic boarding school and seminary before shifting to secular studies, where he excelled in French literature. In 1928, he traveled to France on a scholarship, eventually earning the prestigious Agrégation in French Grammar in 1935. He taught at lycées and universities in France while studying linguistics and social sciences.

### Négritude and Wartime Experience
In Paris, Senghor, along with Aimé Césaire and Léon Damas, developed the concept of **Négritude**, a movement asserting the value of African culture against colonial racism. During World War II, he was conscripted into the French army, captured by Germans in 1940, and spent two years in prison camps, where he wrote poetry. He was released in 1942 and joined the French Resistance.

### Political Career in Colonial and Independent Senegal
Senghor began his political career as a deputy for Senegal-Mauritania in the French National Assembly. He advocated for a federal model within a French union, granting African territories autonomy while retaining French support. After Senegal's independence in 1960, he became its first president. In 1962, he arrested his prime minister and long-time associate, Mamadou Dia, on suspicion of plotting a coup. This led to the establishment of an authoritarian one-party state under Senghor's Senegalese Progressive Union (UPS). In 1976, he allowed a limited number of opposition parties but maintained an uneven political field. He retired voluntarily in 1980, the first African head of state to do so.

### Intellectual Legacy and Later Honors
Senghor's political philosophy was **African socialism**, an alternative to Marxism that drew from Négritude and emphasized dialogue with Western culture. He was a key proponent of **Francophonie**, the international community of French-speaking nations. In 1983, he became the first African elected to the Académie française. His poetry, collected in works like *Chants d'ombre* and *Éthiopiques*, is celebrated for its lyrical exploration of African identity and history.

## Terms
*   **Négritude:** An intellectual movement co-founded by Senghor that sought to assert and valorize African cultural characteristics, values, and aesthetics in response to colonial racism.
*   **African Socialism:** Senghor's political ideology, which rejected Marxism and anti-Westernism, instead advocating for an indigenous socialism based on African communal values and maintained through close ties with France.
*   **Francophonie:** The international community of French-speaking peoples and nations; Senghor was a major proponent and served as vice-president of its High Council.
*   **Décalage:** A term Senghor used to describe the temporal and spatial "lag" or discrepancy between Black Africans and the African Diaspora, questioning the basis for solidarity after centuries of separation.
*   **Bloc Démocratique Sénégalais (BDS):** The political party Senghor co-founded in 1948, which later became the Senegalese Progressive Union (UPS), the ruling party during his presidency.
*   **Académie française:** The prestigious French council for matters pertaining to the French language; Senghor was its first African member, elected in 1983.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Authoritarianism vs. Stability:** Scholars debate whether Senghor's consolidation of a one-party state was a necessary measure for national stability in the post-independence era or a betrayal of democratic ideals.
*   **Legacy of Neocolonialism:** His policy of maintaining close political, economic, and cultural ties with France is contested. Critics argue it perpetuated French influence (e.g., the CFA franc currency), while supporters see it as pragmatic realism that ensured Senegal's stability.
*   **Négritude's Relevance:** The Négritude movement itself has been critiqued by later thinkers for potentially essentializing African identity and for its initial focus on a Francophone, male elite, though its foundational importance is widely acknowledged.
