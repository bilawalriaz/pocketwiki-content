# Thought

## Overview
Thought, or thinking, is a cognitive process in which the mind considers, creates, or manipulates ideas, representations, or information. It encompasses core forms such as judging, reasoning, concept formation, problem solving, and deliberation, and may arise from perception or occur independently of sensory input. The study of thought spans philosophy, psychology, neuroscience, and artificial intelligence, with competing theories attempting to explain its nature, structure, and mechanisms.

## Timeline
- **c. 384–322 BC** — Aristotle develops theory of thinking as instantiation of universal essences
- **c. 428–348 BC** — Plato introduces theory of Forms and recollection as basis for thought
- **1637** — Descartes frames mind as "thinking thing" in *Discourse on Method*
- **1890s** — Behaviorism emerges, reducing thought to behavioral dispositions
- **1950s–60s** — Cognitive revolution; computationalism and language of thought hypothesis developed
- **1960s** — Jerry Fodor formulates the language of thought hypothesis (Mentalese)
- **1970s** — Development of critical thinking as educational goal
- **1990s–2000s** — Embodied cognition challenges traditional mind–body separation

## Body

### Definition and Scope
The terms "thought" and "thinking" are used variably across disciplines. In common usage, they refer to conscious processes occurring independently of direct sensory input, including considering ideas, evaluating propositions, and making judgments. Memory and imagination count as forms of thought, while perception typically does not. In a narrower sense, only conscious, conceptual, or linguistic processes like judging, inferring, problem-solving, and deliberating qualify. Some definitions extend thought to all mental processes, conscious or unconscious, treating it synonymously with "mind." A key distinction is drawn between thinking (rational, dispassionate) and feeling (emotional engagement). The terms may also refer to the results of thought—beliefs, mental states, or systems of ideas.

### Theories of Thinking
**Platonism** holds that thinking is a spiritual activity where the mind perceives eternal Forms (e.g., goodness, beauty) existing in a non-physical realm. Humans access these through recollection, having once directly beheld them before birth. **Aristotelianism** and **conceptualism** reject transcendent Forms, instead locating universals in mental instantiation or concept evocation derived from sensory experience. **Inner speech theory** posits that thinking is silent verbal expression, sometimes in natural language or a specialized "Mentalese." **Associationism** views thought as the succession of ideas governed by laws of similarity, contrast, and contiguity. **Behaviorism** reduces thinking to behavioral dispositions triggered by stimuli. **Computationalism** compares thought to information processing in computers, often aligning with the **language of thought hypothesis**, which claims mental representations follow syntactic and semantic rules akin to language.

### Types of Thinking
Judgment affirms or denies propositions; reasoning draws conclusions from premises. Problem-solving involves overcoming obstacles through divergent (generating alternatives) and convergent (narrowing options) thinking. Deliberation evaluates possible actions before choosing one. Episodic memory relives past events; imagination freely recombines elements to form novel scenarios. Unconscious thought occurs without awareness and may explain sudden insights. Critical thinking involves reflective evaluation of beliefs and evidence, while positive thinking focuses on beneficial aspects of situations.

### Disciplinary Perspectives
**Phenomenology** examines the subjective experience of thinking, including cognitive phenomenology—the felt quality of thought. Debates center on whether thinking has a distinctive experiential character or merely influences sensory experience. **Metaphysics** addresses the mind–body problem: how mental processes relate to physical states. The **embodied cognition** approach rejects separation of mind and body, viewing cognition as emerging from the interaction of brain, body, and environment. **Psychology** treats thought as information processing, with cognitive psychology studying internal mental processes like memory and reasoning. **Developmental psychology**, following Piaget, traces thought’s evolution from sensorimotor action to abstract logical structures. **Psychoanalysis** emphasizes unconscious processes, with Freud’s id/ego/super-ego model and Jung’s collective unconscious highlighting repressed and inherited mental content.

### Related Concepts
The **laws of thought**—identity, non-contradiction, and excluded middle—are foundational to logic, though contested by intuitionists and dialetheists. **Counterfactual thinking** imagines alternatives to reality, playing a role in regret and learning. **Thought experiments** use imagination to test theories, exemplified by Searle’s Chinese Room argument against machine understanding. **Critical thinking** emphasizes reasoned judgment and meta-cognitive evaluation. **Positive thinking**, linked to optimism, correlates with well-being but risks unrealistic expectations.

## Terms
- **Concept formation**: Acquiring general notions that categorize objects and enable thought.
- **Computationalism**: Theory that thinking is information processing analogous to computer operations.
- **Dialectic**: Method of examining ideas through dialogue and contradiction (implied in Platonic inner dialogue).
- **Episodic memory**: Mental time travel involving re-experiencing past events with contextual detail.
- **Heuristic**: Informal rule-of-thumb strategy that aids problem-solving without guaranteeing success.
- **Intentionality**: Property of mental states being "about" or directed toward objects or states of affairs.
- **Mentalese**: Hypothetical mental language proposed by the language of thought hypothesis.
- **Productivity**: Capacity of a representational system to generate infinite unique expressions from finite elements.
- **Systematicity**: Feature of thought where related concepts are cognitively linked and processed together.
- **Turing machine**: Abstract computational model capable of executing any algorithmic procedure.

## Debates and Open Questions
Several unresolved issues persist:
- **Language and thought**: Is thinking necessarily linguistic? Inner speech theory suggests yes, but evidence of non-linguistic thought (e.g., in animals or daydreaming) challenges this.
- **Unconscious thought**: Does unconscious processing surpass conscious thought in complex problem-solving, or is this overstated?
- **Computationalism vs. embodiment**: Can thinking be fully explained as computation, or does embodied interaction with the environment play an irreducible role?
- **Cognitive phenomenology**: Does thinking have a unique subjective character, or is it derivative of sensory experience?
- **Critical thinking universality**: Are there universal standards for good thinking, or do they vary by domain and context?
- **Positive thinking efficacy**: Does positive thinking reliably improve outcomes, or can it lead to harmful complacency?
