# Douglas Engelbart

## Overview
Douglas Engelbart (1925–2013) was an American engineer and pioneer in human–computer interaction. His life's work focused on using computers to augment human intellect and boost collective problem-solving, a vision that led to foundational technologies like the computer mouse, hypertext, and networked computing, famously demonstrated in 1968.

## Timeline
- **1925** — Born in Portland, Oregon.
- **1945** — Reads Vannevar Bush's "As We May Think" while serving in the U.S. Navy.
- **1955** — Earns Ph.D. in electrical engineering from UC Berkeley.
- **1957** — Joins Stanford Research Institute (SRI).
- **1962** — Publishes "Augmenting Human Intellect: A Conceptual Framework."
- **1967** — Applies for a patent for the computer mouse.
- **1968** — Demonstrates the oN-Line System (NLS) in "The Mother of All Demos."
- **1970** — Receives U.S. patent for the computer mouse.
- **1976** — ARC lab transferred to Tymshare; NLS renamed Augment.
- **1984** — Tymshare acquired by McDonnell Douglas.
- **1986** — Retires from McDonnell Douglas.
- **1988** — Founds the Bootstrap Institute with his daughter Christina.
- **1997** — Wins the ACM Turing Award.
- **2000** — Awarded the National Medal of Technology by President Clinton.
- **2013** — Dies in Atherton, California.

## Body

### Guiding Philosophy
Engelbart's career was driven by a philosophy developed in 1950: to make the world better by dramatically improving how humans collectively solve important problems. He believed interactive computers could be the vehicle for this improvement, a radical idea when computers were seen only as number-crunching tools. Inspired by Vannevar Bush's 1945 article, he envisioned "intellectual workers" at display stations "flying through information space" to harness their collective capacity.

### SRI and the Augmentation Research Center
At SRI, Engelbart founded the Augmentation Research Center (ARC) in 1957. His team developed the oN-Line System (NLS), which integrated technologies now fundamental to computing: the mouse (patented in 1970), bitmapped screens, hypertext, and collaborative tools. These were all showcased in the seminal 1968 "Mother of All Demos." Engelbart's lab operated on a "bootstrapping strategy" to accelerate its own innovation.

### Tymshare, McDonnell Douglas, and Later Work
By the mid-1970s, Engelbart's vision of collaborative, networked computing clashed with the emerging personal computer movement. Funding from ARPA and NASA declined, and in 1976, his lab was transferred to Tymshare, which commercialized NLS as "Augment." Engelbart was marginalized at Tymshare and later at McDonnell Douglas (which acquired Tymshare in 1984), retiring in 1986. In 1988, he and his daughter founded the Bootstrap Institute (later the Doug Engelbart Institute) to promote his philosophy of boosting "Collective IQ." The Institute received some DARPA funding in the mid-1990s to modernize Augment's interface.

### Legacy and Recognition
Engelbart received numerous awards, including the 1997 ACM Turing Award and the 2000 National Medal of Technology. He was inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame in 1998. His law, observing that the intrinsic rate of human performance is exponential, is named after him. He died in 2013 after a long battle with Alzheimer's disease.

## Terms
- **Human–Computer Interaction (HCI):** The field of study focusing on the design and use of computer technology, centered on the interfaces between people and computers.
- **oN-Line System (NLS):** The collaborative, networked computer system developed by Engelbart's team, which integrated the mouse, hypertext, and other foundational technologies.
- **Hypertext:** Text displayed on a computer with references (hyperlinks) to other text that the reader can immediately access.
- **Bitmapped Screen:** A display where each pixel is individually controlled, allowing for complex graphics and text rendering.
- **Computer Mouse:** An input device that controls a cursor on a display, patented by Engelbart in 1970 as an "X-Y position indicator."
- **Bootstrapping Strategy:** Engelbart's method for accelerating innovation by having a research team use and improve its own tools and processes.
- **Collective IQ:** Engelbart's concept for dramatically improving a group's ability to solve important problems together.
- **Augment:** The commercialized version of NLS, later used and developed at Tymshare and McDonnell Douglas.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Ideological Conflict:** Engelbart's vision of collaborative, timeshare computing was in direct conflict with the personal computer movement embraced by many younger programmers, who viewed centralized systems with suspicion.
- **Marginalization of Vision:** Despite creating foundational technologies, Engelbart struggled for decades to gain sustained interest and funding for his broader philosophy of augmenting collective intelligence, often being limited by commercial and institutional priorities.
- **Influence vs. Adoption:** While his specific technologies (like the mouse) became ubiquitous, the deeper, systemic vision of using computers to boost "Collective IQ" remains an ongoing, largely unfulfilled challenge.
