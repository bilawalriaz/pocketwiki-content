# Leland Stanford

## Overview
Amasa Leland Stanford (1824–1893) was a lawyer, merchant, and railroad magnate who became the first Republican governor of California (1862–1863) and a U.S. Senator (1885–1893). As one of the "Big Four" investors, he presided over the Central Pacific Railroad during the construction of the western leg of the First Transcontinental Railroad, completed in 1869, and later controlled the Southern Pacific system. His immense wealth—estimated at $50 million—derived from railroad monopolies, land grants, and investments in Wells Fargo and Pacific Life. Stanford is widely classified as a "robber baron" for wielding corporate power to dominate Western politics and economy. His most enduring legacy is Stanford University, founded in 1885 with his wife Jane as a memorial to their deceased son, funded by an endowment of approximately $40 million.

## Body

### Early Life and Legal Career
Born in Watervliet, New York, to a farming family, Stanford attended Clinton Liberal Institute and studied law at Cazenovia Seminary (1841–1845). Admitted to the bar in 1848, he moved to Port Washington, Wisconsin, where he practiced law and was nominated for district attorney by the Whig Party in 1850. A fire destroyed his law library and property that year, prompting his migration to California in 1852 to join his five brothers.

### Mercantile Rise and the "Big Four"
In California, Stanford operated a general store for miners in Michigan Bluff (Placer County) and later a wholesale business in Sacramento. He served as a justice of the peace and helped found the Sacramento Library Association. In 1856, he became a key investor in Theodore Dehone Judah’s plan for the Central Pacific Railroad, joining Charles Crocker, Mark Hopkins, and Collis P. Huntington as the "Big Four" (or "Associates"). Incorporated June 28, 1861, the railroad made Stanford its president, a role he held until his death.

### Governor of California (1862–1863)
Stanford won the governorship in 1861 after a prior loss in 1859. The Great Flood of 1862 forced him to row a boat to his inauguration. As the state's first Republican governor, he cut the state debt in half, advocated forest conservation, and established California’s first state normal school in San Jose (later San Jose State University). Legislation during his term extended the gubernatorial term from two to four years.

**Native American Policy:** Stanford signed a law reversing part of the 1850 Act for the Government and Protection of Indians, which had permitted the enslavement of Native Americans. However, his administration continued the prosecution of the Bald Hills War against Native peoples in Northern California.

**Chinese Immigration:** In an 1862 legislative message, Stanford argued that the "settlement among us of an inferior race is to be discouraged," warning that Chinese immigrants—whom he called the "dregs" of Asia—would degrade the "superior race" and repel "desirable immigration." He lost support when it was revealed the Central Pacific was simultaneously importing Chinese laborers to build the railroad.

### Transcontinental Railroad and Southern Pacific Control
As Central Pacific president, Stanford oversaw construction eastward from Sacramento over the Sierra Nevada. On May 10, 1869, he drove the ceremonial "Last Spike" at Promontory, Utah, joining the Central Pacific with the Union Pacific. In 1868, the Associates acquired control of the Southern Pacific Railroad; Stanford served as its president until 1890 (except 1869–1870). The Southern Pacific Company was organized as a holding company in 1884; Stanford was president until 1890, when Collis Huntington forced him out—allegedly in retaliation for Stanford’s 1885 Senate election over Huntington’s ally, Aaron A. Sargent. Stanford retained the Central Pacific presidency and the Southern Pacific executive committee chairmanship until death.

### Financial and Agricultural Ventures
Stanford co-founded the Pacific Union Express Company (1868), which merged with Wells Fargo in 1870; he served as a Wells Fargo director from 1870–1884 and 1884–1893. He founded Pacific Mutual Life Insurance (now Pacific Life) in 1868, serving as president until 1876. He owned the Leland Stanford Winery (Alameda County) and the 55,000-acre Great Vina Ranch (Tehama County), which contained the world’s largest vineyard at 3,575 acres. His Palo Alto Stock Farm (Santa Clara County) bred Standardbred trotters (notably sire Electioneer) and Thoroughbreds. In 1872, he commissioned Eadweard Muybridge to photograph horse gaits, producing the proto-film *Sallie Gardner at a Gallop* (1878). The farm later became the university campus, earning the nickname "The Farm."

### U.S. Senate Career (1885–1893)
Elected to the Senate in 1885, Stanford chaired the Committee on Public Buildings and Grounds and served on the Naval Committee. He remained Central Pacific president throughout his tenure. He introduced Populist-aligned bills: one to foster worker-owned cooperatives, another to authorize currency backed by land value rather than gold alone. Neither advanced out of committee.

### Stanford University
Following their son’s death in 1884, the Stanfords founded Leland Stanford Junior University via the California Endowment Act (March 9, 1885) and a Grant of Endowment (November 14, 1885). They donated roughly $40 million (approx. $1.4 billion today). The university opened October 1, 1891, initially focused on agricultural studies; its first student was future president Herbert Hoover. Stanford had advocated employee ownership models for over 30 years, ideas he attempted to embed in the university’s structure and his Senate proposals.

### Personal Life and Death
Stanford married Jane Elizabeth Lathrop in Albany, New York, on September 30, 1850. Their only child, Leland DeWitt Stanford, was born in 1868. Stanford was a Freemason (Prometheus Lodge No. 17, Wisconsin; Michigan City Lodge No. 47, California) and an Odd Fellow. He suffered from locomotor ataxia and died of heart failure at his Palo Alto home on June 21, 1893, aged 69. He is buried in the family mausoleum on campus. Jane Stanford died in 1905 from strychnine poisoning.

## Terms
- **The Big Four / The Associates**: The four Sacramento merchants—Leland Stanford, Collis P. Huntington, Mark Hopkins, and Charles Crocker—who financed and controlled the Central Pacific Railroad.
- **Central Pacific Railroad (CPRR)**: Chartered 1861, built the western segment of the First Transcontinental Railroad from Sacramento to Promontory, Utah.
- **First Transcontinental Railroad**: Completed May 10, 1869, linking the CPRR (west) and Union Pacific Railroad (east) at Promontory, Utah.
- **Southern Pacific Railroad**: Acquired by the Associates in 1868; became the dominant rail system in the West, later organized into the Southern Pacific Company (1884).
- **Robber Baron**: Pejorative term for late-19th-century industrialists accused of using monopolistic practices, political corruption, and labor exploitation to amass wealth.
- **Pacific Life**: Founded 1868 as Pacific Mutual Life Insurance Company by Stanford and associates; he served as first president (1868–1876).
- **Locomotor Ataxia**: A neurological disorder (often tertiary syphilis) causing loss of coordination; Stanford suffered from this condition late in life.
- **Endowment Act of 1885**: California legislation (March 9, 1885) authorizing the creation of Leland Stanford Junior University.
- **Sallie Gardner at a Gallop (1878)**: Early motion study by Eadweard Muybridge, commissioned by Stanford to analyze horse gaits; a precursor to motion pictures.
- **Bald Hills War**: Conflict (1858–1864) between settlers/militia and Native American tribes in Northern California; continued under Stanford’s governorship.

## Debates and open questions
- **Robber Baron vs. Builder**: Historians debate whether Stanford’s railroad enterprises primarily represented predatory monopoly and political corruption (Richard White, *Railroaded*) or essential infrastructure development that integrated the West into the national economy.
- **Chinese Labor Hypocrisy**: Stanford’s public anti-Chinese rhetoric as governor contrasted sharply with the Central Pacific’s reliance on ~10,000–15,000 Chinese workers; scholars debate whether this reflects political opportunism, racial hierarchy, or labor economics.
- **Southern Pacific Ouster (1890)**: The source suggests Collis Huntington forced Stanford out of the Southern Pacific presidency in retaliation for Stanford’s Senate victory over Huntington’s friend Aaron Sargent; the extent of personal vendetta versus corporate power struggle is debated.
- **Populist Legislation**: Stanford’s Senate bills for worker cooperatives and land-backed currency aligned with the People’s Party but failed in committee; historians question whether this represented genuine reformism or strategic positioning.
- **Jane Stanford’s Death (1905)**: The source notes she died of strychnine poisoning; the circumstances (murder vs. accident) remain a historical mystery.
