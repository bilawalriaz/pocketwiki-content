# Cologne

## Overview
Cologne (German: Köln) is Germany's fourth-largest city and the largest in North Rhine-Westphalia, with over 1 million inhabitants. Founded by the Romans as *Colonia Claudia Ara Agrippinensium*, it is a major cultural, media, and economic hub on the Rhine, renowned for its Gothic cathedral, Romanesque churches, and vibrant carnival traditions.

## Timeline
- **38 BC** — The Ubii tribe founds the settlement *Oppidum Ubiorum*.
- **AD 50** — Romans establish *Colonia Claudia Ara Agrippinensium* (Cologne).
- **85 AD** — Cologne becomes capital of the Roman province Germania Inferior.
- **462** — The city is occupied by the Ripuarian Franks.
- **1164** — Archbishop Rainald of Dassel brings the relics of the Three Wise Men to the cathedral.
- **1288** — After the Battle of Worringen, Cologne gains independence from the archbishops and becomes a Free Imperial City.
- **1801** — Under the Treaty of Lunéville, Cologne is incorporated into the French Republic.
- **1815** — Cologne becomes part of the Kingdom of Prussia.
- **1880** — The Cologne Cathedral is completed, becoming the world's tallest building.
- **1942** — The first 1,000-bomber RAF raid ("Operation Millennium") devastates the city.
- **1945** — Cologne is taken by the U.S. First Army; its population has been reduced by 95%.
- **1975** — Cologne's population first exceeds 1 million due to incorporation of Wesseling.

## Body

### Roman and Medieval Foundations
Cologne's history begins with the Roman *Colonia Claudia Ara Agrippinensium*, named for Emperor Claudius and his wife Agrippina the Younger, who was born there. It became a major Roman provincial capital and trade center. After Frankish occupation in 462, the city flourished in the Middle Ages due to its strategic location on key European trade routes. It became a Free Imperial City in 1288 and a major member of the Hanseatic League, with its population reaching 50,000–55,000 by 1300. The archbishops of Cologne, as prince-electors, held significant secular power but were often barred from the city itself, residing in Bonn instead.

### Conflict, Destruction, and Rebirth
Cologne lost its free status during the French period (1794–1815) and became part of Prussia, leading to tensions between the Catholic population and the Protestant state. The city grew rapidly during industrialization, and its iconic cathedral was finally completed in 1880. During World War II, Cologne was one of Germany's most heavily bombed cities, with 262 air raids destroying 61% of its built-up area and reducing the population by 95% mainly through evacuation. Post-war reconstruction, guided by a 1947 master plan, rebuilt most historic landmarks but also introduced modern thoroughfares, creating a mixed cityscape.

### Modern City and Economy
Today, Cologne is a major media and insurance center, home to broadcasters like WDR and RTL, and companies such as Lufthansa and Ford Europe. It is a significant cultural hub with over 30 museums, including the Museum Ludwig for modern art, and hosts the Cologne Carnival, one of Europe's largest street festivals. The city is also a key research and transport node, with Cologne/Bonn Airport and a major trade fair grounds.

## Terms
- **Free Imperial City**: A city within the Holy Roman Empire that was subject only to the emperor, not to a local prince or duke.
- **Hanseatic League**: A medieval commercial and defensive confederation of merchant guilds and market towns in Northern Europe.
- **Kölsch**: The local Ripuarian dialect of Cologne and the name of the pale, top-fermented beer brewed there.
- **Eau de Cologne**: A type of perfume first produced in Cologne in 1709; the term became generic for such fragrances.
- **Stadtbahn**: A light rail system, partially underground, serving Cologne and its suburbs.
- **Rote Funken**: The traditional "red sparks" soldiers of the Free Imperial City, now a carnival society.
- **Cologne Mark**: A historical system of weights and measures used throughout medieval Europe.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact location of the early Jewish community approved by Emperor Constantine in 321 AD is assumed but not definitively proven.
- The post-war reconstruction of historic landmarks like the Twelve Romanesque churches was not undisputed among architects and art historians at the time.
- The "Cologne coterie," a web of political, economic, and cultural relationships, has been both a feature of the city's governance and a source of corruption scandals.
