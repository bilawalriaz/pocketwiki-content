# Edsger W. Dijkstra

## Overview
Edsger W. Dijkstra (1930–2002) was a pioneering Dutch computer scientist whose work on algorithms, programming methodology, and operating systems laid foundational principles for modern computing. He is best known for Dijkstra's algorithm, the advocacy of structured programming, and influential essays that shaped computer science as a rigorous discipline.

## Timeline
- **11 May 1930** — Born in Rotterdam, Netherlands.
- **1952** — Became the first computer programmer in the Netherlands at the Mathematical Centre in Amsterdam.
- **1956** — Formulated and solved the shortest path problem for a computer demonstration.
- **1960** — Developed the first compiler for ALGOL 60 with Jaap Zonneveld.
- **1962** — Moved to Eindhoven University of Technology as a professor.
- **Late 1960s** — Built the THE multiprogramming system, pioneering software-based paged virtual memory.
- **1968** — Published "Go To statement considered harmful," sparking debate on structured programming.
- **1972** — Received the ACM Turing Award for contributions to structured programming.
- **1973** — Joined Burroughs Corporation as its sole research fellow.
- **1984** — Accepted the Schlumberger Centennial Chair at the University of Texas at Austin.
- **6 August 2002** — Died in Nuenen, Netherlands, after a long struggle with cancer.

## Body

### Early Career and Education
Dijkstra studied mathematics, physics, and theoretical physics at the University of Leiden. He entered computing accidentally in 1952 when Adriaan van Wijngaarden offered him a job as the Netherlands' first programmer at the Mathematical Centre in Amsterdam. This experience taught him the importance of clear documentation and careful design to avoid debugging. In 1959, he earned his PhD from the University of Amsterdam for a thesis on the assembly language for the Electrologica X1 computer.

### Key Technical Contributions
At the Mathematical Centre, Dijkstra formulated the shortest path problem in 1956, though he did not publish it until 1959. With Jaap Zonneveld, he created the first ALGOL 60 compiler by 1960, a key advance for structured programming. At Eindhoven University of Technology in the late 1960s, he built the THE operating system, which influenced later systems through its use of software-based paged virtual memory. His 1968 letter "Go To statement considered harmful" catalyzed the shift toward structured programming, which uses structured control flow instead of unstructured jumps.

### Later Career and Writing
Dijkstra joined Burroughs Corporation in 1973 as its only research fellow, working from home and producing nearly 500 technical reports in the "EWD" series. In 1984, he moved to the University of Texas at Austin, where he taught until 1999. He was a prolific essayist, authoring over 1,300 papers, many handwritten and circulated privately. His writing emphasized simplicity, elegance, and rigorous thought, often avoiding bibliographies to preserve self-reliance. He received the Turing Award in 1972 and, shortly before his death, the ACM PODC Influential Paper Award for work on self-stabilization in distributed computing.

### Personality and Philosophy
Dijkstra was known for his idiosyncratic teaching style, which included oral exams and proofs on blackboards. He avoided computers in his own work, preferring typewriters and fountain pens, and believed in composing text mentally before writing. He criticized software engineering as a "doomed discipline" and opposed teaching BASIC. His lifestyle was spartan, and he valued classical music, especially Mozart.

## Terms
- **Structured programming**: A programming paradigm using structured control flow (e.g., loops, conditionals) instead of unstructured jumps like the `goto` statement.
- **Shortest path problem**: The problem of finding the shortest route between nodes in a graph, solved by Dijkstra's algorithm.
- **ALGOL 60**: An early programming language that influenced structured programming; Dijkstra built its first compiler.
- **Paged virtual memory**: A memory management technique that uses software to simulate larger memory by transferring data between RAM and disk.
- **THE operating system**: An early multiprogramming system designed by Dijkstra, notable for its layered structure and virtual memory.
- **Self-stabilization**: A property in distributed computing where a system can recover from faults to a valid state without external intervention.
- **EWD reports**: A series of over 1,300 technical documents written by Dijkstra, circulated privately and later made public.
- **Semaphore**: A synchronization construct for coordinating multiple processes in concurrent programming.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The relevance of the "Go To statement considered harmful" debate persists in discussions about programming language design and readability.
- Dijkstra's opposition to software engineering as an academic discipline raises questions about the field's theoretical foundations.
- His avoidance of computers in personal work challenges assumptions about the necessity of digital tools for intellectual productivity.
