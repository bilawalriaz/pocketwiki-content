# Percy Bysshe Shelley

## Overview
Percy Bysshe Shelley (1792–1822) was a major English Romantic poet whose radical politics, avowed atheism, and defiance of social convention led to ostracism during his brief life but secured profound influence on later literature and political thought. Expelled from Oxford for publishing *The Necessity of Atheism* (1811), he spent his adulthood in flight from creditors, government surveillance, and family scandal, producing visionary poetry that fused sceptical intellect with lyrical intensity. His self-exile in Italy (1818–1822) yielded masterpieces such as *Prometheus Unbound*, *The Mask of Anarchy*, and *Adonais*. He drowned at 29 in a boating accident. Though dismissed by contemporaries as a "beautiful and ineffectual angel" (Matthew Arnold), his reputation rose decisively from the 1960s onward; Harold Bloom calls him "a superb craftsman, a lyric poet without rival, and surely one of the most advanced sceptical intellects ever to write a poem."

## Timeline
- **4 Aug 1792** — Born at Field Place, Warnham, Sussex, eldest son of Sir Timothy Shelley, Whig MP.
- **25 Mar 1811** — Expelled from University College, Oxford, with T. J. Hogg for refusing to disavow *The Necessity of Atheism*.
- **28 Aug 1811** — Elopes to Edinburgh and marries Harriet Westbrook (16); both fathers cut allowances.
- **1812–13** — Irish political tour; publishes *Queen Mab* (private edition, 1813), a utopian allegory preaching atheism, republicanism, vegetarianism.
- **28 Jul 1814** — Elopes to Europe with Mary Godwin (16), daughter of William Godwin and Mary Wollstonecraft, and Claire Clairmont.
- **1816** — Summer at Lake Geneva with Lord Byron; writes "Hymn to Intellectual Beauty" and "Mont Blanc"; Mary conceives *Frankenstein*.
- **30 Dec 1816** — Marries Mary Godwin after Harriet’s suicide (Dec 1816); Court of Chancery denies him custody of his children by Harriet (1817).
- **12 Mar 1818** — Leaves England permanently for Italy with Mary, Claire, and children.
- **1818–19** — In Rome: completes *Julian and Maddalo*, *Prometheus Unbound*, *The Cenci*; son William dies (Jun 1819).
- **Aug 1819** — Writes *The Mask of Anarchy* in response to Peterloo Massacre; suppressed until 1832.
- **1820–21** — In Pisa: joins "Pisan circle" with Byron; writes *A Defence of Poetry* (unpublished), *Adonais* (elegy for Keats).
- **8 Jul 1822** — Drowns in the Gulf of La Spezia sailing the *Don Juan* from Livorno to Lerici.
- **16 Aug 1822** — Cremated at Viareggio; ashes buried in Protestant Cemetery, Rome.

## Body

### Early Life and Radicalization
Shelley’s privileged upbringing at Field Place and Syon House Academy gave way to misery at Eton (1804–10), where bullying earned him the nickname "Mad Shelley." He developed lifelong interests in science, the occult, and Platonic idealism, encouraged by teacher James Lind. At Oxford (1810–11), he neglected lectures for reading and experiments, falling under the influence of Thomas Jefferson Hogg, who radicalized him politically and religiously. Their collaboration on *The Necessity of Atheism* and Shelley’s distribution of it to Oxford authorities led to expulsion (25 March 1811). His refusal to submit to his father’s demand for supervised study caused a permanent rupture.

### First Marriage and Political Activism
Shelley married Harriet Westbrook in August 1811, driven by emotional crisis and a desire to "rescue" her. The couple, joined by Harriet’s sister Eliza and Hogg, lived itinerantly in the Lake District, Ireland, Wales, and Devon. Shelley distributed radical tracts in Dublin (1812), advocating Catholic emancipation and repeal of the Acts of Union while rejecting violence. Government surveillance intensified. At Tremadog, Wales (1812–13), he wrote *Queen Mab*, a blank-verse poem with extensive notes attacking religion, monarchy, marriage, and meat-eating; its private edition (250 copies) circulated widely in radical circles. Marital breakdown followed: Shelley’s infatuation with Harriet de Boinville and her daughter Cornelia, combined with Harriet’s resentment of his absences and debts, led to separation by 1814.

### Elopement with Mary Godwin and the Geneva Summer
In May 1814 Shelley began visiting William Godwin, whose *Political Justice* had shaped his youth. He fell in love with Godwin’s daughter Mary; they declared love at her mother Mary Wollstonecraft’s grave (26 June) and eloped to Europe (28 July) with Claire Clairmont. A fraught six-week tour of war-torn France, Switzerland, Germany, and Holland ended in penury. Back in London, Mary’s premature daughter died (Feb 1815); Shelley likely had a sexual relationship with Claire, while Mary may have had one with Hogg. Financial relief came only in April 1816 after his grandfather’s death and a settlement with his father.

In May 1816 Claire initiated a liaison with Lord Byron and arranged a meeting at Lake Geneva. Shelley and Byron formed a close intellectual bond. Discussions on galvanism and "philosophical doctrines" sparked Mary’s *Frankenstein*; Shelley suffered a panic attack with hallucinations during a reading of Coleridge. Boating tours inspired "Hymn to Intellectual Beauty" and "Mont Blanc," the latter an atheistic reply to Coleridge. Shelley’s public atheist declarations in guest books hardened British opinion against him.

### Italian Exile and Major Works
The Shelleys left England for Italy on 12 March 1818, fleeing "tyranny civil and religious" and seeking a climate for Shelley’s lung condition. In Venice they delivered Claire’s daughter Allegra to Byron; their own daughter Clara died in September. In Naples (Dec 1818–Feb 1819) Shelley registered the birth of Elena Adelaide Shelley, naming himself father and Mary mother; Elena’s parentage remains unresolved (adoption, Claire, servant Elise Foggi, or another). Elena died in 1820.

In Rome (1819) Shelley completed three major works despite nephritis and tuberculosis: *Julian and Maddalo* (autobiographical dialogue with Byron), *Prometheus Unbound* (lyrical drama reimagining Aeschylus: Prometheus overcomes Jupiter through love, not force), and *The Cenci* (verse drama on Renaissance incest and parricide, his only work with two authorized editions in his lifetime). The Peterloo Massacre (Aug 1819) provoked *The Mask of Anarchy*, a seminal statement of nonviolent resistance ("Rise like Lions after slumber / In unvanquishable number"), suppressed by Leigh Hunt until 1832.

In Florence and Pisa (1820–22), Shelley engaged with the "Pisan circle" (Byron, Medwin, Trelawny, the Williamses). He wrote *A Philosophical View of Reform* (unpublished, 1820), a sophisticated political essay justifying insurrection only as a last resort against armed tyranny. *Adonais* (1821), a pastoral elegy for Keats, and *Epipsychidion* (for Teresa Viviani) followed. His final months were marked by hallucinations, marital crisis (Mary’s near-fatal miscarriage, Jun 1822), and the unfinished *The Triumph of Life*, a Dantean vision of a chaotic triumphal procession.

### Political, Religious, and Ethical Views
**Politics:** Shelley advocated Catholic emancipation, republicanism, parliamentary reform, franchise extension, free speech/assembly, abolition of aristocratic/clerical privilege, and wealth redistribution. Influenced by Rousseau, Paine, Godwin, Wollstonecraft, and Hunt, he tempered published views to avoid seditious libel prosecution. *Queen Mab* became a Chartist/Owenist bible; *A Philosophical View of Reform* (1820, pub. 1920) is his fullest political statement.

**Nonviolence:** Rooted in his analysis of the French Revolution’s descent into Napoleonic despotism, Shelley argued violence begets military rule. *An Address, to the Irish People* (1812) rejects force; *The Mask of Anarchy* (1819) articulates mass nonviolent resistance. Gandhi knew the poem; influence may have passed via Thoreau.

**Religion:** An avowed atheist influenced by Holbach’s *Système de la nature*, Shelley saw organized religion as inseparable from social oppression. *The Necessity of Atheism* (1811) and *Queen Mab* drew prosecution for religious libel; many works were expurgated pre-publication.

**Free Love:** Drawing on Wollstonecraft and early Godwin, Shelley called marriage "a system... more studiously hostile to human happiness." He argued sexual connection should be free among lovers, lasting only while mutual love endures, and denied this led to promiscuity. He forgave Hogg’s advance on Harriet and likely encouraged a Mary–Hogg liaison.

**Vegetarianism:** Adopted March 1812 under John Frank Newton’s influence, sustained with lapses. Shelley emphasized health, animal suffering, land-use inefficiency, and economic inequality. His advocacy inspired the Vegetarian Society (1847) and George Bernard Shaw.

### Reception and Legacy
In life, Shelley’s works sold poorly (editions of 250); only *The Cenci* reached a second edition. Mainstream reviewers attacked his character and radicalism; Hazlitt dismissed his style as "a passionate dream." Posthumously, Mary Shelley’s editions (1824, 1839) softened his radicalism; Arnold’s "beautiful and ineffectual angel" dominated Victorian reception. He influenced Browning, Swinburne, Hardy, Yeats, and appeared as characters in Peacock, Eliot, Hardy. Twentieth-century critics (Eliot, Leavis, Tate, Auden) faulted his intellect and style. Since the 1960s, scholars (Reiman, Bloom, Leader, O’Neill) have recovered his Spenserian/Miltonic mastery, formal versatility, and the "complex interplay of sceptical, idealist, and materialist ideas." Major editorial projects since the 1980s (Bodleian Manuscripts, Complete Poetry, Prose Works) establish reliable texts. The rediscovered *Poetical Essay on the Existing State of Things* (1811) appeared in 2006. Debate continues on Shelley’s role in *Frankenstein*: Robinson argues for collaboration; Sampson finds his edits minor.

## Terms
- **Romanticism**: Late 18th–early 19th-century literary movement emphasizing emotion, imagination, nature, and individualism; Shelley is a central "second-generation" figure.
- **Atheism**: Disbelief in deities; Shelley’s public avowal (1811) caused expulsion, prosecution risk, and social ostracism.
- **Seditious libel**: Crime of publishing material bringing government into hatred/contempt; constrained Shelley’s publication strategies.
- **Nonviolent resistance**: Political action without physical force; *The Mask of Anarchy* (1819) is an early modern formulation.
- **Free love**: Doctrine that sexual relationships should be voluntary, non-coercive, and dissoluble at will; Shelley derived it from Wollstonecraft/Godwin.
- **Vegetarianism**: Abstention from meat; Shelley adopted it for ethical, health, ecological, and economic reasons (1812).
- **Pastoral elegy**: Poem mourning a death using rural/shepherd imagery; *Adonais* (1821) for Keats is a major example.
- **Verse drama**: Play written in poetry; *The Cenci* (1819) and *Prometheus Unbound* (1820) are Shelley’s chief works.
- **Owenism/Chartism**: British working-class movements (1820s–40s) for cooperative socialism and political reform; *Queen Mab* was a key text.
- **Pisan circle**: Expatriate intellectual group in Pisa (1821–22) centered on Byron, including Shelley, Medwin, Trelawny, the Williamses.

## Debates and open questions
- **Parentage of Elena Adelaide Shelley** (1818–20): Adopted? Shelley’s child by Claire, Elise Foggi, or another? No conclusive evidence.
- **Nature of Shelley’s "attacks"**: Were claimed assaults (1813, 1818, 1820) real, hallucinations from stress/illness, or fabrications to escape surveillance/creditors?
- **Shelley’s contribution to *Frankenstein***: Collaborator (Robinson) vs. minor line-editor (Sampson); manuscript evidence is ambiguous.
- **Sincerity of free-love practice**: Did Shelley’s relationships (with Claire, Jane Williams, encouragement of Mary–Hogg) reflect principle or self-justification?
- **Cause of death**: Boat design defect (Mary Shelley) vs. severe storm and poor seamanship (consensus).
- **Heart relic**: The organ retrieved from the cremation—heart calcified by tuberculosis, or liver? Disposition (Bournemouth vs. Christchurch Priory) uncertain.
