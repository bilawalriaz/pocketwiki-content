# Biology

## Overview

Biology is the scientific study of life and living organisms, encompassing their structure, function, growth, origin, evolution, and distribution. It is organized around five fundamental themes: the cell as the basic unit of life, genes and heredity as the basis of inheritance, evolution as the driver of biological diversity, energy transformation for sustaining life processes, and homeostasis (the maintenance of internal stability). Modern biology is grounded in the theory of evolution by natural selection, first articulated by Charles Darwin, and in the molecular understanding of genes encoded in DNA, which has led to applications in medicine, agriculture, biotechnology, and environmental science.

## Body

### Etymology

The word "biology" comes from Greek *bíos* (βίος, 'life', from the Proto-Indo-European root *gwei-, to live) and *logia* (λογία, 'study of'). The compound appears in the title of Volume 3 of Michael Christoph Hanow's *Philosophiae naturalis sive physicae dogmaticae* (1766). In its modern sense, the term appears to have been introduced independently by Thomas Beddoes (1799), Karl Friedrich Burdach (1800), Gottfried Reinhold Treviranus (*Biologie oder Philosophie der lebenden Natur*, 1802), and Jean-Baptiste Lamarck (*Hydrogéologie*, 1802).

### History

The earliest roots of science, including medicine, trace to ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia (c. 3000–1200 BCE), shaping Greek natural philosophy. Aristotle (384–322 BCE) explored biological causation and diversity; his successor Theophrastus began the scientific study of plants. Medieval Islamic scholars writing on biology included al-Jahiz (781–869) and Al-Dīnawarī (828–896), who wrote on botany.

Biology accelerated with Anton van Leeuwenhoek's improvement of the microscope, revealing spermatozoa, bacteria, infusoria, and microscopic life. Jan Swammerdam's investigations spurred entomology and advanced microscopic dissection and staining techniques. In 1838, Schleiden and Schwann promoted the ideas that the cell is the basic unit of organisms and that individual cells have all characteristics of life—though they opposed the idea that all cells come from division of other cells, supporting spontaneous generation. Robert Remak and Rudolf Virchow reified this third tenet, and by the 1860s most biologists accepted all three, consolidating cell theory.

Taxonomy became a focus of natural historians: Linnaeus published a basic taxonomy in 1735 and introduced scientific names in the 1750s. Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon, treated species as artificial categories and living forms as malleable, even suggesting common descent. Serious evolutionary thinking began with Lamarck's coherent theory. Darwin, combining Humboldt's biogeography, Lyell's uniformitarian geology, Malthus's population writings, and his own morphological expertise, forged a more successful theory based on natural selection; Alfred Russel Wallace independently reached the same conclusions.

Mendel's 1865 work outlined inheritance principles, but its significance was unrealized until the early 20th century, when the modern synthesis reconciled Darwinian evolution with classical genetics. Hershey and Chase's experiments in the 1940s–early 1950s pointed to DNA as the carrier of genes. The discovery of the double-helical structure of DNA by Watson and Crick in 1953 marked the transition to molecular genetics. The genetic code was cracked by Har Gobind Khorana, Robert W. Holley, and Marshall Warren Nirenberg after DNA was understood to contain codons. The Human Genome Project launched in 1990 to map the human genome.

### Fields

**Biochemistry** studies chemical processes within and relating to living organisms. **Molecular biology** seeks to understand the molecular basis of biological activity in and between cells, centered on nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) and proteins, examining their structure, function, and interactions in replication, transcription, translation, and protein synthesis. The 1953 Miller–Urey experiment showed organic compounds could be synthesized abiotically in a closed system mimicking early Earth, suggesting complex organic molecules could have arisen spontaneously in abiogenesis (the origin of life from non-living matter).

**Cell biology** studies the structure, function, and behaviour of cells. **Bioenergetics**, a field in biochemistry and cell biology, concerns energy flow through living systems, including cellular respiration and metabolic and enzymatic processes.

**Genetics** is the scientific study of inheritance. Classical genetics studies how genes and traits pass from parents to offspring via Mendelian inheritance; a Punnett square predicts test cross results. The chromosome theory of inheritance—that genes are found on chromosomes—was supported by Thomas Morgan's fruit fly experiments, which established sex linkage between eye color and sex.

**Evolutionary developmental biology** (evo-devo) compares developmental processes across organisms to infer how they evolved. It grew from 19th-century embryology, which lacked molecular understanding. Darwin noted similar embryos implied common ancestry, but progress waited until the 1970s, when recombinant DNA technology brought embryology together with molecular genetics. A key discovery was homeotic genes that regulate development across eukaryotes. The field explores deep homology: dissimilar organs like insect, vertebrate, and cephalopod mollusc eyes, long thought to have evolved separately, are controlled by similar genes from the evo-devo gene toolkit.

**Evolutionary biology** analyzes evolution's mechanisms. Population genetics studies how genetic variation develops, is inherited, and how evolutionary mechanisms shape a population's genetic composition. Some research addresses phenomena poorly accounted for in the modern synthesis, including speciation, the evolution of sexual reproduction, cooperation, ageing, and evolvability.

**Ecology** studies the distribution and abundance of life and interactions between organisms and their environment. **Systematics** studies the diversification of living forms and their relationships through time, visualized as evolutionary trees via phylogenetics, creating a unified taxonomy. **Conservation biology** studies conservation of Earth's biodiversity, protecting species, habitats, and ecosystems from excessive extinction rates. Concern stems from estimates that up to 50% of all species will disappear within 50 years, contributing to poverty and starvation and resetting evolution's course; conservation biologists research biodiversity loss trends and their effects on sustaining human well-being.

## Terms

- **Homeostasis**: The maintenance of internal stability in living organisms.
- **Cell theory**: The consolidated tenets that the cell is the basic unit of organisms, individual cells have all characteristics of life, and all cells come from division of other cells.
- **Natural selection**: Darwin's mechanism of evolution, where traits favoring survival and reproduction become more common.
- **Modern synthesis**: The early 20th-century reconciliation of Darwinian evolution with classical genetics.
- **Codons**: Units in DNA that encode genetic information; cracking the genetic code revealed how they specify amino acids.
- **Abiogenesis**: The process by which complex organic molecules could have arisen spontaneously on early Earth.
- **Homeotic genes**: Genes that regulate development across a wide range of eukaryotes.
- **Deep homology**: The finding that dissimilar organs are controlled by similar genes from the evo-devo gene toolkit.
- **Phylogenetics**: The study of evolutionary relationships, visualized as evolutionary trees.
- **Punnett square**: A tool used to predict the results of a test cross in classical genetics.

## Debates and open questions

Schleiden and Schwann initially opposed the idea that all cells come from division of other cells, continuing to support spontaneous generation; Remak and Virchow later reified this tenet. Some fields of evolutionary research try to explain phenomena poorly accounted for in the modern evolutionary synthesis, including speciation, the evolution of sexual reproduction, cooperation, ageing, and evolvability. Conservation biology is driven by contested estimates suggesting up to 50% of all species will disappear within the next 50 years.
