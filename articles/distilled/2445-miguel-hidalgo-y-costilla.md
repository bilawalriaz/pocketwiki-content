# Miguel Hidalgo y Costilla

## Overview
Miguel Hidalgo y Costilla (1753–1811) was a Mexican Catholic priest and the primary leader of the initial phase of the Mexican War of Independence. His 1810 "Cry of Dolores" sparked a massive popular uprising against Spanish colonial rule, making him a foundational figure in Mexican history, though the war he started was ultimately won by others.

- **1778** — Ordained as a Catholic priest.

- **1802** — Becomes parish priest in Dolores, Guanajuato.
- **16 September 1810** — Issues the "Cry of Dolores," launching the independence revolt.

- **17 January 1811** — Decisive royalist victory at the Battle of Calderón Bridge.
- **21 March 1811** — Hidalgo and other insurgent leaders are captured at Norias de Baján.
- **30 July 1811** — Hidalgo is executed by firing squad in Chihuahua.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Hidalgo was born to criollo (American-born Spanish) parents. He studied at the Colegio de San Nicolás and later at the Royal and Pontifical University of Mexico, earning degrees in philosophy and theology. His study of French allowed him to read Enlightenment works, which influenced his views. Ordained in 1778, he taught at San Nicolás and became its rector in 1790. He was ousted in 1792 for revising teaching methods and irregular financial handling. As a parish priest in Dolores, he promoted economic activities like olive and grape cultivation to help the poor, but these were prohibited by Spanish mercantilist policies designed to protect imports from Spain.

### Background to the War
A conspiracy against Spanish rule formed in Querétaro, involving figures like Ignacio Allende and the mayor's wife, Josefa Ortiz de Domínguez. The crisis was precipitated by the Napoleonic Wars: in 1808, Napoleon forced the Spanish king to abdicate and installed his brother, Joseph Bonaparte. This created a power vacuum in New Spain (colonial Mexico) and fueled resentment against the peninsulares (Spanish-born officials) who controlled the viceroyalty.

### The Cry of Dolores and the Insurgent Army
On 16 September 1810, Hidalgo issued his famous *Grito de Dolores*, calling for rebellion in the name of the captive King Ferdinand VII. His movement quickly swelled into a massive, undisciplined army of peasants, mestizos, and indigenous people, numbering nearly 90,000 at its peak. The army adopted the Virgin of Guadalupe as its banner. While initially successful, capturing cities like Guanajuato, the insurgents' lack of training and discipline led to atrocities that alienated potential criollo allies. Hidalgo's leadership was more political than military; he was eventually stripped of military command by Allende after a series of defeats.

### Defeat and Execution
After a costly victory at Monte de las Cruces, Hidalgo controversially decided not to attack Mexico City, retreating instead. His army was defeated at Aculco and later, decisively, at the Battle of Calderón Bridge in January 1811. Fleeing north, Hidalgo was betrayed and captured in March 1811. He was defrocked, excommunicated, and executed by firing squad on 30 July 1811. His head was displayed in Guanajuato for ten years as a warning.

## Terms
*   **Criollo:** A person of Spanish descent born in the Americas, often with a distinct identity from peninsulares.
*   **Peninsular:** A person born in Spain who held the highest political and ecclesiastical offices in the colonies.
*   **Mestizo:** A person of mixed European and Indigenous ancestry.
*   **New Spain:** The name of the Spanish viceroyalty that included modern-day Mexico, Central America, and parts of the southwestern United States.
*   **Mercantilism:** An economic system where colonies exist to enrich the mother country, often through trade restrictions.
*   **Grito de Dolores:** The "Cry of Dolores," Hidalgo's call to arms on 16 September 1810, which began the war.
*   **Bajío:** A fertile, economically important region in north-central Mexico where the revolt began.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   Historians debate Hidalgo's reasons for not attacking Mexico City after Monte de las Cruces. Theories include the poor discipline of his army and the strength of the city's defenses.
*   The primary motive of Hidalgo's revolt is debated: was it for independence, or to support Ferdinand VII against the Napoleonic usurper while opposing the local peninsular government?
*   The social composition and motivations of his massive army—whether driven by a nascent nationalism or by local grievances over hunger and oppression—remain subjects of scholarly analysis.
