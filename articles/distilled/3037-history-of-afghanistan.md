# History of Afghanistan

## Overview
Afghanistan's history is defined by its strategic location at the crossroads of Central Asia, the Middle East, and the Indian subcontinent, making it a perpetual "roundabout" for trade, culture, and invasion. Its modern nation-state is traditionally considered to have been founded in 1747 with the Durrani Empire, but the country has been shaped by millennia of conquest and conflict, from ancient empires to the recent 2021 Taliban takeover.

## Timeline
- **c. 52,000 years ago** — Evidence of early human habitation at Darra-e Kur.
- **c. 3300–1300 BCE** — Indus Valley Civilisation extends into northern Afghanistan.
- **330 BCE** — Alexander the Great invades the region.
- **3rd century CE** — First recorded mention of the name "Afghan" by the Sasanian Empire.
- **1747** — Ahmad Shah Durrani establishes the Durrani Empire, considered the foundation of modern Afghanistan.
- **1838–1842** — First Anglo-Afghan War; British forces are repelled.
- **1878–1880** — Second Anglo-Afghan War; Britain gains control over Afghan foreign policy.
- **1919** — Third Anglo-Afghan War; Afghanistan gains full independence.
- **1973** — King Zahir Shah is overthrown in a coup by Mohammad Daoud Khan, ending the monarchy.
- **1979** — Soviet Union invades Afghanistan, backing the communist government.
- **1996** — The Taliban seizes Kabul and establishes the Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan.
- **2001** — US-led invasion topples the Taliban government after the 9/11 attacks.
- **2021** — Taliban recaptures Kabul; the US-backed government collapses.

## Body

### Ancient and Classical Periods
Human habitation in Afghanistan dates back to the early Middle Paleolithic era. The region was part of early urban civilizations, including the Indus Valley Civilisation and the Bactria–Margiana complex. It was later conquered by the Achaemenid Persians, Alexander the Great, and the Maurya Empire, which spread Buddhism. A succession of Hellenistic, Indo-Scythian, Indo-Parthian, and Kushan empires followed, blending Greek, Iranian, and Indian cultures. The Kushan Empire, under Kanishka the Great, was a major patron of Buddhism and linked Indian Ocean trade with the Silk Road.

### Medieval Conquests and Islamic Rule
From the 7th century, Arab armies introduced Islam, though full control was slow. The region saw rule by the Ghaznavid and Ghurid empires before the devastating Mongol invasion of 1221, which destroyed cities like Balkh and Herat. The Timurid Empire later rebuilt infrastructure, making Herat a cultural center. By the 16th century, the area was divided between the Mughal, Safavid, and Uzbek khanates. In 1709, Mirwais Hotak led a successful Afghan revolt against Safavid rule, establishing the short-lived Hotaki dynasty.

### The Durrani Empire and the "Great Game"
After the Hotakis were overthrown by Persia's Nader Shah, his assassination in 1747 allowed Ahmad Shah Durrani to unite the Pashtun tribes and found the Durrani Empire. Following its decline, Afghanistan was reunited in the 19th century by Dost Mohammad Khan of the Barakzai dynasty. This period saw Afghanistan become a buffer state in the "Great Game" rivalry between the British and Russian Empires. The British fought three Anglo-Afghan Wars (1838–1919), ultimately recognizing Afghanistan's full independence in 1919 under King Amanullah Khan.

### Monarchy, Republic, and Soviet War
Amanullah Khan's modernization reforms sparked civil war, leading to his overthrow. Stability returned under Nadir Khan and his son Zahir Shah, whose 1964 constitution allowed limited democracy. In 1973, Zahir Shah was deposed by Mohammad Daoud Khan, who established a republic. In 1978, a communist coup (the Saur Revolution) led to a brutal Soviet invasion in 1979. The ensuing war saw the US and Saudi Arabia fund the mujahideen resistance, causing massive destruction and refugee flows before the Soviet withdrawal in 1989.

### Civil War and the Taliban
After the Soviet-backed government fell in 1992, Afghanistan descended into civil war among mujahideen factions. The Taliban, a fundamentalist movement originating from Pakistani madrasas, captured Kabul in 1996 and imposed a harsh interpretation of Islamic law. They were opposed by the Northern Alliance, led by Ahmad Shah Massoud. Following the 9/11 attacks, a US-led invasion in 2001 toppled the Taliban, establishing the Islamic Republic of Afghanistan. Despite two decades of international aid and NATO presence, the Taliban insurgency persisted.

### The Taliban Return
In 2021, following a US troop withdrawal, the Taliban rapidly seized control of the country, capturing Kabul on August 15 and causing the collapse of the Afghan government. They re-established the Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan with an interim government of Taliban members. The takeover led to a severe humanitarian crisis, with widespread famine and economic collapse, as the international community largely suspended aid and froze Afghan central bank assets.

## Terms
- **Loya Jirga**: A traditional grand assembly of elders and leaders in Pashtun culture, convened to decide on important matters.
- **Silk Road**: An ancient network of trade routes connecting the East and West, central to Afghanistan's history as a crossroads.
- **Satrapy**: A province or administrative region of the ancient Persian Achaemenid Empire.
- **Mujahideen**: Islamic guerrilla fighters, notably those who resisted the Soviet invasion (1979–1989) and later the US-backed government.
- **Northern Alliance**: A military-political coalition of anti-Taliban factions, primarily Tajik and Uzbek, that controlled parts of Afghanistan from 1996 to 2001.
- **Durand Line**: The porous border between Afghanistan and Pakistan, established in 1893, which has been a source of conflict and a route for insurgent movement.
- **Haqqani Network**: A militant group with ties to the Taliban and al-Qaeda, known for high-profile attacks in Afghanistan and alleged links to Pakistan's intelligence services.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent of foreign influence, particularly by Pakistan's ISI, in shaping the Taliban's rise and persistence remains a central point of contention.
- Scholars debate whether the Soviet-Afghan War constitutes a genocide, given the scale of civilian casualties and destruction.
- The effectiveness and legacy of the 2001–2021 international intervention are heavily disputed, with arguments focusing on state-building failures versus gains in education and women's rights.
- The future of the Taliban's unrecognized government and the prospects for an inclusive political settlement in Afghanistan remain uncertain.
