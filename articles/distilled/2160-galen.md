# Galen

## Overview
Galen of Pergamon (129–c. 216 CE) was a Greek physician, surgeon, and philosopher in the Roman Empire whose comprehensive synthesis of medical knowledge dominated Western and Islamic medicine for over 1,300 years. His work in anatomy, physiology, and pharmacology established foundational theories, such as the four humors, that shaped medical practice until the Renaissance. Galen's legacy is defined both by his profound influence and by the later challenges to his authority from empirical observation.

## Timeline
- **129 CE** — Born in Pergamon (modern Bergama, Turkey) to a wealthy Greek architect.
- **c. 145** — Father has a dream from Asclepius, directing Galen to study medicine.
- **148** — Father dies; Galen, age 19, becomes independently wealthy.
- **157** — Returns to Pergamon as physician to gladiators of the High Priest of Asia.
- **162** — Travels to Rome, where his public demonstrations cause conflict with other physicians.
- **169** — Summoned to treat Emperor Marcus Aurelius during the Antonine Plague; becomes court physician.
- **180** — Becomes personal physician to Emperor Commodus.
- **c. 199–216** — Date of death is debated; likely between ages 70 and 87.

## Body

### Biography and Education
Galen received a broad education in philosophy and science from his father. After his father's death, he studied medicine at the Asclepieion in Pergamon and traveled widely, including to Alexandria, to learn various medical theories. He gained practical experience treating gladiators, where he observed wounds as "windows into the body." In Rome, his reliance on observation and prognosis over divination brought him into conflict with rival physicians, leading him to leave the city temporarily. He later served as physician to emperors Marcus Aurelius, Commodus, and Septimius Severus.

### Medical Theory and Practice
Galen's medicine was rooted in the Hippocratic theory of the four humors (blood, yellow bile, black bile, phlegm), which he linked to the four temperaments (sanguine, choleric, melancholic, phlegmatic). He was a skilled surgeon who performed procedures on the brain and eyes and advocated for venesection (bloodletting), a practice he defended against critics. His anatomical knowledge was based primarily on dissections of animals, especially Barbary apes and pigs, as human dissection was forbidden in the Roman Empire.

### Anatomy and Physiology
Galen's anatomical reports, though based on animal models, were largely uncontested until the 16th century. He clarified the anatomy of the trachea and larynx, demonstrated the voice is controlled by the brain, and made key observations on the spinal cord and nerves. His theory of the circulatory system posited that blood was produced in the liver, moved to the heart, and was distributed in two separate systems (venous and arterial), with air from the lungs mixing in the heart via small holes in the septum. This model was not challenged until Ibn al-Nafis described pulmonary circulation c. 1242 and Andreas Vesalius published human dissection findings in 1543.

### Philosophy and Psychology
Galen saw medicine and philosophy as intertwined. He developed a tripartite model of the soul (rational, spiritual, appetitive) localized in the brain, heart, and liver, respectively, opposing Stoic views. He believed the mind and body were not separate, proposing a physiological basis for mental disorders. His work *On the Diagnosis and Cure of the Soul's Passion* is an early text on psychotherapy, advocating counseling to treat psychological issues caused by excessive passions.

### Legacy and Transmission
Galen's prolific writings (possibly 500 treatises) were preserved in the Byzantine Empire and translated into Arabic by scholars like Hunayn ibn Ishaq. In the Islamic world, physicians like al-Razi and Ibn al-Nafis studied and challenged his work. From the 11th century, Latin translations reintroduced Galen to medieval Europe, where his authority became dogmatic. Renaissance anatomists like Vesalius, through human dissection, finally disproved key Galenic theories, though his influence on practice, such as bloodletting, persisted into the 19th century.

## Terms
- **Four Humors**: The Hippocratic theory that health depends on the balance of blood, yellow bile, black bile, and phlegm.
- **Venesection**: The practice of bloodletting to treat disease by restoring humoral balance.
- **Vivisection**: Surgical experimentation on living animals, which Galen used due to the prohibition on human dissection.
- **Pneuma**: A vital spirit or breath that Galen believed operated within the body's systems.
- **Localization of Function**: The theory that specific mental faculties reside in specific organs (e.g., rational thought in the brain).
- **Antonine Plague**: A pandemic (likely smallpox) that struck the Roman Empire from 165–180 CE, which Galen witnessed and described.
- **Galenism**: The medical system and authority derived from Galen's writings, which dominated for centuries.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Date of Death**: Sources conflict; the 11th-century Suda lexicon states age 70 (c. 199 CE), while Arabic sources suggest age 87 (c. 216 CE).
- **Authenticity of Works**: Many texts attributed to Galen are considered spurious, and forgeries were common in antiquity and the Middle Ages.
- **Mind-Body Relationship**: Scholarly debate continues over whether Galen was a materialist who identified the soul with bodily mixtures or held a more dualistic view.
- **Accuracy of Animal Models**: Galen's use of animal anatomy to infer human structure was criticized even in his time and later proven to contain significant errors.
