# Biochemistry

## Overview

Biochemistry is the study of chemical processes within and relating to living organisms, functioning as a sub-discipline of both chemistry and biology. It focuses on how biological macromolecules—proteins, nucleic acids, carbohydrates, and lipids—give rise to cellular processes, relating directly to the understanding of tissues, organs, and organismal structure and function. The field is divided into three core areas: structural biology, enzymology, and metabolism. Over the late 20th century, biochemical methodology became central to almost all life sciences, with applications in medicine (disease causes and cures), nutrition (health maintenance and deficiency effects), agriculture (crop improvement and pest control), and biotechnology (manipulating living systems for research, industry, and diagnostics).

## Timeline

- **1828** — Friedrich Wöhler synthesizes urea from inorganic precursors, challenging vitalism (the belief that life requires a "vital principle" distinct from non-living matter).
- **1833** — Anselme Payen discovers the first enzyme, diastase (amylase).
- **1842** — Justus von Liebig publishes *Animal Chemistry*, presenting a chemical theory of metabolism.
- **1858** — Vinzenz Kletzinsky prints "Compendium der Biochemie" in Vienna, the first use of the term "biochemistry."
- **1877** — Felix Hoppe-Seyler uses "biochemie" as a synonym for physiological chemistry and argues for dedicated institutes.
- **1897** — Eduard Buchner demonstrates alcoholic fermentation in cell-free extracts, proving complex biochemistry occurs outside living cells.
- **1903** — Carl Neuberg often cited as coining the word "biochemistry" (also credited to Franz Hofmeister).
- **1950s** — James D. Watson, Francis Crick, Rosalind Franklin, and Maurice Wilkins solve DNA structure, linking it to genetic information transfer.
- **1958** — George Beadle and Edward Tatum win Nobel Prize for "one gene–one enzyme" hypothesis demonstrated in fungi.
- **1988** — Colin Pitchfork becomes first person convicted of murder using DNA evidence, launching forensic biochemistry.
- **2006** — Andrew Z. Fire and Craig C. Mello win Nobel Prize for discovering RNA interference (RNAi) in gene silencing.

## Body

### History and Foundations
Biochemistry as a specific discipline emerged in the 19th century, though its roots trace to ancient Greek inquiries into life's composition. Early milestones include Lavoisier's 18th-century studies on fermentation and respiration, Wöhler's 1828 urea synthesis (which challenged vitalism by creating an organic compound from inorganic materials), and Payen's 1833 discovery of the first enzyme, diastase. Liebig's 1842 *Animal Chemistry* systematized a chemical theory of metabolism. The term "biochemistry" appeared in 1858 (Kletzinsky) and was institutionalized by Hoppe-Seyler in 1877; Neuberg and Hofmeister are also credited with coining it c. 1903. Buchner's 1897 cell-free fermentation proved that "vital" processes obey standard chemistry. Since the mid-20th century, techniques like chromatography, X-ray diffraction, NMR spectroscopy, radioisotopic labeling, electron microscopy, and molecular dynamics simulations have enabled molecular-level analysis of pathways such as glycolysis and the Krebs cycle.

### Chemical Elements of Life
Approximately two dozen chemical elements are essential for biological life. Just six elements—carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, calcium and phosphorus—make up almost 99% of the mass of living cells, including those in the human body. This short list is abbreviated CHNOPS (carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, and sulfur) as an aid to memory. In addition to the six major elements that compose most of the human body, humans require smaller amounts of possibly 18 more. Most rare elements are unused (exceptions: selenium, iodine), while some common ones (aluminium, titanium) are not biologically required. Elemental needs differ slightly between kingdoms: ocean algae require bromine; animals require sodium (not essential for plants); plants require boron and silicon (animals may need trace amounts or none).

### Biomolecules
Biochemistry centers on four macromolecular classes: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. Many are **polymers** formed from **monomers** via **dehydration synthesis** (water removal); the reverse is **hydrolysis** (water addition).

#### Carbohydrates
Functions include energy storage, structural support, genetic information storage, and cell-cell signaling. The simplest are **monosaccharides** (general formula CₙH₂ₙOₙ, n≥3), e.g., glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), fructose, and deoxyribose (DNA component). Monosaccharides cyclize into **furanoses** (5-membered rings) or **pyranoses** (6-membered rings). Two monosaccharides form a **disaccharide** via a **glycosidic bond** (e.g., sucrose = glucose + fructose; lactose = glucose + galactose). **Oligosaccharides** (3–6 units) serve as markers/signals. **Polysaccharides** are long chains: **cellulose** (plant cell walls) and **glycogen** (animal energy storage) are glucose polymers. Carbohydrates are classified by **reducing ends** (free anomeric carbon able to open to aldehyde/ketone form); sucrose is non-reducing due to full acetal formation between both anomeric carbons.

#### Lipids
A diverse, water-insoluble (nonpolar) group including waxes, fatty acids, phospholipids, sphingolipids, glycolipids, and terpenoids (steroids, retinoids). **Triglycerides** (main bulk lipids) consist of one glycerol + three fatty acids (monomers). Fatty acids are **saturated** (no C=C bonds) or **unsaturated** (one or more C=C). Lipids are **amphiphilic**: hydrophobic tails avoid water; hydrophilic heads associate with water. Phospholipids (large polar heads) form membranes; cholesterol has a minimal –OH polar group. Dietary lipids are digested to fatty acids and glycerol; phospholipids serve as drug carriers (liposomes) and co-solubilizers in pharmaceuticals.

#### Proteins
**Macro-biopolymers** of 20 standard **amino acids** (monomers). Each amino acid has an α-carbon bonded to an amino group (–NH₂/–NH₃⁺), carboxyl group (–COOH/–COO⁻), hydrogen, and a variable side chain (–R) determining properties. **Peptide bonds** link amino acids (dehydration synthesis); short chains are **peptides/polypeptides** (<~30 residues); longer chains are **proteins** (e.g., albumin, 585 residues). Proteins have structural (actin, myosin in muscle contraction) and functional roles. **Antibodies** bind specific targets via variable N-terminal domains; used in **ELISA** diagnostics. **Enzymes** are critical catalysts: they bind specific **substrates**, lower activation energy, and accelerate rates by 10¹¹-fold or more without being consumed; activity is regulated by modifiers.

**Protein structure hierarchy:**
1.  **Primary:** Linear amino acid sequence.
2.  **Secondary:** Local morphology (α-helix coils, β-sheets).
3.  **Tertiary:** Full 3D shape dictated by primary sequence; single substitutions can alter function (e.g., glutamate→valine at position 6 of hemoglobin α-chain causes sickle-cell disease).
4.  **Quaternary:** Assembly of multiple subunits (e.g., hemoglobin, four subunits).

**Amino acid metabolism:** Humans synthesize 10 **nonessential** amino acids (alanine, asparagine, aspartate, cysteine, glutamate, glutamine, glycine, proline, serine, tyrosine) but require 8 **essential** ones from diet (isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine); arginine and histidine are often considered essential because they cannot be produced in sufficient amounts for young, growing animals. **Transaminases** transfer amino groups between α-keto acids and amino acids, linking biosynthesis to central metabolic intermediates. Protein catabolism yields ammonia (toxic); mammals convert it to **urea** via the **urea cycle** for excretion. **Sequence alignment** identifies **homologous** proteins, revealing evolutionary relationships and predicting structure/function.

#### Nucleic Acids
**Biopolymers** (DNA, RNA) conveying genetic information. **Nucleotides** (monomers) comprise a nitrogenous base (purine: adenine, guanine; pyrimidine: cytosine, thymine, uracil), a pentose sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA), and a phosphate group. Phosphate-sugar forms the backbone; base sequence stores information. Base pairing via hydrogen bonds: A–T/U (two bonds), C–G (three bonds). Thymine is DNA-specific; uracil is RNA-specific. Beyond genetics, nucleic acids act as **second messengers** and form **ATP** (adenosine triphosphate), the universal energy carrier.

### Metabolism
The mechanisms cells use to harness energy via chemical reactions.

#### Carbohydrates as Energy Source
**Glucose** is the primary fuel. Polysaccharides (glycogen) are broken down by enzymes (e.g., glycogen phosphorylase); disaccharides are cleaved to monosaccharides.

**Glycolysis (anaerobic):** Ten-step pathway converting one glucose → two pyruvate + net 2 ATP + 2 NADH (reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide). No O₂ required. Without O₂, NAD⁺ is regenerated by converting pyruvate → lactate (humans) or ethanol + CO₂ (yeast). Other monosaccharides (galactose, fructose) enter as glycolytic intermediates.

**Aerobic Respiration:** With sufficient O₂, pyruvate → acetyl-CoA + CO₂ + NADH. Two acetyl-CoA enter the **citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle)** per glucose → 2 ATP + 6 NADH + 2 FADH₂ (reduced flavin adenine dinucleotide) + CO₂. NADH and FADH₂ feed the **respiratory chain (electron transport chain)** on the inner mitochondrial membrane (eukaryotes). Electrons flow to O₂ (reduced to H₂O), creating a proton gradient driving **ATP synthase**. Yield: ~28 additional ATP (24 from 8 NADH + 4 from 2 quinols). **Total 32 molecules of ATP conserved per degraded glucose (two from glycolysis + two from the citrate cycle).** This high yield explains why complex life appeared only after atmospheric O₂ accumulation.

**Gluconeogenesis & Cori Cycle:** During intense exercise, muscle shifts to anaerobic glycolysis → lactate. Liver converts lactate (and amino acids, glycerol, Krebs intermediates) back to glucose via **gluconeogenesis** (reversal of glycolysis requiring 6 ATP vs. 2 gained). This **Cori cycle** (muscle glycolysis → blood lactate → liver gluconeogenesis → blood glucose → muscle) sustains energy during O₂ debt. Large-scale protein/fat catabolism occurs in starvation or endocrine disorders.

### Relationship to Other Molecular-Scale Sciences
Biochemistry overlaps heavily with genetics, molecular biology, biophysics, and chemical biology; boundaries are fluid.
*   **Biochemistry:** Chemical substances/vital processes; role, function, structure of biomolecules; atomic/molecular level.
*   **Molecular Biology:** Molecular underpinnings of biological phenomena; synthesis, modification, mechanisms, interactions. Central dogma (DNA → RNA → protein) is a starting point, revised by novel RNA roles.
*   **Genetics:** Effect of genetic differences/heredity (genome); uses mutants (knockouts) and epistasis analysis.
*   **Chemical Biology:** Develops small-molecule tools for minimal perturbation analysis; creates biomolecule-synthetic hybrids (e.g., viral capsids for gene/drug delivery).

## Terms

- ****Biochemistry**** — Study of chemical processes within and relating to living organisms; sub-discipline of chemistry and biology.
- ****Vitalism**** — Historical doctrine that life possesses a "vital principle" distinct from non-living matter; challenged by Wöhler's urea synthesis (1828).
- ****Polymer / Monomer**** — Large macromolecule built from linked smaller units (monomers) via dehydration synthesis; broken by hydrolysis.
- ****CHNOPS**** — Mnemonic for the six elements (Carbon, Hydrogen, Nitrogen, Oxygen, Phosphorus, Sulfur) comprising ~99% of cell mass.
- ****Amphiphilic**** — Molecule possessing both hydrophobic (water-fearing) and hydrophilic (water-loving) regions (e.g., phospholipids).
- ****Enzyme**** — Protein catalyst that binds specific substrates, lowers activation energy, and accelerates reaction rates (often >10¹¹-fold) without being consumed.
- ****Primary/Secondary/Tertiary/Quaternary Structure**** — Hierarchy of protein architecture: linear sequence → local folds (α-helix/β-sheet) → 3D shape → multi-subunit assembly.
- ****Essential Amino Acids**** — Amino acids humans cannot synthesize (isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine) and must obtain from diet.
- ****ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate)**** — Primary energy-carrier molecule in all living organisms; synthesized via substrate-level phosphorylation and oxidative phosphorylation.
- ****Glycolysis / Citric Acid Cycle / Oxidative Phosphorylation**** — Core aerobic metabolic pathway: glucose → pyruvate (glycolysis) → acetyl-CoA → CO₂ + reduced cofactors (Krebs) → ATP via electron transport chain.

## Debates and Open Questions

*   **Origin of the discipline:** Historians debate whether biochemistry "begins" with ancient Greeks, Lavoisier (18th c. fermentation/respiration), Wöhler (1828 urea synthesis), Payen (1833 first enzyme), Liebig (1842 chemical metabolism theory), Buchner (1897 cell-free fermentation), or the coining of the term (Kletzinsky 1858, Hoppe-Seyler 1877, Neuberg/Hofmeister ~1903).
*   **Wöhler and vitalism:** Whether Wöhler's 1828 urea synthesis definitively "overthrew" vitalism remains controversial; some scholars reject the narrative of a single decisive blow.
*   **Terminology priority:** Credit for coining "biochemistry" is disputed among Vinzenz Kletzinsky (1858), Felix Hoppe-Seyler (1877), Carl Neuberg (1903), and Franz Hofmeister.
*   **Central Dogma revisions:** The classical "DNA → RNA → protein" framework is acknowledged as oversimplified due to emerging novel roles for RNA (e.g., RNAi, catalytic RNA, regulatory RNAs), blurring lines between genetic information storage and functional execution.
