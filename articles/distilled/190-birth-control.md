# Birth control

## Overview
Birth control (contraception) encompasses methods or devices used to prevent pregnancy. While practiced since ancient times, effective and safe methods only became widely available in the 20th century. Access to contraception is a critical public health intervention: in developing countries, it has reduced maternal mortality by 40% (preventing ~270,000 deaths in 2008) and improves women’s earnings, children’s schooling, and economic growth by spacing births and enabling workforce participation. The most effective reversible methods are long-acting (IUDs, implants), while sterilization (vasectomy, tubal ligation) offers near-permanent efficacy. Barrier methods (condoms) uniquely prevent sexually transmitted infections (STIs). Despite proven benefits, an estimated 222 million women in developing countries who wish to avoid pregnancy lack access to modern methods due to cost, legal restrictions, and cultural opposition.

## Timeline
- **1850–1550 BC** — Egyptian Kahun and Ebers Papyri document early contraceptive recipes (honey, acacia, lint).
- **c. 1st century AD** — Silphium plant, used for birth control in ancient Greece/Near East, driven to extinction.
- **1640** — Oldest known condoms (animal gut) found at Dudley Castle, England.
- **1877** — Malthusian League founded in UK to advocate family planning; Annie Besant and Charles Bradlaugh prosecuted for publishing birth control info (Knowlton trial).
- **1914** — Margaret Sanger and Otto Bobsein coin term "birth control" in US; Sanger flees to UK after arrest under Comstock Laws.
- **1916** — Sanger opens first US birth control clinic in Brooklyn; shut down in 11 days.
- **1921** — Marie Stopes opens first permanent birth control clinic in Britain; Sanger founds American Birth Control League (later Planned Parenthood).
- **1936** — *US v. One Package of Japanese Pessaries* rules prescribing contraception for health is legal under Comstock Laws.
- **1951** — Carl Djerassi synthesizes progesterone from Mexican yams in Mexico City.
- **1960** — FDA approves Enovid, the first oral contraceptive pill, for contraceptive use.
- **1965** — *Griswold v. Connecticut* establishes constitutional right to privacy for marital contraception use.
- **1972** — *Eisenstadt v. Baird* extends contraception privacy right to unmarried individuals.
- **2002** — Essure (non-surgical female sterilization device) approved in US; discontinued in 2018 after safety warnings.
- **2010** — Affordable Care Act requires marketplace health plans to cover contraceptive methods.
- **2023** — FDA approves Opill, first over-the-counter daily oral contraceptive pill in US (available 2024).

## Body

### Methods and Effectiveness
Birth control methods are categorized by mechanism: hormonal, barrier, intrauterine devices (IUDs), sterilization, and behavioral. Effectiveness is measured by first-year pregnancy rates with *perfect use* (correct, consistent use) versus *typical use* (real-world adherence). Long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs)—hormonal implants, copper IUDs (0.8% failure), and levonorgestrel IUDs (0.2% failure)—are most effective because they eliminate user adherence errors. Sterilization (vasectomy, tubal ligation) has <1% failure but is considered permanent; reversal success varies (31–88% pregnancy after tubal reversal; 38–84% after vasectomy reversal). Hormonal pills/patches/rings have <1% perfect-use failure but ~9% typical-use failure. Barrier methods (condoms 18% typical, 2% perfect; diaphragm 12% typical, 6% perfect) and behavioral methods (withdrawal 22% typical; fertility awareness 24% typical) are less reliable. Only condoms (latex/polyurethane) significantly reduce STI transmission. Dual protection (condom + another method) is advised for high STI risk or teratogenic drug use (e.g., isotretinoin).

### Hormonal Contraception
Hormonal methods (pills, implants, injections, patches, hormonal IUDs, vaginal ring) suppress ovulation and thicken cervical mucus. **Combined oral contraceptives (COCs)** contain estrogen and progestin; they slightly increase venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk (2.8 to 9.8 per 10,000 woman-years), contraindicating use in women >35 who smoke. COCs reduce ovarian/endometrial cancer risk and menstrual bleeding/cramps. **Progestin-only methods** (pills, injections, hormonal IUDs, implants) carry no VTE risk, are safe for breastfeeding, and are preferred for women with VTE history. Injectable progestin (DMPA) has 0.2% perfect / 6% typical failure. Side effects include irregular bleeding, mood changes, weight gain, and potential bone density loss (DMPA). Fertility returns immediately after stopping most methods.

### Barrier Methods
Barriers physically block sperm. **Male condoms** (latex, polyurethane, lamb intestine) are globally the most used method (80% of users in Japan; 18% in US). They are inexpensive, require no prescription, and prevent STIs (except lambskin). **Female condoms** (nitrile/latex/polyurethane) offer female-controlled STI protection. **Diaphragms/cervical caps** require fitting and spermicide; typical failure 12%. **Contraceptive sponges** (barrier + spermicide) have higher failure in parous women (24% vs 12% nulliparous) and carry toxic shock syndrome risk. Spermicides alone are least effective.

### Intrauterine Devices (IUDs)
IUDs are T-shaped devices inserted into the uterus. **Copper IUDs** (non-hormonal) cause a spermicidal inflammatory reaction; failure ~0.8%. They may increase menstrual bleeding/cramps. **Levonorgestrel IUDs** release progestin locally; failure ~0.2%. They reduce or eliminate menstruation. Both are safe for adolescents, nulliparous women, and immediate postpartum/post-abortion insertion. Complications: expulsion (2–5%), uterine perforation (<0.7%). Current models do not increase pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) risk absent STIs at insertion. IUDs may decrease ovarian cancer risk.

### Sterilization
**Vasectomy** (male): severs vasa deferentia. Outpatient, low complication rate (short-term complications 20x lower than tubal ligation). Chronic scrotal pain affects 1–2%. **Tubal ligation** (female): blocks fallopian tubes (or salpingectomy). Requires anesthesia; serious complications rare but higher than vasectomy. Reduces ovarian cancer risk. Regret rates: ~6% for women >30; 20–24% if sterilized <30 within a year of delivery; <5% for men. Neither protects against STIs.

### Behavioral Methods
**Fertility awareness** (FAM): tracks basal body temperature, cervical mucus, cycle day to identify fertile window. Symptothermal method (temp + mucus) achieves 0.4% perfect / 20% typical failure. High discontinuation in trials limits evidence quality. **Withdrawal (coitus interruptus)**: 4% perfect / 22% typical failure; pre-ejaculate may contain sperm. **Abstinence**: 100% effective if maintained, but "abstinence-only" education correlates with equal/higher teen pregnancy/STI rates vs. comprehensive education. **Lactational Amenorrhea Method (LAM)**: relies on postpartum infertility from exclusive breastfeeding (<6 months, amenorrheic). 93–99% effective for 6 months; fertility returns rapidly if supplementation occurs.

### Emergency Contraception (EC)
Used after unprotected sex. **Levonorgestrel pills** (within 72 hrs): reduce pregnancy risk by 70% (pregnancy rate 2.2%); less effective in obesity. **Ulipristal acetate** (within 120 hrs): ~85% reduction (rate 1.4%); superior to levonorgestrel. **Copper IUD** (within 120 hrs): most effective (>99% prevention; rate 0.1–0.2%); provides ongoing contraception. **Mifepristone** also effective. EC primarily delays ovulation; unlikely to prevent implantation. Advance provision does not increase risk-taking.

### Health Effects
Contraception prevents maternal death by reducing unintended pregnancies/unsafe abortions and high-risk pregnancies. Spacing births >18 months improves child survival. Hormonal methods carry risks: VTE (estrogen), bone density loss (DMPA), glucose metabolism changes, mood effects. However, pregnancy carries higher mortality/morbidity risk than any contraceptive method. WHO Medical Eligibility Criteria guide method safety for specific conditions (e.g., progestin-only for VTE history). Most methods require no pelvic exam/blood work for healthy women.

### Economic Impact
In developing nations, birth control drives economic growth: fewer dependents, more women in workforce, better child health/education. UN estimates $2–6 saved per $1 spent on family planning. Copper IUDs yield greatest savings. In US (2012), average delivery cost: $21,000 (vaginal) / $31,000 (C-section); child-rearing to age 17: ~$235,000.

### Prevalence and Access
Globally (2009), ~60% of married/in-union women use contraception. Method mix varies: developed world favors condoms/pills; Africa: pills; Latin America/Asia: sterilization. Developing world overall: 35% female sterilization, 30% IUDs, 12% pills, 11% condoms, 4% male sterilization. As of 2012, 222 million women in developing countries had unmet need (53M sub-Saharan Africa, 97M Asia), causing 54M unplanned pregnancies and ~80,000 maternal deaths/year. Barriers: restrictive laws, poverty, religious opposition. In US, disparities affect Black, Hispanic, young, and immigrant women (insurance gaps, cost). UK provides free contraception via NHS. France (2022) and Ireland (2022) made contraception free for women <25.

### History
**Ancient/Medieval**: Egyptian papyri (1850–1550 BC) describe vaginal barriers. Silphium extinct by late antiquity. Aristotle, Hippocratic texts, Soranus (c. 98–138 AD) proposed ineffective/dangerous methods. Medieval Europe: Church condemned contraception; folk methods (coitus interruptus, herbal pessaries, weasel testicles) persisted. Animal-gut condoms (1640) used for STI prevention.
**Birth Control Movement (19th–20th C.)**: Malthusian League (1877) advocated family planning. Sanger (US) popularized term "birth control" (1914), opened clinics, founded American Birth Control League (1921). Stopes (UK) opened first permanent clinic (1921). Legal battles: *US v. One Package* (1936) allowed medical prescription; *Griswold* (1965) and *Eisenstadt* (1972) established privacy rights. Federal funding began 1966; ACA (2010) mandated coverage.
**Modern Methods**: Richter (1909) first IUD (silkworm gut); Gräfenberg marketed in 1920s. Djerassi synthesized progestin (1951). Pincus/Rock developed first pills (1950s); Enovid approved 1960. Medical abortion alternatives: prostaglandins (1970s), mifepristone (1980s).

### Society, Culture, and Law
**Human Rights**: International treaties obligate states to provide family planning services, remove access barriers, ensure method variety, and train providers.
**Religious Views**: Catholic Church permits only natural family planning (fertility awareness); many adherents use modern methods. Orthodox Christianity allows exceptions for birth spacing. Protestants range from Quiverfull (none) to full acceptance. Orthodox Judaism restricts; Reform permits most. Hinduism/Buddhism generally accept prevention; Islam permits if health not threatened.
**Policy**: US sees partisan swings (Obama expanded access; Trump restricted funding). Advocacy groups (Free the Pill) push OTC access. FDA approved OTC Opill (2023).

### Research Directions
**Female**: Improved female condoms, diaphragms, progestin-only patches, long-acting vaginal rings (3–4 months), pericoital levonorgestrel. Non-surgical sterilization: quinacrine/polidocanol (scarring; safety concerns), Essure (discontinued 2018).
**Male**: High interest, low industry funding. Targets: hormonal (gels, pills, injectables), non-hormonal (vas-occlusive devices, STK33 kinase inhibitors like CDD-2807 showing reversible infertility in mice). Clinical trials ongoing.

### Animals
Neutering/spaying (castration in large animals) standard for pets; often shelter-mandated. Contraceptive vaccines used for wild population control (alternative to culling). Kenyan herders use *olor* (skirt) on male goats to prevent mating.

## Terms
- ****Long-Acting Reversible Contraception (LARC)**** — Methods (IUDs, implants) effective for years without user action; <1% failure; fertility returns rapidly on removal.
- ****Perfect Use vs. Typical Use**** — Perfect use: method used correctly/consistently. Typical use: real-world adherence including errors; failure rates significantly higher for user-dependent methods (pills, condoms).
- ****Combined Oral Contraceptive (COC)**** — Pill containing estrogen + progestin; inhibits ovulation. Increases VTE risk slightly; contraindicated in smokers >35.
- ****Progestin-Only Methods**** — Pills, injections, implants, hormonal IUDs without estrogen. No VTE risk; safe for breastfeeding, VTE history.
- ****Intrauterine Device (IUD)**** — T-shaped uterine device. Copper (non-hormonal, spermicidal) or Levonorgestrel (hormonal, local progestin release).
- ****Sterilization**** — Permanent surgical contraception: vasectomy (male) or tubal ligation/salpingectomy (female). Reversal possible but not guaranteed.
- ****Fertility Awareness Methods (FAM)**** — Behavioral: tracking biomarkers (temperature, mucus) to avoid sex during fertile window. High typical-use failure (24%).
- ****Lactational Amenorrhea Method (LAM)**** — Postpartum contraception relying on infertility from exclusive breastfeeding (<6 months, no menses). 93–99% effective.
- ****Emergency Contraception (EC)**** — Post-coital intervention (pills: levonorgestrel, ulipristal; or Copper IUD) to prevent pregnancy after unprotected sex.
- ****Medical Eligibility Criteria (WHO MEC)**** — WHO guidelines categorizing safety of each contraceptive method for women with specific medical conditions (1=safe to 4=unacceptable risk).

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Male Contraceptive Development**: Despite demand, progress is stalled by lack of pharmaceutical industry investment; reliance on public/philanthropic funding slows clinical translation of promising targets (e.g., STK33 inhibitors).
*   **Non-Surgical Female Sterilization Safety**: Essure discontinuation highlights difficulty balancing minimally invasive permanence with long-term safety; chemical methods (quinacrine) remain controversial due to potential systemic toxicity.
*   **Abstinence-Only Education Efficacy**: Evidence consistently shows abstinence-only programs do not reduce teen pregnancy/STIs vs. comprehensive education; debate persists politically/ideologically.
*   **Over-the-Counter Access**: Medical bodies (ACOG) support OTC pills to reduce access barriers; regulatory approval (Opill, 2023) achieved in US, but cost/insurance coverage for OTC products remains unresolved.
*   **Contraceptive Access as Human Right**: International treaties mandate access, yet 222M women in developing world have unmet need; enforcement mechanisms are weak against sovereign restrictions (religious, political).
*   **Hormonal Contraception and Mental Health**: Observational data suggest association with depression/anxiety; causality and magnitude remain debated, complicating informed consent counseling.
*   **Emergency Contraception Mechanism**: While primary action is ovulation inhibition, possibility of post-fertilization effect (implantation prevention) is not fully excluded, fueling ethical/legal disputes over classification (contraceptive vs. abortifacient).
