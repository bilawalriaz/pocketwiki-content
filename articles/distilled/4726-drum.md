# Drum

## Overview
The drum is a membranophone—a musical instrument that produces sound via a stretched membrane—making it one of the world's oldest and most ubiquitous instruments. Its basic design has remained virtually unchanged for millennia, serving diverse roles from musical performance and religious ceremony to military communication and therapy.

## Timeline
- **c. 5500–2350 BC** — Drums made with alligator skins are found in Neolithic cultures in China.
- **Bronze Age** — The ornate Dong Son drums are fabricated by the Dong Son culture of northern Vietnam.
- **684 BC** — During a war between Qi and Lu, the effect of tàigǔ drums on soldiers' morale is employed to change the battle's result.
- **Pre-Columbian era** — Aztec nations use drums to send signals to battling warriors.
- **English Civil War** — Rope-tension drums are used by junior officers to relay commands over battle noise.
- **Mid-19th century** — The Scottish military starts incorporating pipe bands into Highland regiments.
- **2000s** — "Cardio drumming" emerges as a form of aerobic exercise.

## Body

### Construction and Sound
A drum consists of a shell, usually with a circular opening, over which a drumhead (membrane) is stretched. The shell's shape varies widely, including cylinders (common in Western tradition), bowls (timpani), frames (Bodhrán), and goblets (djembe). Most modern drums use a counterhoop and tension rods to secure and tune the head, though traditional rope or peg systems are still used on some instruments like the djembe and tabla.

Sound is determined by the drumhead type, head tension, and shell characteristics. The drumhead has the most effect; double-ply heads dampen high frequencies, while coated or dotted heads muffle overtones. Increasing head tension raises pitch but reduces volume. The shell's diameter, depth, and material also influence sound—larger diameter lowers pitch, greater depth increases volume, and materials like mahogany or birch affect tonal qualities.

### Uses and Cultural Significance
Drums are played by striking with hands, sticks, brushes, or beaters. In popular music and jazz, "drums" typically refers to a drum kit. Beyond music, drums hold symbolic and spiritual functions in many cultures, such as the karyenda in Burundi, which symbolized royal power. They are used in religious ceremonies, music therapy, and communication. Talking drums in Africa imitate spoken language tone patterns, and Sri Lankan drums have a 2500-year history of use for state-community communication.

### Military and Historical Roles
Drums have been integral to warfare for millennia. Chinese troops used tàigǔ drums to motivate soldiers and set marching pace. Swiss mercenary fife-and-drum corps used an early snare drum, from which the English word "drum" derives. During the English Civil War, rope-tension drums relayed commands. The ancient Indian Rig Veda references the Dundhubi war drum, considered sacred in battle.

### Animal Drumming
Rhythmic drumming is observed in animals, suggesting an evolutionary origin for social communication. Macaque monkeys drum objects to show social dominance, gorillas drum via chest beating, and kangaroo rats use their paws on the ground.

## Terms
- **Membranophone**: A musical instrument that produces sound primarily by way of a vibrating stretched membrane.
- **Drumhead (drum skin)**: The membrane stretched over the opening of a drum shell.
- **Counterhoop (rim)**: A metal hoop that holds the drumhead onto the shell.
- **Tension rods**: Screws that tighten the counterhoop to adjust drumhead tension.
- **Snare drum**: A drum with wires (snares) held across one or both heads.
- **Talking drum**: A drum used to imitate the tone patterns of spoken language.
- **Cardio drumming**: A form of aerobic exercise using drums, popular in the 2000s.
- **Djembe**: A goblet-shaped drum traditionally played with hands and tuned by ropes.
