# Thomas Hardy

## Overview
Thomas Hardy (1840–1928) was a major English novelist and poet, a Victorian realist whose work critically examined social constraints, fate, and suffering in rural England. His novels, set in the semi-fictional region of Wessex, and his later poetry profoundly influenced 20th-century literature, making him a pivotal figure bridging the Victorian and modern eras.

## Timeline
- **2 June 1840** — Born in Higher Bockhampton, Dorset, England.
- **1862** — Moved to London to study architecture and work as an assistant.
- **1870** — Met Emma Gifford while on an architectural mission in Cornwall.
- **1874** — Published *Far from the Madding Crowd*, his first major literary success.
- **1874** — Married Emma Gifford.
- **1885** — Moved to Max Gate, a house he designed in Dorchester.
- **1891** — Published *Tess of the d'Urbervilles*, causing controversy.
- **1895** — Published *Jude the Obscure*, his final novel, which met severe public criticism.
- **1898** — Published his first poetry collection, *Wessex Poems*.
- **1912** — First wife Emma died; Hardy wrote the *Poems 1912–13* elegies.
- **1914** — Married his secretary, Florence Emily Dugdale.
- **11 Jan 1928** — Died at Max Gate; his heart was buried at Stinsford, his ashes in Poets' Corner.

## Body

### Early Life and Architecture
Hardy was born to a stonemason and a well-read mother, receiving early education at home and local schools. His formal education ended at sixteen when he was apprenticed to a local architect. He later worked in London, winning architectural prizes but feeling socially inferior. Influenced by thinkers like John Stuart Mill, he returned to Dorset in the mid-1860s to dedicate himself to writing.

### Novels and Wessex
Hardy's first novel, *The Poor Man and the Lady* (c. 1867), was deemed too controversial and was never published. His first published works were *Desperate Remedies* (1871) and *Under the Greenwood Tree* (1872). *Far from the Madding Crowd* (1874) was a success, allowing him to become a full-time writer. He established the semi-fictional region of Wessex, based on southwestern England, as the setting for his major novels. These works, including *The Return of the Native* (1878), *The Mayor of Casterbridge* (1886), *Tess of the d'Urbervilles* (1891), and *Jude the Obscure* (1895), are tragic realist narratives exploring characters struggling against social conventions and fate. The controversy surrounding *Tess* and especially *Jude*, which attacked marriage and religion, led Hardy to stop writing novels.

### Poetry and Later Career
Although he considered himself primarily a poet, Hardy's first collection, *Wessex Poems*, was not published until 1898. He wrote in various forms, including the epic verse-drama *The Dynasts* (1904–08) about the Napoleonic Wars. His later poetry, particularly the *Poems 1912–13* elegies for his first wife, is highly regarded. His work, marked by themes of suffering, fate, and irony, influenced later poets like W. H. Auden and Philip Larkin. In his later years, he was active in preservation campaigns and was involved with a local amateur theatre group, the Hardy Players.

### Religious Beliefs
Raised Anglican, Hardy questioned traditional Christian doctrine, influenced by scientific thought and the works of agnostics like Herbert Spencer. He replaced the concept of a personal God with a belief in an "Immanent Will" or "unconscious will of the Universe," a force often depicted as indifferent or capricious in his work. Despite this, he retained an emotional attachment to Christian liturgy and ritual.

## Terms
- **Wessex**: The semi-fictional region of southwestern England where Hardy set most of his novels, based on real counties like Dorset and Devon.
- **Victorian Realist**: A literary style depicting characters and social conditions realistically, which Hardy practiced in his novels.
- **Immanent Will**: Hardy's term for the unconscious, often indifferent or capricious force he believed governed the universe.
- **Cliffhanger**: A narrative device ending a chapter at a moment of suspense, popularized by the serialized publication of Hardy's *A Pair of Blue Eyes*.
- **Georgians**: A group of early 20th-century English poets who admired and were influenced by Hardy.
- **Order of Merit**: A prestigious British honor awarded to Hardy in 1910 for his literary contributions.
- **Hardy Players**: A local amateur dramatic society in Dorchester with which Hardy collaborated on theatrical adaptations of his works.
- **Poets' Corner**: A section of Westminster Abbey where Hardy's ashes are interred.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Scholars have long debated Hardy's precise religious leanings, as he rejected orthodox Christianity but retained a fascination with spiritual and supernatural themes.
- There is discussion about why Hardy stopped writing novels after *Jude the Obscure*. While public criticism was severe, Hardy himself later stated he "never cared very much about writing novels" and had "written quite enough."
- The survival of only 12 of his notebooks after his executors burned many of his papers has limited full scholarly understanding of his creative process.
