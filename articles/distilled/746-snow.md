# Snow

## Overview
Snow is solid atmospheric precipitation composed of ice crystals that nucleate around particles in clouds, grow via the Wegener–Bergeron–Findeisen process, and fall to accumulate as a snowpack. It is a critical component of the global water cycle, storing water seasonally in mid-latitudes and perennially in polar and alpine regions, where it feeds rivers and recharges groundwater upon melt. Snow profoundly influences human civilization—impeding transportation, loading structures, enabling winter sports, and dictating military logistics—while governing ecosystem survival through thermal insulation and habitat creation. Its high albedo and insulating properties make it a significant feedback agent in the global climate system.

## Body

### Formation and Cloud Physics
Snow originates in clouds within larger weather systems, primarily mid-latitude low-pressure areas (cyclones) incorporating warm and cold fronts. Localized heavy snow arises from **lake-effect** (or sea-effect) storms—cold air masses moving over warmer water, picking up vapor, and depositing snow on leeward shores—and **orographic lift**, where moist air is forced up windward mountain slopes, cooling adiabatically to precipitate. In clouds, **supercooled water droplets** (liquid below 0 °C, down to −18 °C) freeze onto **ice nuclei** (rare aerosols like clay, dust, or biological particles; artificial nuclei include silver iodide). Once frozen, crystals grow in a **supersaturated** environment (air saturated with respect to ice) by vapor diffusion (Wegener–Bergeron–Findeisen process), reaching millimeter sizes and aggregating into snowflakes. Crystal morphology—platelets, needles, columns, dendrites, rime—is dictated by temperature and humidity (supersaturation), as mapped by Ukichiro Nakaya; Magono and Lee later classified 80 distinct shapes.

### Accumulation and Distribution
Snow accumulates from discrete events (flurries, showers, storms, blizzards) over cold regions. A **blizzard** (US definition) requires sustained winds ≥35 mph (16 m/s) and visibility <0.4 km for ≥3 hours; falling snow is not required (ground blizzards exist). Snowfall intensity is categorized by visibility: Light (>1 km), Moderate (0.5–1 km), Heavy (<0.5 km). Measurement uses snow gauges (melted for water equivalent) or ultrasonic depth sensors. **Snow water equivalent (SWE)** is the depth of water resulting from complete melt.
Globally, glaciers (permanent snowpack) cover ~10% of Earth’s surface; seasonal snow covers ~9% (mostly Northern Hemisphere). Northern Hemisphere extent varies from ~2 million km² (August minimum) to ~45 million km² (January maximum, ~half the land area). A 1972–2006 study indicates a 0.5 million km² reduction. Records: highest seasonal total 2,896 cm (Mt. Baker, WA, 1998–99); highest annual average 1,764 cm (Sukayu Onsen, Japan, 1981–2010); snowiest major cities are Aomori, Sapporo, Toyama (Japan), St. John’s, Quebec City (Canada), Syracuse (USA).

### Metamorphism: From Deposition to Glacier Ice
**Snow metamorphism** is the transformation from deposition to melt or glacial ice. Fresh powder (low density) settles under its own weight to ~30% water density; further densification occurs via melt-freeze cycles. Seasonal snowpack reaches ~50% density by late spring. Persistent summer snow becomes **névé** (granular, partially melted/refrozen, density ≥500 kg/m³). Multi-year snow recrystallizes into **firn** (caked sugar texture, 550–830 kg/m³), resistant to shoveling. The **firn limit** is the minimum altitude of firn accumulation on a glacier. Further compaction and air expulsion yield **glacial ice**, which flows when thick enough (≥15 m on steep slopes) due to gravity, slope, and pressure. Glaciers form where accumulation exceeds ablation, originating in **cirques** (armchair-shaped basins).

### Movement
Deposited snow moves via four mechanisms: (1) **Drifting**: wind transports unsintered powder, forming meter-deep deposits and **snow slabs** (avalanche hazards) on lee slopes. (2) **Avalanches**: rapid gravity-driven flows. *Slab avalanches* (cohesive block failure, most fatal) occur in wind-deposited snow. *Powder snow avalanches* (dry powder, >300 km/h, up to 10⁷ tonnes) generate powder clouds traveling far on valley floors. *Wet snow avalanches* (water-saturated, 10–40 km/h) exert high destructive force via mass/density. (3) **Snowmelt**: drives seasonal river flow (spring floods in mountain West US, Iran, Afghanistan) or sustained summer flow if sourced from glaciated areas. (4) **Glacier flow**: long-term ice movement.

### Science: Measurement, Remote Sensing, and Modeling
Scientists excavate **snow pits** to measure height, SWE, strength, cover extent, microstructure, grain size/shape, density, liquid water content, and temperature. The *International Classification for Seasonal Snow on the Ground* codifies deposited snow types (natural/man-made, metamorphosing crystals, hoar frost, ice layers). **Satellite remote sensing** (visible/infrared reflectance, passive microwave) maps snow cover extent (SCE), duration (SD), and SWE. Since the 1960s, satellites show decreasing Northern Hemisphere snow cover (–1.3%/decade mean monthly extent), with regional increases (e.g., China 1978–2006) attributed to climate change. Passive microwave penetrates clouds/darkness, providing temporal continuity. **Models**: Global Climate Models (GCMs) incorporate snow albedo (reflectivity) and insulation (slowing sea ice melt). Melt modeling evolved from **degree-day** (air-snow temperature difference) to **energy balance** approaches computing melt energy (Qm) from six heat flow mechanisms (solar flux, ambient temperature, wind, precipitation, etc.). Hydrological runoff models route snowmelt through stream networks to groundwater.

### Effects on Civilization
**Transportation**: Snow reduces tire-road friction. Maintenance (estimated $2B/yr in N. America, 1994) relies on **anti-icing** (preventing bond via chemicals/plowing) rather than de-icing. Snow fences control drifting. Aviation bans chlorides (corrosive to aluminum); uses mechanical brushes, echelon/rotary plows, and aircraft deicing fluid. Rail uses wedge/rotary plows, **flangers** (clear between rails), sand for traction, and **snow sheds** (cover tracks in mountains). **Construction**: Compacted snow forms winter roads/runways (e.g., Phoenix Airfield, Antarctica, rated for 60 heavy military flights/yr).
**Agriculture**: Snow insulates crops (thermal blanket) and provides spring meltwater for irrigation. Key snowmelt-dependent rivers: Ganges (Himalayan tributaries), Indus (Tibet/western Himalayas, rapidly retreating glaciers), Colorado River (Rocky Mountain snowpack, irrigates 4M acres).
**Structures**: Design governed by Eurocode 1 (Europe) and ASCE 7 (N. America), translating ground snow loads to roof loads based on exposure, thermal properties, shape, drifting, and importance. **Ice dams** form when building heat melts roof snow; meltwater refreezes at cold eaves, blocking drainage and causing damage. Utility lines face tree-fall damage (wet snow) or **rime ice sleeves** (accretion measured in kg/m), mitigated by design loads, forecasting, or short-circuit melting.
**Sports/Recreation**: Skiing dominates (1994: 65–75M skiers worldwide; 55M Alpine; 390M annual skier visits at 4,500 areas). Equipment design balances bearing strength and friction; **ski wax** optimizes lubrication via friction melt (static vs. kinetic friction). **Snowmaking** (water + compressed air) supplements natural snow, extending seasons; threshold temperature rises as humidity drops (wet-bulb temperature metric); energy-intensive.
**Warfare**: Snow impairs visibility (falling snow) but enhances target contrast (snowy background); hinders mobility and logistics; provides cover/fortification. Historical impacts: French invasion of Russia (1812, logistics collapse); Winter War (1939–40, Finnish ski tactics); Battle of the Bulge (1944, air support grounded); Eastern Front WWII (1941, ski infantry); Korean War (1950–53, Chosin Reservoir, cold effects on vehicles/weapons).

### Effects on Plants and Animals
**Plants**: Adapt via freeze-tolerant chemistry, dormancy, dieback, seed survival. Vegetation intercepts snow (conifers reduce ground accumulation; tree-suspended snow ablates faster) and promotes retention. Snowmelt water aids growth, but buried plants require snowpack departure to start growth. Avalanches/melt erosion scour vegetation.
**Animals**: Invertebrates (spiders, beetles, springtails, snow scorpionflies) active to −5 °C; strategies: freeze-resistant (antifreeze agents, gut purging) or freeze-avoiding. Small vertebrates active subnivean (beneath snow): alpine salamanders to −8 °C; mammals <250 g (voles, pikas) maintain food caches (voles ≤3 kg, pikas ≤20 kg) and communal nests. Surface predators (wolves, foxes, lynx, weasels) dive into snowpack to hunt subnivean prey.

### Extraterrestrial Snow
"Snow" occurs elsewhere in the Solar System with different compositions: **Mars**: water-ice crystals at high latitudes (Phoenix lander); CO₂ snow at poles in winter (forms seasonal ice caps). **Venus**: metallic "snow" (lead sulfide or bismuth(III) sulfide) on high peaks (Magellan spacecraft). **Titan** (Saturn’s moon): methane/hydrocarbon crystalline deposits (Cassini–Huygens). **Pluto**: methane frost condensing at high altitude (New Horizons).

## Terms
- ****Supercooled water droplets**** — Liquid water droplets remaining unfrozen below 0 °C (down to −18 °C) due to lack of nucleation sites.
- ****Ice nuclei**** — Rare aerosol particles (clays, dust, biological matter, silver iodide) that initiate freezing of supercooled droplets.
- ****Wegener–Bergeron–Findeisen process**** — Crystal growth by vapor diffusion in a supersaturated environment (saturated w.r.t. ice), depleting surrounding water droplets.
- ****Supersaturated (w.r.t. ice)**** — Air containing more water vapor than equilibrium over an ice surface at that temperature, driving deposition onto crystals.
- ****Snow water equivalent (SWE)**** — Depth of water resulting from melting a snow sample completely; measures snowpack volume.
- ****Névé**** — Granular, partially melted/refrozen seasonal snow persisting into summer; density ≥500 kg/m³.
- ****Firn**** — Multi-year snow recrystallized to density 550–830 kg/m³; intermediate between névé and glacial ice.
- ****Firn limit**** — Minimum altitude on a glacier where firn accumulates.
- ****Slab avalanche**** — Cohesive block of snow (often wind-deposited) failing along fractures; primary cause of backcountry fatalities.
- ****Albedo**** — Reflectivity of incident radiation (including light); high for snow, cooling the surface and slowing sea ice melt.

## Debates and open questions
- **GCM melt parameterization**: As of 2011, Global Climate Model snow melt phases perform poorly in regions with complex vegetation cover and terrain regulating melt.
- **Remote sensing accuracy**: Patchy snow cover (during accumulation/ablation, in forests) and cloud cover inhibit optical sensing; passive microwave helps but requires fusion with reflective data for robust SWE retrieval.
- **Hydrological model continuity**: Need for continuous snow cover data under clouds/darkness drives reliance on passive microwave, yet integration with higher-resolution optical data remains a challenge.
- **Climate change attribution**: Observed Northern Hemisphere snow cover decline (–1.3%/decade since 1960s) and regional increases (China 1978–2006) are attributed to global climate change, but regional mechanisms (e.g., higher latitudes north of 40° gaining depth from warmer, moister air) complicate projections.
- **Snowmaking sustainability**: Energy-intensive snowmaking expands as natural snow reliability declines; threshold temperatures rise with lower humidity, but energy consumption limits widespread adoption.
