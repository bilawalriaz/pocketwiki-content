# Jack the Ripper

## Overview
Jack the Ripper was an unidentified serial killer active in the impoverished Whitechapel district of London's East End in 1888. The case is historically significant as the first to create a worldwide media frenzy, shaping modern perceptions of serial killers and highlighting the severe social problems of Victorian London's slums.

## Timeline
- **3 Apr 1888** — Emma Elizabeth Smith is attacked; she dies the next day.
- **7 Aug 1888** — Martha Tabram is murdered in George Yard.
- **31 Aug 1888** — Mary Ann Nichols is found murdered, the first of the "canonical five."
- **8 Sep 1888** — Annie Chapman is murdered in Hanbury Street.
- **25 Sep 1888** — The "Dear Boss" letter, coining the name "Jack the Ripper," is received.
- **30 Sep 1888** — Elizabeth Stride and Catherine Eddowes are murdered in the "double event."
- **16 Oct 1888** — The "From Hell" letter, with a kidney, is received by George Lusk.
- **9 Nov 1888** — Mary Jane Kelly is murdered, the last of the canonical five.
- **10 Sep 1889** — The Pinchin Street torso is discovered.
- **13 Feb 1891** — Frances Coles is murdered, the last in the Whitechapel murders file.

## Body

### Background and Context
In the mid-19th century, London's East End, particularly Whitechapel, became severely overcrowded due to an influx of Irish immigrants and, from 1882, Jewish refugees fleeing pogroms. By 1888, the population was approximately 80,000, living in extreme poverty with high child mortality, endemic crime, and widespread prostitution. Social tensions were high, with frequent demonstrations and unrest. This environment of deprivation and fear set the stage for the public terror that followed the murders.

### The Canonical Five Murders
The core of the Jack the Ripper case involves five victims—Mary Ann Nichols, Annie Chapman, Elizabeth Stride, Catherine Eddowes, and Mary Jane Kelly—murdered between 31 August and 9 November 1888. Their murders share a distinctive modus operandi: deep slash wounds to the throat followed by extensive abdominal mutilation and the removal of internal organs. The mutilations grew progressively more severe, culminating in the extensive evisceration of Mary Jane Kelly. The murders were committed at night, on or near weekends. While the first four victims were found in public spaces, Kelly was killed in her own room. The canonical five are linked by contemporaneous police documents, though some researchers debate whether all five were committed by the same person.

### The Investigation and Letters
The police investigation involved over 2,000 interviews and 80 detentions but failed to identify the killer. A key development was the receipt of hundreds of letters, three of which are prominent: the "Dear Boss" letter (which gave the killer his famous name), the "Saucy Jacky" postcard, and the "From Hell" letter, which arrived with half a human kidney. Police and journalists widely believed the letters were hoaxes, possibly written by journalists to boost newspaper circulation. The investigation was hampered by jurisdictional issues between the Metropolitan and City Police, and by the absence of the head of the CID during key murders. A police surgeon, Thomas Bond, produced an early criminal profile, concluding the killer had no special anatomical knowledge and was likely a solitary man subject to homicidal mania.

### Media Coverage and Legacy
The murders occurred during a boom in cheap, mass-circulation newspapers, leading to unprecedented and often sensationalist coverage that created a worldwide media frenzy. This coverage cemented the legend of "Jack the Ripper" and established the practice of giving serial killers nicknames. The case drew intense public attention to the horrific living conditions in the East End slums, eventually contributing to slum clearance projects in the following decades. The Ripper became a enduring figure in popular culture, inspiring countless works of fiction and the field of "ripperology." Despite modern forensic attempts, including DNA analysis, the killer's identity remains unknown.

## Terms
*   **Canonical Five:** The five victims—Mary Ann Nichols, Annie Chapman, Elizabeth Stride, Catherine Eddowes, and Mary Jane Kelly—whose murders between August and November 1888 are most widely attributed to Jack the Ripper.
*   **Modus Operandi:** The distinctive method of operation used by the killer, characterized by throat slashing followed by abdominal mutilation and organ removal.
*   **Whitechapel Murders:** The official police file name for a series of eleven brutal murders in the area between 1888 and 1891, of which the canonical five are a subset.
*   **Ripperology:** A term coined in the 1970s to describe the study and analysis of the Jack the Ripper case.
*   **Whitechapel Vigilance Committee:** A group of local citizens formed in September 1888 to patrol streets and offer a reward, due to dissatisfaction with the police investigation.
*   **Goulston Street Graffito:** A chalk message found above a piece of Eddowes's apron, which read "The Juwes are The men That Will not be Blamed for nothing," and was ordered washed away by police to prevent antisemitic riots.
*   **Double Event:** The term for the murders of Elizabeth Stride and Catherine Eddowes, which occurred in the early morning hours of 30 September 1888.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Victim Linkage:** Scholars debate whether all eleven murders in the Whitechapel file were committed by one person, or if only the canonical five (or even fewer) are definitively linked.
*   **The Letters:** The authenticity of the "Dear Boss" letter and other correspondence is highly disputed; many believe they were journalist hoaxes.
*   **Identity of the Killer:** Despite over a hundred named suspects and modern forensic attempts, the killer's identity is unknown. DNA studies have been criticized for flawed methodology and inconclusive results.
*   **The "Torso Killer":** It is debated whether the separate series of dismemberment murders known as the "Thames Mysteries" were committed by the same person as the Ripper.
