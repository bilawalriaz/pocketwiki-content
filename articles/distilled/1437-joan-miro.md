# Joan Miró

## Overview
Joan Miró i Ferrà (1893–1983) was a Catalan painter, sculptor, and ceramicist whose work bridged Surrealism, Fauvism, and Expressionism while defying strict movement allegiance. He developed a unique pictorial language of simplified organic forms, flattened picture planes, and recurring symbols—ladders, stars, birds, and Catalan peasants—to explore the unconscious mind and assert Catalan identity under political repression. Miró famously declared an "assassination of painting" to dismantle bourgeois conventions, pioneering automatic drawing and experimental media (ceramics, tapestry, gas sculpture) throughout a career that profoundly influenced American Abstract Expressionism and Color Field painting.

## Timeline
- **20 April 1893** — Born in Barcelona to Miquel Miró Adzerias (goldsmith/watchmaker) and Dolores Ferrà.
- **1907** — Enrolls at La Llotja fine art academy against father's wishes.
- **1918** — First solo show at Galeries Dalmau, Barcelona; work ridiculed and defaced.
- **1920** — Moves to Paris; begins annual summers in Mont-roig del Camp, Catalonia.
- **1921–22** — Paints *The Farm* (purchased by Ernest Hemingway), marking transition to personal symbolism.
- **1924** — Joins Surrealist group; begins "dream paintings" and *Head of a Catalan Peasant* series.
- **1929** — Marries Pilar Juncosa in Palma, Mallorca; daughter María Dolores born 1930.
- **1937** — Paints *The Reaper* mural for Spanish Republican Pavilion at Paris Exhibition; first overtly political work.
- **1940–41** — Creates *Constellations* gouache series (23 works) in exile in Varengeville, Palma, and Mont-roig.
- **1948–49** — Works at Mourlot Studios and Atelier Lacourière, producing over 1,000 lithographic editions.
- **1954** — Wins Venice Biennale printmaking prize.
- **1958** — Wins Guggenheim International Award.
- **1974** — Creates *World Trade Center Tapestry* with Josep Royo (destroyed 9/11/2001).
- **1981** — Unveils *The Sun, the Moon and One Star* (Miró's Chicago) in downtown Chicago.
- **25 Dec 1983** — Dies in Palma de Mallorca, age 90; interred at Montjuïc Cemetery, Barcelona.

## Body

### Early Formation and Catalan Fauvism
Born in Barcelona’s Barri Gòtic, Miró began drawing at seven and entered La Llotja academy in 1907. A teenage nervous breakdown ended a brief clerking career, committing him fully to art. His early work (e.g., *Portrait of Vincent Nubiola*, *Nord-Sud*, 1917) absorbed Vincent van Gogh and Paul Cézanne via Fauve and Cubist exhibitions in Barcelona, earning scholars’ label "Catalan Fauvist period." A 1918 solo debut at Galeries Dalmau was met with ridicule, yet he persisted, moving to Paris in 1920 while retaining summer roots in Mont-roig del Camp.

### Magical Realism and *The Farm*
From 1920, Miró adopted a meticulous, detail-obsessed style—isolating each element in deliberate composition—showing restrained Cubist influence (*House with Palm Tree*, *The Farmer’s Wife*). The culmination, *The Farm* (1921–22), depicts a Catalan rural scene anchored by a French avant-garde newspaper, fusing native nationalism with Parisian modernism. Surrealist peers deemed it too conventional, prompting Miró toward explicit Surrealism.

### Surrealist Pictorial Language
Joining the Surrealist group in 1924, Miró embraced automatism and dream imagery while retaining a schematic, symbolic vocabulary—never fully abstract. *The Tilled Field*, *Catalan Landscape (The Hunter)*, and *Pastoral* (1923–24) replace representation with encoded signs (triangle for head, curves for moustache). *Harlequin’s Carnival* (1924–25) and the *Spanish Dancer* collages (cork, feather, hatpin) extend this language. The *Head of a Catalan Peasant* series (1924–25) and 1926 collaboration with Max Ernst for Diaghilev’s ballet cement his Surrealist maturity.

### Political Engagement and Exile
Previously apolitical, Miró responded to the Spanish Civil War (1936) with *The Reaper* (1937), a mural for the Republican Pavilion in Paris. Fleeing the German invasion of France in 1940, he narrowly escaped to Franco’s Spain, producing the *Constellations* series (23 gouaches) in Varengeville, Palma, and Mont-roig. Celestial symbolism in *Constellations* earned André Breton’s poetic homage seventeen years later. Postwar, Miró mastered printmaking at Mourlot Studios (1948–49), yielding over 1,000 lithographs, and illustrated over 250 *Livres d’Artiste*, notably *Les pénalités de l’enfer* (1974) for poet Robert Desnos, a concentration-camp victim.

### Late Experimental Media and Public Works
From the 1950s onward, Miró expanded into ceramics (*Wall of the Moon* and *Wall of the Sun* at UNESCO, Paris), sculpture, tapestry (with Josep Royo), and theoretical "four-dimensional painting." Major public commissions include the Maeght Foundation garden (1964), *World Trade Center Tapestry* (1974, destroyed 2001), and *Miró’s Chicago* (1981, maquette 1967). He rejected official Surrealist membership to preserve freedom across Expressionism, Lyrical Abstraction, and Color Field painting, famously vowing to "break their guitar" against Picasso’s politicized Cubism.

### Mental Health and Creative Process
Miró suffered episodic depression from age 18 (1911), describing painting as essential relief: without it he fell into "black ideas." *Carnival of the Harlequin* visualizes mental chaos and the ladder motif—recurring across his oeuvre—as escape. Scholars debate whether his depression included manic episodes (bipolar disorder); the creativity–mental illness link remains inconclusive for Miró specifically.

## Terms
- **Automatic drawing**: Surrealist technique of drawing without conscious control to access the unconscious; Miró pioneered this with André Masson.
- **Pictorial sign language**: Miró’s personal vocabulary of simplified, recurring symbols (ladder, star, bird, peasant) functioning as visual shorthand.
- **Catalan Fauvist period**: Early phase (c. 1917–1920) blending Fauve color and Cézanne structure with Catalan subjects.
- **Assassination of painting**: Miró’s declared goal to dismantle bourgeois painting conventions and "established" visual elements.
- **Constellations**: 23 gouaches (1940–41) of celestial symbols painted in exile; pivotal for their poetic density and influence on Breton.
- **Livres d’Artiste**: Artist’s books combining original prints with text; Miró produced over 250.
- **Four-dimensional painting**: Miró’s theoretical concept transcending 2D canvas and 3D sculpture, explored in late writings.
- **Siurells**: Traditional Catalan clay whistles/figurines; Miró cited them as constant observational inspiration.
- **Maeght Foundation**: Art institution in Saint-Paul-de-Vence, France, housing Miró’s garden sculptures and ceramics (completed 1964).
- **Color Field painting**: Abstract style emphasizing large flat color areas; Miró’s late work anticipated this (influencing Frankenthaler, Olitski, Louis).

## Debates and open questions
- **Movement allegiance**: Miró participated in Surrealism but refused formal membership; scholars debate whether he is "the most Surrealist of us all" (Breton) or a sui generis modernist.
- **Political vs. poetic**: Early work encodes Catalan nationalism symbolically; only *The Reaper* (1937) is overtly political. The tension between poetic autonomy and political commitment remains contested.
- **Mental health diagnosis**: Episodic depression is documented; whether manic episodes occurred (bipolar vs. unipolar) is uncertain and inferred, not confirmed.
- **Automatism vs. method**: Sketches reveal careful planning behind seemingly automatic works; the balance between unconscious impulse and deliberate composition is unresolved.
- **Late work evaluation**: Prolific late output (ceramics, tapestry, gas sculpture) is viewed either as vital expansion or commercial repetition; critical consensus is still forming.
