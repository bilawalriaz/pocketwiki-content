# Central Africa

## Overview
Central Africa is a subregion of the African continent with varying definitions, but it is generally centered on the Congo River basin and the Lake Chad basin. Its modern political boundaries were largely shaped by the colonial partition of Africa in the late 19th century, leading to a complex history of pre-colonial empires, colonial exploitation, and post-independence challenges. The region is ecologically and economically significant, with its history deeply influenced by trade, migration, and the impact of the Atlantic slave trade.

## Body

### Definitions and Geography
Central Africa's composition varies by organization. The UN geoscheme for "Middle Africa" includes Angola, Cameroon, Central African Republic, Chad, Democratic Republic of the Congo, Equatorial Guinea, Gabon, Republic of the Congo, and São Tomé and Príncipe. The UN Office for Central Africa also covers Burundi and Rwanda, though they are classified as East African in the geoscheme. The African Development Bank defines a smaller group of seven countries. The Congo River basin is a historically significant ecological and supra-regional organizing feature.

### Prehistory and Early Civilizations
Archaeological evidence in Central Africa dates back over 100,000 years. The Sao civilization (c. 6th century BCE to 16th century CE) was a notable early culture near Lake Chad. The Kanem-Bornu Empire, centered in the Lake Chad Basin, was a major power from the 9th century CE until 1900. In southern Central Africa, Bantu migrations led to the development of kingdoms like the Lunda Empire (from the 1450s) and the Kingdom of Kongo (by the 15th century).

### The Impact of the Atlantic Slave Trade
The Kingdom of Kongo, under rulers like Afonso I (1506–1542), engaged in extensive trade with the Portuguese, which included the slave trade. This trade destabilized the region, leading to conflicts like the Jaga invasion of Kongo (c. 1568–69) and the eventual dissolution of the empire after the Battle of Mbwila (1665). The Portuguese also attempted to control the neighboring Kingdom of Ndongo, leading to resistance and the establishment of the slave-raiding state of Kasanje.

### Colonialism and Independence
The Conference of Berlin (1884–85) formalized the colonial division of Africa. French expansion in the Chad Basin included the occupation of the Wadai capital of Abéché in 1909. Most Central African countries gained independence between 1956 and 1962, retaining colonial administrative boundaries. The Democratic Republic of the Congo experienced the Congo Crisis (1960–1965) after independence from Belgium. Angola gained independence from Portugal in 1975 after a long colonial war.

### Modern Economy and Demographics
The main economic activities are farming, herding, and fishing, with significant poverty in rural areas. Oil is a major export for countries like Chad and South Sudan. The population is primarily composed of Bantu peoples, with significant Nilo-Saharan and Ubangian communities. Christianity and traditional faiths are predominant, with Islam being the majority religion in Chad and significant in Cameroon.

## Terms
- **Bantu Migration:** A millennia-long movement of Bantu-speaking peoples from West-Central Africa that spread agriculture, iron-working, and Bantu languages across much of the continent.
- **Manikongo:** The title of the ruler of the Kingdom of Kongo.
- **Slash-and-burn agriculture:** A farming method where vegetation is cut and burned to create fertile land for crops, common in the region.
- **Flood recession agriculture:** Farming practiced on fertile land exposed as seasonal floodwaters recede, notably around Lake Chad.
- **CFA franc:** A currency used by six Central African countries (CEMAC members) and several West African countries.
- **ECCAS:** The Economic Community of Central African States, a regional organization.
- **CEMAC:** The Economic and Monetary Community of Central Africa, a monetary union of six countries sharing the CFA franc.
- **Ubangian:** A group of languages spoken in Central Africa, part of the Niger-Congo family.
