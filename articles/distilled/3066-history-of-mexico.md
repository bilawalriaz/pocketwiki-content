# History of Mexico

## Overview
The history of Mexico spans over 13,000 years, from the first indigenous migrations to the modern day. It encompasses the rise and fall of complex Mesoamerican civilizations, the Spanish conquest and 300-year colonial rule, a turbulent 19th century of independence and conflict, the transformative Mexican Revolution, and the country's evolution into a modern, industrialized nation facing contemporary challenges like economic inequality and organized crime.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BCE** — Indigenous peoples in western Mexico begin selectively breeding maize.
- **1500–400 BCE** — The Olmec civilization flourishes along the Atlantic coast.
- **1325 CE** — The Aztecs establish their capital, Tenochtitlan.
- **1521** — Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire; creation of New Spain.
- **1810** — Father Miguel Hidalgo issues the "Cry of Dolores," beginning the War of Independence.
- **1821** — Mexico gains independence from Spain via the Treaty of Córdoba.
- **1846–1848** — The Mexican–American War ends with the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, ceding vast northern territories.
- **1876–1911** — The Porfiriato: the long dictatorship of Porfirio Díaz, marked by modernization and social inequality.
- **1910–1920** — The Mexican Revolution, a major social and political upheaval.
- **1917** — The Mexican Constitution of 1917 is promulgated, establishing key social and economic rights.
- **1938** — President Lázaro Cárdenas nationalizes the oil industry.
- **1994** — Mexico enters the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA).
- **2000** — Vicente Fox (PAN) ends the PRI's 71-year hold on the presidency.

## Body

### Pre-Columbian Civilizations
Complex societies developed in Mesoamerica, characterized by urban centers, monumental architecture, social stratification, and trade networks. They developed unique glyph-based writing systems. Major civilizations included the Olmec (1500–400 BCE), the Maya (with a classic period from c. 250–900 CE), the city of Teotihuacan (c. 100 BCE–7th/8th century CE), the Toltec (c. 800–1000 CE), and the Aztec Empire (1325–1521). The Aztecs, based at Tenochtitlan, built a vast tribute empire through military conquest and ritual warfare, practicing large-scale human sacrifice.

### Spanish Conquest and Colonial Rule (1521–1821)
Spanish conquistadors, led by Hernán Cortés, conquered the Aztec Empire in 1521, aided by indigenous allies and devastating smallpox epidemics. This began the 300-year colonial period of New Spain. The Spanish introduced Christianity, the encomienda system of forced labor, and a rigid racial caste system. Colonial wealth, especially from silver mining, enriched Spain. A mixed-race population (mestizaje) emerged. By the early 1800s, American-born Spaniards (criollos) resented the political monopoly of Iberian-born Spaniards.

### Independence and the 19th Century
Inspired by the American and French Revolutions, and triggered by Napoleon's invasion of Spain, Father Miguel Hidalgo launched the independence movement in 1810. After a prolonged war, independence was achieved in 1821 under Agustín de Iturbide. The subsequent decades were marked by extreme political instability, with the presidency changing hands 75 times. The "Age of Santa Anna" (1829–1854) saw the loss of Texas (1836) and the Mexican–American War (1846–48), which cost Mexico half its territory. A struggle between liberals and conservatives culminated in the French intervention (1862–67) and the brief empire of Maximilian I, before the Republic was restored.

### The Porfiriato and Revolution (1876–1920)
Porfirio Díaz ruled as dictator from 1876 to 1911, promoting modernization, foreign investment, and economic growth at the cost of political repression and vast social inequality. The Mexican Revolution (1910–1920) began as a rebellion against Díaz's fraudulent reelection and evolved into a complex civil war with competing factions. It resulted in the Constitution of 1917, which enshrined land reform, labor rights, and state control of subsoil resources, and led to the formation of the dominant Institutional Revolutionary Party (PRI).

### Consolidation and the "Mexican Miracle" (1920–1970)
Revolutionary generals dominated politics in the 1920s, culminating in the anti-clerical Cristero War (1926–29). President Lázaro Cárdenas (1934–40) consolidated the revolution by nationalizing oil and implementing land reform. From the 1940s to 1970, under PRI rule, Mexico experienced the "Mexican Miracle," a period of high economic growth driven by industrialization and political stability, though with growing inequality.

### Contemporary Mexico (1970–Present)
Economic crises in 1976 and 1982, a devastating 1985 earthquake, and growing opposition weakened PRI dominance. The disputed 1988 election accelerated political change. In 1994, Mexico joined NAFTA, but also faced the peso crisis and the Zapatista uprising. The PRI lost the presidency for the first time in 2000 to Vicente Fox (PAN). Subsequent administrations have grappled with the violent drug war, which began in 2006, and persistent corruption. In 2018, leftist Andrés Manuel López Obrador was elected, and in 2024, his successor Claudia Sheinbaum became Mexico's first female president.

## Terms
*   **Mesoamerica:** The cultural region in central and southern Mexico extending into Central America, where complex indigenous civilizations developed.
*   **Codices:** Pre-Columbian manuscripts painted in pictorial and glyphic writing systems.
*   **Encomienda:** A Spanish colonial system granting a colonist the right to the labor and tribute of a group of indigenous people.
*   **Caudillo:** A military and political strongman, common in post-independence Latin America.
*   **Mestizo:** A person of mixed European and indigenous ancestry.
*   **PRI (Institutional Revolutionary Party):** The dominant political party in Mexico from 1929 to 2000, born from the Mexican Revolution.
*   **Cristero War (1926–1929):** A major uprising against the Mexican government's anti-clerical policies.
*   **NAFTA (North American Free Trade Agreement):** A trade agreement between Mexico, the United States, and Canada that took effect in 1994.
*   **Zapatista Army of National Liberation (EZLN):** An indigenous armed revolutionary movement that emerged in Chiapas in 1994.

## Debates and open questions
*   The ethnicity of the inhabitants of Teotihuacan is debated, with candidates including the Nahua, Otomi, or Totonac peoples, or a multiethnic state.
*   The historical accuracy of Aztec narratives about their Toltec predecessors is a matter of scholarly debate.
*   The degree and direction of influence between the Toltec site of Tula and the Maya site of Chichén Itzá remain unconfirmed.
*   The exact death toll of the 1985 Mexico City earthquake is uncertain, with estimates ranging from 6,500 to 30,000.
*   The effectiveness of President López Obrador's "hugs, not gunshots" security policy is highly contested.
