# Fibonacci

## Overview
Leonardo Bonacci (c. 1170 – c. 1240-50), commonly known as Fibonacci, was an Italian mathematician from the Republic of Pisa. He is considered the most talented Western mathematician of the Middle Ages, primarily for popularizing the Hindu–Arabic numeral system in Europe through his 1202 book *Liber Abaci*, which revolutionized commercial calculation and laid groundwork for modern mathematics.

## Timeline
- **c. 1170** — Born in Pisa, Republic of Pisa.
- **c. 1180s** — Traveled with his father to Bugia (modern Béjaïa, Algeria), where he learned the Hindu–Arabic numeral system.
- **1202** — Completed *Liber Abaci*, introducing the Hindu–Arabic numeral system and the Fibonacci sequence to Europe.
- **1220** — Published *Practica Geometriae*, a work on practical geometry.
- **1225** — Published *Flos*, solving problems posed by Johannes of Palermo.
- **1228** — A copy of *Liber Abaci* was made, which is the earliest surviving version.
- **c. 1240** — Honored by the Republic of Pisa with a salary for his advisory services.
- **c. 1240-50** — Died in Pisa.

## Body

### Biography and Education
Fibonacci was born around 1170 to Guglielmo, a merchant and customs official. He traveled with his father to Bugia, where he received an education and first learned the Hindu–Arabic numeral system. Extensive travel around the Mediterranean exposed him to various merchant arithmetic systems. He realized the advantages of the Hindu–Arabic system's place-value notation over Roman numerals. In 1240, the Republic of Pisa officially recognized his expertise in accounting and instruction.

### *Liber Abaci* and the Hindu–Arabic Numerals
Fibonacci's seminal work, *Liber Abaci* (1202), introduced the *modus Indorum* (method of the Indians), the Hindu–Arabic numeral system with ten digits and zero. The book demonstrated its practical application in commercial bookkeeping, currency conversion, interest calculation, and other business practices. This replaced Roman numerals and the abacus, significantly easing calculations and aiding the growth of European banking and accounting. The original 1202 manuscript is lost; the earliest known copy dates to 1228.

### The Fibonacci Sequence
Within *Liber Abaci*, Fibonacci posed a problem about idealized rabbit population growth. The solution generated a sequence where each number is the sum of the two preceding ones, now called the Fibonacci sequence. Although Fibonacci presented the sequence starting with 1, 2, 3, ..., and calculated up to 377, the sequence had been described by Indian mathematicians as early as the sixth century. Fibonacci did not discuss the golden ratio in relation to the sequence.

### Other Works and Legacy
Fibonacci authored several other mathematical works, including *Practica Geometriae* (1220) on surveying and geometry, *Flos* (1225) with solutions to problems from Emperor Frederick II's court, and *Liber quadratorum* on Diophantine equations. His legacy includes numerous mathematical concepts named after him, such as the Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity and the Pisano period. A statue of Fibonacci stands in Pisa's Camposanto.

## Terms
- **Hindu–Arabic numeral system**: The decimal positional numeral system using digits 0–9, which Fibonacci introduced to Europe.
- **Place-value system**: A numeral system where the value of a digit depends on its position, enabling efficient calculation.
- **Roman numerals**: The numeral system used in Europe before the Hindu–Arabic system, based on letters and lacking a zero or place value.
- **Liber Abaci**: Fibonacci's 1202 book that popularized the Hindu–Arabic numeral system in the Western world.
- **Fibonacci sequence**: A sequence of numbers where each number is the sum of the two preceding ones, typically starting with 0 and 1.
- **Diophantine equations**: Polynomial equations where integer solutions are sought, a focus of Fibonacci's *Liber quadratorum*.
- **Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity**: A formula in number theory expressing the product of two sums of two squares as a sum of two squares, named for its independent discovery by both mathematicians.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact dates of Fibonacci's birth and death are uncertain, given as "c. 1170" and "c. 1240-50."
- The origin of the name "Fibonacci" is modern, first appearing in an 1838 text, though he was referred to as "Lionardo Fibonacci" as early as 1506.
- While Fibonacci introduced the Fibonacci sequence to Europe, the sequence was described by Indian mathematicians centuries earlier, raising questions about potential transmission of knowledge.
