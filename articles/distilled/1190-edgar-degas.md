# Edgar Degas

## Overview

Edgar Degas (1834–1917) was a French artist whose work spanned painting, pastel, sculpture, printmaking, and photography. Though associated with Impressionism, he rejected the label, preferring to be called a realist and insisting his art was the product of reflection, not spontaneity. Deeply trained in classical techniques and influenced by old masters like Ingres and Delacroix, Degas became known for depicting modern life—especially dancers, bathers, racehorses, and urban scenes—with psychological depth and unconventional compositions. His career reflected both artistic innovation and personal controversy, including documented antisemitism.

## Timeline

- **1834** — Born in Paris to a moderately wealthy family; mother was Creole from New Orleans
- **1853** — Graduates from Lycée Louis-le-Grand; enrolls at University of Paris law school
- **1855** — Admitted to École des Beaux-Arts; studies under Louis Lamothe
- **1856–1859** — Travels to Italy; begins *The Bellelli Family*
- **1865** — First Salon exhibition: *Scene of War in the Middle Ages*
- **1870** — Enlists in National Guard during Franco-Prussian War; eye problems diagnosed
- **1872–1873** — Extended stay in New Orleans; paints *A Cotton Office in New Orleans*
- **1874** — Joins Impressionists; participates in first independent exhibition
- **1881** — Exhibits *Little Dancer of Fourteen Years*—his only public sculpture
- **1917** — Dies in Paris, nearly blind and isolated

## Body

### Early Life and Training

Born Hilaire-Germain-Edgar De Gas in 1834, Degas came from a cultured, financially stable family. His maternal lineage included Creole roots from New Orleans and Haitian heritage. After his mother’s death at age 13, he was shaped by his father and unmarried uncles. He pursued formal education but showed early artistic promise, converting a room into a studio by 18. Despite enrolling in law school, he focused on copying works at the Louvre. In 1855, he entered the École des Beaux-Arts, where he studied under Louis Lamothe and absorbed the teachings of Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres, who emphasized rigorous drawing.

### Artistic Career and Shift to Modern Life

Initially intent on becoming a history painter, Degas painted ambitious works like *The Daughter of Jephthah* and *Young Spartans Exercising*. However, exposure to Édouard Manet and the realities of contemporary life shifted his focus. By the late 1860s, he turned to racecourse scenes, ballet dancers, laundresses, and café life. The Franco-Prussian War interrupted his career, but it also deepened his commitment to modern themes. Financial hardship after his brother’s debts forced him to rely on art sales, leading to a highly productive decade beginning in 1874.

### Style and Technique

Degas aligned himself with the Impressionists in subject matter but diverged technically. He rejected painting *en plein air*, instead working indoors from memory, photographs, and live models. His compositions often featured cropped figures and unusual angles, influenced by Japanese prints and photography. He experimented across media—pastel, monotype, etching, and sculpture—with a particular mastery of pastel in later years. His style evolved from detailed realism to looser brushwork and abstraction, yet he maintained a deliberate, calculated approach. He famously stated, “In art, nothing should look like chance, not even movement.”

### Sculpture

Though primarily known as a painter, Degas created over 150 wax sculptures, mostly unseen during his lifetime. His only public sculpture, *The Little Dancer of Fourteen Years* (1881), caused scandal for its raw realism and use of real hair and fabric. After his death, many works were cast in bronze by the Hébrard Foundry, though questions remain about the authenticity of later plaster discoveries in 2004.

### Personality and Politics

Degas lived a solitary life, believing artists should remain private. Known for wit and misanthropy, he cultivated an image as a curmudgeonly bachelor. His political views grew increasingly conservative and antisemitic, particularly during the Dreyfus Affair, when he severed ties with Jewish friends and colleagues. These attitudes marred his legacy despite his artistic achievements.

## Terms

- **Impressionism**: An art movement originating in the 1860s–70s emphasizing light, color, and fleeting moments; Degas participated but rejected the term.
- **En plein air**: French term meaning "in the open air"; refers to painting outdoors, which Degas avoided.
- **Pastel**: A drawing medium using powdered pigment bound together; Degas mastered this in his later years.
- **Monotype**: A printmaking technique producing unique impressions; Degas produced around 300 monotypes.
- **Surmoulage**: A bronze casting method involving molding from an existing cast; used for posthumous Degas bronzes.
- **History painting**: A genre depicting scenes from mythology, religion, or literature; Degas’s initial ambition.
- **Genre painting**: Art depicting everyday life; a category Degas embraced in his mature work.
- **Plein air**: See *en plein air*.
- **École des Beaux-Arts**: Prestigious French art school emphasizing classical training; Degas attended briefly.
- **Dreyfus Affair**: A political scandal (1894–1906) involving false conviction of a Jewish officer; Degas supported the antisemitic faction.

## Debates and Open Questions

Several unresolved issues surround Degas’s legacy:

- **Authenticity of plasters**: The 2004 discovery of 73 plaster casts attributed to Degas sparked debate over their origin and legitimacy. Most scholars have declined to authenticate them.
- **Motivations behind unfinished works**: Degas blamed his eye troubles for leaving many paintings incomplete, but critics like Charles Stuckey questioned whether vision alone explained his reluctance to finish.
- **Nature of his sculptures**: Scholars agree Degas did not create sculptures merely as preparatory tools for paintings, yet their exact role in his practice remains debated.
- **Political legacy**: While his antisemitism is well-documented, its impact on interpreting his art—particularly portraits like *Portraits at the Stock Exchange*—continues to generate scholarly discussion.
