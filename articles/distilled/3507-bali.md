# Bali

## Overview
Bali is an Indonesian island and province, renowned as a global tourist destination and the country's only Hindu-majority region. Its economy is overwhelmingly dependent on tourism, which accounts for 80% of its output, making it a case study in both cultural preservation and the challenges of overtourism. The island is celebrated for its highly developed arts, unique irrigation systems, and dramatic volcanic landscapes.

## Timeline
- **c. 2000 BC** — Inhabited by Austronesian peoples.
- **914 CE** — Earliest attestation of the name "Bali" in the Belanjong pillar inscription.
- **1343** — Majapahit Empire from Java founds a Balinese colony.
- **1512** — First known European contact by a Portuguese expedition.
- **1840s** — Dutch political and economic control begins on the north coast.
- **1906** — Dutch subjugation of southern kingdoms; mass ritual suicides (puputan) occur.
- **1942-1945** — Imperial Japan occupies Bali during World War II.
- **1958** — Bali becomes a province of Indonesia.
- **1963** — Mount Agung erupts, killing thousands and causing economic havoc.
- **1965-66** — Anti-communist massacres occur; an estimated 80,000 are killed in Bali.
- **2002** — Terrorist bombings in Kuta kill 202 people, devastating tourism.
- **2017** — Mount Agung erupts again; Bali is named a top global destination by Tripadvisor.
- **2022** — Bali hosts the G20 summit.

## Body

### History and Etymology
Bali was inhabited from around 2000 BC. Its culture was strongly influenced by Indian, Chinese, and Kalinga Hindu culture from around the 1st century AD. The name "Bali" is attested in ancient inscriptions as part of the Sanskrit compound *Vāli-dvīpa*, with debated meanings including "Island of offerings" or "Island of strength." The Hindu-Buddhist Majapahit Empire from Java established a colony in 1343. After the Majapahit fell in 1520, Bali became an independent collection of Hindu kingdoms for nearly four centuries until Dutch subjugation began in the 1840s, culminating in full control by 1906. The island was occupied by Japan in World War II and became a province of Indonesia in 1958. The 1965-66 anti-communist massacres and the 1963 eruption of Mount Agung were pivotal, traumatic events. Tourism began to grow significantly from the 1980s.

### Geography and Climate
Located east of Java, Bali covers approximately 5,590 km². Its central mountains include active volcanoes like Mount Agung (3,031 m), whose eruptions have shaped the island's fertile soil and history. The island lies just west of the Wallace Line, a biogeographical boundary, giving it Asian fauna. It has a tropical climate with an average temperature of 30°C and a rainy season from October to April. The southern coast has white sand beaches, while the north and west have black sand.

### Economy and Tourism
Tourism is the dominant industry, with 80% of the economy related to it. Agriculture remains the largest employer. The industry suffered major blows from the 2002 and 2005 bombings and the COVID-19 pandemic but has shown resilience. Key tourist areas include Kuta, Ubud (the cultural center), and Nusa Dua. In 2025, Bali received nearly 7 million international tourists. The tourism boom has led to significant development, including real estate, but also environmental strain, such as water shortages and plastic pollution.

### Culture and Religion
Bali is the only Hindu-majority province in Indonesia, with 86.40% of the population adhering to Balinese Hinduism, a syncretic blend of local animism, Hinduism, and Buddhism. The culture is renowned for its arts, including dance (e.g., Kecak, Legong), sculpture, painting, and the gamelan orchestra. Traditional society is organized around castes and ancestral villages. The Subak irrigation system, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, reflects the Hindu philosophy of *Tri Hita Karana* (harmony with God, people, and nature). Festivals like Nyepi (Day of Silence) are central to cultural life.

### Government and Demographics
Bali is divided into eight regencies and one city (Denpasar). The population was estimated at 4.46 million in 2024. The majority are ethnic Balinese, with significant Javanese and other Indonesian minorities. The province's politics have been dominated by the Indonesian Democratic Party of Struggle (PDI-P). The first direct gubernatorial election was held in 2008.

## Terms
- **Subak**: A traditional cooperative water management and irrigation system for rice paddies, recognized as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **Puputan**: A ritual mass suicide committed by Balinese royalty and followers to avoid the humiliation of surrender to a colonial power.
- **Wallace Line**: A faunal boundary line between the Indomalayan and Australasian ecozones, running between Bali and Lombok.
- **Gamelan**: A traditional Balinese percussion orchestra music, highly developed and varied.
- **Kecak**: A famous Balinese dance and musical drama, often called the "monkey dance."
- **Tri Hita Karana**: A Balinese Hindu philosophy meaning "three causes of good fortune": harmony with God, other people, and the environment.
- **Nyepi**: The Balinese Hindu New Year, celebrated with a day of silence and fasting.
- **Ogoh-ogoh**: Large, monstrous sculptures paraded and burned on the eve of Nyepi to drive away evil spirits.
- **Bali Aga**: The indigenous, pre-Hindu people of Bali.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise meaning of the element "Vāli" in the ancient name *Vāli-dvīpa* is uncertain, with hypotheses ranging from "offering" to "strength."
- Scholars debate whether the Balinese caste system has direct Indian origins or developed with significant local variation.
- The impact of tourism on Balinese culture is controversial, as it has created new performance opportunities but also commodified sacred rituals for foreign audiences.
- The Indonesian government's official classification of Balinese Hinduism as a monotheistic religion is contested by some scholars as not fully reflecting traditional beliefs and practices.
