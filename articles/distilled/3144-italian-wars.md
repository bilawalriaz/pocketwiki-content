# Italian Wars

## Overview
The Italian Wars (1494–1559) were a series of conflicts fought primarily on the Italian Peninsula, representing a pivotal struggle for European dominance between the Valois dynasty of France and the Habsburg dynasty of Spain and the Holy Roman Empire. These wars marked a turning point in military history, introducing new technologies like the arquebus and mobile artillery, and they ultimately reshaped the European balance of power, leading to a long period of Habsburg influence in Italy and setting the stage for future conflicts like the French Wars of Religion.

## Timeline
- **1494** — Charles VIII of France invades Naples, starting the First Italian War.
- **1495** — League of Venice formed against France; Battle of Fornovo.
- **1499** — Louis XII invades and captures the Duchy of Milan.
- **1503** — Spanish forces defeat the French at Cerignola and Garigliano, expelling them from Naples.
- **1508** — League of Cambrai formed against the Republic of Venice.
- **1515** — Francis I of France defeats the Swiss at Marignano, reasserting control over Milan.
- **1525** — French King Francis I is captured by Imperial forces at the Battle of Pavia.
- **1527** — Imperial troops sack Rome, capturing Pope Clement VII.
- **1530** — Charles V is named King of Italy at Bologna, marking the start of long-term Habsburg dominance.
- **1559** — Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis ends the wars, confirming Spanish hegemony in southern Italy and Milan.

## Body

### Origins and Early Conflicts (1494–1504)
The wars began after the collapse of the Italic League, a 40-year peace pact. In 1494, Charles VIII of France, using an Angevin claim to Naples, invaded the peninsula. His swift advance and the brutal "Sack of Naples" prompted the formation of the anti-French League of Venice. Although Charles withdrew, the invasion revealed Italy's wealth and political fragmentation, inviting further foreign intervention. His successor, Louis XII, conquered Milan in 1499 and, via the secret Treaty of Granada (1500), agreed with Spain to partition Naples. This alliance quickly collapsed into war, ending with decisive Spanish victories that expelled France from southern Italy by 1503.

### The Habsburg-Valois Struggle (1508–1530)
The conflict evolved into a direct rivalry between Francis I of France and Emperor Charles V, who united Spain and the Holy Roman Empire under one rule. Pope Julius II initially allied with Charles against Venice (League of Cambrai, 1508) but later switched sides to oppose French power, forming the Holy League. After years of shifting alliances and battles like Marignano (1515), the Treaty of Noyon (1516) temporarily recognized French control of Milan. The rivalry intensified after Charles V's election as Emperor in 1519. Francis I's catastrophic defeat and capture at Pavia (1525) led to the War of the League of Cognac, which culminated in the 1527 Sack of Rome by mutinous Imperial troops. The subsequent Treaty of Cambrai (1529) and the 1530 Diet of Bologna established Charles V's primacy in Italy.

### Final Phases and Conclusion (1536–1559)
Francis I and his successor Henry II continued to challenge Habsburg power, often allying with the Ottoman Empire. Conflicts in the 1530s and 1540s saw fighting in Piedmont and Flanders but no decisive Italian breakthrough. The final war began in 1551, with France seizing the Three Bishoprics and supporting the capture of Siena. The focus later shifted to Flanders, where Spain defeated France at St. Quentin (1557). The Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis (1559) ended the wars: France retained Calais and the Three Bishoprics but abandoned its Italian claims. Spain secured sovereignty over Naples, Sicily, and Milan, while an independent Duchy of Savoy was restored.

### Military Revolution
The wars were a crucible for military innovation. Charles VIII's 1494 invasion introduced a highly mobile siege train of bronze culverins and bombards using iron cannonballs, which could reduce fortifications in hours. Infantry tactics evolved from reliance on Swiss pikemen to integrated "pike and shot" formations. The arquebus (a light, matchlock handgun) proved its value at the 1503 Battle of Cerignola, where Spanish general Gonzalo de Córdoba used volley fire to devastate French cavalry. These changes, along with the rise of professional armies and printed military manuals, are seen as marking the transition from medieval to modern warfare.

## Terms
- **Italic League:** A 1454 non-aggression pact among Italian states that maintained stability until its collapse in 1492.
- **League of Venice:** A 1495 anti-French alliance of Italian states, Spain, and the Holy Roman Empire formed after the Sack of Naples.
- **Arquebus:** A light, matchlock handgun that became a key infantry weapon during the wars.
- **Culverin:** A type of long, light cannon used in the mobile French siege trains.
- **Landsknechts:** German mercenary pikemen who were prominent in early 16th-century armies.
- **Habsburg Ring:** The encirclement of France by Habsburg territories (Spain, the Empire, the Netherlands) achieved through dynastic marriages.
- **Treaty of Granada (1500):** A secret agreement between France and Spain to partition the Kingdom of Naples.
- **Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis (1559):** The final peace treaty that ended the Italian Wars and confirmed Spanish dominance in Italy.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Spanish Hegemony:** While 20th-century historians often viewed the post-1559 settlement as establishing clear Spanish hegemony in Italy, 21st-century scholarship contests this, arguing that Spain's power was limited and that other entities, especially the Papacy and the Holy Roman Empire, retained significant influence.
- **Impact on State Formation:** Historian Charles Tilly argued the Italian Wars were a key part of his theory of state formation, demonstrating the value of large armies and superior military technology in creating a "comprehensive European state system."
- **Franco-Ottoman Alliance:** The nature and impact of the alliance between Christian France and the Muslim Ottoman Empire, which shocked contemporaries, is a subject of ongoing study regarding its diplomatic and military significance.
