# Paco de Lucía

## Overview
Paco de Lucía (1947–2014) was a Spanish virtuoso flamenco guitarist, composer, and producer who revolutionized the genre by fusing traditional flamenco with jazz, classical, and world music. Born in Algeciras to a musical family, he was rigorously trained by his father from age five, emerging as a child prodigy. His technical innovations—lightning-fast *picados* (fingerstyle runs), harmonic sophistication, and the integration of the Peruvian *cajón* (box drum) and electric bass—transformed flamenco from a rigid folk tradition into a dynamic, globally recognized art form (*nuevo flamenco*). His landmark collaborations with singer Camarón de la Isla and jazz guitarists John McLaughlin, Al Di Meola, and Larry Coryell expanded flamenco’s audience worldwide. Critics and peers, including Eric Clapton, regard him as a "titanic figure" whose influence divides flamenco history into "Before Paco" and "After Paco."

## Timeline
- **21 Dec 1947** — Born Francisco Gustavo Sánchez Gómez in Algeciras, Cádiz, Spain.
- **1958 (age 11)** — First public appearance on Radio Algeciras; meets Sabicas in Málaga.
- **1961 (age 14)** — Records first album *Los Chiquitos de Algeciras* with brother Pepe.
- **1963** — Tours with José Greco troupe in New York; mentored by Sabicas and Mario Escudero, who urge him to compose original material.
- **1967** — Releases debut solo album *La fabulosa guitarra de Paco de Lucía*; appears at Berlin Jazz Festival, sparking lifelong jazz fascination.
- **1968–1977** — Records ten acclaimed albums with singer Camarón de la Isla, defining modern flamenco.
- **1973** — Releases *Fuente y caudal*, featuring hit "Entre dos aguas"; pioneers jazz-flamenco fusion with bongos and electric bass.
- **18 Feb 1975** — First flamenco performer at Madrid's Teatro Real.
- **1977** — Marries Casilda Varela; performs with Carlos Santana; collaborates with Al Di Meola on "Mediterranean Sundance."
- **1979** — Forms The Guitar Trio with John McLaughlin and Larry Coryell; tours Europe.
- **1981** — Forms Paco de Lucía Sextet (introducing *cajón* to flamenco via Rubem Dantas); releases *Sólo quiero caminar*; Al Di Meola replaces Coryell in The Guitar Trio.
- **1981** — *Friday Night in San Francisco* (The Guitar Trio) sells over 1 million copies.
- **1984** — Composes soundtrack for *The Hit* with Eric Clapton; acts in Carlos Saura's *Carmen* (BAFTA nomination for score).
- **1987** — Releases *Siroco*, widely cited as his masterpiece and a flamenco classic.
- **1990** — Records *Zyryab* with Chick Corea and Manolo Sanlúcar, exploring Arabic roots of flamenco.
- **1991** — Performs Joaquín Rodrigo's *Concierto de Aranjuez* with orchestra (learned without reading notation).
- **2004** — Retires from full touring; releases *Cositas Buenas* (Latin Grammy for Best Flamenco Album).
- **2007** — Awarded Doctor Honoris Causa by University of Cádiz.
- **2010** — Honorary doctorate from Berklee College of Music; performs at Montreux Festival.
- **25 Feb 2014** — Dies of heart attack in Playa del Carmen, Mexico, aged 66.
- **2014 (posthumous)** — Latin Grammy Album of the Year for *Canción Andaluza*.

## Body

### Early Life and Formation (1947–1960s)
Paco de Lucía was born into a flamenco dynasty: his father, Antonio Sánchez Pecino, was a guitarist who studied under a cousin of Melchor de Marchena, and his brothers Pepe (singer) and Ramón (guitarist) became his lifelong collaborators. To distinguish himself among many "Pacos" in Algeciras, he adopted the matronymic "de Lucía" to honor his Portuguese mother, Lúcia Gomes. His father enforced a brutal regimen—up to 12 hours of daily practice from age five, eventually withdrawing him from school. Biographer Donn Pohren likened this to Leopold Mozart’s tutelage of Wolfgang. Ramón introduced him to the complex *falsetas* (melodic variations) of Niño Ricardo, his primary early influence. By 11, he was performing on radio; at 12, he won a special prize at the Jerez flamenco competition.

### Traditional Roots and Jazz Awakening (1960s)
De Lucía’s early recordings (*Dos guitarras flamencas*, *12 éxitos para 2 guitarras flamencas*) were strictly traditional. Touring with José Greco’s troupe brought him to New York (1963), where mentors Sabicas and Mario Escudero urged him to compose. His 1967 Berlin Jazz Festival debut exposed him to Miles Davis and Thelonious Monk, igniting a fascination with jazz harmony and improvisation. Simultaneously, he began a decade-long partnership with singer Camarón de la Isla (1968–1977), recording ten albums that modernized *cante* (flamenco singing) accompaniment. He declined lucrative solo contracts, preferring ensemble tours with his brother and Camarón.

### The *Nuevo Flamenco* Revolution (1970s)
The 1970s marked de Lucía’s radical stylistic break. *El duende flamenco* (1972) and *Fuente y caudal* (1973) introduced jazz structures, electric bass, and bongos. "Entre dos aguas" (a rumba referencing the Mediterranean-Atlantic meeting at Algeciras) became a crossover hit. *Almoraima* (1976) deepened Arabic and jazz influences. His 1975 Teatro Real debut legitimized flamenco in Spain’s premier classical venue. Collaborations with Carlos Santana (1977) and Al Di Meola ("Mediterranean Sundance," 1977) drew criticism from purists, though Di Meola insisted Paco was "expanding, not leaving" flamenco. The 1978 *Interpreta a Manuel de Falla* demonstrated classical fluency. Forming The Guitar Trio with McLaughlin and Coryell (1979) forced de Lucía to confront his lack of formal theory: "I have never studied music... playing with McLaughlin and Di Meola was about learning these things."

### Ensemble Innovation and Global Stardom (1980s)
The Guitar Trio’s *Friday Night in San Francisco* (1981) became a platinum milestone. That year, de Lucía formed the Paco de Lucía Sextet, integrating the Peruvian *cajón* (played by Rubem Dantas) as flamenco’s primary percussion—a pivotal timbral shift adopted globally. *Sólo quiero caminar* (1981) and *Castro Marín* (1981) showcased the sextet’s interplay. He collaborated extensively with Chick Corea (adapting "Spain") and scored films (*The Hit* with Eric Clapton, *Carmen* by Carlos Saura). By 1986, both the Trio and Sextet plateaued; Di Meola cited exhaustion from "virtuosity arms races," while Paco sought "controlled expression over velocity." *Siroco* (1987) marked a return to roots, yielding modern classics "La Barrosa" (alegrías) and "Gloria al Niño Ricardo" (soleá).

### Maturity, Classical Crossover, and Retirement (1990s–2014)
*Zyryab* (1990) with Chick Corea and Manolo Sanlúcar explored the 9th-century Persian polymath Ziryab, credited with bringing the lute to Spain. In 1991, de Lucía tackled Rodrigo’s *Concierto de Aranjuez*, learning the score by ear (he never mastered notation); Rodrigo praised his "feeling, far more than is normally heard." He performed at Expo '92 (Seville) and Plaza Mayor (Madrid). *Luzia* (1998), dedicated to his dying mother, is considered his most mature statement. After living in Mexico (1998–2003), he retired from touring in 2004, playing only sporadic festivals. *Cositas Buenas* (2004) won a Latin Grammy. He received honors from the Prince of Asturias (2004), University of Cádiz (2007), and Berklee (2010). He died suddenly of a heart attack in Mexico in 2014.

### Legacy
De Lucía’s impact is equated to Andrés Segovia’s for classical guitar. He codified *nuevo flamenco*, systematizing jazz-Andalusian fusion. The *cajón* is now ubiquitous in flamenco and Latin music. His harmonic vocabulary—abstract chords, scale substitutions—and right-hand velocity/fluidity (*picados*, *rasgueados*) set a new technical ceiling. As *JazzTimes* noted, flamenco history is divided into "Before Paco or After Paco." He remains Spain’s most influential musical export, inducted into the Latin Songwriters Hall of Fame (2015) and honored with a Google Doodle (2016).

## Terms
- ****Picados**** — Flamenco fingerstyle runs: rapid, alternating index/middle finger single-note passages, executed with rest strokes for volume and attack.
- ****Rasgueados**** — Flamenco strumming techniques using fingernails in rhythmic patterns (e.g., 5-stroke *rasgueado*), providing percussive drive.
- ****Falsetas**** — Melodic variations or improvisational passages composed/played by the guitarist between sung verses (*coplas*).
- ****Cajón**** — Afro-Peruvian box drum; adopted by de Lucía (via Rubem Dantas, c. 1981) as flamenco’s standard percussion, replacing hand-clapping (*palmas*).
- ****Nuevo Flamenco**** — "New Flamenco": the post-1970s movement blending traditional forms with jazz harmony, global rhythms, and modern instrumentation, pioneered by de Lucía, Camarón, and Enrique Morente.
- ****Compás**** — The rhythmic cycle (meter + accent pattern) defining each flamenco *palo* (style); e.g., 12-beat cycle for *soleá*, *bulerías*, *alegrías*.
- ****Bulerías**** — Fast, festive 12-beat *palo* (often 220+ BPM) with accents on 12, 3, 6, 8, 10; a showcase for rhythmic virtuosity.
- ****Alegrías**** — Bright, major-key 12-beat *palo* from Cádiz; structurally similar to *soleá* but lighter in character.
- ****Soleá**** — Foundational 12-beat *palo* (slow, profound); "mother of flamenco" forms.
- ****Ziryab (Zyryab)**** — 9th-century Persian polymath at Córdoba’s Umayyad court; credited with introducing the lute (guitar ancestor) and early flamenco elements to Iberia.

## Debates and open questions
- **Purity vs. Evolution**: Traditionalists (critics, older guitarists) condemned de Lucía’s jazz fusions, electric bass, and *cajón* as diluting flamenco’s "duende" (soul/authenticity). De Lucía countered that he kept "one hand on tradition, the other digging for new things."
- **Improvisation vs. Composition**: Jazz collaborators (McLaughlin, Di Meola) operated from harmonic theory; de Lucía relied on ear and *compás* intuition. He admitted struggling to improvise over complex changes, raising questions about the limits of "intuitive" fusion.
- **Commercialization**: *Friday Night in San Francisco*’s massive success sparked debate: did accessibility compromise flamenco’s marginalized, Gitano (Roma) cultural core?
- **Notation vs. Oral Tradition**: De Lucía’s inability to read music (until forced by *Concierto de Aranjuez*) highlights the tension between flamenco’s oral transmission and classical institutionalization.
- **Attribution of Innovations**: While de Lucía popularized the *cajón* in flamenco, Caitro Soto (Peru) and Rubem Dantas (Brazil) were instrumental in its adaptation; the extent of Paco’s sole agency is debated.
