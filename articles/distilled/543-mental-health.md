# Mental health

## Overview
Mental health is a state of well-being enabling individuals to realize their abilities, cope with life stresses, work productively, and contribute to their community. It is not merely the absence of mental illness but a continuum encompassing emotional, psychological, and social well-being. Mental disorders—health conditions altering cognition, emotion, and behavior—affect nearly one billion people globally (970 million as of 2019), with anxiety and depression the most common. Historical treatment shifted from inhumane institutionalization to community-based care (deinstitutionalization), though gaps in funding and "transinstitutionalization" into prisons persist. Mental health is shaped by biological, psychological, social, and environmental determinants, and is heavily influenced by cultural stigma and disparities in access to care.

## Timeline
- **Mid-19th century** — William Sweetser coins "mental hygiene"; Dorothea Dix advocates for state psychiatric hospitals (over 30 built 1840–1880).
- **1896** — Emil Kraepelin develops taxonomy of mental disorders dominating the field for ~80 years.
- **1908** — Clifford Beers publishes *A Mind That Found Itself*; founds National Committee for Mental Hygiene (1909).
- **1939** — Penrose Hypothesis theorizes inverse relationship between psychiatric bed numbers and prison populations.
- **1963** — US Community Mental Health Centers Act enforces deinstitutionalization, restricting state hospital admissions.
- **1975** — NIMH creates Community Support Program (CSP) to fund comprehensive community services (housing, employment).
- **1980** — US Mental Health Systems Act prioritizes service expansion beyond clinical care.
- **1990s** — First US state psychiatric hospitals begin closing; number drops by ~40.
- **2019** — ~970 million people worldwide suffer from a mental disorder (WHO).
- **2021** — Over 22% of US adults meet criteria for mental illness; depression projected leading global disease burden by 2030.

## Body

### Definitions and Models
The WHO defines mental health as a state of well-being involving self-realization, stress coping, productivity, and community contribution. The Public Health Agency of Canada frames it as the capacity to feel, think, and act for quality of life within cultural boundaries. From positive psychology and holism, mental health is a positive attribute—encompassing subjective well-being, self-efficacy, autonomy, and resilience—not just the absence of disease. The **tripartite model** structures well-being into three measurable components: *emotional well-being* (high positive affect), *psychological well-being* (skills for optimal functioning), and *social well-being* (social contribution and integration). The Mental Health Continuum-Short Form (MHC-SF) is the primary scale for this model.

### Epidemiology and Demographics
Mental illnesses are more prevalent than cancer, diabetes, or heart disease. As of 2021, over 22% of US adults had a mental illness; globally, 970 million people had a disorder in 2019. Major depression ranks third in global disease burden, projected to lead by 2030. Suicide claims over 700,000 lives annually (~14 million attempts). The global economic cost was ~$2.5 trillion in 2010 (two-thirds indirect), projected to exceed $6 trillion by 2030. Prevalence rises in younger cohorts: US adults 18–25 report 33.7% prevalence vs. 15% for those 50+. Women show higher rates of depression, anxiety, and eating disorders; men show higher substance use and developmental disorders. Autism carries high comorbidity (~80% lifetime mental health problems). LGBTQ+, homeless, refugee, and dementia populations face elevated risk and access barriers.

### History: Institutionalization to Deinstitutionalization
Early US care involved religious punishment and inhumane confinement. Dorothea Dix’s "mental hygiene movement" (1840s) established state hospitals, though they became understaffed and rights-violating. Clifford Beers (1908) exposed asylum abuses, founding the mental hygiene movement, which later faced criticism for eugenics associations. Post-WWII, "mental health" replaced "mental hygiene" to emphasize prevention. The 1963 Community Mental Health Centers Act drove **deinstitutionalization**, restricting state admissions to imminent danger cases. While beneficial for many (increased satisfaction, quality of life) *if* funded and managed well, critics note persistent poor conditions, family burden, and lack of medical care in community settings. The **Penrose Hypothesis** (1939) describes "transinstitutionalization": as psychiatric beds fell, prison populations rose, with mentally ill individuals cycling between hospitals and criminal justice systems. "Compensation prisoners" (unable to pay fines) exemplify this cycle of homelessness, mental illness, and reoffending.

### Risk Factors and Causes
Mental health problems arise from interacting biological, psychological, and social factors.
*   **Biological:** Neurotransmitter disruptions (dopamine, glutamate, norepinephrine) are implicated in schizophrenia.
*   **Demographic:** Gender, age, ethnicity, and population density modify risk.
*   **Social/Environmental:** Childhood trauma, unhappy marriage/divorce (3–25x depression risk), unemployment (linked to depressive disorders, worse with high inequality/poor protections), poverty, and diet (processed foods, gut microbiota disruption) increase risk.
*   **Stress:** The **vulnerability-stress model** posits stress triggers latent vulnerabilities; the **differential susceptibility hypothesis** suggests sensitive individuals suffer more in poor environments but benefit more in supportive ones.

### Stigma and Disparities
No culture grants people with mental illness equal societal value. Three stigma types operate: **public stigma** (negative attitudes → discrimination: withholding help, avoidance, coercion, segregation), **structural stigma** (policies limiting resources/research funding), and **self-stigma** (internalized shame). Common misconceptions link mental illness to violence, childlike incompetence, or weak character. Disparities intersect with identity:
*   **Race/Ethnicity:** Asian Americans face high self-stigma (Confucian "face," achievement pressure); Latino Americans face structural barriers (documentation, language, *machismo* shame) and discrimination.
*   **Gender:** Men internalize stigma via stoicism norms (isolation, treatment resistance); women face "overly emotional" labeling despite higher help-seeking.
*   **Finance:** Poverty creates physical/logistical barriers; unemployment invites "lazy/irresponsible" stereotypes.
*   **Geography:** Rural areas have higher stigma, stoicism norms, and severe provider shortages vs. urban centers.
*   **Culture/Religion:** Mental health is socially constructed; cultural competency (e.g., Deaf culture, non-Western frameworks) is essential. Rigid fundamentalist faiths may cause harm; US providers often lack non-Western cultural competence.
*   **Media:** Films historically sensationalize violence (e.g., *Joker* 2019 increased prejudice per Scarf et al. 2020) or romanticize "love cures all." Positive portrayals (e.g., *Crazy Ex-Girlfriend* for BPD) are increasing.

### Specific Populations
*   **Adolescents:** 13% global prevalence (2015); 1 in 7 aged 10–19 had a disorder in 2019 (165M). Half of conditions start before age 20. Suicide is 4th leading death cause (15–19). Cyberbullying links to depression/anxiety. Most cases undetected/untreated (only ~25% of children with behavioral problems reach services).
*   **Dementia:** Depression/anxiety common comorbidities, worsening function and suicide risk (esp. early stages). Frontotemporal and young-onset dementia carry higher mood risk. Non-drug therapies (psychological, social, activity-based) are first-line.
*   **Homeless:** High prevalence (e.g., 53% PTSD in homeless women in St. Louis). Case management linked to services reduces hospitalization and substance use.
*   **Refugees/Immigrants:** Pre-migration trauma (torture, loss) + post-migration stressors (discrimination, isolation, legal status) drive depressive disorders. Family reunification is a key protective factor.
*   **Social Work:** Psychiatric social workers (60% of US mental health professionals) address overlapping internal/external problems across settings (hospitals, corrections, community). In Japan, they coordinate procedural services for reintegration.

### Prevention, Promotion, and Treatment
**Prevention** targets risk factors; **promotion** enhances protective factors (self-esteem, mastery, social inclusion). Strategies include WHO’s *Prevention of Mental Disorders* (2004), EU *Pact for Mental Health* (2008), US *National Prevention Strategy* (2011). Early intervention in youth is most cost-effective. **Care navigation** guides patients through fragmented systems, increasing treatment engagement (US treatment-seeking tripled 1990–2003 despite stable prevalence).

**Treatment modalities:**
*   **Pharmacotherapy:** Antidepressants, benzodiazepines, lithium; prescribed by psychiatrists.
*   **Physical activity:** Exercise matches antidepressants for some depression/anxiety via endorphins; cessation worsens outcomes.
*   **Activity/Occupational therapy:** Engagement in meaningful "occupations" (self-care, work, leisure) improves daily functioning and cognitive skills.
*   **Expressive therapies:** Art, music (NICE-approved for psychosis), drama, dance, poetry.
*   **Psychotherapy:** Evidence-based schools include CBT, DBT, psychoanalysis, gestalt, psychedelic therapy, transpersonal psychology. Group formats include psychodynamic, support (12-step), psychoeducation.
*   **Self-compassion:** Components (Self-Kindness, Common Humanity, Mindfulness) predict emotional, social, psychological well-being.
*   **Social-Emotional Learning (SEL):** CASEL framework (self-awareness, management, social awareness, relationships, decision-making) shows small but significant effects on externalizing problems and skills.
*   **Meditation/Mindfulness:** Reduces depression, anxiety, stress; aids substance use disorders.
*   **Lucid dreaming:** Associated with well-being; may reduce nightmares.
*   **Mental fitness:** Intentional regulation via routine, sleep, exercise, mindfulness, social contact to build resilience.
*   **Spiritual counseling:** Integrates spiritual/psychological/theological principles.
*   **Digital tools/EHR access:** Patient access to Electronic Health Records increases understanding, control, trust, medication awareness, and empowerment. Social media enables awareness campaigns but requires managed use.

### Laws and Public Policy (US Focus)
US has highest annual prevalence (26%) among 14 comparable nations. Four major reforms: Dix’s asylum movement (1843), Beers’ mental hygiene (1908), deinstitutionalization (Action for Mental Health, 1961), community support (CMCH Act Amendments, 1975). Key legislation: Community Mental Health Centers Act (1963), Mental Health Systems Act (1980), Medicaid expansions for chronic illness. Helping Families in Mental Health Crisis Act (HR2646, 2013/2015) passed Health Subcommittee 18–12. Persistent issues: decade-long treatment delay, <1/3 receive minimally adequate care, community programs underfunded, homelessness/incarceration cycles.

**Global Perspective:** WHO and international bodies increasing funding. East Asia: Confucian stigma (weakness, "face"), underfunding, school-based awareness rising. Africa: Cultural stigma, physical health prioritization (AIDS/malaria/Ebola), policy gaps, workforce shortages. Latin America: Poverty-driven prevalence, Catholic/Protestant cultural stigma.

## Terms
- ****Mental health**** — A state of well-being where an individual realizes abilities, copes with normal stresses, works productively, and contributes to community (WHO).
- ****Mental disorder / Mental illness**** — Health conditions altering cognitive functioning, emotional responses, and behavior, associated with distress and/or impaired functioning.
- ****Deinstitutionalization**** — Policy of closing state psychiatric hospitals and replacing them with community mental health services (initiated in US by 1963 Act).
- ****Transinstitutionalization**** — The shift of mentally ill populations from psychiatric hospitals into prisons/jails (per Penrose Hypothesis, 1939).
- ****Tripartite model**** — Framework viewing mental well-being as three components: emotional, psychological, and social well-being.
- ****Vulnerability-stress model**** — Theory that stressful life events trigger mental illness in individuals with preexisting biological/psychological vulnerabilities.
- ****Differential susceptibility hypothesis**** — Theory that certain individuals are more sensitive to environment—for worse in negative conditions, better in positive ones.
- ****Public / Structural / Self-stigma**** — Public: others' discriminatory attitudes. Structural: policies limiting opportunities. Self: internalized shame by the affected person.
- ****Psychiatric social work**** — Practice helping individuals attain freedom from overlapping internal/external problems (social, economic, psychiatric) to achieve harmony and adaptation.
- ****Care navigation**** — Service guiding patients/families through fragmented mental health systems to appropriate therapies and referrals (distinct from providing therapy).

## Debates and open questions
*   **Deinstitutionalization outcomes:** Whether community care has failed due to inherent model flaws or chronic underfunding; the extent of "transinstitutionalization" into prisons vs. other drivers of incarceration.
*   **Prevention vs. treatment allocation:** Optimal balance of resources between population-level promotion/prevention and clinical treatment for existing severe illness.
*   **Cultural validity of diagnostics:** Whether ICD-11/DSM-5 categories and Western treatment models apply universally or require fundamental adaptation for non-Western, Indigenous, and minority cultures.
*   **Digital technology net effect:** Whether expanded telehealth access and mental health apps outweigh risks of social media-driven anxiety, cyberbullying, and algorithmic harm, especially for adolescents.
*   **Biological vs. psychosocial primacy:** The relative weight of neurotransmitter pathology vs. social determinants (poverty, trauma, inequality) in etiology and treatment planning.
*   **Stigma reduction efficacy:** Which interventions (education, contact, protest, structural policy change) most durably reduce public, structural, and self-stigma across diverse cultures.
