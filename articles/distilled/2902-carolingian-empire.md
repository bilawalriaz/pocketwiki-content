# Carolingian Empire

## Overview
The Carolingian Empire (800–887) was a Frankish-dominated state in Western and Central Europe during the Early Middle Ages. Ruled by the Carolingian dynasty, it represented the largest western political entity since the fall of Rome and is considered the first phase of the Holy Roman Empire. Its legacy lies in its political structure, legal reforms, and cultural revival, which laid foundations for medieval European governance and identity.

## Timeline
- **732** — Charles Martel defeats a Muslim army at the Battle of Tours.
- **751** — Pepin III becomes King of the Franks with papal approval.
- **768** — Charlemagne begins his rule, initially co-inheriting with his brother Carloman I.
- **774** — Charlemagne defeats the Lombard Kingdom and annexes it.
- **800** — Pope Leo III crowns Charlemagne as Roman Emperor.
- **814** — Charlemagne dies; Louis the Pious inherits the empire.
- **840** — Death of Louis the Pious triggers civil war among his sons.
- **843** — Treaty of Verdun divides the empire into three kingdoms.
- **884** — Charles the Fat briefly reunites the Carolingian kingdoms.
- **887** — Charles the Fat is deposed; the empire fractures permanently.

## Body

### Rise and Reign of Charlemagne
The Carolingian dynasty rose to power under Charles Martel, who cemented his authority with the victory at Tours in 732. His son Pepin III became king in 751, and his grandson Charlemagne began his rule in 768. Charlemagne's reign was marked by near-constant warfare, expanding the empire to include Lombardy, Bavaria, Saxony, and lands up to the Elbe River. In 800, he was crowned Emperor by Pope Leo III, an act that disregarded the weakened Byzantine Empire's claims and established a new imperial tradition in the West.

### Civil War and Division
Charlemagne's son, Louis the Pious, inherited the empire but faced constant dynastic struggles. His attempts to include his fourth son in the succession led to civil war among his sons. After Louis's death in 840, his eldest son Lothar claimed the entire empire, sparking further conflict. The war concluded with the Treaty of Verdun in 843, which divided the empire into three autonomous kingdoms: a central realm for Lothar (with the imperial title), an eastern realm for Louis the German, and a western realm for Charles the Bald. This division marked the beginning of distinct political entities that would evolve into France and Germany.

### Decline and Fragmentation
After 843, the empire was weakened by internal strife and external threats, particularly Viking raids. The title of emperor passed between branches of the family, but real power was fragmented. Charles the Fat briefly reunited the kingdoms in 884 but was deposed in 887. His death in 888 led to the empire's permanent fracture, with regional nobles electing kings from outside the Carolingian dynasty or, in the east, an illegitimate Carolingian line. The legitimate Carolingian dynasty was restored in the west in 898 but ruled until 987.

### Government and Administration
The Carolingian government, forged under Charlemagne, was based on an itinerant court and a system of palaces, with Aachen as a primary residence. Authority was enforced through counts (local administrators), *missi dominici* (royal emissaries), and vassals. The legal system upheld the principle of "plurality of law," where different ethnic groups were tried under their own traditional codes. Charlemagne issued *capitularies* (written royal decrees) to standardize laws and administration across the empire. Justice was dispensed at local assemblies called *mallus*, presided over by counts or royal officials.

### Military and Economy
The Carolingian military relied on annual campaigns, excellent logistics, and siege warfare rather than a revolutionary cavalry force. Soldiers were required to provide their own weapons and supplies. The empire's economy was agrarian, with coinage controlled by the emperor. Charlemagne reformed the silver coinage to resemble Roman models, with his image and title (*imperator augustus*) on the coins.

### Religion and Culture
Charlemagne aimed to unify his empire under Christianity. The *Admonitio Generalis* of 789 set standards for clerical education and made him responsible for his subjects' salvation. This fostered a cultural revival known as the Carolingian Renaissance, where scholars like Alcuin promoted learning and preserved classical texts. The empire was seen as a Christian empire (*Imperium Christianum*), with bishops and abbots playing key governmental roles.

## Terms
- **Carolingian Empire**: The Frankish-dominated empire in Western and Central Europe from 800 to 887, ruled by the Carolingian dynasty.
- **Capitulary**: A written record of decisions made by Carolingian kings, covering royal orders, laws, and deliberations.
- **Missi Dominici**: Royal emissaries sent in pairs (one secular, one ecclesiastical) to enforce the king's will and administer justice in the provinces.
- **Plurality of Law**: A legal principle where different ethnic groups within the empire were tried under their own traditional law codes.
- **Mallus**: A public assembly for dispensing basic justice, presided over by a count or royal official.
- **Bannum**: The emperor's right to rule, command, and exercise supreme jurisdiction over all territories.
- **Itinerant Court**: A royal court that moved constantly between palaces, a common feature of early medieval governance.
- **Admonitio Generalis**: A major capitulary issued by Charlemagne in 789, setting standards for religious and secular life.
- **Treaty of Verdun**: The 843 agreement that divided the Carolingian Empire into three kingdoms among Louis the Pious's sons.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Depth of Imperial Control**: Historians debate how much direct influence the emperor truly had over distant regions, given the reliance on local magnates and the itinerant nature of the court.
- **Feudalism's Origins**: Some scholars see the Carolingian Empire as the template for medieval feudalism, while others, like Marc Bloch, criticize the search for a single "birth" of feudalism.
- **Demographic Trends**: Estimates of the empire's population (10–20 million) are uncertain. Historian Timothy Newfield challenges the idea of steady demographic expansion, pointing to frequent famines.
- **Purpose of Capitularies**: There is debate over whether capitularies were effective laws or merely a "royal wish-list," given limited evidence of their implementation across the realm.
