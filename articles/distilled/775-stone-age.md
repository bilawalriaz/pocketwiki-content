# Stone Age

## Overview
The Stone Age is a prehistoric period defined by the widespread use of stone tools, lasting roughly 3.4 million years and ending between 4000 and 2000 BC with the rise of metalworking. It constitutes 99% of human technological history and is the first of the three-age system (Stone, Bronze, Iron) used in archaeology. The period is subdivided into the Paleolithic (Old Stone Age), Mesolithic (Middle Stone Age), and Neolithic (New Stone Age), reflecting evolving tool technologies and human subsistence strategies.

## Timeline
- **~3.4 mya** — Oldest stone tool evidence at Lomekwi 3, Kenya
- **~2.6 mya** — Earliest dated stone tools at Gona, Ethiopia
- **~1.7 mya** — Acheulean tools appear in Africa
- **~300 kya** — Middle Stone Age begins in Africa
- **~50 kya** — Later Stone Age begins in Africa
- **~10,000 BC** — End of Pleistocene; Mesolithic begins
- **~7000 BC** — Neolithic begins in Fertile Crescent
- **~3000 BC** — Bronze Age begins in Western Asia

## Body

### Origins and Early Tool Use
The Stone Age began with the earliest known stone tools, dated to 3.3 million years ago at Lomekwi 3 in Kenya. These predate the genus Homo, suggesting earlier hominins like Australopithecus or Paranthropus may have created them. The oldest indirect evidence—fossilized animal bones with cut marks—dates to 3.4 million years ago in Ethiopia. The Oldowan industry, characterized by simple flake tools, emerged around 2.6 million years ago and is primarily associated with Homo habilis.

### Three-Age and Three-Stage Systems
The Stone Age is part of the three-age system (Stone, Bronze, Iron), developed in 19th-century Europe. In Africa, the three-stage system (Early, Middle, Later Stone Age) was proposed in 1929 by Goodwin and van Riet Lowe, recognizing that the European three-age model did not apply to sub-Saharan Africa. The Pan-African Congress on Prehistory adopted this system in 1947.

### Technological Evolution
The Lower Paleolithic saw the development of Oldowan (Mode 1) tools, followed by Acheulean (Mode 2) hand axes around 1.7 million years ago, associated with Homo erectus. The Middle Paleolithic featured Mousterian tools linked to Neanderthals. The Upper Paleolithic marked a surge in symbolic behavior, art, and complex toolkits among modern humans. The Mesolithic introduced microliths (Mode 5) adapted to post-glacial environments.

### Regional Variations and Transitions
The transition from Stone Age to metal-based societies varied globally. In Western Asia, bronze smelting began by 3000 BC. Sub-Saharan Africa moved directly from stone to iron. The Americas retained stone technologies until around 2000 BC. The concept of the Stone Age is criticized for its Eurocentric origins and ambiguous application to non-European societies.

### Material Culture and Society
Stone tools were made from flint, chert, basalt, and other materials. Hunter-gatherers relied on wild plants and animals, with evidence of cereal consumption dating back 23,000 years. Shelter construction began around 2 million years ago. Cave paintings (e.g., Chauvet, Lascaux) and petroglyphs reflect symbolic and possibly ritual behavior. Megalithic tombs indicate complex social structures in the Neolithic.

## Terms
- **Lithic analysis**: Scientific study of stone tools, including typology, function, and manufacturing techniques.
- **Flintknapping**: The craft of shaping stone tools by striking flakes from a core.
- **Mode 1**: Oldowan tool technology; simple core-and-flake tools.
- **Mode 2**: Acheulean technology; bifacial hand axes and large cutting tools.
- **Chalcolithic**: Copper Age; transitional period between Stone and Bronze Ages.
- **Paleolithic**: Old Stone Age; earliest period of stone tool use.
- **Neolithic**: New Stone Age; characterized by agriculture and polished stone tools.
- **Mousterian**: Middle Paleolithic tool industry associated with Neanderthals.
- **Microlith**: Small stone tools of the Mesolithic, often used in composite implements.

## Debates and Open Questions
The origins of stone tool technology remain debated, with questions about whether early tools were made by Australopithecus or early Homo. The "Out of Africa" hypothesis for Acheulean technology is supported by evidence from 'Ubeidiya, Israel. The transition from Mode 1 to Mode 2 lacks clear archaeological evidence in North Africa. The authorship of the Châtelperronian industry (associated with both Neanderthals and modern humans) remains contested. The applicability of the Stone Age concept to non-European societies is criticized for its ethnocentric bias. The causes of megafaunal extinctions at the end of the Pleistocene involve multiple factors including climate change and human hunting.

## Additional Context

### End of the Stone Age
Innovation in smelting ore marks the end of the Stone Age and beginning of the Bronze Age. The first significant metal manufactured was bronze, an alloy of copper and tin or arsenic. The transition period during which people could smelt copper but not yet make bronze is known as the Copper Age (Chalcolithic or Eneolithic). The transition out of the Stone Age occurred between 6000 and 2500 BC for much of humanity in North Africa and Eurasia. The first evidence of human metallurgy dates to between the 6th and 5th millennia BC in the Vinča culture sites of modern-day Serbia, including Majdanpek, Jarmovac, Pločnik, and Rudna Glava. Ötzi the Iceman, a mummy from about 3300 BC, carried a copper axe and a flint knife. In some regions, such as Sub-Saharan Africa, the Stone Age was followed directly by the Iron Age. The Middle East and Southeast Asian regions progressed past Stone Age technology around 6000 BC. Europe and the rest of Asia became post-Stone Age societies by about 4000 BC. The proto-Inca cultures of South America continued at a Stone Age level until around 2000 BC, when gold, copper, and silver made their entrance. Stone tool manufacture continued even after the Stone Age ended in a given area; in Europe and North America, millstones were in use until well into the 20th century.

### Concept and Critique
The terms "Stone Age", "Bronze Age", and "Iron Age" are not intended to suggest that prehistoric advancements are only measured by tool material, rather than social organization, food sources, adaptation to climate, adoption of agriculture, cooking, settlement, and religion. The concept of the Stone Age has limitations: its date range is ambiguous, disputed, and variable depending on region. Some groups never developed metal-smelting technology and remained in the "Stone Age" until encountering technologically developed cultures. The term was innovated to describe the archaeological cultures of Europe and may not always be the best for regions such as parts of the Indies and Oceania, where farmers or hunter-gatherers used stone for tools until European colonisation began. As a description of living groups, the term Stone Age is controversial; the Association of Social Anthropologists discourages this use, asserting that describing any living group as "primitive" or "Stone Age" implies they are living representatives of an earlier stage of human development.
