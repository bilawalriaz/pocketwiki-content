# Lhasa

## Overview
Lhasa, the inner urban district of Lhasa City, is the historical and current capital of Tibet. Situated at an altitude of 3,656 meters (11,990 ft), it is one of the world's highest cities and has been the religious and administrative center of Tibet since the mid-17th century. The city is renowned for its UNESCO World Heritage Sites, including the Potala Palace and Jokhang Temple, which are central to Tibetan Buddhism.

## Timeline
- **637 CE** — Songtsen Gampo builds the first structures on the site of the Potala Palace and moves the Tibetan capital to Rasa (Lhasa).
- **639-641 CE** — Songtsen Gampo constructs the Jokhang and Ramoche Temples to house Buddha statues brought by his Nepalese and Chinese wives.
- **9th Century** — Lhasa suffers extensive damage under Langdarma; the Tibetan Empire fragments and political power shifts away from the city.
- **15th Century** — Lhasa rises to prominence again with the founding of the Gelugpa monasteries Ganden, Sera, and Drepung.
- **1642** — The 5th Dalai Lama unifies Tibet and establishes his administration in Lhasa, making it the political and religious capital.
- **1645-1694** — Major construction and expansion of the Potala Palace occurs.
- **1720** — The Qing dynasty army enters Lhasa and establishes resident commissioners (Ambans).
- **1751** — The Qing government reorganizes the Tibetan government and establishes the governing council (Kashag) in Lhasa.
- **1904** — A British expedition briefly occupies Lhasa.
- **1950** — The Chinese People's Liberation Army launches the Battle of Chamdo.
- **1959** — Following a failed uprising, the 14th Dalai Lama flees Tibet.
- **1965** — The Tibet Autonomous Region is established, with Lhasa as its capital.

## Body

### History and Name
The area was originally called Rasa, meaning "goat's place." The name was changed to Lhasa, meaning "place of gods," after it became the capital and the Jokhang Temple was completed. The city's importance waned after the 9th-century collapse of the Tibetan monarchy but was revived in the 15th century by the Gelugpa school of Buddhism. The 5th Dalai Lama solidified Lhasa's status as the unified capital of Tibet in 1642. The city experienced foreign incursions, including a brief British occupation in 1904 and the entry of Qing forces in 1720. Following the 1959 uprising, the 14th Dalai Lama fled, and Lhasa became the capital of the newly established Tibet Autonomous Region in 1965.

### Geography and Climate
Lhasa lies in the center of the Tibetan Plateau at an elevation of about 3,600 meters (11,800 ft), with surrounding mountains rising to 5,500 meters (18,000 ft). The Lhasa River, a tributary of the Yarlung Zangbo (Brahmaputra), flows through the southern part of the city. The climate is a cool semi-arid type with frosty winters and mild summers. It receives nearly 3,000 hours of sunlight annually, earning it the nickname "sunlit city." Annual precipitation is 458 millimeters (18.0 in), falling mostly in July, August, and September.

### Demographics
Historical population figures vary. A 1854 census recorded 42,000 people, while a 1911 encyclopedia estimated about 30,000, including a large monastic population. By 1952, the population was estimated at 25,000–30,000, or 45,000–50,000 including nearby monasteries. The 2010 census recorded 429,104 Tibetans (76.70%) and 121,065 Han Chinese (21.64%) in Lhasa. Official statistics for Chengguan District report a 63% ethnic Tibetan population, though some outside observers suggest the non-Tibetan population may be higher.

### Architecture and Landmarks
Lhasa's core landmarks are UNESCO World Heritage Sites. The **Potala Palace**, the former chief residence of the Dalai Lama, was begun in 1645. The **Jokhang Temple**, founded in the 7th century, is considered the most sacred temple in Tibet. The **Norbulingka**, built from 1755, was the traditional summer residence of the Dalai Lamas. Other significant sites include the **Barkhor** pilgrimage circuit around the Jokhang, the **Ramoche Temple**, and the **Tibet Museum**. Many sites were damaged during the Cultural Revolution but have been restored since the 1980s.

### Economy and Transport
The economy is shifting from traditional agriculture and animal husbandry toward tourism and services. The city is a traditional trading hub. The **Qinghai–Tibet Railway**, opened in 2006, is the world's highest railway and connects Lhasa to China's national network. **Lhasa Gonggar International Airport** provides air links to major Chinese cities and occasional flights to Kathmandu. Major highways connect Lhasa to Xining, Chengdu, and the Nepalese border.

## Terms
- **Chengguan District**: The administrative term for the inner urban area of a Chinese prefecture-level city; in this case, the core of Lhasa City.
- **Gelugpa**: The school of Tibetan Buddhism founded by Je Tsongkhapa in the 15th century, to which the Dalai Lama lineage belongs.
- **Dalai Lama**: The spiritual leader of Tibetan Buddhism; the political ruler of Tibet from the 17th century until 1959.
- **Potala Palace**: The iconic fortress-palace in Lhasa, serving as the winter residence of the Dalai Lama and the seat of government.
- **Jokhang Temple**: The most sacred and important Buddhist temple in Tibet, located in the Barkhor area of Lhasa.
- **Ambans**: Resident commissioners sent by the Qing dynasty to oversee Tibetan affairs in Lhasa.
- **Kashag**: The governing council of Tibet, established by the Qing dynasty in 1751.
- **Barkhor**: The pilgrim circuit and market area surrounding the Jokhang Temple in old Lhasa.
- **Norbulingka**: The park and palace complex that served as the traditional summer residence of the Dalai Lamas.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The historical population of Lhasa is debated, with significant discrepancies between official censuses, historical accounts, and outside estimates, particularly regarding the proportion of Tibetan and non-Tibetan residents.
- A Tibetan tradition claims Chinese troops invaded and burned the Red Palace after Songtsen Gampo's death in 649 CE. However, this event is not recorded in Chinese annals or Tibetan Dunhuang manuscripts, leading scholars to suggest it may be a later interpolation.
- The identity of the town "Barantola" on old European maps is uncertain; it is suggested to be Lhasa but may refer to a location in Qinghai province.
