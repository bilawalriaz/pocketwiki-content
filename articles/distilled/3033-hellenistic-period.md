# Hellenistic period

## Overview
The Hellenistic period (323–30 BC) is the era following Alexander the Great's death, during which Greek culture, language, and political influence spread across the Eastern Mediterranean, Near East, and parts of Asia. It marks the transition from Classical Greek city-states to vast, multicultural empires ruled by Alexander's successors, the Diadochi. This age saw significant cultural fusion, scientific advancement, and the eventual rise of Rome as the dominant Mediterranean power.

## Timeline
- **338 BC** — Philip II of Macedon defeats Athens and Thebes at the Battle of Chaeronea, forming the League of Corinth.
- **323 BC** — Death of Alexander the Great in Babylon, leading to the Wars of the Diadochi.
- **301 BC** — Battle of Ipsus; death of Antigonus I and the definitive split of Alexander's empire into successor kingdoms.
- **281 BC** — Battle of Corupedium; death of Seleucus I, solidifying the tripartite division of the Hellenistic world.
- **217 BC** — Battle of Raphia; Ptolemaic victory over the Seleucid Empire.
- **190 BC** — Battle of Magnesia; Roman victory over the Seleucid Empire, ending Seleucid expansion in Anatolia.
- **168 BC** — Third Macedonian War ends with the defeat of Perseus, ending the Antigonid dynasty.
- **146 BC** — Roman destruction of Corinth and the establishment of a Roman protectorate over mainland Greece.
- **31 BC** — Battle of Actium; Octavian defeats Mark Antony and Cleopatra VII.
- **30 BC** — Roman annexation of Ptolemaic Egypt, ending the last major Hellenistic kingdom.

## Body

### Origins and Successor Kingdoms
The period began with the fragmentation of Alexander the Great's empire after his death in 323 BC. His generals, the Diadochi, fought a series of wars that resulted in the establishment of several major kingdoms: the Ptolemaic Kingdom in Egypt, the Seleucid Empire in the Near East, and the Antigonid dynasty in Macedon. Smaller kingdoms like Pergamon, Pontus, and the Greco-Bactrian Kingdom also emerged. These states were ruled by a Greco-Macedonian elite, with Greek serving as the administrative and cultural lingua franca, known as Koine Greek.

### Cultural Fusion and Spread
Hellenistic culture was a synthesis of Greek traditions with those of the Near East, Egypt, and Persia. This fusion is evident in religion (e.g., the cult of Serapis), art, and philosophy. Greek influence spread through the founding of new cities (like Antioch and Alexandria), military colonies, and trade. While the Greek elite often remained distinct, local elites in regions like Judea, Armenia, and Parthia adopted Greek language and customs to varying degrees, a process known as Hellenization.

### Science, Philosophy, and the Arts
The era was marked by significant intellectual and artistic achievement. Centers like the Library of Alexandria and the Mouseion fostered scholarship. Key figures include the mathematician Euclid, the polymath Archimedes, and the astronomer Hipparchus. Philosophy saw the rise of Stoicism, Epicureanism, and Pyrrhonism, which focused on personal ethics and tranquility. Art moved from Classical idealism to more emotional, realistic, and dramatic styles, exemplified by sculptures like the *Dying Gaul* and the *Venus de Milo*.

### The Rise of Rome
Roman involvement began in Italy and Sicily. After defeating Macedon in the Second Macedonian War (200–197 BC), Rome became the arbiter of Greek affairs. The destruction of Corinth in 146 BC marked the end of Greek political independence. The final Hellenistic kingdoms fell to Rome: the Seleucid rump state was absorbed by Pompey, and Ptolemaic Egypt was annexed by Octavian after the Battle of Actium in 31 BC.

## Terms
- **Diadochi**: The "Successors," the generals of Alexander the Great who fought for control of his empire after his death.
- **Koine Greek**: The common Attic-based dialect that emerged as the lingua franca of the Hellenistic world.
- **Hellenization**: The spread of Greek culture, language, and ideas in non-Greek regions.
- **Syncretism**: The amalgamation of different religions, cultures, or schools of thought, as seen in Hellenistic deities like Serapis.
- **Ptolemaic Kingdom**: The Hellenistic dynasty ruling Egypt from 305 BC to 30 BC, founded by Ptolemy I.
- **Seleucid Empire**: The largest Hellenistic state, founded by Seleucus I, covering much of the Near East.
- **Antigonid dynasty**: The Macedonian royal line that ruled Macedon and parts of Greece.
- **Mouseion**: The "Museum" or "Temple of the Muses," a research institution in Alexandria, home to the famous Library.
- **Proskynesis**: A Persian court ritual of prostration or bowing, which Alexander attempted to introduce to his Greek and Macedonian subjects.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **End Date**: Scholars debate the precise end of the period. While 30 BC (death of Cleopatra) is most common, other proposed dates include 146 BC (Roman destruction of Corinth), AD 330 (Constantine's move to Constantinople), or even AD 138.
- **Nature of Hellenization**: Historians disagree on whether the spread of Greek culture was a deliberate policy of the rulers or a more organic process of cultural diffusion. The extent and depth of its adoption by native populations are also debated.
- **Scientific Legacy**: Some scholars, like Lucio Russo, argue that the scientific method was born in the 3rd century BC, while others contend its significance has been overstated compared to Classical Greek science.
