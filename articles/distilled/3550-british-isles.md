# British Isles

## Overview
The British Isles are a North Atlantic archipelago off north-western Europe, comprising over 6,000 islands, including Great Britain and Ireland. The term is primarily geographical, not political, and its usage is contested, particularly by the Government of Ireland. The islands have a complex geological history, a temperate climate, and a shared yet distinct cultural and political past shaped by successive migrations, invasions, and political unions.

## Timeline
- **c. 12,000 BC** — Re-inhabitation after the last glacial period begins; Great Britain is a peninsula of Europe.
- **c. 7000 BC** — Great Britain becomes an island with the flooding of Doggerland.
- **1st millennium BC** — Inhabited by Gaels, Picts, and Britons speaking Insular Celtic languages.
- **AD 43** — Roman Empire begins conquest of much of Britain.
- **5th century** — Anglo-Saxons arrive as Roman power wanes, eventually dominating England.
- **9th century** — Viking invasions begin, leading to settlements and political change.
- **1066** — Norman conquest of England.
- **1169** — Angevin (Norman) invasion of Ireland begins.
- **1603** — Union of the Crowns: James VI of Scotland inherits the English and Irish crowns.
- **1707** — Acts of Union create the Kingdom of Great Britain from England and Scotland.
- **1801** — Acts of Union create the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland.
- **1919–1922** — Irish War of Independence and Anglo-Irish Treaty lead to the secession of most of Ireland.

## Body

### Geography and Climate
The archipelago's geology records a vast span of Earth's history, shaped by tectonic collisions like the Caledonian orogeny and numerous glaciations. The highest point is Ben Nevis (1,345 m), and the largest lake is Lough Neagh (390 km²). The climate is temperate oceanic, moderated by the North Atlantic Drift, which raises temperatures 11 °C above the global average for the latitude. This climate historically supported temperate rainforest, now largely cleared.

### Flora and Fauna
The islands' plant and animal life is similar to, but less diverse than, north-western Europe. Ireland's native species all migrated from Great Britain before the land bridge melted c. 14,000 years ago. Prehistoric forest cover was extensive but was gradually cleared for agriculture, especially from 6000 BC. Many large mammals like wolves are now extinct, but species like red deer, otters, and seals persist. The islands host about 250 regular bird species and over 21,000 insect species.

### Demographics and Languages
England holds almost 80% of the total population. The Great Irish Famine caused a century-long population decline in Ireland, drastically altering the demographic balance and creating a large global diaspora. The linguistic heritage includes twelve Indo-European languages. The Insular Celtic languages (Goidelic and Brittonic sub-groups) are the only surviving Celtic languages. English is the dominant language, with few monoglots in other languages.

### History and Politics
The islands were inhabited by Celtic tribes before the Roman conquest of southern Britain. Anglo-Saxon settlement followed the Roman withdrawal, creating England. Viking invasions led to the Norman conquest of England in 1066 and later the partial Norman conquest of Ireland. Political unions culminated in the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland in 1801. Most of Ireland seceded in 1919–1922, leaving Northern Ireland within the UK.

Today, the two sovereign states are the Republic of Ireland and the United Kingdom. The Crown Dependencies (Isle of Man, Jersey, Guernsey) are self-governing but under the UK's responsibility for defence and international representation. The UK comprises four nations with devolved governments in Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland. The Northern Ireland peace process, formalized in the 1998 Good Friday Agreement, established power-sharing and cross-border institutions like the North/South Ministerial Council and the British–Irish Council.

### Culture and Transport
Cultural institutions like the Mercury Music Prize and Costa Book Awards span the islands. Many modern sports, including football, rugby, and golf, were codified here. The Dublin-London air route is Europe's busiest. The Channel Tunnel (1994) links Great Britain to France, and proposals for an Irish Sea tunnel or bridge have been made but not realized.

## Terms
- **Archipelago:** A group of islands.
- **Insular Celtic languages:** The branch of Celtic languages native to the British Isles, comprising Goidelic (Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Manx) and Brittonic (Welsh, Cornish, Breton).
- **Crown Dependencies:** The Isle of Man and the Bailiwicks of Jersey and Guernsey, which are not part of the UK but for which the UK is responsible for defence and international representation.
- **Devolved government:** A system where a central government (UK Parliament) transfers certain powers to a regional parliament or assembly (e.g., in Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland).
- **Common Travel Area:** An open borders area comprising the UK, Ireland, the Isle of Man, and the Channel Islands.
- **Good Friday Agreement (1998):** A peace deal that ended most of the violence in Northern Ireland, establishing power-sharing and cross-border institutions.
- **British–Irish Council:** An intergovernmental organization established by the Good Friday Agreement, comprising all states and territories of the British Isles.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Terminology:** The term "British Isles" is politically sensitive and not used by the Irish government, which prefers "these islands" or "Britain and Ireland." Alternatives like "Atlantic Archipelago" or "Anglo-Celtic Isles" have limited academic use.
- **Political Status of Northern Ireland:** The Good Friday Agreement allows for a future vote on reunification with Ireland, a question that remains a central, unresolved issue in the region's politics.
