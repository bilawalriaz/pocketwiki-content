# Yosemite National Park

## Overview
Yosemite National Park is a protected area in California's Sierra Nevada, internationally renowned for its granite cliffs, waterfalls, and giant sequoia groves. It was critical to the development of the U.S. national park concept, serving as a model for federal land preservation and wilderness protection.

## Timeline
- **c. 10,000–8,000 years ago** — First humans may have entered the area.
- **1851** — The Mariposa Battalion, led by James D. Savage, enters Yosemite Valley, marking the first well-documented European American entry.
- **1864** — President Abraham Lincoln signs the Yosemite Grant, the first time land is set aside by the U.S. government for preservation and public use.
- **1890** — Yosemite Valley and surrounding areas are established as a National Park by an Act of Congress.
- **1906** — President Theodore Roosevelt signs a bill transferring control of Yosemite Valley and Mariposa Grove from California to the federal government.
- **1913** — The O'Shaughnessy Dam is approved via the Raker Act, flooding the Hetch Hetchy Valley.
- **1916** — The National Park Service is formed, and Yosemite is transferred to its jurisdiction.
- **1984** — Congress designates about 89% of the park as the Yosemite Wilderness.
- **2016** — The park sets a visitation record, surpassing five million visitors for the first time.

## Body

### Indigenous History and Displacement
The region was inhabited by the Ahwahneechee people, part of the Southern Sierra Miwok. The 1848–1855 California Gold Rush brought over 90,000 European Americans, leading to resource competition and the Mariposa War (part of the California genocide). The Mariposa Battalion drove the Ahwahneechee from the valley. Native Americans continued to live in the area in reduced numbers, adapting to tourism, but were progressively evicted by the National Park Service, with the last village destroyed in 1969. The Southern Sierra Miwuk Nation continues to seek federal recognition.

### Creation and Protection
Concern over commercial development led citizens like Galen Clark to lobby for protection. The 1864 Yosemite Grant set a precedent for federal land preservation. Naturalist John Muir later campaigned against sheep grazing and logging, leading to the 1890 creation of Yosemite National Park. Muir's advocacy, including a 1903 camping trip with President Theodore Roosevelt, resulted in federal control of the entire park in 1906. The Hetch Hetchy Valley was dammed for San Francisco's water supply in 1913 despite Muir's opposition.

### Geology and Landscape
Yosemite's landscape is primarily carved from the granitic Sierra Nevada Batholith. About 10 million years ago, the Sierra Nevada was uplifted, steepening river valleys. Pleistocene glaciers (c. 2–3 million to 10,000 years ago) then sculpted the iconic U-shaped valleys, such as Yosemite Valley. Erosion along joint systems created features like domes (via exfoliation) and pillars. The park contains three groves of giant sequoia and numerous waterfalls, including Yosemite Falls, the fourth tallest in North America.

### Ecology and Management
The park spans five vegetation zones and supports over 250 vertebrate species. Key management issues include human-wildlife conflict (e.g., with black bears), invasive species, and the loss of a natural fire regime. Climate change is causing reduced snowpack, earlier snowmelt, and more intense wildfires. Restoration projects, such as the 2016 purchase and restoration of Ackerson Meadow, aim to repair past damage.

## Terms
- **Batholith**: A large mass of intrusive igneous rock that formed deep below the Earth's surface.
- **Exfoliation**: The process where sheets of rock peel away from a larger rock mass due to pressure release, creating domes.
- **Joint**: A fracture in rock where no movement has occurred, which controls erosion patterns.
- **Mediterranean climate**: A climate with mild, wet winters and hot, dry summers.
- **Orographic lift**: The process where air is forced upward over mountains, causing cooling and precipitation.
- **Pluton**: A body of intrusive igneous rock that crystallized from magma below the surface.
- **Pleistocene**: The geological epoch from about 2.6 million to 11,700 years ago, known for repeated glaciations.
- **Wilderness**: A federally designated area where the land and its biological communities are protected and managed to appear free from human influence.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The proposal to dam the Hetch Hetchy Valley created a lasting conflict between preservationists (like John Muir) and those supporting utilitarian use of public lands for resources.
- The National Park Service's historical treatment of Indigenous peoples, including evictions and cultural suppression, remains a point of contention and ongoing dialogue regarding sovereignty and recognition.
- The impact of climate change on Yosemite's glaciers, ecosystems, and fire regimes presents ongoing scientific and management challenges.
