# Miami

## Overview
Miami is a major coastal city in southeastern Florida and a global hub for finance, commerce, culture, and international trade. Its identity is profoundly shaped by its majority-Hispanic population and deep ties to Latin America, earning it nicknames like the "Gateway to Latin America." The city's history, from its indigenous roots to its modern skyline, reflects waves of migration, economic booms, and environmental challenges.

## Timeline
- **c. 500–600 BCE** — Tequesta tribe village established at the mouth of the Miami River.
- **1566** — Spanish admiral Pedro Menéndez de Avilés claims the area for Spain.
- **1821** — Spain cedes Florida to the United States.
- **1836** — U.S. builds Fort Dallas, leading to conflict in the Second Seminole War.
- **1896** — Miami is officially incorporated as a city with a population of just over 300.
- **1920s** — Population boom; legacy of Jim Crow and Ku Klux Klan influence noted.
- **1926** — A major hurricane and the collapse of the land boom slow development.
- **1940** — Population reaches 172,172, aided by World War II defense base.
- **1959** — Cuban Revolution leads to a wave of Cuban refugees settling in Miami.
- **1972** — Miami hosts both major U.S. political conventions.
- **2001** — City of Miami government goes bankrupt.
- **2020** — Census records population at 442,241; 70.2% identify as Hispanic/Latino.

## Body

### History and Founding
The area was inhabited by the Tequesta tribe for about 2,000 years before European contact. Spain claimed it in 1566 and ruled for centuries, except for a brief British period (1763–1783), until ceding Florida to the U.S. in 1821. The modern city was founded by Julia Tuttle, who convinced railroad tycoon Henry Flagler to extend his railway to the region after the Great Freeze of 1894–1895. Miami was incorporated in 1896. Early 20th-century growth was fueled by Bahamian and African-American migrant labor, though the city enforced strict racial segregation. The 1920s boom was halted by a hurricane and the Great Depression. Post-WWII, the Cuban Revolution (1959) triggered a major demographic shift, solidifying Miami's Hispanic majority and international profile.

### Geography and Climate
Miami sits on a broad, low-elevation plain between the Everglades and Biscayne Bay, averaging about 6 feet (1.8 m) above sea level. The bedrock is Miami oolite, formed from ancient sea deposits. The Biscayne Aquifer provides drinking water but limits underground construction. The climate is tropical monsoon (Köppen *Am*), with hot, wet summers and warm, dry winters. The city is highly vulnerable to sea-level rise and hurricanes, with projections estimating 21 to 40 inches (53 to 100 cm) of rise by 2070.

### Demographics and Culture
Miami is a majority-minority city where 70.2% of residents are Hispanic or Latino (2020). Its largest ancestral groups are Cuban, Central American, and South American. The non-Hispanic Black population has declined since 1990, while the non-Hispanic White population has rebounded since 2000. The city's culture is a blend of Latin American, Caribbean, and American influences, reflected in its cuisine (e.g., the Cuban sandwich), its unique "Miami accent" (a rhotic English dialect with Spanish-influenced rhythm), and its status as a major center for Spanish-language media and music.

### Economy and Infrastructure
Miami's economy is driven by international trade, finance, tourism, and media. It is a major gateway to Latin America, with PortMiami being the world's busiest cruise port and Miami International Airport a top U.S. international gateway. The city has a high concentration of international banks, especially along Brickell Avenue. Tourism is a key industry, with over 15.9 million visitors in 2017. The city's government uses a mayor-commissioner system. Public transportation includes Metrorail, Metromover, and Tri-Rail, though car commuting is dominant.

## Terms
- **Miami oolite**: The surface bedrock of the Miami area, a limestone formed from ancient marine deposits.
- **Biscayne Aquifer**: A natural underground freshwater source that supplies most of the region's drinking water.
- **Köppen climate classification Am**: A climate type denoting a tropical monsoon climate with distinct wet and dry seasons.
- **Manhattanization**: A term for rapid high-rise construction in a city, as seen in Miami's downtown boom.
- **Floribbean cuisine**: A regional cooking style blending Latin American, Caribbean, and American culinary traditions.
- **Miami accent**: A rhotic American English dialect influenced by Spanish, native to second- or third-generation Hispanics in the area.
- **PortMiami**: The city's seaport, recognized as the world's busiest cruise port.
- **Metrorail**: Miami's elevated heavy-rail rapid transit system.
- **Metromover**: A free, elevated people mover system serving downtown Miami and Brickell.
- **Tri-Rail**: A commuter rail system connecting Miami, Fort Lauderdale, and West Palm Beach.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The political shift in Miami-Dade County, traditionally Democratic, toward Republican candidates in recent elections (2020, 2022, 2024) is a significant transformation, with Trump nearly flipping the city of Miami in 2024.
- The long-term impact of climate change and sea-level rise on Miami's infrastructure, real estate values, and water supply remains a critical and ongoing concern.
- The causes and effects of the decline in Miami's non-Hispanic Black population, attributed to gentrification and rising costs, are subjects of demographic and social analysis.
