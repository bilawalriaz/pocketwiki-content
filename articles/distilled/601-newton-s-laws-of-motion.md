# Newton's laws of motion

## Overview
Newton's laws of motion are three fundamental principles formulated by Isaac Newton in his 1687 work *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica*. They describe the relationship between the motion of an object and the forces acting on it, forming the foundation of classical mechanics. These laws explain everything from planetary orbits to everyday motion, though they break down at very small scales or near light speed, where quantum mechanics and relativity take over.

## Timeline
- **1687**: Newton publishes the three laws of motion in the *Principia Mathematica*.
- **1638**: Galileo describes inertia in *Two New Sciences*, influencing Newton's first law.
- **1644**: Descartes publishes early versions of inertia and conservation of momentum.
- **1656**: Christiaan Huygens formulates a concise version of the first law.
- **1673**: Huygens' *Horologium Oscillatorium* contributes to the understanding of force and motion.
- **1716**: Jakob Hermann writes the second law in the form F = ma.
- **1740s**: Leonhard Euler employs F = ma as a basic premise and pioneers rigid body dynamics.
- **1798–1825**: Laplace's *Traité de mécanique céleste* develops mechanics algebraically.
- **19th century**: Conservation of energy established; vector algebra developed.
- **Early 20th century**: Quantum mechanics and relativity reveal limitations of Newtonian physics.

## Body

### First Law (Law of Inertia)
A body remains at rest or in uniform motion in a straight line unless acted upon by a net external force. This principle of inertia implies no privileged inertial observer: all observers moving at constant velocity relative to one another experience equivalent physics. Newton believed in absolute space and time, but acknowledged only relative measurements are experimentally accessible.

### Second Law (Law of Acceleration)
The net force on a body equals the rate of change of its momentum: F = dp/dt. For constant mass, this simplifies to F = ma. Forces are vectors that add component-wise, and a net force of zero implies mechanical equilibrium. Newton's second law also serves as a research program: identifying forces and constituents of matter. For variable-mass systems (e.g., rockets), the full momentum form must be used, as F = ma leads to incorrect results.

### Third Law (Action and Reaction)
Forces between two bodies are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. This law underpins conservation of momentum: in an isolated system, internal forces cancel, leaving total momentum constant. Misinterpretations like "action equals reaction" often confuse students, since action and reaction act on different bodies.

### Candidates for Additional Laws
Some propose a "zeroth law" (additivity of mass) or a "fourth law" (vector addition of forces, superposition principle). These reflect foundational assumptions rather than derived results.

### Examples
- **Uniformly Accelerated Motion**: Free fall near Earth's surface yields constant acceleration g ≈ 9.8 m/s², independent of mass.
- **Uniform Circular Motion**: Centripetal acceleration a = v²/r directed toward the center; orbits approximate this under gravity.
- **Harmonic Motion**: Restoring force F = -kx leads to oscillation with frequency ω = √(k/m).
- **Variable Mass Systems**: Rockets require careful application of momentum conservation.
- **Fan and Sail**: Illustrates subtleties in applying the third law to open systems.

### Work and Energy
Though developed after Newton, energy concepts are central to Newtonian mechanics. Kinetic energy arises from motion; potential energy from position. The work-energy theorem states work done equals change in kinetic energy. Conservative forces derive from scalar potentials (F = -∇U); friction is non-conservative.

### Rigid-Body Motion and Rotation
Rigid bodies maintain shape. Motion splits into translation of the center of mass and rotation about it. Angular momentum L = r × p; torque τ = r × F. Conservation of angular momentum holds when net torque is zero.

### Multi-body Gravitational Systems
Newton's law of universal gravitation explains orbits as conic sections (ellipses, parabolas, hyperbolas). The three-body problem lacks general closed-form solutions, requiring numerical methods.

### Chaos and Unpredictability
Newtonian systems can exhibit chaos—sensitive dependence on initial conditions. Examples include the three-body problem and double pendulum. Fluid dynamics uses Newton's laws via the Euler and Navier-Stokes equations.

### Relation to Other Formulations
- **Lagrangian Mechanics**: Uses kinetic minus potential energy (L = T - V) and the principle of least action.
- **Hamiltonian Mechanics**: Describes dynamics via position and momentum using Hamilton's equations.
- **Hamilton-Jacobi Equation**: Reformulates mechanics analogously to wave optics.

### Relation to Other Physical Theories
- **Thermodynamics/Statistical Physics**: Applies Newton's laws to large ensembles of particles.
- **Electromagnetism**: Coulomb's law mirrors gravity; Lorentz force law integrates with Newton's second law.
- **Special Relativity**: Modifies momentum (p = mγv) and challenges simultaneity in the third law.
- **General Relativity**: Replaces gravity with spacetime curvature; reduces to Newtonian gravity in weak-field limits.
- **Quantum Mechanics**: Position and momentum become operators; Ehrenfest theorem links quantum expectations to Newton's laws.

### History
Predecessors include Aristotle (natural vs. violent motion), Philoponus (impetus theory), and Galileo (inertia). Newton synthesized celestial and terrestrial mechanics into a unified framework. Post-Principia developments refined the laws mathematically and conceptually, culminating in modern formulations and relativistic/quantum extensions.

## Terms
- **Inertia**: Tendency of a body to resist changes in motion.
- **Momentum**: Product of mass and velocity (p = mv).
- **Force**: Vector quantity causing acceleration; measured in newtons (N).
- **Conservation of Momentum**: Total momentum of an isolated system remains constant.
- **Centripetal Force**: Force causing circular motion, directed toward the center.
- **Simple Harmonic Motion**: Oscillatory motion where restoring force is proportional to displacement.
- **Lagrangian**: Function L = T - V used in variational principles.
- **Hamiltonian**: Total energy function H(p, q) governing dynamics.
- **Chaos**: Deterministic yet unpredictable behavior due to sensitivity to initial conditions.
- **Relativity**: Theories modifying Newtonian mechanics at high speeds or strong gravity.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Whether Newton's laws should include implicit assumptions like mass additivity or force superposition.
- The extent to which Newtonian mechanics can be derived from energy-based formulations.
- Whether the Euler and Navier-Stokes equations exhibit finite-time blowup (Millennium Prize Problem).
- Interpretational issues in quantum mechanics regarding the correspondence to classical laws via the Ehrenfest theorem.
