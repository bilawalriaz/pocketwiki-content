# Sanitation

## Overview  
Sanitation encompasses the safe management of human excreta, wastewater, solid waste, and stormwater, along with hygiene promotion, to prevent disease transmission—particularly via the fecal–oral route. It is a cornerstone of public health, human dignity, and environmental protection, and is recognized as a human right by the UN since 2010. Sanitation systems involve the full "sanitation value chain": capture, storage, transport, treatment, and reuse or disposal of waste. Globally, over 3.4 billion people still lack safely managed sanitation, making it a central focus of Sustainable Development Goal 6.

## Body  

### Definitions and Scope  
Sanitation is defined by the WHO as the provision of facilities and services for the safe disposal of human waste, along with maintenance of hygienic conditions through services like garbage collection and wastewater disposal. It includes four technical and non-technical systems: excreta management, wastewater treatment, solid waste management, and stormwater drainage. However, definitions vary; some organizations (e.g., within the WASH sector) limit sanitation to excreta management. The Sphere Handbook includes hygiene promotion, vector control, and WASH in disease outbreaks under sanitation. The term "environmental sanitation" refers to broader control of environmental factors affecting health, including industrial waste and noise pollution.

### Purposes and Benefits  
The primary goals of sanitation are to ensure a healthy living environment, protect natural resources, and uphold human dignity and safety. Effective systems interrupt disease transmission cycles, especially fecal-oral pathogens, using frameworks like the F-diagram (feces, fingers, flies, fields, fluids, food). Economically, every $1 invested in sanitation yields $5.50 in societal returns. In India, inadequate sanitation costs 6.4% of GDP due to healthcare expenses, lost productivity, and reduced tourism. Access to household toilets improves women’s literacy and labor force participation.

### Types of Sanitation Systems  
Sanitation technologies range from centralized sewer systems and wastewater treatment plants to onsite solutions like pit latrines and septic tanks. Onsite sanitation (or non-sewered sanitation systems, NSSS) treats waste locally and accounts for about half of global sanitation provision. Other approaches include ecological sanitation (recycling nutrients), container-based sanitation (collecting waste in sealed containers), and community-led total sanitation (behavioral change without subsidies). The "sanitation ladder" ranks service levels from open defecation to "safely managed," the latter requiring not-shared, improved facilities with proper excreta treatment or transport.

### Global Mechanisms and Initiatives  
Sanitation is a global priority under SDG 6, aiming for universal access by 2030. The Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP) tracks progress, reporting that 4.5 billion people lacked safely managed sanitation in 2017. The Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation launched the Reinvent the Toilet Challenge in 2011 to innovate waste treatment technologies. Capacity development initiatives, such as India’s Sanitation Capacity Building platform (2015–2022), emphasize on-the-job training and institutional learning. Lifecycle cost studies show sewer systems are most expensive, while simpler technologies like pour-flush latrines are cheaper but require careful planning.

### Health and Environmental Aspects  
Sanitation directly reduces diarrheal diseases, helminth infections, cholera, and stunting in children. Indicator organisms like *E. coli* and helminth eggs are used to assess fecal contamination in water, soil, and sludge. Climate impacts include methane emissions from NSSS, estimated at 377 Mt CO₂e annually in 2020. Environmental sanitation protects groundwater and surface water from leachate and runoff, particularly through proper landfill design and wastewater treatment.

## Terms  
- **Safely managed sanitation**: Highest service level per SDG 6; uses improved, non-shared facilities with safe excreta treatment or transport.  
- **Sanitation ladder**: JMP framework ranking service levels from open defecation to safely managed.  
- **Onsite sanitation (NSSS)**: Systems treating waste on the same plot where generated; includes pit latrines and septic tanks.  
- **F-diagram**: Model showing fecal-oral disease transmission routes: feces, fingers, flies, fields, fluids, food.  
- **Sanitation value chain**: Full process of capturing, storing, transporting, treating, and reusing/disposing of excreta and wastewater.  
- **Environmental sanitation**: Control of environmental factors harmful to health, including solid waste, drainage, and industrial waste.  
- **Hygiene promotion**: Behavioral interventions encouraging WASH practices to reduce disease risk.  
- **Fecal sludge management (FSM)**: Emptying, transport, and treatment of sludge from onsite systems.  
- **Basic sanitation service**: Use of improved, non-shared sanitation facilities.  
- **Limited sanitation service**: Use of improved facilities shared between households.

## Debates and Open Questions  
- Whether sanitation should include wastewater, solid waste, and stormwater management or be limited to excreta management.  
- The relative effectiveness of infrastructure-focused vs. behavior-change approaches (e.g., CLT vs. subsidized toilets).  
- Trade-offs between centralized sewer systems and decentralized/on-site solutions in terms of cost, scalability, and sustainability.  
- How to equitably finance sanitation services for marginalized populations, especially in urban informal settlements.  
- Balancing reuse of treated waste (nutrients, water, energy) with public health and environmental safety concerns.
