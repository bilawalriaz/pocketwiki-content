# Tasmania

## Overview
Tasmania is an island state of Australia, separated from the mainland by Bass Strait. It is Australia's smallest and least populous state, known for its unique biodiversity, significant convict history, and a strong environmental movement. Its isolation has fostered distinct ecosystems and a unique cultural identity.

## Timeline
- **c. 42,000 years ago** — Evidence of Aboriginal presence in Tasmania.
- **c. 11,700 years ago** — Rising sea levels isolate Tasmania from mainland Australia, forming Bass Strait.
- **24 November 1642** — Dutch explorer Abel Tasman makes the first reported European sighting.
- **August 1803** — Permanent European settlement begins as a British penal colony.
- **1825** — Van Diemen's Land becomes a separate British colony.
- **1825–1831** — The Black War between Aboriginal people and settlers peaks.
- **1853** — Transportation of convicts to the colony ceases.
- **1 January 1856** — The colony is officially renamed Tasmania.
- **1901** — Tasmania becomes a founding state of the Commonwealth of Australia.
- **1970s** — Opposition to the Lake Pedder dam leads to the formation of the world's first Green party.
- **1996** — The Port Arthur massacre prompts national gun law reform.
- **2011** — The Museum of Old and New Art (MONA) opens, becoming a major tourist attraction.

## Body

### Indigenous History and European Colonization
Aboriginal Tasmanians, who identify as Palawa or Pakana, inhabited the island for at least 42,000 years. They became isolated from the mainland about 11,700 years ago. At the time of British settlement in 1803, the Aboriginal population was estimated between 3,000 and 10,000. Colonization as a penal settlement led to violent conflict known as the Black War (peaking 1825–1831) and the spread of disease, causing a catastrophic population decline. By 1832, most surviving Aboriginal people were relocated to Flinders Island. The near-destruction of the population is described by some historians as genocide, a classification debated by others.

### Colonial Development and Federation
Initially part of New South Wales, Van Diemen's Land became a separate colony in 1825. It received approximately 80,000 convicts before transportation ended in 1853. The colony was renamed Tasmania in 1856 and gained self-government. In 1901, it federated with the other Australian colonies to form the Commonwealth of Australia, with Tasmanians voting in favor by the largest majority of any colony.

### Environment and Politics
Tasmania's environment is a defining feature. About 42% of its land is protected, including the Tasmanian Wilderness World Heritage Area. The state was the birthplace of the world's first Green political party, the United Tasmania Group, formed in the 1970s in response to the flooding of Lake Pedder. Major environmental debates have included the successful campaign to stop the Franklin Dam in the 1980s and ongoing conflicts over logging and mining in regions like the Tarkine.

### Economy and Demographics
Tasmania has the second-smallest economy of the Australian states. Key sectors include tourism, agriculture (notably for wine, cherries, and seafood), aquaculture, and education. The population is the most homogeneous of any Australian state, predominantly of British descent. Hobart is the capital and largest city, housing about 40% of the state's population.

## Terms
- **Palawa kani**: A constructed language representing the languages of Tasmanian Aboriginal peoples.
- **Bass Strait**: The body of water separating Tasmania from the Australian mainland.
- **Black War**: A period of violent conflict between Aboriginal Tasmanians and British colonists, primarily from 1824 to 1831.
- **Transportation**: The British penal practice of sending convicts to overseas colonies, which ended in Tasmania in 1853.
- **Hare-Clark**: A system of multi-seat proportional representation used in Tasmanian elections.
- **Tasmanian Wilderness World Heritage Area (TWWHA)**: A UNESCO World Heritage site noted for meeting seven of the ten criteria for world heritage status.
- **Tarkine**: A large temperate rainforest region in northwest Tasmania, a focus of conservation and resource conflicts.
- **MONA**: The Museum of Old and New Art, a major private museum and tourist attraction in Hobart.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Genocide Thesis**: Historians debate whether the near-destruction of Tasmania's Aboriginal population constitutes genocide. Some argue colonial authorities intended to destroy the population, while others contend the outcome resulted from frontier warfare and disease without specific intent for total destruction.
- **Environmental vs. Economic Development**: Ongoing tension exists between conservation efforts (e.g., protecting old-growth forests and the Tarkine) and proposals for economic development through logging, mining, and hydroelectric power.
- **Political Representation**: There is debate over the size of Tasmania's House of Assembly, with a 2020 committee recommending an increase from 25 to 35 seats to improve democratic representation.
