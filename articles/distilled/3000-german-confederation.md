# German Confederation

## Overview
The German Confederation (1815–1866) was an association of 39 German-speaking states created by the Congress of Vienna to replace the dissolved Holy Roman Empire. It served as a weak political framework for Central Europe, designed by conservative powers like Austria and Prussia to maintain the status quo and prevent the rise of a unified German nation-state. Its inherent structural weaknesses and the rivalry between its two dominant members ultimately led to its collapse and replacement by a Prussian-led German Empire.

- **1866** — Austro-Prussian War leads to the Confederation's dissolution.

- **18 January 1871** — German Empire proclaimed, unifying most former Confederation states.

## Body

### Establishment and Structure
The Confederation was formally established by the 9th Act of the Congress of Vienna in 1815. Its sole governing body was the Federal Convention (Bundesversammlung), a permanent assembly of appointed delegates from member states, presided over by Austria. The Confederation was not a sovereign state but a military alliance for mutual defense. Its key structural feature was the requirement of unanimity for major decisions, such as constitutional changes or admitting new members, giving even the smallest states a veto. This, combined with a limited scope focused on security, made decisive action difficult.

### Military and Political Function
The Confederation's military provisions included federal wars against external enemies, federal executions against states violating federal law, and federal interventions against internal uprisings. Its only federal war was the First Schleswig War (1848) against Denmark. The German Federal Army was a composite force contributed by member states, but mobilization was slow and politically fraught. The Confederation's ineffectiveness was starkly demonstrated in 1866 when it failed to unite its forces against Prussia's secession.

### Economic Integration and Nationalism
Economic integration advanced significantly with the Zollverein (1834), a Prussian-led customs union that created a common market and standardized weights and measures among most states, excluding Austria. This fostered industrial growth and a proto-national economy, strengthening the middle class and inadvertently fueling the nationalist movement for political unity. Meanwhile, liberal and nationalist sentiments, suppressed by the conservative Carlsbad Decrees (1819), erupted in the Revolutions of 1848–49. The Frankfurt Parliament attempted to create a unified German federal state but failed, and the old Confederation was restored in 1850.

### Dissolution and Legacy
Rivalry between Austria and Prussia intensified after 1859. The decisive Austro-Prussian War of 1866 ended with Prussian victory and the Confederation's dissolution by the Peace of Prague. Prussia then established the North German Confederation (1867), a true federal state. Following the Franco-Prussian War, this entity was expanded and proclaimed the German Empire in 1871, unifying most German states under Prussian leadership, but excluding Austria. The Confederation is historically viewed as a weak obstacle to nationhood, yet its structure influenced later German federal institutions like the Bundesrat.

## Terms
- **Bundesversammlung (Federal Convention):** The sole permanent political organ of the Confederation, consisting of delegates from member states.
- **Zollverein:** A customs union established in 1834, led by Prussia, which created a common market and was a key step toward economic integration.
- **Kleindeutsche Lösung:** A "small German solution" for unification, favoring a German state led by Prussia and excluding Austria.
- **Grossdeutsche Lösung:** A "greater German solution" for unification, favoring a German state led by Austria that would include its German-speaking territories.
- **Vormärz:** The period before the March 1848 revolutions, characterized by growing tension between conservative regimes and liberal/nationalist movements.
- **Carlsbad Decrees:** A set of reactionary restrictions issued in 1819 to suppress liberal and nationalist activities, such as student fraternities and the press.
- **Bundeskrieg:** A federal war declared by the Confederation against an external enemy.
- **Bundesexekution:** A federal execution, a military intervention against a member state violating federal law.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Historians debate whether the Confederation was a loose association of states (*Staatenbund*) or had elements of a true federal state (*Bundesstaat*). Its law was superior to state law, and membership was permanent, suggesting more than a loose tie.
- The Confederation's weakness is often seen as intentional, designed by conservative powers like Austria to prevent the emergence of a liberal, nationalist German nation-state that could threaten the European balance of power.
- The role of the Zollverein is debated: while it strengthened Prussia and fostered economic unity, some argue its primary goal was to enhance Prussian power and diminish Austrian influence, rather than to promote German nationalism directly.
