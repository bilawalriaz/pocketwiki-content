# Scipio Africanus

## Overview
Publius Cornelius Scipio Africanus (236/235–c. 183 BC) was a Roman general and statesman, a primary architect of Rome's victory in the Second Punic War. His defeat of Hannibal at the Battle of Zama in 202 BC ended the war and established him as one of history's greatest military commanders. His career and legacy profoundly influenced Roman politics, culture, and the expansion of Roman power beyond Italy.

## Timeline
- **236/235 BC** — Born into the patrician Cornelii Scipiones family.
- **218 BC** — Joins his father's campaign against Hannibal; survives the Battle of Ticinus.
- **216 BC** — Serves as military tribune; survives the Battle of Cannae.
- **210 BC** — Elected to command Roman forces in Hispania, despite never holding consular office.
- **209 BC** — Captures the Carthaginian base of Qart-Hadast (Cartagena).
- **206 BC** — Wins the decisive Battle of Ilipa, securing Roman control of Hispania.
- **205 BC** — Elected consul; secures command for an invasion of Africa.
- **202 BC** — Defeats Hannibal at the Battle of Zama, ending the Second Punic War.
- **199 BC** — Elected censor; named *princeps senatus*.
- **194 BC** — Serves second consulship.
- **190 BC** — Serves as legate to his brother Lucius during the war against Antiochus III.
- **c. 183 BC** — Dies in retirement at Liternum.

## Body

### Second Punic War: Campaign in Hispania
After the deaths of his father and uncle in 211 BC, Scipio was given command in Hispania in 210 BC, an extraordinary appointment as he had never held a magistracy with *imperium*. He captured the strategic Carthaginian base of Qart-Hadast in 209 BC, using a claimed divine vision to motivate his troops. His campaign culminated in the decisive victory at the Battle of Ilipa in 206 BC, which forced the Carthaginians to withdraw from the peninsula. During this time, he also founded the town of Italica.

### Second Punic War: African Campaign and Victory
Elected consul in 205 BC, Scipio launched an invasion of Africa. After initial successes, including a victory at the Battle of the Great Plains, he faced political opposition at home. In 202 BC, he refused peace terms and decisively defeated Hannibal at the Battle of Zama, with crucial support from the Numidian king Massinissa. The subsequent peace treaty severely restricted Carthaginian power, ending the war.

### Later Career and Political Opposition
Despite his triumphs, Scipio faced growing political opposition, led by figures like Cato the Elder. After serving as censor (199 BC) and a second consulship (194 BC), he served as legate to his brother Lucius in the war against Antiochus III (190 BC). The brothers' immense wealth and influence from this war led to legal troubles and accusations of bribery. Scipio, disillusioned, retired to his estate at Liternum, where he died circa 183 BC.

### Personality and Legacy
Scipio was a Hellenophile who introduced Greek culture and the clean-shaven fashion to Rome. He was believed by some to possess divine favor and prophetic dreams. Militarily, he was a brilliant strategist and tactician who inspired great loyalty. Politically, he expanded Roman territory beyond Italy but refused offers of perpetual dictatorship, demonstrating self-restraint. His descendants, including the Gracchi brothers, remained prominent in Roman politics for generations.

## Terms
- **Imperium**: The supreme executive authority held by a Roman magistrate, granting command over armies and provinces.
- **Proconsul**: A governor of a province who held *imperium* equivalent to that of a consul, often a former consul himself.
- **Consul**: One of the two annually elected chief magistrates of the Roman Republic, holding supreme civil and military authority.
- **Censor**: A magistrate responsible for conducting the census, supervising public morality, and managing state finances.
- **Princeps senatus**: The "first man of the Senate," a title of honor given by the censors to the most distinguished senator.
- **Hellenophile**: A person who admires or is strongly influenced by Greek culture.
- **Continence of Scipio**: A famous episode where Scipio, after capturing New Carthage, returned a beautiful captive to her fiancé, showcasing his moderation and virtue.
- **Scipionic legend**: The growing cult of personality around Scipio and his family, which included beliefs in his divine parentage and supernatural abilities.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Date of Death**: Ancient sources conflict, with Polybius and Rutilius reporting 183 BC, Valerius Antias 187 BC, and Livy arguing for c. 185 BC. Most modern sources prefer 183 BC.
- **Burial Place**: His burial location is uncertain, with possibilities including the Tomb of the Scipios in Rome, his villa at Liternum, or the Meta Romuli.
- **Political Eclipse**: Some scholars, like Howard Hayes Scullard, believe Scipio's political influence waned after his censorship in 199 BC, a view that is disputed.
- **Trials of the Scipios**: The exact nature and outcome of the legal charges against Scipio and his brother Lucius regarding alleged bribes from Antiochus III are confused in the ancient sources.
