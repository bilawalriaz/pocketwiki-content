# Charles Hermite

## Overview
Charles Hermite (1822–1901) was a French mathematician whose work in analysis, number theory, and algebra profoundly influenced modern mathematics. His most celebrated achievement was proving the transcendence of the number *e*, a landmark result that opened new avenues in the study of mathematical constants.

## Timeline
- **24 December 1822** — Born in Dieuze, Moselle, France.
- **1842** — Admitted to École Polytechnique; published first paper proving the Abel–Ruffini theorem.
- **1847** — Awarded the baccalauréat after five years of private study.
- **1848** — Elected to the French Academy of Sciences; returned to École Polytechnique as répétiteur.
- **1854** — Introduced the concept of an orthogonal matrix.
- **1855** — Proved eigenvalues of Hermitian matrices are real.
- **1858–1866** — Published papers showing quintic roots can be obtained using elliptic integrals.
- **1864** — Presented Hermite polynomials.
- **1869** — Became professor at École Polytechnique and the University of Paris.
- **1873** — Published proof that *e* is transcendental; also proved π² (and thus π) is irrational.
- **14 January 1901** — Died in Paris, aged 78.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Hermite was born with a deformity in his right foot. After secondary education in Nancy and Paris, he prepared for the École Polytechnique entrance exam with tutor Eugène Charles Catalan. He was admitted in 1842 but left after one year when the school would not accommodate his disability. He spent five years in private study, befriending mathematicians Joseph Bertrand, Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi, and Joseph Liouville, before earning his baccalauréat in 1847.

### Academic Career
In 1848, Hermite returned to the École Polytechnique as a répétiteur and was elected to the French Academy of Sciences. After a smallpox illness in 1856, he resumed his Catholic faith. He lectured at the École Normale Supérieure (1862–1873) and held professorships at the École Polytechnique (until 1876) and the University of Paris (until his death).

### Mathematical Contributions
Hermite's first major work was a proof of the Abel–Ruffini theorem (1842). He later showed that roots of the quintic polynomial could be expressed using elliptic integrals (1858–1866). In algebra, he co-developed the theory of invariants with Arthur Cayley and James Joseph Sylvester.

In linear algebra, Hermite introduced orthogonal matrices (1854) and proved that eigenvalues of Hermitian matrices (matrices equal to their own complex conjugate transposes) are always real (1855). This work generalized Cauchy's earlier result for real symmetric matrices and later became foundational for quantum mechanics.

In analysis, Hermite introduced a new class of special functions, now called Hermite polynomials (1864). His most famous result was the 1873 proof that *e* is transcendental (not a root of any non-zero polynomial with integer coefficients). In the same year, he proved π is irrational, though he did not address its transcendence—a question later solved by Ferdinand von Lindemann in 1882 using similar techniques. Later in life, Hermite worked on the calculus and the theory of linear differential equations.

## Terms
- **Transcendental number**: A number that is not a root of any non-zero polynomial equation with integer coefficients.
- **Elliptic integrals**: Integrals arising from the arc length of an ellipse, used to solve certain algebraic equations.
- **Orthogonal matrix**: A square matrix whose transpose is equal to its inverse.
- **Hermitian matrix**: A complex square matrix that is equal to its own conjugate transpose.
- **Eigenvalues**: Scalars associated with a linear transformation that represent the factor by which an eigenvector is scaled.
- **Hermite polynomials**: A sequence of orthogonal polynomials used in approximations and quantum mechanics.
- **Invariant theory**: The study of algebraic forms that remain unchanged under specific transformations.
