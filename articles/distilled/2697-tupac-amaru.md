# Túpac Amaru

## Overview
Túpac Amaru was the last Sapa Inca (emperor) of the Neo-Inca State, the final independent remnant of the Inca Empire in Vilcabamba. His capture and execution by the Spanish in 1572 marked the definitive end of organized Inca resistance and the completion of the Spanish conquest of Peru. His legacy became a powerful symbol of indigenous resistance, inspiring a major rebellion in 1780.

## Timeline
- **1540** — Manco Inca Yupanqui establishes the Neo-Inca State in Vilcabamba.
- **1544** — Manco Inca is killed in a Spanish attack; his son Sayri Túpac becomes Sapa Inca.
- **1558** — Sayri Túpac accepts Spanish authority and moves to Cusco.
- **1561** — Sayri Túpac dies; his brother Titu Cusi succeeds him in Vilcabamba.
- **1571** — Titu Cusi dies; Túpac Amaru succeeds him as the last Sapa Inca.
- **14 April 1572** — Spanish Viceroy Francisco de Toledo declares war on Vilcabamba.
- **1 June 1572** — First major battle of the war in the Vilcabamba valley.
- **24 June 1572** — Spanish enter a deserted Vilcabamba; the Neo-Inca State ceases to exist.
- **21 September 1572** — Túpac Amaru and captives are marched into Cusco.
- **24 September 1572** — Túpac Amaru is executed by beheading in Cusco's main square.
- **1780** — José Gabriel Condorcanqui (Túpac Amaru II) leads a major indigenous rebellion.

## Body

### The Neo-Inca State and Accession
Following the Spanish conquest of Peru in the 1530s, members of the Inca royal family established the small, independent Neo-Inca State in the remote Vilcabamba region. Founded by Manco Inca Yupanqui, the state saw a succession of rulers: Manco was killed in 1544, followed by his son Sayri Túpac, who later accepted Spanish authority and died in 1561. Sayri Túpac's brother Titu Cusi then ruled until his death in 1571, at which point Túpac Amaru, another brother, became the last Sapa Inca.

### The Final War and Capture
The Spanish used the killing of two ambassadors sent to negotiate with Titu Cusi as justification for war. Viceroy Toledo declared war on 14 April 1572. After initial battles, Spanish forces entered Vilcabamba on 24 June to find it destroyed and abandoned. Túpac Amaru fled with a small party but was pursued. After a months-long chase through the forests, a Spanish unit under Martín García Óñez de Loyola captured him and his family on the night of 23 September 1572. They were brought to Cusco, along with sacred Inca relics which were subsequently destroyed.

### Execution and Controversy
Túpac Amaru was tried for the murder of the Spanish ambassadors and sentenced to be beheaded. The execution took place on 24 September 1572 in Cusco's central square before a massive crowd. Many contemporary and later observers, including some Catholic clerics, argued that Viceroy Toledo exceeded his authority by executing a recognized foreign king. King Philip II of Spain reportedly disapproved of the act. Túpac Amaru's last words, as reported, were a plea to the creator god Pacha Kamaq to witness his enemies shedding his blood.

### Legacy and Descendants
After the conquest, Spanish authorities exiled dozens of Túpac Amaru's royal descendants. His memory endured and became a potent symbol of indigenous resistance. In 1780, José Gabriel Condorcanqui, claiming direct descent, led a major uprising against Spanish rule as Túpac Amaru II, protesting increased taxation under the Bourbon Reforms. The original Túpac Amaru's legacy remains significant in Peruvian history and culture.

## Terms
- **Sapa Inca**: The emperor of the Inca Empire, meaning "only Inca."
- **Neo-Inca State**: The final independent Inca polity, based in Vilcabamba after the Spanish conquest.
- **Vilcabamba**: A remote region in the Upper Amazon that served as the last Inca stronghold.
- **Viceroy**: A royal governor ruling a colony in the name of the monarch; Francisco de Toledo was the Viceroy of Peru.
- **Pachacamac (Pacha Kamaq)**: A major creator deity in Andean religion.
- **Bourbon Reforms**: A series of administrative changes in the Spanish Empire in the 18th century, including increased taxation.
- **Corregidor**: A Spanish colonial official, often a local administrator and tax collector.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary debate concerns the legality of Túpac Amaru's execution. One view holds that Viceroy Toledo, by executing a head of state recognized by the Spanish as an independent king, exceeded his authority and committed a crime under Spanish law. The opposing view argues that Túpac Amaru was in rebellion (as his predecessors had allegedly accepted Spanish authority), that Toledo pursued peaceful means first, and that the murder of his ambassadors justified the military response and subsequent trial. King Philip II's disapproval of the execution adds weight to the controversy.
