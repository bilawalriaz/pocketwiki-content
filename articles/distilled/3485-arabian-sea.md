# Arabian Sea

## Overview
The Arabian Sea, a northern Indian Ocean region, has been a critical nexus for global trade, cultural exchange, and ecological productivity for millennia. Its strategic location, bounded by major landmasses and connected to the Red Sea and Persian Gulf via key straits, made it the central hub of the Maritime Silk Road, facilitating the diffusion of goods, ideas, religions, and peoples. Today, it remains vital for global shipping, particularly for oil and gas, but its unique marine ecosystem faces severe threats from overfishing, pollution, and climate change.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Sea-borne trade begins between Mesopotamia, the Indus Valley, and East Africa.
- **1st millennium BC** — The *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* documents established trade routes.
- **2nd century BC – onward** — The Arabian Sea becomes the central nexus of the Maritime Silk Road.
- **600–1498 CE** — Trade explodes with the rise of Islam; Arabic becomes the lingua franca of Indian Ocean commerce.
- **1405–1433** — Chinese Admiral Zheng He leads seven voyages across the Indian Ocean.
- **15th–16th centuries** — European powers, beginning with the Portuguese, arrive and disrupt existing trade networks.
- **1959** — The International Indian Ocean Expedition conducts early hydrographic surveys.
- **1985–1987** — Rapid sea surface temperature warming begins, establishing a long-term trend.
- **2000s–present** — The Arabian Sea's oxygen minimum zone expands; extreme monsoon events increase.

## Body

### Geography and Hydrography
The Arabian Sea covers approximately 3,862,000 km², reaching a maximum depth of 5,395 meters. Its southern and central regions are dominated by the relatively flat Arabian Basin, exceeding 4,500 meters in depth. The sea connects to the Red Sea via the Gulf of Aden and the Strait of Bab-el-Mandeb, and to the Persian Gulf via the Gulf of Oman and the Strait of Hormuz. The continental shelf is narrow along the Makran and East African coasts but extends over 300 km from much of India. The Indus Fan, a massive underwater sediment deposit, formed after the Last Glacial Maximum. Notable features include the Alula-Fartak Trough and the Owen Fracture Zone, marking the boundary between the Indian and Arabian tectonic plates.

### Climate and Monsoon System
The Arabian Sea has a monsoonal climate. Historically, strong southwesterly winds in summer (the Findlater Jet) carried warm, moist air northeast, causing heavy rains on India's coast and dry conditions in Arabia and East Africa. In winter, cold, dry winds blew from the Himalayas. Sea surface temperatures have risen by about 0.84°C over the past century, with rapid warming from 1985–1987. This warming, combined with an intensifying monsoon, has lengthened the period of high sea temperatures and contributed to more erratic and violent weather. The sea hosts one of the world's largest oxygen minimum zones (OMZs), or "dead zones," driven by pollution, eutrophication, and warming, which reduces oxygen levels to near-undetectable amounts in some areas.

### Ecology and Marine Life
The Arabian Sea Large Marine Ecosystem (LME) is one of the planet's most biologically productive regions, supporting at least 330 coral species, 550 mollusk species, and over 1,200 fish species. It is home to protected marine mammals like blue whales and humpback whales. However, the ecosystem is under severe strain. Over 50% of fish stocks are collapsed or overexploited, with over 80% of catch coming from fully exploited stocks. Destructive fishing practices, bycatch, and pollution threaten species like the critically endangered knifetooth sawfish and the vulnerable dugong. Coastal mangrove forests are limited, and coral bleaching is extensive due to high sea temperatures.

### Historical Trade and Cultural Diffusion
The Arabian Sea has been a trade route since the 3rd millennium BC, linking Mesopotamia, the Indus Valley, and East Africa. Early trade was governed by monsoon winds, creating segmented routes where sailors were "trapped" at destinations until winds reversed. The diffusion of the lateen sail in the 1st millennium CE improved navigation. Trade exploded with the rise of Islam, which unified the region culturally and politically, making Arabic the language of commerce. This maritime network facilitated the spread of Islam, technologies, and crops like millet and sorghum. It also created diasporic merchant communities and, at times, enabled the spread of pathogens. The Maritime Silk Road gradually overtook the overland Silk Road in importance before European arrival in the 15th–16th centuries.

### Modern Economic and Geopolitical Activity
The Strait of Hormuz, leading into the Arabian Sea, handles about 25% of global seaborne oil trade and 19% of LNG trade, mostly bound for Asia. Major ports include Jawaharlal Nehru (India), Karachi and Gwadar (Pakistan), and Salalah (Oman). Commercial fishing yields quadrupled from 1950 to 1990 but have since stagnated, with recent increases due to intensive Chinese industrial fishing. The region faces geopolitical tensions, including piracy off Somalia and recent conflicts affecting port traffic. Coastal pollution from industrial waste and untreated wastewater is a critical threat to ecosystems.

## Terms
*   **Arabian Basin:** The expansive, relatively flat seabed dominating the south and central Arabian Sea, with depths exceeding 4,500 meters.
*   **Findlater Jet:** The strong, uniform southwesterly winds that blow over the Arabian Sea during the summer monsoon.
*   **Large Marine Ecosystem (LME):** A distinct marine region, like the Arabian Sea LME, characterized by its bathymetry, hydrography, productivity, and trophic interactions.
*   **Maritime Silk Road:** The historical network of sea trade routes connecting the Mediterranean, Asia, and East Africa, with the Arabian Sea as its central hub.
*   **Oxygen Minimum Zone (OMZ):** A "dead zone" in the ocean with very low oxygen levels, often caused by eutrophication and warming. The Arabian Sea's OMZ has the world's lowest oxygen levels.
*   **Eutrophication:** The process by which a body of water becomes overly enriched with minerals and nutrients, inducing excessive growth of algae and depleting oxygen.
*   **Monsoon:** A seasonal prevailing wind in the region of South and Southeast Asia, characterized by a reversal of wind direction between summer and winter.
*   **Lateen Sail:** A triangular sail set on a long yard mounted at an angle on the mast, which improved sailing efficiency and maneuverability in the Indian Ocean.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Eastern Boundary Dispute:** The exact eastern border of the Arabian Sea with India is disputed between the NOAA (which uses Devgad Island) and the IHO (which uses Sadashivgad Village).
*   **Role of Roman Traders:** Recent scholarship expresses skepticism about the presence and significance of Mediterranean traders in the Arabian Sea during the 1st millennium CE, as described in the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*, suggesting the work may reflect limited Egyptian-Mediterranean knowledge.
*   **Future of Ocean De-oxygenation:** While some research projects a long-term reversal of global ocean de-oxygenation, the Arabian Sea's OMZ is currently expanding due to human activity, and its recovery timeline remains uncertain.
