# John James Audubon

## Overview
John James Audubon (1785–1851) was a French-American naturalist and artist whose monumental work, *The Birds of America*, created a detailed pictorial record of North American birds and set new standards for ornithological illustration. His legacy is complex, marked by significant artistic and scientific contributions alongside controversies involving scientific fraud, plagiarism, and his involvement in slavery and the trafficking of human remains.

## Timeline
- **1785** — Born Jean-Jacques Rabin in Saint-Domingue (now Haiti).
- **1791** — Sent to France; adopted and renamed Jean-Jacques Fougère Audubon.
- **1803** — Immigrated to the United States, anglicizing his name to John James Audubon.
- **1808** — Married Lucy Bakewell; moved to Kentucky.
- **1811** — Dissolved business partnership with Ferdinand Rozier; witnessed the New Madrid earthquake.
- **1819** — Went bankrupt and was jailed for debt.
- **1820** — Began major ornithological expeditions in the American South.
- **1826** — Traveled to England with his bird drawings to seek a publisher.
- **1827–1838** — Published *The Birds of America* (435 hand-colored prints).
- **1831–1839** — Published the accompanying text, *Ornithological Biography*.
- **1841** — Returned to the United States; purchased an estate in northern Manhattan.
- **1851** — Died in New York City; buried in Manhattan.

## Body

### Early Life and Immigration
Born on a sugarcane plantation in Saint-Domingue, Audubon was the son of a French naval officer and his mistress. After his mother's death, he was sent to France in 1791, where he was adopted by his father and his wife. He developed a passion for birds and nature from an early age. In 1803, to avoid Napoleonic conscription, he emigrated to the United States, settling at his father's farm, Mill Grove, near Philadelphia. There, he began studying American birds and met his future wife, Lucy Bakewell.

### Business Ventures and Early Career
Audubon and a partner, Jean Ferdinand Rozier, pursued various business ventures, including lead mining and general stores in Kentucky and Missouri. His trading business suffered due to the 1807 Embargo Act. He experienced the 1811 New Madrid earthquake while in Missouri. After a period of financial success, he went bankrupt in 1819 and was jailed for debt. During this time, he owned and later sold slaves. He began working as a portrait artist and drawing instructor to support his family.

### Ornithological Work and *The Birds of America*
Determined to create a comprehensive pictorial record of North American birds, Audubon embarked on extensive field expeditions from 1820 onward. He developed a unique method of wiring freshly killed specimens into lifelike poses for drawing. In 1826, he sailed to England with his portfolio, where he gained patronage and subscribers, including King George IV. His monumental work, *The Birds of America*, was published in Edinburgh and London between 1827 and 1838. It consisted of 435 hand-colored, life-size aquatint prints on a "double-elephant folio" scale, illustrating 497 bird species. The accompanying text, *Ornithological Biography*, was co-authored with William MacGillivray.

### Scientific Controversies and Legacy
Audubon's scientific contributions were substantial but marred by accusations of fraud, plagiarism, and misconduct. He was accused of inventing species (e.g., the "Bird of Washington"), falsifying data, and stealing specimens, such as the holotype for the northern Harris's hawk. His famous 1804 account of banding eastern phoebes is now considered likely apocryphal. He also trafficked Native American remains, selling skulls to Samuel George Morton. Despite these controversies, his detailed illustrations and field notes significantly advanced ornithology. He was elected to prestigious scientific societies, and his work influenced later naturalists, including Charles Darwin. The National Audubon Society, founded in 1905, honors his name, though some local chapters have changed theirs due to his legacy of enslavement.

## Terms
- **Double-elephant folio:** The very large paper size (approx. 39 x 26 inches) used for the original edition of *The Birds of America*.
- **Aquatint:** An intaglio printmaking technique that produces tonal effects, used to engrave Audubon's plates.
- **Holotype:** A single physical specimen designated as the name-bearing type of a species or subspecies.
- **Neotype:** A specimen selected to serve as a type when the original type material is lost or destroyed.
- **Natal philopatry:** The tendency of an animal to return to its birthplace to breed.
- **Ornithology:** The branch of zoology that deals with the study of birds.
- **Taxidermy:** The art of preparing, stuffing, and mounting the skins of animals for display or study.
- **Linnaean order:** The system of biological classification developed by Carl Linnaeus, based on hierarchical ranks.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Accuracy of claims:** Scholars debate the veracity of Audubon's autobiographical accounts, such as his phoebe-banding story and details of his birth.
- **Extent of scientific fraud:** The full scope of his plagiarism, data fabrication, and species invention (e.g., the "Bird of Washington") remains a subject of research and critique.
- **Ethical legacy:** There is ongoing discussion about how to reconcile his contributions to natural history with his involvement in slavery and the trafficking of human remains, as reflected in debates over renaming institutions that honor him.
