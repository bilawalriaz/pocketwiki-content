# Epicurus

## Overview

Epicurus (341–270 BC) was an ancient Greek philosopher who founded Epicureanism, a school teaching that the highest good is a life of pleasure understood as the absence of pain and mental disturbance. His system combined atomic physics, empiricist epistemology, and a hedonistic ethics centered on desire management, friendship, and freedom from fear—particularly fear of the gods and death. Epicureanism profoundly influenced later thought, from Lucretius and Roman Enlightenment thinkers to modern utilitarians and materialists.

## Body

### Life and Teaching

Epicurus was born in Samos to Athenian parents and educated in Platonism under Pamphilus. After military service, he studied under Nausiphanes, a Democritean. Influenced by Democritus and possibly Cynicism, he rejected claims of philosophical lineage, asserting he was self-taught. Around age 30, he began teaching in Mytilene but was expelled, then founded a school in Lampsacus before settling in Athens c. 306 BC, where he established The Garden—a communal school welcoming men and women alike.

The Garden practiced a collective lifestyle with shared property and no rigid hierarchy. Epicurus emphasized friendship and simple living as paths to tranquility. He died at 72 from a urinary stone, maintaining cheerfulness and teaching until the end, as recounted in his *Epistle to Idomeneus*.

### Physics and Metaphysics

Epicurus taught an eternal universe composed of atoms and void. Atoms are indivisible, ungenerated, and imperishable, differing only in shape, size, and weight. Infinite worlds exist, separated by void. Atoms move downward through the void but occasionally "swerve" randomly, enabling free will and preventing strict determinism. Sensory perception arises from atoms emitted by objects, which lack intrinsic qualities like color but produce sensations in observers.

### Epistemology

Sense perception is the foundation of knowledge. Epicurus proposed three criteria of truth: sensations (direct perceptions), preconceptions (mental concepts derived from prior sense data), and feelings (pleasure/pain guiding action). Clear vision (*enargeia*) occurs when further examination confirms a perception. Reason organizes sensory input but depends on the senses; abstract Forms are rejected.

The Principle of Multiple Explanations holds that unobservable phenomena should be explained through multiple plausible hypotheses tested against observable facts. The *Epilogism* method uses reflective appraisal when observation fails, such as in conceiving time or moral relations.

### Ethics

Epicurus redefined pleasure as the absence of suffering (*aponia*) and mental tranquility (*ataraxia*). He categorized desires into three types: natural and necessary (food, shelter, friendship); natural but unnecessary (luxury, sex); and unnatural and unnecessary (wealth, fame). Only natural and necessary desires must be fulfilled. Mental pleasures surpass bodily ones because they can access past, present, and future. Virtue is instrumental to pleasure, rooted in practical wisdom.

The Tetrapharmakos—four cures—summarizes his ethical stance: don’t fear gods, don’t fear death, good is easy to get, evil is easy to endure.

### Politics and Justice

Epicurus advocated withdrawal from politics, favoring a community of friends for security and mutual affection. Justice is a social contract based on mutual non-harm, not divine command. Laws promoting happiness are just; others are not. All humans are naturally equal, composed of the same atomic matter, with no metaphysical hierarchy.

### Theology

Epicurus affirmed the gods’ existence but denied their involvement in the world. They dwell in the metakosmia (interworld spaces), living in perfect bliss, untouched by human affairs. The Epicurean paradox questions divine omnipotence and benevolence: if God is willing but unable to prevent evil, He is not omnipotent; if able but unwilling, He is malevolent.

### Death

Death is the dissolution of the body and soul, both material. Since sensation ceases, death is nothing to us. The famous epitaph—"I was not; I have been; I am not; I do not mind"—reflects this view.

### Works and Legacy

Epicurus wrote over 300 treatises, mostly lost. Surviving works include the *Principal Doctrines*, letters (*To Menoeceus*, *To Herodotus*, *To Pythocles*, *To Mother*), and fragments of *On Nature* (37 books) and *Canon*. The *Vatican Sayings* (81 aphorisms) also survive.

After his death, the school continued under Hermarchus, Polystratus, and others. It flourished in Rome through Lucretius, Philodemus, and Siro but declined by late antiquity due to Christian opposition. Rediscovered in the Renaissance via Lucretius, it gained traction in the 17th century through Gassendi, Boyle, and Locke. Modern influence includes utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill) and Marx’s interpretation of Epicurean materialism.

## Terms

- **Ataraxia**: Freedom from mental disturbance; tranquility.
- **Aponia**: Absence of bodily pain.
- **Atomism**: Theory that reality consists of indivisible particles (atoms) moving in void.
- **Katastematic pleasure**: Static pleasure of being free from pain or disturbance.
- **Kinetic pleasure**: Active pleasure involving sensation or mental activity.
- **Metakosmia**: The inter-world space where Epicurean gods reside.
- **Preconceptions (prolepsis)**: Innate mental concepts formed from prior sensory experience.
- **Swerve (clinamen)**: Random deviation of atoms enabling free will.
- **Tetrapharmakos**: The "four-part cure" summarizing Epicurean ethical guidance.

## Debates and Open Questions

- **Divine nature**: Whether Epicurean gods exist as material beings (realist view) or as ideal constructs (idealist view) remains debated, though realism is currently dominant.
- **Authenticity of letters**: The *Epistle to Idomeneus* and *Letter to Mother* are disputed, with some scholars attributing them to later followers or Diogenes of Oenoanda.
- **Marriage and family**: Whether the Epicurean sage should marry and have children is ambiguous, with textual variants suggesting situational ethics.
- **Political engagement**: While Epicurus generally advised withdrawal, some later interpreters allowed conditional political participation for maximizing pleasure.
- **Free will and determinism**: The swerve solves logical determinism but raises questions about randomness versus genuine agency.
