# Rio Negro (Amazon)

## Overview
The Rio Negro is the largest left tributary of the Amazon River, contributing about 14% of its water, and is the world's largest blackwater river. Its dark, tea-colored waters are rich in humic acid from decomposing vegetation, supporting a highly diverse ecosystem despite low nutrient levels. The river is a major geographical and ecological feature of the Amazon basin, originating in Colombia and flowing through Venezuela and Brazil.

- **Mid-17th century** — Jesuits settle along its banks among indigenous tribes.
- **After 1700** — Slaving and Eurasian diseases drastically reduce native populations.
- **1800** — Alexander von Humboldt and Aimé Bonpland explore the river.
- **1852–1854** — Richard Spruce and Alfred Russel Wallace make botanical collections.

- **2003** — The Rio Negro is the filming location for *Survivor: The Amazon*.

## Body

### Geography and Course
The Rio Negro originates in the Guainía Department of Colombia, where it is called the Guainía River. It flows southeast, forming the Colombia-Venezuela border and receiving the Casiquiare canal, a unique natural link to the Orinoco basin. After entering Brazil, its major tributaries include the Uaupés and Branco rivers. The river widens significantly in its lower course, creating vast archipelagos and lagoons during the wet season (April–October). It finally meets the Solimões River near Manaus to form the Amazon, creating the famous "Meeting of Waters" phenomenon.

### Ecology and Productivity
The river's dark color comes from humic acid, a product of incomplete vegetation breakdown in sandy soils. Historically considered a "hunger river," modern research shows the Rio Negro supports a large fishing industry and numerous turtle beaches. It has exceptional biodiversity, with an estimated 800–900 fish species, including nearly 100 endemics and many important to the aquarium trade, such as the cardinal tetra. The Casiquiare canal allows some species exchange with the Orinoco, but only adaptable species can traverse its varying water types.

### History and Exploration
The river was named by Francisco de Orellana in 1541. Jesuit missions were established in the 17th century, but subsequent slaving and disease decimated indigenous populations. Scientific exploration increased in the 19th and 20th centuries, with key expeditions by Humboldt, Wallace, and Rice, the latter pioneering aerial mapping. The river's low sediment load (5.76 million tonnes per year at Manaus) and unique ecology have made it a subject of ongoing study.

## Terms
- **Blackwater river**: A river with dark, tea-colored water stained by tannins and humic acid from decomposing vegetation in sandy, nutrient-poor soils.
- **Tributary**: A river or stream that flows into a larger river.
- **Humic acid**: Organic compounds formed from the incomplete breakdown of plant material, giving blackwater rivers their characteristic color.
- **Tepui**: A table-top mountain or mesa, characteristic of the Guiana Highlands where the Rio Negro originates.
- **Archipelago**: A group of islands; in the Rio Negro, these are large river islands formed by sediment deposition.
- **Meeting of Waters**: The confluence of the dark Rio Negro and the sandy-colored Solimões River, where the waters flow side-by-side without mixing for several kilometers.
- **Endemic species**: A species native and restricted to a certain geographic area, such as the Rio Negro basin.

## Debates and open questions
The older view of blackwater rivers as "hunger rivers" with low productivity is debated and largely overturned by new research showing significant fish and turtle populations. The extent to which the Casiquiare canal facilitates species exchange between the Amazon and Orinoco basins, and the adaptability required for such migration, remains a subject of ecological study.
