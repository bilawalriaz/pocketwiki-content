# Ellesmere Island

## Overview
Ellesmere Island is Canada's northernmost and third-largest island, located in the Arctic Archipelago. It is a remote, mountainous, and ice-covered landmass with a sparse human population, significant geological and paleontological records, and a history of Inuit habitation and European exploration. Its extreme environment makes it a key site for studying climate change and Arctic ecology.

## Timeline
- **c. 2700 BCE** — Independence I culture (Paleo-Eskimos) active in the region.
- **c. 1500–1000 BCE** — Evidence of a trade network at Lake Hazen.
- **c. 1000 CE** — Thule culture moves into the High Arctic during a warming trend.
- **After 1300 CE** — Thule culture completely replaces the Dorset culture.
- **1400–1600 CE** — Little Ice Age forces Thule withdrawal from the island.
- **1616** — William Baffin's expedition first records sighting the island.
- **1818** — John Ross leads the first recorded European expedition to the region.
- **1852** — Edward Augustus Inglefield names the island for Francis Egerton, 1st Earl of Ellesmere.
- **1880** — British Arctic Territories transferred to Canada.
- **1881** — Adolphus Greely's expedition establishes Fort Conger and finds fossil forests.
- **1906** — Robert Peary's expedition documents the continuous Ellesmere Ice Shelf.
- **1922 & 1926** — RCMP detachments established at Craig Harbour and Bache Post to assert sovereignty.
- **2005** — Ayles Ice Shelf calves entirely.
- **2008** — Markham Ice Shelf breaks off completely.

## Body

### Geology and Geography
Ellesmere Island has three major geological regions: the Grant Land Highlands (fold mountains), the Greely-Hazen Plateau (tableland), and a coastal sedimentary plateau. Uplift and faulting before the Pleistocene established its features, later shaped by glacial erosion. The island is the most mountainous in the Arctic Archipelago, with over 40% of its surface covered by ice caps and glaciers. Its northernmost point, Cape Columbia, is one of the closest land points to the North Pole. The climate is tundra or ice cap, with extreme cold, low precipitation, and a polar night lasting several months. Climate change is causing significant ecological shifts, such as the drying of ponds and wetlands.

### Glaciers and Ice Shelves
Large icefields cover much of the island. The northwest coast was once covered by the massive Ellesmere Ice Shelf, which shrank by 90% in the 20th century. The remaining shelves, including the Ward Hunt Ice Shelf, have continued to break apart in the 21st century due to warming. A 2018 study found a 5.9% reduction in glacier area and a 42% loss in ice shelf surface area between 1999 and 2015.

### Paleontology and Ecology
The island hosts important fossil sites, including a Paleocene-Eocene fossil forest at Stenkul Fiord and the discovery of *Tiktaalik roseae*, a transitional fish-tetrapod fossil from the Devonian period. High Arctic ecosystems are young, with low species diversity. The island is the northernmost location for eusocial insects like the bumblebee *Bombus polaris*. Remarkably productive zones exist in the Lake Hazen area and coastal polynyas.

### Human History
Archaeological records show a succession of pre-contact cultures, including the Arctic Small Tool tradition (Paleo-Eskimos) and the Thule culture, which replaced the Dorset people after 1300 CE. The Thule withdrew during the Little Ice Age (c. 1400–1600 CE). European exploration, focused on the Northwest Passage, began in earnest in the 19th century. Following the 1880 transfer of the Arctic to Canada, sovereignty was asserted through RCMP patrols in the early 20th century. Inuit populations returned in the 19th century, and the modern community of Grise Fiord was established in the 1950s via the High Arctic relocation program.

### Population and Settlements
As of 2021, the population was 144, all in Grise Fiord. Alert is the world's northernmost continuously inhabited settlement (pop. 62 in 2016), and Eureka is a weather and research station. Transportation is primarily by sea during a short summer navigation season or over sea ice.

## Terms
- **Arctic Cordillera**: A mountain system covering much of the island, making it the most mountainous in the Arctic Archipelago.
- **Franklinian mobile belt**: A zone of volcanic and intrusive rock from the Cretaceous period that forms part of the island's geology.
- **Polynya**: An area of persistent open water in sea ice, important for marine life and hunting.
- **Ice foot**: A belt of level, secure ice around the shoreline between high and low water marks, used for travel.
- **Qulliq**: A traditional soapstone lamp used by Arctic cultures.
- **Thule culture**: The ancestors of modern Inuit, who replaced the Dorset culture and had technological advantages like kayaks and sled dogs.
- **High Arctic relocation**: A 1950s Canadian government program that forcibly moved Inuit families to Grise Fiord and Resolute Bay.
- **Tiktaalik roseae**: A Paleozoic fossil fish discovered on Ellesmere Island, showing transitional features between fish and tetrapods.
- **Geomagnetic pole**: The point where the Earth's magnetic axis intersects the surface; as of 2025, it is located on Ellesmere Island.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact nature of contact between Norse Greenlanders and Thule peoples on Ellesmere Island is uncertain; Norse artifacts found could be from direct visits, trade networks, or shipwrecks.
- The research on the island's archaeological record is considered incomplete, with only a small proportion of excavation details published.
- The causes and timeline of the breakup of the Ellesmere Ice Shelves are linked to climate change, but the precise mechanisms and future projections are subjects of ongoing study.
