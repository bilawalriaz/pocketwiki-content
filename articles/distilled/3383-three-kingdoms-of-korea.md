# Three Kingdoms of Korea

## Overview
The Three Kingdoms of Korea (c. 1st–7th centuries CE) was a period when the Korean Peninsula was divided among three major states: Goguryeo, Paekche, and Silla. This era saw the consolidation of smaller polities, intense competition for hegemony, and the spread of Buddhism, laying the cultural and political foundations for a unified Korean identity.

## Timeline
- **75 BCE** — First Chinese record of Goguryeo.
- **57 BCE** — Traditional founding date of Silla (as Saro).
- **313 CE** — Goguryeo destroys the Chinese Lelang commandery.
- **372 CE** — Buddhism becomes the state religion of Goguryeo.
- **384 CE** — Buddhism introduced to Paekche from Goguryeo.
- **494 CE** — Buyeo is annexed by Goguryeo.
- **503 CE** — Saro is renamed Silla.
- **528 CE** — Buddhism becomes the state religion of Silla.
- **562 CE** — Silla annexes the Kaya confederacy.
- **660 CE** — Paekche is conquered by the Silla–Tang alliance.
- **668 CE** — Goguryeo is conquered by the Silla–Tang alliance.

## Body

### Foundation and Consolidation
The Three Kingdoms emerged after the fall of Gojoseon and the subsequent collapse of Chinese commanderies in the northwest. From the 1st to 3rd centuries CE, the proto-states of Goguryeo, Paekche, and Silla transitioned from walled-town chiefdoms to centralized kingdoms. They gradually absorbed neighboring polities: Goguryeo conquered Buyeo, Okjeo, and Dongye; Silla annexed the Kaya confederacy. By the 6th century, only three major powers remained on the peninsula.

### The Three Kingdoms
**Goguryeo**, the largest and most militaristic, controlled the northern peninsula and much of Manchuria. It reached its zenith in the 5th century under Kings Gwanggaeto and Jangsu. **Paekche**, a maritime power in the west, was instrumental in spreading continental culture, including Buddhism, to Japan. **Silla**, the smallest and southeasternmost kingdom, used diplomacy and alliances, eventually with Tang China, to overcome its rivals. All three shared a similar language and culture, though their material arts showed distinct influences: northern Chinese for Goguryeo, southern Chinese for Paekche, and Eurasian steppe for Silla.

### Religion and Culture
Buddhism arrived in the 3rd century and was adopted as the state religion by all three kingdoms between the late 4th and 6th centuries. Prior to this, shamanism was prevalent, with increasing influence from Chinese Confucianism and Taoism. The kingdoms possessed a warrior aristocracy, contrasting with China's literary elite.

### Decline and Unification
In the 7th century, Silla allied with the Tang dynasty of China. This alliance conquered Paekche in 660 and Goguryeo in 668. Silla then expelled Tang forces from the peninsula, achieving the first unification of southern Korea and initiating the North–South States period.

### Archaeological Evidence
Archaeology confirms the development of state-level societies between 300 BCE and 400 CE, evidenced by urban centers, monumental architecture, craft specialization, and hierarchical burial practices. Elite cemeteries with large mounded tombs and centralized production sites for pottery and roof tiles demonstrate increasing social stratification and political control.

## Terms
- **Samhan**: Literally "Three Han," a term that became synonymous with the Three Kingdoms of Korea and later a common name for all of Korea.
- **Confederacy**: A union of sovereign states or polities, like the earlier Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan, which preceded the kingdoms.
- **Walled-town state**: An early form of political organization centered on a fortified settlement, characteristic of the Proto-Three Kingdoms period.
- **State-level society**: An archaeological concept defined by correlates like urban centers, monumental architecture, bureaucracy, and control over a large territory.
- **Mahayana Buddhism**: The form of Buddhism that spread from India to China and then to Korea, becoming the state religion.
- **Tang dynasty**: The Chinese dynasty (618–907 CE) that allied with Silla to conquer its rivals.
- **Unified Silla**: The kingdom that ruled most of the Korean Peninsula after 668 CE, following the conquest of the other two kingdoms.
- **Parhae (Balhae)**: A successor state to Goguryeo in Manchuria, founded after the Tang conquest.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Chronology of State Formation**: While historical texts provide founding dates, archaeological evidence suggests state-level correlates developed gradually between 100 and 400 CE, not as a single package.
- **Origins of the Kingdoms**: The precise founding myths and ethnic origins of the kingdoms, particularly the relationship between Goguryeo/Paekche and the northern Buyeo confederacy, are subjects of scholarly discussion.
- **Role of Warfare**: Archaeologists debate the relative importance of internal developments versus frequent warfare in driving the emergence and consolidation of the peninsular states.
