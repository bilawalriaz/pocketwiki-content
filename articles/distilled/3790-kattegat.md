# Kattegat

## Overview
The Kattegat is a shallow, semi-enclosed sea of approximately 24,000 km² that connects the Baltic Sea to the North Sea via the Skagerrak. It is a vital international shipping lane but is hazardous due to reefs and shifting currents. Its unique two-layer salinity structure and ecological history, including early recognition as a marine dead zone, make it a significant area for oceanographic study and environmental regulation.

## Timeline
- **Late Medieval** — Hanseatic captains name the sea "Kattegat" (cat's gate) for its narrow, reef-filled passage.
- **1429** — Denmark begins charging Sound Dues for passage through the Øresund, profiting from Kattegat traffic.
- **1784** — Completion of the Eider Canal provides an alternative land route, reducing the Kattegat's exclusivity as a Baltic access point.
- **1857** — Sound Dues are abolished.
- **1932** — Denmark, Norway, and Sweden sign a convention defining the Kattegat's boundaries for fishing regulation.
- **1953** — The International Hydrographic Organization publishes its definition of the Kattegat's limits.
- **1970s** — The Kattegat is identified as one of the first marine dead zones due to anoxia.
- **1985** — Denmark's first Action Plan for the Aquatic Environment is launched to address eutrophication.
- **2006** — The Kattegat is designated a Sulphur Emission Control Area.
- **2016** — The sulphur fuel benchmark for ships in the area is lowered to 0.1%.

## Body

### Etymology and Boundaries
The name "Kattegat" derives from Dutch words meaning "cat's gate," a late medieval reference to the tight, reef-filled passage. Its precise boundaries are defined by different organizations. A 1932 convention set lines from Skagen to Vinga Lighthouse and from there to Hisingen. Later definitions by the International Hydrographic Organization (IHO) and HELCOM vary slightly, particularly in the north, which can affect whether cities like Gothenburg are considered on the Kattegat or the Skagerrak.

### Geography and Bathymetry
The sea is relatively shallow, with an average depth of 22 meters, though deeper trenches exist. The main islands are Samsø, Læsø, and Anholt. A proposed Kattegat Bridge to connect Jutland and Zealand has been discussed since the 1950s. The bathymetry features the Djupa Rännan trench along the Swedish coast and the Kattegat Trench, which contains the Poseidon Deep, the sea's deepest point at over 150 meters.

### Hydrology and Biology
The Kattegat's defining feature is its two-layer salinity structure. A low-salinity surface layer (1.5–3%) flows north from the Baltic Sea, while a higher-salinity deep layer (3–3.5%) flows in from the Skagerrak. This stratification is maintained by a halocline at about 15 meters depth. The sea has low tides, with wind being the primary driver of water mixing. Unique shallow cold seeps, or "bubbling reefs," support rich biodiversity and are protected under the EU's Natura 2000 program.

### Ecological History and Protections
The Kattegat was one of the first marine areas identified as a dead zone in the 1970s due to anoxia from eutrophication. Since 1985, a series of Danish and EU Action Plans have aimed to repair the damage, with progress noted but goals not yet fully met. Due to heavy shipping, it is a Sulphur Emission Control Area. Numerous coastal zones, including the Bay of Aalborg and the Kullaberg Nature Reserve, are protected under Natura 2000 and the Ramsar Convention for their importance to fish, birds, and seals.

## Terms
- **Kattegat**: A shallow sea connecting the Baltic Sea and the Skagerrak, bounded by Denmark and Sweden.
- **Danish Straits**: The three straits (Øresund, Great Belt, Little Belt) through which the Baltic Sea drains into the Kattegat.
- **Halocline**: A vertical gradient in salinity; in the Kattegat, it separates the low-salinity surface layer from the high-salinity deep layer.
- **Eutrophication**: Excessive nutrient enrichment in water bodies, often leading to algal blooms and oxygen depletion (anoxia).
- **Anoxia**: A condition of severely depleted oxygen in water, creating "dead zones" uninhabitable for most marine life.
- **Natura 2000**: A network of nature protection areas under the European Union's Habitats and Birds Directives.
- **Sulphur Emission Control Area (SECA)**: A region where stricter limits on sulphur emissions from ships are enforced to reduce air pollution.
- **Cold Seep (Bubbling Reef)**: A seabed area where methane and other hydrocarbons seep out, supporting unique ecosystems through chemosynthesis.

## Debates and Open questions
The exact geographical boundaries of the Kattegat remain debated among different international organizations (e.g., IHO, HELCOM, ICES), with implications for jurisdiction and city classifications. The long-term effectiveness of environmental action plans in fully restoring the Kattegat's ecology is ongoing, as work continues and not all goals have been met. The feasibility and potential environmental impact of the proposed Kattegat Bridge remain subjects of political and public discussion.
