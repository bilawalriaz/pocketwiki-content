# Maurya Empire

## Overview
The Maurya Empire (c. 320–185 BCE) was the first pan-Indian imperial power, unifying most of the subcontinent under a centralized administration from its capital in Magadha. It is historically significant for its vast territorial control, its role in spreading Buddhism across Asia under Emperor Ashoka, and its development of sophisticated statecraft and infrastructure. The empire's legacy includes foundational concepts of Indian governance and its enduring cultural and religious impact.

## Timeline
- **c. 320 BCE** — Chandragupta Maurya founds the Maurya Empire after defeating the Nanda dynasty.
- **305–303 BCE** — Chandragupta defeats Seleucus I Nicator, gaining territories in the northwest and receiving 500 war elephants.
- **c. 297–273 BCE** — Reign of Bindusara, who extends the empire into the Deccan plateau.
- **268–232 BCE** — Reign of Ashoka, the empire's most famous ruler, who conquers Kalinga and promotes Buddhism.
- **261 BCE** — Ashoka's conquest of Kalinga leads to massive casualties and his subsequent conversion to Buddhism.
- **c. 250 BCE** — Ashoka erects pillars and rock edicts across the subcontinent, promoting his dhamma (moral law).
- **185 BCE** — The last Mauryan emperor, Brihadratha, is assassinated by his general Pushyamitra Shunga.

## Body

### Founding and Expansion
The empire was founded by Chandragupta Maurya, who overthrew the Nanda dynasty in Magadha around 320 BCE. His origins are legendary, but Greek sources record his confrontation with Alexander the Great's successors. After consolidating power in the east, he defeated the Seleucid general Seleucus I Nicator in a conflict around 305–303 BCE, gaining control of regions like Gandhara and Arachosia in the northwest. This victory was sealed by a dynastic marriage alliance and the transfer of 500 war elephants to Seleucus. Chandragupta established a vast empire, though its control was deepest in the core regions of Magadha, Taxila, Ujjain, and the lower Deccan.

### Administration and Economy
The Mauryan state was a highly centralized monarchy where all land was owned by the emperor. The population, estimated between 15 and 30 million, was administered through a network of provinces governed by princes (kumaras) and a council of ministers. The economy was bolstered by earlier religious movements like Buddhism and Jainism, which reduced transaction costs, and by state-built roads like the Uttarapath, which facilitated trade. Maritime and riverine commerce was essential for acquiring metals and luxury goods. The empire's administration included detailed census-taking and a complex municipal system in cities like Pataliputra.

### Ashoka and the Spread of Buddhism
Ashoka's reign marked the empire's zenith. After a brutal war of conquest against Kalinga (262–261 BCE), which resulted in immense bloodshed, he embraced Buddhism and renounced aggressive warfare. He promoted his ethical code, *dhamma*, through a series of edicts inscribed on rocks and pillars across the subcontinent. Ashoka sponsored Buddhist missions to Sri Lanka, Central Asia, and the Hellenistic world, sending envoys to rulers like Antiochus II Theos and Ptolemy II Philadelphus. This played a key role in transforming Buddhism into a world religion. His edicts also advocated for non-violence, animal welfare, and public works.

### Decline and Fall
After Ashoka's death around 232 BCE, the empire was weakened by a succession of less capable rulers and possible partition. The last Mauryan emperor, Brihadratha, was assassinated in 185 BCE by his general Pushyamitra Shunga during a military parade, ending the dynasty. The decline is attributed to several factors: weak successors, the empire's unwieldy size, growing provincial independence, and external pressures from groups like the Greco-Bactrians. The fall of the Mauryas left the northwest vulnerable to foreign invasions.

## Terms
*   **Mahajanapadas:** The sixteen major kingdoms and oligarchic republics that existed in ancient India from the sixth to fourth centuries BCE.
*   **Dhamma:** Ashoka's concept of a moral and ethical law, promoting non-violence, social welfare, and religious tolerance, as promoted in his edicts.
*   **Edicts of Ashoka:** Inscriptions on rocks and pillars throughout the Indian subcontinent, issued by Emperor Ashoka to communicate his policies and moral principles.
*   **Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW):** A distinctive type of pottery associated with the urbanization of the Gangetic plain and the Mauryan period.
*   **Arthashastra:** An ancient Indian treatise on statecraft and economic policy, now considered to have been composed in the early centuries CE, not the Mauryan era.
*   **Ahimsa:** The principle of non-violence towards all living beings, a key tenet of Jainism and Buddhism that influenced Ashoka's policies.
*   **Kshatriya:** The varna (social class) of warriors and rulers in the traditional Hindu caste system.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **The Arthashastra's Authenticity:** While once attributed to Chandragupta's advisor Chanakya, the text is now believed by scholars to be a compilation from the early centuries CE, limiting its value as a direct source for Mauryan administration.
*   **The Extent of Ashoka's Pacifism:** Historians debate the practical impact of Ashoka's renunciation of violence. Some argue it weakened the empire's military backbone, while others, like Romila Thapar, suggest its effects have been "grossly exaggerated."
*   **The Nature of Mauryan Control:** The empire's control outside core regions may have been indirect, relying on the loyalty of local military commanders and trade routes rather than deep administrative penetration, as suggested by the absence of edicts in large parts of the subcontinent.
