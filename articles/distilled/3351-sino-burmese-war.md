# Sino-Burmese War

## Overview
The Sino-Burmese War (1765–1769) was a series of four Qing dynasty invasions of Burma's Konbaung dynasty, part of the Qianlong Emperor's "Ten Great Campaigns." Despite initial Qing confidence, Burma's successful defense, aided by tropical disease and guerrilla tactics, resulted in a costly stalemate that preserved Burmese independence and established the foundation for the modern border. The war's primary beneficiary was Siam, which reclaimed its territories while Burma was preoccupied.

## Timeline
- **1730s** — Qing dynasty consolidates control over border Shan states, exploiting Toungoo dynasty weakness.
- **1752** — Toungoo dynasty falls; Konbaung dynasty rises and begins reunifying Burma.
- **1758–59** — King Alaungpaya reasserts Burmese authority over farther Shan states, prompting Qing intervention.
- **Dec 1765** — First Qing invasion (3,500 troops) attacks Kengtung; defeated and pursued back into Yunnan.
- **Dec 1766** — Second Qing invasion captures Bhamo but is lured into a trap at Kaungton; army destroyed by disease and Burmese forces.
- **Nov 1767** — Third invasion (50,000 troops, including elite Manchu Bannermen) under Ming Rui penetrates deep into central Burma.
- **Mar 1768** — Ming Rui's army is encircled and annihilated at the Battle of Maymyo; Ming Rui commits suicide.
- **Oct 1768** — Fourth and largest Qing invasion (60,000 troops) under Fuheng launches during monsoon season.
- **Dec 1769** — Qing forces are encircled at Kaungton; a truce (Treaty of Kaungton) is signed by field commanders.
- **1790** — Burma and China resume diplomatic relations; Qing unilaterally claim victory.

## Body

### Background and Causes
The conflict stemmed from a long, vaguely defined border and competing spheres of influence over the Shan States. In the 1730s, the Qing tightened control over Yunnan's borderlands, winning the allegiance of local Shan chiefs (*sawbwas*). This coincided with the decline of Burma's Toungoo dynasty. The new Konbaung dynasty, after reunifying Burma, attempted to reassert authority over the farther Shan states in 1758–59, leading displaced chiefs to seek Qing intervention. The Qianlong Emperor, viewing this as a challenge to his authority, ordered a reconquest.

### The Four Invasions (1765–1769)
The first two invasions, using Yunnan-based Green Standard Army troops, were defeated at the border. The third invasion escalated to a major war, deploying elite Manchu Bannermen. They penetrated to within days of the capital, Ava, but were decimated by unfamiliar tropical terrain, diseases like malaria, and Burmese guerrilla attacks on supply lines. The fourth invasion, the largest, became bogged down at the frontier fortress of Kaungton. After being completely encircled and suffering heavy losses, the Qing field commanders negotiated a truce in December 1769 without imperial approval.

### Aftermath and Significance
The war was a strategic defeat for the Qing, costing over 70,000 soldiers and 9.8 million silver taels. The Qianlong Emperor, however, unilaterally claimed victory when relations resumed in 1790. For Burma, the victory was a moral triumph but came at a high proportional cost in manpower, hampering its military capabilities elsewhere. The main beneficiary was Siam, which reclaimed its independence and territories while Burmese forces were engaged with China. The war established the de facto boundary between the two empires and demonstrated the limits of Qing power in tropical Southeast Asia.

## Terms
- **Green Standard Army**: The regular, predominantly Han Chinese standing army of the Qing dynasty, considered less battle-worthy by the emperor than the Manchu Bannermen.
- **Manchu Bannermen**: Elite military-social units of the Qing ruling class, considered the empire's best soldiers.
- **Sawbwa**: A local Shan chief or ruler of a semi-autonomous state.
- **Konbaung dynasty**: The ruling dynasty of Burma from 1752 to 1885.
- **Toungoo dynasty**: The preceding Burmese dynasty that collapsed in 1752.
- **Kaungton**: A key Burmese fortress on the Irrawaddy River, south of Bhamo, which successfully resisted multiple Qing assaults.
- **Battle of Maymyo (1768)**: The decisive engagement where the main Qing army under Ming Rui was encircled and annihilated.
- **Treaty of Kaungton (1769)**: The truce signed by field commanders to end hostilities, which was not ratified by the Qing court.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Interpretation of the Treaty**: The Qing court viewed the resumption of diplomatic relations in 1790 as Burmese submission, while Burma considered it an exchange between equals. The terms of the 1769 field commander's treaty were never honored by either side.
- **Impact on Regional Power**: Historians debate the war's role in shaping Southeast Asian geopolitics, particularly how it enabled Siam's resurgence and may have limited Qing expansion into the region, potentially saving the independence of other states.
- **Military Assessment**: The war is studied as a case of a smaller power successfully defending against a larger empire by exploiting terrain, climate, and guerrilla tactics, neutralizing the enemy's numerical advantage.
