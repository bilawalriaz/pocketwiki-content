# Sunni Islam

## Overview

Sunni Islam is the largest branch of Islam, representing 85–90% of the global Muslim population (approximately 1.62 billion adherents as of 2010). It holds that Muhammad did not appoint a successor, affirming Abu Bakr as the first rightful caliph through the Meeting of Saqifa. Sunnis revere the first four caliphs—Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, and Ali—as the Rashidun (Rightly-Guided Ones). The term "Sunni" derives from *sunnah*, the practices of Muhammad, and the tradition relies on the Quran, hadith (especially the Six Books), and ijma (scholarly consensus) as foundational sources. Sunni jurisprudence is organized into four legal schools: Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali. In matters of creed, Sunni Islam encompasses the Ash'ari and Maturidi schools of kalam (theology) alongside the textualist Athari school. The tradition emphasizes the six pillars of faith and reveres the salaf (early predecessors) as exemplars.

## Timeline

## Body

### Origins and Early Development

Sunni Islam emerged from early disputes over succession following Muhammad’s death in 632 CE. The community convened at Saqifa, where Abu Bakr was chosen as the first caliph, establishing the principle of collective leadership rather than hereditary succession. This contrasted sharply with Shia belief, which held that Ali, Muhammad’s cousin and son-in-law, was the divinely appointed successor. The first four caliphs—Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, and Ali—are collectively venerated by Sunnis as the Rashidun, or "Rightly-Guided Ones," despite internal conflicts including the assassination of Uthman and the Battle of Karbala.

The term *sunnah* gained political significance after Uthman’s murder, particularly during the Battle of Siffin, where Ali’s supporter Malik al-Ashtar invoked it against Muawiya. The phrase *ahl as-sunna* (people of the Sunna) became a marker of orthodoxy, distinguishing those who adhered to prophetic tradition from heretical sects. Early scholars like Abu Hanifa emphasized *ahl al-‘adl wa-ahl as-sunna* ("people of righteousness and the Sunna"), reflecting the term’s laudatory usage.

### Theological Schools and Doctrinal Evolution

By the 9th century, distinct theological schools crystallized within Sunni Islam. The Ash'ari school, founded by Abu al-Hasan al-Ash’ari (d. 935), emphasized divine revelation over human reason, rejecting Mu'tazilite rationalism while affirming God’s attributes as they “best befit His Majesty.” The Maturidi school, established by Abu Mansur al-Maturidi (d. 944), allowed reason to evaluate hadith but maintained revelation’s role in matters beyond human intellect. The Athari (traditionalist) school, rooted in hadith scholarship and exemplified by Ahmad ibn Hanbal, rejected speculative theology (*kalam*) in favor of literal interpretation (*tafwid*).

Debates over inclusion persisted. Some scholars narrowly defined Sunnism as encompassing only Ash'arites and Maturidites, while others excluded them entirely. Ibn Taymiyyah distinguished between Sunnis in the general sense (recognizing the first three caliphs) and the specific sense (*ahl al-hadith*). Modern tensions between Ash'arites and Salafis reflect these historical divisions, with each side claiming exclusive authenticity.

### Jurisprudence and Legal Frameworks

Sunni jurisprudence (*fiqh*) developed through four primary schools: Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali. These schools differ in methodology—ranging from the Hanafi emphasis on reason (*ra'y*) to the Hanbali prioritization of textual sources (*hadith*). Conflict between schools was historically common but eventually gave way to mutual recognition of validity. The Ottoman Empire formalized the four-school system as a counterpoint to Shia Safavid influence, a structure largely maintained today.

Movements like Barelvism emerged in South Asia, synthesizing Hanafi jurisprudence with Maturidi theology and Sufi practices. Founded in the 19th century, Barelvism emphasized saint veneration and prophetic devotion, positioning itself as the authentic continuation of pre-Salafi orthodoxy.

### Contemporary Challenges and Identity

The abolition of the Ottoman Caliphate in 1924 marked a turning point, prompting Sunni responses ranging from the Khilafat Movement in India to the rise of Saudi Arabia under Wahhabi doctrine. The 20th century witnessed the emergence of Salafism, which revived Athari textualism, and political Islamism, exemplified by the Taliban and ISIL. These movements often clashed with traditional institutions like Al-Azhar University, which upheld Ash'arite-Maturidi synthesis.

Modern Sunni identity remains contested. The 2016 Grozny and Kuwait conferences highlighted ongoing disputes over whether Salafis constitute genuine Sunnis or deviant sects. Meanwhile, institutions like the Turkish Diyanet promote a state-sanctioned Sunni orthodoxy, while scholars debate the boundaries of acceptable belief and practice.

## Terms

- **Sunnah**: The practices and teachings of Prophet Muhammad, forming the basis of Sunni identity.
- **Hadith**: Recorded oral traditions describing Muhammad’s actions and sayings; the Six Books are canonical collections.
- **Ijma**: Scholarly consensus, serving as a source of Islamic law alongside the Quran and hadith.
- **Sharia**: Islamic law derived from the Quran, hadith, and ijma, interpreted through the four legal schools.
- **Rashidun**: The first four caliphs (Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, Ali), considered rightly-guided by Sunnis.
- **Kalam**: Islamic theology; Ash'ari and Maturidi schools represent mainstream Sunni approaches.
- **Salaf**: The pious predecessors (first three generations of Muslims); Salafism seeks to emulate their practices.
- **Tawhid**: The doctrine of God’s absolute oneness, central to Sunni creed.
- **Imam**: A prayer leader or religious authority; in Sunni Islam, not considered infallible.
- **Ummah**: The global community of Muslims, united by shared faith and practice.

## Debates and Open Questions

Several key debates persist within Sunni Islam:

1. **Definition of Orthodoxy**: Whether Ash'arites, Maturidites, and Atharis all qualify as authentic Sunnis, or if one tradition holds exclusive legitimacy.
2. **Role of Reason vs. Text**: Tension between rational theological methods (Ash'ari/Maturidi) and literalist approaches (Athari/Salafi).
3. **Inclusion of Sufism**: Whether Sufi practices align with Sunni orthodoxy or represent heterodox innovations (*bid'ah*).
4. **Political Authority**: The extent to which temporal rulers should adhere to religious scholarship versus asserting independent authority.
5. **Contemporary Movements**: The legitimacy of Salafism, political Islamism, and reformist movements within Sunni framework.
6. **Historical Interpretation**: Disputes over early Islamic history, particularly the legitimacy of the first three caliphs versus Ali’s succession.
