# Improvisational theatre

## Overview
Improvisational theatre is a form of performance where dialogue, action, and story are created spontaneously by performers without a pre-written script. It is a foundational training method for actors and has evolved into a popular comedic art form, with its principles also applied in education, business, and therapy to foster creativity, communication, and collaboration.

## Timeline
- **391 BC** — Earliest well-documented Western use: the Atellan Farce.
- **16th-18th C.** — Commedia dell'arte performers improvise in Italian streets.
- **1890s** — Stanislavski and Copeau utilize improv in acting training.
- **1916** — John Dewey's progressive education movement influences drama exercises.
- **1940s-60s** — Viola Spolin develops and codifies improv games in *Improvisation For The Theater*.
- **1959** — The Second City opens in Chicago, becoming a seminal improv troupe.
- **1960s** — The Committee theater active in San Francisco.
- **1970s** — Playback Theatre founded; Keith Johnstone's Theatresports develops.
- **1977** — Clive Barker's *Theatre Games* spreads improv internationally.
- **1984** — Dick Chudnow founds ComedySportz.
- **1987** — Annoyance Theatre begins in Chicago, emphasizing longform.
- **1990s** — Applied improvisation emerges as a field for non-performance use.

## Body

### History and Development
Improvisational techniques have ancient roots, from the Atellan Farce of 391 BC to the commedia dell'arte of the 16th-18th centuries. Modern improv began as drama exercises for children in the early 20th century, influenced by progressive education. Key figures like Viola Spolin and her son Paul Sills were instrumental in its development. Spolin's exercises, first used with the Compass Players (founded by Sills and David Shepherd), became the cornerstone of training at The Second City, which opened in 1959. This Chicago scene formalized many "rules" of comedic improv. Simultaneously, Keith Johnstone developed Theatresports in London and later Calgary. The form spread globally, with notable troupes like The Committee in San Francisco (1960s) and the founding of ComedySportz in 1984.

### Forms and Styles
Modern improv comedy is generally categorized as shortform or longform. Shortform consists of short scenes based on games and audience suggestions, popularized by shows like *Whose Line Is It Anyway?*. Longform creates interconnected shows, sometimes full-length plays or musicals. A key longform structure is the "Harold," developed by Del Close. Beyond comedy, experimental and dramatic forms exist, such as Playback Theatre (replaying audience stories) and Theatre of the Oppressed. The Open Theater in New York used improvisation to explore political and social issues, while Ruth Zaporah's Action Theatre treats language, movement, and voice equally.

### Structure and Process
Successful improv relies on co-creation and the "Yes, and..." principle. Performers build scenes by accepting each other's "offers" (defining elements of reality) and adding new information. Blocking or negating an offer is generally avoided as it halts the scene's progress. Improvisers use "space work" (mime) to create imaginary props and environments, respecting the shared reality. They must quickly construct characters using physicality, voice, and motivation. Scene changes are often signaled by a "wipe" (a performer running across the stage).

### Applications Beyond Performance
The principles of improv are applied outside theatre in a field called applied improvisation. In education, it's a tool for learning. In business, it develops communication, creative problem-solving, and teamwork. In psychotherapy, techniques like "Yes, and..." are compared to acceptance-based therapies and used for insight and treating social anxiety. The Alan Alda Center for Communicating Science uses improv games to improve scientists' communication skills.

### In Film and Television
Improvisation is used in film and television for script development and in final products. Silent filmmakers like Charlie Chaplin and Buster Keaton improvised gags. Directors like Robert Altman, Christopher Guest, and Mike Leigh extensively use improv with actors. Television shows like *Curb Your Enthusiasm*, *Whose Line Is It Anyway?*, and *Reno 911!* are built on improvised dialogue or scenes.

## Terms
- **Shortform**: Improv consisting of short scenes, often based on games and audience suggestions.
- **Longform**: Improv creating longer, interconnected shows, such as a full play or the "Harold" structure.
- **Harold**: A popular longform improv structure developed by Del Close, involving interconnected scenes and themes.
- **Yes, and...**: The foundational improv rule where performers accept an offer and build upon it.
- **Offer**: Any statement or action that defines an element of the scene's reality.
- **Blocking**: Negating or denying a fellow performer's offer, which stops the scene's progress.
- **Space Work**: The mime technique used to create imaginary props and environments.
- **Applied Improv**: The use of improvisational principles and exercises outside of performance, in fields like education, business, and therapy.
- **Playback Theatre**: An improvised form where audience members share stories that are then enacted by performers.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that the skills of improvisation are used outside performing arts, but the extent and formalization of "applied improvisation" as a distinct field is a more recent development. There is also a noted distinction between ad-libbing and true improvisation in film. Historically, the legality of unscripted performance was a debate, as seen in the prosecution of director Joan Littlewood in Britain before 1968 for allowing actors to improvise.
