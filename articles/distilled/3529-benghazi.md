# Benghazi

## Overview
Benghazi is Libya's second-largest city and the historic capital of the Cyrenaica region. Its strategic location on the Mediterranean has made it a major seaport and a focal point of Libya's political and military conflicts, from ancient Greek colonization to the 2011 revolution and subsequent civil wars.

## Timeline
- **c. 525 BC** — Greek colony of Euesperides founded.
- **246 BC** — City relocated and refounded as Ptolemaic Berenice.
- **3rd century AD** — Becomes the chief center of Roman Cyrenaica.
- **1578** — Conquered by the Ottoman Empire.
- **1911** — Captured by Italy during the Italo-Turkish War.
- **1942-43** — Changes hands multiple times during World War II.
- **1951** — Becomes co-capital of the independent Kingdom of Libya.
- **1969** — Loses capital status after Muammar Gaddafi's coup.
- **15 Feb 2011** — Uprising against Gaddafi begins in the city.
- **19 Mar 2011** — French air intervention halts pro-Gaddafi attack on Benghazi.
- **11 Sep 2012** — Attack on the U.S. diplomatic mission kills four Americans.
- **5 Jul 2017** — Libyan National Army declares city cleared of Islamist militants.

## Body

### Ancient and Medieval History
The site was first settled by Greeks as Euesperides around 525 BC, likely founded by colonists from Cyrene or Barce. It was relocated and renamed Berenice in 246 BC under Ptolemaic rule. The city prospered under the Romans, superseding Cyrene as the regional center after the 3rd century AD. It declined during the Byzantine period and was a small village when conquered by Arab forces in 642–643. After periods of Genoese trade and rule by the Karamanlis from Tripoli, the Ottomans conquered Benghazi in 1578, ruling it directly from 1835 until 1911.

### Colonial and World War II Era
Italy captured Benghazi in 1911, facing a long resistance led by Omar Mukhtar. Under Italian rule, especially in the late 1930s, the city underwent modernization and development, with its population becoming over 35% Italian by 1939. During World War II, Benghazi changed hands several times between Allied and Axis forces, suffering heavy damage. It was last captured by the British Eighth Army in November 1942.

### Post-War to 2011 Revolution
Rebuilt with oil wealth, Benghazi became the co-capital of the Kingdom of Libya in 1951. It lost this status after Gaddafi's 1969 coup, which centralized power in Tripoli. On 15 February 2011, protests erupted, escalating into an armed revolt. By 21 February, opposition forces controlled the city, which became the temporary headquarters of the National Transitional Council. A critical turning point occurred on 19 March when a pro-Gaddafi offensive was halted by local resistance and French air strikes authorized by UN Resolution 1973.

### Recent Conflicts and Governance
Following the 2011 revolution, Benghazi was the site of the 2012 U.S. diplomatic mission attack. During the second Libyan Civil War (from 2014), the city saw intense fighting between the Libyan National Army and Islamist militants, with the LNA declaring it cleared in July 2017. A permanent ceasefire was agreed in October 2020. An interim unity government formed in 2021 has undertaken rebuilding, though controversially razed parts of the historic Italian-era center in 2023.

## Terms
- **Cyrenaica**: The eastern region of Libya, historically and culturally distinct, with Benghazi as its principal city.
- **Ptolemaic**: Relating to the Hellenistic dynasty that ruled Egypt and parts of Libya after Alexander the Great.
- **Senussi**: A political-religious order and former royal dynasty of Libya, with strong traditional support in Cyrenaica.
- **Shabiyah**: A type of administrative district in Libya.
- **National Transitional Council (NTC)**: The political body formed in 2011 to represent the Libyan opposition during the civil war.
- **Libyan National Army (LNA)**: A major military force in eastern Libya, aligned with the House of Representatives government.
- **Ansar al-Sharia**: An Islamist militant group implicated in the 2012 Benghazi attack.
- **Great Manmade River**: A network of pipelines transporting groundwater from aquifers in southern Libya to coastal cities.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise reasons for the relocation of Euesperides to Berenice in 246 BC are debated; it may have been due to silting lagoons or as political punishment.
- The long-term political future of Libya remains uncertain, with tensions between eastern (Tobruk/Benghazi) and western (Tripoli) governments threatening stability despite the 2020 ceasefire and 2021 unity government.
- The controversial demolition of Benghazi's historic Italian-era buildings in 2023 by the military, without municipal consultation, raises questions about post-war reconstruction priorities and heritage preservation.
