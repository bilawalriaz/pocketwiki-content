# Japanese literature

## Overview
Japanese literature evolved from early Chinese-influenced texts to a distinct national tradition, then absorbed Western influences after the Meiji Restoration. Its development reflects Japan's cultural shifts, from aristocratic courts to warrior classes, urban commoners, and modern global engagement, producing internationally recognized works and two Nobel laureates.

## Timeline
- **Early 5th century** — Chinese characters (kanji) introduced to Japan via Korea and China.
- **712** — *Kojiki* compiled, a historical record with mythology and folk songs.
- **759** — *Man'yōshū* poetry anthology completed.
- **Early 11th century** — Murasaki Shikibu writes *The Tale of Genji*.
- **1212** — *The Tale of the Heike* epic emerges, chronicling clan wars.
- **1603–1868** — Edo period sees rise of popular literature (haiku, novels) under Tokugawa shogunate.
- **1868** — Meiji Restoration ends national seclusion, introducing Western literary influences.
- **1906** — Natsume Sōseki publishes *I Am a Cat*; Naturalism gains ground.
- **1914** — Sōseki's *Kokoro* explores modern psychology.
- **1964** — Kenzaburō Ōe publishes *A Personal Matter*.
- **1968** — Kawabata wins Nobel Prize in Literature.
- **1994** — Ōe wins Nobel Prize in Literature.

## Body

### Early Influences and Classical Foundations
Before the 5th century, Japan had no writing system. Chinese characters arrived via Korean and Chinese immigrants, initially used in pure Classical Chinese (*kanbun*). Over time, characters were adapted phonetically into *man'yōgana*, the earliest form of *kana* (Japanese syllabary). The Nara period produced foundational texts: the *Kojiki* (712) and *Nihon Shoki* (720) chronicles, and the *Man'yōshū* (759) poetry anthology.

### Heian Golden Age
The Heian period (794–1185) saw literature flourish among the imperial court's cultural elite. The development of *kana* enabled a distinct Japanese style. Poetry anthologies like the *Kokin Wakashū* (905) became national pastimes. Murasaki Shikibu's *The Tale of Genji* (early 11th century) is considered the pre-eminent novel of the era. Sei Shōnagon's *The Pillow Book* (990s) offered essays on court life. This era established elegant, sophisticated literary traditions centered on aristocratic emotions.

### Kamakura-Muromachi Transition
The Kamakura period (1185–1333) was marked by civil wars and the rise of a warrior class, leading to somber war tales like *The Tale of the Heike* (c. 1212–1309). Themes shifted to life, death, and redemption. Despite the court's decline, aristocratic literature persisted with nostalgia for Heian times. The Muromachi period (1333–1603) saw literature spread to commoners, with new genres like *renga* (linked verse) and Noh theater. Travel literature also gained popularity.

### Edo Popular Flourishing
The peaceful Edo period (1603–1868) under the Tokugawa shogunate saw literature diversify for a growing urban middle class. Literacy rose, and lending libraries appeared. Matsuo Bashō (1644–1694) mastered *haiku* (then *hokku*), transforming it into a literary genre with works like *Oku no Hosomichi*. Ihara Saikaku (1642–1693) pioneered the modern novel with humorous tales of the "floating world." Chikamatsu Monzaemon (1653–1725) became a leading dramatist for *kabuki* and *jōruri* theater. Chinese vernacular fiction was the greatest outside influence on early modern Japanese fiction.

### Meiji Modernization and Westernization
The Meiji Restoration (1868) ended seclusion, introducing Western literature and ideas. Free verse entered poetry, and novelists adapted Western conventions. Natsume Sōseki (1867–1916) used humor and satire in *I Am a Cat* (1905) and explored modern psychology in *Kokoro* (1914). Mori Ōgai and others brought Romanticism and later Naturalism, which spawned the autobiographical "I Novel." The proletarian literary movement of the 1920s–30s depicted workers' struggles. Pre-war writers like Jun'ichirō Tanizaki and Yasunari Kawabata focused on language beauty and sensuality.

### Postwar and Contemporary Literature
World War II's defeat deeply influenced postwar literature, with themes of disaffection and loss. Osamu Dazai's *The Setting Sun* and Shōhei Ōoka's *Fires on the Plain* addressed defeat and disillusionment. Yukio Mishima became known for nihilistic writing and controversial suicide. Kenzaburō Ōe, winner of the 1994 Nobel Prize, raised social and political consciousness. Avant-garde writers like Kōbō Abe explored modern Japanese experience. Contemporary authors like Haruki Murakami and Banana Yoshimoto gained international popularity, though their genre-defying styles sparked debates about "true literature." Manga and cell phone novels have also become significant popular forms.

### Female Authors
Female writers enjoyed success in the Heian period (e.g., Murasaki Shikibu, Sei Shōnagon) but faced declining status later. The Meiji era saw renewed attention to their earlier works, and writers like Higuchi Ichiyō and Yosano Akiko emerged, though they navigated societal constraints.

## Terms
- **Kanbun**: Classical Chinese writing used in early Japanese texts.
- **Kana**: Japanese syllabary developed from adapted Chinese characters (*man'yōgana*).
- **Waka**: A form of classic Japanese poetry, often in a 31-syllable structure.
- **Haiku**: A short poem (originally *hokku*) capturing a moment in nature, mastered by Bashō.
- **Monogatari**: A narrative tale or story, like *The Tale of Genji*.
- **Rangaku**: "Dutch learning," an intellectual movement studying Western science via Dutch imports.
- **Yomihon**: Historical romance novels in prose, influenced by Chinese vernacular fiction.
- **I Novel (Watakushi-shōsetu)**: An autobiographical genre depicting the author's own mental states.
- **Manga**: Japanese comics covering diverse genres, a major popular literature form.
- **Light Novel**: A young adult novel type often with manga-style illustrations.

## Debates and Open questions
- The classification of Haruki Murakami's works as "true literature" versus pop fiction remains contentious, with critics like Kenzaburō Ōe opposing them.
- The status and recognition of female authors have fluctuated historically, with ongoing discussions about their contributions and societal challenges.
- The impact of new media (e.g., cell phone novels, manga) on traditional literary forms and quality is a subject of ongoing debate.
