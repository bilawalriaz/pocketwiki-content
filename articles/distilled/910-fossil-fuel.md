# Fossil fuel

## Overview  
A fossil fuel is a flammable, carbon-rich material formed in Earth’s crust from the buried remains of prehistoric organisms. Over millions of years, heat and pressure transform this organic matter into coal, petroleum, and natural gas. These energy-dense fuels powered the Industrial Revolution and now supply roughly 77% of global primary energy and over 60% of electricity (2023). However, their combustion is the dominant source of anthropogenic greenhouse-gas emissions, driving climate change, air pollution, and associated health and economic costs.

## Timeline  
- **c. 1597** — Andreas Libavius proposes organic origin of fossil fuels in *Alchemia*
- **1757–1763** — Mikhail Lomonosov develops the heat-pressure formation theory
- **1759** — Term “fossil fuel” first recorded by Caspar Neumann
- **19th century** — Commercial petroleum exploitation begins
- **Late 18th–19th century** — Coal and petroleum power the Industrial Revolution
- **2021** — IEA states no new fossil-fuel projects are compatible with climate goals
- **2022** — Russian invasion of Ukraine triggers fossil-driven global inflation spike
- **2023** — Fossil fuels account for 77% of global primary energy use

## Body  

### Origin  
Fossil fuels originate from anaerobic decomposition of buried organisms. Aquatic plankton, buried under sediment in anoxic conditions, undergo catagenesis—heat and pressure convert organic matter first into kerogen (in oil shales), then into liquid and gaseous hydrocarbons. Terrestrial plants form coal and methane, often from Carboniferous-era vegetation. Although continuously generated, fossil fuels are non-renewable due to the millions of years required for formation and rapid depletion of reserves.

### Importance  
Fossil fuels became central to human development due to their high energy density and ease of combustion. Peat use predates recorded history; coal fueled early metal smelting. The 19th-century rise of petroleum and the internal combustion engine transformed transportation. Natural gas, once flared as waste, is now a key resource and helium source. Heavy crude, oil sands, and oil shale gained prominence in the 2000s but faced disinvestment in the 2010s–2020s due to high carbon costs. Fossil fuels also underpin the Green Revolution via fertilizers, pesticides, and irrigation.

### Environmental Effects  
Fossil fuel combustion releases CO₂, the primary driver of climate change, contributing over 70% of human-caused greenhouse-gas emissions in 2022. Burning coal and oil produces particulates, smog, and acid rain. Fossil fuels also emit radioactive materials like uranium and thorium. Air pollution from fossil fuels cost $2.9 trillion globally in 2018 (3.3% of GDP). Health impacts include premature death, respiratory illness, and chronic bronchitis, with over 8 million annual deaths linked to fossil-fuel pollution (2018).

### Phase-Out and Policy  
Growing recognition of climate and health risks has spurred policy shifts toward renewable energy. The Paris Agreement and UN Sustainable Development Goals aim to facilitate a global transition. The International Energy Agency (2021) asserts that avoiding severe climate impacts requires halting new fossil-fuel extraction. A “just transition” seeks to address economic disruption and stranded assets. Despite this, some governments continued subsidizing fossil fuels post-2020, prompting criticism from UN officials.

### Economic Impacts: Fossilflation  
Fossil fuels significantly influence inflation. Energy prices drove major post-WWII inflation episodes. The 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine caused sharp energy-driven price spikes. The U.S. Inflation Reduction Act (2022) aims to reduce fossil-fuel dependence, potentially saving households over $300 annually in energy costs by 2030.

## Terms  
- **Anaerobic decomposition**: Breakdown of organic matter without oxygen, key to fossil fuel formation.  
- **Catagenesis**: Geological process where heat and pressure convert kerogen into hydrocarbons.  
- **Kerogen**: Waxy organic material found in oil shales, precursor to liquid and gaseous fuels.  
- **Non-renewable resource**: A resource that cannot be replenished at a rate comparable to its consumption.  
- **Fossilflation**: Inflation driven by volatile fossil fuel prices.  
- **Stranded assets**: Investments in fossil fuel infrastructure at risk of becoming obsolete due to climate policies.  
- **Just transition**: Policy framework ensuring fair economic shift from fossil fuels to renewables.  
- **Oil sands**: Deposits where bitumen is mixed with sand and clay, requiring processing for fuel.  
- **Pyrolysis**: Thermal decomposition of organic material (e.g., oil shale) to produce synthetic crude.  
- **Fly ash**: Fine residue from coal combustion, used in construction materials.

## Debates and Open Questions  
- Whether existing fossil fuel infrastructure can be retired rapidly enough to meet Paris Agreement targets.  
- The role of natural gas as a “bridge fuel” versus its methane leakage risks.  
- Extent to which renewable energy can fully replace fossil fuels without compromising energy security.  
- Effectiveness of carbon pricing and subsidies in accelerating fossil fuel divestment.  
- Feasibility of a just transition in economies heavily dependent on fossil fuel industries.
