# Jiang Qing

## Overview
Jiang Qing (1914–1991), also known as Madame Mao, was the fourth wife of Mao Zedong and a central political figure during China's Cultural Revolution (1966–1976). As leader of the radical "Gang of Four," she wielded immense power in cultural and political spheres, orchestrating purges and promoting revolutionary art. Her life story—from a traumatic childhood and acting career to political ascendancy and a dramatic downfall—embodies the personal and ideological struggles that shaped modern China.

## Timeline
- **March 1914** — Born Li Yunhe in Zhucheng, Shandong.
- **1933** — Joins the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) in Qingdao.
- **1935–1937** — Pursues acting career in Shanghai under stage name Lan Ping.
- **November 1938** — Marries Mao Zedong in Yan'an.
- **1949–1951** — Serves as deputy director of the Film Guidance Committee.
- **1963–1964** — Leads campaign to reform Peking Opera with revolutionary themes.
- **1966** — Appointed deputy director of the Central Cultural Revolution Group (CCRG).
- **1969** — Becomes a member of the Politburo at the 9th National Congress.
- **9 September 1976** — Mao Zedong dies.
- **6 October 1976** — Arrested with the Gang of Four.
- **November 1980–January 1981** — Stands trial in a nationally televised proceeding.
- **14 May 1991** — Dies by suicide in Beijing.

## Body

### Early Life and Acting Career
Jiang was born into poverty and suffered childhood trauma from her father's domestic violence. After her mother's death, she pursued independence through acting, joining a theatre troupe at age 14. She studied at the Experimental Arts Academy and later worked in Qingdao and Shanghai, where she joined the CCP in 1933. Her acting career and personal relationships were marked by scandal, which tarnished her reputation. Drawn to the progressive ideals of Yan'an, she left Shanghai for the Communist base in 1937.

### Marriage to Mao and Early Political Role
In Yan'an, Jiang became a drama instructor at the Lu Xun Academy of Arts. Her romance with Mao sparked opposition within the party, but they married in 1938 with restrictions: she could not hold party positions for 20 years and was limited to managing Mao's private affairs. After the PRC's founding in 1949, she served as a low-profile first lady and worked in film bureaucracy, where she attempted to enforce socialist art standards, often requiring Mao's intervention. Poor health led to extended medical treatment in the Soviet Union.

### Rise During the Cultural Revolution
Jiang's political influence grew in the early 1960s as Mao grew distrustful of other leaders. She championed revolutionary Peking operas (*yangbanxi*) and, with allies like Zhang Chunqiao and Yao Wenyuan, launched the Cultural Revolution by criticizing the play *Hai Rui Dismissed from Office* in 1965. As deputy director of the CCRG, she wielded significant power, persecuting rivals like Liu Shaoqi and Zhou Enlai. She promoted eight "model operas" as proletarian art and was a key member of the radical Gang of Four.

### Downfall and Trial
After Mao's death in 1976, Jiang and the Gang of Four were arrested in a coup led by Hua Guofeng. She was tried in 1980–1981, defiantly claiming she acted on Mao's orders. Sentenced to death with a two-year reprieve, her sentence was commuted to life imprisonment in 1983. She remained defiant until her suicide in 1991.

### Legacy and Public Image
Jiang is widely viewed as a symbol of the Cultural Revolution's chaos and personal vendettas. Official historiography vilified her as a "white-boned demon," shifting blame for the era's excesses from Mao to the Gang of Four. Alternative biographical works challenge this one-dimensional portrayal, examining her life as a critique of official narratives. Her grave in Beijing has become a site of pilgrimage for leftist activists, though authorities have periodically restricted public mourning.

## Terms
- **Gang of Four**: Radical political faction led by Jiang Qing, Zhang Chunqiao, Yao Wenyuan, and Wang Hongwen, which dominated Chinese politics during the Cultural Revolution.
- **Cultural Revolution (1966–1976)**: A sociopolitical movement launched by Mao Zedong to purge capitalist and traditional elements from Chinese society, resulting in widespread chaos and persecution.
- **Yangbanxi (Model Dramas)**: Revolutionary operas and ballets promoted by Jiang Qing as the standard for proletarian art, blending Chinese and Western performance elements.
- **Central Cultural Revolution Group (CCRG)**: A powerful party organ established in 1966 to direct the Cultural Revolution, where Jiang served as deputy director.
- **Politburo**: The executive committee of the CCP's Central Committee, the top decision-making body in China.
- **First Lady**: The informal title for the wife of China's leader; Jiang's role was concealed until the 1960s.
- **Red Guards**: Paramilitary student groups mobilized by Mao during the Cultural Revolution to attack perceived revisionists.
- **Qincheng Prison**: A high-security prison near Beijing where Jiang was held after her arrest.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Mao's Responsibility**: Jiang insisted she was following Mao's orders, raising questions about his direct culpability for Cultural Revolution excesses. Official CCP historiography blames the Gang of Four while acknowledging Mao's "errors."
- **Burial Site Controversy**: Jiang wished to be buried in her hometown, but was interred in Beijing due to political concerns, reflecting ongoing tensions over her legacy.
- **Commemoration**: Authorities allow leftist groups to honor Jiang at her grave while prohibiting public mourning for other political figures like Zhao Ziyang, highlighting selective historical memory.
- **Personal vs. Political Motives**: Scholars debate whether her actions were driven by ideological conviction or personal vendettas from her early career.
