# Human rights

## Overview
Human rights are inherent, inalienable moral principles establishing standards of behavior protected by national and international law. While philosophical antecedents exist in ancient religious texts, Roman law, and the medieval natural law tradition, the modern universal framework emerged after World War II in response to the Holocaust. The 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR) established a comprehensive, non-binding standard later codified into binding treaties, notably the 1966 International Covenants on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) and Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR). The system operates through UN charter bodies, treaty-monitoring committees, regional courts, and NGOs, though enforcement remains difficult due to state sovereignty, cultural relativism debates, and the non-binding nature of many declarations. Core tensions persist between the indivisibility of rights and their practical prioritization, and between universalist claims and cultural or religious particularism.

## Body

### Historical Foundations
The concept of universal human rights is modern, but its intellectual roots are ancient. Jewish and Christian scriptures provided conceptual foundations, while Roman law offered legal models. The medieval natural law tradition, influenced by St. Augustine and St. Thomas Aquinas, posited that legitimate law derives from a higher moral order discoverable by reason. The 13th-century Kouroukan Fouga of the Mali Empire stands as an early non-Western charter protecting life, physical integrity, and women’s rights. In 16th-century Spain, the School of Salamanca (Francisco Vitoria, Domingo de Soto) developed *iura naturalia* (natural rights), arguing for indigenous rights during the Valladolid Debate (1550–51). The early modern era saw natural rights theory drive the English (1689), American (1776), and French (1789) revolutions. John Locke defined natural rights as "life, liberty, and estate," arguing they cannot be surrendered in the social contract. The term "human rights" appeared in print by 1831 (William Lloyd Garrison), and 19th-century movements (abolition, suffrage, labor) expanded the practical scope of rights claims.

### The Post-War Framework: UDHR and the Covenants
The atrocities of World War II and the Holocaust catalyzed the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR). Drafted by a commission chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt, with key contributions from John Humphrey (structure) and René Cassin (architecture), the UDHR organized rights from dignity and liberty (Articles 1–2) through civil/political, spiritual/public, and economic/social/cultural rights, ending with duties and limits. Adopted unanimously (with Soviet bloc, South Africa, and Saudi Arabia abstaining), it asserted the indivisibility of all rights. However, Cold War divisions split this unity: Western states prioritized civil/political rights (ICCPR), while Socialist states emphasized economic/social rights (ICESCR). Adopted in 1966 and entering force in 1976, the two Covenants made UDHR provisions binding on ratifying states, though the US did not ratify the ICCPR until 1992. Subsequent treaties (Genocide, Racial Discrimination, CEDAW, CAT, CRC, Migrant Workers, Rome Statute) created a dense "international bill of rights."

### Implementation and Enforcement Mechanisms
**UN System:** The Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights (OHCHR) leads the UN human rights machinery. The Human Rights Council (created 2005, 47 elected members) investigates violations via Special Rapporteurs and can refer cases to the Security Council or ICC. Ten treaty bodies monitor compliance through state reporting and, where Optional Protocols exist, individual complaints. Their "views" are not legally binding. The committees are: Human Rights Committee (ICCPR); Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR); Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination (CERD); Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW); Committee Against Torture (CAT); Committee on the Rights of the Child (CRC); Committee on Migrant Workers (ICRMW); Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD); Committee on Enforced Disappearances (ICPPED); and the Subcommittee on Prevention of Torture (SPT). The CESCR, uniquely, reports to ECOSOC rather than being treaty-autonomous, which commentators note weakens its implementation means.
**International Courts:** The International Court of Justice (ICJ) adjudicates disputes between states. The International Criminal Court (ICC, 2002) prosecutes individuals for war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide, operating on complementarity (acting only when national courts cannot or will not).
**Regional Regimes:** Europe has the most developed system (European Convention on Human Rights, European Court of Human Rights, binding on 47 Council of Europe states). The Americas operate via the OAS, Inter-American Commission, and Inter-American Court. Africa has the African Charter, Commission, and Court (integration with Court of Justice pending). Asia lacked a region-wide mechanism until the 2012 ASEAN Human Rights Declaration; the Arab League adopted its Charter in 2004.
**Non-State Strategies:** NGOs (Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch) monitor, document, and advocate. Economic sanctions and conditional aid are used as inducement, though criticized for collective punishment. The "Responsibility to Protect" (R2P) doctrine justifies military intervention for atrocity prevention (e.g., Libya 2011), but remains controversial regarding sovereignty and mission creep. Corporate accountability remains a gap; the 2003 UN Norms on transnational corporations are non-binding.

### Philosophical Justifications
**Natural Rights/Natural Law:** The dominant Western tradition (Stoics, Aquinas, Grotius, Locke) grounds rights in a pre-political moral order. Grotius secularized natural law (*etiamsi daremus non-esse Deum*), arguing it binds even a hypothetical omnipotent being. Locke inverted Hobbes: if the sovereign violates natural law (failing to protect life, liberty, property), the governed may overthrow it. Modern secular versions derive rights from universal human dignity rather than theology.
**Alternative Theories:** David Hume and evolutionary approaches view rights as social products of biological/social evolution (empathy, altruism). John Rawls frames rights as terms of a social contract for mutual advantage. John Finnis justifies rights instrumentally as necessary for human well-being. Zhao Tingyang critiques the Western contingency of "inalienable" rights, proposing "credit human rights" tied to responsibilities.

### Core Concepts: Indivisibility, Universality, and Limits
**Indivisibility vs. Categorization:** The UDHR treats civil/political rights (Articles 3–21) and economic/social/cultural rights (Articles 22–28) as inseparable: "freedom from fear and want" requires both. The "full belly thesis" argues political rights are unusable without subsistence. Yet practice diverges: Western states often prioritize civil/political rights (e.g., US lacks universal healthcare); former Soviet/Asian states often prioritize economic rights while restricting political ones. Karel Vasak’s "three generations" (civil/political; economic/social; solidarity/peace/environment) is influential but criticized for implying hierarchy. Philip Alston warns that treating all rights as equally essential risks trivializing core protections; prioritization must respect non-discrimination and progressive realization. The international community reaffirmed indivisibility in 1995 and at the 2005 World Summit (para. 121).
**Universalism vs. Cultural Relativism:** The UDHR claims universal application. Relativists (citing Vienna Declaration §5 on "national and regional particularities") argue rights conflict with communal cultures. Contested areas include women’s rights (e.g., female genital mutilation) and "Asian values" arguments (Lee Kuan Yew, Mahathir Mohamad) favoring social stability over individual liberty. Critics (Ignatieff, Chee Soon Juan) counter that relativism shields powerful elites, that the UDHR drafters were globally diverse (including a Chinese Confucian, Arab League rep, and Gandhi’s input), and that modern human rights are new to *all* cultures (post-1948). The 2005 World Summit reaffirmed: "The universal nature of human rights and freedoms is beyond question."
**Derogation and Jus Cogens:** In "public emergencies threatening the life of the nation," states may derogate from most rights *except* non-derogable *jus cogens* norms: right to life, freedom from slavery, torture, and retroactive penal laws. These peremptory norms bind all states regardless of treaty consent.

### Emerging Frontiers
The framework continues to expand. The UN Human Rights Council (2021), European Court of Human Rights (2024, *Verein KlimaSeniorinnen v. Switzerland*), and ICJ (2025 advisory opinion) have recognized a human right to a clean, healthy, sustainable environment, linking climate inaction to rights violations. This reflects a shift toward "third-generation" solidarity rights and the justiciability of environmental protection.

## Terms
- ****Inalienable rights**** — Rights fundamental to human dignity that cannot be surrendered, transferred, or revoked by any human authority (e.g., life, freedom from torture).
- ****Natural law**** — A philosophical tradition holding that binding moral principles exist objectively in nature/reason, independent of positive (human-made) law.
- ****UDHR (1948)**** — Universal Declaration of Human Rights; non-binding UN General Assembly resolution establishing the first comprehensive international standard of rights.
- ****ICCPR / ICESCR (1966)**** — The two binding Covenants that codified the UDHR: Civil/Political Rights and Economic/Social/Cultural Rights; entered force 1976.
- ****Indivisibility**** — The principle that all human rights are interdependent and cannot be fully enjoyed in isolation from one another.
- ****Cultural relativism**** — The view that human rights are culturally contingent and should not be imposed universally where they conflict with local traditions or communal values.
- ****Jus cogens / Peremptory norms**** — Fundamental principles of international law (e.g., prohibition of genocide, torture, slavery) binding on all states, non-derogable, and invalidating conflicting treaties.
- ****Complementarity**** — ICC principle: the Court acts only when national legal systems are unwilling or unable genuinely to prosecute international crimes.
- ****Responsibility to Protect (R2P)**** — UN-endorsed doctrine (2005) that states have a duty to protect populations from atrocities; if they fail, the international community may intervene, including militarily.
- ****Treaty bodies**** — Committees of independent experts (e.g., Human Rights Committee, CEDAW Committee) established by each core treaty to monitor state compliance via reporting and complaints.

## Debates and open questions
1. **Hierarchy vs. Indivisibility:** Can a coherent hierarchy of rights (e.g., survival rights first) be established without undermining the legal and moral claim that all rights are equal and interdependent?
2. **Universalism vs. Relativism:** Is the universalist framework a form of Western imperialism, or does relativism primarily serve authoritarian elites? How to reconcile collective cultural rights (e.g., indigenous self-determination) with individual rights (e.g., gender equality)?
3. **Enforcement Gap:** Without a global legislature or police, the system relies on state consent, naming/shaming, and voluntary compliance. Can treaty bodies and regional courts overcome non-compliance by powerful states?
4. **Non-State Actors:** Multinational corporations often exceed host states in economic power but lack binding human rights obligations. Will the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (2011) evolve into a binding treaty?
5. **Climate Justice:** Does the new recognition of a right to a healthy environment create enforceable duties for high-emitting states toward vulnerable populations? How will courts quantify "fair share" obligations?
6. **Derogation Abuse:** States frequently declare emergencies to suspend rights. How to distinguish legitimate security measures from authoritarian consolidation under the guise of emergency powers?
7. **Sovereignty vs. Universal Jurisdiction:** The Pinochet precedent (1998) and ICC investigations challenge absolute sovereignty. Will major powers (US, China, Russia) ever accept universal jurisdiction over their nationals?
