# Human settlement

## Overview

Human settlements are places where people live and conduct social, economic, political, and cultural activities, ranging from hamlets and villages to cities and vast metropolitan regions. Created through human action rather than natural processes, they involve infrastructure construction and environmental modification, developing over long periods through cumulative individual actions. Settlement systems emerged late in human history—permanent communities arose independently in multiple regions roughly 10,000–5,000 years ago—and have since grown in scale and complexity, with industrialization accelerating urban growth and producing megacities. The study of settlements spans classification systems, spatial theories, and planning, making them central to understanding human organization and sustainability.

## Timeline

- **c. 300,000 years ago** — *Homo sapiens* appears; humans live in mobile hunter-gatherer groups
- **c. 10,000–5,000 years ago** — Permanent settlements emerge independently in multiple regions during the Pleistocene–Holocene transition
- **c. 7,000 years ago** — Permanent settlements established with increased population density and complex social systems
- **c. 5,000 years ago** — Cities with substantial populations emerge with formal governance and economic specialization
- **Medieval–early modern period** — Some cities reach populations in the hundreds of thousands
- **Early 20th century** — Patrick Geddes introduces "conurbation"; satellite cities develop via new town programs
- **1933** — Walter Christaller introduces central place theory
- **1945** — Harris and Ullman propose the multiple nuclei model
- **Mid-20th century** — Jean Gottmann popularizes "megalopolis"
- **Late 20th century** — Megacities emerge in large numbers, mostly in Asia and developing regions

## Body

### Definition and scope

A human settlement refers to spatial and operational arrangements created by humans to support life and organize activities across different scales. Settlements are defined by their origin in human action rather than natural processes, involving modification of surrounding environmental systems. They form over time through contributions of many individuals across generations, exist across multiple scales from individual dwellings to interconnected systems, and are typically associated with permanent or semi-permanent habitation and built infrastructure.

### Prehistoric origins and nomadic lifeways

For most of *Homo sapiens*' existence (appearing c. 300,000 years ago), humans lived in mobile social groups adapted to environmental variability. During the Pleistocene, social organization centered on hunting and gathering, requiring flexibility and movement. Key capacities—social cooperation, communication, and technological adaptation—developed gradually in these nomadic societies, forming behavioral and cognitive foundations that later supported sedentary lifeways.

### Emergence of permanent settlements

Permanent settlements developed during the Pleistocene–Holocene transition, arising independently in multiple regions between approximately 10,000–5,000 years ago. Early settlements were modest hamlets associated with gradual subsistence changes and increasing social coordination rather than abrupt transformation. By roughly 7,000 years ago, permanent settlements existed with increased population density, more permanent resource management, and more complex social and economic systems.

### Early cities and complex societies

Some settlements expanded in population and organizational complexity, producing early urban centers with structured social systems, coordination, resource management, and social differentiation. By approximately 5,000 years ago, cities with substantial populations emerged alongside formalized governance. Urbanization continued over subsequent millennia, with settlements reaching larger population thresholds and developing institutional and administrative systems, including centralized authority for maintaining social organization.

### Expansion, industrialization, and modern urban systems

Settlement scale and complexity accelerated in the modern era. By medieval and early modern periods, some cities reached populations in the hundreds of thousands. Industrialization increased productivity and enabled large-scale resource processing and transportation, supporting higher population densities and more complex settlement systems. The twentieth century saw megacity development, supported by infrastructure, transportation, and economic organization. These expansions brought greater environmental impacts, including higher resource demand and changes to Earth's physical and biological systems, contributing to conditions associated with the Anthropocene.

### Settlement types

**Urban areas** have relatively high population density, extensive built environments, and concentrated activity. The term derives from Latin *urbs* (physical structure), contrasted with *civitas* (social/political community). Statistical definitions emphasize population size, density, and built-up land continuity rather than legal status, distinguishing larger urbanized areas from smaller urban clusters. Satellite cities have partial local autonomy but depend economically on nearby metropolitan centers, influenced by Ebenezer Howard's garden city ideas. Urban sprawl is uncontrolled spread into rural land, linked to automobile-based development and associated with increased travel times, pollution, and countryside loss.

**Suburban areas** are residential zones on city outskirts, less dense than urban cores, with governance varying from independent municipalities to integrated metropolitan authorities. Suburban expansion accelerated in the 19th–20th centuries with rail, roads, and automobile dependence. Exurbs are low-density regions beyond suburbs but within metropolitan commuting zones. Peri-urban areas are transitional zones between urban centers and rural hinterlands, particularly in the Global South, often with limited planning and governance. Edge cities are decentralized employment, retail, and service centers outside traditional downtowns, linked by highways.

**Rural settlements** are non-urban habitations characterized by low population density, close-knit social structures, and reliance on local resources, closely associated with agriculture. They reflect long-term people–land relationships and are a major focus of planning and sustainable development policy.

**Temporary settlements** include non-permanent arrangements arising from disasters, displacement, conflict, migration, or personal choice. Refugee camps, though framed as provisional, often persist and grow into city-like settlements with complex internal economies and governance. Post-disaster housing progresses from emergency shelter through temporary housing to permanent reconstruction. Nomadic camps are shifting bases for hunting, fishing, and gathering, tied to seasonal ecological cycles.

### Spatial patterns

Nucleated (clustered) settlements concentrate buildings around a central point such as a crossroads or water source. Linear settlements develop along transport routes—roads, railways, or waterways—often at transfer points. Dispersed settlements spread dwellings across wide areas as individual farmsteads, common in Western Europe and North America, especially in upland regions. Isolated settlements consist of separate farmsteads, common in rural United States, Canada, Australia, and parts of Europe.

### Settlement hierarchy

A settlement hierarchy ranks settlements by size and service range, shown as a pyramid: numerous small settlements at the base, fewer larger ones toward the top. Categories include: homestead (most basic unit), hamlet (very small, lacking key institutions), village (roughly 500–2,500 inhabitants), town (larger than village, smaller than city), city (high density, non-rural activities), conurbation (merged towns/cities forming continuous built-up regions; term by Patrick Geddes), megacity (typically 5–10 million inhabitants), megalopolis (coalesced metropolitan areas; popularized by Jean Gottmann's "BosWash" study), and ecumenopolis (hypothetical global city; coined by Constantinos Doxiadis). Socio-political categories include municipalities (local government units), administrative seats (locations of governing offices), and national capitals (concentrated political/administrative functions, often growing faster due to institutional presence).

## Terms

- **Ekistics**: Science of human settlements introduced by Constantinos Doxiadis, encompassing all scales from dwelling to ecumenopolis, with five elements: nature, society, shells, networks, culture
- **Central place theory**: Christaller's 1933 theory explaining settlement distribution via threshold (minimum market size) and range (maximum travel distance for services)
- **Settlement scaling theory (SST)**: Framework treating settlements as spatially embedded social networks where characteristics scale systematically with population size
- **Conurbation**: Large urban area formed when expanding towns and cities merge into continuous built-up regions
- **Megalopolis**: Super-regional urban system formed by coalescence of multiple metropolitan areas
- **Ecumenopolis**: Hypothetical future global city integrating all major urban areas into one system
- **Peri-urban**: Transitional zones between urban centers and rural hinterlands, often with fragmented land use and limited governance
- **Path dependence**: Concept that earlier development patterns influence subsequent settlement growth

## Debates and open questions

Defining what constitutes an urban area remains complex, as legal or administrative boundaries often fail to correspond to actual population distribution—they may encompass sparsely populated land or exclude densely settled communities. The distinction between temporary and permanent settlement can become unclear, as "temporary" arrangements sometimes extend over long periods. Central place theory assumes simplified conditions (flat homogeneous landscape, even population distribution, equal transport costs, rational consumers) that limit its applicability to real-world complexity. Ekistics did not develop into a fully rigorous scientific discipline with explicit hypotheses, though its concepts influenced ecology, information technology in planning, and New Urbanism. Settlement abandonment results from interacting factors—economic conditions, living standards, demographic change, and adaptation capacity—with researchers arguing that failure factors are as important as success factors for understanding settlement patterns. Evolutionary approaches challenge equilibrium-based models, emphasizing continuous change, stochastic variation, and self-organization over stable end states.
