# Chicken

## Overview
The chicken (*Gallus gallus domesticus*) is the domesticated form of the red junglefowl, native to Southeast Asia, and is the most numerous bird species globally with a population exceeding 26.5 billion as of 2023. Domesticated roughly 8,000 years ago, likely triggered by the red junglefowl's attraction to rice cultivation, chickens spread worldwide via Austronesian maritime migrations and overland trade routes. Humans have since developed specialized breeds—broilers for rapid meat growth and layers for high egg yield—making chickens a central pillar of global animal husbandry. Beyond their economic role as providers of meat and eggs, chickens exhibit complex social structures, cognition, and vocalizations, featuring prominently in cultural folklore, religion, and scientific research as a primary model organism for embryology and genomics.

## Timeline
- **c. 8,000 years ago** — Genomic estimates for initial domestication of red junglefowl in Southeast Asia.
- **c. 3,250 years ago** — Earliest probable domestic chicken bones identified at Ban Non Wat, central Thailand, coinciding with rice cultivation.
- **c. 3000 BC** — Austronesian maritime migrations begin spreading chickens (along with dogs and pigs) from Taiwan across Island Southeast Asia and the Pacific.
- **c. 1400 BC** — Chickens reach Egypt via the Middle East, initially for cockfighting.
- **c. 300 BC** — Chickens become widely bred in Egypt.
- **7th century BC** — First depictions of chickens in Europe appear on Corinthian pottery.

- **2006** — Chicken becomes the first bird species to have its genome fully sequenced.
- **2018** — Global chicken population recorded at 23.7 billion.
- **2023** — Global chicken population exceeds 26.5 billion; over 50 billion produced annually.

## Body

### Phylogeny and Domestication
Chickens belong to the order Galliformes and are descended primarily from the red junglefowl (*Gallus gallus*), classified as the same species. They share 71–79% of their genome with the red junglefowl and freely interbreed with wild populations. Domestic chickens subsequently hybridized with grey junglefowl (contributing the gene for yellow skin), Sri Lankan junglefowl, and green junglefowl. Genomic studies indicate a single domestication event of red junglefowl in present-day Southeast Asia approximately 8,000 years ago. Archaeological re-examination identifies the earliest probable domestic bones at Ban Non Wat, Thailand, dated to ~3,250 years ago. Researchers propose that wild junglefowl were attracted to rice fields, nested nearby, and entered a commensal pathway to domestication, evidenced by chicken skeletons used as grave goods at the site.

### Global Dispersal
Chickens spread via two major vectors. Austronesian peoples carried chickens (*manuk* in Proto-Austronesian) throughout Island Southeast Asia, Micronesia, Melanesia, Polynesia, and Madagascar starting ~3000 BC from Taiwan, with disputed evidence for pre-Columbian introduction to South America via Polynesia. Overland, chickens reached the Middle East (Syria) by ~2000 BC and Egypt by 1400 BC, initially for cockfighting. Phoenicians spread them along Mediterranean coasts to Iberia. In Europe, chickens appear on 7th-century BC Corinthian pottery; during the Hellenistic period (4th–2nd centuries BC), they were widely domesticated for food in the southern Levant. Genetic sequencing of European archaeological bones reveals that during the High Middle Ages, chickens evolved reduced aggression and earlier seasonal egg-laying. Introduction to Africa occurred around the early first millennium AD via the Nile Valley, Indian Ocean trade, or trans-Saharan routes, with earliest remains in Mali, Nubia, and South Africa dating to the mid-first millennium AD.

### Biology and Behavior
Chickens are diurnal, relatively large birds with round bodies, unfeathered legs, and short wings; domestic breeds are generally too heavy for sustained flight. Size and coloration vary widely by breed. Adults possess a fleshy cranial crest (comb) and hanging facial skin flaps (wattles), more prominent in males. As omnivores, they scratch soil for seeds, insects, and small vertebrates. They live 5–10 years (record: 16 years). Chickens are gregarious, forming flocks with a linear dominance hierarchy—the "pecking order"—first described by Thorleif Schjelderup-Ebbe in 1921. Dominant individuals control access to food and nesting sites. They communicate via distinct vocalizations: males crow as a territorial signal; hens cluck after laying and to call chicks; specific alarm calls differentiate aerial versus ground predators. Reproduction involves a courtship dance, cloacal contact ("cloacal kiss") for sperm transfer, and a neuroendocrine system governed by Gonadotropin-Releasing Hormone-I neurons. Hens exhibit broodiness, incubating a clutch communally for ~21 days; high-altitude Tibetan chickens possess hemoglobin adaptations for hypoxic hatching. Inbreeding in commercial lines (e.g., White Leghorn, Langshan) causes depression in egg number and sexual maturity.

### Farming Systems and Welfare
Over 50 billion chickens are reared annually; the US alone slaughters >8 billion for meat and rears >300 million for eggs. The vast majority are raised in intensive factory farms (74% of poultry meat, 68% of eggs per Worldwatch Institute). **Broilers** (meat breeds) reach slaughter weight in <6 weeks (Ross 708: 1.8 kg at 35 days vs. 1.05 kg for heritage breeds). **Layer hens** (egg breeds) can produce >300 eggs/year (record: 371 in 364 days). After ~12 months, commercial laying declines; hens are slaughtered for processed meat or "soup hens," or in some regions **force-moulted** (feed withdrawal for 7–28 days to induce feather loss and reset egg production). In the US, >75% of flocks were force-moulted in 2003. Battery cage systems often result in feather loss, infirmity, and reduced lifespan (<2 years vs. ~7 years natural). Free-range and organic alternatives exist but take longer to reach market weight. Ethical consumerism debates center on environmental impact, human health risks, animal sentience, and resource efficiency.

### Cultural and Scientific Significance
Chickens were likely kept initially for cockfighting (evidenced in Indus Valley, 2500–2100 BC) before food use. They feature globally in folklore, religion (e.g., alectryomancy—divination via rooster sacrifice), literature (*One Hundred Years of Solitude*), and art. In science, the chicken is a premier model organism: embryos are easily accessible for developmental biology (yielding discoveries like the apical ectodermal ridge and zone of polarizing activity in limb development). The chicken was the first bird genome sequenced (1.21 Gb, 19,119 protein-coding genes). In 2006, activating the recessive *talpid2* gene induced tooth formation in embryos, confirming evolutionary links to toothed avian ancestors. Urban pet-keeping surged in the 2000s, valuing chickens for companionship, individual personalities, and egg production.

## Terms
- ****Broiler**** — A chicken breed specialized for rapid meat growth, reaching slaughter weight in under six weeks.
- ****Layer hen**** — A chicken breed specialized for high egg production, capable of laying over 300 eggs per year.
- ****Pecking order**** — A linear social dominance hierarchy in flocks, established by pecking, determining priority access to food and nesting sites.
- ****Cloacal kiss**** — The method of sperm transfer in birds via brief contact between the male and female cloacas.
- ****Broodiness**** — The behavioral state in which a hen ceases laying to incubate a clutch of eggs and rear hatchlings.
- ****Force-moulting**** — A management practice involving feed withdrawal (7–28 days) to induce feather loss and physiologically reset egg production in spent hens.
- ****Red junglefowl**** — The wild progenitor species (*Gallus gallus*) native to Southeast Asia, from which domestic chickens were primarily domesticated.
- ****Alectryomancy**** — A form of divination involving the sacrifice or observation of a sacred rooster, often in a ritual cockfight context.
- ****Talpid2**** — A recessive chicken gene; when activated, causes embryonic jaw tissue to initiate tooth formation, revealing atavistic traits.
- ****Capon**** — A castrated male chicken, historically produced for improved meat quality.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Domestication center and date:** While genomic data points to a single event ~8,000 years ago in Southeast Asia, archaeological evidence (Ban Non Wat) currently confirms domestication only ~3,250 years ago; the gap remains unresolved.
*   **Pre-Columbian Americas:** Blue-egged chickens and Chilean bones (Arauco Peninsula) suggest possible Polynesian introduction, but subsequent DNA studies cast doubt; the evidence is contested.
*   **Dispersal routes in Africa and Asia:** A lack of genetic data from Thailand, Russia, the Indian subcontinent, Southeast Asia, and Sub-Saharan Africa prevents a clear map of secondary dispersal and local breed formation.
*   **Welfare vs. efficiency:** The tension between intensive farming (resource efficiency, controlled environment) and extensive systems (animal welfare, environmental impact) drives ongoing ethical and policy debates regarding force-moulting, cage bans, and slaughter age.
