# Sun

## Overview

The Sun is the star at the center of the Solar System, a massive sphere of hot plasma heated by nuclear fusion in its core. It contains about 99.86% of the Solar System's mass and is the primary energy source for life on Earth, driving climate, weather, and photosynthesis. Formed approximately 4.6 billion years ago, it is a G-type main-sequence star (G2V) currently about halfway through its ~10–11 billion-year main-sequence lifetime, after which it will expand into a red giant and eventually cool into a white dwarf.

## Body

### General characteristics and composition

The Sun is a G2 star, with absolute magnitude +4.83, brighter than about 85% of Milky Way stars. It is a Population I (heavy-element-rich) star; its high abundance of heavy elements like gold and uranium suggests formation was triggered by shockwaves from nearby supernovae. The photosphere (visible surface) consists mostly of hydrogen (~73%) and helium (~25%), with heavier elements (oxygen ~1%, carbon 0.3%, neon 0.2%, iron 0.2%) making up less than 2%. The Sun's original composition was ~71.1% hydrogen, 27.4% helium, and 1.5% heavier elements. The interior composition is not precisely known; models based on nuclear reactions differ from solar seismology observations. Since formation, core helium has increased from ~24% to ~60% due to fusion, while the photosphere's helium fraction has decreased and metallicity is now 84% of its protostellar value.

### Structure

The Sun has no definite boundary; its radius is defined as the distance to the photosphere's edge. The core extends to 20–25% of the solar radius, with density up to 150 g/cm³ and temperature near 15.7 million K. Energy is produced via the proton–proton chain (99.2%) and CNO cycle (0.8%). The core fuses about 600 billion kg of hydrogen per second, converting 4.26 billion kg of matter into energy, producing 384.6 yottawatts. The fusion rate is in stable equilibrium: perturbations self-correct through expansion/contraction. The radiative zone (0.25–0.7 solar radii) transfers energy by radiation; photons take ~1 million years to cross it. The tachocline separates the radiative and convective zones, where a magnetic dynamo is hypothesized to generate the Sun's magnetic field. The convective zone (0.7 solar radii to surface) carries heat outward through thermal cells that appear as granulation on the surface. The Sun's oblateness is 8.2×10⁻⁶, making it the natural object closest to a perfect sphere ever observed.

### Atmosphere

The solar atmosphere comprises the photosphere, chromosphere, transition region, and corona. The photosphere is tens to hundreds of kilometers thick, with a spectrum approximating a black body at 5,777 K. The coolest layer (temperature minimum, ~4,100 K) sits ~500 km above the photosphere, allowing simple molecules like carbon monoxide and water. The chromosphere (~2,000 km thick) is visible as a colored flash during eclipses, with temperatures rising to ~20,000 K. The thin (~200 km) transition region sees temperatures jump to ~1,000,000 K, facilitated by full helium ionization. The corona reaches 1,000,000–2,000,000 K (up to 20,000,000 K in hottest regions); its heating mechanism is not fully explained, though magnetic reconnection contributes. The heliosphere extends where solar wind dominates the interstellar medium; Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause on 25 August 2012 at ~122 au.

### Rotation and magnetic activity

The Sun rotates faster at its equator (~25.6 days) than at its poles (~33.5 days), due to convective motion and the Coriolis force. The early Sun likely rotated up to ten times faster; magnetic braking slowed it, though the core still rotates about once per week. The 11-year solar cycle is the most prominent magnetic variation, with sunspots appearing as dark patches where convective heat transport is inhibited. Sunspots follow Spörer's law (forming closer to the equator as the cycle progresses) and Hale's law (polarity alternates each cycle). The 22-year Babcock–Leighton dynamo cycle involves oscillatory energy exchange between toroidal and poloidal magnetic fields. Solar flares and coronal mass ejections occur at sunspot groups. During the Maunder minimum (17th century), few sunspots were observed, coinciding with the Little Ice Age. Coronal heating is thought to involve either wave heating or magnetic heating via nanoflares; current research favors flare mechanisms.

### Life phases

The Sun formed from collapse of a giant molecular cloud, with a nearby supernova likely triggering collapse. It has expanded 15% in radius and increased luminosity 48% since entering the main sequence, because helium in the core has higher molecular weight than hydrogen, causing core contraction and increased fusion. It brightens by ~1% per 100 million years. In ~5 billion years, core hydrogen fusion will stop; the Sun will expand into a subgiant then red giant, engulfing Mercury and Venus. Earth's fate is uncertain—its orbit may expand to 1.5 au but could shrink due to tidal forces, possibly being engulfed ~7.59 billion years from now. The Sun will reach 256 times its current diameter at the red-giant tip, then undergo a helium flash, shrink, and eventually become an asymptotic-giant-branch star with thermal pulses. It will shed its outer layers as a planetary nebula, leaving a white dwarf containing ~54% of its present mass, which will fade over trillions of years to a hypothetical black dwarf.

### Observational history

Babylonian astronomers noted the Sun's non-uniform motion along the ecliptic. Anaxagoras reasoned it was a giant flaming metal ball. Eratosthenes' 3rd-century-BC distance estimate was correct to within a few percent. Copernicus developed the heliocentric model in the 16th century. Sunspot observations date to the Han dynasty; Galileo argued they were on the Sun's surface. Cassini determined the first accurate distance in 1684. Newton showed sunlight contains many colors (1666); Herschel discovered infrared (1800); Fraunhofer recorded 600+ absorption lines. Helium was hypothesized by Lockyer in 1868 and isolated on Earth 25 years later. Kelvin and Helmholtz's gravitational contraction model gave only 20 million years, contradicting geological evidence. Rutherford suggested radioactive decay (1904); Einstein's E=mc² provided the clue; Eddington proposed fusion (1920); Payne confirmed hydrogen's preponderance (1925); Bethe calculated fusion details in the 1930s. Space missions include Pioneer 6–9, Helios, Skylab, Solar Maximum Mission, Yohkoh, SOHO, Ulysses (polar regions), and Genesis (solar wind sample return).

## Terms

- **Photosphere**: The visible surface of the Sun, below which it becomes opaque to visible light
- **Main sequence**: The stable phase when a star fuses hydrogen into helium in its core
- **Solar wind**: Continuous outflow of charged particles from the Sun's corona
- **Sunspot**: Dark patch on the photosphere where magnetic fields inhibit heat transport
- **Tachocline**: Transition layer between radiative and convective zones where the solar dynamo is hypothesized to operate
- **Heliopause**: Boundary where the solar wind meets the interstellar medium
- **Astronomical unit (au)**: Unit of length defined as 149,597,870,700 m, originally the mean Earth–Sun distance
- **Proton–proton chain**: The primary fusion process converting hydrogen into helium in the Sun's core
- **Solar cycle**: Quasi-periodic ~11-year variation in sunspot number and magnetic activity
- **White dwarf**: Dense cooling star remnant that no longer produces energy by fusion

## Debates and open questions

- The Sun's interior composition is uncertain; stellar nuclear reaction models disagree with solar seismology observations.
- No complete theory explains coronal heating (why the corona is millions of kelvin while the photosphere is ~6,000 K); whether waves or magnetic reconnection dominates remains unclear.
- Earth's fate during the red-giant phase is uncertain because the Sun's total mass loss is uncertain; Earth may or may not be engulfed.
- The cultural reasons why some cultures picture the Sun as yellow or red are debated.
- Whether the Sun's passage through galactic spiral arms coincides with mass extinctions on Earth has been argued but not established.
