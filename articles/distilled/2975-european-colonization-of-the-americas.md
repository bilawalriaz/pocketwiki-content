# European colonization of the Americas

## Overview
The European colonization of the Americas was a centuries-long process beginning with Norse expeditions around 1000 AD and intensifying after Christopher Columbus's 1492 voyage. It involved multiple European powers establishing settlements and empires, leading to massive demographic, cultural, and political transformation. This period fundamentally shaped the modern Western Hemisphere through the displacement and decimation of Indigenous populations, the establishment of settler colonial states, and the creation of enduring social structures.

## Timeline
- **c. 1000 AD** — Norse settlement at L'Anse aux Meadows, Newfoundland.
- **1492** — Christopher Columbus's first voyage, initiating sustained European contact.
- **1494** — Treaty of Tordesillas divides the non-European world between Spain and Portugal.
- **1500** — Pedro Álvares Cabral claims Brazil for Portugal.
- **1519–1521** — Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire by Hernán Cortés.
- **1531–1535** — Spanish conquest of the Inca Empire by Francisco Pizarro.
- **1607** — Founding of Jamestown, the first permanent English settlement.
- **1620** — Pilgrims land at Plymouth Rock.
- **1624** — Dutch establish New Amsterdam (later New York).
- **1742** — Russian expedition under Vitus Bering explores Alaska.
- **1760** — British conquest of New France.
- **1867** — Russia sells Alaska to the United States.

## Body

### Early Exploration and Spanish & Portuguese Empires
Norse Vikings established the first known European settlements in North America around 1000 AD, but these were short-lived. Systematic colonization began in 1492 with Spain, motivated by a search for trade routes to Asia after Ottoman control of the Silk Road. The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) divided the globe between Spain and Portugal. Spain's empire grew rapidly through conquest, notably of the Aztec and Inca empires, which provided access to vast silver deposits like Potosí. Labor systems like the *encomienda* (granted colonists Indigenous labor and tribute) and later the *mit'a* (forced labor drafts) were established. Portugal colonized eastern South America (Brazil), initially focusing on trade before developing sugar plantations reliant on enslaved African labor.

### Other European Colonial Powers
By the 1530s, other nations sought wealth in the Americas. France established colonies in eastern North America (New France) and the Caribbean, with economies based on the fur trade and sugar. England, arriving later, founded settlements like Jamestown (1607) and Plymouth (1620), often driven by commercial ventures and religious dissent. The Dutch established New Netherland and trading posts in the Caribbean and Brazil. Russia expanded into the Pacific Northwest in the 18th century, primarily for the fur trade. These empires competed for territory and resources, leading to frequent conflicts.

### Impact on Indigenous Peoples
European colonization had catastrophic effects on Indigenous populations. Epidemics of diseases like smallpox, to which Native Americans had no immunity, caused a demographic collapse estimated at up to 95% of the pre-contact population. This was compounded by warfare, forced labor, enslavement, and displacement from ancestral lands. Colonial policies often included deliberate violence and cultural suppression, such as the destruction of religious sites and forced conversion to Christianity. The loss of life and social disruption facilitated European control over land and resources.

### Slavery and Labor Systems
The decline of Indigenous populations created a labor shortage, leading to the importation of millions of enslaved Africans, primarily to work on sugar, tobacco, and cotton plantations. The transatlantic slave trade involved an estimated 12 million Africans. In Spanish and Portuguese colonies, a racial caste system developed based on ancestry. In British North America, a sharp separation between colonists and Indigenous peoples persisted, and the economy of the southern colonies became heavily dependent on chattel slavery.

### Long-Term Institutional Legacies
The type of colonial policy and land ownership established during this period influenced long-term political and economic development. Economists argue that colonies with dense Indigenous populations (like Peru) developed coercive labor institutions, while those with sparser populations (like parts of North America) developed institutions more conducive to smallholder farming and, eventually, democratic governance. The Columbian Exchange—the transfer of plants, animals, culture, and diseases between hemispheres—permanently altered global ecology and societies.

## Terms
- **Encomienda**: A Spanish labor system granting colonists the right to demand tribute and forced labor from Indigenous people in exchange for protection and religious instruction.
- **Mit'a**: A system of forced labor drafts, used notably in the Spanish Empire, requiring Indigenous communities to provide workers for mines and public works.
- **Columbian Exchange**: The widespread transfer of plants, animals, culture, human populations, technology, and diseases between the Americas, West Africa, and the Old World following Columbus's voyages.
- **Settler Colonialism**: A form of colonialism where the primary goal is to establish a new society on the land, often involving the displacement or elimination of Indigenous populations.
- **Mestizo**: A term for a person of combined European and Indigenous ancestry.
- **Mulatto**: A term for a person of combined European and African ancestry.
- **Papal Bulls**: Official documents or decrees issued by the Pope; in this context, they were used to legitimize Spanish and Portuguese claims to newly discovered lands.
- **Viceroyalty**: A territory administered by a viceroy, a governor acting as the direct representative of the monarch, such as New Spain or Peru.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Pre-Columbian Population Estimates**: Scholars debate the size of the Indigenous population before 1492, with estimates ranging from 10 million to over 100 million, significantly affecting calculations of the scale of demographic collapse.
- **Causes of Indigenous Population Decline**: While epidemic disease is widely accepted as the primary cause, historians like Andrés Reséndez argue that slavery, overwork, and famine were also major, sometimes underappreciated, factors in the death toll.
- **Genocide Debate**: There is scholarly debate over whether certain colonial policies and actions constitute genocide. Some historians and genocide studies scholars argue that deliberate massacres, forced labor, and cultural destruction meet the definition, while others focus on the unintentional (though catastrophic) impact of disease.
- **Institutional Legacy**: Economists and historians continue to analyze how specific colonial policies regarding land, labor, and governance created path-dependent institutions that explain long-term differences in economic development and democracy across the Americas.
