# Hydrogen

## Overview
Hydrogen (symbol H, atomic number 1) is the lightest and most abundant chemical element in the universe, constituting roughly 75% of normal matter by mass. Under standard conditions it exists as dihydrogen (H₂), a colorless, odorless, highly flammable gas. Its simple atomic structure—one proton and one electron—made it the primary testbed for the development of quantum mechanics and atomic theory. On Earth, hydrogen is rarely found as a free gas; it is overwhelmingly bound in water, hydrocarbons, and organic compounds. Industrially, it is produced almost entirely from fossil fuels (primarily steam methane reforming) and serves as a critical feedstock for ammonia synthesis and petroleum refining, with emerging roles in decarbonization via fuel cells and "green hydrogen" from electrolysis.

## Timeline
- **1671** — Robert Boyle produces hydrogen gas by reacting iron filings with dilute acids.
- **1766–1781** — Henry Cavendish identifies hydrogen as a distinct substance ("inflammable air") and discovers it produces water when burned.
- **1783** — Antoine Lavoisier names the element "hydrogen" (water-former) and uses it to disprove phlogiston theory.
- **1806** — François Isaac de Rivaz builds the first internal combustion engine powered by hydrogen and oxygen.
- **1898** — James Dewar liquefies hydrogen using regenerative cooling and the vacuum flask; solidifies it in 1899.
- **1916** — Gilbert N. Lewis proposes the existence of the hydride anion (H⁻).
- **1931–1934** — Harold Urey discovers deuterium (²H); Urey's group discovers heavy water (1932); Rutherford, Oliphant, and Harteck prepare tritium (³H).
- **1937** — First hydrogen-cooled turbogenerator enters service in Dayton, Ohio.
- **1937** — Hindenburg disaster ends commercial hydrogen airship travel.
- **2025** — Less than 1% of global hydrogen production uses low-emissions technologies; vast majority derived from fossil fuels.

## Body

### Atomic Structure and Isotopes
Hydrogen's ground state electron energy is −13.6 eV. Its energy levels, defined by quantum number *n*, are split by spin interactions into hyperfine levels; high-precision calculations of these levels (involving Dirac eigenvalues, relativistic recoil, self-energy, and vacuum polarization) underpin physical constants. IUPAC nomenclature assigns **protium** (¹H, >99.98% abundance, one proton, no neutrons), **deuterium** (²H or D, one proton, one neutron, stable), and **tritium** (³H or T, one proton, two neutrons, radioactive, half-life 12.32 years). Deuterium forms heavy water (D₂O), used as a neutron moderator; tritium is used in fusion, radioluminescent lighting, and isotopic labeling. Unique among elements, hydrogen's isotopes retain distinct common names. Antihydrogen (antiproton + positron) and muonium (antimuon + electron) are antimatter analogues.

### Dihydrogen (H₂) Properties
H₂ exists as two nuclear spin isomers: **orthohydrogen** (parallel nuclear spins, triplet state *S*=1) and **parahydrogen** (antiparallel spins, singlet *S*=0). At room temperature, equilibrium favors 75% ortho / 25% para; ortho is higher in energy by 1.455 kJ/mol. Conversion to para is exothermic and must be catalyzed (e.g., ferric oxide) during liquefaction to prevent boil-off. H₂ liquefies at 20.28 K (−252.87 °C) and solidifies at 14.01 K (−259.14 °C), exhibiting solid Phases I–V. **Metallic hydrogen**, an electrical conductor, forms above ~400 GPa and is hypothesized to exist in giant planet interiors. Ionized hydrogen forms plasma, the state in stars. Combustion with oxygen releases 286 kJ/mol (141.9 MJ/kg); explosive limits in air are 4–74%; autoignition temperature is 500 °C. Flames are faint blue/UV, nearly invisible in daylight.

### Chemical Behavior
H₂ is relatively unreactive due to a strong H–H bond (435.7 kJ/mol). It forms **dihydrogen complexes** with metals (H–H bond intact but elongated), providing insight into catalytic activation. It reacts with halogens via radical chain reactions (initiation, propagation, termination). **Hydrogenation** (addition to unsaturated organics) requires catalysts (Pt, Ni) even when thermodynamically favorable. Hydrogen exhibits +1 and −1 oxidation states. The **hydron** (H⁺, often called a proton) is central to Brønsted–Lowry acid–base chemistry; in water it exists as hydronium (H₃O⁺) or larger clusters (e.g., H₉O₄⁺), determining pH. **Hydrides** (H⁻) form with metals: ionic/saline (alkali/alkaline earth), covalent (boranes, AlH₃), and metallic (transition metals). **Hydrogen bonding** (H attached to F, O, or N interacting with a lone pair on another electronegative atom) governs water's properties, protein folding, and DNA structure. The trihydrogen cation (H₃⁺) is abundant in the interstellar medium.

### Cosmic and Terrestrial Occurrence
Protons formed within the first second after the Big Bang; neutral hydrogen atoms formed ~370,000 years later during **recombination**. Neutral hydrogen (H I) dominates the early universe; stellar radiation reionizes it to H II, creating expanding bubbles during the **era of reionization**. The 21-cm line (1420 MHz) from hyperfine transition probes primordial hydrogen. Hydrogen powers stars via the proton-proton chain (lower mass) and CNO cycle (higher mass). On Earth, hydrogen is the third most abundant surface element, mostly in compounds. Atmospheric concentration is ~0.53 ppm (light weight permits escape). Large underground deposits have been found (Mali, France, Australia); economic extractability is uncertain as of 2024.

### Production and Storage
**Steam methane reforming (SMR)** dominates: CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂ at 1000–1400 K, high pressure (2.0 MPa). Emits 6.6–9.3 t CO₂ per t H₂ plus fugitive methane. Water-gas shift (CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂) recovers additional H₂. **Partial oxidation** of hydrocarbons and coal gasification are alternatives. **Water electrolysis** (green hydrogen if renewable-powered) uses Ni or Pt catalysts; more expensive than SMR without carbon capture. **Methane pyrolysis** (CH₄ → C + 2H₂) yields solid carbon but faces catalyst fouling. **Thermochemical cycles** (e.g., sulfur–iodine, copper–chlorine) split water using heat (solar/nuclear) without electricity. **Biohydrogen** from hydrogenase enzymes (Fe or Fe–Ni centers) occurs in microbes; some bacteria oxidize atmospheric H₂. **Serpentinization** (water oxidizing Fe²⁺ in olivine) generates H₂ geologically. Storage challenges: low volumetric density, embrittlement of metals, poor solubility. Compressed gas and cryogenic liquid (impractical for bulk due to low critical temp) are used; research focuses on reversible carriers (metal hydrides, ammonia borane, liquid organic hydrogen carriers like tetrahydroquinoline).

### Applications
**Petrochemicals:** Hydrodesulfurization and hydrocracking (hydrogenolysis) upgrade fossil fuels. **Hydrogenation:** Haber–Bosch process (N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃) consumes ~1–2% of global energy and is the largest H₂ use; ammonia enables synthetic fertilizers. Food industry: hydrogenation of unsaturated fats (margarine). Methanol from CO₂ hydrogenation (syngas). **Fuel:** Fuel cells emit only H₂O; combustion risks thermal NOₓ. "Green hydrogen" targets hard-to-electrify sectors: steel/cement/glass heat, ammonia/chemical feedstocks, shipping/aviation (via synthetic fuels). Light-duty vehicles lag behind batteries. Liquid H₂/LOX are cryogenic rocket propellants. **Other:** Nickel–hydrogen batteries (satellites, ISS, Hubble—19+ year life); semiconductor passivation (dangling bonds); n-type doping in ZnO; shielding gas (atomic hydrogen welding); generator coolant (high thermal conductivity); cryogenic research; food additive (E949); leak detection (tracer gas); neutron moderation (D₂O); fusion fuel (D, T); isotopic labeling (D, T); radioluminescent paint (T).

### Safety
Hydrogen embrittles metals (pipelines, vessels). Lighter than air, it disperses rapidly, but high-pressure leaks can autoignite via shock heating. Flammable at 4% in air; ignition source unidentified in ~70% of accidents. Flames are nearly invisible, causing contact burns. Non-toxic but asphyxiant in confined spaces.

## Terms
- ****Dihydrogen (H₂)**** — The diatomic molecular form of hydrogen under standard conditions; colorless, odorless, flammable gas.
- ****Protium (¹H)**** — The most common hydrogen isotope (one proton, zero neutrons); >99.98% natural abundance.
- ****Deuterium (²H, D)**** — Stable isotope with one proton and one neutron; used in heavy water, NMR solvents, fusion fuel.
- ****Tritium (³H, T)**** — Radioactive isotope (one proton, two neutrons); half-life 12.32 years; used in fusion, radiolabels, luminous paint.
- ****Ortho/Parahydrogen**** — Nuclear spin isomers of H₂: ortho (parallel spins, *S*=1, higher energy), para (antiparallel, *S*=0). Ratio is temperature-dependent; conversion is exothermic.
- ****Hydron (H⁺)**** — The hydrogen cation; in solution exists as hydronium (H₃O⁺) or larger clusters; central to acid–base chemistry and pH.
- ****Hydride (H⁻)**** — The hydrogen anion; forms ionic (saline), covalent, or metallic compounds with metals.
- ****Hydrogen bonding**** — Medium-strength noncovalent interaction between H (bound to F, O, N) and a lone pair on another electronegative atom; critical for biomolecular structure.
- ****Steam Methane Reforming (SMR)**** — Primary industrial H₂ production route: CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂ at high temperature/pressure; major CO₂ source.
- ****Green hydrogen**** — Hydrogen produced via water electrolysis powered by renewable energy, yielding near-zero lifecycle emissions.
- ****Metallic hydrogen**** — High-pressure phase (>400 GPa) where hydrogen becomes an electrical conductor; theorized to exist in giant planet cores.
- ****Trihydrogen cation (H₃⁺)**** — Protonated molecular hydrogen; abundant in interstellar medium and Jovian ionosphere; key to interstellar chemistry.

## Debates and open questions
- **Economic viability of natural hydrogen:** As of 2024, the extent to which discovered underground deposits (Mali, France, Australia) can be extracted economically remains uncertain.
- **Green hydrogen cost competitiveness:** Electrolysis remains more expensive than SMR without carbon capture; commercial viability depends on electrolyzer innovation and renewable electricity prices.
- **Methane pyrolysis scalability:** Mechanisms for continuous carbon removal and catalyst protection are unresolved obstacles for industrial deployment.
- **Hydrogen vs. batteries in transport:** For light-duty vehicles, hydrogen fuel cell adoption lags far behind battery electric vehicles; its future role in this sector is debated.
- **Storage density and carrier reversibility:** Metal hydrides offer low weight-percent H₂ (~1%); high-capacity carriers like ammonia borane (19.8 wt%) are often irreversible; reversible liquid organic carriers require energy-intensive dehydrogenation.
- **Climate impact of leakage:** Hydrogen is an indirect greenhouse gas (extends methane lifetime, affects ozone); leakage rates from future infrastructure are a significant uncertainty for net-zero scenarios.
