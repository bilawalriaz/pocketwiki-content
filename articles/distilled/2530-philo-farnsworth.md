# Philo Farnsworth

## Overview
Philo T. Farnsworth (1906–1971) was an American inventor who conceived and demonstrated the first fully electronic television system, a breakthrough that replaced mechanical scanning with electronic methods. His work, protected by key patents, laid the foundation for modern television and video technology. He later pioneered early nuclear fusion devices and held over 300 patents.

## Timeline
- **Aug 19, 1906** — Born in Manderfield, Utah.
- **Sep 7, 1927** — First successful transmission of an electronic television image (a straight line) in his San Francisco lab.
- **Aug 26, 1930** — Granted first U.S. patent for electronic television (Patent No. 1,773,980).
- **1934** — U.S. Patent Office rules in his favor in priority dispute with RCA over the image dissector.
- **1938** — Establishes Farnsworth Television and Radio Corporation in Fort Wayne, Indiana.
- **1939** — RCA agrees to pay royalties for Farnsworth's patents after a decade-long legal battle.
- **1951** — Farnsworth Television and Radio Corporation is purchased by ITT.
- **Late 1960s** — Develops the Farnsworth Fusor, a device for nuclear fusion.
- **Mar 11, 1971** — Dies in Holladay, Utah, at age 64.

## Body

### Early Life and Inspiration
Born into a Latter-day Saint family, Farnsworth moved to an electrified ranch in Idaho as a child, sparking his interest in electronics. He excelled in science and, while in high school, sketched plans for an electronic television system for his teacher, Justin Tolman. After his father's death, he briefly attended the U.S. Naval Academy but left to pursue his television ideas, fearing the government would own his patents. He later attended Brigham Young University and began radio repair work.

### Invention of Electronic Television
Funded by philanthropists, Farnsworth moved to California to develop his ideas. He recognized the limitations of existing mechanical television systems that used spinning "Nipkow disks." His key innovation was the **image dissector**, a camera tube that scanned images electronically. On September 7, 1927, he transmitted the first image. By 1929, his system had no mechanical parts. He demonstrated it publicly in 1928 and at the Franklin Institute in 1934.

### Patent Battles with RCA
In 1930, RCA hired Vladimir K. Zworykin to lead its television development. After Zworykin visited Farnsworth's lab, RCA filed an interference suit claiming Zworykin's 1923 patent had priority. Farnsworth prevailed in 1934 when the U.S. Patent Office awarded him priority for the image dissector. After losing an appeal, RCA eventually agreed in 1939 to pay Farnsworth royalties, clearing the way for commercial television.

### Later Career and Fusion Research
Farnsworth worked with various companies, including Philco and later ITT, where he developed radar and other electronic systems. He also pursued nuclear fusion, creating the **Farnsworth Fusor**, a device that uses inertial electrostatic confinement. While not a practical power source, it demonstrated fusion and is used as a neutron source. His fusion research company, PTFA, ultimately failed due to lack of funding.

## Terms
- **Image Dissector:** Farnsworth's camera tube that converted an image into an electrical signal by scanning it with electrons.
- **Nipkow Disk:** A mechanical scanning device with spiral holes used in early electromechanical television systems.
- **Inertial Electrostatic Confinement (IEC):** A method for achieving nuclear fusion using electric fields to confine ions, as used in the Farnsworth Fusor.
- **Fusor:** A device designed by Farnsworth to create nuclear fusion by injecting high-temperature ions into a chamber.
- **Iconoscope:** A later, more practical camera tube developed by Vladimir Zworykin that incorporated a charge-storage mosaic.
- **Rasterizer:** A device that scans an image in a series of lines to produce an electrical signal.
- **Patent Interference:** A legal proceeding in the U.S. Patent Office to determine which inventor has priority for an invention.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary historical debate concerns the priority of invention between Farnsworth and Vladimir Zworykin/RCA. The U.S. Patent Office ruled in Farnsworth's favor in 1934, but the dispute fueled decades of litigation and public controversy. Scholars continue to examine the extent of Zworykin's independent work versus his exposure to Farnsworth's designs.
