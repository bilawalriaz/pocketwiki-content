# Paul Gauguin

## Overview
Paul Gauguin (1848–1903) was a French Post-Impressionist and Symbolist painter, sculptor, printmaker, and writer whose experimental use of color and Synthetist style profoundly influenced modern art, including Picasso and Matisse. A former stockbroker who turned to art full-time after the 1882 market crash, Gauguin rejected Western convention and Impressionist naturalism, seeking "primitive" authenticity in Brittany, Martinique, Tahiti, and the Marquesas Islands. His work fused flat, non-naturalistic color planes with Symbolist themes, exploring spirituality, mythology, and the perceived harmony of indigenous life. While celebrated posthumously for formal innovations that catalyzed Primitivism and early Modernism, his legacy is deeply contested due to his colonial privilege, sexual relationships with adolescent Tahitian girls, and the ethnographic fabrication in his travelogue *Noa Noa*.

## Body

### Family Background and Formative Years
Gauguin’s lineage combined French journalism and Spanish-Peruvian aristocracy. His father, Clovis, a liberal journalist, died of a heart attack en route to Peru in 1850. His mother, Aline Chazal, was the daughter of André Chazal (an engraver who shot his wife) and Flora Tristan, a socialist writer and activist of Spanish-Peruvian descent. Gauguin spent ages 18 months to six in Lima, enjoying elite status under his great-uncle’s protection until a 1854 coup forced their return to France in poverty. This lost paradise haunted his imagination, later fueling his quest for an unspoiled tropical idyll. He attended Catholic boarding school and a naval preparatory academy, then served in the merchant marine and navy (1865–1871), circumnavigating the globe before settling in Paris.

### Stockbroker, Marriage, and Artistic Awakening
In 1871, family friend Gustave Arosa secured Gauguin a position at the Paris Bourse. He became a competent businessman, earning 30,000 francs annually by 1879, supplemented by art dealing. He married Danish Mette-Sophie Gad in 1873; they had five children. Gauguin painted Sundays with Camille Pissarro, who mentored him and introduced him to Impressionist circles. He exhibited at the 1879, 1881, and 1882 Impressionist exhibitions, though reviews were dismissive. The 1882 stock market crash collapsed his income and the art market; dealer Paul Durand-Ruel stopped buying. Over two years, painting with Pissarro and Cézanne, Gauguin resolved to become a professional artist.

### Brittany, Martinique, and the Break with Impressionism
After a failed attempt to live cheaply in Rouen (1883–84) and a disastrous stint as a tarpaulin salesman in Copenhagen (1884–85), Gauguin returned to Paris alone in 1885. He exhibited 19 works at the final Impressionist exhibition (1886), rejecting Georges Seurat’s Pointillism and breaking with Pissarro. Summer 1886 at Pont-Aven, Brittany, proved pivotal: attracted by low costs, he became a charismatic leader of the student colony. Influenced by Randolph Caldecott’s naive illustrations and Japanese prints, he simplified forms into flat color areas with bold outlines—Cloisonnism. In 1887, desperate for a tropical refuge, he sailed with Charles Laval to Panama, laboring on the canal in misery, then spent June–November in Martinique. There he produced brightly colored figural scenes, incorporated Indian immigrant motifs, and confirmed his direction toward exotic subject matter.

### Arles, Van Gogh, and Symbolist Maturity
Theo van Gogh, impressed by Gauguin’s Martinique paintings, bought three works and promoted him at Goupil & Cie. Vincent van Gogh admired Gauguin intensely; at Theo’s urging, Gauguin joined him in Arles for nine weeks in late 1888. Their collaboration collapsed: on 23 December, Van Gogh allegedly threatened Gauguin with a razor, then severed his own ear. Gauguin fled, never seeing Vincent again, though they corresponded. The trauma informed Gauguin’s 1889 self-portrait *Jug in the Form of a Head*. Meanwhile, Edgar Degas remained Gauguin’s staunchest supporter, buying work, organizing his 1893 Durand-Ruel show, and influencing his monotypes and figure compositions.

### First Tahitian Sojourn (1891–1893)
Funded by a successful 1891 Hôtel Drouot auction (aided by critic Octave Mirbeau), Gauguin sailed for Tahiti on 1 April 1891, vowing to escape European artifice. Disappointed by colonial Papeete, he moved 45 km to Mataiea, living in a bamboo hut. He studied Jacques-Antoine Moerenhout’s 1837 ethnography of Tahiti’s extinct Arioi society and god ’Oro, inventing a visual mythology for a culture he found already erased. Key works: *Ia Orana Maria* (Christian nativity transposed to Tahiti), *Te aa no areois* (The Seed of the Areoi), and *Spirit of the Dead Watching* (featuring his 13-year-old *vahine*, Teha'amana, called Tehura in the travelogue, pregnant by him by late 1892). He carved wood reliefs, kept an illustrated notebook (*Ancien Culte Mahorie*), and wrote *Noa Noa* (published 1901), later criticized as fantasy and plagiarism. He sent nine paintings to Paris; exhibited with Van Gogh in Copenhagen; returned to France in August 1893, broke and suffering heart ailments (possibly early syphilis).

### Interlude in France (1893–1895)
Gauguin staged a Durand-Ruel exhibition (Nov 1894), selling 11 of 40 works at high prices. He adopted a Polynesian persona, conducted a salon, and pursued woodcuts for *Noa Noa*. Durand-Ruel’s patronage ended mysteriously. A 1895 Hôtel Drouot auction failed; Ambroise Vollard showed his work but terms weren’t agreed. Gauguin submitted the ceramic *Oviri* to the Société Nationale des Beaux-Arts 1895 salon; its reception was contested (expelled per Charles Morice; admitted under pressure per Vollard). His marriage to Mette ended definitively after quarrels over a 13,000-franc inheritance. By mid-1895, destitute, he accepted a cheap passage back to Tahiti arranged by Eugène Carrière, never to return to Europe.

### Second Tahitian Period (1895–1901)
Gauguin settled near Papeete at Puna'auia, building a substantial house and studio. He lived relatively comfortably on sales via Georges Chaudet and later Vollard (from 1899: 300 francs/month advance for 25 paintings/year at 200 francs each, plus materials). He contributed to the satirical *Les Guêpes* and eventually edited it from February 1900 until September 1901, attacking the colonial governor and missionary schools, though not advocating native rights. He fathered children with Pau'ura (from age 14½) and later Vaeoho (age 14). Health deteriorated: a 1894 ankle fracture (drunken brawl in Concarneau) never healed; chronic leg sores (diagnosed as eczema, possibly syphilis) required arsenic treatment. In April 1897, learning of daughter Aline’s death and facing eviction and debt, he painted *Where Do We Come From? What Are We? Where Are We Going?*, his declared masterpiece, then attempted suicide after finishing it. The 1898 Vollard exhibition (including this canvas) was a critical success. By 1901, unable to work ceramics (no clay) or woodcut (no press), he turned to monotypes and oil transfer drawings, preparing for the Marquesas.

### Marquesas Islands (1901–1903)
Gauguin arrived at Atuona, Hiva Oa, 16 September 1901. The population had plummeted from ~80,000 (18th c.) to 4,000 due to Western diseases; Catholic missionaries controlled education; French gendarmerie enforced rule. He bought land from the Catholic mission, ingratiating himself with Bishop Martin, then built *Maison du Jouir*, decorated with pornographic photos and satirical sculptures (*Père Paillard* lampooning the bishop; *Thérèse* the mistress). He exploited an 1901 Associations Bill loophole—school attendance compulsory only within 2.5 miles—to "rescue" teenage girls from missionary schools, taking 14-year-old Vaeoho as *vahine*. He painted *Jeune fille à l'éventail*, *Le Sorcier d'Hiva Oa*, and *Contes barbares*, noted for melancholy repose. Feuds escalated: Governor Petit refused his tax protests; gendarme Claverie fined him for libel (500 francs, three months’ prison, March 1903). Gauguin appealed, raising funds to travel to Papeete. Health collapsed (morphine, laudanum, failing sight). He completed the memoir *Avant et après* (published 1918). On 8 May 1903, he died suddenly; neighbor Tioka confirmed death by traditional scalp-biting. An empty laudanum bottle suggested overdose; pastor Vernier cited heart attack.

## Terms
- **Post-Impressionism**: Late 1880s–early 1900s French art movements (including Gauguin, Van Gogh, Cézanne, Seurat) extending Impressionism while rejecting its naturalism, emphasizing structure, symbolism, and subjective expression.
- **Synthetism**: Gauguin/Bernard style (from 1888) where form and color have equal, interdependent roles; subjects are synthesized from memory and imagination rather than observed directly.
- **Cloisonnism**: Style named by critic Édouard Dujardin (1888) for Émile Bernard’s method—flat color zones separated by heavy dark outlines, resembling medieval cloisonné enamel; adopted by Gauguin in *The Yellow Christ* (1889).
- **Primitivism**: Modern art trend valorizing non-Western, prehistoric, or folk art as more authentic, vital, and spiritual than academic tradition; Gauguin was its first systematic practitioner in painting/sculpture.
- **Vahine**: Tahitian word for "woman"; used by Gauguin to denote his adolescent Polynesian companions/wives (Teha'amana, Pau'ura, Vaeoho), contracted under local custom.
- **Noa Noa**: Gauguin’s 1901 travelogue/memoir of his first Tahiti stay, blending observation, fantasy, and plagiarism; illustrated with woodcuts; title means "fragrant" in Tahitian.
- **Oviri**: Tahitian for "savage"; Gauguin’s 1894 ceramic sculpture of a goddess of life/death, phallic and gruesome, intended for his grave; exhibited 1906, directly influenced Picasso’s *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon*.
- **Peinture à l'essence**: Technique draining oil from paint, mixing pigment with turpentine for a matte, absorbent surface; used by Gauguin, Degas, Toulouse-Lautrec.
- **Monotype/Oil Transfer**: Unique print made by drawing on inked surface (monotype) or transferring oil-based drawings through pressure (oil transfer); Gauguin’s late graphic innovations, yielding ghostly, layered images.
- **Pont-Aven School**: Informal group (Gauguin, Bernard, Laval, Schuffenecker, et al.) working in Brittany 1886–1890s, developing Cloisonnism/Synthetism through naive simplification and Symbolist content.

## Debates and open questions
- **Syphilis diagnosis**: Long assumed (cardiovascular syphilis per Mathews); 2014 forensic analysis of probable Gauguin teeth found no traces of mercury, arsenic, or cadmium (standard treatments), challenging the consensus.
- **Authenticity of *Noa Noa***: Critics (Thomson, Goddard) argue it fantasizes and plagiarizes (e.g., Moerenhout); Prideaux (2025) defends its ethnographic value.
- **Colonial complicity vs. critique**: Gauguin attacked colonial officials and missionary schools in *Les Guêpes*, yet exploited colonial structures (repatriation policy, mission land, adolescent *vahines*). Prideaux (2025) frames him as anti-colonial defender; Solomon-Godeau (1992) and others condemn him as "serial rapist" and "sex tourist" enacting patriarchal fantasy.
- **Agency of Tahitian/ Marquesan subjects**: Were Teha'amana, Pau'ura, Vaeoho willing participants in local custom (*vahine* marriage), or victims of power imbalance? Gauguin’s own writings ("She asks to be raped... beating every week makes her obey") contradict Prideaux’s equity claim.
- **Extent of influence on Picasso**: Richardson and Sweetman argue 1906 Gauguin retrospective (especially *Oviri*) was decisive for *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon*; Picasso later downplayed the debt.
- **Dating and location of late paintings**: Marquesas works (1901–03) often indistinguishable from Tahiti works; experts rely on subtle tonal shifts (Szech: "repose and melancholy") and provenance.
