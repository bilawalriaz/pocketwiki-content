# Bean

## Overview
A bean is the edible seed of various leguminous plants (family Fabaceae) consumed as a vegetable or pulse. As legumes, they fix atmospheric nitrogen into the soil, reducing fertilizer needs, and produce seeds high in protein (21–36.5% by weight). Cultivated since the seventh millennium BCE in Thailand and the second millennium BCE in the Americas, Europe, and Africa, beans are now a global staple produced in millions of tons annually, with India as the leading producer. While nutritionally vital, many raw beans contain toxins (phytohaemagglutinin) and indigestible oligosaccharides requiring thorough cooking to ensure safety and digestibility.

## Timeline
- **7th millennium BCE** — Early cultivated beans grown in Thailand, predating ceramics.
- **2nd millennium BCE** — Large-seeded broad beans appear in Aegean, Iberia, and transalpine Europe; cowpeas domesticated in sub-Saharan Africa; oldest domesticated beans in Americas found in Guitarrero Cave, Peru.
- **8th century BCE** — Beans and chickpeas mentioned in Homer's *Iliad*.
- **Pre-Columbian era** — Five *Phaseolus* species domesticated in Americas; "Three Sisters" companion planting (beans, maize, squash) practiced across North and South America.
- **1492 onward** — Columbian Exchange introduces *Phaseolus* beans (common, lima, runner) to Europe; word "bean" extended to New World species.
- **Modern era** — Development of commercial "bush bean" varieties that do not require support and mature simultaneously.
- **2023** — Svalbard Global Seed Vault holds >40,000 *Phaseolus* accessions for conservation.

## Body

### Etymology and Naming
The word "bean" and its Germanic cognates (e.g., German *Bohne*) existed in West Germanic languages before the 12th century, originally referring to broad beans, chickpeas, and other Old World pod-borne seeds. Following the Columbian Exchange, the term was extended to New World genera *Phaseolus* (common bean, runner bean) and *Vigna*, and later applied broadly to similar seeds like soybeans, lupins, and unrelated "beans" such as coffee and vanilla. This article covers only leguminous beans.

### History and Domestication
**Old World:** Beans were cultivated in Thailand by the early seventh millennium BCE and deposited in ancient Egyptian tombs. Large-seeded broad beans reached Europe by the second millennium BCE. Cowpeas (including black-eyed peas) were domesticated in sub-Saharan Africa during the same period.
**New World:** Genetic analysis shows the common bean (*Phaseolus vulgaris*) originated in Mesoamerica and spread southward. The oldest American domesticated beans date to the second millennium BCE in Peru. Pre-Columbian peoples domesticated five *Phaseolus* species, selecting for non-shattering pods (pods that do not scatter seeds when ripe). Cultivation ranged from Chile to the northern United States, including the "Three Sisters" intercropping system where beans fixed nitrogen for maize and used corn stalks as support. Christopher Columbus was the first European to encounter *Phaseolus* beans in the Americas.

### Diversity and Conservation
Beans span many genera native to different regions. Modern plant breeding threatens biodiversity by favoring a few high-yield varieties. Conservation efforts focus on preserving germplasm of older cultivars; as of 2023, the Svalbard Global Seed Vault holds over 40,000 *Phaseolus* accessions.

### Cultivation
**Agronomy:** Most beans are summer crops requiring warm temperatures (peas are the exception). Maturity takes 55–60 days. As legumes, they fix nitrogen via root nodules, needing less fertilizer. Many varieties are vines requiring support (poles, cages, or corn stalks in traditional intercropping); modern "bush beans" are self-supporting and determinate (pods mature simultaneously for mechanical harvest).
**Production:** FAO categorizes legume production into pulses (dry mature seeds, excluding soy/groundnuts), oil crops (soybeans, groundnuts), and fresh vegetables (immature pods). India leads global dry bean (*Phaseolus* spp.) production, followed by Myanmar and Brazil; Tanzania is the leading African producer.

### Uses
**Culinary:** Dry beans are soaked and boiled for use in salads, soups, stews (e.g., chili con carne, French cassoulet), mashes, and baked dishes. Soybeans are processed into tofu (bean curd) or fermented into tempeh. Immature pods are eaten as green beans or edamame; sprouted beans yield beansprouts. Dry beans contain 21–25% protein; dry soybeans 36.5%.
**Industrial:** Guar beans provide guar gum (a galactomannan polysaccharide) used as a thickener and stabilizer in food and industrial products.

### Health Concerns
**Toxins:** Many raw beans contain phytohaemagglutinin, a lectin toxin. Red kidney beans are especially hazardous; as few as 4–5 raw beans can cause severe gastroenteritis. Toxin is destroyed by boiling ≥10 minutes; slow cooking is dangerous as it softens beans without reaching temperatures sufficient to denature the toxin. Canned beans are safe (pre-cooked). Fermentation (used in Africa) also reduces toxins. Bean poisoning is likely underdiagnosed.
**Antinutrients:** Beans contain phytic acid, which inhibits mineral absorption and interferes with vitamin D metabolism.
**Flatulence:** Beans contain oligosaccharides (raffinose, stachyose) that humans cannot digest due to lacking alpha-galactosidase. Gut bacteria ferment these, producing methane and flatulence. Cultural remedies include epazote (Mexico), asafoetida (India), savory (Germany), cumin (Middle East), and kombu (Japan). Commercial alpha-galactosidase (from *Aspergillus niger*) is effective.
**Beansprouts:** Sprouted beans (often mung) carry high risk of bacterial contamination (Salmonella, Listeria, E. coli) if eaten raw or lightly cooked, causing outbreaks with mortality.

### In Human Society
Beans have historically been a "food of the poor," providing protein for those unable to afford meat, leading to what historian Ken Albala terms a "class-based antagonism" in Europe. The flatulence association is universal, inspiring cultural remedies and the children's song "Beans, Beans, the Musical Fruit." The Mexican jumping bean is a novelty item: a seed pod segment containing a moth larva (*Cydia saltitans*) that jumps when warmed, possibly aiding the larva in finding shade.

## Terms
- ****Fabaceae**** — The legume family; plants that bear fruit as pods and typically fix nitrogen via root nodules.
- ****Pulse**** — The dried, mature seed of a leguminous plant used for food (excludes oilseeds like soy and fresh green beans).
- ****Phaseolus**** — A genus of beans native to the Americas, including the common bean (*P. vulgaris*), lima (*P. lunatus*), and runner bean (*P. coccineus*).
- ****Nitrogen fixation**** — Process by which legumes host rhizobia bacteria in root nodules to convert atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia, enriching soil.
- ****Phytohaemagglutinin**** — A toxic lectin found in many raw beans (especially red kidney beans) destroyed by thorough boiling.
- ****Oligosaccharides**** — Complex sugars (raffinose, stachyose) in beans indigestible by humans, causing flatulence via bacterial fermentation in the colon.
- ****Alpha-galactosidase**** — Enzyme (commercially from *Aspergillus niger*) that breaks down oligosaccharides, reducing flatulence.
- ****Three Sisters**** — Pre-Columbian companion planting system interplanting beans (nitrogen fixer), maize (support), and squash (ground cover).
- ****Non-shattering pods**** — Domesticated trait where seed pods do not split open at maturity, allowing seed retention for harvest.
- ****Guar gum**** — Galactomannan polysaccharide from guar beans used as a thickener and stabilizer.

## Debates and open questions
- **Toxin awareness:** Medical recognition of bean poisoning (beyond red kidney beans) remains limited; UK National Poisons Information Service did not flag non-red bean dangers as of 2008, suggesting widespread underreporting and misdiagnosis.
- **Biodiversity loss:** Modern breeding narrows the genetic base; conservation of heirloom germplasm (e.g., Svalbard vault) is a race against cultivar extinction.
- **Sprout safety:** Balancing the nutritional appeal of raw/lightly cooked beansprouts against persistent, sometimes fatal, bacterial contamination outbreaks.
