# Lê dynasty

## Overview
The Lê dynasty (1428–1789) was the longest-ruling Vietnamese dynasty, governing Đại Việt for over 360 years. It is divided into two periods: the Initial Lê (1428–1527), when emperors ruled directly, and the Revival Lê (1533–1789), when emperors were figureheads under the control of the Trịnh lords. The dynasty's era saw the transformation of Vietnam into a Confucian state, major southward expansion, and prolonged civil wars that ultimately led to its downfall.

## Timeline
- **1418–1427** — Lê Lợi leads the Lam Sơn uprising against Ming Chinese rule.
- **1428** — Lê Lợi establishes the Lê dynasty after expelling the Ming.
- **1460–1497** — Reign of Lê Thánh Tông, the dynasty's golden age of reform and expansion.
- **1471** — Lê Thánh Tông conquers the Cham capital Vijaya, accelerating southward expansion.
- **1527** — Mạc Đăng Dung usurps the throne, ending the Initial Lê period.
- **1533** — Lê dynasty is restored, but real power soon passes to the Trịnh lords.
- **1533–1592** — Lê–Mạc War between the restored Lê and the Mạc dynasty for legitimacy.
- **1627–1672** — First series of Trịnh–Nguyễn Wars, partitioning Vietnam.
- **1771** — Tây Sơn rebellion begins, challenging both the Trịnh and Nguyễn lords.
- **1789** — Tây Sơn forces defeat the Qing army, ending the Lê dynasty.

## Body

### Founding and Golden Age (1428–1497)
The dynasty began when Lê Lợi (Emperor Lê Thái Tổ) liberated Vietnam from 20 years of Ming rule. He introduced the Thuận Thiên legal code and land reforms. His successor, Lê Thái Tông, consolidated power but died suddenly, leading to a period of regency and court intrigue. The dynasty reached its zenith under Lê Thánh Tông (r. 1460–1497). He implemented sweeping Confucian reforms modeled on China, including a civil service examination system, a six-ministry government, and the compilation of the national history, *Đại Việt sử ký toàn thư*. His reign also saw major military campaigns, most notably the 1471 conquest of Champa, which dramatically expanded Vietnamese territory southward.

### Decline and Usurpation (1497–1533)
After Lê Thánh Tông's death, the dynasty rapidly declined under a succession of weak or tyrannical rulers. Corruption and court purges weakened central authority. The military leader Mạc Đăng Dung rose to power, eventually usurping the throne in 1527 and establishing the Mạc dynasty. This usurpation triggered a civil war, as loyalist families like the Trịnh and Nguyễn rallied to restore the Lê emperor.

### The Revival Lê and Civil Wars (1533–1789)
The Lê dynasty was restored in 1533, but the emperors were mere puppets. Real power was held by the Trịnh lords in the north, who fought the Mạc dynasty until 1677. From the 17th century, Vietnam was effectively partitioned between the Trịnh lords in the north and the Nguyễn lords in the south, separated by the Gianh River. They fought a series of Trịnh–Nguyễn Wars (1627–1672) that ended in stalemate. This division fostered distinct northern and southern cultures and dialects. In the late 18th century, peasant revolts, exacerbated by heavy taxation and land concentration, culminated in the Tây Sơn rebellion (1771). The Tây Sơn brothers first defeated the Nguyễn, then the Trịnh, and finally repelled a Qing Chinese invasion in 1789, ending the Lê dynasty.

### Culture, Society, and Foreign Relations
The Lê period solidified Confucianism as the state ideology, replacing earlier Buddhist influences. The civil service examination system became the primary path to officialdom. Vietnamese literature flourished in both chữ Hán (Chinese characters) and chữ Nôm (a demotic script). European contact began in the 16th century, with Portuguese traders and Jesuit missionaries like Alexandre de Rhodes, who developed the romanized chữ Quốc ngữ writing system. The dynasty maintained a tributary relationship with Ming and later Qing China. Economically, the period saw growth in handicrafts and maritime trade, with major ports at Đông Kinh (Hanoi) and Hội An.

## Terms
- **Đại Việt**: The historical name for Vietnam used during the Lê dynasty.
- **Confucianism**: A Chinese philosophy emphasizing social harmony, filial piety, and rule by morally upright scholar-officials, which became the state ideology under Lê Thánh Tông.
- **Civil Service Examination System**: A competitive imperial exam system modeled on China's, used to select government officials based on knowledge of Confucian classics.
- **Trịnh Lords**: The family that held de facto power in northern Vietnam during the Revival Lê period, ruling in the name of the Lê emperor.
- **Nguyễn Lords**: The family that ruled southern Vietnam (Đàng Trong) during the Revival Lê period, often in conflict with the Trịnh.
- **Tây Sơn Rebellion**: A major peasant uprising (1771–1802) led by three brothers that overthrew both the Trịnh and Nguyễn lords and ended the Lê dynasty.
- **Chữ Quốc ngữ**: The romanized script for writing Vietnamese, developed by European missionaries in the 17th century.
- **Nam tiến**: The historical process of Vietnamese southward expansion, which accelerated dramatically under the Lê dynasty.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact causes and nature of Lê Thánh Tông's policies toward captured Chinese sailors and Malaccan envoys, including forced castration, are debated. Some historians suggest these individuals were involved in illegal trade rather than being accidental castaways.
- The degree to which the Revival Lê emperors retained any symbolic or cultural authority, versus being entirely powerless under the Trịnh, is a point of scholarly nuance.
- The long-term impact of the Trịnh–Nguyễn partition on the development of distinct northern and southern Vietnamese identities remains a subject of historical analysis.
