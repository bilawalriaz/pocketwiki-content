# Rosa Parks

## Overview
Rosa Parks (1913–2005) was an American civil rights activist whose 1955 refusal to give up her bus seat in Montgomery, Alabama, sparked the Montgomery Bus Boycott, a pivotal event in the civil rights movement. Often called the "mother of the civil rights movement," her act of defiance was not an isolated incident but the culmination of years of activism against racial injustice and segregation.

## Timeline
- **February 4, 1913** — Born Rosa Louise McCauley in Tuskegee, Alabama.
- **December 18, 1932** — Marries Raymond Parks in Montgomery, Alabama.
- **December 1943** — Elected secretary of the Montgomery chapter of the NAACP.
- **1943–1945** — Successfully registers to vote after three attempts.
- **Summer 1955** — Attends the Highlander Folk School in Tennessee for activist training.
- **December 1, 1955** — Arrested in Montgomery for refusing to give up her bus seat.
- **December 5, 1955** — One-day bus boycott begins on the day of Parks's trial.
- **December 20, 1956** — Supreme Court ruling in *Browder v. Gayle* ends the 381-day Montgomery Bus Boycott.
- **1957** — Moves to Detroit, Michigan, due to financial hardship and threats.
- **1965** — Participates in the Selma to Montgomery marches.
- **1987** — Co-founds the Rosa and Raymond Parks Institute for Self Development.
- **October 24, 2005** — Dies of natural causes in Detroit at age 92.

## Body

### Early Life and Activism
Born in Tuskegee, Parks grew up under Jim Crow segregation in Alabama. She married Raymond Parks in 1932, and both became early activists, hosting meetings for the defense of the Scottsboro Boys. In 1943, Parks became secretary of the Montgomery NAACP, where she investigated cases of racial violence, including the gang rape of Recy Taylor, helping to build networks for future activism. She worked at Maxwell Air Force Base, where integrated facilities contrasted sharply with segregated city life, deepening her frustration with Jim Crow laws. In 1955, she attended the Highlander Folk School, an integrated activist training center, which she later described as a rare experience of racial harmony.

### Arrest and the Montgomery Bus Boycott
Montgomery's bus system required Black passengers to sit in the back and yield seats to white riders. On December 1, 1955, Parks, a 42-year-old seamstress, refused to give up her seat to a white passenger. She was arrested. Local activists, including the Women's Political Council (WPC) and the NAACP, had been seeking a suitable test case to challenge bus segregation and chose Parks due to her standing in the community. The WPC organized a one-day boycott for her trial date, which was so successful it was extended indefinitely. The Montgomery Improvement Association (MIA), led by Martin Luther King Jr., sustained the boycott for 381 days. It ended only after the Supreme Court ruled bus segregation unconstitutional in *Browder v. Gayle*.

### Later Life and Continued Activism
After the boycott, Parks faced financial ruin and health problems. She moved to Detroit in 1957, where she continued her activism for decades. She worked for Congressman John Conyers, advocated for political prisoners like Gary Tyler and Angela Davis, supported the Black Power movement, and was an anti-apartheid activist. In 1987, she co-founded the Rosa and Raymond Parks Institute for Self Development to mentor youth. Parks received numerous honors, including the Presidential Medal of Freedom (1996) and the Congressional Gold Medal (1999). She died in 2005 and was memorialized with a statue in the U.S. Capitol's National Statuary Hall, the first Black American to receive this honor.

## Terms
- **Jim Crow**: The system of state and local laws that enforced racial segregation in the Southern United States from the late 19th century until 1965.
- **NAACP**: National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, a civil rights organization founded in 1909 to fight for racial justice.
- **Montgomery Bus Boycott**: A 381-day mass protest against the segregated bus system in Montgomery, Alabama, sparked by Rosa Parks's arrest.
- **Montgomery Improvement Association (MIA)**: The organization formed to lead and sustain the Montgomery Bus Boycott.
- **Women's Political Council (WPC)**: A Black women's civil rights organization in Montgomery that was instrumental in organizing the initial bus boycott.
- **Browder v. Gayle**: The 1956 U.S. Supreme Court case that declared Montgomery's bus segregation laws unconstitutional.
- **Highlander Folk School**: An adult education center in Tennessee that trained civil rights activists in an integrated setting.
- **Spingarn Medal**: The NAACP's highest honor, awarded for outstanding achievement by an African American.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate the popular narrative of Parks as a quiet, accidental heroine. Biographer Jeanne Theoharis argues this portrayal obscures her lifelong radical activism and political philosophy, calling it a "gendered caricature." Others, like sociologist Barry Schwartz, note that while Parks became the celebrated symbol, earlier protesters like Claudette Colvin and Aurelia Browder played equally important roles. Colvin herself expressed mixed feelings, feeling both glad an adult had stood up and abandoned by the community that did not support her own earlier arrest. The historiography also examines how Parks's image has been used in political discourse, sometimes to reinforce conservative narratives of American history by isolating her act as a singular, legally-focused moment of courage.
