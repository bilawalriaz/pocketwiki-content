# Yasser Arafat

## Overview
Yasser Arafat was the preeminent Palestinian political leader for over three decades, serving as chairman of the Palestine Liberation Organization (PLO) and the first President of the Palestinian Authority. He was a central figure in the Israeli–Palestinian conflict, evolving from a militant opponent of Israel's existence to a Nobel Peace Prize-winning negotiator who accepted a two-state solution. His legacy is deeply contested, viewed by Palestinians as a national symbol and by many Israelis as a terrorist.

## Timeline
- **c. August 1929** — Born in Cairo, Egypt.
- **1948** — Fought alongside the Muslim Brotherhood in the Arab–Israeli War.
- **1959** — Co-founded Fatah, a Palestinian nationalist paramilitary organization.
- **1969** — Elected Chairman of the PLO.
- **1968** — Battle of Karameh raised his and Fatah's profile as a resistance force.
- **1974** — PLO recognized as the "sole legitimate representative of the Palestinian people"; addressed the UN.
- **1988** — Proclaimed the State of Palestine and recognized Israel's right to exist.
- **1993** — Signed the Oslo Accords with Israel.
- **1994** — Awarded the Nobel Peace Prize; returned to Gaza to lead the Palestinian Authority.
- **2000** — Camp David Summit with Israel fails to reach a final agreement.
- **2001-2004** — Confined by the Israeli army to his Ramallah compound.
- **11 November 2004** — Died in a French hospital; cause of death remains disputed.

## Body

### Early Life and Militant Beginnings
Born to Palestinian parents in Cairo, Arafat was raised there and in Jerusalem. He studied civil engineering at the University of King Fuad I, where he embraced Arab nationalism. He fought against the creation of Israel in the 1948 war. In the late 1950s, while working in Kuwait, he co-founded Fatah, an organization dedicated to liberating Palestine through armed struggle led by Palestinians themselves, independent of Arab governments. Fatah launched attacks on Israel from bases in Jordan and Lebanon.

### Rise to Leadership and Conflict
Arafat's profile surged after the 1967 Six-Day War, which left Israel occupying the West Bank and Gaza. He joined the PLO and was elected its chairman in 1969. The 1968 Battle of Karameh, where Fatah and Jordanian forces repelled an Israeli raid, made him a national hero. However, Fatah's growing power in Jordan led to violent clashes with King Hussein's government, culminating in the 1970 "Black September" conflict. Expelled from Jordan, the PLO relocated to Lebanon, where it operated as a state-within-a-state and continued attacks on Israel, provoking major Israeli invasions in 1978 and 1982.

### Shift to Negotiation and the Oslo Era
Based in Tunisia after 1983, Arafat shifted strategy from armed conflict to negotiation. He accepted a two-state solution in 1988. Secret talks led to the 1993 Oslo Accords, in which the PLO recognized Israel and Israel recognized the PLO, establishing a framework for Palestinian self-rule. Arafat returned to lead the newly created Palestinian Authority (PA) in 1994. The peace process stalled, notably at the failed 2000 Camp David Summit. The subsequent Second Intifada (2000-2005) saw a resurgence of violence. Arafat was confined by Israel to his Ramallah headquarters from 2002 until his death in 2004.

### Leadership of the Palestinian Authority
As PA President, Arafat established a centralized authority, appointing officials and controlling finances. His rule was criticized for corruption and authoritarianism. The PA's security forces were accused of human rights abuses. His financial dealings were opaque; audits by the IMF and others found he controlled hundreds of millions in public funds, though the IMF noted most were invested in Palestinian assets. His leadership faced growing challenges from Islamist rivals like Hamas.

## Terms
- **Fatah**: A Palestinian nationalist political party and former paramilitary organization founded by Arafat in 1959, advocating for an independent Palestinian state.
- **PLO (Palestine Liberation Organization)**: An umbrella organization established in 1964 to represent the Palestinian people internationally; Arafat led it from 1969.
- **PNA (Palestinian National Authority)**: The provisional self-governing body established by the Oslo Accords to administer parts of the West Bank and Gaza Strip.
- **Intifada**: An Arabic word meaning "tremor," referring to Palestinian uprisings against Israeli occupation (First: 1987-1993; Second: 2000-2005).
- **Oslo Accords**: A 1993 agreement between Israel and the PLO that created a framework for Palestinian self-governance and mutual recognition.
- **Kunya**: A teknonymic name (e.g., Abu Ammar, "father of Ammar") used as a nom de guerre or term of respect.
- **Neopatrimonialism**: A system of governance where formal state institutions are subverted by a leader's personal patronage network.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Cause of Death**: The official cause was a hemorrhagic stroke. However, investigations have been inconclusive and contradictory. A Swiss team found evidence suggesting polonium poisoning, while French and Russian teams found no proof of foul play, attributing his death to natural causes.
- **Role in Terrorism**: Arafat's relationship with militant groups is debated. He was accused of sanctioning attacks like the Munich Olympics massacre (1972) and funding the al-Aqsa Martyrs' Brigades. He denied direct involvement in many incidents but was held responsible by Israel as the PLO leader.
- **Financial Legacy**: Assessments of his financial management vary. Critics, including Palestinian officials, accuse him of massive corruption and diverting public funds. Others, like the IMF, state that while he controlled large sums, the money was largely invested in Palestinian assets.
- **Negotiator or Obstacle?**: Opinions differ on whether Arafat was a genuine partner for peace who made historic compromises or an intransigent leader who missed opportunities for statehood, particularly at the 2000 Camp David Summit.
