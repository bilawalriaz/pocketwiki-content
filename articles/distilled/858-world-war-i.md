# World War I

## Overview

World War I (28 July 1914 – 11 November 1918), known as the Great War, was a global conflict between the Allied Powers (Entente) and the Central Powers. It was one of the deadliest conflicts in history, resulting in an estimated 15 to 22 million deaths, including those in war crimes and genocides. The war also helped spread the Spanish flu pandemic. It marked a turning point in military technology, introducing the first large-scale use of machine guns, artillery, aircraft, chemical weapons, and tanks. The complex web of alliances, imperial rivalries, and the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo were key catalysts for the conflict.

## Body

### Causes and Alliances

The war's origins lay in the complex system of alliances and the balance of power in Europe. The rise of the German Empire under Otto von Bismarck disrupted the long-standing equilibrium. Bismarck's intricate alliance system, including the Dual Alliance (Germany-Austria-Hungary) and the Reinsurance Treaty (Germany-Russia), aimed to isolate France. However, after Bismarck's dismissal in 1890, Wilhelm II's aggressive naval policies under Admiral Tirpitz sparked an Anglo-German naval arms race. The Franco-Russian Alliance (1894) and the Entente Cordiale (1904) with Britain formed the Triple Entente, creating two opposing blocs.

### The July Crisis and Outbreak of War

The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand by Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb nationalist, triggered the July Crisis, a month of diplomatic manoeuvring among Austria-Hungary, Germany, Russia, France and Britain. Believing that Serbian intelligence helped organise Franz Ferdinand's murder, Austrian officials wanted to use the opportunity to end Serbian interference in Bosnia. On 23 July, Austria delivered an ultimatum to Serbia, listing ten demands made intentionally unacceptable to provide an excuse for starting hostilities. Serbia accepted all terms except those empowering Austrian representatives to suppress "subversive elements" inside Serbia. Claiming this amounted to rejection, Austria broke off diplomatic relations and ordered partial mobilisation the next day; on 28 July, they declared war on Serbia and began shelling Belgrade. Russia ordered general mobilisation in support of Serbia on 30 July. Germany's declaration of war on Russia and France, followed by the invasion of Belgium, brought Britain into the conflict. The Schlieffen Plan, designed to quickly defeat France, failed as German forces were halted at the First Battle of the Marne, leading to the establishment of the Western Front.

### Major Theaters and Campaigns

The Western Front became defined by trench warfare, with major battles at Verdun, the Somme, and Passchendaele failing to break the stalemate. The Eastern Front was more dynamic, with significant Russian offensives like the Brusilov Offensive. The Italian Front saw fierce fighting in the Alps, while the Middle Eastern campaigns included the Gallipoli debacle and the Arab Revolt. The war also extended to the colonies, with campaigns in Africa and the Pacific.

### United States Entry and Final Offensives

The U.S. entered the war in April 1917, primarily due to Germany's resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare. The American Expeditionary Forces, under General Pershing, played a crucial role in the final offensives. The German Spring Offensive of 1918 initially succeeded but ultimately failed due to exhaustion and lack of reserves. The Allied Hundred Days Offensive, beginning in August 1918, led to the collapse of the German front line.

### Aftermath and Peace

The Paris Peace Conference imposed harsh terms on the defeated Central Powers, particularly Germany, through the Treaty of Versailles. The treaty's "War Guilt Clause" (Article 231) assigned responsibility for "all the loss and damage... as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies," justifying massive reparations and contributing to economic instability and resentment in Germany. The war led to the collapse of four empires—the German, Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman, and Russian—and the creation of new nations such as Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Yugoslavia. The League of Nations was established to prevent future conflicts, but its weaknesses contributed to the outbreak of World War II.

## Terms

- **Schlieffen Plan**: German military strategy to quickly defeat France by invading through Belgium before turning to Russia.
- **Trench Warfare**: Military strategy characterized by static combat in extensive fortified trench systems.
- **Triple Entente**: Informal alliance between Britain, France, and Russia opposing the Triple Alliance.
- **War Guilt Clause (Article 231)**: Treaty of Versailles provision assigning responsibility for "all the loss and damage... as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies."
- **Unrestricted Submarine Warfare**: German policy of attacking all ships, including neutral and passenger vessels, without warning.
- **Hundred Days Offensive**: Series of Allied attacks in 1918 that led to the end of WWI.
- **October Revolution**: 1917 Russian revolution in which the Bolsheviks seized power and withdrew from WWI.
- **Armistice of 11 November 1918**: Agreement that ended hostilities on the Western Front.
- **League of Nations**: International organization established after WWI to maintain world peace.
- **Conscientious Objector**: Individual who refuses military service on moral or religious grounds.

## Debates and Open Questions

Historians continue to debate the primary causes of World War I, with some emphasizing long-term structural factors like alliance systems and arms races, while others focus on the July Crisis and the role of key individuals. The "war guilt" question remains contentious, with debates over whether Germany bore sole responsibility or if it was a collective failure of all European powers. The effectiveness of military leadership, particularly the high casualty rates and the failure to adapt tactics to new technologies, is another area of ongoing scholarly discussion. The war's legacy, including its impact on the rise of totalitarian ideologies and the interwar peace settlement, continues to be analyzed and reassessed.
