# State (polity)

A state is a political entity that rules and society within a definite territory, defined by its legitimate monopoly on force. For most of prehistory, people lived in stateless societies; the earliest states arose about 5,500 years ago. Today, the modern nation-state predominates, and the international community comprises around 200 sovereign states.

## Timeline

- **c. 3500 BC** — First known states created in Egypt, Mesopotamia, India, China, Mesoamerica, and the Andes
- **c. 3000 BC** — Bronze metallurgy spreads within Afro-Eurasia, facilitating state rise
- **c. 1200** — English noun "state" introduced in generic sense "condition, circumstances"
- **14th century** — Revival of Roman law; term "state" refers to legal standing of persons
- **15th century** — Centralizing process gives rise to the absolutist state
- **Early 16th century** — Machiavelli's works popularize "state" in its modern sense
- **1648** — Westphalian sovereignty notion—though historians argue it "has more to do with 1948 than 1648"
- **1933** — Montevideo Convention provides commonly accepted definition of state
- **1960** — Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples institutes international legal context for popular sovereignty
- **Post-WWII** — Sovereign equality becomes fully global amid decolonization

## Body

### Etymology

The word *state* and its cognates (Italian *stato*, French *état*, German *Staat*) derive from Latin *status*, meaning "condition, circumstances," from *stare*, "to stand," with a sacred connotation. The English noun predates the political sense, introduced c. 1200 from Old French and Latin. With 14th-century Roman law revival, the term referred to legal standing (e.g., "estates of the realm"—noble, common, clerical), especially the king's status, and Roman ideas of "status rei publicae." Machiavelli's early 16th-century works popularized the word in its modern sense, though *stato* then meant the position of those with political power. The church-state contrast dates to the 16th century.

### Definition

There is no academic consensus on the state's definition. Walter Scheidel notes mainstream definitions share "centralized institutions that impose rules, and back them up by force, over a territorially circumscribed population; a distinction between the rulers and the ruled; and an element of autonomy, stability, and differentiation." The most common definition is Max Weber's: a compulsory political organization with a centralized government maintaining a monopoly on legitimate force within a territory.

A state differs from a nation (a cultural-political identity), a government (an organization acting for the state), or a society (groups independent of the state). The contractarian view holds states form from cooperation's benefits; the predatory view, per Charles Tilly, sees states as "coercion-wielding organisations" resembling "a form of organized crime" selling protection from itself. Tilly's essential state activities include war making, state making, protection, extraction, adjudication, distribution, and production. The Montevideo Convention (1933) requires: a permanent population, a defined territory, government, and capacity to enter relations with other states.

### Types of states

Charles Tilly distinguished empires, theocracies, city-states, and nation-states. Josep Colomer contrasted empires with states: empires were larger, lacked fixed boundaries, comprised "diverse groups and territorial units with asymmetric links with the center," and had overlapping jurisdictions. States may be sovereign or subject to external hegemony. Federated states transfer some sovereign powers to a federal government. Modern city-states include Vatican City, Monaco, and Singapore; others survive as federated states (German city-states) or autonomous entities (Hong Kong, Gibraltar, Ceuta).

### State and government, nation, civil society

The state is the organization; the government is the group controlling the state apparatus at a given time. States are immaterial social objects served by successive governments. A nation-state links a single ethnicity with a specific state. Modern thought contrasts the state with civil society as economic society. Antonio Gramsci saw civil society as the primary political locus—where "identity formation, ideological struggle, the activities of intellectuals, and the construction of hegemony take place." Louis Althusser argued civil organizations (church, schools, family) form an "ideological state apparatus" complementing the "repressive state apparatus" (police, military). Jürgen Habermas posited a public sphere distinct from economic and political spheres.

### History

The earliest states emerged when durable power centralization became possible. Agriculture and settled populations were necessary; grain agriculture (wheat, barley, millet) suits concentrated production, taxation, and storage. Writing (or equivalents like Inca quipus) enabled centralizing information. The first states arose in Egypt, Mesopotamia, India, China, Mesoamerica, and the Andes. For most of history, people lived in stateless societies; competing forms were religious organizations and city republics. Since the late 19th century, virtually all inhabitable land has been parcelled into state territories.

Mesopotamia is considered the earliest civilization, with cities, division of labor, writing, and mathematics. Though state-forms predated Ancient Greece, the Greeks first formulated a political philosophy of the state. Medieval feudalism organized the state on lord-vassal relations. Tax struggles produced the *Standestaat* (state of Estates), with parliaments negotiating with kings. From the 15th century, centralization created the absolutist state. Per Hendrik Spruyt, modern states differ from predecessors in greater intervention capacity and international legal sovereignty.

### Theories for the emergence of the state

Theories emphasize grain agriculture and settled populations. Long-shelf-life grains attracted looters and rulers, fostering hierarchical elites collecting taxes, unlike root crops. Irrigation-intensive agriculture (e.g., ancient Egypt) required cooperation and had natural monopoly characteristics; such regions show higher land inequality and less democracy today. Spruyt distinguishes security-based (warfare), economy-based (trade, property rights, capitalism), and institutionalist explanations. The "neo-Darwinian" framework—natural selection and competition—dominates scholarship.

### Theories of state function

Political theories fall into two categories: "liberal" or "conservative" theories treat capitalism as given and the state as neutral; Marxist and anarchist theories see the state as a partisan tool of the upper class. Anarchists view the state as inherently dominating and seek its dismantlement. Anarcho-capitalists (e.g., Murray Rothbard) reach similar conclusions from consent and non-initiation of force, favoring market competition. Marx wrote the state mirrors class relations and dominates for the ruling class; the *Communist Manifesto* calls it "a committee for managing the common affairs of the bourgeoisie." Ralph Miliband argued the ruling class uses the state via elite ties. Nicos Poulantzas introduced the state's "relative autonomy." Pluralists (e.g., Robert Dahl) see the state as a neutral arena for contending interests, calling it a polyarchy. Michel Foucault, deeming political theory too state-centric, developed governmentality, calling the state "a composite reality and a mythologized abstraction." State autonomy theorists (e.g., Theda Skocpol) argue state personnel pursue independent interests.

### Theories of state legitimacy

Social contract theories ground legitimacy in a state of nature incentivizing state formation. Hobbes described it as "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short"; Locke argued for inalienable rights, especially property. Early modern absolutists like Hobbes and Jean Bodin undermined divine right by justifying power via the people. Weber identified three legitimacy sources: traditional, charismatic, and rational-legal. Fukuyama uses Weber's charisma to explain why individuals relinquish freedoms for larger states, citing Muhammad.

### State failure

A "failed state" occurs when "sovereignty over claimed territory has collapsed or was never effectively at all" (David Samuels). State capacity is the ability to fulfill basic functions—security, law and order, public services. Jeffrey Herbst attributes failure to weak institutions; Stephen D. Krasner sees it as context-dependent; Susan Rice argues failure threatens global stability via terrorism and conflict. Early European state formation (c. 1600) was enabled by warfare technology, increased food production, and cultural changes. Late formation occurred amid limited conflict, emerged from colonization in poverty with extractive institutions, and arbitrary borders mixed cultural groups, hindering legitimate state-building.

## Terms

- **State**: Political entity ruling within a definite territory, defined by legitimate monopoly on force
- **Nation**: Cultural-political community, distinct from a state's abstraction
- **Government**: Group controlling the state apparatus at a given time
- **Sovereignty**: Condition of not being subject to any other power
- **Nation-state**: Single ethnicity associated with a specific state
- **Federated state**: Territorial community forming part of a federation, with some powers transferred
- **Civil society**: Organized groups independent of the state
- **State capacity**: Ability to fulfill basic functions
- **Standestaat**: "State of Estates," with parliaments negotiating with the king
- **Governmentality**: Foucault's concept of how governance understanding influences state function

## Debates and open questions

- State definitions are disputed; Weber's monopoly-of-force definition is common but not universal
- Whether states emerged from contract or resemble "extortion rackets" (Tilly) is contested
- Whether Western European state numbers declined from the Late Middle Ages to Early Modern Era is disputed
- Whether "Westphalian sovereignty" dates to 1648 or 1948 is debated
- Whether irrigation necessitated centralized states is challenged by archaeological evidence of non-stratified societies
- Whether state failure stems from weak institutions or context-specific phenomena is debated
- Whether pluralism describes democratic states is challenged by evidence of wealthy leaders
