# Mariana Trench

## Overview
The Mariana Trench is the deepest oceanic trench on Earth, located in the western Pacific Ocean. It is a crescent-shaped geological feature formed by the subduction of one tectonic plate beneath another, creating extreme depths that host unique life forms and have been the site of historic human exploration.

## Timeline
- **1875** — The Challenger expedition first sounds the trench, recording a depth of 8,184 meters.
- **1951** — The survey vessel *Challenger II* uses echo sounding to identify the deepest point, the Challenger Deep, at 10,900 meters.
- **1960** — The bathyscaphe *Trieste* makes the first crewed descent to the bottom, reaching ~10,916 meters.
- **1995** — The uncrewed ROV *Kaiko* sets a diving record of 10,911 meters.
- **2009** — The U.S. establishes the Mariana Trench Marine National Monument.
- **2012** — James Cameron completes a crewed descent to 10,908 meters in the *Deepsea Challenger*.
- **2019** — Victor Vescovo achieves a new record descent to 10,928 meters in the *DSV Limiting Factor*.
- **2020** — The Chinese submersible *Fendouzhe* reaches 10,909 meters.

## Body

### Geology and Formation
The Mariana Trench is part of the Izu–Bonin–Mariana subduction system. It forms where the dense, old (up to 170 million years) western edge of the Pacific Plate is thrust beneath the younger Mariana Plate. This process creates the trench's extreme depth and also causes volcanic activity that formed the nearby Mariana Islands through flux melting of the mantle.

### Exploration and Measurement
The trench's depth has been measured with increasing precision over time. Early soundings used weighted ropes, while modern surveys employ multibeam echo sounders. The maximum known depth is 10,935 ± 6 meters at the Challenger Deep. As of 2022, there have been 22 crewed and seven uncrewed descents. The first crewed dive was by the *Trieste* in 1960. Notable later descents include those by James Cameron (2012) and Victor Vescovo (2019), who also reported finding plastic pollution.

### Life in the Trench
Despite immense pressure (over 1,000 times standard atmospheric pressure) and near-freezing temperatures, life thrives. Early expeditions reported large creatures, though sightings are debated. Modern research has documented giant single-celled organisms (xenophyophores), amphipods, and fish like the Mariana snailfish. Microbial life is abundant, with studies identifying thousands of new species. However, this life is threatened by pollution; crustaceans contain high levels of banned toxins like PCBs, and microplastics have been found in organisms at all depths.

### Human Impact and Proposals
The trench is a U.S. national monument, though the deepest part lies in the Federated States of Micronesia's exclusive economic zone. It has been proposed as a nuclear waste disposal site due to subduction, but this is prohibited by international law and considered unsafe due to unpredictable megathrust earthquakes.

## Terms
- **Subduction**: The geological process where one tectonic plate is thrust beneath another.
- **Bathyscaphe**: A free-diving, self-propelled deep-sea submersible.
- **Echo/Sonar Sounding**: A method of measuring depth by transmitting sound waves and timing their return from the seabed.
- **Hadal Zone**: The deepest region of the ocean, generally below 6,000 meters.
- **Xenophyophores**: Giant, single-celled organisms (foraminiferans) found on the deep seafloor.
- **PCBs (Polychlorinated Biphenyls)**: A class of banned industrial chemicals that persist in the environment.
- **Megathrust Earthquake**: The most powerful type of earthquake, occurring at subduction zones.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact maximum depth is subject to measurement uncertainty and debate, with different surveys reporting slightly different figures.
- The identity of the "flatfish" reportedly seen during the 1960 *Trieste* dive is disputed; it may have been a sea cucumber.
- The safety and ethics of using the trench for nuclear waste disposal remain contentious, with concerns about tectonic activity and long-term containment.
