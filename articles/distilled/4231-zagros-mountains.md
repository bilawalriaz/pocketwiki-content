# Zagros Mountains

## Overview
The Zagros Mountains are a major mountain range in West Asia, spanning Iran, Iraq, and Turkey. They form a significant geographic and cultural barrier between the Mesopotamian Plain and the Iranian Plateau, and their geology, history, and ecology have profoundly shaped the region's development.

## Timeline
- **c. 65,000–35,000 years ago** — Neanderthal remains found in Shanidar Cave.
- **c. 10,600 years ago** — "Proto-Neolithic" cemetery established at Shanidar Cave.
- **c. 9000 BC** — Signs of early agriculture appear in the foothills.
- **c. 5400–3500 BC** — Evidence of wine production found at Hajji Firuz Tepe and Godin Tepe.
- **Early 2nd millennium BC** — Clay tablets at Tell Shemshara detail interactions between local groups and Mesopotamian cities.
- **Miocene (c. 25–5 mya)** — Main collision of the Eurasian and Arabian plates forms the Zagros fold and thrust belt.
- **Cretaceous (145–66 mya)** — Obduction of Neotethys oceanic crust affects northeastern uplift.
- **Eocene (56–34 mya)** — Continental arc collision impacts northeastern uplift.
- **Present** — Tectonic collision continues; GPS shows active shortening of ~5–10 mm/year.

## Body

### Geology and Formation
The Zagros fold and thrust belt was primarily formed by the collision of the Eurasian and Arabian tectonic plates, mainly during the Miocene epoch (about 25–5 million years ago). This collision folded sedimentary rocks deposited from the Paleozoic to the Cenozoic eras. The process remains active today, with GPS measurements showing the Arabian Plate is still pushing against the Eurasian Plate, causing the mountains and Iranian Plateau to rise. The rate of shortening is higher in the southeast (~10 mm/year) than in the northwest (~5 mm/year). The range is divided by the Kazerun strike-slip fault into two zones with different topography, partly due to the presence of a ductile rock salt layer in the southeast. The mountains are composed entirely of sedimentary rock, primarily limestone, with Paleozoic rocks in the higher peaks and younger Mesozoic and Tertiary rocks in the lower, folded sections.

### Human History and Archaeology
The Zagros has been occupied by humans since the Lower Paleolithic Period. Key sites include Shanidar Cave, which yielded Neanderthal fossils and early "proto-Neolithic" cemeteries, and others like Yafteh and Warwasi, which show the progression from Mousterian to Baradostian and Zarzian stone tool cultures. Evidence of early agriculture (c. 9000 BC) and wine production (c. 5400–3500 BC) has been found in foothill settlements like Jarmo, Hajji Firuz Tepe, and Godin Tepe. Genetic analysis of a Neolithic bone from Wezmeh Cave reveals a distinct population most similar to modern Iranian Zoroastrians. The mountains were home to various Pre Indo-European peoples (e.g., Hurrians, Elamites) who interacted with Mesopotamian civilizations.

### Population and Culture
The Zagros has been inhabited for millennia by pastoralist and farming groups. Current major populations include the Iranic-speaking Lurs and Bakhtiari Lurs in the central and southern ranges, the Kurds in the northwest, and the Turkic-origin Qashqai confederation near Shiraz. These groups often practice seasonal transhumance, moving herds between summer and winter pastures. The region's traditional ethno-geographic divisions (e.g., Kurdistan, Luristan) are considered significant cultural and physical entities. Historically, the area was influenced by Mesopotamian and Hurrian religions, then Zoroastrianism and Syriac Christianity; today, the majority are Muslims, with Christian minorities.

### Climate, Flora, and Fauna
The Zagros features a semi-arid, continental Mediterranean climate with snowy winters and mild, rainy springs. The ecosystem is the Zagros Mountains forest steppe. The flora includes remnants of oak woodlands and pistachio/almond steppelands, and is the ancestral home of many crops like wheat, barley, and various fruits. The fauna includes threatened species such as the Zagros Mountains mouse-like hamster, the striped hyena, and the Luristan newt. The Persian fallow deer was rediscovered here after being thought extinct. Historically, the Asiatic lion inhabited the southwestern mountains until the late 19th century.

## Terms
- **Fold and thrust belt:** A geological structure formed by the folding and breaking of rock layers due to compressional forces, like tectonic collision.
- **Decollement:** A detachment surface or layer (like rock salt) above which rock layers deform independently from the basement rock.
- **Transhumance:** The seasonal movement of livestock between fixed summer and winter pastures.
- **Paleolithic:** The Old Stone Age, characterized by the use of rudimentary chipped stone tools.
- **Neolithic:** The New Stone Age, marked by the development of agriculture and settled communities.
- **Endemic:** A species native to and found only in a specific geographic area.
- **Tectonic plate:** A massive, irregularly shaped slab of solid rock that makes up the Earth's lithosphere and moves over the asthenosphere.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact timing and mechanisms of the uplift in the northeastern parts of the belt, involving the obduction of Neotethys oceanic crust and continental arc collision, are noted as having major effects but are distinct from the main Miocene collision.
- The genetic legacy of Neolithic Zagros populations is debated; the Wezmeh Cave individual did not contribute to early European farmers but is genetically similar to some modern Iranian and South Asian groups, suggesting the Zagros was a plausible source of Eurasian ancestry in Central and South Asia.
