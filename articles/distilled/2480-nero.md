# Nero

## Overview
Nero was the final emperor of Rome's Julio-Claudian dynasty, ruling from AD 54 to 68. His reign is remembered for a mix of cultural patronage, administrative success, and later tyranny, culminating in his suicide and a civil war. He remains a controversial figure, with ancient sources overwhelmingly negative but modern scholars noting his popularity among commoners and questioning the reliability of those sources.

## Timeline
- **15 December AD 37** — Born Lucius Domitius Ahenobarbus at Antium.
- **AD 50** — Adopted as son and heir by Emperor Claudius.
- **AD 54** — Becomes emperor at age 16 after Claudius's death.
- **AD 59** — Orders the murder of his mother, Agrippina the Younger.
- **AD 64** — Great Fire of Rome; Nero builds the Domus Aurea.
- **AD 65** — Pisonian conspiracy against Nero is uncovered and fails.
- **AD 67** — Participates in and wins all events at the Olympic Games.
- **March AD 68** — Governor Vindex rebels; Galba is proclaimed emperor.
- **9 June AD 68** — Nero commits suicide, ending the Julio-Claudian dynasty.

## Body

### Early Life and Accession
Nero was born into the Roman aristocracy, the son of Gnaeus Domitius Ahenobarbus and Agrippina the Younger, great-granddaughter of Emperor Augustus. After his father's death and his mother's exile under Caligula, Nero was raised by his aunt. Following Caligula's death, Agrippina married Emperor Claudius, who adopted Nero as his heir in AD 50. Upon Claudius's death in AD 54, Nero ascended to the throne with the support of the Praetorian Guard and Senate, guided by his mother, tutor Seneca, and praetorian prefect Burrus.

### Reign and Decline
Nero's early reign, the *Quinquennium Neronis*, was praised for moderation and good governance, including fiscal reforms and respect for the Senate. He pursued diplomacy, trade, and cultural projects, promoting games and public entertainments that made him popular with the lower classes but scandalized the aristocracy. His relationship with his mother deteriorated, leading to her murder in AD 59. After the deaths of Burrus and the exile of Seneca, his rule became more autocratic, marked by executions, extravagance, and the construction of the vast Domus Aurea palace after the Great Fire of Rome in AD 64. He was accused of starting the fire and scapegoating Christians.

### Military and Foreign Policy
Nero's reign saw significant military events. General Corbulo successfully campaigned in the Roman–Parthian War (58–63), resulting in a peace where the Parthian king Tiridates received his crown from Nero in Rome. In Britain, Governor Suetonius Paulinus crushed the major revolt led by Queen Boudica. The First Jewish–Roman War began in AD 66, with Vespasian dispatched to restore order.

### Downfall and Death
In AD 68, Governor Vindex rebelled against Nero's tax policies, and Galba was proclaimed emperor. The Senate declared Nero a public enemy. Abandoned by his guards and allies, Nero fled Rome and committed suicide on 9 June AD 68. His death ended the Julio-Claudian dynasty and triggered the Year of the Four Emperors, a brief civil war. A legend, the *Nero Redivivus*, arose in the eastern provinces that he would return, leading to several impostor-led rebellions.

## Terms
- **Julio-Claudian dynasty**: The first imperial dynasty of Rome, ruling from Augustus to Nero.
- **Praetorian Guard**: The emperor's elite bodyguard unit, whose support was crucial for imperial succession.
- **Quinquennium Neronis**: The "Five Years of Nero," a term for the first five years of his reign, later remembered as a period of good government.
- **Domus Aurea**: The "Golden House," a vast and lavish palace complex Nero built in Rome after the Great Fire.
- **Damnatio memoriae**: A Roman practice of condemning a person's memory by erasing their name and images from monuments.
- **Nero Redivivus**: The legend that Nero did not die but would return to power, fueling impostor rebellions.
- **Maiestas trial**: A treason trial, which Nero used against political opponents later in his reign.

## Debates and open questions
- **Responsibility for the Great Fire**: Ancient sources are divided. Tacitus is non-committal, while Suetonius and Cassius Dio claim Nero started it. Modern scholars debate the reliability of these accounts.
- **Motives for matricide**: The primary ancient explanation is Nero's affair with Poppaea Sabina, but modern historians note this timeline is problematic and suggest other political or personal reasons.
- **Historical reliability**: The main sources (Tacitus, Suetonius, Cassius Dio) were written decades or centuries after Nero's reign and are consistently hostile. Their accounts of his tyranny are questioned by some modern historians who point to his popularity and administrative successes.
- **Nero in Christian tradition**: He is traditionally considered the first persecutor of Christians and is sometimes linked to the "Number of the Beast" in the Book of Revelation, though this interpretation is debated.
