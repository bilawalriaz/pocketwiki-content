# Abbasid Caliphate

## Overview
The Abbasid Caliphate (750–1258) was the third Islamic caliphate, succeeding the Umayyads. It presided over the Islamic Golden Age, a period of major scientific, cultural, and economic advancement centered in Baghdad. Its decline into political fragmentation and eventual destruction by the Mongols marked a pivotal turning point in Islamic and world history.

## Timeline
- **750** — Abbasid Revolution overthrows the Umayyad Caliphate.
- **762** — Caliph al-Mansur founds Baghdad as the new capital.
- **786–809** — Reign of Harun al-Rashid, the traditional height of Abbasid power.
- **813** — Civil war ends with the execution of Caliph al-Amin.
- **836** — Caliph al-Mu'tasim moves the capital to Samarra.
- **861–870** — "Anarchy at Samarra" period of military coups.
- **945** — The Buyid dynasty seizes control of Baghdad.
- **1055** — The Seljuk Turks take control from the Buyids.
- **1258** — Mongols sack Baghdad, killing Caliph al-Musta'sim.
- **1261** — A powerless Abbasid figurehead caliphate is established in Cairo by the Mamluks.
- **1517** — Ottoman conquest of Egypt ends the Cairo-based Abbasid line.

## Body

### Rise and Establishment
The Abbasids, descended from Muhammad's uncle Abbas ibn Abd al-Muttalib, overthrew the Umayyads in 750. Their revolution began in Khurasan, leveraging discontent among non-Arab Muslims (*mawali*) and those favoring a ruler from Muhammad's family. After victory, they moved the caliphate's center from Syria to Iraq. Caliph al-Mansur consolidated power, executed rivals, and in 762 founded Baghdad, which became the empire's capital and a global center of learning.

### Golden Age and Administrative Innovation
The Abbasid period, especially under Harun al-Rashid and al-Ma'mun, is considered the Islamic Golden Age. The state adopted a sophisticated bureaucracy, with the vizier (*wazir*) becoming a powerful official. The translation movement, particularly of Greek works, flourished, and institutions like the House of Wisdom were established. The caliphate's army was initially based on Khurasani supporters, but later caliphs, notably al-Mu'tasim, recruited Turkic slave-soldiers (Mamluks), whose loyalty was to the caliph alone. This new army led to the creation of the new capital, Samarra, in 836.

### Political Fragmentation and Foreign Domination
From the 9th century, the caliphate fragmented as provinces like Egypt (Tulunids) and Iran (Tahirids, Saffarids, Samanids) became autonomous under local dynasties. The "Anarchy at Samarra" (861–870) saw military coups weaken central authority. In 945, the Iranian Buyid dynasty took control of Baghdad, reducing caliphs to figureheads. They were succeeded by the Seljuk Turks in 1055. Despite this, the Abbasids retained religious prestige and later regained political control in Iraq under caliphs like al-Muqtafi (r. 1136–1160).

### End and Legacy
The caliphate experienced a late revival under al-Nasir (r. 1180–1225). However, in 1258, the Mongols under Hulagu Khan sacked Baghdad and executed Caliph al-Musta'sim, effectively ending the main Abbasid line. A powerless, ceremonial Abbasid caliphate was later established in Cairo by the Mamluk sultans in 1261, lasting until the Ottoman conquest of Egypt in 1517.

## Terms
- **Caliphate:** The political-religious state and office of the successor to the Prophet Muhammad as leader of the Muslim community (*ummah*).
- **Mawali:** Non-Arab Muslims, often Persian, who were integrated into the Islamic community but were sometimes treated as second-class citizens under the Umayyads.
- **Vizier (Wazir):** A high-ranking political advisor or minister, especially in Muslim countries, who often held significant executive power.
- **Mamluk:** A slave-soldier, often of Turkic origin, who formed a powerful military class in the medieval Islamic world.
- **Dhimmi:** A non-Muslim subject living in an Islamic state who paid a special tax (*jizya*) and was granted protection and freedom of religion.
- **Iqta:** A form of land grant or fief given to military officers or officials in lieu of a salary, which later weakened central state revenue.
- **Ummah:** The worldwide community of Muslims, united by their religion.
- **House of Wisdom:** A major intellectual center and library in Baghdad during the Abbasid era, where scholars translated and studied works from various civilizations.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact legal status and degree of enslavement of the early Turkic Mamluk soldiers under al-Mu'tasim is a subject of scholarly debate.
- The reasons for Caliph Harun al-Rashid's sudden and ruthless purge of the powerful Barmakid family in 803 remain unclear and are discussed by contemporary and later historians.
- The claim that the last Abbasid caliph in Cairo "transferred" the caliphate to the Ottoman sultan Selim I in 1517 is a later legend not supported by contemporary accounts.
