# Base (chemistry)

## Overview
In chemistry, a **base** is a substance that reacts with an acid, a concept originating with G.-F. Rouelle (mid-18th century). Three complementary definitions are standard today. **Arrhenius bases** (1884) dissociate in water to release hydroxide ions (OH⁻), yielding slippery, bitter solutions with pH > 7. **Brønsted–Lowry bases** (1923) are proton (H⁺) acceptors, a broader set that includes ammonia and amines which lack OH⁻ but generate it by reacting with water. **Lewis bases** are electron-pair donors that can bond to electron-pair acceptors (Lewis acids) such as BF₃ or metal ions, extending acid–base theory beyond protons. A **strong base** is a base that is quantitatively protonated upon exposure to water; **superbases** (e.g., butyllithium, NaNH₂) are stronger than OH⁻ and cannot exist in aqueous solution. Weak bases (e.g., NH₃) ionize only partially. Bases serve as catalysts, antacids, and industrial reagents (soap, paper, water softening).

## Timeline
- **1717** — Louis Lémery first uses "base" as a synonym for the Paracelsian "matrix."
- **1754** — Guillaume-François Rouelle defines a base as the substance that neutralizes an acid to give a solid salt.
- **1884** — Svante Arrhenius defines a base as a substance dissociating in water to form OH⁻ ions.
- **1923** — Brønsted and Lowry independently define a base as a proton (H⁺) acceptor.
- **1923** — G. N. Lewis defines a base as an electron-pair donor (Lewis acid = electron-pair acceptor).

## Body

### Definitions and scope
All three definitions agree that bases react with acids. Arrhenius bases are metal hydroxides (NaOH, Ca(OH)₂) that release OH⁻ in water; they are a subset of Brønsted bases, which include any proton acceptor (e.g., NH₃, amines). Brønsted bases in non-aqueous solvents (e.g., NH₂⁻ in liquid ammonia) are also covered. Lewis bases are the most general: any species with a high-energy lone pair that can donate it to a low-energy vacant orbital on a Lewis acid (H⁺, BF₃, Ag²⁺, Fe³⁺, Mn⁷⁺). Adducts with metal ions are coordination complexes.

### Etymology
The term derives from the Paracelsian "matrix"—an earthy womb impregnated by a universal acid to grow salts. Lémery (1717) used "base" as a synonym. Rouelle (1754) gave the modern sense: the substance that destroys the volatility ("spirit") of an acid and imparts solidity ("concrete base") to the resulting crystalline salt.

### Properties
Concentrated/strong bases are caustic, react violently with acids, conduct electricity when molten or aqueous, turn red litmus blue, phenolphthalein pink, keep bromothymol blue blue, and turn methyl orange yellow. Aqueous basic solutions have pH > 7 at standard conditions; bases taste bitter.

### Reaction with water (Brønsted equilibrium)
A base B reacts with water:  
B(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ BH⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)  
In this equilibrium, BH⁺ is the conjugate acid of B and OH⁻ is the conjugate base of H₂O. The base-dissociation constant Kb = [BH⁺][OH⁻]/[B]. Bases compete with the extremely strong base OH⁻ for protons, so Kb values are relatively small; a lower Kb means a weaker base.

### Neutralization
In water, strong bases (NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻) and strong acids (HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻) combine: H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2 H₂O. Equal molar amounts yield a neutral salt solution (e.g., NaCl). Weak bases (baking soda, egg white) should neutralize acid spills; strong bases cause violent exothermic reactions and are themselves hazardous.

### Alkalinity without hydroxide groups
Substances lacking OH⁻ can be bases by accepting H⁺ in protic solvents, generating OH⁻:  
Na₂CO₃ + H₂O → 2 Na⁺ + HCO₃⁻ + OH⁻  
NH₃ + H₂O → NH₄⁺ + OH⁻  
Carbon, nitrogen, oxygen (and sometimes fluorine/rare gases) can provide basic sites in compounds such as butyllithium, alkoxides, and metal amides. Without resonance stabilization these are superbases (too strong for water); resonance-stabilized anions (e.g., carboxylates) are weaker bases.

### Strong bases
A strong base is quantitatively protonated by water. This complete protonation is a result of the leveling effect: in water, any base stronger than OH⁻ is "leveled" to OH⁻ strength because water protonates it completely. Examples: alkali and alkaline-earth hydroxides (NaOH, Ca(OH)₂); tetraalkylammonium hydroxides. Low-solubility hydroxides (Al(OH)₃, Mg(OH)₂) are used as antacids—they limit [OH⁻] rise, protecting tissues while neutralizing stomach acid. Under anhydrous conditions, strong bases can deprotonate weakly acidic C–H bonds. Guanidine is exceptionally stable when protonated but is not a strong base because it is not fully protonated by water.

### Superbases
Group 1 salts of carbanions, amide ions, and hydrides (conjugate acids: stable hydrocarbons, amines, H₂) are stronger than OH⁻. They react instantly with water (e.g., CH₃CH₂O⁻ + H₂O → CH₃CH₂OH + OH⁻) and must be used in non-aqueous media. Common superbases: butyllithium, LDA, LDEA, NaNH₂, NaH, lithium bis(trimethylsilyl)amide. The strongest known superbases are gas-phase diethynylbenzene dianions (ortho > meta > para); LiO⁻ was the previous record holder.

### Weak bases
A weak base does not fully ionize in water; protonation is incomplete. Example: NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻, Kb = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ at 25 °C (small degree of ionization).

### Lewis bases
A Lewis base donates a lone pair to a Lewis acid (electron-pair acceptor). Acceptors include H⁺, neutral molecules (BF₃), and high-oxidation-state metal ions (Ag²⁺, Fe³⁺, Mn⁷⁺). Adducts with metals are coordination complexes. Lewis noted that neutral base + neutral acid creates electric stress and a high dipole moment, relieved only by molecular rearrangement.

### Solid bases
Solid bases catalyze reactions via surface basic sites. Examples: oxide mixtures (SiO₂/Al₂O₃, MgO/SiO₂, CaO/SiO₂); mounted bases (Li₂CO₃ on silica; NR₃, NH₃, KNH₂ on alumina; NaOH/KOH on silica/alumina); inorganic chemicals (BaO, KNaCO₃, BeO, MgO, CaO, KCN); anion-exchange resins; activated charcoal (900 °C or N₂O/NH₃/ZnCl₂-NH₄Cl-CO₂ treatment). Basic strength = number of basic sites per unit surface area. Measured by titration with benzoic acid (indicator color change) or gaseous acid adsorption (NO, then CO₂ uptake).

### Bases as catalysts
Insoluble heterogeneous catalysts: metal oxides (MgO, CaO, BaO), KF/alumina, zeolites. Many transition metals form basic catalytic substances. Applications: hydrogenation, double-bond migration, Meerwein–Ponndorf–Verley reduction, Michael reaction. CaO and BaO are highly active when heated to high temperatures.

### Uses
NaOH: soap, paper, rayon. Ca(OH)₂ (slaked lime): bleaching powder; scrubbing SO₂ from flue gas. Mg(OH)₂: antacid. Na₂CO₃: washing soda, water softening. NaHCO₃: baking soda, baking powder, antacid, soda-acid fire extinguishers. NH₄OH: grease-stain removal.

### Monoprotic and polyprotic bases
**Monoprotic (monoacidic)**: one ionizable OH⁻ per formula unit (accepts one H⁺). Examples: NaOH, KOH, AgOH, NH₄OH.  
**Diprotic (diacidic)**: two OH⁻ per formula unit. Examples: Ba(OH)₂, Mg(OH)₂, Ca(OH)₂, Zn(OH)₂, Fe(OH)₂, Sn(OH)₂, Pb(OH)₂, Cu(OH)₂.  
**Triprotic (triacidic)**: three OH⁻ per formula unit. Examples: Al(OH)₃, Fe(OH)₃, Au(OH)₃.  
The number of ionizable OH⁻ ions is also called the **acidity** of the base.

## Terms
- **Arrhenius base** — Substance that dissociates in aqueous solution to form hydroxide ions (OH⁻).
- **Brønsted–Lowry base** — Substance that accepts a proton (H⁺).
- **Lewis base** — Electron-pair donor that shares a pair with an electron-pair acceptor (Lewis acid).
- **Alkali** — A soluble base that releases OH⁻ ions quantitatively in water.
- **Conjugate acid/base** — In B + H₂O ⇌ BH⁺ + OH⁻, BH⁺ is the conjugate acid of B; OH⁻ is the conjugate base of H₂O.
- **Kb (base-dissociation constant)** — Equilibrium constant Kb = [BH⁺][OH⁻]/[B]; lower Kb = weaker base.
- **Leveling effect** — In water, any base stronger than OH⁻ is "leveled" to OH⁻ strength because water protonates it completely.
- **Superbase** — Base stronger than OH⁻ (e.g., organolithiums, metal amides, hydrides); cannot exist in aqueous solution.
- **Mono-/di-/triprotic base** — Base with one, two, or three ionizable OH⁻ ions per formula unit (also called mono-/di-/triacidic).
- **Solid base** — Insoluble material with surface basic sites used as a heterogeneous catalyst; strength measured in basic sites per unit area.

## Debates and open questions
- **Scope of "strong base"**: The term is a formal category (quantitative protonation by water), not a qualitative intensity; guanidine is "very basic" but not a strong base by this definition.
- **Strongest superbase**: Gas-phase diethynylbenzene dianions (ortho, meta, para) currently hold the record, surpassing LiO⁻; synthesis and measurement remain at the frontier.
- **Solid-base quantification**: Two methods (benzoic acid titration vs. gaseous NO/CO₂ adsorption) are used; correlation between surface site count and catalytic activity is an active area.
- **Lewis vs. Brønsted in non-protic media**: Lewis theory covers reactions where no proton transfer occurs (e.g., BF₃ adducts), but operational acidity scales in aprotic solvents are still debated.
