# Francisco Goya

## Overview
Francisco Goya (1746–1828) was the preeminent Spanish painter and printmaker of the late 18th and early 19th centuries, bridging the Old Masters and modern art. His work evolved from court portraiture to increasingly dark, politically charged imagery reflecting war, social corruption, and personal trauma, profoundly influencing later artists like Picasso and Dalí.

- **1824** — Fled to Bordeaux, France
- **1828** — Died in Bordeaux, aged 82

## Body

### Early Years and Training (1746–1771)
Born into a lower-middle-class family in Fuendetodos, Goya received modest schooling and showed early artistic promise. At 14, he apprenticed under José Luzán in Zaragoza, copying prints before moving to Madrid to study with Anton Raphael Mengs. Rejected twice by the Royal Academy of San Fernando, he traveled to Rome at his own expense, producing mythological works like *Sacrifice to Vesta* (1771).

### Tapestries and Court Appointment (1775–1789)
Returning to Madrid, Goya gained patronage through his friendship with the Bayeu family and secured commissions for 42 tapestry cartoons for the Royal Tapestry Factory. These rococo designs, intended for royal palaces, showcased his emerging style. His etching *The Garrotted Man* (c. 1779) foreshadowed later themes of violence and injustice.

### Court Painter and Personal Crisis (1789–1799)
Appointed court painter to Charles IV in 1789, Goya painted portraits of nobility including Manuel de Godoy and the royal family. His *Charles IV of Spain and His Family* (1800–01) subtly critiqued court corruption. Around 1793, an undiagnosed illness caused permanent deafness, shifting his work toward darker, more introspective themes. During convalescence, he created eleven small paintings on tin, including *Yard with Lunatics*, marking a turn toward psychological realism and fantasy.

### The Caprichos and Social Satire (1797–1799)
Between 1797 and 1799, Goya produced 80 aquatinted etchches titled *Caprichos*, targeting societal follies with biting satire. The famous plate *The Sleep of Reason Produces Monsters* encapsulated his critique of superstition and ignorance. Though publicly framed as entertainment, the series revealed his growing disillusionment with human nature.

### Peninsular War and Political Awakening (1808–1814)
Napoleon’s invasion triggered the Peninsular War. Goya remained in Madrid, documenting atrocities through works like *The Second of May 1808* and *The Third of May 1808*, which condemned both French brutality and Spanish repression. Concurrently, he began *The Disasters of War*, a series of 85 etchings depicting famine, execution, and moral decay. Published posthumously in 1863, they stand as one of history’s most powerful anti-war statements.

### Black Paintings and Isolation (1819–1823)
Withdrawing to his rural villa, Quinta del Sordo, Goya painted 14 haunting murals directly onto walls—collectively known as the Black Paintings. Works like *Saturn Devouring His Son* and *The Dog* expressed raw emotion and existential dread. Never meant for public view, these paintings were removed and transferred to canvas decades after his death, altering their original form.

### Final Years in Bordeaux (1824–1828)
After fleeing Spain in 1824, Goya settled in Bordeaux with his loyal servant Leocadia Weiss, possibly his lover. He continued creating art, including bullfighting scenes (*La Tauromaquia*) and satirical etchings (*Los Disparates*). Suffering a paralyzing stroke, he died in 1828 and was buried in Bordeaux. His remains were later reinterred in Madrid’s Ermita de San Antonio de la Florida.

## Terms
- **Tapestry Cartoons**: Full-scale preparatory designs for woven tapestries, often used for decorative wall hangings.
- **Aquatint**: An intaglio printmaking technique using powdered resin to create tonal effects, frequently combined with etching.
- **Rococo**: An artistic style characterized by elegance, playfulness, and asymmetrical ornamentation, popular in the early 18th century.
- **Peninsular War**: Conflict (1808–1814) sparked by Napoleon’s occupation of Spain, marked by guerrilla warfare and civilian suffering.
- **Intaglio**: Printmaking method where ink is held in grooves or recessed areas of a plate, then transferred to paper under pressure.
- **Black Paintings**: Series of 14 disturbing oil paintings created by Goya on the walls of his home, reflecting despair and madness.
- **Disasters of War**: Set of 85 etchings illustrating the horrors of conflict, famine, and social upheaval during and after the Peninsular War.
- **Caprichos**: Collection of 80 satirical etchings critiquing Spanish society, published in 1799.

## Debates and Open Questions
Several aspects of Goya’s life remain debated. The cause of his deafness and subsequent mental decline is uncertain, with theories ranging from viral encephalitis and Ménière’s disease to lead poisoning from his paints. The identity of the model for *La Maja Desnuda* is unconfirmed, though candidates include the Duchess of Alba and Pepita Tudó. Whether Goya had a romantic relationship with Leocadia Weiss lacks definitive proof. Additionally, scholarly debate surrounds the extent of his political engagement during the French occupation and the precise meaning behind many of his symbolic works. The damage incurred during the transfer of the Black Paintings from wall to canvas also raises questions about how much of Goya’s original vision survives today.
