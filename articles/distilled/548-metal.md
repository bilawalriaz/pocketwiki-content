# Metal

## Overview
A metal is a material—element, alloy, or molecular compound—that exhibits a lustrous appearance, high electrical and thermal conductivity, and mechanical ductility and malleability. These properties arise from delocalized electrons available at the Fermi level, enabling charge and heat transport. Metals constitute the majority of the periodic table and underpin modern infrastructure, transportation, and electronics. Their history spans from native copper use ~11,000 years ago to modern engineered alloys like high-entropy and shape-memory materials.

## Timeline
- **~11,000 years ago** — First use of native copper, marking the beginning of refined metal history.
- **5th millennium BCE** — First known appearance of bronze (copper-arsenic/tin) on the Iranian plateau.
- **1800 BCE** — Earliest known steel production (Anatolia); pure tin isolated in China/Japan.
- **500 BCE** — Toledo, Spain produces early alloy steel using wolframite (tungsten/manganese).
- **1250** — Albertus Magnus isolates arsenic.
- **1540** — Vannoccio Biringuccio publishes *De la Pirotechnia*, first systematic metallurgy text.
- **1736–1744** — Platinum discovered in Ecuador by Antonio de Ulloa and Jorge Juan y Santacilia.
- **1809** — Sodium discovered, the first light metal, challenging density as a defining criterion.
- **1855** — Henry Bessemer introduces the Bessemer process, enabling cheap mass production of mild steel.
- **1912** — Industrialization of stainless steel alloys begins in England, Germany, and the US.
- **1960** — First reported metallic glass (Au75Si25) produced at Caltech.
- **1984** — Dan Shechtman discovers quasicrystalline aluminium-manganese alloy (Nobel 2011).
- **2010s** — Research on high-entropy alloys (five or more principal elements) accelerates significantly.

## Body

### Definition and Fundamental Physics
A metal conducts electricity at absolute zero due to delocalized electronic states at the Fermi energy. In the free electron model, an applied voltage shifts electron momenta, creating a net drift velocity (current); the Pauli exclusion principle requires empty states near the Fermi level for this shift, which metals possess but semiconductors and insulators lack (separated by a band gap). Thermal conduction is also electron-dominated, obeying the Wiedemann–Franz law (thermal/electrical conductivity ratio proportional to temperature). Mechanical properties stem from non-directional metallic bonding: low Peierls stress and small Burgers vectors allow dislocation motion, enabling ductility, unlike ionic solids where large Burgers vectors inhibit slip.

### Crystal Structure and Defects
Elemental metals typically adopt body-centered cubic (bcc), face-centered cubic (fcc), or hexagonal close-packed (hcp) structures; some transition with temperature. Complex compounds (e.g., titanium nitride, rock-salt structure; nickelates, perovskite structure) also show metallic conduction. Structural defects—grain boundaries, vacancies, dislocations, stacking faults, twins—mediate temperature-dependent phenomena like creep, fatigue, and internal slip. Elastic deformation follows Hooke’s law up to the proportional limit.

### Chemical Behavior
Metals tend to form cations. Most react with oxygen to form oxides; kinetics vary (potassium in seconds, iron over years). Passivation layers (e.g., on aluminium, chromium) can halt oxidation. Noble metals (gold, platinum, palladium) resist oxidation entirely. Oxides are typically basic, but high oxidation states (CrO₃, Mn₂O₇, OsO₄) are acidic; oxides of less electropositive metals (BeO, Al₂O₃, PbO) are amphoteric.

### Periodic Distribution
Under standard conditions, most elements are metallic (yellow on periodic tables); nonmetals form covalent networks or molecular structures. Highly radioactive elements (Fr, At, Fm+) are unconfirmed in bulk but theoretically metallic (except oganesson, predicted semiconductor). At extreme pressures, all elements metallize (e.g., iodine at 40–170 GPa). Allotropes matter: graphite is metallic, diamond is not; arsenic has both metallic and semiconducting allotropes.

### Alloys: Design and Major Systems
An alloy is a metallic substance of two or more elements (variable or fixed stoichiometry, the latter termed intermetallic compounds). Pure metals are often too soft, brittle, or reactive; alloying tailors hardness, ductility, corrosion resistance, and density.
- **Iron alloys (steels)** dominate by volume and value. Carbon content tunes ductility/toughness; silicon yields cast irons; >10% Cr/Ni/Mo yields stainless steels (passivation via Cr₂O₃).
- **Al, Ti, Mg alloys** offer high strength-to-weight ratios for aerospace/automotive; require electrolytic extraction due to reactivity.
- **Cu alloys** (bronze, brass) date to prehistory; critical for electrical wiring.
- **Superalloys** (Fe/Ni/Co/Cr + W, Mo, Ta, Nb, Ti, Al) retain strength >650 °C for turbines.
- **Multi-element alloys** for jet engines may exceed ten components.

### Special Categories
- **Ferrous** (Fe-containing, often magnetic) vs. **non-ferrous**.
- **Brittle elemental metals**: Be, Cr, Mn, Ga, Bi (low bulk/shear modulus ratio, limited slip systems).
- **Refractory metals**: Nb, Mo, Ta, W, Re (melting points >2000 °C, high hardness).
- **White metals**: Low-melting decorative alloys; also a British trade term for unmarked foreign silver.
- **Heavy vs. light metals**: Density-based; light structural metals (Mg 1.7, Al 2.7, Ti 4.5 g/cm³) vs. Fe (7.9), Cu (8.9).
- **Base** (easily oxidized: Fe, Ni, Pb, Zn, Cu) vs. **noble/precious** (corrosion-resistant: Au, Pt, Ag, Rh, Ir, Pd; ISO 4217 currency codes).
- **Valve metals**: Form insulating oxides allowing unidirectional current (electrochemistry).
- **Metallic ceramics** (e.g., transition metal nitrides): Ionic/covalent bonding, metallic conduction, hard but brittle; used as coatings (TiN).
- **Metallic polymers**: Conduction via extended aromatic systems (directional, like graphite).
- **Half-metals**: Conduct for one spin orientation only; ferromagnetic (e.g., Heusler alloys, oxides).
- **Semimetals**: Small valence/conduction band overlap in energy but not momentum; carriers are both electrons and holes (e.g., As, Sb, Bi, α-Sn, graphite, HgTe).

### Lifecycle: Origin, Abundance, Extraction, Recycling
- **Nucleosynthesis**: Elements up to Fe via stellar fusion; heavier via neutron capture (s-process: slow, β-decay between captures; r-process: rapid, bypasses instability at Bi to make Th, U).
- **Planetary condensation**: Stellar ejecta and neutron-star mergers enrich interstellar medium; gravitational collapse forms planets. Earth’s crust ~25% metal by weight (80% light metals: Na, Mg, Al). Lithophiles (O-affine, silicates) vs. chalcophiles (S-affine, sulfides, denser). Siderophiles (Fe-affine: Au, Pt-group) sank to core.
- **Extraction**: Mining (surface/subsurface) → concentration → reduction. Pyrometallurgy (high-T, C reductant for Fe) or hydrometallurgy (aqueous). Al, Na require electrolysis (no practical chemical reductant). Sulfides roasted to oxides first.
- **Recycling**: In-use stocks now exceed geological reserves for many metals (e.g., US Cu stock rose 73→238 g/person, 1932–1999). Al recycling saves 95% energy vs. primary. Global recycling rates low; critical rare metals (in phones, batteries, fuel cells) risk supply disruption without dramatic rate increases.

### Historical Evolution
- **Prehistory**: Native Cu, Au, Ag, meteoric Fe, Pb, brass (Cu-Zn). Meteoric Fe (Ni-bearing) surpassed industrial steel until 1880s.
- **Antiquity**: Bronze (Cu-As then Cu-Sn) enables harder tools/weapons (5th millennium BCE Iran). Steel ~1800 BCE Anatolia. Toledo steel (W/Mn) arms Rome. Pre-Columbian tumbaga (Cu-Au) and Au-Pt alloys (Ecuador).
- **Middle Ages/Renaissance**: Alchemical sulfur/mercury theory. As, Zn, Sb, Bi isolated (initially "semimetals"). Biringuccio (*De la Pirotechnia*, 1540) and Agricola (*De Re Metallica*, 1556) systematize metallurgy.
- **Light metals (post-1809)**: Na, K, Sr challenge "heaviness" dogma. Al (1824) industrialized 1886; WWI airframes drive demand. Ti (pure 1910) used in 1950s Soviet military/subs, then US jets (F-100, A-12, SR-71). Sc (1937) → Al-Sc alloys (1971).
- **Age of steel**: Bessemer (1855) → cheap mild steel replaces wrought iron. Gilchrist-Thomas (basic liner) removes P. Stainless industrialized 1912 (Cr passivation known 1821).
- **Last stable elements**: Lu (71, 1906), Re (75, 1908/1925), Hf (72, 1922—last stable discovered).
- **Post-WWII**: Transuranics (Np, Pu, Cm, Am, 1940–44) via Manhattan Project. Superalloys for turbines. Metallic glasses (1960, Au₇₅Si₂₅); amorphous steel (3× strength). Shape-memory alloys (Au-Cd 1932, Ni-Ti 1962 → robotics/biomed). Quasicrystals (Shechtman, Al-Mn 1984, 5-fold symmetry; Nobel 2011); ceramic-like properties, low friction. Complex metallic alloys (large unit cells, icosahedral clusters; heat insulation, thermoelectrics). High-entropy alloys (5+ near-equal elements; high entropy of mixing; superior strength/weight, fracture/corrosion resistance; research surge 2010s). MAX phases (M=early TM, A=IIIA/IVA, X=C/N; conductive, machinable, thermal shock resistant; nuclear/turbine coatings).

## Terms
- ****Fermi level**** — The highest energy level occupied by electrons at absolute zero; availability of empty states here enables metallic conduction.
- ****Metallic bonding**** — Non-directional electrostatic attraction between a lattice of positive ions and a delocalized "sea" of electrons; responsible for ductility and conductivity.
- ****Burgers vector**** — A vector describing the magnitude and direction of a lattice dislocation; small vectors in close-packed metals lower the energy for plastic deformation.
- ****Passivation**** — Formation of a thin, adherent oxide layer that acts as a diffusion barrier, halting further corrosion (e.g., Cr₂O₃ on stainless steel, Al₂O₃ on aluminium).
- ****Intermetallic compound**** — An alloy of fixed stoichiometric ratio (e.g., TiSi₂), distinct from variable-composition solid solutions.
- ****Superalloy**** — High-performance alloy (Fe/Ni/Co/Cr-based) retaining strength >650 °C with oxidation resistance; used in turbines.
- ****Metallic glass**** — Amorphous (non-crystalline) solid alloy with metallic conductivity; formed by extreme cooling rates or vapor deposition.
- ****Shape-memory alloy (SMA)**** — Alloy that returns to a memorized shape upon heating (e.g., Ni-Ti); also ferromagnetic variants (FSMA) actuated by magnetic fields.
- ****Quasicrystal**** — Ordered but non-periodic structure with forbidden symmetries (e.g., 5-fold); infinite unit cell, often ceramic-like properties.
- ****High-entropy alloy (HEA)**** — Alloy of five or more principal elements in near-equal proportions; high configurational entropy stabilizes simple solid-solution phases with exceptional properties.

## Debates and open questions
- **Oganesson’s nature**: DFT calculations predict a semiconductor, conflicting with the trend of heavy congeners being metallic; bulk production impossible to verify.
- **High-pressure metallization**: All elements predicted to metallize at sufficient pressure, but experimental validation for many remains elusive.
- **Recycling viability**: Critical rare metals (e.g., in batteries, phones, fuel cells) have very low end-of-life recycling rates; whether economic/technological shifts can raise them fast enough to avoid supply crises is unresolved.
- **HEA optimization**: Vast compositional space of high-entropy alloys is largely unexplored; predictive models for phase stability and properties are still maturing.
- **Metallic glass scaling**: Bulk metallic glasses with high strength exist, but processing large sections without crystallization remains a manufacturing challenge.
