# William the Conqueror

## Overview
William the Conqueror (c. 1028–1087) was the first Norman king of England, whose 1066 invasion and victory at the Battle of Hastings fundamentally reshaped English society, government, and culture. His reign established a new Norman aristocracy, introduced feudal military obligations, and initiated the compilation of the Domesday Book, a comprehensive survey of English landholdings. His legacy is a subject of enduring historical debate, viewed variously as a transformative creator of medieval England or as an imposing conqueror.

## Timeline

- **1035** — Becomes Duke of Normandy after his father's death on pilgrimage.
- **1047** — Defeats rebels at the Battle of Val-ès-Dunes, beginning his consolidation of power.
- **1050s** — Marries Matilda of Flanders, securing a powerful ally.
- **1062** — Secures control of the County of Maine.
- **1066** — Invades England after the death of Edward the Confessor.
- **14 October 1066** — Defeats and kills Harold Godwinson at the Battle of Hastings.
- **25 December 1066** — Crowned King of England at Westminster Abbey.
- **1069–1070** — Suppresses northern English rebellions in the "Harrying of the North."
- **1086** — Orders the compilation of the Domesday Book.
- **9 September 1087** — Dies in Rouen, Normandy, during a campaign in northern France.

## Body

### Early Life and Duke of Normandy
William was the illegitimate son of Duke Robert I of Normandy. He succeeded his father as duke in 1035 at age seven or eight, facing immediate anarchy as Norman nobles fought for control of the child ruler. With support from his great-uncle Archbishop Robert and King Henry I of France, he survived assassination attempts and quashed a major rebellion at Val-ès-Dunes in 1047. Through persistent warfare against rebellious nobles and external threats from the Counts of Anjou and the French king, he consolidated his authority by about 1060. His strategic marriage to Matilda of Flanders in the 1050s provided a crucial ally and strengthened his position.

### Claim to England and the Invasion
In the 1050s, William became a contender for the English throne held by his childless first cousin once removed, Edward the Confessor. After Edward's death in January 1066, the powerful English earl Harold Godwinson was crowned king. William argued that Edward had previously promised him the throne and that Harold had sworn to support his claim. He assembled a large Franco-Norman invasion force, which landed in England on 28 September 1066. On 14 October, William decisively defeated Harold's army at the Battle of Hastings, where Harold was killed. William marched on London, securing submissions from English leaders, and was crowned king on Christmas Day 1066.

### Consolidation of Power in England
William's early years as king were marked by repeated English rebellions. He responded by building a network of castles, including the White Tower in London, to secure his control. He replaced most of the native Anglo-Saxon aristocracy with Norman and continental followers, granting them lands in exchange for military service organized through fixed quotas of knights. A major rebellion in the north in 1069–1070, supported by Danish invaders, was brutally suppressed in the "Harrying of the North." William also reformed the English Church, deposing native bishops and abbots and appointing Norman clerics like Lanfranc as Archbishop of Canterbury.

### Later Reign and Domesday Book
William's later years were troubled by conflicts with his continental enemies, his rebellious eldest son Robert Curthose, and Danish threats. In 1086, he ordered the compilation of the Domesday Book, a comprehensive survey of English landholdings, their value, and their pre-Conquest and current holders. This survey, completed in 1086, was likely intended to justify taxation and record feudal obligations. William died in September 1087 from an injury sustained during a campaign in northern France. His lands were divided: Normandy went to his eldest son Robert, and England to his second surviving son, William Rufus.

## Terms
- **Feudalism**: The social and legal system where land is held in exchange for military service and loyalty to a lord.
- **Subinfeudation**: The process by which a tenant grants part of their land to a sub-tenant in exchange for service.
- **Danegeld**: A land tax originally levied to pay off Viking raiders, which William continued to collect.
- **Domesday Book**: A great survey of England ordered by William in 1086, recording landholdings, resources, and values.
- **Harrying of the North**: William's devastating 1069–1070 campaign to suppress rebellion in northern England.
- **Housecarls**: Elite, professional soldiers in Anglo-Saxon England, forming the core of a lord's military force.
- **Hide**: An Anglo-Saxon unit of land measurement used for taxation and military obligations.
- **Sheriff**: A royal official responsible for administering a shire (county), collecting taxes, and maintaining order.
- **Writ**: A written administrative order or document, a key feature of Anglo-Saxon and later Norman English government.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Papal Support**: The extent of Pope Alexander II's pre-invasion endorsement of William's conquest is debated; some sources claim he provided a papal banner, while others suggest approval came only after the victory.
- **Harold's Oath**: Whether Harold Godwinson swore a binding oath to support William's claim to the English throne is a central point of contention between Norman and English sources.
- **Impact of the Conquest**: Historians disagree on the radicalism of the changes brought by the Conquest. Some, like Richard Southern, see it as a profound transformation, while others, like H. G. Richardson, view the changes as less abrupt.
- **The New Forest**: The extent to which William depopulated the area to create his hunting ground is debated; modern studies suggest the region was already sparsely settled.
