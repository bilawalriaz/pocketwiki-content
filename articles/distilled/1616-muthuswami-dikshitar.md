# Muthuswami Dikshitar

## Overview
Muthuswami Dikshitar (1776–1835) was a South Indian poet, vocalist, veena player, and composer who forms the Trinity of Carnatic music alongside Tyagaraja and Syama Sastri. His roughly 500 compositions, predominantly in Sanskrit, are distinguished by their scholarly integration of temple architecture, Hindu iconography, Advaita Vedanta philosophy, and Jyotisha (astrology) within the *Kriti* format. A master of the *vainika* (veena) style, he captured the essence of *ragas* through intricate *gamakas* (ornamental oscillations) and typically employed a slow tempo (*chowka kala*). His exposure to Western bands at Fort St. George led to the innovative *nottusvara sahitya*—Sanskrit lyrics set to Celtic and Baroque melodies—while his adherence to the Venkatamakhin *Asampurna Mela* system preserved numerous rare *ragas*.

## Timeline
- **24 Mar 1776** — Born in Tiruvarur, Tamil Nadu, into an Auttara Vadama Brahmin family.
- **c. 1780s** — Educated by father Ramaswami Dikshitar in Vedas, music, astronomy, and the Venkatamakhin raga system.
- **c. 1790s** — Moves to Manali; exposed to Western orchestral music and violin at Fort St. George.
- **c. 1790s–1800s** — Studies music, yoga, philosophy, and Hindustani Dhrupad under Chidambaranatha Yogi in Varanasi (Benares).
- **c. 1800** — Legendary initiation at Tiruttani: Murugan places sugar candy in his mouth; adopts *mudra* "Guruguha".
- **c. 1800–1820** — Extensive pilgrimage composing *Kritis* at major temples (Varanasi, Tirupati, Kanchipuram, Chidambaram, etc.).
- **c. 1820s** — Returns to Tiruvarur; composes *Kamalamba Navavarna Kritis*, *Navagraha Kritis*, and *Nilotpalamba Kritis*.
- **21 Oct 1835** — Dies on Deepavali in Ettayapuram; *samadhi* erected.
- **1904** — Grandson Subbarama Dikshitar publishes *Sangeeta Sampradaya Pradarshini*, recording 229 *Kritis*.

## Body

### Family and Early Life
Dikshitar was born in Tiruvarur near Thanjavur to Ramaswami Dikshitar (b. c. 1735), a scholar from Virinchipuram who had migrated south due to political turbulence in the Kanchipuram region. The family belonged to the Auttara Vadama Tamil Brahmin community. Dikshitar had two brothers—Chinnaswami (1778–c. 1823) and Balaswami (1786–1858)—and a sister, Balamba. His name *Muthuswami* ("pearl" in Tamil) is linked to the deity Selvamuthukumaraswamy of Vaitheeswaran Koil, though his compositional signature (*mudra*) is uniformly "Guruguha," a name for the deity Murugan.

### Education and Formative Influences
Ramaswami Dikshitar, trained in the *veena* lineage of Govinda Dikshitar and Venkatamakhin, instructed his son in the Vedas, poetry, music, astronomy, and the Venkatamakhin *raga* system. A pivotal relocation to Manali, sponsored by the *zamindar* Venkatakrishna Mudaliar, brought the brothers to Fort St. George (Madras). There, they encountered Western orchestral music and the violin—an encounter that later birthed the *nottusvara* corpus and prompted Balaswami to pioneer the violin in Carnatic music. Subsequently, the ascetic Chidambaranatha Yogi took Dikshitar to Varanasi (Benares), where he studied music, esotericism, philosophy, yoga, and Hindustani classical music, specifically the *Dhrupad* style, which scholars suggest influenced his compositional gravity.

### Career and Major Compositional Groups
Dikshitar’s first composition, *Śrināthādi guruguho jayati jayati* (raga Mayamalavagaula), initiated a career defined by temple pilgrimage. He composed at sites including Varanasi, Tirupati, Srirangam, Chidambaram, Kanchipuram, Thanjavur, Palani, Tiruvannamalai, and Kalahasthi. Upon returning to Tiruvarur, he exhaustively catalogued the deities of the Tyagaraja Temple complex—Tyagaraja (Shiva), Nilotpalambal, and the tantrically significant Kamalambal—producing the *Kamalamba Navavarna Kritis* (nine *avarana* songs in all eight Sanskrit declensions). Other major grouped works include the *Navagraha Kritis* (nine planets, integrating *Mantra* and *Jyotisha sastras*), *Abhayamba Vibhakti Kritis*, *Guruguha Vibhakti Kritis*, and *Panchabhuta Sthala Kritis* (five elements). *Vatapi Ganapatim* (raga Hamsadhvani) remains his most widely recognized piece. He also composed the *Nilotpalamba Kritis*, reviving near-extinct *ragas* like Narayanagaula and Purvagaula.

### Technical Mastery and Innovations
Dikshitar is the only composer on record with *Kritis* in all seven basic *Talas* (rhythmic cycles) of the Carnatic scheme. He composed in all 72 *Melakarta ragas* under the *Asampurna Mela* scheme, preserving rare *ragas* such as Chayagaula. He pioneered the *Samashti Charanam* format, condensing the standard two-stanza structure into a single stanza after the *Pallavi* (refrain). His Sanskrit lyrics exhibit sophisticated alliteration (*prasa*) and utilize all eight grammatical declensions (*vibhaktis*). A proficient *vainika* (veena player), he signed compositions as *vainika gayaka*; the *veena*’s portamento technique directly informs the pervasive *gamakas* in his vocal writing.

### Western Influence and *Nottusvara Sahitya*
Exposure to British military bands at Fort St. George inspired approximately forty *nottusvara sahitya* compositions—Sanskrit lyrics fitted to Western folk tunes (Celtic and Baroque) mapped onto *ragas* like Shankarabharanam. A notable example is *Sakthi Sahitha Ganapatim*, based on the tune "Voulez-vous Dancer." The source explicitly debunks the claim that these were commissioned by C.P. Brown (Collector of Cuddapah), noting Dikshitar left Madras by 1799, whereas Brown arrived in 1817.

### Death, Disciples, and Legacy
Dikshitar died on Deepavali (21 October 1835) in Ettayapuram, where a *samadhi* stands. Childless, his lineage continued through disciples: the Tanjore Quartet (Ponnayya, Vadivelu, Chinnayya, Sivanandam), who codified Bharatanatyam music; *mridangam* player Tambiyappa; *veena* player Venkatarama Ayyar; vocalists Tiruvarur Kamalam, Vallalarkoil Ammani, and Subbaraya Sastri (son of Syama Sastri). His brothers Chinnaswami and Balaswami performed as a vocal duo propagating his works; Balaswami’s grandson, Subbarama Dikshitar (1839–1906), documented 229 *Kritis* in the *Sangeeta Sampradaya Pradarshini*. Modern tributes include compositions by M. Balamuralikrishna (raga Sucharitra) and Koteeswara Iyer (raga Devamanohari).

## Terms
- ****Carnatic music**** — The classical music tradition of South India, characterized by *raga* (melodic framework) and *tala* (rhythmic cycle).
- ****Trinity of Carnatic music**** — The three preeminent 18th–19th century composer-saints: Tyagaraja, Syama Sastri, and Muthuswami Dikshitar, all from Tiruvarur.
- ****Kriti**** — The standard compositional form in Carnatic music, typically comprising *Pallavi* (refrain), *Anupallavi*, and *Charanam* (stanzas).
- ****Raga**** — A melodic framework for improvisation and composition defined by specific ascending/descending notes and characteristic phrases.
- ****Tala**** — A cyclic rhythmic meter; the Carnatic system recognizes seven basic *talas* (e.g., *Adi*, *Rupaka*).
- ****Gamaka**** — Ornamental oscillation or glide between notes, essential for expressing a *raga*’s identity; central to the *vainika* style.
- ****Mudra**** — A composer’s signature pseudonym embedded in lyrics; Dikshitar used "Guruguha" (a name for Murugan).
- ****Asampurna Mela**** — The 72-*melakarta* raga classification system of Venkatamakhin (17th c.), which allows "incomplete" scales, distinct from the later *Sampurna* scheme.
- ****Nottusvara sahitya**** — Dikshitar’s ~40 compositions setting Sanskrit lyrics to Western (Celtic/Baroque) tunes; *nottusvara* = "notes" (*swara*).
- ****Samashti Charanam**** — A structural innovation by Dikshitar: a *Kriti* with only one *Charanam* stanza after the *Pallavi*, omitting the *Anupallavi*.

## Debates and open questions
- **Hindustani influence:** Scholars debate the extent to which Dikshitar’s Varanasi studies under Chidambaranatha Yogi—specifically exposure to *Dhrupad*—shaped his slow tempo (*chowka kala*), *gamaka* density, and structural gravity.
- **Authorship of *Rama Ashtapadi*:** Tradition holds Dikshitar co-composed a *Rama Ashtapadi* with Upanishad Brahmendral at Kanchipuram, but the work is lost; its existence and scope remain unverified.
- **C.P. Brown commission myth:** The source refutes the claim that *nottusvara* pieces were commissioned by Collector C.P. Brown, citing irreconcilable chronology (Dikshitar left Madras by 1799; Brown arrived 1817), yet the misconception persists in secondary literature.
- **Exact composition count:** While "around 500" is the accepted estimate, Subbarama Dikshitar’s 1904 *Pradarshini* records only 229; the total relies on oral transmission and manuscript discovery, leaving the definitive canon open.
