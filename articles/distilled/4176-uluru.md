# Uluru

## Overview
Uluru is a massive sandstone monolith in Australia's Northern Territory, sacred to the local Aṉangu people and a UNESCO World Heritage Site. It is a globally recognized natural landmark and a focal point for discussions on indigenous land rights, cultural preservation, and tourism management.

## Timeline
- **c. 550–530 Ma** — The Mutitjulu Arkose sandstone is deposited during the Petermann Orogeny.
- **c. 400–300 Ma** — The rock strata are tilted to near-vertical during the Alice Springs Orogeny.
- **>10,000 years ago** — Archaeological evidence indicates human settlement in the area.
- **1872** — Ernest Giles sights Kata Tjuta (Mount Olga).
- **1873** — William Gosse sights and names the landmark Ayers Rock.
- **1920** — Part of the area is declared an Aboriginal Reserve.
- **1936** — The first tourists arrive.
- **1958** — The area is excised from the reserve and named Ayers Rock–Mount Olga National Park.
- **1985** — Ownership of Uluru is returned to the Pitjantjatjara people, with a 99-year leaseback agreement.
- **1993** — Official dual naming as "Ayers Rock / Uluru" is adopted.
- **2002** — The name order is reversed to "Uluru / Ayers Rock".
- **2019** — Climbing Uluru is officially banned.

## Body

### Name and Description
The local Aṉangu (Pitjantjatjara people) call the landmark Uluṟu, a proper noun with no further meaning. European surveyor William Gosse named it Ayers Rock in 1873. A dual-naming policy was adopted in 1993, with the order officially reversed to "Uluru / Ayers Rock" in 2002. The sandstone formation stands 348 m high, has a perimeter of 9.4 km, and is notable for appearing to change colour, especially glowing red at dawn and sunset due to iron oxide. It is located 25 km east of the Kata Tjuta formation.

### History and Ownership
Humans have lived in the area for over 10,000 years. Europeans first mapped the area in the 1870s. Pastoral attempts in the late 19th century led to conflict and resource depletion. The area was declared an Aboriginal Reserve in 1920. Tourism began in 1936, leading to infrastructure development. In 1958, the national park was established. A pivotal moment came on 26 October 1985, when the Australian government returned ownership to the Pitjantjatjara people, who leased it back for 99 years of joint management.

### Tourism and Climbing
Tourism infrastructure was initially built at the base but was relocated to Yulara in the 1970s. Visitor numbers exceeded 400,000 by 2000. The Aṉangu have long requested visitors not climb Uluru due to its spiritual significance and safety concerns. A chain handhold was added in 1963. Despite a 2010 management plan condition to close the climb being met by 2013, it remained open. Following controversial incidents and a unanimous board vote in 2017, climbing was officially banned on 26 October 2019.

### Geology
Uluru is an inselberg, an isolated hill rising from a flat plain. It is composed of coarse-grained arkose (a feldspar-rich sandstone) and some conglomerate, known as the Mutitjulu Arkose. The rock was deposited as part of an alluvial fan from ancient mountains to the south. Its strata are nearly vertical, tilted during a later mountain-building event. The rock's homogeneity and lack of joints have allowed it to resist erosion while surrounding rocks wore away.

### Aboriginal Culture and Traditions
Uluru is central to Aṉangu Tjukurpa (Dreaming) stories. The land is believed to be inhabited by ancestral creator beings. Major stories explain the rock's creation and features, such as the Mala (rufous hare-wallaby) people's story involving a devil-dog named Kurpany. The Aṉangu request that visitors do not photograph certain sections linked to gender-specific ceremonies. They also believe misfortune will befall those who remove rocks from the site.

### Flora, Fauna, and Climate
The park has a hot desert climate with an average annual rainfall of 284.6 mm. The Aṉangu recognize five seasons. Historically, 46 native mammal species lived near Uluru; now 21 remain. The park hosts 73 reptile species and rare plants, many dependent on irregular rainfall. Introduced species like camels and foxes are present. Flora includes trees (punu), shrubs (puti), and grasses (ukiri), many with cultural significance.

## Terms
*   **Aṉangu:** The local Aboriginal people of the area, specifically the Pitjantjatjara.
*   **Arkose:** A type of sandstone characterized by an abundance of feldspar.
*   **Inselberg:** A prominent, isolated residual hill that rises abruptly from a surrounding plain.
*   **Monolith:** A large single continuous block of stone; a term often avoided by geologists for Uluru.
*   **Mutitjulu Arkose:** The specific geological name for the rock strata making up Uluru.
*   **Tjukurpa:** The Aṉangu term for Dreaming; the foundation of their law, religion, and knowledge.
*   **Petermann Orogeny:** A mountain-building episode (c. 550–530 Ma) that created the source mountains for Uluru's sand.
*   **Alice Springs Orogeny:** A later mountain-building event (c. 400–300 Ma) that tilted Uluru's strata.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Climbing Ban:** Public opinion was divided, with a 2017 poll finding 63% opposed the ban and a 2019 poll showing 44% in support.
*   **Sacred Site Photography:** The Aṉangu request to not photograph certain areas creates a tension between cultural respect and tourist documentation.
*   **Balancing Tourism and Conservation:** Managing over 400,000 annual visitors while protecting cultural values and the fragile environment remains an ongoing challenge.
