# Music genre

## Overview
A music genre is a conventional category that groups pieces of music based on shared traditions, conventions, or characteristics. It is a fundamental concept for organizing and understanding music, though classifications are often subjective and can overlap. Genres help listeners, artists, and scholars navigate the vast landscape of musical creation, from broad categories like "art music" and "popular music" to thousands of specific subgenres.

## Body

### Definitions and Distinctions
A music genre is a category identifying pieces with shared conventions. It is distinct from musical form (the structure of a piece) and musical style (the characteristic sound), though these terms are sometimes used interchangeably. Douglass M. Green clarified this by noting that two violin concertos (same genre) can have different forms, while two pieces with similar forms (like a rondo and a mass movement) can be different genres. Franco Fabbri's 1982 definition, now considered normative, describes a genre as "a set of musical events... governed by a definite set of socially accepted rules." Genres may be defined by musical techniques, cultural context, themes, or geographical origin.

### Subtypes and Categorization
Genres proliferate into subgenres (subcategories with distinct characteristics), fusion genres (blending two or more parent genres, like jazz fusion), and microgenres (niche categories). The 20th century saw the creation of over 1,200 definable subgenres. New genres typically emerge from the influence of pre-existing ones. Philip Tagg proposed a common trichotomy of "folk," "art," and "popular" music, distinguishing them by criteria like distribution methods and production patterns. Alternative approaches, like Caporaletti's Audiotactile Music Theory, classify music based on the "formative medium" (the creative interface), separating written music, oral music, and audiotactile music (produced via recording technology).

### Major Genre Categories
**Art music** (or classical music) emphasizes formal styles and technical detail, demanding focused listening. In the West, it is primarily a written tradition preserved in notation, with the identity of a "work" defined by the score. **Popular music** is conceived for mass distribution to heterogeneous audiences, stored in non-written form, and exists within an industrial monetary economy. The boundary between art and popular music can blur. **Traditional and folk music** are closely related; folk music is orally transmitted across generations, often with unknown authors and multiple versions. Traditional music encompasses folk music and refers to songs performed over long periods. **Religious music** is composed for religious use or influence.

### Psychology of Music Preference
Music preference is influenced by social identity and personality; for example, self-identified "rebels" may prefer heavier music. A model identifies five genre-free factors underlying preference: Mellow (smooth/relaxing), Urban (rhythmic/percussive), Sophisticated (operatic/world), Intensity (forceful/loud), and Campestral (singer-songwriter/country). Individual factors like gender (men may prefer bass-heavy music) and age (adolescents often prefer pop, while older adults prefer rock, opera, and jazz) also play a role.

## Terms
- **Genre**: A conventional category grouping pieces of music with shared traditions or conventions.
- **Subgenre**: A subordinate category within a genre that adopts its basic characteristics but has its own distinct features.
- **Fusion genre**: A genre that blends characteristics from two or more parent genres.
- **Microgenre**: A niche genre or a subcategory within major genres or their subgenres.
- **Art music**: Music emphasizing formal styles and technical detail, often preserved in written notation.
- **Popular music**: Music conceived for mass distribution and dissemination via mass media.
- **Folk music**: Music orally passed from generation to generation, often with unknown authors.
- **Formative medium**: The creative interface (e.g., visual notation vs. audiotactile recording) used to create music, as proposed by Caporaletti.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The distinction between genre and style is debated; some scholars treat them as synonymous, while others see them as separate concepts.
- The boundaries between major categories like "art music" and "popular music" are often blurred (e.g., minimalist music, film scores).
- The subjective and controversial nature of genre classification means categories can overlap and are constantly evolving as novel music is created.
- The role of technology (e.g., sound recording, digital distribution) in creating new genres and altering traditional transmission methods is a key area of study.
