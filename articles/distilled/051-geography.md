# Geography

## Overview

Geography (from Ancient Greek *geōgraphía*, combining *gê* 'Earth' and *gráphō* 'write', lit. 'Earth writing') is the study of the lands, features, inhabitants, and phenomena of planet Earth. It is an all-encompassing discipline that seeks an understanding of Earth's human and natural complexities—not merely where objects are, but also how they have changed and come to be. Geography has been called "a bridge between natural science and social science disciplines," and its core concepts—space, place, time, and scale—are consistent across all approaches. The main branches are physical geography (the natural environment), human geography (how humans interact with the Earth), and technical geography (developing tools for understanding geography).

## Timeline

- **9th century BC** — Earliest attempted world map in ancient Babylon
- **c. 610–545 BC** — Anaximander, considered by later Greek writers the true founder of geography, credited with inventing the gnomon
- **c. 276 BC – c. 195/194 BC** — Eratosthenes of Cyrene may have coined the term "geographia"
- **600 BC** — Imago Mundi, the best-known Babylonian world map, created

- **976–1048** — Abu Rayhan Biruni develops new trigonometric methods for measuring Earth's circumference
- **1650** — *Geographia Generalis* by Bernhardus Varenius published, seen by some as the division between ancient and modern geography in the West
- **1760** — John Harrison invents the chronometer H-4, solving the longitude problem
- **1821–1888** — Major geographic societies founded (Société de Géographie, Royal Geographical Society, National Geographic Society, etc.)
- **1950s–60s** — Quantitative revolution revitalizes geography with scientific testing and laws
- **1962** — William Bunge's *Theoretical Geography* published, described as "perhaps the seminal text of the spatial-quantitative revolution"
- **1970** — Waldo Tobler proposes the first law of geography

## Body

### Fundamentals and Core Concepts

Geography is a systematic study of the Earth and its features and phenomena. For something to fall into the domain of geography, it generally needs some spatial component that can be placed on a map, such as coordinates, place names, or addresses. Because space and place affect topics like economics, health, climate, plants, and animals, geography is highly interdisciplinary. The 1st edition of "Key Concepts in Geography" identified "Space," "Place," "Time," "Scale," and "Landscape" as key concepts; the 2nd edition added "Environmental systems," "Social Systems," "Nature," "Globalization," "Development," and "Risk." Another teaching approach is the Five themes of geography (Location, place, relationships within places, movement, and regions), established in 1984 by the National Council for Geographic Education and the Association of American Geographers.

**Space** is the most fundamental concept at geography's foundation. Absolute space is the exact site, or spatial coordinates, of objects, persons, places, or phenomena under investigation. Today, geographers are trained to recognize the world as a dynamic space where all processes interact, rather than as a static image on a map.

**Place** is one of the most complex and important terms in geography. In human geography, place is the synthesis of coordinates, the activity and use that occurs at those coordinates, and the meaning ascribed to the space by human individuals and groups. In physical geography, a place encompasses all physical phenomena occurring in space, including the lithosphere, atmosphere, hydrosphere, and biosphere. As Yi-Fu Tuan explained, geography is the study of Earth as a home for humanity, making place central to the discipline.

**Time** is a significant concern in geography because space and time are not separated in physics—they combine into spacetime. Time in geography includes modeling the dynamic movement of people, organisms, and things through space, and it constrains the possible paths that can be taken through space given a starting point, possible routes, and rate of travel.

**Scale** is the ratio between a distance measured on a map and the corresponding distance on the ground. Phenomena being investigated appear different depending on the scale used, making scale the frame geographers use to measure space and understand a place.

### Laws of Geography

During the quantitative revolution, geography shifted to an empirical law-making (nomothetic) approach. Several laws have been proposed, most notably by Waldo Tobler, though some dispute the entire concept of laws in geography and the social sciences. Proposed laws include: Tobler's first law ("Everything is related to everything else, but near things are more related than distant"), Tobler's second law ("The phenomenon external to a geographic area of interest affects what goes on inside"), Arbia's law (things observed at a coarse spatial resolution are more related than at a finer resolution), spatial heterogeneity (geographic variables exhibit uncontrolled variance), and the uncertainty principle (the geographic world is infinitely complex, and any representation must contain elements of uncertainty). An amended version, the Tobler–von Thünen law, states near things are more related "as a consequence of accessibility."

### Sub-disciplines

William D. Pattison proposed the "Four traditions of Geography" in 1964: the Spatial or Locational Tradition, the Man-Land or Human-Environment Interaction Tradition, the Area Studies or Regional Tradition, and the Earth Science Tradition.

The UNESCO Encyclopedia of Life Support Systems organizes geography into three branches. **Physical geography** (or physiography) focuses on the Earth as a science, studying seasons, climate, atmosphere, soil, streams, landforms, and oceans, and often works in identifying and monitoring natural resources. **Human geography** (or anthropogeography) studies the patterns and processes that shape human society, including political, cultural, social, and economic aspects; practitioners often work in city planning, public health, or business analysis. **Technical geography** involves studying and developing tools, techniques, and statistical methods for collecting, analysing, and understanding spatial data. It is the most recently recognized and controversial branch, though its use dates back to 1749 when Edward Cave organized the discipline into a section containing cartographic techniques and globes. Terms used interchangeably include "Geographic Information Science," "geoinformatics," "geomatics," and "information geography."

### Methods

All geographic research starts with asking "where," followed by "why there." Geographers use four interrelated approaches: Analytical (why features exist in a specific area), Descriptive (specifying locations), Regional (examining relationships for a specific region), and Systematic (grouping knowledge into globally explored categories).

**Quantitative methods** became influential during the quantitative revolution of the 1950s and 60s, allowing scientific testing of hypotheses and proposing geographic theories and laws. **Quantitative cartography** is the art, science, and technology of making maps; cartographers must learn cognitive psychology, ergonomics, geodesy, and advanced mathematics. **Geographic information systems (GIS)** store information about the Earth for accurate, automated retrieval; the science of using GIS to represent, analyse, and predict spatial relationships is called geographic information science (GISc). **Remote sensing** obtains information from a distance, using passive data (traditional photography) or active data (LiDAR), from platforms including satellites, aerial photography, and hand-held sensors. **Geostatistics** applies statistical methodology to geographic phenomena, used in hydrology, geology, petroleum exploration, weather analysis, urban planning, logistics, and epidemiology.

**Qualitative methods** are descriptive rather than numerical, adding context and exploring human concepts like beliefs and perspective. Human geography employs these more than physical geography. **Qualitative cartography** includes Chorochromatic maps of nominal data (like land cover) and deep maps that combine geography and storytelling. **Ethnography** uses participant observation and in-depth interviews, techniques also used in anthropology and sociology. **Geopoetics** combines geography and poetry to explore interconnectedness between humans, space, place, and environment, often addressing complex topics like the anthropocene. **Interviews** gather data about encounters, outlooks, and opinions concerning spatial phenomena, typically using structured or semi-structured approaches with open-ended questions.

### Origin and History

The oldest known world maps date to ancient Babylon from the 9th century BC. The best-known Babylonian map, the Imago Mundi of 600 BC, shows Babylon on the Euphrates surrounded by a circular landmass and a "bitter river" (Oceanus). Anaximander is credited with the invention of the gnomon, an instrument allowing early measurement of latitude. There is debate about who first asserted the Earth is spherical—Parmenides or Pythagoras—while Anaxagoras demonstrated the Earth's circular profile by explaining eclipses but still believed it was a flat disk. Eratosthenes made one of the first estimates of Earth's radius. Hipparchus created the first rigorous system of latitude and longitude lines, employing a sexagesimal system from Babylonian mathematics. Roman mapping provided information for Ptolemy to construct detailed atlases, using a grid system and adopting a degree length of 56.5 miles.

From the 3rd century onwards, Chinese geographical study was more comprehensive than in Europe until the 13th century, with scholars like Liu An, Pei Xiu, Jia Dan, Shen Kuo, and Xu Xiake writing important treatises. During the Middle Ages, the fall of the Roman empire shifted geography's evolution to the Islamic world. Muslim geographers like Muhammad al-Idrisi produced detailed world maps (such as Tabula Rogeriana). Abu Rayhan Biruni (976–1048) described a polar equi-azimuthal equidistant projection, developed methods of pinpointing locations by recording degrees of latitude and longitude, and calculated the Earth's radius as 6,339.9 km—only 16.8 km less than the modern value of 6,356.7 km. He developed a new method using trigonometric calculations based on the angle between a plain and mountain top, allowing measurement by a single person from a single location.

The European Age of Discovery in the 16th and 17th centuries revived desire for accurate geographic detail. In 1650, Bernhardus Varenius published *Geographia Generalis*, later edited by Isaac Newton, which sought to integrate new scientific discoveries into classical geography. John Harrison solved the longitude problem by inventing the chronometer H-4 in 1760, and in 1884 the International Meridian Conference adopted the Greenwich meridian as zero. The 18th and 19th centuries saw geography recognized as a distinct academic discipline, becoming part of university curricula in Europe (especially Paris and Berlin). Immanuel Kant, Alexander von Humboldt, Carl Ritter, and Paul Vidal de la Blache marked a turning point from philosophy to an academic subject; Humboldt and Ritter are regarded as founders of modern geography. In the 20th century, the discipline went through four major phases: environmental determinism, regional geography, the quantitative revolution, and critical geography.

### Related Fields

**Geology** and physical geography have significant overlap, often sharing academic departments. Both seek to understand rocks on the Earth's surface and the processes that change them, though geology includes research beyond the spatial component, such as chemical analysis and biogeochemistry. **History** overlaps with human geography; geography provides the spatial context within which historical events unfold, and physical features shape settlements, trade routes, and economic activities. **Planetary science** uses the term geography informally for other worlds; alternative terms like areography (geography of Mars) exist, and geography may be considered a subdiscipline within planetary science.

## Terms

- **Cartography**: The art, science, and technology of making maps
- **Geographic information system (GIS)**: Computer-based systems that store information about the Earth for accurate, automated retrieval
- **Geomatics**: A modern development in geography involving computer technology for spatial data
- **Gnomon**: A Greek instrument allowing early measurement of latitude
- **Nomothetic**: An empirical law-making approach to geography
- **Remote sensing**: Obtaining information about Earth's features from measurements made at a distance
- **Spatial heterogeneity**: The principle that geographic variables exhibit uncontrolled variance
- **Toponymy**: The study of place names

## Debates and open questions

- Whether the concept of laws in geography and the social sciences is valid at all; criticisms have been addressed by Tobler and Michael Frank Goodchild, but this remains an ongoing source of debate unlikely to be resolved soon.
- Whether geographic laws need to be numbered; the existence of a first law "invites a second," and some have proposed that Tobler's first law should be moved to second place and replaced with another.
- Who first asserted that the Earth is spherical—Parmenides or Pythagoras—remains debated.
- Narrowing geography down to a few key concepts is "extremely challenging and subject to tremendous debate within the discipline," as demonstrated by the expanding list of key concepts between editions of "Key Concepts in Geography."
- Technical geography's status as a branch is controversial, being the most recently recognized of the three branches.
