# Cardinal direction

## Overview
Cardinal directions are the four primary compass points—north, east, south, and west—used universally for navigation and spatial orientation. They form the foundation of a system of intercardinal and secondary intercardinal points, creating a 16- or 32-point compass rose. The system's significance extends beyond geography into astronomy, mathematics, and diverse cultural cosmologies, where directions often carry symbolic meaning.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Early compass use in China for geomancy (feng shui).
- **Migration Period** — Germanic names for cardinal directions enter Romance languages, replacing Latin terms.
- **Medieval Era** — Compass roses with 8 to 32 named points are used in Europe, often linked to Mediterranean winds.
- **Modern Era** — Azimuth system (degrees from north) standardizes directional measurement for navigation and GPS.

## Body

### Compass Points and Azimuths
The four cardinal directions are north (0°/360°), east (90°), south (180°), and west (270°). Between them are the four intercardinal (ordinal) directions: northeast (45°), southeast (135°), southwest (225°), and northwest (315°). Further subdivision creates eight secondary intercardinal directions (e.g., north-northeast, east-southeast), resulting in 16 named points. A 32-point compass adds tertiary intercardinal directions (e.g., north by east). Azimuth, the clockwise angle from north, is essential for trigonometric navigation and GPS.

### Cultural and Linguistic Variations
Many cultures associate cardinal directions with colors, elements, or deities. In traditional Chinese cosmology, a five-point system (including center) links directions to colors (e.g., east=green/blue, south=red), seasons, and elements. Similar five-point systems exist in Turkic, Tibetan, and Ainu cultures. In the Arabic world, directions are used for geographic subdivisions. Some languages, like Estonian and Finnish, have unique names for intercardinal directions. Indigenous Australian groups, such as the Guugu Yimithirr, use absolute cardinal directions for relative positioning.

### Beyond Geography
In astronomy, cardinal points on a celestial body are defined by the directions to the celestial poles, with the order reversed from terrestrial maps (clockwise from north: west, south, east). Directions can extend to three dimensions (up/down) or be combined with elevation for spherical coordinates. Some cultures use non-compass systems, like "toward the sea" or "upstream/downstream" (as in ancient Egypt).

## Terms
- **Cardinal directions**: The four main compass points: north, east, south, west.
- **Intercardinal directions**: The four intermediate points halfway between cardinal directions (e.g., northeast).
- **Azimuth**: The clockwise horizontal angle from north, measured in degrees (0° to 360°).
- **Compass rose**: A diagram showing the 16 or 32 named points of the compass.
- **Celestial poles**: The points where Earth's axis meets the celestial sphere, used to define astronomical cardinal points.
- **Wu Xing**: The Chinese five-element system (wood, fire, earth, metal, water) associated with cardinal directions.
- **Dikpālas**: In Hinduism, the ten deities guarding the eight cardinal and intercardinal directions plus up and down.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise origin and dating of the compass in China is debated, with early use possibly for geomancy before navigation.
- The extent to which Germanic direction names influenced Romance languages during the Migration Period is a subject of historical linguistic study.
- Cultural variations in direction symbolism (e.g., color associations) show significant diversity even among neighboring groups, raising questions about diffusion versus independent development.
