# National anthem

## Overview
A national anthem is a patriotic musical composition that symbolizes a country's history and traditions. Most are marches or hymns, and their adoption often marks national identity, independence, or political change. The practice became widespread in the 19th and 20th centuries, especially with new nations, and is now a key element of international diplomacy and sporting events.

## Timeline
- **1619** — "God Save the King/Queen" first performed as a royal anthem.
- **1770** — "La Marcha Real" adopted as the royal anthem of Spain.
- **1795** — France adopts "La Marseillaise" by decree, becoming the first country with an official national anthem.
- **1813** — Argentina adopts its national anthem, early in the Latin American wars of independence.
- **1920** — The Olympic Charter introduces the ritual of playing gold medal winners' national anthems.
- **1931** — The United States officially adopts "The Star-Spangled Banner."
- **1963** — Kenya commissions its national anthem, "Ee Mungu Nguvu Yetu," upon independence.
- **1993** — Luxembourg officially adopts its long-standing de facto anthem, "Ons Heemecht."
- **2004** — Israel officially adopts "Hatikvah" as its national anthem.
- **2017** — Italy grants de jure status to "Il Canto degli Italiani" as its national anthem.

## Body

### History and Adoption
The concept evolved from royal anthems used by European monarchies, such as "God Save the King/Queen" (1619) and "La Marcha Real" (1770). France pioneered the official national anthem with "La Marseillaise" in 1795. Adoption accelerated in the 19th century with newly independent states, particularly in Latin America and Europe. The 20th century saw anthems become standard for new nations, especially during decolonization in the 1960s, sometimes via official commissions. The 1920 Olympic Charter, which began playing anthems for gold medalists, created a strong incentive for countries to formalize an anthem. Some nations, like the United Kingdom and Sweden, still use established de facto anthems without de jure status.

### Usage and Etiquette
National anthems are performed at state occasions, national holidays, and diplomatic events, with specific etiquette like standing at attention. They are deeply connected to sports, a practice started by Wales in a 1905 rugby match and formalized by the Olympics. In many countries, anthems are broadcast daily on radio and television or played in schools. Their use can be legally mandated, as in Colombia and Thailand. Interpretation can vary within a country, and international recognition affects their use; for example, Taiwan competes as "Chinese Taipei" in the Olympics and uses a different song.

### Creators and Languages
Most famous anthems were composed by relatively unknown figures. Exceptions include melodies by renowned composers like Joseph Haydn (Germany) and Charles Gounod (Vatican City). Nobel laureate Rabindranath Tagore wrote the anthems for both India and Bangladesh. Lyrics are typically in the national language, but multilingual countries often have multiple versions (e.g., Switzerland, Canada, South Africa). Some anthems, like Spain's "Marcha Real," have no official lyrics. Unique cases include Singapore's anthem, which must be sung in Malay by law, and Pakistan's, which is almost entirely in Persian.

## Terms
- **National Anthem**: A patriotic musical composition symbolizing a country's history and traditions.
- **Royal Anthem**: A musical composition for a monarch or royal family, sometimes distinct from the national anthem.
- **De Facto Anthem**: An anthem used in practice at events but not officially adopted by law.
- **De Jure Anthem**: An anthem officially adopted by law or constitution.
- **Decolonization**: The process of colonies gaining independence, often leading to the adoption of new national symbols like anthems.
- **Olympic Charter**: The set of rules and guidelines for the Olympic Games, which introduced the playing of national anthems at medal ceremonies in 1920.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The authorship of "God Save the King/Queen" remains unknown and disputed.
- Some nations, like the UK and Sweden, maintain de facto anthems without official legal status, raising questions about formal recognition.
- The choice of language for an anthem can be contentious in multilingual societies, as seen with debates over the use of Irish in Ireland's anthem or the exclusive Malay lyrics of Singapore's anthem.
- The use of national anthems in international sports can be politically sensitive, as illustrated by the compromise "Arirang" used by the unified Korean team in 2018.
