# Benin City

## Overview
Benin City is the capital of Edo State in southern Nigeria and the country's fourth-largest city. It is historically significant as the heart of the ancient Benin Empire, a powerful and wealthy kingdom known for its sophisticated art, governance, and trade. The city's history encompasses a pre-colonial golden age, European contact, a devastating British punitive expedition, and its modern role as a commercial and cultural center.

## Timeline
- **c. 13th century** — Benin imperialism begins under Oba Ewedo.
- **c. 1470** — Oba Ewuare changes the state's name to Ẹdo.
- **c. 1485** — The Portuguese first visit Benin City.
- **1691** — Portuguese captain Lourenco Pinto describes a large, well-governed city.
- **1701/1702** — Dutch merchant David van Nyendael describes the city as a "mere village," noting decline.
- **1820** — British officer John King notes the city's walls are largely destroyed and it is depopulated.
- **17 February 1897** — Benin City falls to the British during the "Punitive Expedition."
- **1914** — The British permit the restoration of the Benin monarchy.
- **1960** — Nigeria gains independence; Benin City becomes capital of the Mid-Western Region (1963).
- **1991** — Benin City becomes the capital of the newly created Edo State.

## Body

### History and European Contact
The Benin Empire grew wealthy in the 16th and 17th centuries through trade in pepper, ivory, and other goods with Europeans, primarily the Portuguese. Early European accounts, like that of Lourenco Pinto in 1691, described a magnificent, orderly city larger than Lisbon. However, later accounts, such as David van Nyendael's in 1701/1702, noted a significant decline, describing it as a "mere village" with many half-ruined houses. By the time of the British Punitive Expedition in 1897, the city had shrunk considerably. The expedition, commanded by Admiral Sir Harry Rawson, captured and sacked the city after a previous British delegation was ambushed. This event paved the way for British colonization. The British later restored the monarchy in 1914, but political power remained with the colonial administration.

### The Edo People and Founding Legends
The indigenous people are the Edo, who speak the Edo language. Tradition holds that the kingdom was originally ruled by the Ogiso dynasty. After a succession crisis, the exiled prince Ekaladerhan (known in Ife as Oduduwa) sent his son Ọranmiyan to rule. Ọranmiyan married a local woman, Ẹrinmwide, and their son Ẹwẹka I became the first Oba (king) of the current dynasty. Ọranmiyan renamed the capital "Ubinu," which the Portuguese later rendered as "Benin." The state was renamed Ẹdo around 1470 by Oba Ewuare.

### Economy and Environment
Benin City is a major commercial and industrial center, particularly for Nigeria's rubber industry, hosting processing plants and the Rubber Research Institute. Agriculture, including palm oil and kernel exports, is vital, supported by the Nigerian Institute for Oil Palm Research. Other industries include furniture, brewing, and soft drinks. The city faces environmental challenges, including regular flooding exacerbated by poor storm drains, an urban heat island effect making it warmer than surrounding areas, and inadequate waste management practices that residents often do not fully understand.

### Culture and Art
The city is famed for the "Benin Bronzes," intricate brass sculptures and plaques looted by the British in 1897 and now held in museums worldwide, with ongoing calls for their return. The Oba's Palace and Igun Street (a center for bronze casting) are key attractions. The annual Igue festival, held between Christmas and New Year, is a major cultural event believed to renew the Oba's powers. The Benin City Film Festival also promotes local and international filmmakers.

## Terms
- **Oba**: The traditional king and paramount ruler of the Benin Kingdom.
- **Ogiso**: The title of the early kings of Benin before the current Oba dynasty.
- **Edo/Bini Language**: The indigenous language spoken by the people of Benin City.
- **Benin Bronzes**: A large group of metal plaques and sculptures created in Benin, looted by the British in 1897.
- **Punitive Expedition**: The 1897 British military campaign that captured and sacked Benin City.
- **Igue Festival**: An annual Benin cultural festival held to usher in the new year and renew the Oba's powers.
- **Köppen Am/Aw**: Climate classification codes; Am is tropical monsoon, Aw is tropical savanna.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The historical accounts of Benin City's size and condition vary significantly between different European visitors (e.g., Pinto vs. van Nyendael), reflecting possible periods of growth and decline or differing observer perspectives.
- The practice of human sacrifice in the Benin state is noted in some firsthand accounts (like Alan Boisragon's) but is debated by historians. James D. Graham states it was "an integral part" of the state religion, but acknowledges that firsthand accounts varied significantly.
- The origins and identity of the legendary figure Oduduwa are interpreted differently by Edo and Yoruba traditions, though both agree he sent his son Oranmiyan to found the Benin dynasty.
