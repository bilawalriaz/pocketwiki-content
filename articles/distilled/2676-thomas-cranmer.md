# Thomas Cranmer

## Overview
Thomas Cranmer (1489–1556) was a leading theologian of the English Reformation and Archbishop of Canterbury under Henry VIII, Edward VI, and briefly Mary I. He was instrumental in establishing the reformed Church of England, creating its foundational liturgy and doctrine. His legacy endures through the Book of Common Prayer and the Thirty-nine Articles, and he is venerated as a martyr in the Church of England.

## Timeline
- **2 July 1489** — Born in Aslockton, Nottinghamshire.
- **c. 1520** — Ordained a Catholic priest; elected preacher at Cambridge.
- **1527** — Begins assisting Henry VIII with annulment proceedings.
- **1532** — Marries Margarete Osiander in Nuremberg.
- **1 October 1532** — Appointed Archbishop of Canterbury.
- **23 May 1533** — Pronounces Henry's marriage to Catherine of Aragon null.
- **1539** — Act of the Six Articles passed, reversing some reforms.
- **1547** — Edward VI ascends; Cranmer accelerates reforms.
- **1549** — First Book of Common Prayer authorized.
- **1552** — Second, more reformed Book of Common Prayer authorized.
- **1553** — Mary I becomes queen; Cranmer arrested for treason.
- **21 March 1556** — Burned at the stake in Oxford after withdrawing his recantations.

## Body

### Early Life and Service to Henry VIII
Born to a modest gentry family, Cranmer was educated at Jesus College, Cambridge, where he studied theology and humanism. After his first wife Joan died, he was ordained and gained a Doctor of Divinity. In 1527, he was drawn into Henry VIII's annulment case, suggesting a canvassing of European university opinions to build support. This work brought him to the king's attention. In 1532, while serving as ambassador, he secretly married Margarete Osiander, a Lutheran, signaling a move away from Catholic celibacy doctrine. He was appointed Archbishop of Canterbury in late 1532, a promotion secured by Anne Boleyn's family. As archbishop, he validated Henry's marriage to Anne and crowned her queen, actions that led to a provisional excommunication from the Pope.

### Archbishop Under Henry VIII
Cranmer's tenure under Henry was marked by cautious reform amid political and religious conflict. He helped establish the principle of royal supremacy, making the king the head of the English Church. He published the first authorized English-language service, the *Exhortation and Litany* (1544). However, conservative opposition limited radical change. The *Ten Articles* (1536) and the *Bishops' Book* (1537) were compromised documents balancing reformist and traditionalist views. Cranmer's own theology evolved towards Protestant ideas like justification by faith, but he was often outmaneuvered politically, especially after the fall of his ally Thomas Cromwell in 1540. He survived the conservative *Act of the Six Articles* (1539) and the Prebendaries' Plot (1543) largely due to Henry VIII's personal trust.

### Accelerated Reform Under Edward VI
With the accession of the devout Protestant Edward VI in 1547, Cranmer could enact sweeping reforms. He invited Continental reformers like Martin Bucer and Peter Martyr to England for scholarly support. He authored the first *Book of Common Prayer* (1549), a complete vernacular liturgy that replaced Latin services. Its introduction sparked the conservative Prayer Book Rebellion. A more reformed second edition followed in 1552, clarifying a spiritual (not corporeal) Eucharistic presence and removing prayers for the dead. Cranmer also drafted the *Forty-two Articles* (1553), a doctrinal statement later revised into the Thirty-nine Articles. His reform program included the *Homilies* and the *Ordinal* for ordaining clergy, establishing the core structure of the Anglican Church.

### Trial, Recantation, and Martyrdom
After Edward VI's death, Cranmer supported the Protestant Lady Jane Grey's claim to the throne. When the Catholic Mary I took power, he was arrested for treason and heresy. After two years in prison, he was pressured into making several recantations, fully reconciling with the Catholic Church. However, Mary I insisted on his execution for treason. On his execution day, 21 March 1556, Cranmer publicly withdrew his recantations, declared the Pope an Antichrist, and thrust his writing hand into the fire first before being burned at the stake. His death made him a celebrated martyr for the English Reformation.

## Terms
- **Royal Supremacy**: The principle, enacted by Henry VIII, that the English monarch is the sovereign head of the Church of England, not the Pope.
- **Book of Common Prayer**: The foundational prayer book of the Church of England, compiled by Cranmer, which standardized worship in English.
- **Justification by Faith (Sola Fide)**: A core Protestant doctrine, championed by Cranmer, that salvation is achieved through faith alone, not by good works.
- **Transubstantiation**: The Catholic doctrine that the bread and wine of the Eucharist become the actual body and blood of Christ; rejected by Cranmer.
- **Thirty-nine Articles**: The defining statements of Anglican doctrine, derived from Cranmer's earlier Forty-two Articles.
- **Vicegerent**: The deputy appointed by the king to oversee ecclesiastical affairs, a role held by Thomas Cromwell that initially eclipsed Cranmer's authority.
- **Homilies**: A collection of sermons authorized for use in churches, several written by Cranmer, to teach reformed doctrine.
- **Ordinal**: The liturgy for ordaining deacons, priests, and bishops, first published in 1550 under Cranmer's direction.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Cranmer's Theological Evolution**: Scholars debate the pace and sincerity of Cranmer's shift from Catholic humanism to Protestantism, with evidence of early anti-Lutheran views giving way to later reformed theology.
- **Authorship of the Prayer Book**: While Cranmer is credited as the primary editor and architect of the Book of Common Prayer, the exact extent of his personal composition versus his use of existing sources (like Lutheran and Sarum Rite materials) is difficult to determine.
- **Motivations for Recantations**: The reasons behind Cranmer's multiple recantations under pressure from Mary I's government—whether from fear, a desire to live, or a genuine crisis of faith—remain a subject of historical interpretation, especially given his dramatic final reversal.
