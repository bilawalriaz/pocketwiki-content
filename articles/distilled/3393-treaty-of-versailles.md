# Treaty of Versailles

## Overview
The Treaty of Versailles was the primary peace agreement that ended World War I between the Allied Powers and Germany. Signed on 28 June 1919, it formally ended the state of war and imposed severe penalties on Germany, including territorial losses, military restrictions, and reparations. The treaty's terms, particularly the "War Guilt" clause, generated deep resentment in Germany and are widely considered a contributing factor to the rise of the Nazi Party and the outbreak of World War II.

## Timeline
- **28 June 1914** — Assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand, triggering World War I.
- **11 Nov 1918** — Armistice signed, ending fighting on the Western Front.
- **8 Jan 1918** — U.S. President Woodrow Wilson outlines his Fourteen Points for peace.
- **18 Jan 1919** — Paris Peace Conference negotiations begin.
- **28 June 1919** — Treaty of Versailles signed at the Palace of Versailles.
- **1921** — London Schedule of Payments sets final German reparations at 132 billion gold marks.
- **1923** — Franco-Belgian occupation of the Ruhr region after German defaults.
- **1924** — Dawes Plan reorganizes reparations, leading to a period of relative stability.
- **1933** — Adolf Hitler becomes Chancellor of Germany; Germany later leaves the League of Nations.
- **1935** — Germany openly repudiates military clauses, reintroducing conscription.
- **1936** — German troops reoccupy the demilitarized Rhineland.
- **1 Sept 1939** — Germany invades Poland, beginning World War II.

## Body
### Negotiations and Aims
The treaty was negotiated at the Paris Peace Conference by the "Big Four": Georges Clemenceau (France), David Lloyd George (Britain), Woodrow Wilson (USA), and Vittorio Emanuele Orlando (Italy). Germany was excluded from negotiations. The Allies' goals conflicted: France sought security through harsh terms and territorial gains; Britain wanted a moderate peace to preserve Germany as a trading partner and counterbalance France; the U.S. promoted Wilson's Fourteen Points, emphasizing self-determination and a League of Nations. The resulting treaty was a compromise that satisfied none of the parties fully.

### Key Provisions
The treaty's core was Article 231, the "War Guilt Clause," which assigned sole responsibility for the war to Germany and its allies, providing the legal basis for reparations. Germany was forced to cede 65,000 km² of territory, including Alsace-Lorraine to France, and parts of Upper Silesia and West Prussia to the newly reconstituted Poland. Its military was drastically reduced to a 100,000-man volunteer army, with no air force, a minimal navy, and the Rhineland demilitarized. The German colonial empire was dismantled and redistributed as League of Nations mandates. A Reparation Commission was established to determine the final sum, initially set at 20 billion gold marks.

### Implementation and Violations
Germany viewed the treaty as a *Diktat* (dictated peace) and sought to undermine it. Reparations payments were inconsistent, leading to the 1923 Franco-Belgian occupation of the Ruhr and German hyperinflation. The Dawes Plan (1924) and Young Plan (1929) restructured payments. Germany systematically violated military clauses by secretly rearming, developing weapons abroad, and expanding its police forces. Territorial provisions were implemented via plebiscites, some of which were contentious, such as in Upper Silesia. The trial of Kaiser Wilhelm II (Article 227) never occurred as the Netherlands refused extradition.

### Historical Impact and Debate
The treaty's legacy is fiercely debated. Critics like economist John Maynard Keynes called it a "Carthaginian peace" that crippled Germany and destabilized Europe. Others, like French Marshal Ferdinand Foch, argued it was too lenient, failing to permanently weaken Germany. Historians disagree on whether the treaty directly caused World War II. Some, like Detlev Peukert, argue the treaty was reasonably moderate but was perceived as intolerable in Germany, and that the Great Depression, not Versailles, doomed the Weimar Republic. The treaty's "War Guilt" clause and reparations fueled nationalist resentment, which Adolf Hitler expertly exploited in his rise to power.

## Terms
- **War Guilt Clause (Article 231):** The article in the treaty assigning sole responsibility for causing World War I to Germany and its allies.
- **Reparations:** Compensation demanded from Germany for civilian damages caused by the war, the final sum set at 132 billion gold marks.
- **Diktat:** A term used by Germans to describe the treaty as a dictated peace, imposed without negotiation.
- **League of Nations:** An international organization proposed by Woodrow Wilson to maintain world peace, established by the treaty.
- **Mandate:** A territory administered by an Allied power on behalf of the League of Nations, formerly belonging to Germany.
- **Rhineland Demilitarized Zone:** The area west of the Rhine and 50km east of it, where Germany was forbidden from stationing troops or building fortifications.
- **Fourteen Points:** U.S. President Woodrow Wilson's principles for a just peace, emphasizing self-determination and open diplomacy.
- **Hyperinflation:** The severe economic crisis in Germany (1921-1924) where currency became worthless, partly triggered by reparations and the Ruhr occupation.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Was the treaty too harsh or too lenient?** Historians remain divided. Some argue it was excessively punitive and destabilizing, while others contend it was relatively moderate compared to the peace Germany would have imposed (e.g., Treaty of Brest-Litovsk) and that Germany's post-war position was strategically strong.
- **Did the treaty cause World War II?** This is a central debate. While the treaty created resentment and provided a propaganda tool for Hitler, some historians argue the Great Depression and the failures of the Weimar Republic were more direct causes. Others maintain the treaty's terms made another conflict almost inevitable.
- **Were the reparations economically viable?** Economist Étienne Mantoux disputed Keynes's view that reparations were impossibly high, arguing Germany could have paid them. The actual economic impact and whether hyperinflation was a deliberate policy to avoid payment are still analyzed.
- **How did the treaty affect Central and Eastern Europe?** The treaty created new nations and borders, which some historians argue was a necessary correction of imperial rule, while others highlight the ethnic conflicts and instability it generated, particularly regarding German minorities in Poland and Czechoslovakia.
