# Laser

## Overview  
A laser (light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation) is a device that emits coherent light via stimulated emission, producing beams with high spatial and temporal coherence. Invented in 1960 by Theodore Maiman based on theoretical work by Townes, Schawlow, and Gould, lasers have become ubiquitous across science, medicine, industry, and consumer technology due to their ability to deliver intense, narrow, and spectrally pure light.

## Body  

### Fundamentals  
Lasers operate on stimulated emission: when an excited atom is triggered by an incoming photon of matching energy, it emits an identical photon. For sustained amplification, a population inversion—more atoms in an excited state than a lower one—is required. Metastable states in the gain medium allow this inversion to persist long enough for chain reactions. Coherence distinguishes lasers from thermal sources: spatial coherence enables tight focusing and collimation; temporal coherence yields narrow spectral linewidths.

### Design  
A laser comprises three elements: a gain medium (solid, gas, liquid, or semiconductor), a pump mechanism (optical or electrical), and an optical cavity (two mirrors). Feedback from the cavity selects frequencies near the gain peak, leading to dominant oscillation. Pumping sustains population inversion; saturation occurs when stimled emission depletes the excited state, balancing losses to set output power.

### Theory of Operation  
Einstein’s 1917 quantum theory introduced coefficients for absorption (B₁₂), stimulated emission (B₂₁), and spontaneous emission (A₂₁). Population inversion breaks thermal equilibrium, enabling net optical gain. Three- or four-level systems are typically used because two-level systems cannot sustain inversion under steady pumping.

### Modes of Operation  
Continuous-wave (CW) lasers maintain steady output via constant pumping. Pulsed lasers emit energy in bursts—Q-switched lasers produce high-peak-power pulses via rapid loss modulation; mode-locked lasers generate ultrashort femtosecond pulses through phase-locked longitudinal modes. Pulsed pumping uses external pulsed sources for short-lived gain media like dye lasers.

### Types and Operating Principles  
Gas lasers (HeNe, CO₂, excimer) use gas discharge or chemical reactions. Solid-state lasers (ruby, Nd:YAG, Ti:sapphire) employ doped crystals pumped optically. Fiber lasers guide light in doped optical fibers, offering efficient cooling and high power. Semiconductor lasers (diodes, VCSELs, quantum cascade) use electron-hole recombination in junctions. Dye lasers offer wide tunability. Free-electron lasers use relativistic electron beams for broad tunability. Exotic approaches explore nuclear transitions and biological gain media.

### History  
Foundational work began with Einstein (1917) and was confirmed experimentally by Lamb and Retherford (1947). Townes and Schawlow developed the maser (1953) and proposed optical analogs. Gould coined "laser" and patented key concepts, though Bell Labs secured earlier patents. Maiman built the first laser (1960); subsequent decades saw gas, semiconductor, and solid-state innovations.

### Uses  
Applications span communications (fiber optics, WDM), medicine (surgery, phototherapy), industry (cutting, welding, 3D printing), military (targeting, directed energy), consumer goods (printers, scanners, pointers), and research (spectroscopy, metrology). Global industrial laser sales reached $21.85 billion in 2023.

### Safety  
Lasers are classified by hazard level (Class 1–4). Even low-power visible lasers can cause retinal damage due to focusing by the eye. Eye-safe wavelengths (>1.4 μm) reduce retinal risk but may still burn the cornea. Aviation safety concerns arise from laser illumination of aircraft.

## Terms  
- **Stimulated emission**: Photon-triggered release of an identical photon from an excited atom  
- **Population inversion**: Condition where more atoms occupy a higher energy state than a lower one  
- **Gain medium**: Material that amplifies light via stimulated emission  
- **Optical cavity**: Pair of mirrors providing feedback to sustain lasing  
- **Coherence**: Correlation between phases of emitted photons (spatial and temporal)  
- **Pumping**: Process of supplying energy to achieve population inversion  
- **Q-switching**: Technique for generating high-peak-power pulses by modulating cavity losses  
- **Mode locking**: Method for producing ultrashort laser pulses via phase-locked cavity modes  
- **Metastable state**: Excited atomic state with unusually long lifetime  
- **Monochromaticity**: Emission of light at a single wavelength or narrow band  

## Debates and Open Questions  
- **Inventorship disputes**: Gould’s 28-year legal battle over laser patents highlights contested credit among researchers.  
- **Gamma-ray lasers**: Despite decades of research, no operational gamma-ray laser exists; nuclear isomer transitions remain unproven.  
- **Silicon lasers**: Integration with CMOS electronics remains challenging; hybrid and nanowire approaches are promising but not yet mainstream.  
- **White lasers**: While demonstrated, practical deployment lags behind LEDs due to efficiency and stability issues.  
- **Laser weapons**: Atmospheric distortion limits effectiveness; deployment remains experimental despite prototypes like LaWS and HELIOS.
