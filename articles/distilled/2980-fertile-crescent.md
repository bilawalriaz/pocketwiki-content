# Fertile Crescent

## Overview
The Fertile Crescent is a crescent-shaped region in the Middle East, spanning parts of modern-day Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Israel, Palestine, Jordan, Turkey, and Iran. It is considered the first region where settled farming emerged, leading to the rise of early human civilizations like Sumer. The area's unique geography and biodiversity made it a crucial "bridge" for the spread of agriculture and people into Europe and beyond.

## Timeline
- **c. 11,400 years ago** — Prehistoric seedless figs discovered at Gilgal I, suggesting early planting.
- **c. 9,000 BCE** — First known Neolithic farming settlements (Pre-Pottery Neolithic A) appear, including Göbekli Tepe and Jericho.
- **c. 2,500 BCE** — Oldest known libraries appear in Nippur (Sumer) and Ebla (Syria).
- **3rd–2nd millennium BCE** — Multiple language groups, including Sumerian and Semitic languages, are established in the region.
- **1914–1916** — Archaeologist James Henry Breasted popularizes the term "Fertile Crescent."

## Body

### Terminology and Geography
The term "Fertile Crescent" was popularized by archaeologist James Henry Breasted in 1914. It describes a semicircle of land with one wing along the eastern Mediterranean and the other reaching the Persian Gulf. The region's boundaries are defined by the Syrian Desert to the south, the Sahara to the west, the Anatolian and Armenian highlands to the north, and the Iranian plateau to the east. It includes the Tigris, Euphrates, and Jordan river systems.

### Biodiversity and Climate
The Fertile Crescent acted as a land bridge between North Africa and Eurasia, allowing it to retain greater biodiversity than either continent after Ice Age extinctions. Its diverse climates and elevations encouraged the evolution of many annual plants with edible seeds. Crucially, it was home to the eight Neolithic founder crops (including emmer wheat, barley, and lentils) and four of the five most important domesticated animals (cows, goats, sheep, pigs). Many of its plants are "selfers," capable of self-pollination, which aided early cultivation.

### History and Agriculture
The region contains sites with remains of early modern humans and later hunter-gatherers. Its most famous contribution is the origin of agriculture. The first farming settlements emerged around 9,000 BCE. This agricultural revolution supported the rise of early complex societies and cities during the Bronze Age, earning the region the nickname "The cradle of civilization." Empires like Sumer, Akkad, Assyria, and Babylonia arose in the Tigris-Euphrates basin. Irrigation was developed to manage annual flooding and extend natural fertility, though long-term irrigation has led to soil salination.

### Cosmopolitan Diffusion
Craniometric studies show the pre-Neolithic to Bronze Age population of the Fertile Crescent was genetically diverse. Evidence indicates that agriculture spread from the region not just through knowledge sharing, but through the actual migration of people. These early farmers moved west into Europe and North Africa, north to Crimea, and northeast to Mongolia, interbreeding with local hunter-gatherer populations. Modern genetic research confirms that most present-day Europeans descend from at least three ancient populations, including Early European Farmers who originated from these Near Eastern migrants.

### Languages
The Fertile Crescent was linguistically diverse. Semitic languages (like Akkadian and Aramaic) were common in the lowlands, while language isolates (like Sumerian, Elamite, and Hattic) were spoken in mountainous areas. The origins and relationships of some language families, such as Hurro-Urartian, are debated. The arrival of Indo-European languages (e.g., Hittite) after 2000 BCE is generally accepted, though some evidence suggests the family may have emerged in the region as early as 6000 BCE.

## Terms
- **Fertile Crescent**: A crescent-shaped region in the Middle East, key to the origins of agriculture and early civilizations.
- **Neolithic**: The New Stone Age, characterized by the development of farming and settled communities.
- **Pre-Pottery Neolithic A (PPNA)**: The earliest phase of the Neolithic in the Levant, dating to around 9,000 BCE.
- **Sprachbund**: A group of languages that have become similar due to prolonged contact and borrowing.
- **Language isolate**: A language with no demonstrable genetic relationship to other languages.
- **Salination**: The gradual concentration of salt in soil, often a result of long-term irrigation.
- **Saharan pump theory**: The hypothesis that the Middle East land bridge was crucial for the migration of flora, fauna, and humans between Africa and Eurasia.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise affiliation and arrival dates of ancient language isolates (e.g., Elamite, Hattic) remain topics of scholarly discussion due to a lack of early textual evidence.
- The origins of the Indo-European language family are debated; while generally considered intrusive after 2000 BCE, some evidence suggests it may have emerged within the Fertile Crescent as early as 6000 BCE.
- The linguistic consensus on the "Proto-Euphratean" substrate hypothesis has shifted, with current views favoring multiple unknown substrata rather than a single language.
