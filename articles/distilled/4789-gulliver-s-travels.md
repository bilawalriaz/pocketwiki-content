# Gulliver's Travels

## Overview
*Gulliver's Travels* (1726) is a satirical novel by Jonathan Swift that uses the fictional voyages of Lemuel Gulliver to critique human nature, politics, and society. Through visits to fantastical lands, it explores themes of reason, corruption, and the flaws of civilization, making it a foundational work of English literature and a enduring political satire.

## Timeline
- **1713** — Swift and the Scriblerus Club may have begun planning a satire of "travellers' tales."
- **1720** — Composition of *Gulliver's Travels* begins, starting with Parts I and II.
- **1723** — Part IV is written.
- **1724** — Part III is written.
- **August 1725** — The manuscript is complete.
- **March 1726** — Swift travels to London to arrange publication.
- **28 October 1726** — The first edition is published anonymously by Benjamin Motte.
- **1735** — George Faulkner publishes an Irish edition, closer to Swift's original text.
- **1899** — The suppressed "Lindalino" passage is restored in a new edition.

## Body

### Composition and Publication
It is uncertain when Swift began writing, but composition started in 1720. The novel was finished by August 1725. As a political satire, Swift likely had the manuscript copied to avoid legal repercussions. The publisher Benjamin Motte, fearing prosecution, altered some passages. The book was an immediate success. In 1735, an Irish edition by George Faulkner, believed to be based on Swift's original manuscript, was published. This edition included a letter from Gulliver complaining of Motte's changes and is now considered the standard text.

### Plot Summary
The novel follows Lemuel Gulliver on four voyages. In **Lilliput** (1699–1702), he is a giant among tiny people whose petty political disputes satirize European conflicts. In **Brobdingnag** (1702–1706), he is a miniature man among giants who critique European customs and warfare. In **Laputa** (1706–1710), he visits a flying island of impractical intellectuals and a land of absurd scientific experiments, satirizing the Royal Society. In the final voyage (1710–1715), he encounters the **Houyhnhnms**, rational horses, and the savage, human-like **Yahoos**. Gulliver comes to admire the Houyhnhnms and despise humanity, but is expelled and returns to England as a misanthrope.

### Themes and Interpretation
The novel is a multi-layered satire. It critiques European government, religion, and the "ancients versus moderns" debate. A central theme is whether humans are inherently corrupt. Scholars debate whether Gulliver's misanthropy is Swift's own view or a comic exaggeration. The relationship between the rational Houyhnhnms and brutish Yahoos has been interpreted as a master/slave dynamic and a satire on human reason versus animal instinct. The novel also contains political allusions, such as the Lilliput-Blefuscu war representing England and France, and the Academy of Lagado satirizing the Royal Society and speculative projects like the South Sea Bubble.

### Reception and Legacy
The book was an immediate bestseller but also drew criticism for its perceived misanthropy and blasphemy. Some critics, like William Thackeray, found its view of humanity overly harsh. Despite this, it remains a classic, popularly known as a children's story but written as adult satire. It has spawned countless adaptations and its terms—*Lilliputian* (small), *Brobdingnagian* (huge), *Yahoo* (ruffian), and *big-endian/little-endian* (in computing)—have entered the language.

## Terms
- **Menippean satire**: A genre of satire that ridicules mental attitudes rather than specific individuals or institutions.
- **Travellers' tales**: A literary subgenre of fictional adventure narratives, often presented as factual accounts of distant lands.
- **Scriblerus Club**: A group of writers, including Swift, who aimed to satirize bad writing and learning.
- **Houyhnhnms**: The intelligent, rational horse-like beings in Part IV, representing pure reason.
- **Yahoos**: The brutish, human-like creatures in Part IV, representing the worst aspects of human nature.
- **Lilliputian**: An adjective meaning "small and delicate," derived from the tiny inhabitants of Lilliput.
- **Brobdingnagian**: An adjective meaning "very large or gigantic," from the giant land of Brobdingnag.
- **Big-endian / Little-endian**: Terms in computing for byte-order, derived from the trivial war in Lilliput over which end of an egg to crack.
- **Editio princeps**: The first published edition of a work.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Swift's Intent**: Scholars debate whether Gulliver's final misanthropy represents Swift's own cynical view of humanity or is a comic exaggeration meant to be rejected by the reader.
- **Political Satire**: The specific political targets, especially in Part I (Lilliput), are debated, though parallels to English politics (Whigs/Tories, England/France) are widely accepted.
- **The "Lindalino" Passage**: The omission of this allegorical episode about Dublin's rebellion against British currency in early editions raises questions about censorship and Swift's intended political message.
- **Gender and Misogyny**: Critics analyze whether Gulliver's misogynistic descriptions are Swift's own views or a satirical device to mock such attitudes.
