# Jules Verne

## Overview
Jules Verne (1828–1905) was a French novelist, poet, and playwright whose collaboration with publisher Pierre-Jules Hetzel produced the *Voyages extraordinaires*, a bestselling series of adventure novels—including *Journey to the Center of the Earth* (1864), *Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Seas* (1870), and *Around the World in Eighty Days* (1872)—that integrated contemporary scientific knowledge into fictional narratives. Though celebrated in Europe as a major literary figure influencing the avant-garde and surrealism, Verne was long dismissed in the Anglosphere as a writer of children's genre fiction due to heavily abridged and altered translations. Since the 1980s, his literary reputation has recovered globally; he has been the second most-translated author in the world since 1979 and is frequently cited, alongside H. G. Wells and Hugo Gernsback, as a "father of science fiction," though he himself insisted his novels were geographical romances rather than scientific prophecy.

## Body

### Early Life and Formative Influences
Verne was born in Nantes, a port city on the Loire, into a bourgeois family of lawyers and shipowners. His childhood fascination with maritime departure was cemented by his teacher Madame Sambin, who told pupils her missing husband would return like Robinson Crusoe, embedding the *robinsonade* (castaway survival) motif in Verne’s imagination. At the Catholic École Saint‑Stanislas, he excelled in geography and recitation. Family vacations at Chantenay and Brains deepened his love for the river and merchant vessels; his uncle Prudent Allotte, a circumnavigator, inspired the character Robur in *Robur the Conqueror* (1886). A legendary 1839 attempt to run away as a cabin boy—likely invented by his niece and first biographer—nonetheless reflects the tension between his domestic destiny and imaginative escape.

### Paris, Law, and Literary Debut
In 1847, Verne’s father sent him to Paris to study law, partly to separate him from his cousin Caroline, whose marriage in April 1847 plunged Verne into despair. During the turbulent French Revolution of 1848, Verne immersed himself in literary salons and theater, befriending composer Aristide Hignard and falling under Victor Hugo’s dramatic influence. He suffered recurring stomach cramps (likely colitis) and, in 1851, the first of four facial paralysis attacks caused by middle-ear inflammation. Though he earned his law degree in January 1851, he refused his father’s offer of a Nantes practice, declaring his intent to pursue literature. Through Alexandre Dumas fils, Verne placed his first play, *Les Pailles rompues* (1850), and met *Musée des familles* editor Pitre-Chevalier, who published his first stories blending adventure with geographical research—most notably "A Voyage in a Balloon" (1851), which Verne later called the genesis of his destined novelistic line.

### The Hetzel Partnership and the *Voyages extraordinaires*
In 1862, Verne submitted *Voyage en Ballon* to publisher Pierre-Jules Hetzel, who envisioned a family magazine (*Magasin d'Éducation et de Récréation*) combining entertainment with scientific education. Hetzel accepted the revised novel, *Five Weeks in a Balloon* (1863), and signed Verne to a long-term contract for three volumes yearly. Hetzel publicly defined the series’ mission: to outline modern geographical, geological, physical, and astronomical knowledge through an entertaining "history of the universe." Hetzel exerted strong editorial control early on: he forced a happy ending for *The Adventures of Captain Hatteras* (1866) and rejected the dystopian *Paris in the Twentieth Century* as too subversive. The relationship cooled in 1869 when Hetzel, protecting the Russian market, demanded Captain Nemo’s anti-Russian vengeance in *Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Seas* be replaced with a generic anti-slavery motive; Verne compromised by obscuring Nemo’s past. Thereafter, Verne published two-plus volumes annually, achieving wealth largely through stage adaptations of *Around the World in Eighty Days* (1874) and *Michel Strogoff* (1876).

### Family, Later Years, and Death
Verne married Honorine Morel in 1857 after securing a brokerage position on the Paris Bourse, rising early to write before work. He purchased a succession of yachts (*Saint-Michel* I–III) for European voyages. His only biological son, Michel, caused persistent strife—marrying against Verne’s wishes, fathering children with an underage mistress, and accumulating debts—though relations improved later. Raised Catholic, Verne drifted toward deism; scholars detect a deist philosophy in novels invoking divine providence without Christ. On 9 March 1886, his nephew Gaston shot him, leaving a permanent limp; the incident was suppressed, and Gaston was institutionalized. After his mother’s and Hetzel’s deaths (1886), Verne’s works darkened. He served as Amiens town councillor (1888–1903) and was made a Legion of Honour Officer (1892). He died in 1905; his son Michel oversaw posthumous publications, later revealed to have been extensively altered. Original versions were restored by the Société Jules Verne in the late 20th century.

### Literary Reception and the Translation Problem
In France, Verne was initially admired by George Sand and Théophile Gautier, but commercial success—especially the hit stage version of *Around the World in Eighty Days*—led critics like Émile Zola to dismiss him as a mere genre storyteller, blocking his Académie Française nomination. Verne lamented never taking a place in French literature. A "Jules Verne cult" of scholars and surrealists arose posthumously, culminating in 1960s–70s academic rehabilitation (Roland Barthes, Michel Foucault) and canonization in French universities. In the Anglosphere, reputation lagged due to early English translations (from 1852 onward) marketed exclusively to children and riddled with omissions, errors, and inventions—e.g., Griffith & Farran’s 1872 *Journey to the Center of the Earth* altered names and added pages. Adam Roberts and Michael Crichton have condemned these versions as obliterating Verne’s lean, modern prose. Accurate translations appeared only after 1965, but defective public-domain versions remain widely circulated.

### Relationship with Science Fiction
Verne is frequently named a founder of science fiction alongside H. G. Wells, yet the classification is disputed. Maurice Renard argued Verne "never wrote a single sentence of scientific-marvelous." Verne himself denied writing scientific fiction or predicting the future, insisting his goal was geographical romance and stylistic beauty: "I wrote *Five Weeks in a Balloon*, not as a story about ballooning, but as a story about Africa." He attributed technological coincidences to exhaustive research—copying notes from every scientific report he read—rather than prophecy. Theodore L. Thomas (1961) demonstrated that readers’ faulty memories exaggerate the predictive accuracy of *Twenty Thousand Leagues*. The genre’s vague definition allows earlier claimants (Lucian, Voltaire, Mary Shelley), making Verne’s status as "father of science fiction" a retrospective label he rejected.

### Legacy
Verne influenced a vast range of writers (Marcel Aymé, Roland Barthes, Julio Cortázar, Ray Bradbury, Rick Riordan) and scientists/explorers (Richard E. Byrd, Yuri Gagarin, Konstantin Tsiolkovsky, Wernher von Braun, Guglielmo Marconi). He is credited with inspiring the steampunk genre. His cultural presence extends to the DS "Jules Verne Edition" luxury car trim and NASA missions: Apollo 11’s crew referenced *From the Earth to the Moon* during their 1969 return broadcast, noting the fictional *Columbiad* launched from Florida and splashed in the Pacific, mirroring their *Columbia* command module; in 2026, NASA commentator Rob Navias invoked Verne at the Artemis II splashdown, linking "the pages of Jules Verne to a modern day mission to the moon."

## Terms
- ****Voyages extraordinaires**** — The title of Verne’s novel sequence (1863–1905), defined by Hetzel as an entertaining outline of modern scientific knowledge—geography, geology, physics, astronomy—presented as a "history of the universe."
- ****Robinsonade**** — A literary genre derived from *Robinson Crusoe* featuring castaways surviving on deserted islands; a recurring theme in Verne’s works (e.g., *The Mysterious Island*, *The School for Robinsons*).
- ****Agent de change**** — A licensed stockbroker on the Paris Bourse; Verne held this position (1857–c. 1863) to secure income before living solely by his pen.
- ****Magasin d'Éducation et de Récréation**** — Hetzel’s biweekly family magazine (launched 1864) in which most *Voyages extraordinaires* novels were serialized before book publication.
- ****Sortition**** — The drawing of lots for military conscription; Verne was spared service in 1851, reinforcing his lifelong antiwar stance.
- ****Deism**** — A theological position acknowledging a creator God or divine providence but rejecting organized religion and the divinity of Christ; scholars identify this philosophy in Verne’s later novels.
- ****Scientific-marvelous**** — A term (French *merveilleux scientifique*) used by critic Maurice Renard to denote fiction centered on speculative scientific invention; Renard argued Verne did not write in this mode.
- ****Steampunk**** — A late-20th-century literary and aesthetic movement glamorizing 19th-century steam-powered technology; Verne’s works are cited as a primary inspiration.
- ****Hermione complex**** — Scholar Christian Chelebourg’s term for the recurring motif in Verne’s novels of young women married against their will, traced to Verne’s aborted love for Rose Herminie Arnaud Grossetière (married to another in 1848).
- ****Société Jules Verne**** — Academic society founded by the "Jules Verne cult" in the 20th century; responsible for publishing restored original texts of posthumously altered novels.

## Debates and open questions
- **Genre classification**: Whether the *Voyages extraordinaires* constitute science fiction, geographical romance, or a unique hybrid remains unresolved; Verne’s own rejection of the "scientific-marvelous" label conflicts with his retrospective canonization as a genre founder.
- **Prophetic accuracy**: The popular view of Verne as a technological prophet (predicting submarines, moon rockets, etc.) is contested by scholars who argue his "predictions" are either research-based extrapolations or reader projections amplified by faulty memory and poor translations.
- **Textual integrity of posthumous works**: Michel Verne extensively edited manuscripts after 1905 (e.g., *Invasion of the Sea*, *The Lighthouse at the End of the World*, *The Barsac Mission*); the original versions were only recovered in the late 20th century, raising questions about the authenticity of the standard editions read for decades.
- **Anglophone reception lag**: The extent to which Verne’s 20th-century marginalization in English-speaking academia was caused by deficient translations versus cultural bias against popular scientific literature remains a subject of translation-studies debate.
- **Hetzel’s editorial influence**: The degree to which Hetzel shaped the *Voyages*’ tone, ideology (e.g., softening anti-colonial or anti-Russian themes), and scientific content—and whether Verne’s later resistance to edits reflects artistic maturity or commercial compromise—is an ongoing focus of textual scholarship.
