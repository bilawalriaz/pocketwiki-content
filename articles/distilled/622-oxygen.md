# Oxygen

## Overview
Oxygen (symbol O, atomic number 8) is a highly reactive nonmetal and potent oxidizing agent in the chalcogen group. It is the most abundant element in Earth's crust (49.2% by mass) and the third-most abundant in the universe. At standard conditions, it exists as dioxygen (O₂), a colorless, odorless gas constituting 20.95% of Earth's atmosphere. This high atmospheric concentration is unusual among planets and is maintained by the oxygen cycle, driven primarily by oxygenic photosynthesis in cyanobacteria, algae, and plants, which splits water to release O₂. All eukaryotic organisms require O₂ for cellular respiration to extract energy from organic molecules. Oxygen's reactivity underpins combustion, corrosion, and industrial processes like steelmaking, while its allotrope ozone (O₃) forms a protective UV-shielding layer in the stratosphere.

- **1805** — Gay-Lussac and Humboldt determine water forms from two volumes of hydrogen and one of oxygen.

## Body

### Discovery and the Phlogiston Barrier
The identification of oxygen spanned centuries, hindered by the phlogiston theory (established 1667, modified 1731), which posited that a fire-like element called phlogiston was released during combustion, leaving behind a "calx." Early experiments by Philo of Byzantium (2nd c. BCE), Leonardo da Vinci, and John Mayow (1668) noted air was consumed in combustion and respiration, with Mayow correctly inferring a specific component ("nitroaereus") combined with metals to increase their weight. Michael Sendivogius isolated oxygen from potassium nitrate before 1604, calling it "cibus vitae," but his finding was overlooked. Robert Hooke, Ole Borch, Mikhail Lomonosov, and Pierre Bayen later produced oxygen experimentally but failed to identify it as an element, largely because phlogiston theory did not assign air a role in combustion.

### The Chemical Revolution (Scheele, Priestley, Lavoisier)
Carl Wilhelm Scheele produced "fire air" by heating mercuric oxide (1771–72) but published only in 1777 after reading of Joseph Priestley's work. On August 1, 1774, Priestley used a burning lens on mercuric oxide to liberate "dephlogisticated air," noting enhanced combustion and mouse survival; he published in 1775. Priestley visited Antoine Lavoisier in October 1774, describing his method. Lavoisier conducted the first quantitative oxidation experiments (1774 onward), proving that tin heated in a closed container gained weight equal to the air consumed, demonstrating air is a mixture of "vital air" (oxygen) and "azote" (nitrogen). In 1777, Lavoisier named the gas *oxygène* (Greek *oxys* "acid/sharp" + *-genēs* "producer"), mistakenly believing it constituted all acids—a view later disproven (e.g., HCl), but the name persisted.

### Physical and Molecular Properties
Dioxygen (O₂) is a colorless, odorless gas with a covalent double bond (bond length 121 pm, energy 498 kJ/mol). Molecular orbital theory explains its paramagnetism: two unpaired electrons in degenerate antibonding π* orbitals create a triplet ground state (triplet oxygen). This spin state causes slow reaction with organic molecules (paired spins), preventing spontaneous combustion. Singlet oxygen (paired spins, higher energy) is far more reactive, formed in photosynthesis and by immune cells. Oxygen condenses at 90.20 K (−182.95 °C) and freezes at 54.36 K (−218.79 °C); liquid and solid O₂ are pale blue. It dissolves in water roughly twice as well as nitrogen (1:2 ratio in water vs 1:4 in air), with solubility doubling from 20 °C to 0 °C, critically supporting polar marine life.

### Allotropes: O₂, Ozone, and High-Pressure Phases
The primary allotrope is dioxygen (O₂). Ozone (O₃), formed when UV radiation splits O₂, absorbs UVB/UVC, creating the protective stratospheric ozone layer; at ground level, it is a toxic smog component. Under high pressure, oxygen forms exotic allotropes: metastable O₄ (discovered 2001) forms rhombohedral O₈ clusters at 20 GPa, a potential high-energy oxidizer for rocket fuel. A metallic phase appears above 96 GPa, becoming superconducting at very low temperatures (1998).

### Isotopes and Stellar Nucleosynthesis
Natural oxygen comprises three stable isotopes: ¹⁶O (99.762%), ¹⁷O, and ¹⁸O. ¹⁶O is a primary fusion product in massive stars (triple-alpha and neon burning). ¹⁷O arises from hydrogen burning via the CNO cycle; ¹⁸O forms when ¹⁴N captures a ⁴He nucleus in helium-rich zones of evolved stars. Fifteen radioisotopes (¹¹O–²⁸O) exist; ¹⁵O (t₁/₂ 122.24 s) and ¹⁴O (t₁/₂ 70.606 s) are the most stable. Paleoclimatologists use the ¹⁸O/¹⁶O ratio in marine shells and ice cores to reconstruct past temperatures (lighter ¹⁶O evaporates preferentially, enriching seawater in ¹⁸O during cold periods). Genesis spacecraft data revealed the Sun is enriched in ¹⁶O relative to Earth, implying an unknown process depleted ¹⁶O from the protoplanetary disk before Earth formed.

### Occurrence and the Oxygen Cycle
Oxygen constitutes 49.2% of Earth's crust by mass (oxides, silicates), 88.8% of oceans (water), and 20.8% of atmospheric volume (23.1% mass, ~10¹⁵ tonnes). Earth's high O₂ is unique in the Solar System (Mars: 0.1%; Venus: trace), resulting from the oxygen cycle between atmosphere, biosphere, and lithosphere. Photosynthesis (mainly marine photoautotrophs: ~70% of production) releases O₂; respiration, decay, and combustion consume it. Current equilibrium shows production ≈ consumption, though atmospheric O₂ is trending slightly downward, likely due to fossil fuel burning. The Great Oxygenation Event (GOE) began ~2.45 Ga, raising O₂ to <10% present levels; a Neoproterozoic Oxygenation Event ~500 Mya brought levels to near-modern highs, enabling complex life.

### Biological Role: Photosynthesis and Respiration
Oxygenic photosynthesis splits water in thylakoid membranes using four photons: 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + photons → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂. The proton gradient drives ATP synthesis via photophosphorylation. Aerobic respiration in mitochondria reverses this: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + 2880 kJ/mol. Vertebrates use lungs and hemoglobin (O₂ capacity ~200 cm³/L blood) for transport; insects use tracheal tubes; aquatic animals use gills/skin. Reactive oxygen species (superoxide, H₂O₂, singlet O₂) are toxic byproducts used by immune systems to kill pathogens and by plants in hypersensitive responses. Obligate anaerobes dominated early life until O₂ accumulation ~2.5 Ga.

### Industrial Production and Storage
~100 million tonnes/year are extracted from air. The primary method is fractional distillation of liquefied air (N₂ boils off, O₂ remains liquid). Pressure swing adsorption (PSA) uses zeolite molecular sieves to adsorb N₂, yielding 90–93% O₂ continuously. Lab preparation uses KClO₃ + MnO₂; electrolysis of water (DC only) yields H₂ and O₂. Chemical oxygen generators (NaClO₃ + Fe filings) provide emergency O₂ on aircraft/submarines. For transport, O₂ is liquefied (1 L LOX = 840 L gas at 20 °C) in insulated tankers; smaller high-pressure cylinders serve medical/welding needs.

### Applications
**Medical:** Oxygen therapy treats hypoxia (emphysema, pneumonia, heart failure), reduces pulmonary vascular resistance, and aids carbon monoxide poisoning/gas gangrene/decompression sickness via hyperbaric chambers. **Life Support:** Space suits use pure O₂ at ~⅓ atm for flexibility; submarines/scuva use scrubbed air or mixed gas (diluted with N₂/He to avoid toxicity at depth). **Industrial (55% steel):** High-pressure O₂ lances remove S (as SO₂) and C (as CO₂) from molten iron (exothermic, 1700 °C). **Chemical (25%):** Ethylene + O₂ → ethylene oxide → ethylene glycol (antifreeze, polyesters). **Remaining 20%:** Metal cutting/welding (oxyacetylene), rocket oxidizer, water treatment. **Recreational:** Oxygen bars (placebo effect likely); pyrotechnics.

### Compounds
Oxygen exhibits oxidation state −2 in most compounds; −1 in peroxides; rarer states include −½ (superoxides), −⅓ (ozonides), 0 (O₂, HOF), +½ (O₂⁺), +1 (O₂F₂), +2 (OF₂). **Inorganic:** Water (H₂O) has hydrogen bonding (23.3 kJ/mol/H). Metal surfaces passivate via oxide films (Al₂O₃, TiO₂). Non-stoichiometric oxides exist (e.g., Fe₁₋ₓO). Crustal rocks are dominated by SiO₂, Al₂O₃, Fe₂O₃, CaCO₃, and complex silicates (Mg/Fe silicates in mantle). Water-soluble silicates serve as detergents/adhesives. PtF₆ oxidizes O₂ to O₂⁺PtF₆⁻. **Organic:** Key classes: alcohols (R-OH), ethers (R-O-R), ketones (R-CO-R), aldehydes (R-CHO), carboxylic acids (R-COOH), esters (R-COOR), amides (R-CONR₂). Common solvents: acetone, methanol, ethanol, THF, DMSO. Biomolecules (proteins, nucleic acids, carbohydrates, lipids) all contain O. Autoxidation occurs spontaneously; industrial direct oxidations produce ethylene oxide and peracetic acid.

### Safety and Toxicity
NFPA 704: Compressed O₂ is non-flammable/non-reactive but an oxidizer; liquid O₂ (LOX) has health hazard 3 (hyperoxia, frostbite). **Toxicity:** CNS/pulmonary toxicity occurs at elevated partial pressures (>50 kPa / 50% O₂ at 1 atm). Not an issue for medical masks (30–50% O₂). Premature babies blinded by high O₂ incubators (historical). Space suits use pure O₂ at low total pressure (~30 kPa), avoiding toxicity. Divers risk pulmonary fibrosis (>60 kPa prolonged) and convulsions (>160 kPa / 1.6 atm); 21% O₂ becomes toxic at 66 m depth; 100% O₂ at 6 m. **Fire Hazard:** O₂-rich environments lower ignition temperatures and accelerate combustion. Apollo 1 fire (1967) spread rapidly in pure O₂ at >1 atm. Steel pipes act as fuel in O₂ systems; special design minimizes ignition sources. LOX spills on organics (wood, asphalt) create unpredictable detonation hazards.

## Terms
- ****Dioxygen (O₂)**** — The common allotrope of elemental oxygen; two oxygen atoms covalently double-bonded; paramagnetic due to two unpaired electrons (triplet ground state).
- ****Ozone (O₃)**** — A reactive allotrope formed by UV splitting of O₂; absorbs UVB/UVC in the stratosphere (protective layer) but is a toxic pollutant at ground level.
- ****Phlogiston Theory**** — An obsolete 17th–18th century theory positing that a fire-like element ("phlogiston") is released during combustion; delayed recognition of oxygen as an element.
- ****Great Oxygenation Event (GOE)**** — A paleogeological event ~2.45 Ga when photosynthetic cyanobacteria caused free O₂ to accumulate in Earth's atmosphere, rising from trace to <10% present levels.
- ****Oxygenic Photosynthesis**** — The process (in cyanobacteria, algae, plants) using photon energy to split H₂O, releasing O₂ and fixing CO₂ into carbohydrates.
- ****Cellular Respiration**** — The metabolic process in eukaryotes where O₂ oxidizes organic molecules (glucose) in mitochondria to produce ATP, CO₂, and H₂O.
- ****Fractional Distillation**** — The primary industrial method for O₂ production: air is liquefied and components separated by boiling point (N₂ boils first, O₂ remains liquid).
- ****Pressure Swing Adsorption (PSA)**** — A non-cryogenic O₂ production method using zeolite sieves to adsorb N₂ under pressure, releasing concentrated O₂ (90–93%).
- ****Singlet Oxygen**** — A high-energy, metastable state of O₂ with paired electron spins; far more reactive than triplet ground-state O₂; produced in photosynthesis and immune response.
- ****Oxygen Isotope Ratio (¹⁸O/¹⁶O)**** — A paleoclimate proxy: lighter ¹⁶O evaporates preferentially; during cold periods, precipitation is enriched in ¹⁶O, leaving seawater (and marine shells) enriched in ¹⁸O.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Oceanic vs. terrestrial oxygen production:** Estimates vary widely; marine photoautotrophs are cited as providing ~70% of O₂, but other estimates suggest ~45% or higher/lower values.
*   **Abiotic oxygen as a biosignature:** While O₂ is considered the strongest biosignature for exoplanet life detection (biological origin, persistence, detectability), abiotic production mechanisms (e.g., photodissociation of water on Europa/Ganymede, or peculiar hydrosphere conditions) could produce false positives.
*   **Oxygen–evolution causal links:** Detailed mechanisms linking specific O₂ concentration changes to evolutionary innovations (e.g., Cambrian explosion) remain elusive despite broad correlation.
*   **Solar oxygen isotope anomaly:** The Sun's higher ¹⁶O/¹⁸O ratio vs. Earth implies an unknown process depleted ¹⁶O from the protoplanetary disk before planetary accretion; the mechanism is unidentified.
*   **Atmospheric O₂ decline:** Current slight downward trend is attributed to fossil fuel burning, but long-term consequences and feedbacks with carbon cycle/climate are under study.
