# Yellow Sea

## Overview
The Yellow Sea is a shallow, marginal sea of the Western Pacific Ocean located between mainland China and the Korean Peninsula. Named for the golden-yellow color of its silt-laden waters, it is a vital ecological and economic region that is now considered one of the most degraded marine areas on Earth due to extensive land reclamation and pollution.

## Timeline
- **c. 10,000 years ago** — The sea formed as a flooded continental shelf after the last glacial period.
- **1975** — The "Jindo Sea Parting" phenomenon was described to the outside world by French ambassador Pierre Randi.
- **1979** — China initiated collaborations with foreign oil companies for exploration in the sea.
- **1985-1996** — China's commercial fish production in the sea rose from 619,000 to 1,984,400 tonnes.
- **1991-2006** — South Korea dammed the Saemangeum estuary, drying the tidal flat.
- **1950s-2002** — Land reclamation destroyed 65% of the intertidal area around the Yellow Sea.
- **24 November 1999** — The Chinese ferry *Dashun* capsized near Yantai, killing about 300 people.
- **16 July 2010** — A pipeline explosion at Dalian port caused a major oil spill of about 1,500 tonnes.
- **1 November 2018** — North and South Korea established "peace zones" in the Yellow Sea near their demarcation line.

## Body

### Geography and Hydrology
The Yellow Sea is a flooded section of the continental shelf, extending about 960 km north to south and 700 km east to west, with an average depth of only 44 m. Its waters are characterized by a warm cyclone current from the Kuroshio Current and strong seasonal monsoons. Winters are cold and dry, with drift ice forming in the north, while summers are warm and wet. Tides are semidiurnal and notably high along the Korean coast, creating phenomena like the Jindo Sea Parting.

### Flora, Fauna, and Ecology
The sea is highly productive, rich in seaweed, crustaceans, and about 200 commercially exploited fish species. Its southern intertidal mudflats are critically important for migratory birds along the East Asian–Australasian Flyway. However, populations of large marine mammals, including whales and seals, have severely declined due to historical hunting and pollution. The ecosystem is now endangered, with over 60% of tidal wetlands lost to reclamation and widespread issues like harmful algal blooms and invasive species.

### Economy and Human Impact
The densely populated coasts rely on the sea for fishing, navigation, and oil resources. Major ports include Dalian and Qingdao in China, and Incheon in South Korea. While fishing intensity has increased for China and Korea, all species are now overfished. Oil exploration has proven significant reserves, but international collaboration has been limited. The sea's environment is severely threatened by pollution, habitat destruction, and industrial accidents like the 2010 Dalian oil spill.

## Terms
- **Marginal sea:** A sea adjacent to a continent, partially enclosed by islands or peninsulas.
- **Continental shelf:** The submerged edge of a continent, typically shallow and rich in resources.
- **Semidiurnal tide:** A tidal pattern with two nearly equal high and low tides each day.
- **Intertidal mudflat:** A coastal wetland that is submerged and exposed by tides, crucial for bird migration.
- **Benthic:** Relating to the bottom of a body of water.
- **Overfished:** The harvesting of a fish population at a rate faster than it can replenish.
- **Land reclamation:** The process of creating new land from sea, riverbeds, or other water bodies.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary debate concerns the balance between economic development and environmental conservation. The extensive land reclamation projects, such as the Saemangeum dam, are defended for providing land for agriculture and industry but are heavily criticized for destroying irreplaceable ecosystems and migratory bird habitats. The future of the sea's fisheries and the effectiveness of international cooperation to manage pollution and overfishing remain critical open questions.
