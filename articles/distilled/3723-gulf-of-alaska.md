# Gulf of Alaska

## Overview
The Gulf of Alaska is a large arm of the Pacific Ocean along the southern coast of Alaska, known for its productive marine ecosystem, dramatic glacial coastline, and role as a major storm generator for the North American west coast. Its unique oceanography and climate shifts have significant impacts on regional weather and commercial fisheries.

## Body

### Geography and Extent
The Gulf of Alaska is defined by the curve of Alaska's southern coast, stretching from Kabuch Point and Kodiak Island in the west to Cape Spencer in the east, where the Inside Passage begins. Its shoreline is a mix of forest, mountains, and tidewater glaciers, including Alaska's largest glaciers, the Malaspina and Bering Glaciers. The coast is heavily indented with major bodies of water like Cook Inlet and Prince William Sound. The International Hydrographic Organization and the US Geological Survey provide slightly different official boundary definitions for the Gulf.

### Ecosystem and Oceanography
The Gulf is a highly productive marine ecosystem, classified as Class I with over 300 grams of carbon per square meter per year. It hosts deep-water corals, such as *Primnoa pacifica*, found between 150 and 900 meters deep, which have led to the designation of Habitat Areas of Particular Concern. The Gulf remains ice-free year-round due to the warm North Pacific Current, which is fed by the Kuroshio Current.

### Climate and Meteorology
The Gulf is a major generator of storms that bring heavy snow and ice to southern Alaska. These storms often move south, providing much of the seasonal rainfall and snowfall for the Pacific Northwest and as far south as Southern California, especially during El Niño events. In 1977, the Gulf experienced a significant and then-inexplicable climate shift, leading to warmer sea surface temperatures and an increase in commercial fish species. Similar volatile changes were observed across the North Pacific in the 1970s.

## Terms
- **Tidewater glacier**: A glacier that terminates in the sea.
- **Fjord**: A long, narrow, deep inlet of the sea between high cliffs, as formed by a glacier.
- **Class I ecosystem**: A designation for a highly productive marine ecosystem, with over 300 grams of carbon per square meter per year.
- **Habitat Area of Particular Concern**: A region identified as important for the protection of specific marine life, such as deep-water corals.
- **North Pacific Current**: A warm ocean current that flows eastward across the Pacific, keeping the Gulf of Alaska ice-free.
- **Kuroshio Current**: A powerful warm ocean current in the western Pacific that feeds into the North Pacific Current.
- **El Niño**: A climate pattern describing unusual warming of surface waters in the eastern tropical Pacific Ocean, affecting global weather.
