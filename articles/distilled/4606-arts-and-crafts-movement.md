# Arts and Crafts movement

## Overview
The Arts and Crafts movement was an international trend in decorative and fine arts that began in the British Isles in the 1880s and spread globally. It was a reaction against the perceived impoverishment of design and the poor conditions of industrial production, advocating for handcraftsmanship, simple forms, and the unity of art and labor. The movement influenced architecture, design, and social reform, peaking around 1920 before being largely superseded by Modernism.

## Timeline
- **1851** — Great Exhibition in London sparks criticism of ornate, poorly made industrial goods.
- **1859** — Philip Webb designs Red House for William Morris, an early Arts and Crafts architectural example.
- **1861** — William Morris founds Morris, Marshall, Faulkner & Co., producing handcrafted furniture and decor.
- **1882** — A. H. Mackmurdo forms the Century Guild of designers.
- **1884** — The Art Workers Guild is founded to unite fine and applied arts.
- **1887** — T. J. Cobden-Sanderson first uses the term "Arts and Crafts"; the Arts and Crafts Exhibition Society is formed.
- **1888** — C. R. Ashbee establishes the Guild and School of Handicraft in London.
- **1897** — The Society of Arts and Crafts is incorporated in Boston, USA.
- **1903** — The Wiener Werkstätte is founded in Vienna, influenced by Arts and Crafts principles.
- **1907** — The Deutscher Werkbund is formed in Germany, advocating for standardization in industry.
- **1914** — The movement is in decline in Britain by the outbreak of World War I.

## Body

### Origins and Influences
The movement emerged from mid-19th century British design reform, which criticized the "ignorance" and "vulgarity" of machine-made goods seen at the 1851 Great Exhibition. Reformers like Owen Jones and Richard Redgrave published principles advocating for logic, utility, and simplified ornament. However, the Arts and Crafts movement went further, linking design reform to social reform. Its philosophy was deeply influenced by John Ruskin, who condemned industrial "servile labour," and William Morris, who championed the medieval craftsman as a model for dignified, creative work.

### Social and Design Principles
At its core, the movement critiqued industrial society, idealizing pre-industrial craftsmanship and the medieval period. While practitioners like Morris denounced the factory system and division of labour, their attitudes toward machinery were often inconsistent; Morris would sometimes use machines if they met quality standards. The movement was closely tied to socialism, with many key figures like Morris, Crane, and Ashbee being active socialists. It also associated with other reform movements advocating for a "Simple Life," such as dress reform and the garden city movement.

### Development and Spread
Morris's popular designs and the formation of groups like the Arts and Crafts Exhibition Society (1887) helped spread the style. By the late 19th century, it dominated British domestic design but was often copied by industrial methods. The movement spread internationally: in Scotland, it evolved into the Glasgow Style with Charles Rennie Mackintosh; in Ireland, it fueled a Celtic revival; and in continental Europe, it influenced Art Nouveau and groups like the Wiener Werkstätte. In the United States, it inspired the "Craftsman" style, promoted by figures like Gustav Stickley, and adapted ideals to focus on well-decorated middle-class homes.

### Architecture and Legacy
The movement had its most lasting impact on architecture, rejecting classical revival for vernacular forms, honest use of materials, and picturesque asymmetry. Key examples include Red House and the garden suburb of Bedford Park. Arts and Crafts principles also transformed art education, emphasizing practical craft over abstract design. Although the movement declined after World War I, its ideas persisted in studio pottery, influenced figures like Bernard Leach, and informed later design movements, including British Utility furniture and the Festival of Britain.

## Terms
- **Arts and Crafts movement:** An international design movement (c. 1880–1920) advocating for handcraftsmanship, simple forms, and the integration of art and labor in reaction to industrialization.
- **Vernacular architecture:** Building styles using local materials and traditional techniques, which the movement admired and often emulated.
- **Division of labour:** The separation of tasks in production, which Arts and Crafts practitioners criticized for dehumanizing work.
- **Gothic Revival:** An architectural style emphasizing medieval forms, which influenced early Arts and Crafts thinkers like A. W. N. Pugin.
- **Mingei movement:** A Japanese folk art movement from the 1920s, influenced by Ruskin and Morris, that promoted traditional craftsmanship.
- **Wiener Werkstätte:** A Viennese production community of artists and craftsmen founded in 1903, influenced by Arts and Crafts ideals.
- **Deutscher Werkbund:** A German association (1907) of artists, architects, and industrialists that debated standardization versus artistic individuality.
- **Craftsman style:** The American adaptation of Arts and Crafts principles in architecture and design, popularized by Gustav Stickley.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Relationship to Art Nouveau:** Scholars debate whether the Arts and Crafts movement was the root of Art Nouveau, its English incarnation, or a movement in opposition to it due to its criticism of industrial materials like iron.
- **Use of machinery:** There is inconsistency in the movement's stance; while ideologically anti-machine, practitioners like Morris and later C. R. Ashbee acknowledged machinery's role, leading to debate about what constituted "true" craftsmanship.
- **Designer vs. Maker:** A core debate existed over whether the designer should also be the maker. While Walter Crane advocated for this unity, others like Lewis Foreman Day argued that specialization allowed for better design and execution.
