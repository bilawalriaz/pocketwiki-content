# Indo-Greek Kingdom

## Overview
The Indo-Greek Kingdoms were a series of Hellenistic states in the northwestern Indian subcontinent (modern Pakistan, NW India, E Afghanistan) from c. 200 BC to c. 10 AD. Founded by the Greco-Bactrian king Demetrius I's invasion, they represent a unique cultural synthesis where Greek and Indian languages, religions, and artistic traditions blended. Their legacy, particularly through Greco-Buddhist art and the spread of Buddhism, had a lasting impact on the region.

## Timeline
- **c. 200 BC** — Demetrius I of Bactria invades India, founding the Indo-Greek realm.
- **c. 185–170 BC** — Bactrian kings Pantaleon and Agathocles issue the first bilingual (Greek/Indian) coins.
- **c. 180–175 BC** — Apollodotus I becomes the first major Indo-Greek king ruling primarily from India.
- **c. 165–130 BC** — Menander I rules, expanding the kingdom to its greatest extent; capital at Sagala.
- **c. 130 BC** — Death of Menander I; his empire fragments.
- **c. 130 BC** — Greco-Bactrian Kingdom falls to Scythian/Yuezhi invasions.
- **c. 115 BC** — Embassy of Heliodorus to the Shunga court; dedication to Vāsudeva.
- **c. 100–95 BC** — King Philoxenus briefly reunites western Indo-Greek territories.
- **c. 80 BC** — Indo-Scythian king Maues begins ruling in parts of NW India.
- **c. 48/47 BC** — Indo-Scythian Azes I establishes Indo-Scythian rule after defeating Hippostratus.
- **c. 10 AD** — Last Indo-Greek kings (Strato II & III) in Punjab are conquered by the Saka ruler Rajuvula.

## Body

### Origins and Expansion
The Indo-Greek kingdoms emerged from the Greco-Bactrian Kingdom, which had seceded from the Seleucid Empire c. 250 BC. The expansion into India began when Demetrius I, son of the Greco-Bactrian king Euthydemus I, invaded c. 200 BC. This move was likely encouraged by the collapse of the Mauryan Empire and the rise of the Shunga dynasty. The Greeks established control over the Kabul Valley, Gandhara, and Punjab, with regional capitals like Taxila and Sagala. Their rule was characterized by the issuance of bilingual coins and the blending of Greek and Indian cultural elements.

### The Reign of Menander I
Menander I (c. 165–130 BC) was the most powerful Indo-Greek king, expanding the realm to its maximum extent from the Kabul Valley to eastern Punjab and possibly Mathura. His coins are the most widely found. In Buddhist literature, he is known as Milinda and is celebrated in the *Milinda Panha* for his dialogue with the monk Nagasena and his conversion to Buddhism. His death led to the fragmentation of the kingdom into smaller, competing Indo-Greek states.

### Cultural and Religious Synthesis
Indo-Greek rule fostered significant cultural exchange. Their bilingual coins (Greek and Kharoshthi script) were a major concession to local culture. They patronized Buddhism, as evidenced by Menander I's conversion and the use of the Buddhist Dharmachakra on coins. Some kings, like those of the late 2nd century BC, used the title "Dharmikasa" ("Follower of the Dharma"). They also interacted with Hinduism; the Heliodorus pillar (c. 113 BC) records a Greek ambassador's devotion to Vāsudeva. This synthesis laid the groundwork for Greco-Buddhist art.

### Decline and Legacy
The Indo-Greeks were progressively displaced from the west by the Indo-Scythians (Sakas) and from the east by Indian republics like the Yaudheyas. The last known Indo-Greek ruler, Strato II, was defeated c. 10 AD. Greek communities persisted under subsequent rulers like the Indo-Parthians and Kushans. Their legacy endured through the use of the Greek language, calendrical systems, and profound artistic influence, particularly in the development of Buddhist sculpture in Gandhara.

## Terms
*   **Hellenistic:** Relating to Greek culture, political systems, or language in the period from the death of Alexander the Great (323 BC) to the Roman conquest of Egypt (30 BC).
*   **Greco-Bactrian Kingdom:** A Hellenistic kingdom centered in Bactria (modern northern Afghanistan) that seceded from the Seleucid Empire c. 250 BC.
*   **Bilingual Coins:** Coins issued by Indo-Greek kings bearing inscriptions in both Greek and an Indian language (usually Pali in Kharoshthi script).
*   **Kharoshthi:** An ancient Indian script derived from Aramaic, used in Gandhara and for Indo-Greek coin inscriptions.
*   **Milinda Panha:** A Buddhist text recording the dialogue between the Indo-Greek king Menander I (Milinda) and the monk Nagasena.
*   **Dharmachakra:** The "Wheel of Dharma," a key symbol in Buddhism representing the Buddha's teachings.
*   **Yavana/Yona:** Indian terms derived from "Ionian," used to refer to Greeks, particularly the Indo-Greeks.
*   **Shunga Empire:** An Indian dynasty that succeeded the Mauryan Empire c. 185 BC, ruling from Pataliputra.
*   **Indo-Scythians (Sakas):** Iranian nomadic peoples who invaded from Central Asia, gradually displacing the Indo-Greeks from the west.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Extent of Conquests:** The exact eastern limit of Indo-Greek rule is debated. Some sources suggest they reached the Shunga capital Pataliputra, but this is not conclusively supported by archaeological evidence.
*   **Chronology and Sequencing:** The precise order and dates of rule for many Indo-Greek kings are uncertain and reconstructed primarily from numismatic (coin) evidence, such as overstrikes.
*   **Ethnicity of Later Rulers:** The ethnic background of later Indo-Greek kings is sometimes unclear, with some possibly having mixed heritage (e.g., Indo-Scythian).
*   **Nature of Decline:** The process of decline—whether through gradual assimilation, military conquest, or a combination—is a subject of ongoing scholarly analysis.
