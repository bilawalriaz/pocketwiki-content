# Mathematics

## Overview

Mathematics is a field of knowledge concerned with abstract concepts such as numbers, geometric shapes, sets, functions, and probabilities. It uses logical reasoning and proof to establish their properties, often expressed as theorems, formulas, and equations. Mathematics is used to model and solve problems in science, engineering, technology, economics, and everyday life. Its history spans from ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia through Greek rigor, Islamic algebra, and modern axiomatic foundations, making it essential across all sciences.

## Timeline

- **c. 3000 BC** — Babylonians and Egyptians begin using arithmetic, algebra, and geometry for taxation, construction, and astronomy
- **c. 300 BC** — Euclid systematizes geometry in *Elements*, introducing the axiomatic method
- **3rd century AD** — Diophantus writes on solving equations, a precursor to algebra
- **9th century** — Al-Khwarizmi develops systematic algebra methods; term "algebra" derives from his work
- **1540–1603** — François Viète introduces variables and symbolic notation
- **1614** — John Napier introduces logarithms
- **17th century** — Descartes introduces Cartesian coordinates; Newton and Leibniz develop calculus
- **19th century** — Non-Euclidean geometries discovered; foundational crisis begins; algebra expands to abstract structures
- **1900** — Hilbert compiles his list of 23 open problems
- **1931** — Gödel publishes incompleteness theorems
- **2000** — Millennium Prize Problems published; only Poincaré conjecture solved (by Perelman) to date

## Body

### Areas of mathematics

Before the Renaissance, mathematics was divided into arithmetic (study of numbers) and geometry (study of shapes). Beginning with the Renaissance, modern algebra and calculus became predominant, creating four main areas that endured until the end of the 19th century. The foundational crisis and systematic use of the axiomatic method then led to an explosion of new areas; the 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification lists sixty-three first-level areas.

**Number theory** evolved from manipulation of natural numbers to integers and rational numbers. It became a distinct discipline in Ancient Greece, with early contributors Euclid and Diophantus. Modern abstract number theory is attributed to Fermat and Euler, coming to full fruition with Legendre and Gauss. Many easily stated problems require sophisticated methods: Fermat's Last Theorem (stated 1637, proved 1994 by Andrew Wiles using scheme theory, category theory, and homological algebra) and Goldbach's conjecture (stated 1742, still unproven).

**Geometry** started with empirical recipes for surveying and architecture. The Greeks introduced proofs, systematized by Euclid around 300 BC. Euclidean geometry was unchanged until Descartes introduced Cartesian coordinates in the 17th century, allowing algebra and calculus to solve geometrical problems. This split geometry into synthetic and analytic geometry. In the 19th century, non-Euclidean geometries were discovered, contributing to the foundational crisis. Today's subareas include projective, affine, differential, algebraic, and discrete geometry, plus topology and manifold theory.

**Algebra** had precursors in Diophantus (3rd century) and al-Khwarizmi (9th century). It became an area in its own right with Viète's introduction of variables. Until the 19th century, algebra consisted mainly of linear equations and polynomial equations. During the 19th century, mathematicians began using variables for non-numbers (matrices, modular integers, geometric transformations), leading to the concept of algebraic structures. This "modern algebra" was established through Emmy Noether's influence and popularized by Van der Waerden's book *Moderne Algebra*. Subareas include group theory, field theory, ring theory, homological algebra, and Boolean algebra.

**Calculus and analysis**: Calculus was introduced independently by Newton and Leibniz in the 17th century, studying relationships between continuously dependent variables. Euler expanded it in the 18th century with the function concept. "Calculus" now refers to the elementary part; "analysis" to advanced parts, subdivided into real and complex analysis, including functional analysis, measure theory, differential equations, and numerical analysis.

**Discrete mathematics** studies individual, countable objects like integers. Algorithms and computational complexity play a major role. The four color theorem and optimal sphere packing were solved in the second half of the 20th century; the P versus NP problem remains open. It includes combinatorics, graph theory, coding theory, game theory, and discrete optimization.

**Mathematical logic and set theory** have belonged to mathematics since the end of the 19th century. Cantor's study of infinite sets showed different sizes of infinity, causing controversy. The foundational crisis was solved by systematizing the axiomatic method inside formalized set theory, embodied in formalism as founded by David Hilbert around 1910. Gödel's incompleteness theorems assert that in every consistent formal system containing natural numbers, there are true theorems not provable inside the system. Brouwer challenged this with intuitionistic logic. Subareas include model theory, proof theory, type theory, and computability theory.

**Computational mathematics** studies problems too large for human computing capacity. It includes numerical analysis (approximation with floating-point arithmetic), computer algebra (manipulating formulas on computers), automated theorem proving, and algorithmics.

### History

The word "mathematics" comes from Ancient Greek *máthēma* ('something learned, knowledge, mathematics'). The Pythagoreans were likely first to constrain the word to arithmetic and geometry. Until around 1700, "mathematics" in Latin and English more commonly meant "astrology"; the meaning gradually changed from about 1500 to 1800.

Evidence for complex mathematics appears around 3000 BC in Babylon and Egypt. The oldest mathematical texts from Mesopotamia and Egypt date to 2000–1800 BC. Babylonian mathematics first shows elementary arithmetic in the archaeological record, with a place-value system and sexagesimal numerals still used for angles and time. Greek mathematics emerged as a distinct discipline by the 5th century BC. Euclid's *Elements* (c. 300 BC) is considered the most influential textbook ever. Archimedes (c. 287–c. 212 BC) developed methods for surface areas and volumes, using the method of exhaustion reminiscent of modern calculus. The Hindu–Arabic numeral system evolved in India over the first millennium AD and was transmitted West via Islamic mathematics.

During the Golden Age of Islam (9th–10th centuries), algebra was developed, along with advances in spherical trigonometry and the decimal point. Greek and Arabic texts were translated into Latin during the Middle Ages. The early modern period saw accelerating development in Western Europe: Viète's variables, Napier's logarithms (1614), Descartes' coordinates, and Newton's and Leibniz's calculus. Euler unified these with standardized terminology. Gauss was perhaps the foremost 19th-century mathematician. Gödel's incompleteness theorems transformed mathematics in the early 20th century. The Mathematical Reviews database has contained more than 1.9 million items since 1940, with over 75,000 added annually.

### Symbolic notation and terminology

Mathematical notation uses symbols for operations, relations, and objects, assembled into expressions and formulas. Variables are generally Latin or Greek letters; operations use glyphs like +, ×, ∫, =, and <. An axiom is a statement taken as true without proof; a conjecture is unproven; a proven statement is a theorem; a lemma mainly proves another theorem; a corollary is a proven instance of a more general finding. Many technical terms are neologisms (polynomial, homeomorphism); others use common words with different meanings (in mathematics, "or" means "one, the other or both").

### Relationship with sciences

Mathematics models phenomena, allowing predictions from experimental laws. Inaccurate predictions imply the need to change the model, not invalid mathematical concepts—for example, Mercury's perihelion precession required Einstein's general relativity. Whether mathematics is a science is debated, but in practice mathematicians are grouped with scientists. Mathematics is falsifiable (a counterexample can disprove a result) and theories are often obtained from experimentation, though it differs from modern science by not relying on empirical evidence.

**Pure and applied mathematics** were not clearly distinguished until the 19th century, when mathematicians like Weierstrass and Dedekind focused on internal problems. The aftermath of World War II led to a surge in applied mathematics. The distinction is now more a question of personal research aim than a division into broad areas.

**Unreasonable effectiveness**: Named by physicist Eugene Wigner, this is the phenomenon that many mathematical theories have applications outside their initial object. Examples: prime factorization (discovered over 2,000 years before use in RSA cryptosystem), ellipses (studied by Greeks, used by Kepler for planetary orbits nearly 2,000 years later), and non-Euclidean geometries (developed in the 19th century, used by Einstein for relativity in the early 20th century). Mathematics also drives physics research, as with the positron and baryon Ω⁻ discoveries.

**Specific sciences**: Physics and mathematics have influenced each other throughout modern history. Computing is closely related to mathematics; theoretical computer science is considered mathematical in nature. Statistics employs probability theory for data processing and decision problems. Biology uses probability (evolutionary fitness, ecology modeling with Lotka–Volterra equations) and statistical hypothesis testing for clinical trials. Earth sciences use probabilistic models for natural catastrophe prediction. Social sciences use probability/statistics and differential equations; mathematical economics postulates *Homo economicus* (the rational individual maximizing self-interest), though this concept is criticized.

### Philosophy

**Reality**: Philosophical debates about mathematics and material reality date to Pythagoras. Plato argued abstractions have reality outside space and time; this view is called Platonism. Modern mathematicians may generally be considered Platonists, though Platonism doesn't explain the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics.

**Proposed definitions**: There is no consensus on the definition of mathematics. Aristotle defined it as "the science of quantity," prevailing until the 18th century. With new areas appearing since the early 20th century, defining mathematics by its object has become difficult. Another approach defines it by its methods: an area is mathematics if one can prove theorems.

**Rigor**: Mathematical reasoning requires unambiguous definitions and proofs reducible to inference rules without empirical evidence. Rigorous proofs can require hundreds of pages (the 255-page Feit–Thompson theorem). The concept dates to ancient Greece. At the end of the 19th century, paradoxes (non-Euclidean geometries, Russell's paradox) showed definitions were inaccurate; this was solved by including axioms with inference rules. "Rigor" is no longer a relevant concept—a proof is either correct or erroneous—but remains useful for teaching beginners.

### Training and practice

Mathematics education occurred as early as the second millennium BCE in Babylonia. The oldest known mathematics textbook is the Rhind papyrus (c. 1650 BCE in Egypt). Mathematics is a core part of the STEM curriculum. Mathematical anxiety is the most prominent disorder impacting academic performance and can be countered by instructional changes and tailored treatments. Creativity is essential: many important results solve problems others failed to solve, as with Apéry's theorem. Mathematicians find aesthetic value in elegance—simplicity, symmetry, completeness, generality. G. H. Hardy believed aesthetic considerations justify pure mathematics; Paul Erdős spoke of "The Book," a divine collection of the most beautiful proofs.

### Cultural impact

Notes sounding well together have fundamental frequencies in simple ratios (an octave doubles frequency; a perfect fifth multiplies by 3/2). Humans find symmetric patterns beautiful; symmetries form a group (the symmetry group). Popular mathematics presents mathematics without technical terms, though mathematics is rarely the topic of popularization in printed or televised media. The most prestigious award is the Fields Medal (established 1936, awarded every four years to up to four individuals). Other awards include the Abel Prize (2003), Chern Medal (2010), AMS Leroy P. Steele Prize (1970), and Wolf Prize (1978). Hilbert's problems (1900) included 23 open problems, at least thirteen solved. The Millennium Prize Problems (2000) list seven problems with $1 million rewards; only the Poincaré conjecture has been solved, by Grigori Perelman.

## Terms

- **Axiom/postulate**: A mathematical statement taken as true without proof
- **Theorem**: A statement proven true through rigorous deductive reasoning
- **Conjecture**: A mathematical statement not yet proven or disproven
- **Algebraic structure**: A set with operations and rules those operations must follow
- **Axiomatic method**: Systematizing mathematics by defining objects through axioms and inference rules
- **Foundational crisis**: Late 19th-century realization that basic mathematical definitions were insufficient for rigor, solved by formalized set theory
- **Unreasonable effectiveness**: The phenomenon where mathematical theories find applications outside their initial object
- **Platonism**: The view that mathematical objects exist independently in abstraction
- **Mathematical anxiety**: Apprehension or fear about performance in mathematics
- **Fields Medal**: The most prestigious mathematics award, established 1936

## Debates and open questions

- Whether mathematics is a science or an art; there is no consensus on its definition or epistemological status
- The relationship between mathematical truth, logic, and reality is a subject of philosophical debate
- Platonism doesn't explain why mathematics matches reality (the unreasonable effectiveness)
- Goldbach's conjecture (1742) remains unproven: every even integer greater than 2 is the sum of two primes
- The P versus NP problem remains open; its solution would impact many computationally difficult problems
- Whether mathematical results are created (as in art) or discovered (as in science)
- The concept of *Homo economicus* in mathematical economics is criticized because real people have limited information and care about fairness, not just personal gain
- The choice of counting criteria (e.g., for unemployment) or models in complex systems can be subject to controversy
