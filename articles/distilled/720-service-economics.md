# Service (economics)

## Overview
A service is an intangible act or performance provided by a service provider to a consumer, company, or government in exchange for payment. Unlike physical goods, services are produced and consumed simultaneously, cannot be stored, and are inherently variable. Understanding services is essential because they dominate modern economies, comprising the majority of employment and value creation in developed nations.

## Timeline
- **Late 18th century** — Adam Smith distinguishes "productive" (goods) from "unproductive" (services) labor in *The Wealth of Nations* (1776)
- **Early 19th century** — Jean-Baptiste Say argues production and consumption are inseparable in services; coins "immaterial products"
- **Modern era** — Gustafsson & Johnson describe the service-commodity goods continuum

## Body

### Characteristics of Services
Services possess three defining characteristics: **intangibility**, **perishability**, and **variability**. Services are intangible—they are not manufactured, transported, or stocked. They are produced and consumed simultaneously, meaning they cannot be inventoried for future use. Perishability manifests in two ways: first, service-relevant resources assigned for delivery during a specific period may go unused if not consumed (e.g., an empty airplane seat); second, once a service is fully rendered, it irreversibly vanishes (e.g., a completed flight). Variability means each service delivery is unique due to differences in time, location, circumstances, or assigned resources, making exact repetition impossible.

### Service Quality
Mass generation and delivery of services require mastering service quality, which is challenging because both inputs and outputs are highly variable. Many services depend on variable human activity rather than precisely determined processes, making consistency difficult. Demand fluctuates by season, time of day, and business cycle. Consistency is necessary to build enduring customer relationships.

### Specification
Services can be specified using standard attributes following the MECE principle (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive). Key attributes include: service consumer benefits (triggerable, consumable benefits rendered upon request); service-specific functional parameters (essential dimensions like seat type); service delivery point (physical or logical location of benefit rendering); service consumer count; service delivery readiness time; service consumer support times and language; service fulfillment target (ratio of successful deliveries to requests); service impairment duration; service delivery duration; service delivery unit (scope of actions constituting a service); and service delivery price (access fee plus consumption fee).

### Delivery
Service delivery involves six factors: the service provider (workers and managers), equipment, physical facilities, the service consumer, other customers at the delivery location, and customer contact. The service encounter encompasses all activities in the delivery process. Business theorists view service provision as a performance, with the delivery location as the "stage," facilitating objects as "props," and behavioral sequences as "scripts." Role congruence occurs when all actors' scripts harmonize. In some industries (healthcare, social services), the concept of "caseload" governs how employees balance individual cases against collective needs. Under English law, delivering services to a dishonest client via deception is an offense under the Theft Act 1978. Lovelock's 2×3 matrix classifies services by delivery sites and methods, noting convenience decreases when customers must visit a single specific outlet.

### Service-Commodity Goods Continuum
The distinction between goods and services remains disputed. Classical economists (Smith, Say) viewed goods as storable, ownable objects and services as perishable "immaterial products." The modern view, per Gustafsson & Johnson, places pure services and pure commodity goods at opposite ends of a continuum, with most offerings falling between. A restaurant, for example, delivers both a physical good (food) and services (ambience, table setting). Utilities typically deliver physical goods (water) but are treated as services.

### Service Types
Service industries span numerous sectors: business functions (consulting, HR, maintenance); death care (coroners, funeral homes); dispute resolution (arbitration, courts, mediation); education (schools, libraries, museums); entertainment (performing arts, sports); financial services (banking, insurance, real estate); foodservice; healthcare; hospitality; information services; logistics; personal grooming; public utilities (power, gas, telecom, waste, water); risk management; and social services (childcare, elderly care).

## Terms
- **Intangibility**: Services cannot be touched, stored, or inventoried; they are produced and consumed simultaneously.
- **Perishability**: Services and their delivery resources cannot be saved for later use; unused capacity is lost.
- **Variability (Heterogeneity)**: Each service delivery differs due to unique time, place, circumstances, or resources.
- **Service encounter**: All activities involved in the service delivery process.
- **Caseload**: The total number of cases (patients, clients, etc.) for which an employee is responsible.
- **Service-commodity goods continuum**: A spectrum from pure services to pure tangible goods, with most offerings falling between.
- **MECE**: Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive—a principle for organizing service specifications.
- **Service delivery unit**: The scope or number of actions constituting a delivered service; basis for pricing and billing.
- **Role congruence**: When each service participant follows a script that harmonizes with others' roles.
- **Dramaturgical approach**: Viewing service provision as a performance with stages, props, and scripts.

## Debates and Open Questions
The boundary between goods and services remains disputed. Classical economists like Adam Smith categorized services as "unproductive" labor because they perish at production and do not contribute to stored wealth, while Jean-Baptiste Say emphasized their immaterial nature. Modern perspectives reject this binary, favoring a continuum where most offerings blend tangible and intangible elements. Whether utilities that deliver physical goods (e.g., water) should be classified as services or goods also remains debated.
