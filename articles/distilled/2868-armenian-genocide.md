# Armenian genocide

## Overview
The Armenian genocide was the systematic, state-sponsored destruction of the Armenian people and identity in the Ottoman Empire during World War I. Orchestrated by the ruling Committee of Union and Progress (CUP), it resulted in the murder of an estimated one million Armenians through death marches and massacres, and the forced Islamization of others. It is considered a foundational event for the modern Republic of Turkey and a defining example of 20th-century genocide.

## Timeline
- **1894–1896** — Hamidian massacres kill at least 100,000 Armenians.
- **1908** — CUP comes to power in the Young Turk Revolution.
- **1909** — Adana massacre kills 20,000–25,000, mostly Armenians.
- **1912–1913** — Balkan Wars cause Ottoman territorial losses and mass Muslim expulsion.
- **29 Oct 1914** — Ottoman Empire enters World War I.
- **Dec 1914–Jan 1915** — Battle of Sarikamish; Enver Pasha blames Armenians for defeat.
- **24 Apr 1915** — Ottoman authorities arrest Armenian intellectuals in Constantinople.
- **27 May 1915** — Temporary Law of Deportation grants legal cover for mass removals.
- **Feb–Oct 1916** — Second wave of massacres kills over 200,000 deportees in Syria.
- **1918** — Armistice of Mudros ends Ottoman participation in WWI.
- **1919–1920** — Ottoman Special Military Tribunal sentences CUP leaders for genocide.
- **1923** — Republic of Turkey is proclaimed.

## Background and Causes
Armenians, a Christian minority, had a long-standing but subordinate place in the multi-ethnic Ottoman Empire. By 1914, about two million Armenians lived in Ottoman territory, mostly in Anatolia. The CUP, which seized power in 1913, promoted an exclusionary Turkish nationalism. The empire's devastating losses in the Balkan Wars (1912–1913) intensified anti-Christian sentiment and fear that Anatolia, considered the last refuge of the Turkish nation, would be lost. CUP leaders, including Talaat Pasha and Enver Pasha, came to view the Armenian population as an internal threat and a potential fifth column for Russia.

## The Genocide (1915–1916)
The genocide began on 24 April 1915 with the arrest of Armenian leaders. The CUP ordered the mass deportation of Armenians from eastern Anatolia and later the entire empire, ostensibly for security reasons during WWI. The deportations were a death sentence. Able-bodied men were typically separated and executed. Women, children, and the elderly were forced on death marches toward the Syrian desert, deprived of food and water, and subjected to robbery, rape, and massacre. An estimated 800,000 to 1.2 million Armenians were deported, with only about 200,000 surviving by late 1916. The Special Organization, a paramilitary unit, and local militias carried out the killings. A second wave of massacres in 1916 targeted survivors in concentration camps in Syria.

## Islamization and Property Confiscation
A key component of the genocide was the forced Islamization of Armenians, primarily women and children. An estimated 100,000 to 200,000 were converted to Islam and integrated into Muslim households, losing their Armenian identity. The CUP simultaneously confiscated Armenian property, businesses, and land. This was part of a broader project to restructure Anatolia's demographics and create a Turkish-Muslim middle class. The proceeds funded the genocide and later formed the economic basis of the Republic of Turkey.

## Aftermath and Legacy
The genocide destroyed over two millennia of Armenian civilization in eastern Anatolia. It enabled the creation of an ethnonationalist Turkish state. The postwar Ottoman government held trials that convicted CUP leaders of "mass murder," but most perpetrators escaped punishment. The Turkish War of Independence (1919–1923) completed the ethnic cleansing, and the Republic of Turkey was founded in 1923. The Turkish government officially denies the genocide, maintaining the deportations were a legitimate wartime measure. As of 2026, 36 countries have formally recognized it. The genocide is extensively documented in international archives and is recognized by the overwhelming consensus of historians.

## Terms
*   **Committee of Union and Progress (CUP):** The secret revolutionary party that ruled the Ottoman Empire from 1913, also known as the Young Turks, which orchestrated the genocide.
*   **Millet system:** The Ottoman system granting non-Muslim religious communities (like Armenians) limited autonomy and protection in exchange for a special tax and subordinate status.
*   **Special Organization (Teşkilât-ı Mahsusa):** A paramilitary unit, often composed of released convicts, used by the CUP to carry out massacres during the genocide.
*   **Temporary Law of Deportation (Tehcir Law):** A 1915 law that provided a legal pretext for the mass deportation of Armenians.
*   **Islamization:** The forced conversion of Armenians to Islam, a systematic policy aimed at erasing their cultural and religious identity.
*   **Abandoned Properties Laws:** Ottoman and Turkish laws used to confiscate and redistribute Armenian property after their deportation.
*   **Vorpahavak:** The post-genocide Armenian effort to reclaim kidnapped and Islamized women and children.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Turkish Government Position:** Turkey acknowledges wartime suffering and deaths but denies the events constitute genocide, arguing deportations were a legitimate security measure and that deaths resulted from civil war and disease.
*   **Death Toll:** While the academic consensus is around one million, estimates range from 600,000 to 1.5 million. The exact figure is debated due to the destruction of records and lack of a pre-war Ottoman census.
*   **German Role:** The extent of knowledge and complicity of Germany, the Ottoman Empire's WWI ally, is a subject of ongoing historical research and debate.
*   **International Recognition:** Despite growing recognition, many countries have avoided formal designation to maintain diplomatic relations with Turkey.
