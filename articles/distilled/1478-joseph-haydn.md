# Joseph Haydn

## Overview
Franz Joseph Haydn (1732–1809) was an Austrian composer whose structural innovations defined the Classical era. Rising from rural poverty to become Kapellmeister for the wealthy Esterházy family, he leveraged isolation at their remote palace to "become original," standardizing the four-movement symphony, the string quartet, and sonata form. His 1779 contract renegotiation allowed publication, making him Europe’s most celebrated composer by 1790. Two triumphant London journeys (1791–92, 1794–95) produced his final twelve symphonies and secured his finances. Late oratorios *The Creation* (1798) and *The Seasons* (1801) shifted the paradigm from courtly service to public art for posterity. He mentored Mozart, taught Beethoven, and, alongside them, forms the "First Viennese School."

## Timeline
- **31 Mar 1732** — Born in Rohrau, Austria, to a wheelwright father and cook mother.
- **1740–1749** — Chorister at St. Stephen’s Cathedral, Vienna; minimal theory instruction.
- **1749–1757** — Freelance in Vienna; studies Fux’s *Gradus ad Parnassum* and C.P.E. Bach’s sonatas; valet for Nicola Porpora (1752).
- **1757–1761** — Kapellmeister to Count Morzin; writes first symphonies (~10–20) and string quartets.
- **1761–1790** — Vice-Kapellmeister, then full Kapellmeister (1766), for Esterházy family at Eisenstadt and Eszterháza; composes ~200 baryton works, operas, symphonies, quartets.
- **1779** — Contract renegotiated: retains copyright, enabling international publication (Op. 33 quartets, Paris symphonies).
- **1790–1795** — Two London journeys organized by J.P. Salomon; premieres 12 "London" symphonies; awarded Oxford doctorate (1791).
- **1792** — Meets Beethoven in Bonn; teaches him counterpoint in Vienna (until 1794).
- **1795–1803** — Part-time Kapellmeister for Nikolaus II; composes six masses, *The Creation* (1798), *The Seasons* (1801), late quartets, Trumpet Concerto.
- **1797** — Writes "Gott erhalte Franz den Kaiser" (Emperor’s Hymn); later German national anthem melody.
- **1803–1809** — Debility (likely arteriosclerosis) halts composition; final public appearance 1808; dies in Vienna 31 May 1809, aged 77.

## Body

### Early Life and Musical Formation
Haydn’s musical aptitude led his parents to apprentice him at age six to relative Johann Matthias Frankh in Hainburg, where he endured hunger and neglect but learned harpsichord, violin, and singing. In 1739, Georg Reutter the Younger recruited him for the St. Stephen’s Cathedral choir in Vienna. For nine years, Haydn absorbed professional standards by osmosis—Reutter gave only two theory lessons—while suffering similar privation. Dismissed in 1749 when his voice broke, he survived as a freelance teacher, street serenader, and valet-accompanist to Nicola Porpora (1752), from whom he learned "the true fundamentals of composition." Self-taught via Fux’s counterpoint treatise and C.P.E. Bach’s keyboard sonatas—"I owe a great deal to Emanuel Bach"—Haydn composed his first opera, *Der krumme Teufel* (1753), and attracted aristocratic patronage: Countess Thun, Baron Fürnberg (first string quartets at Weinzierl), and finally Count Morzin (1757), his first full-time employer.

### The Esterházy Decades: Isolation as Laboratory
At Count Morzin’s court (1757–61), Haydn wrote his first symphonies and married Maria Anna Keller (1760), an unhappy, childless union. Financial trouble forced Morzin to dismiss his orchestra, but Prince Paul Anton Esterházy hired Haydn as Vice-Kapellmeister (1761), promoting him to full Kapellmeister upon Gregor Werner’s death (1766). For nearly thirty years, Haydn served the music-loving princes Paul Anton and Nikolaus I at Eisenstadt and the new, remote palace Eszterháza in rural Hungary. His duties were exhaustive: composition, orchestra management, chamber music performance, and eventually opera production (a major season from ~1775). Yet the post offered a private orchestra for daily experimentation: "I could... make experiments, observe what enhanced an effect... I was set apart from the world... and so I had to be original." Prince Nikolaus’s hobby shifts dictated output: ~200 works for baryton (1765–75), then intensive opera management. The 1779 contract revision—granting publication rights—pivoted Haydn toward instrumental music (Op. 33, 50, 54/55, 64 quartets; Paris symphonies) and international fame, even as Eszterháza’s isolation deepened his loneliness, relieved partly by correspondence with Maria Anna von Genzinger (from 1789) and friendship with Mozart (from ~1784), including quartet-playing and shared Masonic lodge membership (1785).

### London Journeys and International Celebrity
Prince Nikolaus’s death (1790) brought his son Anton, who dismissed most musicians but retained Haydn with a pension and travel permission. Impresario Johann Peter Salomon engaged him for London, where Haydn’s music had dominated concerts since J.C. Bach’s death (1782). The first journey (1791–92) yielded symphonies Nos. 95, 96, 93, 94 ("Surprise"), 97, 98, and the "Oxford" No. 92 (performed for his honorary doctorate). A rival series hired his former pupil Ignaz Pleyel, but the two refused manufactured rivalry. The second journey (1794–95) premiered Nos. 99–104 ("Drumroll," "London") at the King’s Theatre (800 seats). Haydn profited handsomely, met Beethoven in Bonn (1790) and taught him in Vienna (1792–94), visited William Herschel (inspiring *The Creation*), and called his English days "the happiest of his life."

### Late Vienna: Artist for Posterity
Returning to Vienna (1795), Haydn served Nikolaus II part-time, writing six masses (including *Lord Nelson*, 1798). Wealthy and honored, he shifted from deadline-driven production to deliberate creation for posterity. With librettist Gottfried van Swieten and funding from the *Gesellschaft der Associierten*, he composed the oratorios *The Creation* (1798)—depicting chaos resolving into blinding C-major light at "Let there be light"—and *The Seasons* (1801), plus late quartets (Op. 76: "Emperor," "Sunrise"), the Trumpet Concerto, and the "Emperor’s Hymn" (1797), adopted as Austria’s anthem and later Germany’s. This "popular style" fused folk-like material with rigorous structure, anchoring large forms. His approach influenced Beethoven (Waldstein: "receive the spirit of Mozart from the hands of Haydn") and, per E.T.A. Hoffmann (1814), the trio "developed a new art."

### Decline and Death
From 1803, arteriosclerosis (per Jones) caused weakness, dizziness, swollen legs, and concentration loss, though musical ideas "pursued" him to torture. The Esterházys retained him honorifically but appointed successors (Fuchs 1802, Hummel 1804). He abandoned a *Missa brevis* revision (1805) and published a two-movement final quartet. A 1808 *Creation* performance in his honor—greeted by Beethoven and Salieri—moved and exhausted him. During Napoleon’s bombardment of Vienna (May 1809), he calmed servants: "where Haydn is, no harm can reach you." He died 31 May 1809, aged 77. His skull, stolen by phrenologists, was reunited with his remains in 1954.

### Musical Structure and Innovation
Haydn built large forms from tiny motifs, often standard accompaniment figures, creating formally concentrated movements where events unfold rapidly—music that "lives on its nerves" (Sutcliffe). He codified sonata form but diverged from Mozart/Beethoven: frequent **monothematic exposition** (dominant theme = opening theme), and **rearranged recapitulations** with extensive development, losing "first tame symmetry to regain a balance of a far higher... order" (Hughes). He integrated fugue into Classical style (e.g., three Op. 20 quartet finales), enriched rondo with tonal logic (**sonata rondo form**), and pioneered **double variation form** (alternating major/minor themes). His 107 symphonies and 68 quartets combine unmatched productivity, quality, and historical weight.

### Expressive Character: Joy, Wit, and Depth
Karl Geiringer roots Haydn’s pervasive gaiety in "victorious optimism maintained through all vicissitudes"; Charles Rosen identifies a unique "ecstasy... completely unsensual, almost amiable." Exceptions probe darkness: *The Seven Last Words of Christ*, the "mesto" (sorrowful) slow movement of Op. 76 No. 5, the F-minor Variations coda. Humor is structural: the "Surprise" Symphony’s loud chord (to outshine rival Pleyel), false endings (Op. 33 No. 2, Op. 50 No. 3), rhythmic illusions (Op. 50 No. 1 trio). Fast finales are "rollicking" and propulsive (Symphony 104, Op. 50 No. 1). Slow movements deepen from relaxed reflection to profound feeling (Op. 76 Nos. 3, 5; Symphonies 98, 102). Minuets evolve from strong-downbeat dances into fast, one-beat **scherzi**.

### Stylistic Evolution Across Six Decades
Born 18 years before J.S. Bach’s death, Haydn was shaped not by Baroque giants but by Viennese pre-Classical figures (Reutter, Wagenseil, Monn) and, crucially, C.P.E. Bach’s emotional depth ("deeply stirred and excited him"). The **Sturm und Drang** period (late 1760s–early 1770s) brought minor-key intensity, longer forms, and fugal finales (Symphonies 44 "Trauer," 45 "Farewell"; Op. 20 "Sun" Quartets). A lighter, opera-focused phase followed (trumpets/timpani in symphonies, recycled opera material). The 1779 contract freedom sparked the **Classical style in full flower** (Op. 33 quartets, 1781): fluid phrasing, accompaniment-to-melody evolution, "Classical counterpoint" with independent parts. The **London-inspired "popular style"** (1790s) deployed folk-like material at structural anchors (exposition ends, finale openings) for stability within rigor. Finally, as a wealthy Viennese celebrity (1795–1802), he wrote slowly for posterity, tackling the sublime in *The Creation* and *The Seasons*.

## Terms
- ****Kapellmeister**** — Music director of a court or chapel; responsible for composition, performance, and personnel.
- ****Sonata form**** — A structural paradigm (exposition, development, recapitulation) for single movements, codified in the Classical era.
- ****Monothematic exposition**** — A sonata-form exposition where the secondary theme in the dominant key is derived from or identical to the opening theme.
- ****Double variation form**** — A movement form alternating variations on two themes, often major and minor versions of the same idea.
- ****Sturm und Drang**** — "Storm and stress"; a late-1760s/early-1770s stylistic phase of heightened minor-key expression and formal expansion.
- ****Baryton**** — A bowed string instrument (bass viol family) with plucked sympathetic strings; Prince Nikolaus’s hobby prompted ~200 Haydn works.
- ****Sonata rondo form**** — A hybrid form (ABACABA) merging rondo’s recurring refrain with sonata’s tonal development.
- ****First Viennese School**** — The trio Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven, viewed as the central architects of Viennese Classicism.
- ****Hoboken catalogue**** — The definitive thematic catalogue of Haydn’s works (Anthony van Hoboken, 1978), assigning Hob. numbers.
- ****Fortepiano**** — The early piano (leather hammers, lighter frame) for which Haydn wrote late keyboard works; distinct from modern piano.

## Debates and open questions
- **Symphony count**: The exact number of early symphonies for Count Morzin is unknown (source cites "perhaps about 10–20").
- **Illness diagnosis**: The precise cause of Haydn’s final debility (1803–09) is uncertain; Jones suggests arteriosclerosis, but period medicine lacked definitive diagnostics.
- **Sturm und Drang causality**: Whether the musical *Sturm und Drang* preceded or followed the literary movement is debated; the source notes musical development may have preceded the literary by a few years.
- **Op. 33 "new and special way"**: Charles Rosen argues Haydn’s 1781 claim was substantive (marking full Classical style), not mere salesmanship; the precise technical boundaries of this advance remain a scholarly focus.
- **Folk material authenticity**: The extent to which Haydn’s "folk-like" themes quote actual folk songs versus composed imitation is an open musicological question (referenced under "Haydn and folk music").
- **Business ethics**: Webster describes Haydn’s publishing practices (selling same work to multiple publishers) as "sharp practice" bordering on fraud; scholars debate whether this reflects ruthlessness or adaptation to nascent copyright and rampant piracy.
