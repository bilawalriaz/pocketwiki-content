# Kanishka

## Overview
Kanishka I (r. c. 127–150 CE) was the emperor of the Kushan dynasty whose reign marked the empire's zenith. He ruled a vast territory from Central Asia to the Gangetic plain and is renowned for his military conquests, administrative reforms, and crucial patronage of Buddhism, which facilitated the religion's transmission along the Silk Road to China.

## Timeline
- **c. 127 CE** — Kanishka ascends the Kushan throne, replacing Greek with Bactrian as the official administrative language.
- **c. 127–150 CE** — Reign of Kanishka I; the Kushan Empire reaches its greatest territorial extent.
- **90 AD** — (Earlier) Kushan viceroy Xie fights Chinese general Ban Chao near Khotan, indicating prior Kushan presence in Central Asia.
- **c. 127 CE** — Kanishka administers the 4th Buddhist Council in Kashmir.
- **c. 127 CE** — The "Kanishka casket" is dedicated, dated to the first year of his reign.
- **c. 178 CE** — Kushan monk Lokaksema begins translating Mahayana Buddhist scriptures into Chinese at Loyang.

## Body

### Genealogy and Origins
Kanishka was of Yuezhi ethnicity, likely a native Tocharian speaker. The Rabatak inscription provides a clear genealogy: he was the son of Vima Kadphises, grandson of Vima Taktu, and great-grandson of the empire's founder, Kujula Kadphises. Two theories about his specific origins propose he was either summoned from Khotan as an ethnic ally or was originally the king of Kashmir before becoming the dynasty's suzerain.

### Empire and Conquests
Kanishka's empire was vast, extending from southern Uzbekistan and Tajikistan to northern India, including Kashmir and the Gangetic plain. The Rabatak inscription claims he held Pataliputra and Sri Champa. His hold on Central Asia is less certain, though coins have been found in the Tarim Basin. He possibly repelled a Parthian invasion. His main capitals were Puruṣapura (Peshawar) and Mathura.

### Coinage and Religious Syncretism
Kanishka's coins demonstrate religious syncretism, initially depicting Greek deities with legends in Greek script. Later coins feature Iranian and Indic deities with legends in Bactrian (written in a modified Greek script). The king is typically shown as a bearded figure in a long coat and trousers, with flames emanating from his shoulders. Buddhist deities like Buddha, Shakyamuni, and Maitreya appear on a small fraction of his coins, often in a frontal, symbolic style distinct from other deities.

### Patronage of Buddhism
Although Kanishka never converted to Buddhism, he was its greatest royal patron. He administered the 4th Buddhist Council in Kashmir, presided over by Vasumitra and Ashwaghosha. His patronage supported the Gandhara and Mathura schools of art. His greatest architectural contribution was the colossal Kanishka Stupa at Peshawar, estimated to be 180–210 meters tall. The "Kanishka casket," discovered at the stupa's base, is a key relic container from his reign.

### Transmission to China
Kanishka's reign and the Kushan Empire's position on the Silk Road were pivotal for spreading Mahayana Buddhism eastward. The Kushan monk Lokaksema established the first translation bureau for Buddhist scriptures in the Chinese capital Loyang around 178 CE, initiating a centuries-long exchange of ideas.

## Terms
- **Kushan Dynasty:** A syncretic empire that ruled parts of Central and South Asia from the 1st to 3rd centuries CE.
- **Bactrian:** An Iranian language that became the official administrative language under Kanishka, written in a modified Greek script.
- **Rabatak Inscription:** A key historical record listing Kanishka's genealogy and confirming his succession from Vima Kadphises.
- **4th Buddhist Council:** A council held under Kanishka's patronage in Kashmir, traditionally associated with the compilation of Abhidharma texts.
- **Gandhara School:** A Greco-Buddhist art style from the region of Gandhara, characterized by Hellenistic influences on Buddhist iconography.
- **Mathura School:** An Indian art style centered at Mathura, known for its distinct Buddhist and Hindu sculptures.
- **Kanishka Stupa:** A massive Buddhist stupa at Peshawar, built by Kanishka and described by Chinese pilgrims.
- **Kanishka Casket:** A relic container from the first year of Kanishka's reign, inscribed in Kharoshthi and signed by a Greek artist.
- **Silk Road:** Ancient trade and cultural exchange network connecting East and West Asia, facilitated by the Kushan Empire.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Accession Date:** Earlier scholars dated Kanishka's accession to 78 CE, linking it to the Saka calendar era. This is now disputed, with 127 CE being the current estimate.
- **Origins:** Theories about Kanishka's specific origin—whether from Khotan or Kashmir—remain speculative.
- **Religious Affiliation:** While a patron of Buddhism, Kanishka's personal beliefs are debated; his coins show he embraced a syncretic mix of Buddhist, Iranian, and other deities.
- **Attribution of the Kanishka Casket:** Recent stylistic analysis has disputed the casket's attribution to Kanishka I, suggesting it may belong to his successor Huvishka.
