# Antigua and Barbuda

## Overview
Antigua and Barbuda is a small Caribbean twin-island nation with a complex history shaped by indigenous settlement, European colonization, slavery, and a modern struggle for political and economic development. It is a high-income, tourism-dependent country and the most developed in the Caribbean by the Human Development Index, yet it faces significant challenges from climate change, political polarization, and human rights concerns.

## Body

### History and Politics
The islands were first settled by hunter-gatherers around 3700 BC, followed by Arawak and later Carib peoples. Christopher Columbus sighted Antigua in 1493, but effective colonization began with the English in 1632. A plantation economy based on sugar and enslaved African labor was established. Slavery was abolished in 1834, and the islands slowly gained autonomy, becoming an associated state in 1967 and achieving full independence in 1981 as a constitutional monarchy within the Commonwealth.

Politics were dominated by the Bird family from 1960 until 2004. The current government is a unitary parliamentary democracy with Charles III as head of state. The executive branch consists of a Governor-General and a Prime Minister. The legislature is bicameral, with a Senate and an elected House of Representatives. Barbuda maintains significant autonomy, administered by the Barbuda Council since 1976. The two major parties are the Antigua and Barbuda Labour Party (ABLP) and the United Progressive Party (UPP).

### Geography and Climate
Antigua and Barbuda is a low-lying archipelago formed by limestone, not volcanic activity. The highest point is Boggy Peak (402 m) on Antigua. The climate is tropical savanna, with average temperatures between 23°C and 30°C. Rainfall is low and variable, leading to droughts. Hurricanes are a major threat; Hurricane Irma in 2017 rendered Barbuda "barely habitable," destroying 95% of its buildings. The country has a biocapacity deficit, using more ecological resources than its territory contains.

### Economy and Demographics
The economy is service-based, dominated by tourism, which accounts for over half of GDP. Financial services and a Citizenship by Investment program are also significant. The country abolished personal income tax in 2016 to attract high-net-worth individuals. Agriculture is limited by water scarcity and labor shortages.

The population is estimated at 106,365 (2026), with a high proportion of foreign-born residents (30% in 2011). The majority are of African descent. The most spoken language is Antiguan and Barbudan Creole, though English is the working language. Christianity is the dominant religion. Education is free and compulsory for ages 5-16.

### Culture
Culture reflects African and European influences. The national music genre is Benna, a call-and-response style. The Antigua Carnival, celebrating freedom from slavery, is a major annual event. Cricket is the most popular sport. National dishes include fungee (cornmeal) and pepperpot.

## Terms
- **Constitutional monarchy:** A system of government where a monarch (e.g., King Charles III) is the head of state, but executive power is exercised by an elected government.
- **Unitary state:** A system of political organization where the central government is supreme and any administrative divisions exercise only powers delegated by it.
- **Bicameral legislature:** A legislative body with two chambers or houses (here, the Senate and House of Representatives).
- **Associated state:** A free association where a smaller state is internally self-governing but relies on a larger state for defense and foreign affairs.
- **Benna:** A traditional Antiguan music genre characterized by call-and-response singing, often conveying social commentary.
- **Biocapacity deficit:** A situation where a population's ecological footprint exceeds the biocapacity of its territory, meaning it consumes more resources than its land can regenerate.
- **Citizenship by Investment (CBI):** A program that grants citizenship to foreign individuals in exchange for a significant financial investment in the country.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Barbudan autonomy and land rights:** The central government's post-Hurricane Irma proposal to revoke Barbuda's communal land ownership system and allow private land sales has been criticized as "disaster capitalism" and has fueled a significant Barbudan independence movement.
- **Human rights and press freedom:** Reports of human rights violations have increased since 2017. Freedom of the press is constitutionally guaranteed but not fully protected, with government officials known to sue opponents and media, leading to widespread self-censorship.
- **Political polarization:** The country's politics are dominated by two parties, the ABLP and UPP, leading to significant polarization. The government has expressed opposition to local governance structures like parish councils.
