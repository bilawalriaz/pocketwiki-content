# Chichen Itza

## Overview
Chichen Itza was a major pre-Columbian Maya city in the Yucatán Peninsula, Mexico, that flourished from the Late Classic through the early Postclassic period (c. 600–1200 AD). It was a powerful regional capital and economic hub, known for its diverse architecture blending Maya and central Mexican styles. The site is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of Mexico's most visited archaeological landmarks.

## Timeline
- **c. 750–900 AD** — Site core layout develops during the Late Classic period.
- **c. 800 AD** — The Itza group begins arriving and establishing itself.
- **832 AD** — Earliest known hieroglyphic date at the site.
- **900–1050 AD** — Chichen Itza expands to become a powerful regional capital.
- **998 AD** — Last known hieroglyphic date recorded at the Osario temple.
- **c. 1100 AD** — Archaeological evidence indicates decline as a regional center.
- **1526–1535** — Spanish conquistador Francisco de Montejo attempts to conquer the region; his son briefly occupies Chichen Itza.
- **1843** — John Lloyd Stephens' book popularizes the site internationally.
- **1894–1930s** — Edward Herbert Thompson purchases the hacienda and conducts excavations, including dredging the Sacred Cenote.
- **1923–1940s** — Carnegie Institution conducts major excavation and restoration projects.
- **2007** — El Castillo (Temple of Kukulcán) is named a New Seven Wonder of the World.
- **2010** — The state of Yucatán purchases the land under the core monuments.

## Body

### Name and Location
The name "Chichén Itzá" means "At the mouth of the well of the Itzá" in Maya. The Itzá were an ethnic-lineage group that gained dominance in the region. The city is located in a karst landscape with no surface rivers. Its settlement was enabled by four natural sinkholes (cenotes), most famously the Cenote Sagrado (Sacred Cenote), which was a major pilgrimage site where offerings and possibly human sacrifices were made to the rain god Chaac.

### Political Organization and Economy
Chichen Itza was a major economic power, controlling a circum-peninsular trade route via its port, Isla Cerritos. It obtained distant resources like obsidian and gold through trade and tribute from conquered cities. Its political structure is debated; a "multepal" system of council rule was once popular but has been discredited, with current scholarship favoring a more traditional model of dynastic kingship.

### History and Decline
The city rose to prominence as major southern Maya centers declined. It dominated the northern Yucatán from the 10th century. Its decline as a regional center occurred by c. 1100, before the rise of the later capital Mayapan, which colonial sources claim conquered it in the 13th century. The Spanish found a local population in the area in the 16th century.

### Architecture and Site Description
The site covers at least 5 km² and features diverse architectural styles, including Puuc and Chenes, alongside central Mexican influences. Major structures include:
*   **El Castillo (Temple of Kukulcán):** A 30-meter step-pyramid dedicated to the feathered serpent deity. It encases an earlier pyramid. A shadow effect resembling a serpent appears on its balustrade during equinoxes, though its intentionality is debated.
*   **Great Ball Court:** The largest and best-preserved in Mesoamerica, featuring carved panels and temples.
*   **Temple of the Warriors:** A complex fronted by columns, analogous to structures at the Toltec capital Tula.
*   **Sacred Cenote:** A 60-meter wide sinkhole used for pilgrimage and offerings.
*   **Las Monjas & El Caracol:** A Puuc-style palace complex and a round building theorized to be an observatory aligned with Venus.

### Modern History and Tourism
The site entered popular imagination in 1843. Edward Thompson's excavations (1894–1930s) and later Carnegie Institution work (1923–1940s) were foundational. Tourism grew significantly in the 20th century. In 2007, El Castillo was voted a New Seven Wonder of the World. The site receives millions of visitors annually, with equinox events being particularly popular. Access to climbing structures like El Castillo has been closed for preservation and safety.

## Terms
*   **Cenote:** A natural sinkhole in limestone bedrock that exposes groundwater, crucial for water supply in the Yucatán.
*   **Puuc style:** An architectural style from the northern Yucatán characterized by intricate stone mosaics on upper facades.
*   **Chenes style:** A regional architectural style featuring ornate, mask-like facades.
*   **Sacbe (pl. sacbeob):** A raised, paved causeway connecting structures or sites in Maya cities.
*   **Multepal:** A proposed political system of rule by a council of elite lineages, once theorized for Chichen Itza.
*   **Chac Mool:** A type of stone statue depicting a reclining figure with a tray on its stomach, found in Mesoamerican temples.
*   **Tzompantli:** A platform or rack for displaying the skulls of sacrificed individuals or war captives.
*   **Itzá:** The ethnic-lineage group that gave its name to the city and gained political dominance in the region.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Political Structure:** The nature of Chichen Itza's governance—whether by a single ruler or a council (multepal)—is debated, with current trends favoring traditional kingship models.
*   **Cultural Influence:** The presence of central Mexican architectural styles was once attributed to conquest or migration but is now largely interpreted as cultural diffusion.
*   **Decline and Conquest:** The chronological relationship between Chichen Itza's decline (c. 1100 AD) and its claimed conquest by Mayapan in the 13th century is a "chronological conundrum" requiring further research.
*   **Equinox Phenomenon:** The intentionality of the serpent shadow effect on El Castillo during equinoxes is questioned, as the effect is observable for weeks around the dates.
*   **Cenote Sacrifices:** While artifacts and human remains are found in the Sacred Cenote, the exact nature and scale of sacrificial practices remain debated, with some Spanish sources potentially carrying colonial bias.
