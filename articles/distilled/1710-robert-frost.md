# Robert Frost

## Overview
Robert Frost (1874–1963) was an American poet celebrated for realistic depictions of rural New England life and a mastery of American colloquial speech. He used these settings to examine complex social and philosophical themes, often exploring the loneliness of the individual in an indifferent universe. Frost is the only poet to win four Pulitzer Prizes for Poetry and became a rare "public literary figure," serving as U.S. Poet Laureate (1958–59) and reading at John F. Kennedy’s 1961 inauguration. His work bridges 19th-century traditional forms and 20th-century modernism; he famously rejected free verse as "playing tennis without a net," arguing that meter’s restrictions freed him to focus on content.

## Timeline
- **March 26, 1874** — Born in San Francisco to William Prescott Frost Jr. and Isabelle Moodie.
- **May 5, 1885** — Father dies of tuberculosis; family moves to Lawrence, Massachusetts.
- **1892** — Graduates Lawrence High School (co-valedictorian with future wife Elinor White); publishes first poem.
- **Dec 19, 1895** — Marries Elinor Miriam White.
- **1897–1899** — Attends Harvard University; leaves voluntarily due to illness.
- **1900–1911** — Works farm in Derry, NH (purchased by grandfather); writes early poems; teaches at Pinkerton Academy and New Hampshire Normal School.
- **1912–1915** — Lives in England (Beaconsfield); publishes *A Boy's Will* (1913) and *North of Boston* (1914); meets Edward Thomas, Ezra Pound, T.E. Hulme.
- **1915** — Returns to U.S. due to WWI; buys farm in Franconia, NH; begins career of writing, teaching, lecturing.
- **1924, 1931, 1937, 1943** — Wins four Pulitzer Prizes for *New Hampshire*, *Collected Poems*, *A Further Range*, *A Witness Tree*.
- **1921–1962** — Teaches summers at Bread Loaf School of English, Middlebury College, Ripton, VT.
- **1958–1959** — Serves as United States Poet Laureate.
- **Jan 20, 1961** — Reads "The Gift Outright" at Kennedy inauguration (sunlight prevents reading new poem "Dedication").
- **Jan 29, 1963** — Dies in Boston, age 88; buried in Bennington, VT. Epitaph: "I had a lover's quarrel with the world."

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Frost was born in San Francisco but moved to Lawrence, Massachusetts, at age 11 after his father’s death. Though later associated with rural life, he grew up in the city. He graduated Lawrence High School in 1892 as co-valedictorian with Elinor White. He attended Dartmouth College for two months and Harvard University (1897–99) but left both without a degree. His education was heavily classical, a foundation scholar Helen H. Bacon argues deeply influenced his imagery and technique, linking poems like "Birches" to Euripides’ *Bacchae* and "One More Brevity" to Virgil’s *Aeneid*.

### Marriage, Farming, and the English Turning Point
Frost married Elinor White in 1895. His grandfather purchased a farm in Derry, New Hampshire, where Frost worked for nine years, writing in the early mornings. Farming proved unsuccessful, leading him to teach English at Pinkerton Academy (1906–11) and the New Hampshire Normal School. In 1912, the family sailed to Great Britain, settling in Beaconsfield. There, Frost published his first two collections, *A Boy's Will* (1913) and *North of Boston* (1914), and formed crucial literary relationships. He met Edward Thomas (inspiration for "The Road Not Taken"), T.E. Hulme, and Ezra Pound. Pound wrote the first favorable American review of Frost’s work, though Frost later resented Pound’s attempts to manipulate his prosody.

### Career Establishment and Academic Life
Returning to America in 1915 due to World War I, Frost bought a farm in Franconia, New Hampshire (now The Frost Place museum). He launched a career combining writing, teaching, and lecturing. He taught at Amherst College intermittently (1917–20, 1923–25, 1926–38), encouraging students to capture the "sound of sense"—the myriad intonations of spoken English. From 1921 to 1962, he spent nearly every summer and fall at the Bread Loaf School of English at Middlebury College, shaping its development. He also held a fellowship at the University of Michigan (1921–27), receiving a lifetime appointment as Fellow in Letters.

### Personal Tragedy
Frost’s life was marked by profound grief. His father died in 1885 (leaving $8); his mother died of cancer in 1900. He committed his sister Jeanie to a mental hospital in 1920 (she died 1929). Mental illness and depression affected Frost, his mother, and his daughter Irma (committed 1947). Of six children, only two (Lesley and Irma) outlived him: Elliott died of cholera (1900), Carol by suicide (1940), Marjorie of puerperal fever (1934), and Elinor Bettina lived one day (1907). His wife Elinor, who had heart problems, developed breast cancer in 1937 and died of heart failure in 1938.

### Poetic Style and Critical Reception
Frost’s style sits at the crossroads of 19th-century formal tradition and modernist idiomatic language. He used traditional meter and forms, believing "self-imposed restrictions" helped him focus on content. Critics initially viewed him as a genial New England "folkie." Randall Jarrell’s essays (notably "To The Laodiceans," 1952) revealed a "dark" Frost—desperate, frightened, brave—changing the canon to include poems like "Home Burial," "Directive," "Desert Places," and "Acquainted with the Night." Later, Lawrance Thompson’s biography (1977) portrayed a nastier personality, prompting a reappraisal by critics like Harold Bloom and Joseph Brodsky, who recast Frost as a "bleak and unforgiving modernist." James Radcliffe Squires noted Frost’s distinction lay in the *difficulty and skill* of attaining his visions, not their philosophical purity, as he refused single solutions (science, solipsism, religion) in favor of an amalgam of fact and intuition.

### Themes
Critics identify a tension in Frost’s work. T.K. Whipple emphasized the "dark background" of rural New England life in *North of Boston*, where degeneration sinks into madness, exploring fundamental existential loneliness. Conversely, Harriet Monroe celebrated the "Yankee spirit" and "sympathetic humor" in his character-based narratives, focusing on human reaction to nature’s processes. Frost’s 1939 essay "The Figure a Poem Makes" articulates his poetics: poetry must be a "revelation" for poet and reader alike, born of "the surprise of remembering something I didn't know I knew," requiring "the greatest freedom of the material to move about... regardless of time and space... everything but affinity."

### Influences and Legacy
Frost was influenced by Robert Graves, Rupert Brooke, Thomas Hardy, W.B. Yeats, John Keats, and Ralph Waldo Emerson. He in turn influenced Robert Francis, Seamus Heaney, Richard Wilbur, Edward Thomas, and James Wright. His cultural footprint is vast: "Nothing Gold Can Stay" appears in *The Outsiders* (novel/film); "Fire and Ice" inspired George R.R. Martin’s *A Song of Ice and Fire* and appears in *Twilight* and *Life Is Strange*; "Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening" was quoted at JFK’s casket arrival and in Justin Trudeau’s eulogy for Pierre Trudeau. Major manuscript collections reside at Dartmouth (most significant working manuscripts), the Jones Library (Amherst), Amherst College, and the University of Michigan.

## Terms
- **Sound of sense**: Frost’s term for his colloquial approach to language, capturing the myriad intonations and rhythms of actual spoken English within formal verse.
- **The Figure a Poem Makes**: Title of Frost’s 1939 essay outlining his poetics: poetry as revelation via surprise and affinity, not logic or chronology.
- **Dymock Poets**: A group of poets (including Edward Thomas) in Gloucestershire, England, with whom Frost associated during his 1912–15 stay.
- **Bread Loaf School of English**: Middlebury College’s summer graduate program in Ripton, VT, where Frost taught almost annually 1921–62 and heavily influenced its writing programs.
- **Pulitzer Prize for Poetry**: Award Frost won four times (1924, 1931, 1937, 1943), a record for a poet.
- **United States Poet Laureate**: Official consultant in poetry to the Library of Congress; Frost served 1958–59.
- **Congressional Gold Medal**: Highest civilian award bestowed by U.S. Congress; awarded to Frost in 1960, presented by JFK in 1962.
- **Free verse**: Poetry without regular meter or rhyme; Frost famously dismissed it as "playing tennis without a net."
- **Colloquialism**: Use of ordinary, everyday language in literature; Frost strove for "utter colloquialism" within traditional forms.
- **Epitaph**: Inscription on a tombstone; Frost’s reads "I had a lover's quarrel with the world" (from "The Lesson for Today," 1942).

## Debates and open questions
- **Biographical reputation vs. poetic persona**: Lawrance Thompson’s authorized biography (1966–77) depicted Frost as selfish and vindictive, sharply contrasting his public "rustic sage" image. Scholars debate how much this should color readings of the poems.
- **Modernist or traditionalist?**: Critics dispute whether Frost is a modernist using traditional forms (per The Poetry Foundation) or a 19th-century holdout. Jarrell and Bloom argue for a "dark," complex modernist; earlier views saw only the "folkie."
- **Classical influence extent**: Helen H. Bacon argues for deep, specific Greek/Latin influence (e.g., *Bacchae* in "Birches," Virgil in "One More Brevity"). The degree to which Frost consciously deployed classical allusion versus absorbed it through education remains discussed.
- **Political conservatism**: Frost’s public positions (e.g., skepticism of New Deal, "Balfour Israelite" declaration) and his 1962 Soviet trip to lobby Khrushchev for peace complicate simple labels. Scholars assess how politics inform poems like "Build Soil" or "The Gift Outright."
- **Textual authority of late poems**: Frost revised poems across editions (e.g., "The Gift Outright" line changes for JFK inauguration). The multi-volume *Letters* edition (2014–2026) and *Notebooks* (2007) fuel ongoing editorial and interpretive debates.
