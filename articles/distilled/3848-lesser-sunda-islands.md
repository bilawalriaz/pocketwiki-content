# Lesser Sunda Islands

## Overview
The Lesser Sunda Islands, also known as Nusa Tenggara, are a volcanic archipelago in Indonesia and Timor-Leste. They are geologically and ecologically significant as a zone of transition between Asian and Australian wildlife, famously bisected by the Wallace Line.

## Timeline
- **c. 15 million years ago** — The northern volcanic archipelago begins forming from the collision of the Australian and Asian tectonic plates.
- **1930** — The population of the islands is recorded as 3,460,059.
- **1945** — Most islands are administered as the Lesser Sunda Islands (Sunda Kecil) Province of Indonesia.
- **1958** — The province is split into Bali, West Nusa Tenggara, and East Nusa Tenggara.
- **Present** — The population exceeds 17 million.

## Body

### Geology and Formation
The Lesser Sunda Islands consist of two distinct geological archipelagos. The northern chain (Bali, Lombok, Sumbawa, Flores, Wetar) is volcanic, formed by the subduction of the Australian Plate beneath the Asian Plate along the Sunda Trench. This process began in the Pliocene epoch, about 15 million years ago. The southern islands (Sumba, Timor, Babar) are non-volcanic and are part of the Australian Plate. The province of Bali is the only part of the group located on the Sunda Shelf and west of the Wallace Line.

### Ecology and Biodiversity
The islands' ecology is defined by the Wallace Line, which runs through the Lombok Strait. This deep-water channel created a barrier to species migration even during periods of lower sea levels. Consequently, the islands east of the strait (part of the Wallacea region) have a mix of Asian and Australasian fauna, with Asian species predominating. The islands have Indonesia's driest climate, supporting tropical dry broadleaf forests. They are home to unique endemic species, most famously the Komodo dragon, the world's largest lizard.

### Ecoregions and Threats
The islands are divided into six ecoregions. Bali, in the Indomalayan realm, once hosted large Asiatic mammals like the extinct Bali tiger. The northern chain features deciduous forests with endemic birds and mammals like the Flores shrew and Komodo rat. Over half of the original vegetation has been cleared for agriculture and settlement. Only Sumbawa retains large intact forests, while Komodo, Rincah, and Padar are protected as Komodo National Park. Small island ecosystems are particularly vulnerable to environmental pressures and economic migration.

### Administration
From 1945, most islands were administered as the Lesser Sunda Islands Province. In 1958, this was divided into the provinces of Bali, West Nusa Tenggara, and East Nusa Tenggara. The eastern half of Timor island constitutes the independent nation of Timor-Leste.

## Terms
- **Wallace Line**: A faunal boundary line drawn by Alfred Wallace that separates the ecozones of Asia and Wallacea (a transitional zone between Asia and Australia).
- **Wallacea**: The biogeographical region between the Wallace Line and the Lydekker Line, characterized by a blend of Asian and Australasian species.
- **Sunda Shelf**: A large, shallow extension of the Southeast Asian continental shelf, part of the Sunda Islands.
- **Sunda Arc**: The volcanic arc formed by the subduction of the Indo-Australian Plate beneath the Eurasian Plate.
- **Endemic species**: A species native and restricted to a certain place or area.
- **Tectonic plates**: The massive, moving pieces of Earth's lithosphere whose interactions cause geological activity.
- **Subduction**: A geological process where one tectonic plate moves under another and is forced into the mantle.
- **Ecoregion**: An ecologically and geographically defined area of smaller scale than a biorealm.

## Debates and Open Questions
The geological formation and progression of the islands are not fully understood. Theories of their geological evolution changed extensively during the last decades of the 20th century, indicating ongoing scientific debate.
