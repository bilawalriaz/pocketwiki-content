# Limpopo River

## Overview
The Limpopo River is a major river in southern Africa, flowing approximately 1,750 km from South Africa through Botswana and Zimbabwe to Mozambique and the Indian Ocean. It is the second-largest African river draining to the Indian Ocean after the Zambezi, and its basin supports about 14 million people. The river is significant for its role as an international border, its seasonal and often sluggish flow, and its ecological and economic importance, including mining and wildlife.

## Timeline
- **c. 3.5 million years ago** — Australopithecus fossils found in Makapans Valley near the river's basin.
- **Late Pliocene/Pleistocene** — The upper Zambezi River drained into the Limpopo; later uplift diverted it.
- **1498** — Vasco da Gama becomes the first European to sight the river's mouth.
- **1868–69** — St Vincent Whitshed Erskine explores the river's lower course.
- **1870** — Captain J F Elton travels down the river's middle course.
- **July 1950** — A Zambezi shark is caught hundreds of kilometres upriver at the Limpopo-Luvuvhu confluence.
- **February 2000** — Heavy rainfall from a cyclone causes the catastrophic 2000 Mozambique flood.
- **2013** — Approximately 15,000 Nile crocodiles are accidentally released into the river from a nearby crocodile farm.

## Course
The Limpopo River flows in a great arc, zigzagging north, then northeast, east, and finally southeast. It forms an international border for about 640 km, separating South Africa from Botswana and Zimbabwe. The river is named the Limpopo at the confluence of the Marico and Crocodile Rivers. Its main tributary is the Olifants River, which contributes about 1,233 million m³ of water annually. Other major tributaries include the Shashe, Mzingwane, Crocodile, Mwenezi, and Luvuvhu Rivers. The river borders Kruger National Park in northeastern South Africa and reaches the sea near Xai-Xai, Mozambique, though a sandbar limits access for large ships except at high tide.

## Basin Characteristics
The Limpopo's waters flow sluggishly with high silt content. Rainfall is seasonal and unreliable; in dry years, upper sections may flow for 40 days or less. The upper basin in the Kalahari Desert is arid, while lower reaches are less arid, passing through the Waterberg Massif's semi-deciduous forest and into fertile lowlands. Flooding is an occasional problem in the lower reaches, as seen in the 2000 Mozambique flood. The basin has significant mining activity, with about 1,900 functioning mines and roughly 1,700 abandoned ones. The highest concentration of hippopotamus is found between the Mokolo and Mogalakwena Rivers.

## History
Human habitation in the region dates back millions of years, with Australopithecus fossils in the Makapans Valley. The first European to sight the river was Vasco da Gama in 1498, who named it Rio do Espírito Santo. Later explorers included St Vincent Whitshed Erskine (1868–69) and Captain J F Elton (1870). The river is called Vhembe by local Venda communities, a name adopted by the South African government for a District Municipality. Ecological events include the 1950 capture of a Zambezi shark far upriver and the 2013 accidental release of Nile crocodiles.

## Terms
- **Drainage basin**: The area of land where all water drains into a common outlet, here the Limpopo River.
- **Epeirogenic movement**: Large-scale, slow uplift or subsidence of the Earth's crust, which altered ancient river drainage patterns.
- **Escarpment**: A steep slope or cliff, here referring to the inland edge from which the river falls via rapids.
- **Tributary**: A river or stream that flows into a larger main river.
- **Sandbar**: A submerged or exposed bank of sand near a river mouth that can obstruct navigation.
- **Transfrontier Park**: A conservation area that spans international borders, like the Great Limpopo Transfrontier Park.
