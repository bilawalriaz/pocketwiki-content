# Tbilisi

## Overview
Tbilisi is the capital and largest city of Georgia, located on the Kura River. Founded in the 5th century CE, its strategic position at the crossroads of Europe and Asia, near the Silk Road, has made it a historically contested prize for regional powers. This history of diverse rule has shaped its multicultural population and eclectic architecture, while its modern role as Georgia's political, economic, and cultural center underscores its enduring regional importance.

## Body

### Founding and Early History
Archaeological evidence shows habitation in the Tbilisi area since the Paleolithic age. The city's traditional founding is dated to the 5th century CE, linked to King Vakhtang I of Iberia and the discovery of hot springs. His successor, King Dachi, moved the capital to Tbilisi in the 6th century, and its strategic location on trade routes fostered steady growth.

### A Contested Crossroads
Tbilisi's favorable location made it a target for successive foreign powers, including the Romans, Sassanid Persians, Arabs, Byzantines, Seljuk Turks, Mongols, and Timurids. Arab rule from the 8th to 11th centuries included the establishment of a mint producing bilingual coins. The city endured repeated sackings, notably by the Khwarezmians in 1226 and Timur in 1386. From the 16th to 18th centuries, it was a point of contention between Safavid Iran and the Ottoman Empire.

### Georgian Golden Age and Later Instability
In 1122, King David IV captured Tbilisi, making it the capital of a unified Georgian state and inaugurating a "Golden Age" of cultural and economic prosperity. This period ended with the Mongol invasions of the 13th century. The city later recovered but faced further devastation, including a 1795 invasion by Iran's Qajar dynasty, which prompted Georgia to seek Russian protection.

### Russian Imperial and Soviet Rule
Annexed by the Russian Empire in 1801, Tbilisi (then Tiflis) became the seat of the Caucasus Viceroyalty. Russian administrators modernized the city with Western-style planning and infrastructure. After a brief period as the capital of an independent Democratic Republic of Georgia (1918–1921), the city was incorporated into the Soviet Union. It served as the capital of the Transcaucasian SFSR and later the Georgian SSR, experiencing significant population growth and industrialization.

### Post-Independence
Following the dissolution of the Soviet Union, Tbilisi endured civil war, crime, and economic turmoil in the 1990s. The 2003 Rose Revolution brought political change, leading to a period of greater stability, economic growth, and urban development, though the city was hit by Russian air attacks during the 2008 South Ossetia war.

## Terms
- **Raion**: An administrative district; Tbilisi is divided into these for local governance.
- **Kura River (Mtkvari)**: The major river flowing through Tbilisi, on whose banks the city is built.
- **Georgian Golden Age**: A period of political and cultural flourishing in the 12th–13th centuries under kings like David IV and Tamar.
- **Safavid Iran**: A Persian dynasty that ruled from 1501 to 1736, frequently controlling Tbilisi.
- **Tiflis**: The historical name for Tbilisi used during Russian and Persian rule, derived from Persian pronunciation.
- **Rose Revolution**: A 2003 non-violent change of government in Georgia following disputed parliamentary elections.
- **Marshrutka**: A private minibus that forms part of the public transit network.
- **Art Nouveau**: An ornamental artistic style prominent in Tbilisi's architecture from the 1890s to the early Soviet era.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source presents the city's founding legend as "one widely accepted variant," implying other versions exist. The precise ethnic composition of Tbilisi during different historical periods, such as when Armenians formed a large portion of the population in the 19th century, is noted but not deeply explored. The long-term impact of the city's frequent destructions and rebuildings on its cultural continuity is an implicit theme.
