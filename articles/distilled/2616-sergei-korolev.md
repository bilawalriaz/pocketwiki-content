# Sergei Korolev

## Overview
Sergei Korolev was the principal Soviet rocket engineer and spacecraft designer who masterminded the USSR's early space program, leading to historic firsts like the first satellite, first animal in orbit, and first human in space. His work was central to the Space Race, but his identity was kept secret during his lifetime, known only as the "Chief Designer." His death in 1966 ended the Soviet Union's direct chance to land a crew on the Moon before the United States.

## Timeline
- **12 Jan 1907** — Born in Zhytomyr, Russian Empire (now Ukraine).
- **1938** — Arrested during Stalin's Great Purge; imprisoned in Gulag camps.
- **1944** — Released from prison; continued engineering work.
- **1945** — Commissioned into Red Army; sent to Germany to study V-2 rocket technology.
- **1946** — Appointed chief designer of long-range missiles at NII-88.
- **21 Aug 1957** — R-7 rocket successfully delivers a dummy payload, proving the world's first ICBM.
- **4 Oct 1957** — Launch of Sputnik 1, the first artificial satellite.
- **12 Apr 1961** — Yuri Gagarin becomes the first human in space aboard Vostok 1.
- **18 Mar 1965** — Alexei Leonov performs the first spacewalk during the Voskhod 2 mission.
- **14 Jan 1966** — Dies during surgery in Moscow.

## Body

### Early Life and Imprisonment
Born in Zhytomyr, Korolev studied aviation at the Kiev Polytechnic Institute and Bauman Moscow State Technical University, mentored by aircraft designer Andrei Tupolev. In 1931, he co-founded the Group for the Study of Reactive Motion (GIRD), an early Soviet rocket center. His career was interrupted in 1938 when he was arrested during Stalin's Great Purge. He endured years in prison and forced labor camps, including a brutal stint in a Kolyma gold mine, which severely damaged his health. Released in 1944 thanks to Tupolev's intervention, he returned to engineering work in "sharashkas" (prison design bureaus).

### Ballistic Missiles and the R-7
After World War II, Korolev was tasked with replicating the German V-2 rocket. He pushed for more advanced designs, leading the development of the R-1 and R-2 missiles. His crowning achievement was the R-7 Semyorka, the world's first true intercontinental ballistic missile (ICBM). After three initial failures, the R-7 successfully tested on 21 August 1957. Its clustered design and payload capacity made it the foundation for the Soviet space program.

### The Space Program: Sputnik and Early Missions
Korolev recognized the R-7's orbital potential and lobbied to launch a satellite. Using the US announcement of its own program as leverage, he secured approval. Fearing a US first launch, he simplified the initial design from a complex lab ("Object D") to a basic transmitter ("Object PS"). This resulted in Sputnik 1's launch on 4 October 1957, igniting the Space Race. He followed with Sputnik 2 (carrying the dog Laika) and the Luna missions, achieving the first lunar impact (Luna 2) and first photos of the Moon's far side (Luna 3).

### Human Spaceflight: Vostok and Voskhod
Korolev's Vostok spacecraft was a fully automated, spherical capsule. After successful uncrewed tests and animal flights, he directed the mission that launched Yuri Gagarin on 12 April 1961. Under political pressure from Premier Nikita Khrushchev, Korolev adapted the Vostok design into the Voskhod spacecraft. This led to the first multi-person spaceflight (Voskhod 1) and, after hastily adding an airlock, the first spacewalk by Alexei Leonov on Voskhod 2. These missions often prioritized political goals over engineering caution.

### Later Projects and Death
In his final years, Korolev developed the massive N-1 rocket for lunar missions and the Soyuz spacecraft. He also oversaw planetary probes to Mars and Venus, though most failed in his lifetime. His health, ruined by his Gulag imprisonment, declined sharply. He died on 14 January 1966 during surgery for a tumor, though the exact cause remains debated. His death was a severe blow to the Soviet lunar program, which ultimately failed to reach the Moon.

## Terms
- **ICBM (Intercontinental Ballistic Missile):** A guided missile with a minimum range of 5,500 kilometers, designed to deliver a nuclear warhead.
- **R-7 Semyorka:** The world's first ICBM, developed by Korolev. Its clustered rocket design became the basis for Soviet space launch vehicles.
- **Sputnik:** The name for the first series of Soviet artificial satellites. Sputnik 1 was the first to orbit Earth.
- **Vostok:** The Soviet spacecraft design used for the first human spaceflights, including Gagarin's mission.
- **Voskhod:** An upgraded version of the Vostok, designed to carry multiple cosmonauts and support spacewalks.
- **Sharashka:** A secret research and development laboratory within the Soviet Gulag system, where imprisoned scientists worked on state projects.
- **GIRD (Group for the Study of Reactive Motion):** An early Soviet rocket research group founded in 1931, where Korolev began his rocketry work.
- **Glavny konstruktor:** Russian for "chief designer," the secret title used for Korolev and other leading Soviet engineers to protect their identities.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Cause of Death:** Official reports stated he died from a cancerous tumor, but associate Valentin Glushko claimed it was due to a botched operation for hemorrhoids. Another account suggests his damaged jaw from the Gulag complicated intubation during surgery.
- **Rivalry with Chelomey:** Korolev's intense rivalry with fellow designer Vladimir Chelomey is cited as a major reason for the failure of the Soviet lunar program. Some historians argue their competition led to duplicated efforts and political infighting that undermined a unified Moon mission.
- **Leadership Style:** While revered as a genius manager, some, like engineer Sergei Khrushchev, criticized Korolev's approach as overly reliant on existing technology (like the N-1 rocket) and lacking a staged, methodical development process, which contributed to the lunar program's failure.
- **Secrecy:** The policy of keeping his identity secret, while protecting him, also meant his achievements were not publicly celebrated until after his death, limiting his ability to build public and political support for long-term goals like the Moon landing.
