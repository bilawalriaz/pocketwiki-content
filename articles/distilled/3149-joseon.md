# Joseon

## Overview
Joseon (1392–1897) was a dynastic kingdom that ruled the Korean peninsula for over 500 years. It was founded after overthrowing the Goryeo dynasty and was replaced by the Korean Empire. Joseon is defined by its entrenchment of Neo-Confucianism as state ideology, its development of a sophisticated bureaucratic government, and its creation of the Hangul alphabet. Its history is marked by periods of cultural flourishing, devastating foreign invasions, intense factional strife, and a late-era decline leading to colonization by Japan.

## Timeline
- **1392** — Yi Sŏnggye overthrows Goryeo and establishes the Joseon dynasty.
- **1393** — The kingdom is retitled "Joseon" and the capital moves to Hanseong (modern Seoul).
- **1418–1450** — Reign of Sejong the Great, marked by scientific advances and the creation of Hangul (1443).
- **1455** — Prince Suyang usurps the throne, becoming King Sejo.
- **1498–1506** — Reign of Yeonsangun, marked by bloody "literati purges."
- **1592–1598** — Japanese invasions (Imjin War) devastate the peninsula.
- **1627 & 1636–37** — Invasions by the Jurchen-led Later Jin and Qing dynasties.
- **1724–1800** — Reigns of Kings Yeongjo and Jeongjo, a period of reform and cultural renaissance.
- **1800–1863** — Era of "Sedo Politics," where royal in-law families dominate the government.
- **1876** — Japan forces Joseon to sign the Treaty of Ganghwa, opening ports to trade.
- **1894** — Donghak Peasant Revolution leads to the First Sino-Japanese War.
- **1897** — The Joseon dynasty declares the Korean Empire, marking the official end of the Joseon period.

## Body

### Founding and Early Consolidation
Joseon was founded in 1392 by General Yi Sŏnggye (Taejo), who staged a coup against the declining Goryeo dynasty. The new dynasty adopted Neo-Confucianism as its state ideology, discouraging Buddhism. Early reigns were marked by violent power struggles among princes, notably the "First and Second Strife of Princes" (1398, 1400), which ultimately consolidated royal power under King Taejong. His successor, Sejong the Great (r. 1418–1450), presided over a golden age, expanding borders, promoting science and literature, and creating the Hangul alphabet in 1443.

### Factional Strife and Foreign Invasions
The mid-Joseon period was defined by intense factional conflict among scholar-officials (yangban) and catastrophic foreign invasions. Political factions, such as the Easterners and Westerners, engaged in bloody purges, exemplified by the 1589 rebellion of Chŏng Yŏrip. The kingdom was severely weakened by the Japanese invasions of 1592–1598, which were repelled with Ming Chinese aid and the naval genius of Admiral Yi Sun-sin. Shortly after, Joseon faced invasions by the Jurchen-led Later Jin (1627) and Qing dynasty (1636–1637), which forced it into the Qing tributary system. These events led to the development of the "Little China" (sojunghwa) ideology, where Joseon saw itself as the true preserver of Ming Confucian culture.

### Late Period: Renaissance and Decline
After the invasions, Joseon experienced a 200-year peace. The emergence of Silhak (Practical Learning) advocated social and administrative reforms. Kings Yeongjo and Jeongjo (r. 1724–1800) enacted policies to balance factions and fostered a cultural renaissance. However, after Jeongjo's death, the government fell under the control of powerful royal in-law families ("Sedo Politics"), leading to corruption and social unrest. In the 19th century, Joseon faced increasing pressure from Western powers and an expansionist Japan. The Donghak Peasant Revolution (1894) and the subsequent Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895) shattered its isolation. In 1897, King Gojong declared the Korean Empire to assert sovereignty, but Japan's victory in the Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905) led to Korea becoming a Japanese protectorate (1905) and its annexation in 1910.

### Government and Society
Joseon was a centralized, hereditary monarchy with a bureaucracy codified in the *Kyŏngguk taejŏn* (a constitution). The king's power was checked by tradition, Confucian principles, and the "Three Offices" (censorate and advisory bodies). Officials were selected through rigorous civil service exams (*gwageo*). Society was rigidly hierarchical, dominated by the *yangban* scholar-official class. Below them were the *jungin* (professional class), commoners (*sangmin*), and a hereditary slave class (*nobi*), which was gradually emancipated in the 19th century. Neo-Confucian ideals enforced strict gender roles and a complex social etiquette.

### Culture and Technology
Joseon culture was deeply influenced by Neo-Confucianism, emphasizing frugality and propriety. This was reflected in its white porcelain ceramics and the development of a "true view" landscape painting style. The creation of Hangul was a monumental cultural achievement. Scientific advances peaked under Sejong, with inventions like an automated water clock and astronomical instruments. The *Veritable Records of the Joseon Dynasty*, a comprehensive annal, is a UNESCO Memory of the World document. Music forms like *sijo* (poetic song) and *pansori* (narrative singing) also flourished.

## Terms
- **Yangban**: The hereditary scholar-official aristocracy that dominated Joseon's government and society.
- **Neo-Confucianism**: The state ideology emphasizing social hierarchy, filial piety, and ethical governance, which shaped all aspects of Joseon life.
- **Hangul**: The Korean alphabet, created in 1443 under King Sejong to promote literacy.
- **Gwageo**: The civil service examination system used to select government officials, based on knowledge of Confucian classics.
- **Nobi**: A class of hereditary slaves or serfs in Joseon, who could own property and were gradually emancipated.
- **Silhak**: "Practical Learning," a 17th-18th century reform movement advocating administrative and social reforms.
- **Sedo Politics**: A period (early 19th century) when powerful royal in-law families monopolized government power.
- **Sadae**: The diplomatic principle of "serving the great," referring to Joseon's tributary relationship with China.
- **Gyorin**: The "neighborly relations" diplomatic framework used for interactions with non-Chinese states like Japan.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The nature and purpose of Chŏng Yŏrip's group in 1589, which some sources suggest desired a classless society.
- The exact population figures of Joseon, as government household records are considered unreliable.
- The degree to which the *nobi* should be classified as "slaves" versus "serfs," given their legal rights and property ownership.
- The extent of social mobility in the late Joseon period, as the old class system weakened but sumptuary laws were also reinforced.
