# Olusegun Obasanjo

## Overview
Olusegun Obasanjo is a Nigerian statesman who served as the country's military Head of State from 1976 to 1979 and as its elected president from 1999 to 2007. A pivotal figure in post-colonial Africa, he is renowned for voluntarily transferring power to a civilian government in 1979 and for his later role in stabilizing Nigeria's democracy after a period of military dictatorship. His legacy is complex, marked by significant economic reforms and Pan-African diplomacy, but also by controversies over corruption, human rights, and attempts to extend his own power.

## Timeline
- **c. 5 March 1937** — Born in Ibogun-Olaogun, Nigeria.
- **1958** — Enlists in the Nigerian Army.
- **1960–1961** — Serves in UN peacekeeping force in the Congo.
- **1967–1970** — Plays a major role in the Nigerian Civil War, accepting Biafran surrender in 1970.
- **1975** — Becomes part of the military triumvirate after a coup.
- **13 February 1976** — Appointed Head of State after the assassination of Murtala Muhammed.
- **1979** — Oversees transition to civilian rule, transferring power to President Shehu Shagari.
- **1995** — Arrested and imprisoned by the Sani Abacha regime on charges of treason.
- **1998** — Released from prison after Abacha's death.
- **1999** — Elected President of Nigeria in the return to civilian rule.
- **2003** — Re-elected for a second presidential term.
- **2007** — Leaves office at the end of his second term.

## Body

### Early Life and Military Career
Born to a farming family, Obasanjo's early education was in Baptist schools. He joined the Nigerian Army in 1958, seeing it as a path to education and a salary. He trained in Ghana and England, where he developed a negative view of British colonialism. He served in the UN peacekeeping force in the Congo (1960–61) and specialized in engineering. During the Nigerian Civil War (1967–1970), he rose to command the Third Marine Commando Division, accepting the surrender of Biafran forces in January 1970. His conduct during the war's end, emphasizing magnanimity and reconstruction, made him a national figure.

### Military Head of State (1976–1979)
Following a coup and the assassination of Head of State Murtala Muhammed in 1976, Obasanjo was appointed to lead the military government. He continued Murtala's policies, implementing austerity to combat high inflation and expanding free primary education. His foreign policy aligned Nigeria more closely with the United States and focused on supporting anti-apartheid and anti-colonial movements in southern Africa. Committed to a planned transition, he oversaw the drafting of a new constitution and the 1979 election, after which he transferred power to the elected civilian president, Shehu Shagari, establishing Nigeria's Second Republic.

### Pre-Presidency and Imprisonment (1979–1998)
After leaving office, Obasanjo retired to farming and became an international statesman, involved in various African peace initiatives and the Commonwealth's Eminent Persons Group on South Africa. He became a vocal critic of subsequent military regimes. Following the annulment of the 1993 election and Sani Abacha's seizure of power, Obasanjo was arrested in 1995 on charges of treason. He was imprisoned until 1998, a period during which he became a born-again Christian, an experience that profoundly shaped his worldview.

### Presidency (1999–2007)
Obasanjo was elected president in 1999, marking Nigeria's return to democracy. His administration focused on economic liberalization, including privatization and securing significant debt relief. He established anti-corruption agencies and expanded the police force to combat widespread ethnic and religious violence. His government navigated the reintroduction of Sharia law in northern states and unrest in the Niger Delta. Internationally, he restored Nigeria's standing, played a key role in forming the African Union, and served as its chair. His second term was marred by a failed attempt to amend the constitution to allow a third term, which drew widespread criticism.

### Post-Presidency
Since leaving office, Obasanjo has remained influential in Nigerian and African politics, serving as a UN envoy and mediator in conflicts such as the Tigray War in Ethiopia. He earned a PhD in theology in 2017 and has continued to write extensively. He has intermittently engaged in partisan politics, notably criticizing subsequent administrations.

## Terms
- **Triumvirate:** A group of three people ruling jointly. After the 1975 coup, Obasanjo was part of a three-man military leadership.
- **Sharia:** Islamic law. Its expansion as a basis for penal codes in northern Nigerian states during Obasanjo's presidency was a major source of national tension.
- **Pan-Africanism:** A movement advocating solidarity and unity among all people of African descent. A core principle of Obasanjo's foreign policy.
- **Providentialism:** The belief that events are guided by divine will. This became central to Obasanjo's worldview after his imprisonment.
- **Privatization:** The transfer of state-owned enterprises to private ownership. A key economic policy of Obasanjo's presidency aimed at reducing debt and state expenditure.
- **ECOWAS:** Economic Community of West African States, a regional economic union. Obasanjo gave it low priority during his first rule.
- **OAU:** Organisation of African Unity, the predecessor to the African Union. Obasanjo was an active participant and later helped form its successor.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Legacy of Democracy vs. Authoritarianism:** Scholars debate whether Obasanjo was a genuine democrat who voluntarily handed over power in 1979 or an authoritarian figure who later sought to extend his rule unconstitutionally.
- **Economic Impact:** His economic reforms are contested. Supporters credit him with stabilizing the economy and securing debt relief, while critics argue his policies increased poverty and that corruption persisted.
- **Human Rights Record:** His administrations were accused of human rights abuses, including the violent military response in Odi (1999) and Zaki-Biam (2001), and the suppression of protests.
- **Third Term Agenda:** The extent of his personal involvement in the failed 2006 bid to remove presidential term limits remains a point of political contention, with Obasanjo denying direct responsibility.
