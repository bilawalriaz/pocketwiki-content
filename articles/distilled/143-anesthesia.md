# Anesthesia

## Overview

Anesthesia is a controlled, temporary loss of sensation or awareness induced for medical or veterinary purposes, encompassing analgesia (pain relief), paralysis (muscle relaxation), amnesia (memory loss), and unconsciousness. It enables painless procedures that would otherwise require restraint or be technically unfeasible. Three broad categories exist: general anesthesia (suppressing central nervous system activity into unconsciousness), sedation (lesser suppression, inhibiting anxiety and memory without unconsciousness), and regional/local anesthesia (blocking nerve impulses from a specific body part). The clinician selects drugs to achieve the endpoints required for the procedure—hypnosis, analgesia, and muscle relaxation—with the least risk to the patient.

## Body

### Medical uses and goals

Anesthesia serves three basic endpoints: hypnosis (temporary loss of consciousness and memory; in pharmacology this technical meaning contrasts with the lay meaning of an altered psychological state), analgesia (lack of sensation that also blunts autonomic reflexes), and muscle relaxation. Different anesthetics affect endpoints differently—regional anesthesia affects analgesia, benzodiazepine sedatives favor amnesia, and general anesthetics can affect all. Drugs act on interconnected parts of the nervous system: hypnosis arises from actions on brain nuclei similar to sleep activation; amnesia results from effects on multiple brain regions, with each anesthetic producing amnesia through unique effects on memory formation. Inhalational anesthetics produce amnesia at doses below those needed for unconsciousness. Notably, an estimated 22% of people dream under general anesthesia, and one or two cases per thousand experience "anesthesia awareness" (consciousness during the procedure).

### Pre-operative assessment and safety

The first stage is pre-operative risk assessment: medical history, physical examination, and lab tests. A well-completed history arrives at the correct diagnosis 56% of the time, increasing to 73% with physical examination; lab tests help in only 3% of cases. Incorrect pre-operative assessments cause 11% of adverse anesthetic events. The ASA physical status classification stratifies patients into six tiers based on pre-operative health. The history aims to discover genetic disorders (malignant hyperthermia, pseudocholinesterase deficiency), habits (tobacco, drug, alcohol use), physical attributes (obesity, difficult airway), and coexisting diseases. International standards endorsed by the WHO and World Federation of Societies of Anaesthesiologists recommend anesthesia be provided or led by anesthesiologists, with continuous monitoring of oxygenation, perfusion, blood pressure, and airway device placement.

### General anesthesia

General anesthesia has three main goals: paralysis, unconsciousness, and blunting of the stress response. Harvey Cushing identified the need to blunt the surgical stress response by injecting local anesthetic before hernia repairs, leading to drugs that lowered surgical mortality. Inhaled general anesthetics are the most common approach; potency correlates with solubility in oil because drugs bind directly to cavities in central nervous system proteins. Potency is quantified by minimum alveolar concentration (MAC)—the dose preventing response to painful stimulus in 50% of subjects; higher MAC means less potency. The immobilizing effect acts on the spinal cord, while sedation, hypnosis, and amnesia involve brain sites. In the 1930s, intravenous general anesthetics were added to inhaled ones, offering better risk profiles and quicker recovery; combinations were shown to lower odds of dying in the first seven days. The anesthetic machine provides gas at constant pressure, oxygen, and removes carbon dioxide; intravenous drugs are delivered by bolus or infusion pump. Monitoring includes ECG, heart rate, blood pressure, inspired/expired gases, oxygen saturation, and temperature.

### Sedation and regional anesthesia

Sedation (or twilight anesthesia) creates hypnotic, sedative, anxiolytic, amnesic, anticonvulsant, and muscle-relaxing properties. Sedatives like benzodiazepines are usually given with pain relievers because they don't provide significant pain relief alone. The advantage over general anesthesia is that it generally doesn't require airway support or mechanical ventilation. Regional anesthesia blocks pain using local anesthetics, including infiltrative anesthesia (small injection, e.g., freezing a tooth), peripheral nerve blocks (injection near a nerve supplying a region), intravenous regional anesthesia (Bier block, with tourniquet), central nerve blocks (spinal, epidural, caudal), topical anesthesia (diffusing through mucous membranes), tumescent anesthesia (large dilute volumes for liposuction), and systemic local anesthetics (for neuropathic pain). A 2018 Cochrane review found moderate evidence that regional anesthesia reduces persistent postoperative pain following thoracotomy and caesarean. Central neuraxial anesthesia (spinal, epidural, caudal) causes vasodilation and blood pressure drops, especially above the 5th thoracic vertebra.

### Acute pain management and recovery

Pain sensation is dynamic—persistent stimuli can sensitize the system, so preemptive management may reduce both acute and chronic pain. On-demand medications include opioids or NSAIDs, with patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) providing slightly better control and satisfaction. Epidural blockade after abdominal aortic surgery provides better pain relief, reduces tracheal intubation duration by roughly half, and reduces prolonged ventilation and myocardial infarction. Emergence (immediate post-anesthesia period) requires monitoring: nausea/vomiting occurs in 9.8%, airway support needed in 6.8%, hypotension in 2.7%. Postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) includes emergence delirium and early cognitive dysfunction; delirium predicts early POCD but neither predicts long-term POCD. Long-term POCD involves subtle cognitive deterioration lasting weeks or months, well-evidenced after cardiac surgery (due to microemboli) and also occurring after non-cardiac surgery, with older age as a risk factor.

### Risks and complications

Risks are classified as morbidity or mortality, but quantifying anesthesia's contribution is difficult since patient health and surgical complexity also contribute. Before anesthesia, surgical stress caused many deaths from shock; faster surgery meant fewer complications. The advent of anesthesia allowed more complex surgery but added risk—the first death directly related to anesthesia occurred two years after ether's introduction. Death rates attributed to patient health alone are 1:870, to surgical factors 1:2860, and to anesthesia alone 1:185,056, showing patient health is the greatest factor. The first mortality study (1954) reported 1:75 from all causes and 1:2680 from anesthesia alone. Risk factors include age 60–79 (2.3 times risk), ASA score 3–5 (10.7 times), age over 80 (3.3 times), emergency procedures (4.4 times), and provider experience. In 2016, the FDA warned that repeated or lengthy general anesthesia in children under 3 or pregnant women in the third trimester may affect brain development; the warning was criticized by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists for lacking direct evidence. A cohort study found children exposed to two anesthetics before age four had increased learning disability risk (hazard ratio 1.59), rising with three or more exposures (2.60), but no significant increase with a single exposure.

### History

Herbal remedies were the first attempts at anesthesia. Alcohol was used in ancient Mesopotamia; the Sumerians cultivated opium poppy by 3400 BCE. The Chinese physician Hua Tuo was the first verifiable historical figure to develop an anesthetic mixture. In 13th-century Italy, Theodoric Borgognoni used opiate mixtures; the "soporific sponge" (soaked in opium, mandragora, hemlock) was introduced to Europe by the Salerno school. Inca shamans chewed coca leaves and spat into skull wounds to anesthetize; cocaine was isolated and first used in eye surgery in 1884 by Karl Koller. Ether may have been synthesized as early as the 8th century, with Paracelsus noting its anesthetic properties in the 16th century, but it was used recreationally until the 19th century. In 1804, Hanaoka Seishū performed the first successful surgery under general anesthesia using tsūsensan, but Japan's isolation policy prevented this from reaching the world until 1854. Crawford Long used ether in 1842 but didn't announce until 1849. Horace Wells' 1845 public demonstration of nitrous oxide failed, but Morton's 1846 ether demonstration succeeded, with Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr. proposing the name "anesthesia." Chloroform, discovered in 1831 and characterized in 1834, was demonstrated on humans by Simpson in 1847 and gained royal approval when John Snow administered it to Queen Victoria in 1853, but the first fatality occurred in 1848. Alice Magaw, "The Mother of Anesthesia," documented over 14,000 anesthetics without an anesthetic-related death, establishing that nurse-delivered anesthesia could be safe. Of the early anesthetics, only nitrous oxide is still widely used.

## Terms

- **Analgesia**: Relief from or prevention of pain
- **Hypnosis**: In pharmacology, temporary loss of consciousness and memory (distinct from the psychological meaning)
- **Minimum alveolar concentration (MAC)**: The percentage dose of inhalational anesthetic preventing response to painful stimulus in 50% of subjects
- **ASA physical status classification**: Six-tier scale stratifying a patient's pre-operative physical state
- **Neuraxial blockade**: Injection of local anesthetic around the spinal cord (spinal, epidural, or caudal)
- **Patient-controlled analgesia (PCA)**: System allowing the patient to self-administer pain medication
- **Postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD)**: Disturbance in cognition after surgery, potentially lasting weeks or longer
- **Malignant hyperthermia**: Genetic disorder that can be triggered by anesthetics
- **Anesthesia awareness**: Consciousness during general anesthesia, occurring in one or two cases per thousand
- **Soporific sponge**: Sponge soaked in opium, mandragora, and hemlock used to induce unconsciousness

## Debates and open questions

The FDA's 2016 warning about anesthetic effects on children's brain development was criticized by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, which cited the absence of direct evidence for pregnant women and concern that the warning could dissuade providers from medically indicated care. Some patient advocates argue a randomized clinical trial would be unethical and that the mechanism is well-established in animal studies. Direct comparisons of mortality statistics over time and across countries cannot reliably be made due to differences in risk stratification, though there is evidence anesthetics have significantly improved safety—to what degree is uncertain. It is not known whether animals dream under general anesthesia.
