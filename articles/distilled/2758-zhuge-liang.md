# Zhuge Liang

## Overview
Zhuge Liang (181–234 AD) was a Chinese statesman and strategist who served as the principal architect of the Shu Han state during the Three Kingdoms period. As chancellor and regent, he governed with strict discipline, blending Confucian benevolence with Legalist institutions, and led repeated military campaigns against the rival state of Cao Wei. His legacy as a symbol of wisdom and loyalty was immortalized in the 14th-century novel *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*.

## Timeline
- **181 AD** — Born in Yangdu County, Langya Commandery.
- **c. 207 AD** — Liu Bei visits him three times; Zhuge Liang presents the Longzhong Plan.
- **208 AD** — Serves as diplomat to Sun Quan, helping form the Sun-Liu alliance.
- **208 AD** — Alliance defeats Cao Cao at the Battle of Red Cliffs.
- **221 AD** — Appointed Imperial Chancellor of the newly founded Shu Han state.
- **223 AD** — Becomes regent for Emperor Liu Shan after Liu Bei's death.
- **225 AD** — Leads the Southern Campaign, pacifying the Nanzhong region.
- **227 AD** — Submits the *Chu Shi Biao* memorial and begins the Northern Expeditions.
- **228 AD** — First Northern Expedition; defeat at the Battle of Jieting.
- **234 AD** — Dies of illness during the stalemate at the Wuzhang Plains.

## Body
### Early Life and Rise to Power
Orphaned young, Zhuge Liang was raised by a relative and later lived in seclusion at Longzhong, where his intellect earned him the nickname "Sleeping Dragon." In 207 AD, warlord Liu Bei sought him out three times. Zhuge Liang proposed the Longzhong Plan: secure Jing and Yi provinces, ally with Eastern Wu, and resist Cao Wei. He became Liu Bei's chief strategist, playing a key role in the alliance that defeated Cao Cao at the Battle of Red Cliffs in 208 AD.

### Chancellor and Regent of Shu Han
After helping found Shu Han in 221 AD, Zhuge Liang served as chancellor with near-total authority over civil and military affairs. Following Liu Bei's death in 223 AD, he became regent for the young emperor Liu Shan. He governed with strict laws, emphasizing meritocracy, agricultural development, and administrative integrity. His political thought blended Confucianism with Legalism. He first pacified rebellions in the southwest (225 AD) before turning his attention north.

### Northern Expeditions and Death
From 227 to 234 AD, Zhuge Liang led a series of military campaigns (the Northern Expeditions) against Cao Wei. The first expedition (228 AD) saw initial success with three commanderies defecting, but ended in defeat at the Battle of Jieting due to his general Ma Su's disobedience. Subsequent campaigns, including the Battle of Mount Qi (230 AD), were marked by logistical challenges and stalemates. He died in 234 AD during a prolonged standoff at the Wuzhang Plains, after which the Shu army retreated.

### Governance and Legacy
Zhuge Liang's administration was noted for its fairness and efficiency. He implemented economic reforms, including state-run agriculture (tuntian) and industries like salt and silk, which strengthened Shu's resources. His legal code was strict but transparent, earning him respect even from those he punished. His memorial *Chu Shi Biao* is a masterpiece of Chinese prose, expressing his loyalty and statecraft. He was posthumously honored as Marquis Zhongwu.

## Terms
- **Longzhong Plan**: Zhuge Liang's strategic proposal to Liu Bei for a tripartite division of China, involving securing Jing and Yi provinces, allying with Sun Quan, and resisting Cao Cao.
- **Chancellor (丞相)**: The highest executive official in imperial China, a role Zhuge Liang held under Liu Bei and later as regent.
- **Regent**: A person appointed to administer a state because the monarch is a minor, absent, or incapacitated. Zhuge Liang served as regent for Emperor Liu Shan.
- **Northern Expeditions**: A series of military campaigns led by Zhuge Liang from 227 to 234 AD against the rival state of Cao Wei.
- **Chu Shi Biao**: A famous memorial written by Zhuge Liang to Emperor Liu Shan in 227 AD, outlining his reasons for war and advice on governance.
- **Legalism**: A Chinese philosophy emphasizing strict laws and harsh punishments to maintain order, which Zhuge Liang blended with Confucian ethics.
- **Tuntian**: A system of state-sponsored agricultural colonies used to increase food production, implemented by Zhuge Liang in Shu.
- **Wooden Ox and Flowing Horse**: Mechanical transportation devices attributed to Zhuge Liang, used to supply his armies during the Northern Expeditions.
- **Romance of the Three Kingdoms**: A 14th-century historical novel that romanticizes Zhuge Liang as a near-sage strategist of legendary wisdom.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Liu Bei's Deathbed Command**: The phrase "you may make your own decision" (君可自取) is ambiguous. Scholars debate whether it meant Zhuge Liang could assume the throne, replace Liu Shan with another son, or was merely a test of loyalty.
- **Authenticity of the Second *Chu Shi Biao***: Historians like Qian Dazhao question the authenticity of a second memorial attributed to Zhuge Liang, noting discrepancies in tone and anachronistic details.
- **Guo Chong's Five Anecdotes**: These stories, which portray Zhuge Liang in various lights (e.g., using harsh laws, the Empty Fort Strategy), were deemed unreliable by the historian Pei Songzhi and later scholars due to contradictions with official records.
- **Battle of Wuzhang Plains Accounts**: Conflicting historical sources exist on the circumstances of Zhuge Liang's death. The *Book of Wei* claims he vomited blood in frustration, while the *Han-Jin Chunqiu* places his death in a different location, both disputed by later historians.
