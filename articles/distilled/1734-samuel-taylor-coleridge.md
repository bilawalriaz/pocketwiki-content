# Samuel Taylor Coleridge

## Overview
Samuel Taylor Coleridge (1772–1834) was an English poet, critic, philosopher, and theologian who co-founded the Romantic Movement in England alongside William Wordsworth. His major poems—*The Rime of the Ancient Mariner*, *Kubla Khan*, and *Christabel*—defined the era’s supernatural and imaginative strain, while his "conversation poems" pioneered a naturalistic blank verse that shaped the "greater Romantic lyric" genre. Coleridge’s critical masterpiece, *Biographia Literaria* (1817), fused German idealist philosophy (especially Kant and Schelling) with literary analysis, establishing him as a foundational figure in English criticism. His theological writings influenced the Oxford Movement and Anglican thought. Throughout his life, Coleridge suffered from anxiety, depression, and physical ailments, leading to a debilitating opium addiction that disrupted his career and relationships, yet his posthumous reputation grew to rank him among the most influential figures in English letters.

- **1814–1816** — Resides in Calne, Wiltshire; drafts *Biographia Literaria*.

- **1817** — Publishes *Biographia Literaria*, *Sibylline Leaves*.
- **1825** — Publishes *Aids to Reflection* (influential on Emerson and American Transcendentalism).
- **1830** — Publishes *On the Constitution of the Church and State*.
- **25 Jul 1834** — Dies in Highgate, London, of heart failure and lung disorder; buried in St Michael's Church, Highgate (reinterred 1961).

## Body

### Early Life, Education, and Formative Influences
Coleridge was born in Devon to a scholarly clergyman. After his father’s death in 1781, he was sent to Christ’s Hospital, a charity school in London. There, under the severe master James Bowyer, he learned that poetry possessed "a logic of its own, as severe as that of science" and was drilled to reject unsupported metaphor. He read incessantly—Virgil, William Lisle Bowles, and *Arabian Nights*—and formed a lifelong friendship with Charles Lamb. At Jesus College, Cambridge (1791–94), he encountered radical political and theological ideas, won the Browne Medal for an ode against the slave trade, but fled debts and romantic disappointment by enlisting in the dragoons under a false name. His brothers bought his discharge on grounds of "insanity." He left Cambridge without a degree but with a foundation in classics and a growing interest in German philosophy.

### Pantisocracy, Marriage, and the Somerset Years (1795–1798)
With Robert Southey, Coleridge devised **Pantisocracy** (equal government by all), a plan to establish a utopian agricultural community in Pennsylvania. The scheme collapsed, but it cemented their alliance; both married Fricker sisters (Coleridge to Sara) in 1795. The marriage proved unhappy, leading to separation by 1804. In 1796, Coleridge published his first volume and met William Wordsworth. The years 1797–98 at Nether Stowey, Somerset—near Wordsworth at Alfoxton—were his *annus mirabilis*. He composed *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner* (a supernatural ballad of guilt and redemption), *Kubla Khan* (a fragment from an opium dream, famously interrupted by a "person from Porlock"), the Gothic narrative *Christabel*, and the meditative "conversation poems" (*Frost at Midnight*, *This Lime-Tree Bower My Prison*, *The Nightingale*). In 1798, *Lyrical Ballads* (joint with Wordsworth) launched English Romanticism; Coleridge’s *Ancient Mariner* was the volume’s centerpiece.

### Germany, Philosophy, and the Turn to Criticism (1798–1806)
Coleridge spent 1798–99 in Germany, primarily at the University of Göttingen. He mastered German and absorbed the **transcendental idealism** of Immanuel Kant (the mind actively structures experience via innate categories), the literary criticism of Gotthold Lessing, and the drama of Friedrich Schiller (whose *Wallenstein* he translated). He also studied the associationist psychology of David Hartley, which informed *Frost at Midnight*. Returning to England in 1800, he settled in the Lake District (Greta Hall, Keswick) near the Wordsworths. His health, marriage, and opium use deteriorated. He traveled to Malta and Italy (1804–06, 1807–08) seeking climate relief, serving briefly as Acting Public Secretary in Malta, but his addiction deepened—reportedly consuming two quarts of laudanum weekly.

### London Lectures, *The Friend*, and Highgate Residence (1808–1834)
Back in London, Coleridge lectured on Shakespeare and Milton (1810–11, 1818–19). His 1812 lecture on *Hamlet* reversed centuries of critical dismissal (from Voltaire to Johnson), establishing the play’s psychological depth. He edited the eclectic journal *The Friend* (1809–10, 27 issues), drawing on law, philosophy, and criticism; a revised 1818 edition influenced J.S. Mill and Emerson. In 1816, with addiction and depression worsening, he placed himself under the care of Dr. James Gillman in Highgate. Gillman stabilized him sufficiently to complete *Biographia Literaria* (1817), a 23-chapter mix of autobiography, literary theory, and metaphysics. Later works included *Lay Sermons* (1816–17), *Aids to Reflection* (1825), and *On the Constitution of the Church and State* (1830). He died at Highgate in 1834.

### Poetic Achievement: Major Poems and Conversation Poems
Coleridge’s three major poems define the Romantic supernatural. *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner* (1798) introduced enduring metaphors: the albatross as burden, "water, water everywhere, nor any drop to drink," and the "sadder and wiser man." *Christabel* (unfinished) is noted for musical rhythm and Gothic atmosphere. *Kubla Khan* (1816), a 54-line fragment on the Mongol emperor’s pleasure dome, exemplifies "exquisite metrical movement" and imaginative phrasing. The eight **conversation poems** (e.g., *The Eolian Harp*, *Frost at Midnight*) employ a middle register of speech—"merely talk" rather than "rapturous song"—using blank verse more fluent than Milton’s. M.H. Abrams identified their structure: landscape description triggers memory/thought/feeling, yielding insight or resolution, returning to the outer scene with altered mood. Abrams termed this the "greater Romantic lyric," a genre originating with Coleridge and inherited by Wordsworth, Shelley, Keats, Arnold, Whitman, Stevens, and Auden.

### Literary Criticism: *Biographia Literaria* and Shakespeare
*Biographia Literaria* (1817) is Coleridge’s critical landmark. It analyzes philosophical principles from Aristotle to Kant and Schelling, applying them to Wordsworth’s poetry. Coleridge distinguished **fancy** (mechanical aggregation of images) from **imagination** (the living power of synthesis: primary as perception, secondary as poetic recreation). He coined **"suspension of disbelief"**—the reader’s voluntary avoidance of critical thinking to follow a narrative. T.S. Eliot called him "perhaps the greatest of English critics," though Hugh Kenner and Norman Fruman argued the work is more biography than rigorous criticism, marred by unacknowledged borrowings (especially from Schelling) and emotional interference. His Shakespeare lectures, particularly on *Hamlet*, remain foundational.

### Theology, Politics, and Philosophy
Coleridge moved from Unitarian preaching (1796–97) back to the Church of England (1814). His theological works (*Lay Sermons*, *Aids to Reflection*, *Constitution of Church and State*) addressed scripture, the Fall, justification, and the personality of God, influencing Anglican theologians (F.D. Maurice, B.F. Westcott) and the Oxford Movement. Politically, he shifted from radical support for the French Revolution to a conservative organicism akin to Edmund Burke, criticizing the 1799 French Constitution as oligarchic. J.S. Mill later valued three Coleridgean insights: the "Idea" (social function) behind institutions vs. flawed implementation; the need for **Permanence** (shared community, national education) to balance Progress; and an organic metaphor of natural growth (common law tradition) for historical sociology. He rejected Adam Smith and Malthus. His unfinished *Opus Maximum* (published 2002) aimed at a post-Kantian philosophical synthesis; critics debate its success.

## Terms
- ****Pantisocracy**** — A utopian scheme devised by Coleridge and Southey (1794) for a community in Pennsylvania governed equally by all members ("equal government by all").
- ****Conversation Poems**** — Eight meditative blank-verse poems (1795–1807) where a natural landscape triggers memory and thought, returning to the scene with deepened understanding; coined as a group by George McLean Harper (1928).
- ****Suspension of Disbelief**** — Coleridge’s term for the reader’s voluntary, temporary avoidance of critical faculties to accept a narrative’s implausibilities for the sake of poetic faith.
- ****Primary Imagination**** — The living power of human perception, a repetition in the finite mind of the eternal act of creation; the basis of all knowledge.
- ****Secondary Imagination**** — The poetic faculty that "dissolves, diffuses, dissipates, in order to recreate"; it struggles to idealize and unify, co-existing with the conscious will.
- ****Fancy**** — A mechanical mode of memory that receives ready-made images and reassembles them without true synthesis; distinct from imagination.
- ****Transcendental Idealism**** — Kant’s philosophy that the mind imposes innate categories (space, time, causality) on sensory data; Coleridge adapted this via Schelling to argue for the mind’s creative role in perception.
- ****Associationism (Hartleyan)**** — David Hartley’s psychology: ideas derive from vibrations in the nervous system caused by sensory impressions; associations form by contiguity. Coleridge used but transcended this in *Frost at Midnight*.
- ****The "Idea" (of an Institution)**** — Coleridge’s political concept: the essential social function or purpose of an institution, distinct from its historical accidents or corrupt implementation.
- ****Permanence vs. Progress**** — Coleridge’s dialectic: a stable polity requires *Permanence* (shared national education, community, tradition) to counterbalance the disruptive force of *Progress*.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Opium and Creativity**: Did opium fuel Coleridge’s visionary fragments (*Kubla Khan*) or cause his failure to complete major projects (*Christabel*, *Opus Maximum*)? Thomas De Quincey claimed addiction replaced lost vigour; modern scholars debate whether De Quincey projected his own experience.
- **Plagiarism in *Biographia Literaria***: Norman Fruman (1972) documented extensive unacknowledged borrowings from Schelling and others. Defenders argue Coleridge viewed philosophy as a communal "science" where attribution was less rigid; critics (Kenner, Eliot) see a failure of critical rigor.
- **The "Person from Porlock"**: The interruption of *Kubla Khan* is often cited as a symbol of fragmented genius. Some scholars question the literal truth of Coleridge’s preface account, suggesting it mythologizes the poem’s fragmentary nature.
- **Opus Maximum’s Coherence**: Published only in 2002, this late philosophical manuscript aims to synthesize Kantian epistemology with Trinitarian theology. Critics are divided on whether it resolves Coleridge’s lifelong tensions or remains a chaotic testament to his "indolence."
- **Marriage and Misogyny**: Coleridge blamed his wife Sara for their separation; biographers (e.g., Molly Lefebure) challenge his account, suggesting his addiction and instability were primary causes.
- **German Sources**: The extent of Coleridge’s originality vs. transmission of Kant, Schelling, Lessing, and Schiller remains contested. He introduced German idealism to England but often without citation.
