# Leon Battista Alberti

## Overview
Leon Battista Alberti (1404–1472) was the quintessential Italian Renaissance polymath: humanist author, architect, artist, priest, mathematician, and cryptographer. He epitomized the ideal of the *uomo universale*, arguing that mathematics was the foundation of the arts. His treatises—*De pictura* (1435), *De re aedificatoria* (1452), and *De statua* (1464)—codified linear perspective, classical architectural theory, and the concept of *historia* (narrative composition), transforming painting and building from manual crafts into liberal arts. As a practicing architect, he pioneered the use of classical orders on civic façades (Palazzo Rucellai) and solved the visual problem of joining nave and aisles with scrolls (Santa Maria Novella), setting precedents for centuries. He is credited as a founder of Western cryptography for inventing the polyalphabetic cipher and cipher disk.

## Timeline

- **25 Apr 1472** — Dies in Rome, aged 68.

## Body

### Early Life and Humanist Formation
Alberti was born in Genoa in 1404 to a wealthy Florentine exile. Educated at a Paduan boarding school and the University of Bologna (law), he displayed early literary talent, writing a Latin comedy at twenty that fooled scholars into thinking it ancient. In 1431 he entered the papal court in Rome, took holy orders, and immersed himself in the study of ancient ruins—a decisive influence on his architectural theory. His position in the Roman curia and noble lineage granted him access to princely courts (Este in Ferrara, Montefeltro in Urbino), where he functioned as a humanist intellectual rather than a workshop craftsman.

### Theoretical Treatises: Painting, Architecture, Sculpture
**De pictura (1435)** provided the first systematic, mathematical account of linear perspective (the "Albertian Window"), grounding art in optics derived from Alhazen (Ibn al-Haytham) via the *Perspectivae* tradition. Alberti defined beauty as "the harmony of all parts in relation to one another" realized through number, proportion, and arrangement—a Pythagorean concept refreshed for Renaissance discourse. He introduced *historia*: the harmonious accumulation of figures, animals, and buildings that holds the viewer’s eye with pleasure and emotion. The treatise was dedicated to Brunelleschi (Italian) and Gianfrancesco Gonzaga (Latin).

**De re aedificatoria (1452, pub. 1485)**, modeled on Vitruvius’s *De architectura*, was the first Renaissance architectural treatise. It covered history, town planning, engineering, and aesthetics, addressing not only craftsmen but "anyone interested in the noble arts." Alberti emphasized visual correctness of classical orders over structural innovation and reflected on urban social effects (e.g., street hierarchy, public benches). Pope Nicholas V, the dedicatee, sought to rebuild Rome but realized only fragments.

**De statua (1464)** examined sculpture, stressing that all learning comes from nature, though the artist selects nature’s most beautiful parts. Alberti’s only known sculpture is a self-portrait medallion (winged eye, *Quid tum?* reverse), sometimes attributed to Pisanello.

### Architectural Works
Alberti designed but rarely supervised construction, viewing the architect as designer, not builder. Key works:
- **Palazzo Rucellai façade (1446–51)**: grid of shallow pilasters and cornices over rusticated masonry; introduced classical elements to Florentine civic architecture; executed by Bernardo Rossellino.
- **Santa Maria Novella upper façade (1448–70)**: integrated existing Gothic lower level (three portals, polychrome marble, ocular window) with classical proportions, pilasters, pediment, and **two large scrolls** bridging nave and aisle roofs—a solution adopted for four centuries.
- **Tempio Malatestiano, Rimini (1447–60)**: rebuilding of a Gothic church; dynamic classical façade left incomplete.
- **Pienza (c. 1459)**: consultant for Pope Pius II’s redesign of his birthplace (Corsignano) into a trapezoidal piazza focused on the cathedral, with Palazzo Piccolomini (loggia, garden, views) and supporting buildings—an early example of Renaissance urban planning.
- **San Sebastiano, Mantua (begun 1458)**: unfinished; intention debated.
- **Basilica of Sant’Andrea, Mantua (designed 1471, begun 1472)**: his most significant work; employs the triumphal arch motif on façade and interior, influencing generations.

He also restored the Acqua Vergine aqueduct for Nicholas V (basin later replaced by Trevi Fountain) and may have designed the Villa Medici at Fiesole (first *villa suburbana* type).

### Cryptography and Other Contributions
Alberti invented the **polyalphabetic cipher** (Alberti cipher) and a **cipher disk** for machine-assisted encryption—called the "Father of Western Cryptography" by David Kahn for three advances: earliest Western exposition of cryptanalysis, polyalphabetic substitution, and enciphered code (preceded in cryptanalysis by 9th-century Al-Kindi). He wrote the first Italian grammar (*Grammatica della lingua toscana*), collaborated with Paolo Toscanelli on astronomy/cartography (*Descriptio urbis Romae*), and authored numerous Latin works: *Momus* (satirical comedy, 1450), *De iciarchia* (On Ruling the Household, 1468), *I Libri della famiglia* (education, marriage, household management, in Tuscan, printed 1843), and autobiographical sketches claiming athletic feats (leaping over a man’s head, throwing a coin to ring a cathedral vault).

### Artistic Practice and Reception
Vasari and Jacob Burckhardt judged Alberti a *dilettante* painter ("achieved nothing of any great importance") but a "truly universal genius." He appears in Mantegna’s *Camera degli Sposi* frescoes (older man in dark red whispering to Ludovico Gonzaga). His theoretical impact is debated: his ideals appear in Mantegna, Piero della Francesca, and Fra Angelico; his praise of the *Calumny of Apelles* spurred Botticelli and Signorelli. However, it is "impossible to ascertain" how far he drove innovations versus articulating existing trends. Modern scholars (e.g., Borsi) trace his influence on Wright, Mies van der Rohe, Le Corbusier, and Kahn.

## Terms
- ****Albertian Window**** — The conceptual frame in *De pictura* through which the painter views the scene, establishing a single vanishing point and founding linear perspective.
- ****História**** — Alberti’s term for narrative composition: the harmonious arrangement of figures, animals, and buildings that engages both learned and unlearned viewers.
- ****Polyalphabetic cipher**** — Encryption method using multiple substitution alphabets (invented by Alberti), vastly more secure than monoalphabetic ciphers.
- ****Cipher disk**** — Mechanical device (two rotating concentric scales) invented by Alberti to implement polyalphabetic substitution.
- ****De re aedificatoria**** — Alberti’s ten-book treatise on architecture (1452, pub. 1485), the first of the Renaissance, based on Vitruvius but expanded to town planning and aesthetics.
- ****Tempio Malatestiano**** — Sigismondo Malatesta’s memorial chapel in Rimini (1447–60), a Gothic church reclad with an incomplete classical façade by Alberti.
- ****Scrolls (volutes)**** — Large S-shaped ornaments Alberti introduced at Santa Maria Novella to visually bridge the higher central nave and lower side aisles; became standard in church façades.
- ****Villa suburbana**** — Country residence type theorized by Alberti; the Villa Medici at Fiesole (possibly his design) is considered the first example.
- ****Triumphal arch motif**** — Architectural schema using a monumental arch as organizing element for façade and interior, deployed at Sant’Andrea, Mantua.
- ****Quattrocento**** — The 15th century in Italy; the early Renaissance period when Alberti interacted with Brunelleschi, Donatello, and Ghiberti.

## Debates and open questions
- **Autobiographical reliability**: The c. 1438 Latin "autobiography" (surviving via Muratori’s 18th-century transcription) claims extraordinary athletic feats; scholars debate its authenticity versus Renaissance self-fashioning.
- **Musical ability**: Alberti claimed to be an accomplished musician/organist; Vasari agreed, but no hard evidence exists—musical posing was common.
- **Villa Medici attribution**: Some researchers attribute the Villa Medici at Fiesole to Alberti rather than Michelozzo; unresolved.
- **Practical impact of treatises**: Unclear how widely *De pictura* and *De re aedificatoria* were implemented during Alberti’s lifetime versus articulating trends among artists like Brunelleschi, Donatello, and Ghiberti.
- **Cryptography precedence**: Alberti shares "founder of European cryptography" status with Johannes Trithemius; his polyalphabetic principle was not properly used for centuries.
- **Sant’Andrea authorship**: The design was completed in 1471, construction began 1472 (posthumous); extent of later alterations debated.
- **Modern influence lineage**: Borsi traces Alberti’s ideas to Wright, Mies, Le Corbusier, Kahn; the directness of this transmission is interpretive.
