# Aq Qoyunlu

## Overview
The Aq Qoyunlu ("White Sheep Turkomans") was a Turkoman tribal confederation that ruled parts of eastern Turkey, Armenia, Azerbaijan, and Iran from 1378 to 1508. They were culturally Persianate and Sunni Muslim, reaching their zenith under the ruler Uzun Hasan. Their empire was a significant regional power that clashed with the Ottoman Empire and the Timurids, and its eventual decline paved the way for the rise of the Safavid Empire.

## Body

### Origins and Etymology
The name "Aq Qoyunlu" means "those with white sheep," likely referring to the white sheep painted on their flags. They were an Oghuz Turkoman confederation, first recorded in the 1340s in the Diyarbakir region. Their leaders, including founder Qara Yuluk Uthman Beg, claimed descent from the legendary Oghuz Khan through his grandson Bayandur Khan, using the Bayandur tribal seal on their coins and documents.

### Rise and Zenith under Uzun Hasan
The Aq Qoyunlu first gained territory in 1402 when Timur granted them Diyarbakir. Their major expansion came under Uzun Hasan (r. 1452–1478), who defeated their rivals, the Qara Qoyunlu, in 1467. He subsequently defeated Timurid forces in 1469 and took Baghdad and territories around the Persian Gulf. Despite seeking an alliance with Venice, Uzun Hasan was defeated by the expanding Ottoman Empire at the Battle of Otlukbeli in 1473, though this did not destroy his state. He adopted Iranian customs and titles, ruling as a Persianate king from his capital in Tabriz.

### Decline and Fall
After Uzun Hasan's death in 1478, the dynasty was weakened by succession conflicts. His son Ya'qub (r. 1478–1490) alienated the Turkoman population, who increasingly supported the militant Safavid order. Ya'qub's forces killed the Safavid leader Sheikh Haydar in 1488, further strengthening pro-Safavid sentiment. Following Ya'qub's death, civil war fragmented the state. The Safavid leader Ismail I decisively defeated the Aq Qoyunlu in 1501 and conquered their remaining territories by 1508, killing the last sultan, Murad.

### Governance and Culture
The Aq Qoyunlu administration had a military caste of Turkomans and a civil staff of Persian officials. Uzun Hasan adopted Iranian royal traditions, using titles like "Padishah of Iran." Despite their Turkic origins, they were great patrons of Persian culture, literature, and architecture. Uzun Hasan funded mosque renovations, and his son Ya'qub sponsored a major artistic workshop in Tabriz, producing celebrated manuscripts in the vibrant "Turkman style" of miniature painting. The court attracted prominent Persian poets and scholars.

### Legacy and Connection to the Safavids
The Aq Qoyunlu have a direct dynastic link to their successors, the Safavid Empire. The Safavid order's leader, Junayd, married Uzun Hasan's sister. Their son, Haydar, married Uzun Hasan's daughter. Their grandson was Ismail I, founder of the Safavid Empire. Ismail I's main wife was also an Aq Qoyunlu princess, making the Safavid rulers their direct descendants.

## Terms
- **Aq Qoyunlu**: "White Sheep Turkomans," a Turkoman tribal confederation and dynasty.
- **Qara Qoyunlu**: "Black Sheep Turkomans," the rival Turkoman confederation.
- **Oghuz Turks**: A major group of Turkic peoples from whom the Aq Qoyunlu claimed descent.
- **Persianate**: Culturally influenced by Persian language, customs, and administration.
- **Safaviyya**: A Sufi order that evolved into the militant, Shia Safavid Empire.
- **Ulama**: The Muslim scholarly and religious class.
- **Divan**: The great council or bureaucratic administration.
- **Tamga**: A tribal seal or symbol used for legitimacy.
- **Padishah**: A title meaning "emperor" or "king of kings."

## Debates and Open Questions
The exact origin of the name "Aq Qoyunlu" is debated; while likely referring to flags, hypotheses about totemic symbols or flock colors exist. The historicity of their early claimed ancestors, beyond figures like Tur Ali Beg, is uncertain, with many names appearing to be drawn from the Oghuz legend. The precise reasons for the rapid popular shift towards the Safavid order under Ya'qub's rule involve complex interplay between religious patronage, tribal politics, and Sunni-Shia dynamics.
