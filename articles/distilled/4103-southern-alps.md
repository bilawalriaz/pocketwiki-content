# Southern Alps

## Overview
The Southern Alps (Kā Tiritiri o te Moana) is the major mountain range running along New Zealand's South Island. It forms the island's Main Divide, separating eastern and western water catchments and regions, and is a defining feature of the country's geography, climate, and ecology.

## Timeline
- **1642** — Abel Tasman notes the South Island's west coast as "a land uplifted high."
- **1770** — James Cook may have named the range the "Southern Alps" on 23 March.
- **c. 45 million years ago** — The collision of the Pacific and Indo-Australian plates begins, eventually forming the Alps.
- **Last 5 million years** — Uplift of the Southern Alps has been most rapid.
- **1960s** — The Tasman Glacier reached a recent maximum length of 29 km.
- **Late 1970s** — An inventory finds over 3,000 glaciers larger than one hectare in the range.
- **1998** — The Ngāi Tahu Claims Settlement Act officially names the range Southern Alps / Kā Tiritiri o te Moana.
- **2017** — Scientists report "extreme" hydrothermal activity beneath Whataroa on the Alpine Fault.

## Body

### Names and Geography
The Māori name, Kā Tiritiri o te Moana, means "the Mirage of the Ocean," from a legend about the explorer Māui. The English name was likely given by James Cook in 1770. The range extends approximately 500 km northeast to southwest. Its highest peak is Aoraki / Mount Cook at 3,724 m, with sixteen other points exceeding 3,000 m. The mountains are bisected by glacial valleys, many containing lakes on the eastern side. Major road crossings include Lewis Pass, Arthur's Pass, and Haast Pass.

### Climate and Geology
The range lies perpendicular to prevailing westerly winds, creating a dramatic climate divide. Annual precipitation varies from 15,000 mm near the Main Divide to 1,000 mm just 30 km east, which sustains glaciers. This orientation also creates wave soaring conditions for gliders and the "Nor'west arch" cloud pattern. Geologically, the Alps sit on the Pacific Ring of Fire, formed by the collision of the Pacific and Indo-Australian plates. Uplift has been rapid over the last 5 million years, with ongoing tectonic activity causing earthquakes along the Alpine Fault.

### Flora and Fauna
About 25% of New Zealand's plant species are found in the alpine habitats above the treeline, including shrubs like Hebe and Dracophyllum, and the snow tōtara conifer. Lower elevations feature mountain beech forest on the east and tussock grasslands. The fauna includes endemic species like the rock wren and the mountain stone wētā, which can freeze solid to survive winter. The beech forests are habitat for birds such as the great spotted kiwi, and the kea, the world's only alpine parrot.

### Threats and Preservation
The mountains are largely inaccessible and retain natural vegetation. Large areas are protected within national parks like Westland Tai Poutini, Mount Aspiring, and Aoraki / Mount Cook. Introduced animals such as red deer, chamois, and Himalayan tahr affect native plant life and have been targeted for culling. Native birds and reptiles are also vulnerable to introduced predators.

## Terms
- **Main Divide**: The primary watershed of the South Island, running along the crest of the Southern Alps.
- **Alpine Fault**: A major geological fault line where the Pacific and Indo-Australian plates meet, causing frequent earthquakes.
- **Fellfield**: A windswept, rocky alpine habitat with sparse vegetation.
- **Foehn wind**: A dry, warm wind descending the lee side of a mountain range, like the Nor'west arch.
- **Treeline**: The altitude above which trees cannot grow due to harsh conditions.
- **Glacial valley**: A valley carved by the movement of a glacier.
- **Endemic**: A species found only in a specific geographic location.

## Debates and Open questions
The source notes that while the name "Southern Alps" generally refers to the entire range, separate names are given to many of the smaller ranges that form part of it, implying a potential debate about the range's precise boundaries and nomenclature.
