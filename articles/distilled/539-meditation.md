# Meditation

## Overview
Meditation is a family of self-regulation practices that train attention and awareness to detach from discursive, ruminating thought, achieving a mentally clear and emotionally stable state without analyzing or judging the process. Originating in ancient religious contexts—earliest records appear in the Indian Upanishads (c. middle 1st millennium BCE)—it is central to Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism, and appears in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam as devotional prayer or remembrance of God. Since the late 19th century, Asian techniques have spread globally, spawning secular applications in healthcare, education, and the workplace. Modern research, now employing randomized controlled trials, links meditation to reduced blood pressure, stress hormones, and sympathetic nervous system activity, as well as improvements in pain, anxiety, depression, and prosocial behavior, though adverse effects (e.g., anxiety, depersonalization, psychosis) occur in a minority of practitioners, particularly those with pre-existing mental health conditions or intensive deconstructive practices.

## Timeline
- **c. middle 1st millennium BCE** — Earliest clear references to meditation (*dhyana*) appear in the middle Upanishads and the Mahabharata (including the Bhagavad Gita); Brihadaranyaka Upanishad describes perceiving the self (Ātman) through calm concentration.
- **c. 4th century BCE** — Taoist *Neiye* ("Inward Training") essay describes *qi* cultivation and breath-control meditation.
- **c. 3rd century BCE** — Taoist *Zhuangzi* records *zuowang* ("sitting forgetting") meditation.
- **c. 20 BCE** — Philo of Alexandria writes on "spiritual exercises" involving attention (*prosoche*) in the Roman Empire.
- **c. 3rd century CE** — Plotinus develops meditative techniques in the Roman Empire.
- **2nd century CE** — Buddhism transmitted to China via the Silk Road.
- **5th century CE** — Buddhaghosa composes *Visuddhimagga* ("Path of Purification"), systematizing Theravada *samatha* and *vipassana*; Taoist practices influence Chinese Buddhism.
- **6th century CE** — Buddhism reaches Japan; Benedictine monks develop *Lectio Divina* (divine reading).
- **8th–9th century** — Islamic *Dhikr* (repetition of the 99 Names of God) systematized in Sufism.
- **12th century** — Monk Guigo II formalizes *meditatio* terminology and the four-step *Lectio Divina* ladder (read, ponder, pray, contemplate) in Catholic tradition; Sufism includes specific meditative techniques and breathing controls.
- **16th century** — Saints Ignatius of Loyola and Teresa of Avila further develop Western Christian meditation.
- **1893** — World Parliament of Religions in Chicago introduces Asian spiritual teachings to Western audiences directly.
- **1960s** — Surge in Western interest; Asian teachers arrive as refugees; secular, stress-reduction meditation takes root.
- **1975** — Herbert Benson publishes *The Relaxation Response* based on clinical tests of meditators.
- **2012** — US National Health Interview Survey finds 8% of US adults use meditation.
- **2017** — American Heart Association states meditation may be a reasonable adjunct to reduce cardiovascular risk.
- **2019** — Matko & Sedlmeier propose new classification dimensions (activation, body orientation) and seven technique clusters.
- **2020** — Farias et al. report 8.3% prevalence of adverse effects in meditators, similar to psychotherapy.

## Body

### Definitions and Classification Challenges
Meditation lacks a universally accepted definition due to the wide range of dissimilar practices across traditions. Scholars note that techniques are rarely solitary but are artificially separated from entire systems of belief and practice (Ornstein). Bond et al. (2009), using a Delphi panel of seven experts, identified three essential criteria: a defined technique, logic relaxation (non-judgment of the process), and a self-induced state. Other important but non-essential criteria include psychophysical relaxation, a self-focus anchor, suspension of logical thought, a spiritual context, or mental silence. Walsh & Shapiro (2006) define it as a family of self-regulation practices training attention for voluntary control and mental well-being; Cahn & Polich (2006) identify regulation of attention as the central commonality.

In the West, techniques are broadly classified as **focused (concentrative)**—sustaining attention on one object (breath, mantra, *kōan*, *mettā*)—and **open monitoring (mindfulness)**—observing all mental events entering awareness. These are often combined. Alternative typologies include concentrative, generative (cultivating qualities like compassion), receptive, and reflective practices. Buddhist tradition distinguishes *samatha* (calm abiding) and *vipassana* (insight). Matko and Sedlmeier (2019) challenge the binary, proposing two orthogonal dimensions—activation and body orientation—yielding seven clusters: mindful observation, body-centered, visual concentration, contemplation, affect-centered, mantra, and meditation with movement. Jonathan Shear describes Transcendental Meditation as "automatic self-transcending," where practice effortlessly ceases, a state also reached in Zen and Qigong after years of practice.

### Technique: Posture, Frequency, and Aids
**Posture:** Cross-legged positions (*padmasana*, half-lotus), *seiza*, kneeling, sitting, supine (*shavasana*), and standing are used. Walking meditation (*kinhin*) and mindful task performance (*samu*) are also practiced. A 2018 pilot study found a *zafu* cushion provided the most stability against side-to-side sway; chairs are recommended for beginners or those with back pain. **Frequency:** Transcendental Meditation recommends 20 minutes twice daily; research suggests benefits from as little as 8 minutes daily (Davidson), with improvement via simple training. **Supporting aids:** Prayer beads are used across traditions (Catholic rosary: 5 sets of 10; Hindu/Buddhist *japa mala*: 108 beads; Muslim *misbaha*: 99 beads). In Zen, an "encouragement stick" (*keisaku*) is used to strike earnest sitters. Richard Davidson notes a narrative (e.g., practicing for others' benefit) aids maintenance. Studies suggest psychedelics (psilocybin, DMT) may enhance training.

### Meditation Traditions

#### Indian Religions
**Hinduism:** *Yoga* and *Dhyana* aim to recognize pure awareness/consciousness as the eternal self. *Advaita Vedanta* equates individual self (*jivatman*) with non-dual *Ātman-Brahman*; dualistic *Yoga/Samkhya* calls the Self *Purusha* (pure consciousness) distinct from *Prakriti* (nature). Liberation is *moksha*, *vimukti*, or *kaivalya*. Patañjali’s *Yoga Sutras* (c. 400 CE) outline eight limbs: ethical disciplines (*yamas*), rules (*niyamas*), postures (*āsanas*), breath control (*prāṇāyāma*), sense withdrawal (*pratyāhāra*), concentration (*dhāraṇā*), meditation (*dhyāna*), and absorption (*samādhi*). Later developments include *Hatha Yoga*, *Bhakti yoga* (devotion), and *Tantra*. The *Bhagavata Purana* shifts focus from impersonal *Om* to devotional mantra meditation on Krishna.

**Jainism:** Meditation aims to remain in the pure state of the soul (pure consciousness) as a "knower-seer" (*gyata-drashta*). The *Ratnatraya* (Three Jewels) are right perception, knowledge, and conduct. Categories include *Dharma dhyana* (discriminating knowledge of *tattvas*) and *Shukla dhyana* (meditation proper). Techniques include mantra focus (*padāstha dhyāna*) and contemplation (*vichāya*) on seven facts (life/non-life, karma inflow/bondage/stoppage/removal, liberation), incorrect insights, eight karma types, and the universe's vastness.

**Buddhism:** Meditation (*bhāvanā*, "development") is part of the path to awakening and *nirvana*. Core practices are body contemplations and *anapanasati* (mindfulness of breathing) culminating in *jhāna/dhyāna* or *samādhi*. *Theravāda* stresses *samatha* (calm) and *vipassana* (insight), with 50+ mindfulness methods (*Satipatthana Sutta*) and 40 concentration methods (*Visuddhimagga*). *Samatha* steadies the mind to *samādhi*, supporting *vipassana* to discern "formations" (conditioned phenomena) as impermanent, suffering, not-self, and empty, leading to dispassion and liberation. *Tibetan* tradition merges *Sarvastivada*, Tantra, and *Madhyamaka* philosophy with thousands of visualizations. *Zen* (Chan) uses *zazen* (sitting), *wu nian* ("no thought"), *hishiryō* ("nonthinking"), and *kanxin* ("observing mind") to turn attention to the perceiving subject (Buddha-nature). *Dhyana* is reinterpreted by contemporary scholars as perfected equanimity/mindfulness induced by open monitoring of breath, not forced concentration.

#### Taoism
Taoist meditation includes concentration, visualization, *qi* cultivation, contemplation, and mindfulness. Livia Kohn distinguishes concentrative (*Ding*: deep concentration/absorption), insight (*Guan*: observing to merge with the Dao), and visualization (*Cun*: actualizing solar/lunar essences and deities for health/longevity/immortality *xian*). The *Neiye* (late 4th c. BCE) is the oldest text on *qi* cultivation: "enlarge your mind... relax your vital breath... maintain the One and discard the myriad disturbances." *Zhuangzi* (c. 3rd c. BCE) records *zuowang* ("sitting forgetting"): "slough off limbs... dim intelligence... become identical with the Transformational Thoroughfare." Practices influence Chinese medicine, martial arts (*neijia*: *daoyin*, *qigong*, *neigong*, *neidan*, *tai chi* as moving meditation), and Chinese Buddhism (from ~5th century).

#### Abrahamic Religions
**Judaism:** Biblical patriarchs and prophets meditated (*lasuach*, *hāgâ*, *sîḥâ*). *Mishnah* describes sages "waiting" to direct hearts to God. *Merkabah* mysticism involves chariot visualization. *Kabbalah* uses mental visualization of supernal realms to cleave to the Divine. Modern practices include *hitbodedut* (solitude, per Rabbi Nachman of Breslav), *hisbonenus* (analytical reflection on mystical concepts, Habad), and *Musar* introspection for moral character (Rabbi Israel Salanter).

**Christianity:** A structured prayer reflecting on God's revelations (e.g., biblical scenes). *Hesychasm* (10th–14th c., Mount Athos) repeats the Jesus Prayer. Western meditation (from 6th c. *Lectio Divina*) uses Guigo II's four steps: *lectio, meditatio, oratio, contemplatio*; developed by Ignatius of Loyola and Teresa of Avila. It contrasts with Eastern styles by not relying on mantras, aiming to heighten personal relationship with God. The Rosary (Catholic), *chotki* (Orthodox), and Anglican beads aid meditation. The Vatican warns against mixing Christian and Eastern/New Age concepts.

**Islam:** *Dhikr* (remembrance/mentioning God) repeats the 99 Names (since 8th/9th c.), central to Sufism. *Fikr* (thinking) leads to knowledge; *Dhikr* leads to presence. By 12th c., Sufism included breathing controls and holy word repetition. *Muraqabah* (e.g., *tamarkoz*: concentration) resembles Buddhist concentration. *Tafakkur/tadabbur* reflects on the universe for divine inspiration. Dervishes practice whirling (physically active meditation).

**Baháʼí Faith:** Meditation is a primary tool for spiritual development, reflecting on God's words to strengthen understanding and transform the soul. Bahá'u'lláh specified no form but mandated reading Baháʼí writings twice daily and meditating on them, plus daily self-reflection.

#### Modern Spirituality and Dissemination
Asian practices spread West from the late 19th c. (Transcendentalists 1840s–1880s). The 1893 World Parliament of Religions was pivotal (Vivekananda, Dharmapala, Shaku). The 1960s saw a surge from Asian refugees and secularization (stress reduction over spiritual growth). New Age meditation (1960s–70s) blended Eastern philosophy with Western rebellion against traditional religion, using mantra/object focus for "blanking out" the mind. **Guided meditation** synthesizes music, imagery, relaxation, mindfulness, and journaling under a teacher or media; difficult to study as a single technique but shows therapeutic benefits (stress, pain, anxiety, sleep).

### Effects: Physiological, Psychological, and Clinical
Modern research (fMRI, EEG) shows meditation lowers heart rate, oxygen consumption (10–20% in first 3 mins vs 8% over hours in sleep), breathing rate (to 3–4 breaths/min in long-term practitioners), stress hormones, lactate, and sympathetic nervous system activity (fight-or-flight), with modest blood pressure decline. Brain waves slow from beta/alpha to delta/theta. Meta-analyses confirm significant blood pressure reduction and improved cardiovascular biomarkers via reduced sympathetic activity, cortisol regulation, and decreased inflammation. Meditation improves pain measures, quality of life, and mental health in chronic pain. Luberto et al. (2017) found small-to-medium effects on empathy, compassion, and prosocial behavior, though a *Scientific Reports* meta-review found effects weak and significant only vs. passive controls. Meditation supports psychological resilience via emotional regulation, reduced rumination, and adaptive coping.

### Adverse Effects ("Challenging" Experiences)
Documented in East Asian traditions (*zouhuorumo* "fire possession," *Zenbyō* "Zen sickness," *mojing* "demonic states"). Farias et al. (2020): most common in those with anxiety/depression history; symptoms include narcissistic/sociopathic behavior, depersonalization, distorted emotions/thoughts, mild psychosis (hallucinations), and rare self-harm. Prevalence: 8.3% (similar to psychotherapy). Schlosser et al. (2019): ~25% of 1,232 regular meditators reported unpleasant experiences; predictors: high repetitive negative thinking, forced breathing (hypoxia), deconstructive-only meditation (*vipassana*). Religious participants had lower odds; female participants weakly less likely. Traditional texts (*Visuddhimagga*, Mahāsi Sayādaw) note unpleasant stages and visions; cures exist (e.g., Hakuin Ekaku for Zen sickness). Salguero notes problematic experiences (strange sensations, pains, instability, hallucinations, suicidality) are well-documented across traditions.

### Secular Applications
**Psychotherapy:** Jung explored Eastern practices cautiously; Fromm later engaged them. **Clinical:** Since 1970s, used for depression, stress, anxiety, addiction (evidence quality poor). NCCIH notes potential benefits but warns of optimistic interpretation. 2014 review: 2–6 months mindfulness yields moderate improvements in pain, anxiety, depression. 2017 AHA: reasonable adjunct for cardiovascular risk. Evidence for migraines, IBS, insomnia, cognitive decline, PTSD (2016 review: moderate decrease in veterans). **Workplace:** ~25% of US employers (2016) use stress reduction (Aetna, Google's "Search Inside Yourself" since 2007, General Mills). Military organizations use it for mental health, clarity, focus, stress control. **Schools:** 15-study review: Transcendental Meditation shows moderate wellbeing, small social competence effects; insufficient evidence for academic achievement; benefits for student stress, cognition, emotion regulation. **Relaxation Response/Biofeedback:** Benson's *Relaxation Response* (1975), Carrington's *Clinically Standardized Meditation*, Norway's *Acem Meditation*. Biofeedback used since 1950s for deeper states.

## Terms
- ****Dhyana**** — Sanskrit term for meditation (from *dhyai*, to contemplate); in Hinduism/Buddhism/Jainism, a core practice of concentrated absorption or perfected equanimity.
- ****Samatha**** — Buddhist "calm abiding" or tranquility meditation; steadies, composes, and concentrates the mind to achieve *samādhi*.
- ****Vipassana**** — Buddhist "insight" meditation; investigates conditioned phenomena (formations) to see impermanence, suffering, not-self, leading to wisdom (*Prajñā*).
- ****Dhikr**** — Islamic "remembrance"; meditative repetition of the 99 Names of God, central to Sufism since 8th/9th century.
- ****Hesychasm**** — Eastern Christian contemplative tradition (10th–14th c., Mount Athos) involving repetition of the Jesus Prayer.
- ****Lectio Divina**** — Western Christian "divine reading" (6th c. Benedictine); four-step ladder: *lectio* (read), *meditatio* (ponder), *oratio* (pray), *contemplatio* (contemplate).
- ****Transcendental Meditation (TM)**** — Mantra-based technique (20 mins 2x/day) classified by Shear as "automatic self-transcending," where practice effortlessly ceases.
- ****Mindfulness (Sati)**** — Buddhist term for present-moment awareness; modern secular adaptation used in therapies (MBSR, MBCT) for stress reduction and emotional regulation.
- ****Zouhuorumo**** — Chinese term ("fire possession" or "qigong deviation") for adverse effects of incorrect meditation; documented in East Asian traditions with prescribed cures.
- ****Relaxation Response**** — Physiological state (lowered heart rate, metabolism, breathing) elicited by meditation, coined by Herbert Benson (1975) as a secular counterpart to the stress response.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Definition:** No consensus on necessary/sufficient criteria; debate whether "family resemblances" or "prototype" models fit better than essentialist definitions.
- **Classification:** Validity of the focused vs. open monitoring binary is contested (Matko & Sedlmeier 2019 propose activation/body orientation dimensions); status of "automatic self-transcending" as a distinct category.
- **Mechanism:** Whether benefits are specific to meditation or comparable to active controls (e.g., nature video, relaxation); *Scientific Reports* meta-review suggests weak evidence for unique compassion effects.
- **Adverse Effects:** Prevalence estimates vary (8.3% to 25%); difficulty distinguishing "challenging" experiences that are constitutive of the path (per traditional texts) from genuine adverse events requiring intervention; lack of accessible support frameworks for novices.
- **Secularization:** Tension between extracting techniques for health (psychotherapy, workplace, schools) and preserving traditional ethical/soteriological contexts; Vatican warnings on mixing Christian and Eastern/New Age practices.
- **Research Quality:** Historical methodological weaknesses; ongoing need for higher-quality RCTs, active controls, and clearer operational definitions of meditation types in clinical studies.
