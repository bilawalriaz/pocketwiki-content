# Laozi

## Overview
Laozi (Old Master) is the legendary figure traditionally credited with authoring the *Dào Dé Jīng*, a foundational text of Taoism. Traditional accounts place him in the 6th-century BC state of Chu as a Zhou archivist named Li Er who met Confucius, but modern scholarship regards the biography as largely invented and the text as a multi-author compilation finalized during the Warring States period (c. 475–221 BC). The *Dào Dé Jīng* presents the Tao as the immanent source of existence and advocates *wu wei* (non-action/non-forcing) as a means to harmonize with the natural order (*ziran*). Historically, Laozi was deified during the Han dynasty, claimed as an imperial ancestor by the Tang dynasty, and interpreted in modern times as a progenitor of libertarian and anarchist political thought.

## Timeline
- **c. 6th century BC** — Traditional lifetime of Laozi (Li Er) in state of Chu; alleged meeting with Confucius.
- **c. 475–221 BC** — Warring States period; scholarly consensus dates composition of *Dào Dé Jīng* to this era.
- **c. late 4th century BC** — Oldest manuscript fragments (Guodian Chu Slips) containing ~1/3 of received text.
- **c. early 2nd century BC** — Oldest complete manuscripts (Mawangdui silk texts) of *Dào Dé Jīng*.
- **c. 91 BC** — Sima Qian’s *Records of the Grand Historian* provides earliest biographical references.
- **2nd century AD** — Han dynasty: Laozi deified; formation of Way of the Celestial Masters sect.
- **666 AD** — Tang Emperor Gaozong titles Laozi "Supremely Mysterious and Primordial Emperor."
- **743–754 AD** — Tang Emperor Xuanzong elevates Laozi to "Sage Ancestor" and deifies his parents.
- **20th century** — Textual criticism establishes multi-author accretion theory; modern political reinterpretations emerge.

## Body

### Name and Titles
*Laozi* (Lǎozǐ) is an honorific meaning "Old Master" or "Venerable Teacher," structurally parallel to *Kongzi* (Confucius). It is not a personal name. Traditional accounts assign the personal name **Li Er** (Lǐ Ěr), with the surname Li (plum) linked to a birth legend involving a plum tree. He is also identified as **Lao Dan** (Lǎo Dān), meaning "Long-Eared One," a name used by Sima Qian and in the *Zhuangzi*. His courtesy name is given as **Boyang** (Bóyáng), combining a term for eldest son/aristocratic respect with *yang* (solar/masculine force).

### Historicity and Textual History
By the mid-20th century, Western scholars reached a consensus that a historical Laozi is doubtful and the *Dào Dé Jīng* is a compilation by many hands. The text lacks a central "Master" figure, contrasting with other early Chinese philosophical works. Archaeological evidence supports an accretionary evolution: the Guodian Chu Slips (late 4th century BC) contain roughly one-third of the received text in similar order but mixed with unknown passages, indicating ongoing revision. The Mawangdui texts (early 2nd century BC) are the oldest complete versions. Analysis of early commentaries further supports gradual compilation over singular authorship.

### Traditional Biographical Accounts
Sima Qian (c. 91 BC) offers three distinct, contradictory accounts, expressing doubt about his sources.
1.  **Zhou Archivist:** Li Er, son of the Censor-in-Chief, serves as Keeper of Archives at Wangcheng, accesses ancient classics, writes a two-part book, meets Confucius, and departs west.
2.  **Lao Laizi:** A different contemporary of Confucius, author of a 15-part book, revealed as the father of the warrior Zong; teaches respect for enemies to secure peace.
3.  **Lao Dan the Astrologer:** Lives in 4th century BC under Duke Xian of Qin; leaves Chengzhou at age 80, writes *Dào Dé Jīng* at the western gate for guard Yinxi, then vanishes. Later variants claim he traveled to India to teach or become the Buddha.
Common motifs include no formal school but many disciples, a famous encounter with Confucius (recorded in *Zhuangzi* and *Book of Rites*), and a son who became a Wei soldier.

### The *Dào Dé Jīng*: Core Concepts
The text is a treatise on Chinese cosmogony, often called the *Laozi*. It uses paradox, analogy, and rhyme to convey a timeless worldview, conspicuously avoiding historical references.
*   **The Tao:** The unseen, immanent source and ideal of all existence—powerful yet humble, the root of all things.
*   **Human Nature & *Ziran*:** Humans have desires and free will, often acting "unnaturally" and disrupting the Tao’s balance. The text aims to guide a "return" to *ziran* (naturalness/harmony with Tao).
*   **Language:** Viewed as inherently biased and artificial; paradoxes are used to transcend conventional wisdom.
*   **Wu wei (Non-action):** A multifaceted central concept meaning "not forcing," "acting spontaneously," "flowing with the moment," or "creating nothingness." It explains *ziran* and values simplicity and humility over selfish ambition. Politically, it implies avoiding war, harsh laws, and heavy taxes.
*   **Metaphysics vs. Ethics:** Terms like "valley spirit" (*gushen*) and "soul" (*po*) suggest a metaphysical context that complicates purely political or ethical readings. Alan Chan interprets Laozi as advocating a calm *wu wei* state rather than rejection of technology; others view passages urging rulers to keep people "ignorant" as literal political advice or religious metaphor.

### Historical Influence and Deification
**Han Dynasty (206 BC–220 AD):** Laozi was worshipped as a god. Belief in his divine revelation of the Tao formed the **Way of the Celestial Masters**, the first organized religious Taoist sect. Mythology expanded: virgin birth via a falling star, 62-year gestation, birth from a plum tree as a grey-bearded adult, and 13 incarnations spanning 990 years.
**Tang Dynasty (618–907):** Emperors claimed descent via the **Longxi Li lineage**, venerating Laozi as dynastic ancestor. Emperors Gaozong and Xuanzong bestowed escalating temple titles (e.g., "Supremely Mysterious and Primordial Emperor," "Sage Ancestor") and deified his parents. The *Sandong Zhunang* (7th century) codified Laozi as the perfect master and Yinxi as the ideal student.
**Siddhar Tradition (India):** The Tamil Siddhar **Bhogar** (one of 18 Siddhars, caste noted as "Chinese potter") is identified as Laozi. His *Bhogar 7000* describes travels to China teaching sublimation of sexual energy for self-realization; his *jeeva samadhi* is located in Palani, Tamil Nadu.

### Modern Political Reception
*   **Libertarian/Right:** Murray Rothbard called Laozi the first libertarian, comparing *wu wei* to Hayek’s spontaneous order. James A. Dorn and David Boaz (Cato Institute) cite his advocacy for minimal government so individuals develop spontaneously.
*   **Anarchist/Left:** Peter Kropotkin (1910) and Rudolf Rocker (1937) identified Laozi as an early anarchist proponent, praising his critique of state power vs. community culture. John P. Clark and Ursula K. Le Guin highlight the affinity: Le Guin reads the *Dào Dé Jīng* as viewing rightful power as earned, wrongful as usurped, and sacrifice as corruption.
*   **Critique:** Philosopher Roderick Long argues libertarian themes in Taoism derive from earlier Confucian writers.

## Terms
- ****Laozi (Lǎozǐ)**** — Honorific title meaning "Old Master" or "Venerable Teacher"; not a personal name.
- ****Li Er (Lǐ Ěr)**** — Traditional personal name of Laozi; surname Li means "plum."
- ****Lao Dan (Lǎo Dān)**** — Alternate traditional identity ("Long-Eared One"); used by Sima Qian and *Zhuangzi*.
- ****Dào Dé Jīng (Tao Te Ching)**** — Foundational Taoist text ("Classic of the Way and Virtue"); traditionally attributed to Laozi.
- ****Tao (Dào)**** — The Way; the unseen, immanent source and ideal of all existence in Taoist cosmogony.
- ****Wu wei**** — "Non-action" or "non-forcing"; central concept meaning spontaneous action, flowing with the moment, or refraining from coercive interference.
- ****Ziran**** — "Self-so" or "naturalness"; harmony with the Tao, the goal of *wu wei* practice.
- ****Way of the Celestial Masters**** — First organized religious Taoist sect (Han dynasty), founded on belief in Laozi’s divine revelation.
- ****Longxi Li Lineage**** — Aristocratic clan claiming descent from Laozi (Li Er); basis for Tang imperial ancestry claims.
- ****Guodian Chu Slips**** — Bamboo slip manuscripts (late 4th century BC) containing earliest *Dào Dé Jīng* fragments (~1/3 of text).

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Historicity:** Did a single historical figure "Laozi" exist, or is the name a retrospective personification of a textual tradition?
*   **Dating and Authorship:** Is the *Dào Dé Jīng* a Warring States compilation (c. 475–221 BC) by multiple authors, or does it contain an earlier core? The Guodian slips suggest fluidity into the 4th century BC.
*   **Textual Priority:** Do the Guodian slips represent an earlier edition, a parallel tradition, or excerpts for a specific pedagogical purpose?
*   **Philosophy vs. Religion:** Is the *Dào Dé Jīng* primarily a political/ethical manual (advocating *wu wei* governance) or a metaphysical/mystical text (referencing *gushen*, *po*, immortality)?
*   **Political Appropriation:** Are modern libertarian and anarchist readings anachronistic projections, or valid interpretations of the text’s anti-statist implications?
*   **Confucian Influence:** To what extent are "Taoist" political ideas (e.g., limited government) actually borrowed from or reactive to Confucian thought, as Roderick Long suggests?
