# Biological sex

## Overview
Sex is the biological classification of sexually reproducing organisms based on the size and type of gametes they produce: males produce small, motile gametes (sperm), while females produce large, non-motile gametes (ova). This fundamental asymmetry, called anisogamy, drives the evolution of distinct sexes and underlies sexual dimorphism in anatomy, physiology, and behavior. In most animals, sex is determined genetically at fertilization via chromosomal systems (e.g., XY in mammals, ZW in birds), though environmental factors like temperature determine sex in many reptiles. Sexual reproduction—exclusive to eukaryotes—combines meiosis (halving chromosome number and shuffling genes via crossover) with fertilization (restoring diploidy), generating genetic variation that masks deleterious mutations and repairs DNA damage.

## Timeline
- **1.2 billion years ago** — Oldest fossil evidence of differentiated male/female reproductive types (*Bangiomorpha pubescens*).
- **Early eukaryotes** — Isogamy (equal-sized gametes) is ancestral state; anisogamy evolves independently in multiple lineages (protists, algae, plants, animals).
- **Vertebrate land colonization** — Internal fertilization evolves and becomes dominant, replacing ancestral external fertilization.
- **Present** — ~95% of animal species are gonochoric (separate sexes); ~5% hermaphroditic. Majority of plants are bisexual (hermaphroditic or monoecious); ~5% of flowering plants and ~65% of gymnosperms are dioecious.

## Body

### Sexual Reproduction and Gametes
Sexual reproduction is exclusive to eukaryotes. Diploid organisms (with paired homologous chromosomes) produce haploid gametes via meiosis, a process involving chromosomal crossover that recombines parental genes. Fertilization fuses two gametes, restoring diploidy in the zygote. In animals, the haploid stage exists only as single-celled gametes; in plants, a multicellular haploid phase (gametophyte) occurs between meiosis and gamete formation. Gametes may be similar (isogamy) or differ in size and motility (anisogamy). The extreme form, oogamy, involves a small, motile sperm fusing with a large, nutrient-rich, immobile ovum. By convention, the producer of the larger gamete is female; the producer of the smaller is male. Organisms producing both are hermaphrodites. Fungi and many unicellular organisms lack gamete size differences; they possess mating types rather than sexes.

### Animal Reproduction
Most animals are diploid throughout life. Male gametes (spermatozoa) are produced in testes; they are highly reduced, motile cells specialized for locating and fusing with ova. Female gametes (ova) are produced in ovaries; they are large, immobile cells containing nutrients for embryonic development. Aquatic animals often use external fertilization (releasing gametes into water). Terrestrial animals typically use internal fertilization to prevent gamete desiccation: birds transfer sperm via cloacal contact ("cloacal kissing"); mammals use intromittent organs (penis) to deposit semen into the female reproductive tract (vagina), where fertilization occurs and the embryo develops in the uterus (gestation). Animal motility enables coercive mating behaviors, such as traumatic insemination in some insects.

### Plant Reproduction
Land plants produce male gametes within pollen grains (protected by hard coats) and female gametes within ovules. In flowering plants (angiosperms), sexual organs reside in flowers: stamens (male) produce pollen in anthers; pistils (female) contain ovules in ovaries. Most flowering plants are hermaphroditic (both sexes in one flower or plant); ~5% are dioecious (separate sexes). Self-incompatibility mechanisms (molecular, morphological, sequential hermaphroditism) often prevent self-fertilization. In conifers (gymnosperms), male cones release wind-dispersed pollen to female cones. Immobile plants rely on passive vectors: wind (light pollen in conifers, grasses) or animals (heavy, sticky pollen in flowering plants attracted by nectar/pollen rewards).

### Fungal Reproduction
Most fungi reproduce sexually with both haploid and diploid phases. They are typically isogamous, lacking male/female distinction; compatible haploid individuals fuse cells. Asymmetric fusion (one donating only a nucleus) may loosely resemble male function. Many fungi use allelic mating types (e.g., baker's yeast) where fusion occurs only between different types. Higher fungi produce mushrooms where diploid cells form and undergo meiosis to produce haploid spores.

### Sexual Systems
A sexual system describes the distribution of male/female functions across individuals. ~95% of animal species are gonochoric (separate sexes); hermaphroditism (~5%) is rare, partly due to its absence in speciose insects. ~99% of vertebrates are gonochoric; hermaphroditic vertebrates are almost all fishes. Most plants are bisexual (hermaphroditic or monoecious); dioecy occurs in ~5% of flowering plants (from ~5000 independent origins) and ~65% of gymnosperms (though most conifers are monoecious).

### Terminology: Layers of Sex
Sex can be analyzed at multiple levels, which may not align in intersex or mosaic individuals:
- **Genetic sex**: Determined by functional genes (e.g., presence of *SRY* gene in humans); fixed at fertilization.
- **Chromosomal sex**: Indicated by sex chromosomes (e.g., XX/XY); fixed at fertilization.
- **Gonadal sex**: Indicated by gonads (testes/ovaries); requires biopsy; simultaneous hermaphrodites have both.
- **Gametic sex**: Based on gametes produced; specific for mosaic individuals.
- **Phenotypic/Anatomical sex**: Based on developed body structures (internal/external genitalia).
- **Hormonal sex**: Influenced by sex hormones (natural, pathological, or exogenous); can alter anatomy and secondary sex characteristics (e.g., caponization in chickens) but not genetic/gametic sex.

### Sex-Determination Systems
The biological trigger for male vs. female development varies widely.

**Genetic (Chromosomal):**
- **XY system** (mammals, some insects/plants): Y chromosome carries male-determining factors (e.g., *SRY*). Male gamete determines offspring sex. XX = female, XY = male (exceptions: XXY/XYY = male; X/XXX = female). Platypus has 10 sex chromosomes (5X5Y male, 10X female). In fruit flies, X chromosome *number* determines sex (XO = male, XX = female; XXY = female).
- **ZW system** (birds, some fish/crustaceans, Lepidoptera): W chromosome carries female-determining factors; default is male. Female gamete determines offspring sex. ZZ = male, ZW = female (females can be Z, ZZW, ZZWW).
- **XO system** (many arachnids, insects, nematodes): Males have one X (XO); females have two (XX). In *C. elegans*, XX are self-fertilizing hermaphrodites; XO are males.
- **ZO system** (some moths): Males ZZ, females Z.

**Environmental:**
- **Temperature-dependent sex determination (TSD)**: In all crocodiles and most turtles, embryo temperature determines sex. Patterns vary: in some turtles, low temps produce males, high temps females; in *Macroclemys*, females at <22°C or >28°C, males in between.
- **Social/pheromonal**: In fern *Ceratopteris*, default is hermaphrodite; pheromone antheridiogen from established hermaphrodites induces male development. Bonellia larvae become male only upon encountering a female.

**Sequential Hermaphroditism:** Sex change during lifespan. Occurs in teleost fishes (only vertebrate lineage): clownfish (male → female when dominant), wrasses (female → male at size). Also in plants (e.g., *Arisaema triphyllum*).

**Haplodiploidy** (honey bees, ants): Diploid (fertilized egg) → female; haploid (unfertilized egg) → male. Sex determined by fertilization status, not chromosome assortment; produces biased sex ratios.

### Evolution of Sex
Isogamy is ancestral; anisogamy evolved independently multiple times in eukaryotes, creating male/female distinction and initiating sexual dimorphism. Whether anisogamy first led to hermaphroditism or gonochorism is unclear; no fossil record of sperm/egg evolution exists. A 1.2 Ga *Bangiomorpha* fossil shows earliest sex differentiation. Green algae genetics link mating types to sexes. External fertilization is ancestral; internal fertilization evolved later, dominating in terrestrial vertebrates.

**Adaptive Function:** Meiosis conserves genome integrity. Its two pillars—meiotic recombination and outcrossing—are maintained by recombinational repair of DNA damage and genetic complementation (masking deleterious recessive mutations). Resulting genetic variation provides long-term advantages for outcrossing lineages.

### Sex Differences
Anisogamy is the root of all sex differences (Dawkins).

**Sexual Dimorphism:** Phenotypic differences in size, color, structure. Often driven by sexual selection (mate competition/choice). In stalk-eyed flies, female preference drives male eye-stalk elongation. Humans: males larger, more body hair; females larger breasts, wider hips, higher body fat. Females are larger in most animals (e.g., black widow spiders 2× male length), linked to high nutritional cost of eggs. Males larger in polygynous mammals (elephant seals), driven by male-male competition. Extreme dimorphism: male anglerfish live parasitically on females. In some mosses/liverworts, females larger; may link to sex chromosomes or female chemical signaling. Bird males often colorful/ornamented (peacock tail); handicap principle suggests these signal genetic fitness by demonstrating survival despite costs.

**Behavioral Differences:** Across gonochoric species, females typically invest more in parental care (exceptions: some coucals). Females are generally choosier about mates (most birds). Males are typically more competitive for mating opportunities.

## Terms
- ****Anisogamy**** — Sexual reproduction involving fusion of two gametes differing in size and/or motility; defines male (small) and female (large).
- ****Isogamy**** — Sexual reproduction involving fusion of morphologically similar gametes; no male/female distinction; mating types instead.
- ****Oogamy**** — Extreme anisogamy: small, motile sperm fuses with large, non-motile, nutrient-rich ovum.
- ****Hermaphhrodite**** — Organism producing both male and female gametes; may be simultaneous or sequential.
- ****Gonochoric**** — Species with separate male and female individuals (dioecious in plants).
- ****Monoecious**** — Plant species with separate male and female flowers on the same individual.
- ****Dioecious**** — Plant species with male and female flowers on separate individuals.
- ****Meiosis**** — Specialized cell division halving chromosome number (diploid → haploid) with chromosomal crossover.
- ****Sexual Dimorphism**** — Systematic phenotypic differences between sexes of same species (size, color, ornamentation).
- ****Haplodiploidy**** — Sex-determination system where fertilized (diploid) eggs become female, unfertilized (haploid) eggs become male.

## Debates and open questions
- **Origin of anisogamy pathway**: Unclear whether anisogamy first led to hermaphroditism or gonochorism; no fossil evidence of sperm/egg evolution.
- **Maintenance of sex**: While recombinational repair and mutation masking explain meiosis/outcrossing, the long-term advantage of genetic variation vs. twofold cost of males remains a central evolutionary puzzle.
- **Sex-determination diversity**: Why such varied systems (XY, ZW, XO, TSD, haplodiploidy) evolved repeatedly; what selective pressures drive transitions between them.
- **Intersex alignment**: In species with genetic sex determination, the frequency and fitness consequences of mismatches between genetic, gonadal, gametic, and phenotypic sex layers are incompletely quantified.
