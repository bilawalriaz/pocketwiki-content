# History of Sudan

## Overview
The history of Sudan encompasses the modern Republic of the Sudan and South Sudan, which became independent in 2011. The region's story is defined by its position between the Nile Valley and the Sahel, interactions with ancient Egypt, the rise and fall of kingdoms like Kush and Meroë, the spread of Christianity and Islam, and a modern era marked by colonialism, independence, and civil wars.

## Timeline
- **c. 2500–1500 BC** — Kerma culture flourishes as a major Bronze Age civilization in Upper Nubia.
- **c. 1070 BC** — Kingdom of Kush gains independence from Egypt after the New Kingdom's decline.
- **c. 750 BC** — Kushite king Kashta conquers Upper Egypt, initiating the 25th Dynasty.
- **c. 590 BC** — Kushite capital moves from Napata to Meroë after an Egyptian sack.
- **6th century AD** — Nubian kingdoms of Nobatia, Makuria, and Alodia convert to Christianity.
- **1504** — Funj people found the Islamic Sultanate of Sennar.
- **1820–1821** — Egyptian forces under Muhammad Ali conquer northern Sudan.
- **1881** — Muhammad Ahmad proclaims himself the Mahdi, launching a revolt.
- **1899** — Anglo-Egyptian Sudan condominium is established after the reconquest.
- **19 December 1955** — Sudanese parliament declares independence, effective 1 January 1956.
- **1955–1972** — First Sudanese Civil War between the north and south.
- **1983–2005** — Second Sudanese Civil War reignites.
- **9 July 2011** — South Sudan becomes an independent country.
- **15 April 2023** — War breaks out between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces.

## Body

### Prehistory and Ancient Kingdoms
The Nile Valley in northern Sudan shows evidence of human habitation dating back 50,000 years. By the eighth millennium BC, Neolithic communities lived in fortified villages, practicing agriculture and cattle herding. This cultural mixing led to the emergence of the Kingdom of Kush, with its capital at Kerma, around 1070 BC. The Kerma culture (c. 2500–1500 BC) was a distinct, complex society that rivaled ancient Egypt. After a period of Egyptian domination during the New Kingdom, Kush re-emerged as an independent power from Napata. In the 8th century BC, Kushite kings conquered Egypt, establishing the 25th Dynasty. Later, after an Egyptian attack in c. 590 BC, the capital moved to Meroë. Meroë developed its own alphabet and a unique political system where succession was often matrilineal. The kingdom was eventually destroyed by the Kingdom of Aksum around 350 AD.

### Medieval Christian and Islamic Periods
Following the collapse of Meroë, three Christian Nubian kingdoms—Nobatia, Makuria, and Alodia—emerged by the 6th century. They successfully repelled early Arab invasions, securing a non-aggression pact known as the Baqt. This era, from the mid-8th to mid-11th century, was a "Golden Age" for Christian Nubia, marked by political power and a flourishing "Afro-Byzantine" culture. From the 14th century, Bedouin migrations and internal decline weakened these kingdoms. The Islamic Sultanate of Sennar, founded by the Funj in 1504, came to control much of the Nile Valley. In the west, the Keira Sultanate of Darfur rose to prominence. Islam spread gradually through Sufi influence, and Arabic became the dominant language in central Sudan.

### Colonial Era and Independence
In the 1820s, Muhammad Ali of Egypt conquered northern Sudan, establishing a Turco-Egyptian administration. Exploitative rule led to the Mahdist revolt (1881–1898), which briefly established an independent state. British and Egyptian forces reconquered the territory in 1898, creating the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan condominium. The British administered the north and south as separate regions, discouraging Islam and allowing Christian missions in the south. In 1946, this policy reversed, and the regions were integrated for independence. Many southerners felt betrayed, as they were largely excluded from the new government. Sudan declared independence on 1 January 1956.

### Civil Wars and Modern Conflict
Post-independence politics were dominated by Arab-Muslim elites, leading to the First Sudanese Civil War (1955–1972), which ended with a brief period of southern autonomy. The war reignited in 1983 as the Second Sudanese Civil War, sparked by the government's Islamization policies. This conflict displaced millions and caused widespread devastation. A 2005 peace treaty granted the south autonomy and a referendum, leading to the independence of South Sudan in 2011. Meanwhile, the War in Darfur began in 2003, involving rebel groups and government-allied militias accused of ethnic cleansing. After the ouster of long-time ruler Omar al-Bashir in 2019, a fragile transitional government was formed. This collapsed in October 2021 with a military coup. In April 2023, a full-scale war erupted between the army and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces, causing a massive humanitarian crisis.

## Terms
*   **Kush/Meroë:** Successive ancient kingdoms in the Nile Valley (northern Sudan) that were major political and cultural powers, sometimes ruling Egypt itself.
*   **Baqt:** A non-aggression pact and treaty of friendship between the Christian Nubian kingdom of Makuria and the Arab Caliphate, established in the 7th century.
*   **Funj:** An African people who founded the Islamic Sultanate of Sennar in 1504, which controlled much of central Sudan for centuries.
*   **Mahdist State:** An Islamic state established by Muhammad Ahmad (the Mahdi) following a revolt against Turco-Egyptian rule, lasting from 1885 to 1898.
*   **Anglo-Egyptian Sudan:** The condominium (joint sovereignty) established in 1899 by Britain and Egypt to administer Sudan until independence in 1956.
*   **Sharia:** Islamic law. Its imposition by the central government was a key cause of the Second Sudanese Civil War.
*   **Janjaweed:** Arab militias accused by rebels and international observers of committing atrocities and ethnic cleansing in the Darfur conflict.
*   **Rapid Support Forces (RSF):** A powerful paramilitary group in Sudan, originally formed from Janjaweed militias, which is a principal faction in the ongoing civil war.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The exact objectives of the rebel groups in the 2003 Darfur conflict are debated—whether they sought greater autonomy within Sudan or outright secession.
*   The number of casualties in the Darfur conflict is highly uncertain, with estimates ranging from under twenty thousand to several hundred thousand dead.
*   The long-term viability of any peace agreement in Sudan is questioned, given the history of broken promises and the deep-seated political, ethnic, and religious divisions.
