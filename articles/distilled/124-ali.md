# Ali

Ali ibn Abi Talib (c. 600–661 CE) was the fourth Rashidun caliph (r. 656–661) and the first Shia Imam. As the cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet Muhammad, he was among the first to accept Islam, served as Muhammad's secretary, deputy, and standard-bearer, and is revered by Sunnis as the last "rightly-guided" caliph and by Shias as Muhammad's rightful successor. His caliphate was consumed by civil war—the First Fitna—against rebels and the governor Mu'awiya, ending with Ali's assassination by a Kharijite in 661. His legacy, collected in works like *Nahj al-balagha*, has made him the archetype of uncorrupted Islam and Islamic chivalry.

## Timeline

- **c. 600 CE** — Ali born to Abu Talib and Fatima bint Asad; possibly the only person born inside the Ka'ba
- **c. 611** — Aged about eleven, among the first to accept Muhammad's teachings
- **622** — Acts as Muhammad's decoy during the hijra to Medina; later marries Fatima (c. 623–625)
- **624–630** — Standard-bearer at Badr and Khaybar; defends Muhammad at Uhud and Hunayn
- **632** — Ghadir Khumm sermon: "Whoever I am his mawla, then Ali is his mawla"
- **632** — Muhammad dies; Abu Bakr appointed caliph at the Saqifa; Ali later pledges allegiance
- **644** — Electoral council appoints Uthman over Ali, who refuses to follow the precedent of the first two caliphs
- **656** — Uthman assassinated; Ali elected caliph in Medina
- **656** — Defeats Talha, Zubayr, and Aisha at the Battle of the Camel
- **657** — Inconclusive Battle of Siffin against Mu'awiya; ends in failed arbitration
- **658** — Crushes the Kharijites at the Battle of Nahrawan
- **661** — Assassinated by Ibn Muljam at the Great Mosque of Kufa

## Body

### Birth and early life

Ali was born to Abu Talib, a leading member of the Banu Hashim clan of the Quraysh tribe, and Fatima bint Asad, around 600 CE, possibly on 13 Rajab. When Abu Talib fell into poverty, Muhammad—raised by Abu Talib—took Ali into his guardianship at about age five. Aged about eleven, Ali professed Islam, either after Khadija or after Khadija and Abu Bakr; the earliest sources place Ali before Abu Bakr. At a feast for Muhammad's relatives around three years after the first revelation, the teenage Ali was the only relative to offer support, after which Muhammad called him his brother and successor, according to al-Tabari.

### Companionship of Muhammad

In 622, Ali risked his life as Muhammad's decoy during the escape to Medina, linked to the Quranic verse, "But there is also a kind of man who gives his life away to please God." In Medina, Muhammad selected Ali as his brother in a fraternity pact and gave him his daughter Fatima in marriage (c. 623–625). Ali served as Muhammad's secretary, wrote down the Treaty of al-Hudaybiya (628), and was one of the scribes of the Quran. He accompanied Muhammad on all military missions except the Expedition of Tabuk (630), when the hadith of the position was uttered: "Are you not content, Ali, to stand to me as Aaron stood to Moses, except that there will be no prophet after me?" Ali was renowned for bravery—tearing off the iron gate at Khaybar, defeating the champion Amr ibn Abd al-Wud at the Trench—and for magnanimity toward defeated enemies.

### Ghadir Khumm

On returning from the Hajj in 632, Muhammad halted the caravan at Ghadir Khumm and, after asking if he was not *awla* (closer to) the believers than themselves, declared, "He whose *mawla* I am, Ali is his *mawla*." The authenticity of this event is rarely contested, but *mawla* is polysemous: Shias interpret it as 'leader' and view this as Ali's investiture with Muhammad's authority; Sunnis read it as friendship or support. Shia sources link the announcement to Quranic verses 5:3 and 5:67.

### Life under the first three caliphs

After Muhammad's death in 632, a group at the Saqifa appointed Abu Bakr while Ali prepared the burial. Ali and the Banu Hashim protested; Umar reportedly led an armed mob to Ali's house and threatened to set it on fire. Ali likely did not pledge allegiance until Fatima died within six months of her father; Shia sources attribute her death and miscarriage to this attack, which Sunnis reject. Abu Bakr also confiscated from Fatima the lands of Fadak. Ali retired from public life under Abu Bakr and Umar, though he advised them; his refusal to follow their practices cost him the caliphate in 644, when the electoral committee chose Uthman. Ali was highly critical of Uthman's nepotism and corruption, yet mediated between the caliph and provincial dissidents. After a second siege, Uthman was assassinated in June 656 by Egyptian rebels; Ali played no role, and his son Hasan was injured guarding Uthman's house.

### Caliphate

Ali was elected caliph in Medina after Uthman's assassination, filling the power vacuum, but faced accusations of complicity. He dismissed nearly all of Uthman's governors, distributed treasury funds equally among Muslims, and showed zero tolerance for corruption—policies that antagonized the Quraysh elite. He viewed himself as both temporal leader and exclusive religious authority. The triumvirate of Talha, Zubayr, and Aisha captured Basra but were defeated at the Battle of the Camel (December 656). Mu'awiya, whom Ali had removed from Syria, refused to recognize him and fought Ali at the inconclusive Battle of Siffin (657), which ended in a failed arbitration that alienated the Kharijites ("seceders"), crushed by Ali at Nahrawan (658). Ali lost Egypt to Mu'awiya in 658 and could not mount a successful second Syrian campaign before his death.

### Assassination and succession

Ali was struck by the Kharijite Ibn Muljam with a poison-coated sword during morning prayer on 28 January 661, dying about two days later, aged sixty-two or sixty-three. His burial place was kept secret; the shrine in Najaf, Iraq, identified under Harun al-Rashid, is a major Shia pilgrimage site. His son Hasan was acknowledged as caliph but abdicated to Mu'awiya in August 661, who founded the Umayyad Caliphate and persecuted Ali's family. Ali's descendants, the Alids, include the Shia Imams; the Twelver line believes the twelfth imam, Muhammad al-Mahdi, entered occultation in 874.

### Works and legacy

Most works attributed to Ali were speeches later committed to writing. *Nahj al-balagha* (compiled by Sharif al-Radi, d. 1015) is the most famous collection of sermons, letters, and sayings; recent scholarship attributes most of it to Ali. *Ghurar al-hikam* contains thousands of short aphorisms. The Mushaf of Ali is his recension of the Quran. Ali is credited with the standard recitation of the Quran, the first systematic evaluations of hadiths, and is regarded by some as the founder of Islamic theology. In Sufism, nearly all orders trace their lineage to Muhammad through Ali. In Shia Islam, Ali is second only to Muhammad, infallible, and the interpreter par excellence of the Quran. The Alawites venerate Ali as the physical manifestation of God; the Druze consider him a "minor prophet."

## Terms

- **Rashidun**: The first four "rightly-guided" caliphs after Muhammad; Ali was the fourth.
- **Ghadir Khumm**: An event in 632 where Muhammad declared, "He whose mawla I am, Ali is his mawla"; interpretation is split along sectarian lines.
- **Mawla**: A polysemous Arabic word meaning 'leader' (Shia reading) or 'friend/supporter' (Sunni reading).
- **Kharijites**: "Seceders" who abandoned Ali over the arbitration at Siffin; crushed at Nahrawan.
- **First Fitna**: The first Islamic civil war, spanning Ali's caliphate.
- **Nahj al-balagha**: Eleventh-century collection of sermons, letters, and sayings attributed to Ali.
- **Walaya**: The all-encompassing spiritual loyalty and authority binding Shia Muslims to their imams.
- **Ahl al-bayt**: "People of the house," Muhammad's family, including Ali, Fatima, Hasan, and Husayn.
- **Mubahala**: "Mutual cursing" ritual; Muhammad appeared with Ali, Fatima, and their sons for this event.

## Debates and open questions

- The meaning of *mawla* at Ghadir Khumm: Shias see it as investiture of authority; Sunnis as mere friendship.
- The precise order of early conversions to Islam is debated, though earliest sources place Ali before Abu Bakr.
- The attack on Fatima's house after the Saqifa: Shia sources report violence and miscarriage; Sunnis categorically reject these reports, though early Sunni sources contain evidence of a forced entry.
- The historicity of the Banu Qurayza massacre account, which Ali and Zubayr allegedly oversaw, has been doubted.
- The authenticity of *Nahj al-balagha* has been polemically debated, though recent research attributes most of it to Ali.
- Ali's burial place remains uncertain, with several sites claimed.
- Historical reliability of early sources is poor: the period is archaeologically scarce, and much material is hagiographic, compiled centuries later; Western scholars debate whether tendentious reports are necessarily fabricated.
