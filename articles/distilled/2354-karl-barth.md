# Karl Barth

## Overview
Karl Barth (1886–1968) was a Swiss Reformed theologian who became the most influential Protestant theologian of the 20th century. He is best known for his radical break from liberal theology, his staunch opposition to Nazism, and his monumental, unfinished work, the *Church Dogmatics*. His thought re-centered Christian theology on the revelation of God in Jesus Christ, profoundly shaping modern Christian ethics and doctrine.

## Timeline
- **10 May 1886** — Born in Basel, Switzerland.
- **1911–1921** — Serves as Reformed pastor in Safenwil, Switzerland.
- **1919** — Publishes first edition of *The Epistle to the Romans*.
- **1921** — Publishes revised second edition of *The Epistle to the Romans*, breaking from liberal theology.
- **1921–1935** — Teaches theology at German universities (Göttingen, Münster, Bonn).
- **1934** — Authors the Barmen Declaration, a founding document of the Confessing Church opposing Nazi influence.
- **1935** — Deported from Germany for refusing an oath of loyalty to Hitler; returns to teach in Basel.
- **1932–1967** — Publishes volumes of his major work, *Church Dogmatics*.
- **20 April 1962** — Featured on the cover of *Time* magazine.
- **10 December 1968** — Dies in Basel, Switzerland.

## Body

### Early Life and Break from Liberal Theology
Educated in the liberal Protestant tradition of Adolf von Harnack and Wilhelm Herrmann, Barth began his pastoral career in Safenwil. His disillusionment with liberal Christianity, which he felt could not effectively preach God's message, was cemented in 1914 when his teachers signed a manifesto supporting Germany in World War I. This led him to seek a "wholly other" theological foundation. Influenced by his friendship with Eduard Thurneysen and the social-theological ideas of Christoph Blumhardt, Barth developed a "Dialectical" approach. This method used negation and affirmation to challenge liberal assumptions, arguing that God's righteousness is revealed "like a trumpet blast from another world."

### The Epistle to the Romans and Major Works
Barth's break from liberal theology was publicly announced with the second edition of his commentary *The Epistle to the Romans* (1921). He argued that the God revealed in Jesus Christ overthrows any human attempt to ally God with culture or achievement. His theology found its fullest expression in the *Church Dogmatics* (published 1932–1967), a five-volume, over-six-million-word summa covering the doctrines of the Word of God, God, Creation, Reconciliation, and Redemption. The work was left unfinished at his death.

### Political Resistance and Later Life
Barth was a leading figure in the Confessing Church, which opposed the Nazification of German Protestantism. He was the primary author of the 1934 Barmen Declaration, which rejected allegiance to Hitler. For refusing to swear an oath to Hitler, he was dismissed from his post in Bonn in 1935 and returned to Basel. After World War II, he advocated for German penitence and reconciliation, authoring the Darmstadt Declaration (1947). He was a vocal critic of anticommunism during the Cold War, stating he regarded it "as a matter of principle an evil even greater than communism itself."

### Theology and Influence
Barth's theology is fundamentally Christocentric, reorienting all discussion around Jesus. He recovered the doctrine of the Trinity, arguing God's revelation comes "vertically from above." His doctrine of election (predestination) is controversial; he rejected the idea of a hidden divine decree for individual salvation or damnation, instead making Jesus Christ the object of both God's election of humanity and God's rejection of sin. While he denied being a universalist, he asserted that the hope for the salvation of all should be held as a matter of grace. His work influenced theologians like Dietrich Bonhoeffer and Jürgen Moltmann, and ethicists like Stanley Hauerwas.

### Personal Life
Barth's long-time theological collaborator was Charlotte von Kirschbaum, who lived with his family for 37 years and was indispensable to the writing of the *Church Dogmatics*. The publication of intimate letters in 2017 revealed a complex, decades-long love triangle between Barth, his wife Nelly, and von Kirschbaum, causing a crisis among some of his followers.

## Terms
*   **Liberal Theology:** A 19th/20th-century Protestant trend that reinterpreted traditional beliefs using historical-critical methods and engaging with modern science and philosophy.
*   **Dialectical Theology:** Barth's early approach that used negation and affirmation to disrupt liberal theological assumptions and highlight the "wholly other" nature of God.
*   **Confessing Church:** A movement of Protestant clergy in Germany who opposed the Nazi regime's attempts to control and align the church with Nazi ideology.
*   **Barmen Declaration (1934):** A founding document of the Confessing Church, authored by Barth, rejecting Nazism's influence on Christianity.
*   **Church Dogmatics:** Barth's unfinished, five-volume magnum opus of systematic theology, published between 1932 and 1967.
*   **Christocentric:** A theological approach that places Jesus Christ at the center of all doctrine and revelation.
*   **Election (Predestination):** The doctrine concerning God's choice regarding salvation. Barth reinterpreted it to focus on Christ as both the electing God and the elected human.
*   **Universalism:** The theological belief that all people will ultimately be saved. Barth denied this label but argued Christians should hope for the salvation of all.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Doctrine of Election:** Critics, such as Emil Brunner, charge that Barth's Christ-centered revision of predestination amounts to a "soft universalism," departing from traditional Calvinism.
*   **Scripture and Revelation:** Barth's view of revelation and the Word of God has been criticized by confessional Reformed theologians like Cornelius Van Til for not upholding a traditional doctrine of biblical inspiration.
*   **Personal Ethics:** The revelation of his decades-long love triangle with Charlotte von Kirschbaum has prompted debate about the consistency between his theological statements on marriage and his personal life.
