# 1911 Revolution

## Overview
The 1911 Revolution, also known as the Xinhai Revolution, was a pivotal uprising that ended China's last imperial dynasty, the Qing, and led to the establishment of the Republic of China (ROC). It concluded over two millennia of imperial rule, marking the beginning of China's early republican era. The revolution was the culmination of a decade of agitation and uprisings, driven by debates over modernization, foreign aggression, and the role of the monarchy.

## Timeline
- **10 October 1911** — Wuchang Uprising begins the revolution.
- **1 November 1911** — Qing court appoints Yuan Shikai as Prime Minister.
- **29 December 1911** — Sun Yat-sen elected Provisional President.
- **1 January 1912** — Republic of China officially proclaimed in Nanjing.
- **12 February 1912** — Six-year-old Xuantong Emperor (Puyi) abdicates.
- **10 March 1912** — Yuan Shikai inaugurated as President in Beijing.
- **December 1915** — Yuan Shikai declares himself Emperor, sparking opposition.
- **March 1916** — Yuan abdicates; Republic is reinstated.
- **June 1916** — Yuan Shikai dies, leading to warlordism.

## Body

### Background and Revolutionary Movements
The Qing dynasty struggled with internal decay and foreign pressure after defeats in the Opium Wars and the First Sino-Japanese War (1895). Failed reforms like the Hundred Days' Reform (1898) and the Boxer Rebellion (1900) eroded its legitimacy. Revolutionary groups, most notably Sun Yat-sen's Tongmenghui (United League, formed 1905), advocated overthrowing the Qing to establish a republic. These groups, supported by overseas Chinese, students, and secret societies, launched numerous failed uprisings between 1895 and 1911.

### The Revolution and Provincial Uprisings
The flashpoint was the Wuchang Uprising on 10 October 1911, led by New Army soldiers. Success in Wuchang triggered a cascade of provincial declarations of independence. By late 1911, most provinces had renounced the Qing. Revolutionary forces, including the Tongmenghui, established military governments. The revolution was not uniform; some uprisings were anti-Manchu, while others sought regional autonomy.

### Establishment of the Republic
On 1 January 1912, the Republic of China was proclaimed in Nanjing with Sun Yat-sen as President. A brief civil war ended in compromise: Sun resigned in favor of Yuan Shikai, who secured the Qing emperor's abdication on 12 February 1912. Yuan was inaugurated as President in Beijing. His 1915 attempt to restore the monarchy failed, and his death in 1916 led to decades of warlordism and political division.

### Legacy and Significance
The revolution ended feudalism and monarchy, introducing republican ideals. However, it did not fundamentally restructure society, and power often remained with military and bureaucratic elites. Both the ROC (Taiwan) and the People's Republic of China (PRC) claim its legacy, honoring Sun Yat-sen and the ideals of nationalism, republicanism, and modernization. The revolution's success is debated, with critics noting it replaced one form of autocracy with another.

## Terms
- **Qing Dynasty**: China's last imperial dynasty (1644–1912), ruled by the Manchu people.
- **Tongmenghui**: The United League, a secret revolutionary alliance led by Sun Yat-sen.
- **New Army**: Modernized military units formed by the Qing, which became a source of revolutionary recruits.
- **Wuchang Uprising**: The armed rebellion on 10 October 1911 that sparked the revolution.
- **Republic of China (ROC)**: The state established in 1912 after the fall of the Qing.
- **Yuan Shikai**: A Qing general and politician who became the first official President of the Republic.
- **Warlordism**: The period of political fragmentation and military rule following Yuan's death.
- **Xinhai**: The Chinese calendar year 1911, giving the revolution its alternative name.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Degree of Success**: Historians debate whether the revolution achieved its democratic goals or merely replaced one autocrat with another, leading to warlordism.
- **Role of Foreign Powers**: The extent of foreign influence, particularly from Japan and Western nations, in shaping the revolution's outcome is contested.
- **Social Impact**: Scholars question whether the revolution brought meaningful social change or primarily benefited existing elites.
- **Legacy in Modern China**: Both the ROC and PRC interpret the revolution's legacy to legitimize their own rule, creating ongoing political and historical narratives.
