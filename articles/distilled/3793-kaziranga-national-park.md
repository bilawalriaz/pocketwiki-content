# Kaziranga National Park

## Overview
Kaziranga National Park is a UNESCO World Heritage Site in Assam, India, renowned for hosting the world's largest populations of Indian rhinoceroses, wild water buffalo, and eastern swamp deer. Established in 1905, its unique floodplain ecosystem along the Brahmaputra River makes it a critical biodiversity hotspot, but also subjects it to severe annual flooding and persistent poaching threats.

## Body

### History and Etymology
The park's protection began in 1904 after Mary Curzon, Baroness Curzon, failed to see a rhinoceros and urged her husband, the Viceroy of India, to act. This led to the creation of the Kaziranga Proposed Reserve Forest in 1905. The name "Kaziranga" has uncertain origins, with local legends linking it to a couple, Kazi and Rawnga, or to the Karbi words for "goat" and "red." Records from the 17th-century Ahom king Pratap Singha also mention the name.

### Geography and Climate
Located between the Brahmaputra River and the Karbi Anglong Hills, the park covers 378 km². Its landscape of grasslands, marshes (*beels*), and elevated mounds (*chapories*) is shaped by the annual monsoon. From June to September, floods submerge up to 80% of the park, forcing animals to migrate to higher ground. This cycle is crucial for grassland renewal but causes significant wildlife mortality. The park experiences a dry winter and a hot summer, with vegetation management including controlled burns to promote fresh grass growth.

### Flora and Fauna
Kaziranga's vegetation includes alluvial grasslands and tropical moist forests. It is a critical habitat for the "Big Five": the Indian rhinoceros, royal Bengal tiger, Asian elephant, wild water buffalo, and swamp deer. The park holds 57% of the world's wild water buffalo population. It is also a major breeding ground for large cats like tigers and leopards, and an Important Bird Area for species like the greater adjutant and Bengal florican. Reptiles include the king cobra and reticulated python.

### Governance and Conservation
Managed by the Assam Forest Department, the park faces chronic underfunding despite government and international aid. Anti-poaching is a primary concern, with 567 rhinos killed between 1980 and 2005. Measures include drone surveillance, intelligence networks in local villages, and anti-poaching camps. Conservation is complicated by natural disasters, water pollution from nearby industries, and the need for wildlife corridors across National Highway 37.

### Ecotourism and Controversy
Tourism, primarily via guided jeep and elephant safaris, provides economic benefits to local communities through employment and homestays. However, the park's hardline anti-poaching tactics drew international criticism in 2017 following a BBC documentary that reported high human casualties. This sparked a debate about the ethics and effectiveness of "shoot-at-sight" policies in conservation.

## Terms
- **Beel**: A term for a riverine flood-formed lake or wetland.
- **Chapori**: An elevated region or mound within the floodplain that provides animal refuge during floods.
- **Alluvial grasslands**: Grasslands formed by sediment deposited by rivers.
- **Tropical moist broadleaf forest**: A dense forest ecosystem with high rainfall and broad-leaved trees.
- **Biodiversity hotspot**: A region with significant levels of biodiversity that is under threat from human activity.
- **Shoot-at-sight**: A controversial policy allowing authorities to shoot poachers on sight.
- **Corridor**: A strip of natural habitat connecting larger areas, allowing wildlife movement.

## Debates and Open Questions
The park's conservation strategy is debated, particularly the use of lethal force against poachers. While authorities argue it is necessary to protect endangered species, critics question its human cost and effectiveness. The long-term impact of climate change on flood patterns and the park's ecology is another major concern, as is balancing tourism revenue with the needs of local communities and wildlife protection.
