# Folklore

## Overview

Folklore is the body of expressive culture shared by a particular group, culture, or subculture, encompassing oral traditions (tales, legends, proverbs, poems, jokes), material culture (traditional building styles, handcrafted objects), and customary lore (folk beliefs, festivals, weddings, folk dances, initiation rites). These artifacts are passed informally from one individual to another—by verbal instruction or demonstration—not through formal schooling or fine arts study. The academic study of folklore is called folklore studies or folkloristics, explorable at undergraduate, graduate, and Ph.D. levels. Folklore matters because it defines group identity, differentiates "us" from "them," and continues to be created and transmitted in all social groups, from families to surfers to computer programmers.

## Timeline

- **1770s** — Johann Gottfried von Herder presents oral traditions as organic processes grounded in locale, influencing later folklore studies.
- **1812** — Brothers Grimm publish "Kinder- und Hausmärchen," a landmark collection of verbal folklore.
- **1846** — William Thoms coins the term "folklore" as a replacement for "popular antiquities."
- **1910** — Antti Aarne publishes the first classification system for folktales, later expanded into the Aarne–Thompson system.
- **1960s** — Folklorists expand the concept of "folk" to include all social groups; Alan Dundes publishes "Texture, Text and Context" (1964).
- **1967** — Dan Ben-Amos presents the behavioral approach to folklore at the American Folklore Society.
- **1975** — Journal of American Folklore publishes an issue dedicated exclusively to women's folklore.
- **January 1976** — U.S. Congress passes the American Folklife Preservation Act (Public Law 94-201), marking folkloristics' coming of age in America.

## Body

### Origin and development of folklore studies

Folklore began distinguishing itself as an autonomous discipline during European romantic nationalism. Herder's 1770s writings presented oral traditions as organic processes grounded in locale; after Napoleonic France invaded German states, Germans adopted his approach, systematizing folk traditions for nation building. Smaller nations like Finland, Estonia, and Hungary, seeking political independence, embraced this enthusiastically. In the 19th century, European scholars contrasted tradition with modernity, focusing on oral folklore of rural peasants as "residues and survivals" of the past. By the turn of the 20th century, American folklorists led by Franz Boas and Ruth Benedict studied Native American cultures, including the totality of their customs and beliefs, aligning American folkloristics with cultural anthropology and ethnology, while Europeans remained focused on oral folklore of homogenous peasant populations. This division—humanities in Europe, social sciences in America—offers diverse theoretical vantage points. The term "folkloristics" became widely used in the 1950s to distinguish the academic study from the artifacts themselves. The 1976 American Folklife Preservation Act defined folklife as traditional expressive culture shared within familial, ethnic, occupational, religious, and regional groups, learned orally, by imitation, or in performance, without formal instruction. William Bascom identified four functions of folklore: letting people escape societal repressions, validating culture, serving as a pedagogic device reinforcing morals, and applying social pressure and control.

### Definition of "folk"

The 19th-century folk were rural, illiterate, and poor peasants, contrasted with urban populations; only toward the century's end did the urban proletariat (via Marxist theory) join them, with the common feature being identification as the underclass. By the 1960s, folklorists understood that social groups are all around us—each individual is enmeshed in multiple identities (family, age, language, ethnicity, occupation), each with its own folklore. This folklore includes jokes, sayings, and expected behavior in multiple variants, transmitted informally through observation, imitation, repetition, or correction. It can be used internally to express common identity (initiation ceremonies) or externally to differentiate from outsiders (folk dance demonstrations). Starting in the 1960s, researchers identified previously overlooked folk groups, including women (notably in a 1975 Journal of American Folklore issue), non-traditional families, occupational groups, and multi-generational folk-producing families. Richard Dorson explained in 1976 that folklore studies concern "traditional culture, or the unofficial culture" as opposed to elite culture, learning about the masses overlooked by conventional disciplines.

### Folklore genres

Individual folklore artifacts are classified as material, verbal, or customary lore, with a fourth subgenre for children's folklore and games (childlore). Each artifact is unique, with variation within genres—in direct contrast to manufactured goods where variations are mistakes. Genre classifications are necessary but oversimplify; artifacts never stand in isolation but are particulars of a community's self-representation. A birthday celebration combines verbal lore (song), material lore (cake, presents), and customs (blowing out candles, making a wish), with each performance being a unique variant.

**Verbal tradition** is words, written or oral, "spoken, sung, voiced forms of traditional utterance that show repetitive patterns." Examples include joke formulas ("An elephant walks into a bar…") and songs like "Old MacDonald Had a Farm," which teach cultural values. Verbal folklore was the original folklore Thoms documented. Aarne's 1910 classification system, expanded by Stith Thompson into the Aarne–Thompson system, remains standard for European folktales. Similarities across regions gave rise to the Historic–Geographic Method, dominant in the first half of the 20th century. Thoms believed these artifacts would die out with literacy, but this proved wrong; verbal lore continues to be generated in new forms.

**Material culture** includes all tangible artifacts—objects that can be touched, held, lived in, or eaten. These can be handcrafted or mass-produced (dreidels, Christmas decorations), considered folklore due to long pre-industrial history and customary use. Folklorists study physical form, manufacture method, pattern of use, and raw material procurement. Before the Industrial Revolution, everything was handmade; some 19th-century folklorists sought to identify hand-crafted objects before production processes were lost. Handmade objects are often prestigious, embodying multifaceted relationships lost with mass production. Many crafts have been elevated to fine arts or repurposed as folk art (hex signs, tin man sculptures, decorated lockers, tattoos), characterized by decorative form superseding utility. Living museums, beginning in Scandinavia at the end of the 19th century, display and demonstrate material artifacts, with actors reenacting everyday pre-industrial life.

**Customs** are remembered enactment—"the traditional and expected way of doing things." A custom can be a single gesture (thumbs down, handshake) or complex interaction (birthday party combining verbal, material, and customary lore). Folklorists divide customs into categories: seasonal celebrations (Thanksgiving, New Year's), life cycle celebrations (baptism, birthday, wedding), community festivals (Carnival in Cologne, Mardi Gras in New Orleans, Smithsonian Folklife Festival), folk belief customs (walking under a ladder as unlucky), occupational traditions (sailors, lumberjacks), and ecclesiastical folklore (modes of worship not sanctioned by established church). Customary folklore is always a performance; participating signifies acknowledgment of the social group. Some customs are intended only for group members (handkerchief code in the gay community, Freemason initiation rituals); others represent the group to outsiders (St. Patrick's Day Parade). Public folklorists engage in documenting and presenting these traditions; community celebrations are becoming more numerous, with economic groups discovering they are good for business, paradoxically authenticating true community by parading diversity.

**Childlore and games** is a distinct branch dealing with activities passed on by children to other children, away from adult influence. It contains artifacts from all standard genres, but the child-to-child conduit distinguishes it. Iona and Peter Opie's pioneering book *Children's Games in Street and Playground* studied children's culture on its own terms, showing it is distinctive and unaffected by the adult world. Transmission occurs exclusively within an informal pre-literate children's network; children can transform adult-taught artifacts (turning "Old MacDonald" into scatological versions). This is as close as folklorists can observe transmission before 19th-century literacy. Early collectors (Alice Gomme in Britain, William Wells Newell in the United States) feared childhood culture would die out, but this proved unfounded—games from Pieter Breugel the Elder's 1560 painting "Children's Games" are recognizable today. Childlore serves developmental functions: rhymes develop balance and coordination, verbal tongue-twisters increase oral and aural acuity, songs aid memorization (Alphabet song), and physical games develop strength and social skills.

**Folk history** has been proposed as a distinct sub-category, represented in *The Folklore Historian*, an annual journal sponsored by the History and Folklore Section of the American Folklore Society.

### Folklore performance in context

Lacking context, folklore artifacts are uninspiring; only through performance do they come alive. Roger D. Abrahams described it: "Folklore is folklore only when performed." Without transmission, items are not folklore—just individual quirky tales and objects. This understanding emerged in the second half of the 20th century, with "folklore performance" and "text and context" dominating discussions. Performance ties to verbal and customary lore; context to material lore. Victor Turner identified four universal characteristics of cultural performance: playfulness, framing, symbolic language, and the subjunctive mood. Material culture requires moulding to fit performance language; context works better for describing quilting patterns copied from grandmothers or gifting quilts to signify important events.

**Backstory**: Before World War II, folk artifacts were collected as cultural shards with little contemporary function; the goal was to capture them before disappearance. After the war, folklorists articulated a holistic approach. Alan Dundes's 1964 essay "Texture, Text and Context" and Dan Ben-Amos's 1967 presentation brought the behavioral approach into debate. Richard Dorson called out the "young Turks" in 1972. This approach "shifted the conceptualization of folklore as an extractable item or 'text' to an emphasis on folklore as a kind of human behavior and communication." Folklore became a verb—something people do.

**Tradition-bearer and audience**: Transmission requires a binary. The tradition-bearer actively passes along knowledge—a mother singing a lullaby or an Irish dance troupe performing. They are named individuals, usually well known in the community, not the anonymous "folk." The audience listens, watches, and remembers; few become active tradition-bearers, many more are passive tradition-bearers maintaining memory. There is active communication between audience and performer; the purpose is to re-create something already known and valued by both.

**Framing the performance**: A frame indicates what follows is performance, bracketing it outside normal discourse. Life cycle celebrations are framed by location; games by rules. Barre Toelken described a Navaho family playing string figure games, with members shifting between performer and audience. Verbal lore uses linguistic formulas: "Have you heard the one...", "An elephant walks into a bar", "Once upon a time" and "They all lived happily ever after."

**In the subjunctive voice**: Framing signals the narrative is fiction, moving it into the subjunctive mood—a space where "fiction, history, story, tradition, art, teaching, all exist within the narrated or performed expressive 'event' outside the normal realms and constraints of reality or time." This shift from realis to irrealis mood is understood by all participants and enables fictional events to contain real meaning.

**Anderson's law of auto-correction**: Walter Anderson articulated this theory in the 1920s, positing a feedback mechanism keeping folklore variants close to the original form. The audience censors narrators who deviate too far from the known text; negative feedback motivates performers to conform. "Social reward by an audience [is] a major factor in motivating narrators." The model is not simplistic—there are multiple redundancies: the performer has heard the tale multiple times from different tellers and tells it multiple times; corrective feedback is immediate.

**Context of material lore**: For material artifacts, Dundes's terminology of text and context is fruitful. The text is the physical artifact; the context is unmasked by observation and questions about production and usage. Michael Owen Jones studied southeastern Kentucky chair makers within the context of the craftsman's life; Henry Glassie's *Folk Housing in Middle Virginia* investigated historical patterns in dwellings. The consumer's role "is the basic force responsible for the continuity and discontinuity of behavior." These artisans are not anonymous folk but individuals making a living with skills learned within their community.

**Toelken's conservative-dynamic continuum**: No two performances are identical. Deviations can be unintentional or intentional creative touches. Barre Toelken identifies the tension as "a combination of both changing ('dynamic') and static ('conservative') elements that evolve and change through sharing, communication and performance." As context changes, artifacts must change to remain meaningful; joke cycles come and go reflecting new concerns. Once an artifact loses relevancy, transmission stops and it becomes an historic relic.

**In the electronic age**: The internet is modifying folkloric process, not killing it; people continue using traditional expressive forms in new media. Jokes are as plentiful as ever. Snow White is now offered in multiple media forms including television and video games. Yeh et al. (2023) suggest user-generated content (UGC) should be considered folklore, especially in mental health communities, as it conveys informal, unofficial knowledge through first-hand stories of treatment experiences, often shared on YouTube, serving to educate and transmit culture like traditional folklore.

## Terms

- **Folklore**: Body of expressive culture shared by a group, including oral, material, and customary traditions, passed informally.
- **Folkloristics**: The academic study of folklore, also called folklore studies.
- **Verbal lore**: Words, written or oral, with repetitive patterns—tales, jokes, proverbs, songs.
- **Material lore**: Tangible artifacts—objects that can be touched, held, lived in, or eaten.
- **Customary lore**: Patterns of expected behavior within a group—gestures, rituals, celebrations.
- **Childlore**: Folklore passed on by children to other children, away from adult supervision.
- **Tradition-bearer**: The individual who actively passes along knowledge of an artifact.
- **Aarne–Thompson classification system**: Standard system for classifying European folktales, first published by Aarne in 1910, expanded by Thompson.
- **Historic–Geographic Method**: Methodology dominant in early 20th-century folkloristics, tracking artifacts across space and time.
- **Subjunctive mood**: Narrative mode signaling fiction, moving the story outside normal reality.

## Debates and open questions

The divided alliance of folkloristics—between humanities in Europe and social sciences in America—continues to be a point of discussion within the field. The classification of folklore into genres is necessary but misleading in oversimplification, as artifacts are never self-contained. The question of whether user-generated content on the internet should be considered folklore is a recent open question, as is how electronic communications modify performance and transmission. The theory of Anderson's law of auto-correction, while influential, is acknowledged as not simplistic, with multiple redundancies in the active folklore process.
