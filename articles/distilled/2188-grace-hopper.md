# Grace Hopper

## Overview
Grace Hopper (1906–1992) was a pioneering American computer scientist and U.S. Navy rear admiral who fundamentally shaped modern computing. She developed the first compiler and was instrumental in creating COBOL, an early high-level programming language that used English words, making computers accessible to non-specialists and establishing a foundation for business computing still in use today.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Grace Brewster Murray was born in New York City, the eldest of three children. She was known for her childhood curiosity, famously dismantling alarm clocks to understand their mechanics. After preparatory school, she earned a bachelor's degree in mathematics and physics from Vassar College in 1928, followed by a master's from Yale in 1930. In 1934, she completed her Ph.D. in mathematics at Yale under Øystein Ore. She married Vincent Foster Hopper in 1930; they divorced in 1945, and she retained his surname.

### World War II and Early Computing Career
Hopper initially tried to join the Navy early in WWII but was rejected for being over age and underweight. In 1943, she obtained a leave from her teaching position at Vassar and joined the Navy Reserve WAVES. After graduating first in her class from the Naval Reserve Midshipmen's School, she was assigned to the Harvard Mark I computer project under Howard H. Aiken. She co-authored papers on the Mark I and wrote its first manual. She remained at Harvard until 1949, turning down a professorship to continue research.

### UNIVAC and the Invention of the Compiler
In 1949, Hopper joined the Eckert–Mauchly Computer Corporation as a senior mathematician, working on the UNIVAC I, the first large-scale electronic computer on the market. She championed the idea that programming should use English words, not just mathematical symbols. In 1952, she developed the A-0 System, an operational program linker she called a compiler, which translated mathematical notation into machine code. Despite initial skepticism, her department later released early compiled languages like FLOW-MATIC.

### COBOL and Standardization
In 1959, Hopper served as a technical consultant to the CODASYL consortium, which created COBOL (COmmon Business-Oriented Language). The language extended her FLOW-MATIC work and embodied her belief in English-based programming. From 1967 to 1977, as director of the Navy Programming Languages Group, she developed validation software and promoted standards for languages like COBOL and FORTRAN, leading to significant industry convergence.

### Later Navy Career and Retirement
Hopper retired from the Naval Reserve in 1966 but was repeatedly recalled to active duty. She was promoted to captain in 1973 and to commodore (later rear admiral) in 1983. She retired for the final time in 1986 as the oldest active-duty commissioned officer. She then worked as a senior consultant for Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC) until her death, serving as a goodwill ambassador and lecturer.

## Terms
- **Compiler**: A program that translates high-level programming language code into machine code understood by a computer.
- **FLOW-MATIC**: An early programming language developed by Hopper that used English-like commands for business data processing.
- **COBOL**: A high-level programming language created in 1959 for business use, based on English words, which Hopper helped pioneer.
- **A-0 System**: The first compiler, developed by Hopper in 1952, which translated mathematical notation into machine code.
- **CODASYL**: The Conference on Data Systems Languages, a consortium that developed the COBOL standard in 1959.
- **WAVES**: Women Accepted for Volunteer Emergency Service, the women's branch of the U.S. Naval Reserve during WWII.
- **Mark I**: The Harvard Mark I, an early automatic sequence-controlled calculator completed in 1944.
- **UNIVAC I**: The first commercially produced electronic digital computer in the United States, delivered in 1951.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that the term "debugging" was in use in other fields before Hopper's team famously logged a moth in the Mark II in 1947, though she is credited with popularizing it in computing. The exact origin of the term "compiler" is also noted, as Hopper's A-0 system was initially called a compiler but functioned as a linker/loader.
