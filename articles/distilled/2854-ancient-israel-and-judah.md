# Ancient Israel and Judah

## Overview
Ancient Israel and Judah were two Israelite kingdoms in the Southern Levant during the Iron Age, emerging from the indigenous Canaanite population. Their history, known primarily through biblical narratives and archaeological evidence, details their formation, division, and eventual destruction by Assyrian and Babylonian empires, culminating in the Babylonian exile. This period was foundational for the development of Judaism and the Hebrew Bible.

## Timeline
- **c. 1208 BCE** — Earliest extra-biblical mention of "Israel" on the Merneptah Stele.
- **c. 1150–950 BCE** — Iron Age I: Emergence of Israelite highland villages.
- **c. 1000–930 BCE** — Traditional period of the United Monarchy under Saul, David, and Solomon.
- **c. 930 BCE** — Division of the monarchy into the northern Kingdom of Israel and southern Kingdom of Judah.
- **853 BCE** — Israelite King Ahab fights in the Battle of Qarqar against Assyria.
- **c. 841 BCE** — Jehu of Israel pays tribute to Assyria, depicted on the Black Obelisk.
- **722/720 BCE** — Fall of the Kingdom of Israel to the Neo-Assyrian Empire; population deported.
- **701 BCE** — Assyrian King Sennacherib invades Judah; besieges but does not capture Jerusalem.
- **622 BCE** — Religious reforms of King Josiah in Judah.
- **597 BCE** — First Babylonian deportation of Judahite elites, including King Jeconiah.
- **587/586 BCE** — Destruction of Jerusalem and Solomon's Temple by Nebuchadnezzar II; start of the Babylonian exile.
- **c. 538 BCE** — Cyrus the Great of Persia issues the Edict of Cyrus, allowing Jews to return.

## Body

### Origins and Early Settlement
The Israelites emerged from the diverse population of Late Bronze Age Canaan. Archaeological evidence shows a rapid increase in small, unwalled villages in the central hill country from the 13th century BCE onward. These settlements exhibited cultural continuity with Canaan but also distinct features like collared-rim jars, four-room houses, and a lack of pig bones. The earliest known reference to Israel is on the Egyptian Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BCE). Early Israelite society was organized into kinship-based tribes, likely forming a loose confederation.

### The United Monarchy and Its Debate
The Hebrew Bible describes a unified kingdom under Kings Saul, David, and Solomon in the 11th-10th centuries BCE, with Jerusalem as its capital. The historicity of this "United Monarchy" is heavily debated. Skeptical scholars note a lack of clear archaeological evidence for a large, centralized state in the 10th century BCE and argue Jerusalem was then a modest settlement. However, discoveries like the Tel Dan Stele (9th century BCE), which references the "House of David," confirm the Davidic dynasty's early recognition. Sites like Khirbet Qeiyafa suggest a centralized polity in Judah by the early 10th century. Most scholars agree the biblical account blends early memories with later elaboration.

### The Divided Kingdoms
After Solomon's death (c. 930 BCE), the monarchy split. The northern Kingdom of Israel was larger and more powerful but politically unstable, with frequent coups. The southern Kingdom of Judah was smaller, poorer, but enjoyed dynastic stability under the Davidic line. Both kingdoms navigated threats from neighbors like Aram-Damascus and the expanding Neo-Assyrian Empire. Israel reached a peak under the Omride dynasty (9th century BCE) but was destroyed by Assyria in 722/720 BCE, with its population deported. Judah survived as an Assyrian vassal, experiencing significant population growth, likely from Israelite refugees.

### The Fall of Judah and the Babylonian Exile
In the late 7th century BCE, King Josiah of Judah implemented religious reforms centralizing worship in Jerusalem. After his death, Judah became entangled in the conflict between Egypt and the rising Neo-Babylonian Empire. Following revolts, Nebuchadnezzar II destroyed Jerusalem and Solomon's Temple in 587/586 BCE, exiling much of the population to Babylon. This catastrophe, interpreted as divine punishment, was a pivotal crisis that led to profound theological reflection. During the exile, biblical texts were compiled and edited, and the Israelite religion evolved towards strict monotheism, with increased emphasis on law, Sabbath, and circumcision as identity markers.

### Religion and Economy
Israelite religion evolved from Canaanite polytheism through henotheism (worshipping Yahweh as supreme without denying other gods) to full monotheism. Worship was initially decentralized at local shrines but was progressively centralized in Jerusalem, especially after the reforms of Hezekiah and Josiah. The economy was based on agriculture, with olive oil and wine production being major industries, especially in the northern kingdom. Judah's economy later specialized regionally under Assyrian influence.

## Terms
- **Henotheism**: Worship of a single god as supreme without denying the existence of other deities.
- **Monolatry**: The worship of a single god while acknowledging the existence of others.
- **Merneptah Stele**: An Egyptian inscription from c. 1208 BCE containing the earliest extra-biblical reference to "Israel."
- **United Monarchy**: The biblical concept of a single Israelite kingdom under Saul, David, and Solomon; its historicity is debated.
- **Deuteronomistic History**: A scholarly term for the biblical books from Joshua to 2 Kings, interpreted as a theological history written during the Babylonian exile.
- **Zion Theology**: The belief that God chose Jerusalem and the Davidic dynasty to rule forever; challenged by the city's destruction.
- **Second Temple Period**: The era from the rebuilding of the Temple in Jerusalem (c. 516 BCE) to its destruction by Rome in 70 CE.
- **Pax Assyriaca**: The "Assyrian Peace," a period of relative stability and economic growth in the Levant under Assyrian hegemony.

## Debates and open questions
- **Historicity of the United Monarchy**: Scholars debate whether a large, centralized kingdom existed in the 10th century BCE or if the biblical account is a later idealization of a smaller chiefdom.
- **Origins of the Israelites**: Theories range from a gradual emergence from indigenous Canaanites to a significant influx of groups from outside, including possible refugees from Egypt.
- **Nature of the Babylonian Exile**: While the biblical focus is on the exiled elite, the demographic impact on those remaining in Judah is debated, with some scholars arguing for a significant population collapse and others for continuity.
- **Development of Monotheism**: The process and timeline by which Israelite religion transitioned from henotheism to strict monotheism are subjects of ongoing scholarly analysis.
