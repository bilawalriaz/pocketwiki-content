# Madame Bovary

## Overview
*Madame Bovary* is the debut novel by Gustave Flaubert, first published in 1856-1857. It is a seminal work of literary realism that follows the tragic story of Emma Bovary, a provincial doctor's wife whose romantic delusions and extravagant spending lead to her ruin. The novel is celebrated for its stylistic perfection and its critique of bourgeois society, and it caused a major scandal upon publication, leading to an obscenity trial.

## Timeline
- **1 Oct – 15 Dec 1856** — *Madame Bovary* is serialized in *Revue de Paris*.
- **Jan 1857** — Public prosecutors attack the novel as obscene, leading to a trial.
- **7 Feb 1857** — Flaubert is acquitted of obscenity charges.
- **Apr 1857** — The novel is published in two volumes and becomes a bestseller.
- **1886** — Eleanor Marx publishes the first English translation.

## Body

### Plot
Charles Bovary, a mediocre country doctor, marries Emma Rouault, a farmer's daughter with romantic dreams fueled by novels. Disillusioned by provincial marriage and motherhood, Emma seeks escape through passionate affairs with the law student Léon Dupuis and the landowner Rodolphe Boulanger. Both relationships end in abandonment. To fund her luxurious tastes, she falls into crippling debt with the merchant Lheureux. Facing financial and emotional ruin, she commits suicide by swallowing arsenic. Charles, heartbroken, dies shortly after, and their daughter Berthe is sent to work in a cotton mill. The novel ends with the town pharmacist Homais, a symbol of shallow bourgeois ambition, being awarded the Legion of Honour.

### Style and Realism
Flaubert aimed to write a "book about nothing," held together by the "internal strength of its style." This focus on precise, impersonal prose made *Madame Bovary* a landmark of literary realism, a movement reacting against romanticism. The novel's realism was central to its obscenity trial; the prosecutor argued it was an "offence against art and decency." While Emma embodies romantic delusion, Flaubert's narrative voice often mocks her sentiments. The novel is seen as a commentary on the bourgeoisie, critiquing its "intellectual and spiritual superficiality" and "mindless parroting of sentiments."

### Literary Significance and Reception
Long established as one of the greatest novels, *Madame Bovary* has been praised by writers like Henry James, Marcel Proust, and Vladimir Nabokov for its stylistic perfection. It exemplifies the 19th-century shift in realism toward psychological depth, prefiguring modernist authors like Proust and Woolf. Despite its initial controversy and criticism from publications like *Le Figaro*, it is now considered a masterpiece that elevated the art of the novel to the level of poetry.

## Terms
- **Literary Realism**: A 19th-century literary movement aiming to represent subject matter truthfully, without romantic idealization.
- **Bourgeoisie**: The social class of the middle class, characterized in the novel by materialism and intellectual superficiality.
- **Officier de santé**: A medical practitioner with less training than a full physician, which is Charles Bovary's profession.
- **Ennui**: A feeling of listlessness and dissatisfaction arising from a lack of excitement or stimulation.
- **Eponymous**: Giving one's name to something. Emma Bovary is the eponymous character of *Madame Bovary*.
- **Received Ideas**: Conventional or commonplace opinions, often unexamined, as catalogued in Flaubert's *Dictionary of Received Ideas*.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Flaubert's Identification with Emma**: The accuracy of the famous assertion "Madame Bovary, c'est moi" ("Madame Bovary is me") is debated. Flaubert's letters suggest he distanced himself from the novel's sentiments.
- **The Novel's Social Critique**: Scholars debate whether the novel is primarily a critique of bourgeois society, romantic illusions, or the gap between desire and reality in modern life.
- **The Obscenity Trial**: The trial's focus on "realism" as an "offence against art" raises ongoing questions about the relationship between artistic representation, morality, and censorship.
