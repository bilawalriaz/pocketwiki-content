# Darius the Great

## Overview
Darius I (c. 550–486 BCE), known as Darius the Great, was the third King of Kings of the Achaemenid Empire. He ruled during its territorial peak, stretching from the Balkans to the Indus Valley. His reign is defined by the centralization of a vast, diverse empire through administrative, economic, and infrastructural reforms, and by major military campaigns that set the stage for the Greco-Persian Wars.

## Timeline
- **c. 550 BCE** — Darius is born, son of Hystaspes.
- **522 BCE** — Darius seizes power after killing the alleged usurper Gaumata.
- **522–521 BCE** — Darius quells widespread revolts across the empire.
- **c. 518 BCE** — Construction begins on the Behistun Inscription.
- **516–515 BCE** — Darius campaigns in Central Asia and conquers the Indus Valley.
- **513 BCE** — Darius invades European Scythia.
- **492 BCE** — Mardonius, Darius's general, re-subjugates Thrace and Macedonia.
- **490 BCE** — Persian forces are defeated by Athens at the Battle of Marathon.
- **486 BCE** — Darius dies, leaving plans for a second Greek invasion to his son Xerxes.

## Body

### Accession and Early Reign
Darius came to power after the death of Cambyses II. He claimed to have overthrown an imposter, Gaumata, who had seized the throne by pretending to be Cambyses's murdered brother, Bardiya. With six noble allies, Darius killed Gaumata and was crowned king. His account, recorded on the Behistun Inscription, was likely a fabrication to legitimize his overthrow of the rightful Achaemenid line. He immediately faced and suppressed major revolts in Elam, Babylonia, Media, and elsewhere, executing nine "lying kings."

### Administrative and Economic Reforms
To unify and control his vast realm, Darius reorganized the empire into approximately twenty provinces called satrapies, each governed by a satrap (governor). He established a system of checks with separate military commanders and royal inspectors. Darius introduced a uniform monetary system centered on the gold daric and silver siglos, which boosted trade and standardized taxation. He made Aramaic a co-official language for administration and commissioned the creation of the Old Persian cuneiform script for royal inscriptions.

### Military Campaigns
Darius expanded the empire to its greatest extent. He secured Egypt, campaigned eastward to conquer the Indus Valley (c. 515 BCE), and led a punitive expedition into Scythian territory north of the Black Sea in 513 BCE. The Scythians avoided direct battle, leading to a stalemate. Darius's European campaign resulted in the subjugation of Thrace and the vassalage of Macedonia. His involvement in the Ionian Revolt led to the first Persian invasion of Greece, culminating in the Persian defeat at Marathon in 490 BCE.

### Building Projects and Religion
Darius was a prolific builder, initiating major construction at Susa, Persepolis, and Babylon. He built a canal linking the Nile to the Red Sea and improved the Royal Road system. A devout believer in Ahura Mazda, whom he credited for his right to rule, Darius nonetheless practiced religious tolerance, funding the restoration of the Jerusalem Temple and supporting local cults in Egypt and elsewhere.

## Terms
*   **Satrapy:** A province of the Achaemenid Empire, governed by a satrap.
*   **Satrap:** A governor of a province in the Achaemenid Empire.
*   **Daric:** A standard gold coin introduced by Darius to regulate trade and collect taxes.
*   **Behistun Inscription:** A trilingual (Old Persian, Elamite, Babylonian) rock inscription commissioned by Darius to record his lineage and victories.
*   **Achaemenid Empire:** The First Persian Empire, founded by Cyrus the Great and ruled by the Achaemenid dynasty.
*   **Ahura Mazda:** The supreme deity worshipped by Darius, whom he credited with his divine right to rule.
*   **Aramaic:** A Semitic language that Darius made a co-official administrative language of the empire.
*   **Ionian Revolt:** A rebellion of Greek city-states in Ionia (western Anatolia) against Persian rule, which triggered the Greco-Persian Wars.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Legitimacy of Accession:** Scholars debate whether Darius's account of overthrowing an imposter (Gaumata) was true or a propaganda story to conceal his usurpation of the legitimate heir, Bardiya.
*   **Religious Influence:** There is no consensus on whether Darius and his predecessors were directly influenced by Zoroastrianism, though his devotion to Ahura Mazda is well-documented.
*   **Scythian Campaign Outcome:** The success of Darius's 513 BCE campaign against the Scythians is debated; while he built forts and forced a retreat, he did not achieve a decisive victory or lasting control.
