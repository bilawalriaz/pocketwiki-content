# Feudalism

## Overview
Feudalism was a medieval European social and political system, flourishing from the 9th to 15th centuries, that structured society around land-based relationships of service and obligation. It is a central concept for understanding medieval power structures, though its precise definition and usefulness as a historical model are subjects of ongoing scholarly debate.

## Timeline
- **c. 884** — First attestation of the Latin adjective *feudālis* in a charter of Charles the Fat.
- **9th century** — Feudal structures emerge in the Carolingian Empire due to decentralization and the need to support cavalry.
- **10th century** — The term *feudum* (fief) begins to replace *beneficium* in land grants.
- **11th century** — A "feudal revolution" or "fragmentation of powers" occurs in France, with local lords usurping state prerogatives.
- **12th century** — The concept of a "liege lord" develops in France to clarify superior vassal obligations.
- **1736** — The phrase "feudal system" appears in Thomas Madox's *Baronia Anglica*.
- **1776** — Adam Smith uses "feudal government" and "feudal system" in *The Wealth of Nations*.
- **1789** — The French National Assembly abolishes the feudal system.
- **1939** — Marc Bloch publishes *Feudal Society*, offering a broad sociological definition.
- **1944** — François Louis Ganshof publishes *Feudalism*, defining the classic legal-military model.
- **1974** — Elizabeth A. R. Brown's "The Tyranny of a Construct" challenges the term's validity.
- **2008** — The last feudal regime, on the island of Sark, is abolished.

## Body

### Definition and Etymology
The term "feudalism" was not used by medieval people to describe their own society. The adjective "feudal" appeared by 1406, and the noun "feudalism" emerged in the 18th century. Its root is the medieval Latin *feudum* (fief), first attested in 884. The origin of *feudum* is debated; theories link it to Germanic words for cattle (*fehu*) or to an Arabic loanword (*fuyū*). Initially, land grants were called *beneficia*; the shift to *feudum* occurred around the 10th century.

### Classic and Broader Definitions
Two primary definitions dominate. The classic model, articulated by François Louis Ganshof (1944), describes a narrow set of reciprocal legal and military obligations among the warrior nobility, centered on lords, vassals, and fiefs. A vassal received a fief (land) from a lord in exchange for military service and counsel, formalized through homage and an oath of fealty (fidelity). A broader definition, from Marc Bloch's *Feudal Society* (1939), encompasses the entire social order, including the peasantry bound by manorialism and the clergy, creating a "feudal society."

### Historical Development and Decline
Feudalism typically emerged from the decentralization of empires, like the Carolingian, which lacked the bureaucracy to support cavalry without granting land. This granted land became hereditary, transforming a personal bond into a "politics of land." In 11th-century France, a "feudal revolution" saw a fragmentation of power as local lords privatized state rights. The system's military aspects ended around 1500 due to the rise of professional armies and the Black Death. Vestiges persisted until formal abolition, notably during the French Revolution (1789) and later in parts of Eastern Europe (e.g., Russian serfdom abolished in 1861).

### Historiography and Debate
The concept of feudalism is a modern construct. Enlightenment writers used it to criticize the Ancien Régime. In the 19th century, historians like Heinrich Brunner linked its origins to Charles Martel's land grants for military service. Karl Marx later used "feudalism" as a mode of production preceding capitalism. The 20th century saw major scholarly divergence: Bloch's broad sociological view versus Ganshof's narrow legal-military one. Since the 1970s, scholars like Elizabeth A. R. Brown and Susan Reynolds have challenged feudalism as an anachronistic and overly uniform construct, arguing it lacks a coherent basis in medieval reality. The term's application to non-European societies (e.g., Japan) is also contested.

## Terms
- **Feudalism**: A modern term for a medieval system of reciprocal obligations centered on land tenure, primarily among the nobility.
- **Fief (Feudum)**: A grant of land or revenue given by a lord to a vassal in exchange for service.
- **Vassal**: A person who holds a fief from a lord and owes them service, typically military.
- **Lord**: A noble who grants a fief to a vassal and receives service in return.
- **Homage**: A ceremony where a vassal formally submits to a lord, acknowledging the feudal relationship.
- **Fealty**: An oath of fidelity sworn by a vassal to his lord.
- **Manorialism**: The economic and social system binding peasants (serfs) to a lord's estate (manor) through labor and dues.
- **Seigneurie Banale**: The collection of rights and dues (e.g., justice, mill fees) usurped by local lords during the feudal fragmentation.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Usefulness of the Concept**: Since Brown (1974) and Reynolds (1994), there is ongoing debate whether "feudalism" is a valid analytical tool or an anachronistic construct that imposes false uniformity on diverse medieval practices.
- **Definition Scope**: Scholars disagree on whether feudalism should be defined narrowly (Ganshof: nobility only) or broadly (Bloch: entire society including peasantry and clergy).
- **Origins and Applicability**: The precise origins of feudal institutions are unclear. The term's application outside Europe (e.g., to Japan, Ethiopia, or the American South) is frequently questioned as potentially misleading.
