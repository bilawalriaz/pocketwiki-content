# Timur

## Overview
Timur (c. 1320s–1405), known in the West as Tamerlane, was a Turco-Mongol conqueror who founded the Timurid Empire across Central Asia, Iran, and Afghanistan. Renowned as an undefeated military tactician, he also patronized scholars and artists, catalyzing the Timurid Renaissance. His campaigns reshaped the political landscape of Eurasia, setting the stage for later gunpowder empires.

## Timeline
- **c. 1320s**: Born in Transoxiana (modern Uzbekistan) into the Barlas tribe.
- **1360s**: Gains prominence as a military leader under the Chagatai Khanate.
- **1370**: Proclaimed sovereign at Balkh after defeating rival Amir Husayn.
- **1380–1392**: Conquers Persia, including Herat, Isfahan, and Baghdad.
- **1392–1395**: Wars with the Golden Horde; defeats Tokhtamysh at the Terek River.
- **1398**: Invades the Delhi Sultanate; defeats Sultan Nasir-ud-Din at Delhi.
- **1400–1401**: Campaigns in the Caucasus and Levant; sacks Aleppo and Damascus.
- **1402**: Defeats Ottoman Sultan Bayezid I at the Battle of Ankara.
- **1404–1405**: Begins campaign against Ming China; dies en route at Farab.
- **1409**: Younger son Shah Rukh secures succession after civil war among Timur’s descendants.

## Body

### Ancestry and Identity
Timur was born into the Barlas, a Mongol tribe that had become Turkicized. His father, Taraghai, was a minor noble. Though later Timurid sources claimed descent from Genghis Khan through his ancestor Qarachar Noyan, no contemporary evidence supports a direct genealogical link. His mother, Tekina Khatun, remains enigmatic—possibly of low origin or linked to the Yasa'uri tribe. Contemporaries consistently identified Timur as a Mongol, and he embraced this identity, aligning himself with Chagatai traditions and Genghisid legitimacy.

### Rise to Power
By 1370, Timur had eliminated rivals like Amir Husayn, marrying his widow Saray Mulk Khanum, a descendant of Genghis Khan, to legitimize his rule over the Chagatai Khanate. He installed puppet khans while wielding real power, using titles like *Sahib Qiran* (“Lord of Conjunction”) rooted in astrology to assert divine sanction. This strategy allowed him to govern without claiming the Mongol title of *khan*, which was reserved for Genghisid descendants.

### Military Campaigns
Timur’s expansion spanned decades, targeting the Golden Horde, Mamluk Sultanate, Delhi Sultanate, and Ottoman Empire. Notable victories include the 1398 sack of Delhi, where war elephants were neutralized via fire-laden camels, and the 1402 Battle of Ankara, which captured Bayezid I. His tactics emphasized psychological warfare and selective brutality—massacring resisting populations while sparing artisans and scholars. Cities like Isfahan and Baghdad suffered catastrophic losses, with tens of thousands killed.

### Religious Views and Legacy
A practicing Sunni Muslim, possibly affiliated with the Naqshbandi Sufi order, Timur styled himself a *ghazi* (“warrior of faith”) in later life. However, his adherence to Islam was questioned by contemporaries due to his continued use of Mongol legal customs (*Yassa*) and shamanic practices. His campaigns caused immense destruction, particularly in Iraq, Persia, and India, earning him both reverence in Central Asia and vilification elsewhere. He influenced later conquerors like Nader Shah and helped establish Persian as the lingua franca of administration and culture across his realm.

### Death and Aftermath
Timur died in 1405 during a winter campaign against China, having fallen ill near the Syr Darya River. His death triggered a succession crisis among his sons, resolved only in 1409 when Shah Rukh emerged victorious. The empire fragmented rapidly, but Timur’s cultural patronage endured through figures like his grandson Ulugh Beg and great-great-grandson Babur, founder of the Mughal Empire.

## Terms
- **Barlas**: A Mongol tribe that had adopted Turkic language and customs.
- **Chagatai Khanate**: A Mongol khanate in Central Asia, named after Genghis Khan’s son Chagatai.
- **Ghazi**: A term denoting a warrior who fights in defense or expansion of Islam.
- **Sahib Qiran**: A title meaning “Lord of Conjunction,” referencing an astrological alignment seen as auspicious.
- **Timurid Renaissance**: A cultural revival under Timur’s rule, marked by advances in art, science, and literature.
- **Yassa**: The legal code of the Mongol Empire, emphasizing strict discipline and order.
- **Ulus**: A Mongol administrative unit or appanage, often hereditary.
- **Diwan**: A term referring to the administrative bureaucracy or chancery in Islamic states.
- **Tamerlane chess**: A chess variant invented by Timur, played on a 10×11 board.
- **Gur-e-Amir**: Timur’s mausoleum in Samarkand, a masterpiece of Timurid architecture.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Genealogy**: Whether Timur was directly descended from Genghis Khan remains disputed; contemporary sources do not confirm it.
- **Religiosity**: Scholars debate whether Timur genuinely adhered to Islam or used it instrumentally, given his retention of Mongol legal and spiritual practices.
- **Motivations**: His true motives—imperial ambition versus religious duty—are contested, especially regarding campaigns against fellow Muslims.
- **Historical Sources**: The reliability of Timurid-sponsored chronicles (*Zafarnama*) versus critical accounts like those of Ibn Arabshah reflects bias in portraying his legacy.
- **Curse of Timur**: The alleged curse inscribed on his tomb is widely regarded as a modern fabrication, despite its association with the 1941 German invasion of the USSR.
