# Guru Nanak

## Overview
Guru Nanak (1469–1539) was an Indian spiritual teacher and poet, revered as the founder of Sikhism and the first of its ten Gurus. His teachings, recorded in the Guru Granth Sahib, emphasize the oneness of God (Ik Onkar), equality of all humanity, and a life of honest work, devotion, and sharing. He established a unique spiritual and social platform that rejected caste and religious divisions, forming the foundation of the Sikh faith.

## Timeline
- **15 April 1469** — Born in Rāi Bhoi Dī Talvaṇḍī (present-day Nankana Sahib, Pakistan).
- **c. 1487** — Married Sulakhani; later had two sons, Sri Chand and Lakhmi Chand.
- **c. 1487–1500** — Worked in the modikhana (storehouse) in Sultanpur, a formative period.
- **c. 1496 (age 27) or 33** — Experienced a pivotal spiritual revelation after disappearing into the Bein river.
- **c. 1499–1521** — Embarked on extensive travels (udasis) across Asia, teaching his message.
- **c. 1521** — Witnessed Babur's invasion of India at Saidpur.
- **c. 1521** — Settled in Kartarpur, establishing a community based on his teachings.
- **1539** — Appointed Bhai Lehna (Guru Angad) as his spiritual successor.
- **22 September 1539** — Died in Kartarpur at age 70.

## Body

### Early Life and Revelation
Born into a Hindu Khatri family, Nanak showed early spiritual inclinations, rejecting orthodox rituals like the sacred thread ceremony. He worked as an accountant in Sultanpur, where his daily routine of bathing in the Bein river culminated in a transformative event around age 27 or 33. He disappeared for several days and returned with the declaration "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim," signaling his divine commission to teach the unity of God and the equality of mankind.

### Travels (Udasis)
After his revelation, Nanak left his employment and embarked on extensive travels across the Indian subcontinent and beyond, accompanied by his Muslim friend Mardana. These journeys, known as udasis, took him to major Hindu and Muslim pilgrimage centers, where he engaged in discourses and debated religious ideas. The exact routes and authenticity of many travel stories are debated by scholars, with some accounts appearing in later hagiographies. His travels concluded after he witnessed Babur's invasion, an event recorded in his compositions.

### Teachings and Community at Kartarpur
Nanak's teachings, recorded in 974 hymns in the Guru Granth Sahib, center on Ik Onkar (One God), honest work (kirat karo), sharing with others (vand chhako), and meditating on God's name (naam japo). He rejected the caste system and ritualism, emphasizing a life of active, ethical householder devotion. Around age 50, he established the settlement of Kartarpur, where he formalized community practices like langar (communal kitchen) and sangat (congregation), creating a model society based on equality and service.

### Succession and Legacy
Nanak did not pass the Guruship to his sons, instead appointing his devoted follower Bhai Lehna as successor, renaming him Guru Angad. This act ensured the continuity of his spiritual mission. He died in 1539, and his compositions form the core of the Sikh scripture. His birthday is celebrated annually as Guru Nanak Gurpurab.

## Terms
- **Ik Onkar**: The Sikh concept of One God, the eternal, formless creator.
- **Guru Granth Sahib**: The central holy scripture of Sikhism, considered the final and perpetual Guru.
- **Janamsakhis**: Traditional hagiographic "birth stories" of Guru Nanak, containing a mix of history, legend, and teaching.
- **Udasis**: The extensive spiritual journeys undertaken by Guru Nanak.
- **Sangat**: The Sikh congregation or community gathered for worship.
- **Langar**: The communal kitchen and free meal service in a Gurdwara, symbolizing equality.
- **Kirat Karo**: The Sikh principle of earning an honest living.
- **Naam Japo**: The practice of meditating on or remembering God's name.
- **Vand Chhako**: The principle of sharing one's earnings and resources with others.
- **Gurmukh**: A person oriented towards the Guru and divine wisdom, as opposed to a manmukh (self-oriented).

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Birth Date**: There is scholarly debate over whether Nanak was born in the month of Vaisakh (April) or Kattak (November). Most Janamsakhis support the Kattak date, but historical records of his death suggest a Vaisakh birth. The adoption of the Kattak date for Gurpurab may relate to his enlightenment or later historical factors.
- **Authenticity of Travels**: The details and extent of Nanak's travels are disputed. Early Sikh texts do not contain many travel stories, which appear and become more elaborate in later hagiographies, paralleling miracle stories in other traditions.
- **Nature of Teachings**: Scholars debate whether Nanak's teachings were a divine revelation, a social protest movement, or an attempt to synthesize Hinduism and Islam. Recent scholarship moves away from the "syncretic" label, viewing Sikhism in the context of medieval Indian Bhakti and Sant movements.
- **Historical vs. Legendary Nanak**: Due to a lack of contemporary factual documentation, a distinction is made between the "historical Nanak" and the "legendary Nanak" of hagiography. The Janamsakhis, the primary sources, were written for religious, not historical, purposes.
