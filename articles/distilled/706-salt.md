# Salt

## Overview
Salt (sodium chloride, NaCl) is a mineral essential to life and one of humanity’s oldest food seasonings and preservatives. Its scarcity and universal need made it a driver of trade, taxation, and warfare across civilizations, while its role in preserving meat and enhancing flavor shaped dietary practices worldwide. Today, salt remains critical not only in food but also in chemical manufacturing, de-icing, and water treatment, though excessive consumption poses significant health risks.

## Timeline
- **c. 6050 BC** — Neolithic salt extraction via briquetage at Poiana Slatinei, Romania
- **c. 6000 BC** — Salt harvesting from Xiechi Lake, China; earliest verifiable saltworks
- **c. 5400 BC** — Solnitsata, Bulgaria, established as Europe’s first known salt-mining city
- **c. 2800 BC** — Egyptians begin exporting salted fish to Phoenicians
- **c. 800 BC** — Hallstatt culture begins systematic salt mining in Austria
- **400 BC** — Open-pan salt making adopted in Hallstatt region
- **1304** — Venice wins Salt War against Padua over salt supply control
- **1930** — Mahatma Gandhi leads Dandi March protesting British salt tax

## Body

### Historical Significance and Trade
Salt has been pivotal to civilization due to its role in food preservation and taste enhancement. Early extraction methods included boiling salt-laden spring water using briquetage, as seen in Neolithic Romania and China. As agriculture replaced nomadic lifestyles, salt became a vital dietary supplement and a major trade commodity. Ancient societies—including the Hebrews, Greeks, Romans, and Egyptians—valued salt highly, using it in religious rituals, as currency, and in long-distance trade networks. In Africa, rock salt slabs served as coins among the Tuareg and in Abyssinia, while trans-Saharan caravans transported salt pillars across deserts.

### Cultural and Religious Roles
Salt features prominently in religious traditions globally. In Judaism, salt accompanies Sabbath bread to symbolize covenant; in Christianity, it is used in consecration rites and holy water. Hindu, Jain, Buddhist, and Shinto practices incorporate salt for purification, protection from evil, and ceremonial offerings. The Bible references salt metaphorically (“salt of the earth”) and narratively (Lot’s wife turned to salt). Salting the earth—a symbolic act of destruction—was associated with biblical and later military conquests, though accounts like Rome’s salting of Carthage are now considered apocryphal.

### Production and Industrial Uses
Globally, only a small fraction of salt production is for human consumption. Major producers include China, the U.S., and India. Salt is extracted from seawater through evaporation ponds or mined from sedimentary deposits. The largest underground mine, Canada’s Sifto Mine, operates beneath Lake Huron. Industrially, salt serves as a feedstock for producing caustic soda and chlorine, key inputs for PVC, paper pulp, and plastics. It is also used in aluminum production, soap manufacturing, pottery glazing, drilling fluids, and de-icing.

### Health Implications
While sodium is essential for nerve and muscle function and fluid balance, excessive intake increases cardiovascular disease risk. The WHO recommends under 2,000 mg of sodium (5 g of salt) daily. In Western diets, 75% of sodium comes from processed and restaurant foods. High intake correlates with hypertension, stroke, and kidney disease. Some studies suggest a U-shaped relationship between sodium intake and mortality, indicating harm at both very low and high levels. Fortification strategies, such as iodized salt, have successfully reduced iodine deficiency disorders affecting billions.

## Terms
- **Briquetage**: Archaeological evidence of salt-boiling installations using clay or stone tanks.
- **Halite**: The mineral form of sodium chloride, commonly known as rock salt.
- **ENaC (Epithelial Sodium Channel)**: Protein receptor responsible for detecting salt taste in humans.
- **Iodized salt**: Table salt fortified with potassium iodide or iodate to prevent deficiency diseases.
- **Doubly fortified salt**: Salt enriched with both iodine and iron to combat multiple deficiencies.
- **Salting the earth**: Symbolic scattering of salt to render land infertile after conquest.
- **Room and pillar mining**: Underground extraction method leaving pillars of ore to support roof structures.
- **Alberger process**: Vacuum evaporation technique producing flake-like salt crystals.

## Debates and Open Questions
The health impacts of extremely low sodium intake remain debated. While some reviews advocate reducing intake below 2,300 mg/day, others cite insufficient evidence for benefits or harms at lower thresholds. The U-shaped association between sodium consumption and cardiovascular mortality suggests optimal ranges rather than universal minimums. Additionally, the historical claim that Roman soldiers were paid in salt ("salary") lacks credible support and is widely dismissed by scholars.
