# Humayun

## Overview
Humayun (1508–1556) was the second Mughal emperor, whose tumultuous reign was marked by the loss and eventual restoration of his empire. His 15-year exile in Safavid Persia profoundly influenced Mughal court culture, introducing Persian artistic and literary traditions that became central to the empire's identity. His restoration secured the Mughal dynasty for his son, Akbar, setting the stage for its golden age.

## Timeline
- **6 March 1508** — Born as Nasir al-Din Muhammad to Babur.
- **26 December 1530** — Succeeds his father Babur as Mughal emperor.
- **1535–1536** — Campaigns against Sultan Bahadur of Gujarat, capturing Mandu and Champaner.
- **June 1539** — Defeated by Sher Shah Suri at the Battle of Chausa.
- **17 May 1540** — Defeated by Sher Shah Suri at the Battle of Kannauj, losing the Mughal throne.
- **15 October 1542** — His son, the future Emperor Akbar, is born in Amarkot.
- **1543–1545** — Takes refuge in the Safavid court of Persia.
- **21 March 1545** — With Safavid aid, besieges and captures Kandahar.
- **18 November 1545** — Enters Kabul, reuniting with his son Akbar.
- **22 June 1555** — Defeats Sikandar Shah Suri at the Battle of Sirhind, re-establishing Mughal rule.
- **23 July 1555** — Re-enters Delhi, restored to the throne.
- **24 January 1556** — Dies from a fall down the stairs of his library.

## Body
**Early Reign and Loss of Empire**
Humayun ascended the throne at age 22, inheriting a realm divided by his father's Central Asian tradition of splitting territories among sons. His inexperience was compounded by rivalries with his half-brothers, particularly Kamran Mirza, and powerful Afghan nobles. His primary rivals were Sultan Bahadur of Gujarat and Sher Shah Suri in Bihar. After initial campaigns, Humayun was decisively defeated by Sher Shah at Chausa (1539) and Kannauj (1540), losing his empire and beginning a 15-year exile.

**Exile and Persian Influence**
Humayun's retreat took him through the Thar Desert and to Sindh, where his son Akbar was born in 1542. He eventually sought refuge with the Safavid Shah Tahmasp I in Persia. To gain military support, Humayun and his followers converted to Shia Islam. His time in Persia was transformative; he was introduced to Persian miniaturists and absorbed its art, architecture, and literature. With Safavid troops, he recaptured Kandahar (1545) and Kabul (1545), but his return was accompanied by Persian nobles, signaling a major cultural shift at the Mughal court.

**Struggle for Kabul and Restoration**
Humayun's reconquest of Afghanistan was a protracted struggle against his brothers. He captured Kabul but faced repeated revolts from Kamran Mirza, who allied with Uzbeks and Afghan tribes. After years of conflict, Humayun had Kamran blinded and exiled in 1553. With his brothers eliminated and the Sur dynasty in India weakened by civil war after Sher Shah's death, Humayun launched a successful campaign. Under the command of his general Bairam Khan, he defeated the Sur forces at Sirhind (1555) and recaptured Delhi.

**Death and Legacy**
Humayun's second reign was brief. In January 1556, he died after falling down the stairs of his library. His tomb in Delhi, commissioned by his wife Bega Begum, was the first grand garden tomb in Mughal architecture, setting a precedent for later monuments like the Taj Mahal. His son Akbar, who was crowned shortly after his death, would build the empire into its most powerful form. Humayun's Persian exile is now seen by historians as a substantive period of cultural exchange that enriched the Mughal court, rather than a mere interlude.

## Terms
- **Mughal Empire:** A Muslim empire in the Indian subcontinent, founded by Babur in 1526.
- **Timurid:** A dynasty descended from Timur (Tamerlane), to which the Mughals belonged.
- **Safavid Empire:** The ruling dynasty of Iran (Persia) from 1501 to 1736, which was Shia Muslim.
- **Sher Shah Suri:** An Afghan leader who defeated Humayun and founded the short-lived Sur dynasty.
- **Appanage system:** A Central Asian tradition of dividing an empire among a ruler's sons, leading to succession conflicts.
- **Bairam Khan:** A powerful Persian nobleman and general who served Humayun and later Akbar.
- **Khutba:** The Islamic sermon delivered in mosques, often recited in the name of the reigning sovereign.
- **Pir:** An Islamic religious guide or spiritual teacher.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Historians debate whether Humayun's difficulties were primarily due to his personal character flaws (e.g., indecisiveness, opium use) or were structural consequences of the Timurid appanage system, which institutionalized succession conflicts.
- The significance of Humayun's Persian exile is reassessed: was it a passive period of refuge or an active and substantive transmission of Persian culture that fundamentally shaped the Mughal court's identity?
