# Harmony

## Overview
Harmony is the musical concept of combining different pitches to create chords and chord progressions, forming a core element of Western music theory. It involves both vertical (simultaneous) and horizontal (sequential) dimensions, and its perception relies on the balance between consonance (pleasant, stable sounds) and dissonance (tense, unstable sounds). The study of harmony has evolved over centuries, influencing composition and performance across genres.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Pythagoreans experiment with integer ratios for consonant intervals (e.g., 1:2 for octave).
- **c. 350 BC** — Aristoxenus writes *Elements of Harmony*, arguing the ear, not just math, determines harmony.
- **c. 1600** — Shift from Renaissance counterpoint to emphasis on vertical harmony in Western music.
- **17th–18th C** — Common practice period establishes rules for chord resolution and tonal harmony.
- **19th C** — Increased harmonic complexity in Romantic music (e.g., Wagner's *Tristan und Isolde*).
- **20th C** — Development of jazz and popular harmony with extended chords and tensions.
- **1990** — Carl Dahlhaus distinguishes coordinate (Medieval) and subordinate (tonal) harmony.

## Body

### Etymology and Definitions
The term "harmony" derives from the Greek *harmonia* ("joint, agreement"). Early theorists like Aristoxenus debated whether harmony is determined by mathematical ratios or the listener's ear. Modern definitions often highlight ambiguities between aesthetic (pleasing concords) and textural (simultaneous vs. successive pitches) perspectives. The view that modern tonal harmony began around 1600, replacing horizontal counterpoint with vertical emphasis, is now seen as an oversimplification; theorists like Carl Dahlhaus argue harmony encompasses both chord structure and movement.

### Historical Rules and Types
Early Western music used parallel perfect intervals, later replaced by English-style thirds and sixths for sweeter polyphony. Harmony can be coordinate (Medieval/Renaissance, goal-directed) or subordinate (tonal, hierarchical). Types include tertian (chords built on thirds), quartal/quintal (fourths/fifths), and interval cycles. Close and open harmony refer to chord voicings.

### Intervals and Chords
An interval is the distance between two pitches. In 12-tone equal temperament, intervals are standardized but slightly out of tune with pure ratios. Consonant intervals (e.g., unison, octave, fifth) sound stable; dissonant intervals (e.g., second, seventh) create tension. Chords are combinations of pitches; triads (three-note chords) are basic in tertian harmony. Seventh chords add a seventh to a triad, and extended chords (ninths, elevenths, thirteenths) are common in jazz and modern music. Tensions are dissonant additions that typically resolve to consonance.

### Perception
Harmony perception involves tonal fusion (pitches heard as a single tone), roughness (from interfering harmonics), and familiarity (learned associations). Neural correlates show the brain processes consonance more robustly. The balance between consonance and dissonance creates musical drama.

## Terms
- **Consonance**: Sounds perceived as pleasant, stable, often with simple frequency ratios.
- **Dissonance**: Sounds perceived as tense, unstable, requiring resolution.
- **Interval**: The distance between two pitches.
- **Chord**: A combination of three or more pitches sounded together.
- **Triad**: A three-note chord built on thirds.
- **Tension**: An additional chord member creating dissonance, often resolved.
- **Counterpoint**: The relationship between independent melodic lines.
- **Equal Temperament**: A tuning system dividing the octave into 12 equal semitones.
- **Enharmonic**: Notes with different spellings but the same pitch (e.g., E and F♭).

## Debates and Open Questions
- Whether harmony is primarily determined by mathematical ratios or cultural/aural perception.
- The extent to which Western harmony theory applies to non-Western music (e.g., Indian raga, which emphasizes melody over vertical harmony).
- The historical narrative that counterpoint was "replaced" by harmony around 1600, versus the view that both evolved together.
- How familiarity and cultural context influence the perception of consonance and dissonance over time.
