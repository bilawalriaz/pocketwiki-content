# Sahara

## Overview

The Sahara is the world's largest hot desert, covering approximately 9.2 million square kilometers across North Africa. It spans from the Red Sea and Mediterranean in the east to the Atlantic Ocean in the west, bounded by the Sahel to the south. The Sahara's climate has alternated between arid desert and green savanna over hundreds of thousands of years due to Earth's orbital precession, profoundly shaping human migration, settlement, and cultural development across the region.

## Geography and Climate

The Sahara spans 9.2 million square kilometers, covering parts of Algeria, Chad, Egypt, Libya, Mali, Mauritanau, Niger, Western Sahara, and Sudan, plus portions of Morocco and Tunisia. It constitutes 31% of the African continent. The desert features diverse landscapes including hamada (rocky plateaus), ergs (sand seas), gravel plains (reg), dry valleys (wadi), dry lakes, and salt flats (shatt or chott). The highest peak is Emi Koussi, a shield volcano in the Tibesti range of northern Chad.

The Sahara's hyperarid climate results from its position in the horse latitudes under the subtropical ridge, where descending upper-tropospheric air warms and dries the lower atmosphere, preventing cloud formation. The continental tropical (cT) air mass dominates, creating extremely high temperatures exceeding 40°C across most of the desert. Daily temperatures can reach 80°C on sand surfaces, while nights cool dramatically due to clear skies and low humidity. Annual rainfall ranges from virtually zero in the central-eastern regions to 100-250mm in the northern and southern fringes. Potential evaporation rates of 2,500-6,000mm annually make it the driest region on Earth.

## Prehistoric Climate Cycles

The Sahara has alternated between desert and savanna grassland in 20,000-year cycles driven by Earth's axial precession. Scientists have identified more than 230 humid periods in North Africa, occurring every 21,000 years for the past eight million years. During wet phases ("Green Sahara"), the North African monsoon strengthens, bringing increased precipitation and vegetation. The Orbital Monsoon Hypothesis, proposed by John Kutzbach in 1981, explains these cycles through insolation changes caused by Earth's orbital variations. The cycle is caused by a 41,000-year cycle in which the tilt of the earth changes between 22° and 24.5°. Speleothem growth patterns provide evidence of these cycles, showing wet periods around 125,000, 90,000, and 12,500 years ago. The last major transition to desert conditions occurred around 5,400 years ago, with the Sahara reaching modern aridity by 3,400 BCE. At present, it is in a dry period, but it is expected that the Sahara will become green again in 15,000 years.

## Archaeological Cultures

The Kiffian culture (10,000-8,000 BCE) represents early Holocene inhabitants who lived during the wet phase, as evidenced by remains found at Gobero, Niger. These people were tall hunters who relied on lake resources. Following desiccation, the Tenerian culture emerged around 4,600 BCE, characterized by shorter stature and distinct burial practices, including elaborate triple graves with flower pollen. The Tashwinat Mummy from Uan Muhuggiag, dating to approximately 5,600 years ago, represents early mummification practices predating Egyptian mummies by 1,000 years. Lake Mega-Chad, which existed during the African humid period, was the largest of four Saharan paleolakes, estimated to have covered an area of 350,000 km2.

## Historical Civilizations

The Garamantes civilization arose around 500 BCE in Libya's Wadi al-Ajal, achieving prosperity through sophisticated qanat systems that tapped fossil water. They built eight major towns before collapsing due to aquifer depletion. The Phoenicians established coastal settlements and trading networks, influencing the Libyco-Berber alphabet that evolved into Tifinagh script still used by Tuareg peoples. Greek and Roman expeditions explored the desert margins, while the Byzantine Empire controlled northern coasts until the 7th century Islamic conquest.

## Medieval and Modern Periods

Islamic expansion in the 7th-8th centuries intensified trans-Saharan trade, including a significant slave trade transporting 6,000-7,000 individuals annually from the 10th to 19th centuries. The Ottoman Empire controlled northern fringe regions from the 16th century, maintaining Arabic language and Islamic culture while facilitating trade routes. European colonialism began in the 19th century, with France conquering Algeria (1830) and expanding control across the region. The French established air links and trans-Saharan transport networks, including the Compagnie Transsaharienne bus services.

## Contemporary Issues

Post-independence (1951-1975), most Saharan states gained sovereignty, though Western Sahara remains disputed between Morocco and the Polisario Front. Modern challenges include resource extraction (oil, gas, phosphates), large-scale irrigation projects like Libya's Great Man-Made River, and ongoing conflicts involving Tuareg separatists and Islamist groups. Proposed Trans-African highways aim to reconnect the region, though many remain incomplete.

## Terms

**Hamada**: Rocky plateau terrain characteristic of desert landscapes  
**Erg**: Sand sea covered with sand dunes  
**Reg**: Gravel plain found in desert regions  
**Wadi**: Dry valley or channel that fills with water during rare rainfall  
**Shatt/Chott**: Salt flat or dry salt lake  
**Horse latitudes**: Subtropical high-pressure zones where air descends, creating arid conditions  
**Subtropical ridge**: Belt of semi-permanent subtropical warm-core high pressure systems  
**Continental tropical (cT) air mass**: Hot, dry air mass forming over North African desert regions  
**Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)**: Belt of low-pressure systems near the equator bringing seasonal rainfall  
**Orbital Monsoon Hypothesis**: Theory linking Earth's orbital variations to monsoon intensity and Saharan climate cycles  

## Debates and Open Questions

The role of human activity in accelerating desertification remains debated, with some researchers proposing that pastoralist overgrazing between 6000-2500 BCE contributed to the drying period. Genetic studies of ancient remains continue to refine understanding of population movements, with recent 2025 research on Takarkori individuals suggesting the Green Sahara was not a corridor connecting Northern and sub-Saharan Africa as previously thought. The classification of genetic haplogroups as "African" versus "Eurasian" in North African populations remains contentious among scholars, with debates over racial categorization in genomic studies. The exact timing and mechanisms of the transition from wet to dry phases, particularly the role of Dansgaard-Oeschger and Heinrich events, continue to be refined through paleoclimatic research.
