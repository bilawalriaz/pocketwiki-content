# East China Sea

## Overview
The East China Sea is a marginal sea of the Western Pacific Ocean, bordered by China, Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan. It is a strategically vital and resource-rich body of water, significant for international shipping, fisheries, and major disputes over maritime boundaries and undersea resources, particularly natural gas.

## Timeline
- **1849–1892** — American whaleships cruise for right whales in the sea.
- **1982** — The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) is adopted, providing the legal framework for later disputes.
- **1995** — The People's Republic of China (PRC) discovers the Chunxiao undersea natural gas field.
- **2004** — Japan officially switches its standard name for the sea from "East Shina Sea" to "East China Sea" in official documents.
- **2006** — Commercial operations begin at the Chunxiao gas field.
- **2008** — China and Japan agree to jointly develop the Chunxiao gas fields, but fail to agree on execution.
- **2012** — China submits a claim to the UN regarding the outer limits of its continental shelf in the East China Sea.

## Body

### Geography and Extent
The East China Sea covers an area of roughly 770,000 square kilometers (300,000 sq mi). It is bounded by the Asian continent to the west, Kyushu and the Ryukyu Islands of Japan to the east, Taiwan to the south, and opens north to the Yellow Sea. It connects to the Sea of Japan via the Korea Strait, the South China Sea via the Taiwan Strait, and the Philippine Sea via gaps between the Ryukyu Islands. The International Hydrographic Organization defines its precise limits. The sea is mostly shallow, with an average depth of 350 metres (1,150 ft), though the Okinawa Trough reaches a maximum depth of 2,716 metres (8,911 ft). The Yangtze River is the largest river flowing into it.

### Nomenclature
The sea is called the "East Sea" (东海; 東海; Dōng Hǎi) in Chinese, one of the Four Seas of Chinese literature. In Japan, it was historically called the "East Shina Sea" until 2004, when the standard name became "East China Sea" (東シナ海). In Indonesia, the standard name changed from "Laut Cina Timur" to "Laut Tiongkok Timur" in 2014, though the older name persists in common usage.

### Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) Disputes
Major disputes exist between China, Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan over the extent of their respective EEZs (zones granting sovereign rights to explore and use marine resources). The central dispute between China and Japan concerns the application of UNCLOS. China claims its EEZ extends to the Okinawa Trough based on the natural prolongation of its continental shelf. Japan proposes a median line division, as the sea is only 360 nautical miles wide. A key flashpoint is the Chunxiao gas field, which China developed in an area it considers its undisputed EEZ, but which Japan argues may tap into reserves extending under the median line. Gas fields in the Xihu Sag area are estimated to hold proven reserves of 364 billion cubic feet (BCF) of natural gas. A separate dispute involves South Korea's construction of the Ieodo Ocean Research Station on Socotra Rock, a submerged reef, which China objects to as a breach of its EEZ rights.

### Military and Strategic Importance
The East China Sea is a region of significant military activity, with Taiwan, Japan, China, and South Korea all conducting exercises there. The U.S. Indo-Pacific Command has prioritized naval operations to counter China's growing strength in the region. The sea is considered a potential flashpoint for conflict between major powers.

## Terms
- **Marginal Sea:** A sea partially enclosed by islands, archipelagos, or peninsulas, and connected to an ocean.
- **Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ):** A maritime zone under UNCLOS where a coastal state has sovereign rights to explore and exploit natural resources, extending up to 200 nautical miles from its coast.
- **Continental Shelf:** The natural prolongation of a country's land territory under the sea, which under UNCLOS can extend beyond 200 nautical miles if certain geological conditions are met.
- **Median Line:** A proposed boundary line equidistant from the coasts of two adjacent states, often used in maritime delimitation.
- **UNCLOS:** The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, the international treaty establishing the legal framework for ocean governance.
- **Okinawa Trough:** A deep, arc-shaped depression in the seabed of the East China Sea, central to China's continental shelf claim.
- **Submerged Reef:** A reef that is underwater at all tides, not qualifying as an island under UNCLOS for generating territorial waters.

## Debates and Open Questions
The core debate is the legal and geological basis for maritime boundary delimitation: should it be based on the principle of natural prolongation (China's stance) or the equidistance/median line principle (Japan's stance)? Another open question is the future of joint development agreements, such as the stalled 2008 Sino-Japanese plan for the Chunxiao gas field, and whether resource competition will escalate or lead to cooperative frameworks. The status and resource rights around submerged features like Socotra Rock also remain unresolved.
