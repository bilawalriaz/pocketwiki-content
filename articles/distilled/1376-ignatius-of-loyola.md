# Ignatius of Loyola

## Overview

Ignatius of Loyola (c. 23 October 1491 – 31 July 1556) was a Spanish Basque Catholic priest and theologian who, with six companions, founded the religious order of the Society of Jesus (Jesuits), and became its first Superior General in Paris in 1541. A former soldier whose military career ended after a cannonball injury, he underwent a spiritual conversion and developed a method of prayer and discernment known as the Spiritual Exercises, which became foundational to Ignatian spirituality. The Jesuits, distinguished by a fourth vow of obedience to the Pope, became instrumental in the Counter-Reformation through missionary work, teaching, and the establishment of schools and colleges worldwide.

## Body

### Early life and military career

Ignatius was born Íñigo López de Oñaz y Loyola, the youngest of thirteen children of Don Beltrán Ibáñez de Oñaz y Loyola and Doña María Sáenz de Licona y Balda, minor nobility involved in the Basque war of the bands. His mother died soon after his birth, and maternal care fell to María de Garín, wife of the local blacksmith. He was baptized "Íñigo" in honour of Íñigo of Oña, Abbot of Oña; the name also is a medieval Basque diminutive for "My little one." He later adopted the surname "de Loyola" in reference to the Basque village of Loyola where he was born, and the Latin name "Ignatius" for use in France and Italy where it was better understood.

Instead of the ecclesiastic career his father anticipated (he had been tonsured), Íñigo became a page in the service of a relative, Juan Velázquez de Cuéllar, treasurer of Castile, where he took up dancing, fencing, gambling, and duelling. He joined the army at seventeen, driven by desire for fame and patterned after stories of knights and chivalry. At the Battle of Pamplona on 20 May 1521, a cannonball ricocheting off a nearby wall fractured his right leg. Returned to his father's castle, he underwent several surgical operations without anesthetics, with his bones set and rebroken; the operations left his right leg shorter than the other. He would limp for the rest of his life, with his military career over.

### Religious conversion and visions

During convalescence, Íñigo asked for romances of chivalry but none were in the castle; instead, his sister-in-law, Magdalena de Araoz, brought him the lives of Christ and of the saints. The De Vita Christi of Ludolph of Saxony particularly influenced him, inspiring his method of meditation—placing oneself mentally at the scene of the Gospel story, a technique known as Simple Contemplation that became the basis for his Spiritual Exercises. He noticed that dreams of romantic heroism left him desolate and dissatisfied, while saintly dreams ended with joy and peace—his first lesson in discernment.

In March 1522, he visited the Benedictine monastery of Santa Maria de Montserrat, confessed his sins, gave his fine clothes to the poor, and hung his sword and dagger at the Virgin's altar during an overnight vigil. He then walked to Manresa, where he lived for about a year, begging for his keep and doing chores at a local hospital. He prayed for seven hours daily in a nearby cave, practising rigorous asceticism and formulating the fundamentals of the Spiritual Exercises. He also experienced visions, including one of a beautiful serpent-like form that he came to interpret as diabolical.

### Period of studies

After a brief pilgrimage to the Holy Land in September 1523 (from which the Franciscans sent him back), Íñigo returned to Barcelona and, at age 33, attended a free public grammar school. He studied theology and Latin at the University of Alcalá from 1526 to 1527, where he encountered devout women called alumbrados—a group linked to Franciscan reforms but suspected by the Inquisition. When three of these women experienced ecstatic states while he preached on the street without a theology degree, he was singled out for interrogation by the Inquisition but released.

He moved to France to study at the University of Paris, first at the ascetic Collège de Montaigu, then Collège Sainte-Barbe for a master's degree. He arrived during anti-Protestant turmoil that had forced John Calvin to flee France. He gathered six companions—Alfonso Salmeron, Diego Laynez, Francis Xavier, Nicholas Bobadilla, Simão Rodrigues, and Peter Faber. On 15 August 1534, in the chapel of the Martyrium of Saint Denis in Montmartre, they took solemn vows. Ignatius gained his Magisterium from the University of Paris in 1535 at age 43.

### Foundation of the Jesuit order

In 1539, with Peter Faber and Francis Xavier, Ignatius formed the Society of Jesus, approved in 1540 by Pope Paul III. He was chosen as first Superior General, invested with the title "Father General." In addition to the vows of chastity, obedience, and poverty, Ignatius instituted a fourth vow of obedience to the Pope for projects ordained by the pontiff. He sent companions on missions across Europe to create schools, colleges, and seminaries; a successful Jesuit college in Messina, Sicily, became a model for later institutions. In a 1541 letter to Francis Xavier before his departure to India, he wrote "Ite, inflammate omnia"—"Go, set the world on fire."

With secretary Juan Alfonso de Polanco, Ignatius wrote the Jesuit Constitutions, adopted in 1553. They created a centralized organization stressing absolute self-denial and obedience, summarized in the motto perinde ac cadaver ("as if a dead body"). The overarching Jesuit principle became Ad maiorem Dei gloriam ("for the greater glory of God").

### Death and canonization

By 1553, Ignatius' health had begun to fail; he dictated his autobiography to Louis Gonzalez, published in 1555. He died in Rome on 31 July 1556, probably of "Roman Fever," a severe variant of malaria endemic in Rome. An autopsy revealed kidney and bladder stones; anatomist Matteo Colombo described finding stones in multiple organs, but the exact cause of death cannot be established beyond doubt due to inadequate sixteenth-century protocols. He was buried in the Maria della Strada Church, later reinterred in the Church of the Gesù when the original church was demolished in 1568. He was beatified by Pope Paul V on 27 July 1609 and canonized by Pope Gregory XV on 12 March 1622. His feast day is 31 July.

### Legacy

Numerous institutions worldwide bear his name, including Loyola University Maryland (1852, the first American university so named), Loyola University Chicago, Loyola University New Orleans, Loyola Marymount University, Ateneo University institutions in the Philippines, Loyola College in Chennai (founded 1925), and Loyola School in Jamshedpur (founded 1947). He was the subject of a 1949 Spanish biographical film, Loyola, the Soldier Saint, and a 2016 Filipino film, Ignacio de Loyola. He is honoured in the Church of England and Episcopal Church on 31 July. The Shield of Oñaz-Loyola—seven maroon bars on a gold field combined with rampant grey wolves flanking a cooking pot—symbolizes his family lineage and is used by Jesuit institutions worldwide. Pope Pius XI declared him patron saint of all spiritual retreats in 1922.

## Terms

- **Society of Jesus (Jesuits)**: Religious order founded by Ignatius with six companions in 1540, focused on missionary work, teaching, and obedience to the Pope.
- **Spiritual Exercises**: Ignatius' method of meditation and discernment, published 1548, based on placing oneself mentally at Gospel scenes.
- **Simple Contemplation**: Meditation technique from Ludolph of Saxony's De Vita Christi, visualizing oneself at the scene of Gospel stories.
- **Discernment**: The process of distinguishing spiritual experiences by their after-effects—desolation versus joy and peace.
- **Alumbrados**: A group of devout women linked to Franciscan reforms, suspected by the Inquisition, whom Ignatius encountered at Alcalá.
- **Perinde ac cadaver**: Latin motto meaning "as if a dead body," expressing Jesuit self-denial and obedience.
- **Ad maiorem Dei gloriam**: Latin motto meaning "for the greater glory of God," the overarching Jesuit principle.
- **Roman Fever**: Severe variant of malaria endemic in Rome, probable cause of Ignatius' death.
- **Tonsured**: Having one's hair partially shaved as a sign of ecclesiastical status, done to Ignatius as a child.

## Debates and open questions

- It is not clear when Íñigo began using the Latin name "Ignatius" instead of his baptismal name; historian Gabriel María Verd argues he did not intend to change his name but adopted what he believed was a simple variant for use in France and Italy.
- The exact cause of Ignatius' death cannot be established beyond doubt; the autopsy findings suggest possible nephrolithiasis and cholelithiasis (kidney and gall bladder stones), thrombosed haemorrhoids, or a malignant gastro-intestinal growth with metastases, but sixteenth-century protocols are inadequate for a definitive diagnosis.
