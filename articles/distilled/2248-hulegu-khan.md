# Hulegu Khan

## Overview
Hulegu Khan (c. 1217–1265) was a Mongol ruler and grandson of Genghis Khan who led the conquest of much of West Asia. His campaigns destroyed the Abbasid Caliphate in Baghdad and the Nizari Ismaili state, fundamentally shifting the center of Islamic power from Baghdad to Cairo. He founded the Ilkhanate, a Mongol state in Persia that lasted for over a century, blending Mongol rule with Persian administrative traditions.

## Timeline
- **c. 1217** — Hulegu is born to Tolui and Sorghaghtani Beki.
- **1251** — His brother Möngke becomes Great Khan and charges Hulegu with conquering southwestern Asia.
- **1253** — Hulegu assembles a massive army, comprising two-tenths of the empire's fighting men.
- **Early 1256** — The Nizari Ismaili state (the Assassins) surrenders its fortress of Alamut.
- **Jan–Feb 1258** — The Siege of Baghdad; the city is sacked and the Abbasid Caliphate is destroyed.
- **1 March 1260** — Mongol and Christian vassal forces capture Damascus.
- **Sept. 1260** — The Mamluk Sultanate defeats the Mongols at the Battle of Ain Jalut in Palestine.
- **1262** — Hulegu returns to his lands and is drawn into civil war with Berke Khan of the Golden Horde.
- **10 April 1262** — Hulegu sends a letter to Louis IX of France proposing a Franco-Mongol alliance.
- **8 February 1265** — Hulegu dies and is buried on Shahi Island in Lake Urmia.

## Body

### Early Life and Military Campaigns
Hulegu was born in 1217 to Tolui, a son of Genghis Khan, and Sorghaghtani Beki, an influential Keraite princess. In 1251, his brother Möngke Khan appointed him to lead a massive western campaign. Hulegu's army, which included Christians and Muslims from across the empire, was ordered to subjugate or destroy remaining Muslim states. He first destroyed the Lurs of southern Iran and accepted the surrender of the Nizari Ismaili state in 1256. He then established his power base in Azerbaijan.

### The Siege of Baghdad
In late 1257, Hulegu marched on Baghdad, the seat of the Abbasid Caliphate. After Caliph Al-Musta'sim refused to surrender, the Mongols besieged the city beginning in January 1258. The city fell on 10 February, followed by a week of destruction. The Grand Library of Baghdad was burned, and death estimates range from 90,000 to a million. The caliph was killed, ending the Abbasid dynasty. While Baghdad declined severely, Hulegu later ordered the city rebuilt and its libraries reopened.

### Conquest of Syria and the Battle of Ain Jalut
In 1260, Hulegu's forces, allied with Christian vassals like the Armenians and Franks, conquered Muslim Syria, capturing Aleppo and Damascus. This effectively destroyed the Ayyubid dynasty and shifted Islamic power to the Mamluk Sultanate in Cairo. Hulegu withdrew his main force to Iran, leaving a smaller garrison under the Christian general Kitbuqa. The Mamluk Sultan Qutuz, allied with Baybars, then invaded and defeated the Mongols at the Battle of Ain Jalut in September 1260. This defeat marked the high-water mark of Mongol expansion in the region.

### Civil War and European Diplomacy
After the succession of his brother Kublai Khan, Hulegu returned to his lands in 1262. He was prevented from avenging Ain Jalut by a civil war with Berke Khan of the Golden Horde, a Muslim convert who allied with the Mamluks. This conflict was the first open war between Mongol factions and signaled the end of the unified empire. Hulegu also pursued a Franco-Mongol alliance against the Muslims, sending letters to European rulers like Louis IX of France, but these efforts failed.

### Legacy and Assessment
Hulegu's legacy is complex. His conquests caused immense destruction, particularly the sack of Baghdad, giving him a negative reputation in Muslim historical memory. However, he founded the durable Ilkhanate state, which integrated Mongol rule with Persian administrative traditions. He patronized scholars like Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, supporting the Maragheh observatory. His religious attitude was nuanced; while his campaigns targeted Muslim states and his diplomacy used anti-Muslim rhetoric, he employed and patronized prominent Muslims and had personal ties to Christianity and Buddhism.

## Terms
- **Ilkhanate**: The Mongol-ruled state in Persia and Mesopotamia founded by Hulegu, which lasted for over a century.
- **Abbasid Caliphate**: The Islamic dynasty based in Baghdad that was destroyed by Hulegu in 1258.
- **Nizari Ismaili state**: A Shia Muslim sect known as the Assassins, whose fortress of Alamut surrendered to Hulegu in 1256.
- **Ayyubid dynasty**: The Muslim dynasty based in Syria and Egypt that was conquered by Hulegu's forces in 1260.
- **Mamluk Sultanate**: The Muslim military caste that ruled Egypt and defeated the Mongols at Ain Jalut, becoming the new center of Islamic power.
- **Sahib-divan**: A high administrative office; Hulegu appointed the Persian administrator Shams al-Din Juvayni to this role.
- **Tumen**: A Mongol military unit of approximately 10,000 men.
- **Franco-Mongol alliance**: A proposed but never realized military cooperation between the Mongols and European Christian powers against Muslim states.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Religious Attitude**: Scholars debate whether Hulegu's actions constituted a general war against Islam. Evidence shows he targeted specific Muslim political institutions, employed Muslim administrators, and had personal ties to Christianity and Buddhism, complicating a simple characterization.
- **Scale of Destruction**: Death tolls from the sack of Baghdad vary widely, from 90,000 to over a million, and cannot be easily substantiated.
- **Battle of Damascus Narrative**: Some modern historians question the traditional account of the Christian rulers Hethum, Bohemond, and Kitbuqa entering Damascus together in triumph, suggesting it may be apocryphal.
