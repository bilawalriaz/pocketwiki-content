# Nikolay Karamzin

## Overview
Nikolay Mikhailovich Karamzin (12 December [O.S. 1 December] 1766 – 3 June [O.S. 22 May] 1826) was a Russian historian, writer, poet, and critic who shaped modern Russian literary language and established the first comprehensive national history. His *History of the Russian State* (1816–1826) replaced earlier rough chronicles with a polished, 12-volume narrative that legitimized autocracy and influenced imperial ideology for decades. As a sentimentalist prose writer, he introduced European literary modes—exemplified by *Poor Liza* (1792)—and pioneered the French-influenced, "feminine" style that enabled Alexander Pushkin’s later innovations. He also introduced the letter **ë** to the Russian alphabet (post-1795) to mark stressed /jo/.

## Body

### Early Life and Literary Debut
Karamzin was born in the village of Mikhailovka (modern Karamzinka, Ulyanovsk Oblast) near Simbirsk on the Znamenskoye family estate; an alternative version places his birth in 1765 in Orenburg Governorate where his father served. His father, Mikhail Yegorovich Karamzin (1724–1783), was a retired captain of the Imperial Russian Army from a noble family founded by Semyon Karamzin in 1606; the earliest documented ancestor, Kara-mirza, a baptized Tatar, appears in 1534. His mother, Ekaterina Petrovna Karamzina (née Pazukhina), came from a noble family founded in 1620; her father Peter Pazukhin rose from Praporshchik to Colonel in the Simbirsk infantry regiment. She died in 1769 when Nikolay was two. In 1770 his father married Evdokia Gavrilovna Dmitrieva (1724–1783), who became his stepmother. Karamzin had three siblings—Vasily, Fyodor, and Ekaterina—and two agnate siblings.

Educated in Moscow by Swiss-German Johann Matthias Schaden, Karamzin moved to St. Petersburg, where he met poet Ivan Dmitriev and began translating foreign essays. A 1789–1790 European tour produced *Letters of a Russian Traveller*, modelled on Laurence Sterne’s *A Sentimental Journey*. Published first in Karamzin’s *Moscow Journal*, the letters appeared in six volumes (1797–1801) and established his reputation. In the same journal he printed *Poor Liza* and *Natalya the Boyar's Daughter* (1792), which introduced Russian readers to sentimentalism and earned him the epithet "a Russian Sterne."

### Fiction and the Creation of a Literary Language
Karamzin’s prose—admired by Pushkin and Vladimir Nabokov—replaced the heavy, periodic Slavonic style with flowing, French-modelled sentences. He advocated a "feminine" style (lighter, more emotional) that proved crucial for forging a modern Russian literary language. His miscellanies *Aglaia* (1794) and *The Aonides* (1797–1799, with Gavrila Derzhavin and Dmitriev) contained early Gothic experiments (*The Island of Bornholm*) and adaptations of folklore (*Ilya Muromets*). *My Trifles* collected his lighter pieces. By 1802–1803, editing *Envoy of Europe*, he recognized his true vocation was history.

### Historian of the Russian State
Before Karamzin, Vasily Tatishchev’s attempt was a "rough sketch, inelegant in style, and without the true spirit of criticism." Karamzin was most industrious in accumulating materials; in order to accomplish the task, he secluded himself for two years at Simbirsk. His footnotes remain "mines of interesting information." The *History* (1816–1826) covers events through Michael Romanov’s accession in 1613 (Volume 11; Volume 12 unfinished). Karamzin wrote as a "panegyrist of the autocracy"; the work was styled the "Epic of Despotism." He praised Ivan III as the architect of Russian greatness (earlier he had credited Peter the Great), described Ivan the Terrible’s deeds "with disgust," and excelled in battle descriptions and bold character portraits. Alexander I read drafts with him at Tsarskoye Selo and valued his political counsel.

### Political Thought and Conservatism
Karamzin is considered a founding father of Russian conservatism. His *Memoir on Old and New Russia* (1812), written for Alexander I, launched a scathing attack on Mikhail Speransky’s reform proposals. It became a cornerstone of official imperial ideology. Karamzin strongly supported anti-Polish policies, hoping "there would be no Poland under any shape or name."

### Linguistic Contribution
Sometime after 1795, Karamzin introduced the letter **ë** (yo) to distinguish stressed /jo/ from /je/. It was patterned after the existing **ю** (yu). The letter remains non-obligatory; standard **е** is still used in most books outside dictionaries and primers.

## Terms
- ****Sentimentalism**** — A literary mode emphasizing emotion, sensibility, and the inner life of ordinary people; Karamzin introduced it to Russia via Sterne’s influence.
- ****Letter ë (yo)**** — A Cyrillic letter introduced by Karamzin post-1795 to mark stressed /jo/; non-mandatory in modern orthography.
- ****Autocracy**** — System of government where the monarch holds absolute power; Karamzin’s *History* served as its ideological "Epic."
- ****Mikhail Speransky**** — Alexander I’s liberal reformer (1772–1839); Karamzin’s *Memoir* attacked his constitutional proposals.
- ****Vasily Tatishchev**** — Earlier Russian historian (1686–1750) whose work Karamzin deemed a rough, uncritical sketch.
- ****Envoy of Europe (*Vestnik Evropy*)**** — Journal edited by Karamzin (1802–1803); its publication clarified his shift to history.
- ****Michael Romanov**** — First Romanov tsar (r. 1613–1645); Karamzin’s *History* terminates at his accession.
- ****Tsarskoye Selo**** — Imperial palace near St. Petersburg where Alexander I read Karamzin’s manuscript in the gardens.

## Debates and open questions
- **Birthplace dispute**: Official version places birth in Mikhailovka near Simbirsk (1766); Orenburg historians argue for 1765 in Orenburg Governorate where his father served.
- **Historical methodology**: Critics charge Karamzin with casting a "false gloss and romantic air" over early annals, akin to Sir Walter Scott’s romantic historiography, sacrificing critical rigor for narrative elegance.
- **Political legacy**: Whether Karamzin’s *Memoir* merely reflected Alexander I’s turn toward conservatism or actively entrenched reactionary ideology for decades remains debated.
- **Literary paternity**: While hailed as "a Russian Sterne," the extent of direct borrowing versus independent adaptation of sentimentalist tropes is discussed.
