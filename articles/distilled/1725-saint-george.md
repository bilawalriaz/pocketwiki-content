# Saint George

## Overview
Saint George (died 23 April 303) was an early Christian martyr of Cappadocian Greek origin, traditionally a Roman soldier and member of the Praetorian Guard under Emperor Diocletian. He was executed for refusing to recant his faith during the Diocletianic Persecution. While his historical existence is widely accepted by scholars, no reliable details of his life survive; the earliest hagiographies date to the 5th century, and the famous dragon-slaying legend appeared only in the 11th century. Venerated as a "Great Martyr" and military saint, he became a central figure of chivalry during the Crusades. His cult uniquely bridges Christian, Muslim, and Druze traditions in the Levant, where he is often identified with the prophetic figure al-Khidr. He is the patron saint of numerous nations and regions, including England, Georgia, Ethiopia, Portugal, and Catalonia.

## Body

### Historical Figure and Early Sources
Almost nothing is historically certain about George beyond his martyrdom. Edward Gibbon’s 18th-century theory identifying him with George of Cappadocia, a 4th-century Arian bishop, is dismissed by scholars (e.g., J.B. Bury) as improbable. The Diocletianic Persecution (303), which targeted Christian soldiers, provides the plausible historical context. The earliest textual evidence is a 5th-century Greek palimpsest identified by the Bollandists; a Coptic manuscript from Qasr Ibrim (dated 350–500) gives a specific biography: his father Gerontius was a pagan Cappadocian official in Nobatia, his mother Polychronia a Christian who secretly baptised him. Eusebius (4th century) mentions a church at Diospolis for an unnamed distinguished man, later associated with George. Pope Gelasius I (494) confirmed his veneration while acknowledging his acts were unknown. The Bollandists’ 17th-century research in *Bibliotheca Hagiographica Graeca* established the baseline for his historicity.

### Hagiographic Traditions: Greek and Latin
Greek tradition places his birth in Cappadocia to noble Christian parents; after his father’s death, his mother returned to Lydda. He became a soldier, was arrested, tortured "at or near Lydda," and beheaded on 23 April 303. A witness converted Empress Alexandra, who was also martyred. Later Greek versions rationalise the persecutor as Diocletian and shift the scene to Nicomedia, though the earliest cult centred on Diospolis. The 6th-century Latin *Passio Sancti Georgii* changes the persecutor to Dacian, "Emperor of the Persians," extends the ordeal to seven years and over twenty tortures, claims 40,900 pagan conversions, and ends with Dacian carried off in a whirlwind of fire. Later Latin versions substitute the persecutor with Emperor Decius or a judge named Dacian under Diocletian.

### The Dragon Legend
The dragon-slaying narrative is absent from early hagiographies. The earliest record is an 11th-century Georgian text, reaching Latin Europe in the 12th century. In Jacobus de Voragine’s *Golden Legend* (13th century), George arrives at Silene in Libya where a dragon demands human sacrifices. He slays the dragon with a lance (later named Ascalon) when the king’s daughter is chosen, refuses treasure, and converts the populace. William Caxton’s 15th-century English translation cemented this version. The lance Ascalon was later used by Winston Churchill as his WWII aircraft call sign. The motif of a horseman spearing evil has deep roots in Christian iconography.

### Greek Folklore and Syncretism
In Greece, George is a folk hero, patron of farmers and shepherds. His feast (23 April/6 May) involves spring agricultural rituals: blessing vines, processions with flowers, and in the Dodecanese/Crete, blessing new wine ("St. George the Drunken"). Legends often involve liberation from Ottoman kidnappers or pirates attacking feast-day celebrants. He is also a protector against vampires (*vrykolakes*); a 1950s exorcism in Argolis converted a Mycenaean tomb into a shrine to him, and on Mykonos, vampires were buried on a St. George islet. Scholars note structural parallels between George killing the dragon and earlier Greek myths: Apollo slaying Python, Heracles killing the Hydra, and Bellerophon slaying the Chimera.

### Muslim and Druze Traditions
In Islamic sources, George (Jirjis/Girgus) is a prophetic figure, a rich merchant who opposed King Dadan of Mosul’s idol of Apollo. He endures torture, is aided by angels, exposes idols as Satan-possessed, and is martyred when God destroys the city with fire. Al-Tabari’s Persian version adds miracles: resurrecting the dead, making dry wood flower, and cosmic darkness at his death. Al-Thaʿlabi places him in Palestine among Jesus’ disciples; he turns dry wood green and is killed/resurrected multiple times. Ibn al-Athir describes his death with crucifixion-like portents (darkness, storms). He is frequently identified with al-Khidr ("the Verdant Prophet"), Elijah, or Mar Elis. The Mosque of Nabi Jurjis in Mosul (restored by Timur, 14th century) was reputed to hold his tomb until its 2014 destruction by ISIL. Wall paintings at Belisırma (1282–1304) depict him as a mounted knight between donors, with Seljuk and Byzantine rulers named in inscriptions. For the Druze, George (and Saint Elias) are paramount venerated figures due to Christian influence; they are seen as warrior saints mirroring Druze militarised society, with churches or *maqams* dedicated to them in mixed villages.

### Veneration History
The cult originated in Diospolis (Lydda), Syria Palaestina. A basilica existed there by the early Muslim conquests; the current church dates to 1872. Veneration spread through Lebanon to the Byzantine Empire and, by the 5th century, to the Western Roman Empire. Theodosius (518–530) first describes Diospolis as a pilgrimage site for relics. By the late 6th century, the centre shifted to Cappadocia. In England, Bede (8th century) mentions him; the *Georgslied* (Old High German, late 9th century) adapts his legend. Alfred the Great’s will references a church at Fordington. George became England’s patron saint in the 14th century, overtaking Edward the Confessor; the 1552 Reformation abolished all other saints' banners. Crusader apparitions (Antioch 1098, Jerusalem 1099) boosted his military profile. The Order of the Garter (1348) and *Golden Legend* (printing press bestseller) codified his chivalric status. The 1415 council made his feast a *festum duplex*. His lack of a specific English shrine allowed widespread local cults, guilds, and church dedications.

### Veneration in the Levant
George is patron of Lebanese, Palestinian, and Syrian Christians. A shrine at Al-Khader (near Beit Jala/Bethlehem), frequented by Christians, Muslims, and Jews since at least 1907, functioned as a "madhouse" where the mentally ill were chained for 40 days. William Dalrymple (1995) found it thriving: Palestinian Christians preferred it to the Holy Sepulchre; Muslims (calling him Al-Khadr) filled the aisles, seeking cures from lamp oil. G.A. Smith (1894) noted Muslims identify George with Elijah and conflate his dragon with the Antichrist (Dajjal) slain by Jesus at the Gate of Lydda, possibly deriving from a bas-relief on the Lydda church. Charles Simon Clermont-Ganneau traced the dragon myth to the pagan hero Perseus (slayer of the sea-monster at Joppa/Apollonia), whose veneration transferred to George at Lydda and spread via Crusaders.

### Feast Days
*   **General Roman Calendar:** 23 April (optional memorial since 1969; formerly Semidouble, Simple, Commemoration).
*   **England:** Solemnity (RC) / Feast (CoE); transferred to Monday after 2nd Sunday of Easter if conflict.
*   **Eastern Orthodox:** 23 April (Julian = 6 May Gregorian); "Great Martyr." If before Easter, celebrated Easter Monday. Additional feasts: 3 Nov (consecration Lydda cathedral, Constantine era), 26 Nov (Kyiv church, c. 1054), 27 Jan (Zakynthos plague deliverance 1689), 10 Nov (torture commemoration).
*   **Coptic Orthodox:** 23 Paremhat (1 May) – "Prince of Martyrs"; 7 Hatour (17 Nov) – church consecration.
*   **Bulgaria/Serbia/Bosnia:** 6 May (Đurđevdan/Гергьовден); public holiday in Bulgaria; common *slava* for Serbs.
*   **India (Syro-Malabar/Malankara):** 27 April–14 May pilgrimage at Edathua; main feast 7 May; relics brought from Antioch (900) to Kerala (1912).
*   **Catholic supplementary:** 24 April (Poland), 7 May (martyrdom Lydda), 20 June (relics translation to Anchin Abbey).

### Patronages
*   **England:** Cross forms national flag (St George’s Cross), component of Union Jack. Patron of royal family by 14th century.
*   **Georgia:** Not named after him (back-formation), but 365 Orthodox churches bear his name (one per day); legend says his 365 pieces spread across country.
*   **Ethiopia:** Patron of nation and Orthodox Church; dragon-slaying is dominant icon subject.
*   **Gozo (Malta):** Protector of island and patron of Victoria; alleged apparition with St. Paul and St. Agatha during battle vs Moors.
*   **Portugal:** Devotion from 12th century; Nuno Álvares Pereira credited 1385 Aljubarrota victory to him. John I made him patron (1357–1433); image carried in Corpus Christi; flag used by troops 15th century. "Portugal and Saint George" remains Army battle cry; "Saint George" for Navy.
*   **Brazil:** Unofficial patron of Rio de Janeiro and São Jorge dos Ilhéus; patron of Scouts and Army Cavalry; 2019 made official patron of Rio de Janeiro state. Syncretised with orisha Ogun in Umbanda; uniquely associated with the Moon (spots = saint, horse, sword).
*   **Aragon (Spain):** Patron since Pedro I’s 1096 Battle of Alcoraz victory (legend: George appeared leading cavalry). Cross of Alcoraz on provincial flags. Celebrated 23 April (Aragon Day). Cervantes references St. George jousting festivals in Zaragoza.
*   **Catalonia:** Patron since 1456 (Catalan Courts). Legend localised to Montblanc. Rose-giving tradition. *Creu de Sant Jordi* high civil award; Sant Jordi Awards since 1957.
*   **Crown of Aragon territories** (Valencia, Balearics, Malta, Sicily, Sardinia): Shared veneration via common history.
*   **Order of St. George (Habsburg-Lorraine):** Founded 1469 by Frederick III/Paul II; promoted by Maximilian I; dissolved by Nazis; reactivated post-1989.

### Arms and Flag
Attributed arms (red cross on white field) emerged in 15th-century classical heraldry, likely originating in Genoa, which adopted George as patron and this flag in the 12th century. Genovese annals record a *vexillum beati Georgii* (red flag with George/dragon) in 1198; illumination in 1227. The plain red cross (*insignia cruxata comunis Janue*) used alongside from 1218; principal war flag showed the saint, plain cross used 1240s. Edward III used red-on-white cross on Royal Standard (1348). Thomas Fuller (1647) called the plain Greek cross touching field edges "the mother of all the others."

### Iconography
Early icons/mosaics/frescos depict him in contemporary armour (gilded/silver) as a Roman soldier. Post-Fall of Constantinople (1453) and Crusades association, he is often mounted on a white horse (e.g., 2003 Vatican stamp). Eastern Orthodoxy permits white horse (Russian icon, British Museum). Unique full-life cycle icons commissioned 2012 at Mieh Mieh, Lebanon (Melkite). Often paired with Saint Demetrius (soldier saint); together they evoke archangels Michael/Gabriel. Distinction: George rides white horse, spears dragon; Demetrius rides red (often bituminised to black) horse, spears human figure (Maximian).

## Terms
- ****Diocletianic Persecution**** — The final and most severe Roman imperial persecution of Christians (303–311), targeting clergy and Christian soldiers, under Emperor Diocletian.
- ****Hagiography**** — The writing of saints' lives; the body of literature describing the lives and miracles of saints.
- ****Palimpsest**** — A manuscript page from which the text has been scraped off and reused; the 5th-century Greek hagiography of George survives only as a palimpsest.
- ****Bollandists**** — A society of Jesuit scholars (founded 17th century) dedicated to the critical study and publication of hagiographical texts (*Acta Sanctorum*).
- ****Golden Legend** (*Legenda Aurea*)** — A 13th-century collection of hagiographies by Jacobus de Voragine, Archbishop of Genoa; the primary vehicle for the dragon legend in medieval Europe.
- ****Al-Khidr**** — "The Verdant/Green One"; a Quranic figure associated with immortality and hidden knowledge, widely identified with Saint George in Muslim, Christian, and Druze folk piety in the Levant.
- ****Megalomartyr**** — A title for prominent early martyrs (Greek: *megalomartys*, "great martyr"); George is a primary example in Eastern Orthodoxy.
- ****Slava**** — A Serbian Orthodox tradition of celebrating a family's patron saint; George (Đurđevdan, 6 May) is among the most common.
- ****Syncretism**** — The blending of different religious beliefs; George’s veneration shows Christian/Islamic/Druze syncretism (e.g., identification with al-Khidr/Elijah, Umbanda/Ogun in Brazil).
- ****Attributed Arms**** — Coats of arms assigned retrospectively to historical or legendary figures who predate heraldry (c. 12th century); George’s red cross on white is a prime example.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Historicity vs. Legend:** While most scholars accept a historical martyr named George died at Lydda c. 303, the *Acta Sanctorum* and modern historians (e.g., Donald Attwater) stress that "no historical particulars of his life have survived"; the soldier/guard biography is a later hagiographic construction.
*   **Gibbon’s Identification:** Edward Gibbon’s claim that the saint is a transfigured memory of the Arian bishop George of Cappadocia (d. 361) is universally rejected by editors (Bury) and modern scholarship as chronologically and biographically impossible.
*   **Dragon Legend Origins:** The dragon narrative appears only in the 11th century (Georgian source). Debate continues whether it derives from: (a) Christianisation of the Perseus/Andromeda myth at Joppa/Lydda (Clermont-Ganneau); (b) allegorical reading of "crushing the dragon" (Satan) in Psalms/Revelation; (c) confusion with St. Theodore Tiro (also a dragon-slayer); or (d) independent folklore attachment.
*   **Lydda vs. Nicomedia:** Earliest cult and pilgrimage (Theodosius, 518–530) centre on Diospolis (Lydda). Later Greek *Passio* shifts martyrdom to Nicomedia under Diocletian. Scholars view the Nicomedia setting as secondary, intended to align him with the "Great Persecution" narrative.
*   **Relic Authenticity:** Multiple sites claim relics (Lydda, Antioch, Mosul, Kerala, Venice, etc.). The 1872 Lydda church houses a sarcophagus *traditionally believed* to contain them; relics translated to Kerala in 1912 from Antioch (where they arrived from Mardin in 900). No scientific authentication exists.
*   **Al-Khidr Conflation:** The identification of George with al-Khidr is pervasive in Levantine folk religion but theologically complex: al-Khidr is a Quranic immortal servant of God (Surah 18), while George is a 4th-century martyr. The mechanism and timeline of this fusion remain debated.
*   **England’s Patronage Shift:** The process by which George displaced Edward the Confessor as England’s patron (14th century) is attributed to Edward III’s Order of the Garter (1348) and the saint’s "non-localised" nature allowing broad adoption, but the precise political and cultural drivers are still analysed.
