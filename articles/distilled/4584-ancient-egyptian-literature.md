# Ancient Egyptian literature

## Overview
Ancient Egyptian literature, written in the Egyptian language from the pharaonic period until Roman domination, is one of the world's oldest literary corpora alongside Sumerian literature. It evolved from practical and religious texts into a diverse body of work encompassing narratives, teachings, hymns, and letters, reflecting the social, religious, and educational functions of writing in a society where literacy was largely monopolized by a scribal elite.

## Timeline
- **c. 3100 BC** — Hieroglyphs and hieratic scripts are well-established in the late Predynastic/Early Dynastic Period.
- **26th–22nd century BC** — Old Kingdom: Literary works include funerary texts, hymns, and commemorative autobiographies.
- **21st–17th century BC** — Middle Kingdom: Narrative literature is created; Middle Egyptian becomes the classical language.
- **16th–11th century BC** — New Kingdom: Middle Egyptian is canonized; Late Egyptian appears in writing; commemorative graffiti flourishes.
- **4th century BC–1st century BC** — Ptolemaic Period: Prophetic texts are revived; Demotic script is used for everyday writing.
- **394 AD** — The last known hieroglyphic inscription is carved at the Temple of Philae.
- **1799** — Discovery of the Rosetta Stone provides the key to decipherment.
- **1822–1832** — Jean-François Champollion and Thomas Young decipher hieroglyphs and Demotic.

## Body

### Scripts, Media, and Languages
Ancient Egyptians used three main scripts: **hieroglyphs** (monumental and sacred), **hieratic** (cursive for everyday use), and later **Demotic** (a more cursive form for administrative texts). The language evolved from Old Egyptian to Middle Egyptian (the classical language of the Middle Kingdom), then to Late Egyptian and Demotic. Literature was preserved on papyrus, ostraca (potsherds), stone, and wood. Preservation is uneven; texts survive best from dry desert settlements and tombs, not the moist Nile floodplain.

### Social, Religious, and Educational Functions
Literature was primarily created and maintained by a literate scribal class, which comprised an estimated 1–15% of the population. Writing served state administration, religious purposes (e.g., funerary texts to protect souls), and education. Scribal training involved copying classic works like the *Story of Sinuhe* to instill ethical values. Literature could also be performed orally for audiences, including non-literate ones.

### Dating, Setting, and Authorship
Most literary texts are pseudonymous, falsely attributed to historical figures like kings or viziers. Dating is difficult due to stylistic conventions and authors copying older archetypes. Fictional works were often set in remote historical or mythical times. True authorship was rarely acknowledged, except in some private letters and late-period texts.

### Literary Genres
Egyptologists categorize the literature into several genres:
*   **Instructions and Teachings (sebayt):** Didactic wisdom literature, such as the *Maxims of Ptahhotep* and *Instructions of Amenemhat*, stressing social conformity.
*   **Narrative Tales:** Fictional stories like the *Story of Sinuhe* and *Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor*, which often incorporated other genres and themes.
*   **Laments, Discourses, and Prophecies:** Reflective works like the *Admonitions of Ipuwer* and *Prophecy of Neferti*, often describing social chaos and renewal.
*   **Poems, Songs, and Hymns:** Included funerary texts (e.g., Pyramid Texts), royal victory hymns, and love songs.
*   **Letters and Epistles:** Private correspondence, model letters for schoolboys, and official decrees.
*   **Biographical and Autobiographical Texts:** Commemorative inscriptions on tomb stelae celebrating the careers of officials.
*   **Graffiti:** Formulaic messages left by scribes at sacred sites during the New Kingdom.

## Terms
*   **Hieroglyphs:** The formal, pictorial script used for monuments and sacred texts.
*   **Hieratic:** A cursive script derived from hieroglyphs, used for everyday writing on papyrus.
*   **Demotic:** A later, more cursive script used for administrative and private documents.
*   **Ostraca:** Limestone flakes or ceramic potsherds used as a cheaper writing surface for short texts.
*   **Papyrus:** A writing material made from the pith of the Cyperus papyrus plant.
*   **Sebayt:** The ancient Egyptian term for the "teaching" or "instruction" literary genre.
*   **Scribe:** A literate official who served in government, religion, and as the keeper of literature.
*   **Pseudonymous:** The practice of falsely attributing a text to a famous historical figure.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   There is no full consensus on the degree to which ancient Egyptian literature depended on the sociopolitical order of the royal courts.
*   Scholars debate whether true biographies or autobiographies existed in the ancient world, as the modern concept of individuality may not apply.
*   The percentage of literacy in ancient Egyptian society is uncertain, estimated between 1% and 15%.
*   Modern scholarly approach has shifted from using literature as a direct historical source to a multifaceted analysis considering its cultural and social context.
