# Nitrogen

## Overview

Nitrogen (N, atomic number 7) is a nonmetal and the lightest member of group 15 (the pnictogens), constituting roughly 78% of Earth's atmosphere as the diatomic gas N₂. Its chemistry is dominated by the exceptionally strong N≡N triple bond (945 kJ/mol), which makes elemental nitrogen inert but nitrogen compounds rich in energy—key to fertilizers, explosives, and biological molecules. The element is essential to life, forming the backbone of amino acids, nucleic acids, and ATP, and its global movement is described by the nitrogen cycle. Industrially, nitrogen fixation via the Haber-Bosch process sustains half of global food production, while excess reactive nitrogen drives environmental eutrophication and ozone depletion.

## Body

### Discovery and Naming

Nitrogen was discovered in 1772 by Daniel Rutherford, who called it "noxious air" because it extinguished flames and killed animals. Around the same time, Carl Wilhelm Scheele, Henry Cavendish, and Joseph Priestley studied it as "burnt air" or "phlogisticated air." Antoine Lavoisier named it "azote" (from Greek *azōtikos*, "no life") due to its asphyxiant properties. The modern name "nitrogen" was coined in 1790 by Jean-Antoine Chaptal, derived from *nitre* (potassium nitrate) and the suffix *-gène* ("producing"), reflecting its presence in nitric acid.

### Atomic and Physical Properties

A nitrogen atom has seven electrons with configuration 1s²2s²2p³, giving five valence electrons (three unpaired in the 2p orbitals). It has high electronegativity (3.04 on the Pauling scale) and a small covalent radius (71 pm). The N≡N triple bond in dinitrogen is the second-strongest diatomic bond after CO, making N₂ chemically inert at room temperature. Elemental nitrogen exists as colorless, odorless N₂ gas, liquefying at 77 K and freezing at 63 K. Liquid nitrogen is a widely used cryogen.

### Isotopes

Nitrogen has two stable isotopes: ¹⁴N (99.634%) and ¹⁵N (0.366%), giving an atomic weight of ~14.007 u. Both are produced in stellar CNO cycles, with ¹⁴N being more abundant due to its role as the rate-limiting step in proton capture. The ¹⁵N:¹⁴N ratio (δ¹⁵N) is used in geochemistry, hydrology, and paleoclimatology. ¹⁵N is valuable in NMR spectroscopy due to its spin-½ nucleus, though its low natural abundance and gyromagnetic ratio limit sensitivity. The synthetic isotope ¹³N (half-life 10 min) is used in PET imaging.

### Allotropes

Besides molecular N₂, nitrogen has highly reactive atomic (monatomic) allotropes—"active nitrogen"—produced by electrical discharge. These triradicals react vigorously with most elements. Other neutral allotropes (e.g., N₆, synthesized in 2025) are typically unstable and exist only under extreme conditions, with potential applications as high-energy-density materials.

### Chemistry and Compounds

#### Dinitrogen Complexes

Dinitrogen can coordinate to transition metals in five modes, most commonly end-on (η¹) or bridging (μ-bis-η¹). These complexes model how N₂ binds in nitrogenase enzymes and the Haber-Bosch catalyst, crucial for biological and industrial nitrogen fixation.

#### Nitrides, Azides, and Nitrido Complexes

Nitrogen forms binary compounds (nitrides) with nearly all elements except helium and neon. These range from ionic (e.g., Li₃N, Ca₃N₂) to covalent (e.g., Si₃N₄) to metallic interstitial nitrides (e.g., TiN), which are hard, inert, and conductive. The azide anion (N₃⁻) is linear and isoelectronic with CO₂ and cyanate. Nitrido complexes feature terminal {≡N}³⁻ groups and are strong π-donor ligands.

#### Hydrides

Ammonia (NH₃) is the most important nitrogen hydride, produced industrially via the Haber-Bosch process. It is a weak base (pKb 4.74) with strong hydrogen bonding, giving it unusually high melting and boiling points for its size. Other hydrides include hydrazine (N₂H₄), a stable but highly exothermic reducing agent and rocket fuel, and hydrogen azide (HN₃), a toxic, explosive compound.

#### Oxides and Oxoacids

Nitrogen forms nine molecular oxides (N₂O, NO, N₂O₃, NO₂, N₂O₄, N₂O₅, etc.), all thermally unstable. Nitric acid (HNO₃) is the most stable oxoacid and a major industrial chemical. Its salts (nitrates) are key fertilizers but also pollutants driving eutrophication. Nitrous acid (HNO₂) and hyponitrous acid (H₂N₂O₂) are less stable intermediates in the nitrogen cycle.

#### Organic Nitrogen Compounds

Nitrogen is central to organic chemistry, appearing in amines, amides, nitriles, azides, azo compounds, and heterocycles. C–N bonds are polarized toward nitrogen, and the lone pair often confers basicity (except when delocalized, as in amides or pyrrole). The Kjeldahl method quantifies nitrogen content in organic substances.

### Occurrence and the Nitrogen Cycle

Nitrogen is the most abundant pure element in Earth's crust (78.1% of the atmosphere by volume), but only ~19 ppm by mass in the crust itself. The nitrogen cycle describes its movement from atmosphere to biosphere and back: atmospheric N₂ is fixed by lightning or diazotrophic bacteria (via nitrogenase enzymes) into ammonia, which plants assimilate into proteins. Animals consume plants, excrete nitrogenous waste (urea, uric acid), and decompose back to N₂ via denitrification. Industrial fixation now rivals biological fixation in magnitude.

### Production

Elemental nitrogen is produced industrially by fractional distillation of liquid air or by pressure swing adsorption (PSA) from compressed air. In laboratories, it is generated by reacting ammonium chloride with sodium nitrite or by decomposing sodium azide. Liquid nitrogen is produced by cryogenic distillation and is used extensively as a coolant.

### Applications

Two-thirds of industrial nitrogen is sold as gas, one-third as liquid. Gaseous nitrogen is used as an inert atmosphere in food packaging, electronics manufacturing, fire suppression, and tire inflation. Liquid nitrogen is used in cryopreservation, cryotherapy, cold traps, and materials testing. Nitrogen gas also inflates airbags via sodium azide decomposition.

### Safety

Gaseous nitrogen is non-toxic but can cause asphyxiation by displacing oxygen in enclosed spaces. At high partial pressures (>4 bar), it causes nitrogen narcosis. Rapid decompression can lead to decompression sickness ("the bends") from nitrogen bubble formation. Liquid nitrogen causes severe cold burns and can generate explosive forces if vaporized in confined spaces. Oxygen deficiency monitors are standard safety equipment in facilities using large quantities of nitrogen.

## Terms

- **Pnictogen**: Group 15 elements (N, P, As, Sb, Bi), named from Greek *pnigein* ("to choke"), referring to nitrogen's asphyxiant properties.
- **Nitrogen fixation**: Conversion of atmospheric N₂ into ammonia or related compounds usable by organisms; occurs biologically (via nitrogenase), by lightning, or industrially (Haber-Bosch).
- **Haber-Bosch process**: Industrial synthesis of ammonia from N₂ and H₂ at high pressure (150–300 atm) and temperature (~500°C) using an iron catalyst.
- **Cryogen**: A substance at extremely low temperatures (below −150°C); liquid nitrogen is a common cryogen.
- **Dinitrogen (N₂)**: The diatomic molecular form of nitrogen, comprising ~78% of Earth's atmosphere; characterized by a strong N≡N triple bond.
- **Azide (N₃⁻)**: A linear anion with three nitrogen atoms; salts like NaN₃ are used in airbag inflators.
- **Nitrate (NO₃⁻)**: A trigonal planar anion; nitrates are key fertilizers but contribute to eutrophication when over-applied.
- **Nitrogen cycle**: The biogeochemical cycling of nitrogen through atmosphere, soil, organisms, and water via fixation, assimilation, ammonification, and denitrification.
- **Pressure swing adsorption (PSA)**: A technology for separating nitrogen from air by adsorbing other components (O₂, CO₂) under pressure, releasing purified N₂.
- **Leidenfrost effect**: The phenomenon where a liquid near a surface much hotter than its boiling point forms an insulating vapor layer, delaying boiling; provides brief protection from liquid nitrogen burns.

## Debates and Open Questions

- **Hexanitrogen stability**: The 2025 synthesis of N₆ raised questions about its potential as a high-energy-density material, but its practical applications remain speculative.
- **Nitrogen's role in astrobiology**: The presence of solid nitrogen on Pluto and Triton raises questions about its role in prebiotic chemistry on outer Solar System bodies.
- **Eutrophication thresholds**: The precise relationship between fertilizer application rates, nitrogen leaching, and marine dead zone formation remains debated, particularly under climate change scenarios.
- **¹⁵N fractionation mechanisms**: While δ¹⁵N ratios are widely used as proxies in paleoclimatology, the exact controls on isotopic fractionation during denitrification and assimilation are still being refined.
