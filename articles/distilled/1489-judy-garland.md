# Judy Garland

## Overview
Judy Garland (1922–1969) was an American actress, singer, and vaudevillian whose contralto voice and emotional intensity made her a defining star of Hollywood’s Golden Age. She achieved global fame as Dorothy Gale in *The Wizard of Oz* (1939), introducing her signature song "Over the Rainbow," and anchored MGM musicals like *Meet Me in St. Louis* (1944) and *Easter Parade* (1948). Her career was shaped by a studio system that controlled her diet, sleep, and schedule from adolescence, prescribing amphetamines and barbiturates that seeded a lifelong substance dependency. Despite professional triumphs—including a historic Carnegie Hall concert (1961) and an Oscar-nominated dramatic turn in *A Star Is Born* (1954)—she battled financial ruin, five marriages, and deteriorating health. She died of an accidental barbiturate overdose at 47. Her legacy endures as a "triple threat" performer whose vulnerability and vocal power created a template for modern stardom.

## Timeline
- **June 10, 1922** — Born Frances Ethel Gumm in Grand Rapids, Minnesota, to vaudevillian parents.
- **1924** — Stage debut at age two singing "Jingle Bells" with sisters as The Gumm Sisters.
- **1935** — Signed by MGM at age 13; father Francis Gumm dies of meningitis shortly after.
- **1939** — Stars as Dorothy in *The Wizard of Oz*; receives Academy Juvenile Award.
- **1944** — *Meet Me in St. Louis* released; introduces standards "The Trolley Song" and "Have Yourself a Merry Little Christmas"; marries director Vincente Minnelli (1945).
- **1950** — Suspended and eventually released by MGM after years of absenteeism and health crises.
- **1951** — Triumphant comeback at London Palladium and New York’s Palace Theatre; wins Special Tony Award.
- **1954** — Stars in *A Star Is Born* (Warner Bros.); nominated for Best Actress Oscar; loses to Grace Kelly.
- **1961** — *Judy at Carnegie Hall* live album wins Grammy Album of the Year (first woman to win); stars in *Judgment at Nuremberg* (Supporting Actress nomination).
- **1963–64** — Hosts *The Judy Garland Show* on CBS; canceled after one season (26 episodes).
- **June 22, 1969** — Dies in London at 47 from accidental barbiturate overdose.

## Body

### Early Life and Vaudeville Roots
Garland was born into a performing family; her parents operated a movie theater featuring vaudeville acts in Grand Rapids, Minnesota. After rumors regarding her father’s sexuality prompted a move to Lancaster, California, in 1926, the Gumm Sisters (Frances and her two elder sisters) toured the vaudeville circuit. In 1934, comedian George Jessel suggested a name change after "Gumm" drew audience laughter; the trio became the Garland Sisters, and Frances adopted "Judy," inspired by a Hoagy Carmichael song. The act dissolved in 1935 when sister Suzanne married.

### The MGM Machine (1935–1950)
Louis B. Mayer signed Garland without a screen test, but the studio struggled to categorize the 13-year-old: too old for child roles, too young for adult leads, and physically mismatched to the era’s glamour ideal. Mayer called her "little hunchback"; she was fitted with dental caps and nose discs, fed chicken soup and coffee to suppress weight, and paired with Mickey Rooney in profitable "backyard musicals." Garland later stated the studio prescribed amphetamines for energy and barbiturates for sleep, initiating her addiction; Rooney denied MGM’s responsibility. Her breakthrough came with *The Wizard of Oz* (1939), though she was not the first choice (Shirley Temple was pursued). The film’s success made her a top asset, but the grueling pace continued. She matured on screen in *Meet Me in St. Louis* (1944), directed by Vincente Minnelli, who refined her image via makeup artist Dorothy Ponedel. They married in 1945; daughter Liza was born in 1946.

By the late 1940s, Garland’s mental and physical health collapsed. She suffered a nervous breakdown during *The Pirate* (1947), attempted suicide, and underwent electroconvulsive therapy. Though *Easter Parade* (1948) with Fred Astaire was a blockbuster, her unreliability—fueled by pills, alcohol, and migraines—led to suspensions from *The Barkleys of Broadway* and *Annie Get Your Gun*. She completed a final MGM film, *Summer Stock* (1950), but was fired from *Royal Wedding* and parted with the studio after 15 years.

### Comeback and *A Star Is Born* (1950–1954)
Bing Crosby revived Garland’s career via his radio show in 1950, leading to a record-breaking 1951 engagement at the London Palladium ("Judy Garland was reborn") and a historic run at New York’s Palace Theatre, earning a Special Tony Award. She married tour manager Sidney Luft in 1952; they had two children, Lorna (1952) and Joey (1955). Luft produced *A Star Is Born* (1954) through their company Transcona Enterprises. Garland’s performance as Vicki Lester was hailed as "the greatest one-woman show in modern movie history" (*Time*), but Warner Bros. cut 30 minutes post-premiere, and the film lost money despite grossing $6 million. She lost the Oscar to Grace Kelly—a result Groucho Marx called "the biggest robbery since Brinks"—but won a Golden Globe.

### Television, Carnegie Hall, and Decline (1955–1969)
Garland headlined TV specials (notably the 1955 *Ford Star Jubilee*, CBS’s first full-color broadcast) and a record-setting 1956 Las Vegas engagement. In 1959, acute hepatitis nearly killed her; doctors predicted five years to live and permanent vocal loss. She recovered to deliver the legendary April 23, 1961, Carnegie Hall concert. The double album *Judy at Carnegie Hall* spent 13 weeks at #1 on *Billboard*, won four Grammys (including Album of the Year), and is often cited as "the greatest night in show business history."

CBS offered $24 million for a weekly series, *The Judy Garland Show* (1963–64), which garnered critical praise and four Emmy nominations but was canceled after one season due to a timeslot opposite *Bonanza*. Her finances collapsed: IRS liens seized her home and earnings (she owed ~$500,000, equivalent to $3.7M today), exacerbated by embezzlement from agents Freddie Fields and David Begelman. Final film roles included *Judgment at Nuremberg* (1961), *Gay Purr-ee* (1962), and *I Could Go On Singing* (1963). A disastrous 1964 Australia tour and dismissal from *Valley of the Dolls* (1967) marked her professional nadir. She married fifth husband Mickey Deans in March 1969; three months later, he found her dead in London. The coroner ruled "incautious self-overdosage" of barbiturates (equivalent to ten 1.5-grain Seconal capsules), accidental, with no suicide intent. Her estate was valued at only $40,000.

### Artistry and Voice
Critics describe Garland’s contralto as brassy, resonant, and tremulous, with a paradoxical blend of fragility and power. Though her range was modest, she shifted timbres effortlessly—from tender ballads to belted numbers likened to Al Jolson and Ethel Merman. Jonathan Riggs noted her "pronounced enunciation and projection of vulnerability"; Michael Bronski heard "a hurt in her voice and an immediacy... that gave the impression that it was her pain." Maria Callas called it "the most superb voice she had ever heard"; Bing Crosby said "no other singer could be compared to her." Fred Astaire deemed her "the greatest entertainer who ever lived." Garland’s live performances were marked by intense audience communion: "A really great reception makes me feel like I have a great big warm heating pad all over me."

### Political Activism
A lifelong Democrat, Garland supported FDR, Adlai Stevenson, JFK, RFK, and Henry Wallace. In 1947, she joined the Committee for the First Amendment (with Bogart, Bacall, Hepburn, Kelly, Wilder) defending the Hollywood Ten against HUAC, recording the *Hollywood Fights Back* broadcast. She escorted Brigadier General Benjamin O. Davis Sr. (first Black U.S. general) to a 1944 reception. She marched in the 1963 March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom and held a press conference protesting the 16th Street Baptist Church bombing with daughter Liza Minnelli.

## Terms
- ****Vaudeville**** — A theatrical genre of variety entertainment popular in the U.S. from the 1880s–1930s, featuring unrelated acts (song, dance, comedy); Garland’s family performed in this circuit.
- ****Contralto**** — The lowest female singing voice type; Garland’s "deep, velvety" contralto was noted for its power and emotional weight.
- ****Academy Juvenile Award**** — A special honorary Oscar (1934–1960) for performers under 18; Garland received it in 1939 for *The Wizard of Oz* and *Babes in Arms*—only the fourth recipient.
- ****Backyard Musicals**** — Low-budget MGM series starring Garland and Mickey Rooney as teens putting on shows; formulaic, profitable, and grueling to produce.
- ****Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT)**** — A psychiatric treatment inducing seizures via electric current; Garland underwent it in 1949 for depression during *Annie Get Your Gun*.
- ****Transcona Enterprises**** — Garland and Sidney Luft’s production company, formed to produce *A Star Is Born* (1954); dissolved after the film’s financial loss.
- ****Grammy Album of the Year**** — The recording industry’s top honor; *Judy at Carnegie Hall* (1961) made Garland the first female winner.
- ****House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC)**** — A U.S. House committee (1938–1975) investigating alleged communist influence; Garland publicly opposed its tactics in 1947.
- ****Triple Threat**** — Industry term for a performer equally skilled in singing, acting, and dancing; critics consistently apply this to Garland.
- ****Barbiturates**** — Central nervous system depressants (sedatives); prescribed by MGM for sleep, they became the core of Garland’s addiction and cause of death.

## Debates and open questions
- **Studio culpability for addiction**: Garland asserted MGM systematically drugged young performers; Mickey Rooney and some historians counter that the studio did not sanction it and Garland made personal choices. The debate centers on institutional responsibility vs. individual agency.
- **Suicide vs. accident at death**: The 1969 coroner ruled "incautious self-overdosage" (accidental), citing a half-empty prescription bottle and no suicide note. However, her history of attempts (1947, 1950, 1963) and the lethal blood level (ten Seconal capsules) fuel speculation about intent.
- **Cirrhosis and alcoholism**: A British specialist at autopsy claimed cirrhosis from alcoholism; a second autopsy found no evidence. The discrepancy remains unresolved in biographies.
- **Financial collapse causes**: Sources cite IRS debts (unpaid 1951–52 taxes), *A Star Is Born* losses, agent embezzlement (Fields, Begelman), and Garland’s generosity. The relative weight of mismanagement vs. personal spending is debated.
- **Legacy as "tragic figure" vs. resilient artist**: Garland and daughter Lorna Luft rejected the "tragic" label, emphasizing her humor, warmth, and triumphs. Critics like Camille Paglia argue her art *required* the chaos; others (e.g., William Ruhlmann) warn the tragedy narrative obscures her multimedia accomplishments.
