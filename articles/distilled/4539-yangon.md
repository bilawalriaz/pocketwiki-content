# Yangon

## Overview
Yangon (formerly Rangoon) is Myanmar's largest city and its most important commercial, cultural, and historical center. Once the national capital until 2005, it remains the country's primary economic hub and is renowned for its remarkably intact colonial-era urban core and sacred Buddhist pagodas. The city's development has been shaped by British colonial rule, post-independence nation-building, military governance, and recent political upheaval.

## Timeline
- **c. 1028–1043** — Founded as Dagon by the Mon people.
- **1460** — Queen Shin Saw Pu builds a palace next to Shwedagon Pagoda.
- **1755** — King Alaungpaya captures Dagon and renames it Yangon.
- **1824–26** — British capture Yangon during the First Anglo-Burmese War but return it after the war.
- **1852** — British capture Yangon and Lower Burma in the Second Anglo-Burmese War, making it their capital.
- **1948** — Yangon becomes the capital of independent Burma.
- **1988** — The 8888 Uprising sees mass protests and hundreds of deaths in the city.
- **1989** — Military junta changes the city's English name to "Yangon."
- **2005** — Military government relocates administrative capital to Naypyidaw.
- **2007** — Saffron Revolution protests are violently suppressed in Yangon.
- **2008** — Cyclone Nargis destroys three-quarters of the city's industrial infrastructure.
- **2021** — Mass protests against the military coup occur in the city.

## Body

### Etymology and History
The name "Yangon" derives from Burmese words meaning "End of Strife." The city was founded as the Mon settlement of Dagon in the early 11th century, becoming a pilgrimage town centered on the Shwedagon Pagoda. In 1755, King Alaungpaya expanded and renamed it Yangon. The British captured it in 1852, transforming it into the commercial and political hub of colonial Burma. They rebuilt the city on a grid plan, and by the early 20th century, its infrastructure was on par with London. Yangon became the capital of independent Burma in 1948 and remained so until the military government moved administrative functions to Naypyidaw in 2005.

### Urban Landscape and Architecture
Yangon's downtown core is famed for having the largest collection of colonial-era buildings in Southeast Asia, centered around the ancient Sule Pagoda. The city's skyline is uniquely low-rise due to a rule prohibiting buildings from exceeding 75% of the height of the Shwedagon Pagoda. A hallmark of residential architecture is the eight-story apartment building, built without elevators to avoid cost. The city has expanded dramatically through satellite towns, many of which lack basic infrastructure. A 1996 heritage list and a 2012 demolition moratorium aim to protect historic structures.

### Demographics and Society
Yangon is Myanmar's most populous and ethnically diverse city. While once majority Indian/South Asian pre-WWII, it is now predominantly Bamar, with significant Indian, Chinese, Karen, and Rakhine communities. Burmese is the main language, with English widely used by the educated class. The city is the national hub for media, all of which is state-controlled and censored. It is also the center of the country's film, music, and publishing industries.

### Economy and Infrastructure
Yangon represents about one-fifth of Myanmar's national economy. It is the country's main trading hub, with Thilawa Port handling most legal imports/exports. Manufacturing, especially garments, is a major employer, though factories suffer from chronic power shortages. Tourism is a key foreign currency source but is limited by modest hotel infrastructure. The city's transport network includes Yangon International Airport, a central railway station, and an extensive but crowded bus system. Roads are often in poor condition, and car ownership is prohibitively expensive for most residents due to import restrictions.

### Governance and Recent Challenges
Administered by the Yangon City Development Committee (YCDC), the city is divided into 33 townships. Since independence, successive governments have built satellite towns to accommodate population growth. Yangon has been the epicenter of major pro-democracy protests in 1974, 1988, 2007, and 2021, often met with violent state crackdowns. The 2020s have been marked by the economic and social impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic and the 2021 military coup, which triggered further protests and lockdowns.

## Terms
- **Satellite towns**: New residential areas built on the outskirts of Yangon to accommodate population growth, often lacking basic services.
- **Yangon City Development Committee (YCDC)**: The municipal body responsible for administering and planning the city.
- **Shwedagon Pagoda**: Myanmar's most sacred Buddhist pagoda, a central landmark in Yangon.
- **8888 Uprising**: A series of nationwide pro-democracy protests in August 1988, violently suppressed by the military.
- **Saffron Revolution**: The 2007 pro-democracy protests led by Buddhist monks, which were also violently suppressed.
- **Naypyidaw**: The purpose-built administrative capital of Myanmar, to which government functions were moved from Yangon in 2005.
- **Yangon City Heritage List**: A 1996 list of historic buildings in Yangon that cannot be modified or demolished without approval.

## Debates and Open questions
- The 1989 change of the city's English name from "Rangoon" to "Yangon" by the military junta remains contested by many Burmese and international entities, including the BBC and the UK and US governments.
- The long-term impact of the 2021 coup and subsequent protests on Yangon's economy, social fabric, and urban development is ongoing and uncertain.
- The effectiveness of heritage conservation efforts, like the 2012 demolition moratorium, in protecting Yangon's colonial architecture amidst development pressures is a subject of local and international concern.
