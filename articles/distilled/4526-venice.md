# Venice

## Overview
Venice is a historic coastal city in northeastern Italy, built on 126 islands within a shallow lagoon. It was the capital of the powerful Republic of Venice for nearly a millennium (810–1797), a major maritime and financial power during the Middle Ages and Renaissance. Today, it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and major tourist destination facing significant challenges from overtourism, flooding, and population decline.

## Timeline
- **c. 568** — The *tribuni maiores* form the first central governing committee of the lagoon islands.
- **697** — Paolo Lucio Anafesto is traditionally elected as the first Doge of Venice.
- **810** — Venice becomes the capital of the Republic of Venice after the seat moves from Malamocco to Rialto.
- **1204** — Venice leads the Fourth Crusade, resulting in the sack of Constantinople and the establishment of the Latin Empire.
- **1497–1499** — Vasco da Gama's voyage to India opens a sea route that undermines Venice's trade monopoly.
- **1797** — Napoleon Bonaparte conquers Venice, ending the Republic's independence.
- **1866** — Venice becomes part of the Kingdom of Italy.
- **1987** — Venice and its lagoon are inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **2019** — The MOSE flood barrier system is activated for the first time.
- **2024** — A €5 access fee for day-trippers is implemented on peak visitor days.

## Body

### Origins and Rise to Power
Venice was founded by refugees fleeing Germanic and Hun invasions in the 5th century. Its strategic position in the lagoon provided isolation and growing autonomy from the Byzantine Empire. The city elected its first independent leader, a doge, in 726. After resisting a siege by Charlemagne's son Pepin in 810, Venice was recognized as Byzantine territory with trading rights. The acquisition of St. Mark's relics in 828 boosted its prestige.

### Maritime Empire and Golden Age
From the 9th to 12th centuries, Venice developed into a dominant maritime republic (*thalassocracy*). It controlled trade routes, eliminated pirates, and expanded its territory along the Adriatic and in the Mediterranean, including Crete and Cyprus. The Fourth Crusade (1204) was a decisive turning point, allowing Venice to seize vast Byzantine plunder and establish a sphere of influence. By the late 13th century, Venice was Europe's most prosperous city, with a powerful navy and a unique republican government led by a Doge and an oligarchic Great Council.

### Decline and Modern Era
Venice's long decline began in the 15th century due to Ottoman wars and the loss of its trade monopoly after Portuguese voyages to India. The Black Death and Italian plagues further devastated its population. The Republic fell to Napoleon in 1797 and later became part of Italy in 1866. In the 20th century, industrialization moved to the mainland, and the historic city's population plummeted from 120,000 in 1980 to about 50,000 in 2021.

### Geography and Environmental Challenges
Venice is built on alluvial silt in a shallow lagoon. Its foundations are wooden piles driven into the mud. The city faces chronic flooding (*acqua alta*) due to its low elevation, subsidence (sinking), and sea-level rise exacerbated by climate change. The MOSE Project, a system of mobile flood barriers, was inaugurated in 2003 and first activated in 2019 to protect the city during high tides.

### Tourism and Economy
Tourism is the city's primary modern industry, hosting up to 60,000 visitors per day. This "overtourism" causes overcrowding, environmental damage, and rising living costs, driving residents away. Mitigation efforts include banning new hotels, limiting tour group sizes, and implementing a day-tripper access fee. The economy also relies on traditional crafts like Murano glass and Burano lace.

### Culture and Legacy
Venice was a cradle of the Renaissance, with a distinctive painting style emphasizing color (*colorito*). It was a major center for printing, music (home to composers like Vivaldi), and architecture (Venetian Gothic). The city's unique character, reflected in its canals, festivals like Carnival, and cultural institutions like the Venice Biennale and Film Festival, continues to inspire global art and literature.

## Terms
- **Doge**: The chief magistrate and leader of the Republic of Venice, elected for life by the city's nobility.
- **Thalassocracy**: A maritime empire or state whose power is primarily based on its navy and control of sea trade.
- **Acqua alta**: Italian for "high water," referring to the periodic tidal flooding in Venice.
- **Sestieri**: The six historical districts that divide the historic center of Venice.
- **Vaporetto**: A public water bus that serves as the main mode of transportation in Venice.
- **MOSE Project**: An engineering system of mobile gates designed to protect the Venetian Lagoon from high tides.
- **Terraferma**: The mainland territory of the Venetian Republic, contrasting with its maritime domains.
- **Ghetto**: The area in Venice where Jews were compelled to live under the Republic; the term originated here.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact founding date of Venice is traditional (25 March 421) and not supported by historical records.
- The effectiveness and cost of the MOSE Project are debated, with critics calling it a "pharaonic project" that may not solve the flooding problem and was plagued by corruption.
- The impact of the day-tripper access fee is contested; some argue it reinforces Venice's image as a "theme park," while others see it as a necessary tool for managing tourism.
- The origin of Venetian glass beads found at prehistoric Inuit sites in Alaska is debated, with challenges to the radiocarbon dating and the proposed overland trade route.
