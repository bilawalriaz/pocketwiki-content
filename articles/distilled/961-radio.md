# Radio

## Overview
Radio is the technology of communicating using radio waves—electromagnetic waves with frequencies between 3 Hz and 300 GHz. Generated by transmitters and received by antennas, radio waves carry information via modulation (impressing a signal on a carrier wave) for applications including broadcasting, mobile phones, radar, navigation (GPS), and remote control. First proven by Heinrich Hertz in 1886 and developed into long-distance communication by Guglielmo Marconi in the 1890s, radio became a commercial broadcast medium in 1920. Because the radio spectrum is a finite, shared resource, its use is strictly regulated by the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) and national governments to prevent interference.

## Body

### Etymology and Terminology
The term *radio* derives from the Latin *radius* ("ray" or "beam"). Following Hertz’s 1886 discovery, the radiation was initially called "Hertzian waves." Early systems by Marconi (1894–1895) transmitted telegraph signals, leading to the term "wireless telegraphy," which confusingly also covered non-electromagnetic induction systems. French physicist Édouard Branly coined *radio-conducteur* for his detector in 1890; the *radio-* prefix spread in Europe (e.g., *radiotelegraphy* by 1898). The standalone word "radio" appeared in British Post Office instructions on 30 December 1904 and was codified internationally by the 1906 Berlin Radiotelegraphic Convention. Lee de Forest and the U.S. Navy popularized "radio" over "wireless" in the U.S., with the public adopting it during 1920s broadcasting.

### History: From Theory to Broadcast
Maxwell’s 1873 equations predicted that coupled oscillating electric and magnetic fields travel as waves at light speed. Hertz confirmed this in 1886 using a spark-gap transmitter; subsequent experiments by Bose, Lodge, Rayleigh, and Righi proved radio waves exhibit reflection, refraction, diffraction, and polarization—confirming they are electromagnetic waves differing from light only in frequency. Marconi built the first practical communication system in 1895, achieving transatlantic signaling by 1901. During the "radiotelegraphy era" (first two decades), spark-gap transmitters produced only pulsed waves, limiting use to text messaging (Morse code) for commercial, diplomatic, and military traffic. By 1908, industrial nations built global transoceanic networks. The development of continuous wave transmitters and improved detectors (electrolytic, crystal) around 1908–1920 enabled amplitude modulation (AM) radiotelephony by Reginald Fessenden and others, allowing audio transmission. The first commercial broadcast (KDKA, Pittsburgh) occurred on 2 November 1920.

### Principles of Propagation
Radio waves are radiated by accelerating electric charges (time-varying currents in a metal antenna). Signal strength decreases with distance per the inverse-square law, limiting range based on transmitter power, antenna pattern, receiver sensitivity, noise, and obstructions. *Omnidirectional antennas* radiate in all directions; *directional antennas* focus energy in a beam. Radio waves travel at light speed in vacuum. Their long wavelengths give desirable propagation: they penetrate atmosphere, foliage, and building materials in any weather, and diffract (bend) around obstacles. Unlike higher-frequency electromagnetic waves (infrared, light, X-rays), radio waves tend to scatter off objects larger than their wavelength rather than be absorbed.

### Communication Systems and Modulation
In a radio system, a *transducer* converts information (sound, video, data) into a *modulation signal* (time-varying electrical signal). A transmitter’s *oscillator* generates a *carrier wave* (alternating current at radio frequency). The modulation signal *modulates* the carrier, impressing information onto it. Key methods:
*   **Amplitude Modulation (AM):** Varies carrier amplitude.
*   **Frequency Modulation (FM):** Varies carrier frequency.
*   **Frequency-Shift Keying (FSK):** Shifts carrier between discrete frequencies for digital data.
*   **Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing (OFDM):** Splits data across many closely spaced carriers transmitted in parallel; used in Wi-Fi, cellular (4G/5G), digital TV, and DAB for high spectral efficiency and fading resistance.
The modulated carrier is amplified and radiated by the transmitting antenna. The receiving antenna induces a tiny replica voltage; the receiver amplifies it, *demodulates* it to extract the modulation signal, and a transducer converts it back to sound, images, or data. *Tuned circuits* (resonators) in the receiver select the desired frequency (tuning) and reject others, preventing interference from simultaneous transmissions on different frequencies.

### Bandwidth and Spectrum Management
A modulated signal occupies a frequency range called *bandwidth (BW)* (highest minus lowest frequency), concentrated in *sidebands* around the carrier. Bandwidth measures information-carrying capacity; for a given signal-to-noise ratio, a specific bandwidth carries the same data regardless of its spectral location. Required bandwidth depends on data rate (e.g., video > audio) and modulation *spectral efficiency* (bits per Hz). The radio spectrum is a limited economic resource; congestion drives a transition from analog to digital (late 1990s onward) because digital modulation offers higher spectral efficiency, loss compression, noise immunity, and flexibility. Innovations to increase capacity include trunked radio, spread spectrum (ultra-wideband), frequency reuse, dynamic spectrum management, and cognitive radio.

### ITU Frequency Bands
The ITU divides the spectrum into 12 bands, each spanning a decade of frequency/wavelength, starting at wavelengths of 10ⁿ meters (frequencies of 3×10ⁿ Hz). Bandwidth increases exponentially: each band contains ten times the bandwidth of the previous one. Traditional names range from Extremely Low Frequency (ELF, 3–30 Hz) to Extremely High Frequency (EHF, 30–300 GHz). *Tremendously Low Frequency (TLF, 1–3 Hz)* is used informally but not defined by the ITU.

### Regulation
To prevent interference (which can be life-threatening for emergency/aviation comms), national laws license transmitters, restricting frequencies, power levels, and requiring call signs for broadcasters. Operators need licenses (e.g., US General Radiotelephone Operator License) for repair/adjustment. The ITU coordinates international allocation. *Unlicensed* operation is permitted for low-power, short-range devices (cell phones, Wi-Fi, garage openers, baby monitors) under rules like US FCC Part 15, often in *ISM bands* reserved for such use; devices still require type approval. *Radio jamming* (deliberate interference) is used militarily and by censoring regimes but is illegal for non-military sale/operation in the US (including GPS, cellular, Wi-Fi, radar jammers).

### Applications
Radio enables: **Broadcasting** (audio/video to public); **Voice communication** (mobile phones, two-way radio); **Data communication** (wireless networking, satellite links); **Radar** (detecting/tracking objects via reflected waves); **Radiolocation/Navigation** (GPS, VOR—measuring signal arrival time from known beacons to calculate position); **Remote control** (drones, keyless entry, garage doors).

## Terms
- ****Radio waves**** — Electromagnetic waves with frequencies between 3 Hz and 300 GHz.
- ****Transmitter**** — Electronic device generating radio frequency alternating current fed to an antenna to radiate radio waves.
- ****Modulation**** — Process of varying a property (amplitude, frequency, phase) of a carrier wave to impress an information signal onto it.
- ****Carrier wave**** — The unmodulated radio frequency alternating current generated by a transmitter's oscillator; "carries" the information.
- ****Demodulation**** — Extraction of the original modulation signal from the modulated carrier wave at the receiver.
- ****Bandwidth (BW)**** — The width in hertz of the frequency range occupied by a radio signal (highest minus lowest frequency); measure of information capacity.
- ****Sidebands**** — Narrow frequency bands just above and below the carrier frequency where the information in a modulated signal is concentrated.
- ****Tuned circuit**** — A resonant circuit in a receiver that selects a specific frequency (like a tuning fork), enabling tuning to a desired station.
- ****ISM bands**** — Industrial, Scientific, and Medical frequency bands reserved internationally for unlicensed, low-power radio equipment.
- ****OFDM**** — Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing: a digital modulation method using many parallel, closely spaced carriers for high spectral efficiency and fading resistance.
