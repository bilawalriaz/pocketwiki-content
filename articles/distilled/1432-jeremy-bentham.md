# Jeremy Bentham

## Overview
Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832) was an English philosopher, jurist, and social reformer who founded modern utilitarianism, defining its "fundamental axiom" as the principle that "it is the greatest happiness of the greatest number that is the measure of right and wrong." A radical thinker, he opposed natural law and natural rights as "nonsense upon stilts" while advocating for individual liberties, the separation of church and state, women's rights, the decriminalization of homosexuality, the abolition of slavery and capital punishment, and animal welfare. His insistence on the codification of common law into coherent statutes and his design for the Panopticon prison—though never built—profoundly influenced legal theory, penology, and the architecture of surveillance. His preserved body, the "auto-icon," remains on display at University College London, which regards him as its spiritual founder.

## Timeline
- **15 Feb 1748** — Born in Houndsditch, London, to a wealthy Tory family.
- **1760–1767** — Attends Westminster School and The Queen's College, Oxford (BA 1764, MA 1767).
- **1769** — Called to the bar; begins lifelong frustration with English common law ("Demon of Chicane").
- **1776** — Publishes *A Fragment on Government* (critique of Blackstone) and *Short Review of the Declaration* (attack on US Declaration of Independence).
- **1786–1787** — Visits brother Samuel in Krichev, White Russia; develops the Panopticon prison concept based on Samuel's industrial inspection design.
- **1789** — Publishes *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, the foundational text of utilitarianism.
- **1791** — Publishes *Panopticon; or, the Inspection-House*.
- **1794–1803** — Secures government backing (Pitt administration) for a National Penitentiary at Millbank; project abandoned by Addington administration in 1803.
- **1800** — Collaborates with Patrick Colquhoun on the *Depredations on the Thames Act*, creating the first preventive police force (Thames River Police).
- **1811** — Writes to President James Madison and all US state governors offering to draft a complete legal code; all decline.
- **1823** — Co-founds *The Westminster Review* with James Mill as the organ of the "Philosophical Radicals."
- **6 Jun 1832** — Dies in London; body dissected and preserved as an auto-icon per his will.
- **1850** — Auto-icon transferred to University College London.
- **1968–present** — Bentham Project at UCL publishes definitive *Collected Works* (projected 80 volumes).

## Body

### Early Life and Intellectual Formation
Bentham was a child prodigy from a wealthy attorney family, reading history as a toddler and studying Latin at three. He entered Oxford at twelve but found the curriculum sterile. Called to the bar in 1769, he never practiced, instead turning to legal theory. His early frustration with the obscurity and fictions of English common law—termed the "Demon of Chicane"—drove his lifelong project of codification. Intellectually, he synthesized Epicurean hedonism (via Helvétius) with the empiricism of Hume and Locke, adopting the "principle of utility" as his foundational standard. His first major publications in 1776 attacked William Blackstone’s defense of judge-made law and mocked the natural rights philosophy of the American Declaration of Independence.

### The Panopticon and Penal Reform
While visiting his brother Samuel in Russia (1786–87), Bentham adapted Samuel’s circular workshop design for a prison where a central inspector could observe all inmates without being seen. He refined this "Panopticon" over sixteen years, adding contract management (the governor profits by lowering mortality) and inmate labor to offset costs. He sought to become contractor-governor of a National Penitentiary. Despite initial support from Prime Minister William Pitt, the project foundered on legal disputes over sites (Battersea, Tothill Fields, Millbank) and lack of genuine governmental commitment. The Addington administration cancelled it in 1803; Bentham eventually accepted £23,000 compensation in 1812. Though never built, the Panopticon became a paradigmatic model for 19th-century disciplinary institutions, later analyzed by Michel Foucault as a mechanism of power through visibility.

### Utilitarianism: Principle and Calculus
Bentham’s *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789) established utilitarianism as a systematic moral and legislative science. He posited two "sovereign masters," pain and pleasure, governing all human action. Right actions maximize net pleasure (happiness); wrong actions maximize pain. To make this calculable, he devised the **felicific (hedonistic) calculus**, weighing pleasures and pains by seven "dimensions": intensity, duration, certainty, proximity, productiveness, purity, and extent. He classified 14 pleasures and 12 pains. This calculus aimed to guide legislators: punishment is justified only if it excludes a greater evil; unnecessary laws create worse vices. Bentham equated all pleasures quantitatively ("push-pin is of equal value with the arts and sciences"), rejecting qualitative distinctions later introduced by John Stuart Mill.

### Law Reform and Theory of Fictions
Bentham coined the verb "to codify" and campaigned aggressively for the replacement of chaotic common law with systematic statutory codes. He offered to write codes for the US federal government and every state; all refused. His efforts failed in England during his life but influenced David Dudley Field II’s later codification work in America. Complementing this, his **Theory of Fictions** distinguished "fabulous entities" (pure inventions like centaurs) from "fictitious entities" (necessary constructs like laws, rights, obligations, corporations). While acknowledging their utility for discourse, he warned that legal fictions (e.g., corporate personhood, sovereignty) obscure truth and enable deception. He demanded empirical clarity: every term must refer to a perceptible reality or be defined by such terms.

### Social and Political Radicalism
Bentham’s utilitarianism drove radical positions decades ahead of his time.
*   **Rights and Government:** He rejected natural rights as metaphysical nonsense ("nonsense upon stilts"), arguing rights are created by law for utility. He supported democracy, suffrage extension, and transparency (publicity) as a check on power, envisioning society as a gymnasium where the powerful are constantly observed.
*   **Economics:** He generally favored *laissez-faire* ("Be quiet") but recognized diminishing marginal utility of wealth, justifying some redistributive taxation to prevent starvation, while warning against taxing "industry for the support of idleness." His monetary theories anticipated modern income/employment analysis (forced saving, propensity to consume).
*   **Gender and Sexuality:** He advocated complete legal equality for women (suffrage, divorce, office-holding) from age eleven. His c. 1785 essay "Paederasty (Offences Against One's Self)" argued homosexual acts were "irregularities of the venereal appetite," not unnatural crimes, and should be decriminalized as private offenses; it remained unpublished until 1978.
*   **Animal Rights:** He famously argued the "insuperable line" for moral consideration is not reason but sentience: "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" He permitted killing for food or necessary medical experiments with a clear human benefit, but condemned cruelty for amusement.
*   **Imperialism and Race:** He opposed empire as financially ruinous and morally corrupting for both metropole and colony ("Emancipate Your Colonies!"). However, he held hierarchical racial views, asserting innate differences independent of climate (e.g., English ascendancy over others "like a Brahmin over a Parias") and describing Aboriginal Australians as "brutes in human shape," revealing a tension between his universalist principle and the prejudices of his era.

### Religion, Death, and the Auto-Icon
Bentham was a determined opponent of religion, conducting an "exhaustive examination" (1809–1823) to extirpate religious belief. He prepared meticulously for death. As early as 1769, he willed his body for dissection. In 1832, he directed his friend Dr. Southwood Smith to create an **auto-icon**: his skeleton articulated, dressed in his clothes, seated in his chair. Smith’s mummification of the head (based on Māori techniques) failed aesthetically, so a wax head with Bentham’s hair was substituted. The auto-icon resided with Smith, then at 36 Percy Street, before transfer to UCL in 1850. It now stands in the Student Centre entrance. Bentham viewed this as a utilitarian act: dead bodies should serve the living (anatomy, memorial, even garden ornaments).

### Legacy and Posthumous Publication
Bentham left ~30 million words of manuscripts. His disciple John Bowring produced a flawed 11-volume edition (1838–43), omitting religious works. Werner Stark edited economic writings (1952–54). Since 1959, the **Bentham Project** at UCL has published the definitive *Collected Works* (38 of 80 projected volumes by 2017). The *Transcribe Bentham* crowdsourcing initiative digitizes manuscripts. His influence spans Victorian laissez-faire, welfare economics, legal positivism, and the "Philosophical Radicals" (James Mill, John Stuart Mill, Edwin Chadwick, John Austin). Critics (Mill, Marx, Himmelfarb) charged him with ignoring conscience, justice, and liberty; defenders note his theory of distributive justice and security-focused framework. UCL honors him as "spiritual founder," housing his papers and auto-icon.

## Terms
- ****Utilitarianism**** — The ethical theory founded by Bentham holding that the moral worth of an action is determined by its contribution to "the greatest happiness of the greatest number."
- ****Principle of Utility**** — The "fundamental axiom" that actions are right insofar as they promote happiness (pleasure over pain) and wrong as they produce the reverse.
- ****Felicific (Hedonistic) Calculus**** — Bentham’s method for quantifying the moral value of an action by summing its pleasures and pains across seven dimensions (intensity, duration, certainty, proximity, productiveness, purity, extent).
- ****Panopticon**** — A circular prison design with a central inspection tower allowing a single watchman to observe all inmates without them knowing if they are watched, internalizing surveillance.
- ****Codification**** — The process (verb coined by Bentham) of systematically arranging the entire body of law into a coherent, authoritative statutory code, replacing judge-made common law.
- ****Auto-icon**** — Bentham’s preserved skeleton, dressed in his clothes and seated in his chair, intended as a utilitarian memorial replacing traditional burial.
- ****Fictitious Entities**** — In Bentham’s Theory of Fictions, necessary conceptual constructs (e.g., laws, rights, corporations) that lack physical existence but structure reasoning; distinct from "fabulous entities" (pure fantasy).
- ****Natural Rights**** — Rights viewed as inherent, "God-given," or pre-political; Bentham rejected these as "nonsense upon stilts," arguing rights are solely created by positive law for utility.
- ****Philosophical Radicals**** — A group of younger disciples (including James Mill, John Stuart Mill, Edwin Chadwick, John Austin) through whom Bentham exerted major influence on British reform politics via *The Westminster Review*.
- ****Diminishing Marginal Utility**** — The economic principle, recognized by Bentham, that each additional unit of wealth (e.g., £1) yields less utility to a rich person than to a poor one, grounding arguments for redistribution.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Justice vs. Utility:** Critics (Mill, Himmelfarb, Rawls) argue Bentham’s calculus permits sacrificing an innocent individual’s rights (e.g., torture, punishment) if it maximizes aggregate happiness. Defenders (P.J. Kelly) contend Bentham’s emphasis on security and expectation-utility (law protects "spheres of personal inviolability") prevents such tyranny.
*   **Qualitative vs. Quantitative Hedonism:** Bentham equated all pleasures quantitatively ("push-pin = poetry"). John Stuart Mill famously revised this, introducing qualitative distinctions ("higher" intellectual vs. "lower" bodily pleasures), fundamentally splitting the utilitarian tradition.
*   **Natural Rights Rejection:** By denying pre-legal rights, did Bentham undermine the liberal safeguards against state power? He argued legal rights secured by utility are more real; critics say this leaves minorities vulnerable to majority calculation.
*   **Racial Hierarchy vs. Universalism:** How to reconcile Bentham’s universal "greatest number" principle with his explicit assertions of innate racial hierarchy and dehumanizing descriptions of Aboriginal Australians? Scholars debate whether this reflects inconsistent application or a structural limit of his empiricism.
*   **Panopticon: Reform or Control?** Foucault framed the Panopticon as the diagram of modern disciplinary power (surveillance, normalization). Bentham intended it as a humanitarian, cost-saving reform. The tension between therapeutic intent and structural authoritarianism remains central to surveillance studies.
*   **Codification Failure:** Why did common law jurisdictions universally reject Bentham’s codification project? Debates focus on the flexibility of precedent vs. statutory rigidity, the political power of the legal profession, and the theoretical impossibility of a complete, gapless code.
