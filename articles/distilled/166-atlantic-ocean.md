# Atlantic Ocean

## Overview

The Atlantic Ocean is the second largest of the world's five oceans, covering about 85,133,000 square kilometers (32,870,000 sq mi), approximately 17% of Earth's surface and about 24% of its water surface area. It separates the Americas from Afro-Eurasia and has played a central role in human globalization, from Columbus's 1492 voyage to the trans-Atlantic slave trade and the Columbian exchange. The ocean is underlain by the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, a submarine mountain range whose exploration led to the acceptance of seafloor spreading and plate tectonics.

## Timeline

- **c. 200 Ma** — Central Atlantic Magmatic Province eruption initiates break-up of Pangaea and opening of the Atlantic
- **133–130 Ma** — Paraná–Etendeka Large Igneous Province magmatism in the South Atlantic
- **62 Ma** — Iceland begins forming due to a mantle plume; Labrador Sea spreading begins ~61 Ma
- **865–930 CE** — Norse settlement of Iceland following a warm period
- **Before 1000 CE** — Norse settlement established on Greenland; contact lost in 1409
- **1492** — Columbus reaches the Americas under the Spanish flag
- **1497** — John Cabot discovers abundant Atlantic cod off Newfoundland
- **1500** — Pedro Álvares Cabral reaches Brazil via South Atlantic Gyre currents
- **16th–19th centuries** — Atlantic slave trade ships an estimated 9.5 million enslaved Africans to the New World
- **1950s** — Exploration of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge leads to acceptance of seafloor spreading and plate tectonics

## Body

### Names and extent

The oldest known mentions of an "Atlantic" sea come from Stesichorus around mid-sixth century BC and Herodotus around 450 BC, referring to "the sea beyond the pillars of Hercules" (the Strait of Gibraltar), named for Atlas, the Titan in Greek mythology. Early Greek sailors believed the Atlantic was part of Oceanus, the great sea surrounding all land, in contrast to the enclosed Mediterranean and Black Seas. The southern Atlantic was called the "Aethiopian Ocean" as late as the mid-19th century. During the Age of Discovery, English cartographers called it the Great Western Ocean. "The pond" is a humorous British and American term for the northern Atlantic, dating to 1640.

The International Hydrographic Organization defined ocean limits in 1953, but definitions vary among authorities. The Atlantic connects to the Arctic Ocean through the Labrador Sea, Denmark Strait, Greenland Sea, Norwegian Sea, and Barents Sea; to the Mediterranean and Black Seas via the Strait of Gibraltar; and merges into the Indian Ocean at the 20° East meridian south from Cape Agulhas. Including marginal seas, the Atlantic covers 106,460,000 km² (23.5% of the global ocean) with a coastline of 111,866 km. The average depth is 3,646 m; the maximum depth, the Milwaukee Deep in the Puerto Rico Trench, is 8,376 m.

### Bathymetry and geology

The Mid-Atlantic Ridge (MAR) runs from 87°N to Bouvet Island at 54°S, rising 2–3 km above the surrounding ocean floor. Its rift valley is the divergent boundary between the North American and Eurasian plates in the North Atlantic and the South American and African plates in the South Atlantic. The MAR is interrupted by transform faults at the Romanche Trench near the Equator and the Gibbs fracture zone at 53°N, where deep water currents can pass between sides. The Challenger expedition in the 1870s discovered parts of the ridge; the German Meteor expedition in the 1920s found the remainder using echo-sounding equipment.

The break-up of Pangaea began in the central Atlantic between North America and Northwest Africa during the Late Triassic and Early Jurassic, with timing estimates ranging from 200 to 170 Ma. The eruption of the Central Atlantic Magmatic Province (CAMP) at 200 Ma, associated with the Triassic–Jurassic extinction event, covered an estimated 4.5×10⁶ km². The North Atlantic opened in six stages following the margins of the predecessor Iapetus Ocean. West Gondwana (South America and Africa) broke up in the Early Cretaceous to form the South Atlantic, divided into four geological segments. The formation of the Central American Isthmus at the end of the Pliocene (2.8 Ma) closed the Central American Seaway, affecting ocean currents, salinity, and temperatures in both the Atlantic and Pacific.

### Water characteristics

Surface water temperatures range from below −2 °C to over 30 °C, reflecting latitudinal solar energy distribution. The Coriolis effect circulates North Atlantic water clockwise and South Atlantic water counter-clockwise. The Atlantic is the saltiest major ocean, with surface salinity ranging from 33 to 37 parts per thousand. Maximum salinity occurs at about 25° north and south in subtropical regions; minimum values are in high latitudes and near large rivers.

The Atlantic consists of four major upper water masses and five intermediate waters. The North Atlantic deep water (NADW) is a complex of four water masses fed by a flow of warm shallow water into the northern North Atlantic, responsible for the anomalous warm climate in Europe. The clockwise North Atlantic Gyre and counter-clockwise South Atlantic Gyre dominate surface circulation. The Gulf Stream flows north-east from Cape Hatteras; its branch, the North Atlantic Current, transports warm water into the North Atlantic, without which temperatures in Europe would plunge dramatically. The subpolar gyre feeds the meridional overturning circulation (MOC), whose northward heat transport is threatened by anthropogenic climate change.

The Sargasso Sea in the western North Atlantic is defined by floating Sargassum seaweed, an area 4,000 km wide encircled by the Gulf Stream, North Atlantic Drift, and North Equatorial Current. It is the spawning ground for both European and American eels, which migrate more than 5,000 km and 2,000 km respectively.

### Climate and natural hazards

Maritime climates are more moderate than inland climates because of the ocean's capacity to store and release heat. The Gulf Stream moderates winter temperatures along southeastern North America; the North Atlantic Drift keeps the British Isles and northwestern Europe mild and cloudy. Cold water currents contribute to heavy fog off eastern Canada and northwestern Africa. The Icelandic Low produces frequent storms every winter. Icebergs are common from early February to the end of July near the Grand Banks of Newfoundland. Hurricanes are a hazard in the western North Atlantic during summer and autumn; South Atlantic tropical cyclones are rare due to strong wind shear and a weak Intertropical Convergence Zone.

### History and economy

Mitochondrial DNA studies indicate a major demographic expansion within Africa 80,000–60,000 years ago, with dispersal out of Africa around 65,000 years ago. During the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM) 20,000 years ago, humans abandoned European North Atlantic coasts and retreated to the Mediterranean; the region was repopulated by Magdalenian culture after rapid climate changes. European coasts of the North Atlantic were permanently populated about 9,000–8,500 years ago. The Norse settled the Faroe Islands and Iceland during the 9th and 10th centuries; the Greenland settlement was abandoned during the early Little Ice Age due to unsustainable economy, conflicts with Inuit, colder climate, and economic marginalization.

Columbus reached the Americas in 1492; Vasco da Gama reached India in 1498, proving the Atlantic and Indian Oceans are connected. Spain and Portugal conquered and colonized large territories, forcing Amerindian populations into slavery. The Atlantic slave trade replaced depleted indigenous populations; between the 15th century and 1888, an estimated 9.5 million enslaved Africans were shipped to the New World. Trans-Atlantic trade from 1500–1800 made Atlantic-facing European countries richer than those in Eastern Europe and Asia; urbanization in Atlantic-facing countries grew from 8% in 1300 to 24.5% in 1850, and GDP doubled. By the end of the 17th century, trans-Atlantic trade volume surpassed Mediterranean trade.

The Atlantic offers petroleum deposits, fish, marine mammals, sand and gravel, placer deposits, polymetallic nodules, and precious stones, though underwater gold deposits are not cost-effective to mine. The most productive fisheries include the Grand Banks, Georges Bank, and waters around Iceland. The Northwest Atlantic cod fishery collapsed in the early 1990s after overfishing was recognized in the 1960s but regulation came only in the late 1970s. Overfishing affects 21% of stocks in the Northeast Atlantic and 31% in the Northwest Atlantic.

### Environmental issues

Endangered species include the manatee, seals, sea lions, turtles, and whales. Drift net fishing kills dolphins, albatrosses, and other seabirds. Rivers carry agricultural fertilizer chemicals and waste, causing hypoxia and dead zones. The North Atlantic garbage patch is estimated to be hundreds of kilometers across. A USAF C-124 aircraft jettisoned two nuclear bombs over the Atlantic, which were never recovered. North Atlantic hurricane activity has increased due to sea surface temperature rises attributed to human activities; the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation slowed by 30% between 1957 and 2004 and weakened by approximately 12% over the past two decades as of 2024. Thermal expansion from heat uptake contributes to sea level rise.

## Terms

- **Mid-Atlantic Ridge (MAR)**: Submarine mountain range dividing the Atlantic longitudinally; the divergent boundary between tectonic plates.
- **Thermohaline circulation**: Ocean circulation driven by temperature and salinity differences; the Atlantic's high salinity is essential to it.
- **North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW)**: Complex of four water masses that feeds the meridional overturning circulation.
- **Gulf Stream**: Warm current flowing north-east from Cape Hatteras; moderates climate in North America and Europe.
- **Sargasso Sea**: Western North Atlantic area defined by floating Sargassum seaweed; spawning ground for eels.
- **Coriolis effect**: Force causing clockwise circulation in the North Atlantic and counter-clockwise in the South Atlantic.
- **Central Atlantic Magmatic Province (CAMP)**: Large igneous province whose eruption at 200 Ma initiated Pangaea's break-up.
- **Columbian exchange**: Trans-Atlantic transfer of goods, crops, and diseases following Columbus's voyage.
- **Meridional overturning circulation (MOC)**: Northward heat transport system fed by the NADW, threatened by climate change.

## Debates and open questions

The exact timing of the central Atlantic opening is controversial, with estimates ranging from 200 to 170 Ma. The "blitzkrieg" and "three-wave" hypotheses for the settlement of the Americas are challenged by archaeological discoveries in South America, and genetic data support neither hypothesis while delivering mutually ambiguous results. Alternative theories include the Solutrean hypothesis and other Pre-Columbian trans-oceanic contact theories, ranging from hypothetical to mostly disputed. The future of the Atlantic is debated: one theory proposes Atlantic closure via the Gibraltar Arc and Caribbean subduction systems, while others propose the Pacific closes first, creating supercontinents Amasia, Novopangaea, or Aurica. Recent but disputed research suggests eels may use Earth's magnetic field for navigation.
