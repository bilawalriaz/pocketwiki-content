# Classicism

## Overview
Classicism is an aesthetic and intellectual movement in the arts that draws its standards of taste, form, and ideals from the culture, art, and literature of ancient Greece and Rome. It emphasizes principles like form, simplicity, proportion, clarity, and restrained emotion, appealing to the intellect. This classical tradition has been a recurring force in post-medieval European and European-influenced cultures, experiencing major revivals during periods like the Renaissance and the Age of Enlightenment.

## Timeline
- **c. 5th century B.C.E.** — Flourishing of Classical art and political philosophy in ancient Greece.
- **15th–16th centuries** — Italian Renaissance revives classical models in art, architecture, and literature.
- **17th century** — French playwrights develop classicism in theatre based on Aristotelian rules.
- **17th century** — Architects like Inigo Jones and Christopher Wren establish classicism in England.
- **18th century** — Age of Enlightenment fosters Neoclassicism, a movement explicitly aligned with classical ideals.
- **Late 18th–19th centuries** — Neoclassicism becomes a major style in painting, sculpture, and architecture.
- **20th century** — Classicism is invoked both by traditionalists and by modern movements valuing formal coherence.

## Body

### General Principles and Historical Revivals
Classicism is a recurrent tendency in Western culture, defined by an aesthetic attitude based on ancient Greek and Roman principles. It prioritizes form, simplicity, proportion, clarity, and restrained emotion. A major, durable revival began in the Italian Renaissance, fueled by renewed contact with classical antiquity. Renaissance classicism introduced elements like mathematical application, humanism, realism, and a juxtaposition of ancient and modern. By the 16th and 17th centuries, classicism evolved to emphasize order, predictability, geometry, and disciplined pedagogy, exemplified by the court of Louis XIV.

### In the Theatre
Classicism in theatre was systematized by 17th-century French playwrights who derived rules from Greek models, notably Aristotle's "Poetics." Central to this were the "Classical unities": unity of time (action within 24 hours), unity of place (a single location), and unity of action (a single plot-line). Key classicist playwrights include Pierre Corneille, Jean Racine, and Molière. The influence of these rules on other national theatres, like England's, is debated.

### In Architecture and Fine Arts
Classical architecture, revived during the Italian Renaissance, emphasizes symmetry, proportion, geometry, and regularity based on ancient Roman models. It features orderly columns, semicircular arches, and domes, replacing complex medieval forms. This style spread across Europe. In the fine arts, Renaissance painting and sculpture renewed classical forms and subjects. Later, from the mid-18th century, this revival is generally termed Neoclassicism.

### In Literature and Political Philosophy
Literary classicism drew inspiration from ancient Greek and Latin literature, with significant practitioners in the 17th–18th centuries including Pope, Voltaire, and Dryden. In political philosophy, classicism traces back to ancient Greek thinkers like Plato, Aristotle, and Socrates, who debated constitutions and the relationship between ethics and political life. Their work formed the foundation of Western political theory.

## Terms
- **Classicism**: An aesthetic and intellectual movement emphasizing standards of taste, form, and ideals derived from ancient Greece and Rome.
- **Neoclassicism**: A major revival of classical styles, particularly in the 18th-century Age of Enlightenment.
- **Classical Unities**: Rules for drama (time, place, action) derived from Aristotle's "Poetics" and central to 17th-century French classicism.
- **Humanism**: A Renaissance intellectual movement emphasizing human potential and classical learning.
- **Formalism**: An emphasis on form and structure over emotional or narrative content.
- **Proportion**: The harmonious and balanced relationship between parts of a whole, a key classical principle.
- **Palladian Architecture**: A style derived from the work of Andrea Palladio, based on classical temple architecture.
- **Academicism**: A 19th-century approach emphasizing rigorous rules and categories in the arts and sciences.

## Debates and Open questions
The influence of French classicist theatre rules on playwrights in other nations, such as England, is noted as debatable. The article also presents classicism as a force invoked by opposing sides in the 20th century—both by those rejecting modern changes and by modern movements themselves.
