# History of Scandinavia

## Overview
The history of Scandinavia covers the development of the region comprising Denmark, Norway, and Sweden, with Finland and Iceland sometimes included. It traces the journey from prehistoric hunter-gatherers to the formation of modern nation-states, marked by the Viking Age, the Kalmar Union, and the development of contemporary welfare states and international alignments.

## Timeline
- **c. 12,000 BCE** — Permanent, nomadic habitation begins as glaciers recede.
- **c. 3000 BCE** — Funnelbeaker culture marks the Neolithic transition to agriculture.
- **c. 1800–500 BCE** — Nordic Bronze Age, a period of advanced civilization and trade.
- **793 CE** — Viking raid on Lindisfarne marks the traditional start of the Viking Age.
- **1066 CE** — Failed invasion of England by Harald Hårdråde marks the end of the Viking Age.
- **1397–1520** — Kalmar Union unites Denmark, Norway, and Sweden under one monarch.
- **1523** — Dissolution of the Kalmar Union; Swedish independence.
- **1648** — End of Thirty Years' War; Sweden emerges as a major European power.
- **1721** — End of Great Northern War; Russia replaces Sweden as the dominant Baltic power.
- **1814** — Treaty of Kiel transfers Norway from Denmark to Sweden in a personal union.
- **1905** — Peaceful dissolution of the union between Sweden and Norway.
- **1940–1945** — Denmark and Norway are occupied by Nazi Germany; Sweden remains neutral.

## Body

### Prehistory to the Viking Age
Scandinavia's Stone Age was delayed by glaciation. Permanent settlement began around 12,000 BCE with nomadic hunters. The Mesolithic (c. 9,000–6,000 BP) saw mobile groups like the Maglemosian and Kongemose cultures. The Neolithic transition began in the 5th millennium BCE with the Ertebølle people adopting pottery and later agriculture via the Funnelbeaker culture. By the end of the 3rd millennium BCE, the Battle-Axe culture, possibly Proto-Indo-European speakers, arrived, likely providing the ancestor of modern Scandinavian languages.

The Nordic Bronze Age (c. 1800–500 BCE) was a wealthy period with trade links to Mediterranean cultures, evidenced by imported bronze and gold. A subsequent colder, wetter climate ended this era and may have triggered southward migrations. The Pre-Roman and Roman Iron Ages saw increased contact with Rome, including trade and conflict, such as the Cimbrian War (113–101 BC). The Germanic Iron Age followed the Roman Empire's fall, featuring rich gold artifacts like the Golden horns of Gallehus and the evolution of distinct animal art styles.

### The Viking Age and Christianization
The Viking Age (c. 793–1066) was characterized by Scandinavian expansion, trade, and settlement across Europe, the North Atlantic, and into the Slavic lands of Rus'. Swedes (Varangians) were prominent in the east, while Norwegians and Danes focused on the west, founding settlements like Dublin and Iceland. This period saw significant cultural exchange with Slavic peoples.

Christianization occurred gradually from the 10th century. Denmark was Christianized c. 965 under Harald Bluetooth. Norway's conversion was led by Olaf Tryggvason and Olaf II Haraldsson. Iceland adopted Christianity in 1000 AD through a legal compromise. Sweden's transition was slower, with indigenous practices persisting until the 12th century. The adoption of Christianity integrated Scandinavia into the broader European cultural framework.

### The Kalmar Union and Early Modern Era
The Kalmar Union (1397–1520) united the three kingdoms under a single monarch to counter German influence, but internal tensions, especially Swedish dissatisfaction with Danish dominance, led to its dissolution in 1523. The Protestant Reformation arrived in the 1530s, making Lutheranism the dominant faith.

The 17th century saw Sweden rise as a great power through wars like the Thirty Years' War, where it intervened to protect Protestant interests. This expansion was checked by the Great Northern War (1700–1721), which ended Swedish dominance and established Russia as the leading Baltic power. Both Sweden and Denmark-Norway also maintained overseas colonies.

### 19th Century: Unions, Nationalism, and Emigration
The Napoleonic Wars reshaped the region. Denmark-Norway's alliance with France led to the Treaty of Kiel (1814), which ceded Norway to Sweden. Norway briefly declared independence before entering a personal union with Sweden, which lasted until its peaceful dissolution in 1905. The 19th century also saw the rise of Scandinavism, industrialization, and massive emigration to North America, driven by population growth and economic opportunity. A Scandinavian Monetary Union was established in 1873.

### 20th Century: World Wars and Modern Integration
Scandinavian countries remained neutral in World War I. In World War II, Denmark and Norway were invaded and occupied by Germany in 1940, while Sweden maintained neutrality. Post-war, a proposed Scandinavian defense union failed due to Cold War pressures; Norway and Denmark joined NATO, while Sweden remained non-aligned until joining in 2024. The Nordic Council (1952) fostered regional cooperation. Denmark and Sweden joined the European Union (1973 and 1995, respectively), while Norway rejected membership in referendums.

## Terms
- **Petroglyphs**: Prehistoric rock carvings, a major source of information about early Scandinavian cultures.
- **Funnelbeaker culture**: The first farming culture in Scandinavia and Northern Europe, c. 4300–2800 BCE.
- **Battle-Axe culture**: A Late Neolithic culture, possibly Indo-European-speaking, that introduced new technologies and social structures.
- **Varangians**: The name for Swedish Vikings who traveled east into Slavic lands, founding Kievan Rus'.
- **Kalmar Union**: A personal union (1397–1520) of Denmark, Norway, and Sweden under a single monarch.
- **Scandinavism**: A 19th-century political movement advocating for closer cooperation and unity among Scandinavian nations.
- **Personal union**: A political arrangement where separate states share the same monarch but retain their own governments and laws.
- **Nordic Council**: An inter-parliamentary body established in 1952 to promote cooperation among Nordic countries.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact origins and social role of the creators of Bronze Age petroglyphs versus the makers of high-quality metalwork are debated.
- The extent and nature of interaction between Slavic and Viking cultures in the Baltic region is noted as not "adequately investigated."
- The primary motivations behind the large-scale 19th-century Scandinavian emigration are analyzed as a combination of economic opportunity, population pressure, and social factors.
