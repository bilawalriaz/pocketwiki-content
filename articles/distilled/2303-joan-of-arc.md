# Joan of Arc

## Overview
Joan of Arc (c. 1412–1431) was a French peasant girl who, claiming divine guidance, led the French army to key victories during the Hundred Years' War, enabling the coronation of Charles VII. Her subsequent capture, trial for heresy, and execution made her a symbol of French national identity and a Catholic saint. Her story is central to understanding the final phase of the war and the development of French nationalism.

## Body

### Early Life and Visions
Joan was born to a peasant family in Domrémy during the Hundred Years' War, a period of French civil strife and English invasion. She reported that at age thirteen (c. 1425), she began having visions of the archangel Michael, Saint Margaret, and Saint Catherine, who instructed her to help Charles VII, the disinherited Dauphin, save France from English domination. In 1428, she began petitioning the local garrison commander, Robert de Baudricourt, for an escort to the Dauphin's court. After initial refusals and a Burgundian raid on her village, she gained support from two soldiers and was finally granted an audience in early 1429.

### Military Campaigns and Coronation
Joan arrived at the besieged city of Orléans in April 1429, where her presence boosted the demoralized French army. Within nine days, the English abandoned the siege. She then advocated for an aggressive campaign to clear the Loire Valley, leading to victories at Jargeau and Patay. These successes opened the way for Charles VII to march to Reims, the traditional coronation site. Joan insisted on this advance, and Charles was crowned on 17 July 1429, with Joan at his side. This event legitimized Charles's claim to the throne.

### Capture and Trial
After the coronation, Joan's military successes waned. She was wounded during a failed assault on Paris in September 1429 and later participated in an unsuccessful siege of La Charité. In May 1430, while attempting to relieve Compiègne, she was captured by Burgundian forces. The English, who saw her as a threat to their claim on France, negotiated her transfer and put her on trial for heresy in Rouen. The trial, led by Bishop Pierre Cauchon, focused on charges including wearing men's clothes and claiming her visions were divine. Found guilty, Joan abjured but was later declared a relapsed heretic for resuming male attire and reaffirming her visions. She was burned at the stake on 30 May 1431.

### Legacy and Rehabilitation
Joan's execution created a political liability for Charles VII, implying his coronation was achieved through a heretic. In 1456, an inquisitorial court reinvestigated her trial, declared it unjust, and nullified the verdict. Joan was beatified in 1909 and canonized in 1920. She became a potent symbol of French national unity, claimed by diverse political movements. Her story has inspired countless cultural works, and she is venerated as a martyr and a model of autonomous faith.

## Terms
- **Hundred Years' War**: A series of conflicts between England and France from 1337 to 1453 over territorial and dynastic claims.
- **Dauphin**: The title given to the heir apparent to the throne of France. Charles VII held this title before his coronation.
- **Armagnacs**: The faction supporting the Dauphin Charles VII, named after Bernard VII, Count of Armagnac.
- **Burgundians**: The faction allied with England, supporting Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy.
- **Siege of Orléans**: A pivotal siege (1428–1429) where Joan's arrival helped lift the English blockade, turning the tide of the war.
- **Coronation at Reims**: The traditional ceremony for crowning French kings, which legitimized Charles VII's rule.
- **Heresy**: The belief or opinion contrary to orthodox religious doctrine, especially Christian doctrine.
- **Abjuration**: A formal renunciation of a belief or cause, which Joan signed under pressure during her trial.
- **Rehabilitation trial**: A posthumous inquest (1455–1456) that overturned Joan's conviction for heresy.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Nature of Visions**: Scholars debate whether Joan's visions were divine, neurological (e.g., epilepsy), psychiatric (e.g., schizophrenia), or strategic fabrications. The rehabilitation trial did not rule on their authenticity.
- **Cross-Dressing**: The theological and practical reasons for her wearing men's clothes remain discussed. Was it for practicality, protection, or a deliberate challenge to gender norms?
- **Political Motivations**: The extent to which her trial was a genuine ecclesiastical proceeding versus a political tool to discredit Charles VII is a key historical question.
- **Military Role**: Historians assess whether Joan was a true military strategist or primarily a charismatic figurehead who inspired others.
