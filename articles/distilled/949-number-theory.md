# Number theory

## Overview
Number theory is the branch of mathematics dedicated to the study of integers and their properties, relations, and generalizations (such as algebraic integers). One of the oldest mathematical disciplines alongside geometry, it is characterized by problems that are simple to state—like Fermat's Last Theorem or Goldbach's conjecture—but notoriously difficult to solve. Historically regarded as the epitome of pure mathematics with no practical use, number theory became foundational to modern cryptography in the 1970s when the difficulty of factoring large integers enabled public-key systems like RSA. The field employs diverse methods, ranging from elementary arithmetic to complex analysis, algebraic geometry, and probability theory.

## Timeline
- **c. 1800 BC** — Plimpton 322 tablet (Babylonia) lists Pythagorean triples, implying systematic generation methods.
- **c. 300 BC** — Euclid's *Elements* proves infinitude of primes, gives Euclidean algorithm for greatest common divisor.
- **c. 250 AD** — Diophantus of Alexandria writes *Arithmetica*, seeking rational solutions to polynomial equations (Diophantine equations).
- **3rd–5th c. AD** — *Sunzi Suanjing* (China) presents the Chinese remainder theorem.
- **476–550 AD** — Āryabhaṭa (India) develops *kuṭṭaka* (pulveriser) algorithm for solving simultaneous congruences.
- **628 AD** — Brahmagupta (India) begins systematic study of Pell's equation ($x^2 - Ny^2 = 1$).
- **9th c.** — Translation of Greek and Sanskrit works into Arabic (House of Wisdom); Qusta ibn Luqa translates Diophantus.
- **1607–1665** — Pierre de Fermat states Fermat's Little Theorem, Fermat's Last Theorem, and works on Pell's equations via correspondence.
- **1729** — Leonhard Euler begins work on number theory, proving Fermat's claims and founding analytic number theory.
- **1736–1813** — Joseph-Louis Lagrange proves four-square theorem, Wilson's theorem, and develops Pell's equation theory.
- **1798/1801** — Adrien-Marie Legendre states law of quadratic reciprocity; conjectures prime number theorem.
- **1801** — Carl Friedrich Gauss publishes *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae*, proving quadratic reciprocity, developing quadratic forms, and linking roots of unity to number theory.
- **1837** — Dirichlet proves theorem on arithmetic progressions using L-functions, founding modern analytic number theory.
- **1859** — Bernhard Riemann introduces complex zeta function, conjecturing nontrivial zeros lie on critical line (Riemann Hypothesis).
- **Late 19th c.** — Development of ideal theory (Kummer, Dedekind) and valuation theory resolves non-unique factorization in algebraic number fields; class field theory initiated.
- **1949** — Erdős and Selberg find elementary proof of Prime Number Theorem (avoiding complex analysis).
- **1970s** — Public-key cryptography (RSA) invented, based on difficulty of integer factorization; number theory gains major practical applications.
- **1994** — Andrew Wiles proves Fermat's Last Theorem using techniques from Diophantine geometry and modular forms.

## Body

### Definition and Scope
Number theory studies the integers ($\mathbb{Z} = \{\dots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \dots\}$) and objects constructed from them (rational numbers) or generalizing them (algebraic integers). It is traditionally called "higher arithmetic" to distinguish it from elementary arithmetic (numerical operations on real numbers). The field splits into subdisciplines based on methods: **elementary** (basic arithmetic proofs), **analytic** (real/complex analysis), **algebraic** (fields, rings, ideals), **geometric/Diophantine** (geometry of solution sets), plus probabilistic, combinatorial, computational, and applied branches.

### Historical Development
**Ancient Foundations:** The earliest artifact is the Babylonian Plimpton 322 (c. 1800 BC), listing Pythagorean triples generated by algebraic identities. Greek mathematics treated arithmetic (discrete numbers) separately from geometry (continuous magnitudes). Euclid (c. 300 BC) established the infinitude of primes and the Euclidean algorithm. Diophantus (c. 250 AD) pioneered the search for rational solutions to polynomial equations.
**Asian Contributions:** Chinese mathematics produced the Chinese remainder theorem (*Sunzi Suanjing*, 3rd–5th c.) and a general solution (*Da-yan-shu*, 1247). Indian mathematics developed independently: Āryabhaṭa's *kuṭṭaka* (pulveriser) solved linear congruences for astronomy; Brahmagupta (628) studied Pell's equation; Bhāskara II (12th c.) gave a general solution (chakravala method).
**Islamic Golden Age:** 9th-century translations of Greek and Sanskrit works preserved and extended knowledge. Al-Karajī built on Diophantus; Ibn al-Haytham knew Wilson's theorem.
**European Renaissance to 19th Century:** Fermat (1607–1665) revitalized the field via marginal notes and letters, stating key theorems without proof. Euler (1707–1783), prompted by Goldbach, proved Fermat's claims, linked continued fractions to Pell's equation, and initiated analytic methods. Lagrange, Legendre, and Gauss systematized the field. Gauss's *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801) proved quadratic reciprocity, developed binary quadratic forms, standardized congruence notation, and foreshadowed algebraic number theory via roots of unity.
**Modern Subdivisions (19th Century Onward):** The field gained self-consciousness as modern mathematics (complex analysis, group theory, Galois theory) provided new tools. Dirichlet (1837) founded analytic number theory proper using L-functions. Riemann (1859) connected the complex zeta function to prime distribution. Kummer and Dedekind developed ideal theory to restore unique factorization in algebraic number fields, leading to class field theory (1900–1950) for abelian extensions. The Langlands program now seeks to generalize this to non-abelian extensions.

### Elementary Number Theory
This subfield uses only basic arithmetic methods (no complex analysis). Core topics include:
*   **Divisibility and Primes:** $a$ divides $b$ ($a|b$) if $b=aq$. The Euclidean algorithm computes $\gcd(a,b)$ via repeated division ($a=bq+r$) and solves $ax+by=\gcd(a,b)$. The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic states every integer $>1$ has a unique prime factorization (e.g., $120 = 2^3 \times 3 \times 5$). The sieve of Eratosthenes finds primes by eliminating composites.
*   **Arithmetic Functions:** Euler's totient function $\varphi(n)$ counts integers $\le n$ coprime to $n$. Divisor functions count or sum divisors.
*   **Modular Arithmetic:** Congruence $a \equiv b \pmod n$ means $n | (a-b)$. Residue classes partition $\mathbb{Z}$. Fermat's Little Theorem: $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$ for prime $p \nmid a$. Euler's Theorem generalizes this: $a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$ for $\gcd(a,n)=1$. The Chinese Remainder Theorem solves systems of congruences with coprime moduli.
*   **Diophantine Equations:** Polynomial equations seeking integer/rational solutions. Linear cases solved by extended Euclidean algorithm. Pythagorean triples ($x^2+y^2=z^2$) and Pell's equation ($x^2-Ny^2=1$) are classical quadratic examples. Continued fractions represent numbers as nested fractions and solve Pell's equation.

### Analytic Number Theory
Defined by its tools (real/complex analysis) or concerns (estimates of size/density rather than exact identities). Key objects and problems:
*   **Prime Distribution:** The prime-counting function $\pi(x)$ is approximated by $x/\log x$ (Prime Number Theorem, proved 1896 by Hadamard/de la Vallée Poussin using complex analysis; elementary proof by Erdős/Selberg 1949). A better approximation is the logarithmic integral $\text{li}(x)$.
*   **Riemann Zeta Function:** $\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty n^{-s}$ for $\Re(s)>1$. Euler product $\zeta(s) = \prod_p (1-p^{-s})^{-1}$ links it to primes. Riemann extended it to $\mathbb{C}$ and hypothesized all nontrivial zeros have $\Re(s)=1/2$; this implies sharp bounds on prime distribution errors.
*   **Tools:** Dirichlet L-functions (generalize $\zeta$ for arithmetic progressions), sieve methods (estimate sizes of sifted sets), circle method (Hardy-Littlewood, for additive problems like Waring's problem), and modular/automorphic forms (central to modern proofs, e.g., Fermat's Last Theorem).
*   **Famous Conjectures:** Riemann Hypothesis, Goldbach (every even $>2$ is sum of two primes), Twin Primes (infinitely many $p, p+2$), Hardy-Littlewood conjectures.

### Algebraic Number Theory
Studies **algebraic numbers** (roots of polynomials with rational coefficients) and **number fields** (finite extensions of $\mathbb{Q}$, e.g., $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$).
*   **Failure of Unique Factorization:** In $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$, $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$; all four factors are irreducible. Unique factorization fails.
*   **Ideal Theory:** Kummer introduced "ideal numbers"; Dedekind replaced them with **ideals** (subsets of the ring of integers closed under addition and multiplication by ring elements). In the ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$, every nonzero ideal factors uniquely into prime ideals, restoring unique factorization at the ideal level.
*   **Valuation Theory:** An alternative/complementary approach using $p$-adic valuations to measure divisibility.
*   **Class Field Theory:** Classifies abelian extensions $L/K$ (where Galois group $\text{Gal}(L/K)$ is abelian) via generalized ideal class groups. Largely completed 1900–1950.
*   **Current Frontiers:** Iwasawa theory (growth of class groups in towers of fields); Langlands program (reciprocity laws for non-abelian extensions, connecting Galois representations to automorphic forms).

### Diophantine Geometry (Arithmetic Geometry)
Reframes Diophantine equations geometrically: solutions to $f(x_1,\dots,x_n)=0$ are points on an algebraic variety. The central question: are there rational/integer points, and are they finite or infinite?
*   **Curves and Genus:** For a curve $f(x,y)=0$, the genus $g$ (topological holes) dictates rational points: $g=0$ (conics) has 0 or infinitely many (parametrizable); $g=1$ (elliptic curves) has finitely generated group (Mordell-Weil theorem); $g \ge 2$ has finitely many rational points (Faltings' theorem, 1983, formerly Mordell conjecture).
*   **Fermat's Last Theorem:** $x^n+y^n=z^n$ has no nontrivial integer solutions for $n>2$. Wiles (1994) proved this by showing a hypothetical solution would yield an elliptic curve not modular, contradicting the Modularity Theorem (Taniyama-Shimura-Weil).
*   **Diophantine Approximation:** How well can a real $x$ be approximated by rationals $a/q$? Measure: $|x - a/q| < 1/q^c$. Roth's theorem: algebraic irrationals have approximation exponent $c=2$ (best possible). If a number admits approximations with $c>2$ infinitely often, it is transcendental (used to prove $\pi, e$ transcendental). Height functions measure arithmetic complexity of points, crucial for both geometry and approximation.

### Other Subfields
*   **Probabilistic Number Theory:** Treats integers as random variables. Example: Erdős–Kac theorem: number of prime factors of $n \le x$ is normally distributed with mean $\log\log x$ and variance $\log\log x$.
*   **Combinatorial Number Theory:** Studies additive structure. Van der Waerden's theorem: any finite coloring of $\mathbb{N}$ contains monochromatic arithmetic progressions. Szemerédi's theorem: sets of positive upper density contain arbitrarily long progressions. Green-Tao: primes contain arbitrarily long progressions.
*   **Computational Number Theory:** Focuses on algorithmic efficiency. Primality testing: AKS algorithm (2002) is polynomial time; Miller-Rabin is fast probabilistic. Factoring: no polynomial-time classical algorithm known (basis of RSA); Shor's algorithm (quantum) factors in polynomial time. Discrete logarithm and elliptic curve arithmetic underpin modern cryptography.

### Applications
*   **Cryptography:** RSA (factoring), Diffie-Hellman (discrete log), Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECDLP). Basis of TLS/SSL, digital signatures, blockchain.
*   **Computer Science:** Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) for polynomial multiplication and signal processing; hash tables, checksums, pseudorandom generators using primes/modular arithmetic.
*   **Error-Correcting Codes:** Reed-Solomon codes (polynomials over finite fields) used in CDs, QR codes, deep-space communication; algebraic geometry codes (Goppa) beat Gilbert-Varshamov bound.
*   **Physics:** Riemann zeta zeros statistically match energy levels of chaotic quantum systems (Montgomery-Odlyzko); $p$-adic strings, adelic physics.
*   **Numerical Analysis:** Number-theoretic transforms (NTT) for exact convolution; quasi-Monte Carlo methods using low-discrepancy sequences (e.g., Halton sequence based on prime bases).
*   **Music Theory:** Equal temperament divides octave (frequency ratio 2:1) into 12 equal semitones (ratio $2^{1/12}$); continued fractions explain rational approximations (e.g., 12/19, 53/84) for alternative tunings.

## Terms
- ****Integer**** — A whole number: positive, negative, or zero ($\mathbb{Z}$).
- ****Prime Number**** — Integer $p > 1$ whose only positive divisors are $1$ and $p$.
- ****Congruence**** — $a \equiv b \pmod n$ means $n$ divides $a-b$; $a,b$ have same remainder modulo $n$.
- ****Diophantine Equation**** — Polynomial equation (usually multi-variable) for which integer or rational solutions are sought.
- ****Algebraic Number**** — A complex number that is a root of a non-zero polynomial with rational coefficients.
- ****Number Field**** — A finite-degree field extension of the rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$ (e.g., $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$).
- ****Ideal**** — A subset of a ring closed under addition and under multiplication by any ring element; generalizes "multiples of $n$" to restore unique factorization.
- ****Zeta Function**** — $\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty n^{-s}$ ($\Re(s)>1$); analytically continued to $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{1\}$; encodes prime distribution via Euler product.
- ****Genus**** — Topological invariant of an algebraic curve (number of "holes"); determines finiteness of rational points (Faltings).
- ****Elliptic Curve**** — Smooth projective curve of genus 1 with a specified rational point; forms an abelian group; central to Wiles's proof and modern cryptography.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Riemann Hypothesis:** All nontrivial zeros of $\zeta(s)$ have real part $1/2$. Implications: optimal error bound for Prime Number Theorem, distribution of primes in short intervals, Lindelöf hypothesis.
*   **Goldbach's Conjecture:** Every even integer $>2$ is the sum of two primes. (Verified computationally to $4 \times 10^{18}$; weak form—odd $>5$ sum of three primes—proved by Helfgott 2013).
*   **Twin Prime Conjecture:** Infinitely many primes $p$ such that $p+2$ is prime. (Zhang 2014: bounded gaps $\le 70,000,000$; Polymath: $\le 246$).
*   **Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer Conjecture:** For an elliptic curve $E/\mathbb{Q}$, the order of vanishing of $L(E,s)$ at $s=1$ equals the rank of $E(\mathbb{Q})$; leading coefficient relates to arithmetic invariants (regulator, Tamagawa numbers, Sha).
*   **Langlands Program:** Vast web of conjectures connecting Galois representations (number theory) to automorphic forms (harmonic analysis/representation theory); "non-abelian class field theory."
*   **ABC Conjecture:** For coprime $a+b=c$, $\text{rad}(abc)^{1+\epsilon} > c$ for all but finitely many triples. Implies Fermat's Last Theorem for large $n$, Mordell conjecture, Szpiro's conjecture. (Mochizuki's claimed proof via Inter-universal Teichmüller theory remains controversial/unverified by consensus).
*   **Computational Hardness:** Is integer factoring in P? Is discrete log in P? Quantum algorithms (Shor) break both; post-quantum cryptography (lattices, isogenies, codes, hashes) seeks replacements.
*   **Elementary vs. Analytic Proofs:** Some theorems (e.g., Prime Number Theorem) have both; "elementary" avoids complex analysis but may be longer/less illuminating. Debate on what constitutes "elementary" (e.g., Wiener-Ikehara Tauberian theorem uses Fourier analysis).
