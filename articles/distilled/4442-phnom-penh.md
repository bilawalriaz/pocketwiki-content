# Phnom Penh

## Overview
Phnom Penh is the capital and most populous city of Cambodia, serving as the nation's political, economic, and cultural center. Its history is marked by periods of growth, abandonment, and dramatic upheaval, including its role as a royal capital, its near-destruction under the Khmer Rouge, and its modern reconstruction. The city's location at the confluence of major rivers has been central to its development and identity.

## Timeline
- **1372** — Legend of founding by Lady Penh, who discovered Buddha statues in a floating tree.
- **1434** — Becomes the national capital after the fall of Angkor Thom.
- **1505** — Capital status abandoned by subsequent kings for 360 years.
- **1865** — Regains capital status during the French colonial era.
- **1960s-70s** — Population swells with refugees from the Vietnam War and Cambodian civil war.
- **17 April 1975** — Khmer Rouge captures the city and forcibly evacuates its entire population.
- **1979** — Vietnamese-backed forces take the city; people begin to return and reconstruction starts.
- **2010** — Boeung Kak lake, the city's largest, is controversially filled for development.
- **2019** — Population reaches over 2.2 million.
- **2025** — Techo International Airport opens, replacing the old airport.

## Body

### Etymology and Founding
The city's name, Phnom Penh, means "Penh's hill," derived from Wat Phnom, a temple built on a hill by its legendary founder, Lady Penh. According to 14th-century legend, she found sacred statues in a floating tree, which was seen as a sign to move the capital from Angkor. The city's former official name, Krong Chaktomuk, means "city of four faces," referring to its location at the confluence of rivers.

### Historical Capital and Abandonment
Phnom Penh first became the capital in 1434 after King Ponhea Yat moved the royal court from Angkor Thom. It remained the capital for 73 years until 1505, when internal conflicts led subsequent kings to abandon it and establish capitals elsewhere. The city was largely uninhabited for 360 years until it was re-established as the capital in 1865 during French colonial rule.

### Modern Growth and Conflict
During Cambodia's independence in the mid-20th century, Phnom Penh modernized and earned the nickname "Pearl of Asia." Its population exploded in the 1960s and 1970s due to refugees fleeing war. The Khmer Rouge's capture of the city in 1975 led to a catastrophic forced evacuation, with the entire population expelled to rural labor camps. The city remained largely empty until the regime's fall in 1979.

### Reconstruction and Contemporary City
Since 1979, Phnom Penh has undergone reconstruction with international aid. It is now a primate city, home to over 2 million people (2019 census). Its economy is based on garments, trade, and tourism. The city hosts major events like ASEAN Summits and is developing its infrastructure, including new airports and expressways. It is also a cultural hub, hosting the annual Water Festival.

## Terms
- **Primate city**: A city that is disproportionately larger and more influential than any other city in the country.
- **Confluence**: The junction of two rivers.
- **Khmer Rouge**: The radical communist movement that ruled Cambodia from 1975 to 1979.
- **New Khmer Architecture**: A modernist architectural movement in the 1950s-60s that fused international styles with traditional Khmer elements.
- **Khan**: An administrative division of a Cambodian city.
- **Sangkat**: A subdivision of a khan.
- **Phum**: A village, the smallest administrative unit.
- **Tuk-tuk**: A motorized auto rickshaw common in Southeast Asia.
- **Cyclo**: A cycle rickshaw.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact date and nature of the initial settlement at Phnom Penh are debated, with archaeological evidence suggesting a 5th-century community but the legendary founding dating to 1372.
- The city's population figures from the 1998 and 2008 censuses are noted to conflict with other historical population data, requiring clarification.
- The controversial filling of Boeung Kak lake in 2010 for property development highlights ongoing tensions between urban development and environmental/social concerns.
