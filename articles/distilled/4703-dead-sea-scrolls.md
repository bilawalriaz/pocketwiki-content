# Dead Sea Scrolls

## Overview
The Dead Sea Scrolls are a collection of ancient Jewish manuscripts discovered in caves near the Dead Sea, dating from the 3rd century BCE to the 1st century CE. They are the oldest surviving biblical manuscripts and include texts from the Hebrew Bible, deuterocanonical works, and sectarian writings. Their discovery revolutionized understanding of the Second Temple period, the formation of the biblical canon, and the origins of Rabbinic Judaism and Christianity.

## Timeline
- **c. 3rd–1st century BCE** — Scrolls are written and hidden in caves near Qumran.
- **1946–1947** — Initial discovery of seven scrolls in Cave 1 by Bedouin shepherds.
- **1948** — Scrolls are first photographed and announced by ASOR.
- **1949** — Cave 1 is officially rediscovered and excavated.
- **1951–1956** — Major excavations at Qumran uncover scrolls in Caves 2–11.
- **1953** — Scrolls are moved to the Palestine Archaeological Museum in Jerusalem.
- **1967** — Israel acquires the scrolls following the Six-Day War.
- **1991** — Access restrictions are lifted; facsimiles are published.
- **2011** — Digital images of scrolls are made public online.
- **2017** — A 12th cave is discovered, but it was looted in the 1950s.
- **2021** — New biblical fragments are found in a cave near the Dead Sea.

## Body

### Discovery and Contents
The scrolls were discovered between 1946 and 1956 in 12 caves near the Hellenistic settlement of Khirbet Qumran. The initial find by Bedouin shepherds led to archaeological excavations. The 981 manuscripts from Qumran are mostly fragments, with Cave 4 alone yielding about 15,000 pieces from 500 texts. The texts are categorized into three groups: biblical manuscripts (about 40%), non-biblical Second Temple texts (about 30%), and sectarian documents (about 30%). They are written primarily in Hebrew, with some in Aramaic and Greek, on parchment, papyrus, and one copper scroll.

### Origin and Dating
The dominant theory, the Qumran–Essene hypothesis, posits that the scrolls were written by the Essene sect living at Qumran and hidden during the Jewish Revolt (66–68 CE). Evidence includes parallels between the Community Rule and descriptions of Essenes by Josephus, and archaeological finds like ritual baths and a possible scriptorium. Alternative theories suggest a Sadducean origin, a Jerusalem Temple library origin, or that the scrolls were brought from Jerusalem during the Roman destruction in 70 CE. Radiocarbon and paleographic dating place most scrolls between the 3rd century BCE and the 1st century CE. A 2025 study using AI and new radiocarbon data suggests some manuscripts may be older, with Herodian and Hasmonean script styles overlapping into the 2nd and 3rd centuries BCE.

### Preservation and Publication
The scrolls were preserved by the arid cave conditions. After discovery, poor handling and storage caused significant deterioration. Publication was initially controlled by a small editorial team, causing controversy. In 1991, restrictions were lifted, and the 40-volume *Discoveries in the Judaean Desert* series was completed in 2009. Digital imaging projects, including partnerships with NASA and Google, have made high-resolution images publicly accessible.

### Significance and Ownership
The scrolls pushed the date of the oldest Hebrew biblical manuscripts back over a millennium, demonstrating remarkable textual stability while also revealing variant texts. They include parts of all Hebrew Bible books except Esther. Ownership is disputed: Israel claims the collection based on historical and religious significance, while Jordan and the Palestinian Authority contest this, citing discovery during Jordanian administration and humanitarian grounds. The main collection is housed in the Shrine of the Book at the Israel Museum, Jerusalem.

## Terms
- **Qumran–Essene theory**: The hypothesis that the scrolls were produced by the Essene Jewish sect living at Khirbet Qumran.
- **Deuterocanonical**: Books included in the Catholic and Orthodox biblical canons but not in the Hebrew Bible's Tanakh (e.g., Tobit, Sirach).
- **Sectarian manuscripts**: Texts outlining the specific rules and beliefs of a particular Jewish group, not part of the standard biblical canon.
- **Paleography**: The study of ancient handwriting to date manuscripts based on letter forms.
- **Radiocarbon dating**: A scientific method to determine the age of organic materials by measuring the decay of carbon-14.
- **Genizah**: A storage area in a synagogue or cemetery for worn-out sacred texts.
- **Miqvah**: A ritual bath used for Jewish purification.
- **Masoretic Text**: The authoritative Hebrew text of the Jewish Bible, standardized by Jewish scribes (Masoretes) around the 10th century CE.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Origin of the scrolls**: The Qumran–Essene theory is challenged by scholars proposing a Sadducean, Jerusalem Temple, or other sectarian origins.
- **Ownership and custody**: Israel, Jordan, and the Palestinian Authority all claim rights to the scrolls.
- **Dating**: New AI-assisted studies suggest some scrolls may be older than previously thought, with overlapping script styles.
- **Significance of textual variants**: Scholars debate whether the scrolls show a fluid biblical text before canonization or support the fidelity of the Masoretic tradition.
