# Lilongwe

## Overview
Lilongwe is the capital and largest city of Malawi, serving as the nation's political and administrative center. Located in the central region, it is a major economic and transportation hub whose rapid growth from a small trading post to a metropolis reflects Malawi's post-independence development strategy.

## Timeline
- **c. 1902** — Lilongwe is established as a *boma* (administrative post) by local leader Njewa.
- **1904** — Becomes an administrative centre.
- **1920s** — Grows as an agricultural market due to its location at a road junction.
- **1947** — Officially recognized as a town.
- **1965** — President Hastings Kamuzu Banda selects it as an economic growth point.
- **1968** — Lilongwe Master Plan is completed to guide its development as the future capital.
- **1975** — Officially becomes the capital of Malawi, replacing Zomba.
- **1978** — Lilongwe Urban Structure Plan is adopted.
- **1986** — Initial stage of planned urban development is completed.
- **2005** — Final government offices are relocated from Zomba.
- **2011** — A new Urban Development Master Plan is approved.

## Body

### History and Planning
Lilongwe was founded around 1902 as a local administrative post and grew into an agricultural market centre. In 1965, it was chosen as an economic growth point, and in 1975, it became the national capital, replacing Zomba. The move aimed to centralize government administration and stimulate development in the central and northern regions. A 1968 Master Plan established a multi-centred urban form with four sectors (Old Town, City Centre, Kanengo, Lumbadzi) to avoid congestion. Development was guided by a series of zoning plans, with major infrastructure like the international airport and rail links built in the 1970s and 1980s. Despite planning, rapid population growth has led to significant unplanned settlements and urban sprawl, particularly to the south and west.

### Demographics and Society
Lilongwe's population has grown from under 20,000 in 1966 to 989,318 in the 2018 Census, with an annual growth rate of 4.3%. The Chewa are the largest ethnic group (42.28%), followed by the Ngoni (17.13%). The dominant religion is the Church of Central Africa Presbyterian (23.15%), with Catholicism (17.28%) and Islam (11.12%) as significant minorities. The city is divided into numbered "Areas," with land use zoned for residential (low, medium, high density), commercial, industrial, and government purposes. The economy is dominated by government and public institutions, with key industries in Kanengo including food and tobacco processing.

### Geography and Infrastructure
The city is located on the East African Rift Valley plateau at an altitude of 1,050 m (3,440 ft) along the Lilongwe River. It has a humid subtropical climate with warm summers and mild winters. Infrastructure includes Kamuzu International Airport, a primary road network with ring roads, and the Sena railway line connecting to Zambia and Salima. The city is divided into a modern "New City" with government offices and an "Old City" with markets and traditional commerce.

## Terms
- **Boma**: An administrative post or fort, typically established during the colonial era.
- **Central Region Plateau**: A fertile agricultural area in central Malawi, important for tobacco cultivation.
- **Humid subtropical climate (Köppen: Cwa)**: A climate with hot, humid summers and mild, drier winters.
- **Multi-centred urban form**: A city planning model that clusters development around several distinct centres to reduce congestion.
- **Unplanned settlements**: Areas of urban development that occur without official zoning or infrastructure, often housing the urban poor.
- **Urban sprawl**: The uncontrolled expansion of urban areas into surrounding land.
- **Zoning scheme**: A legal plan that designates specific land uses (e.g., residential, commercial, industrial) for different parts of a city.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary debate concerns the effectiveness of Lilongwe's urban planning versus its rapid, often unplanned, growth. While the original Master Plan aimed for orderly, multi-centred development, the city has experienced significant sprawl and the proliferation of informal settlements, creating challenges for service delivery and land management. The need to urgently secure land for planned residential development is a key ongoing issue.
