# Franks

## Overview
The Franks were a group of Germanic tribes who first appeared near the Rhine River in the 3rd century AD. They evolved from a collection of raiders and Roman allies into the dominant political force in Western Europe, establishing the Merovingian and Carolingian dynasties. Their legacy includes the formation of medieval France and the Holy Roman Empire, and the term "Frank" became a generic identifier for Western Europeans in many non-European languages.

## Timeline
- **c. 250 AD** — The term "Franks" first appears, used for tribes like the Bructeri and Chattuari near the Rhine.
- **287/288 AD** — Frankish king Genobaud is forced to become a Roman client by Emperor Maximian.
- **358 AD** — Julian the Apostate makes agreements with the Salian Franks, allowing them to settle in Roman territory.
- **406 AD** — Franks attempt to block Vandals and Alans from crossing the Rhine into Gaul.
- **c. 440s** — Frankish king Chlodio pushes south into more Romanized lands in northern France.
- **451 AD** — Franks fight on both sides in the Battle of the Catalaunian Plains against Attila's Huns.
- **481/482 AD** — Childeric I dies; his son Clovis I becomes king and begins uniting Frankish kingdoms.
- **496 AD** — Clovis I is baptized, converting to Catholic Christianity.
- **509 AD** — Clovis I becomes the first king of all Franks after conquering Cologne.
- **751 AD** — Pepin the Short deposes the last Merovingian king, inaugurating the Carolingian dynasty.
- **800 AD** — Charlemagne is crowned Holy Roman Emperor by the Pope.
- **870 AD** — The Frankish realm is permanently divided into western and eastern kingdoms.

## Body

### Origins and Early History
The Franks emerged during the Crisis of the Third Century (235–284 AD) as Roman control over the Rhine frontier weakened. Pre-existing tribes like the Bructeri and Chattuari began being called Franks. Archaeological evidence shows a population decrease in the Roman province of Germania Inferior around 250 AD, possibly due to Roman deportations. Early Franks were active as raiders, including by sea, and the Romans did not initially distinguish them clearly from Saxons. By the 4th century, "Franks" became a common collective term for several tribes, eclipsing older names like "Germani."

### Integration with Rome and Expansion
Franks lived semi-independently on both sides of the Rhine and were recruited into the Roman army, with some achieving high rank. After Roman administration collapsed in Gaul in the 5th century, Frankish kings divided Germania Inferior. King Chlodio expanded south into northern France. Following the death of the last stable Western Roman emperor in 455, Frankish leader Childeric I and then his son Clovis I consolidated power. Clovis defeated other Frankish kings and the Roman ruler Syagrius, uniting most of Gaul by 509 and establishing the Merovingian dynasty.

### The Merovingian and Carolingian Kingdoms
Clovis's conversion to Catholic Christianity in 496 created a strong alliance with the Church. The Merovingian realm was divided among heirs, leading to sub-kingdoms like Austrasia and Neustria. Royal authority declined until Pepin the Short, a Carolingian mayor of the palace, deposed the last Merovingian king in 751. The Carolingian Empire, under Charlemagne, unified much of Western Europe. His coronation as Emperor in 800 symbolized a revival of Western imperial authority. After Charlemagne's death, the empire was divided among his grandsons, leading to the permanent split of the Frankish realm in 870.

### Language and Culture
The early Frankish language, a Germanic tongue, influenced Old French and is ancestral to Old Dutch. Northern Gaul became bilingual for centuries. Frankish art initially belonged to the Migration Period, with few remains. The later Carolingian Renaissance saw a revival of learning and art, with major architectural projects like the Palatine Chapel at Aachen. Frankish law was codified, with the Salic Law applying to Salian Franks and the Ripuarian Law to others.

### Religion
Before their conversion, the Franks practiced Germanic paganism, likely polytheistic with rituals centered on local deities. The Merovingian dynasty claimed descent from the water-god Quinotaur. Clovis I's baptism in 496 was pivotal, as the Franks became the only major Germanic tribe to convert to Catholic Christianity rather than Arianism. This fostered a close relationship with the Papacy, though full Christianization of the population took centuries and was aided by monasteries.

## Terms
- **Merovingian dynasty**: The first ruling dynasty of the Franks, founded by Clovis I, known for its long-haired kings.
- **Carolingian dynasty**: The Frankish dynasty that succeeded the Merovingians, most famous for Charlemagne.
- **Salian Franks**: A subgroup of Franks whose legal code, the Salic Law, became foundational in medieval France.
- **Ripuarian Franks**: A subgroup of Franks living in the Rhineland, whose laws were recorded in the Lex Ribuaria.
- **Lex Salica (Salic Law)**: The ancient Frankish legal code, notable for its role in later French succession laws.
- **Austrasia**: The northeastern region of the Frankish kingdom, heartland of the Carolingian family.
- **Neustria**: The western region of the Frankish kingdom, centered in northern France.
- **Magister militum**: A high-ranking military commander in the late Roman Empire, a position held by several Franks.
- **Leudes**: Sworn followers of a Frankish king, forming the core of his military retinue.
- **Antrustiones**: Senior, aristocratic soldiers who served in the king's bodyguard.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact etymology of "Frank" is uncertain, with proposals linking it to words for "free," "javelin," or "fierce."
- The historicity and precise meaning of Frankish mythological origin stories, which link them to Troy and Pannonia, are debated.
- The nature of the relationship between the Roman commander Aegidius and the Frankish leader Childeric I in the mid-5th century is unclear.
- The extent to which early Franks were primarily infantry or cavalry is debated, as Byzantine sources conflict with archaeological and Frankish legal evidence.
