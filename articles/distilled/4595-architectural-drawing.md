# Architectural drawing

## Overview
An architectural drawing is a technical representation of a building or project, used to develop, communicate, and document design ideas. These drawings follow specific conventions to ensure clarity and accuracy, serving as a critical bridge between concept and construction. Their methods have evolved from manual drafting to digital tools like CAD and BIM, fundamentally changing architectural practice.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Earliest known architectural drawings appear (e.g., Egyptian tomb plans).
- **Renaissance** — Architects study and record classical ruins, using drawings to develop new designs.
- **18th–19th C.** — Standardized paper sizes and scales become common in practice.
- **Early 20th C.** — Shift from ink on paper to tracing paper for mechanical copying.
- **1970s** — Axonometric drawings gain popularity in presentation, notably by Modern Movement architects.
- **1980s–90s** — Computer-Aided Design (CAD) software begins to replace manual drafting.
- **2000s** — Building Information Modeling (BIM) emerges, enabling integrated 3D modeling.
- **2010s** — Parametric design tools allow for complex organic forms.
- **2020s** — Virtual reality and digital collaboration tools become more prevalent in practice.

## Body

### Conventions and Scale
Architectural drawings use standardized views (plans, sections, elevations) and are drawn to scale to represent relative sizes accurately. Common scales range from 1:100 for overall layouts to full-size for construction details. Dimensions are typically written ("figured") on drawings to prevent errors from copying, as scaling directly from a print is unreliable.

### Standard Views
The **floor plan** is a horizontal section cut through a building, showing spatial arrangement. A **site plan** shows the building in its broader context, including property boundaries and access. **Elevations** are flat views of a building's façades. **Cross sections** are vertical cuts revealing relationships between levels. **Isometric and axonometric projections** are 3D views that maintain scale, with axonometrics using a 45-degree grid for easier drafting from plans.

### Purpose and Types
Drawings are classified by purpose. **Presentation drawings** explain a design's merits. **Survey drawings** document existing conditions. **Record drawings** preserve historical or "as-built" information. **Working drawings** are comprehensive sets used for construction, subdivided into location, assembly, and component drawings.

### Drafting and Digital Tools
Traditionally, drawings were manually produced on drafting tables with tools like T-squares and ink pens. The late 20th century saw a major shift to **Computer-Aided Design (CAD)**, which increased speed and precision. **Building Information Modeling (BIM)** is a collaborative 3D modeling process where 2D views are generated from a shared model, improving coordination. **Parametric design** uses rules to create complex, scalable forms.

### Perspective and Visualization
**Perspective** drawing creates a realistic view from a fixed point, typically using two-point perspective. **Architectural animations** are short films generated from 3D models to show a proposed building in motion. **Virtual reality** allows for immersive, interactive exploration of designs.

## Terms
*   **Floor Plan:** A horizontal section cut through a building, viewed from above, showing the arrangement of spaces.
*   **Elevation:** A flat, orthographic view of one side or façade of a building.
*   **Cross Section (Section):** A vertical cut through a building, showing the relationship between different levels.
*   **Scale:** The ratio of a drawn dimension to the actual dimension of the object.
*   **CAD (Computer-Aided Design):** The use of computer software to create technical drawings.
*   **BIM (Building Information Modeling):** A collaborative process using a shared 3D model to generate coordinated 2D drawings and data.
*   **Parametric Design:** A method where relationships between elements are defined by rules, allowing for complex, scalable forms.
*   **Axonometric Projection:** A 3D view where the plan is drawn on a skewed grid (often 45 degrees) and verticals are projected straight up, maintaining scale.

## Debates and Open questions
*   The distinction between **isometric** and **axonometric** terms is noted as confusing, with different disciplines using them differently.
*   A debate exists around **parametric design**, questioning whether its complex forms properly respond to user needs and practical requirements.
*   The move to digital tools like **BIM** and **CAD** has separated skilled operators from the design process, as many architects have little knowledge of the software.
*   The **COVID-19 pandemic** accelerated digital collaboration, raising questions about the reduced role of physical review (e.g., touching materials, reviewing printed drawings together).
