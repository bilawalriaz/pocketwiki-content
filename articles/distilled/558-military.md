# Military

## Overview
A military is a heavily armed, highly organized force authorized by a sovereign state primarily for warfare and defence against external threats. It functions as a distinct social subculture with dedicated infrastructure—housing, legal systems, logistics, and healthcare—and operates under a strict command hierarchy governed by military law. Beyond combat, militaries perform diverse roles including internal security, disaster relief, and ceremonial duties. The profession of soldiering is older than recorded history. Some images of classical antiquity portray the power and feats of military leaders. The Battle of Kadesh in 1274 BC from the reign of Ramses II features in bas-relief monuments. The first Emperor of a unified China, Qin Shi Huang, created the Terracotta Army to represent his military might.

## Timeline
- **1274 BC** — Battle of Kadesh depicted in bas-relief monuments under Ramses II.
- **6th century BCE** — Sun Tzu writes *The Art of War*, influencing Asian and later Western doctrine.
- **c. 50 BC** — Julius Caesar writes commentaries on the Gallic Wars and Roman Civil War.
- **Late Roman period** — Vegetius writes *De Re Militari*, basis for European tactics until late 17th century.
- **1582** — First recorded English use of the word "military" (spelled *militarie*).
- **1625** — Hugo Grotius publishes *On the Law of War and Peace*, shaping humanitarian warfare law; Gustavus Adolphus echoes humanitarian approach.
- **1741 / 1802** — Royal Military Academy (UK) and United States Military Academy (US) named reflecting "military" adjective usage.
- **19th–20th centuries** — Trench warfare dominates WWI (Gallipoli, Western Front), causing stalemates and high casualties.
- **1939–1945** — WWII drives rapid advances: radar, penicillin, jet aircraft, guided missiles, nuclear weapons.
- **1945 onward** — Cold War institutionalizes constant arms race and capability development.
- **2017** — 50 states still rely partly on personnel under age 18 (usually 16–17).
- **2020** — Study suggests US military personnel socio-economic status at parity or slightly higher than civilian population regarding parental income, parental wealth, and cognitive abilities.

## Body

### Etymology and Definitions
The term "military" derives from Latin *militaris* (from *miles*, "soldier") via French, first recorded in English in 1582. As a noun, it refers to a country's armed forces or its senior officer corps; as an adjective, it originally described soldiers and soldiering but broadened during the Napoleonic Wars to encompass all armed forces activity (e.g., military intelligence, military history). The names of both the Royal Military Academy (1741) and United States Military Academy (1802) reflect this adjective usage. A technical distinction sometimes separates "military" (core branches: army, navy, air force, etc.) from "armed forces," which may include paramilitary forces like armed police.

### Organization: Personnel, Rank, and Recruitment
Military activity depends fundamentally on people despite technological advances. Organization relies on a universal command hierarchy: **officers** (strategic decision-makers), **non-commissioned officers** (NCOs), and **enlisted personnel** (lowest rank, e.g., private). Personnel also fill **trade roles**: combat (infantry), combat support (engineers), and combat service support (logistics). Recruitment is either voluntary or via conscription. As of 2017, 50 states still enlisted children under 18 (typically 16–17). Female representation varies widely (3% India to 27% South Africa). Historically, enlisted recruits often came from lower socio-economic backgrounds, though a 2020 US study found parity or slight improvement relative to civilians regarding parental income, parental wealth, and cognitive abilities, attributing the shift to higher modern entry standards.

### Obligations, Law, and Training
Full-time service typically requires 2–6 years; reserve service allows civilian careers with periodic training. Military law creates unique offences (AWOL, desertion, disobedience) adjudicated by court martial, and restricts rights like unionizing and free speech. **Initial training** resocializes recruits through suppression of individuality (uniforms, head shaving), rigid routine control, psychological stressors (sleep/food deprivation), and drill instructors modeling the "ideal soldier" to condition conformity and readiness to kill and face death.

### Intelligence and Capability Development
**Military intelligence (MI)** identifies threats through analysis of the operational environment, hostile/friendly forces, and civilian populations. It quantifies capability comparisons (e.g., "largest armed forces," "strongest military") and informs national defence policy resource allocation. **Capability development** translates threats into requirements across strategic, operational, and tactical levels. It involves doctrine creation (determining *how* forces fight), design specifications, procurement, force structuring, realistic training, logistics (supply, maintenance, medical), and post-conflict demobilization. **Military science** researches psychology, communications, intelligence tech, and weapons systems (from carriers to rations) not found commercially.

### Logistics
Logistics enables deployment and sustainment of capability. It manages the supply chain—transport (trucks to container ships), field supply dumps, forward supply points, engineering recovery, maintenance, and munitions storage/disposal. Life-extension programs maintain equipment in peacetime.

### Levels of Combat Victory
*   **Strategic:** Managed by commander-in-chief; employs national/allied forces (army groups, fleets); long-term (months/years); focuses on war supply and planning over field management.
*   **Operational:** Coordinates tactics with strategy (operational art); scale between tactical (line of sight) and strategic (production/politics); executed by Field Armies/Corps; duration one week to a month. Pioneered by the German army pre-WWII.
*   **Tactical:** Methods for direct enemy engagement; hours/days; unit-level (squadrons to divisions). Evolved from ancient formations to modern ambushes, air assaults, guerrilla hit-and-run, and suicide attacks. Key historical texts: Sun Tzu (*Art of War*), Caesar, Aelianus Tacticus, Vegetius (*De Re Militari* maxim: "let he who desires peace prepare for war"). Clausewitz (*On War*, 1832) defined strategy as "the employment of battles to gain the end of war," subordinating military goals to political aims. Trench warfare (19th–20th C., WWI) caused stalemates broken only by massive casualties crossing "no man's land."

### Technology Evolution
Tools distinguish the military. Progression: Stone Age flint → Bronze/Iron Age swords → ranged weapons (sling, bow) → horse domestication (cavalry/logistics) → wheel (chariot, siege engines) → composite/long/crossbows → gunpowder (China, then Europe: mortars, muskets, rifles, artillery) → industrialization (machine gun, breech-loading artillery, smokeless powder, internal combustion, submarines, torpedoes) → WWI tanks/aviation → WWII radar, penicillin, jets, guided missiles, helicopters, **nuclear weapons** (radiation effects known by early 1950s) → Cold War institutionalized arms race (main battle tanks, aircraft, ships) → guided missiles (all branches) → information technology/space reconnaissance → robotic systems (UAVs/UGVs) → renewable fuels (US target: 50% alternative energy).

### Society, Law, and Ethics
In democracies, the military is a public force; support varies by threat perception and cost. **Military humanism** justifies actions via humanitarian causes (disaster relief). Transparency fights corruption (per Transparency International 2013). Militaries often form "societies within societies" with internal economies, education, and medicine. **Private Military Companies (PMCs)** provide security where state forces are absent. **Militarism** posits civilian dependence on military goals for independence. **Laws of war** evolved from Grotius (1625) and Gustavus Adolphus; post-1945 **Geneva Conventions** constrain treatment of prisoners/civilians but rarely apply to internal security use. International protocols ban WMDs; national codes (e.g., US Uniform Code of Military Justice) prosecute war crimes via court martial.

## Terms
- ****Armed Forces**** — State-authorized military branches (army, navy, air force, etc.); sometimes includes paramilitary forces in technical usage.
- ****Non-Commissioned Officer (NCO)**** — Enlisted leader (e.g., sergeant) ranking above personnel but below commissioned officers.
- ****Conscription**** — Mandatory state enrollment of personnel into military service.
- ****Military Intelligence (MI)**** — Organization, system, and process for identifying threats and analyzing capabilities of potential aggressors.
- ****Doctrine**** — Formalized concepts and methods governing how military forces are employed to achieve war objectives.
- ****Operational Art / Mobility**** — Command level linking tactical details to strategic goals; executed by Field Armies/Corps over weeks.
- ****Logistics**** — Management of supply chain, transport, maintenance, munitions, and field engineering to sustain combat operations.
- ****Court Martial**** — Military court trying personnel for offences under military law (e.g., AWOL, desertion).
- ****Geneva Conventions**** — International treaties (post-1945) establishing humanitarian constraints on treatment of prisoners and civilians in war.
- ****Private Military Company (PMC)**** — Private entity hired for security, escort, or protection where state forces are absent or untrusted.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Socio-economic representation:** Whether modern volunteer forces reflect national demographics or disproportionately recruit the disadvantaged (US data shows conflicting trends between post-1973 and 2020 studies).
*   **Child recruitment:** Persistence of under-18 enlistment in 50 states as of 2017 despite global adult-only norms.
*   **Civilian control vs. military autonomy:** Balancing Clausewitz’s principle of political primacy with operational independence.
*   **Internal security role:** Application of laws of war (Geneva Conventions) rarely extends to military use against domestic protests/uprisings.
*   **Transparency vs. secrecy:** Public oversight requirements conflict with operational secrecy needs for intelligence and capability.
*   **Autonomous weapons:** Growing role of robotic systems (UAVs/UGVs) and information warfare raises unresolved ethical and legal questions.
*   **Militarism vs. soft power:** Societal debate on whether security is best served by military culture/capability (hard power) or diplomatic/economic influence (soft power).
