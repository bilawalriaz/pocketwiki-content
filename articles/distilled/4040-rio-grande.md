# Rio Grande

## Overview
The Rio Grande (known as the Río Bravo in Mexico) is one of the principal rivers of the Southwestern United States and Northern Mexico. It is a vital water source for seven U.S. and Mexican states, flowing through arid and semi-arid lands. Since the mid-20th century, only about 20% of its water reaches the Gulf of Mexico due to heavy consumption for irrigation and urban use. The river also forms a significant portion of the international border between the two countries.

## Timeline
- **c. 13 million years ago** — An axial river exists in the Española Basin, an early part of the ancestral Rio Grande system.
- **c. 2.06 million years ago** — The ancestral Rio Grande integrates southward and begins flowing into Texas.
- **1598** — Don Juan de Oñate establishes the Spanish colony of Santa Fe de Nuevo Méjico near the Rio Grande.
- **1846** — The disputed border between Mexico and Texas contributes to the start of the Mexican–American War.
- **1848** — The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo establishes the Rio Grande as the U.S.–Mexico border from El Paso to the Gulf.
- **1905** — The Rio Grande Project is approved, beginning federal allocation of the river's water between states and Mexico.
- **1938** — The Rio Grande Compact is signed, creating an interstate agreement for water sharing between Colorado, New Mexico, and Texas.
- **1944** — The U.S. and Mexico sign a treaty regarding the river's water allocation.
- **2001** — A sandbar blocks the river's mouth, preventing it from reaching the Gulf of Mexico for the first time in recorded history.
- **2022** — The Rio Grande runs dry for about 50 miles in the Middle Rio Grande Valley, including through Albuquerque.

## Body

### Geography and Course
The Rio Grande originates in the San Juan Mountains of south-central Colorado. It flows south through New Mexico, passing through the Rio Grande Gorge and the Middle Rio Grande Valley, which includes the cities of Albuquerque and Las Cruces. South of El Paso, Texas, and Ciudad Juárez, Chihuahua, the river forms the international border. Its major tributary by discharge is the Rio Conchos from Mexico; by drainage basin size, it is the Pecos River from the U.S. The river ends in a delta at the Gulf of Mexico.

### History and Human Use
Human presence in the valley dates back to at least 10,800 BCE (Folsom culture). The Ancestral Puebloan culture migrated to the valley around 1130 CE due to drought, leading to conflict and cultural merging. Spanish exploration began in the 16th century, with colonization starting in 1598. The river's role as a border was formalized after the Mexican–American War in 1848. It was also an escape route for enslaved people from Texas, as Mexico had abolished slavery.

### Water Rights and Management
Water allocation is governed by a complex series of compacts and treaties. The 1905 Rio Grande Project was the first congressionally directed allocation of an interstate river, dividing water between New Mexico, Texas, and Mexico. The 1938 Rio Grande Compact created a commission and system of gaging stations to manage flows between Colorado, New Mexico, and Texas. The U.S. and Mexico share water under treaties administered by the International Boundary and Water Commission (IBWC). A major engineering project, the San Juan–Chama Diversion, transports water from Colorado River basin tributaries to supplement the Rio Grande for New Mexico municipalities like Albuquerque.

### Navigation and Crossings
The river is generally not navigable except near its mouth. In the 19th century, over 200 steamboats operated on its lower reaches, and during the Mexican–American War, steamboats were used to transport troops. Major international border crossings exist at cities like El Paso–Ciudad Juárez, Laredo–Nuevo Laredo, and Brownsville–Matamoros.

### Current Challenges
The river's future is uncertain due to chronic drought, climate change, and over-appropriation (more legal water rights exist than water in the river). The section from Las Cruces downstream frequently runs dry. Elephant Butte Reservoir, the main storage facility, has been at critically low capacity (e.g., 5.9% in November 2021). In 2022, the water debt owed to Texas increased significantly. These shortages threaten ecosystems and species like the Rio Grande silvery minnow.

## Terms
- **Endorheic basin:** A drainage basin that retains water and allows no outflow to other external bodies of water.
- **Rio Grande Compact:** An interstate agreement (1938) between Colorado, New Mexico, and Texas that allocates the river's water.
- **San Juan–Chama Diversion Project:** A system that transports water from tributaries of the Colorado River to the Rio Grande basin.
- **International Boundary and Water Commission (IBWC):** A joint U.S.–Mexico agency that administers treaties and manages border water issues.
- **Over-appropriated:** A situation where more water rights have been legally allocated than the river's actual flow.
- **Bosque:** A gallery forest or woodland found along the floodplains of rivers in the Southwestern U.S.
- **Elephant Butte Reservoir:** The largest reservoir on the Rio Grande, located in New Mexico, critical for water storage and irrigation.
- **Rio Grande silvery minnow:** An endangered fish species native to the Rio Grande, whose survival is threatened by low water flows.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Water Allocation:** There is ongoing tension over water sharing, particularly due to Texas's claims of under-provision since 1992 and the growing water debt.
- **Treaty Reexamination:** Drought conditions have led to calls from locals in New Mexico, Texas, and Mexico to reexamine the 1944 U.S.–Mexico water treaty.
- **Future Flow:** The river's flow through areas like Las Cruces is projected to shorten dramatically (e.g., only June-July in 2021) due to climate change and overuse, with severe ecological consequences.
- **Management of Drought:** Strategies like subsidizing farmers to fallow fields and shifting to groundwater pumping are being implemented but face challenges in the face of increasing drought.
