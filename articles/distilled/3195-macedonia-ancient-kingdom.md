# Macedonia (ancient kingdom)

## Overview
Ancient Macedonia was a kingdom on the periphery of Classical Greece that rose to dominate the Greek world under Philip II and his son Alexander the Great. Its conquests under Alexander spread Greek culture across the Near East, inaugurating the Hellenistic period and fundamentally shaping the ancient world. The kingdom's power declined after Alexander's death, eventually falling to the Roman Republic in the 2nd century BC.

## Timeline
- **c. 7th century BC** — The Argead dynasty establishes Aigai (modern Vergina) as the capital.
- **511/510 BC** — Amyntas I submits to the Achaemenid Persian Empire as a vassal.
- **359–336 BC** — Reign of Philip II; he reforms the army, subdues Greece, and establishes the League of Corinth.
- **336–323 BC** — Reign of Alexander the Great; he conquers the Achaemenid Empire, creating a vast Hellenistic empire.
- **323 BC** — Death of Alexander; the Wars of the Diadochi (successors) begin.
- **277 BC** — Antigonus II Gonatas defeats Gallic invaders and establishes the Antigonid dynasty.
- **215–205 BC** — First Macedonian War against the Roman Republic.
- **200–197 BC** — Second Macedonian War; Rome defeats Philip V at the Battle of Cynoscephalae.
- **171–168 BC** — Third Macedonian War; Rome defeats Perseus at the Battle of Pydna, abolishing the monarchy.
- **148 BC** — Fourth Macedonian War ends with Roman victory, establishing the Roman province of Macedonia.

## Body

### Rise and Dominance under Philip II and Alexander
Before the 4th century BC, Macedonia was a small kingdom often subordinate to Persian or Thracian powers. King Philip II (r. 359–336 BC) transformed it through military reform, introducing the Macedonian phalanx armed with the sarissa pike. He used diplomacy and conquest to subdue mainland Greece, defeating Athens and Thebes at the Battle of Chaeronea in 338 BC. Philip formed the League of Corinth, positioning himself to lead a Greek invasion of Persia.

Alexander the Great (r. 336–323 BC) fulfilled this plan, destroying Thebes to secure Greek loyalty and then conquering the Achaemenid Empire from Egypt to the Indus River. His empire became the definitive Hellenistic state, spreading Greek arts, literature, philosophy, and science. The philosopher Aristotle, Alexander's tutor, was a key figure of this era.

### The Diadochi and Hellenistic Kingdoms
Alexander's death in 323 BC led to the Wars of the Diadochi, as his generals fought for control. Macedonia remained a major Greek political center alongside Ptolemaic Egypt and the Seleucid Empire. The Antipatrid and later Antigonid dynasties ruled Macedonia. Key cities included the capital Pella and new foundations like Thessalonica. The kingdom's decline began with conflicts with Rome.

### Conflict with Rome and Fall
Macedonia's rivalry with Rome escalated through three major wars. The Second Macedonian War (200–197 BC) ended with a Roman victory at Cynoscephalae, forcing Macedonia to relinquish its Greek territories. After the Third Macedonian War (171–168 BC), the Roman general Aemilius Paullus defeated King Perseus at Pydna. Rome abolished the monarchy, dividing Macedonia into four client republics. A brief revolt under Andriscus in the Fourth Macedonian War (150–148 BC) was crushed, leading to the establishment of the Roman province of Macedonia.

### Government and Society
Macedonian kings held absolute power, supported by a court of royal pages, bodyguards, and companions (hetairoi). While theoretically limited by the army assembly, kings were autocratic. The economy relied on mining gold, silver, and timber, with revenues funding a professional army and, under Philip II, a navy. Society was less urbanized than southern Greece, with an aristocracy engaged in hunting and warfare. The Macedonian language was a dialect of Greek, later replaced by Koine Greek.

### Culture and Legacy
Macedonian culture blended Greek traditions with influences from its northern neighbors. The elite imported southern Greek art and literature, as seen in the royal tombs at Vergina. Macedonians worshipped the Greek pantheon, with kings serving as high priests. Alexander's conquests diffused Greek culture across the Near East, creating a cosmopolitan Hellenistic world. This cultural fusion, including the spread of Greek language and religion, laid the groundwork for the later Roman Empire and Byzantine civilization.

## Terms
*   **Argead dynasty:** The first royal dynasty of Macedonia, claiming descent from Heracles and ruling until the death of Alexander IV.
*   **Hetairoi (Companions):** The elite cavalry and the core of the Macedonian aristocracy, forming the king's advisory council.
*   **Sarissa:** A long pike (c. 18 feet) used by Macedonian phalanx infantry, giving them a tactical advantage.
*   **Diadochi (Successors):** The generals of Alexander the Great who fought wars for control of his empire after his death.
*   **Hellenistic:** The period following Alexander's conquests (323–31 BC), characterized by the spread of Greek culture and the rule of Greek-speaking kingdoms in the Near East.
*   **League of Corinth:** A federation of Greek states led by Philip II and later Alexander, formed to invade Persia.
*   **Koine Greek:** The common dialect of Greek that emerged in the Hellenistic period, serving as a lingua franca.
*   **Phalanx:** A military formation of infantry fighting in a close-order, rectangular mass.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Ethnic Identity:** Scholars debate whether ancient Macedonians were "truly Greeks" or a distinct people. The predominant view is that they were Greeks with a more archaic lifestyle, but ancient sources present conflicting views, sometimes labeling them as barbarians.
*   **Military Reforms:** The extent to which Philip II's military innovations were original versus influenced by his time as a hostage in Thebes (and exposure to the general Epaminondas) is debated.
*   **Royal Tombs at Vergina:** The identity of the occupants of the main tombs discovered in 1977–78, particularly whether one is Philip II, has been subject to scholarly debate, though recent forensic research supports this identification.
