# Chinese characters

## Overview

Chinese characters are logographs—graphemes representing morphemes (units of meaning) rather than phonemes (units of sound). They constitute the only one of the four independently invented writing systems (alongside cuneiform, Egyptian hieroglyphs, and Maya script) to remain in continuous use for over three millennia. Characters evolved from pictographic oracle bone inscriptions (c. 1250 BCE) through seal, clerical, and cursive stages to the modern regular script. Structural analysis reveals most characters are phono-semantic compounds combining a semantic component (hinting at meaning) and a phonetic component (hinting at pronunciation). The script spread across the Sinosphere (China, Japan, Korea, Vietnam), where it was adapted to write local languages via native readings, Sino-Xenic loanwords, and newly invented characters. In the 20th century, mainland China and Japan simplified character forms; Korea and Vietnam largely replaced characters with alphabets (hangul and the Latin-based Vietnamese alphabet), leaving Japanese as the only major non-Sinitic language still using them extensively.

## Timeline

- **c. 1250–1050 BCE** — Oracle bone script attested at Anyang (Late Shang divinations).
- **c. 1046–256 BCE** — Zhou dynasty; gradual evolution toward small seal script in Qin.
- **221–206 BCE** — Qin dynasty standardizes small seal script; vulgar/clerical forms coexist.
- **202 BCE – 220 CE** — Han dynasty; clerical script matures (bāfēn); paper invented (1st c. CE).
- **25–220 CE** — Eastern Han; neo-clerical script emerges; early semi-cursive and cursive appear.
- **220–589 CE** — Northern and Southern dynasties; regular script (kǎishū) becomes predominant.
- **581–618 CE** — Sui dynasty institutes imperial examinations requiring regular script.
- **618–907 CE** — Tang dynasty; woodblock printing invented (6th–9th c.); sawndip (Zhuang) writing emerges.
- **960–1279 CE** — Song dynasty; moveable type invented (11th c.); printing standardizes forms.
- **1368–1912 CE** — Ming and Qing dynasties; print orthography (jiu zixing) solidified (e.g., Kangxi Dictionary, 1716).
- **1911–1949** — Republican era; Literary Chinese replaced by written vernacular Chinese; first simplification list (1935) rescinded.
- **1949–present** — PRC promulgates simplified characters (1956, 1964); second round (1977) rescinded (1986).
- **1946–present** — Japan promulgates shinjitai (tōyō/jōyō kanji lists); expands to 2136 (2010).
- **1949–present** — North Korea nearly eliminates hanja in writing; South Korea restricts hanja to disambiguation/names.
- **1954–present** — Vietnam adopts Latin-based alphabet as sole official script; chữ Nôm abandoned.
- **2025** — Unicode 17.0 defines 102,998 Chinese characters (CJK Unified Ideographs).

## Body

### Independent invention and proto-writing
Chinese characters are one of four independently invented writing systems. Writing emerged from proto-writing—systems using pictographs (depicting objects) and conventional signs insufficient to carry full spoken language. The critical innovation was the **rebus technique**: using a symbol for one word to write a different, similarly pronounced word, enabling representation of abstract vocabulary. Pictographs gradually abstracted and regularized (e.g., 大 'large' from a picture of a man) until symbols represented spoken words exclusively.

### Structural classification (Qiu Xigui’s three principles)
Modern palaeography classifies characters by functional components (bùjiàn):
- **Semantographs**: forms wholly related to meaning. Includes **pictographs** (xiàngxíng, e.g., 日 'sun'), **indicatives** (zhǐshì, abstract concepts like 上 'up'), and **compound ideographs** (huìyì, combining meanings, e.g., 休 'rest' = 人 'person' + 木 'tree'). Many traditional compound ideographs are now reanalyzed as phono-semantic compounds obscured by sound change.
- **Phonographs (phono-semantic compounds, xíngshēng)**: combine a semantic component (e.g., 氵 'water' in 河 'river', 湖 'lake') and a phonetic component (e.g., 胡 hú in 湖 hú). Phonetic hints often become approximate or obsolete due to sound shifts; characters sharing a phonetic form a **phonetic series**.
- **Loangraphs (jiǎjiè)**: existing characters borrowed to write homophonous words lacking a character (e.g., grammatical particles 之, 其; foreign names like 加拿大 Jiānádà 'Canada'). Distinct from semantic extension (one word gaining senses).
- **Signs**: pure form distinctions without semantic/phonetic motivation (e.g., numerals 五 'five', 八 'eight').

The 2nd-century *Shuowen Jiezi*’s "six writings" (liùshū) remained the standard framework for two millennia but is now considered problematic: categories overlap (e.g., 大 as pictograph vs. indicative), and the sixth category (zhuǎnzhù) is undefined.

### Historical evolution of scripts
Scripts evolved gradually with coexistence, not sudden replacement.
- **Oracle bone script** (jiǎgǔwén, c. 1250 BCE): mature system on ox scapulae/turtle plastrons for divination; stroke order already conventionalized.
- **Bronze script** (jīnwén): less angular forms from clay-mould casting; coexisted with bamboo-book script.
- **Small seal script** (xiǎozhuàn): standardized by Qin (221 BCE) under Li Si; vulgar rectilinear style also used.
- **Clerical script** (lìshū): matured by Han (bāfēn); **libian** transformation simplified/regularized components, losing pictorial quality. Neo-clerical (xīnlìtǐ) emerged mid-Eastern Han as transitional form.
- **Cursive** (cǎoshū): from 24 BCE; synthesized Qin vulgar writing with flowing brushwork. **Zhangcǎo** (orderly cursive) → **jīncǎo** (modern cursive, Jin dynasty, e.g., Wang Xizhi).
- **Semi-cursive** (xíngshū): late Han; faster clerical writing with strokes flowing together; common in modern handwriting.
- **Regular script** (kǎishū): based on clerical/semi-cursive; credited to Zhong Yao (Cao Wei, 220–266); dominant by Northern/Southern period (420–589); cemented by Sui imperial examinations.

### Character structure and layout
Characters occupy a uniform square. Components are built from **strokes** (bǐhuà)—eight traditional categories (Eight Principles of Yong) or five modern basic types. Combining forms vary by position (e.g., ⼑ 'knife' → 刂 on right, ⺈ on top). Assembly follows fixed orders: generally left-to-right, top-to-bottom, enclosures before contents. **Variant forms** (yìtǐzì) arise from simplification, aesthetic preference, or component substitution (e.g., 明/朙/眀 'bright'); standardization selects one as canonical. **Layout** was vertical (top-to-bottom, columns right-to-left) until 20th-century Western influence introduced horizontal left-to-right rows and punctuation.

### Methods of writing and technology
Media evolved: bone/bronze → bamboo/wood/silk → paper (1st c. CE, credited to Cai Lun) → woodblock printing (6th–9th c.) → moveable type (Bi Sheng, 11th c.). **Calligraphy** (ink brush) was a scholarly art balancing dualities (yin/yang: fast/slow, heavy/light, concrete/abstract). **Print typefaces**: Song/Ming (serif), Heiti/Gothic (sans-serif), Kai (handwritten regular), Fangsong (semi-script). **Computers** required overcoming alphabet-only encodings (Morse, ASCII). **Input methods (IMEs)**: phonetic (pinyin, bopomofo, Jyutping, Hepburn) or form-based (Wubi, Cangjie). **Encoding**: Unicode (since 2025 v17.0: 102,998 characters) assigns code points; rendering engine chooses allograph.

### Vocabulary adaptation across the Sinosphere
**Old Chinese** (single-syllable morphemes) → **Classical Chinese** (5th c. BCE–2nd c. CE) → **Literary Chinese** (lingua franca until 20th c., akin to Latin in Europe). Sound mergers increased homophony, driving multi-syllable compound formation. **Sino-Xenic vocabulary** (Chinese loanwords) entered Vietnamese, Korean, Japanese.
- **Japan (kanji)**: Mixed script with **kana** syllabaries (hiragana/katakana from man'yōgana). **On'yomi** (Sino-Japanese) and **kun'yomi** (native) readings; **kokuji** (Japanese-invented characters, mostly ideographic); **ateji** (phonetic borrowings, e.g., 亜米利加 → アメリカ 'America').
- **Korea (hanja)**: Early Literary Chinese → **idu** (Sino-Korean) → **gugyeol** annotations. **Hangul** alphabet invented 1443, adopted late 19th c.; hanja now limited to disambiguation (many homophones in hangul, e.g., 기사 gisa >30 entries), names, academia. Few **gukja** (Korean-invented).
- **Vietnam (chữ Hán/chữ Nôm)**: Literary Chinese official from 1010. **Chữ Nôm** (mature by 13th c.) used phono-semantic and unique **phonetic–phonetic compounds** for consonant clusters (e.g., 𢁋 blăng 'moon' = 巴 ba + 陵 lăng). Literacy ≤5%; replaced by Latin alphabet (1954).
- **Other languages**: **Sawndip** (Zhuang, Guangxi) uses borrowed/locally created characters (some phonetic–phonetic); still used despite Latin alphabet promotion. Miao, Yao, Bouyei, Bai, Hani now use Latin scripts. **Graphically derived scripts** (Khitan, Tangut, Jurchen, Nüshu, bopomofo, Geba, Yi, Lisu) inspired by but not directly using characters.

### Literacy, lexicography, and neurolinguistics
Literacy requires 3,000–4,000 active characters (specialists 5,000–6,000); primers: *Thousand Character Classic* (6th c.), *Three Character Classic* (13th c.). Modern lexicon ~15,000 characters. **Dictionaries**: *Erya* (semantic categories); *Shuowen Jiezi* introduced **radical** (bùshǒu) indexing (540 → 214 Kangxi radicals); radical-and-stroke sorting standard; modern dictionaries add pinyin ordering. **Rhyme dictionaries** used **fanqie** (reverse cut) for pronunciation before romanization. **Neurolinguistics (fMRI)**: character reading engages additional visual-processing areas vs. phonetic scripts; component regularity and phonetic reliability affect recognition; orthography plays larger role in speech recognition due to homophony. **Dyslexia** in character readers involves co-occurring visuospatial and phonological deficits, distinct from alphabetic dyslexia.

### Reform and standardization
- **PRC**: 1951 Script Reform Committee; 1956 draft → 1964 list (2,235 simplified). Merged forms (e.g., 雲/云 → 云). Second round (1977) rescinded (1986) due to unfamiliar forms. 2013 *List of Commonly Used Standard Chinese Characters*: 8,105 total.
- **Japan**: Post-WWII **shinjitai** (simplified) vs. **kyūjitai**; *tōyō kanji* (1946, 1,850) → *jōyō kanji* (1981, 1,945 → 2010, 2,136); name-use restricted to jōyō + *jinmeiyō* (983).
- **South Korea**: Hanja restricted to disambiguation/names/calligraphy; education policy vacillates. 1972 Basic Hanja (1,800); 2022 name-list 8,319.
- **North Korea**: Hanja nearly eliminated in writing by 1949; still taught (~3,000 by university).
- **Taiwan**: 1982 Chart of Standard Forms (4,808 traditional); dictionaries for Hokkien/Hakka.
- **Singapore**: Three rounds (1969, 1974, 1976); aligned with PRC 1986 revisions.
- **Hong Kong**: 4,762 traditional characters for education.

## Terms

- ****Logograph**** — A grapheme representing a morpheme (unit of meaning) rather than a phoneme (unit of sound).
- ****Morpheme**** — The smallest meaningful unit in a language; in Chinese, nearly always a single syllable.
- ****Phono-semantic compound (xíngshēng)**** — A character combining a semantic component (meaning hint) and a phonetic component (pronunciation hint); ~58% of frequent characters.
- ****Loangraph (jiǎjiè)**** — An existing character borrowed to write a different, similarly pronounced word (e.g., grammatical particles, foreign names).
- ****Semantic component**** — A character part indicating an element of meaning (e.g., 氵 'water' in 河 'river').
- ****Phonetic component**** — A character part suggesting pronunciation; often approximate or obsolete due to sound change.
- ****Radical (bùshǒu)**** — A visually prominent component used to index characters in dictionaries (214 Kangxi radicals standard).
- ****Sino-Xenic vocabulary**** — Chinese loanwords systematically borrowed into Vietnamese, Korean, and Japanese, preserving Middle Chinese phonology.
- ****Kana**** — Japanese syllabaries (hiragana, katakana) representing moras, derived from cursive/simplified man'yōgana.
- ****Chữ Nôm**** — Vietnamese demotic script using Chinese characters (including phonetic–phonetic compounds) to write spoken Vietnamese.

## Debates and open questions

- **Compound ideograph existence**: Scholars (Boodberg, Boltz) argue no true compound ideographs were created in antiquity; apparent cases are phono-semantic compounds with lost secondary readings. Other scholars reject this, maintaining huìyì as a valid creation method.
- **Shuowen Jiezi classification validity**: The "six writings" framework is acknowledged as problematic—categories overlap (pictograph vs. indicative), definitions are unclear, and etymologies are sometimes implausible—yet it remains a pedagogical tool.
- **Neolithic symbols as writing**: Symbols at Jiahu, Dadiwan, Banpo (7th–5th millennium BCE) and Dawenkou (c. 4300–2600 BCE) are debated as proto-writing or mere symbols; consensus holds they do not yet record language.
- **Dyslexia mechanism**: Whether developmental dyslexia in character readers is a distinct double-deficit (visuospatial + phonological) or a variant of universal dyslexia remains under study.
- **Simplification vs. tradition**: Ongoing tension between simplified (PRC, Singapore, Malaysia) and traditional (Taiwan, Hong Kong, Macau) standards; Japan’s shinjitai represent a third, partially overlapping set.
- **Han unification in Unicode**: Philosophical debate over assigning single code points to distinct regional allographs (CJK Unified Ideographs) vs. encoding them separately; rendering engine chooses glyph.
