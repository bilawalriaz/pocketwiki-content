# Svante Arrhenius

## Overview
Svante Arrhenius (1859–1927) was a Swedish scientist and a founder of physical chemistry. He is best known for his 1896 work that first used physical chemistry principles to estimate how increases in atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO₂) could raise Earth's surface temperature through the greenhouse effect, laying foundational work for modern climate science. He also developed the theory of ionic dissociation, for which he won the 1903 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

- **1889** — Developed the Arrhenius equation, introducing the concept of activation energy.
- **1896** — Published first calculations linking atmospheric CO₂ increases to global warming.
- **1901** — Elected to the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences.
- **1903** — Awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for his electrolytic dissociation theory.
- **1905** — Appointed director of the Nobel Institute for Physical Research.
- **1927** — Died on 2 October in Stockholm.

## Body

### Ionic Dissociation and Physical Chemistry
Arrhenius's foundational work was his 1884 dissertation on electrolytic conductivity. He proposed that solid crystalline salts disassociate into charged particles (ions) when dissolved in water, and that these ions exist in solution even without an electric current. This challenged the prevailing view that ions were only produced during electrolysis. He also defined acids as substances that produce hydrogen ions (H⁺) in solution and bases as substances that produce hydroxide ions (OH⁻). His ideas, initially met with skepticism in Sweden, were championed by European physical chemists like Wilhelm Ostwald and Jacobus van 't Hoff.

### The Greenhouse Effect and Climate Science
In 1896, Arrhenius became the first scientist to quantitatively estimate the impact of atmospheric CO₂ on Earth's temperature. Using infrared observations and the Stefan-Boltzmann law, he formulated a rule stating that if CO₂ increases geometrically, temperature increases arithmetically. He calculated that halving CO₂ could lower temperatures by about 4°C, while doubling it could raise temperatures by about 4°C. He was the first to predict that fossil-fuel burning could emit enough CO₂ to cause significant global warming, potentially preventing a new ice age. His work, though containing omissions like cloud effects, established the core principle that anthropogenic CO₂ emissions drive global warming.

### Later Career and Other Contributions
After his theories gained acceptance, Arrhenius explored diverse fields. He applied chemical theory to physiology, studying toxins and antitoxins in a work titled *Immunochemistry* (1907). He also investigated the origin of ice ages, the solar system's formation via interstellar collision, and proposed panspermia—the idea that life could be transported between planets via spores. He was influential in the Nobel Prize committees and served as rector of the Nobel Institute until his death.

## Terms
- **Ionic Dissociation**: The process where a compound separates into charged particles (ions) when dissolved in a solvent like water.
- **Activation Energy**: The minimum energy required for a chemical reaction to proceed, as described by the Arrhenius equation.
- **Arrhenius Equation**: A formula that relates the rate of a chemical reaction to temperature and activation energy.
- **Greenhouse Effect**: The process by which certain gases in a planet's atmosphere trap heat, raising the surface temperature.
- **Radiative Forcing**: A measure of the influence a factor (like CO₂) has on the energy balance of the Earth, expressed in Watts per square meter (W/m²).
- **Panspermia**: The hypothesis that life exists throughout the universe and can be distributed by meteoroids, asteroids, and comets.
- **Carbonic Acid**: The term Arrhenius used for CO₂; in modern chemistry, it specifically refers to the aqueous solution of CO₂ (H₂CO₃).

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Early Criticism**: In 1900, Knut Ångström published experimental results suggesting that CO₂ absorption of infrared radiation was already "saturated," meaning more CO₂ would have no effect. Arrhenius dismissed this critique in 1901.
- **Quantitative Accuracy**: While qualitatively correct, Arrhenius's quantitative estimates for warming from doubled CO₂ (about 4°C) were later found to be higher than some subsequent models, though within the range of modern estimates. His work omitted key factors like clouds and convection.
- **Consensus on Ice Ages**: The modern consensus is that orbital forcing sets the timing for ice ages, with CO₂ acting as an essential amplifying feedback, rather than the primary driver as Arrhenius initially focused on.
