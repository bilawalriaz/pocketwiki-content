# Pancho Villa

## Overview
Francisco "Pancho" Villa was a central military and political figure of the Mexican Revolution (1910–1920). A former bandit who became a revolutionary general, he led the powerful División del Norte, helped overthrow two presidents, and briefly governed the state of Chihuahua. His campaigns were decisive in ending the old regime, but his later conflict with the United States and his assassination cemented his legacy as a complex, popular hero who stood against foreign intervention and the established order.

## Timeline
- **5 June 1878** — Born José Doroteo Arango Arámbula in Durango.
- **1910** — Joins Francisco Madero's rebellion against dictator Porfirio Díaz.
- **1911** — Captures Ciudad Juárez, helping force Díaz's resignation.
- **1912** — Imprisoned by President Madero's government; escapes to the U.S.
- **1913** — Returns to Mexico to fight the coup government of Victoriano Huerta.
- **1913-14** — Serves as provisional governor of Chihuahua, building his División del Norte.
- **June 1914** — Wins the decisive Battle of Zacatecas, breaking Huerta's regime.
- **August 1914** — Breaks with Venustiano Carranza after Huerta's defeat.
- **December 1914** — Occupies Mexico City in alliance with Emiliano Zapata.
- **1915** — Defeated by Álvaro Obregón at Celaya and other battles; his army disintegrates.
- **March 1916** — Raids Columbus, New Mexico, provoking a U.S. military expedition.
- **20 July 1923** — Assassinated in Parral, Chihuahua.

## Body

### Early Life and Path to Revolution
Born into poverty, Villa worked various jobs before becoming a bandit in the Sierra Madre. His life changed when he met Abraham González, a supporter of presidential candidate Francisco Madero. González convinced Villa that banditry could be a tool to fight for the people against the hacienda owners. At the outbreak of the Revolution in 1910, Villa joined Madero's rebellion, quickly proving himself a skilled guerrilla leader.

### Military Success and Governorship
Villa's military victories, including the capture of Ciudad Juárez in 1911, were instrumental in ousting dictator Porfirio Díaz. After President Madero was overthrown and killed in 1913, Villa joined the Constitutionalist Army led by Venustiano Carranza to defeat the usurper, Victoriano Huerta. As provisional governor of Chihuahua (1913-14), Villa raised funds through forced loans and bank confiscations, printed his own currency, and built the formidable División del Norte. His victory at the Battle of Zacatecas in June 1914 was the single bloodiest battle of the Revolution and directly led to Huerta's collapse.

### Break with Carranza and Alliance with Zapata
After Huerta's defeat, Villa broke with Carranza, viewing him as a dictator-in-waiting. At the Convention of Aguascalientes, Villa allied with the southern revolutionary Emiliano Zapata. Together, their forces occupied Mexico City in late 1914. However, Carranza's general, Álvaro Obregón, proved a superior tactician. In 1915, Obregón decisively defeated Villa's División del Norte at the Battle of Celaya, effectively ending Villa's power as a national military leader.

### Guerrilla War and the U.S. Expedition
Reduced to a guerrilla force, Villa raided the border town of Columbus, New Mexico, in 1916, likely to provoke a U.S. invasion that would embarrass Carranza. The U.S. sent General John J. Pershing on a Punitive Expedition to capture him, but Villa eluded capture. The expedition, which used aircraft and trucks for the first time in U.S. Army history, ended in 1917 without success. Villa's military power was permanently broken.

### Final Years and Assassination
After Carranza's assassination in 1920, Villa negotiated an amnesty with interim president Adolfo de la Huerta. He retired to a hacienda in Canutillo, Chihuahua. On 20 July 1923, Villa was assassinated in an ambush in Parral. The killing was most likely ordered by his political enemies, Plutarco Elías Calles and Álvaro Obregón, to prevent Villa from re-entering politics ahead of the 1924 elections.

## Terms
*   **División del Norte:** The powerful and well-equipped army commanded by Pancho Villa, considered the most formidable military force in Mexico during the Revolution.
*   **Constitutionalist Army:** The revolutionary faction led by Venustiano Carranza that opposed Victoriano Huerta's usurpation of power.
*   **Hacendado:** An owner of a hacienda, a large landed estate. The hacendado class was a primary target of revolutionary land reform.
*   **Soldaderas:** Women who accompanied revolutionary armies, serving as soldiers, cooks, nurses, and logistical support.
*   **Corridos:** Narrative ballads that celebrated revolutionary heroes and events, playing a key role in shaping Villa's popular image.
*   **Plan de San Luis Potosí:** Francisco Madero's 1910 proclamation calling for an armed rebellion against Porfirio Díaz.
*   **Plan de Guadalupe:** Venustiano Carranza's 1913 political plan to oust Victoriano Huerta as an unconstitutional usurper.
*   **Convention of Aguascalientes:** A 1914 meeting of revolutionary generals that attempted to establish a post-Huerta government, leading to an alliance between Villa and Zapata against Carranza.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Villa's Motives for the Columbus Raid:** Historians debate whether Villa attacked Columbus, New Mexico, primarily to provoke a U.S. invasion to embarrass Carranza, to acquire military supplies, or in retaliation for U.S. support for Carranza.
*   **German Involvement:** While documented contacts existed between Villa's agents and German officials after 1915, scholars disagree on their significance. Most conclude Villa was not a German agent but a desperate leader seeking arms after being cut off by the U.S.
*   **Responsibility for His Assassination:** Although Jesús Salas Barraza claimed sole responsibility, most historians attribute the assassination to a conspiracy involving Plutarco Elías Calles and Joaquín Amaro, with at least the tacit approval of President Álvaro Obregón.
*   **Historical Legacy:** Assessments of Villa vary. Some historians, like Friedrich Katz, credit his governorship with effective social and economic reforms. Others, like Alan Knight, emphasize his bandit origins and view the Revolution as a continuation of his criminality.
