# Lake Victoria

## Overview
Lake Victoria is Africa's largest lake by area and the world's largest tropical lake. It is a critical freshwater resource and ecosystem, shared by Tanzania, Uganda, and Kenya, and serves as the principal source of the White Nile. The lake is globally significant for its unique biodiversity, particularly its cichlid fish, which have undergone a rapid adaptive radiation but now face mass extinction due to human activity.

## Timeline
- **c. 400,000 years ago** — Lake Victoria forms as tectonic activity creates a shallow depression.
- **c. 17,300 years ago** — Lake Victoria dries out completely for the last time.
- **c. 14,700 years ago** — The lake refills as the African humid period begins.
- **1858** — British explorer John Hanning Speke first documents the lake for Europe and names it after Queen Victoria.
- **1927–1928** — Michael Graham conducts the first systematic fisheries survey.
- **1950s** — Invasive species, including the Nile perch and Nile tilapia, are introduced to the lake.
- **1954** — The Owen Falls Dam (Nalubaale Dam) is completed on the lake's outlet, controlling its level.
- **1990s** — The invasive water hyacinth reaches peak biomass, causing major ecological and economic problems.
- **1996** — The ferry MV Bukoba sinks, killing between 800 and 1,000 people.
- **2010–2022** — The lake's surface area increases by 15%, flooding lakeside communities.

## Body

### Geology and Hydrology
Lake Victoria is geologically young, formed about 400,000 years ago when tectonic activity associated with the East African Rift System created a shallow basin. It has dried up completely at least three times, most recently about 17,300 years ago, before refilling 14,700 years ago. The lake is shallow, with an average depth of 40 m and a maximum of 80–81 m. It receives 80% of its water from direct rainfall and is drained solely by the Nile River at Jinja, Uganda. This outflow makes it the principal source of the White Nile, though the river's ultimate source is considered to be a tributary of the Kagera River in Rwanda or Burundi.

### Biodiversity and Extinction Crisis
Lake Victoria is renowned for its extraordinary fish diversity, especially the haplochromine cichlids. This group underwent a rapid adaptive radiation in the last 15,000 years, producing over 500 endemic species with diverse ecologies. However, the introduction of the invasive Nile perch in the 1950s, combined with eutrophication (nutrient pollution), has caused a mass extinction. It is estimated that at least 200 haplochromine species (about 40%) have been lost. Some species have survived in refugia or adapted to changed conditions, but many others are critically endangered. The lake also hosts other native fish, mammals like hippos, reptiles including Nile crocodiles, and unique invertebrates.

### Fisheries and Economy
Lake Victoria supports Africa's largest inland fishery. The fishery initially relied on native species like tilapia and cichlids. Following the introduction of the Nile perch and Nile tilapia, the catch composition shifted dramatically. By the 1970s, the fishery was dominated by the non-native Nile perch (60% of catch) and Nile tilapia (7%), alongside the native Lake Victoria sardine (30%). Nile perch landings peaked at 500,000 tonnes annually in the early 1990s but have since declined. The lake is also vital for transportation, with ferry services connecting the three bordering countries.

### Environmental Challenges
The lake faces severe environmental pressures. The introduction of the Nile perch is cited as the most dramatic example of human-caused ecosystem extinction. Pollution from untreated sewage, industrial waste, and agricultural runoff has caused eutrophication, fueling algal blooms and the invasion of water hyacinth. This invasive plant creates anoxic (oxygen-depleted) zones, blocks waterways, and interferes with hydroelectric power. Water levels have fluctuated significantly, with a major increase between 2010 and 2022 flooding communities, while earlier declines were linked to Uganda releasing more water than agreed for hydroelectric generation.

## Terms
- **Endemic**: A species found only in a specific geographic location, like Lake Victoria.
- **Haplochromine cichlid**: A diverse group of fish that underwent rapid speciation in the lake's recent history.
- **Eutrophication**: Excessive nutrient enrichment in a water body, often from pollution, leading to algal blooms and oxygen depletion.
- **Nile perch (*Lates niloticus*)**: A large, predatory fish introduced to the lake in the 1950s, which has decimated native fish populations.
- **Water hyacinth**: An invasive aquatic plant that forms dense mats, causing ecological and economic problems.
- **Nalubaale Dam (Owen Falls Dam)**: The dam at Jinja, Uganda, that controls the lake's only outflow into the Nile.
- **Agreed curve**: The standard governing the rate of water release from the Nalubaale Dam to mimic natural outflow.
- **Anoxic**: A condition of total oxygen depletion in water, harmful to most aquatic life.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact origin of the first Nile perch introductions to Lake Victoria in the 1950s is disputed, with circumstantial evidence pointing to the Uganda Game and Fisheries Department, which has denied involvement.
- The ultimate source of the Nile River is debated; while Lake Victoria is the principal source of the White Nile, the most distal source is considered to be a tributary of the Kagera River in Rwanda or Burundi.
- The overall ecological impact of the water hyacinth invasion is complex and not fully understood, as the plant's mats can also create refuges for some fish from overfishing.
