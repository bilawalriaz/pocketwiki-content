# Maxim Gorky

## Overview
Maxim Gorky (born Alexei Maximovich Peshkov, 1868–1936) was a foundational figure in Russian and Soviet literature, nominated five times for the Nobel Prize. Rising from poverty and orphanhood, he travelled the Russian Empire, experiences that fueled his early romantic, Nietzschean stories of marginalized "former people" and his famous play *The Lower Depths* (1902). A proponent of socialism, he funded and aligned with the Bolshevik wing of the RSDLP, befriending Lenin while maintaining a volatile, critical independence. He opposed Tsarism, supported pacifism in WWI, but condemned Bolshevik repression during the Civil War, publishing *Untimely Thoughts* (1918). After a long exile (1906–1913 on Capri; 1921–1932 in Sorrento), he returned to the USSR on Stalin’s invitation in 1928, becoming the official "founder of Socialist Realism" and head of the Union of Soviet Writers. Despite public loyalty, he privately defended persecuted intellectuals (Bakhtin, Bulgakov, Mandelstam, Bukharin, Kamenev) and resisted total party control over culture. His final years were marked by the death of his son, increasing isolation, unannounced house arrest, and death in 1936 under suspicious circumstances. His legacy remains contested: a literary innovator of the autobiographical trilogy and *The Artamonov Business*, yet compromised by his role in whitewashing the Gulag (the White Sea Canal volume) and his advocacy for the 1934 anti-homosexuality law.

## Body

### Early Life and Literary Emergence
Born in Nizhny Novgorod, Gorky was orphaned at eleven and raised by his grandmother. At twelve he ran away, spending five years wandering the Russian Empire, working menial jobs, and attempting suicide in 1887. These "universities of life" provided the material for his writing. As a journalist for provincial newspapers, he wrote under the pseudonym Jehudiel Khlamida. Adopting the pseudonym "Gorky" ("bitter") in 1892, he published his first story "Makar Chudra" in the newspaper *Kavkaz* in Tiflis, where he spent several weeks doing menial work for the Caucasian Railway workshops. His 1898 collection *Essays and Stories* brought instant fame. He viewed literature as a moral-political act to expose the brutalization of the "lowest strata" while affirming their "inner spark of humanity." His early work—stories like "Chelkash," "Old Izergil," and "Twenty-six Men and a Girl"—combined romanticism with a Nietzschean celebration of the strong, willful individual against a degrading environment.

In 1896, he met Zinovy Peshkov, who upon being baptized in 1902 adopted Gorky's last name, as Gorky was Peshkov's godfather.

### Political Engagement and the 1905 Revolution
By 1899 Gorky openly associated with Marxist social-democracy, becoming a celebrity among intelligentsia and workers. He befriended Lenin in 1902, funding the RSDLP and its Bolshevik wing (via Bogdanov’s *Vpered*), though he never formally joined the party. The Tsar’s annulment of his Academy election in 1902 prompted Chekhov and Korolenko to resign in solidarity.

In 1904, having severed his relationship with the Moscow Art Theatre in the wake of conflict with Vladimir Nemirovich-Danchenko, Gorky returned to Nizhny Novgorod to establish a theatre of his own. Both Konstantin Stanislavski and Savva Morozov provided financial support for the venture. Stanislavski believed that Gorky's theatre was an opportunity to develop the network of provincial theatres which he hoped would reform the art of the stage in Russia. He sent pupils from the Art Theatre School—as well as Ioasaf Tikhomirov, who ran the school—to work there. By the autumn, however, after the censor had banned every play that the theatre proposed to stage, Gorky abandoned the project.

The 1905 "Bloody Sunday" massacre radicalized him; he assisted the Moscow uprising, was briefly imprisoned in Peter and Paul Fortress (writing *Children of the Sun*), and fled to Finland and then the US on a Bolshevik fund-raising trip. In the US he wrote *Mother* (1906), a novel of revolutionary conversion he later dismissed as artistically flawed but which became a global socialist classic. A scandal over his unmarried partner Maria Andreyeva deepened his contempt for the "bourgeois soul."

### Capri, "God-Building," and World War I
From 1906 to 1913 Gorky lived on Capri for health and safety. There he collaborated with Lunacharsky, Bogdanov, and Bazarov on a projected socialist Encyclopedia and developed "God-Building" (*bogostroitel'stvo*): a philosophy seeking to harness religious myth’s emotional power for the revolution, placing collective humanity in the role of God. Lenin ridiculed it, but Gorky insisted "culture"—moral-spiritual self-worth—was more decisive than political economy.

An amnesty for the Romanov tercentenary allowed his return in 1914. Horrified by WWI patriotism and the destruction of Rheims Cathedral, he launched *Parus* and *Letopis* to defend "international culture" against nationalism. He condemned Tsarist antisemitism and urged Russia to shed its "Eastern-Asiatic" passivity for European progress. Gorky's best-known publication of the period were concerning antisemitism, written in response to the severe Tsarist repressions against the Jews, and an essay "Two Souls", which contrasted "the passive East" with "the active West" and promoted the values of European culture and progress and urged Russia to break free from the "Eastern-Asiatic" "soul" and encouraged the Russian bourgeoisie to participate "in the work of reform". Lenin dismissed his politics as "weak-willed" and emotional. Gorky initially distrusted the February Revolution, predicting "Asiatic savagery," but supported Kerensky, then the Bolsheviks after the Kornilov affair.

### Revolution, Civil War, and First Exile
Gorky’s apartment became a Bolshevik staff room in 1917. He accepted the October Revolution calmly but soon clashed with the new regime. He accused Lenin and Trotsky of being "poisoned with the filthy venom of power," experimenting on the "living flesh of Russia" like a chemist. His newspaper *Novaya Zhizn* was censored; he published *Untimely Thoughts* (1918), denouncing Lenin as a tyrant and anarchist comparable to the Tsar and Nechayev. He intervened for political prisoners (e.g., historian Yevgeny Tarle) and served on the Committee Against Antisemitism. In 1921, amid the Povolzhye famine (5 million dead), he organized relief (Pomgol) but left for Berlin after most Pomgol members were arrested. In Berlin, he denounced the Moscow Trial of 12 Socialist Revolutionaries as "premeditated murder," drawing contempt from Lenin and Trotsky. Denied re-entry to Fascist Italy, he settled in Sorrento (1922–1932) with a large household including Moura Budberg, his ex-wife, and his son’s family.

### Return to the USSR and the Stalin Era
Stalin courted Gorky back as a propaganda prize. His 1928 visit coincided with the Shakhty Trial; unlike his defense of SRs, Gorky accepted the engineers’ guilt, regretting past interventions for "persecuted professionals." During the visit, he struck up friendships with Genrikh Yagoda (deputy head of the OGPU) who vested interest in spying on Gorky, and two other OGPU officers, Semyon Firin and Matvei Pogrebinsky, who held high office in the Gulag. Pogrebinsky was Gorky's guest in Sorrento for four weeks in 1930. The following year, Yagoda sent his brother-in-law, Leopold Averbakh to Sorrento, with instructions to induce Gorky to return to Russia permanently.

In 1932 he returned permanently: decorated with the Order of Lenin, given a mansion (ex-Ryabushinsky), a dacha, and the presidency of the new Union of Soviet Writers. Nizhny Novgorod was renamed Gorky. At a 1931 reading of "A Girl and Death," a drunken Stalin inscribed the manuscript: "This piece is stronger than Goethe's Faust (love defeats death)"; Gorky was reportedly offended.

### The Gulag Visit and White Sea Canal
In 1933 Gorky co-edited the celebratory volume *The White Sea–Baltic Canal*, claiming "successful rehabilitation" of "enemies of the proletariat" and denying prisoner deaths. He visited Solovki, the model camp, on 20 June 1929 (per Likhachov/Solzhenitsyn). Witnesses allege a Potemkin transformation: prisoners relocated, torture rooms hidden, starving men dressed in new clothes. A 14-year-old boy allegedly exposed the truth—starvation, pole torture, summary executions—to Gorky in a cleared room; the boy was executed after Gorky left. Gorky wrote in the visitor book of "bold creators of culture" but never published the boy’s testimony. Scholars debate the boy’s historicity (Solovki Museum finds no record), but agree Gorky either ignored or whitewashed the camp’s reality. He did, however, use influence to commute sentences for individuals like Mikhail Bakhtin (Solovki to exile) and Julia Danzas.

### Cultural Politics, Opposition, and House Arrest
As "founder of Socialist Realism," Gorky enforced party control but resisted bureaucratization. He backed the 1934 law criminalizing homosexuality ("Destroy homosexuality and fascism will disappear"), influenced by Nazi SA homosexuality. Yet he defended "fellow travellers" and oppositionists: he secured staging for Bulgakov, publication for Platonov, exit visas for Zamyatin and Victor Serge, and a keynote slot for Bukharin (who hailed Pasternak as "first poet"). He supported Kamenev (appointing him to *Academia* publishing) and tried to save Radek and Bukharin. This drew Politburo ire: Kaganovich accused him of ideological faults, RAPP sympathies, and being influenced by Kamenev/Krupskaya. Kaganovich demanded Gorky’s Congress speech be rewritten; Gorky’s rebuttals to critics like Zaslavsky were suppressed. He defended Shostakovich against the 1936 campaign. As Stalinist repression escalated post-Kirov (Dec 1934), Gorky was placed under unannounced house arrest in Gorki-10. His secretary Kryuchkov was an NKVD informer. Stalin ceased visits and calls. Gorky died June 1936; his son predeceased him (May 1934). The 1938 Bukharin trial charged Yagoda with poisoning both on Stalin’s orders.

### Major Works: Reception and Significance
*   **The Lower Depths (1902):** His only play retaining a major Western stage presence; a naturalistic portrait of shelter denizens debating truth vs. comforting illusion.
*   **Mother (1906):** Written in the US for revolutionary agitation. Gorky called it "long, boring, carelessly written... insufficiently democratic." Critics note one-dimensional characters serving as "eloquent mouthpieces," redeemed partly through the mother Nilovna’s perception. Remains his most famous novel globally.
*   **Autobiographical Trilogy (*My Childhood*, 1913–14; *In the World*, 1916; *My Universities*, 1923):** Hailed by Britannica as "one of the finest Russian autobiographies." D.S. Mirsky called it "one of the strangest autobiographies ever written... about everyone except himself," a gallery of portraits linked by the narrator’s acute observation.
*   **The Artamonov Business (1925):** Called Gorky’s "best novel" (Freeborn) or "finest long work" (Weil). A *Buddenbrooks*-like chronicle of a pre-revolutionary industrial dynasty’s decline (1860s–1917).
*   **The Life of Klim Samgin (1925–1936, unfinished):** Gorky’s intended masterpiece. Four volumes (three completed) tracing the intelligentsia’s decay from the 1870s to 1917 through a petit-bourgeois intellectual. Praised by Pasternak and Brian Howard; later critics compare its polyphonic, experimental modernism to Mann’s *Magic Mountain* and Musil’s *Man Without Qualities*. Unavailable in English since the 1930s.

## Terms
- ****Socialist Realism**** — The official Soviet aesthetic doctrine (codified 1932–34) requiring art to depict reality in its "revolutionary development" to educate workers in the spirit of socialism; Gorky was declared its "founder."
- ****God-Building (*bogostroitel'stvo*)**** — Gorky’s philosophy (c. 1908–1910) with Lunacharsky/Bogdanov: recasting religious myth’s emotional force for the revolution, placing collective humanity as the divine object of worship, promising deliverance from suffering and death.
- ****RSDLP / Bolsheviks**** — Russian Social Democratic Labour Party (founded 1898); split in 1903 into Bolshevik (Lenin’s majority faction) and Menshevik wings. Gorky funded and politically aligned with Bolsheviks but never formally joined.
- ****Bloody Sunday (9 Jan 1905)**** — Tsarist troops fired on peaceful workers petitioning the Tsar in St. Petersburg, sparking the 1905 Revolution; radicalized Gorky toward "radical solutions."
- ****Povolzhye Famine (1921–22)**** — Catastrophic famine in Volga/Ural regions killing ~5 million; Gorky led international relief appeal (Pomgol) but fled before Soviet arrests of relief workers.
- ****Shakhty Trial (1928)**** — First major Stalinist show trial; 53 engineers accused of wrecking. Gorky accepted their guilt, marking his public accommodation with the regime.
- ****White Sea–Baltic Canal (Belomorkanal)**** — Forced-labor canal (1931–33) built by Gulag prisoners; Gorky co-edited a 1933 volume celebrating it as "rehabilitation," denying deaths later documented by Solzhenitsyn.
- ****Solovki (Solovetsky Islands)**** — The "original" Soviet forced-labor camp (est. 1923), model for the Gulag system; Gorky visited in 1929, allegedly shown a staged version.
- ****Untimely Thoughts (1918)**** — Essay collection condemning Bolshevik terror, censorship, and Lenin’s "tyranny"; banned in USSR until Perestroika.
- ****"Former people" (*byvshie lyudi*)**** — Term from Gorky’s 1897 story *Creatures That Once Were Men*; entered Russian language denoting those who suffered catastrophic social decline.

## Debates and open questions
*   **The Solovki Boy:** Did the 14-year-old boy who exposed camp horrors to Gorky exist? Solzhenitsyn and Likhachov affirm it; the Solovki Museum finds no record; scholars (Bykov, Basinsky) argue the "legend" captures the "essence of reality" regardless of factuality.
*   **Gorky’s Complicity:** Was Gorky a willing propagandist for the Gulag (editing the Canal volume, praising Solovki) or a compromised figure trying to mitigate harm? His private interventions for prisoners contrast with his public whitewashing.
*   **Cause of Death:** Natural pneumonia (official) vs. NKVD poisoning (Bukharin trial charge, supported by some historians citing NKVD lab substances and Yagoda’s role). Stalin and Molotov carrying the urn fuels suspicion.
*   **Political Alignment:** Was Gorky ever a "Stalinist"? Armin Knigge argues "never"; he enforced Socialist Realism but fought party bureaucratization and defended oppositionists (Kamenev, Bukharin) until his death.
*   **Literary Canon:** Why are major works (*Klim Samgin*, *Artamonov Business*, post-revolutionary plays) unavailable in English? Cold War politics? Soviet-era editorial neglect? Aaron Lake Smith calls the absence "almost suspicious."
*   **Anti-Homosexuality Law (1934):** Gorky’s vigorous support ("exterminate all homosexuals and fascism will vanish")—was it genuine conviction, political survival, or both? His biographers note the Nazi SA context but also his rigid cultural conservatism.
