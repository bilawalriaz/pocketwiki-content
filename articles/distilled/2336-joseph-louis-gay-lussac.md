# Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac

## Overview
Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac (1778–1850) was a foundational French chemist and physicist whose work established key principles of gas behavior and chemical composition. His discoveries, including the law of combining volumes and the composition of water, were critical to the development of modern chemistry and physics, and his practical innovations in measurement are still used today.

## Timeline
- **6 December 1778** — Born in Saint-Léonard-de-Noblat, France.
- **1798** — Entered the École Polytechnique in Paris.
- **1802** — Published the law relating gas volume to temperature (often called Charles's law).
- **1804** — Made a solo hydrogen balloon ascent to 7,016 meters to study the atmosphere.
- **1805** — With Alexander von Humboldt, determined water's composition (2 parts H, 1 part O by volume).
- **1808** — Co-discovered the element boron.
- **1808-1809** — Discovered and announced the law of combining volumes of gases.
- **1810** — Developed a method for quantitative organic combustion analysis with Louis Jacques Thénard.
- **1811** — Recognized iodine as a new element and suggested its name.
- **1815** — Synthesized and named cyanogen.
- **1824** — Developed an improved burette and coined the terms "pipette" and "burette."
- **9 May 1850** — Died in Paris.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Gay-Lussac's early education was overseen by a local abbot before he moved to Paris to study. He entered the prestigious École Polytechnique in 1798, later transferring to the École des Ponts et Chaussées. He became an assistant to chemist C. L. Berthollet and, in 1809, succeeded Antoine François Fourcroy as a professor of chemistry at the École Polytechnique. He also held a professorship of physics at the Sorbonne from 1809 to 1832. His political career included election to the Chamber of Deputies in 1831 and the Chamber of Peers in 1839.

### Scientific Achievements in Physics and Chemistry
Gay-Lussac's work fundamentally shaped gas laws and chemical analysis. In 1802, he published the principle that a gas's volume increases proportionally with its absolute temperature at constant pressure, a relationship now commonly known as Charles's law. His 1804 balloon ascents were early experiments in atmospheric science. In 1805, collaborating with Alexander von Humboldt, he proved that atmospheric composition is constant with altitude and that water is composed of two parts hydrogen and one part oxygen by volume. In 1808, he co-discovered boron and announced the law of combining volumes, which states that gases react in simple, whole-number volume ratios. With Louis Jacques Thénard in 1810, he created a method for organic combustion analysis by measuring carbon dioxide and water produced. He also identified iodine (1811) and synthesized cyanogen (1815).

### Practical Innovations and Honors
Beyond theoretical work, Gay-Lussac made lasting practical contributions. In 1824, he improved the burette and introduced the terms "pipette" and "burette" for laboratory glassware. His research on alcohol-water mixtures led to the Gay-Lussac scale for measuring alcoholic beverage strength, used internationally. He received significant honors, including a 30,000 franc prize from Napoleon in 1809 for his research with Thénard. His name is among the 72 inscribed on the Eiffel Tower, and the mineral gaylussite is named in his honor.

## Terms
- **Law of Combining Volumes**: A principle stating that when gases react together to form other gases, and all volumes are measured at the same temperature and pressure, the ratios between the volumes of the reactant gases and the products can be expressed in simple whole numbers.
- **Charles's Law**: The physical law that the volume of an enclosed gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature at constant pressure.
- **Burette**: A graduated glass tube with a tap at one end, used for delivering known volumes of a liquid, especially in titrations.
- **Pipette**: A slender tube used to transfer or measure out small amounts of liquid.
- **Combustion Analysis**: A method for determining the elemental composition (especially carbon and hydrogen) of an organic compound by burning it and measuring the products.
- **Cyanogen**: A toxic, colorless gas with the formula (CN)₂, which Gay-Lussac first synthesized and named.
- **Gay-Lussac Scale**: A system for measuring the alcoholic strength of a liquid, based on the percentage of alcohol by volume at a reference temperature.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes a minor historical attribution debate: the gas law Gay-Lussac published in 1802 is usually called Charles's law because he cited earlier, unpublished work by Jacques Charles, though some sources use the name Gay-Lussac's law. The law was also independently stated by John Dalton nearly simultaneously.
