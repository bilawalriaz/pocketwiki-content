# Surveying

## Overview
Land surveying is the science and art of determining the precise positions of points on Earth's surface using distances and angles. It is fundamental to construction, land ownership, mapping, and legal boundary definition, forming the basis for property sales and civil infrastructure. The profession combines mathematics, physics, engineering, and law to create accurate representations of the physical world.

## Body

### History and Development
Surveying has been essential since ancient times, with Egyptians using geometry to re-establish boundaries after Nile floods. The Romans recognized it as a profession, and medieval Europe maintained boundaries through practices like "beating the bounds." The modern era saw key instrument advances: the theodolite (1571), the telescope-equipped theodolite (1725), and the precision theodolite (1787). Systematic triangulation was introduced in 1615, enabling large-scale national surveys like the Principal Triangulation of Britain (1784-1853) and the Great Trigonometric Survey of India (1801). The Industrial Revolution increased demand for surveying in infrastructure projects. The 20th century brought electronic distance measurement (EDM) in the 1950s and satellite positioning with GPS in the 1970s, revolutionizing accuracy and speed.

### Equipment and Technology
Core instruments include the theodolite (for measuring angles), the total station (a theodolite with an integrated EDM), and GPS/GNSS receivers. Modern total stations are robotic and can connect to satellite systems. GPS surveying uses static methods for high-accuracy baselines over 20 km or Real Time Kinematic (RTK) surveying with a base and rover for real-time centimeter-level accuracy. Ancillary equipment includes tripods, prisms, and data collectors. Software for CAD and data processing is now integral to the profession.

### Techniques and Methods
Surveyors measure angles and distances to determine positions, often splitting work into horizontal and vertical components. Key methods include:
*   **Triangulation:** Establishing a baseline and measuring angles to form triangles, a primary method before EDM and GPS.
*   **Traversing:** Measuring bearings and distances between a series of points, often in a loop, to cover an area.
*   **Leveling:** Using an instrument and rod to determine height differences between points (differential leveling).
*   **Offsetting:** Measuring perpendicular distances from a baseline to map irregular features like riverbanks.

Measurements are adjusted to distribute errors, using methods like the Bowditch (compass) rule or least squares. A fundamental principle is that no measurement is perfect; errors are classified as gross (blunders), systematic (consistent patterns), or random (unavoidable fluctuations).

### Types of Surveying
Specializations are defined by purpose:
*   **Cadastral/Boundary Surveying:** Establishes legal property boundaries.
*   **Topographic Surveying:** Maps the positions and elevations of natural and man-made features.
*   **Construction/Engineering Surveying:** Lays out structures and checks as-built locations.
*   **Control Surveying:** Establishes precise reference points for other surveys.
*   **Hydrographic Surveying:** Maps shorelines and underwater features.
*   **Deformation Surveying:** Monitors changes in the shape or position of structures over time.

Surveys are also classified as **plane** (assuming a flat Earth for small areas) or **geodetic** (accounting for Earth's curvature for large areas).

### The Profession
Surveyors require knowledge of mathematics, law, and engineering. Qualification levels typically include assistants/chainmen, technicians, and licensed/chartered surveyors. Licensing involves education, examinations, and supervised practice. The profession is regulated by government agencies and professional institutions like the International Federation of Surveyors (FIG). Key roles include cadastral surveying for land titles and building surveying for structural advice.

## Terms
*   **Theodolite:** An instrument for measuring precise horizontal and vertical angles.
*   **Total Station:** An electronic surveying instrument combining a theodolite with an electronic distance measurement (EDM) device.
*   **GNSS/GPS:** Global Navigation Satellite System/Global Positioning System; satellite-based systems for determining position.
*   **RTK (Real Time Kinematic):** A GPS technique using a fixed base station and a rover to provide centimeter-level accuracy in real-time.
*   **Triangulation:** A survey method that determines the location of a point by forming triangles to it from known points.
*   **Cadastral Survey:** A survey that establishes or re-establishes legal boundaries of land parcels.
*   **Datum:** A reference point or set of points used as a basis for calculating coordinates.
*   **EDM (Electronic Distance Measurement):** A device that uses electromagnetic waves (light or microwave) to measure distances.
*   **Leveling:** The process of determining the difference in elevation between points.
