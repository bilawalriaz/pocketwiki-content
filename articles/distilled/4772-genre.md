# Genre

## Overview
Genre is a category of artistic communication (e.g., literature, music, film) defined by socially agreed-upon conventions of style, form, or theme. It is a dynamic tool that helps audiences make sense of art, adapting to changes in creators and society. The concept originated in ancient Greek classification systems and remains central to how we create, categorize, and consume art.

## Timeline
- **c. 350 BC** — Plato creates the first recorded genre system, classifying literature into dramatic dialogue, pure narrative, and epic.
- **c. 330 BC** — Aristotle revises Plato's system, eliminating pure narrative and adding criteria of object and medium.
- **17th-19th C.** — The "hierarchy of genres" becomes dominant in European art theory, especially in France.
- **1920s-1930s** — Mikhail Bakhtin develops the concept of "speech genres" and heteroglossia.
- **1970s** — Gérard Genette publishes *The Architext*, analyzing the history of genre taxonomy.
- **1980s** — Carolyn Miller argues genres are "typified rhetorical actions," not just text types.
- **21st C.** — The term "microgenre" emerges, describing highly specialized categories in music and streaming media.

## Body

### Origins and Theory
The academic study of genre is called genre studies or genre theory. Critical discussion began with Plato and Aristotle's classification systems for ancient Greek literature. Plato distinguished three imitational genres by mode: dramatic dialogue, pure narrative, and epic. Aristotle revised this by eliminating pure narrative and distinguishing genres by the object of imitation (superior or inferior) and the medium (words, gestures, verse). This created a system of tragedy, epic, comedy, and parody. Later, lyric poetry was integrated, forming a tripartite system (lyrical, epical, dramatic) that dominated German Romanticism. Theorist Gérard Genette critiqued later, more complex taxonomies as flawed by their "inclusive and hierarchical" nature.

### In the Visual Arts
In art history, "genre painting" specifically refers to scenes of everyday life with anonymous figures. A powerful concept was the "hierarchy of genres," strongest in 17th-19th century France, which ranked painting types. From highest to lowest: history painting, portrait, genre painting, landscape, animal painting, and still life. This hierarchy privileged idealism (rendering universal essences) over realism (copying appearances).

### In Literature
A literary genre is a category of composition determined by technique, tone, content, or length. The most general genres are epic, tragedy, comedy, novel, and short story, which can exist in prose or poetry. Genres like satire can mix with others. They are also defined by the historical cultural movement in which they were composed. The distinction between genre and format (e.g., graphic novel) or age category (e.g., young adult) is important.

### In Film, Music, and Other Media
Film's basic genres are drama and documentary, with many subgenres like Western or horror. A music genre is a category identifying pieces with shared conventions, distinct from musical form or style, though the terms are often used interchangeably. Genre classifications in music can be arbitrary and controversial. The concept is also applied to other media, like video games, and significantly affects popular culture by aiding classification for publicity and consumer search.

### In Linguistics and Rhetoric
In linguistics, philosopher Mikhail Bakhtin introduced "speech genres"—socially specified modes of speaking or writing (e.g., formal letter, lecture). In rhetoric, theorists like Carolyn Miller understand genres as "typified rhetorical actions" or recurring responses to recurring problems, rather than just text types. This view sees genres as actions enacted through texts, deriving meaning from other genres in "genre systems" or "genre ecologies."

## Terms
- **Genre**: A category of artistic communication with socially agreed-upon conventions.
- **Genre studies**: The academic discipline focused on analyzing genres.
- **Hierarchy of genres**: A historical ranking system for visual art, privileging intellectual subjects over everyday ones.
- **Speech genres**: Mikhail Bakhtin's concept for socially recognized modes of speaking or writing.
- **Typified rhetorical actions**: Carolyn Miller's definition of genres as recurring, socially constructed responses to situations.
- **Subgenre**: A subordinate category within a broader genre (e.g., dark fantasy within fantasy).
- **Microgenre**: A highly specialized, narrow classification, often used for music or streaming media recommendations.
- **Heteroglossia**: Mikhail Bakhtin's idea that language is a mix of diverse social speech types.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Taxonomy vs. Rhetoric**: A key debate is whether genres are best understood as fixed categories for classification (taxonomy) or as dynamic social actions and processes (rhetoric).
- **Stability vs. Change**: Genres are defined by conventions, but they must adapt to changing audiences and creators. How they maintain identity while evolving is a central question.
- **Arbitrariness**: Especially in music, genre classifications are often seen as arbitrary, controversial, and overlapping, reflecting sociological differences more than strict musical criteria.
- **Educational Application**: There is debate about whether standard composition courses teach authentic, useful genres or merely inauthentic "mutt genres" with little real-world application.
