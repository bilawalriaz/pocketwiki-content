# African art

## Overview
African art encompasses the diverse visual arts of the African continent, created within specific social, political, and religious contexts. It prioritizes conceptual and symbolic representation over realism, aiming to visualize spiritual essence rather than physical appearance. This art has significantly influenced global artistic traditions, particularly European Modernism, and remains a vital expression of cultural identity and heritage.

## Timeline
- **c. 100,000 years ago** — Evidence for complex paint-making processes in Africa.
- **c. 72,000 years ago** — Oldest known beads, made from Nassarius shells, worn as ornaments.
- **c. 3000 BC** — Nok culture emerges in Nigeria, producing terracotta sculptures.
- **12th–14th centuries** — Bronze casting for reliefs and naturalistic royal heads develops in West Africa (e.g., Benin Bronzes).
- **1400–1900** — Akan goldweights, small metal sculptures representing proverbs, are produced in West Africa.
- **17th–19th centuries** — Kuba Kingdom flourishes in Central Africa, producing extensively decorated court art.
- **Late 19th century** — Increasing amount of African art enters Western collections.
- **Early 20th century** — African art inspires European Modernist artists like Picasso and Matisse.
- **1950s–1960s** — Foundations of contemporary African artistic expression laid in South Africa.
- **1968** — Edward Said Tingatinga begins painting Tinga Tinga art in Tanzania.
- **2018** — French-commissioned report on restitution of African cultural heritage marks a key moment for artifact returns.

## Body

### Thematic Elements and Materials
African art often prioritizes visual abstraction and symbolic representation over naturalism, using generalized forms to reflect cultural and spiritual values. The human figure is a central subject, symbolizing the living, the dead, deities, or serving spiritual functions. Masks, frequently used in ritual ceremonies, are a major art form, often representing spirits and worn by initiated dancers to communicate with ancestors. Materials are diverse, with wood being predominant for sculptures. Other materials include clay, ivory, metal (bronze, brass, gold), stone, and textiles like Kente and mudcloth. Jewelry made from various stones, beads, and wood indicates rank or affiliation.

### History and Regional Traditions
African art's origins predate recorded history, with early decorative objects like shell beads dating to the Middle Stone Age. Ancient civilizations contributed significantly, including Egyptian painting, Nubian sculpture, and the terracotta figures of the Nok culture (c. 1500 BC–500 AD). More complex methods, such as bronze work at Igbo Ukwu and Ile-Ife, developed around the 10th century. West African cultures, particularly in the Niger and Congo river basins, are known for sculpture. Regional styles are discernible: Mande-speaking peoples create pieces with broad, flat surfaces, while Central African art often features heart-shaped faces with circle and dot patterns. The arrival of Christianity and Islam also influenced artistic traditions, integrating new themes and forms.

### Influence on Western Art and Contemporary Scene
During the colonial period, Westerners often mischaracterized African art as "primitive." However, in the early 20th century, artists like Picasso and Matisse were inspired by its abstract depiction and expressive power, which facilitated a shift in Western art toward abstraction and emotional exploration. Contemporary African art is thriving, with artists like El Anatsui and William Kentridge gaining international recognition. Many contemporary works borrow from traditional styles, sometimes ironically seen by Westerners as imitating the European Modernists who were originally inspired by African art. Art biennials in Dakar and Johannesburg and support from global galleries have increased its visibility.

### Traditional Art and Religious Context
Traditional African art is deeply intertwined with Traditional African religions, often serving functional, spiritual, or utilitarian purposes. Art is used to contact ancestral spirits, depict deities, and facilitate rituals. Masks and statues are not merely aesthetic objects but tools for ceremonies, initiations, and offerings. The Dogon of Mali, for example, create sculptures that are often hidden from public view due to their sacred symbolic meaning. In West Africa, the Bambara people produce masks and statuettes for specific societies (e.g., N'tomo, Komo) that structure community life. The human figure remains a central motif, often stylized to convey spiritual or social concepts rather than individual likeness.

## Terms
- **Terracotta**: A type of fired clay used for sculptures and pottery, as seen in Nok and Djenné-Djenno figurines.
- **Benin Bronzes**: A group of over a thousand metal plaques and sculptures that decorated the royal palace of the Kingdom of Benin, created from the 13th century onwards.
- **Kente cloth**: A traditional, multi-colored, hand-woven cloth from Ghana, made from silk and cotton, with patterns and colors holding specific meanings.
- **Masks**: Stylized representations of human, animal, or spiritual faces, worn in ceremonies to transform the wearer and communicate with ancestors or spirits.
- **Visual abstraction**: A stylistic tendency in African art that prioritizes symbolic and conceptual forms over realistic representation.
- **Functionalism**: The principle that art is created for a specific practical, spiritual, or social purpose rather than for art's sake alone.
- **Patina**: A coating or encrustation on sculptures, often from sacrificial materials like blood or libations, which adds spiritual significance and shows age.
- **Dama**: A Dogon memorial ceremony involving masqueraders to guide the dead to the ancestral realm and restore cosmic order.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The original function of many ancient artworks, such as Nok terracotta figures, remains unknown, with theories ranging from ancestor portrayal to grave markers.
- There is ongoing scholarly debate about the classification and stylistic influences of art from groups like the Bwa, Gurunsi, and Mossi in Burkina Faso, partly due to historical confusion during the colonial era.
- The restitution of African artifacts held in Western museums is a major contemporary issue, sparked by reports like the 2018 French commission, with activists like Mwazulu Diyabanza taking direct action for their return.
- The scope of "African art" is debated, with recent movements to include the visual culture of all African regions and time periods, as well as art from the African diaspora, to gain a fuller understanding of the continent's aesthetics.
