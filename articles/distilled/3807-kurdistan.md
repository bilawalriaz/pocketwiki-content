# Kurdistan

## Overview
Kurdistan is a geo-cultural region and stateless nation in West Asia where Kurdish people form a prominent majority. It is not a sovereign state but a territory divided among several countries, primarily Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria. The Kurdish struggle for autonomy or independence within these borders is a central and ongoing political issue in the region.

## Timeline
- **11th century** — The term "Kurdistan" first appears in Seljuk chronicles.
- **1514** — The Battle of Chaldiran leads to a major division of Kurdish areas between the Ottoman and Safavid empires.
- **1639** — The Treaty of Zuhab formalizes the Ottoman-Safavid border, splitting Kurdistan.
- **1920** — The Treaty of Sèvres (unratified) proposes a Kurdish state.
- **1923** — The Treaty of Lausanne establishes modern Turkey's borders, leaving Kurds without a state.
- **1946** — The short-lived Republic of Mahabad is established in Iran.
- **1992** — The Kurdistan Region emerges as an autonomous entity in Iraq after the Gulf War.
- **2005** — Iraq's new constitution recognizes the Kurdistan Region as a federal region.
- **2017** — Iraqi Kurds hold an independence referendum; the Iraqi government subsequently reasserts control.

## Body

### Etymology and Delineation
The name "Kurdistan" means "Land of the Kurds," with the suffix "-stan" being Persian for "land." Its exact delineation is disputed and varies, with some maps greatly exaggerating its boundaries. The region generally encompasses southeastern Turkey, northern Iraq, northwestern Iran, and northern Syria. The 19th-century Kurdistan Eyalet was the first Ottoman administrative unit to use the term.

### Ancient and Post-Classical History
The region was home to ancient groups like the Guti and Hurrians and was later ruled by Persian empires. The Kingdom of Corduene (189 BC–AD 384) existed as a vassal between Rome and Persia. From the 10th to 11th centuries, several Kurdish principalities emerged. In the Middle Ages, Kurdistan was a collection of semi-independent emirates. A major historical division occurred after prolonged wars, splitting Kurdish areas between the Ottoman and Safavid empires, formalized in 1639.

### Modern Political History
Following the Ottoman Empire's collapse, the Treaty of Sèvres (1920) proposed a Kurdish state, but the subsequent Treaty of Lausanne (1923) left Kurds divided among Turkey, Iraq, and Syria. In Turkey, Kurdish identity was suppressed, leading to rebellions and a long-running conflict with the state. In Iraq, Kurds gained autonomy after the 1991 Gulf War, which was constitutionally recognized in 2005. A 2017 independence referendum failed, leading to punitive measures from Baghdad that have weakened Kurdish autonomy. In Syria, Kurds established the self-governing Democratic Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria (Rojava) during the civil war.

### Geography and Resources
Kurdistan covers approximately 392,000 km², with major cities including Diyarbakır, Erbil, and Sanandaj. The region is mountainous, containing the Zagros and Taurus ranges, and is the source of the Tigris and Euphrates rivers. It is rich in petroleum, with estimated reserves of 45 billion barrels, and other minerals like coal and copper. The climate is continental, with fertile areas supporting agriculture.

### Demographics and Culture
The total population is estimated at 34.5 million, with Kurds forming the majority. Minorities include Arabs, Turkic peoples, Assyrians, and Armenians. Kurdish culture is deeply tied to the mountains, which are considered sacred.

## Terms
- **Kurdistan**: A geo-cultural region in West Asia, not a sovereign state, where Kurds are the majority.
- **Emirate**: A semi-independent state or principality, historically common in Kurdistan.
- **Federal region**: A constitutionally recognized autonomous area within a country, like the Kurdistan Region in Iraq.
- **Rojava**: The common name for the Democratic Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria, a self-governing Kurdish-led area.
- **PKK**: The Kurdistan Workers' Party, a militant separatist organization in Turkey.
- **No-fly zone**: An area where aircraft are forbidden to fly, established over northern Iraq after the 1991 Gulf War.
- **GAP**: The Southeast Anatolia Project, a Turkish dam and development system on rivers originating in Kurdistan.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary debate is over the political future of Kurdistan: whether it should be an independent state, a federation, or have greater autonomy within existing countries. The delineation of Kurdistan's borders is highly contested, with maps often being criticized as exaggerated propaganda. The failure of the 2017 Iraqi Kurdish referendum and subsequent punitive measures by Baghdad have called the future of Kurdish autonomy in Iraq into question. Scholars also debate the historical connection between the ancient Kingdom of Corduene and modern Kurds.
