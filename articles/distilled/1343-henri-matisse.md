# Henri Matisse

## Overview
Henri Matisse (1869–1954) was a French painter, sculptor, and printmaker who, alongside Pablo Picasso, defined modern art’s revolutionary trajectory in the early 20th century. Renowned for his expressive use of colour and fluid draughtsmanship, Matisse evolved from a traditional academic training to become a leader of Fauvism, later embracing bold simplification and, in his final decade, pioneering cut-paper collage (decoupage) after illness confined him to a wheelchair.

## Timeline
- **1869** — Born in Le Cateau-Cambrésis, France
- **1889** — Began painting during convalescence from appendicitis
- **1891** — Studied at Académie Julian under Bouguereau and École Nationale des Beaux-Arts under Moreau
- **1896** — Met John Russell on Belle Île, adopted bright colours
- **1905** — Fauv exhibition at Salon d'Automne; "Donatello chez les fauves" coined
- **1917** — Relocated to Cimiez, French Riviera
- **1930s** — Bold simplification of form; murals for Barnes Foundation
- **1941** — Diagnosed with duodenal cancer; began cut-outs while bedridden
- **1943** — Moved to Vence
- **1948** — Began designs for Chapelle du Rosaire de Vence
- **1954** — Died in Nice; buried at Cimiez

## Body

### Early Life and Training
Matisse was born into a bourgeois family in northern France and initially studied law in Paris. After a bout of appendicitis in 1889, his mother provided art supplies, sparking his decision to pursue painting. He trained at the Académie Julian under William-Adolphe Bouguereau and later at the École Nationale des Beaux-Arts under Gustave Moreau, whose encouragement to think beyond formal constraints shaped Matisse’s independent vision. Early influences included Chardin, Poussin, Watteau, Manet, and Japanese art. A pivotal encounter with John Russell in 1896 introduced him to Impressionism and Van Gogh’s work, prompting a shift toward vivid colour.

### Fauvism and Rise to Prominence
Between 1900 and 1905, Matisse’s intense, emotionally charged palette earned him notoriety as a leader of the Fauves (“wild beasts”), a term coined by critic Louis Vauxcelles after viewing Matisse’s *Woman with a Hat* at the 1905 Salon d’Automne. Alongside André Derain, Georges Braque, and others, Matisse rejected naturalistic colour in favour of expressive, dissonant hues. Despite harsh criticism, support from collectors like Gertrude and Leo Stein bolstered his reputation. His travels to North Africa and Spain deepened his engagement with Primitivism and Islamic art, leading to bolder compositions such as *L’Atelier Rouge* (1911).

### Classical Period and Post-War Evolution
After relocating to Cimiez on the French Riviera in 1917, Matisse entered a phase of stylistic relaxation, aligning with post-war neoclassicism seen in works by Picasso and Stravinsky. His odalisque paintings, though commercially successful, drew mixed reviews for their decorative qualities. In the 1930s, he embraced greater abstraction and simplification, culminating in large-scale works like *Large Reclining Nude* (1935). During World War II, Matisse remained in Vichy France, producing illustrations and lithographs despite restrictions. His daughter Marguerite’s resistance activities and survival of Gestapo imprisonment underscored the personal toll of the era.

### Final Years: Cut-Outs and Legacy
Diagnosed with duodenal cancer in 1941, Matisse turned to paper cut-outs (decoupage) as his primary medium, transforming his studio practice into a collaborative effort with assistants who painted sheets of paper for him to cut. These vibrant compositions ranged from intimate book illustrations (*Jazz*, 1947) to monumental murals for the Chapelle du Rosaire in Vence (1948–1951). His final work was the design for a stained-glass window installed at the Union Church of Pocantico Hills; the maquette was on the wall of his bedroom when he died in November 1954, though installation was completed in 1956. Matisse’s legacy spans painting, sculpture, and innovative collage, cementing his role as a central figure in modern art.

## Terms
- **Fauvism**: An early 20th-century avant-garde movement characterised by wild, non-naturalistic colour and loose brushwork.
- **Decoupage**: A technique involving cutting painted or coloured paper into shapes arranged into compositions; Matisse’s primary medium in his final decade.
- **Primitivism**: An artistic movement drawing inspiration from non-Western art, particularly African masks and sculptures.
- **Neoclassicism**: A return to classical ideals of order and clarity in art, prominent in the 1920s among modernists including Matisse and Picasso.
- **Odalisque**: A genre painting depicting a reclining female figure, often in exotic settings, associated with Matisse’s Nice period.
- **Divisionism**: A technique using small dots or brushstrokes of pure colour, later adopted by Matisse before Fauvism.
- **Académie Julian**: A private art school in Paris where Matisse studied under William-Adolphe Bouguereau.
- **Salon d’Automne**: An annual independent art exhibition in Paris where the Fauves first exhibited together in 1905.
- **Montparnasse**: A Parisian district that became a hub for artists and intellectuals in the early 20th century.
- **Vichy France**: The authoritarian regime governing southern France during WWII, where Matisse chose to remain during the occupation.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate the extent to which Matisse’s cut-outs represent a culmination of his artistic philosophy versus a pragmatic response to physical limitation. While some view the late works as evidence of continued innovation, others question whether they dilute the painterly concerns of his earlier career. Additionally, the interpretation of his orientalist odalisques remains contested, with critics divided between viewing them as aesthetic explorations and dismissing them as shallow exoticism. The provenance of several Matisse works looted during the Nazi era continues to prompt restitution claims, raising ongoing questions about museum ethics and ownership.
