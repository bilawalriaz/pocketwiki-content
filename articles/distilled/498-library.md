# Library

## Overview
A library is a curated collection of materials—books, media, and digital resources—organized for access by a defined community. It functions as both a physical or virtual space and a service institution staffed by information professionals (librarians) who manage collections, assist research, and teach information literacy. Libraries vary by type (public, academic, national, special) but share core missions: preserving cultural memory, providing equitable access to information, and supporting education and research. Since the late 20th century, libraries have navigated a shift from print-centric repositories to hybrid "Library 2.0" hubs offering digital databases, makerspaces, and internet access, while facing budget pressures and the challenge of marketing their value in an era of ubiquitous online search.

## Timeline
- **2600 BC** — Earliest known libraries: clay tablet archives in cuneiform script appear in Sumer.
- **5th century BC** — Private written-book libraries emerge in classical Greece.
- **6th century AD** — Great libraries of the Mediterranean world centered in Constantinople and Alexandria.
- **909–1171** — Fatimid Caliphate maintains vast palace libraries; historian Ibn Abi Tayyi’ calls one a "wonder of the world."
- **1256** — Mongol conquest of Alamut results in massacre of Nizaris and destruction of their famed library.
- **14th century** — Libraries of Timbuktu established, attracting global scholars.
- **1732** — Załuski Library founded in Warsaw; oldest modern public library, later becomes National Library of Poland.
- **19th century** — Stack system (separate storage from reading rooms) develops; library instruction movement begins in US (led by John Cotton Dana).
- **1965** — Arrowhead Library System established in Minnesota (later merges into first regional multitype system in 2012).
- **2005** — Term "Library 2.0" coined, marking adoption of Web 2.0 technologies (tagging, social networks) to meet user needs.
- **1991–2001** — University of California system sees 54% decline in book circulation (8.3M to 3.8M) amid digital shift.
- **2002–2004** — Average US academic library transactions decline ~2.2% as internet usage grows.

## Body

### Definition and Core Functions
A library provides physical (hard copy) or digital (soft copy) materials and may exist as a physical location, a virtual space, or a hybrid. Collections typically include circulating printed materials and a reference section restricted to on-site use. Resources span books, periodicals, manuscripts, films, maps, microform, optical media (DVDs, Blu-rays, CDs), audio formats, e-books, databases, and even table or video games. Beyond storage, libraries offer quiet study areas, collaborative spaces, and public technology access (computers, internet). The defining professional service is provided by librarians—trained experts in finding, selecting, organizing, and interpreting information—forming the academic discipline of library and information science.

### Historical Development
Library history begins with the organization of documents. The earliest archives used clay tablets in Sumer (c. 2600 BC). Private book collections appeared in 5th-century BC Greece. By the 6th century AD, the major Mediterranean repositories were in Constantinople and Alexandria. The Fatimid Caliphate (909–1171) held immense collections, described as wonders of the world. Conversely, library destruction has been a tactic of conquest, notably the 1256 Mongol sack of the Nizari library at Alamut. The Timbuktu libraries (14th century) became global scholarly centers. The modern public library model originates with the 1732 Załuski Library in Warsaw, which evolved into the National Library of Poland.

### Internal Organization and Standards
Libraries use classification systems (e.g., Dewey Decimal Classification) to arrange materials for efficient retrieval. Larger libraries divide operations into departments: **Circulation** (user accounts, loans, shelving); **Collection Development** (acquisitions, budgets); **Reference** (user assistance, instruction, programming); **Electronic Library** (digital access); and **Technical Services** (cataloging, processing, deaccessioning). "Closed stacks" restrict public access, requiring staff retrieval via "stack requests." Management tasks include acquisition planning, preservation (especially for fragile manuscripts), deaccessioning ("weeding"), and technology administration. The International Organization for Standardization (ISO) Technical Committee 46 publishes standards for library statistics (ISO 2789), performance indicators (ISO 11620), document storage (ISO 11799), binding (ISO 14416), and electronic service metrics (ISO/TR 20983).

### Usage, Discovery, and the Internet
Historically, the **card catalogue**—cabinets of index cards—mediated access. This was replaced by **OPACs (Online Public Access Catalogues)** and "webcats," enabling remote searching. Large libraries span multiple buildings/floors with resources on shelves (bays); retrieval uses signage, maps, GPS, or RFID. Usage barriers include poor search systems, untrained staff, and user hesitation. The 19th-century US library instruction movement, championed by John Cotton Dana, promoted **information literacy** to address this. Finland leads globally in registered borrowers per capita (over 50% of population). US public library book circulation per user dropped ~50% from 1978–2004; growth in audiovisual circulation (25% of total by 2004) accounted for roughly half the decline.

The internet relationship is complex. Libraries provide computers and internet access, create websites, and digitize collections (e.g., Google Books partnerships). Specialized tools like **Google Scholar** and **WorldCat** (via OCLC) extend discovery. However, the convenience of internet search challenges libraries to market **information literacy** as a quality differentiator. Reference service usage has declined, prompting the "Library 2.0" response (2005): adopting Web 2.0 features (tagging, commenting, social media, widgets) to become more user-driven. Privacy advocacy has grown; the Library Freedom Project trains librarians in tools like the Tor network to resist surveillance. Marketing remains difficult for publicly funded institutions with tight budgets.

### Librarians and Professionalization
Staffing combines professional librarians, paraprofessionals (library technicians), and support staff. Education covers collection accessibility, acquisition, arrangement tools, the book trade, physical properties of writing materials, language distribution, literacy rates, budgeting, architecture, usage patterns, cultural heritage roles, and sponsorship models. Since the 1960s, computerization and digitization have become central curricular issues. Professional bodies like **IFLA** (International Federation of Library Associations and Institutions) provide global coordination; national associations (ALA, CILIP, etc.) advocate for the profession and information literacy teaching.

### Types of Libraries

**Library Systems and Branches**
A **library system** centrally manages multiple branches via an **Integrated Library System (ILS)**—an enterprise resource planning tool tracking items, orders, bills, and patrons. Examples: Arrowhead Library System (Minnesota, 7 counties, 29 members); London Public Library (Canada, 16 branches); Helsinki Metropolitan Area Libraries (Finland, 63 libraries); National Library of Venezuela (685 branches). A **branch** uses the system's classification and ILS interconnectivity.

**Academic Libraries**
Located on college/university campuses, serving students and faculty. Functions: research support, consultancy, resource linkage, and curriculum support (course reserves: short-term textbook/article loans). Increasingly digital: subscribing to e-journal databases, providing research software, computer labs, and institutional repositories for theses/dissertations. Many act as **Open Access publishers**. Offer workshops on citations, search techniques, and citation software (information literacy). Provide quiet and group study spaces.

**Public Lending Libraries**
Serve the general public; funded/protected by the community. Issue library cards for borrowing; staff set loan limits/periods. Often part of countywide systems allowing cross-branch access. Function as community hubs: free events (story times, reading groups), combating illiteracy (per Pennsylvania Library Association study). Physical infrastructure evolved from 19th-century **stack systems** (cast iron/steel frames, translucent floors for light, later metal grating for air circulation) to **compact shelving** (mobile shelves on tracks). Budgets frequently face legislative cuts, forcing reduced hours/staff.

**National Libraries**
Serve as national repositories with **legal deposit** rights (publishers must submit copies). Rarely lend to citizens; hold rare/valuable/significant works. Origins often in royal/state collections. Cooperate via IFLA National Libraries Section and The European Library (CENL service).

**Reference and Research Libraries**
**Reference libraries** do not lend; materials used on-site (e.g., university research). Lending libraries often contain reference sections (dictionaries, newspapers). **Research libraries** support scholarly/scientific work with primary/secondary sources and permanent collections. Often academic or national libraries; large public libraries may qualify. In the US, major ones belong to the **Association of Research Libraries**; in the UK, **Research Libraries UK (RLUK)**. Examples: British Library, Bodleian Library (Oxford), NYPL Main Branch, Siberian Branch of Russian Academy of Science library. May be reference-only or lending (academic research libraries typically lend books but not periodicals).

**Digital Libraries**
House curated digital resources (text, images, audio) managed by digital librarians. Growth correlates with declining physical transactions (US academic libraries: -2.2% 2002–2004; UC system: -54% circulation 1991–2001).

**Special Libraries**
Maintained by private businesses, hospitals, churches, museums, law firms, government agencies for specialized employee research. Access varies; staff often subject specialists rather than general librarians. Includes **women's libraries** (e.g., Vancouver Women's Library, Women's Library @LSE) and **LGBTQ libraries/archives**. Distinct from **special collections** (rare materials within a library), though special libraries may have their own. Sub-types: law, medical, music, transportation libraries.

**Media Libraries (Médiathèques)**
Distinct in some countries (notably France, 1970s origin) for holding multimedia (video, sound recordings) vs. physical books. Examples: Sendai Mediatheque (Japan), Médiathèque Musicale Mahler, Médiathèques de Saint-Étienne (France).

**Children's Libraries**
Special collections for juvenile readers, often in separate rooms/wings of public libraries. Educational mission: acquaint youth with literature, cultivate reading love, supplement schools. Services: storytelling, after-school programs, summer reading programs. **PAWS TO READ** programs let children read to therapy dogs to build confidence.

**Library Makerspaces**
Creative hands-on spaces within libraries providing tools for design, prototyping, and creation: 3D printers, CNC machines, laser cutters, sewing machines, electronics, recording studios, woodshops, and software for graphic design, video editing, 3D modeling, coding. Aim to expand library role from information access to **active learning**, supporting STEM/STEAM, digital literacy, and lifelong learning (code clubs, robotics, arts).

### Protection and Advocacy
Libraries are cultural heritage assets at risk in conflicts (destruction, looting). **Blue Shield International** and **UNESCO** coordinate military/civil protection; "no-strike lists" preserve coordinates of key sites. **Public library advocacy** seeks financial/philosophical support via donations, campaigning, and groups like Friends of Libraries. Modern advocacy emphasizes demonstrating "economic value to the community" beyond circulation metrics.

## Terms
- ****Legal Deposit**** — A legal requirement that publishers deposit a copy of each publication with the national library.
- ****OPAC (Online Public Access Catalogue)**** — An electronic database allowing users to search a library's holdings from any internet-connected location.
- ****Integrated Library System (ILS)**** — Enterprise resource planning software for libraries to track items, orders, bills, and patron borrowing.
- ****Stacks**** — The shelving areas where library collections are stored; "closed stacks" require staff retrieval, "open stacks" allow public browsing.
- ****Reference Section/Collection**** — Materials (e.g., dictionaries, encyclopedias, periodicals) restricted to in-library use; non-circulating.
- ****Information Literacy**** — The ability to locate, evaluate, and effectively use information; a core instructional goal of modern librarianship.
- ****Library 2.0**** — A service model (coined 2005) adopting Web 2.0 technologies (tagging, social media, widgets) to make libraries more user-driven.
- ****Médiathèque**** — A multimedia library holding videos, sound recordings, and other non-print media, distinct from traditional book libraries (French origin, 1970s).
- ****Deaccessioning (Weeding)**** — The formal process of permanently removing materials from a library collection.
- ****Makerspace**** — A collaborative workspace in a library providing tools (3D printers, laser cutters, sewing machines, coding software) for hands-on creation and STEM/STEAM learning.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Print vs. Digital Balance:** How to allocate shrinking budgets between physical collections and expanding digital resources (databases, e-books, streaming) as circulation of print declines sharply (e.g., -54% at UC 1991–2001).
*   **Marketing and Value Proposition:** Whether publicly funded libraries can justify spending on branding/marketing to compete with the convenience of commercial search engines (Google, Amazon) and demonstrate "economic value" beyond book lending.
*   **Information Literacy vs. Convenience:** How to prioritize teaching critical evaluation skills when users increasingly prefer frictionless, algorithmic search results that may sacrifice quality for speed.
*   **Privacy vs. Analytics:** Tension between patron privacy (advocated by Library Freedom Project via tools like Tor) and the library's need for usage data to improve services and secure funding.
*   **Space Repurposing:** Converting stack space (reduced by digital shift) into collaborative study areas, makerspaces, or community hubs without alienating traditional users.
*   **Sustainability of Special Collections:** Preserving rare/unique physical materials (manuscripts, fragile items) requires specialized, costly conservation amid general budget cuts.
*   **Role of Librarians:** Evolving from collection gatekeepers to educators, data curators, publishers (Open Access), and technology facilitators—requiring continuous re-skilling since the 1960s computerization wave.
