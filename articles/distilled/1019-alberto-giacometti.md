# Alberto Giacometti

## Overview
Alberto Giacometti (1901–1966) was a Swiss sculptor, painter, draftsman, and printmaker regarded as one of the 20th century’s most important sculptors. Initially influenced by Cubism and Surrealism in Paris, he broke with the Surrealists around 1935 to pursue an obsessive, phenomenological analysis of the human figure. His mature work—extremely tall, thin bronze figures such as *Walking Man*—emerged after World War II and embodies existential themes of isolation, the "surrounding void," and the impossibility of capturing perceived reality. His relentless reworking of models, driven by self-doubt, resulted in heavily eroded surfaces that evoke "lone trees in winter."

- **10 Oct 1901** — Born in Borgonovo, Switzerland, to post-Impressionist painter Giovanni Giacometti.
- **1922** — Moves to Paris; studies under Antoine Bourdelle at the Académie de la Grande Chaumière.
- **1926** — Settles into studio at 46 rue Hippolyte-Maindron, Montparnasse, where he remains for life.
- **1930–1935** — Leading Surrealist sculptor; associates include Miró, Ernst, Picasso, and Balthus.
- **c. 1935** — Breaks with Surrealism to work from live models, focusing on the human head and gaze.
- **1938–1944** — Sculptures shrink to max 7 cm (2.75 in), reflecting the distance between artist and model.
- **1941–1945** — Takes refuge in Switzerland during WWII; meets Annette Arm (1946), marries her 1949.
- **1945–1960** — Creates signature tall, slender bronze figures (*Walking Man*, *Standing Woman*).
- **1958** — Commissioned for Chase Manhattan Bank plaza, NYC; produces four *Grande femme debout* but abandons project.
- **1962** — Wins Grand Prize for Sculpture at Venice Biennale, securing worldwide fame.
- **11 Jan 1966** — Dies of pericarditis and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease in Chur, Switzerland.
- **2015** — *L’Homme au doigt* sells for $141.3 million (with fees), becoming the most expensive sculpture at auction. |

## Body

### Early Life and Artistic Formation
Giacometti was born into an artistic family in Borgonovo; his father Giovanni was a noted post-Impressionist painter. Encouraged by his father and godfather, he attended the École Supérieure des Beaux-Arts in Genève. His brothers Diego and Bruno also became artists, and his cousin Zaccaria Giacometti grew up with them after being orphaned in 1905. In 1922, Giacometti moved to Paris to study under Antoine Bourdelle, an associate of Rodin. By 1926, he occupied the cramped Montparnasse studio that became his lifelong laboratory.

### Surrealist Period and the Break (1926–1935)
In Paris, Giacometti experimented with Cubism and Surrealism, becoming a leading Surrealist sculptor alongside figures like Miró, Max Ernst, and Picasso. Around 1935, he abandoned Surrealist automatism to pursue a "more in-depth analysis of figurative compositions." He began working daily from live models—primarily his sister and the artist Isabel Rawsthorne—obsessively attempting to capture the construction of the head and the sitter's gaze. He found the head "an object completely unknown and without dimensions," a realization that fueled decades of struggle.

### Wartime Miniatures and the Shift to Scale (1938–1945)
Between 1938 and 1944, Giacometti’s sculptures diminished to a maximum height of seven centimeters. He explained this shrinkage as a faithful rendering of the optical distance between his position and the model: "wanting to create from memory what I had seen, to my terror the sculptures became smaller and smaller." During WWII, he lived in Switzerland, where he met Red Cross secretary Annette Arm in 1946; they married in 1949. After the war, as his figures grew taller, they became paradoxically thinner, establishing the attenuated proportions of his mature style.

### Mature Style: The Walking Man and Existential Space (1945–1960)
Giacometti’s most famous works—bronze figures like *Walking Man*, *Standing Nude Woman*, and busts—were made between 1945 and 1960. He viewed people in motion as "a succession of moments of stillness." The Metropolitan Museum of Art describes the "rough, eroded, heavily worked surfaces" of works like *Three Men Walking (II)* as evoking "lone trees in winter." Giacometti wrote that figures were "never a compact mass but like a transparent construction." Philosophers such as William Barrett linked these emaciated forms to existential "Nothingness," seeing figures "invaded by the surrounding void." A 2011–2012 Pinacothèque de Paris exhibition also highlighted the influence of Etruscan art on his formal vocabulary.

### Late Career: Public Commissions, Painting, and Printmaking
In 1958, Giacometti accepted a commission for the Chase Manhattan Bank plaza in New York, a city he had never visited. He produced his largest sculptures, *Grande femme debout I–IV* (1960), but abandoned the project, unsatisfied with the site relationship. After 1957, his figurative paintings—nearly monochrome and isolated—achieved parity with his sculptures. His printmaking, though overlooked, produced significant images in editions as small as 30 copies. He continued to rework and destroy models until his death, preparing the lithograph sequence *Paris sans fin* as his final project.

### Death and Posthumous Legacy
Giacometti died in 1966 in Chur. His widow Annette became sole rights holder, combating forgeries until her death in 1993, when the French state established the Fondation Alberto et Annette Giacometti (holding ~5,000 works). A parallel Alberto-Giacometti-Stiftung exists in Zürich. Giacometti’s market dominance is historic: *L’Homme qui marche I* sold for £65 million ($104.3M) in 2010, and *L’Homme au doigt* reached $141.3M with fees in 2015, the auction record for sculpture. His *L’Homme qui marche I* appeared on the 100 Swiss franc banknote; he designed a tree for Beckett’s *Waiting for Godot* (1961) and the monument for Gerda Taro at Père Lachaise. Major retrospectives continue globally, including Tate Modern (2017) and the National Gallery of Ireland (2022).

## Terms
- **Post-Impressionism**: Late 19th-century art movement (e.g., Cézanne, Van Gogh) emphasizing structure and subjective expression over naturalism; practiced by Giacometti’s father Giovanni.
- **Surrealism**: 1920s–30s avant-garde movement exploring the unconscious, dreams, and irrational juxtaposition; Giacometti was a leading sculptor in this circle until ~1935.
- **Phenomenology**: Philosophical study of structures of consciousness and phenomena as experienced from the first-person view; central to Giacometti’s attempt to render "the way he saw them."
- **Existentialism**: Philosophy stressing individual freedom, isolation, and the absurdity of existence; critics (e.g., William Barrett) read Giacometti’s attenuated figures as embodiments of existential "Nothingness."
- **Attenuated**: Drawn out, elongated, and thinned to an extreme degree; the hallmark of Giacometti’s mature figures.
- **Armature**: Internal framework (often wire) supporting a sculpture modeled in clay or plaster; Giacometti’s relentless reworking often exposed or distorted this structure.
- **Catalogue raisonné**: Comprehensive, annotated listing of all known works by an artist; Herbert Lust’s 1970 volume documents Giacometti’s graphics and drawings.
- **Blocs of sensation**: Gilles Deleuze’s term (from his study of Francis Bacon) for non-representational forces of color and line; applied by some critics to Giacometti’s textured surfaces.
- **Etruscan art**: Pre-Roman Italian civilization (c. 800–100 BC) known for elongated bronze votive figures; cited as a formal influence on Giacometti in a 2011 Paris exhibition.
- **Pericarditis**: Inflammation of the pericardium (heart sac); cause of Giacometti’s death alongside chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

## Debates and open questions
- **Categorization**: Scholars dispute whether Giacometti’s mature work is formalist (focus on structure), expressionist (emotional distortion), or phenomenological (capturing lived perception); Deleuze’s "blocs of sensation" offers a further framework.
- **Surrealist legacy**: Though excommunicated by the Surrealist group, his later imitative intent produced results that remained expressions of emotional response—whether this constitutes a continuation or rupture is debated.
- **Etruscan influence**: The extent to which Etruscan bronzes directly shaped his attenuated forms versus serving as a retrospective affinity remains under discussion.
- **Market vs. scholarship**: Record auction prices ($141.3M for *L’Homme au doigt*) raise questions about whether market narratives overshadow art-historical analysis of his painting, drawing, and printmaking.
- **Authenticity and forgeries**: The high value of his work fuels a persistent market in fakes; the Fondation’s authentication efforts (and the 2007 conviction of Roland Dumas and Jacques Tajan) highlight ongoing provenance challenges.
