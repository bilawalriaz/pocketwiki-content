# Gothic architecture

## Overview
Gothic architecture is a medieval European style (c. 1140–1500) characterized by pointed arches, ribbed vaults, and flying buttresses, which allowed for unprecedented height, light, and structural daring. It evolved from Romanesque architecture and was succeeded by Renaissance architecture, representing a major shift in engineering and aesthetics that defined the High and Late Middle Ages.

## Timeline
- **c. 1140–1144** — Choir of Abbey of Saint-Denis reconstructed, combining early Gothic features.
- **c. 1135–1164** — Sens Cathedral built, one of the first fully Gothic structures.
- **1160s** — Notre-Dame de Paris begun, introducing flying buttresses.
- **1194–1220** — Chartres Cathedral rebuilt, exemplifying High Gothic.
- **c. 1241–1248** — Sainte-Chapelle built, a masterpiece of Rayonnant Gothic.
- **1344–** — Construction of St. Vitus Cathedral in Prague begins under Peter Parler.
- **1387–** — Milan Cathedral project starts, a rare Italian attempt at French Gothic.
- **1420–1436** — Brunelleschi's dome for Florence Cathedral marks the Renaissance transition.
- **1550** — Giorgio Vasari uses "Gothic" pejoratively in *Lives of the Artists*.
- **1722–1745** — Gothic Revival begins with Westminster Abbey's western towers.

## Body

### Origins and Defining Features
Gothic architecture originated in the Île-de-France region of northern France. Its defining element is the pointed arch, which, combined with ribbed vaults and flying buttresses, enabled taller, thinner walls filled with stained glass. The style was first called *opus Francigenum* ("French work"). The term "Gothic" was coined later as a pejorative by Renaissance figures like Giorgio Vasari, who associated it with the "barbarous" Goths.

### Structural Innovations
The rib vault channeled weight to piers, reducing wall thickness. Flying buttresses transferred thrust outward, allowing for vast clerestory windows. Early Gothic used six-part vaults (e.g., Sens Cathedral), but High Gothic simplified to four-part vaults (e.g., Chartres), enabling greater height. Later styles developed elaborate decorative vaults like fan vaults in England.

### Chronological Development
*   **Early Gothic (c. 1130–1240):** Focused on structural experimentation, seen at Saint-Denis and Notre-Dame de Paris.
*   **High Gothic (c. 1194–1250):** Achieved great height and light, exemplified by Chartres, Reims, and Amiens Cathedrals.
*   **Rayonnant (c. 1230–1350):** Maximized window area with intricate tracery, as in Sainte-Chapelle.
*   **Late Gothic (c. 1350–1500):** Featured elaborate decoration, including Flamboyant (France) and Perpendicular (England) styles.

### Regional Variations
*   **France:** The heartland, emphasizing height and light (e.g., Beauvais Cathedral).
*   **England:** Developed the Perpendicular style with fan vaults and emphasized length (e.g., King's College Chapel, Cambridge).
*   **Germany:** Produced hall churches and elaborate brick Gothic (*Backsteingotik*) in the north.
*   **Italy:** Adapted Gothic minimally, preserving wall mass for frescoes (e.g., Florence Cathedral).
*   **Iberia:** Blended Gothic with Moorish influences (e.g., Toledo Cathedral).

### Influence and Decline
Gothic spread via Cistercian monks, trade, and the Crusades, with possible influences from Islamic and Armenian architecture. It was supplanted by Renaissance architecture from the mid-15th century but survived in some regions. A major Gothic Revival began in 18th-century England.

## Terms
*   **Pointed Arch:** The defining Gothic element, allowing for greater height and weight distribution.
*   **Rib Vault:** A vault where stone ribs support the ceiling, directing weight to piers.
*   **Flying Buttress:** An external half-arch that transfers vault thrust away from walls.
*   **Tracery:** Stonework that supports and decorates windows, evolving from plate to bar tracery.
*   **Clerestory:** The upper level of a church wall, filled with windows.
*   **Triforium:** A shallow gallery above the arcade and below the clerestory.
*   **Lancet Window:** A tall, narrow window with a pointed arch.
*   **Flamboyant:** A Late Gothic style with flame-like, curvilinear tracery.
*   **Perpendicular:** An English Late Gothic style emphasizing vertical lines and fan vaults.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Origins:** The extent of influence from Islamic architecture (via the pointed arch) versus independent Romanesque evolution is debated. Christopher Wren argued for a "Saracen style," but early European pointed arches predate the Crusades.
*   **Terminology:** The pejorative use of "Gothic" by Renaissance writers like Vasari is well-documented, but its accuracy is contested by scholars who note the style's sophistication.
*   **Structural Limits:** The collapse of Beauvais Cathedral's choir in 1284 raised questions about the engineering limits of Gothic height, leading to more conservative designs thereafter.
