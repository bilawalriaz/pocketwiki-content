# Kiel Canal

## Overview
The Kiel Canal (German: Nord-Ostsee-Kanal) is a 98-kilometer freshwater canal in Germany that links the North Sea to the Baltic Sea, significantly reducing maritime travel between the two by 460 kilometers. It is one of the world's busiest artificial waterways, vital for both commercial shipping and naval strategy.

## Timeline
- **1784** — The Eider Canal, a 175 km waterway using the Eider River, is completed under Danish rule.
- **1864** — The Second Schleswig War places Schleswig-Holstein under Prussian control.
- **June 1887** — Construction of the new canal begins at Holtenau.
- **20 June 1895** — Kaiser Wilhelm II officially opens the canal, naming it the Kaiser Wilhelm Canal.
- **1907–1914** — The canal is widened to allow dreadnought battleships to pass.
- **Post-WWI** — The Treaty of Versailles mandates the canal be open to international commerce and warships of nations at peace with Germany.
- **1936** — Adolf Hitler's government repudiates the canal's international status.
- **1948** — The canal is renamed the Nord-Ostsee-Kanal (Kiel Canal).
- **March 2013** — Partial closure occurs due to failed lock gates at Brunsbüttel.
- **2020** — Widening and deepening of the eastern part of the canal begins.
- **10 November 2025** — The first expanded section of the eastern canal opens.

## Body

### History and Construction
The first connection between the seas was the Eider Canal, completed in 1784. It was limited by its narrow width (29 m) and shallow depth (3 m), restricting vessel size. After Prussia gained control of Schleswig-Holstein in 1864, merchants and the German navy sought a new, larger canal to link their North Sea and Baltic bases without sailing around Denmark. Construction began in 1887 and was completed in 1895. To accommodate growing traffic and the Imperial German Navy's dreadnought battleships, the canal was widened between 1907 and 1914.

### International Status and Modern Use
After World War I, the Treaty of Versailles required the canal to remain under German administration but be open to international traffic. This international status was repudiated by Germany in 1936 but restored after World War II. The canal is governed by strict traffic rules, with vessels categorized into six groups. Larger ships must accept pilots and may need tugboat assistance. The maximum ship dimensions are a length of 235.50 m, a beam (width) of 32.50 m, and a draught (depth below waterline) of up to 9.50 m for shorter ships. All fixed bridges have a clearance of 42 m.

### Crossings and Infrastructure
The canal is crossed by eleven fixed links, including railway lines, autobahns, and federal roads. Notable crossings include the Rendsburg High Bridge, which has a suspended "hanging ferry" for local traffic, and the Kanaltunnel Rendsburg road tunnel. The oldest bridge still in use is the Levensau High Bridge (1894). Local traffic is also served by 14 free ferry lines operated by the Canal Authority.

## Terms
- **Draught**: The vertical distance between the waterline and the bottom of a ship's hull.
- **Beam**: The width of a ship at its widest point.
- **Dreadnought**: A type of battleship that became dominant in the early 20th century, characterized by an "all-big-gun" armament scheme.
- **Aviso**: A small, fast naval vessel used for dispatch and scouting.
- **Bollard**: A sturdy post on a ship or quay to which a ship's mooring rope may be attached.
- **Transporter bridge**: A type of movable bridge that carries a segment of roadway across a river, suspended from a high overhead structure.
- **Federal Transport Ministry**: The German government ministry responsible for transport infrastructure and policy.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes a financial and administrative dispute between the German Federal Government and the state of Schleswig-Holstein, which was blamed for the neglect leading to the 2013 lock gate failure. The ongoing expansion project, particularly the widening of the eastern section, represents a major modern investment to maintain the canal's capacity.
