# Marilyn Monroe

## Overview
Marilyn Monroe (1926–1962), born Norma Jeane Mortenson, rose from a traumatic childhood in Los Angeles foster homes and an orphanage to become the defining sex symbol of 1950s Hollywood. Her "blonde bombshell" persona—crafted through platinum hair, breathy voice, and calculated vulnerability—masked a shrewd businesswoman who fought the studio system for creative control, founding her own production company (Marilyn Monroe Productions) in 1954 and winning a landmark contract granting her script and director approval. She delivered acclaimed performances in *Bus Stop* (1956), *The Prince and the Showgirl* (1957), and the comedy classic *Some Like It Hot* (1959), earning a Golden Globe. Her marriages to baseball icon Joe DiMaggio and playwright Arthur Miller ended in divorce amid intense public scrutiny. Struggling with chronic insomnia, endometriosis, and escalating barbiturate dependence, she died at 36 of acute barbiturate poisoning, ruled a probable suicide. Posthumously, she remains a contested cultural icon: simultaneously a victim of studio exploitation and a proactive architect of her image, embodying tensions between female agency, media commodification, and the "sexual revolution" she came to symbolize.

## Body

### Childhood and Early Instability (1926–1943)
Monroe’s mother, Gladys Pearl Baker (born in Piedras Negras, Mexico), worked as a film negative cutter but suffered paranoid schizophrenia, leading to institutionalization in 1934. Monroe spent years with foster parents Albert and Ida Bolender (Hawthorne), then briefly with her mother in Hollywood before Gladys’s breakdown. A 16-month stay with lodgers George and Maude Atkinson may have included sexual abuse; Monroe developed a stutter and withdrew. After the Los Angeles Orphans Home (1935–1937), she endured alleged molestation by Erwin "Doc" Goddard and shuffled among relatives. At 12, she found stability with Grace Goddard’s aunt Ana Lower in Sawtelle, excelling in writing at Emerson Junior High. In 1942, to avoid the orphanage when the Goddards relocated to West Virginia, she married neighbor James Dougherty, five years her senior. She later described the marriage as "dying of boredom."

### Modeling, First Contracts, and the "Blonde Bombshell" Template (1944–1952)
With Dougherty deployed, Monroe worked at Radioplane Company (Van Nuys). In late 1944, Army photographer David Conover shot morale photos there; though her images weren’t used, she quit in January 1945 to model for Conover and friends, signing with Blue Book Model Agency in August 1945. Owner Emmeline Snively noted her ambition; by early 1946 she had 33 magazine covers. She straightened and dyed her naturally curly brown hair platinum blonde. A screen test by Fox executive Ben Lyon led to a six-month contract (August 1946); Lyon chose "Marilyn" (after Broadway’s Marilyn Miller), she supplied "Monroe" (mother’s maiden name). Fox dropped her in August 1947 after bit parts in *Dangerous Years* and *Scudda Hoo! Scudda Hay!*. A brief Columbia stint (March–September 1948) yielded *Ladies of the Chorus*, modeled on Rita Hayworth’s image, coached by Natasha Lytess (mentor until 1955).

Agent Johnny Hyde (William Morris VP) became her protégé and lover in 1949. He secured pivotal small roles: *All About Eve* (1950, 14 Oscar nominations; Bette Davis praised her ambition) and *The Asphalt Jungle* (1950, noir by John Huston; *Photoplay* noted her transition to serious actress). Hyde negotiated a seven-year Fox contract (December 1950) but died days later. In 1951–52, Monroe starred in Fox comedies (*As Young as You Feel*, *Love Nest*, *Let's Make It Legal*) and dramas (*Clash by Night* with Fritz Lang, *Don't Bother to Knock*). The March 1952 nude-calendar scandal—she admitted posing while broke—generated sympathy and *Life* cover ("Talk of Hollywood"). By year-end she was "it girl" of 1952, yet gained a reputation for lateness, line-forgetting, and dependence on acting coaches (Lytess, then Paula Strasberg), attributed to perfectionism, low self-esteem, stage fright, and escalating barbiturate/amphetamine use for insomnia.

### 1953: Sex Symbol Cemented
Three releases made Monroe a top box-office draw. *Niagara* (January, Technicolor noir) showcased her as a murderous femme fatale; her "trademark" look (dark arched brows, pale skin, red lips, beauty mark) and a 30-second hip-swaying walk became marketing icons. *Gentlemen Prefer Blondes* (June, musical comedy with Jane Russell) cemented the "dumb blonde" persona; her "Diamonds Are a Girl's Best Friend" number was hailed for "sex[ing] a song." *How to Marry a Millionaire* (November, second CinemaScope film) repeated the formula. She won *Photoplay*’s "Fastest Rising Star," appeared on *Life* covers, and in December Hugh Hefner used her 1952 Miss America parade photo and a 1949 nude for the first *Playboy* cover/centerfold—without her consent. Fox historian Aubrey Solomon called her the studio’s "greatest asset" alongside CinemaScope.

### Conflict with Fox, MMP, and Marriage to DiMaggio (1954–1955)
Despite stardom, Monroe’s 1950 contract paid far less than peers and barred project choice. Studio head Darryl F. Zanuck disliked her and canceled "serious films" to focus on entertainment. In January 1954, he suspended her for refusing *The Girl in Pink Tights*. She countered by marrying DiMaggio (January 14), a USO tour in Korea (60,000 Marines), and winning *Photoplay*’s "Most Popular Female Star." Fox settled in March: $100k bonus, new contract promise, *The Seven Year Itch*. *River of No Return* (April) and *There's No Business Like Show Business* (late 1954) followed. Filming *The Seven Year Itch* (September 1954), Fox staged the Lexington Avenue subway-grate scene (white dress, 2,000 spectators), generating global headlines. DiMaggio’s jealousy and alleged abuse ended the marriage (divorce filed October 1954).

In November 1954, Monroe left Hollywood, founding Marilyn Monroe Productions (MMP) with photographer Milton Greene—later called "instrumental" in the studio system’s collapse. She claimed Fox breached contract (unpaid bonus) and began a year-long legal battle. The press ridiculed her; Broadway’s *Will Success Spoil Rock Hunter?* parodied her with Jayne Mansfield. In 1955, she moved to Manhattan, studied method acting at Lee Strasberg’s Actors Studio (private lessons at Strasberg home, replaced Lytess with Paula Strasberg), and began psychoanalysis (Strasberg believed actors must confront trauma). She briefly dated Marlon Brando (intermittent until her death) and began a serious affair with Arthur Miller. The FBI opened a file on her due to Miller’s HUAC subpoena and alleged communism; years later, the FBI concluded she was "leftist" but not a Party agent. By late 1955, Fox signed a new seven-year deal: $400k for four films, creative control (projects, directors, cinematographers), and one MMP film per Fox film.

### Critical Acclaim, Miller Marriage, and *Some Like It Hot* (1956–1959)
Monroe legally became Marilyn Monroe (February 23, 1956); *Time* called her a "shrewd businesswoman." *Bus Stop* (March–August 1956, dir. Joshua Logan): she learned an Ozark accent, deliberately mediocre singing/dancing, and headed MMP on location (Idaho/Arizona). Logan revised his opinion, comparing her to Chaplin blending comedy and tragedy. *Bus Stop* earned a Golden Globe nomination and *Saturday Review* declared she "dispels once and for all the notion that she is merely a glamour personality."

June 29, 1956: civil and Jewish weddings to Miller (White Plains, then Waccabuc). She converted to Judaism; Egypt banned her films. Media framed it as "Egghead Weds Hourglass." *The Prince and the Showgirl* (August–December 1956, Pinewood Studios, dir./co-star Laurence Olivier): conflicts over Olivier’s patronizing "be sexy" demand, insistence on replicating Vivien Leigh, and Paula Strasberg’s presence. Monroe retaliated with lateness; drug dependence escalated; she suffered a miscarriage; she and Greene clashed over MMP. Released June 1957 to mixed U.S. reviews but European acclaim (David di Donatello, Crystal Star, BAFTA nomination).

After an 18-month hiatus (ectopic pregnancy 1957, miscarriage 1958, barbiturate overdose hospitalization, bought out Greene’s MMP share), she returned for *Some Like It Hot* (July–November 1958, dir. Billy Wilder, co-stars Jack Lemmon, Tony Curtis). She accepted the "dumb blonde" Sugar Kane for 10% of profits and Miller’s encouragement. Production became legendary: dozens of retakes, forgotten lines, Wilder-Monroe clashes over interpretation. Wilder ultimately praised her: "Anyone can remember lines, but it takes a real artist to come on the set and not know her lines and yet give the performance she did!" Released March 1959: critical/commercial triumph, Golden Globe for Best Actress, *Variety* lauded her "sex appeal and timing that just can't be beat." Voted among greatest films by BBC, AFI, *Sight & Sound*.

### Final Films, Health Collapse, and Death (1960–1962)
*Let's Make Love* (late 1959–September 1960, dir. George Cukor, Miller rewrites): delayed by absences, affair with Yves Montand publicized, flopped; Crowther called her "untidy," lacking "old Monroe dynamism." *The Misfits* (July–November 1960, dir. John Huston, written by Miller): Monroe as Roslyn, a divorcee befriending aging cowboys (Clark Gable, Eli Wallach, Montgomery Clift). Nevada desert shoot: marriage over (Miller with photographer Inge Morath), Monroe resented Roslyn’s basis in herself, gallstone pain, severe addiction (makeup applied while asleep under barbiturates), week-long detox hospitalization. Huston said her acting was "the real thing... go deep down within herself." Released February 1961: box-office failure, mixed reviews (Variety: "choppy"; Crowther: "completely blank"); 21st-century reappraisal calls her performance "most mature" (Tony Tracy) and "extraordinary empathy" (Geoffrey McNab).

Mexican divorce from Miller (January 1961). No films in 1961: endometriosis/gallbladder surgery, four-week depression treatment (Payne Whitney Psychiatric Clinic—erroneously placed in locked psychosis ward for three days, moved to Columbia University Medical Center with DiMaggio’s help). Dated Frank Sinatra late 1961; bought Brentwood house (12305 Fifth Helena Drive). Spring 1962: Golden Globe "World Film Favorite"; began *Something's Got to Give* (Fox, dir. Cukor, co-stars Dean Martin, Cyd Charisse). Sinusitis delayed her; Fox pressured her, alleged faking. May 19: "Happy Birthday, Mr. President" at Madison Square Garden (rhinestone nude-illusion dress). Nude pool scene filmed with press invited—first major star to pose nude at career height. June 7: Fox fired her, sued for $750k damages; replaced with Lee Remick; Martin refused, Fox sued him, shut production. Fox spread negative publicity (mental instability). Late June: Fox reopened negotiations; settlement reached for new contract (*Something's Got to Give* restart, *What a Way to Go!*). Publicity repair: *Life*, *Cosmopolitan* interviews, *Vogue* shoot with Bert Stern (fashion editorial + nude series *The Last Sitting*, published posthumously).

August 4–5, 1962: Housekeeper Eunice Murray found her dead (nude, hand on phone). Toxicology: acute barbiturate poisoning (chloral hydrate 8 mg%, pentobarbital 4.5 mg% blood/13 mg% liver—several times lethal limit). Deputy coroner Thomas Noguchi, aided by LA Suicide Prevention Team, ruled probable suicide (history of overdoses, severe fears/depressions, abrupt mood changes, no foul play). Funeral August 8 (private, DiMaggio arranged, barred Hollywood). 1982 LA District Attorney "threshold investigation" found no evidence of murder (conspiracy theories fueled by Norman Mailer’s 1973 biography).

### Screen Persona, Reception, and Cultural Significance
Fox positioned Monroe as 1950s successor to Betty Grable, targeting male audiences. She actively crafted her image: publicity strategies, gossip columnist alliances (Skolsky, Louella Parsons), image control. She invoked Jean Harlow (childhood idol, hired Harlow’s hair stylist). Persona centered on blonde stereotypes—dumbness, naïveté, sexual availability, artificiality—breathy voice, "Monroeisms" (e.g., asked what she wore in 1949 nude shoot: "I had the radio on"). Roles: "the girl" defined by gender (chorus girls, secretaries, models), hourglass figure displayed via pin-up framing, hip-swinging walk ("horizontal walk"), white/revealing clothing. Press framed her as American Dream embodiment (exaggerated foster/orphanage past). Contrasted with Grace Kelly’s unattainable sophistication, Monroe’s working-class roots made her "ideal playmate" (Thomas Harris).

Critics/audiences mistook persona for personality, hindering serious roles and business respect. Sarah Churchwell: "biggest myth is she was dumb... she was very smart indeed—and very tough... The dumb blonde was a role." Lois Banner: she parodied her sex-symbol status, a "brilliant archetype... between Mae West and Madonna... gender tricksters." Monroe studied comedy with Lotte Goslar; inserted line in *Gentlemen Prefer Blondes*: "I can be smart when it's important, but most men don't like it."

Richard Dyer: she became "virtually a household name for sex" amid 1950s flux (Freud, Kinsey Report 1953, Friedan 1963). Unlike 1940s femmes fatales, she presented sex as natural, vulnerable, "ice cream" (Norman Mailer). Donald Spoto: "postwar ideal of the American girl... offering sex without demands." Molly Haskell: "Fifties fiction... woman had no sexual needs... cater to a man's needs." Less popular with women initially; shifted after death.

Dyer argued her platinum blonde made her "racially unambiguous" as civil rights movement began; Banner noted she associated with "white ethnics" (DiMaggio Italian-American, Miller Jewish) and challenged norms (1951 *Look* photo with African-American coach Phil Moore). She aided Ella Fitzgerald’s career (promised to attend Mocambo shows). Photoplay: "national institution as well known as hot dogs, apple pie, or baseball." Banner: symbol of *populuxe*—glamorous joy coping with Cold War/atomic paranoia. Fiona Handyside: French audiences linked blondness to American modernity/liberation. Laura Mulvey: "export the democracy of glamour... classless glamour, available to anyone using American cosmetics, nylons and peroxide." Studios cultivated lookalikes: Jayne Mansfield, Sheree North (Fox); Mamie Van Doren (Universal); Kim Novak (Columbia); Diana Dors (Rank).

Constance Collier (acting teacher): "presence, luminosity, flickering intelligence... only a camera can freeze the poetry of it." Political: admired Lincoln (paternal), registered Democrat, supported JFK/RFK/William O. Douglas, donated $1k to Democrats via JFK birthday event. Civil rights priority: questioned RFK at dinner on department plans.

## Terms
- **Blonde bombshell**: 1940s–50s studio archetype (exemplified by Betty Grable) combining platinum hair, curvaceous figure, and comedic sex appeal; Monroe inherited and subverted it.
- **Method acting**: Stanislavski-derived technique (taught at Actors Studio by Lee Strasberg) emphasizing emotional memory and psychological realism; Monroe studied it from 1955 to deepen craft beyond "dumb blonde" typecasting.
- **Marilyn Monroe Productions (MMP)**: Independent company founded 1954 with Milton Greene; produced *The Prince and the Showgirl* (1957); challenged studio control over casting, direction, and profit participation.
