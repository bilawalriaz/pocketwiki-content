# Physical cosmology

## Overview
Physical cosmology is a branch of physics that models the universe using physical laws, addressing its origin, structure, evolution, and fate. Emerging in 1915 with Einstein’s general relativity, it became a predictive science after 1920s discoveries—Hubble’s galaxy observations and evidence for universal expansion—which established the Big Bang as the dominant model. Since the 1990s, precision data from the cosmic microwave background (CMB), supernovae, and galaxy surveys have refined a standard model (ΛCDM) requiring dark matter and dark energy, whose natures remain unknown.

## Timeline
- **1915** — Einstein publishes general relativity
- **1917** — Einstein introduces the cosmological constant for a static universe
- **1920s** — Friedmann derives expanding universe solutions; Slipher measures galactic redshifts
- **1927** — Lemaître proposes the “primeval atom” (Big Bang)
- **1929** — Hubble confirms galactic distances and redshift-distance relation (Hubble’s law)
- **1948** — Gamow, Alpher, and Herman develop Big Bang nucleosynthesis
- **1965** — Penzias and Wilson discover the cosmic microwave background
- **1990s** — COBE, WMAP, and supernova surveys establish the ΛCDM standard model
- **2016** — LIGO detects gravitational waves from merging black holes

## Body

### Foundations and Early Development
Modern cosmology began with Einstein’s 1916 general relativity, which describes gravity as spacetime curvature. Einstein initially favored a static universe and added a cosmological constant (Λ) in 1917 to counteract gravitational collapse, but this model was unstable. Alexander Friedmann soon showed that general relativity allows dynamic solutions—expanding or contracting universes described by the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) metric, which can be open, flat, or closed.

### Observational Breakthroughs and the Big Bang
In the 1920s, Vesto Slipher observed redshifts in spiral nebulae, interpreted as Doppler shifts indicating recession. Edwin Hubble confirmed these were external galaxies and established Hubble’s law: recession velocity is proportional to distance. Georges Lemaître linked this expansion to a primordial “explosion,” forming the basis of the Big Bang theory. Competing models included Fred Hoyle’s steady state theory, but the 1965 discovery of the CMB by Penzias and Wilson decisively favored the Big Bang.

### Standard Model and Modern Cosmology
The ΛCDM (Lambda Cold Dark Matter) model emerged from 1990s data, positing a universe dominated by dark energy (Λ) and cold dark matter. It requires ~68% dark energy, ~27% dark matter, and ~5% ordinary matter. Observations of the CMB, large-scale structure, and accelerating expansion (discovered in 1999 via supernovae) strongly support this framework, though the physical nature of dark components remains unknown.

### Energy Components and Cosmic Evolution
The universe evolves through radiation-dominated and matter-dominated eras. In the early universe, relativistic particles (radiation) dominated, followed by non-relativistic matter as expansion cooled the cosmos. Big Bang nucleosynthesis produced light elements (hydrogen, helium, lithium) within the first three minutes. Later, gravitational collapse formed stars, galaxies, and quasars. Today, dark energy drives accelerated expansion.

### Key Phenomena and Open Questions
Dark matter, inferred from galaxy rotation curves and structure formation, behaves as a cold, non-luminous fluid forming halos around galaxies. Its particle nature is unknown. Dark energy, responsible for acceleration, may be vacuum energy or a dynamical field (quintessence). The cosmological constant problem—predicted values exceeding observations by 120 orders of magnitude—remains unresolved. Baryogenesis, the matter-antimatter asymmetry, requires CP-violation beyond current measurements. Cosmic inflation explains flatness and homogeneity but lacks direct confirmation.

### Frontiers and Emerging Tools
Gravitational wave astronomy, initiated by LIGO in 2016, opens new probes of black hole mergers and the early universe. Large surveys (e.g., Sloan Digital Sky Survey) map structure formation. The CMB’s polarization and secondary anisotropies offer insights into inflation and reionization. Alternative theories, such as MOND (Modified Newtonian Dynamics) and string landscape models, challenge standard assumptions.

## Terms
- **Cosmological constant (Λ):** A constant energy density filling space homogeneously, introduced by Einstein to allow a static universe; now associated with dark energy.
- **Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) metric:** A solution to Einstein’s equations describing a homogeneous, isotropic, expanding or contracting universe.
- **Hubble’s law:** The empirical relation that galaxies recede from us at speeds proportional to their distance, indicating universal expansion.
- **ΛCDM model:** The standard cosmological model incorporating dark energy (Λ) and cold dark matter.
- **Dark matter:** Non-luminous matter inferred from gravitational effects, essential for structure formation and galaxy dynamics.
- **Dark energy:** A hypothetical form of energy causing the accelerated expansion of the universe.
- **Big Bang nucleosynthesis:** The process producing light elements (hydrogen, helium, lithium) in the first few minutes after the Big Bang.
- **Cosmic microwave background (CMB):** Thermal radiation left over from recombination, providing a snapshot of the early universe.
- **Baryogenesis:** The process generating an excess of matter over antimatter in the early universe.
- **Inflation:** A period of rapid exponential expansion in the early universe, resolving horizon and flatness problems.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Nature of dark matter and dark energy:** No direct detection of dark matter particles; dark energy’s identity and the cosmological constant discrepancy remain unsolved.
- **Baryogenesis mechanism:** Observed CP-violation in particle accelerators is insufficient to explain the matter-antimatter asymmetry.
- **Cosmic inflation vs. alternatives:** Inflation is widely accepted but unconfirmed; string theory and brane cosmology offer alternatives.
- **Fate of the universe:** Will expansion continue indefinitely (Big Freeze), accelerate into a Big Rip, or reverse?
- **Validity of general relativity on cosmic scales:** Some propose modified gravity (e.g., MOND) to explain observations without dark matter.
