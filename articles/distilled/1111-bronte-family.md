# Brontë family

## Overview
The Brontës were a 19th-century literary family from Yorkshire, England, whose three surviving sisters—Charlotte (1816–1855), Emily (1818–1848), and Anne (1820–1849)—became renowned novelists and poets. Publishing under male pseudonyms (Currer, Ellis, and Acton Bell), they produced works that combined gothic intensity with social realism, shaped by early trauma, isolation, and a rich inner imaginative life. Their novels, including *Jane Eyre*, *Wuthering Heights*, and *The Tenant of Wildfell Hall*, are now considered classics of English literature.

## Body

### Origins and Early Life
The Brontë family traced its roots to the Irish clan Ó Pronntaigh, anglicized as Prunty or Brunty. Patrick Brontë (born Brunty) changed the spelling to "Brontë" with a diaeresis over the final ⟨e⟩, possibly to obscure his humble origins or to honor Horatio Nelson, who held the title of Duke of Brontë. Born in Ireland, Patrick studied at St John’s College, Cambridge, and became a Church of England clergyman. He married Maria Branwell in 1812, and they settled at the Haworth parsonage. Maria died in 1821 of uterine cancer, leaving Patrick to raise six children with the help of his sister-in-law, Elizabeth Branwell ("Aunt Branwell").

### Education and Trauma
In 1824, the four eldest daughters were sent to the Clergy Daughters’ School at Cowan Bridge, a low-cost institution for clergy families. The harsh conditions—poor food, inadequate clothing, and neglect—led to Maria and Elizabeth contracting tuberculosis and dying in 1825. Charlotte and Emily were withdrawn and returned home. This trauma deeply influenced Charlotte’s portrayal of Lowood School in *Jane Eyre*. Later, Charlotte attended Miss Wooler’s school at Roe Head, where she formed lasting friendships and eventually worked as a teacher.

### Imaginary Worlds and Literary Beginnings
The Brontë children developed elaborate fictional universes—Glass Town, Angria, and Gondal—through collaborative storytelling and miniature handwritten books. Inspired by periodicals like *Blackwood’s Magazine* and *Leeds Intelligencer*, as well as figures like Lord Byron and artist John Martin, they created complex narratives featuring Byronic heroes and fantastical settings. These early works laid the foundation for their mature literary styles.

### Governesses and the Brussels Experiment
Financial necessity forced the sisters into roles as governesses and teachers. Charlotte worked for various families and briefly taught in Brussels with Emily, where they studied under Constantin Heger. Though intellectually stimulating, the experience was emotionally difficult for Charlotte, who developed unrequited feelings for Heger. The Brussels episode ended in 1843, but it sharpened Charlotte’s literary ambitions.

### Publication and Fame
In 1846, the sisters published a joint poetry collection under pseudonyms. The following year, their first novels appeared: *Jane Eyre* (Charlotte), *Wuthering Heights* (Emily), and *Agnes Grey* (Anne). *Jane Eyre* achieved immediate success, while *Wuthering Heights* received mixed reviews for its dark themes. Anne’s second novel, *The Tenant of Wildfell Hall* (1848), was controversial but commercially successful. In 1850, Charlotte publicly revealed the sisters’ identities in a preface to a revised edition of *Wuthering Heights* and *Agnes Grey*.

### Deaths and Legacy
Branwell died in 1848, followed by Emily and Anne in 1848 and 1849, all from tuberculosis. Charlotte, the sole survivor, continued writing until her death in 1855, likely from complications of pregnancy and illness. Elizabeth Gaskell’s 1857 biography cemented the myth of the Brontës as isolated geniuses. Their works remain central to English literature, studied for their psychological depth, gothic elements, and critique of Victorian society.

## Terms
- **Pseudonym**: A fictitious name used by authors, especially when social norms restrict publication under real names.
- **Byronic hero**: A literary archetype characterized by arrogance, passion, and moral ambiguity, inspired by Lord Byron.
- **Parsonage**: A residence provided by a church for its clergy; the Brontës lived in the Haworth parsonage.
- **Tuberculosis**: A bacterial infection that was a leading cause of death in 19th-century England; claimed the lives of several Brontës.
- **Cowan Bridge School**: A real-life boarding school that inspired Lowood School in *Jane Eyre*.
- **Glass Town / Angria / Gondal**: Fictional worlds created by the Brontë children during their youth.
- **Perpetual curate**: A clergyman who served a parish without being its rector or vicar; Patrick Brontë held this position.
- **Diaeresis**: A diacritical mark (¨) placed over a vowel to indicate separate pronunciation, as in "Brontë."

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Name origin**: Whether Patrick Brontë adopted the spelling "Brontë" to honor Nelson or distance himself from Irish roots remains debated.
- **Cowan Bridge’s impact**: While Charlotte blamed the school for her sisters’ deaths, some scholars argue conditions were typical for the era.
- **Emily’s deathbed scene**: Popular myth places Emily’s death on the dining room sofa, but no contemporary evidence supports this.
- **Unfinished manuscripts**: Some accounts suggest Emily left an unpublished novel that Charlotte destroyed, though no proof exists.
- **Anne’s reputation**: Charlotte’s decision to suppress *The Tenant of Wildfell Hall* after Anne’s death contributed to Anne’s lesser-known status compared to her sisters.
