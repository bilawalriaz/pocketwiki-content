# Prague Spring

## Overview
The Prague Spring was a period of political liberalization and mass protest in Czechoslovakia from January to August 1968. It represented a significant attempt to reform communism from within, granting greater freedoms before being forcibly suppressed by a Warsaw Pact invasion. The event became a defining moment of Cold War history, illustrating the limits of reform within the Soviet bloc and inspiring subsequent movements for change.

## Timeline
- **5 January 1968** — Alexander Dubček elected First Secretary of the Communist Party of Czechoslovakia (KSČ).
- **April 1968** — Dubček launches the "Action Programme" of liberalizations, termed "socialism with a human face."
- **29 July – 1 August 1968** — Bilateral talks between Czechoslovakia and the USSR at Čierna nad Tisou.
- **3 August 1968** — Bratislava Declaration signed by the "Warsaw Five" and Czechoslovakia, affirming Marxism-Leninism.
- **20–21 August 1968** — Warsaw Pact armies (USSR, Bulgaria, Poland, Hungary) invade Czechoslovakia.
- **21 August 1968** — Czechoslovak Presidium declares the invasion illegal; Dubček and other leaders are arrested.
- **16 January 1969** — Student Jan Palach self-immolates in Prague to protest the invasion and renewed censorship.
- **April 1969** — Gustáv Husák replaces Dubček as First Secretary, beginning the period of "normalization."
- **1989** — The Velvet Revolution peacefully ends communist rule in Czechoslovakia.
- **1991** — The last Soviet troops leave Czechoslovakia.

## Body

### Background and Rise of Reform
The Prague Spring emerged from a slow process of de-Stalinization and economic stagnation in Czechoslovakia. Cultural liberalization began symbolically with the 1963 Liblice Conference on Franz Kafka and intensified at the 1967 Writers' Congress, where intellectuals challenged party doctrine. As President Antonín Novotný lost support, Alexander Dubček replaced him as First Secretary in January 1968. Under Dubček, the KSČ launched an "Action Programme" in April, aiming for "socialism with a human face." This included increased freedoms of the press, speech, and movement, economic reforms mixing planned and market elements, and the federalization of the state into two republics. The uncensored journal *Literární listy* became a key platform for public debate.

### Soviet Reaction and Invasion
The reforms alarmed the Soviet leadership, which feared a weakening of the Eastern Bloc. After failed negotiations at Čierna nad Tisou and the Bratislava conference, the USSR and four other Warsaw Pact nations invaded on the night of 20–21 August 1968. Approximately 165,000 troops and 4,600 tanks entered, occupying the country within hours. The invasion was justified by the "Brezhnev Doctrine," which asserted the Soviet right to intervene in any socialist state where socialism was deemed threatened. Czechoslovak resistance was largely nonviolent, involving civil disobedience like removing street signs and giving invaders wrong directions. Dubček and other leaders were arrested and taken to Moscow, where they signed the Moscow Protocol under pressure, agreeing to curb reforms.

### Aftermath and Normalization
Following the invasion, a period of "normalization" began under Gustáv Husák. He reversed most Prague Spring reforms, purged liberals from the party and public life, reinstated strict censorship, and re-centralized the economy. The only formal change that survived was the 1969 federalization into the Czech and Slovak Socialist Republics. A wave of emigration followed the invasion, with an estimated 300,000 citizens eventually fleeing. The invasion and subsequent repression deeply scarred Czechoslovak society, leading to decades of dissent that culminated in the 1989 Velvet Revolution.

## Terms
- **Action Programme**: The April 1968 reform plan by Dubček's government, outlining political and economic liberalizations.
- **Brezzhnev Doctrine**: The Soviet policy asserting its right to intervene militarily in any Warsaw Pact country where socialism was threatened.
- **Normalization**: The post-invasion period (1969–1989) of political repression and reversal of reforms under Gustáv Husák.
- **Velvet Revolution**: The non-violent 1989 revolution that ended communist rule in Czechoslovakia.
- **Federalization**: The 1969 restructuring of Czechoslovakia into two equal republics: the Czech Socialist Republic and the Slovak Socialist Republic.
- **Warsaw Pact**: The military alliance of the Soviet Union and its Eastern European satellite states.
- **Self-immolation**: The act of setting oneself on fire as a form of protest, most famously by student Jan Palach.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent of internal Czechoslovak complicity in the invasion remains debated. While the official KSČ stance was that no leadership member invited intervention, later evidence suggests conservative party members sent a request to the Soviets.
- The long-term legacy of the Prague Spring is contested. It is remembered both as a symbol of crushed hope and as a vital cultural and intellectual renewal that prefigured the end of communism.
- The memory of the event is conflicted in modern Czech and Slovak societies, sometimes viewed as a painful defeat and at other times as a necessary precursor to freedom.
