# Maximilien Robespierre

## Overview
Maximilien Robespierre was a French lawyer and statesman who became one of the most influential and divisive figures of the French Revolution. As a leading member of the radical Jacobin faction and the Committee of Public Safety, he was a central architect of the Reign of Terror, a period of revolutionary repression. His legacy remains deeply controversial, debated as either that of a principled champion of the people or a fanatical dictator.

## Timeline
- **6 May 1758** — Born in Arras, France.
- **1789** — Elected deputy to the Estates-General; advocates for universal male suffrage.
- **1791** — Proposes the Self-Denying Ordinance; opposes war with Austria.
- **10 Aug 1792** — Insurrection at the Tuileries; monarchy suspended.
- **21 Jan 1793** — Louis XVI is executed.
- **27 Jul 1793** — Joins the Committee of Public Safety.
- **5 Feb 1794** — Delivers "Report on the Principles of Political Morality," justifying the Terror.
- **10 Jun 1794** — Law of 22 Prairial accelerates the Great Terror.
- **27 Jul 1794** — Arrested by the Convention (9 Thermidor).
- **28 Jul 1794** — Executed without trial.

## Body

### Early Life and Political Rise
Born in 1758, Robespierre was educated at the prestigious Collège Louis-le-Grand, where he developed a profound admiration for the Roman Republic and the philosophy of Jean-Jacques Rousseau. After becoming a lawyer, he was elected to the Estates-General in 1789. He quickly emerged as a radical voice, consistently advocating for universal male suffrage, the abolition of the death penalty, and the rights of marginalized groups, positions that often placed him in the minority within the early revolutionary assemblies.

### Opposition to War and Rise to Power
From 1791 to 1792, Robespierre vehemently opposed the push for war with Austria, arguing it would lead to military dictatorship and counter-revolution. His stance alienated him from the moderate Girondin faction. Following the insurrection of 10 August 1792, which overthrew the monarchy, Robespierre was elected to the new National Convention. There, as a leader of the radical Montagnard faction, he helped orchestrate the purge of the Girondins in June 1793, securing Jacobin control of the government.

### The Committee of Public Safety and the Terror
Robespierre joined the Committee of Public Safety in July 1793, becoming its most prominent member. Facing civil war and foreign invasion, the Committee established a "Revolutionary Government." Robespierre defended the resulting Reign of Terror as a necessary tool of virtue, arguing that "terror is nothing other than justice, prompt, severe, inflexible." He justified the execution of both royalist enemies and revolutionary rivals, including the Hébertists and Dantonists, to preserve the Republic. The Law of 22 Prairial (June 1794) further centralized and accelerated the Terror, leading to a spike in executions.

### Downfall and Execution
Robespierre's power waned in mid-1794 due to his deteriorating health, increasing paranoia, and political missteps. His promotion of the Cult of the Supreme Being alienated radicals, while his attacks on fellow Committee members created a coalition of enemies. On 9 Thermidor (27 July 1794), he was shouted down in the Convention and arrested. After a failed rescue attempt by the Paris Commune, he was executed the next day along with his allies, ending the most radical phase of the Revolution.

### Abolition of Slavery
Robespierre's stance on slavery was complex. He consistently denounced the slave trade as a crime against humanity and supported the 1791 decree granting citizenship to free people of color. While he did not initially advocate for the immediate abolition of slavery, the radical 1793 constitution he championed condemned it. The Convention ultimately abolished slavery in all colonies in February 1794, a decree Robespierre endorsed, though its implementation was limited.

## Terms
*   **Jacobin Club:** A radical political club that became the dominant force in the later stages of the French Revolution.
*   **Committee of Public Safety:** The executive committee of the Convention that effectively ruled France during the Reign of Terror.
*   **Reign of Terror:** A period of revolutionary repression (1793-1794) characterized by mass executions of perceived enemies.
*   **Montagnards ("The Mountain"):** The radical, anti-monarchist faction in the National Convention, seated on the high benches.
*   **Girondins:** A more moderate republican faction in the Convention, often in conflict with the Montagnards.
*   **Sans-culottes:** The militant working-class radicals of Paris, key supporters of the Montagnards.
*   **Law of 22 Prairial:** A law that streamlined the Revolutionary Tribunal, eliminating defendants' rights and accelerating executions.
*   **Cult of the Supreme Being:** A deistic state religion promoted by Robespierre to replace both Catholicism and atheism.

## Debates and Open Questions
Historians remain deeply divided on Robespierre's legacy. One school of thought, influenced by historians like Albert Mathiez, views him as a principled defender of the poor and the Republic, whose actions were a necessary response to existential threats. Another, represented by revisionists like François Furet, argues that the Terror was an inherent outcome of the Revolution's radical ideology, with Robespierre as its key enforcer. A central debate concerns his personal responsibility: was he the dictatorial mastermind of the Terror, or was he one member of a collective Committee government, later scapegoated by his Thermidorian successors? His role in the abolition of slavery and his vision for a "Republic of Virtue" continue to be subjects of scholarly reassessment.
