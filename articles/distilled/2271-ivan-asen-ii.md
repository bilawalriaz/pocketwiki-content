# Ivan Asen II

## Overview
Ivan Asen II (r. 1218–1241) was the most powerful ruler of the Second Bulgarian Empire, transforming it into the dominant Balkan state through military victory and diplomatic skill. His reign marked the empire's territorial and cultural zenith, but his death coincided with the Mongol invasion, which triggered a period of decline.

## Timeline
- **1196** — Ivan Asen I, father of Ivan Asen II, is killed.
- **1207** — Kaloyan is murdered; Boril usurps the throne.
- **c. 1217** — Ivan Asen returns from exile in Rus' and defeats Boril.
- **1218** — Ivan Asen captures Tarnovo, blinding Boril, and becomes emperor.
- **1221** — Marries Maria of Hungary, gaining Belgrade and Braničevo.
- **1228** — Fails to secure regency of the Latin Empire after Baldwin II's accession.
- **1230** — Defeats Theodore Komnenos Doukas at the Battle of Klokotnitsa.
- **1230s** — Conquers Macedonia, Thessaly, and Thrace; establishes mint at Ohrid.
- **1235** — Alliance with Nicaea; Bulgarian Church granted autocephaly (patriarchal rank).
- **1237** — Breaks alliance with Nicaea; wife, son, and patriarch die.
- **May/June 1241** — Ivan Asen II dies.

## Body

### Rise to Power
Ivan Asen II was the son of Ivan Asen I, a founder of the Second Bulgarian Empire. After his father's murder in 1196 and his uncle Kaloyan's in 1207, the teenage Ivan Asen was passed over for the throne by Boril. He fled to the Rus' principalities, where he gathered supporters and funds from Boril's opponents. Returning to Bulgaria, he defeated Boril, captured the capital Tarnovo after a long siege, and blinded his rival in 1218.

### Consolidation and Expansion
His early reign focused on securing alliances with neighboring Catholic powers, including Hungary and the Latin Empire. His major military triumph came in 1230 at the Battle of Klokotnitsa, where he crushed Theodore Komnenos Doukas of Thessalonica. This victory allowed Bulgaria to annex vast territories in Macedonia, Thrace, and Thessaly, making it the dominant Balkan power. Control of the Via Egnatia trade route funded an ambitious building program in Tarnovo and the minting of gold coins.

### Diplomacy and the Church
Initially aligned with the Papacy, Ivan Asen shifted policy after the Latin Empire elected John of Brienne regent in 1229. He reopened negotiations with the Orthodox Ecumenical Patriarch. In 1235, he allied with Emperor John III Vatatzes of Nicaea against the Latin Empire. At a joint conference, the Ecumenical Patriarch recognized the head of the Bulgarian Church as patriarch, confirming its autocephaly (independence). However, Ivan Asen soon broke with Nicaea, fearing Vatatzes would be the primary beneficiary of the Latin Empire's fall.

### Final Years and Legacy
The later years were marked by Mongol invasions, which forced Cuman tribes to flee into Bulgaria. Ivan Asen's personal life was turbulent; after the deaths of his wife and son in 1237, he married Irene Komnene Doukaina, a captive from his 1230 victory. He died in 1241 as the Mongols invaded, leaving a weakened state to his young successor. He is remembered as the last truly powerful Bulgarian medieval ruler, who expanded the empire to its greatest extent.

## Terms
- **Tsar**: The title for Bulgarian emperors, equivalent to "Caesar" or "Emperor."
- **Autocephaly**: The status of a church that is self-governing and independent of higher ecclesiastical authority.
- **Via Egnatia**: A major Roman road and trade route across the Balkans, crucial for controlling regional commerce.
- **Latin Empire**: The Crusader state established in Constantinople after the Fourth Crusade (1204), ruling over former Byzantine territories.
- **Cumans**: Semi-nomadic Turkic people from the Pontic steppes who were frequent allies and mercenaries in the Balkans.
- **Boyar**: A member of the Bulgarian nobility.
- **Primate**: The head of a national or independent church, a title granted to the Bulgarian church leader by the Pope before full patriarchal rank was achieved.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Siege of Tarnovo**: The 13th-century historian George Akropolites claimed the siege lasted seven years, but most modern historians believe he confused months for years. One scholar, Genoveva Cankova-Petkova, accepts the seven-year chronology.
- **Ivan Asen's First Wife**: There is scholarly debate about whether his first wife, Anna (later the nun Anisia), was a legitimate spouse or a concubine, and whether she was the widow of his uncle Kaloyan.
- **Children by Irene Komnene Doukaina**: Primary sources give conflicting lists of his children with his third wife, leading to uncertainty about their names and identities.
