# Nicolaus Otto

## Overview
Nicolaus Otto was a German engineer whose work on the internal combustion engine was foundational to modern automotive technology. He successfully developed the first commercially practical four-stroke engine, a design now known as the Otto cycle, which became the standard for gasoline engines.

## Timeline
- **10 June 1832** — Born in Holzhausen an der Haide, Germany.
- **1860** — Learns of the Lenoir gas engine; builds a copy with his brother.
- **1861** — Experiments with a four-stroke compressed charge engine, which fails.
- **31 March 1864** — Partners with Eugen Langen to form NA Otto & Cie, the first internal combustion engine company.
- **1867** — The Otto & Langen atmospheric engine wins a gold medal at the Paris World Exhibition.
- **1876** — The "Otto Silent Engine," the first commercially successful four-stroke compressed charge engine, is introduced.
- **1882** — Receives an Honorary Doctorate from the University of Würzburg.
- **26 January 1891** — Dies in Cologne, Germany.

## Body

### Early Life and First Engine
Born in 1832, Otto worked as a salesman after leaving school. In 1860, he and his brother built a copy of Jean Joseph Etienne Lenoir's gas engine. Their subsequent patent application for a liquid-fueled version was rejected. In 1861, Otto attempted a four-stroke compressed charge engine, but it broke down almost immediately.

### Partnership and the Atmospheric Engine
In 1864, Otto partnered with investor Eugen Langen to form the world's first company dedicated to internal combustion engines. Their initial product was a successful "atmospheric" engine, which used a vacuum created by an explosion to draw in atmospheric pressure for power. While commercially successful, this design was a technical dead end due to its low power and large size.

### The Four-Stroke "Otto Cycle" Engine
Otto returned to the four-stroke concept. With help from engineers Franz Rings and Herman Schumm, he developed the "Otto Silent Engine," introduced in 1876. This was the first commercially successful engine to use in-cylinder compression of a fuel-air mixture. The four-stroke cycle it employed—intake, compression, power, and exhaust—became the standard design for most modern gasoline engines and is named the Otto cycle.

### Legacy and Patents
Over 50,000 Otto engines were produced in the 17 years following the 1876 model's introduction. Otto held many patents, but a prior 1862 patent by French engineer Beau de Rochas for the four-stroke concept was later discovered, which affected Otto's patent rights. In 1936, the Association of German Engineers (VDI) established DIN standard 1940, defining the "Otto Engine" as any internal combustion engine using a compressed fuel-air mixture ignited by a timed spark.

## Terms
*   **Internal Combustion Engine:** An engine in which the combustion of a fuel occurs with an oxidizer (usually air) in a combustion chamber.
*   **Four-Stroke Cycle:** The operating cycle of an engine comprising four piston strokes: intake, compression, power, and exhaust.
*   **Otto Cycle:** The thermodynamic cycle describing the functioning of a typical spark-ignition piston engine, based on the four-stroke design.
*   **Atmospheric Engine:** An early type of internal combustion engine where the power stroke is produced by atmospheric pressure pushing a piston into a vacuum created by the combustion.
*   **Compressed Charge:** The process of compressing the fuel-air mixture within the engine cylinder before ignition to increase efficiency and power.
*   **DIN Standard 1940:** A German standard that formally defines the "Otto Engine" type.

## Debates and Open Questions
The article notes that the concept of the four-stroke cycle was patented by Beau de Rochas in 1862, before Otto's successful 1876 engine. Neither Otto nor his contemporary Gottlieb Daimler were aware of this prior patent at the time. Rochas never built an engine, and it is debated whether he could have done so.
