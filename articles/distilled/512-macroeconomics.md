# Macroeconomics

## Overview
Macroeconomics is the branch of economics that studies the performance, structure, and decision-making of an economy as a whole—regional, national, or global. It focuses on aggregate variables such as gross domestic product (GDP), unemployment, inflation, and long-run economic growth. The field formally emerged in 1936 with John Maynard Keynes’s *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*, which challenged the prevailing view that markets always clear and introduced the central role of aggregate demand. Since then, macroeconomics has evolved through competing schools of thought—Keynesian, monetarist, new classical, and new Keynesian—culminating in the modern "new neoclassical synthesis" embodied in dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models used by central banks. The discipline is organized by time horizon: short-run business cycle fluctuations, medium-run structural determinants of unemployment and output, and long-run growth driven by technology and capital accumulation.

## Timeline
- **16th century** — Martín de Azpilcueta describes the quantity theory of money.
- **1898** — Knut Wicksell publishes *Interest and Prices*, pioneering macroeconomic monetary theory.
- **1933** — Ragnar Frisch introduces the terms "macrodynamics" and "macroanalysis."
- **1936** — John Maynard Keynes publishes *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*, founding modern macroeconomics; John Hicks invents the IS–LM model.
- **1940s** — Harrod-Domar model attempts a Keynesian long-run growth framework.
- **1950s** — Solow–Swan model establishes the neoclassical growth framework; neoclassical synthesis (Keynesian macro + neoclassical micro) becomes dominant.
- **1960s–1970s** — Milton Friedman updates the quantity theory (monetarism); Friedman and Edmund Phelps introduce the augmented Phillips curve, rejecting a long-run inflation-unemployment tradeoff.
- **1970s** — Oil shocks cause stagflation, vindicating the augmented Phillips curve; Robert Lucas introduces rational expectations and the Lucas critique.
- **1980s** — Real business cycle (RBC) models (Kydland, Prescott) explain fluctuations via technology shocks; endogenous growth theory (Romer, others) endogenizes technological progress.
- **1990s** — New Keynesian models incorporate sticky prices/wages and rational expectations; fusion into DSGE models ("new neoclassical synthesis") becomes standard for central banks.
- **2008** — Global financial crisis prompts reassessment: focus on financial frictions, heterogeneous agent models (HANK), empirical credibility revolution, and behavioral macroeconomics.
- **1970s–present** — Climate change integrated into growth models via integrated assessment models (IAMs), pioneered by William Nordhaus; Nicholas Stern’s 2006 *Stern Review* models climate damages to GDP.

## Body

### Core Variables and Time Horizons
Macroeconomics centers on three variables: **output** (total goods/services produced, measured by GDP), **unemployment** (percentage of the labor force actively seeking work), and **inflation** (general price increase). Analysis is structured by time horizon. The **short run** (a few years) focuses on business cycles driven by aggregate demand shifts; stabilization policies (monetary/fiscal) are active here. The **medium run** (a decade) sees output determined by supply factors—capital stock, technology, labor force—with unemployment reverting to a "natural rate" shaped by labor market frictions and institutions. The **long run** (decades+) emphasizes growth drivers: physical/human capital accumulation, technological innovation, and demographics. Policy levers differ across horizons: demand management in the short run, structural reforms in the medium run, and education/R&D incentives in the long run.

### Output, Unemployment, and Inflation Mechanics
**Output and income** are equivalent: everything produced generates equal income. GDP measures domestic output; adding net factor income from abroad yields gross national income (GNI). Growth stems from technology, capital, and human capital, but business cycles cause recessions. **Unemployment** splits into cyclical (demand-driven, per Okun’s law: a 3% output rise cuts unemployment ~1%) and structural/natural components. Structural unemployment persists even at cyclical neutrality due to search frictions (heterogeneous workers/firms, imperfect information), efficiency wages (firms pay above market-clearing to boost productivity), union market power, or minimum wages (though monopsony power can reverse this effect). **Inflation** rises when the economy overheats (tight labor markets → wage increases → price increases via the Phillips curve) or from adverse supply shocks (e.g., 1970s oil crises, 2021–2023 energy crisis). The monetarist quantity theory links money supply growth to inflation long-run, but central banks target interest rates because short-run money-demand instability makes quantity targeting unreliable. Most now pursue flexible inflation targeting (~2%), balancing price stability with output/employment stabilization.

### Measuring the Economy
**GDP via expenditure approach** sums consumption (C), government spending (G), investment (I), and net exports (X–M). Transfers (welfare, social security) are excluded as they are not final goods/services. The **GDP deflator** = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100 strips out inflation; a value >100 indicates inflation since the base year. **Money supply** is measured as M1 (cash, checking deposits—highly liquid) and M2 (M1 plus savings deposits, time deposits, money market funds—less liquid). The **money multiplier** (1 / reserve requirement) shows how banking expands spendable deposits: a 20% reserve ratio yields a multiplier of 5, turning $5 of reserves into $25 of money supply. **Open economy** macroeconomics adds trade in goods, financial assets, and factor mobility; key issues include exchange rate regimes (fixed vs. floating, currency unions) and net foreign asset accumulation, analyzed via optimum currency area theory.

### Historical Evolution of Thought
**Pre-Keynes**: Two traditions dominated—business cycle theory (William Stanley Jevons) and monetary theory (quantity theory from Azpilcueta, developed by Marshall, Wicksell, Fisher). **Keynesian Revolution (1936)**: Keynes argued markets need not clear; aggregate demand ("effective demand") determines output. He introduced liquidity preference (money demand), the multiplier (small demand drops amplify), and active fiscal policy. The **neoclassical synthesis** (1950s, Samuelson, Modigliani, Tobin, Solow) merged Keynesian macro with neoclassical micro. **Austrian rival** (Hayek, Mises) emphasized capital structure and business cycles driven by credit distortion, but lost dominance post-WWII. **Monetarism** (Friedman, 1960s): Updated quantity theory; money supply explains the Great Depression; monetary > fiscal policy; no long-run Phillips curve tradeoff (vindicated by 1970s stagflation). Fell from favor when money-demand instability made money-supply targeting impractical. **New Classical** (Lucas, 1970s): Introduced **rational expectations**—agents use all information, not just past trends—limiting policy effectiveness. Lucas critique: empirical models fail when policy regimes change; models need microfoundations. **Real Business Cycle** (Kydland, Prescott): Fluctuations stem from technology shocks, not demand; criticized for implausible "technological regress" and ignoring monetary non-neutrality, but pioneered microfounded general equilibrium modeling. **New Keynesian** (Fischer, Taylor, Blanchard, Yellen, Mankiw, Woodford, Bernanke): Adopted rational expectations but added **nominal rigidities** (sticky prices/wages from imperfect competition, contracts, efficiency wages, search frictions, credit market imperfections) restoring monetary/fiscal potency. **New Neoclassical Synthesis (1990s)**: DSGE models fuse New Keynesian rigidities, rational expectations, and RBC methodology—now central bank workhorses. **Post-2008**: Crisis exposed neglected financial frictions. New directions: macro-financial linkages (leverage, liquidity, macroprudential policy), heterogeneous agent models (HANK), empirical "credibility revolution" (causal identification), and behavioral macroeconomics.

### Growth Theory
**Harrod-Domar (1940s)**: Demand-driven growth. **Solow–Swan (1950s)**: Output = f(capital, labor) with constant returns. Growth per capita requires technological progress (total factor productivity); saving raises the level of output temporarily but not the long-run growth rate (diminishing returns to capital). **Ramsey–Cass–Koopmans**: Endogenizes saving via intertemporal utility optimization. **Endogenous growth (1980s–90s, Romer, etc.)**: Growth driven by increasing returns, learning-by-doing, or explicit R&D by profit-maximizing firms—technology is not exogenous. **Climate change integration**: From Jevons’s *Coal Question* (1865) to 1970s resource scarcity models (Stiglitz, Solow) to Nordhaus’s integrated assessment models (IAMs) and Stern’s 2006 *Stern Review*. IAMs couple economic growth with climate dynamics, modeling "source functions" (resource provision) and "sink functions" (waste absorption); environmental accounting remains debated.

### Macroeconomic Policy
**Stabilization policy** (short-run) uses **monetary** and **fiscal** tools to dampen cycles. **Monetary policy**: Central banks adjust short-term rates (via administered rates or open market operations). Transmission: rates → investment, consumption, asset prices, exchange rates → aggregate demand → inflation. **Inflation targeting** (explicit or flexible, ~2%) dominates advanced economies; emerging economies often fix exchange rates. **Liquidity trap** (near-zero rates) necessitates unconventional policy: quantitative easing (buying broad assets) or yield curve control (Operation Twist). **Fiscal policy**: Government spending/taxation influences demand. Multiplier effect: $1 spending → >$1 output via induced consumption. Limits: **crowding out** (government borrowing raises rates, displacing private investment; or resource competition). **Automatic stabilizers** (unemployment benefits, progressive taxes) act without legislative lag. **Policy choice consensus**: Monetary preferred for moderate fluctuations (independent central banks, shorter lags). Exceptions: major shocks (fiscal supplement), liquidity traps (fiscal more potent), fixed exchange rate regimes (monetary tied to peg, fiscal only domestic tool).

### Macroeconomic Models
Models range from simple theoretical frameworks to large quantitative systems. **Key theoretical models**: IS–LM (Hicks, 1936)—goods market (IS: investment = saving) and money market (LM: money supply = liquidity preference) equilibrium at a given price level; downward-sloping IS, upward-sloping LM. Modern variants replace LM with a central bank reaction function (interest rate rule). **AD–AS**: Aggregate demand (downward: real balance, interest rate, net export effects) and aggregate supply (horizontal at low output, vertical at potential). Modern version plots inflation vs. output: AD slopes down because central bank raises rates when inflation rises; AS slopes up via Phillips curve (activity → wages → inflation). **Growth models**: Solow–Swan, Ramsey, overlapping generations (Diamond). **Quantitative models**: Large-scale macroeconometric (early), RBC (microfounded fluctuations), CGE (structural/trade/tax), DSGE (business cycles, central banks), IAMs (climate-economy, e.g., DICE).

### Heterodox and Austrian Approaches
**Heterodox macroeconomics** (ecological, modern monetary theory, Marxian) rejects mainstream neoclassical/Keynesian foundations; e.g., some view price *flexibility* (not stickiness) as the source of instability. **Austrian school** (Mises, Hayek) uses **praxeology**—deductive reasoning from purposeful action—rejecting aggregate constructs (GDP, price level) as masking heterogeneous capital structure and subjective value. Core contribution: **Austrian business cycle theory**—credit expansion distorts interest rates, misallocates capital across production stages, causing boom-bust cycles.

## Terms
- ****Gross Domestic Product (GDP)**** — Total market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given period; measured via expenditure (C+G+I+X–M), income, or output approaches.
- ****Natural rate of unemployment**** — The structural unemployment rate prevailing in medium-run equilibrium (cyclical unemployment = 0), determined by search frictions, wage-setting institutions, and efficiency wages.
- ****Okun’s law**** — Empirical relationship: a ~3% increase in output relative to potential reduces the unemployment rate by ~1 percentage point.
- ****Phillips curve**** — Inverse relationship between unemployment and wage/price inflation; "augmented" version (Friedman/Phelps) adds expected inflation, eliminating a stable long-run tradeoff.
- ****Rational expectations**** — Agents form forecasts using all available information, including current policy rules, not just past trends (Lucas, 1970s).
- ****Lucas critique**** — Empirical models without microfoundations fail when policy regimes change because agents’ decision rules shift.
- ****DSGE models**** — Dynamic stochastic general equilibrium models: microfounded, rational expectations, nominal rigidities, stochastic shocks; standard for central bank analysis.
- ****Money multiplier**** — 1 / reserve requirement ratio; shows maximum potential expansion of money supply from a given reserve base via bank lending.
- ****Liquidity trap**** — Situation where nominal interest rates are near zero, rendering conventional monetary policy (rate cuts) ineffective.
- ****Crowding out**** — Government borrowing/spending displaces private investment (via higher interest rates) or uses resources that would otherwise be privately employed.

## Debates and open questions
- **Financial frictions and macro-financial linkages**: How to model leverage, liquidity, and complexity in the financial sector; optimal macroprudential tools; interaction with public debt sustainability (post-2008 focus).
- **Heterogeneity and distribution**: Representative-agent DSGE models ignore inequality; heterogeneous agent New Keynesian (HANK) models attempt to integrate distributional dynamics and marginal propensity to consume differences.
- **Empirical credibility**: Moving from correlation to causal identification in macroeconometrics (the "credibility revolution") to improve policy relevance.
- **Behavioral macroeconomics**: Integrating psychological biases (non-rational expectations, heuristics) into aggregate models.
- **Climate-economy integration**: Calibrating damage functions, discount rates, and tipping points in IAMs; designing carbon pricing and green transition policies within macro frameworks.
- **Monetary policy framework**: Whether inflation targeting remains optimal post-pandemic (supply shocks, fiscal dominance); role of average inflation targeting or nominal GDP targeting.
- **Fiscal-monetary interaction**: Limits of monetary policy at the effective lower bound; coordination with fiscal policy; debt sustainability under low rates.
- **Heterodox validity**: Whether alternative paradigms (MMT, ecological, Marxian) offer superior explanations for instability, inequality, or ecological constraints.
