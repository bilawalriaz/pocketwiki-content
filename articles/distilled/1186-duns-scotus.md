# Duns Scotus

## Overview
John Duns Scotus (c. 1265/66 – 8 November 1308) was a Scottish Franciscan friar, priest, and university professor ranked among the most significant philosopher-theologians of the late medieval period alongside Thomas Aquinas and William of Ockham. Known as the *Doctor Subtilis* ("the Subtle Doctor") for his nuanced distinctions, he founded the intellectual tradition called Scotism. His principal contributions include the **univocity of being** (the claim that "being" has exactly the same meaning when applied to God and creatures, contra Aquinas’s analogy), the **formal distinction** (an intermediate distinction between entities inseparable in reality but non-identical in definition), and **haecceity** ("thisness," the principle of individuation). He developed a metaphysical argument for God’s existence as First Efficient Cause and defended the **Immaculate Conception** of Mary—arguing she was preserved from original sin *in advance* by Christ’s merits—a view later defined as dogma in 1854. His voluntarism emphasized divine will and human freedom over intellectual necessity.

## Timeline
- **c. 1265/66** — Born near Duns, Berwickshire, Scotland, into a leading family.
- **17 Mar 1291** — Ordained priest at St Andrew’s, Northampton (minimum canonical age 25).
- **c. 1295–1300** — Lectures on Aristotle’s *Organon* and *Metaphysics* at Oxford; writes early *Lectura* on the *Sentences*.
- **1300–01** — Participates in disputation under Philip of Bridlington at Oxford.
- **Late 1302** — Begins lecturing on Peter Lombard’s *Sentences* at University of Paris.
- **1303** — Expelled from Paris for siding with Pope Boniface VIII against King Philip IV over taxation of church property.
- **1304** — Returns to Paris; continues lecturing; revises Oxford lectures into the *Ordinatio* (Opus oxoniense).
- **Oct 1307** — Suddenly dispatched by Franciscan Minister General to the *studium* at Cologne.
- **8 Nov 1308** — Dies unexpectedly in Cologne; buried in the Church of the Friars Minor.
- **1991** — Declared Venerable by Pope John Paul II.
- **20 Mar 1993** — Beatified by Pope John Paul II.

## Body

### Life and Career
Scotus entered the Order of Friars Minor at Dumfries under his uncle, Elias Duns. He was likely educated at the Franciscan *studium generale* at Oxford (established after the University of Paris dispersal of 1229–30). His career centered on the standard scholastic curriculum: lecturing on the *Sentences* of Peter Lombard, first at Oxford (c. 1300) then at Paris (1302). His Parisian tenure was interrupted by the conflict between Pope Boniface VIII and King Philip IV; Scotus supported the pope and was expelled in 1303. He returned in 1304 and remained until 1307, when the Franciscan Minister General abruptly transferred him to Cologne. He died there months later. A 15th-century account by William Vorilong describes his departure as sudden, leaving belongings behind. The Merton College colophon also places him at Cambridge, though details are sparse.

### Works and Textual Transmission
Scotus’s magnum opus is his commentary on the *Sentences*, existing in three main versions: the *Lectura* (early Oxford notes), the *Ordinatio* (revised Oxford lectures, also *Opus oxoniense*), and the *Reportatio parisiensis* (student transcriptions of Paris lectures, some *examinata* or checked by Scotus). By his time, *Sentences* commentaries were vehicles for original philosophical theology rather than literal exegesis. He also wrote early logical works (*Parva logicalia*: questions on Porphyry and Aristotle’s *Categories*, *Peri hermeneias*, *De sophisticis elenchis*), a commentary on Aristotle’s *Metaphysics* (partly lost, partly rediscovered as the *Expositio*), 46 *Collationes* (short disputations, 1300–05), *De primo principio* (natural theology), and *Quaestiones Quodlibetales* (Advent 1306 or Lent 1307). Several works once attributed to him—*De modis significandi*, *Questions on the Prior Analytics*—are now assigned to Thomas of Erfurt and others. His early death left many works unedited; disciples’ editorial activity caused misattributions and textual confusion.

### Metaphysics

**Realism and Universals**  
Scotus is a realist: universals are real, existing both in particular things and as concepts in the mind, not in a Platonic separate realm. He argues against a proto-nominalist position (later associated with Ockham) that things share a *common nature* (e.g., humanity in Socrates, Plato, Plutarch) which is really the same in each.

**Univocity of Being**  
Following Aristotle, Scotus holds that metaphysics studies "being qua being" (*ens inquantum ens*). "Being in general" (*ens in communi*) is a **univocal** concept—identical in meaning—whether applied to God or creatures. This denies Aquinas’s **real distinction** between essence and existence in finite beings. For Scotus, we cannot conceive *what* a thing is (*quid est*) without conceiving *that* it is (*si est*); essence and existence are **formally distinct** (inseparable in reality, non-identical in definition) but not really distinct.

**Individuation and Hylomorphism**  
Scotus advances three strong theses: (1) **Prime matter** exists without any form, underlying all change (contra Aquinas). (2) Not all created substances are form-matter composites; purely spiritual substances exist. (3) One substance can have **multiple substantial forms**: humans have at least the rational soul and the *forma corporeitas* (form of the body). The principle of individuation is **haecceity** ("thisness," *haecceitas*), a positive entity distinct from the common nature (*natura communis*). Only individuals exist *per se*; knowing them requires **intuitive cognition** (grasping present existence/non-existence), not merely abstract cognition. The separated soul retains intuitive knowledge of spiritual realities.

**Formal Distinction**  
The **formal distinction** (*distinctio formalis a parte rei*) mediates between a mere conceptual distinction and a real (separability) distinction. It holds between entities inseparable in reality but with non-identical definitions. Examples: the divine attributes and the divine essence; the persons of the Trinity and the essence; haecceity and the common nature; the soul’s powers and its essence.

### Theology

**Voluntarism and Divine Knowledge**  
Scotus is a theological voluntarist: God’s will and human freedom are primary. Against Aquinas’s analogical predication, Scotus argues certain predicates apply **univocally** to God and creatures. In *Lectura* I 39 (1297–99), he defends contingency against necessitarianism. God has **one simple volition** (*unica volitione*) *ad intra*, but this single act can relate to opposite states of affairs *ad extra* at different times (diachronic) or simultaneously (synchronic). God’s certain knowledge is compatible with contingency because divine knowledge is not causally determined by the objects known; Scotus reconciles this with Aristotle’s *De Interpretatione* IX without rejecting the text.

**Metaphysical Argument for God’s Existence**  
Scotus prefers a metaphysical proof (from efficient causality) over Aquinas’s physical argument from motion. The *Ordinatio* I.2 proof: (1) Something can be produced. (2) Not by nothing; not by itself. (3) Therefore by another (A). (4) If A is first, done; if not, iterate. (5) An infinite *essentially ordered* series is impossible. (6) Therefore a first efficient cause exists. Scotus addresses two objections: (a) He does not beg the question—philosophers admit no infinite regress in essentially ordered series. (b) The premise "something is produced" is contingent; he repairs this by a modal move: "It is possible something is produced" is necessary. From the possibility of production, he infers the possibility of a first cause, and from its possibility, its actual existence (since a first cause cannot *not* exist if possible). The *De Primo Principio* version adds that the First Being is intellectual, volitional, infinite, and identical with its essence.

**Illuminationism**  
Scotus rejects Henry of Ghent’s divine illumination theory. In *Ordinatio* I.3.1.4, he argues that if human intellect were as fallible as Henry claimed, divine illumination could not guarantee "certain and pure knowledge" even in principle.

**Immaculate Conception**  
Scotus’s most influential theological stance: Mary was conceived without original sin. The objection: Christ’s death removes sin; how could Mary be preserved *before* it? Using Anselm’s *potuit, decuit, ergo fecit* (God could, it was fitting, therefore He did), Scotus argues Mary needed redemption like all humans, but Christ’s merits were applied *preventively* at her conception. God could preserve her (1) never in sin, (2) in sin only instantaneously, (3) in sin briefly then purged; the most excellent option (1) befits Mary. This argument underlies Pope Pius IX’s 1854 dogmatic definition. Scotus also defended the universal primacy of Christ (basis for the 1925 feast of Christ the King) and opposed forced conversion of Jews.

## Terms
- ****Univocity of being**** — The doctrine that "being" (*ens*) has exactly the same meaning when predicated of God and creatures, making it the first object of the intellect.
- ****Formal distinction**** — A distinction *a parte rei* (in the thing) between entities inseparable in reality but non-identical in definition; intermediate between real and conceptual distinctions.
- ****Haecceity** (*haecceitas*)** — The positive, individualizing principle ("thisness") that makes an entity this particular individual, distinct from its common nature.
- ****Common nature** (*natura communis*)** — The nature (e.g., humanity) really identical in each individual, indifferent to singularity, contracted by haecceity.
- ****Prime matter**** — Pure potentiality, matter devoid of all form, serving as the ultimate substrate of substantial change.
- ****Forma corporeitas**** — A substantial form distinct from the rational soul, giving the human body its specific corporeal organization.
- ****Intuitive cognition**** — Direct, non-abstractive knowledge of an individual’s present existence or non-existence; required for knowing singulars.
- ****Voluntarism**** — The view that the will (divine or human) is metaphysically prior to the intellect; God’s will grounds truth and morality.
- ****Univocal predication**** — Predication in which a term signifies exactly the same concept across different subjects (e.g., "being" of God and creatures).
- ****Essentially ordered series**** — A causal series where each member’s causality depends *here and now* on the prior member (e.g., hand moving stick moving stone); cannot be infinite.

## Debates and open questions
- **Univocity and modernity**: Since the 1960s, scholars (e.g., Jean-François Courtine, Heidegger, Radical Orthodox theologians) debate whether Scotus’s univocity inaugurated "onto-theology" and modernity by collapsing the Creator-creature distinction into a single ontological plane. Defenders (Daniel Horan, Thomas Williams) argue univocity is **semantic/epistemological** (one concept abstracted from diverse realities), not ontological, citing *Ordinatio* 1, d. 3, pars 1, q. 3, n. 163.
- **Textual authenticity**: The editorial work of Scotus’s disciples produced multiple versions (*Lectura*, *Ordinatio*, *Reportatio*) and spurious attributions (*De modis significandi*, *Theoremata*). The Vatican critical edition (1950–) is still completing the *Ordinatio*; the *Lectura* Book 4 and parts of the *Reportatio* remain lost or disputed.
- **Relation to Ockham**: Whether Ockham’s nominalism develops directly from Scotus’s formal distinction and univocity, or constitutes a rupture, remains a central historiographical question.
- **Beatification and influence**: The 1993 beatification revived institutional interest, but the philosophical reception remains divided between Thomistic neo-Scholasticism and contemporary analytic/phenomenological engagements.
