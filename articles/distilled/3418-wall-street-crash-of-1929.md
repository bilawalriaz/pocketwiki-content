# Wall Street crash of 1929

## Overview
The Wall Street Crash of 1929 was a catastrophic collapse of U.S. stock prices that began in October 1929. It shattered public confidence in the financial system and is widely regarded as the trigger for the Great Depression, the decade-long global economic crisis that followed. The crash ended the speculative boom of the "Roaring Twenties" and led to major regulatory reforms in American finance.

## Timeline
- **Sep 3, 1929** — Dow Jones Industrial Average peaks at 381.17.
- **Sep 5, 1929** — Roger Babson predicts a coming crash, initiating the "Babson Break."
- **Oct 23, 1929** — A sharp, unexpected decline in stock prices begins.
- **Oct 24, 1929** — "Black Thursday": Panic selling; bankers intervene to stabilize the market.
- **Oct 28, 1929** — "Black Monday": Dow falls 12.82%.
- **Oct 29, 1929** — "Black Tuesday": Record 16.4 million shares traded; Dow falls 11.73%.
- **Nov 13, 1929** — Market hits an interim bottom at Dow 198.60.
- **Apr 17, 1930** — A bear market rally peaks at Dow 294.07.
- **Jul 8, 1932** — Dow reaches its ultimate low of 41.22, down 89.2% from its 1929 peak.
- **1933** — Congress passes the Glass-Steagall Act and Securities Act.
- **Nov 23, 1954** — Dow Jones finally returns to its September 1929 peak.

## Body

### Background and Causes
The "Roaring Twenties" were marked by industrial expansion and widespread stock market speculation. Many small investors, seeking better returns than bank deposits, bought stocks on margin—borrowing money to purchase shares. By 1929, signs of economic trouble appeared, including a depressed agricultural sector and slowing consumer goods production. Despite this, stock prices soared far above their underlying value. A speculative bubble formed, fueled by easy credit and optimism.

Several events may have triggered the final collapse. Conservative economists cite investor fears over the pending Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act. Others point to regulatory actions against public utilities in New York and Massachusetts, or a damaging fraud case in London. The market had already experienced a "healthy correction" in September, but selling intensified in mid-October.

### The Crash
The crash began with a sharp decline on October 23, 1929. The next day, "Black Thursday," saw record trading volume and panic. A consortium of leading bankers, including Thomas Lamont and Charles Mitchell, intervened by buying stocks above market value, temporarily halting the slide. This tactic failed to restore lasting confidence. Selling resumed on "Black Monday" (October 28) and culminated in "Black Tuesday" (October 29), when 16.4 million shares were traded and billions in value were wiped out. Despite a brief recovery, the market entered a long, steady decline, losing nearly 90% of its value by July 1932.

### Aftermath and Regulation
The crash prompted a major regulatory response. The Pecora Commission (1932) investigated its causes. Congress passed the Glass-Steagall Act (1933), separating commercial and investment banking. The Securities Act of 1933 and Securities Exchange Act of 1934 created the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) to regulate markets and curb the speculative practices that contributed to the crash. Stock exchanges also began implementing trading suspensions during rapid price declines.

### Economic Impact
The crash and subsequent depression were the largest financial crisis of the 20th century. It caused widespread bank failures, business closures, and mass unemployment. While only 16% of American households held stocks, the psychological impact was profound, reducing consumer spending and business investment. The crisis spread globally, leading to strikes and social unrest in Europe. Historians debate whether the crash caused the Great Depression or was one stage of a broader business cycle.

## Terms
- **Margin buying**: Purchasing stocks with borrowed money, using the stocks themselves as collateral.
- **Bull market**: A period of rising stock prices and investor optimism.
- **Bear market**: A period of falling stock prices and pessimism.
- **Blue chip stocks**: Shares of large, well-established, and financially sound companies.
- **Lender of last resort**: An institution, typically a central bank, that provides loans to banks or other institutions facing financial crisis.
- **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)**: U.S. government agency created to regulate the stock market and protect investors.
- **Glass-Steagall Act**: 1933 law that separated commercial banking (taking deposits) from investment banking (underwriting securities).
- **Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act**: A 1930 law that raised U.S. tariffs on over 20,000 imported goods, which critics argue worsened the global depression.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars disagree on the crash's role in the Great Depression. Some argue the price swings alone were not severe enough to cause a systemic collapse. Others, like Milton Friedman, contend that the subsequent collapse of the banking system was the critical factor. Marxist historian Eric Hobsbawm viewed the crash as a pivotal 20th-century event that discredited liberal capitalism and boosted support for state planning. The debate continues over whether the crash was the primary cause of the Depression or merely a symptom of deeper economic weaknesses.
