# Meat

## Overview
Meat—animal tissue, primarily skeletal muscle—has been a human food source since prehistory. The Neolithic Revolution (c. 11,000 years ago) enabled the domestication of vertebrates such as cattle, pigs, and chickens, allowing systematic production and selective breeding for desired traits. While meat provides high-quality protein and essential micronutrients (e.g., vitamin B12, iron), its consumption—especially red and processed meat—is linked to higher risks of cancer, cardiovascular disease, and type 2 diabetes. Meat production is a major driver of environmental degradation, accounting for ~20% of global greenhouse gas emissions, ~40% of global land use, and significant biodiversity loss and water pollution. Intensive animal farming (factory farming) rose from 30% of world production in 1990 to 40% by 2005, raising concerns about animal welfare, antimicrobial resistance, and zoonotic disease risk. Cultural, religious, and ethical factors shape consumption patterns globally, though omnivorous diets remain dominant (~87% of people).

## Timeline
- **c. 11,000 years ago** — Neolithic Revolution begins; domestication of chickens, sheep, goats, pigs, horses, and cattle enables systematic meat production.
- **1966** — Industrialized nations (US, UK) begin factory farming of beef/dairy cattle and domestic pigs.
- **1990** — Intensive animal farming accounts for 30% of world meat production.
- **2005** — Intensive animal farming rises to 40% of world meat production.
- **2015–2020** — US Dietary Guidelines advise men/teenage boys to reduce intake of protein foods (meats, poultry, eggs).
- **2018** — Ipsos MORI study (28 countries): ~87% of people include meat in their diet; 73% regularly.
- **2019** — *The Lancet* recommends 50% reduction in global meat/sugar consumption; IPCC calls for significant reduction in wealthy countries.
- **2021** — Review finds 11–51% increased cancer risk per 100g/day red meat; 8–72% per 50g/day processed meat.
- **2022** — Umbrella reviews link 100g/day red meat to 17% higher type 2 diabetes risk and 15% higher coronary heart disease risk; 50g/day processed meat to 37% higher diabetes risk and 27% higher coronary heart disease risk.
- **2023** — Global meat production reaches 370 million tonnes (60% increase since 2000); chicken (34.2%), pig (33.7%), cattle dominate (~90% combined).

## Body

### Etymology and Definition
The word *meat* derives from Old English *mete*, meaning food in general. Modern usage primarily denotes skeletal muscle with associated fat and connective tissue, but can include offal (edible organs like liver, kidney). In a restrictive sense, it refers to mammalian flesh (pigs, cattle, sheep) excluding fish, poultry, insects, and seafood.

### History of Production
**Domestication:** Early humans relied on organized hunting of large game (bison, deer). The Neolithic Revolution enabled domestication and selective breeding for improved meat yield.
**Intensive Farming:** Post-WWII, guaranteed prices spurred output but increased reliance on feed, veterinary drugs, and caused pollution/disease. Factory farming began in 1966 in the US/UK and globalized by the late 20th century, replacing traditional husbandry.
**Selective Breeding:** Modern techniques (progeny testing, genetic mapping) accelerate trait selection. After 1980s health concerns over saturated fat, UK beef/pork/lamb fat content dropped from 20–26% to 4–8% via breeding for leanness and butchery changes. Genetic engineering and cloning of commercial animals (sheep, pig, cattle) have been experimentally successful. Consumer demand for pre-packaged cuts drives larger cattle breeds; novel species (antelope, crocodile, ostrich) are now farmed.

### Animal Growth and Development
**Genetics:** Economically important traits (growth, muscle hypertrophy/double-muscling, dwarfism) are heritable. Recessive genes in cattle complicate breeding. Genetic engineering allows isolation/reincorporation of desired genes; recombinant bacteria improve cattle digestion; reproductive cloning is viable.
**Environment:** Mammals regulate body temperature; low temperatures prolong development, high temperatures delay it. Static magnetic fields retard development for unknown reasons.
**Nutrition:** Meat yield depends on the animal's *plane of nutrition* (feeding level). Protein content regulates growth; ruminants digest cellulose but ruminal microbes degrade excess high-quality protein. Techniques to maximize protein utilization include formalin-treated feed, manure recycling, and microbial conversion of petroleum hydrocarbons to protein. Soil micronutrient deficiencies (phosphate, cobalt) and plant toxins (sodium fluoroacetate) affect livestock; pollutants (methylmercury, pesticides) bioaccumulate in meat.
**Welfare:** Confinement in factory farming causes abnormal behaviors (tail-biting, cannibalism, feather pecking). Invasive procedures (beak trimming, castration, ear notching) and breeding for rapid growth (broiler chickens) cause leg deformities, lameness, and transport stress mortality.
**Human Intervention:** Hormones (gonadotrophic, anabolic steroids) enhance fertility and muscle growth; steroid use sparked the "beef hormone controversy" (trade dispute) and may reduce tenderness. Antibiotics fed to animals increase growth rates but are banned in the EU due to antimicrobial resistance; prevalent in the US. Sedatives reduce stress/weight loss. Artificial insemination with synchronized ovulation is routine for genetic quality.

### Composition
**Biochemical:** Adult mammalian muscle: ~75% water, 19% protein, 2.5% intramuscular fat, 1.2% carbohydrates, 2.3% soluble substances. Proteins: sarcoplasmic (11.5%, glycolytic enzymes for glycolysis/ATP production) and myofibrillar (5.5%, myosin/actin for structure/contraction). Connective tissue: collagen, elastin. Fat: adipose tissue (energy storage, triglycerides) and intramuscular fat (phospholipids, cholesterol).
**Nutritional:** Complete protein (all essential amino acids). Red meat: rich in vitamin B12, selenium, niacin, B6, iron, vitamin K. Low carbohydrates, no fiber. Fat content varies by species, breed, feed, cut, and cooking. Wild game leaner than farmed. Marbling (3–7% intramuscular fat) modestly improves palatability (~5% contribution). Post-1960 consumption rise correlates with dietary fat/cholesterol imbalances.

### Production Process
**Transport:** Livestock moved to slaughterhouses; stress/injuries/deaths en route lower meat quality (low water/glycogen, high pH).
**Slaughter:** Stunning (CO2, captive bolt, electric current) followed by exsanguination (severing carotid/jugular in cattle/sheep; anterior vena cava in pigs). Blood removal prevents unappealing appearance and microbial growth.
**Dressing/Cutting:** Removal of head, feet, hide, viscera, offal. Cattle/pig carcasses split ventrally; automation increasing.
**Conditioning (Aging):** Stored at −1.5 °C up to six weeks. Glycolysis continues ~1 day (pH → 5.5), improving water-holding/tenderness. *Rigor mortis* sets in hours (ATP depletion → rigid actomyosin), causing toughness if cooked during this phase; resolves as proteins denature (except collagen/elastin). Proteolysis yields flavor compounds (hypoxanthine from ATP).
**Additives:** Industrial processing uses additives to modify flavor/color, improve tenderness/juiciness/cohesiveness, and preserve.

### Consumption Patterns
**Historical:** Isotopic analysis of early medieval England shows high-meat diets rare; elites did not eat more than non-elites, men not more than women. 19th-century Britain led Europe in consumption (34 kg/capita in 1830s → 59 kg in 1912; laborers 39 kg vs aristocrats 140 kg in 1904). US became net meat importer by 1926. Global cattle ~600M, sheep/goats ~700M, pigs ~300M in 1929.
**Trends (2000–2023):** White meat consumption rose sharply. Poultry +76.6% kg/capita; pig +19.7%; bovine fell 10.4→9.6 kg/capita. 2023 production: 370M tonnes (+60% since 2000). Chicken (34.2%, +116% since 2000), pig (33.7%, down from 38%), cattle dominate (~90% total). 2018 Ipsos: 87% eat meat (73% regularly, 14% occasionally). Consumption varies by culture, income; horse (France, Italy, Germany, Japan), dog (China, Korea, Vietnam), guinea pig (Andes), whale/dolphin (several nations). 2013 European horse meat scandal revealed mislabeling.
**Preparation:** Cooking (braising, broiling, frying, grilling, roasting). Curing (smoking, pickling, salting, air-drying). Raw dishes (steak tartare, pâtés).

### Red vs. White Meat
**Nutritional context:** Red meat = mammalian flesh (beef, pork, lamb, mutton, veal, venison, goat); color not definitive. White meat = poultry (chicken, turkey); some sources include fish. **Culinary context:** White meat = poultry breast/wings; legs/thighs = dark meat.

### Health Effects
**Contamination:** Heavy metals, mycotoxins, pesticide residues, dioxins, PCBs contaminate meat via feed, drug residues, processing. Processed/smoked/cooked meat contains carcinogens (polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons). Nitrosamines in processed meat and formed from heme iron (high in red meat) are highly carcinogenic. Heterocyclic amines (HCAs) and PAHs form during high-temperature cooking (pan-frying, grilling); mutagenic in lab studies. Microwaving before cooking reduces HCAs.
**Cancer:** IARC (WHO): Processed meat = Group 1 (carcinogenic; sufficient evidence for colorectal cancer). Red meat = Group 2A (probably carcinogenic; limited human evidence, strong mechanistic evidence). Major health bodies (Cancer Research UK, NHS, NCI, ACS, Canadian Cancer Society) confirm increased bowel cancer risk. 2021 review: +11–51% cancer risk per 100g/day red meat; +8–72% per 50g/day processed meat.
**Bacterial Contamination:** 2011 US study: 47% meat/poultry contaminated with *S. aureus* (52% antibiotic-resistant). 2018 investigation: ~15% US population suffers foodborne illness annually; unsanitary plant conditions noted. Thorough cooking and avoiding recontamination reduce risk.
**Diabetes:** 2022 umbrella review: 100g/day red meat → +17% type 2 diabetes risk; 50g/day processed meat → +37% risk. Diabetes UK advises limiting intake.
**Infectious Diseases:** Meat production/trade increases zoonosis/pandemic risks (contact with wild/farmed animals, environmental impact). Avian influenza from poultry; antibiotic use drives antimicrobial resistance (millions of deaths annually).
**Cardiovascular Disease:** 2022 umbrella review: 100g/day red meat → +15% coronary heart disease, +14% hypertension, +12% stroke. 50g/day processed meat → +27% coronary heart disease, +17% stroke, +8% heart failure, +15% all-cause mortality.
**Behavioral Shifts:** US beef consumption fell 21% (1970–1994) while chicken rose 90% due to price, saturated fat/cholesterol concerns.

### Environmental Impact
**Overview:** Livestock sector = largest water pollution source (wastes, fertilizers, pesticides); >8% global human water use; major driver of biodiversity loss, deforestation, ocean dead zones, species extinction, land degradation, overfishing, global warming. Cattle farming caused ~80% of Amazon deforestation (2008) for feed (soya) and ranching. Grazing can benefit some wildlife but harms others; targeted grazing substitutes for herbicides.
**Land Use:** Meat production uses ~40% global land surface. Contiguous US: 34% pasture/rangeland (265M ha), 20% cropland (158M ha, partly for feed). ~75% deforested land used for livestock pasture. Deforestation releases CO2 and removes carbon sinks. Pressure on fertile soils threatens food security.
**Climate Change:** Meat/dairy/egg = 57% of food-system GHGs, 20% of total GHGs. Rising consumption "exploded" agriculture's carbon footprint. Brazil/Australia emissions >200% above income-group averages due to meat. UNEP: global shift to meat/dairy-free diet "indispensable" to prevent adverse climate change. *Nature* (2018): Western meat consumption must fall ~90%. IPCC (2019): significant reduction needed, especially in wealthy countries.
**Biodiversity Loss:** Primary driver of sixth mass extinction. WWF (2017): 60% global biodiversity loss attributable to meat-based diets (feed crop land use → habitat loss). Livestock = 60% mammalian biomass; humans 36%; wild mammals 4%. 15,364 scientists (2017) called for drastic per capita reduction and plant-based shifts. Chatham House (2021): plant-based diets free land for ecosystem restoration. Rising population/affluence predicts increased consumption, emissions, and biodiversity loss.
**Mitigation:** On-farm: convert human-inedible crop residues; use manure as fertilizer (substitutes synthetic N fertilizers, saving 43–88 MJ fossil energy/kg N). Systemic: IPCC urges substantial production reduction via plant-based diets where consumption is high. Alternatives: legumes, fungi, supplements (B12), fortified foods, cultured meat, microbial protein, mycoprotein, mushroom/legume-based substitutes. Rewilding freed land. Biologists Dirzo, Ceballos, Ehrlich: curb "massive planetary monopoly of industrial meat production" while respecting indigenous cultural traditions.

### Cultural Aspects
**Ethics:** Objections to killing animals (animal rights, environmental ethics, sentience) or production practices. Cultural/religious taboos on specific animals. Western philosophy divided: Plato/Pythagoras/Plutarch favored vegetarianism; Aristotle/Augustine justified hierarchy (animals serve humans). Descartes: animals as machines; Kant: means not ends. Voltaire/Rousseau dissented (Rousseau: meat-eating social, not natural). Modern theorists (Elias, Vialles, Braudel): Renaissance onward, meat detached from animal form ("ellipsis"); European colonialism exported meat-heavy diets globally.
**Religion:** Jainism prohibits meat. Some Buddhist/Hindu schools advocate vegetarianism. Some Sikh groups oppose meat. Jewish *Kashrut*: permits kosher, forbids *treif*. Islamic *Halal*: forbids carrion, blood, pork (*haram*).
**Psychology:** Meat eating correlates with masculinity, reduced openness to experience. Consumer psychology relevant to marketing and reduction advocacy.
**Gender:** Meat perceived as masculine (unlike most foods). Cross-cultural data: men more involved in meat preparation (traditional "male familiarity with killing"; Lévi-Strauss: roasting more violent than boiling). Modern: men consume more meat, prefer red meat; women prefer chicken/fish.

## Terms
- ****Neolithic Revolution**** — The transition (~11,000 years ago) from hunter-gatherer societies to agriculture, enabling domestication of vertebrates for systematic meat production.
- ****Intensive animal farming (factory farming)**** — Industrialized livestock production using high inputs (feed, veterinary drugs, confinement) to maximize output; rose from 30% (1990) to 40% (2005) of global production.
- ****Selective breeding**** — Choosing parent animals with desired heritable traits (e.g., leanness, muscle hypertrophy) to improve meat quality/yield; accelerated by progeny testing and genetic engineering.
- ****Plane of nutrition**** — The level of an animal's feeding (over- or underfed), which determines the quality and quantity of usable meat.
- ****Rigor mortis**** — Post-mortem stiffening caused by ATP depletion, binding actin/myosin into rigid actomyosin; reduces water-holding capacity and causes toughness if meat cooked during this phase.
- ****Conditioning (aging)**** — Storing meat at ~−1.5 °C for up to six weeks; proteolysis resolves rigor, increasing tenderness and flavor (hypoxanthine from ATP breakdown).
- ****Red meat (nutritional)**** — Flesh of mammals (beef, pork, lamb, goat, etc.), defined by source not color; rich in heme iron; classified Group 2A (probably carcinogenic) by IARC.
- ****Processed meat**** — Meat transformed by salting, curing, fermentation, smoking (e.g., bacon, ham, sausages); classified Group 1 (carcinogenic) by IARC.
- ****Zoonosis**** — Infectious disease transmitted from animals to humans; meat production/trade increases pandemic risks (e.g., avian influenza).
- ****Antimicrobial resistance (AMR)**** — Microorganisms evolving resistance to antibiotics; driven by non-therapeutic antibiotic use in livestock (banned in EU, prevalent in US), causing millions of human deaths annually.

## Debates and open questions
- **Nutrition science:** Scientists disagree on exactly how the *plane of nutrition* influences carcass composition.
- **Growth hormones:** Research is inconclusive on whether anabolic steroids decrease meat tenderness; international trade dispute ("beef hormone controversy") persists.
- **Environmental accounting:** Nations show vastly different emissions intensities (Brazil, Australia >200% above income-group averages); optimal mitigation policies for diverse production systems are debated.
- **Cultural vs. planetary boundaries:** Biologists argue industrial meat's "planetary monopoly" must be curbed, but emphasize respecting indigenous cultures where meat is a vital protein source.
- **Alternative proteins:** Scalability, consumer acceptance, and regulatory pathways for cultured meat, microbial protein, and plant-based substitutes remain open questions.
