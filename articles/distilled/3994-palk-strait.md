# Palk Strait

## Overview
The Palk Strait is a narrow, shallow body of water separating the Indian state of Tamil Nadu from Sri Lanka, connecting the Bay of Bengal to the Laccadive Sea. It is a significant geographical and cultural boundary, defined by the chain of shoals known as Ram Setu (Adam's Bridge) and the Gulf of Mannar. Its shallow depth and reefs have historically limited navigation, making proposals for dredging and bridge-building a recurring topic of debate.

## Timeline
- **c. 115,000–11,700 BP** — During the Last Glacial Period, lower sea levels exposed the strait as dry land.
- **c. 7,000 years ago** — Rising Holocene sea levels submerged the strait, forming its current geography.
- **1860** — The first proposal to create a shipping channel through the strait was made to the British government of India.
- **1914** — The Pamban Bridge, linking Pamban Island to the Indian mainland, opened for traffic.
- **1964** — The Dhanushkodi cyclone destroyed the railway line to Dhanushkodi and a passenger train, killing around 200 people.
- **1984** — The Indian Government discontinued the ferry service between Rameswaram and Talaimannar.
- **1988** — An adjacent road bridge near the Pamban Bridge was opened.
- **2002** — The Palk Strait Bridge, an undersea tunnel and bridge, was first discussed.
- **2005** — The Government of India commissioned the Sethusamudram Shipping Canal Project.
- **2023** — Rail transportation on the old Pamban Bridge was suspended permanently due to stability concerns.
- **2024** — A new railway bridge was completed near the old Pamban Bridge.

## Body

### Geography and Geology
The Palk Strait stretches about 137 km (85 mi) long and 64 to 137 km (40 to 85 mi) wide. It is bounded by Ram Setu (Adam's Bridge) on one side and the Gulf of Mannar on the other. The strait is relatively shallow, with depths around the shoals typically 1–3 m (3 ft 3 in – 9 ft 10 in) and a central depth of about 20 m (66 ft), reaching a maximum of 35 m (115 ft). Geologically, the strait was exposed dry land during the Last Glacial Period due to sea levels up to 120 m (390 ft) lower than today. It became submerged around 7,000 years ago during the Holocene sea-level rise. The wave patterns differ significantly, with swells in the Bay of Bengal to the north and smaller wind waves in the Palk Bay to the south.

### Transport and Navigation
The shallow waters, reefs, and small islands make the strait impassable for large ships, though fishing boats and small craft navigate it. Proposals to improve navigation, including dredging a shipping channel and building a bridge, have been made since 1860. The most recent major proposal was the Sethusamudram Shipping Canal Project (2005), which faced religious opposition. Existing infrastructure includes the Pamban Bridge (road and rail links to Pamban Island) and a proposed Palk Strait Bridge (an undersea tunnel and bridge). Historical transport included a rail-ferry connection from India to Sri Lanka, which was disrupted by the 1964 cyclone and officially discontinued in 1984.

## Terms
- **Strait:** A narrow passage of water connecting two larger bodies of water.
- **Ram Setu (Adam's Bridge):** A chain of low-lying islands and shoals connecting Pamban Island to Mannar Island.
- **Shoal:** A sandbar or shallow place in a body of water, making navigation hazardous.
- **Holocene:** The current geological epoch, beginning about 11,700 years ago after the last glacial period.
- **Swell:** A series of mechanical waves in the ocean that have moved away from their point of origin.
- **Dredging:** The process of removing sediment from the bottom of a body of water to deepen it.
- **Sethusamudram Shipping Canal Project:** A 2005 Indian government proposal to create a shipping channel through the Palk Strait.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary debate concerns the proposed Sethusamudram Shipping Canal Project and other dredging or bridge-building plans. These proposals face opposition on religious grounds, as the shoals of Ram Setu are considered sacred in Hinduism, believed to be the bridge built by Rama's army as described in the epic Ramayana. The environmental and economic impacts of altering the strait's natural geography are also subjects of ongoing discussion.
