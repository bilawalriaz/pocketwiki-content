# Gondar

## Overview
Gondar is a historic city in Ethiopia's Amhara Region, north of Lake Tana. It served as the capital of the Ethiopian Empire from the 17th to the 19th century and is renowned for its royal castles, including the UNESCO World Heritage Site Fasil Ghebbi. Its history reflects the rise and fall of imperial power, ethnic and religious dynamics, and the impacts of foreign occupation and internal conflict.

## Timeline
- **1636** — Emperor Fasilides selects Gondar as his royal camp (*katama*), establishing it as the capital.
- **1667** — By Fasilides' death, Gondar is well-established; his successor Yohannes I makes it a permanent capital.
- **1669–1670** — Emperor orders Muslims and Jews to live in separate quarters.
- **1767** — Ras Mikael Sehul kills Emperor Iyoas I, marking a decline in central authority.
- **1769–1855** — The *Zemene Mesafint* ("Era of Princes") period; Gondar's power wanes.
- **1864** — Emperor Tewodros II orders the sacking of Gondar.
- **1888–1889** — Mahdist Sudanese forces invade, burning churches and enslaving inhabitants.
- **1936** — Italian forces occupy Gondar without a fight during the Second Italo-Ethiopian War.
- **1941** — British and Italian forces fight in Gondar, ending Italian occupation.
- **1991** — Gondar is occupied by the Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front (EPRDF).
- **2023–present** — The War in Amhara brings violence and conflict to the city.

## Body

### Imperial Capital and Golden Age
Emperor Fasilides founded Gondar as his capital in 1636, choosing it for its strategic location at the convergence of trade routes from Sudan and the Red Sea. He constructed the Fasil Ghebbi (Royal Enclosure) and several churches. His successors, Yohannes I and Iyasu I, continued building, and by Iyasu's reign, the city's population exceeded 60,000. The city was a cosmopolitan center, with distinct quarters for Christians, Muslims, and Beta Israel (Ethiopian Jews).

### Decline and the *Zemene Mesafint*
After Iyasu I's death in 1706, emperors became less politically active, preferring luxury. Power shifted to regional warlords, notably the Tigrayan Ras Mikael Sehul, who gained control of Gondar in the 1750s. The city became a pawn in power struggles, and the Oromo influence increased. The *Zemene Mesafint* (Era of Princes) saw Gondar's imperial authority collapse, though it remained a symbolic capital.

### Sackings, Occupation, and Modern Era
Gondar suffered severe damage in the 19th century. Emperor Tewodros II sacked it in 1864, and Mahdist Sudanese invasions in 1888–1889 left it a "ghost town." The Italian occupation (1936–1941) introduced modern infrastructure but segregated the population. After a period of decline, the city saw some revival in the early 20th century as a commercial hub. It was occupied by the EPRDF in 1991 and has recently been affected by the War in Amhara, with a major offensive in September 2024.

### Cityscape and Culture
Gondar's traditional quarters included Addis Alem (Muslim), Kayla Mayda (Beta Israel), and Qagn Bet (nobility). It was a major center for the Ethiopian Orthodox Church, with 44 churches. The Fasil Ghebbi royal enclosure, with its castles and churches, is the primary tourist attraction. The Italian colonial period left a distinct "piazza" district with modernist architecture.

## Terms
- **Katama**: A royal camp or temporary capital.
- **Fasil Ghebbi**: The Royal Enclosure in Gondar, a UNESCO World Heritage Site containing castles and churches.
- **Zemene Mesafint**: The "Era of Princes" (c. 1769–1855), a period of decentralized rule and conflict.
- **Beta Israel**: The historical community of Ethiopian Jews.
- **Ras**: A high-ranking noble or warlord title in the Ethiopian Empire.
- **Ečagē**: The chief of customs or a high official in the Ethiopian court.
- **Woreda**: An administrative division of Ethiopia, similar to a district.
- **Amharic**: The official working language of Ethiopia and the primary language of Gondar.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact population of Gondar at its peak is debated, with estimates for the 18th century ranging from over 60,000 to much higher figures.
- The precise causes and timeline of the city's decline after the 18th century are subjects of historical analysis, involving factors like internal strife, external invasions, and shifts in trade routes.
- The impact and legacy of the Italian colonial period on Gondar's urban fabric and social structure continue to be studied.
