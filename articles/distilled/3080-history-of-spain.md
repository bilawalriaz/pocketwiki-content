# History of Spain

## Overview
Spain's history on the Iberian Peninsula spans from pre-Roman settlements to its current status as a democratic constitutional monarchy in the EU. Its significance lies in its vast early modern empire, the complex interplay of Christian, Muslim, and Jewish cultures in the Middle Ages, and a turbulent 20th-century transition from dictatorship to democracy.

## Timeline
- **c. 15,000 BC** — Cave paintings created at Altamira.
- **218–201 BC** — Second Punic War; Rome defeats Carthage in Hispania.
- **19 BC** — Roman conquest of the Iberian Peninsula completed.
- **711** — Umayyad conquest begins; most of the peninsula becomes Al-Andalus.
- **1469** — Dynastic union of Castile and Aragon via marriage of Isabella I and Ferdinand II.
- **1492** — Conquest of Granada; Columbus's first voyage; expulsion of Jews.
- **1588** — Destruction of the Spanish Armada.
- **1700–14** — War of the Spanish Succession; Bourbon dynasty ascends the throne.
- **1808–14** — Peninsular War against Napoleonic France.
- **1898** — Spanish–American War; loss of major overseas colonies.
- **1936–39** — Spanish Civil War; victory of Francisco Franco's Nationalists.
- **1975** — Death of Franco; transition to democracy under King Juan Carlos I begins.
- **1978** — New Constitution establishes Spain as a constitutional monarchy.
- **1986** — Spain joins the European Economic Community (EU predecessor).

## Body

### Prehistory and Antiquity
The earliest human remains in Western Europe are in Spain, with *Homo* fossils at Atapuerca dating to ~1.2 million years ago. Pre-Roman Iberia hosted diverse peoples, including Iberians, Celts, and Tartessians. From the 9th century BC, Phoenicians, Greeks, and Carthaginians established coastal trading colonies. The peninsula became a theater of the Second Punic War (218–201 BC). Following Roman victory, the entire peninsula was gradually conquered and unified as Hispania by 19 BC. Roman rule brought urban development, economic expansion, and cultural Romanization, producing emperors like Trajan and Hadrian.

### Visigothic and Islamic Rule
The decline of the Western Roman Empire led to Germanic invasions in the 5th century, culminating in the Visigothic Kingdom, which controlled most of Hispania by the late 6th century. The Visigoths maintained Roman institutions and legal codes, such as the *Liber Iudiciorum*. In 711, an Islamic Berber force from North Africa invaded, defeating the Visigothic king and rapidly conquering nearly the entire peninsula, which became Al-Andalus. This period saw the spread of Islam, Arabic language, and a culture of relative religious tolerance known as *La Convivencia*. A small Christian Kingdom of Asturias survived in the north, initiating the centuries-long Reconquista.

### The Reconquista and the Catholic Monarchs
From the 8th century, Christian kingdoms in the north gradually expanded southward. Key victories, like the Battle of Las Navas de Tolosa (1212), broke Almohad power. By the mid-13th century, only the Emirate of Granada remained under Muslim rule. The 1469 marriage of Isabella I and Ferdinand II united the Crowns of Castile and Aragon, creating a powerful joint monarchy. In 1492, they completed the Reconquista by conquering Granada. That same year, they authorized Columbus's voyage to the Americas and issued the Alhambra Decree, expelling Jews who refused to convert.

### Empire, Golden Age, and Decline
The union of Castile and Aragon, followed by the Habsburg dynasty's accession under Charles I, launched Spain's imperial era. Conquest of the Americas brought vast wealth, particularly silver, funding European wars. The 16th century, under Philip II, was the height of Spanish power and its cultural Golden Age, but also saw costly conflicts like the Eighty Years' War and the Armada's defeat (1588). The 17th century was marked by economic crises, state bankruptcies, and gradual decline. The extinction of the Spanish Habsburg line in 1700 led to the War of the Spanish Succession, after which the Bourbon dynasty took the throne and centralized the state.

### 19th and 20th Century Turmoil
The 19th century was characterized by political instability, alternating between liberal and monarchical rule, and the loss of most American colonies in the 1820s. The "Disaster of 1898" in the Spanish–American War ended Spain's remaining major colonial empire. The early 20th century saw the collapse of the Restoration monarchy, the brief Second Spanish Republic (1931–36), and the devastating Spanish Civil War (1936–39). The war was won by Francisco Franco's Nationalist forces, leading to a nearly four-decade dictatorship. Franco's death in 1975 initiated a peaceful transition to democracy, culminating in the 1978 Constitution and Spain's integration into the European community.

## Terms
*   **Reconquista:** The centuries-long period of Christian reconquest of the Iberian Peninsula from Muslim rule, lasting from the 8th to the 15th century.
*   **Al-Andalus:** The term for the Iberian Peninsula under Muslim rule, from the Umayyad conquest in 711 until the fall of Granada in 1492.
*   **La Convivencia:** "The coexistence," referring to the period of relative religious tolerance between Christians, Muslims, and Jews in medieval Iberia.
*   **Catholic Monarchs:** The joint title for Isabella I of Castile and Ferdinand II of Aragon, whose marriage united Spain's two most powerful kingdoms.
*   **Habsburg Dynasty:** A European royal house that ruled Spain from 1516 to 1700, presiding over its imperial peak and subsequent decline.
*   **Bourbon Dynasty:** A French royal house that has ruled Spain since 1700, following the War of the Spanish Succession.
*   **Peninsular War:** The 1808–1814 conflict in the Iberian Peninsula between Napoleon's Empire and a coalition of Spain, Britain, and Portugal.
*   **Second Spanish Republic:** The democratic government in Spain from 1931 to 1936, overthrown by the military coup that sparked the Civil War.
*   **Francoist Spain:** The period of dictatorship under General Francisco Franco from 1939 to 1975.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The nature and degree of cultural and social interaction (*La Convivencia*) between Christians, Muslims, and Jews in medieval Al-Andalus is debated.
*   Historians analyze the complex causes of the Spanish Civil War, weighing political polarization, economic inequality, religious conflict, and foreign intervention.
*   The extent to which American silver influx caused Spain's 17th-century economic decline, versus other factors like war expenditure and domestic policy, is debated.
*   The historical significance and interpretation of the Reconquista remain contested, viewed by some as a unifying national epic and by others as colonial expansion and religious intolerance.
