# East India Company

## Overview
The East India Company (EIC) was a British joint-stock company founded in 1600 to trade with the East Indies. It evolved from a trading enterprise into a powerful political and military force, ruling large parts of the Indian subcontinent and initiating the British Raj. Its history illustrates the transition from commercial monopoly to colonial governance, culminating in its dissolution after the Indian Rebellion of 1857.

## Timeline
- **1600** — Chartered by Queen Elizabeth I as the "Governor and Company of Merchants of London Trading into the East Indies."
- **1612** — Victory over the Portuguese at the Battle of Swally, securing a foothold in India.
- **1615** — Sir Thomas Roe secures a commercial treaty with Mughal Emperor Jahangir.
- **1757** — Victory at the Battle of Plassey marks the start of major territorial control in Bengal.
- **1770** — The Great Bengal Famine, exacerbated by company policies, kills an estimated 10 million people.
- **1773** — Regulating Act gives the British government oversight of the EIC's affairs.
- **1833** — Saint Helena Act ends the company's trade monopoly with China, making it purely administrative.
- **1857** — The Indian Rebellion leads to the British Crown assuming direct control.
- **1858** — Government of India Act nationalises the company's possessions and powers.
- **1874** — Dissolved under the East India Stock Dividend Redemption Act.

## Body

### Formation and Early Trade
The EIC was formed by London merchants seeking to break the Spanish-Portuguese monopoly on Eastern trade. Its first voyage in 1601, commanded by James Lancaster, was profitable and established trading posts in Java. Early competition with the Dutch East India Company (VOC) was fierce, leading to military skirmishes. A major setback in 1623, when the Dutch attacked an EIC factory in Amboyna, pushed the company to focus on India.

### Expansion in India
The company established its first Indian factory at Masulipatnam in 1611 and secured a treaty with the Mughal Empire in 1615. It expanded through a network of fortified trading posts ("factories") like Fort William and Fort St George. After the Mughal Empire's decline following Emperor Aurangzeb's death in 1707, the EIC exploited the ensuing chaos. It used locally raised soldiers (sepoys) trained in European tactics to defeat larger Indian forces. The pivotal Battle of Plassey in 1757 gave the company control of Bengal, from which it expanded its territorial rule through wars and diplomacy, often installing puppet rulers.

### Governance and Exploitation
The EIC transitioned from trade to territorial rule, becoming a de facto government. It imposed heavy taxes, leading to widespread poverty and events like the Great Bengal Famine of 1770. The company's wealth grew immensely, with Bengal's resources transferred to Britain. Its monopoly was challenged in Parliament, leading to the Regulating Act of 1773, which began government oversight. The company's trade monopoly with India was abolished in 1813.

### Global Trade and Conflicts
The EIC traded in commodities like cotton, silk, spices, tea, and opium. In China, it illegally sold opium, leading to the First Opium War (1839-1842) and the Treaty of Nanjing, which ceded Hong Kong to Britain. The company also operated in Japan (1613-1623) and was involved in the slave trade from the 1620s until 1834. Its financial crises, like the credit crisis of 1772-1773, had global repercussions, contributing to the events leading to the American Revolution.

### Disestablishment
Following the Indian Rebellion of 1857, the British government passed the Government of India Act 1858, nationalising the EIC's assets and administrative functions. The company was formally dissolved in 1874, its role fully absorbed by the British Raj.

## Terms
- **Joint-stock company:** A business entity where shares can be bought and sold by investors, pooling capital for trade ventures.
- **Charter:** A formal document granting a company exclusive rights, such as a trade monopoly, from a sovereign authority.
- **Factory:** In this context, a fortified trading post or warehouse, not a manufacturing plant.
- **Sepoy:** A soldier in the service of the EIC, locally recruited but trained and equipped in the European style.
- **Presidency armies:** The three private armies of the EIC, based in Bengal, Madras, and Bombay.
- **Mughal Empire:** The dominant Islamic empire in the Indian subcontinent from the 16th to 19th centuries.
- **Saltpetre:** Potassium nitrate, a key ingredient in gunpowder, a major EIC export.
- **Opium:** A narcotic drug derived from poppies, which the EIC grew in India and illegally sold in China.
- **Letters of marque:** Government authorisation for private ships to engage in warfare and capture enemy vessels during wartime.

## Debates and open questions
- The extent to which the EIC's actions directly caused famines like the Great Bengal Famine of 1770 is a subject of historical analysis, focusing on the impact of its tax and trade policies.
- The company's role in the origins of the American Revolution is debated, particularly how its financial crisis and the resulting Tea Act directly precipitated the Boston Tea Party.
- Historians debate the degree to which the EIC's early relationship with the Mughal Empire was one of vassalage versus a partnership of convenience, and how this shifted to outright domination.
