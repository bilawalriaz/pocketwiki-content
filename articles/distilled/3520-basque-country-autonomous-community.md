# Basque Country (autonomous community)

## Overview
The Basque Country (Euskadi) is an autonomous community in northern Spain, comprising the provinces of Álava, Biscay, and Gipuzkoa. It is a distinct nationality with a unique language (Basque) and a high degree of self-governance, including its own parliament, police, and fiscal system. Its modern political structure and economic prosperity are rooted in a long history of negotiated autonomy within Spain.

## Timeline
- **1199–1200** — Castilian invasion; Basque provinces retain their own laws and institutions.
- **1833** — Provinces formally named "Provincias Vascongadas" in Spanish administrative system.
- **1839 & 1876** — Limited self-government partially, then totally, suppressed in exchange for tax agreements.
- **1936** — Short-lived Statute of Autonomy enforced only in Biscay during the Spanish Civil War.
- **1978** — Spanish Constitution of 1978 acknowledges historical rights and establishes autonomous communities.
- **1979** — Gernika Statute of Autonomy approved by referendum, forming the Basque Autonomous Community.
- **1982** — Statute of Autonomy enacted; Navarre receives a separate chartered statute.
- **2003–2005** — Ibarretxe Plan for enhanced autonomy is approved by Basque Parliament but rejected for debate by Spanish Parliament.
- **2011** — ETA declares a permanent ceasefire.
- **2016** — Basque regional election results in a hung parliament; PNV forms government with PSE.

## Body

### Political Structure and Autonomy
The Basque Country is one of the most decentralized regions in Europe. Its autonomy is defined by the Gernika Statute (1979), which established the Basque Parliament, Government, and its own police force (Ertzaintza). A key feature is the system of *Diputaciones Forales* (provincial councils), which administer each province and collect taxes, coordinating with the central Basque Government. The Spanish Constitution of 1978 acknowledges the region's historical rights, though many Basques feel the statute does not fully reflect their national identity, citing high abstention and "NO" votes in the 1978 constitutional referendum. Political dynamics have been shaped by the legacy of ETA; following its 2011 ceasefire, parties like EH Bildu have entered mainstream politics. The Basque Nationalist Party (PNV) has dominated governance since 1978, except for a 2009–2012 term.

### Language and Demographics
Spanish and Basque are co-official languages. The Basque language experienced severe decline in the 20th century due to immigration and restrictive policies but has seen a revival since the 1982 Statute of Autonomy through education. Sociolinguistic surveys show a steady increase in fluent speakers, from 24.1% in 1991 to 36.2% in 2021, with the highest concentration in Gipuzkoa. The population is 2,227,684, with nearly half living in the Bilbao metropolitan area. Immigration, historically from other Spanish regions and now increasingly from abroad (especially South America), is crucial to demographics. Roman Catholicism is the largest religion, though the region is highly secularized.

### Economy and Infrastructure
The Basque Country is Spain's most economically productive region, with a GDP per capita 30% higher than the Spanish average in 2016. Its economy transitioned from 19th-century steel and shipbuilding to modern manufacturing, aeronautics, finance, and energy. Major companies include BBVA, Iberdrola, and the Mondragón Cooperative Corporation. Unemployment is consistently lower than the Spanish average. Infrastructure includes a strategic road network (AP-8, A-1), a government-owned railway system (Euskal Trenbide Sarea), and three airports, with Bilbao as the main international hub. A high-speed rail network, the "Basque Y," is under construction.

### Culture and Society
Basque culture is renowned for its cuisine, with San Sebastián considered a world gastronomic capital. The region pioneered Spain's *nouvelle cuisine* movement and has a high density of Michelin-starred restaurants. Traditional rural sports (*Herri Kirolak*) like Basque pelota are popular. Football is central to identity, with Athletic Club Bilbao famous for its *cantera* policy of using only locally trained players. The region has produced notable figures in arts, philosophy, and sports, including sculptors Eduardo Chillida and Jorge Oteiza, and footballer Xabi Alonso.

## Terms
- **Autonomous Community**: A first-level political and administrative division of Spain with devolved powers.
- **Gernika Statute**: The foundational law (1979) establishing the political framework of the Basque Autonomous Community.
- **Diputaciones Forales**: Provincial councils with significant administrative and fiscal powers in each Basque province.
- **Lehendakari**: The title for the President of the Basque Autonomous Community.
- **Euskal Herria**: The term for the wider Basque cultural region, including the autonomous community, Navarre, and the French Basque Country.
- **Ertzaintza**: The autonomous police force of the Basque Country.
- **Cantera Policy**: A football club policy of recruiting only players born or trained in a specific region.
- **Pintxos**: A type of Basque tapa, typically a small snack served on bread and held with a skewer.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Scope of Autonomy**: There is ongoing political discussion about the full transfer of powers devolved by the Gernika Statute, with nationalists often attributing delays to concessions made after the 1981 coup attempt.
- **Constitutional Legitimacy**: Many Basques argue they are not bound by the 1978 Spanish Constitution due to high abstention and opposition during its ratification.
- **Territorial Integration**: The statute allows for neighboring Navarre to join the community, a point of contention. The status of the enclaves Treviño and Valle de Villaverde is also a recurring political issue.
- **Police Presence**: Despite ETA's ceasefire, the Basque Country has the highest rate of police per capita in Western Europe, leading to debates about the necessity of Spanish national police forces.
