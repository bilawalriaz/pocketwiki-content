# Time

## Overview

Time is the continuous progression of existence from the past, through the present, and into the future, dictating all forms of action, age, and causality. It is a fundamental quantity used to sequence events, compare durations, and quantify rates of change, and is often called the fourth dimension alongside the three spatial dimensions. Time is measured practically via clocks and calendars (reflecting a 24-hour day and 365-day year linked to Earth's motion) and scientifically from Planck time to billions of years, with measurable time believed to have begun with the Big Bang 13.8 billion years ago. Modern physics, via general relativity, treats time as inextricable from space in the concept of spacetime, where time can be dilated by velocity and gravity, though this is negligible outside extreme conditions like relativistic speeds or black holes.

## Body

### Definition and measurement

Defining time without circularity has eluded scholars, but diverse fields incorporate time into their measuring systems. Traditional definitions involved observing periodic motion (sun, moon, pendulum); modern systems include GPS, Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), and mean solar time. In physics, time is operationally defined as "what a clock reads"—a count of repeating events such as the SI second—though this does not address time's essence. Physicists developed the spacetime continuum, assigning events four coordinates (three space, one time), with the spacetime interval classified as space-like or time-like based on causal structure. Since light's travel time is constant for all observers (demonstrated by the Michelson–Morley experiment), observers agree on time as a causal relation. General relativity does not address quantum-scale time; quantum mechanics treats time as universal and absolute, and reconciling these (the "problem of time") remains unresolved as of 2026.

Chronometry takes two forms: calendars (for periods longer than a day) and clocks (for shorter periods). The SI base unit of time is the second, defined by measuring the electronic transition frequency of caesium atoms. Lunar calendars appeared early (years of 12 or 13 lunar months, 354 or 384 days), requiring intercalation to prevent seasonal drift; lunisolar calendars add a thirteenth month to some years. The Maya developed multiple interrelated calendars, including the Ha'b (18 months of 20 days plus five epagomenal days) and the 260-day Tzolk'in. The Julian calendar (45 BC) allowed solstices to advance by about 11 minutes per year; the Gregorian calendar (1582) corrected this and became the world's most common calendar. The French Republican Calendar (abolished 1806) used a decimal system of ten-hour days.

### History of timekeeping devices

Horology is the study of time-measuring devices, driven by gravity, springs, or electrical power. Sundials use sunlight to cast shadows from a gnomon; Egyptians credited with dividing the day into smaller parts, with the number 12 reflecting lunar cycles and stars counted at night. Water clocks (clepsydra) were the most precise ancient devices, found even in Egyptian tombs, but required manual upkeep. Arab inventors improved water clocks into the Middle Ages; Chinese inventors made the first mechanical clocks with escapements in the 11th century. Incense sticks, candles, and bells marked time in temples, abbeys, and at sea. The hourglass used sand flow and was used in navigation—Magellan used 18 per ship in 1522. Galileo and especially Christiaan Huygens advanced pendulum clocks; Jost Burgi invented the minute hand. Atomic clocks, accurate to seconds in many millions of years, use caesium's electron transition frequency; since 1967, the SI second is defined as 9,192,631,770 cycles of that radiation. A chronometer meets precision standards, initially referring to marine chronometers for determining longitude (first achieved by John Harrison). As of May 2010, the smallest time interval uncertainty in direct measurements is about 12 attoseconds (1.2 × 10⁻¹⁷ seconds).

### Units and standards

The second is the SI base unit; a minute is 60 seconds (occasionally 59 or 61 with leap seconds), an hour is 60 minutes, and a day is usually 24 hours (86,400 seconds), varying with daylight saving time and leap seconds. International Atomic Time (TAI) is the primary international standard; Universal Time (UT1) is mean solar time at 0° longitude, varying from TAI due to Earth's rotation irregularities. Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) approximates UT1, kept within 0.9 second via leap seconds. Time zones offset from UTC, usually by whole hours, with some varying twice yearly for daylight saving. Scientific standards include Terrestrial Time, Geocentric Coordinate Time, and Barycentric Coordinate Time (relativistic scales).

### Philosophy and religion

Many ancient cultures, especially in the East, held cyclical views of time. Hindu philosophy depicts time as the "Kalachakra" (Wheel of Time), with endless cycles of creation, preservation, and destruction; Mayan, Aztec, and Chinese cultures similarly associated cyclical time with astronomical observations. Abrahamic religions (Jewish, Christian, Islamic) generally view time as linear and directional, beginning with God's creation. Ecclesiastes, traditionally attributed to King Solomon (970–928 BC), depicts time as cyclical and beyond human control. The Christian view sees time ending teleologically with the "end time," though some theologians (Augustine, Aquinas) hold that God is outside time. Greek distinguishes Chronos (numeric, chronological time) from Kairos (the right or opportune moment, qualitative and Divine). Kabbalists see time as a paradox and illusion; Advaita Vedanta views time as integral to the phenomenal world, a product of maya, impermanent and subject to change.

In Western philosophy, two contrasting views divide thinkers. Isaac Newton's realist view holds time as a fundamental dimension independent of events (Newtonian time). Gottfried Leibniz and Immanuel Kant's opposing view holds time as part of a fundamental intellectual structure for sequencing events, not a "container" or flowing entity. Kant described time as an a priori intuition allowing comprehension of sense experience. Plato identified time with the period of motion of heavenly bodies; Aristotle defined time as "number of movement in respect of the before and after." Augustine called time a "distention" of the mind (grasping past in memory, present by attention, future by expectation). Henri Bergson posited "Duration" as creativity and memory essential to reality; Martin Heidegger argued "we are time," able to step out of sequential time (ecstasis). Modern debates include the tenseless B-theory (tensed terminology replaceable with tenseless) versus the tense A-theory (future cannot be determined), and Stephen Hawking's concept of imaginary time (finite but without boundaries). Antiphon the Sophist (5th century BC) held time is not a reality but a concept or measure; Parmenides argued time, motion, and change were illusions. J. M. E. McTaggart's 1908 article argued time is self-contradictory. Presentism holds only the present is real; eternalism holds all time is real; the growing block theory holds present and past are real but not the future.

### Physical definition and spacetime

Until Einstein's 1907 reinterpretation, time was considered the same everywhere. Special relativity postulated the constancy and finiteness of light speed, requiring that distances compress and time intervals lengthen for objects in motion relative to an inertial observer. Minkowski spacetime combines three spatial dimensions with one time dimension; events are separated by an invariant interval (space-like, light-like, or time-like). Time-like separated events cannot be simultaneous in any frame; space-like separated events are simultaneous in some frame. Different observers measure different distances and time intervals, but the invariant interval is independent of observer and velocity.

The arrow of time refers to time's apparent one-directionality, despite most physical laws allowing reversal. Acknowledged examples include: the radiative arrow (waves expand, not focus), the entropic arrow (second law of thermodynamics: isolated systems evolve toward disorder), the quantum arrow (irreversibility of measurement in the Copenhagen interpretation), the weak arrow (CP symmetry violation in particle physics), and the cosmological arrow (accelerated expansion after the Big Bang). The relationships between these arrows are hotly debated. Brian Greene theorizes entropy increases symmetrically in both time directions, making our low-entropy universe a statistical aberration, though this is not empirically supported locally.

In classical mechanics, Newton's "relative, apparent, and common time" works for everyday phenomena, but Maxwell's equations (1860s) showed light travels at constant speed, contradicting the fixed reference frame assumption; the Michelson–Morley experiment confirmed this. Einstein's clock synchronization method led to the conclusion that observers in relative motion measure different elapsed times. In spacetime, a spaceship traveling near light speed appears flattened and its clock slow to a stationary observer, while the crew perceives the outside universe as flattened and themselves traveling quickly between distant regions; causality remains unchanged (the past is events that can send light signals to an entity; the future is events to which it can send signals). Time dilation also occurs in stronger gravitational fields (gravitational time dilation); near a black hole's event horizon, an external observer sees a subject appear "frozen" in time. Newtonian descriptions treat time as absolute; relativistic descriptions make observability absolute via the light cone. Time quantization is hypothetical; Planck time (c. 5.4 × 10⁻⁴⁴ seconds) is the unit in Planck units, where current theories are believed to fail. Loop quantum gravity suggests time is quantized if gravity is.

### Travel, perception, and use

Time travel—moving backwards or forwards to different points in time—has never been verified and presents theoretical problems, notably causality violation and temporal paradoxes. The many-worlds interpretation resolves paradoxes via branching timelines; the Novikov self-consistency principle asserts time travel to the past is impossible due to causality constraints. General relativity permits closed timelike curves (e.g., Gödel metric), but these require a globally rotating universe, contradicted by observations, though a slowly rotating model may solve the Hubble tension.

The specious present is the duration wherein perceptions are considered present—an interval, not a durationless instant (term introduced by psychologist E. R. Clay, developed by William James). The brain's time judgment involves the cerebral cortex, cerebellum, and basal ganglia; the suprachiasmatic nuclei handle circadian rhythms. Mental chronometry uses response time to infer cognitive operations. Judgments of time are altered by temporal illusions (kappa effect), age, psychoactive drugs, and hypnosis; impaired in Parkinson's disease and attention deficit disorder. Stimulants lead to overestimating time intervals; depressants have the opposite effect, likely due to neurotransmitter firing rates. Time seems to go faster with age, though the literature remains controversial. Time is conceptualized spatially as a mental timeline (MTL): Western cultures unfold it rightward (past left, future right) due to reading direction; Arabic, Farsi, Urdu, and Hebrew speakers unfold it leftward. Some cultures use allocentric spatialization—the Yupno people of Papua New Guinea gesture downhill for past and uphill for future; the Pormpuraawans of Australia place youngest photos east and oldest west, regardless of facing direction.

Time discipline refers to social and economic rules governing time measurement and observance. Time-use research studies how time is allocated across activities; time use changes with technology (television, Internet), though travel-to-work time remains stable at about 20–30 minutes one-way across many cities. Time management involves estimating task duration and scheduling accordingly. A sequence of events arranges items in chronological order, often with causality (cause precedes effect); uses include stories, history, procedures, and timetables, with applications in machines, law, finance, computer simulation, and electric power transmission.

## Terms

- **Spacetime**: The four-dimensional continuum combining three spatial dimensions with time, central to Einstein's relativity.
- **SI second**: The base unit of time, defined since 1967 as 9,192,631,770 cycles of radiation from a caesium-133 atom's electron transition.
- **Arrow of time**: Time's apparent one-directionality (past to future), despite most physical laws being time-reversible.
- **Time dilation**: The relativistic effect where time passes slower for objects in high-speed motion or strong gravitational fields relative to an external observer.
- **Chronometry**: The science of time measurement, using calendars (long periods) and clocks (short periods).
- **Specious present**: The experienced interval of "now," which is an interval rather than a durationless instant.
- **Mental timeline (MTL)**: The spatial representation of time in the mind, shaped by cultural factors like reading direction.
- **Presentism**: The view that only the present is real; past and future are interpretations, not coexisting dimensions.
- **Leap second**: A one-second step added to UTC to keep it within 0.9 second of UT1 (astronomical time).
- **Closed timelike curve**: A general relativity solution that could theoretically allow backward time travel, requiring a rotating universe.

## Debates and open questions

- **The problem of time**: Reconciling general relativity (time as relative, spacetime-dependent) with quantum mechanics (time as universal and absolute) remains unresolved; no generally accepted theory of quantum general relativity exists as of 2026.
- **Time quantization**: Whether time has a smallest unit (Planck time, c. 5.4 × 10⁻⁴⁴ seconds) is hypothetical; loop quantum gravity suggests yes, but established theories treat time as unquantized.
- **Reality of time**: Philosophers debate whether time is real (Newtonian realism), a mental construct (Leibniz/Kant), or an illusion (Parmenides, McTaggart, some Buddhists); presentism, eternalism, and growing block theory offer competing accounts.
- **Arrows of time**: The relationships between radiative, entropic, quantum, weak, and cosmological arrows are hotly debated; Brian Greene's symmetric-entropy theory lacks empirical local support.
- **Time travel**: Whether backward time travel is possible remains unresolved; closed timelike curves require a rotating universe (contradicted by observations, though a slowly rotating model may solve the Hubble tension), and paradoxes (grandfather paradox) remain theoretically problematic.
- **Age-related time perception**: Whether time speeds up with age is supported by some but remains controversial in the literature.
