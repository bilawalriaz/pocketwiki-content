# Konbaung dynasty

## Overview
The Konbaung dynasty (1752–1885) was the last ruling dynasty of Burma, creating the second-largest empire in Burmese history. It continued administrative reforms from the earlier Toungoo dynasty, laying foundations for the modern state, but ultimately failed to prevent British colonization, which ended the millennium-old monarchy after three Anglo-Burmese Wars.

## Timeline
- **1752** — Alaungpaya founds the dynasty against the Restored Hanthawaddy Kingdom
- **1759** — Alaungpaya reunites Burma and Manipur, expelling foreign arms suppliers
- **1767** — Hsinbyushin captures Ayutthaya after seven years of war with Siam
- **1765–1769** — Burma defeats four Qing Chinese invasions
- **1784–1817** — Bodawpaya acquires Arakan, Manipur, and Assam, bordering British India
- **1824–1826** — First Anglo-Burmese War: Burma cedes Arakan, Manipur, Assam, and Tenasserim
- **1852** — Second Anglo-Burmese War breaks out during King Pagan's reign
- **1857** — Mindon Min inaugurates cash taxes and standardized silver coinage
- **1872** — Mindon hosts the Fifth Great Buddhist Synod at Mandalay
- **1885** — Third Anglo-Burmese War: British annex Burma, exiling King Thibaw

## Body

### Establishment and Expansion
Founded in 1752 by village chief Alaungpaya to oppose the Restored Hanthawaddy Kingdom, the dynasty reunited Burma and Manipur by 1759. His son Hsinbyushin destroyed Ayutthaya in 1767 and successfully defended against four Qing invasions (1765–1769). Burma and Siam fought repeatedly until 1855, exchanging Tenasserim and Lan Na.

### Western Expansion and British Wars
The Konbaung kings expanded westward, acquiring Arakan (1784), Manipur (1814), and Assam (1817), creating an ill-defined border with British India. The British defeated Burma in the First Anglo-Burmese War (1824–1826), forcing territorial cessions and a large indemnity. Internal instability followed, including King Tharrawaddy's 1837 usurpation and King Pagan's executions. The Second Anglo-Burmese War (1852) occurred under Pagan, who was succeeded by the progressive Mindon.

### Reforms and Modernization
Mindon and Crown Prince Kanaung established state-owned factories for modern weaponry, though these proved costly and ineffective. The dynasty extended administrative reforms, tightened lowland control, reduced Shan chiefs' hereditary privileges, and instituted commercial reforms. In 1857, a full cash tax and salary system was introduced with standardized silver coinage. Mindon attempted tax reform but local elites increased burdens. He hosted the Fifth Great Buddhist Synod (1872) and avoided annexation in 1875 by ceding the Karenni States. However, reforms were uneven and insufficient against British colonialism.

### Fall of the Dynasty
Mindon died without naming a successor; Thibaw, a lesser prince, was placed on the throne by Queen Hsinbyumashin and her daughter Supayalat. Under Supayalat's direction, Thibaw massacred potential rivals. The British, alarmed by French consolidation in Indochina, annexed Burma in the Third Anglo-Burmese War (1885). The king and royal family were exiled to India, ending the monarchy. The annexation was announced as a New Year gift to Queen Victoria on 1 January 1886.

### Government and Administration
The Konbaung dynasty was an absolute monarchy. Kings aspired to be Chakravartins (Universal Monarchs) with their own mandala (field of power). The kingdom was divided into provinces (myo) administered by governors (Myosa), subdivided into towns and municipalities. Peripheral coastal provinces had viceroys (Myowun) with full powers. Tributary fiefdoms (Shan, Palaung, Kachin, Manipuri) were autonomous in practice, with princes pledging allegiance through rituals.

Central administration involved several royal agencies: the Hluttaw (Council of State) with legislative, ministerial, and judicial functions; the Byedaik (Privy Council) handling court affairs; and the Shwedaik (Royal Treasury) maintaining archives and records. The royal court was centered on the king, with an extended family competing for influence, often leading to succession crises and massacres.

### Royal Rituals and Culture
Court life combined Buddhist, Hindu, and animist traditions. Brahmins performed essential rituals including abhiseka (consecration ceremonies) where water was poured on the king's head. The coronation (Rajabhiseka) involved elaborate preparations, anointing with regalia, and the king's oath. Other significant ceremonies included the Royal Ploughing Ceremony to ensure rainfall, the Obeisance Ceremony where tributary princes paid homage, and ancestor worship at the Zetawunsaung. Foundation sacrifices (myosade) involved burying victims at city corners and gates to propitiate guardian spirits, though this practice may have been discontinued by Mindon's time.

### Society and Culture
Konbaung society was highly stratified into four classes: rulers, ritualists, merchants, and commoners, plus slaves. Sumptuary laws (yazagaing) dictated housing, clothing, and ceremonies based on rank. The Burmese language and culture predominated the Irrawaddy valley by 1830. Captives from military campaigns enriched society with new skills; Manipuri captives formed elite cavalry and artillery units. A small community of Europeans, including missionaries and merchants, lived in Burma. Literacy was high for the era, with half of adult males literate. Siamese captives influenced Burmese theatre and dance, leading to adaptations of the Ramayana and other epics. Architecture saw planned cities like Mandalay, with intricate wooden monasteries and stupa developments.

## Terms
- **Chakravartin**: Universal Monarch concept in Southeast Asian kingship
- **Mandala**: A field of power or political sphere in the Jambudipa universe
- **Hluttaw**: Council of State with legislative, ministerial, and judicial functions
- **Byedaik**: Privy Council handling court internal affairs
- **Shwedaik**: Royal Treasury maintaining state archives and records
- **Abhiseka**: Consecration rituals involving water pouring to reinforce the king's role
- **Rajabhiseka**: The coronation ceremony, the most important royal ritual
- **Myosa**: Provincial governors administering the kingdom's divisions
- **Sawbwa**: Title for tributary princes, especially Shan lords
- **Yazagaing**: Sumptuary laws dictating consumption and behavior based on social rank

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent and effectiveness of Konbaung administrative reforms in preventing British colonization remains debated among historians.
- Whether foundation sacrifices (myosade) were actually practiced at Mandalay's founding in 1857 is disputed, with contemporary records suggesting alternatives like buried oil jars.
- The precise nature and scale of King Pagan's executions (sources vary from thousands to 6,000) is uncertain.
- The succession after the monarchy's abolition is unclear, with competing claims among descendants of Thibaw's daughters.
