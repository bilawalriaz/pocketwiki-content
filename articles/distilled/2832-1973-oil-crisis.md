# 1973 oil crisis

## Overview
The 1973 oil embargo was an economic weapon deployed by the Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries (OAPEC) against nations supporting Israel during the Yom Kippur War. It caused the first major global oil shock, quadrupling prices and triggering a worldwide recession that ended the post-WWII economic boom. The crisis reshaped global politics, accelerated energy diversification, and permanently altered the automotive industry.

## Timeline

- **Oct 19, 1973** — U.S. requests $2.2 billion in emergency aid for Israel; OAPEC announces total embargo on the U.S. and Netherlands. |
- **Nov 5, 1973** — Arab producers announce a 25% output cut.
- **Dec 25, 1973** — OPEC announces a price increase to $11.65/barrel. |
- **Jan 18, 1974** — Israel signs a withdrawal agreement for the Sinai Peninsula.
- **Mar 17, 1974** — Arab oil ministers (except Libya) announce the end of the U.S. embargo.

## Body

### Background and Causes
The embargo emerged from the long-running Arab-Israeli conflict and the growing power of oil-producing nations. Following the 1967 Six-Day War, the U.S. had become increasingly dependent on oil imports as domestic production peaked in 1970. The Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC), founded in 1960, gained strength in the early 1970s. The "Nixon Shock" of 1971, which ended the dollar's convertibility to gold, eroded the real income of oil producers, leading to price hikes. In 1973, Egyptian President Anwar Sadat and Saudi King Faisal secretly agreed to use the "oil weapon" in any future conflict with Israel.

### The War and the Embargo
When Egypt and Syria attacked Israel on October 6, 1973, Saudi Arabia initially pushed for a modest 5% monthly production cut. However, on October 18, President Nixon requested a massive $2.2 billion arms package for Israel, which enraged King Faisal. He perceived this as duplicity after promising a more "evenhanded" U.S. policy. On October 19, Faisal announced a total embargo on oil exports to the United States. Most other Arab producers followed, with production cuts eventually reaching 25% of September levels. The embargo was lifted on March 18, 1974, after U.S. diplomacy secured Israeli withdrawals from the Sinai.

### Immediate Effects and Global Impact
The embargo caused oil prices to rise from $3 to nearly $12 per barrel globally. The U.S. experienced gasoline shortages, long lines, and rationing, while the economy entered a severe recession with high inflation and unemployment (stagflation). The crisis ended the "long summer" of post-war prosperity. In Europe, the embargo was uneven; the Netherlands faced a total ban, while Britain and France were largely spared. Japan, importing 90% of its oil from the Middle East, was hit hard and quickly shifted its foreign policy to a more pro-Arab stance. The crisis also contributed to political instability, influencing events like the 1974 Carnation Revolution in Portugal and the fall of South Vietnam in 1975.

### Long-Term Consequences
The oil shock prompted a permanent shift in global energy policy. Western nations invested in alternative energy, conservation, and strategic reserves (e.g., the U.S. Department of Energy was created in 1977). The automotive industry was transformed, with demand shifting from American "gas guzzlers" to smaller, fuel-efficient Japanese and European cars. OPEC's power peaked and then declined in the 1980s due to new oil sources (North Sea, Alaska), conservation, and internal divisions. The crisis also led to the "petrodollar recycling" system, where OPEC surpluses were invested in Western capital markets.

## Terms
- **OAPEC**: Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries, the group that implemented the embargo.
- **OPEC**: Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries, a cartel of oil-producing nations founded in 1960 to coordinate policy.
- **Oil Weapon**: The strategic use of oil embargoes and production cuts as a tool of political coercion.
- **Stagflation**: An economic condition of stagnant growth combined with high inflation, a key result of the oil shock.
- **Nixon Shock**: President Nixon's 1971 actions that ended the dollar's direct convertibility to gold, contributing to oil price instability.
- **Petrodollar Recycling**: The process by which OPEC nations invested their oil revenue surpluses in Western financial systems.
- **Strategic Petroleum Reserve**: A U.S. government stockpile of crude oil established in 1975 to mitigate future supply disruptions.
- **Corporate Average Fuel Economy (CAFE)**: U.S. standards enacted in 1975 to improve the average fuel economy of cars and light trucks.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate the embargo's effectiveness. Most conclude it failed its primary political objective, as Israel did not withdraw to its pre-1967 borders. Historian Roy Licklider argued the embargo was a failure because targeted nations did not change their policies. However, Daniel Yergin contends it "remade the international economy." Robert Lacey noted that while the embargo itself was not decisive, its interaction with other forces had a massive multiplier effect. The long-term economic and political consequences, such as the rise of political Islam and the decline of OPEC's dominance, remain subjects of analysis.
