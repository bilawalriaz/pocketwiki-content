# Rome

## Overview

Rome, the capital and most populous city of Italy, is a major human settlement with a continuous history spanning over 28 centuries. Located in the central-western Italian Peninsula along the Tiber River, it is uniquely home to Vatican City, the world's smallest independent country. Rome is widely regarded as a cradle of Western civilization and the center of the Catholic Church, having served as the capital of the Roman Kingdom, Republic, and Empire, and later as the seat of the Papal States and the Kingdom of Italy.

## Body

### Early History and Foundation

Rome's origins are steeped in myth, with the legendary tale of Romulus and Remus, twins suckled by a she-wolf, dating to 753 BC. Archaeological evidence suggests human habitation in the area as early as 14,000 years ago, with settlements on the Palatine Hill developing into a city by the mid-eighth century BC through a process called synoecism—the gradual aggregation of villages. The name "Roma" may derive from the Tiber River (Rumon), the Etruscan word for "teat," or the Greek word for "strength," though its exact etymology remains uncertain.

### Monarchy and Republic

According to tradition, Rome was ruled by seven kings over 244 years, beginning with Romulus and ending with the Etruscan tyrant Lucius Tarquinius Superbus. In 509 BC, the Romans expelled the last king and established a republic governed by two annually elected consuls. The republic was marked by struggles between patricians and plebeians, and by expansion through wars against the Etruscans, Latins, and other Italic peoples. The Punic Wars (264–146 BC) against Carthage and the Macedonian Wars established Roman hegemony over the Mediterranean. Internal conflicts, including the rise of the optimates and populares factions, led to civil wars and the eventual rise of Julius Caesar, whose assassination in 44 BC led to the rise of Augustus and the end of the republic.

### Empire and Decline

Augustus established the principate in 27 BC, marking the beginning of the Roman Empire. The empire reached its zenith under Trajan in the second century AD, with territory stretching from Britain to Mesopotamia. The Great Fire of Rome during Nero's reign (64 AD) led to the persecution of Christians. The Crisis of the Third Century (AD 235–284) weakened the empire through civil wars and economic collapse. Diocletian's reforms in 285 divided the empire into eastern and western halves, and Constantine the Great legalized Christianity in 313 AD via the Edict of Milan. The Western Roman Empire declined after the death of Theodosius I in 395 AD, culminating in the sack of Rome by the Visigoths in 410 AD and the Vandals in 455 AD. The deposition of Romulus Augustus in 476 AD marked the traditional end of the Western Roman Empire.

### Middle Ages

After the fall of the Western Empire, Rome came under the control of the Papal States, established in 756 AD when Pepin the Short granted the Pope temporal jurisdiction. The city became a focal point of medieval politics, with the papacy often clashing with secular rulers. The Western Schism (1377–1418) divided the Church, and the Avignon Papacy (1309–1377) briefly relocated the papacy to France. The Renaissance began in Rome in the 15th century under popes like Nicholas V, who initiated a massive architectural and artistic program. The city became a center of Renaissance art and culture, with masterpieces by Michelangelo, Raphael, and others. The Counter-Reformation in the 16th century reinforced the Catholic Church's influence, and Rome continued to grow as a cultural and religious center.

### Early Modern and Contemporary Period

The Roman Republic was briefly established in 1798 during the French Revolution, but the Papal States were restored in 1800. Napoleon annexed Rome as a French department from 1809 to 1814. The unification of Italy in 1861 led to Rome becoming the capital of the Kingdom of Italy in 1871, though the Pope refused to recognize the Italian state until the Lateran Treaty of 1929. Rome experienced rapid growth in the 20th century, hosting the 1960 Summer Olympics and becoming a major center of cinema with Cinecittà Studios. Today, Rome is a global city, home to numerous international organizations, including UN agencies, and remains a major tourist destination.

## Terms

- **Synoecism**: The gradual aggregation of several villages into a single city, as theorized in Rome's early development.
- **Principate**: The political system established by Augustus in 27 BC, marking the beginning of the Roman Empire, characterized by a diarchy between the emperor and the Senate.
- **Papal States**: The territories in central Italy under the direct political control of the Pope, established in 756 AD and lasting until 1870.
- **Rione**: One of the 22 traditional administrative divisions of Rome's historic center, originating from the 14 regions of Augustan Rome.
- **Comune Speciale**: A special municipality in Italy with unique administrative status; Rome is designated as "Roma Capitale."
- **Ab Urbe Condita**: Latin for "from the founding of the City," a chronological system used by the Romans counting years from the legendary founding of Rome in 753 BC.
- **Urbs Aeterna**: Latin for "Eternal City," a poetic name for Rome first used by the poet Tibullus in the 1st century BC.
- **Caput Mundi**: Latin for "Capital of the World," a title reflecting Rome's central role in the ancient world.
- **Dominate**: The authoritarian political system introduced by Diocletian in 284 AD, replacing the Principate, where the emperor ruled as an absolute monarch.
- **ZTL (Zona a Traffico Limitato)**: Limited Traffic Zone, areas in Rome's historic center where vehicle access is restricted to reduce congestion.

## Debates and Open Questions

Several historical and archaeological questions about Rome remain debated among scholars. The exact date and nature of Rome's founding are contested, with the traditional date of 753 BC based on legend rather than definitive evidence. The population size of ancient Rome is highly speculative, with estimates ranging from 450,000 to over 1 million during the Augustan period, depending on methodologies used (grain distribution, aqueduct capacity, etc.). The etymology of "Roma" remains uncertain, with multiple theories proposed but no consensus. The extent of damage caused by the sack of Rome in 410 AD and 455 AD is debated, with some scholars arguing the impact was exaggerated. The transition from the Roman Empire to the Middle Ages in Rome is also a subject of scholarly discussion, particularly regarding the continuity of urban life and administrative structures.
