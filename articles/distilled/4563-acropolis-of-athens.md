# Acropolis of Athens

## Overview
The Acropolis of Athens is an ancient citadel on a rocky outcrop above Athens, Greece, containing the remains of several architecturally and historically significant buildings, most famously the Parthenon. It is a critical symbol of Classical Greece and Western civilization, with its major structures built during the 5th century BC under the leadership of Pericles.

## Timeline
- **c. 4th millennium BC** — Earliest evidence of habitation on the hill.
- **Late Bronze Age** — A Mycenaean megaron palace and Cyclopean circuit wall are constructed.
- **570–550 BC** — The Hekatompedon (a temple to Athena Polias) is built.
- **529–520 BC** — The Old Temple of Athena (Arkhaios Neōs) is built.
- **c. 500 BC** — Construction begins on the Older Parthenon (Pre-Parthenon).
- **480 BC** — The Persians sack Athens, destroying the Older Parthenon and other temples.
- **460–430 BC** — The Periclean building program constructs the Parthenon, Propylaea, Erechtheion, and Temple of Athena Nike.
- **437–432 BC** — Mnesicles builds the Propylaea, the monumental western gateway.
- **421–406 BC** — The Erechtheion is constructed.
- **1687** — Venetian bombardment severely damages the Parthenon, which was being used as a gunpowder magazine.
- **1975** — The modern Acropolis Restoration Project begins.

## Body

### Early Settlement and Archaic Period
The Acropolis hill was inhabited from the Neolithic period. A Mycenaean palace and a massive defensive wall, the Cyclopean circuit, were built in the Late Bronze Age. During the Archaic period (7th–6th centuries BC), the site saw political coups and the construction of early temples, including the Hekatompedon (c. 570–550 BC) and the Old Temple of Athena (529–520 BC). Around 500 BC, the Older Parthenon was begun to replace the Hekatompedon, but its construction was halted by the Persian invasion of 480 BC, which destroyed the unfinished building and other structures on the hill.

### The Periclean Building Program
Following the Persian Wars, the Athenian statesman Pericles initiated a major rebuilding campaign during Athens' "Golden Age" (460–430 BC). This program, led by the sculptor Phidias and architects Ictinus and Callicrates, created the site's most iconic structures. The Parthenon, dedicated to Athena Parthenos, was the central temple. The Propylaea, a monumental gateway, was built by Mnesicles. The elegant Erechtheion, with its unique Caryatid porch, and the small Temple of Athena Nike were also constructed. A colossal bronze statue of Athena Promachos by Phidias once dominated the site.

### Later History and Modern Restoration
The Acropolis was repaired and added to during the Hellenistic and Roman periods. Its buildings were later repurposed: the Parthenon became a Byzantine church, then a Frankish cathedral, and finally an Ottoman mosque. A catastrophic explosion in 1687, when Venetian artillery hit Ottoman gunpowder stores in the Parthenon, caused severe damage. After Greek independence in the 19th century, many later additions were removed to "restore" the classical appearance. The modern Acropolis Restoration Project, begun in 1975, uses advanced techniques to stabilize and reassemble the ancient monuments, prioritizing the use of original materials.

## Terms
- **Acropolis**: From Greek *akron* ("highest point") and *polis* ("city"); a generic term for a fortified hilltop citadel, with the Athens example being the most famous.
- **Cyclopean wall**: A fortification made of massive, irregular stone blocks, named by later Greeks who believed only the mythical Cyclopes could have built it.
- **Hekatompedon**: "Hundred-footed"; the name for an early temple on the site, predating the Parthenon.
- **Propylaea**: The monumental gateway to the Acropolis.
- **Parthenon**: The main temple on the Acropolis, dedicated to Athena Parthenos (Athena the Virgin).
- **Erechtheion**: A temple on the Acropolis known for its complex plan and the Porch of the Caryatids.
- **Caryatids**: Sculpted female figures serving as architectural support columns, famously used on the Erechtheion.
- **Anastylosis**: A restoration technique that reassembles original architectural fragments, using new material sparingly for structural support.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that the exact purpose of the Hekatompedon—whether it replaced an older temple or just a sacred precinct—is unknown. The fate of the Old Temple of Athena (Arkhaios Neōs) after its probable reconstruction in 454 BC is uncertain; it may have burned down in 406/405 BC, but the 2nd-century AD traveler Pausanias does not mention it. The original interior plan of the Erechtheion is little known, as it was destroyed by fire in the 1st century BC.
