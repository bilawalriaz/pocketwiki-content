# Alloy

## Overview

An alloy is a mixture of chemical elements in which at least one is a metallic element, with the atoms joined by metallic bonding rather than covalent bonds. Alloys typically have properties—such as strength, hardness, or corrosion resistance—that differ from those of their pure constituent elements, which is why the vast majority of commercial metals are alloys. They are classified by atomic arrangement (substitutional or interstitial) and by structure (homogeneous, heterogeneous, or intermetallic), and they underpin applications from steel in buildings and surgical tools to titanium in aerospace and beryllium-copper in non-sparking tools.

## Timeline

- **c. 10,000 years ago** — Humans in Anatolia (Turkey) learn to smelt metals such as copper and tin from ore.
- **c. 2500 BC** — People begin alloying tin and copper to form bronze, much harder than its ingredients.
- **c. 1450 BC** — Earliest examples of pewter appear in ancient Egypt.
- **c. 1200 BC** — Pig iron (hard, brittle iron-carbon alloy) is produced in China.
- **c. 800 AD** — Pewter arrives in Japan; Japanese begin folding bloomery-steel and cast iron for swords.
- **Middle Ages** — Blister steel introduced; blast furnace reaches Europe, enabling higher-volume pig iron production.
- **1740** — Benjamin Huntsman melts blister steel in a crucible, creating the first mass-production process for tool steel.
- **1858** — Henry Bessemer develops a steel-making process by blowing hot air through liquid pig iron.
- **1882** — Robert Hadfield produces mangalloy (c. 12% manganese steel), the first commercially viable alloy-steel.
- **1900** — Taylor and White develop tungsten carbide steel, replacing Mushet's earlier high-speed steel.
- **1906** — Alfred Wilm discovers precipitation hardening alloys (duralumin).
- **1912** — Krupp Ironworks develops the first stainless steel (21% chromium, 7% nickel).

## Body

### Characteristics

An alloy is an impure substance (admixture) that retains the characteristics of a metal. It is made by mixing two or more elements, at least one being a metal—the primary or base metal—into which other constituents dissolve when molten. Mechanical properties often differ greatly from those of the constituents: soft aluminium and copper combine into a much stronger alloy, while adding small amounts of carbon to iron trades ductility for the greater strength of steel. Adding chromium to steel creates corrosion-resistant stainless steel; adding silicon produces silicon steel with altered electrical characteristics.

Not all molten metals are miscible; lithium, magnesium, or silver are almost completely insoluble in pure iron, and each soluble constituent has a saturation point (iron can stably hold a maximum of 6.67% carbon, forming cementite). If constituents remain soluble when solid, the alloy forms a homogeneous solid solution (a single phase of identical crystals). If they become insoluble on cooling, they separate into a heterogeneous microstructure of different phases. In some alloys, rapid cooling first creates a supersaturated homogeneous phase, from which atoms later separate to form a reinforcing second phase.

Some alloys occur naturally: electrum (silver and gold) and meteoric iron-nickel alloys. Bronze (tin and copper) was among the first human-made alloys, much stronger and harder than either component. Ancient alloys also included pewter, brass, and pig iron. Modern steel comes in many forms—carbon steels (mild or spring steel) and alloy steels with chromium, molybdenum, vanadium, or nickel (e.g., high-speed or tool steel). Manganese is usually added to remove impurities like phosphorus, sulfur, and oxygen. Most alloys, however, were not created until the 1900s, including aluminium, titanium, nickel, and magnesium alloys, and modern superalloys like incoloy, inconel, and hastelloy.

Impurities are undesirable elements introduced from base metals or alloying elements. Sulfur in steel forms brittle iron sulfide, creating weak spots; lithium, sodium, and calcium are common impurities in aluminium alloys. Oxygen readily combines with metals at alloying temperatures, so fluxes, chemical additives, or extractive metallurgy methods are used to remove excess impurities.

### Theory

The most common alloying process is heating the base metal beyond its melting point and dissolving solutes into the molten liquid, even if the solute's melting point is far higher. Titanium, for example, is a strong solvent but readily absorbs gases and burns in nitrogen, so it must be melted in vacuum induction-heating with water-cooled copper crucibles. Carbon's very high melting point (achievable only under high pressure) made liquid-state alloying impossible for ancients, so alternative methods were used: interstitial alloying in gaseous state (blast furnace for pig iron, nitriding, case hardening, cementation for blister steel) or solid-state methods (pattern welding, shear steel, crucible steel) using solid-state diffusion.

Differences in atomic size create internal stresses in the crystal lattice, often enhancing properties. Electrical and thermal conductivity of alloys is usually lower than pure metals. Physical properties like density and Young's modulus may not differ greatly, but engineering properties like tensile strength, ductility, and shear strength can differ substantially. Larger atoms exert compressive force on neighbors; smaller atoms exert tensile force, helping resist deformation. Impurities in semiconducting ferromagnetic alloys can lead to different properties, as first predicted by White, Hogan, Suhl, Tian Abrie and Nakamura.

Unlike pure metals, most alloys have a melting range (a slush of solid and liquid) rather than a single melting point. The temperature at which melting begins is the solidus; when melting is complete, the liquidus. Some alloy proportions form a eutectic mixture or peritectic composition with a unique, low melting point and no slush transition.

### Heat treatment

Alloying elements induce hardness, toughness, ductility, or other properties. Work hardening creates permanent defects in the crystal structure through plastic deformation (hammering, bending, extruding). Nearly all metals can be softened by annealing, which recrystallizes the alloy and repairs defects, but fewer can be hardened by controlled heating and cooling. Aluminium, copper, magnesium, titanium, and nickel alloys can be strengthened by heat treatment, but few respond as strongly as steel.

Iron undergoes an allotropic change (a change in atomic arrangement) at about 820 °C (1,500 °F) or more, depending on carbon content, allowing smaller carbon atoms to enter the interstices of the iron crystal, forming a homogeneous phase called austenite. Slow cooling lets carbon diffuse out, precipitating iron carbide (Fe3C, cementite) between pure iron crystals (ferrite), producing a heterogeneous, soft steel. Rapid cooling traps carbon in solution via a diffusionless (martensite) transformation, deforming the crystals and leaving them very hard but brittle.

Most heat-treatable alloys are precipitation hardening alloys, which depend on diffusion of alloying elements. Heated to form a solution and then cooled quickly, they become softer than normal, then harden as they age, with solutes precipitating over time into intermetallic phases within the same crystal. Discovered by Alfred Wilm in 1906, these alloys (e.g., certain aluminium, titanium, and copper alloys) soften when quenched and harden over time. Wilm, seeking to harden aluminium for machine-gun cartridge cases, found that an aluminium-copper-magnesium alloy hardened when left to age at room temperature. Though unexplained until 1919, duralumin became one of the first "age hardening" alloys, used in the first Zeppelins and later widely in aircraft construction.

### Mechanisms

Two mechanisms form alloys: atom exchange and the interstitial mechanism. When atoms are relatively similar in size, atom exchange occurs—some atoms in the metallic crystals are substituted with atoms of the other constituent, forming a substitutional alloy (e.g., bronze and brass, where copper atoms are substituted with tin or zinc). When one atom is much smaller, it cannot substitute but becomes trapped in interstitial sites between the atoms of the crystal matrix, forming an interstitial alloy (e.g., steel, where small carbon atoms fit into iron interstices). Stainless steel combines both mechanisms: carbon fits into interstices, while nickel and chromium substitute for some iron atoms.

### History and examples

**Meteoric iron.** The first human use of alloys was meteoric iron, a naturally occurring nickel-iron alloy and the main constituent of iron meteorites. Used as found, it could be forged from a red heat into tools, weapons, and nails, or cold-hammered into knives and arrowheads. It was rare, valuable, and difficult to work.

**Bronze and brass.** Native copper, silver, gold, and platinum were used since Neolithic times. Around 10,000 years ago in Anatolia, humans learned to smelt copper and tin from ore. Early bronze used copper and arsenic (toxic to workers); around 2500 BC, tin and copper were alloyed to form much harder bronze. Tin was rare, found mostly in Great Britain. In the Middle East, copper was alloyed with zinc to form brass. Ancient civilizations mapped properties like hardness, toughness, and melting point under various conditions, developing much of modern alloy phase diagram information. Chinese Qin dynasty arrowheads (c. 200 BC) combined a hard bronze-head with a softer bronze-tang to prevent dulling and breaking.

**Amalgams.** Mercury, smelted from cinnabar for thousands of years, dissolves many metals (gold, silver, tin) to form amalgams—soft paste or liquid alloys at ambient temperature. Used since 200 BC in China for gilding armor and mirrors, and by ancient Romans for gilding, the amalgam was applied as a paste and heated until mercury vaporized, leaving the precious metal. Mercury was also used in mining to extract gold and silver from ores.

**Precious metals.** Ancient Egypt and Mycenae alloyed gold with copper for red-gold or iron for burgundy-gold; gold was often found naturally alloyed with silver. Copper was added to silver to make sterling silver for dishes and silverware. Around 250 BC, Archimedes was commissioned by the King of Syracuse to check the purity of a gold crown, leading to his "Eureka" discovery of Archimedes' principle.

**Pewter.** Pewter covers tin-based alloys. Pure tin is too soft for most purposes, so workers alloyed it with lead, antimony, bismuth, or copper, individually or together, for items from dishes and surgical tools to jewelry. Earliest examples come from ancient Egypt (c. 1450 BC). Pewter was widespread across Europe and reached Japan around 800 AD for ceremonial vessels and Shinto shrine items.

**Iron.** The first known smelting of iron began in Anatolia around 1800 BC via the bloomery process, producing soft but ductile wrought iron. By 800 BC, the technology had spread to Europe, arriving in Japan around 700 AD. Pig iron was produced in China as early as 1200 BC but reached Europe only in the Middle Ages. Crucible steel appeared around 300 BC, and pattern welding (c. 1st century AD) laminated steels to balance extreme properties. Around 700 AD, Japanese swordsmiths folded bloomery-steel and cast iron in alternating layers, using clay fluxes to remove slag, producing one of the purest ancient steel alloys. Steel (iron with ~1% carbon) was a byproduct of the bloomery process; heat treatment to modify hardness was known by 1100 BC. Blister steel (heating wrought iron in charcoal) was slow and non-homogeneous until Huntsman's 1740 crucible process. The blast furnace enabled higher-volume pig iron production; puddling (stirring molten pig iron in air to oxidize carbon) was used in China since the first century and in Europe from the 1700s. Bessemer's 1858 process of blowing hot air through liquid pig iron enabled the first large-scale steel manufacture.

The term "alloy steel" usually refers to steels with other elements (vanadium, molybdenum, cobalt) in sufficient amounts to alter properties. Steel-making methods were closely guarded secrets; Sheffield, England, barred visitors to deter industrial espionage, and almost no metallurgical information existed until 1860. Steel was not generally considered an alloy until the decades between 1930 and 1970, due to work by William Chandler Roberts-Austen, Adolf Martens, and Edgar Bain. After Huntsman's crucible steel, manganese (as spiegeleisen) was added to remove impurities. In 1882, Robert Hadfield produced mangalloy (~12% manganese), the first commercially viable alloy-steel, followed by silicon steel. Robert Forester Mushet added tungsten to create the first high-speed steel; Taylor and White's 1900 tungsten carbide steel doubled the tungsten and added chromium and vanadium. The Wright brothers used chromium-nickel steel for their 1903 airplane engine crankshaft; Henry Ford used vanadium steels in the 1908 Model T. In 1912, Krupp Ironworks developed the first stainless steel (21% chromium, 7% nickel). A typical modern alloy is 304 grade stainless steel (18/8), broadly 74% iron, 18% chromium, and 8% nickel, where chromium and nickel add strength and corrosion resistance.

**Others.** Most metals were not discovered until the 19th century due to high reactivity. Humphry Davy proposed extracting aluminium from bauxite in 1807 using an electric arc; first sales of pure aluminium reached the market by 1855. Early extraction processes produced unintended aluminium-copper alloys ("aluminium bronze"), which were stronger and harder than pure aluminium but found little practical use until the Wright brothers used an aluminium alloy for the first airplane engine in 1903. Between 1865 and 1910, extraction processes for chromium, vanadium, tungsten, iridium, cobalt, and molybdenum were discovered. After 1910, alloy research became an industrial effort as aircraft and automotive industries grew, developing magnesium alloys for pistons and wheels, pot metal for levers, and aluminium alloys for airframes. The Doehler Die Casting Co. of Toledo, Ohio produced Brastil, a high tensile corrosion-resistant bronze alloy.

### Industry

For precision alloy production, intermediate "master alloy" ingots or powder are produced with a consistent mixture of desired metal content, then combined to produce the final alloy. In the aluminium alloy industry, master alloy is commonly added to adjust the metal grain of the final alloy.

## Terms

- **Base metal**: The primary metal in an alloy, whose name may also name the alloy.
- **Substitutional alloy**: An alloy where atoms of similar size substitute for atoms in the crystal matrix (e.g., bronze, brass).
- **Interstitial alloy**: An alloy where much smaller atoms fit into spaces between the matrix atoms (e.g., steel).
- **Phase**: A homogeneous structure of identical crystals within an alloy.
- **Solidus/Liquidus**: The temperatures at which melting begins and is just complete, respectively.
- **Eutectic mixture**: A particular alloy proportion with a unique, low melting point and no solid/liquid slush transition.
- **Austenite**: A homogeneous crystalline phase of iron with carbon in solution, formed at high temperature.
- **Martensite**: A hard, brittle phase formed by rapid cooling that traps carbon in solution via a diffusionless transformation.
- **Precipitation hardening**: Heat treatment where solutes precipitate over time to form intermetallic phases, hardening the alloy (e.g., duralumin).
- **Master alloy**: An intermediate alloy ingot or powder with a consistent metal content, used to produce final alloys.

## Debates and open questions

Additionally, the explanation for precipitation hardening (age hardening) was not provided until 1919, well after Wilm's 1906 discovery, indicating an open scientific question that was later resolved.
