# Sojourner Truth

## Overview
Sojourner Truth (c. 1797–1883) was an American abolitionist and activist for African-American civil rights, women's rights, and temperance. Born into slavery in New York, she escaped and became a powerful orator and advocate, famously challenging the intersecting oppressions of race and gender. Her life and speeches, particularly "Ain't I a Woman?", made her a central figure in 19th-century social reform movements.

## Timeline
- **c. 1797** — Born Isabella Bomefree into slavery in Swartekill, New York.
- **1826** — Escapes to freedom with her infant daughter, Sophia.
- **1828** — Wins a court case to recover her son Peter, becoming one of the first Black women to sue a white man successfully.
- **1843** — Changes her name to Sojourner Truth, feeling called by God to preach.
- **1850** — Her narrative, *The Narrative of Sojourner Truth*, is published.
- **1851** — Delivers her famous extemporaneous speech at the Ohio Women's Rights Convention in Akron, later known as "Ain't I a Woman?".
- **1864** — Meets President Abraham Lincoln at the White House.
- **1870-1877** — Unsuccessfully lobbies the federal government for land grants for formerly enslaved people.
- **1883** — Dies in Battle Creek, Michigan.
- **2009** — A memorial bust is unveiled in the U.S. Capitol Visitor Center, the first for an African American woman.

## Body

### Early Life and Slavery
Sojourner Truth was born Isabella Bomefree in Swartekill, New York, to James and Elizabeth Bomefree, enslaved people captured from West Africa. Her first language was Dutch. After her father's death in 1806, she was sold multiple times, enduring harsh treatment and rape by her owner, John Dumont. Around 1815, she fell in love with a slave named Robert from a neighboring farm, but their relationship was forbidden, and Robert was savagely beaten and later died. She eventually married an enslaved man named Thomas and had five children.

### Freedom and Early Activism
In 1799, New York began legislating the abolition of slavery, which was completed in 1827. Dumont had promised Truth freedom but reneged. In late 1826, she escaped with her infant daughter Sophia, leaving her other children behind as they were not yet legally free. She found refuge with the Van Wagenen family in New Paltz. In 1828, with their help, she sued for the return of her illegally sold son Peter and won, a landmark case. After gaining freedom, she became a Christian, joined the John Street Methodist Church in New York City, and briefly became involved with a religious sect led by Robert Matthews (Prophet Matthias) before leaving in 1835.

### Public Career and "Ain't I a Woman?"
In 1843, she changed her name to Sojourner Truth and began traveling and preaching against slavery. She joined the Northampton Association of Education and Industry in Massachusetts, a utopian community supporting abolition and women's rights, where she met figures like William Lloyd Garrison and Frederick Douglass. Her most famous speech was delivered extemporaneously at the 1851 Ohio Women's Rights Convention in Akron. The speech, later published as "Ain't I a Woman?" in 1863, argued for equal human rights by drawing on her experience as a laborer and enslaved woman. The 1863 version, which depicted her speaking in a Southern dialect, is historically inaccurate, as Truth spoke Dutch as her first language and had a New York accent.

### Later Years and Legacy
During the Civil War, Truth helped recruit Black men for the Union Army and worked for the National Freedman's Relief Association in Washington, D.C. After the war, she campaigned for land grants for freed people, meeting with President Ulysses S. Grant but without success. She continued advocating for women's suffrage, prison reform, and other causes until her death in 1883. Her legacy is commemorated through numerous statues, memorials, and institutions, including a bust in the U.S. Capitol and a ship named in her honor.

## Terms
*   **Abolitionist:** A person who advocated for the end of slavery.
*   **Extemporaneously:** Spoken or done without preparation.
*   **Millerites:** Followers of William Miller, who predicted Jesus would return between 1843 and 1844.
*   **Northampton Association of Education and Industry:** A utopian community in Massachusetts that supported abolition, women's rights, and religious tolerance.
*   **Freedman's Relief Association:** An organization that provided aid and improved conditions for formerly enslaved people after the Civil War.
*   **"Forty acres and a mule":** A phrase summarizing the broken federal promise to provide land to formerly enslaved people.
*   **Intersectionality:** A framework for understanding how various social and political identities (like race and gender) combine to create unique modes of discrimination and privilege.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **The "Ain't I a Woman?" Speech:** The most significant debate concerns the accuracy of the 1863 version published by Frances Gage. Scholars note it likely fabricated the stereotypical dialect and the claim that Truth had 13 children sold away (she had five). The earlier 1851 account by Marius Robinson is considered more reliable.
*   **Authorship of "The Valiant Soldiers":** Truth is credited with writing this Civil War song, but its authorship is disputed, with some linking it to the "Marching Song of the First Arkansas."
*   **The "40 acres and a mule" Campaign:** The ultimate failure of Truth's seven-year lobbying effort for land grants highlights the federal government's abandonment of economic justice for freed people after the Civil War.
