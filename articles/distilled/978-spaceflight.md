# Spaceflight

## Overview  
Spaceflight is the application of astronautics to move spacecraft into or through outer space, either crewed or uncrewed. It began in the 1950s with satellite launches and has since enabled orbital operations, interplanetary exploration, and human presence in space. The field relies on rocket propulsion, orbital mechanics, and careful management of launch, transit, and reentry phases, while facing challenges such as space debris, radiation, and cost.

## Body  

### History and Theory  
The theoretical foundation of spaceflight was laid by Konstantin Tsiolkovsky in 1903 with the rocket equation, which relates delta-v (change in velocity) to exhaust velocity and mass ratio. Robert H. Goddard’s 1919 work on liquid-fuel rockets and the de Laval nozzle made practical spaceflight possible. World War II’s V-2 rocket, developed by Nazi Germany, became the first human-made object to reach space. Post-war, captured German scientists like Wernher von Braun advanced U.S. missile programs, leading to the R-7-based Sputnik 1 launch by the Soviet Union in 1957. The U.S. responded with Explorer 1 (1958), and the Space Race escalated with human flights like Vostok 1 (1961) and the Apollo Moon landings (1969–1972).

### Phases of Spaceflight  
A launch begins at a spaceport using multistage rockets to reach orbit. The Kármán line (100 km) marks the boundary of space. Orbital insertion requires high tangential velocity; missions beyond low Earth orbit may use parking orbits or direct injection. Rendezvous and docking enable access to space stations. Reentry subjects vehicles to intense heat, managed by blunt-body designs based on Harry Julian Allen’s theory. Recovery methods vary: splashdown for capsules, runway landing for spaceplanes.

### Types of Spaceflight  
Spaceflight ranges from suborbital (brief ballistic trajectories) to orbital, interplanetary, and interstellar. Crewed missions include Soyuz, Shenzhou, and the former Apollo and Space Shuttle programs. Uncrewed missions include satellites, probes, and deep-space explorers like Voyager and New Horizons. Point-to-point suborbital transport is proposed for rapid Earth travel.

### Spacecraft and Propulsion  
Spacecraft are vehicles designed for operation in space. Rockets dominate propulsion, though ion drives and solar sails offer alternatives. Launch systems include expendable (e.g., Falcon 9) and reusable (e.g., Space Shuttle, Starship) configurations. Reusability has reduced costs, exemplified by SpaceX’s vertical landings starting in 2015.

### Challenges  
Safety risks include launch explosions and equipment failure. Microgravity causes bone loss and muscle atrophy; radiation from the Van Allen belts and cosmic rays poses long-term health risks. Life-support systems must manage air, water, waste, and temperature. Space weather affects satellite operations and orbital decay. Environmental concerns include rocket emissions and space debris, with Kessler syndrome describing cascading collisions in orbit. Regulation and access remain issues, addressed partly by the 1967 Outer Space Treaty.

### Applications and Spacefaring  
Spaceflight supports Earth observation, communications, navigation, space tourism, and scientific research. Private companies like SpaceX have lowered costs, enabling commercial satellite deployment and crewed missions. Spacefaring nations include Russia, the U.S., and China for crewed missions; many others have uncrewed capabilities. The UN coordinates global space activity through UNOOSA.

## Terms  
- **Delta-v (Δv)**: Change in velocity required for maneuvers.  
- **Kármán line**: 100 km altitude boundary of space.  
- **Tsiolkovsky rocket equation**: Relates delta-v to exhaust velocity and mass ratio.  
- **Reentry**: Return of a spacecraft into Earth’s atmosphere.  
- **Docking**: Joining two free-flying spacecraft.  
- **Berthing**: Mating an inactive vehicle via robotic arm.  
- **Kessler syndrome**: Cascading collisions in orbit increasing debris.  
- **Microgravity**: Near-weightless environment in orbit.  
- **ECLSS**: Environmental Control and Life-Support System.  
- **Space weather**: Solar and cosmic effects on space and technology.

## Debates and Open Questions  
The cost and sustainability of spaceflight are debated, especially regarding space tourism and colonization. Radiation exposure limits long-duration missions, and space debris threatens orbital access. Ethical questions surround planetary protection and equitable access to space resources. Whether reusable systems can fully replace expendable ones remains uncertain. The feasibility of interstellar travel hinges on breakthroughs in propulsion technology.
