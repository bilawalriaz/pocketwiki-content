# Harold Urey

## Overview
Harold Clayton Urey (1893–1981) was an American physical chemist whose work fundamentally shaped 20th-century science. He is best known for discovering deuterium (heavy hydrogen), for which he won the 1934 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, and for his leadership in uranium enrichment during the Manhattan Project. His later research pioneered the fields of paleoclimatology and cosmochemistry, and his theories on the early Earth's atmosphere inspired the famous Miller-Urey experiment on the origins of life.

## Timeline
- **1893** — Born in Walkerton, Indiana.
- **1923** — Earns PhD from UC Berkeley; studies at Niels Bohr Institute in Copenhagen.
- **1929** — Becomes associate professor of chemistry at Columbia University.
- **1931** — Begins work on isotope separation, leading to the discovery of deuterium.
- **1932** — Publishes paper announcing the discovery of heavy hydrogen (deuterium).
- **1934** — Awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
- **1941** — Appointed to the Committee on Uranium; leads a mission to the UK for atomic bomb cooperation.
- **1943** — Heads the Substitute Alloy Materials (SAM) Laboratories at Columbia for the Manhattan Project.
- **1945** — Leaves the Manhattan Project; awarded the Medal for Merit.
- **1952** — Publishes *The Planets: Their Origin and Development*; becomes Ryerson Professor at University of Chicago.
- **1958** — Joins the new University of California, San Diego (UCSD) as a professor at large.
- **1981** — Dies in La Jolla, California.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born in Indiana, Urey's family moved to California for his father's health before returning after his father's death. He taught school before earning a zoology degree from the University of Montana in 1917. During World War I, he worked as a chemist making TNT due to his pacifist beliefs. He later earned his PhD in thermodynamics from UC Berkeley in 1923, studying under Gilbert N. Lewis. A fellowship allowed him to study in Copenhagen with Niels Bohr and meet other leading physicists. He joined Johns Hopkins University in 1924 and co-authored one of the first English texts on quantum mechanics.

### Discovery of Deuterium
In the early 1930s, Urey set out to find a predicted heavy isotope of hydrogen. Using a sensitive spectrograph at Columbia, he and his colleagues calculated the spectral lines for this isotope should be slightly blueshifted. To confirm this, they needed a concentrated sample. Working with Ferdinand Brickwedde at the National Bureau of Standards, they distilled liquid hydrogen to enrich the heavy isotope. A second sample, evaporated at a lower temperature, showed the predicted spectral lines seven times more intensely. The discovery of deuterium was published in 1932, leading to Urey's Nobel Prize. His work on isotope separation also enabled new tracer techniques in biochemistry.

### The Manhattan Project
As a world expert on isotope separation, Urey was recruited for the Manhattan Project to enrich uranium for the atomic bomb. He coordinated research on centrifugal, gaseous diffusion, and thermal diffusion methods. By 1943, he headed the SAM Laboratories at Columbia, which focused on gaseous diffusion. His team overcame immense technical challenges, particularly in developing a suitable diffusion barrier, to build the massive K-25 plant. Gaseous diffusion became the primary method for uranium enrichment in the post-war period. Urey left the project in 1945, exhausted but awarded the Medal for Merit.

### Post-War Science and Cosmochemistry
After the war, Urey moved to the University of Chicago. He applied his isotope expertise to oxygen, realizing the ratio of oxygen-18 to oxygen-16 in carbonate fossils could reveal past ocean temperatures, pioneering paleoclimatology. He later developed theories on the abundance of elements in the solar system, coining the term "cosmochemistry." He speculated the early Earth's atmosphere contained ammonia, methane, and hydrogen. His student, Stanley Miller, tested this in the Miller-Urey experiment, producing amino acids—the building blocks of life—from such a mixture. In 1958, Urey joined UCSD, helping build its science faculty. He became deeply involved in lunar science, examining Apollo 11 moon rocks and arguing for a common origin for the Earth and Moon.

## Terms
- **Isotope**: Variants of a particular chemical element which differ in neutron number, and consequently in nucleon number.
- **Deuterium**: A stable isotope of hydrogen with a nucleus containing one proton and one neutron, also known as heavy hydrogen.
- **Gaseous diffusion**: A technology used to produce enriched uranium by forcing uranium hexafluoride gas through a series of porous barriers.
- **Paleoclimatology**: The study of past climates, often using geological evidence like isotopic ratios in fossils.
- **Cosmochemistry**: The study of the chemical composition of celestial bodies and the chemical processes that occur in them.
- **Miller-Urey experiment**: A 1952 experiment that simulated the conditions thought at the time to be present on the early Earth, resulting in the synthesis of organic compounds from inorganic precursors.
- **Urey ratio**: The ratio of a planet's internal heat generation to its surface heat flux.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The composition of Earth's early atmosphere is debated; while Urey proposed a reducing atmosphere of ammonia, methane, and hydrogen, modern research suggests it was likely dominated by carbon dioxide and nitrogen.
- The exact mechanism and location for the origin of life on Earth remains a fundamental open question in science.
- The precise origin and differentiation history of the Moon, which Urey studied, continues to be an active area of planetary science research.
