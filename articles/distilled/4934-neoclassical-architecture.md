# Neoclassical architecture

## Overview
Neoclassical architecture is an 18th- and 19th-century architectural movement that revived and adapted the forms of ancient Greek and Roman architecture. It emerged as a reaction against the ornate Baroque and Rococo styles, seeking a return to the perceived purity, symmetry, and rationality of classical antiquity. The style became internationally dominant, shaping public buildings, private homes, and city planning across the Western world and beyond, often symbolizing Enlightenment ideals, national identity, and political power.

## Timeline
- **c. 1715** — Publication of *Vitruvius Britannicus* by Colen Campbell, promoting Palladian architecture.
- **1729** — Chiswick House designed by Lord Burlington and William Kent, a key Palladian building.
- **1730s** — First truly neoclassical structures built in Italy by architects like Giovanni Antonio Medrano.
- **1750s** — Neoclassicism gains influence in England and France, aided by archaeological discoveries.
- **1758–59** — First Greek-style building in England, a garden temple at Hagley Hall, designed by James Stuart.
- **1773–79** — Publication of *The Works in Architecture* by Robert and James Adam, spreading the Adam style.
- **c. 1800** — Greek Revival begins, fueled by new archaeological knowledge and etchings of Greek buildings.
- **1806** — Design of the Arc de Triomphe in Paris by Jean-François Chalgrin, representing the Empire style.
- **1823–48** — Construction of the British Museum in London by Robert Smirke, a major Greek Revival building.
- **1836–43** — Old Royal Palace in Athens built, one of the first major neoclassical buildings in Greece.
- **1856** — Completion of Dolmabahçe Palace in Istanbul, blending neoclassical elements with Ottoman layouts.

## Body

### Origins and Palladianism
Neoclassicism emerged in the mid-18th century as a reaction against the excesses of the Rococo and Late Baroque styles. Its development was crucially aided by archaeology and the publication of accurate records of classical buildings. The movement initially drew heavily on Roman architecture, with a precursor in the Palladian architecture of Georgian Britain and Ireland, which emphasized simplicity and purity based on the designs of Andrea Palladio. Key early works include Chiswick House (1729), a purified reinterpretation of Palladio's Villa Capra.

### Development and International Spread
By the 1750s, the movement broadened to include Greek influences. It first gained prominence in England and France, propelled by figures like William Chambers, Robert Adam, and French art students trained in Rome. The style became international, exemplified by Karl Friedrich Schinkel's Altes Museum in Berlin, Sir John Soane's Bank of England, and the White House and U.S. Capitol in Washington, D.C. A second, more severe wave of neoclassicism is associated with the First French Empire (c. 1800–1815), known as the Empire style.

### Interior Design and Greek Revival
Neoclassical interior design was inspired by the rediscoveries at Pompeii and Herculaneum, seeking an authentic Roman vocabulary with flatter motifs and "Pompeian red" backgrounds. The Adam brothers published influential designs that simplified earlier styles. From about 1800, a fresh influx of Greek examples sparked the Greek Revival, a more severe and archaeological phase. In Britain, this style was used for major public buildings like the British Museum, asserting national identity. In France, the concurrent Empire style was a grandiose, Roman-inspired variant.

### Characteristics and City Planning
Neoclassical architecture emphasizes planar qualities, symmetry, simple geometry, and social function over elaborate ornament. It is the first "stripped down" classical architecture. The style also influenced city planning, adopting the orderly Roman grid system with central forums and boulevards. This model was applied to new planned cities like Washington, D.C., Saint Petersburg, and Karlsruhe.

### Regional Trends
The style adapted to local contexts. In the United States, it evolved into Federal architecture and later the Greek Revival. In the Ottoman Empire, it was often layered over traditional layouts, as seen in Dolmabahçe Palace. In Greece, it became the national style after independence, shaping Athens. In Mexico, it was promoted by the Academy of San Carlos, blending with local traditions. The style also flourished in Germany, Russia, Spain, and across Latin America, often symbolizing modernity and state power.

## Terms
- **Palladianism:** An architectural style based on the designs of the 16th-century Venetian architect Andrea Palladio, emphasizing symmetry and classical forms.
- **Baroque:** A highly ornate and dramatic architectural style prevalent in Europe from the late 16th to early 18th century.
- **Rococo:** An 18th-century style characterized by lightness, elegance, and exuberant naturalistic ornament, which Neoclassicism reacted against.
- **Greek Revival:** A phase of Neoclassicism (c. 1800–1850) that more strictly followed ancient Greek architectural models.
- **Empire style:** A grandiose, Roman-inspired phase of Neoclassicism associated with the First French Empire under Napoleon I.
- **Adam style:** A refined, elegant interior design style developed by the Adam brothers, simplifying Rococo and Baroque elements.
- **Architecture parlante:** ("Speaking architecture") A concept where a building's design immediately communicates its function to the viewer.
- **Federal architecture:** The American adaptation of Neoclassical style in the late 18th and early 19th centuries.
- **Biedermeier:** A simplified, bourgeois Neoclassical style popular in German-speaking lands from 1815 to 1848.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise boundaries of Neoclassicism are debated; the term is often used loosely for any building using classical vocabulary.
- The relationship between Neoclassicism and later revival styles (post-1840) is a point of scholarly classification, with the former considered a specific movement and the latter a series of revivals.
- The Empire style is sometimes seen not as the culmination but as the decline of the Neoclassical movement, drained of its original high-minded ideals.
