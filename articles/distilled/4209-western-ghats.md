# Western Ghats

## Overview
The Western Ghats, also known as the Sahyadri, is a 1,600 km mountain range along India's west coast, forming a continuous chain along the Deccan Plateau. It is a critical watershed, climate regulator, and one of the world's eight "hottest hotspots" of biodiversity, with a vast number of endemic species. Its ecological importance led to its designation as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2012.

## Timeline
- **Late Jurassic–Early Cretaceous** — Mountains form during the break-up of the supercontinent Gondwana as India separates from Africa.
- **500 million years ago** — The Palghat Gap, the oldest and widest gap in the range, is formed.
- **65–80 million years ago** — The Goa Gap is formed.
- **Post-British colonial rule** — Large-scale forest clearing begins for agricultural plantations and timber.
- **2006** — India applies to UNESCO for World Heritage Site status.
- **August 2011** — The Western Ghats Ecology Expert Panel (WGEEP) designates the entire region as an Ecologically Sensitive Area (ESA).
- **2012** — 39 sites across the Western Ghats are declared UNESCO World Heritage Sites.
- **2015** — The endemic Nilgiri tahr population is estimated at 3,122 individuals.
- **2022** — The tiger population in the Western Ghats is estimated at 985.

## Body

### Geology and Geography
The Western Ghats are the faulted and eroded edge of the Deccan Plateau, formed during the break-up of Gondwana. The range extends approximately 1,600 km from the Tapti River to Swamithoppe in Kanyakumari district, covering 160,000 km² across six Indian states. It is divided into three sections: a northern section (900–1,500 m elevation), a lower middle section south of Goa (<900 m), and a southern section where altitude rises again. The highest peak is Anamudi at 2,695 m. Three major gaps—the Goa, Palghat, and Sengottai Gaps—interrupt the otherwise continuous chain.

### Hydrology and Climate
The range is a major watershed, feeding perennial river systems like the Godavari, Kaveri, and Krishna that drain nearly 40% of India. Due to the plateau's westward elevation, most rivers flow east to the Bay of Bengal. The Ghats are crucial to India's climate: they block moisture-laden monsoon winds from the Arabian Sea, causing heavy orographic rainfall on the western coast and creating a rain shadow region on the leeward Deccan Plateau side. Climate varies with altitude, from warm and humid tropical conditions to subtropical or temperate climates at higher elevations.

### Biodiversity
The Western Ghats are a global biodiversity hotspot, hosting nearly 30% of India's flora and fauna, with high endemism. Flora includes over 7,400 flowering plant species, with 1,273 species exclusive to the region. Fauna includes at least 325 globally threatened species, such as the tiger, lion-tailed macaque, Nilgiri tahr, and Malabar large-spotted civet. The region is exceptionally rich in amphibians, reptiles, and freshwater fish, with many endemic genera. It also supports over 6,000 insect species, including 334 butterfly species.

### Threats and Conservation
Historically dense forests were fragmented by colonial-era plantations. Primary threats include habitat loss, overexploitation, mining, poaching, and invasive species. Conservation efforts include the establishment of protected areas like the Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve (5,500 km²). The 2011 WGEEP report recommended classifying the entire region as an ESA. In 2012, 39 sites were inscribed as UNESCO World Heritage Sites to promote protection.

## Terms
- **Biodiversity hotspot**: A biogeographic region with significant levels of biodiversity that is threatened by human habitation.
- **Endemic**: A species native and restricted to a certain place.
- **Orographic precipitation**: Rainfall caused by air being forced to rise over a mountain range.
- **Rain shadow**: A dry area on the leeward (downwind) side of a mountainous area.
- **Perennial river**: A river that has continuous flow in some parts of the year.
- **Deccan Plateau**: A large plateau in southern India, bordered by mountain ranges.
- **Gondwana**: An ancient supercontinent that broke up about 180 million years ago.
- **Shola**: A unique type of stunted tropical montane forest found in the valleys of the southern Western Ghats.
- **Ecologically Sensitive Area (ESA)**: A region designated for special protection due to its ecological significance.

## Debates and Open questions
- The precise boundaries and levels of ecological sensitivity for the ESA designation recommended by the WGEEP panel have been subject to ongoing debate and subsequent committee recommendations.
- The impact of climate change on the range's unique ecosystems and endemic species is a critical area of ongoing research and concern.
