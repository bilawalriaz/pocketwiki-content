# Korean War

## Overview
The Korean War (1950–1953) was a major Cold War conflict between North Korea (DPRK) and South Korea (ROK), with their respective allies, that solidified the division of the Korean Peninsula. It was the first large-scale military confrontation of the Cold War, demonstrating the policy of containment and establishing a pattern of proxy wars. The conflict ended in a stalemate, leaving the peninsula technically at war and divided by a heavily fortified Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) that persists today.

## Timeline
- **10 August 1945** — Soviet forces enter northern Korea following Japan's surrender.
- **15 August 1948** — Republic of Korea (South Korea) is established.
- **10 July 1948** — Democratic People's Republic of Korea (North Korea) is established.
- **25 June 1950** — North Korea invades South Korea across the 38th parallel.
- **27 June 1950** — UN Security Council recommends member states aid South Korea; US President Truman orders air and sea support.
- **15 September 1950** — UN forces launch an amphibious landing at Inchon, behind North Korean lines.
- **19 October 1950** — Chinese People's Volunteer Army (PVA) crosses the Yalu River to intervene on North Korea's behalf.
- **4 January 1951** — Communist forces recapture Seoul.
- **11 April 1951** — President Truman relieves General MacArthur of command.
- **10 July 1951** — Armistice negotiations begin at Kaesong.
- **27 July 1953** — Korean Armistice Agreement is signed, ending active combat.

## Body

### Origins and Division
Following 35 years of Japanese colonial rule (1910–1945), Korea was divided at the 38th parallel by the United States and the Soviet Union as a temporary administrative measure. Political disagreements led to the establishment of two separate governments in 1948: the communist North under Kim Il Sung and the anti-communist South under Syngman Rhee. Both claimed legitimacy over the entire peninsula. Border clashes and internal insurgencies, such as the Jeju uprising, preceded the full-scale invasion.

### Invasion and UN Intervention
On 25 June 1950, the North Korean People's Army (KPA) invaded the South, rapidly capturing Seoul. The UN Security Council, absent a Soviet veto due to a boycott, called for a collective response. A US-led UN Command (UNC) intervened, with the US providing 90% of military personnel. After initial setbacks that pushed UN forces to the Pusan Perimeter, a successful UNC amphibious landing at Inchon in September 1950 reversed the tide. UNC forces recaptured Seoul and advanced into North Korea, approaching the Chinese border.

### Chinese Intervention and Stalemate
Fearing a US advance to its border, China entered the war in October 1950, sending the PVA. Their intervention forced a major UN retreat and led to the recapture of Seoul in January 1951. Subsequent battles stabilized the front near the 38th parallel. The war then devolved into a brutal stalemate of attrition, characterized by trench warfare and massive artillery duels. Armistice negotiations began in July 1951 but dragged on for two years, primarily over the issue of prisoner-of-war repatriation.

### Air War and Bombing Campaign
The conflict featured the first large-scale jet aircraft battles, notably between US F-86 Sabres and Soviet-made MiG-15s over "MiG Alley." The UNC conducted a devastating bombing campaign against North Korea, destroying virtually all its major cities and industrial infrastructure. The US dropped 635,000 tons of bombs, more than in the entire Pacific Theater of WWII, making North Korea one of the most heavily bombed countries in history.

### Armistice and Aftermath
The Korean Armistice Agreement was signed on 27 July 1953, establishing a ceasefire and the 4-kilometer-wide DMZ. South Korea's President Rhee refused to sign. No peace treaty was ever concluded, leaving the two Koreas in a state of technical war. The conflict caused an estimated 1.5 to 3 million civilian deaths and around 1 million military deaths. North Korea was devastated, while South Korea began a long recovery under a US security umbrella. The war cemented the division of the peninsula and entrenched the Cold War in Asia.

## Terms
*   **38th Parallel**: The line of latitude that served as the boundary between the Soviet and US occupation zones after WWII, and later the border between North and South Korea before the war.
*   **Demilitarized Zone (DMZ)**: A 4-kilometer-wide buffer zone established by the 1953 armistice, running along the final front line, which remains one of the world's most heavily fortified borders.
*   **United Nations Command (UNC)**: The multinational military force authorized by the UN Security Council to assist South Korea, led by the United States.
*   **Korean People's Army (KPA)**: The military forces of North Korea.
*   **Republic of Korea Army (ROKA)**: The military forces of South Korea.
*   **Chinese People's Volunteer Army (PVA)**: The Chinese military forces that intervened in the war on behalf of North Korea.
*   **Pusan Perimeter**: A defensive line held by UN and ROK forces in the southeast of the peninsula from August to September 1950, before the Inchon landing.
*   **Armistice**: A formal agreement of truce to stop fighting, which ended the Korean War but did not constitute a peace treaty.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Who fired the first shot?** While the full-scale invasion was launched by the North, there is debate over which side initiated the initial border skirmishes on 25 June 1950.
*   **Mao's approval of the invasion**: Historical interpretations differ on whether Chinese leader Mao Zedong fully approved Kim Il Sung's invasion plan or merely accepted the decision made by Kim and Soviet leader Stalin.
*   **Scale of casualties**: Precise casualty figures are disputed. Estimates for total deaths range from 1.5 to 3 million, with significant variation in military loss figures for China and North Korea between official and scholarly sources.
*   **Use of nuclear weapons**: The US seriously considered using atomic weapons during the war, particularly after Chinese intervention. The decision not to do so was influenced by allied opposition and concerns about Soviet retaliation, but the threat remained a factor in diplomacy.
