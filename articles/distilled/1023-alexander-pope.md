# Alexander Pope

## Overview
Alexander Pope (1688–1744) was the preeminent English poet of the early 18th century and a defining figure of Augustan literature. Barred by his Catholicism from public office and university education under the Test Acts, and crippled by Pott disease (spinal tuberculosis) from age 12, he became a self-taught literary celebrity whose satirical verse and Homeric translations earned him unprecedented professional recognition and financial independence. His work, characterized by the mastery of the heroic couplet, fused classical precedent with contemporary social critique, targeting pedantry, political corruption, and the moral confusion of the emerging commercial society. Pope’s career illustrates the tensions of the Enlightenment: a Catholic outsider dominating a Protestant literary establishment, a physically disabled man wielding immense cultural authority, and a satirist who sought order through reason while exposing the chaos of human pride.

## Timeline
- **21 May 1688** — Born in London to Catholic parents; year of the Glorious Revolution.
- **c. 1700** — Family moves to Binfield, Berkshire, due to statute banning Catholics from living within 10 miles of London; formal education ends, self-directed study begins.
- **1709** — *Pastorals* published in Tonson’s *Poetical Miscellanies*, bringing instant fame.
- **15 May 1711** — *An Essay on Criticism* published anonymously; establishes poetic manifesto.
- **1711–1714** — Forms Scriblerus Club with Swift, Parnell, Arbuthnot; publishes *The Rape of the Lock* (1712, enlarged 1714) and *Windsor Forest* (1713).
- **1715–1720** — Publishes translation of the *Iliad* by subscription, securing financial independence.
- **1719** — Moves to villa at Twickenham; constructs famous grotto and gardens.
- **1725–1726** — Publishes *Odyssey* translation (collaborating with Broome and Fenton); publishes controversial edition of Shakespeare.
- **1728** — *The Dunciad* published anonymously in Dublin, targeting Lewis Theobald and "dunces."
- **1731–1735** — Publishes *Moral Essays* (including *Epistle to Burlington*).
- **1732–1734** — Publishes *An Essay on Man*, a philosophical poem in heroic couplets.
- **1733–1738** — Publishes *Imitations of Horace*, satirizing Walpole’s ministry and George II’s court.
- **1742–1743** — Publishes Book Four and complete revision of *The Dunciad*, replacing Theobald with Colley Cibber as "king of dunces."
- **30 May 1744** — Dies at Twickenham; buried in St Mary’s Church, Twickenham.

## Body

### Early Life, Disability, and Education
Pope’s life was shaped by the intersection of religious persecution and severe physical disability. Born the year of the Glorious Revolution to a Catholic linen merchant, he was subject to the Test Acts, which barred Catholics from universities, public office, and, after 1700, residence within ten miles of London. His formal education ended around age 12 when the family moved to Binfield; thereafter, he educated himself through extensive reading in French, Italian, Latin, and Greek, discovering Homer at age six. At 12, he contracted Pott disease (tuberculosis of the spine), which stunted his growth to 4 feet 6 inches, caused a severe hunchback, and left him with lifelong respiratory difficulties, fevers, and pain. This physical isolation reinforced his social marginalization as a Catholic, driving him toward literature as a vocation. Despite these constraints, he wrote *Ode on Solitude* at 12 and entered London literary circles by his early twenties through patrons like John Caryll and William Walsh.

### The Scriblerus Club and Tory Satire
Around 1711, Pope allied with Tory writers Jonathan Swift, Thomas Parnell, and John Arbuthnot to form the Scriblerus Club, a collective dedicated to satirizing ignorance and pedantry through the fictional scholar Martinus Scriblerus. Simultaneously, he maintained friendships with Whig writers Joseph Addison and Richard Steele, contributing to *The Spectator* and *The Guardian*. This dual allegiance reflected the fluid political landscape before the 1714 Hanoverian succession. The death of Queen Anne and the Jacobite rising of 1715 devastated Tory fortunes; Pope’s friend Henry St John, Viscount Bolingbroke, fled to France. Pope’s own political stance remains debated—scholar Maynard Mack argues it "can probably never be confidently known"—but his satire increasingly targeted the Whig establishment of Robert Walpole, whom he viewed as the architect of a corrupt financial revolution.

### Major Satirical Poems: *The Rape of the Lock* and *The Dunciad*
*The Rape of the Lock* (1712, enlarged 1714) is a mock-epic based on a real quarrel: Lord Petre snipped a lock of hair from Arabella Fermor. Using the elevated machinery of epic poetry—gods, battles, arming scenes—Pope satirizes the triviality of aristocratic society while simultaneously reveling in its "beau-monde" rituals. The revised version sharpens the critique toward "acquisitive individualism," where purchased artifacts displace human agency. *The Dunciad* (1728, revised 1742–43) began as a personal vendetta against Lewis Theobald, who had exposed errors in Pope’s Shakespeare edition. It expanded into a vast satire on the "dunces" of Grub Street, personifying the goddess Dulness. Maynard Mack calls its publication "the greatest act of folly in Pope's life," as it provoked relentless physical threats and slander from victims. The final revision replaced Theobald with Poet Laureate Colley Cibber as "king of dunces," but the true target remained Walpole’s political culture.

### Philosophical Poetry: *An Essay on Criticism* and *An Essay on Man*
*An Essay on Criticism* (1711), written in heroic couplets, functions as a poetic manifesto engaging the debate between "natural" genius and classical rules. Pope argues that true wit is "nature to advantage dressed," advocating for critics who aid poets rather than merely attack them, and defining the ideal critic as a figure of moral virtue. *An Essay on Man* (1732–34), addressed to Bolingbroke, attempts to "vindicate the ways of God to Man" (echoing Milton). It posits a rational, divinely ordered universe—the "Great Chain of Being"—where apparent evil results from human limited perception. Man’s duty is to accept his middle station between angels and beasts, strive for virtue, and rely on hope leading to faith. The poem is not strictly Christian but assumes a fallen humanity seeking salvation within a providential structure.

### Translations of Homer and the Shakespeare Edition
Pope’s *Iliad* (1715–20), published by subscription with a revolutionary deal earning 200 guineas per volume, made him the first English poet to live solely by his pen. Samuel Johnson praised it as unequalled; classical scholar Richard Bentley dismissed it as "a pretty poem... but you must not call it Homer." The *Odyssey* (1725–26) was a collaboration with William Broome and Elijah Fenton, which Pope tried to conceal; the leak damaged his reputation temporarily. His 1725 Shakespeare edition for Jacob Tonson "regularised" metre, rewrote verse, and cut 1,560 lines. Lewis Theobald’s *Shakespeare Restored* (1726) catalogued these errors, making Theobald the primary target of *The Dunciad*. Later editors dismissed Pope’s "creative" textual approach, though his preface remained influential.

### Later Years: *Imitations of Horace* and the Revised *Dunciad*
The *Imitations of Horace* (1733–38) updated the Roman satirist to attack George II’s court and Walpole’s corruption. The introductory epistle includes famous portraits of Lord Hervey ("Sporus") and Addison ("Atticus"). After 1738, Pope wrote little original verse, focusing on expanding *The Dunciad*. Book Four (1742) and the complete revision (1743) intensified the attack on Walpole. His health failing, Pope died at Twickenham in 1744, receiving Last Rites the day before. He was buried in the nave of St Mary’s Church.

## Terms
- ****Augustan Literature**** — Early 18th-century English literature (c. 1700–1745) modeled on the classical ideals of the Roman Emperor Augustus’s reign, emphasizing order, reason, satire, and the heroic couplet.
- ****Heroic Couplet**** — A pair of rhyming lines in iambic pentameter, the dominant verse form of Augustan poetry, perfected by Pope for satirical and philosophical precision.
- ****Test Acts**** — English penal laws (1673, 1678) requiring officeholders to swear allegiance to the Church of England and reject transubstantiation, effectively excluding Catholics from public life, universities, and (after 1700) residence near London.
- ****Pott Disease**** — Tuberculosis of the spine; contracted by Pope at age 12, causing spinal deformity, stunted growth (4 ft 6 in), and chronic pain.
- ****Mock-Epic**** — A satirical form applying the elevated style, conventions, and machinery of classical epic (e.g., *Iliad*, *Aeneid*) to a trivial subject.
- ****Scriblerus Club**** — Informal group (c. 1711–1714) of Tory writers—Pope, Swift, Arbuthnot, Parnell, Gay—who collaborated on satires of pedantry and false learning via the persona Martinus Scriblerus.
- ****Great Chain of Being**** — A hierarchical metaphysical structure (God → angels → humans → beasts → plants → matter) central to *An Essay on Man*, positing that cosmic order depends on each entity accepting its assigned station.
- ****Grub Street**** — A London district synonymous with hack writers and low-quality literary production; in *The Dunciad*, the kingdom of the goddess Dulness.
- ****Subscription Publishing**** — A pre-publication funding model where subscribers paid in advance for a work, often receiving a premium copy; Pope used this for his *Iliad*, securing unprecedented authorial profit.
- ****Imitation (Augustan)**** — A poetic form adapting a classical author (e.g., Horace) to contemporary circumstances, distinct from translation; Pope’s *Imitations of Horace* satirize 1730s politics through a Roman lens.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Political Allegiance:** Scholars debate whether Pope was a covert Jacobite sympathizer or a pragmatic observer. Maynard Mack argues his true stance "can probably never be confidently known," noting his Catholic background versus his Tory/Whig friendships and reliance on Hanoverian patronage for his Homer subscription.
*   **The *Odyssey* Collaboration:** The extent of Pope’s authorial control over the *Odyssey* remains contested. He concealed the contributions of Broome and Fenton; Leslie Stephen judged Pope’s portions inferior to the *Iliad*, suggesting the collaboration diluted his distinctive style.
*   **Shakespeare Edition Methodology:** Pope’s "creative" editing—regularizing meter, cutting lines deemed inauthentic—was condemned by Lewis Theobald and later scholars as textual corruption, yet his preface shaped 18th-century editorial theory regarding actor interpolations.
*   **Misogyny in *The Rape of the Lock*:** Critics dispute whether the poem’s "voyeuristic interest" in Belinda’s toilette and the sylphs reinforces or subverts patriarchal objectification of aristocratic women.
*   **Philosophy of *An Essay on Man*:** The poem’s optimism—"whatever is, is right"—has been read as a genuine theodicy or a satiric target (later mocked by Voltaire in *Candide*). The tension between its deistic "Great Chain" and Christian assumptions about the Fall remains unresolved.
*   **Martha Blount Relationship:** The nature of Pope’s lifelong attachment to Martha Blount—described by friend William Cheselden as the reason he left a "gay [hedonistic]" life—is debated as romantic, platonic, or a sublimated literary partnership.
