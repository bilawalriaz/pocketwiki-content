# Sargon of Akkad

## Overview
Sargon of Akkad (died c. 2279 BC) was the founder of the Akkadian Empire, the first known empire in recorded history. He conquered the Sumerian city-states and established a multi-ethnic state that ruled most of Mesopotamia and parts of the Levant and Elam, setting a model for future Mesopotamian kings for nearly two millennia.

## Body

### Rise to Power and Conquests
Primary sources, including the Sumerian King List, describe Sargon as the son of a gardener and former cup-bearer to King Ur-Zababa of Kish. He usurped the kingship from Lugal-zage-si of Uruk. His Nippur inscription details his conquest of Uruk, where he defeated Lugal-zage-si and led him "in a collar to the gate of Enlil." He then conquered Ur, E-Ninmar, Lagash, and Umma, claiming dominion from the "Upper Sea" (Mediterranean) to the "Lower Sea" (Persian Gulf). He submitted to the god Dagan to conquer territories in Upper Mesopotamia and the Levant, including Mari, Yarmuti, and Ibla. He also conquered Elam and Marhashi to the east, claiming to have triumphed over 34 cities in total.

### The Akkadian Empire
Sargon ruled from his capital, Akkad, whose archaeological site remains unidentified. His empire is considered the first in recorded history, encompassing most of Mesopotamia and parts of the Levant, Hurrian, and Elamite territory. He maintained a standing army of 5,400 men and a court that "ate bread daily before him." Trade flourished, with ships from Meluhha, Magan, and Dilmun anchoring in Akkad. He founded the "Sargonic" or "Old Akkadian" dynasty, which ruled for about a century until the Gutian conquest. His reign is dated to c. 2334–2279 BC in the middle chronology, though the Sumerian King List gives his reign as 40 or 54–56 years.

### Later Legends and Legacy
Sargon became a legendary figure in later Mesopotamian literature. The "Sargon Birth Legend," a Neo-Assyrian text, describes his birth as the illegitimate son of a priestess, his exposure in a basket on the Euphrates, and his rise from gardener to king—a motif with parallels to the biblical story of Moses. The "Sumerian Legend" details his rise from cup-bearer to king through divine intervention. Later chronicles, like the Chronicle of Early Kings, describe revolts in his old age and divine punishment via famine. He was regarded as a model king for millennia; Assyrian and Babylonian rulers saw themselves as his heirs. His grandson, Naram-Sin, also became a great king of the dynasty.

## Terms
- **Akkadian Empire**: The first known empire in history, founded by Sargon of Akkad, ruling Mesopotamia and surrounding regions.
- **Sumerian King List**: A historical document listing rulers of Sumer, including Sargon, with reign lengths.
- **Cup-bearer**: A high-ranking court official who served drinks to the king; Sargon held this position under Ur-Zababa.
- **Middle chronology**: A scholarly system for dating ancient Near Eastern events, placing Sargon's reign c. 2334–2279 BC.
- **Lugal-zage-si**: King of Uruk whom Sargon defeated to seize power.
- **Enheduanna**: Sargon's daughter, high priestess of the moon god in Ur, and a known composer of hymns.
- **Gutian conquest**: The invasion by the Gutians that ended the Sargonic Dynasty's rule.
- **Sargon Birth Legend**: A later literary text describing Sargon's miraculous birth and rise to power.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Chronology**: The exact dates of Sargon's reign are debated, with the Sumerian King List giving reign lengths of 40 or 54–56 years. The middle chronology places it c. 2334–2279 BC, but other systems exist.
- **Founder of Akkad**: The claim that Sargon founded Akkad is questioned by an inscription mentioning the city in the reign of Enshakushanna, who likely preceded him.
- **Name**: Whether his name was Šarru-ukīn ("the king has established") or Šarru-kēn ("the king is legitimate") is debated, as is whether it was a birth name or regnal name.
- **Later References**: Some chronicles attributing building works at Babylon to Sargon may actually refer to the Neo-Assyrian king Sargon II.
- **Historical vs. Legendary**: Much of what is known about Sargon comes from later legendary texts, making it difficult to separate historical fact from myth.
