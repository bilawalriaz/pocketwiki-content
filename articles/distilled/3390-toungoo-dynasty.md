# Toungoo dynasty

## Overview
The Toungoo dynasty (1510–1752) was the ruling dynasty of Burma (Myanmar) that created what was likely the largest empire in Southeast Asian history. It ruled in two phases: the First Toungoo Empire (1510–1599) and the Nyaungyan or Restored Toungoo Dynasty (1599–1752). The dynasty's peak under King Bayinnaung saw the reunification of territories from Assam to Cambodia, but its later period established administrative systems that endured for centuries.

## Timeline
- **1510** — King Mingyi Nyo founds the First Toungoo Empire at Taungoo.
- **1527** — Mohnyin-led Shan sawbwas conquer Inwa, causing Burmese migration to Taungoo.
- **1544** — King Tabinshwehti is crowned king of all Burma at Bagan.
- **1550** — Tabinshwehti is assassinated; Bayinnaung succeeds to the throne.
- **1560–1564** — Bayinnaung invades Manipur and Ayutthaya.
- **1581** — Bayinnaung dies; his son Nanda Bayin faces rebellions.
- **1599** — The First Toungoo Empire collapses; the Nyaungyan Restoration begins.
- **1613** — King Anaukpetlun reunites Burma and defeats Portuguese incursions.
- **1635** — King Thalun's revenue inquest estimates Irrawaddy valley population at ~2 million.
- **1740** — Mon people in Lower Burma rebel, founding the Restored Hanthawaddy Kingdom.
- **1752** — Hanthawaddy armies capture Inwa, ending the Toungoo dynasty.

## Body

### First Toungoo Empire (1510–1599)
Founded by Mingyi Nyo, the dynasty's early kings Tabinshwehti and Bayinnaung reunified the territories of the Pagan Kingdom for the first time since 1287 and incorporated the Shan States. Tabinshwehti unified most of Burma, moving the capital to Bago for trade. After his assassination in 1550, his brother-in-law Bayinnaung launched a 30-year campaign of conquest, making Taungoo the most powerful state in Southeast Asia. The empire collapsed in the 18 years following Bayinnaung's death in 1581 due to rebellions.

### Nyaungyan Restoration (1599–1752)
Facing rebellion and Portuguese incursions, the rulers withdrew from southern Burma and founded a second dynasty at Ava (Inwa). Bayinnaung's grandson, Anaukpetlun, reunited Burma by 1613. The Restored Toungoo kings created a legal and political system whose features continued under the later Konbaung dynasty. They replaced hereditary chieftainships with appointed governorships in the Irrawaddy valley and reduced the hereditary rights of Shan chiefs. Trade and administrative reforms built a prosperous economy for over 80 years.

### Decline and Fall
The kingdom entered a gradual decline due to the "palace rule" of its kings. From the 1720s, it was beset by Meitei raids from the Chindwin River and a rebellion in Chiang Mai. In 1740, the Mon people in Lower Burma began a rebellion, founding the Restored Hanthawaddy Kingdom. Encouraged by the French in India, Hanthawaddy armies captured Inwa in 1752, ending the 266-year-old dynasty. The downfall is broadly ascribed to institutional weaknesses, factionalism, succession disputes, and the uneven impact of growing trade.

## Terms
- **Suzerainty**: A relationship in which one state controls the foreign affairs of another, while the latter retains internal autonomy.
- **Shan States**: A collection of principalities in the highlands of present-day eastern Myanmar, historically ruled by hereditary chiefs (sawbwas).
- **Ahmudan system**: A system of corvée labor or service groups attached to the crown or elite households.
- **Palace rule**: A period of weak central authority where court factions and courtiers wielded significant power over the monarch.
- **Revenue inquest**: A comprehensive survey of land, population, and tax obligations conducted by the state.
- **Hanthawaddy**: The historical Mon kingdom in Lower Burma, which was a rival to the Toungoo dynasty.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that the dynasty's downfall has been "more broadly ascribed to institutional weaknesses in the capital, which intensified factionalism and succession disputes, and the uneven impact of growing trade and potential price inflation, on the elite's income streams." This suggests scholarly debate on the precise combination of political, economic, and social factors that led to its collapse.
