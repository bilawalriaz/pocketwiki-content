# Pampas

## Overview
The Pampas are vast, fertile low grasslands covering over 1.2 million km² in southern South America, primarily in Argentina, Uruguay, and southern Brazil. This temperate ecoregion is a major agricultural area, but its natural landscape has been significantly altered by human activity, particularly the introduction of livestock and farming. Its history includes conflict between indigenous peoples and European settlers, followed by large-scale immigration.

## Timeline
- **c. 12,000 years ago** — End-Pleistocene extinction of most large native mammals (e.g., *Megatherium*, *Smilodon*).
- **16th century** — Spanish colonists arrive; first confrontations with local tribes like the Querandí.
- **Late 18th century** — The Salado River marks the boundary between Spanish civilization and indigenous lands.
- **c. 1830** — Mapuche leader Calfucurá crosses the Andes into the Pampas.
- **1870s** — Argentina constructs the Zanja de Alsina trench to counter cattle raids.
- **1870s-1880s** — Argentina launches the "Conquest of the Desert" against indigenous tribes.
- **1840s onward** — European immigration begins, intensifying after the 1880s.
- **Post-1930s** — Industrialization leads many immigrants to move from rural areas to cities.

## Body

### Geography and Climate
The Pampas are a natural region of low elevations, generally not exceeding 600 m, with the highest point at 1,239 m in the Sierra de la Ventana. The climate is temperate, with annual precipitation ranging from 400 mm in the drier west to 1,400 mm in the northeast. Summers are hot (28-33°C) and winters are mild (12-19°C), though cold waves occur. The region experiences intense thunderstorms, frequent lightning, and is one of the most active tornado zones outside the United States.

### Vegetation and Ecoregions
The dominant vegetation is grassy prairie and steppe, featuring species like *Stipa* grasses and the iconic Pampas grass (*Cortaderia selloana*). The World Wildlife Fund divides the Pampas into three ecoregions: the Uruguayan Savanna, the Humid Pampas, and the Semiarid Pampas. The reason the pristine Pampas were treeless is debated, with hypotheses including seasonal drought, competition from grass roots, heavy clay soils, and historical fires or herbivory.

### Wildlife
Human activity, especially agriculture and ranching, has drastically changed Pampas wildlife. Most large native mammals, such as the puma, rhea, and Pampas deer, have lost habitat and are rare. The jaguar and guanaco have been extirpated. Before human arrival, the ecosystem hosted megafauna like the giant ground sloth *Megatherium* and the sabertooth cat *Smilodon*. Today, surviving mammals include the coypu, Pampas fox, and armadillos, while invasive species include the European hare and wild boar.

### Human History and Economy
Spanish colonization began in the 16th century, leading to conflict with indigenous tribes. In the 19th century, Mapuche raids for cattle prompted Argentina to build defensive trenches and eventually launch the "Conquest of the Desert." From the 1840s, European immigrants settled the land, first in rural colonization schemes and later in cities as industrialization grew after the 1930s. The Pampas are now a core agricultural region for crops, cattle, and vineyards.

## Terms
- **Ecoregion:** A large area of land or water that contains a distinct assemblage of natural communities.
- **Köppen climate classification:** A system for classifying the world's climates based on temperature and precipitation patterns.
- **End-Pleistocene extinction:** A period around 12,000 years ago when most large mammals in the Americas died out.
- **Malones:** Cattle raids conducted by indigenous peoples like the Mapuche.
- **Zanja de Alsina:** A defensive trench built by Argentina in the 1870s to prevent cattle raids.
- **Conquest of the Desert:** A military campaign by Argentina in the 1870s-80s to gain control of indigenous lands in the Pampas.
- **Stipa:** A genus of grasses that is particularly conspicuous in Pampas vegetation.
- **Coxilhas/Cuchillas:** Low hills covered by grasslands, typical of the northern Pampas landscape.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary scholarly debate concerns why the pristine Pampas were treeless. Proposed explanations include seasonal drought, competition from grass roots, heavy clay soils limiting tap roots, fires set by indigenous peoples, the presence of large herbivores, and relict drier past climates. These mono-causal explanations have been criticized, with current thinking suggesting a synergistic influence of multiple factors like low propagule pressure, abiotic stresses, and biotic resistance.
