# American Museum of Natural History

## Overview
The American Museum of Natural History (AMNH) is a private research and educational institution on Manhattan's Upper West Side, housing over 32 million specimens and artifacts. Founded in 1869, it is a world leader in scientific research, field expeditions, and public education, attracting approximately five million visitors annually. Its iconic halls of dioramas, fossils, and cultural artifacts make it a cornerstone of scientific understanding and public engagement with the natural world.

## Body

### Founding and Early Growth
The museum was conceived by naturalist Albert S. Bickmore in 1861 and officially established by New York State in 1869. It opened in 1871 in Central Park's Arsenal, becoming immensely popular. A permanent, purpose-built structure in Manhattan Square (later Theodore Roosevelt Park) opened in 1877. Throughout the late 19th century, the museum expanded rapidly with new wings and halls to accommodate its growing collections and public demand, including the opening of Sunday hours in 1892.

### Collections and Exhibits
The AMNH's collections contain approximately 32 million specimens, though only a small fraction can be displayed. Exhibits are organized into thematic halls. Famous diorama halls, like the Akeley Hall of African Mammals (completed 1936), were pioneered to create immersive, scientifically realistic habitats. The fourth floor houses the world's largest collection of fossilized mammals and dinosaurs, including iconic mounts of *Tyrannosaurus rex* and *Apatosaurus*. Cultural anthropology halls, such as the Northwest Coast Hall (est. 1899), display artifacts from global human societies, though their presentation has evolved in response to ethical and decolonization debates.

### Architecture and Expansion
The original 1877 building was designed by Calvert Vaux and J. Wrey Mould in a Victorian Gothic style. It was soon overshadowed by the neo-Romanesque southern wings designed by J. Cleaveland Cady. The main entrance, the Theodore Roosevelt Memorial Hall, was completed in 1936 in a Beaux-Arts style. Major 20th-century additions include the Childs Frick Building (1973) for fossil storage and the Rose Center for Earth and Space (2000). The most recent major expansion is the Richard Gilder Center (2023), designed by Studio Gang, which added 230,000 square feet of exhibition, research, and educational space.

### Research and Education
With a scientific staff of over 225, the AMNH sponsors more than 120 field expeditions annually. Its research spans paleontology, astrophysics, anthropology, and genomics. The museum operates the Richard Gilder Graduate School, which awards PhDs and Master's degrees, and runs extensive educational outreach programs for schoolchildren. It also publishes peer-reviewed scientific journals.

## Terms
- **Diorama**: A three-dimensional, full-size model of a natural habitat, typically featuring taxidermied animals against a painted background, used for scientific and educational display.
- **NAGPRA**: The Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (1990), a U.S. federal law requiring institutions to return Native American cultural items and human remains to lineal descendants and affiliated tribes.
- **Cladogram**: A diagram used in cladistics to show relations among organisms, often used in the museum's fossil halls to illustrate evolutionary relationships.
- **Shotcrete**: Concrete projected at high velocity onto a surface, used to create the continuous, curvilinear forms of the Gilder Center's atrium.
- **Holotype**: A single physical example of an organism known to have been used when the species was formally described, often housed in museum collections.
- **Repatriation**: The process of returning cultural artifacts or human remains to their original owners or communities of origin.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Ethical Display of Human Remains**: The museum has faced criticism and legal action under NAGPRA for its long-held collection of Native American remains. In 2023, it announced it would stop displaying human remains and began repatriation processes, closing related halls in 2024.
- **Colonial and Racist Legacies**: Scholars have critiqued certain exhibits, like the Hall of Asian Peoples, for portraying cultures as static and "othering" non-Western societies. The museum's historical association with the eugenics movement, including hosting international eugenics congresses in the 1920s and 1930s, is also a subject of critical examination.
- **The Roosevelt Statue**: The museum removed a controversial 1940 equestrian statue of Theodore Roosevelt in 2022. The statue depicted Roosevelt on horseback, flanked by a Native American and an African American figure, which was widely seen as symbolizing racial hierarchy.
