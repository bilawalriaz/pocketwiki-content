# Jane Jacobs

## Overview
Jane Jacobs (1916–2006) was an American-Canadian journalist, author, and activist whose 1961 book *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* challenged the dominant "urban renewal" paradigm. She argued that cities function as complex ecosystems requiring diversity, density, mixed uses, and "eyes on the street" (natural surveillance by residents) rather than top-down master plans that separated uses and demolished neighborhoods. Jacobs led successful grassroots campaigns to stop the Lower Manhattan Expressway in New York and the Spadina Expressway in Toronto, embodying her "bottom-up" planning philosophy. Though often criticized as an uncredentialed "housewife" in the male-dominated field of urban planning, the influence of her concepts was eventually acknowledged by highly respected professionals, influencing the New Urbanist movement and housing policy (e.g., HOPE VI).

## Body

### Early Life and Intellectual Formation
Born in Scranton, Pennsylvania, to physician John Decker Butzner and former teacher/nurse Bess Robison Butzner, Jacobs had a brother, John Decker Butzner Jr., who served as a judge on the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit. After graduation from Scranton High School, she worked for a year as the unpaid assistant to the women's page editor at the *Scranton Tribune* before moving to New York City in 1935 during the Great Depression. She fell in love with Greenwich Village’s non-grid street pattern. Working as a stenographer and freelance writer covering working districts, she gained firsthand knowledge of urban economies. She studied at Columbia University’s School of General Studies for two years, pursuing an eclectic curriculum (geology, zoology, law, economics) but avoided Barnard College’s rigid requirements due to poor high school grades. This self-directed education fostered her interdisciplinary, observational approach.

### Early Career and Political Pressure
Jacobs worked at *Iron Age* magazine, where a 1943 article on Scranton’s decline helped attract a warplane factory. She advocated for equal pay and unionization. She then wrote for *Amerika*, a State Department Russian-language publication, where she met her husband. During the McCarthy era, she faced a loyalty questionnaire; though anti-communist, her pro-union stance and appreciation for Saul Alinsky drew suspicion. Her 1952 response defended the right of extremists to speak, arguing that suppressing them endangered everyone’s rights.

### *Architectural Forum* and the Break with Planning Orthodoxy
At *Architectural Forum* (1952–1962), Jacobs was assigned to cover urban "blight" and renewal projects. A 1954 assignment on Edmund Bacon’s Society Hill development in Philadelphia shocked her: "development" destroyed street life and displaced poor African Americans. Meeting minister William Kirk in 1955 introduced her to East Harlem’s reality. Her 1956 Harvard lecture, standing in for Douglas Haskell, urged planners to "respect... strips of chaos that have a weird wisdom of their own." The speech, printed in the magazine, marked her as a threat to the establishment. A 1958 *Fortune* article, "Downtown Is for People," criticized Robert Moses and Lincoln Center, prompting the publisher to ask, "Who is this crazy dame?"

### *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* (1961)
Funded by the Rockefeller Foundation and affiliated with The New School, Jacobs spent three years researching. The book attacked 1950s urban renewal as a pseudoscience that destroyed communities. She identified four generators of diversity: (1) mixed primary uses (districts serving multiple functions), (2) short blocks (promoting encounters and commerce), (3) buildings of varied age/condition (accommodating diverse users), and (4) sufficient concentration of people. She coined "eyes on the street" (natural surveillance) and the "ballet of the sidewalk." The profession retaliated with ad hominem attacks ("militant dame," "housewife," "bitter coffee-house rambling"); Robert Moses called it "intemperate and also libelous." Later criticism from the left noted the book’s insufficient treatment of race and its endorsement of "unslumming" (gentrification).

### The Battle for Greenwich Village and LOMEX
Jacobs translated theory into activism. She joined Shirley Hayes’s committee to close Washington Square Park to traffic (successful in 1958). She then chaired the Joint Committee to Stop the Lower Manhattan Expressway (LOMEX), a Moses project connecting bridges to the Holland Tunnel through SoHo, Little Italy, and Chinatown. *The Village Voice* supported her; *The New York Times* backed Moses. She fought revivals in 1962, 1965, and 1968. At an April 1968 hearing, the crowd charged the stage; Jacobs was arrested for inciting a riot, criminal mischief, and obstructing administration. After months of commuting from Toronto for trials, the charge was reduced to disorderly conduct. Robert Caro’s *The Power Broker* largely omitted her role due to space constraints, a decision Caro later regretted.

### Life in Toronto: Activism and Citizenship
Jacobs moved to Toronto in 1968, driven by opposition to the Vietnam War, fear for her draft-age sons, and exhaustion fighting New York City Hall. She settled in The Annex, an area dubbed the "American ghetto" for draft resisters. She became a leading figure against the Spadina Expressway, asking whether cities were for people or cars. Arrested twice in demonstrations, she influenced the successful regeneration of the St. Lawrence neighbourhood. She became a Canadian citizen in 1974 and later told writer James Howard Kunstler that dual citizenship was not possible at the time, implying that her U.S. citizenship was lost. She advocated for a "Province of Toronto" to separate the city politically from Ontario. She advised David Miller’s successful 2003 mayoral campaign and helped stop a bridge to the island airport. She was made an Officer of the Order of Canada (1996) and received the American Sociological Association’s Outstanding Lifetime Contribution award (2002).

### Economic Theory: Cities as Engines of Growth
Jacobs considered her economic work her greatest legacy. In *The Economy of Cities* (1969), she argued cities—not nations—are the primary units of macroeconomics. She proposed "import replacement" (producing locally what was imported) as the mechanism of expansion: as a city replaces imports, it shifts rather than reduces imports, growing the total economic pie. She distinguished "development" (differentiation of existing things) from "expansion" (growth in volume). She controversially argued cities preceded agriculture, defining cities by dense trade and division of labor rather than literacy or monumentality. *Cities and the Wealth of Nations* (1984) elaborated a spectrum of regional economies (backward, supply, transplant, entrepôt, hub, and "forward" import-replacing metropolises) and advocated city currencies as feedback mechanisms for innovation.

### Moral and Ecological Frameworks
*Systems of Survival* (1992), written as a Platonic dialogue, identified two mutually exclusive moral syndromes: "Commercial" (honesty, collaboration, novelty—applicable to trade, science, farming) and "Guardian" (loyalty, hierarchy, tradition—applicable to government, charities, religion). Mixing them produces corruption (e.g., Mafia, communism). *The Nature of Economies* (2000) argued economies and ecosystems share principles: development via differentiation, expansion via diverse energy use, self-maintenance via self-refueling. She explored "dynamically stable systems" avoiding collapse through bifurcations, feedback loops, and emergency adaptations. *Dark Age Ahead* (2004) warned of decline in five cultural pillars: family/community, education, science, government, and professional accountability, though she acknowledged ambiguity in interpreting signs like suburban sprawl.

### Legacy and Honors
Jacobs inspired New Urbanism and influenced Oscar Newman’s "defensible space" theory, which shaped the HOPE VI program demolishing high-rise projects for low-rise mixed-income housing. Economist Edward Glaeser confirmed her prescience on Moses’s Le Corbusier-inspired towers becoming centers of crime. Critics argue her diversity prescriptions inadvertently fueled gentrification in places like the West Village, as preserved older buildings became luxury assets. Tyler Cowen contends her ideas lack scalability for megacities in developing nations. Posthumous honors include Jane Jacobs Day (NYC and Toronto), the global "Jane's Walks" festival (212 cities by 2016), the Rockefeller Foundation’s Jane Jacobs Medal, the Canadian Urban Institute’s Lifetime Achievement Award, streets and parks named for her in NYC, Toronto, and the U.S., a Google Doodle (2016), and the 2017 documentary *Citizen Jane: Battle for the City*.

## Terms
- ****Eyes on the street**** — Natural surveillance provided by residents and pedestrians going about daily activities, deterring crime and ensuring sidewalk safety.
- ****Mixed primary uses**** — Districts serving multiple functions (residential, commercial, industrial) simultaneously, ensuring activity at different times of day.
- ****Import replacement**** — The process by which a city produces goods locally that it previously imported, building local skills/infrastructure and shifting (not reducing) import patterns to drive economic expansion.
- ****Unslumming**** — Jacobs’s term for the gradual, organic upgrading of a neighborhood by its residents, later criticized as a euphemism for gentrification.
- ****Ballet of the sidewalk**** — The intricate, improvised social dance of diverse people using city sidewalks throughout the day, generating safety and vitality.
- ****Commercial moral syndrome**** — One of two moral syndromes in *Systems of Survival*; values honesty, collaboration, novelty, and voluntary agreements; applies to trade, science, farming.
- ****Guardian moral syndrome**** — The other moral syndrome; values loyalty, hierarchy, tradition, and prowess; applies to government, military, charities, religion.
- ****Forward cities / Jacobs agglomeration**** — Growing metropolitan areas that expand beyond political borders via import replacement and innovation; core is "import-replacing city," periphery is "city-region."
- ****Urban renewal / Slum clearance**** — Mid-20th-century government policy using federal funds (Title I, Housing Act of 1949) to demolish "blighted" areas for highways, towers, or civic centers; Jacobs’s primary target.
- ****Bottom-up planning**** — Planning approach prioritizing local knowledge, community needs, and organic growth over top-down master plans by experts/officials.

## Debates and open questions
- **Gentrification vs. Diversity**: Did Jacobs’s prescriptions for preserving old buildings and mixed-use neighborhoods inadvertently create the conditions for displacement and hyper-gentrification, as seen in Greenwich Village? She argued older buildings were "guarantors of social diversity" due to low cost; critics note their value skyrocketed unpredictably.
- **Scalability**: Tyler Cowen and others argue her theories—developed observing mid-century New York—fail in rapidly growing megacities (1M to 10M+) in the Global South, where infrastructure deficits and scale differ fundamentally.
- **Race and Equity**: Critics from the left contend *Death and Life* insufficiently addressed racial segregation and structural racism, focusing on physical form over power dynamics.
- **Cities vs. Nations in Economics**: Mainstream economics retains the nation-state as the primary macroeconomic unit; Jacobs’s insistence on the city as the fundamental unit remains a heterodox position.
- **Agriculture vs. Cities Origin**: Her claim that cities preceded agriculture (based on a trade-based definition of "city") contradicts archaeological consensus (Vere Gordon Childe) defining civilization by literacy, stratification, and monumentality.
- **Moral Syndromes Rigidity**: *Systems of Survival* posits fixed, non-fluctuating moral syndromes; critics question whether this binary adequately captures hybrid modern institutions (e.g., social enterprises, state-owned firms).
