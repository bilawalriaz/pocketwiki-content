# Tsardom of Russia

## Overview
The Tsardom of Russia was the centralized Russian state from 1547, when Ivan IV assumed the title of tsar, until 1721, when Peter the Great proclaimed the Russian Empire. This period saw massive territorial expansion, the consolidation of autocratic rule, the establishment of serfdom, and the transformation of a relatively isolated state into a major European power.

## Timeline
- **1547** — Ivan IV is crowned tsar, proclaiming the Tsardom of Russia.
- **1552-1556** — Ivan IV conquers the Khanates of Kazan and Astrakhan, gaining control of the Volga.
- **1558-1583** — The Livonian War ends in failure, denying Russia access to the Baltic.
- **1565-1572** — Ivan IV implements the *oprichnina*, a reign of terror against the nobility.
- **1598** — The Rurik dynasty ends with the death of Feodor I.
- **1601-1603** — A major famine contributes to the "Time of Troubles."
- **1613** — The *Zemsky Sobor* (national assembly) elects Mikhail Romanov as tsar, beginning the Romanov dynasty.
- **1649** — The *Sobornoye Ulozheniye* legal code officially enshrines serfdom.
- **1654** — The Treaty of Pereyaslav brings the Cossack Hetmanate under Russian sovereignty.
- **1667** — The Truce of Andrusovo ends the war with Poland, splitting Ukraine along the Dnieper.
- **1689** — Peter the Great becomes ruler in his own right.
- **1721** — Following victory in the Great Northern War, Peter proclaims the Russian Empire.

## Body

### Name and Identity
The state's official name evolved from "Rus'" to "Rossiya" (Russia) in the 15th-16th centuries. Ivan IV's coronation document called it the "Great Russian Tsardom." In Western Europe, particularly Catholic regions, it was often called "Moscovia" (Muscovy), a term never used internally. This external usage was partly due to Polish-Lithuanian political interests and a lack of direct knowledge. By the 17th century, "Russia" and "Moscovia" were often used interchangeably in Western sources.

### Ivan IV and the Autocracy
Ivan IV ("the Terrible") centralized power, claiming the Byzantine imperial title of *tsar* (from Caesar). This was supported by the "Third Rome" ideology, which held Moscow as the successor to Rome and Constantinople. Ivan's early reign saw useful reforms, but his later rule was marked by the *oprichnina* (1565-1572), a campaign of terror against the boyar (noble) class that destroyed political and economic power centers, leading to stagnation and setting the stage for future crisis.

### The Time of Troubles and the Romanovs
The death of Ivan's son Feodor in 1598 ended the Rurik dynasty, triggering a civil war and foreign intervention known as the Time of Troubles (1598-1613). Chaos included pretenders to the throne, Polish and Swedish occupation, and popular revolts. Order was restored in 1613 when the *Zemsky Sobor* elected Mikhail Romanov as tsar. The early Romanovs faced weak rule and ongoing conflicts, but the state bureaucracy remained strong.

### Legal and Social Order: The 1649 Code
The *Sobornoye Ulozheniye* (1649) was a comprehensive legal code that defined the social order. It merged the old and new nobility into the *dvoryanstvo* (service nobility), which received land and peasants in exchange for state service. Crucially, the code officially attached peasants to the land, fully sanctioning serfdom and restricting urban tradesmen. This increased state control and taxation, leading to social unrest, including major uprisings like that of Stenka Razin (1670-1671).

### Expansion: West and East
Territorial growth was a constant. In the southwest, Russia incorporated the "Wild Fields" (modern Eastern Ukraine) after the Cossack Hetman Bohdan Khmelnytsky allied with Tsar Aleksey I in 1654. The subsequent war with Poland was settled by the Truce of Andrusovo (1667), which split Ukraine. This expansion brought Western intellectual influences via Kiev, contributing to the Raskol (Church Schism) of 1667, when Patriarch Nikon's reforms to align with Greek practice were rejected by the Old Believers. Eastward, Russian explorers and Cossacks conquered Siberia with little resistance, reaching the Pacific by the mid-17th century. The Treaty of Nerchinsk (1689) with China established a border and secured trade rights.

### Peter the Great and the Empire
Peter the Great (r. 1689-1725) radically modernized Russia, forcing Westernization on the nobility and creating a new bureaucracy (e.g., the Governing Senate, Collegiums). His victory in the Great Northern War (1700-1721) against Sweden secured Russia's Baltic coast and the new capital, St. Petersburg. In 1721, he proclaimed the Russian Empire, ending the Tsardom.

## Terms
- **Tsar**: The title adopted by Ivan IV, derived from "Caesar," signifying an emperor-level autocrat.
- **Oprichnina**: Ivan IV's private domain (1565-1572), where his agents terrorized and dispossessed the nobility.
- **Zemsky Sobor**: A national assembly of boyars, clergy, and commoners that advised the tsar and, in crises, elected new rulers.
- **Dvoryanstvo**: The service nobility formed by the merger of old boyar families and new elite servitors under the 1649 code.
- **Sobornoye Ulozheniye**: The 1649 legal code that codified serfdom and defined the social hierarchy.
- **Raskol**: The 1667 schism in the Russian Orthodox Church between the reformed Nikonians and the traditionalist Old Believers.
- **Hetman**: The elected leader of the Cossack Host, such as Bohdan Khmelnytsky.
- **Prikaz**: A government department or bureau in the tsardom's expanding bureaucracy.
- **Third Rome**: The ideology that Moscow was the final legitimate successor to the Roman and Byzantine empires.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The causes of Ivan IV's turn to terror during the *oprichnina* are debated: were they driven by policy, personal animosity, or mental instability?
- The extent to which the Time of Troubles was a popular revolution versus a boyar power struggle remains a subject of historical interpretation.
- The impact of Western influences via Ukraine on the Russian cultural synthesis and the causes of the Raskol continue to be analyzed.
