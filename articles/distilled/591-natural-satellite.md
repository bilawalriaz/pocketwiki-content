# Natural satellite

## Overview
A natural satellite is an astronomical body that orbits a planet, dwarf planet, or small Solar System body. Colloquially called "moons," these objects range from massive, planet-sized worlds to kilometer-scale "moonlets." The Solar System contains six planetary satellite systems with 455 confirmed moons, plus 625 minor planets known to host satellites. Most large, close-in "regular" satellites formed from the same protoplanetary disk as their primary, while distant "irregular" satellites are likely captured asteroids. Exceptions like Earth's Moon and Pluto's Charon likely formed from giant impacts, and Neptune's Triton is a captured dwarf planet. Many large moons are tidally locked (showing one face to their primary), and several—such as Io, Europa, and Enceladus—exhibit current geological activity driven by tidal heating. As of 2024, no exomoons have been confirmed.

## Timeline
- **1543** — Copernicus publishes *De revolutionibus orbium coelestium*, reclassifying the Moon from a "planet" to a body orbiting Earth.
- **1610** — Galileo discovers the four Galilean moons of Jupiter; Johannes Kepler coins the term "satellite" (from Latin *satelles*, "attendant") in his pamphlet *Narratio de Observatis a se quatuor Iouis satellitibus erronibus*.
- **1957** — Launch of Sputnik shifts the term "satellite" primarily to artificial objects, reviving "moon" as the standard scientific term for natural satellites.
- **1990s** — Discovery of Dactyl, moon of asteroid 243 Ida, confirms asteroids host natural satellites.
- **2006–2007** — Asteroid 2006 RH120 becomes the first confirmed temporary satellite of Earth, captured for nine months.
- **2008** — Detection of a possible ring system around Saturn's moon Rhea suggests potential for stable subsatellites; later Cassini images fail to confirm rings.
- **2023** — Four temporary Earth satellites identified via simulation and observation: 1991 VG, 2006 RH120, 2020 CD3, and 2022 NX1.
- **2024** — Numerous exomoon candidates detected, but none confirmed.

## Body

### Terminology and Definition
The Moon was historically considered a planet until Copernicus (1543). With the discovery of the Galilean moons (1610), a class term was needed; Galileo called them *Planetæ*, but Johannes Kepler introduced "satellite" (Latin *satelles*: guard, attendant) later that year. The 1957 launch of Sputnik caused "satellite" to become synonymous with artificial objects, restoring "moon" (lowercase) as the generic scientific term. "Moon" (capitalized) remains the proper noun for Earth's satellite. There is no formal lower size limit for a moon; bodies down to ~1 km are moons, while smaller ring objects are "moonlets." The upper limit is vague: the Pluto–Charon system is sometimes called a double dwarf planet because their barycenter lies outside Pluto, unlike all planetary moon systems where the barycenter lies inside the primary.

### Origin and Orbital Characteristics
**Regular satellites** orbit close to the primary on prograde, low-inclination, circular paths. They are thought to have formed *in situ* from the same collapsing region of the protoplanetary disk that created the planet. **Irregular satellites** orbit distantly on inclined, eccentric, and often retrograde paths; they are likely captured asteroids, possibly fragmented by later collisions. Most major moons are regular; most small moons are irregular. **Exceptions:** Earth's Moon and Pluto's moons likely formed from giant impacts between protoplanetary bodies (the giant impact hypothesis), with debris reaccreting in orbit. Triton is a large, close, circular moon but orbits retrograde; it is thought to be a captured dwarf planet. **Trojan satellites** occupy L4/L5 Lagrangian points (60° ahead/behind) of a larger moon: Telesto and Calypso (Tethys), Helene and Polydeuces (Dione). **Temporary satellites** are asteroids captured from heliocentric orbits for finite periods; simulations suggest they are common. As of 2023, four have been observed for Earth (e.g., 2006 RH120, captured for nine months).

### Tidal Locking
Most regular moons are **tidally locked**: tidal forces raised by the primary dissipate rotational energy until the moon's rotation period equals its orbital period, fixing one hemisphere toward the planet. Exceptions include Saturn's Hyperion, which rotates chaotically due to Titan's gravitational perturbations, and Pluto's four small circumbinary moons, which rotate chaotically under Charon's influence. Irregular satellites are too distant to have locked; e.g., Jupiter's Himalia, Saturn's Phoebe, and Neptune's Nereid have rotation periods of ~10 hours but orbital periods of hundreds of days.

### Satellites of Satellites (Subsatellites)
No "moons of moons" are currently known. Tidal effects from the primary planet usually destabilize such orbits. Potential stable hosts include large, wide-orbit moons like Titan, Iapetus, Callisto, and Earth's Moon, though other instabilities (e.g., lunar mass concentrations/mascons) may prevent formation. A 2008 candidate ring system around Rhea suggested possible shepherd moonlets, but Cassini imaging found no rings. Iapetus's equatorial ridge has been hypothesized as debris from a past subsatellite. Light-curve analysis suggests Saturn's irregular moon Kiviuq is a contact binary or binary moon.

### Shape
Moons massive enough for gravity to overcome material strength achieve **hydrostatic equilibrium** (rounded ellipsoids). Neptune's Proteus (~420 km) is the largest irregularly shaped icy moon. Tidally locked moons develop **ovoid (egg-like) shapes**: squat at poles with the long equatorial axis pointed toward the primary. Saturn's Mimas shows a major axis 9% greater than its polar axis; tiny Methone (~3 km) is visibly egg-shaped. The effect diminishes for larger moons with stronger self-gravity (e.g., the Moon). The twenty known rounded moons are sometimes termed "satellite planets" or "planetary-mass moons."

### Geological Activity
Of the nineteen gravitationally rounded moons, several are currently active. **Io** is the most volcanically active body in the Solar System. **Europa, Enceladus, and Triton** show ongoing tectonics and **cryovolcanism** (eruption of volatiles like water). For Io, Europa, and Enceladus, **tidal heating** from eccentric orbits near giant planets powers activity; Triton's past eccentricity (before circularization) would have driven similar heating. Many others (Moon, Ganymede, Titan, Tethys, Miranda) show evidence of past activity from primordial radioisotope decay, past orbital resonances, or interior differentiation/freezing. **Enceladus and Triton** have active geyser-like plumes (Triton's possibly solar-driven). **Titan and Triton** possess significant atmospheres; Titan uniquely hosts surface hydrocarbon lakes. Four Galilean moons have tenuous atmospheres. **Europa, Ganymede, Callisto, Titan, and Enceladus** are thought to harbor subsurface liquid water oceans.

### Occurrence in the Solar System
**Inner planets:** Mercury and Venus have none; Earth has one (the Moon); Mars has two small moons (Phobos, Deimos). **Giant planets** host extensive systems, including seven moons >2,500 km: the four Galilean moons, Titan, Triton, and Earth's Moon. Saturn has six additional mid-sized round moons; Uranus has five. **Dwarf planets:** Ceres and Sedna have none. Pluto has Charon (large, barycenter outside Pluto) plus Styx, Nix, Kerberos, Hydra. Haumea has two; Orcus, Quaoar, Makemake, Gonggong, and Eris have one each. **Small bodies:** 76 asteroid belt objects (five binary), four Jupiter trojans, 39 near-Earth objects (two with two moons), 14 Mars-crossers, and 84 trans-Neptunian objects host satellites. Asteroid satellites allow density determination of the primary without spacecraft visits. ~150 objects observed in Saturn's rings; few have confirmed orbits. **Exomoons:** Candidates exist, but none confirmed as of 2024.

### Dimensions
The seven largest moons (>2,500 km) are Ganymede, Callisto, Io, Europa, Titan, Moon, Triton. Ganymede and Titan exceed Mercury in diameter; Callisto matches it. Nine mid-sized moons (1,000–1,600 km): Titania, Oberon, Rhea, Iapetus, Charon, Ariel, Umbriel, Dione, Tethys. Planets typically have >10,000× the mass of their moons. **Earth–Moon is unique**: Moon is 0.273× Earth's diameter and 1/80 its mass. Next ratios: Neptune–Triton (0.055, 1/4790 mass), Saturn–Titan (0.044, 1/4225), Jupiter–Ganymede (0.038, 1/12810), Uranus–Titania (0.031, 1/25125). For dwarf planets, Charon is 0.52× Pluto's diameter and 12.2% its mass. Planetary moons are named for mythological figures (Greek, except Uranus's Shakespearean names).

## Terms
- ****Natural satellite**** — An astronomical body orbiting a planet, dwarf planet, or small Solar System body (as opposed to an artificial satellite).
- ****Regular satellite**** — A moon orbiting close to its primary on a prograde, low-inclination, near-circular orbit; thought to have formed *in situ* from the protoplanetary disk.
- ****Irregular satellite**** — A moon on a distant, inclined, eccentric, and/or retrograde orbit; thought to be a captured asteroid.
- ****Tidal locking**** — A state where a moon's rotation period equals its orbital period due to tidal dissipation, causing one hemisphere to permanently face the primary.
- ****Barycenter**** — The center of mass of a two-body system; if it lies outside the primary, the system is sometimes classified as a double planet.
- ****Giant impact hypothesis**** — The theory that Earth's Moon (and Pluto's system) formed from debris ejected by a collision between two protoplanetary bodies.
- ****Cryovolcanism**** — Volcanic activity involving the eruption of volatiles (water, ammonia, methane) rather than silicate rock, observed on icy moons.
- ****Tidal heating**** — Internal heating of a moon caused by friction from tidal forces varying due to an eccentric orbit or orbital resonance.
- ****Subsatellite (moon of a moon)**** — A hypothetical natural satellite orbiting another moon; none confirmed, though large, wide-orbit moons are potential hosts.
- ****Trojan moon**** — A small moon sharing the orbit of a larger moon, librating around its L4 or L5 Lagrangian points (60° ahead/behind).
- ****Hydrostatic equilibrium**** — The state where a body's gravity pulls it into a rounded ellipsoidal shape; defines "round" moons (satellite planets).
- ****Moonlet**** — A very small natural satellite, typically <1 km, often found within planetary rings.

## Debates and open questions
- **Definition of a moon:** No formal lower or upper size limits exist. The distinction between a "moon" and a "double planet" (e.g., Pluto–Charon) relies on the barycenter location, but this criterion is not universally codified.
- **Origin of Triton:** While capture is the leading hypothesis, the specific mechanism (e.g., three-body interaction, gas drag in a primordial nebula) remains under investigation.
- **Subsatellite stability:** Theoretical work suggests large, distant moons could host submoons, but none have been observed; the role of mascons, tidal evolution, and primordial formation conditions in preventing them is unresolved.
- **Exomoon confirmation:** Numerous candidates have been identified (e.g., via transit timing variations), but none have reached statistical confirmation as of 2024.
- **Interior oceans:** The existence and habitability of subsurface oceans on Europa, Ganymede, Callisto, Titan, and Enceladus are inferred from magnetic and gravity data but await direct verification.
- **Ring-moon interactions:** The dynamics of Saturn's ring moons and moonlets (e.g., propeller features, F ring shepherds) and the potential past rings of Rhea remain active research areas.
