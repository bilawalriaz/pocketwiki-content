# Arabic script

## Overview

The Arabic script is a writing system originally developed for Arabic, most notably for the Quran, and has since been adapted for dozens of languages across Asia and Africa. It is the second-most widely used alphabetic writing system in the world (after Latin), the second-most by number of countries using it, and the third-most by number of users (after Latin and Chinese). Written right-to-left in a cursive, unicase style (no capitals), it functions primarily as an abjad (consonants and a few vowels), though some languages use it as a true alphabet or abugida. Its spread with Islam and subsequent adaptations make it a foundational script for Persian, Urdu, Malay, and many other language families.

## Timeline

- **6th–5th centuries BCE** — Northern Arab tribes found a kingdom at Petra, Jordan; these people become known as Nabataeans.
- **2nd–1st centuries BCE** — First known records of the Nabataean alphabet are written in Aramaic, with some Arabic features.
- **c. 7th century CE** — Garshuni originates: Arabic becomes dominant spoken language in the Fertile Crescent, but Arabic script is not yet fully developed, so Syriac alphabet is used.
- **16th century** — Arabic script still used for some Spanish texts; later replaced.
- **17th century** — Earliest attested use of Arabic script for Yoruba (verifiable use dates to 19th century).
- **19th century** — Bilali Document (Arabic manuscript on West African Islamic law) written by Bilali Mohammet; Omar Ibn Said (1770–1864) writes in Arabic script.
- **1857** — British commissioner in Sindh orders change to Arabic script for Sindhi (August 29).
- **1928** — Turkey changes from Arabic to Latin script under Mustafa Kemal Atatürk; Ottoman Turkish becomes a distinct historical language.
- **1932** — Northern Kurdish in Turkey and Syria switches from Arabic script to a modified Kurdish Latin alphabet introduced by Jaladat Ali Badirkhan in Syria.
- **1969–1983** — Uyghur changes to Latin script in 1969, then back to a simplified, fully voweled Arabic script in 1983.
- **1991** — After Soviet collapse, several Turkic ex-USSR languages attempt Turkish-style Latin alphabets; Tajikistan sees limited renewed Arabic script use.

## Body

### Origins and derivation

The Arabic alphabet derives either from the Nabataean alphabet or (less widely believed) directly from the Syriac alphabet, both of which descend from the Aramaic alphabet, which in turn descends from the Phoenician alphabet (also the ancestor of Greek, Cyrillic, and Latin scripts). In the 6th–5th centuries BCE, northern Arab tribes founded a kingdom at Petra, Jordan; these people, called Nabataeans (from the tribe Nabatu), spoke Nabataean Arabic but wrote in Aramaic, the language of trade. Their Aramaic-based script split into a monumental form for inscriptions and a cursive, joined form for papyrus. Over time, the cursive form increasingly influenced the monumental form, gradually evolving into the Arabic alphabet.

### Script characteristics and adaptation

The script is written right-to-left in cursive style, with most letters changing form depending on whether they stand alone or join a following/preceding letter. It is unicase (no capitals). In most languages it transcribes consonants plus a few vowels, making it an abjad; however, for Sorani Kurdish, Kashmiri, Gorani, Uyghur, and Serbo-Croatian it functions as a true alphabet with mandatory vowels, and for Mandarin it is an abugida using diacritics instead of full letters. Adaptations often add letters for phonemes absent in Arabic—e.g., the voiceless bilabial plosive [p]—with the specific letter varying by language. Modifications cluster by region: Indian and Turkic languages tend to use Persian modified letters (the Perso-Arabic script), while Indonesian languages imitate Jawi. In West African languages (especially the Sahel), style follows the Maghreb (e.g., dot positions in fāʼ and qāf), and additional diacritics represent non-Arabic sounds; such orthographies are called ʻAjamī, from the Arabic root for "foreign."

### Current geographic use

Iran, Afghanistan, and Pakistan are the main non-Arabic states using the Arabic alphabet for official languages, including Azerbaijani, Baluchi, Brahui, Persian, Pashto, Central Kurdish, Urdu, Sindhi, Kashmiri, and Punjabi. In India and China, it writes Kashmiri (Jammu and Kashmir), Urdu (Uttar Pradesh, Telangana), and Uyghur (Xinjiang). In Africa, it is used for Arabic, Berber languages (with political connotations versus Latin and Tifinagh), Tuareg, Coptic, Beja, Somali (Wadaad's writing), Nubian languages (Dongolawi, Nobiin—the latter not yet standardized), Fur, Comorian, Swahili (originally Arabic script, now Latin), Zarma, Tadaksahak, Hausa (Ajami), Dyula, Jola-Fonyi, Balanta, Mandinka, Fula (Pular), Wolof (Wolofal), and Yoruba (Anjẹmi). In the Americas, Arabic-script writings survive from enslaved Africans, including Omar Ibn Said, the Bilali Document, Ayuba Suleiman Diallo, and Abdul Rahman Ibrahima Sori. In the Middle East and Central Asia, it is used for Arabic, Azerbaijani (Torki in Iran), Baluchi, Kazakh (in China, Iran, Afghanistan), Kurdish (northern Iraq, northwest Iran), Kyrgyz (in Xinjiang, Pakistan, Afghanistan), Pashto, Persian/Dari, Lori/Bakhtiari, Turkmen (Afghanistan, Iran), Uyghur, and Uzbek (Afghanistan). In East Asia, Xiao'erjing writes Chinese for some Hui, and the Salar language uses it. In Europe, Dobrujan Tatar is written in it (Romania, Bulgaria). In South Asia, it covers Balochi, Dari, Kashmiri, Pashto, Khowar, Punjabi (Shahmukhi), Saraiki (45 letters), Sindhi, Aer, Bhadrawahi, Ladakhi (rarely), Balti, Brahui, Burushaski, Urdu, Dogri (more commonly Devanagari), Arwi (Arabic-Tamil mixture with 13 added letters, Sri Lanka and Tamil Nadu), Arabi Malayalam (Kerala, Lakshadweep), Rohingya (also Hanifi script), and Ishkashimi. In Southeast Asia, Jawi writes Malay—co-official in Brunei and in Malaysian states Kelantan, Kedah, Pahang, Terengganu; co-used with Latin in Indonesian provinces Aceh, Riau, Riau Islands, Jambi; used in southern Thailand, Muslim areas of the Philippines (Maguindanaon, Tausug), for Ida'an (Sabah), and Cham (Cambodia, Vietnam).

### Former use

Arabic script was historically used for many languages now written otherwise. In the 20th century it was generally replaced by Latin in the Balkans, parts of Sub-Saharan Africa, and Southeast Asia; in the Soviet Union, Cyrillic was mandated after a brief Latinisation. Turkey switched to Latin in 1928. Former users include: Hebrew (in some places), Northern Kurdish (until 1932), Ottoman Turkish, Afrikaans (among Cape Malays), Berber (Shilha in Morocco), French (in colonial Algeria), Harari (now Geʻez/Latin), Hausa/Fula/Mandinka/Wolof (officially Latin now), Kinyarwanda, Kirundi, Malagasy (Sorabe), Nubian, Shona, Somali (Latin since 1972), Songhay (Timbuktu), Swahili (Latin since 19th century), Yoruba, Albanian (Elifbaja shqip), Aljamiado (Mozarabic, Berber, Aragonese, Portuguese, Ladino, Spanish in Iberia), Belarusian (among Tatars), Bosnian (Arebica, literary only), Crimean Tatar, Greek (Cappadocian Greek in Perso-Arabic), Polish (Lipka Tatars), Adyghe (until 1927), Avar and other Daghestani languages, Azeri, Bashkir (1917–1928), Chaghatay, Chechen (officially 1917–1928), Circassian, Ingush, Karachay-Balkar, Karakalpak, Kazakh (until 1930s), Kyrgyz (until 1930s), Mandarin/Dungan (Xiao'erjing), Tat, Tatar (before 1928), Turkmen, Uzbek, and several Northeast Caucasian languages (1918–1928). In South/Southeast Asia, former users include Acehnese, Assamese, Banjarese, Bengali (Dobhashi), Maguindanaon, Malay (except Brunei and southern Thailand), Minangkabau, Pegon (Javanese, Madurese, Sundanese—now only in Islamic schools), Tausug, Ternate-Tidore, Wolio, and Yakan.

### Unicode

Arabic characters are encoded in Unicode ranges: Arabic (0600–06FF), Arabic Supplement (0750–077F), Arabic Extended-A (08A0–08FF), Arabic Extended-B (0870–089F), Arabic Extended-C (10EC0–10EFF), Arabic Presentation Forms-A (FB50–FDFF), Arabic Presentation Forms-B (FE70–FEFF), Arabic Mathematical Alphabetic Symbols (1EE00–1EEFF), Rumi Numeral Symbols (10E60–10E7F), Indic Siyaq Numbers (1EC70–1ECBF), and Ottoman Siyaq Numbers (1ED00–1ED4F).

### Letter construction

Most languages using Arabic-based alphabets share the same base shapes. Additional letters are typically built by adding or removing diacritics to existing Arabic letters. Some stylistic variants carry distinct meanings in other languages: variant forms of kāf (ك, ک, ڪ) have specific usages; Urdu and neighboring languages split Hā into dō-čašmī hē (ھ) and gōl hē (ہ), and use baṛī yē (ے) at word endings.

## Terms

- **Abjad**: A writing system that transcribes consonants and a few vowels, not all vowels.
- **Abugida**: A script where diacritics mark vowels (here, used for Mandarin).
- **Nabataean alphabet**: Aramaic-derived script of the Nabataeans, ancestor of Arabic script.
- **Perso-Arabic script**: The modified Arabic script originally devised for Persian.
- **Jawi**: Arabic script adaptation for Malay, co-official in Brunei and some Malaysian states.
- **Pegon**: Arabic script variant for Javanese, Madurese, and Sundanese, used in Islamic schools.
- **ʻAjamī**: Arabic-based orthographies of African languages; from Arabic root for "foreign."
- **Garshuni (Karshuni)**: Writing Arabic in Syriac letters, originating in the 7th century.
- **Xiao'erjing**: Arabic-derived alphabet for Chinese (and Dungan) among Hui people.
- **Wadaad's writing**: Arabic script adaptation used in Somalia.

## Debates and open questions

- The direct derivation of the Arabic alphabet is debated: from Nabataean (widely believed) or directly from Syriac (less widely believed).
- The Nobiin language is not yet standardized, being written variously in Latinized and Arabic scripts, with recent efforts to revive the Old Nubian alphabet.
- Use of Arabic script for Berber languages carries political connotations versus competing Latin and Tifinagh scripts.
- Yoruba's earliest attested use is 17th century, but earliest verifiable use dates to the 19th century.
- Ottoman Turkish is held by many to be a different language from modern Turkish due to its higher percentage of Persian and Arabic loanwords.
