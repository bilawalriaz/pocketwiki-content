# Niger River

## Overview
The Niger River is the principal river of West Africa, stretching about 4,180 km (2,600 miles) in a distinctive boomerang shape from the Guinea Highlands to the Gulf of Guinea. It is the 14th-longest river in the world and the third-longest in Africa, after the Nile and Congo. Its basin covers 2,117,700 km² across ten countries, making it a critical ecological and economic lifeline for the region, supporting agriculture, fishing, trade, and major historical empires.

## Timeline
- **c. 3500 BC** — End of the African humid period triggers desertification of the Sahara, pushing human settlement toward the fertile Niger bend.
- **c. 2000 BC** — Domestication of key crops like yams, African rice, and pearl millet begins in the Niger River region.
- **c. 4th century BC** — Classical writer Ptolemy mentions the "Nigir" river, though its exact location was speculative.
- **14th century CE** — Islam spreads to the Niger bend region via trans-Saharan trade routes.
- **1550** — Leo Africanus publishes *Description of Africa*, using the name "Niger" for the river in European literature.
- **1796** — Scottish explorer Mungo Park becomes the first European since antiquity to see the middle Niger.
- **1830** — Richard Lander and his brother become the first Europeans to trace the Niger's course to the ocean.
- **1946-1947** — French expedition led by Jean Sauvy, Pierre Ponty, and Jean Rouch travels the river's full length.
- **Late 20th century** — Construction of major dams (e.g., Sélingué in Mali, Kainji in Nigeria) for hydropower and irrigation.

## Body

### Etymology and Historical Knowledge
The river has many local names, such as *Joliba* ("great river" in Manding) and *Kwara* (Hausa). The name "Niger" first appears in European texts with Leo Africanus in 1550, possibly derived from a Berber phrase meaning "river of rivers." For centuries, Europeans were confused about its course, often linking it to the Nile or Senegal River. The true path was only confirmed by Mungo Park's expedition in 1796 and fully mapped by the Lander brothers in 1830. The modern nations of Niger and Nigeria derive their names from the river.

### Geography and Hydrology
The Niger has an unusual boomerang-shaped course, believed to result from the ancient upper Niger being captured by the lower Niger. It flows northeast into the Sahara before turning southeast toward the Gulf of Guinea. A key feature is the Inner Niger Delta, a vast area of braided streams and marshes where the river's gradient drops sharply. The river loses nearly two-thirds of its flow here to evaporation and seepage. Its main tributary is the Benue River, which joins it in Nigeria. The river carries relatively little sediment due to its source in ancient rocks.

### Human and Ecological Importance
The fertile Niger bend region became a center for early agriculture and settlement, such as Djenné-Djenno. The river's course was vital for trans-Saharan trade, fueling the Ghana, Mali, and Songhai Empires and spreading Islam. Today, the basin supports millions through fishing, farming, and pastoralism, especially in the Inner Delta. However, it faces pressures from increased water abstraction for irrigation, dam construction, climate change, and pollution. Management bodies like the Niger Basin Authority work to promote sustainable use.

## Terms
- **Drainage basin**: The area of land where all water drains into a single river system.
- **Inner Niger Delta**: A large inland delta in Mali where the river spreads into marshes and lakes, highly productive for fishing and agriculture.
- **Sahel**: The semi-arid region south of the Sahara Desert that the Niger River flows through.
- **Trans-Saharan trade**: Historic trade routes crossing the Sahara Desert, connecting West Africa with North Africa and the Mediterranean.
- **Stream capture**: A geological process where a river erodes and diverts the flow of another river.
- **Hydropower**: Electricity generated from the energy of falling or flowing water, typically via dams.
- **Distributary**: A stream that branches off and flows away from a main river channel, common in deltas.

## Debates and Open questions
- The origin of the name "Niger" is debated; it may derive from a Berber phrase, a Tuareg name, or a misinterpretation of classical texts.
- The exact course of the river described by ancient writers like Ptolemy and Pliny is uncertain, with theories linking it to the modern Niger, the Senegal River, or other watercourses.
- The Niger River's unusual course was a major geographical mystery for centuries, with incorrect theories (like a connection to the Nile or Congo) persisting until the 19th century.
