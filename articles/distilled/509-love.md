# Love

## Overview
Love is a complex range of strong positive emotional and mental states, spanning from sublime virtue and deep interpersonal affection to simple pleasure. It functions as a major facilitator of human relationships and a central theme in the creative arts, postulated by evolutionary theory to bind humans against menaces and ensure species continuation. The concept resists a single universal definition due to diverse cultural expressions (e.g., Greek *agape*, Chinese *Ren*, Sanskrit *bhakti*), psychological models (triangular theory, color wheel theory), and biological mechanisms involving distinct neurochemical stages. Philosophically, love oscillates between altruistic concern for another's good and narcissistic self-interest, while religious traditions universally position it as a divine attribute or the highest spiritual goal.

## Timeline
- **c. 4th century BCE** — Chinese philosopher Mozi develops concept of *jiān'ài* (universal love) in reaction to Confucian *Ren*.
- **c. 380–322 BCE** — Aristotle analyzes *philia* (virtuous friendship) in *Nicomachean Ethics*; Plato refines *eros* as a ladder to spiritual truth.
- **1st century CE** — New Testament texts establish *agape* as selfless, divine love; *phileo* as brotherly love.
- **c. 400 CE** — Augustine of Hippo distinguishes love (*caritas*) from lust (*cupiditas*) in *De Doctrina Christiana*.
- **13th century** — Thomas Aquinas defines love as "to will the good of another," synthesizing Aristotle with Christian theology.
- **12th–14th century** — Courtly love conventions flourish in medieval Europe; historians often date modern romantic love concepts here.
- **1970s** — Psychologist Zick Rubin develops psychometric scales identifying attachment, caring, and intimacy as love's factors.
- **1986** — Robert Sternberg publishes triangular theory of love (intimacy, passion, commitment).
- **1990s** — Helen Fisher proposes three independent brain systems for mating: lust, attraction, attachment.
- **2005** — Enzo Emanuele identifies elevated nerve growth factor (NGF) in early-stage romantic love, returning to baseline after one year.

## Body

### Definitions and Conceptual Scope
The English word "love" covers a semantic range that other languages parse into distinct terms. Ancient Greek identifies six forms: *storge* (familial), *philia* (platonic/friendly), *eros* (romantic), *philautia* (self-love), *xenia* (guest-friendship/hospitality), and *agape* (divine/unconditional). Modern psychology adds varieties like unrequited, companionate, consummate, and infatuated love. Culturally unique concepts include Chinese *Ren* (benevolent duty), *Yuanfen* (predestined affinity), Japanese *amae* (desire for indulgent dependence), Sanskrit *bhakti* (devotional love), and Arabic *Ishq* (divine passion). Definitional strategies often rely on antonyms: love contrasts with hate (apathy), lust (sexual drive without intimacy), and friendship (though "platonic love" blurs this). Philosophical definitions vary: Aquinas calls it "willing the good of another"; Russell, a condition of "absolute value"; Leibniz, "delight in another's happiness"; Fromm, an active commitment rather than a feeling; Griffith, "unconditional selflessness."

### Impersonal and Interpersonal Love
Impersonal love denotes profound dedication to objects, principles, causes, or animals, driven by altruism, spiritual conviction, or identification; if sexual passion attaches to non-human objects, it is termed *paraphilia*. Interpersonal love—between humans—is far more potent than liking. It encompasses family, friends, and couples, with *unrequited love* describing non-reciprocated feelings. Disorders like *erotomania* (delusional belief another loves you) represent pathological extremes. Historically, philosophy and religion dominated love's study; the 20th century added psychology, anthropology, neuroscience, and biology.

### Biological Basis: Three Brain Systems
Anthropologist Helen Fisher models romantic love as a mammalian drive system with three independent but interacting brain circuits:
1.  **Lust**: Sexual desire driven by testosterone and estrogen; promotes mating indiscriminately; lasts weeks to months.
2.  **Attraction**: Focused romantic desire for a specific partner; conserves mating energy. Neurochemistry involves high dopamine and norepinephrine (amphetamine-like stimulation: euphoria, insomnia, appetite loss) and low serotonin (obsessive focus). Duration: 1.5 to 3 years.
3.  **Attachment**: Long-term bonding for parental duties and mutual defense; mediated by oxytocin and vasopressin. Associated with calm, security, and stability lasting decades.
Research by Enzo Emanuele shows nerve growth factor (NGF) spikes during early attraction but normalizes after one year, marking the transition from attraction to attachment.

### Psychological Theories
**Triangular Theory (Sternberg)**: Love comprises three components—*intimacy* (sharing confidences), *passion* (physical/romantic drive), and *commitment* (decision to maintain the relationship). Their combinations yield eight types: non-love (none), liking (intimacy only), infatuation (passion only), empty love (commitment only), romantic love (intimacy + passion), companionate love (intimacy + commitment), fatuous love (passion + commitment), and consummate love (all three).
**Rubin’s Psychometrics (1970s)**: Identifies attachment, caring, and intimacy as measurable factors.
**Similarity vs. Complementarity**: Despite the "opposites attract" analogy from Coulomb's law, mating research shows people prefer similar character and personality; exceptions exist in immune system genetics (MHC), where dissimilarity may optimize offspring immunity.
**Human Bonding Theories**: Frame love as attachments, ties, bonds, and affinities.
**Altruism vs. Narcissism**: Scott Peck views love as "concern for the spiritual growth of another" mixed with narcissism—an activity, not just a feeling. Erich Fromm (*The Art of Loving*) argues love is primarily a conscious choice and series of actions; the feeling is superficial and involuntary initially, but mature love depends solely on commitment to loving actions over time.

### Evolutionary and Adaptive Foundations
Evolutionary psychology frames love as a survival tool. Human infants require extended parental care compared to other mammals; love bonds parents to offspring for this duration. Darwin noted love creates social support systems enabling human expansion. Monogamy may be favored over polygamy because sexually transmitted diseases cause reduced fertility, fetal injury, and childbirth complications.
**Adaptive Benefit of Sexual Reproduction (Michod)**: Interpersonal love facilitates mating. Sexual reproduction (vs. asexual) offers two advantages: (1) DNA repair during meiosis fixes parental damage before transmission; (2) harmful mutations from one parent are masked by unaffected homologous genes from the other (heterozygosity advantage).

### Health Correlates
Abraham Maslow placed feeling truly loved as a foundational human need (belongingness). Love correlates strongly with happiness, relationship building, and community development. Conversely, feeling unloved correlates with adverse outcomes like post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).

### Cultural Views
**Ancient Greek**: Four primary terms (often overlapping): *storge* (natural parental affection), *philia* (Aristotelian virtuous friendship requiring equality and mutual benefit), *eros* (passionate longing; Plato: ladder from physical beauty to beauty itself and spiritual truth), *agape* (pure, ideal, "love of the soul"). *Xenia* (ritualized guest-friendship) was a sacred social institution in Homeric epics.
**Ancient Roman (Latin)**: *Amare* covers affectionate, romantic, and sexual senses; *amans* (lover), *amator* (professional lover/lecher), *amica* (girlfriend/euphemism for prostitute). *Amor* (love affairs) anagrams to *Roma*, the city's secret name. *Diligere* implies esteem/affection (suitable for male friendship); *caritas* (charitable love) appears only in Christian Latin.
**Chinese/Sinic**: Confucian *Ren* (仁) emphasizes duty/action/attitude (filial piety, loyalty) over feeling. Mohist *jiān'ài* (兼愛, 4th c. BCE) counters Confucian graded love with universal, unconditional, equal care for all. Buddhist *Ai* (愛) denotes passionate caring, capable of selfish or selfless (enlightening) forms. Modern Mandarin *ài* carries heavy responsibility/commitment; casual affection uses *xǐhuan* ("I like you").
**Japanese**: Three terms: *ai* (maternal/selfless, religious beauty origin; post-Meiji 1868 mapped to Western "love"), *koi* (romantic longing, originally lonely solitude; Man'yōshū poetry; modern: sexual/infatuation), *ren'ai* (modern compound of *ai* + *koi*, romantic love). *Amae* (甘え, desire for indulgent dependence on authority) analyzed by Takeo Doi (*The Anatomy of Dependence*).
**Indian**: Sanskrit *sneha* (love), *priya* (innocent), *prema* (spiritual), *kama* (sensory/sexual/aesthetic pleasure; personified by Kamadeva; third life goal *purushartha*). *Bhakti* (loving devotion to God) has nine or eleven forms (Bhagavata Purana, Narada Bhakti Sutra). Gaudiya Vaishnavism prizes *Krishna-prema* (love for Godhead) as supreme goal—transcending liberation, manifesting as *sambhoga* (union) and *vipralambha* (separation). Radha’s love for Krishna is the paradigm.
**Persian**: *Ishq* (divine love, from Arabic) is the ultimate life goal, encompassing human and divine love. Sufi poets (Rumi, Hafiz, Sa'di) depict the soul's return to God through love; God as Lover, Loved, Beloved.

### Religious Views
**Judaism**: *Ahava* (interpersonal and divine love); *Chesed* (loving-kindness). Torah commands: "Love your neighbor as yourself" (Lev 19:18) and "Love God with all heart/soul/might" (Deut 6:5), interpreted by Mishnah as good deeds, martyrdom readiness, property sacrifice, and gratitude. Marriage love is essential (Eccl 9:9). Rabbi Dessler: "giving without expecting to take."
**Christianity**: God *is* love (1 John 4:8). *Agape* (selfless, divine, parental) and *eros* (human romantic) are "ascending" and "descending" but ultimately unified. Greatest commandments: love God, love neighbor (Mark 12:28–34). Paul’s hymn (1 Cor 13): love is patient, kind, not envious/boastful/proud/rude/self-seeking/angry; keeps no record of wrongs; rejoices in truth; always protects/trusts/hopes/perseveres. Augustine: only God loves perfectly; human love flawed by jealousy/fear. Aquinas: "to will the good of another." Enemy love (Matt 5:43–48) distinguishes Christians (Tertullian). Pope Francis: Cross is supreme love; key is allowing oneself to be loved by God.
**Islam**: Universal brotherhood. Allah is *Al-Wadud* (the Loving One, Qur'an 11:90, 85:14); *Ar-Rahman/Ar-Rahim* (Most Compassionate/Merciful) open every surah. *Birr* (deep kindness) mandated toward non-persecutors and parents. Sufism emphasizes *Ishq* (divine love): love projects God's essence into universe; seeing beauty in ugliness reflects God; "drunk" saints use wine imagery for divine intoxication.
**Bahá'í**: `Abdu'l-Bahá identifies four loves: God→human, human→God, God→God's Self, human→human.
**Buddhism**: *Kāma* (sensuous/sexual) is an obstacle (selfish). *Karuṇā* (compassion/mercy) reduces suffering; necessary for enlightenment with wisdom. *Adveṣa/mettā* (benevolent/unconditional love) requires self-acceptance; distinct from attachment-based ordinary love. Bodhisattva ideal: total self-renunciation for suffering world.
**Hinduism**: *Kāma* (pleasurable/sexual, god Kamadeva) is third life goal. *Prema* (elevated love). *Karuṇā* (compassion). *Bhakti* (devotion to supreme God) has nine (Bhagavata Purana) or eleven (Narada Bhakti Sutra) forms. Gaudiya Vaishnavism: *Krishna-prema* burns material desires, pierces heart, grants transcendental ecstasy; Radha-Krishna reciprocal love is supreme paradigm. Love for God is perfection; liberation is mere by-product.

### Political Views: Free Love
The "free love" movement rejects marriage as social bondage, seeking state separation from sexual matters (marriage, birth control, adultery). It arose in contrast to 19th-century middle-class ideals of stable home and rigid gender roles. Core tenets: opposition to forceful sex in relationships; advocacy for women's bodily autonomy—aligning with feminism.

### Philosophical Views
Philosophy of love (social philosophy/ethics) investigates: distinguishing love types; justifying love; love's value; impact on autonomy of lover and beloved.

## Terms
- ****Agape**** — Greek: selfless, unconditional, divine love; parental love creating goodness; Christian ideal for human relations.
- ****Eros**** — Greek: passionate, romantic, sensual desire; in Plato, a ladder from physical beauty to contemplation of Beauty itself and spiritual truth.
- ****Philia**** — Greek: dispassionate virtuous friendship (Aristotle); requires loyalty, virtue, equality, familiarity; motivated by mutual benefit.
- ****Storge**** — Greek: natural familial affection, especially parental.
- ****Xenia**** — Greek: ritualized guest-friendship/hospitality; sacred bond between host and stranger (Homeric epics).
- ****Ren (仁)**** — Confucian: "benevolent love" expressed through duty, action, and attitude (filial piety, loyalty) rather than feeling alone.
- ****Jiān'ài (兼愛)**** — Mohist: "universal love"—unconditional, equal care for all people, rejecting Confucian graded affection.
- ****Amae (甘え)**** — Japanese: desire to be loved, indulged, and cared for by an authority figure; key to Japanese dependency psychology (Doi).
- ****Bhakti**** — Sanskrit: loving devotion to the supreme God; practice yields love for God (*prema*) and liberation.
- ****Triangular Theory of Love**** — Sternberg's model: love = intimacy + passion + commitment; combinations yield eight types (e.g., consummate = all three).
- ****Nerve Growth Factor (NGF)**** — Protein elevated in early romantic love (attraction stage); returns to baseline after ~1 year (Emanuele et al.).

## Debates and open questions
- **Universality vs. Cultural Relativity**: Can a universal definition of love exist given radically different cultural lexicons (Greek *agape/eros*, Chinese *Ren/jiān'ài*, Japanese *ai/koi/amae*, Sanskrit *kama/prema/bhakti*) and historical shifts (courtly love as modern romance origin)?
- **Feeling vs. Action**: Is love fundamentally an involuntary emotion (Fisher’s biological drive, "falling in love") or a voluntary commitment/action (Fromm, Aquinas, Peck, Rabbi Wolpe)?
- **Altruism vs. Egoism**: Does love transcend self-interest (Leibniz, *agape*, *karuṇā*, *bhakti*) or necessarily incorporate narcissism (Peck, evolutionary gene-propagation models)?
- **Nature of Romantic Love**: Is it a cultural invention (medieval courtly Europe) or a human universal with biological substrates (Fisher’s three systems, 1.5–3 year attraction window)?
- **Love and Autonomy**: How does love affect the autonomy of lover and beloved? (Philosophical ethics: does love enhance or diminish agency?)
- **Health Causality**: Does love cause happiness/health, or do healthy/happy people attract love? (Correlation noted; direction debated.)
- **Sexual vs. Non-Sexual Boundaries**: Where does love end and lust/paraphilia begin? (Augustine’s distinction; *philia* vs. *eros*; impersonal love vs. paraphilia.)
