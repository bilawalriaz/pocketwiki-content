# Indian art

## Overview
Indian art encompasses a vast array of forms, including painting, sculpture, pottery, and textiles, spanning South Asia. It is characterized by a strong sense of design and has been profoundly shaped by religious traditions like Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Islam, while also influencing art across Asia. Its study reveals a continuous dialogue between indigenous styles and external influences, making it a key to understanding the region's cultural and historical development.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BCE** — Earliest rock art at Bhimbetka shelters.
- **c. 3300–1750 BCE** — Indus Valley Civilisation produces seals and small figurines.
- **c. 322–185 BCE** — Mauryan Empire; Ashoka's pillars and early monumental sculpture.
- **2nd century BCE** — Creation of colossal Yaksha statuary, first large stone anthropomorphic figures.
- **c. 150 BCE–500 CE** — Major Buddhist art flourishes at stupas like Sanchi and in rock-cut caves.
- **c. 320–550 CE** — Gupta period, a classical peak for sculpture and temple architecture.
- **c. 600–1206 CE** — Regional Hindu temple styles mature in South, East, and North India.
- **1526–1707 CE** — Mughal Empire fosters a synthesis of Persian and Indian painting styles.
- **Early 20th century** — Bengal School of Art emerges, promoting nationalist and pan-Asian styles.
- **1947–present** — Post-independence contemporary art movements, like the Progressive Artists' Group.

## Body

### Early and Ancient Art
The earliest Indian art consists of prehistoric rock paintings, such as those at Bhimbetka, depicting human and animal scenes. The Indus Valley Civilisation (c. 3300–1750 BCE) is noted for small, sophisticated seals and figurines, but a gap in artistic sophistication followed its collapse. Monumental stone sculpture began with the Mauryan Empire (c. 322–185 BCE), exemplified by the Pillars of Ashoka. In the 2nd century BCE, colossal Yaksha statues marked the first large-scale anthropomorphic stone carvings, showing possible Hellenistic influence.

### Buddhist, Hindu, and Jain Art
Buddhist art became a major force from c. 150 BCE, with elaborately carved stupas at Sanchi and rock-cut caves. The Kushan Empire (c. 30–375 CE) saw the first human depictions of the Buddha, blending Indian and Greco-Roman styles in Gandhara. The Gupta period (c. 320–550 CE) is considered a classical golden age, establishing iconic forms for Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain deities. In the medieval period, Hindu temple architecture and sculpture flourished in regional styles across South India (e.g., Chola bronzes), Odisha, and Karnataka.

### Islamic and Colonial Influences
The Mughal Empire (1526–1707) developed a distinctive painting style that fused Persian techniques with Indian realism. As Mughal power waned, regional schools like Rajput and Pahari painting thrived. British colonial rule (1857–1947) introduced Western art education and the hybrid "Company style." In response, the early 20th-century Bengal School of Art, led by Abanindranath Tagore, sought to revive pre-colonial Indian aesthetics and foster a nationalist art movement.

### Modern and Contemporary Art
Post-independence, the Bombay Progressive Artists' Group (founded 1952) aimed to create a new, modern Indian idiom free from colonial and traditional constraints. Contemporary Indian art is diverse, with artists exploring new media and global themes while often engaging with folk traditions and social commentary. Critical discourse has also evolved, with scholars like R. Siva Kumar proposing concepts like "Contextual Modernism" to describe an alternative, transcultural modernity that emerged in places like Santiniketan.

## Terms
*   **Stupa:** A Buddhist religious monument, usually a domed mound, built to enshrine relics and serve as a focus for worship.
*   **Torana:** An ornamental gateway, often elaborately carved, facing the cardinal directions around a stupa.
*   **Yaksha/Yakshini:** A broad class of benevolent nature-spirits in Indian mythology, associated with fertility and wilderness, later incorporated into major religions.
*   **Greco-Buddhist art:** An artistic style that developed in Gandhara (modern Afghanistan/Pakistan) from the 1st century BCE, blending Hellenistic Greek and Buddhist Indian elements.
*   **Gopuram:** A monumental, ornately sculpted entrance tower, typical of South Indian Hindu temples.
*   **Company style:** A hybrid painting style developed in the 18th-19th centuries by Indian artists for European patrons, combining Indian and Western techniques.
*   **Bengal School of Art:** An early 20th-century Indian art movement that rejected Western academic styles in favor of reviving traditional Indian and Asian aesthetics.
*   **Contextual Modernism:** A term describing an alternative, transcultural modernism in Indian art that engaged with both local traditions and global influences, as seen in the Santiniketan artists.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The religious or cultic significance of the "unicorn" animal depicted on many Indus Valley seals is debated due to insufficient evidence.
*   The exact cultural and periodization of artifacts from the Copper Hoard culture (2nd millennium BCE) is subject to varying interpretations.
*   The origins of anthropomorphic deity depictions in the mid-1st millennium BCE are debated, with possible influences from the Achaemenid conquest and the rise of new faiths like Buddhism.
*   The classification of Santiniketan artists under the "Bengal School" is considered misleading by some scholars, who argue their style and worldview were distinct.
