# Pierre Curie

## Overview
Pierre Curie (1859–1906) was a French physicist and chemist who made foundational contributions to crystallography, magnetism, and the study of radioactivity. He shared the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics with his wife, Marie Curie, and Henri Becquerel, becoming part of the first married couple to win a Nobel Prize and launching a family legacy of five Nobel laureates.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Pierre Curie was educated at home before entering the University of Paris, where he earned his physics degree in 1878. He worked as a laboratory demonstrator until 1882, when he joined ESPCI Paris. He later received his doctorate in 1895 for his research on magnetism and was appointed Professor of Physics at the University of Paris.

### Scientific Research
Curie's early work with his brother Jacques on piezoelectricity—the generation of an electric potential from compressed crystals—led to the invention of the piezoelectric electrometer, a tool crucial for later radioactivity research. His doctoral work on magnetism defined the Curie scale and led to the discovery of Curie's law (the effect of temperature on paramagnetism) and the Curie temperature (the critical point where ferromagnetic substances lose their magnetism). He formulated the Curie Dissymmetry Principle, stating that a physical effect cannot have a dissymmetry absent from its efficient cause.

With his wife Marie, Curie pioneered the study of radioactivity, a term they coined. They isolated polonium and radium, and their work was honored with the 1903 Nobel Prize. With his student Albert Laborde, Curie made the first discovery of nuclear energy by identifying continuous heat emission from radium. He also used magnetic fields to classify radiation emissions into positively charged, negative, and neutral streams, corresponding to alpha, beta, and gamma radiation.

### Personal Life and Spiritualism
Curie was introduced to Marie Skłodowska by a mutual friend and married her in 1895. They had two daughters, Irène and Ève. In the late 19th century, Curie investigated spiritualist experiments, viewing them as potential physics questions rather than supernatural phenomena. He attended séances as a scientific experiment, taking detailed notes, though he considered himself an atheist.

### Death and Legacy
Curie died instantly on 19 April 1906 after slipping under a horse-drawn cart in Paris. He and Marie had both suffered radiation sickness and burns from their research. Their papers and even cookbooks remain radioactive and are stored in lead-lined boxes. In 1995, the Curies were enshrined in the Panthéon. His family continued his scientific legacy: daughter Irène and son-in-law Frédéric Joliot-Curie won a Nobel Prize in Chemistry, and granddaughter Hélène Langevin-Joliot is a nuclear physics professor.

## Terms
- **Piezoelectricity**: The electric potential generated when certain crystals are compressed.
- **Ferromagnetism**: Strong magnetism produced by certain materials, like iron.
- **Paramagnetism**: Weak magnetism induced in materials by an external magnetic field.
- **Curie's Law**: The principle that the magnetic susceptibility of a paramagnetic material is inversely proportional to its temperature.
- **Curie Temperature**: The critical temperature above which a ferromagnetic material becomes paramagnetic.
- **Curie Dissymmetry Principle**: A physical effect cannot exhibit a dissymmetry (asymmetry) that is not present in its cause.
- **Radioactivity**: The spontaneous emission of radiation from unstable atomic nuclei.
- **Alpha, Beta, Gamma Radiation**: The three main types of radiation emitted by radioactive substances, classified by their charge and penetrating power.
