# George Washington Carver

## Overview
George Washington Carver (c. 1864–1943) was a pioneering African American agricultural scientist and inventor who promoted sustainable farming for poor Southern farmers. His work on crop rotation and alternative crops like peanuts and sweet potatoes aimed to restore depleted soils and improve livelihoods, making him one of the most prominent Black scientists of the early 20th century.

## Timeline
- **c. 1864** — Born into slavery in Diamond Grove, Missouri.
- **1891** — Enrolled at Iowa State Agricultural College, becoming its first Black student.
- **1896** — Received MSc in Botany; joined Tuskegee Institute to head Agriculture Department.
- **1916** — Published popular bulletin on peanut cultivation and uses.
- **1921** — Testified before U.S. Congress on peanut tariff, gaining national fame.
- **1923** — Awarded NAACP Spingarn Medal.
- **1938** — Established George Washington Carver Foundation at Tuskegee.
- **1943** — Died January 5 from complications of a fall; buried at Tuskegee.
- **1943** — U.S. Congress designated January 5 as George Washington Carver Recognition Day.
- **1953** — George Washington Carver National Monument opened in Missouri.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born into slavery, Carver was orphaned as an infant and raised by his former enslavers after emancipation. Denied public schooling due to his race, he pursued education independently, eventually earning a high school diploma. He faced racial barriers at colleges before studying art at Simpson College, where a teacher encouraged his botanical talent. He transferred to Iowa State Agricultural College, earning a bachelor's (1894) and master's (1896) in agriculture and botany, becoming the institution's first Black faculty member.

### Career at Tuskegee Institute
In 1896, Booker T. Washington recruited Carver to lead Tuskegee's Agriculture Department. Carver taught crop rotation and promoted nitrogen-fixing crops like peanuts and soybeans to restore soil depleted by cotton monoculture. He developed a mobile classroom, the "Jesup wagon," to educate farmers. Despite administrative clashes with Washington, Carver focused on research, publishing over 40 practical bulletins with recipes and farming advice. His work aimed to help poor farmers achieve self-sufficiency.

### Scientific Work and Rise to Fame
Carver's research emphasized chemurgy—developing industrial and food products from crops. He advocated for peanut and sweet potato cultivation as alternatives to cotton. His 1921 Congressional testimony on peanut uses, showcasing products like dyes and flours, brought widespread attention. He advised figures like Henry Ford and three U.S. presidents. While popular media exaggerated his impact on the peanut industry, his core mission was soil conservation and farmer education, not commercial invention.

### Personal Life and Legacy
Carver never married and was deeply religious, integrating faith with science. He was known for his high-pitched voice, likely from childhood illnesses. Frugal in life, he donated his savings to establish a museum and foundation at Tuskegee. He received numerous honors, including the Spingarn Medal and a posthumous doctorate. The first U.S. national monument dedicated to an African American was established in his honor in 1943.

## Terms
- **Crop Rotation**: Alternating different crops in a sequence on the same land to improve soil health and reduce pests.
- **Chemurgy**: The branch of applied chemistry concerned with preparing industrial products from agricultural raw materials.
- **Nitrogen Fixation**: The process by which certain crops (like legumes) convert atmospheric nitrogen into a form plants can use, enriching the soil.
- **Monoculture**: The agricultural practice of growing a single crop repeatedly in the same area, which can deplete soil nutrients.
- **Spingarn Medal**: An annual award given by the NAACP for outstanding achievement by an African American.
- **Jesup Wagon**: A mobile classroom designed by Carver to bring agricultural education directly to rural farmers.
- **Appropriate Technology**: A movement promoting small-scale, locally controlled technologies suited to the needs of communities, foreshadowed by Carver's work for subsistence farmers.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Scope of Inventions**: Popular folklore often credits Carver with inventing peanut butter and hundreds of peanut products. Scholarly analysis suggests he compiled and promoted existing recipes and uses rather than personally discovering all of them, and he did not keep detailed laboratory records.
- **Impact on Peanut Industry**: Some media claimed Carver was "almost solely responsible" for the rise in U.S. peanut production after the boll weevil. Historians note this is an exaggeration; his primary goal was soil restoration and farmer self-sufficiency, not transforming the commercial peanut industry.
- **Administrative Skills**: Booker T. Washington's writings indicate he believed Carver lacked ability in farm management and school organization, though he praised his scientific and teaching talents. This highlights a tension between Carver's research focus and institutional administrative demands.
