# Johannes Brahms

## Overview
Johannes Brahms (1833–1897) was a German composer, pianist, and conductor whose music synthesized the structural rigor of the Viennese Classical tradition—particularly Beethoven and Bach—with the expressive harmonic language of the mid-Romantic period. Though positioned as a conservative figure in the "War of the Romantics" against the New German School of Liszt and Wagner, his works employed innovative techniques such as "developing variation" (motivic-harmonic transformation) and complex rhythmic displacement, which later modernists like Arnold Schoenberg hailed as progressive. His output centers on four symphonies, four concertos, the *German Requiem*, extensive chamber music, and hundreds of *Lieder* (German art songs) and folk arrangements. Brahms's career was shaped by early mentorship from Robert and Clara Schumann, a lifelong devotion to Clara, and a meticulous, self-critical compositional process that led him to destroy many early works. He settled in Vienna, where he directed major musical institutions and programmed early music, cementing a legacy that bridges the Classical past and the Second Viennese School.

## Timeline
- **7 May 1833** — Born in Hamburg to Johann Jakob Brahms (musician) and Johanna Henrika Christiane Nissen.
- **1845–1848** — Studies piano and composition with Eduard Marxsen, who grounds him in Bach, Mozart, Haydn, and Beethoven.
- **1853** — Concert tour with Ede Reményi; meets Joseph Joachim, who introduces him to Franz Liszt in Weimar and Robert and Clara Schumann in Düsseldorf.
- **Oct 1853** — Robert Schumann publishes "Neue Bahnen," hailing Brahms as the future of German music; Brahms moves to Düsseldorf to support Clara after Robert's confinement.
- **1857–1860** — Court musician in Detmold; writes two Serenades and works on Piano Concerto No. 1.
- **1859** — Premiere of Piano Concerto No. 1 in Hamburg fails disastrously; engagement to Agathe von Siebold broken off.
- **1862** — Moves to Vienna; appointed conductor of Wiener Singakademie (resigns 1864).
- **1865–1868** — Composes *A German Requiem*, Op. 45 (premiered complete in Bremen 1868), establishing international fame.
- **1869** — Publishes first set of *Hungarian Dances*; completes Alto Rhapsody, Op. 53, amid unrequited feelings for Julie Schumann.
- **1872–1875** — Directs Vienna Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde concerts, programming early and "serious" repertoire.
- **1876** — Premieres Symphony No. 1, Op. 68 (begun c. 1855), dubbed "Beethoven's Tenth" by Hans von Bülow.
- **1877–1885** — Premieres Symphonies No. 2, 3, and 4; Violin Concerto (1878); Piano Concerto No. 2 (1882).
- **1889** — Makes experimental cylinder recording for Thomas Edison's representative; named honorary citizen of Hamburg.
- **1890–1894** — Late creative surge inspired by clarinetist Richard Mühlfeld: Clarinet Trio, Quintet, and two Sonatas.
- **1896** — Completes *Vier ernste Gesänge* (Four Serious Songs) and Eleven Chorale Preludes, Op. 122 (final works).
- **3 April 1897** — Dies in Vienna of liver/pancreatic cancer; buried in Vienna Central Cemetery.

## Body

### Youth and Training (1833–1853)
Brahms was born into a struggling musical family in Hamburg. His father, Johann Jakob, played double bass, horn, and flute in the city orchestra. Johannes received his first training from his father, then studied piano with Otto Cossel and, from 1845, with Eduard Marxsen. Marxsen, a devotee of J.S. Bach and a personal acquaintance of Beethoven and Schubert, instilled a rigorous grounding in counterpoint and Classical form. Brahms gave public recitals by 1847 and composed prolifically, though he later destroyed nearly all juvenilia. In 1850, he met Hungarian violinist Ede Reményi, who introduced him to "gypsy-style" music (the *csárdás*), later the basis for his lucrative *Hungarian Dances*. A failed attempt to contact Robert Schumann in 1850 preceded a 1853 tour with Reményi that brought Brahms to Joseph Joachim in Hanover. Joachim, overwhelmed by Brahms's talent, became a lifelong friend and the crucial link to the Schumanns.

### The Schumann Circle and Early Crisis (1853–1862)
In October 1853, Brahms visited Düsseldorf with a letter from Joachim. Robert Schumann, deeply impressed, published "Neue Bahnen" ("New Paths") in the *Neue Zeitschrift für Musik*, declaring the 20-year-old destined to express the times ideally. This accolade intensified Brahms's innate self-criticism; he wrote to Schumann fearing he could not fulfill such expectations. Weeks later, Robert attempted suicide and was confined to an asylum (dying 1856). Brahms based himself in Düsseldorf to manage household affairs and act as intermediary for Clara, who was barred from visiting Robert until his deathbed. A profound, emotionally intense bond formed between Brahms and Clara, but he resisted a romantic union, choosing bachelorhood to focus on his art. Professionally, the period was turbulent: the 1859 premiere of his Piano Concerto No. 1 in Hamburg was hissed, causing his publisher Breitkopf & Härtel to reject new works. In 1860, Brahms and Joachim drafted a manifesto against the "New German School" (Liszt/Wagner), which was leaked and parodied, humiliating Brahms and ending his public polemics. He also lost the Hamburg Philharmonic conductorship to Julius Stockhausen in 1862.

### Vienna, the Requiem, and Breakthrough (1862–1876)
Brahms settled in Vienna in 1862, directing the Wiener Singakademie (1863–64) and programming early masters like Schütz, Gabrieli, and Bach. He met Wagner (cordially) and joined a circle including critic Eduard Hanslick, conductor Hermann Levi, and surgeon Theodor Billroth. The death of his mother in 1865 catalyzed *A German Requiem*, Op. 45, a non-liturgical setting of Luther Bible texts reflecting his humanist/agnostic views (Antonín Dvořák later marveled: "He believes in nothing!"). Premiered complete in Bremen (1868) with a seventh movement added for Leipzig (1869), the work secured his international reputation. Popular success followed with the *Hungarian Dances* (1869) and *Liebeslieder Waltzes*. During this decade, Brahms completed long-gestating works: the cantata *Rinaldo*, first two string quartets, third piano quartet, and, most significantly, Symphony No. 1, which he had wrestled with since the 1850s. He also directed the Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde concerts (1872–75), enforcing professional standards and a "serious" repertoire excluding the New German School.

### High Maturity and Fame (1876–1889)
Symphony No. 1 (1876) was hailed as "Beethoven's Tenth" for its C-minor-to-C-major trajectory and finale theme echoing Beethoven's Ninth. Brahms extensively revised it post-premiere. A stream of masterpieces followed: Symphony No. 2 (1877), Violin Concerto (1878, for Joachim), Academic Festival and Tragic Overtures (1880), Piano Concerto No. 2 (1882, dedicated to Marxsen), Symphony No. 3 (1883), and Symphony No. 4 (1885). Hans von Bülow, leading the Meiningen Court Orchestra, championed him as the third "B" after Bach and Beethoven. Wagner mocked this, calling Brahms a "street-singer" masquerading as "Symphony Number Ten." Brahms received honors (Maximilian Order, 1874; Meiningen Commander's Cross, 1881) and served on juries awarding the Vienna State Prize to Dvořák (1875, 1876, 1877), recommending him to publisher Simrock. In 1889, he made a historic but noisy cylinder recording for Edison's agent Theo Wangemann and was named an honorary citizen of Hamburg.

### Final Years and Late Style (1889–1897)
Brahms formed a close friendship with Johann Strauss II in the 1890s, admiring his waltzes and facilitating his contract with Simrock. After the String Quintet No. 2 (1890), Brahms considered retiring at 57, but the playing of Meiningen clarinetist Richard Mühlfeld inspired a final flowering of chamber music: Clarinet Trio (1891), Clarinet Quintet (1891), and two Clarinet Sonatas (1894). The deaths of close friends—especially Clara Schumann (1896)—prompted the *Vier ernste Gesänge* (Four Serious Songs), Op. 121, and the Eleven Chorale Preludes for organ, Op. 122, the last a setting of "O Welt ich muss dich lassen" ("O world I must leave thee"). Diagnosed with jaundice, pancreatic, and liver cancer in 1896, Brahms attended a performance of his Fourth Symphony in March 1897 (ovation after each movement) and died on 3 April 1897 in Vienna.

### Musical Style: Developing Variation and Absolute Music
Brahms's language is rooted in Viennese Classicism but extends Beethovenian motivic-harmonic integration. Instead of expository modulating sequences, he employs **developing variation**—a process of continuous motivic-harmonic transformation that maintains large-scale tonal coherence while delaying or deflecting tonic resolution. Cast as **absolute music** (non-programmatic, valued for formal logic) against the New German School's program music, his works nonetheless integrate vocal melody: hundreds of *Lieder* and folk arrangements feed his instrumental themes. He venerated Beethoven (a bust overlooked his desk); Symphony No. 1 explicitly engages Beethoven's Fifth and Ninth. He revered Mozart (declaring him the greatest composer in 1896) and Haydn, and studied **Alte Musik** (early music): Palestrina, Gabrieli, Schütz, Handel, and especially Bach (the Fourth Symphony's passacaglia finale derives from Bach's Cantata No. 150). His textures are defined by masterful **counterpoint** (canon, fugue, inversion) used for lyrical expression, and sophisticated **rhythm and meter**—irregular phrases, hemiolas, and rubato influenced by Hungarian folk music—that Michael Musgrave likened to Stravinsky's advancement.

### Performance Practice and Perfectionism
Brahms was an extreme perfectionist who destroyed vast quantities of music (claiming 20 string quartets before Op. 51). He labored 15 years on Symphony No. 1 and transformed a symphonic project into Piano Concerto No. 1. He preferred German/Viennese pianos: a Baumgarten & Heins early on, a Streicher (Op. 6713, 1873–death) for composing ("There I always know exactly what I write"), a Conrad Graf (Clara's wedding gift), and Bösendorfer, Bechstein, Steinweg, and Blüthner for concerts. His manuscripts were vetted by a trusted circle (Clara, Joachim, Billroth, Elisabeth von Herzogenberg), who noted his "steely" or "cutting" dissonances, often placed on strong beats and resolving "lightly and gently."

### Reception: Conservative vs. Progressive
Brahms was criticized in his time as academic, dense, or muddy (even Hanslick initially called Symphony No. 1 academic). Gustav Mahler rated him technically superior to Bruckner but more "earth-bound" than Wagner. In the 20th century, **Arnold Schoenberg** spearheaded a reevaluation with "Brahms the Progressive" (1933/1947), arguing that Brahms's motivic saturation, rhythmic irregularity, and "enriched harmony" anticipated the Second Viennese School. Schoenberg highlighted **developing variation** as a forward-looking structural principle. Anton Webern saw Brahms's passacaglia techniques and fragment redistribution as prefiguring serialism. Composers from Max Reger and Alexander Zemlinsky (Schoenberg's teacher) to Milton Babbitt, György Ligeti, Wolfgang Rihm, and Thomas Adès have engaged deeply with his legacy. His symphonies remain second only to Beethoven's in performance frequency.

### Influence and Legacy
Brahms directly influenced his circle (Herzogenberg, Fuchs, Röntgen) and sole pupil Gustav Jenner. Dvořák absorbed his idiom (Symphony No. 7, F minor Piano Trio). A "Brahms style" merged with Wagnerian trends in Max Reger, Franz Schmidt, and Hans Rott. British composers (Parry, Elgar—"I feel like a pygmy" viewing Symphony No. 3), French (Fauré), Russian (Taneyev, "the Russian Brahms"), and American (Amy Beach) all testified to his impact. Through Zemlinsky to Schoenberg, Webern, and Berg, Brahms's cohesive textures and variation techniques became a foundation for the Second Viennese School. His archive, held by the Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde, was inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in 2005.

## Terms
- **Absolute music**: Instrumental music valued for its formal structure and logic, without extramusical narrative or program; Brahms's stance against the New German School.
- **Developing variation**: Schoenberg's term for Brahms's core technique: continuous transformation of motivic-harmonic material to generate structure, replacing traditional thematic exposition/development.
- **Lieder**: German art songs for voice and piano; Brahms composed hundreds, often on folk or nature themes, which seeded his instrumental melodies.
- **Neue Bahnen ("New Paths")**: Robert Schumann's 1853 article in *Neue Zeitschrift für Musik* launching Brahms's career with messianic praise.
- **New German School**: Term for the progressive circle around Liszt and Wagner advocating program music, *Gesamtkunstwerk* (total artwork), and harmonic innovation; opposed by Brahms and Hanslick.
- **Passacaglia**: A variation form over a repeating bass line (ground bass); the finale of Brahms's Symphony No. 4 is a landmark example, modeled on Bach's Cantata No. 150.
- **War of the Romantics**: Mid-19th-century aesthetic conflict between conservative/absolute music (Brahms, Hanslick, Joachim) and progressive/programmatic music (Liszt, Wagner).
- **Csárdás**: Hungarian folk dance with slow (*lassú*) and fast (*friss*) sections; source of the "gypsy style" Brahms encountered via Ede Reményi, basis for *Hungarian Dances*.
- **Minnesang**: Medieval German courtly love song; Brahms used a Minnesang melody for the Andante of his first published work, Piano Sonata No. 1.
- **Alte Musik**: "Old music"; Brahms's scholarly engagement with pre-Classical repertoire (Renaissance/Baroque), which informed his counterpoint and choral writing.

## Debates and open questions
- **Conservative vs. Progressive**: The central historiographical debate. Contemporaries (Wagner, Hanslick early on) and early critics saw Brahms as a traditionalist; Schoenberg, Webern, Adorno, and Dahlhaus argued his structural innovations (developing variation, rhythmic complexity, remote harmonies) make him a modernist precursor.
- **Relationship with Clara Schumann**: The nature of their 40-year bond—deeply emotional, possibly romantic but unconsummated—remains interpreted through destroyed letters and diary entries (e.g., Clara calling the Alto Rhapsody his "wedding song").
- **Self-criticism and destroyed works**: Brahms burned vast juvenilia and unfinished pieces (allegedly 20 string quartets, a violin sonata). Scholars debate whether this reflects artistic integrity or pathological perfectionism, and what lost masterpieces might have revealed.
- **Programmatic allusions in absolute music**: Despite rejecting *Gesamtkunstwerk*, Brahms embedded cryptic references (Beethoven, Bach, Clara's cryptograms, Minnesang, possible Wagner allusions in Symphony No. 3). The extent and intent of these "hidden programs" are debated.
- **Religious stance in *A German Requiem***: Brahms selected Luther Bible texts avoiding Christian dogma (rejecting John 3:16), stating he would replace "German" with "Human." Scholars debate whether this reflects agnosticism, humanism, or a universalist spirituality.
- **Influence on Second Viennese School**: Schoenberg's "Brahms the Progressive" is acknowledged as partly polemical/self-serving. The precise lineage—via Zemlinsky, developing variation, and passacaglia technique—remains a musicological focus.
