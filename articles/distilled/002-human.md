# Human

## Overview

Humans (*Homo sapiens*, meaning 'wise man' or 'knowledgeable man') are the most abundant and widespread species of primates, distinguished by bipedalism, minimal body hair, and large, complex brains enabling advanced technology, culture, and language. Highly social beings living in structures from families to political states, their curiosity drove science, philosophy, religion, and other fields. Humans evolved from other hominins in Africa, emerged around 300,000 years ago, and have spread across nearly all of Earth, becoming the first known species to visit the Moon, while also contributing to environmental destruction and a mass extinction of other life forms.

## Timeline

- **c. 8–4 million years ago** — Human lineage splits from chimpanzee–bonobo lineage
- **c. 2.8 million years ago** — Earliest record of *Homo* (specimen LD 350-1, Ethiopia)
- **c. 2 million years ago** — *Homo erectus* evolves; first archaic human to leave Africa
- **c. 300,000 years ago** — *Homo sapiens* emerges in Africa
- **c. 130,000–50,000 years ago** — "Out of Africa" migration in at least two waves
- **c. 12,000 years ago** — Neolithic Revolution (agriculture) begins in Southwest Asia
- **c. 3000 BCE** — Cuneiform script appears in Sumerian city-states; Caral–Supe civilization emerges
- **c. 1200 BCE** — Late Bronze Age collapse; Iron Age begins
- **476 CE** — Fall of Western Roman Empire; Europe enters Middle Ages
- **c. 1450–1800** — Early modern period: Renaissance, Age of Discovery, Scientific Revolution
- **1914–1945** — First and Second World Wars; collapse of most global empires
- **2022** — Human population passes eight billion

## Body

### Etymology and definition

All modern humans are classified as *Homo sapiens*, coined by Carl Linnaeus in his 1735 work *Systema Naturae*. The generic name *Homo* derives from Latin *homō*, referring to humans of either sex. Debate exists over whether extinct members like Neanderthals should be a separate species or a subspecies of *H. sapiens*. "Human" comes from Old French *humain*, ultimately from Latin *hūmānus*. Philosophical debate questions whether personhood applies to all humans or all sentient beings, and whether a human can lose personhood (e.g., in a persistent vegetative state).

### Evolution

Humans belong to the great ape family (Hominidae). The lineage split successively from gibbons, orangutans, gorillas, and finally chimpanzees and bonobos. During the chimpanzee split, chromosome 2 formed from the joining of two chromosomes, leaving humans with 23 pairs versus 24 in other apes. The genus *Homo* evolved from *Australopithecus*, though timing is debated—estimates range from 4.30–2.56 million years ago to about 1.87 million years ago. *Homo erectus* evolved 2 million years ago, first to leave Africa and develop a characteristically human body plan. *Homo sapiens* emerged in Africa from either *H. heidelbergensis* or *H. rhodesiensis*, then migrated out, gradually replacing or interbreeding with archaic humans. Neanderthals and Denisovans may have contributed up to 6% of their genome to present-day non sub-Saharan-African humans. Key adaptations include hairlessness, obligate bipedalism, increased brain size, and decreased sexual dimorphism (neoteny).

### History

Until about 12,000 years ago, all humans were hunter-gatherers. The Neolithic Revolution (agriculture) first occurred in Southwest Asia, spreading through the Old World, and independently in Mesoamerica (about 6,000 years ago), China, Papua New Guinea, and parts of Africa. An urban revolution in the 4th millennium BCE saw Sumerian city-states in Mesopotamia, where cuneiform appeared around 3000 BCE. Major civilizations included Ancient Egypt, the Indus Valley Civilization, and Caral–Supe in the Americas. The Late Bronze Age collapse around 1200 BCE led to the Greek Dark Ages and Iron Age. Classical antiquity saw Greece and Rome flourish, while the Maya, Kingdom of Aksum, Achaemenid Empire, Gupta Empire, and Han dynasty rose. After the fall of the Western Roman Empire in 476, Europe entered the Middle Ages, while Islam expanded and an Islamic Golden Age occurred. The Mongol Empire conquered much of Eurasia in the 13th–14th centuries. The early modern period (c. 1450–1800) brought the Renaissance, Age of Discovery, colonization of the Americas, and Scientific Revolution. The late modern period saw the Industrial Revolution, Age of Revolution, and European expansion into Africa (from 10% to almost 90% control in less than 50 years). The World Wars led to decolonization, followed by the Cold War between the US and Soviet Union, and the current Information Age.

### Habitat and population

Early settlements depended on proximity to water and resources, but modern humans greatly alter habitats through technology, irrigation, and urban planning. Humans are present in all eight biogeographical realms, though Antarctic presence is limited to research stations. Most humans (61%) live in Asia; the remainder in the Americas (14%), Africa (14%), Europe (11%), and Oceania (0.5%). Population at agriculture's emergence (c. 10,000 BC) ranged from 1–15 million, reaching one billion in 1800, two billion in 1930, and eight billion in 2022. In 2018, 4.2 billion humans (55%) lived in urban areas.

### Biology

Humans share most physiology with other animals, having a dental formula of 2.1.2.3/2.1.2.3 like other catarrhines, but with proportionately shorter palates and smaller teeth. They are the only primates with short, relatively flush canine teeth. Worldwide average height is about 171 cm for males and 159 cm for females; average mass is 77 kg for males and 59 kg for females. Humans are among the best long-distance runners, aided by thinner body hair and more productive sweat glands.

Genetically, humans are diploid with 23 pairs of chromosomes (22 autosomes plus one pair of sex chromosomes). Sex determination is primarily mediated by the SRY gene on the Y chromosome. Two humans average 99.5%–99.9% genetic similarity, more homogeneous than other great apes—suggesting a population bottleneck during the Late Pleistocene (around 100,000 years ago). The human genome project (HGP) began in 1990; by 2010 estimates, humans have approximately 22,000 genes. Mitochondrial Eve, the last female common ancestor, lived around 90,000–200,000 years ago.

Human gestation averages 38 weeks. Childbirth is dangerous compared with other species, with higher risk of complications. Both parents typically provide care, unlike other primates. Females undergo menopause around age 50; the grandmother hypothesis proposes this increases reproductive success by allowing investment in existing offspring. Women live on average about four years longer than men; as of 2018, global life expectancy at birth is 74.9 years for girls and 70.4 for boys.

Humans are omnivorous and can survive up to eight weeks without food and three to four days without water. Agriculture began about 11,700 years ago, leading to dietary changes such as adult lactose digestion in some populations. In 2020, an estimated 9 million humans died annually from starvation-related causes, while worldwide over one billion people are obese.

Biological variation exists in blood type, facial features, height, and skin color. Populations have adapted genetically: lactose tolerance correlates with cattle domestication history, and sickle cell anemia (which may resist malaria) is frequent where malaria is endemic. African populations are the most genetically diverse, with diversity decreasing with migratory distance from Africa. The greatest genetic variation exists between males and females (1%–2% versus 0.1%–0.5% within sexes). Males average 15% heavier and 15 cm taller, with 40–50% more upper-body strength.

### Psychology

The human brain has a larger and more developed prefrontal cortex than other primates, associated with higher cognition. Humans may be the only animals with episodic memory and "mental time travel," and are the only animals known to cry emotional tears. Humans are generally diurnal, sleeping seven to nine hours daily. Dreaming occurs mostly during REM sleep, stimulated by the pons, with dreams lasting from seconds to 30 minutes. Consciousness—sentience or awareness of existence—remains enigmatic and poorly understood. Human motivation is studied through theories like Maslow's hierarchy of needs. Emotions are biological states associated with the nervous system that significantly influence behavior. Human sexuality involves biological, emotional, and social aspects; most humans are heterosexual, and only humans and domestic sheep exhibit exclusive same-sex preference. Love involves neurochemicals like dopamine and oxytocin stimulating the brain's reward system.

### Culture

Humans are the only animals known to teach generalizable information, use recursive embedding for complex concepts, or cook food in the wild. The cumulative preservation of knowledge across generations is the "ratchet effect." Language is unique to humans—open, with infinite meanings from limited symbols, and modality independent (speech, sign, writing, braille). As of 1996, humans used approximately six thousand languages. Art is a defining characteristic; the earliest evidence was shell engravings by *Homo erectus* 300,000 years before modern humans evolved, with *H. sapiens* art dating to at least 75,000 years ago. Stone tools date to at least 2.5 million years ago, with controlled fire use beginning around 1 million years ago. Religion, with credible evidence dating to the Middle Paleolithic (45–200 thousand years ago), may have evolved to encourage cooperation. In 2015, the plurality were Christian, followed by Muslims, Hindus, and Buddhists; about 16% were irreligious. Science and philosophy are defining human attributes; Aristotle has been described as the first scientist, and the scientific revolution led to modern science.

### Society

Society is the system of organizations arising from human interaction. Human societies typically exhibit gender roles distinguishing masculine and feminine characteristics; the most common categorization is a gender binary, though some societies recognize a third or fourth gender. Kinship—relationships based on consanguinity, affinity, or fictive ties—is among the most important social organizing principles, with all societies having incest taboos. Genetic evidence indicates humans were predominantly polygynous for most of their existence, shifting toward monogamy during the Neolithic. Ethnic groups are social categories based on shared attributes, separate from race. Approximately 47% of humans live in some form of democracy, 17% in a hybrid regime, and 37% in an authoritarian regime. Trade is seen as differentiating humans from other animals; early trade involved materials like obsidian. Humans commit violence at rates comparable to other primates, with phylogenetic analysis predicting 2% of early *H. sapiens* would be murdered, rising to 12% in the medieval period, then dropping below 2% in modern times. An estimated 167–188 million people died from war during the 20th century.

## Terms

- **Bipedalism**: Walking on two legs; a defining human adaptation
- **Neoteny**: Decreased sexual dimorphism and retention of juvenile features in adults
- **Ratchet effect**: Cumulative preservation and advancement of knowledge across generations
- **Theory of mind**: Ability to attribute mental states to oneself and others
- **Mitochondrial Eve**: The last female common ancestor whose genetic marker is found in all modern humans
- **Neolithic Revolution**: The invention of agriculture, first occurring in Southwest Asia about 12,000 years ago
- **Gender binary**: Categorization of gender into men and women only
- **Kinship**: Culturally defined social relationships based on blood, marriage, or fictive ties
- **Episodic memory**: Memory of specific events, possibly unique to humans
- **Entrainment**: Brain synchronization with musical rhythm and beat

## Debates and open questions

- Whether extinct members of the genus *Homo* (particularly Neanderthals) should be classified as a separate species or a subspecies of *H. sapiens*
- The timing of the divergence to the genus *Homo*, with estimates ranging from 4.30–2.56 million years ago to about 1.87 million years ago
- The relationship between human adaptations (hairlessness, bipedalism, brain size, neoteny) remains the subject of ongoing debate
- Whether personhood applies to all humans or all sentient beings, and what constitutes the beginning of human personhood
- The underlying nature of consciousness remains enigmatic and poorly understood
- Whether war evolved as an innate human characteristic or is a relatively recent phenomenon due to changing social conditions
- The reason for dangerous human childbirth (fetal head size matched closely to pelvis) is not completely understood
