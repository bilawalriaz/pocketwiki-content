# Robert Hooke

## Overview
Robert Hooke (1635–1703) was a pivotal English polymath whose work as a scientist, architect, and surveyor was foundational to the Scientific Revolution and the rebuilding of London. He was a central figure in the early Royal Society, pioneering experimental science across disciplines from microscopy to geology. His reputation, long overshadowed by rivals like Isaac Newton, has been restored as a key innovator whose insights into gravity, elasticity, and the microscopic world were decades ahead of their time.

## Timeline
- **18 July 1635** — Born in Freshwater, Isle of Wight.
- **1653** — Secured a place at Christ Church, Oxford.
- **1655** — Became assistant to Robert Boyle, helping with gas law experiments.
- **1660** — Discovered the law of elasticity (Hooke's Law).
- **1662** — Appointed first Curator of Experiments for the Royal Society.
- **1665** — Published *Micrographia*, coining the term "cell" and detailing microscopic observations.
- **1666** — After the Great Fire of London, began work as a surveyor and architect for the city's reconstruction.
- **1674** — Published lecture proposing gravitational attraction between all celestial bodies.
- **1678** — Published his law of elasticity as "Ut tensio, sic vis" (As the extension, so the force).
- **1679–1680** — Correspondence with Isaac Newton on planetary motion and gravity.
- **3 March 1703** — Died in London.

## Body

### Early Life and Oxford
Born to a curate, Hooke's early education was neglected, but he showed mechanical aptitude. After his father's death in 1648, he moved to London and later attended Westminster School. In 1653, he entered Christ Church, Oxford, as a chorister and servitor. There, he became assistant to physician Thomas Willis and, crucially, to the scientist Robert Boyle. Hooke built air pumps for Boyle's experiments on gas pressure, which contributed to Boyle's Law. His time at Oxford, where he met figures like Christopher Wren, formed the foundation of his scientific career.

### The Royal Society and Scientific Work
Hooke was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1663 and became its Curator of Experiments in 1662, a role vital to the Society's survival. He conducted weekly demonstrations on topics from gravity to meteorology. His 1665 book *Micrographia*, based on microscopic observations, was a landmark. It contained the first recorded observation of a microorganism and coined the term "cell" for plant structures. Hooke also proposed a wave theory of light, identified the organic origin of fossils, and was among the first to hypothesize an inverse-square law for gravitational attraction, a principle later formalized by Newton.

### Gravitation and the Newton Rivalry
Hooke argued for a universal attracting principle of gravitation in *Micrographia* (1665) and in a 1674 lecture. In 1679, he initiated correspondence with Newton, suggesting that planetary motion resulted from a direct motion and an attractive motion towards a central body. In 1680, Hooke communicated his "supposition" that attraction was "in a duplicate proportion to the Distance from the Center Reciprocall." When Newton presented his *Principia* in 1686, Hooke claimed priority for the inverse-square law idea. Newton denied this, citing prior work by others, though he acknowledged Hooke in his text. Historians note the inverse-square assumption was common by the late 1660s, but Hooke lacked Newton's mathematical demonstration.

### Architecture and Surveying
After the Great Fire of London in 1666, Hooke became a successful surveyor and architect. He assisted Christopher Wren in rebuilding the city, designing the Monument to the Great Fire and other buildings. He performed over half of the property line surveys, a task requiring meticulous work to resolve disputes. His surveys enabled the first large-scale, accurate map of London in 1677. He proposed a grid-pattern redesign for London, but the city was rebuilt on its original lines for speed and cost.

### Personality and Legacy
Contemporary accounts describe Hooke as "melancholy, mistrustful, and jealous," leading to a long-standing negative reputation. However, his diaries reveal a social man with friendships and a complex personal life. He suffered from chronic health issues, including migraines and a spinal deformity. No authenticated portrait of him exists, though written descriptions and a possible seal provide clues. His reputation was restored in the 20th century, and he is now celebrated as "England's Leonardo da Vinci."

## Terms
- **Polymath**: A person of wide-ranging learning and expertise.
- **Curator of Experiments**: A role at the Royal Society responsible for demonstrating experiments at meetings.
- **Inverse-square law**: A principle stating that a specified physical quantity is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from a source (e.g., gravity, light).
- **Hooke's Law**: The principle that the force needed to extend or compress a spring is directly proportional to the distance.
- **Micrographia**: Hooke's 1665 book detailing observations made with microscopes and telescopes.
- **Cell**: The term Hooke coined for the basic unit of plant tissue, resembling a monk's cell.
- **Parallax**: The apparent shift in position of an object when viewed from two different lines of sight, used to measure distance.
- **Catenary**: The curve formed by a hanging chain or cable, which Hooke identified as the ideal shape for an arch.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Priority of the inverse-square law**: The extent to which Hooke's ideas influenced Newton's formulation of universal gravitation remains debated. Newton denied Hooke's priority, while Hooke claimed he provided the key "notion."
- **The lost portrait**: The fate of Hooke's portrait, once shown at the Royal Society, is unknown. Claims that Newton or his allies destroyed it are considered a myth by some biographers, but the disappearance coincides with Newton's presidency.
- **Hooke's model of memory**: His 1682 mechanical model of human memory, which addressed encoding, retrieval, and forgetting, is noted for its surprisingly modern concepts but its influence is unclear.
