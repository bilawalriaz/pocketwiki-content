# Citizen Kane

## Overview
*Citizen Kane* (1941) is an American drama film directed by, produced by, and starring Orson Welles in his feature debut. It is a quasi-biographical examination of fictional newspaper magnate Charles Foster Kane, a composite character based on figures like William Randolph Hearst. Widely regarded as one of the most influential films ever made, it is celebrated for its innovative narrative structure, cinematography, and sound design, which redefined cinematic storytelling.

## Body

### Production and Screenplay
After the success of his Mercury Theatre and the controversial 1938 "War of the Worlds" broadcast, Orson Welles was courted by Hollywood. He signed a contract with RKO Pictures in 1939 that gave him unusual creative control, including final cut. After two failed projects, Welles and screenwriter Herman J. Mankiewicz developed the screenplay. The authorship became a long-standing controversy. Mankiewicz, who based the outline on his knowledge of Hearst, initially received no credit. After threatening legal action, he was awarded a co-writing credit. The dispute was revived by critic Pauline Kael in 1971, though subsequent research has affirmed Welles's substantial contribution.

The cast was largely composed of actors new to motion pictures, many from Welles's Mercury Theatre. Principal photography began in 1940, with cinematographer Gregg Toland, who sought out Welles to experiment with innovative techniques. Welles taught himself filmmaking by studying films like John Ford's *Stagecoach* 40 times. The production was marked by secrecy, with Welles initially deceiving RKO executives by claiming he was only shooting "camera tests."

### Style and Innovation
*Citizen Kane* is celebrated for its revolutionary techniques. Its most noted achievement is the extended use of **deep focus cinematography**, where foreground, middle ground, and background are all in sharp focus, allowing complex staging within a single shot. This was achieved through new lenses and lighting by Toland. The film also broke convention by showing ceilings on its sets, using low-angle shots that required cameras to be placed in holes dug into the floor.

The narrative rejects a linear structure, telling Kane's story through flashbacks from multiple, often conflicting, perspectives—a technique akin to the unreliable narrator in literature. This structure, combined with innovative sound design using overlapping dialogue and "lightning mixes" from radio, creates a complex portrait. The score by Bernard Herrmann, his first for a feature, used brief musical cues and recurring motifs to bridge scenes and underscore themes.

### Reception and Legacy
Upon its 1941 release, *Citizen Kane* received widespread critical acclaim but was a commercial failure, partly due to a campaign by newspaper magnate William Randolph Hearst, who believed the film was a libelous attack. Hearst banned any mention of the film in his newspapers and pressured theaters not to show it. The film faded from public view until championed by French critics like André Bazin in the 1940s and re-released in 1956.

Its reputation grew steadily, and it has since been consistently voted the greatest film ever made in major polls, including the British Film Institute's *Sight & Sound* poll for 50 years (1962-2002). The film's narrative depth, technical mastery, and exploration of themes like power, corruption, and the elusive nature of identity have made it a cornerstone of film studies and a profound influence on generations of filmmakers.

## Terms
- **Deep Focus Cinematography:** A technique where both foreground and background elements are kept in sharp focus simultaneously, allowing for complex visual storytelling within a single shot.
- **Final Cut Privilege:** The contractual right of a director to approve the final version of a film, overriding the studio's authority to make edits.
- **Mise-en-scène:** The arrangement of everything that appears in the framing of a shot—actors, lighting, décor, props, costume—and the way it is staged to convey meaning.
- **Leitmotif:** A recurring musical theme or motif associated with a particular character, idea, or situation in a film's score.
- **Lightning Mix:** A sound editing technique, borrowed from radio, that uses a series of related sounds or phrases to link complex montage sequences.
- **Yellow Journalism:** Journalism that is based upon sensationalism and crude exaggeration, a practice associated with the character Kane's newspaper, *The Inquirer*.
- **Unreliable Narrator:** A narrator whose credibility is compromised, forcing the audience to question the truth of the story being told, as used in the film's multiple flashback structure.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Authorship of the Screenplay:** A major controversy concerns the relative contributions of Orson Welles and Herman J. Mankiewicz. While the official credit lists both, debates—sparked by Pauline Kael's 1971 essay "Raising Kane"—have questioned whether Welles downplayed Mankiewicz's role. Scholarly research, including Robert L. Carringer's 1978 study of the script drafts, generally concludes that Welles's contribution was "substantial but definitive."
- **The Meaning of "Rosebud":** The film's central mystery, the meaning of Kane's dying word, is deliberately left unresolved for the characters. While the audience learns it refers to his childhood sled, scholars debate its symbolic significance—whether it represents lost innocence, the futility of materialism, or a key to Kane's psyche.
- **Basis for Charles Foster Kane:** While the character is widely seen as a composite based on William Randolph Hearst, Welles stated that "many people sat for it," specifically naming Chicago tycoons Samuel Insull and Harold McCormick. The character of Susan Alexander is often assumed to be based on Hearst's mistress, Marion Davies, a belief that fueled Hearst's anger, though Welles denied this connection.
