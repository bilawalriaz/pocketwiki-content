# Puerto Rico

## Overview
Puerto Rico is a Caribbean archipelago and unincorporated territory of the United States, organized as a commonwealth. Its political status—neither a state nor an independent nation—is a central and ongoing debate. The island has a complex history of Indigenous settlement, Spanish colonization, and U.S. acquisition, resulting in a unique cultural blend and a distinct identity. Its economy is developed but faces significant challenges from debt, natural disasters, and its territorial relationship with the U.S.

## Timeline
- **c. 4000 BC** — Earliest known settlement by the Ortoiroid culture.
- **1493** — Christopher Columbus claims the island for Spain.
- **1508** — Spanish colonization begins under Juan Ponce de León.
- **1898** — Puerto Rico is acquired by the United States following the Spanish–American War.
- **1917** — The Jones–Shafroth Act grants U.S. citizenship to Puerto Ricans.
- **1950** — The Puerto Rico Federal Relations Act establishes the commonwealth status.
- **1952** — A territorial constitution is approved, creating the current government structure.
- **2006–2011** — Puerto Rico experiences a major economic recession.
- **2017** — Hurricanes Irma and Maria cause catastrophic damage; a federal financial oversight board is established.
- **2020** — A referendum shows 52% voter approval for statehood.
- **2024** — Pro-statehood candidate Jenniffer González-Colón wins the gubernatorial election.

## Body

### History and Political Status
Puerto Rico was settled by successive Indigenous peoples, including the Taíno, before being colonized by Spain in 1508. It remained a Spanish possession for 400 years until the 1898 Spanish–American War, after which it became a U.S. territory. The Jones–Shafroth Act of 1917 made Puerto Ricans U.S. citizens. In 1950, Congress approved the Puerto Rico Federal Relations Act, and in 1952, a territorial constitution established a local government with an elected governor and legislature. The fundamental political question—whether Puerto Rico should remain a territory, become a state, or gain independence—remains unresolved. Referendums in 2012, 2017, and 2020 have shown varying levels of support for statehood, though voter turnout has been inconsistent. As an unincorporated territory, Puerto Rico is subject to the plenary powers of the U.S. Congress; its residents cannot vote in U.S. presidential elections and have no voting representation in Congress, though they pay federal taxes.

### Geography and Climate
The archipelago consists of the main island and smaller islands like Vieques and Culebra. The topography is mostly mountainous, with the Cordillera Central range running east-west. The highest point is Cerro de Punta at 4,390 feet (1,340 m). Puerto Rico lies at the boundary of the Caribbean and North American tectonic plates, making it prone to earthquakes and tsunamis. The climate is tropical rainforest, with warm temperatures year-round. The island is vulnerable to hurricanes, with the 2017 storms causing widespread devastation to infrastructure, including the power grid.

### Economy
Puerto Rico is classified as a high-income economy by the IMF and World Bank. Its economy is driven by manufacturing, especially pharmaceuticals, petrochemicals, and electronics, followed by services like tourism. Agriculture accounts for less than 1% of GNP. The economy has struggled with a long-term recession, exacerbated by the 2006 expiration of key federal tax incentives (Section 936). A severe government-debt crisis led to the 2016 creation of a federal financial oversight board under the PROMESA act. The cost of living is high due to factors like the Jones Act (Merchant Marine Act of 1920), which restricts shipping, and high energy costs.

### Demographics and Culture
The population is approximately 3.2 million and is predominantly Hispanic or Latino (98.9%). The demographic makeup reflects a mix of Indigenous, European, and African ancestry. Spanish is the dominant language, spoken by nearly 95% of the population, while English is the primary language for less than 10%. Christianity is the main religion, with Catholicism historically dominant but Protestantism, particularly Pentecostalism, growing significantly. Puerto Rican culture is a fusion of Spanish, African, and U.S. influences, evident in its music (salsa, reggaeton), cuisine, and festivals. Emigration to the U.S. mainland has been a major trend, leading to a population decline in the 21st century.

## Terms
- **Commonwealth**: The official political designation of Puerto Rico, established in 1950, signifying a self-governing territory in association with the United States.
- **Unincorporated territory**: A U.S. jurisdiction that is not part of any state and to which only select parts of the U.S. Constitution apply, as determined by Congress.
- **Resident commissioner**: The nonvoting delegate elected by Puerto Rico to represent its interests in the U.S. House of Representatives.
- **PROMESA**: The Puerto Rico Oversight, Management, and Economic Stability Act of 2016, which created a federal financial oversight board to manage the island's debt crisis.
- **Jones Act (Merchant Marine Act of 1920)**: A U.S. federal law requiring goods shipped between U.S. ports to be carried on U.S.-flagged ships, which critics argue increases costs in Puerto Rico.
- **Taíno**: The Indigenous people who inhabited Puerto Rico and much of the Caribbean at the time of European contact.
- **Coquí**: A small, endemic tree frog that is a cultural symbol of Puerto Rico, named for its distinctive call.
- **Boricua**: A term derived from the Taíno name for the island (Boriquen), used to identify people of Puerto Rican heritage.

## Debates and Open Questions
The central debate is Puerto Rico's political status: whether it should remain a territory, achieve statehood, or become an independent nation. This is an ongoing discussion in Puerto Rico, the U.S. Congress, and the United Nations. The economic impact of federal policies, such as the Jones Act and the expiration of tax incentives, is also a major point of contention. The effectiveness of the PROMESA oversight board and the path to resolving the debt crisis are actively debated. Finally, the causes and solutions for the island's population decline, driven by emigration and economic hardship, are critical open questions.
