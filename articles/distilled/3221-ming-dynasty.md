# Ming dynasty

## Overview
The Ming dynasty (1368–1644) was the last Han-ruled imperial dynasty of China, succeeding the Mongol-led Yuan. It was a period of cultural flourishing, administrative innovation, and global engagement, but also of internal decay and fiscal crisis. Its legacy includes the consolidation of modern Chinese provincial borders, the flourishing of vernacular literature, and a bureaucratic system that influenced later dynasties.

## Timeline
- **1368** — Zhu Yuanzhang (Hongwu Emperor) founds the Ming after overthrowing the Yuan.
- **1380** — Hongwu executes Chancellor Hu Weiyong, abolishes the Chancellery, and centralizes power.
- **1398** — Hongwu dies; grandson Jianwen Emperor succeeds, sparking civil war.
- **1402** — Zhu Di (Yongle Emperor) seizes the throne after the Jingnan campaign.
- **1405–1433** — Admiral Zheng He leads seven major voyages to the Indian Ocean.
- **1421** — Beijing becomes the primary capital; the Forbidden City is completed.
- **1449** — Tumu Crisis: Emperor Yingzong is captured by Oirat Mongols.
- **1557** — Portuguese establish a permanent trade base at Macau.
- **1592–1598** — Imjin War: Ming intervenes in Korea against Japanese invasion.
- **1616** — Nurhaci unifies Jurchen tribes and establishes the Later Jin dynasty.
- **1644** — Li Zicheng's rebels capture Beijing; Chongzhen Emperor commits suicide.
- **1644** — Ming general Wu Sangui opens the Great Wall to Manchu forces, who establish the Qing dynasty.

## Body

### Founding and Early Consolidation
Founded by peasant rebel Zhu Yuanzhang, the Ming overthrew the Yuan. As Hongwu Emperor, he rebuilt state infrastructure, created the Great Ming Code, and established a rigid social order. He abolished the Chancellorship, executed thousands in purges, and used the Embroidered Uniform Guard secret police. He expanded Ming rule into Inner Asia and the southwest, resettling Han colonists in regions like Yunnan.

### The Yongle Reign and Expansion
The Yongle Emperor (r. 1402–1424), who seized power from his nephew, is considered a "second founder." He moved the capital to Beijing, constructed the Forbidden City, and sponsored Zheng He's voyages. His reign marked the height of Ming power but also increased eunuch influence as a counterweight to scholar-bureaucrats.

### Mid-Ming Crises and Adaptation
The 1449 Tumu Crisis ended major northern campaigns. The dynasty turned inward, fortifying the Great Wall and allowing the navy to decline. The economy monetized with New World silver influx, replacing paper currency. New crops like maize and potatoes spurred population growth. However, the tax system struggled, and powerful eunuch factions led to corruption and factional strife.

### Decline and Fall
The late Ming faced converging crises: the Little Ice Age caused famines, and silver import disruptions led to fiscal collapse. Peasant rebellions, notably by Li Zicheng, erupted. The Manchus, unified under Nurhaci, grew into a major threat. In 1644, Li Zicheng captured Beijing, and the last Ming emperor hanged himself. The Manchus, allied with Wu Sangui, defeated Li Zicheng and established the Qing dynasty. Ming loyalist regimes (Southern Ming) resisted until 1662.

### Government and Society
The Ming government was a centralized bureaucracy staffed by scholar-officials selected through civil service examinations on Confucian classics. The system favored the gentry. Power was contested between the emperor, Grand Secretariat, civil bureaucracy, and eunuch agencies. Society was stratified, with strict sumptuary laws (later relaxed), and a vibrant urban culture emerged, especially in the south.

### Culture and Thought
Ming culture saw the peak of vernacular literature with novels like *Water Margin* and *Journey to the West*. Painting and porcelain reached new sophistication. Wang Yangming's Confucianism, emphasizing innate moral knowledge, challenged orthodox Neo-Confucianism. Contact with Europeans via Jesuits introduced new scientific ideas, though impact was initially limited.

## Terms
- **Grand Secretariat:** Advisory body that evolved into the de facto top executive organ after the Chancellery's abolition.
- **Civil Service Examination:** Rigorous tests on Confucian classics used to select scholar-officials.
- **Eunuch:** Castrated palace servants who gained political power through control of court agencies and secret police.
- **Tributary System:** Diplomatic framework where foreign states acknowledged Ming suzerainty through tribute missions for trade rights.
- **Haijin:** Maritime trade bans intended to protect the coast from piracy, which often spurred smuggling.
- **Columbian Exchange:** Transfer of plants, animals, and technology between the Americas and Old World, introducing new crops to China.
- **Little Ice Age:** Global cooling period (14th–19th centuries) causing crop failures and famines in the late Ming.
- **Eight-Legged Essay:** Rigid essay format required in examinations, criticized for stifling intellectual creativity.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Sovereignty over Tibet:** Scholars debate whether Ming exercised loose suzerainty, a primarily religious relationship, or no effective control.
- **Population Figures:** Official census data is unreliable due to underreporting. Late Ming population estimates vary from 160 to 200 million.
- **Causes of Collapse:** The relative importance of fiscal crisis, natural disasters, peasant rebellions, and Manchu invasion is debated.
- **Social Mobility:** Historians question whether the examination system expanded or contracted upward mobility, noting it practically favored the wealthy gentry.
