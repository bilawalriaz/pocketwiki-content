# Georgia O'Keeffe

## Overview
Georgia O'Keeffe (1887–1986) was an American modernist painter whose seven-decade career produced iconic abstractions and large-scale depictions of natural forms—flowers, skulls, desert landscapes—largely independent of major art movements. Called the "Mother of American modernism," she shaped modern visual art through abstraction, natural motifs, and monumental scale, while consistently rejecting categorization as a "woman artist."

## Body

### Early Life and Education (1887–1916)
Born into a farming family in Wisconsin, O'Keeffe was the second of seven children. Her maternal grandfather was a Hungarian count. By age 10, she resolved to become an artist. After moving to Virginia in 1903, she graduated from Chatham Episcopal Institute in 1905. Financial hardship following her father’s bankruptcy ended formal training after brief stints at the Art Institute of Chicago and the Art Students League. A four-year hiatus from painting followed due to illness and disillusionment with academic realism.

### First Abstractions
In 1912, O'Keeffe took a summer class at the University of Virginia under Alon Bement, who introduced her to Arthur Wesley Dow’s theories emphasizing design and composition over realism. This marked her shift toward abstraction. Teaching in Texas Panhandle schools (1912–1914), she developed watercolors expressing personal sensations. She took classes at the University of Virginia for two more summers and a spring 1914 class at Teachers College with Dow. Her 1915 charcoal abstractions, created while teaching in South Carolina, caught Stieglitz’s attention via Anita Pollitzer. Ten were shown at 291 in April 1916, launching her career.

### New York Period (1918–1930s)
In June 1918, Stieglitz invited O'Keeffe to live and work in New York City and offered to underwrite her year of painting. She resigned from teaching at West Texas State to do so. Their professional and romantic relationship deepened; they married in 1924. During the 1920s, she painted urban scenes (*My New Yorks*) and began large-scale flower paintings like *Petunia No. 2* (1924), first exhibited in 1925. Critics sexualized her work, but she rejected such interpretations. In 1925, moving to the Shelton Hotel, she painted skyscrapers including *Radiator Building – Night, New York*. A 1927 Brooklyn Museum retrospective affirmed her rising prominence.

### New Mexico (1929–1986)
Starting in 1929, O'Keeffe spent increasing time in New Mexico, drawn by its stark landscapes. She first traveled to Santa Fe accompanied by Rebecca Strand and stayed in Taos with Mabel Dodge Luhan. She settled permanently there in 1949 after Stieglitz’s death, having spent the next three years mostly in New York settling his estate. Her work featured desert vistas, animal skulls (*Cow’s Skull: Red, White, and Blue*, 1931; *Summer Days*, 1936), and architectural elements. The 1938 Hawaiian Pineapple Company commission led to a series of tropical flower paintings. In 1945, she bought and renovated an Abiquiú hacienda into a home and studio. Major retrospectives at MoMA (1946) and the Whitney (1970) solidified her legacy.

### Personal Life and Relationships
Her relationship with Stieglitz was complex—emotionally intimate yet structured by unspoken agreements. He made over 350 portraits and 200 nudes of her. Affairs, particularly with Dorothy Norman, caused emotional strain. Close friendships included Rebecca Strand and Frida Kahlo. She adopted gender-neutral clothing and lived independently in New Mexico, cultivating a reclusive persona.

### Late Career and Death
By 1972, O'Keeffe had lost much of her eyesight due to macular degeneration, leaving her with only peripheral vision. She stopped oil painting without assistance in 1972. With assistant Juan Hamilton’s help, she resumed painting and published her autobiography (1976). She continued drawing until 1984. Dying in Santa Fe in 1986, her estate dispute over her will became a notable legal precedent. The Georgia O'Keeffe Museum opened in Santa Fe in 1997.

## Terms
- **Abstraction**: Art that simplifies or distorts natural forms to focus on shapes, colors, and lines rather than realistic representation.
- **Precisionism**: An American modernist style characterized by clean, geometric renderings of industrial and architectural subjects.
- **New Woman**: A cultural archetype of the early 20th century representing educated, independent women challenging traditional gender roles.
- **Charcoal abstraction**: Non-representational drawings using charcoal, emphasizing form and gesture over literal depiction.
- **Macular degeneration**: Age-related eye condition causing loss of central vision, affecting O'Keeffe’s ability to paint precisely.
- **Ghost Ranch**: Remote area in northern New Mexico where O'Keeffe maintained a summer home and found artistic inspiration.
- **291 Gallery**: New York gallery co-owned by Alfred Stieglitz, pivotal in promoting early American modernism.
- **Catalogue raisonné**: Comprehensive listing of an artist’s complete works, often including scholarly commentary.

## Debates and Open Questions
O'Keeffe’s work has long been interpreted through feminist and psychoanalytic lenses, particularly regarding flower paintings seen as vaginal symbols. Art historian Linda Nochlin argued *Black Iris III* (1926) functions as a "morphological metaphor for a vulva." O'Keeffe consistently denied intentional sexual meaning, stating viewers projected their own associations onto her work. Feminist scholars like Linda Grasso situate her within broader suffrage-era modernist movements, arguing her independence aligned with "New Woman" ideals despite her rejection of feminist labels. Her estate dispute with Juan Hamilton also raised enduring questions about artistic legacy and control over posthumous reputation.
