# Shah Jahan

## Overview
Shah Jahan (r. 1628–1658) was the fifth Mughal Emperor, whose reign is celebrated as the zenith of Mughal architectural and cultural achievement. He is most famous for commissioning the Taj Mahal and presiding over a period of immense wealth, military expansion, and artistic patronage, though his later years were marked by a devastating famine and a brutal war of succession.

## Timeline
- **5 January 1592** — Born as Prince Khurram in Lahore.
- **1612** — Marries Mumtaz Mahal (Arjumand Banu Begum).
- **1617** — Granted the title "Shah Jahan" ("King of the World") by his father, Jahangir.
- **1622** — Rebels against his father, Jahangir, but is defeated.
- **January 1628** — Executes rival claimants and ascends the Mughal throne.
- **1630–1632** — Deccan famine devastates the empire, killing an estimated two million people.
- **1632** — Captures Daulatabad and imprisons the ruler of Ahmednagar.
- **1638** — Mughals capture Kandahar from the Safavids.
- **1646–1647** — Unsuccessful invasion of Central Asia (Balkh and Badakhshan).
- **1658** — Falls ill, triggering a war of succession among his sons.
- **July 1658** — Defeated son Aurangzeb seizes power and imprisons him in Agra Fort.
- **22 January 1666** — Dies in captivity; buried beside Mumtaz Mahal in the Taj Mahal.

## Body

### Early Life and Accession
Born Prince Khurram, he was the favored grandson of Emperor Akbar, who raised him in his own household. He gained military experience in campaigns against the Rajputs and in the Deccan. His path to the throne was complicated by the immense influence of his stepmother, Empress Nur Jahan, who favored her own son-in-law, Shahryar Mirza. After a failed rebellion against his father in 1622, Khurram reconciled. Upon Jahangir's death in 1627, he defeated Shahryar and executed most rival claimants to secure his rule.

### Reign and Military Campaigns
Shah Jahan's reign saw the Mughal Empire expand moderately and its army grow to nearly a million soldiers. He pursued aggressive campaigns against the Deccan sultanates, forcing Golconda and Bijapur into submission. His wars with the Safavid dynasty over Kandahar were largely unsuccessful, with the city changing hands multiple times. An invasion of Central Asia (1646–47) was also abandoned as fruitless. He also dealt with conflicts with the Portuguese in Bengal and suppressed local rebellions, such as those by the Kolis of Gujarat.

### Famine and Administration
A catastrophic famine struck the Deccan, Gujarat, and Khandesh from 1630 to 1632, causing widespread starvation and social breakdown. Shah Jahan responded by establishing free kitchens (*langar*). Despite this crisis, his administration was centralized and the empire's economy strong, with India's share of global GDP growing to an estimated 24.4% by 1700.

### Architecture and Culture
Shah Jahan was the greatest patron of Mughal architecture, ushering in its "golden age." His most famous work is the Taj Mahal, built over 20 years as a mausoleum for his wife Mumtaz Mahal, who died in 1631. Other major commissions include the Red Fort and Jama Masjid in Delhi, sections of the Agra and Lahore Forts, and the Shah Jahan Mosque in Thatta. His reign also saw the creation of the legendary Peacock Throne.

### Illness, Succession, and Death
In 1658, Shah Jahan's illness sparked a war of succession among his sons. His eldest, Dara Shikoh, was defeated by Aurangzeb at the Battle of Samugarh. Aurangzeb declared his father incompetent and imprisoned him in the Agra Fort for eight years until his death in 1666. He was buried beside Mumtaz Mahal in the Taj Mahal.

## Terms
- **Mansab**: A rank in the Mughal administrative system, denoting a noble's military standing and salary.
- **Zamindar**: A landowner or tax collector in Mughal India, responsible for revenue collection.
- **Sarkar**: A Mughal administrative province or district.
- **Langar**: Free kitchens established by rulers or religious institutions to provide food to the poor.
- **Hukum**: An official order or decree issued by a Mughal emperor or empress.
- **Farman**: A royal decree or charter issued by a Mughal emperor.
- **Diwan/Shura**: The imperial council or assembly of ministers and nobles.
- **Takht ya Takhta**: A Persian phrase meaning "Throne or Coffin," referring to the Mughal custom of succession wars where princes fought to the death for the throne.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact nature and extent of Nur Jahan's political power during Jahangir's reign is a subject of historical analysis.
- The cause of the Deccan famine of 1630–32 is attributed to crop failures, but the effectiveness and scale of Shah Jahan's relief efforts are debated.
- The motivations behind Shah Jahan's costly and ultimately abandoned campaigns in Central Asia are interpreted differently by scholars, with some seeing them as attempts to reclaim Timurid heritage.
- The accusations of an incestuous relationship between Shah Jahan and his daughter Jahanara, propagated by some contemporary travelers, are dismissed by modern historians as court gossip and rumor.
