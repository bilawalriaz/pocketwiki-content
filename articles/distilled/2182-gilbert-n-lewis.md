# Gilbert N. Lewis

## Overview
Gilbert Newton Lewis (1875–1946) was a foundational American physical chemist whose work on chemical bonding and thermodynamics shaped modern chemistry. He is best known for discovering the covalent bond and the concept of electron pairs, and for proposing the Lewis acid-base theory. Despite being nominated for the Nobel Prize 41 times, he never won, making his case a major Nobel controversy. His mentorship at UC Berkeley helped establish it as a world-leading center for chemistry.

## Timeline
- **1875** — Born in Weymouth, Massachusetts.
- **1899** — Earns Ph.D. in chemistry from Harvard University.
- **1912** — Becomes professor and dean of the College of Chemistry at UC Berkeley.
- **1916** — Publishes classic paper "The Atom and the Molecule," proposing the shared electron pair (covalent) bond.
- **1923** — Formulates the electron-pair theory of acids and bases.
- **1926** — Coins the term "photon" for the smallest unit of radiant energy.
- **1933** — Produces the first pure sample of heavy water (deuterium oxide).
- **1946** — Found dead in his Berkeley laboratory; cause of death debated.

## Body

### Early Career and Thermodynamics
After earning his Ph.D. from Harvard, Lewis studied in Germany and taught at MIT. His early work focused on chemical thermodynamics, where he sought to create a rigorous system for chemists. He introduced the concepts of "fugacity" (an effective pressure for real gases) and "activity" to describe the tendency of substances to undergo chemical change. He championed the use of free energy and entropy over simple heat of reaction, laying groundwork for modern chemical thermodynamics.

### Theory of Chemical Bonding
Lewis's most famous contribution came in 1916 with his theory of the shared electron pair bond. Building on his earlier "cubical atom" model, he proposed that atoms achieve stability by sharing electrons, forming what we now call a covalent bond. He introduced Lewis dot structures to visualize valence electrons and defined "odd molecules" (now known as free radicals). This theory provided a concrete model for chemical bonding that was later expanded by Irving Langmuir and Linus Pauling.

### Acids, Bases, and Other Work
In 1923, Lewis proposed a broader definition of acids and bases based on electron pairs: a Lewis acid is an electron-pair acceptor, and a Lewis base is an electron-pair donor. This theory unified many chemical reactions beyond the traditional hydrogen-ion definition. His other significant work included the first production of pure heavy water in 1933, the discovery of tetraoxygen (O₄) in 1924, and early papers on relativity. In his final years, he researched photochemistry, establishing that phosphorescence involves light emission from an excited triplet state.

### Legacy and Nobel Controversy
Lewis was a transformative figure at UC Berkeley, where he mentored numerous future Nobel laureates, including Harold Urey, William Giauque, and Glenn Seaborg. His 41 Nobel nominations without a win are attributed to various factors, including a possible personal enmity with Walther Nernst and the influence of Nernst's colleague on the Nobel committee. He died in 1946 under disputed circumstances, with some believing his death was a suicide following a lunch with his rival Irving Langmuir.

## Terms
- **Covalent Bond:** A chemical bond formed by the sharing of an electron pair between atoms.
- **Lewis Acid:** A chemical species that accepts an electron pair.
- **Lewis Base:** A chemical species that donates an electron pair.
- **Fugacity:** A thermodynamic property that describes the "escaping tendency" of a substance from a phase; an effective pressure for real gases.
- **Free Energy:** A thermodynamic potential that measures the useful work obtainable from a system at constant temperature and pressure.
- **Photon:** The term coined by Lewis for the smallest unit of radiant energy (light).
- **Heavy Water:** Deuterium oxide (D₂O), a form of water where hydrogen is replaced by deuterium.
- **Triplet State:** An excited electronic state where two electrons have parallel spins.

## Debates and open questions
- **Cause of Death:** The official cause was coronary artery disease, but many believe Lewis died by suicide, possibly triggered by a lunch with his Nobel-winning rival Irving Langmuir.
- **Nobel Prize Omission:** The reasons for his repeated failure to win the Nobel Prize are debated, involving personal rivalries (notably with Walther Nernst) and possible procedural blocking by members of the Nobel committee.
