# Medication

## Overview
Medication (or medicament) is a substance used to diagnose, cure, treat, or prevent disease. Drug therapy (pharmacotherapy) relies on pharmacology for advancement and pharmacy for management. Drugs are classified by control level (prescription vs. over-the-counter), mechanism, route, or therapeutic effect. Discovery and development are lengthy, expensive processes (averaging ~US$1.8 billion per new molecular entity in 2010) involving pharmaceutical companies, academia, and governments. Regulation aims to ensure safety, quality, and efficacy, while pricing policies vary globally. Controversies persist regarding access to unapproved drugs, affordability of essential medicines, and environmental contamination from pharmaceutical pollutants.

## Timeline

- **50–70 CE** — *De materia medica* by Pedanius Dioscorides becomes standard pharmacological text for 1,500+ years.
- **9th century AD** — Al-Kindi writes *De Gradibus*; Ibn Sina (Avicenna) writes *The Canon of Medicine* (medieval Islamic world).
- **1842** — Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr. critiques 19th-century drug efficacy.
- **1932** — Gerhard Domagk introduces first antibiotics (sulfa drugs/"wonder drugs").
- **1938** — U.S. Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act passed after Elixir Sulfanilamide disaster; establishes FDA filing requirements.
- **1940s** — Mass production of penicillin enables widespread antimicrobial therapy (WWII collaboration).
- **1951** — U.S. Humphrey-Durham Amendment mandates prescription-only status for certain drugs.
- **1960** — FDA approves Enovid, the first oral contraceptive.
- **1962** — U.S. Kefauver-Harris Amendment requires clinical trials proving efficacy and safety for new drugs.
- **1980s** — ACT UP forms to pressure faster AIDS drug approvals.
- **1998** — Viagra released for erectile dysfunction.
- **2001** — Abigail Alliance founded to advocate for terminally ill patient access to investigational drugs.
- **2010** — Estimated R&D cost per new molecular entity reaches ~US$1.8 billion. |

## Body

### Definition and Classification
Medication is defined variably by jurisdiction: the EU defines "medicinal product" as any substance presented for treating/preventing disease or modifying physiological functions via pharmacological, immunological, or metabolic action. The US defines "drug" as a substance (other than food) intended to affect body structure/function or diagnose/cure/prevent disease. A placebo is a medication without an active ingredient used in research. Key classifications include **small molecule drugs** (chemically synthesized) vs. **biological medical products** (recombinant proteins, vaccines, gene/cell therapies). The **Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) Classification System** organizes drugs by organ/system, therapeutic, and chemical properties. "Specialty drugs" are an ill-defined class requiring special handling, monitoring, or regulatory restrictions, typically at high cost.

### Usage Patterns
Surveys of elderly Americans (avg. age 71) show high utilization: in 2005–2006, 84% used ≥1 prescription drug, 44% used ≥1 OTC drug, and 52% used ≥1 dietary supplement. By 2010–2011, prescription use rose to 88%, OTC use fell to 38%, and supplement use rose to 64%.

### Drug Discovery
Historically, drugs were found via traditional remedies or serendipity. **Classical pharmacology** screened chemical libraries in whole organisms. **Reverse pharmacology** (post-human genome) uses high-throughput screening against isolated biological targets hypothesized to modify disease. Modern discovery optimizes "hits" for affinity, selectivity (reducing side effects), efficacy, metabolic stability (half-life), and oral bioavailability. The process remains "lengthy, expensive, difficult, and inefficient" with low success rates. The output is a patent; subsequent clinical trials (Phases I–III) are costly and failure-prone. Small firms often discover leads and sell rights to large firms with trial resources.

### Drug Development
Development brings a lead compound to market. Steps: **Discovery** (identifying the molecule); **Development** (formulation into pills, injections, etc.); **Preclinical research** (lab/animal safety testing); **Clinical testing** (human trials for safety/efficacy); **Regulatory Review** (e.g., FDA); **Post-Market Surveillance** (ongoing safety monitoring). Biological products follow similar laws but differ in manufacturing (biological vs. chemical processes).

### Regulation
Regulation protects public health by ensuring safety, quality, and efficacy. Most jurisdictions require registration before marketing. Drugs are typically split into **OTC** (no prescription, deemed safe for self-use), **Prescription** (require licensed prescriber due to risks), and sometimes **Behind-the-counter** (sold only by pharmacist without prescription). The **International Narcotics Control Board** enforces global prohibitions on specific substances. **Off-label use** allows physicians to prescribe approved drugs for unapproved indications. Medical errors include over-prescription, polypharmacy, and dosage ambiguities; a 2000 Delphi conference sought a uniform definition for prescription errors.

### Drug Pricing
Pricing models differ: **UK** uses the Pharmaceutical Price Regulation Scheme (PPRS) negotiating prices with industry (4.75% payment percentage in 2017). **Canada** uses the Patented Medicine Prices Review Board and International Therapeutic Class Comparison Test (vs. 7 comparator countries). **Brazil** regulates via *Medicamento Genérico* legislation (since 1999). **India** uses the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority. **US** prices result from negotiations between manufacturers and insurers; costs are among the highest globally, attributed to government-granted monopolies and rising R&D costs. US innovation remains high (29 of 75 top-selling drugs from US firms in 2000).

### Blockbuster Drugs
A **blockbuster drug** generates >$1 billion annual revenue. Cimetidine was the first. These typically treat highly prevalent chronic conditions, ensuring long-term patient use.

### History of Pharmacology
**Ancient:** Plant-based treatments date to prehistory. Key texts: Kahun Papyrus (1800 BC), *Atharvaveda* (2nd millennium BC, India), *De materia medica* (50–70 CE, Greece). Ayurveda (from ~400 BC) required knowledge of ten arts (distillation, metallurgy, pharmacy, etc.). Babylonian medicine used creams/pills.
**Medieval:** Islamic scholars (Al-Kindi, Avicenna) cataloged drugs. Western Europe relied on opium and quinine; folklore and toxic metal compounds were common. Theodoric Borgognoni advanced antiseptics/anesthetics.
**Modern:** 19th-century drugs were largely ineffective. WWI saw Carrel-Dakin wound irrigation. Inter-war period brought sulfa antibiotics. WWII drove mass penicillin production. Late 1920s staples: aspirin, insulin, digitalis. 1930s: antibiotics emerge. 1950s: corticosteroids, antipsychotics (chlorpromazine), antihistamines. **US Regulatory Milestones:** 1938 FD&C Act (safety filing); 1951 Humphrey-Durham (prescription mandate); 1962 Kefauver-Harris (efficacy trials). 1970s: generic substitution laws address chronic disease costs. 2006: Medicare Part D adds drug coverage. As of 2008, US leads research; US firms developed 29 of 75 top-selling drugs in 2000.

### Controversies
**Access to Unapproved Drugs:** "Compassionate use"/"expanded access" programs (US: treatment INDs; elsewhere: Named Patient Programs) allow pre-approval access for exhausted alternatives. Advocacy groups (ACT UP, Abigail Alliance) push broader access. BioMarin (2013) faced scrutiny over cancer drug access.
**Access and Pricing:** WHO **Essential Medicines** should be affordable/available. Most are off-patent in developing world; access barriers are infrastructure/poverty. **TRIPS Agreement/Doha Declaration** balance IP rights (developed world) vs. generic access/compulsory licenses (developing world). Critics argue patents enable unaffordable prices; marketing spend often doubles R&D. *Novartis v. Union of India* (Gleevec patent) highlighted tensions; Supreme Court ruled against Novartis.
**Environmental Issues:** Pharmaceuticals are "ubiquitous" in water/soil (ng/L–μg/L) from wastewater effluent (industrial/municipal) and agricultural antibiotic runoff. Classified as **Environmentally Persistent Pharmaceutical Pollutants (EPPPs)**: inherently biodegradable but "pseudopersistent" due to constant replenishment. They bioaccumulate in species and act as **Ecological Disrupting Compounds** with gradual food-web effects.

## Terms
- ****Pharmacotherapy**** — Drug therapy; treatment of disease using pharmaceutical drugs.
- ****Small Molecule Drug**** — A low molecular weight organic compound, usually derived from chemical synthesis, that can regulate a biological process.
- ****Biological Medical Product**** — A medicinal product derived from biological sources (e.g., recombinant proteins, vaccines, blood products, gene/cell therapies).
- ****Reverse Pharmacology**** — Drug discovery approach screening compound libraries against isolated biological targets (target-based), enabled by genome sequencing.
- ****New Molecular Entity (NME)**** — A novel active substance not previously approved for marketing; the output of drug discovery.
- ****Off-Label Use**** — Prescribing an approved drug for an indication, dosage, or population not formally approved by the regulatory agency.
- ****Blockbuster Drug**** — A drug generating >$1 billion in annual revenue for its manufacturer. |
- ****Essential Medicines**** — WHO-defined drugs satisfying priority health needs, intended to be available, affordable, and in appropriate dosage forms.
- ****Environmentally Persistent Pharmaceutical Pollutants (EPPPs)**** — Pharmaceuticals that are biodegradable but continuously replenished in the environment, leading to pseudo-persistence and ecological disruption.
- ****Compassionate Use / Expanded Access**** — Regulatory pathways providing pre-approval drug access to patients with serious conditions who lack alternatives and cannot enter clinical trials.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **R&D Cost Justification:** Whether high drug prices (enabled by patents) are necessary to recoup R&D investments, given evidence that marketing expenditures often exceed R&D.
- **Access vs. Innovation:** Balancing intellectual property protections (TRIPS) incentivizing innovation against the need for affordable generics and compulsory licensing in developing nations.
- **Pre-Approval Access Ethics:** Defining fair criteria for "compassionate use" programs; managing data collection and distribution control for unapproved drugs.
- **Environmental Impact Management:** Determining regulatory thresholds for EPPPs; mitigating "pseudopersistent" contamination from wastewater and agriculture without compromising human health access.
- **Prescription Error Standardization:** Establishing a universal definition for prescription errors to enable consistent study and reduction of medical errors (polypharmacy, dosing ambiguities).
