# Swahili

## Overview  
Swahili (Kiswahili) is a Bantu language of the Atlantic–Congo family, originally spoken by the Swahili people along the East African coast. With 150–200 million speakers, it serves as a major lingua franca across East and Southern Africa and is an official language of the East African Community. Its vocabulary is heavily influenced by Arabic (about 40%), reflecting centuries of coastal trade and cultural exchange, while its grammar remains fundamentally Bantu.

## Timeline  
- **Pre-1600 CE**: Core vocabulary develops from Sabaki Bantu languages along the East African coast  
- **16th–18th centuries**: Arabic loanwords accumulate through Arab trade and Islamic missionary activity  
- **1711**: Earliest known Swahili documents—letters from Kilwa in Arabic script  
- **1884–85**: Berlin Conference assigns German East Africa; Germans formalize Swahili in schools and switch script from Arabic to Latin  
- **1919**: Swahili becomes medium of instruction in Tanganyika under British mandate  
- **1928**: Inter-territorial conference adopts Zanzibar dialect as standard Swahili  
- **1961**: Independent Tanganyika adopts Swahili as national language under Julius Nyerere  
- **1964**: Tanzania formed; Taasisi ya Uchunguzi wa Kiswahili (TUKI) established  
- **2010**: Kenya adopts Swahili as official language  
- **2015**: East African Kiswahili Commission (EAKC) begins operations  
- **2022**: UN declares July 7 as Swahili Language Day; Uganda makes Swahili official  

## Body  

### Classification and Origin  
Swahili belongs to the Bantu branch of the Niger–Congo (Atlantic–Congo) family, specifically the Sabaki subgroup. In Guthrie’s classification, it falls under Bantu zone G. Approximately 40% of its vocabulary consists of Arabic loanwords, primarily from Omani Arabic, introduced during centuries of Arab trade and Islamic influence. However, Thomas Spear argues that core vocabulary—72–91% of basic word lists—is inherited from Sabaki, with Arabic contributing only 2–8%. The name "Swahili" itself derives from the Arabic *sawāḥilī*, meaning "of the coasts."

### Colonial Period and Standardization  
Colonial powers significantly shaped Swahili’s spread. Arabs used it as a trade language and tool for Islamic instruction, leading to its initial writing in Arabic script. Portuguese contact added vocabulary. Germans, after the Berlin Conference, formalized Swahili in education and administration, switching the script to Latin. After World War I, Britain expanded Swahili’s use across East Africa. A 1928 conference in Mombasa selected the Zanzibar dialect (Kiunguja) as the standard, establishing the orthography still used today.

### Current Status  
Swahili is an official language of the East African Community (EAC) and a working language of the African Union and SADC. It is widely spoken as a first or second language across Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Rwanda, Burundi, DR Congo, and parts of Mozambique, Somalia, and Malawi. In Tanzania, it unifies over 120 ethnic groups and is used in primary education, government, and media. Kenya made it official in 2010; Uganda followed in 2022. The EAC established the East African Kiswahili Commission (EAKC) in 2015 to promote and standardize the language regionally.

### Dialects and Related Varieties  
Modern Standard Swahili is based on Kiunguja, the dialect of Zanzibar City. Other significant coastal dialects include Kiamu (Lamu), Kimvita (Mombasa), and Pemba Swahili. Some varieties, like Chimwiini and Kibajuni in Somalia, are considered distinct languages by some scholars. Shikomor (Comorian), spoken in Comoros and Mayotte, is closely related but often treated as separate. Several Swahili-based pidgins and creoles exist, including Sheng (Nairobi youth slang) and Settler Swahili.

### Grammar and Phonology  
Swahili is not tonal, unlike most other Bantu languages. It has five vowel phonemes (/ɑ/, /ɛ/, /i/, /ɔ/, /u/) and a consonant system that includes prenasalized stops. Noun class agreement is central: prefixes like *m-/wa-* (singular/plural humans), *ki-/vi-* (artifacts/diminutives), and *m-/mi-* (trees/plants) govern concord in adjectives, verbs, and pronouns. Borrowed words are often reanalyzed to fit these classes—for example, Arabic *kitāb* becomes *kitabu* (class 7), plural *vitabu* (class 8).

### Religious and Political Identity  
Swahili played a key role in spreading Islam through coastal madrasas and later Christianity via European missionaries. Politically, it became a symbol of unity: Nyerere used it to forge a national identity in post-independence Tanzania, and it remains a powerful marker of East African regional identity.

## Terms  
- **Bantu**: A branch of the Niger–Congo language family, spoken across much of sub-Saharan Africa  
- **Sabaki**: A subgroup of Bantu languages, including Swahili, spoken along the East African coast  
- **Lingua franca**: A common language used between speakers of different native languages  
- **Noun class**: A grammatical system in Bantu languages where prefixes categorize nouns and govern agreement  
- **Concord**: Grammatical agreement where words change form to match the noun class of their associated noun  
- **Ajami script**: An Arabic-based writing system adapted for non-Arabic languages, historically used for Swahili  
- **Kiunguja**: The Zanzibar City dialect that forms the basis of Modern Standard Swahili  
- **Prenasalized consonant**: A consonant sound preceded by a nasal sound, common in Bantu languages  
- **Ujamaa**: Nyerere’s policy of African socialism, which promoted Swahili as a unifying national language  
- **BAKITA**: Baraza la Kiswahili la Taifa, Tanzania’s national Swahili council responsible for language standardization  

## Debates and Open Questions  
- **Arabic vs. Bantu influence**: While some sources claim 40% of Swahili vocabulary is Arabic, Thomas Spear argues core vocabulary is predominantly Bantu, with Arabic contributing only a small fraction of everyday words.  
- **Dialect classification**: The status of varieties like Shikomor (Comorian), Chimwiini, and Kibajuni remains debated—some classify them as dialects of Swahili, others as distinct languages.  
- **Kingozi’s role**: The historical significance of Kingozi, an extinct coastal dialect, is contested among scholars, with some viewing it as a literary prestige dialect and others as a minor variant.  
- **Future adoption in Somalia**: Despite Somalia’s EAC membership, the spread of Swahili faces competition from English and Arabic, creating uncertainty about its adoption.
