# Enclosure

## Overview
Enclosure in England was the process of appropriating common land, fencing it off, and converting it into private property. This practice, which peaked between 1760 and 1832, fundamentally transformed English agriculture and society by ending the medieval open-field system. It was driven by a desire for greater agricultural efficiency and profit, but it also caused significant social upheaval, displacing commoners and provoking widespread protest.

## Body

### The Open-Field System and Its Decline
Before enclosure, much of lowland England used the open-field system. Manors were organized into large common fields divided into narrow strips farmed by the lord and tenants. This system used a three-field crop rotation to maintain soil fertility, with one field left fallow each year for grazing. Tenants had customary rights to pasture animals on stubble and common waste land. This system began to decline as landowners sought more efficient, profitable farming methods. Innovations like the Norfolk four-course system (wheat, turnips, barley, clover) increased yields but required enclosed fields to protect crops like turnips from common grazing animals.

### Methods and Legislation
Enclosure occurred through both informal and formal processes. Informally, it could happen via "unity of possession" when one owner consolidated all strips in an area. Formally, it was achieved through acts of Parliament, which became the dominant method after 1750. Between 1604 and 1914, over 5,200 acts enclosed about 6.8 million acres, or one-fifth of England. The process was managed by appointed commissioners who divided land, often replacing old roads with new, straight "enclosure roads." While the stated goal was agricultural improvement, motives also included increasing land value and profit, especially from wool production in the Tudor period.

### Social and Economic Impact
The enclosure movement had profound social consequences. It displaced many small farmers, cottagers, and squatters, destroying the traditional peasant way of life and creating a landless labor force that migrated to towns for industrial work. Historians debate the net effect: some, like the Hammonds, saw it as fatal to the common people, while others, like Chambers and Mingay, argue it increased food production and employment. Marxist historians view enclosure as "primitive accumulation" that fueled the rise of capitalism. The process was a major cause of social unrest, with enclosure riots being the pre-eminent form of protest from the 1530s to the 1640s.

### Social Unrest and Rebellion
Protests against enclosure were widespread and sometimes violent. The Midland Revolt of 1607, led by "Captain Pouch," saw villagers in Leicestershire, Warwickshire, and Northamptonshire pulling down hedges. The conflict culminated in the Newton Rebellion of 8 June 1607, where a pitched battle between protesters and gentry forces left 40-50 people dead. Earlier, enclosure was a grievance in the Peasants' Revolt (1381) and a central issue in Kett's Rebellion (1549). Urban artisans also rioted against enclosure in towns across England.

## Terms
- **Enclosure**: The appropriation of common land, enclosing it with fences or hedges, and depriving commoners of traditional access and usage rights.
- **Open-field system**: A medieval agricultural system where land in a manor was divided into large common fields, with tenants farming scattered strips.
- **Common land**: Land over which certain people held traditional rights, such as grazing animals or gathering wood.
- **Statute of Merton (1235)**: The first law allowing manorial lords to enclose common land if they left sufficient pasture for commoners.
- **Norfolk four-course system**: An agricultural rotation system (wheat, turnips, barley, clover) that increased yields but required enclosed fields.
- **Inclosure Act (1773)**: Legislation that created a legal process for enclosure, removing commoners' rights, often with inadequate compensation.
- **Commissioners of inclosure**: Officials appointed by Parliament to oversee the division and allotment of common fields.
- **Tillage Acts (1489-1597)**: Tudor laws passed to limit enclosure and prevent the depopulation caused by conversion to sheep pasture.
- **Copyhold**: A form of land tenure where a tenant held land according to the manor court's custom, recorded on a "copy" of the court roll.

## Debates and Open Questions
Historians disagree on the net impact of enclosure. The traditional view, held by historians like the Hammonds, is that it was a social disaster that impoverished the common people. A revisionist perspective, from scholars like Chambers and Mingay, contends that enclosure was necessary for agricultural progress, increased food production, and ultimately benefited the growing population. Economists like M.E. Turner and D. McCloskey have questioned whether enclosure actually led to a clear increase in efficiency over the open-field system. The role of enclosure in the rise of capitalism and the creation of a proletariat remains a key point of Marxist analysis.
