# Shinto

## Overview

Shinto is Japan's indigenous polytheistic and animistic religion, centered on the veneration of kami—supernatural entities believed to inhabit all things, including natural forces and landscape features. With no central authority, founder, or canonical text, Shinto exists in diverse local and regional forms, emphasizing ritual practice over doctrine. Its historical development is debated: while kami veneration traces to the Yayoi period (300 BCE–250 CE), scholars argue that Shinto as a distinct religion was largely "invented" during the Meiji era (1868–1912), when State Shinto separated kami worship from Buddhism and aligned it with imperial nationalism.

## Timeline

- **300 BCE–250 CE** — Yayoi period: earliest traces of kami veneration
- **300–538 CE** — Kofun period: Buddhism enters Japan; syncretization begins
- **712** — Kojiki compiled, recording earliest kami traditions
- **718–927** — Ritsuryō legal codes and ritual manuals standardize state rites
- **1868–1912** — Meiji era: State Shinto established; Buddhism expelled from shrines
- **1945** — Post-war constitution separates Shinto from state
- **1946** — Emperor denies divinity; shrines lose state support

## Body

### Definition and Categorization

There is no universally agreed definition of Shinto. Scholars broadly define it as belief in kami, with institutions, rituals, and communal life based on kami worship. The term "Shinto" (meaning "way of the kami") has varied in meaning throughout history. Some practitioners view it as a "way" or tradition rather than a religion, partly to circumvent the post-war separation of religion and state. Unlike Western religions, Shinto has no single founder, canonical text, or exclusive doctrine. Japanese religious practice is highly pluralistic, with most people participating in both Shinto and Buddhist activities. Scholars debate whether pre-modern practices constitute "Shinto" as a distinct phenomenon, with many arguing it was institutionalized only in the 19th century.

### Kami

Shinto is polytheistic, venerating kami—supernatural entities that inhabit all things, including natural forces, deceased humans, and prominent landscape features. The term "kami" is conceptually fluid, also applying to the numinous power inspiring awe. Practitioners believe in "eight million kami," connoting infinity. Kami are not omnipotent or necessarily immortal; they can be benevolent or destructive. They are usually associated with specific places and may have messengers taking animal forms (e.g., Inari's fox, Hachiman's dove). Humans can become kami after death, particularly ancestors. Kami are enshrined in physical objects called go-shintai, such as mirrors, swords, or stones, which are concealed from view.

### Cosmology and Afterlife

Shinto cosmology includes three realms: Takama-no-hara (High Heaven, home of kami), Utsushi-yo (human world), and Yomotsu-kuni (Nether World of unclean spirits). The creative principle is musubi, associated with its own kami. There is no Shinto eschatology. The mitama (spirit/soul) has four aspects. After death, spirits survive and assist the living, becoming family kami after 33 years. Ancestral spirits are sometimes thought to reside in mountains. Restless spirits (obake) who died violently may inflict suffering unless pacified.

### Purity and Impurity

A central theme is avoiding kegare (pollution/impurity) and ensuring harae (purity). Humans are fundamentally pure; kegare is temporary and correctable. Pollutants include death, disease, witchcraft, blood from childbirth or menstruation, and certain words (imi-kotoba) like "death" or "meat." Purification rituals include misogi (water/salt cleansing), temizu (hand/face washing), and harae ceremonies using haraigushi (paper streamers) or o-nusa (evergreen branches). The yaku-barai prevents misfortune; the oharae is a major end-of-year purification.

### Practices

Shinto emphasizes ritual over doctrine. Jinja (shrines) number around 100,000 in Japan, marked by torii gateways. Worship involves offerings (saisen), bell-ringing, bowing, clapping, and silent prayer (kashiwade). Priests (kannushi) oversee rituals, assisted by miko (shrine maidens). Home shrines (kamidana) are common. Festivals (matsuri) mark seasons and agricultural cycles. Kagura dance, ema plaques, omikuji divination, and amulets (omamori, ofuda) are widespread practices. Rites of passage include hatsumiyamairi (child's first shrine visit) and seijin shiki (coming-of-age).

### History

Kami veneration began in the Yayoi period. Buddhism's arrival in the Kofun period led to shinbutsu-shūgō (religious syncretization), making kami worship and Buddhism functionally inseparable. The 8th-century Kojiki and Nihon Shoki recorded kami traditions. During the Meiji era, nationalist leaders expelled Buddhist influence, creating State Shinto and promoting emperor worship. Post-WWII, the 1947 constitution separated religion from state. Modern Shinto has increasingly emphasized environmentalist and nature-centered themes.

## Terms

- **Kami**: Supernatural entities inhabiting all things; gods or spirits
- **Jinja**: Public shrine dedicated to kami worship
- **Kannushi**: Shinto priest who oversees shrine rituals
- **Torii**: Two-post gateway marking shrine entrance
- **Kegare**: Pollution or impurity requiring purification
- **Harae**: Purification ritual restoring spiritual cleanliness
- **Matsuri**: Shinto festival, often seasonal or agricultural
- **Kamidana**: Household shrine for kami veneration
- **Shinbutsu-shūgō**: Syncretization of kami worship and Buddhism
- **State Shinto**: Meiji-era system merging Shinto with imperial nationalism

## Debates and Open Questions

Scholars debate when Shinto became a distinct religion. Some argue it was "invented" during the Meiji era, while others see continuity from ancient practices. The classification of Shinto as "indigenous" is contested, given its absorption of Chinese and Buddhist influences. Whether pre-Meiji practices constitute "Shinto" or merely "kami worship" remains debated. The post-war separation from state and modern emphasis on nature-based spirituality are seen by critics as rhetorical strategies to distance the tradition from militarist associations. Academic research has historically been dominated by Shinto theologians, raising questions about objectivity in historical analysis.
