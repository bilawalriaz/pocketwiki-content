# Masaccio

## Overview
Masaccio (1401–1428), born Tommaso di Ser Giovanni di Simone, was a Florentine painter regarded as the founder of Early Italian Renaissance painting. In a career spanning roughly six years, he broke decisively with the International Gothic style by introducing systematic linear perspective, chiaroscuro (modeling form through light and shadow without outlines), and a sculptural, gravity-bound naturalism to figure painting. His frescoes in the Brancacci Chapel and the *Holy Trinity* in Santa Maria Novella established a new visual language that influenced generations of artists, including Leonardo da Vinci, Michelangelo, and Raphael. He died at age twenty-six, leaving the Brancacci Chapel unfinished.

- **1423 (per Vasari)** — Travels to Rome with Masolino; absorbs classical antiquity influence.
- **Feb 19, 1426** — Commissioned for Pisa Altarpiece (80 florins); works in Pisa and Florence simultaneously.
- **Sept 1425** — Masolino departs for Hungary; Masaccio continues Brancacci Chapel alone.
- **1426** — Leaves Brancacci Chapel unfinished to pursue other commissions.
- **1427** — Returns to paint *Resurrection of the Son of Theophilus* in Brancacci Chapel; leaves it unfinished.
- **c. 1427** — Completes *Holy Trinity* fresco in Santa Maria Novella, earliest surviving systematic linear perspective.
- **Summer 1428** — Dies in Rome at age 26; cause unknown (legend cites poisoning).
- **1480s** — Filippino Lippi completes the Brancacci Chapel.

## Body

### Early Life and Training
Masaccio was born into a family of carpenters (the name *Cassai* derives from *casse*, chests) in Castel San Giovanni di Altura. His father, a notary, died in 1406; his mother subsequently married an apothecary connected to the local artistic community. No records document his apprenticeship, though convention suggests he would have begun around age twelve, likely in Florence. He first appears in documents on January 7, 1422, when he enrolled in the Florentine painters' guild as an independent master, signing as "Masus S. Johannis Simonis pictor populi S. Nicholae de Florentia."

### First Works and Collaboration with Masolino
His earliest surviving work, the *San Giovenale Triptych* (1422), discovered in 1961 near his hometown, revives Giotto’s volumetric solidity rather than following contemporary Gothic elegance. Around 1424, he collaborated with the established painter Masolino da Panicale on the *Virgin and Child with Saint Anne* (Sant'Anna Metterza). The division of labor is stark: Masolino painted the delicate, flat St. Anne and angels, while Masaccio rendered the solid, hefty Virgin and Child, immediately distinguishing his gravitational naturalism from his senior partner’s International Gothic grace.

### Maturity and the Roman Journey
In Florence, Masaccio studied Giotto’s works and befriended the architect Filippo Brunelleschi and sculptor Donatello. According to Vasari, they prompted a trip to Rome with Masolino in 1423. This exposure to ancient art freed Masaccio from Gothic and Byzantine conventions, a shift visible in the lost *Sagra* fresco (1422, known via copies) and the Pisa Altarpiece. In Pisa, where Donatello was simultaneously working, Masaccio likely absorbed sculptural plasticity from Donatello before mastering Brunelleschi’s mathematical perspective system.

### The Brancacci Chapel
In 1424, Felice Brancacci commissioned Masaccio and Masolino to fresco the Brancacci Chapel in Santa Maria del Carmine. Work began c. 1425. The program depicts the life of St. Peter as an allegory of salvation, flanked by the *Temptation* and *Expulsion of Adam and Eve*. Masaccio’s scenes (*Expulsion*, *Tribute Money*, *Resurrection of the Son of Theophilus*) employ linear and atmospheric perspective, directional light (shadows fall consistently toward the chapel window in *The Tribute Money*), and chiaroscuro to create figures of unprecedented physical presence and emotional weight. Masolino left for Hungary in September 1425; Masaccio continued alone but abandoned the project in 1426, possibly due to the patron’s financial decline. He returned briefly in 1427 to begin the *Resurrection of the Son of Theophilus* but left it unfinished. The chapel was completed by Filippino Lippi in the 1480s. A 1771 fire destroyed some scenes; others were obscured by smoke and later marble slabs, removed in the 20th century.

### The Pisa Altarpiece
Commissioned February 19, 1426, for the Carmelite church in Pisa, this polyptych was dismantled in the 18th century; eleven of ~twenty panels survive. The central *Madonna and Child* (National Gallery, London) demonstrates a sculptural Virgin and a rigorously perspectival throne, marking Masaccio’s synthesis of Donatello’s tactile form and Brunelleschi’s optical science.

### The Holy Trinity
Painted c. 1427 for Santa Maria Novella, the *Holy Trinity* is widely considered Masaccio’s masterwork and the earliest surviving painting to use systematic linear perspective, likely devised with Brunelleschi’s assistance. Masaccio incised perspective lines into the wet plaster using strings radiating from a nail at the vanishing point (at the base of the cross), marks still visible today. The composition places the Trinity above a trompe-l'œil chapel housing a skeleton on a sarcophagus bearing the memento mori inscription: "I once was what now you are and what I am, you shall yet be." The iconography transposes the Golgotha chapel of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem, fusing theological doctrine with architectural illusionism.

### Final Works and Death
Before departing for Rome in 1428, Masaccio painted a lost *Nativity* and *Annunciation*. In Rome, Masolino was frescoing the Basilica di San Clemente; Masaccio may have contributed a panel of *St. Jerome and St. John the Baptist* to Masolino’s altarpiece for Santa Maria Maggiore. He died in Rome in summer 1428 at age twenty-six. Vasari records a legend of poisoning by a jealous rival; Brunelleschi reportedly mourned, "We have suffered a great loss."

### Legacy
Masaccio redirected Italian painting from Gothic idealization toward a humanist, naturalistic world grounded in optical truth. Vasari asserted that all major artists from Leonardo to Raphael studied the Brancacci Chapel as a fundamental academy. His influence permeated Florentine minor masters (Andrea di Giusto, Giovanni dal Ponte) and echoed centuries later in works like Auguste Couder’s *The Death of Masaccio* (1817). Only four frescoes indisputably by his hand survive, yet they constitute the pivot point of Renaissance painting.

## Terms
- ****Quattrocento**** — The 15th century (1400s) in Italian art, marking the Early Renaissance.
- ****Linear perspective**** — A mathematical system for creating the illusion of three-dimensional depth on a flat surface using converging orthogonals meeting at a vanishing point.
- ****Chiaroscuro**** — The modeling of form through gradations of light and shadow rather than outlines; Italian for "light-dark."
- ****Foreshortening**** — A perspective technique depicting an object or figure receding sharply into space by shortening its longitudinal axis.
- ****Fresco**** — *Buon fresco*: pigment applied to wet lime plaster so colors become integral with the wall as it dries.
- ****Vanishing point**** — The single point on the horizon line where parallel lines appear to converge in linear perspective.
- ****International Gothic**** — A late medieval style (c. 1375–1425) characterized by elegant elongation, decorative detail, and flattened space, which Masaccio rejected.
- ****Polyptych**** — A multi-panel altarpiece, typically hinged; the Pisa Altarpiece was a large example later dismantled.
- ****Sagra**** — A fresco commemorating a church consecration; Masaccio’s lost *Sagra* (1422) for Santa Maria del Carmine is known through copies, including one by Michelangelo.
- ****Memento mori**** — Latin: "remember you must die"; a visual reminder of mortality, exemplified by the skeleton in the *Holy Trinity*.

## Debates and open questions
- **Apprenticeship**: No documentary evidence survives for Masaccio’s training; the identity of his master remains unknown.
- **Masolino collaboration**: The nature of their relationship—whether Masolino was mentor, partner, or subordinate—is debated; the *Virgin and Child with Saint Anne* shows a startlingly equal or even Masaccio-dominant division.
- **Roman trip (1423)**: Vasari is the sole source for the journey with Masolino; some scholars question its timing or occurrence, though classical influence in Masaccio’s work is undisputed.
- **Brancacci Chapel abandonment**: Masolino’s 1425 departure for Hungary and Masaccio’s 1426 exit are attributed variously to artistic disputes, patron insolvency, or pre-planned schedules; no consensus exists.
- **Holy Trinity patron**: No contemporary document names the patron; recent archival research suggests the working-class Berti family, based on tomb ownership records at the fresco’s base.
- **Cause of death**: Unknown; the poisoning legend reported by Vasari is uncorroborated.
- **Attribution of Roman works**: Masaccio’s possible contributions to Masolino’s San Clemente frescoes or the Santa Maria Maggiore altarpiece remain hypothetical.
