# French Wars of Religion

## Overview
The French Wars of Religion (1562–1598) were a series of eight or nine devastating civil wars between French Catholics and Protestant Huguenots. The conflict, which killed between two and four million people, severely weakened the monarchy and was only resolved when the Protestant leader Henry of Navarre converted to Catholicism, became King Henry IV, and issued the Edict of Nantes, granting Huguenots substantial rights. This compromise ended the main conflict but did not resolve underlying religious tensions, which resurfaced in the 17th century.

- **2 May 1598** — Peace of Vervins ends the war with Spain.
- **14 May 1610** — Henry IV is assassinated.
- **October 1685** — Louis XIV revokes the Edict of Nantes with the Edict of Fontainebleau.

## Body

### Background and Outbreak
Tensions between Catholics and Protestants (Huguenots) had been building since the 1530s, fueled by the spread of Calvinism, which appealed to many nobles. The accidental death of King Henry II in 1559 created a power vacuum, leading to a struggle between the Catholic Guise faction and Protestant leaders like Condé and Jeanne d'Albret. Regent Catherine de' Medici initially sought a middle path, issuing the tolerant Edict of Saint-Germain in 1562. However, the Massacre of Vassy by Guise partisans in March 1562 triggered the First War.

### The Wars and the St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre (1562–1573)
The first three wars (1562–1570) ended with edicts granting limited toleration but were followed by renewed conflict. The fragile peace was shattered by the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre in August 1572, where Catholic mobs killed thousands of Huguenots in Paris and across France. This atrocity, initially a targeted assassination of leaders, spiraled into widespread slaughter, devastating the Huguenot movement and provoking international outrage.

### The Catholic League and the War of the Three Henrys (1574–1589)
The formation of the ultra-Catholic League by the Guise family in 1576 further destabilized the kingdom. The death of the king's heir in 1584 made the Protestant Henry of Navarre the next in line to the throne, leading the League to ally with Spain and suppress Protestantism. This escalated into the War of the Three Henrys (Henry III, Henry of Guise, Henry of Navarre). King Henry III, after having Guise assassinated in 1588, was himself murdered in 1589, leaving Henry of Navarre as the rightful but unrecognized king.

### Henry IV's Conquest and the Edict of Nantes (1589–1598)
Henry IV fought a long campaign to secure his throne against the League and its Spanish allies. Realizing he could not take the Catholic capital of Paris as a Protestant, he converted in 1593, reportedly saying "Paris is well worth a mass." This decisive move allowed him to be crowned and enter Paris in 1594. To end the wars and rebuild the kingdom, he issued the Edict of Nantes in 1598, which granted Huguenots freedom of conscience, limited worship rights, and political safeguards, effectively creating a "state within a state."

### Aftermath
The Edict of Nantes provided a grudging peace but did not end religious strife. Huguenot rebellions in the 1620s were suppressed, and their political privileges were gradually eroded. In 1685, Louis XIV revoked the Edict with the Edict of Fontainebleau, forcing many Huguenots to flee France, which damaged the French economy and state.

## Terms
*   **Huguenots:** French Protestants who followed the Reformed tradition of Calvinism.
*   **Edict of Nantes:** A 1598 decree by Henry IV that granted substantial civil and religious rights to Huguenots, ending the main phase of the wars.
*   **Politiques:** A moderate faction during the wars who prioritized national unity and civil order over religious uniformity.
*   **Catholic League:** An ultra-Catholic political faction, led by the House of Guise, that opposed the monarchy and Protestantism.
*   **Salic Law:** The French law excluding women from inheriting the throne, which made the Protestant Henry of Navarre the heir after 1584.
*   **Edict of Fontainebleau:** A 1685 decree by Louis XIV that revoked the Edict of Nantes and outlawed Protestantism in France.
*   **Calvinism:** A Protestant theological system developed by John Calvin, emphasizing predestination and a strict moral code, which became the dominant form of French Protestantism.
*   **Iconoclasm:** The deliberate destruction of religious images and symbols, a practice that occurred on both sides during the wars.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Periodization:** Historians debate the exact start and end of the wars. While 1562–1598 is the core period, some extend the conflict to the Peace of Alès in 1629, which ended Huguenot political privileges.
*   **Causes of Violence:** Explanations for the turn to widespread violence vary, from the influence of radical leaders and eschatological preaching to the politicization of factions and peasant responses to seigneurial pressures.
*   **Nature of the Conflict:** Scholars debate whether the wars were primarily about religion or were driven by political power struggles among the nobility, with religion serving as a mobilizing force.
