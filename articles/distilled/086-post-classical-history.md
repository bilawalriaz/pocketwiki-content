# Post-classical history

## Overview

Post-classical history (c. 500–1450/1500 CE) is the periodization used by world historians to describe the era between classical societies and the early modern world. It roughly corresponds to the European Middle Ages but is applied globally, covering the expansion of civilizations, the spread of universal religions (Buddhism, Christianity, Islam), and the rapid growth of long-distance trade networks across Afro-Eurasia. The period saw the world's population roughly double from about 210 million in 500 CE to some 461 million in 1500 CE, despite major demographic catastrophes including the Plague of Justinian, the Mongol invasions, and the Black Death.

- **1492** — Columbus reaches Americas; Columbian exchange begins

## Body

### Historiography and periodization

Post-classical history is a framework developed by world historians in the late 20th and early 21st centuries. The term sometimes avoids preconceptions around "Middle Ages" or "Dark Ages," though applying it globally may itself be Eurocentric. Beginning and ending dates vary by region: Han China ended in 220 CE, the Western Roman Empire in 476 CE, the Gupta Empire in 543 CE, and the Sasanian Empire in 651 CE. World historians divide history into five or six major periods: early civilization, classical societies, post-classical, early modern, long nineteenth century, and contemporary/modern.

World history research has focused heavily on early modern globalization (post-1500), and historians around 2020 acknowledged that "a global history of the period between 500 and 1500 is still wanting." Regional historiographies are well-developed for Europe and the Islamic world, but understanding of sub-Saharan Africa and the Americas is more limited. Researchers have proposed three promising approaches: comparative history, connectedness/transnational relationships, and the study of globalization as an ancient process rather than a modern phenomenon. Climate history has also been proposed as a useful lens, since climate events affected all human populations.

### Global trends

The post-classical era saw three major developments: expansion of civilization into new geographic areas, the spread of universalizing missionary religions, and rapidly expanding trade networks. Historians identify three large self-contained world regions: Afro-Eurasia, the Americas, and Oceania.

**Growth of civilization:** China continued its dynastic cycle with improving bureaucracy. Islamic empires formed in the Middle East, North Africa, and Central Asia. The Mali and Songhai Empires arose in West Africa. Western Europe developed feudalism (authority fragmented into personal relationships with military elites) and manorialism, while Byzantium retained Roman features. In the Americas, the Mississippian culture, Aztec Empire, Wari Empire, and later Inca Empire developed. In Oceania, the Tuʻi Tonga Empire collected tribute across island chains by the 13th century.

**Spread of religions:** Buddhism, Christianity, and Islam—all universalizing, missionary religions—became widespread and often politically dominant across the Old World. Buddhism spread from India to China, Japan, Korea, and Vietnam. Christianity spread into northern and eastern Europe; the Catholic–Orthodox split encouraged religious diversity. Islam unified Arabian Bedouin clans and spread through rapid conquests across North Africa, Iberia, Persia, Central Asia, India, and Indonesia.

**Trade and communication:** The Silk Road continued spreading cultures and ideas. The camel enabled trans-Saharan trade connecting West Africa to Eurasia. Islamic empires adopted Greek, Roman, and Indian advances and spread them across their sphere of influence. The Mongol Empire established the Pax Mongolica, allowing goods, cultures, ideas, and disease to spread across Asia, Europe, and Africa. The Americas had separate, more restricted trade networks; Polynesia maintained long-distance communication of over 2,300 miles between Hawaii and Tahiti.

**Climate:** The Late Antique Little Ice Age, Medieval Warm Period (950–1250), and Little Ice Age affected regions globally, though effects varied. The extreme weather events of 536–537 were likely initiated by the eruption of Lake Ilopango caldera in El Salvador, causing global cooling, migrations, and crop failures. The Medieval Warm Period may have allowed Norse colonization of Greenland. After 1250, cooling affected the North Atlantic; the Little Ice Age persisted until the Industrial Revolution.

### Eurasian trends

**Feudalism:** Used to describe agricultural societies where central authority broke down into warrior aristocracies, feudalism characterized medieval Europe, the Islamic iqta' system, Indian feudalism, and Heian Japan. China, however, employed centralized bureaucracy, especially after 1000. The term's usefulness is debated; comparisons between feudal Europe and Japan have been particularly controversial, as Japan balanced decentralized military power with centralized imperial and monastic authority until the Sengoku period.

**Mongol Empire:** The largest continuous land empire in history (13th–14th centuries) emerged from nomadic tribes unified under Genghis Khan in 1206. It stretched from Central Europe to the Sea of Japan. Succession disputes after Möngke Khan's death led to civil war between Ariq Böke and Kublai Khan. By Kublai's death in 1294, the empire had fractured into four khanates: Golden Horde, Chagatai Khanate, Ilkhanate, and Yuan dynasty. The Yuan was overthrown by the Ming in 1368; all original khanates collapsed by 1500, but Chagatai descendants created the Mughal Empire in India.

**Silk Road:** This Eurasian trade route stimulated cultural exchange, spread religion and disease, and connected China to the Roman Empire. The rise of Islam closed the route to Christian Europe for centuries. It flourished again under Mongol rule in the 13th century. The route was vulnerable to plague: the Plague of Justinian (542) killed a quarter of the Mediterranean's population, and the Black Death reached Sicily via merchant ships in 1347. After the 15th century, sea travel pioneered by Europeans replaced the overland route.

**Plague and disease:** The first plague pandemic (Yersinia pestis, 541–549) originated possibly in the Tian Shan mountains or East Africa. It massively depopulated urban civilizations, destabilized empires, and contributed to early Muslim conquests by weakening Byzantium and Persia. The second pandemic (Black Death, 1347–1351) killed 25–50% of populations. Its Chinese origin is considered unlikely by historians given the fragmentation of the Mongol Empire and the 5,000-mile journey through sparsely populated Central Asia. In Western Europe, the plague led to rising wage labor, the return of slavery, and specialist economies.

**Science and arts:** Post-classical science combined European and Islamic medieval science, which interacted with each other. Chinese innovations (astronomy, papermaking) entered the Islamic world via Persian trade and the Battle of Talas River. Gunpowder's transmission to Europe is debated. The Yuan dynasty combined Chinese and Islamic cartography in 1303. In literature, poetry dominated in Europe, West Asia, South Asia, and China; Japan's Tale of Genji (9th century) is considered the world's first realistic novel. Medieval Europe uniquely developed harmonic music.

### History by region

**Africa:** Islam affected North Africa, the Sudan, and the east coast, though lower classes hardly changed beliefs. African civilizations divided into Christian (Horn of Africa), Islamic (Niger River Valley, Swahili Coast), and traditional societies. The trans-Saharan trade enabled empires of Ghana, Mali, and Songhai; Mansa Musa of Mali may have been the wealthiest person of his time. Timbuktu's University of Sankoré was internationally renowned. Egypt became a global trading power linking the Mediterranean to the Indian Ocean.

**Europe:** Western civilization reconstituted after Rome's fall into the Early Middle Ages (500–1000). Charlemagne's coronation in 800 began the church–monarch conflict that defined European politics for 500 years. The Byzantine Empire created the Code of Justinian and Christianized Bulgaria and Kievan Rus'. The High Middle Ages (c. 1000–1300) saw Crusades, chivalry, and Scholastic philosophy. The Black Death transformed feudalism and contributed to the Renaissance and Age of Discovery.

**West and Central Asia:** The weakened Byzantine and Sasanian Empires (after the war of 602–628) fell to rapid Muslim conquests. The Rashidun, Umayyad, and Abbasid Caliphates unified the Middle East and sparked the Islamic Golden Age. The Seljuk Turks captured Baghdad in 1055; the Crusades followed. The Mongols sacked Baghdad and advanced to Egypt's border before retreating in 1335. Timur's raids further devastated the region before the Ottomans arose.

**South Asia:** From the 8th–16th centuries, 20–40 states hosted diverse cultures. Buddhism predominated under the Pala Empire; the Nalanda mahavihara was a global scholarly center. The Chola kingdom controlled overseas territories and spread Hinduism. The Delhi Sultanate (from 1206) conquered North India; Buddhism declined while Hinduism survived. The Vijayanagara Empire remained unconquered in the south.

**Southeast Asia:** Trade through the Strait of Malacca funded kingdoms including the Khmer Empire (Angkor Thom), Srivijaya (a thalassocracy controlling the Sunda and Malacca Straits), and Pagan. Vietnam gained independence from China in the 10th century. Indonesian sailors may have colonized Madagascar. Islamization occurred in Indonesia through missionaries and merchants.

**East Asia:** China (Sui, Tang, Song dynasties, 581–1279) remained the world's largest economy and most technologically advanced society, with gunpowder, woodblock printing, and the magnetic compass. The Tang secured the Silk Road; the Song specialized in overseas trade, with economic advances considered an early industrial revolution. The Mongols conquered all five Chinese kingdoms by 1279. Korea and Japan underwent voluntary Sinicization; Sejong the Great created Hangul in the 15th century.

**Oceania:** Aboriginal Australians changed little since their arrival c. 50,000 BCE. Polynesians colonized Hawaii, New Zealand, and Easter Island using knowledge of wind and currents. Some settlements caused mass extinctions (moa hunting, Polynesian rat introduction); Easter Island experienced complete ecological destruction.

**Americas:** Teotihuacan (over 125,000 people) declined in the 6th–7th centuries possibly from environmental damage. The Maya developed the concept of zero and a 365-day calendar; their cities declined after 900 due to drought and warfare. The Aztec Empire (Triple Alliance, formed 1428) functioned as a tribute system with Tenochtitlan at its center. In the Andes, the Wari and Tiwanaku Empires dominated until the 8th century; the Inca Empire later annexed all other nations, building the advanced Inca road system. Norse explorers colonized Greenland (c. 980–1450) and Newfoundland (L'Anse aux Meadows) but settlements failed.

### End of the period

The Byzantine Empire fell to the Ottomans in 1453, disrupting overland routes between Asia and Europe. Europeans sought sea routes: Vasco da Gama reached India by sea in 1498; Columbus reached the Americas in 1492, triggering the Columbian exchange and the first pan-oceanic globalization. Magellan's expedition completed the first circumnavigation in 1521.

## Terms

- **Post-classical history**: Period from c. 500–1450/1500 CE used by world historians; roughly synonymous with the European Middle Ages
- **Feudalism**: Agricultural society where central authority is replaced by a warrior aristocracy relying on personal lord–vassal obligations
- **Pax Mongolica**: Mongol-enforced peace allowing safe trade and exchange across Eurasia
- **Silk Road**: Eurasian trade route connecting China to the Mediterranean, spreading goods, religion, and disease
- **Yersinia pestis**: Bacterium causing bubonic plague; responsible for the Plague of Justinian and Black Death
- **Caliphate**: Islamic state ruled by a caliph (successor to Muhammad); includes Rashidun, Umayyad, Abbasid dynasties
- **Sinicization**: Adoption of Chinese cultural and political ideas, voluntarily undertaken by Japan and Korea
- **Thalassocracy**: Maritime empire controlling trade routes and city-states, such as Srivijaya
- **Columbian exchange**: Post-1492 transfer of goods and diseases between the Americas and Afro-Eurasia
- **Manorialism**: Economic system in medieval Europe organizing rural society around the manor

## Debates and open questions

- Whether "post-classical" is a useful global category or remains Eurocentric; applying it to the Americas is problematic since their development timeline differs
- Whether Islamic scientists merely preserved Greek knowledge or built upon it
- Whether gunpowder weapons were transmitted from China by the Mongols or independently invented in Europe
- Whether the Black Death originated in China; historians consider this unlikely but lack direct evidence for alternative origins
- Whether 14th-century Asian epidemics (Deccan Plateau, Hebei Province) were bubonic plague or other diseases, since surviving accounts lack symptom descriptions
- The usefulness of "feudalism" as a comparative category across Eurasia, especially for Japan
- The extent of Toltec influence on later Mesoamerican civilizations; much knowledge may be Aztec-inspired myth
- Whether the Taíno had influence from the Maya civilization, with little evidence
- The causes of the Little Ice Age: sunspots, orbital cycles, volcanic activity, ocean circulation, or population decline
- The extent of Norse–Thule interaction in Greenland and whether Norse continued visiting Canada after initial colonization
