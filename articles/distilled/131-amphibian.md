# Amphibian

## Overview

Amphibians are ectothermic (cold-blooded), anamniotic (lacking the protective amniotic membrane), four-limbed vertebrates comprising the class Amphibia. All living amphibians belong to the monophyletic subclass Lissamphibia, with three orders: Anura (frogs and toads), Urodela/Caudata (salamanders), and Gymnophiona (caecilians). They are ecologically significant as indicators of habitat health, but have suffered dramatic population declines since the late 1980s due to habitat destruction, pollution, disease, and other factors.

## Timeline

- **~370 million years ago (Devonian)** — First major amphibian groups develop from lobe-finned fish with primitive lungs and limb-like fins
- **~360 million years ago (end Devonian)** — Early amphibians like *Ichthyostega* may have hauled themselves onto land
- **Carboniferous (360–323 mya)** — Amphibians diversify and become ecologically dominant, top predators in swamp ecosystems
- **After Carboniferous rainforest collapse** — Reptiles begin displacing amphibians; amphibians further devastated by Permian–Triassic extinction
- **Permian period** — Temnospondyls (possible ancestors of modern amphibians) thrive; some reach 6 m (20 ft)
- **Early Triassic (~250 mya)** — First lissamphibians appear; modern orders begin diverging
- **Early Jurassic** — Oldest "true frog" *Prosalirus bitis* (Arizona); oldest caecilians *Funcusvermis gilmorei* and *Eocaecilia micropodia*
- **Late Jurassic** — Earliest salamander *Beiyanerpeton jianpingensis* (northeastern China)
- **~2 million years ago** — Albanerpetontidae, a fourth lissamphibian group, goes extinct
- **2012** — Smallest vertebrate *Paedophryne amauensis* (7.7 mm) discovered in New Guinea

## Body

### Classification and evolutionary history

The word "amphibian" derives from Greek *amphíbios* meaning "both kinds of life," initially used for any animal living on land or in water, including seals and otters. Traditionally, Amphibia includes all tetrapod vertebrates that are not amniotes (tetrapods with an amniotic membrane, such as reptiles, birds, and mammals). In its widest sense, it has three subclasses: Lepospondyli† (possibly polyphyletic, likely closer to amniotes), Temnospondyli† (diverse, some large predators), and Lissamphibia (all modern amphibians). The taxon Labyrinthodontia has been discarded as polyparaphyletic. As of 31 March 2019, exactly 8,000 living amphibian species were known, nearly 90% being frogs.

The earliest amphibians evolved in the Devonian from tetrapodomorph sarcopterygians (lobe-finned fish with articulated limb-like fins) that had evolved primitive lungs for oxygen-poor swamps. *Ichthyostega* was among the first, with nostrils, efficient lungs, and a skull similar to the lobe-finned fish *Eusthenopteron*. Adaptations for land included heavier skeletons, digits, skin retaining body fluids, and the hyomandibula bone becoming the stapes (ear bone). The origin of modern lissamphibians is debated: the most popular hypothesis places them within temnospondyls (specifically dissorophoids), while another suggests lepospondyls. A 2005 molecular phylogeny suggests salamanders and caecilians are more closely related to each other than to frogs, with divergence around 250 million years ago. The 2008 discovery of *Gerobatrachus*, a dissorophoid temnospondyl from the Early Permian of Texas, provided a missing link with many modern frog characteristics.

### Anatomy and physiology

Modern amphibians have simplified anatomy compared to ancestors due to paedomorphosis (retention of juvenile traits), caused by miniaturization and unusually large genomes. Their skin is permeable, containing mucous glands for moisture and granular glands secreting distasteful or poisonous substances; some toxins like tetrodotoxin (TTX) in rough-skinned newts are deadly. Skin colour comes from three pigment cell layers (melanophores, guanophores, lipophores). Gas exchange occurs through the skin (cutaneous respiration), allowing some small salamanders (Plethodontidae) to lack lungs entirely. Adults breathe via buccal pumping (forcing air into lungs by throat contraction). The heart has three chambers (two atria, one ventricle); tadpoles have a fish-like two-chambered heart. Amphibians have a urinary bladder, excrete nitrogen primarily as urea (ammonia in aquatic forms, uric acid in some tree frogs), and possess pedicellate teeth (base and crown of dentine separated by an uncalcified layer), unique to amphibians. The smallest amphibian and vertebrate is *Paedophryne amauensis* (7.7 mm); the largest living is the South China giant salamander (*Andrias sligoi*) at 1.8 m, dwarfed by the extinct temnospondyl *Prionosuchus* (9 m, middle Permian of Brazil).

### Reproduction and life cycle

Most amphibians require fresh water to breed, though some lay eggs on land with strategies to keep them moist; there are no true marine amphibians. Fertilisation is external in most frogs (with the male grasping the female in amplexus), internal in most salamanders (via spermatophore) and all caecilians (via an intromittent organ, the phallodeum). Unisexual female mole salamanders (*Ambystoma*) in the Great Lakes region are the oldest known unisexual vertebrate lineage (~5 million years old). Metamorphosis is regulated by thyroxine (stimulating) and prolactin (counteracting). Typical larvae (tadpoles) are aquatic with gills; some species undergo direct development, hatching as miniature adults. Parental care occurs in up to 20% of species, including the male midwife toad carrying eggs on his thighs and the (now extinct) gastric-brooding frog rearing larvae in its stomach.

### Feeding, defence, and cognition

Adult amphibians are predators, eating anything that moves they can swallow, mostly small prey like beetles, caterpillars, and earthworms. Food is selected primarily by sight, with smell secondary. Defence mechanisms include mucous secretions, toxins, bright warning colouration, camouflage, tail autotomy (self-amputation), and defensive postures. Amphibians show evidence of habituation, associative learning, and numerical discrimination (frogs distinguish 1 vs 2 and 2 vs 3, but not 3 vs 4 prey). They are widely considered sentient, able to feel anxiety and fear.

### Conservation

Dramatic declines have been noted since the late 1980s worldwide. In 2004, the IUCN reported amphibian extinction rates at minimum 48 times greater than natural—possibly 1,024 times higher. Of 4,035 water-dependent species in 2006, 1,356 (33.6%) were threatened. Causes include habitat destruction, over-exploitation, pollution, introduced species, global warming, endocrine-disrupting pollutants, ozone depletion (ultraviolet damage), and chytridiomycosis (a fungal disease). Declines affect food webs: fewer tadpoles mean less algae control, potentially depleting oxygen when algae die. Conservation efforts include the IUCN Amphibian Specialist Group's global strategy and Amphibian Ark's ex-situ assurance colonies, such as the Panama Amphibian Rescue and Conservation Project.

## Terms

- **Lissamphibia**: The monophyletic subclass containing all living amphibians (frogs, salamanders, caecilians)
- **Anamniotic**: Lacking the amniotic membrane; eggs that dry out and require water or moisture
- **Temnospondyls**: Diverse prehistoric amphibian group, the most likely ancestors of modern amphibians
- **Paedomorphosis**: Retention of juvenile characteristics into adulthood, caused by miniaturization and large genomes
- **Cutaneous respiration**: Gas exchange through the skin, requiring a moist, vascularised surface
- **Spermatophore**: A packet of sperm deposited by male salamanders, picked up by the female
- **Amplexus**: The mating embrace in frogs where the male grasps the female
- **Chytridiomycosis**: A fungal disease implicated in amphibian declines worldwide
- **Autotomy**: Self-amputation of the tail as a defence mechanism, used by some salamanders
- **Neoteny**: Retention of larval features into adulthood, as in the axolotl

## Debates and open questions

The origin of modern lissamphibians remains contentious: the dominant hypothesis places them within dissorophoid temnospondyls, but alternative hypotheses suggest lepospondyl ancestry or even amniote relationships. Molecular studies suggest the frog–salamander divergence occurred considerably earlier than palaeontological evidence indicates, with one study proposing the last common ancestor of all modern amphibians lived ~315 million years ago. Many causes of amphibian declines remain poorly understood and are topics of ongoing discussion.
