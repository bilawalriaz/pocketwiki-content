# Elizabeth of Russia

## Overview
Elizabeth Petrovna was Empress of Russia from 1741 to 1762. As the daughter of Peter the Great, she seized power in a bloodless coup and ruled during a period of significant cultural flourishing and major European wars. Her reign is noted for its lavish court, architectural achievements, and strong opposition to Prussia, ending with her death just as Prussia faced imminent defeat.

## Timeline
- **18 December 1709** — Born at Kolomenskoye Palace, daughter of Peter the Great and Catherine I.
- **1727** — Her fiancé, Charles Augustus of Holstein-Gottorp, dies; her mother, Catherine I, had died two weeks prior.
- **25 November 1741** — Seizes power in a bloodless coup with the Preobrazhensky Life Guards Regiment.
- **25 April 1742** — Crowns herself Empress in Moscow.
- **7 November 1742** — Proclaims her nephew, Peter of Holstein-Gottorp, as her heir.
- **7 August 1743** — Treaty of Åbo with Sweden, gaining Finnish territories.
- **21 August 1745** — Arranges the marriage of her heir Peter to Sophia Augusta Frederica (future Catherine the Great).
- **18 October 1748** — Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle ends the War of Austrian Succession.
- **16 January 1756** — Convention of Westminster between Britain and Prussia prompts Elizabeth to ally with France and Austria.
- **12 August 1759** — Russian forces defeat Prussia at the Battle of Kunersdorf.
- **25 December 1761** — Russian forces capture the Prussian fortress of Kolberg.
- **5 January 1762** — Elizabeth dies in Saint Petersburg.

## Body

### Early Life and Path to Power
Elizabeth was the second daughter of Peter the Great. Though her parents married officially in 1712, her birth was initially illegitimate, which hindered European marriage prospects. She received a limited formal education but became fluent in several languages and was known for her beauty and vivacity. After the deaths of her parents and the failure of her marriage plans, she lived at the Russian court under the reigns of her nephew Peter II and her cousin Empress Anna, who resented her popularity. Elizabeth cultivated support among the Russian Guards regiments. Following the death of Empress Anna in 1740, the regency for the infant Ivan VI was unpopular. With backing from the French ambassador and Swedish interests, Elizabeth led a coup on 25 November 1741, arresting the infant emperor and his family without bloodshed. She vowed never to sign a death sentence, a promise she kept.

### Domestic Rule and Culture
Elizabeth's reign is characterized as a Russian "Age of Enlightenment." She exiled unpopular German advisers and reformed the Senate. Domestically, she expanded noble privileges while shortening their state service terms. She promoted education, founding the University of Moscow and the Imperial Academy of Arts. Her court became one of Europe's most splendid, famous for its lavish balls, including "metamorphoses" balls where guests dressed as the opposite sex. Her favorite architect, Bartolomeo Rastrelli, designed major Baroque projects like the Winter Palace and the Smolny Cathedral. She also established parishes for social welfare. Her long-term companion was Alexei Razumovsky, a Ukrainian Cossack she may have secretly married.

### Foreign Policy and Wars
Elizabeth's foreign policy was guided by her vice-chancellor, Aleksey Bestuzhev-Ryumin, who secured an alliance with Austria and France. This alliance helped end the War of Austrian Succession in 1748. Later, Elizabeth's personal dislike of Frederick the Great of Prussia led her to oppose the Convention of Westminster. She allied with France and Austria, entering the Seven Years' War in 1757. Russian troops achieved significant victories, including the crushing defeat of Prussia at Kunersdorf in 1759 and the brief occupation of Berlin in 1760. By 1761, Frederick the Great was on the verge of surrender. However, Elizabeth's death on 5 January 1762 is considered the "Miracle of the House of Brandenburg" that saved Prussia from destruction.

### Selection of an Heir
As childless, Elizabeth needed a secure successor. She chose her nephew, Peter of Holstein-Gottorp, whom she brought to Russia and proclaimed heir in 1742. She arranged his marriage to Sophia Augusta Frederica (Catherine) in 1745. Their son, the future Paul I, was born in 1754, though his paternity was later speculated to be that of Sergei Saltykov. Elizabeth took control of the child's upbringing, effectively separating him from his parents.

## Terms
- **Agnatic line**: A line of succession traced exclusively through male heirs.
- **Boyar**: A member of the old Russian aristocracy.
- **Coup**: A sudden, illegal seizure of power from a government.
- **Gallomania**: An excessive admiration for French culture.
- **Metamorphoses ball**: A costume ball where guests were required to dress as the opposite sex.
- **O.S. (Old Style)**: Refers to the Julian calendar, used in Russia before 1918.
- **Regency**: The rule of a regent or group of regents governing a state because the monarch is a minor, absent, or incapacitated.
- **Serf**: A laborer bound to the land and owned by the landowner.
- **Tsesarevna**: The title for the daughter of a Tsar or the wife of a Tsarevich (heir apparent).

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Paternity of Paul I**: There is considerable speculation that Elizabeth's grandson, Paul I, was not the biological son of her heir Peter III but of Sergei Saltykov, with Elizabeth's consent.
- **Legitimacy of Elizabeth's birth**: Her parents' marriage was not official until 1712, after her birth, which was used by political opponents to challenge her right to the throne.
- **Role of foreign powers in her coup**: The involvement of the French ambassador and Swedish interests in planning her 1741 coup highlights the extent of foreign interference in Russian succession politics.
