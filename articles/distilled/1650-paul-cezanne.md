# Paul Cézanne

## Overview
Paul Cézanne (1839–1906) was a French Post-Impressionist painter whose radical restructuring of pictorial space bridged 19th-century Impressionism and early 20th-century Cubism. Rejecting traditional linear perspective and academic finish, he developed a method of "modulation"—building form through patches of colour applied in parallel, constructive brushstrokes—to capture the underlying geometric architecture of nature (cylinders, spheres, cones). Initially ridiculed by critics and rejected by the official Salon, he worked in relative isolation in Provence, supported by his banker father’s inheritance and championed only by peers like Camille Pissarro and dealer Ambroise Vollard. Vollard’s 1895 solo exhibition catalyzed recognition; by 1907, posthumous retrospectives cemented his status as a "father of us all" for artists including Matisse, Picasso, and Braque. His analytical approach to perception—treating distance and object as equivalent colour planes—redefined painting as a structural harmony parallel to nature rather than an imitation of it.

## Body

### Early Formation and the Dark Period (1839–1870)
Cézanne’s privileged upbringing—his father co-founded the Banque Cézanne et Cabassol—provided financial security rare among artists. At Collège Bourbon, he formed a tight trio with Zola and Baille, swimming, debating art, and reading classics. Despite his father’s pressure to study law (1859–1861), Cézanne pursued drawing at the Musée Granet under Joseph Gibert. In 1861, encouraged by Zola, he moved to Paris but was rejected by the École des Beaux-Arts. He attended the free Académie Suisse, copying Old Masters at the Louvre and meeting Pissarro, Monet, and Renoir. Paris felt alien; he returned to Aix in late 1861, working briefly in his father’s bank.  

His "Dark Period" (1861–1870) reflects the influence of Romanticism (Delacroix) and Realism (Courbet). Paintings feature thick impasto, sombre palettes (black, brown, Prussian blue), and violent or erotic themes (*The Murder*, *The Abduction*). In 1866–67, emulating Courbet, he used a palette knife for a series he called *une couillarde* ("ostentatious virility"), which Lawrence Gowing later identified as the birth of modern Expressionism. The Salon rejected his work annually (1864–1869); only the 1863 Salon des Refusés accepted him. In 1869 he met Marie-Hortense Fiquet, a bookbinder’s assistant, who became his lifelong companion and frequent model.

### Impressionist Collaboration and the Constructive Period (1870–1878)
During the Franco-Prussian War, Cézanne and Hortense lived in L'Estaque near Marseille, a Mediterranean landscape that became a recurring motif. After the war, they settled in Auvers-sur-Oise (1872–1874), where Dr. Paul Gachet provided studio space. Crucially, Pissarro invited him to Pontoise (1872), initiating a decade of side-by-side landscape painting. Pissarro urged him to abandon dark tones, paint only with the three primary colours and their immediate deviations, and define form through colour modulation rather than contour. Cézanne adopted the Impressionist broken brushstroke but retained a structural rigour: he reduced objects to basic geometric volumes and treated "distance" with the same painterly gesture as foreground objects, collapsing traditional illusionistic depth.  

He exhibited in the first (1874) and third (1877) Impressionist exhibitions. Critics mocked his *Modern Olympia* (a confrontational response to Manet) and *The Hanged Man's House* (sold to Count Doria for 300 francs). Collector Victor Chocquet became his most loyal patron from 1875. In 1878, his father discovered the hidden family and halved his allowance, causing a financial crisis resolved only when the elder Cézanne relented, granting 400 francs monthly.

### Maturity and Synthesis in Provence (1878–1890)
Cézanne increasingly withdrew to Provence, stabilizing at Jas de Bouffan (his father’s estate, where he painted murals in the 1860s). The 1886 publication of Zola’s *L'Œuvre*—whose failed painter Claude Lantier echoed Cézanne’s life—severed their friendship, though later letters show intermittent contact. That April, Cézanne married Hortense to secure their son’s inheritance; his father died in October, leaving him 25,000 francs annual income.  

In this "Constructive" or "Synthetic" period, he fully broke from Impressionism. He solidified forms with diagonal, parallel brushstrokes ("constructive stroke"), eliminated linear perspective, and sought compositional equilibrium through the balance of colour planes. As he wrote to Joachim Gasquet: "The coloured surfaces, always the surfaces! [...] The areas must be clearly visible [...] but they have to be distributed correctly, they have to flow into one another." Still lifes (*Still Life with Apples*, *The Basket of Apples*) became laboratories: fruit is oversized, tables tilted, perspective distorted to achieve internal harmony. He hired professional models (e.g., Michelangelo di Rosa for *The Boy in the Red Vest*, 1888–90). Landscapes of Montagne Sainte-Victoire and Gardanne (1885–88) show faceted, quasi-cubist planes.

### Final "Lyrical" Period (1890–1906)
Diabetes (diagnosed 1890) worsened his temperament. After a failed reconciliation attempt in Switzerland, he separated from Hortense; she and their son moved to Paris. He lived with his mother and sister, turning to Catholicism (1891). Vollard’s 1895 solo show (50 of ~150 works sent) triggered a market surge: Monet, Degas, Renoir, and Pissarro bought works; prices rose a hundredfold. His mother died in 1897; he sold Jas de Bouffan in 1899 and moved to a city apartment. In 1901–02 he built a custom studio at Chemin des Lauves with a light shaft for large canvases.  

Three major series dominate: **Bathers** (~140 works, three monumental *Great Bathers* up to 208×249 cm), merging figures and landscape in Arcadian harmony; **Card Players** (five versions, 1890–95), using local farmers as models for严密构图的色彩与形式练习; and **Mont Sainte-Victoire** (>30 oils, 45 watercolours), painted from the *Terrain des Paintres* above his studio. He declared: "Art is a harmony parallel to nature." Watercolour became primary; its transparency influenced late oils (*Bathers*, 1902–06), leaving "empty spaces" that function as active colour. Émile Bernard, visiting in 1904, observed Cézanne building form by layering modulated patches from shadow outward: "a law of harmony was guiding his work." Cézanne’s 1904 letter to Bernard crystallized his method: "Treat nature according to cylinder, sphere, and cone... each side of an object... leads to a central point."

### Method: Optical Phenomena and *Réalisation*
Cézanne’s practice centred on three concepts: **motif** (the observed subject and the inner impulse to paint it), **sensation** (the fused optical impression and emotional response), and **réalisation** (the act of translating sensation into painted structure). He rejected single-point perspective—derived from reading Hippolyte Taine’s Berkelean spatial theory and the era’s stereoscopy craze—in favour of binocular vision: rendering slightly different simultaneous perceptions to create a lived depth. His famous directive to "treat nature in terms of the cylinder, the sphere and the cone" was not abstraction for its own sake but a means to honour the object’s structural reality under changing light. As he told collector Karl Ernst Osthaus (1906), "the main thing in a picture is the meeting of the distance. The colour must express every leap into the depths."

## Terms
- ****Modulation**** — Cézanne’s technique of building form and depth through subtle shifts in colour temperature and value (warm/cool, light/dark) rather than linear contour or chiaroscuro.
- ****Constructive stroke**** — Parallel, diagonal brushstrokes applied systematically across the canvas to "construct" volume and unify surface, replacing Impressionism’s fleeting, multidirectional touches.
- ****Motif**** — In Cézanne’s usage, both the external subject observed and the internal artistic necessity it provokes; *aller sur le motif* means engaging with the subject in situ to translate sensation.
- ****Sensation**** — The indivisible fusion of optical perception ("impression") and emotional reaction; the true target of painting: "Painting from nature does not mean copying the object, it means realizing its sensations."
- ****Réalisation**** — The painterly act of simultaneously realizing the motif, the artist’s feelings, and the painting itself; a merging of impression (input) and expression (output) in a single gesture.
- ****Binocular vision**** — Cézanne’s rendering of slightly disparate views from each eye to create a dynamic, lived spatial depth, rejecting monocular, single-point perspective.
- ****Cylinder, sphere, cone**** — The three elemental geometric forms Cézanne used to analyze and reconstruct natural objects (tree trunks, fruit, mountains) in his 1904 letter to Émile Bernard.
- ****Salon des Refusés**** — Exhibition authorized by Napoleon III in 1863 for works rejected by the official Salon de Paris; Cézanne showed there in its inaugural year.
- ****Les Trois Inséparables**** — The childhood friendship trio of Cézanne, Émile Zola, and Baptistin Baille formed at Collège Bourbon (1852–1858).
- ****Terrain des Paintres**** — The vantage point above Cézanne’s Lauves studio from which he painted numerous views of Mont Sainte-Victoire in his final decade.

## Debates and open questions
- **Zola friendship rupture**: The 1886 novel *L'Œuvre* was long thought to have ended their relationship permanently; recently discovered letters (1887) prove contact continued, but the emotional and artistic impact remains debated.
- **Cubist misinterpretation**: Cézanne expert Götz Adriani argues that Gleizes and Metzinger’s *Du "Cubisme"* (1912) arbitrarily appropriated Cézanne, ignoring his nature-based motivation and reducing his "cylinder, sphere, cone" advice to formalist abstraction.
- **Dating and catalogue raisonné**: Cézanne rarely dated works and considered many unfinished. Competing catalogues (Venturi 1936, Rewald 1996, online Feilchenfeldt/Warman/Nash) disagree on attributions and chronology; Rewald rejected stylistic analysis as unscientific, relying on documentary itineraries and sitter memories.
- **Optical vs. conceptual**: Scholars dispute whether Cézanne’s doubled contours and shifting viewpoints stem from physiological binocular vision, deliberate conceptual restructuring, or both.
- **Late watercolour autonomy**: Roger Fry (1927) argued watercolour technique reshaped Cézanne’s oil painting after 1885; the degree to which late watercolours are independent masterpieces versus studies remains a critical question.
- **Nazi-era provenance**: Multiple works (*The Sea at L'Estaque*, *Paysage*, *Fruits et pot de gingembre*) have been subject to restitution claims or settlements (2000, 2020, 2023), highlighting ongoing gaps in ownership history 1933–1945.
