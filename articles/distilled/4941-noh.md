# Noh

## Overview
Noh is a major form of classical Japanese dance-drama originating in the 14th century. It is Japan's oldest major theater art still regularly performed, integrating stylized dance, masks, costumes, and music to convey stories often featuring supernatural beings. Its highly codified tradition, emphasizing preservation over innovation, has made it a cornerstone of Japanese cultural heritage and a significant influence on global theatre.

## Timeline
- **8th century** — Sangaku, a Chinese performance art, is introduced to Japan, becoming a forerunner of Noh.
- **14th century** — Kan'ami and his son Zeami Motokiyo reinterpret traditional arts, establishing Noh in its present form under the patronage of Shogun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu.
- **Edo period (1603-1868)** — Noh is supported by the shogunate and aristocracy, becoming highly codified. The Tokugawa shogunate appoints the Kanze school as head of the four major schools.
- **1868** — The Meiji Restoration ends state support, causing a financial crisis for Noh.
- **1957** — The Japanese Government designates nōgaku (Noh and kyōgen) as an Important Intangible Cultural Property.
- **1983** — The National Noh Theatre is founded by the government.
- **2008** — UNESCO inscribes Nōgaku theatre on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

## Body

### Origins and Establishment
Noh evolved from earlier Japanese performing arts like sarugaku (popular entertainment) and dengaku (rural celebration dances). In the 14th century, during the Muromachi period, Kan'ami Kiyotsugu and his son Zeami Motokiyo synthesized these elements into a new, refined form. Their work gained the crucial patronage of Shogun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu, elevating Noh to a courtly art. Zeami's writings codified its aesthetic principles, and his family established the Kanze school, one of the five major schools that still train performers today.

### Structure and Performance
Noh is governed by the aesthetic concept of *jo-ha-kyū* (beginning, breaking, rapid), which dictates the increasing tempo of a program, a play, and even individual movements. A traditional program consists of five plays from different categories, often interspersed with comedic *kyōgen* plays. Performances are highly stylized, with emotions conveyed through precise gestures and the use of iconic masks (*nō-men*) carved from cypress. The stage is minimalist, featuring an independent roof and a bridge (*hashigakari*) symbolizing the connection between the human and spirit worlds.

### Roles and Training
Performers are categorized into roles: the *shite* (main protagonist, often masked), the *waki* (secondary character), a chorus (*jiutai*), and instrumentalists (*hayashi*). Training begins in early childhood within one of the five hereditary schools (iemoto system), which strictly preserve tradition. The *iemoto* head holds authority over artistic decisions. Historically an all-male profession, opportunities for women have gradually increased since the early 20th century.

### Repertoire and Aesthetics
The current repertoire contains about 240 plays, classified by theme into five categories: god plays, warrior plays, woman plays, miscellaneous plays, and demon plays. Aesthetically, Noh values concepts like *yūgen* (profound, mysterious beauty) and *hana* (the "flower" of a perfect performance). The goal is not naturalistic imitation but a stylized essence, where the actor achieves a state of "no-mind" (*mushin*) to embody the role completely.

## Terms
- **Nōgaku**: The umbrella term encompassing both Noh (serious drama) and kyōgen (comic interludes).
- **Shite**: The main protagonist or leading role in a Noh play, often a supernatural being.
- **Waki**: The secondary character, typically a human who serves as a foil to the shite.
- **Hayashi**: The instrumental ensemble accompanying Noh, consisting of flute and three drums.
- **Jo-ha-kyū**: A core aesthetic principle dictating a progression from slow beginning to rapid climax.
- **Iemoto**: The hereditary head of a Noh school, who holds ultimate authority over its traditions.
- **Yūgen**: An aesthetic ideal of profound, mysterious beauty and grace.
- **Mugen Noh**: A category of play featuring supernatural worlds and non-linear time.
- **Hashigakari**: The narrow bridge connecting the backstage to the main stage, symbolizing a passage between worlds.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact origins of Noh are debated, with theories ranging from its development by outcaste performers seeking status to its evolution from specific folk and court arts.
- The historical accuracy of legends, such as the oldest Noh mask being carved by Prince Shōtoku in the 6th-7th century, is contested by scholars, though the legend itself is ancient.
- The current repertoire is noted to reflect the tastes of the aristocratic Tokugawa period and may not represent the full historical popularity of certain plays among commoners.
