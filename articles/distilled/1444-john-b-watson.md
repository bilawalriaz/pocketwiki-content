# John B. Watson

## Overview
John B. Watson (1878–1958) was an American psychologist who popularized the scientific theory of behaviorism, establishing it as a psychological school. Rejecting introspection and the study of consciousness as unscientific, Watson argued that human actions result from stimulus-response associations shaped by environment, not heredity. His 1913 "Behaviorist Manifesto" and the controversial 1920 "Little Albert" experiment—which conditioned fear in an infant—cemented his influence. He served as editor of *Psychological Review* from 1910 to 1915. Forced out of academia in 1920 due to a scandalous affair, he built a lucrative second career in advertising, applying behaviorist principles to consumer marketing. Though his extreme environmentalist claims (e.g., "twelve infants" quote) were later qualified, his insistence on observable data redirected 20th-century psychology toward empirical rigor. A 2002 survey ranked him the 17th most cited psychologist of the 20th century.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Watson was born into poverty and instability in rural South Carolina. His father abandoned the family when Watson was 13; his devoutly religious mother subjected him to harsh moral training, fostering a lifelong antipathy toward religion. A move to Greenville (pop. ~8,000) exposed him to diverse social environments he later credited for his psychological theories. Despite arrests for fighting and discharging firearms, and poor academic performance, he entered Furman University at 16 via family connections, earning a master’s by 21. A pivotal petition to the University of Chicago president gained him admission, where he studied under John Dewey, James Rowland Angell, Henry Herbert Donaldson, and Jacques Loeb. Their combined influence fostered his descriptive, objective approach to behavior. His 1903 dissertation, "Animal Education," demonstrated that myelinization levels in rat brains correlated with learning ability, and he later identified the kinesthetic sense as the primary driver of maze-running behavior.

### The Behaviorist Manifesto (1913)
In 1913, Watson published the article "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It" and delivered an address at Columbia University. He declared psychology must become a "purely objective experimental branch of natural science" with the goal of "prediction and control of behavior." He rejected introspection, consciousness, and the mind-body distinction, arguing no dividing line exists between human and animal behavior. He initially viewed Pavlov’s conditioned reflex as merely a physiological glandular mechanism and dismissed Thorndike’s "law of effect" for its subjective elements. By 1916, he recognized Pavlov’s broader significance, making it central to his APA presidential address. This framework—Methodological Behaviorism—emphasized determinism, evolutionary continuism, and empiricism, distinct from Skinner’s later Radical Behaviorism. Watson claimed psychology had been in "confusion and anarchy" since Wundt; his behaviorism would pave the way for a scientific discipline.

### Theory of Language, Memory, and Emotion
In *Behaviorism* (1924), Watson redefined mental constructs as observable physical habits. **Language** was a "manipulative habit" involving laryngeal manipulation; infants learn by imitation (e.g., conditioning a baby to say "da-da" for a bottle). **Words** were "substitutes for objects and situations," built by associating sounds with stimuli. **Memory** was the persistence of these stimulus-response pathways; Watson illustrated this with an anecdote where revisiting shared locations (stimuli) retrieved a forgotten name (response). **Emotions** were mere physical responses. He posited three unlearned reactions at birth: **Fear** (to sudden noise or loss of support), **Rage** (to physical constraint), and **Love** (to tactile stimulation like tickling). Complex adult emotions (e.g., fear of animals, love for specific people) resulted from conditioning these innate responses to new stimuli.

### The "Little Albert" Experiment (1920) and Deconditioning
With assistant Rosalie Rayner, Watson conditioned a 9-month-old ("Little Albert") to fear a white rat by pairing its presentation with a loud clang (clanging an iron rod). The fear generalized to a rabbit, dog, and fur coat. The study demonstrated emotions could be conditioned responses. However, Albert was removed before deconditioning could occur—a major ethical breach. Later analysis of film footage suggested Albert (possibly Douglas Merritte, who died at 6 of hydrocephalus) was developmentally disabled, raising questions about Watson’s awareness and the study’s validity. Watson and Mary Cover Jones later developed **systematic desensitization** to decondition fears in another child, Peter, by gradually pairing a feared rabbit with pleasant stimuli (snacks), a technique still used in behavior therapy.

### Child-Rearing and *Psychological Care of Infant and Child* (1928)
Watson argued children should be treated as "young adults," warning against excessive maternal affection because love is conditioned. He believed society does not offer such comfort, so parents create unrealistic expectations. He opposed thumb-sucking, masturbation, and homosexuality, and advocated honesty about sex. His slogan—"not more babies but better brought up babies"—reflected his extreme nurture stance: he proposed a 20-year moratorium on pregnancies to perfect child-rearing data. The book sold 100,000 copies rapidly but drew fierce criticism. Critics (e.g., Nance, Houk, Berk) argued his views stemmed from his traumatic upbringing, his affair/scandal, and the Little Albert findings. J.M. O’Donnell called his "happy child" ideal—one who cries only from physical pain, solves problems independently, and asks no questions—"radical calculations." Watson later regretted the book, admitting he "did not know enough."

### The "Twelve Infants" Quote and Eugenics Context
Watson’s famous boast—"Give me a dozen healthy infants... and I'll guarantee to take any one at random and train him to become any type of specialist... regardless of his talents... and race of his ancestors"—is frequently cited out of context. The omitted final sentence reads: "I am going beyond my facts and I admit it, but so have the advocates of the contrary and they have been doing it for many thousands of years." In context, the passage argued *against* eugenics, which claimed heredity determined destiny. Watson’s actual starting point acknowledged organisms adjust via "hereditary and habit equipments," but he emphasized nurture to counter the eugenic orthodoxy of his era.

### Advertising Career
After leaving Johns Hopkins, Watson joined J. Walter Thompson (late 1920) via contacts from E.B. Titchener. He learned the trade from the ground up (including a stint as a shoe salesman) and rose to vice-president within two years, earning far more than in academia. He led campaigns for Ponds cold cream and Maxwell House (popularizing the "coffee break"). He has been widely but erroneously credited with re-introducing the "testimonial" advertisement; however, testimonial advertisements had been in use for years before Watson entered advertising. An example of a campaign he developed for Pebeco Toothpaste featured a seductively dressed woman and coaxed women to smoke, as long as they used Pebeco toothpaste; the toothpaste was positioned not as a means to benefit health or hygiene, but as a way to heighten the sexual attraction of the consumer. Watson stated that he was not making original contributions, but was just doing what was normal practice in advertising. He retired from advertising at about age 65 and ceased popular writing in 1936.

### Personal Life and Legacy
Watson married Mary Ickes in graduate school; they had two children (John and Mary). Mary divorced him in 1920 after discovering love letters to student Rosalie Rayner. Watson married Rayner in 1921; they had two sons (William, James). Rayner died in 1935. Tragically, Mary Ickes Watson attempted suicide; both sons later attempted suicide (William died in 1954). Watson burned his personal papers, limiting historical insight. Late in life, historian John Burnham described him as opinionated and bitter. He received the APA Gold Medal in 1957.

## Terms
- ****Behaviorism**** — Watson’s school of psychology defining the field as a purely objective, experimental natural science aimed at predicting and controlling observable behavior, rejecting introspection and consciousness.
- ****Methodological Behaviorism**** — Watson’s specific form of behaviorism focusing on observable behavior as the only valid data; distinct from Skinner’s Radical Behaviorism, which includes private events.
- ****Classical Conditioning**** — A learning process (derived from Pavlov) where a neutral stimulus (white rat) paired with an unconditioned stimulus (loud noise) elicits a conditioned response (fear).
- ****Stimulus-Response (S-R) Association**** — The core unit of behaviorist analysis: a specific environmental event (stimulus) becomes linked to a specific observable action (response).
- ****Systematic Desensitization**** — A behavior therapy technique (developed with Mary Cover Jones) to eliminate phobias by gradually exposing a subject to a feared stimulus while maintaining a relaxed/pleasant state.
- ****Stimulus Generalization**** — The tendency for a conditioned response (fear) to be elicited by stimuli similar to the original conditioned stimulus (e.g., fear of rat generalizing to rabbit, dog, fur coat).
- ****Manipulative Habit**** — Watson’s term for language/speech, defined as the physical manipulation of the larynx, throat, and tongue to produce sound.
- ****Unlearned Emotional Reactions**** — Watson’s three innate responses at birth: Fear (to noise/loss of support), Rage (to physical constraint), Love (to tactile stimulation).
- ****Nature vs. Nurture**** — The debate over whether behavior is determined by heredity (nature) or environment (nurture); Watson took an extreme nurture position to counter eugenics.
- ****Eugenics**** — A pseudo-scientific movement (prevalent in Watson’s era) advocating selective breeding to improve human genetic quality; Watson opposed its hereditary determinism.

## Debates and open questions
- **Little Albert’s identity and condition:** Historians debate whether "Albert" was Douglas Merritte (who died at 6 of congenital hydrocephalus) or another child (Albert Barger). Film analysis suggests developmental disability; a 2012 report alleged Watson knew of abnormalities, though 2014 journal articles contested this.
- **Ethics of the Little Albert study:** The failure to decondition Albert remains a primary ethical indictment; scholars debate whether this was negligence or a consequence of the scandal-driven departure.
- **Origins of Watson’s extreme environmentalism:** Critics argue his "twelve infants" claim and child-rearing advice were driven by personal trauma (abandonment, religious rigidity), the 1920 scandal, or over-generalization from the Albert study, rather than pure data.
- **Rosalie Rayner’s agency:** Historians question the extent to which Rayner (co-author of the Albert study and *Psychological Care*) genuinely endorsed Watson’s views versus conforming to professional/personal pressure.
- **Impact on advertising:** Scholars debate whether Watson genuinely revolutionized advertising (e.g., testimonials, coffee breaks) or merely adopted existing techniques; his own claim was the latter.
- **Legacy of child-rearing advice:** The long-term psychological effects on Watson’s own grandchildren (e.g., Mariette Hartley) and the broader cultural influence of his detached parenting model remain subjects of biographical and historical analysis.
