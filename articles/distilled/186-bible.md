# Bible

## Overview
The Bible is an anthology of religious texts central to Christianity and Judaism, comprising instructions, narratives, poetry, prophecy, and letters. The Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), written mostly in Biblical Hebrew with some Aramaic, is organized into the Torah (Teaching), Nevi'im (Prophets), and Ketuvim (Writings); its authoritative medieval edition is the Masoretic Text. The Christian Bible adds the New Testament—27 books written in Koine Greek (1st century CE)—to a reorganized Old Testament, which includes the Hebrew Bible books plus deuterocanonical/apocryphal texts accepted variably by Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant canons. Compiled over roughly a millennium (oldest parts c. 1200 BCE), the texts originated in oral tradition, were hand-copied on scrolls and later codices, and exist in thousands of manuscripts with textual variants. With over five billion copies sold, the Bible is history’s best-selling publication and has profoundly shaped Western law, ethics, literature, art, and global culture, while also generating intense scholarly debate regarding its historicity, moral teachings, and interpretation.

## Timeline
- **c. 1200 BCE** — Composition of oldest Hebrew Bible parts (e.g., Song of Deborah, Samson story) in early Iron Age.
- **5th century BCE** — Torah (Pentateuch) accepted as Jewish canon.
- **3rd century BCE** — Nevi'im (Prophets) canonized; Septuagint (Greek translation of Hebrew scriptures) begun in Alexandria.
- **132 BCE** — Septuagint completed.
- **2nd century BCE – 2nd century CE** — Ketuvim (Writings) canonized; Tanakh (Hebrew Bible) closed.
- **1st century CE** — New Testament books written in Koine Greek; early Christian communities copy and circulate texts.
- **70 CE** — Destruction of Jerusalem Temple by Romans.
- **c. 300–500 CE** — Masoretes begin developing authoritative Hebrew text (Talmudic period).
- **382 CE** — Council of Rome establishes Catholic biblical canon (73 books).
- **385–405 CE** — Jerome translates Bible into Latin Vulgate.
- **393, 397 CE** — Synods of Hippo and Carthage affirm canon.
- **c. 750–950 CE** — Tiberian Masoretes develop standard vocalization (Tiberian Hebrew).
- **9th century** — Masoretic Text standardized; codex form replaces scrolls.
- **1008 CE** — Leningrad Codex (oldest complete Masoretic Text) copied.
- **1488 CE** — First complete printed Hebrew Bible produced.
- **1522–1534 CE** — Luther translates Bible into German.
- **1545–1563 CE** — Council of Trent affirms Vulgate and Catholic canon (73 books) in response to Reformation.
- **1611 CE** — King James Version (Authorized Version) published in English.
- **1947 CE** — Dead Sea Scrolls discovered at Qumran (copies dated 250 BCE – 100 CE).
- **2017 CE** — Museum of the Bible opens in Washington, D.C.

## Body

### Etymology and Definition
The English word "Bible" derives from the Koine Greek *ta biblia* ("the books"), from *biblion* ("scroll"), a diminutive of *byblos* (Egyptian papyrus), likely named for the Phoenician port Byblos. Hellenistic Jews used *ta biblia* for their sacred books; John Chrysostom (c. 386–388 CE) appears first to apply it to both Old and New Testaments together. Latin *biblia sacra* ("holy books") became a feminine singular noun in Medieval Latin, entering Western vernaculars as a singular term. "Bible" can refer to the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh) or the Christian Bible (Old and New Testaments).

### Development and Transmission
The Bible’s composition spanned roughly 1,200 years, reflecting vastly different political, cultural, and ecological conditions—from nomadic life to monarchy, exile, and Hellenistic/Roman rule. Texts began as oral songs and stories; writing and orality were intertwined, learned in communal performance. Authors are mostly unknown. No original autographs survive; texts were hand-copied on papyrus scrolls, later codices (folded sheets forming pages, popularized by Christians). Scribes (Levites, later Masoretes) preserved texts with great care but also updated scripts, archaic forms, and made corrections. The Dead Sea Scrolls (250 BCE – 100 CE) are the oldest substantial Hebrew Bible manuscripts, 1,000+ years older than the Leningrad Codex (1008 CE), confirming much of the Masoretic Text but also showing variants aligning with the Septuagint or Samaritan Pentateuch. New Testament manuscripts are more numerous than any other ancient work; early copies were made by lay Christians, creating local "text types" (Alexandrian, Western, Caesarean, Byzantine). Variants (deviations between texts) are mostly accidental (spelling, word order); some are intentional (grammar, harmonization, theology). Critical editions note variants in ~10% of Hebrew words and ~7% of Greek New Testament words.

### Hebrew Bible (Tanakh)
The Tanakh is an acronym for its three divisions: **Torah** (Teaching/Law), **Nevi'im** (Prophets), **Ketuvim** (Writings). Written mainly in Biblical Hebrew, with Aramaic portions (Ezra, Jeremiah 10:11, Daniel). The Masoretic Text (standardized 9th century, oldest complete copy Leningrad Codex, 1008 CE) is authoritative for Rabbinic Judaism. The Samaritan community uses only the Samaritan Pentateuch (oldest copies c. 1100 CE).

*   **Torah (Pentateuch):** Five books (Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy). Traditionally ascribed to Moses; modern scholarship sees multiple anonymous sources, final form likely under Persian Achaemenid Empire (450–350 BCE) or early Hellenistic period. Contains 613 commandments (*mitzvot*). Narrative moves from creation/patriarchs (Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph) to Moses, Exodus, Sinai covenant, wilderness wanderings, and Moses’ death.
*   **Nevi'im:** Former Prophets (Joshua, Judges, Samuel, Kings—narrative history of monarchy, division into Israel/Judah, exile) and Latter Prophets (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Twelve Minor Prophets—poetic oracles on justice, idolatry, judgment, restoration). Ends with Assyrian conquest of Israel and Babylonian conquest of Judah/Temple destruction (586 BCE).
*   **Ketuvim:** Diverse writings (Psalms, Proverbs, Job, Five Scrolls [Song of Songs, Ruth, Lamentations, Ecclesiastes, Esther], Daniel, Ezra-Nehemiah, Chronicles). Believed inspired by *Ruach HaKodesh* (Holy Spirit) but with less authority than prophecy. Psalms, Proverbs, Job (*Sifrei Emet*) have unique poetic cantillation. Canon finalized by 2nd century CE; book order varies between Babylonian and Tiberian traditions.

### Septuagint and Deuterocanonical Books
The Septuagint (LXX) is a Koine Greek translation of Hebrew scriptures begun late 3rd century BCE in Alexandria for Greek-speaking diaspora Jews, completed by 132 BCE. It expanded to include hagiographical works and newer books (Maccabees, Wisdom of Sirach) later termed "apocrypha" (doubted authenticity). Rabbinic Judaism rejected it (careless translation, Christian adoption, preference for Hebrew). The Book of Daniel exists in two Greek versions: the original Septuagint and Theodotion’s (c. 2nd century CE), which adds three narratives (Susanna, Bel and the Dragon, Prayer of Azariah) and became standard in the Early Church. Dead Sea Scrolls reveal the Septuagint often translates a different Hebrew recension than the Masoretic Text, fueling ongoing textual debate.

### Pseudepigrapha and Book of Enoch
Pseudepigrapha are works with falsely attributed authorship (not necessarily forgeries). Jewish works c. 300 BCE – 300 CE include 1–3 Enoch, Jubilees, 2 Baruch, Psalms of Solomon, etc. **1 Enoch** (oldest parts c. 300 BCE) is quoted in Jude and Hebrews but excluded from most Jewish/Christian canons. It is canonical only in the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo and Eritrean Orthodox Churches, which have an 81-book canon including Jubilees and Enoch.

### Christian Bible: Old Testament
The Early Church used the Septuagint (Greek) or Targums (Aramaic). Modern Protestant Old Testaments (39 books) follow the Jewish Tanakh canon (different division only). Roman Catholics (46 books) and Eastern Orthodox (additional books: 3 Maccabees, 1 Esdras, Prayer of Manasseh, Psalm 151, etc.) accept deuterocanonical books from the Septuagint. The Syriac Peshitta (2nd century CE Hebrew→Syriac OT, Greek→Syriac NT) adds some apocrypha. The Ethiopian Orthodox canon (81 books) includes Enoch, Jubilees, 1–3 Meqabyan. Protestants excluded apocrypha after the Reformation; Luther placed them in a separate section (followed by KJV).

### Christian Bible: New Testament
Written in Koine Greek (majority view; some argue for Aramaic originals or heavily Semitized Greek) in the 1st century CE. 27 books, four genres: **Gospels** (Synoptics Matthew, Mark, Luke; John—narratives of Jesus’ ministry, death, resurrection), **Acts** (early Apostolic history), **Epistles** (Pauline—letters to churches/individuals; Pastoral—church oversight; Catholic/General—universal), **Apocalypse** (Revelation—end-times prophecy). Canon formation: 1) continuation of Jewish tradition of inspired writings; 2) concept of "canon" emerges 2nd century against heresy; 3) formal closure 4th century (Synods of Hippo 393, Carthage 397; Jerome’s Vulgate 385–405 CE). By the 4th century, councils codified near-universal acceptance. All major Christian traditions share the same 27-book NT canon (order varies in Syriac, Slavonic, Ethiopian traditions).

### Textual History and Translation
Original autographs lost. Three main Greek NT text-types: Alexandrian (minimalist), Byzantine (maximalist), Western (wild). Early shift from scrolls to codices (3rd century). **Key translations:** Septuagint (Hebrew→Greek, 3rd c. BCE); Targum Onkelos (Hebrew→Aramaic, 2nd c. CE); Old Latin/Vetus Latina (multiple authors); Jerome’s Latin **Vulgate** (4th c. CE, from Hebrew/Greek, declared sole authentic Latin Bible at Trent 1546); Syriac **Peshitta** (2nd c. CE); Coptic, Ethiopic, Gothic, Armenian, Georgian versions. **English:** 7th c. fragments; Wycliffite (late Middle English); Tyndale (1525, shaped English discourse); Luther’s German (1522/1534); King James Version (1611, dominant for centuries); modern translations based on Leningrad Codex (OT) and critical Greek texts (NT). Bible translated into ~half the world’s languages; translation drives vernacular literacy and cultural indigenization.

### Content and Themes
The Bible addresses universal human concerns: gender, sexuality, family, authority, economics, justice, nature, suffering, joy, morality, epistemology. Narrative often withholds explicit moral judgment, leaving inference to the reader; texts juxtapose contradictory ideas. **Key theological themes:** God’s character, creation, divine providence/intervention, basis for morality, human nature, theodicy (problem of evil). **Hebrew Bible metaphysics:** Relative free will; natural law applies to all nations (Amos 1–2); criminal justice rooted in God as ultimate judge; politics in covenant/law/prophecy (early democratic ethics). **Wisdom literature** (Proverbs): human reason as condition for right living. **New Testament:** Fulfillment of Hebrew scriptures in Jesus; new covenant (Jeremiah 31); healing/social responsibility as discipleship (Matthew 10:8, Acts 5:16).

### Influence
**Politics/Law:** Inspired revolution (God chooses weak to confound mighty), democracy, religious freedom, abolitionism, civil rights, anti-apartheid, liberation theology. Legal principles: due process, impartial judges, fair trial, protection of vulnerable (Deut. 16:19, Lev. 19:15). **Nationalism:** Hebrew Bible as national history/law for ancient Jews; model for modern nation-states (Adrian Hastings). **Social Responsibility:** >2,000 verses on poverty/justice (Lockton). Monasticism (3rd c.) → first hospital (Caesarea, 4th c.), destigmatized illness, founded public health. Bible-based schools → medieval universities; Reformation → compulsory education; translations → national literatures/alphabets. **Sexual Morality:** Shifted Roman status-based ethics to individual agency/libertarian paradigm (Kyle Harper). **Literature/Arts:** Augustine’s *Confessions*, Aquinas’ *Summa*, Dante, Shakespeare, Tolkien; Michelangelo, da Vinci, Raphael; Eve/Delilah as artistic archetypes. **Criticism:** Used to support slavery, patriarchy, death penalty, colonialism, just war/crusade, genocide (Canaanites/Amalekites); feminist critique (Phyllis Trible’s *Texts of Terror*); John Riches: source of both great truth/beauty and savagery/oppression.

### Interpretation and Religious Significance
**Judaism:** Primary commentary is the **Talmud** (oral law + commentary, c. 550 CE), "central pillar" of Judaism. Torah observance defines Jewish identity (conversion possible). Canonization closed written text; commentary remains external (Yeshiva). **Christianity:** Views range from fundamentalist literalism (inerrant, clear meaning) to liberal/moderate (inspired but imperfect collection). **Inspiration theories:** 1) God influenced words/message/collation; 2) Infallible in faith/practice only; 3) Inerrant in all aspects (Chicago Statement 1978: autographic text only). **Hermeneutics:** Context of church history and contemporary culture. **Significance:** Not a constitution but a repository shaping/shaped by faiths. Catholics/Orthodox: Bible + Sacred Tradition. Protestants: *sola scriptura* (scripture alone) or *prima scriptura* (scripture primary). **Islam:** Bible = original revelation corrupted (*tahrif*); Quran corrects it. **Rastafari:** Essential. **Unitarian Universalists:** One of many important texts.

### Archaeology and Historical Research
**Biblical archaeology** illuminates lifestyle/practices. Spectrum: **Maximalism** (OT largely historical, few in mainstream) vs. **Minimalism** (Bible purely post-exilic, 5th c. BCE+). **Exodus/Conquest:** No Egyptian evidence for Israel in Egypt; traditions written long after, based on lost sources. **Monarchy:** Tel Dan Stele (9th/8th c. BCE) mentions "House of David," confirming Davidic dynasty not late invention; but no 10th c. evidence for David/Solomon/First Temple. **Babylonian Captivity (586 BCE):** Supported by archaeology (destruction layers, 2 Kings 18:13). **Acts of the Apostles:** Historicity debated 200 years (British conservative vs. German skeptical); genre now questioned (history vs. fiction); consensus: "history but not the kind that precludes fiction."

### Biblical Criticism
Analytical investigation of the Bible as text (history, authorship, dates, intention)—distinct from theological criticism. Secularized, democratized study; Bible no longer solely a religious artifact. **Impact:** "Desacralization" enabled modern world (Fishbane); threatened faith; accused of unwarranted arrogance; praised for "intelligently grounded, intellectually honest" faith (Rogerson). Compared to Job: destroying self-serving visions for honest crossing from divine text to human understanding.

### Material Culture: Manuscripts, Illumination, Museums
**Illuminated manuscripts** (medieval): decorated initials, borders, miniatures. Produced in monastic *scriptoria* (up to 12th c.), then secular scribes/laybrothers (14th c.). Notable: **Book of Kells** (c. 800, four Gospels). **Museums:** Dunham (Houston), Museum of the Bible (DC, 2017), St Arnaud (Australia), Eureka Springs (AR), Collierville (TN), Biedenharn (LA).

## Terms
- ****Tanakh**** — Acronym for the Hebrew Bible’s three divisions: **T**orah (Teaching), **N**evi'im (Prophets), **K**etuvim (Writings).
- ****Masoretic Text**** — The authoritative medieval Hebrew/Aramaic text of the Tanakh (standardized 9th c., oldest complete copy Leningrad Codex, 1008 CE), preserved by Masoretes (scribes who added vowels/accents).
- ****Septuagint (LXX)**** — Koine Greek translation of Hebrew scriptures (3rd–2nd c. BCE, Alexandria), including deuterocanonical books; used by Early Church.
- ****Deuterocanonical books**** — Books in Catholic/Orthodox Old Testaments (e.g., Tobit, Wisdom, Maccabees) from the Septuagint but not in the Hebrew canon; Protestants call them Apocrypha.
- ****Codex**** — Bound book format (folded sheets), forerunner of modern book; replaced scrolls, popularized by Christians by 3rd century.
- ****Text-type**** — Families of New Testament manuscripts sharing readings due to local copying traditions (Alexandrian, Western, Caesarean, Byzantine).
- ****Variant**** — Any deviation between two manuscript texts; mostly accidental (spelling), some intentional (harmonization, theology).
- ****Pseudepigrapha**** — Works with falsely attributed authorship (not necessarily forgeries); includes 1–3 Enoch, Jubilees, etc.
- ****Vulgate**** — Jerome’s 4th-century Latin translation from Hebrew/Greek; declared sole authentic Latin Bible by Council of Trent (1546).
- ****Canon**** — Official list of books accepted as authoritative scripture; varies: Jewish (24), Protestant (66), Catholic (73), Ethiopian Orthodox (81).

## Debates and open questions
*   **Dating and authorship of the Torah/Pentateuch:** Documentary Hypothesis (multiple sources J, E, D, P) vs. supplementary/fragmentary models; final form date (Persian vs. Hellenistic period).
*   **Historicity of the Exodus, Conquest, and United Monarchy:** Absence of Egyptian/archaeological evidence for Israel in Egypt or 10th-century David/Solomon/Temple vs. Tel Dan Stele confirming "House of David" dynasty.
*   **Septuagint vs. Masoretic Text priority:** Dead Sea Scrolls show LXX often reflects a different Hebrew *Vorlage*; debate whether LXX is a "careless" translation or a careful witness to an alternate recension.
*   **New Testament original language:** Consensus = Koine Greek; minority argue for Aramaic originals or heavily Semitized Greek syntax.
*   **Canon formation criteria:** Was the canon closed by the 1st century (Zahn) or only in the 4th (synods)? Role of "inspiration" vs. "orthodoxy" vs. "apostolicity."
*   **Genre and historicity of Acts:** History, fiction, or theological narrative? "History but not the kind that precludes fiction."
*   **Inspiration and inerrancy:** Extent of divine influence (words vs. concepts); scope of inerrancy (faith/practice only vs. all history/science); authority of autographs vs. translations (King-James-Only).
*   **Ethical evaluation:** Bible used to justify slavery, patriarchy, violence, genocide (Canaanites) vs. abolition, civil rights, liberation theology; feminist critique of "Texts of Terror."
*   **Biblical criticism’s legitimacy:** Secularized scholarship vs. faith community; "desacralization" as condition for modernity vs. threat to belief.
