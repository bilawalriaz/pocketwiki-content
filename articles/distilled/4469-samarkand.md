# Samarkand

## Overview
Samarkand is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in Central Asia, located in southeastern Uzbekistan. Its strategic position on the Silk Road made it a vital crossroads for trade, culture, and power between China, Persia, and Europe. The city's identity is defined by its monumental Islamic architecture, particularly from the Timurid and Uzbek periods, and its multicultural, multilingual history.

## Timeline
- **c. 700 BC** — Proposed founding date; archaeological evidence of human activity dates to the late Paleolithic.
- **329 BC** — Conquered by Alexander the Great; known as Maracanda.
- **c. 260 AD** — Conquered by the Persian Sasanian Empire.
- **c. 710 AD** — Captured by the Umayyad Caliphate; Islam introduced.
- **875–999** — Capital of the Samanid dynasty; New Persian becomes dominant language.
- **1220** — Conquered and devastated by the Mongol Empire under Genghis Khan.
- **1370–1405** — Made capital of the Timurid Empire by Timur (Tamerlane); major architectural renaissance.
- **1417–1420** — Ulugh Beg builds a madrasa on Registan Square, establishing a center for science.
- **1500** — Taken by Uzbek warriors; becomes capital of the Shaybanid (Bukhara) Khanate.
- **1868** — Annexed by the Russian Empire.
- **1925–1930** — Serves as the initial capital of the Uzbek Soviet Socialist Republic.
- **2001** — UNESCO designates Samarkand as a World Heritage Site ("Crossroads of Cultures").

## Body

### Early History and Silk Road Prominence
Samarkand is among the oldest cities in Central Asia, with evidence of human activity from the late Paleolithic. Its founding is uncertain, possibly between the 8th and 7th centuries BC. It was a principal center of Sogdian civilization and became the capital of the Sogdian satrapy under the Achaemenid Persian Empire. Its location on the Silk Road ensured its prosperity as a major trade and cultural hub.

### Conquests and Cultural Transitions
Alexander the Great conquered the city in 329 BC, introducing Hellenistic influence. It later fell under Sasanian, Hephthalite, and Turkic Khaganate rule. The Umayyad Caliphate captured it c. 710 AD, establishing an Arab garrison and introducing Islam, which gradually replaced Zoroastrianism and other faiths. Under the Samanid dynasty (875–999), New Persian replaced Sogdian as the dominant language. The city was devastated by the Mongol conquest in 1220.

### Timurid Renaissance and Architectural Golden Age
In 1370, Timur made Samarkand the capital of his empire, initiating a massive rebuilding program. He populated the city with artisans and craftsmen from across his conquests, creating a noted center of Islamic scholarship and the birthplace of the Timurid Renaissance. His grandson, Ulugh Beg, further established Samarkand as a world center for astronomy and mathematics in the 15th century. The Timurid and later Uzbek Khanate periods produced the city's most iconic architecture, including the Registan Square, Gur-i Amir Mausoleum, and Bibi-Khanym Mosque, characterized by cobalt and turquoise tilework.

### Russian, Soviet, and Modern Era
The city was annexed by the Russian Empire in 1868, with a new Russian section built west of the old city. It was the capital of the Uzbek SSR from 1925 to 1930. After Uzbek independence, Samarkand has remained a vibrant cultural and economic center. In 2022, it hosted the SCO summit. The city's population is officially majority Uzbek, but sources indicate a large Tajik-speaking population, with demographic figures complicated by Soviet-era national delimitation policies.

## Terms
- **Sogdiana:** An ancient Iranian civilization and satrapy (province) of the Achaemenid Empire, of which Samarkand was a capital.
- **Silk Road:** An ancient network of trade routes connecting China with the West, crucial for commerce and cultural exchange.
- **Timurid Empire:** A Persianate, Turco-Mongol empire founded by Timur in the 14th century, known for its cultural and architectural achievements.
- **Majolica:** A type of tin-glazed pottery, often decorated with intricate patterns, characteristic of Islamic architecture in Samarkand.
- **Registan:** A historic public square in Samarkand, flanked by three monumental madrasas (Islamic schools).
- **Madrasa:** An Islamic educational institution, often part of a larger architectural complex.
- **Samanid dynasty:** A Persianate Muslim dynasty that ruled from 819 to 999, promoting Persian culture and language.
- **Karakhanid Khanate:** A Turkic dynasty that ruled parts of Central Asia from the 9th to the 13th century.
- **National delimitation:** The Soviet policy of drawing borders in Central Asia in the 1920s, which created modern republics and affected ethnic identities.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Ethnic Composition:** There is a significant debate about the ethnic makeup of Samarkand. While official Uzbek reports state a majority Uzbek population, many independent sources and historical census data (e.g., from 1920 and 1926) suggest a Tajik majority, with figures complicated by Soviet-era registration practices.
- **Language Demographics:** The de facto most common native language is debated. Official policy states Uzbek is dominant, but some data indicate only about 30% speak it natively, with Tajik being the native tongue for approximately 70% of residents. No census has been taken since 1989 to confirm this.
- **Shiite Population:** The exact number of Shiites in Samarkand is unknown. Estimates vary widely, with one leaked diplomatic cable citing an imam's claim of 1 million in the Samarkand Vilayat, a figure the U.S. Ambassador doubted.
