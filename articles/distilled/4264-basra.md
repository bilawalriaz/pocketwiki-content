# Basra

## Overview
Basra is a major port city in southern Iraq, situated on the Shatt al-Arab waterway near the Persian Gulf. Founded in 636 as a military camp, it became a vital intellectual, commercial, and agricultural hub during the Islamic Golden Age. Its strategic location and oil reserves have made it a perennial center of trade and conflict, shaping its history through numerous conquests and modern wars.

## Body

### Foundation and Early Islamic Period
Basra was founded in 636 as a military encampment for Arab tribesmen fighting the Sassanid Empire. In 639, Caliph Umar officially established it as a city. Despite its initial location lacking fresh water and arable land, massive state and private investment in irrigation created a vast agricultural system. This infrastructure, built with significant labor from Zanj slaves, allowed Basra to overcome its natural disadvantages and grow into a major city. The irrigation canals, however, were ultimately unsustainable due to soil salinity.

### Golden Age and Decline
Under the Abbasid Caliphate (750–1258), Basra flourished as an intellectual center, home to scholars like Ibn al-Haytham and the Basra School of Grammar. It was a key node in trade networks. This prosperity was interrupted by the destructive Zanj Rebellion (871) and later invasions by the Qarmatians (923). After the Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258, Basra's political importance waned, though it remained a noted commercial port.

### Ottoman, Safavid, and European Influence
From the 16th century, Basra was contested between the Ottoman and Safavid Empires, with local Arab dynasties often holding de facto control. The Portuguese established a presence in the 16th century, acting as military protectors and sharing in customs revenue. The Ottomans solidified control in 1668, making Basra the center of its own vilayet (province) in 1884.

### Modern Era and Conflict
British forces captured Basra in 1914, modernizing its port. After Iraq's independence, the city's strategic location made it a battlefield. It was devastated during the Iran–Iraq War (1980–1988), suffering heavy shelling and population flight. It endured further damage during the 1991 Gulf War and was the site of uprisings against Saddam Hussein. In 2003, it was the first major city captured in the Iraq War. Post-2003, it faced instability from Shia militias before a period of reconstruction and development, including the major Grand Faw Port project.

### Economy and Demographics
Basra is Iraq's economic capital, with an economy dominated by oil and petrochemical industries. It is the country's only coastal region and main port, with the deep-water port of Umm Qasr. The population is predominantly Arab Shia Muslim, with significant minorities including Sunni Arabs, Feyli Kurds, Afro-Iraqis (Zanj), and smaller communities of Christians, Mandaean, and Jews (now nearly absent).

## Terms
- **Shatt al-Arab**: A waterway formed by the confluence of the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, flowing into the Persian Gulf; vital for Basra's location and trade.
- **Zanj Rebellion**: A major revolt (869–883) by East African slaves and others working in Basra's salt flats and plantations, which sacked the city in 871.
- **Vilayet**: An administrative division of the Ottoman Empire; Basra became its own vilayet in 1884.
- **Grand Faw Port**: A massive port project under construction in Basra Governorate, intended to be one of the largest in the Middle East.
- **Usuli Shiism**: A dominant branch of Shia Islam emphasizing scholarly interpretation of religious law, which became predominant in Basra.
- **Zanj**: A term for East African peoples, many brought as slaves to work in southern Iraq; their descendants form a community in Basra.
- **Mandaean**: An ethno-religious group practicing Mandaeism, a Gnostic faith; Basra has one of their largest communities.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact reason for the choice of Basra's original site, which lacked water and farmland, remains unclear to historians.
- The sustainability and long-term impact of the massive early Islamic irrigation system built around Basra are subjects of study, with evidence of its eventual failure due to salinity.
- The genetic studies of Basra's population reveal a complex history, with paternal lineages showing strong ancient Mesopotamian affinity and maternal lineages reflecting broader diversity from its role as a trade hub.
