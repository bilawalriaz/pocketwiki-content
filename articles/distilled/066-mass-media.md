# Mass media

## Overview

Mass media refers to the forms of media that reach large audiences via mass communication, including broadcast media, digital media, print media, social media, streaming media, advertising, and events. It encompasses news, advocacy, entertainment, and public service announcements, and intersects with fields such as marketing, propaganda, public relations, political communication, journalism, art, drama, computing, and technology. Mass media is often controlled by media conglomerates and may be susceptible to media capture. Its influence on individuals and groups has been analyzed from the standpoints of anthropology, economics, history, law, philosophy, psychology, and sociology.

## Body

### Definition and characteristics

In the late 20th century, mass media was classified into eight industries: books, the Internet, magazines, movies, newspapers, radio, recordings, and television. By the early 2000s, a "seven mass media" classification emerged: print (late 15th century), recordings (late 19th century), cinema (c. 1900), radio (c. 1910), television (c. 1950), the Internet (c. 1990), and mobile phones (c. 2000). Internet and mobile phones are collectively called digital media; radio and TV are called broadcast media. Some argue video games have developed into a distinct mass form.

Sociologist John Thompson of Cambridge University identified five characteristics of mass communication: it comprises both technical and institutional methods of production and distribution; involves the "commodification of symbolic forms"; has separate contexts between production and reception; reaches those "far removed" in time and space; and is a "one-to-many" form of communication. The term "mass" denotes that products are available in principle to a plurality of recipients, not that a specific number receives them. "Mass media" is sometimes used synonymously with "mainstream media," though it may include alternative outlets using mass communication technology.

### History

The first dated print book, the Diamond Sutra, was printed in China in 868 AD, though books were printed earlier. Movable clay type was invented in 1041 in China, but due to slow literacy spread and high paper costs, the earliest printed mass medium was probably European popular prints from about 1400. Johannes Gutenberg's printing press with movable type, used for the Latin Bible in 1453, enabled mass production of books, though books remained too expensive to be a mass medium for at least a century. Newspapers developed from c. 1612, with the first English example in 1620, but took until the 19th century to reach mass audiences. The first high-circulation newspapers arose in London in the early 1800s, enabled by the high-speed rotary steam printing press and railroads; however, increased circulation led to declining feedback and interactivity, making newspapers a one-way medium.

The phrase "the media" began in the 1920s, and "mass media" was restricted to print until after World War II, when radio, television, and video became popular for providing information and entertainment passively. During the 20th century, duplication technologies (printing, record pressing, film duplication) and electronic duplication (radio, TV) drove growth. Mass media had linear replication economics: unit costs fell as volumes rose, increasing profit margins. In democratic societies, media can serve the electorate, though some consider media ownership concentration a threat to democracy. The Internet has become the latest and most popular mass medium, with information available through websites and search engines.

Between 1985 and 2018, about 76,720 media industry deals were announced, totaling around US$5,634 billion. Three major M&A waves occurred (2000, 2007, 2015), with 2007 the most active year (~3,808 deals). The United States dominates, with 41 of the top 50 deals having a US acquirer. The largest deal was AOL Inc.'s acquisition of Time Warner for US$164,746.86 million.

### Influence and sociology

In 1997, J. R. Finnegan Jr. and K. Viswanath identified three main effects of mass media. First, the Knowledge Gap: media influences knowledge gaps based on content appeal, accessibility of information channels, and social conflict and diversity in a community. Second, Agenda Setting: people are influenced in how they think about issues by what media groups select for public consumption; framing—"taking a leadership role in the organisation of public discourse about an issue"—is useful, and groups compete to define issues symbolically. Third, Cultivation of Perceptions: prolonged media exposure shapes audience perceptions over time; television can be a "homogenising agent," though effects often depend on socioeconomic factors. Exposure to TV or movie violence may affect viewers' perceptions of community violence, and media normalizing violence can contribute to a culture of violence and rape culture.

Since the 1950s, cinema, radio, and TV became central instruments of mass control in highly industrialized countries. Mass media shape public perceptions and modern culture by portraying a particular interpretation of reality as reality, and play a role in spreading civil unrest. Media artist Joey Skaggs has demonstrated the ease of manipulating mass media through fabricated press releases and staged events, revealing how news outlets can publish stories with minimal fact-checking.

Limited-effects theory posits that people choose media based on existing beliefs, so media exerts negligible influence. Class-dominant theory argues media reflects the views of a minority elite controlling it. Culturalist theory combines both, claiming people create their own meanings from media messages. In 2012, an article asserted that 90 percent of US mass media was owned by six companies (GE, News-Corp, Disney, Viacom, Time Warner, and CBS).

### Discrimination

Mass media, through framing and agenda-setting, can affect how individuals perceive groups, as coverage may be limited or reflect a particular perspective. Media and propaganda can reinforce or introduce stereotypes. Historical media focus on African Americans in contexts of crime, drug use, and gang violence has resulted in distorted public perception; Stephen Balkaran states the media has played a key role in perpetuating historical oppression and contributing to African Americans' status as second-class citizens. Negative portrayal of men in media has also contributed to harmful public perception of men.

### Ethical issues and criticism

A common criticism is the lack of local or specific topic focus, as mass outlets cater to wide demographics and skip local stories. The term "mass" suggests passive, undifferentiated recipients, an image associated with critiques of "mass culture" that assume mass communication negatively impacts social life, creating bland, homogeneous culture. However, interactive digital media have challenged the read-only paradigm of earlier broadcast media. Since the 1950s, mass media have a key role in political power in industrialized countries. Contemporary research shows increasing media ownership concentration.

Some critics argue mass media no longer exists in its original form. Theorist Lance Bennett notes it is uncommon for a group large enough to be labeled a mass to watch the same news via the same medium; today people receive different stories from different sources. Social media confuses mass and interpersonal communication. The Hallin and Mancini media model, based on traditional indicators, no longer fully aligns with today's media ecosystem: television targets mature audiences and upholds professional journalism standards, while digital journalism and social media adapt standards to audience preferences.

### Forms

Broadcast media includes radio and television, including cable and pay-per-view services; digital radio and TV may transmit multiplexed programming. Internet broadcasting is called webcasting; podcasting emerged in 2004. Film encompasses motion pictures, named after photographic film stock; films create an illusion of motion through persistence of vision and beta movement. Video games are computer-controlled games with a video display as primary feedback and an input device. Sound recording and reproduction advanced from the phonograph through electrical recording, the 78 record, tape recorders, the vinyl LP, the compact cassette (1960s), and the compact disc (1983). The Internet is a worldwide network of interconnected computer networks transmitting data by packet switching using Internet Protocol (IP); the World Wide Web is the contents linked by hyperlinks and URLs. Mobile phones were introduced in Japan in 1979 but became mass media in 1998 with downloadable ringing tones in Finland. Print media includes magazines (periodicals financed by advertising or purchase) and newspapers (serially published, informing the public of significant events; the first was the German-language Relation aller Fürnemmen und gedenckwürdigen Historien, 1605). Outdoor media comprises billboards, signs, placards, flying billboards, blimps, skywriting, and augmented reality advertising.

### Professions

Journalism is the discipline of collecting, analyzing, verifying, and presenting information about current events. Public relations is the management of communication between an organisation and its publics to build and sustain a positive image. Publishing is the industry concerned with making information available for public view, traditionally printed works, now expanded to websites and blogs. A software publisher sits between developer and distributor. An internet celebrity is anyone who gained fame on the Internet.

## Terms

- **Mass communication**: One-to-many form of communication where products are mass-produced and disseminated to large audiences.
- **Broadcast media**: Radio and television, the fourth and fifth mass media.
- **Digital media**: The Internet and mobile phones, the sixth and seventh mass media, collectively.
- **Framing**: Taking a leadership role in organising public discourse about an issue.
- **Agenda setting**: The influence of media groups' selective choices on how people think about issues.
- **Cultivation**: The extent to which media exposure shapes audience perceptions over time.
- **Limited-effects theory**: Theory that media exerts negligible influence because people choose media based on existing beliefs.
- **Class-dominant theory**: Theory that media reflects and projects the views of a minority elite controlling it.
- **Culturalist theory**: Theory that people interact with media to create their own meanings from messages.
- **Persistence of vision**: Effect whereby the eye retains a visual image for a fraction of a second after the source is removed.

## Debates and open questions

Scholars debate whether mass media still exists in its original form, given social media's rise and the fragmentation of audiences across different sources. The concentration of media ownership is debated as a potential threat to democracy. The Hallin and Mancini media model, based on traditional indicators, no longer fully aligns with today's media ecosystem, raising questions about how to classify and analyze contemporary media systems.
