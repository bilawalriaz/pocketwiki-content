# Lake Vostok

## Overview
Lake Vostok is the largest of Antarctica's known subglacial lakes, located beneath Russia's Vostok Station under the East Antarctic Ice Sheet. It is a scientifically significant site because its isolation for millions of years may preserve a unique ecosystem, offering insights into extreme life on Earth and potential analogs for life on icy moons like Europa and Enceladus.

## Timeline
- **Late 19th C.** — Peter Kropotkin first proposes the theory of fresh water under Antarctic ice sheets.
- **1959, 1964** — Andrey Kapitsa collects seismic soundings suggesting water under the ice at Vostok.
- **Early 1970s** — British airborne radar surveys detect unusual readings suggesting a subglacial lake.
- **1967** — Igor Zotikov writes his PhD thesis developing the theory of subglacial lakes.
- **1991** — J. P. Ridley directs the ERS-1 satellite to survey the area, confirming the lake's presence.
- **1993** — Ridley's findings are published, confirming the lake's existence.
- **1996** — The lake's discovery and delineation are published in the journal *Nature*.
- **1998** — A 3,623 m ice core is drilled, halting ~100 m above the lake to avoid contamination.
- **2005** — An island is discovered in the central part of the lake.
- **5 Feb 2012** — Russian scientists complete a 3,768 m ice core, piercing the lake's surface.
- **10 Jan 2013** — The first core of freshly frozen lake ice is obtained.
- **2015** — A "clean" borehole is attempted, but results are unreported and work is later suspended.

## Body

### Discovery and Confirmation
The concept of subglacial water was theorized by Peter Kropotkin in the late 19th century and further developed by Igor Zotikov. Initial evidence came from seismic data collected by Andrey Kapitsa in 1959 and 1964. British airborne radar in the early 1970s provided further clues. The lake's existence was definitively confirmed in 1993 by J. P. Ridley using ERS-1 satellite laser altimetry, with the full discovery published in 1996. The lake is named after Vostok Station, which itself is named after the ship *Vostok* ("East" in Russian).

### Geological History and Traits
The Lake Vostok basin is a tectonic feature within a continental collision zone. The lake water is estimated to have been sealed off under the ice sheet about 15 million years ago. While initially thought to be ancient, modern research suggests the water is continually replaced, with an estimated residence time of 13,300 years. The lake is kept liquid by immense pressure (~345 bars) and geothermal heat, with an average water temperature of about −3 °C. It is an oligotrophic (nutrient-poor) environment supersaturated with nitrogen and oxygen, and exhibits tides of about 12 mm. The lake measures 250 km long by 50 km wide, covering 12,500 km².

### Research and Drilling
The primary research goal has been to access the lake's water and sediments. A major milestone was the 1998 drilling of a 3,623 m ice core, which provided a 400,000-year paleoclimatic record. Drilling was deliberately halted above the lake to prevent contamination from the 60-ton column of Freon and kerosene used to keep the borehole open. In February 2012, Russian scientists finally pierced the ice shield. However, lake water gushed up the borehole, mixing with the drilling fluids. Subsequent attempts to obtain clean water samples have been problematic, with the 2015 "clean" probe method proving inappropriate and work later suspended due to funding issues.

### Biology and Controversy
Analysis of accretion ice (ice formed from lake water) has yielded conflicting biological results. A U.S.-led team reported finding thousands of unique gene sequences from bacteria and eukaryotes, suggesting a diverse ecosystem. However, other scientists, including Russian experts, argue these findings likely represent contamination from the drilling process, as the original cores were not collected for biological study. Russian scientists reported finding an unknown bacterium in 2012 water samples, but acknowledged it could be a contaminant that feeds on kerosene. The drilling project remains controversial. Environmental groups and some scientists oppose the use of kerosene and Freon, advocating for cleaner hot-water drilling to protect the lake's potentially unique and isolated microbial life.

## Terms
- **Subglacial Lake:** A lake that lies under a glacier or ice sheet.
- **Oligotrophic:** An environment with very low nutrient levels, supporting limited biological activity.
- **Accretion Ice:** Ice formed by the freezing of lake water onto the base of the overlying ice sheet.
- **Clathrate:** A chemical structure where gas molecules are enclosed within an icy, cage-like lattice.
- **Paleoclimatic Record:** The historical record of past climates, often preserved in ice cores.
- **Extremophile:** An organism that thrives in physically or geochemically extreme conditions.
- **Borehole:** A narrow, deep hole made in the ground, especially to access water or for scientific sampling.
- **Antarctic Treaty System:** An international agreement governing Antarctica, including its environmental protection.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Contamination vs. Discovery:** The central debate is whether biological findings from ice cores and water samples represent a genuine, isolated ecosystem or are the result of contamination from drilling fluids and equipment.
- **Drilling Methodology:** There is significant disagreement over the environmental safety of using kerosene and Freon versus alternative methods like hot-water drilling.
- **Connectivity of Subglacial Lakes:** It is hypothesized that Lake Vostok and other subglacial lakes may be interconnected by a network of rivers, which has implications for their ecology and the impact of drilling.
- **Astrobiological Implications:** If confirmed, life in Lake Vostok would strongly support the possibility of life in similar environments on icy moons in our solar system.
