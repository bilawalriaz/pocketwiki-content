# Liberia

## Overview
Liberia is a West African nation founded in the 19th century as a colony for freed African Americans. It is Africa's oldest republic, having declared independence in 1847, and was spared European colonization. Its modern history is marked by a long period of Americo-Liberian elite rule, followed by devastating civil wars in the late 20th century, and a recent, fragile return to democracy.

## Timeline
- **1822** — American Colonization Society (ACS) begins settling free Black Americans in Liberia.
- **1847** — Liberia declares independence; the U.S. does not recognize it until 1862.
- **1926** — Firestone Tire and Rubber Company secures a 99-year lease for a vast rubber plantation.
- **1944** — President Tubman launches the "Open Door" policy to encourage foreign investment.
- **1980** — Military coup led by Samuel Doe ends Americo-Liberian rule.
- **1989-1997** — First Liberian Civil War; President Doe is assassinated in 1990.
- **1997** — Charles Taylor is elected president after the war.
- **1999-2003** — Second Liberian Civil War; Taylor resigns in 2003.
- **2005** — Ellen Johnson Sirleaf is elected Africa's first female head of state.
- **2018** — George Weah is inaugurated in Liberia's first fully democratic transition in 74 years.
- **2024** — Joseph Boakai is sworn in as president after defeating Weah in the 2023 election.

## Body

### Founding and Americo-Liberian Rule
Liberia was established in the early 19th century by the American Colonization Society (ACS), which promoted the emigration of free Black Americans to Africa. Between 1822 and 1861, over 15,000 settlers, including some Afro-Caribbeans, relocated. These Americo-Liberians, who developed a distinct identity, declared independence in 1847. They established a political and economic elite, dominating the coastal areas and maintaining power through the True Whig Party from 1877 until 1980. Indigenous peoples did not gain birthright citizenship until 1904.

### Early 20th Century and Foreign Investment
In the early 1900s, Liberia's economy declined as European powers controlled regional trade. To address debt, Liberia granted Firestone Tire and Rubber Company a 99-year lease on one million acres for rubber plantations in the 1920s. This investment reshaped Liberia's economy and labor systems. During World War II, the U.S. built key infrastructure like the Freeport of Monrovia to support the war effort. Post-war, President Tubman's "Open Door" policy attracted further foreign investment, and Liberia became a founding member of the United Nations.

### Civil Wars and Aftermath
Political tensions led to a 1980 military coup by Samuel Doe, ending Americo-Liberian rule. Doe's dictatorial regime and a failed coup attempt in 1985 intensified ethnic conflicts, triggering the First Liberian Civil War (1989-1997). Doe was assassinated in 1990. Rebel leader Charles Taylor won the subsequent election. His rule led to the Second Liberian Civil War (1999-2003), during which he was indicted for war crimes. The wars killed about 250,000 people and caused a 90% economic contraction. A 2003 peace deal led to democratic elections in 2005, won by Ellen Johnson Sirleaf.

### Economy and Environment
Liberia's economy depends on natural resources like iron ore, rubber, and timber, with significant foreign investment. Mining was a major economic driver from the 1960s until halted by the civil wars, after which industrial mining resumed. Large-scale agriculture, particularly rubber and palm oil plantations, has driven deforestation, water pollution, and loss of biodiversity. Liberia holds the world's second-largest shipping registry as a flag of convenience. The country remains one of the world's poorest, with high unemployment and reliance on foreign aid.

## Terms
- **Americo-Liberians**: Descendants of African American and Caribbean settlers who established and dominated Liberia's political and social elite until 1980.
- **American Colonization Society (ACS)**: The organization founded in 1816 that promoted and facilitated the emigration of free Black Americans to Liberia.
- **True Whig Party**: The political party that dominated Liberian government from 1877 to 1980, primarily representing Americo-Liberian interests.
- **Flag of convenience**: A business practice where a ship's owner registers a vessel in a foreign country to benefit from lower taxes or regulations; Liberia has the world's second-largest registry.
- **Jus sanguinis**: A principle of nationality law where citizenship is determined by the nationality of one's parents, not by birth within a country's territory.

## Debates and Open questions
- The long-term impact of Firestone and other foreign corporations on Liberia's economic autonomy and development is debated, with scholars arguing they created exploitative labor systems that persist in new forms.
- The environmental and social costs of large-scale industrial mining and palm oil plantations versus their economic benefits remain a contentious issue, particularly regarding deforestation, pollution, and displacement of local communities.
- The effectiveness of post-war institutions, such as the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, in achieving lasting peace and justice is questioned amid ongoing challenges like corruption and inequality.
