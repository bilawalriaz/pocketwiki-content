# Seed

## Overview

A seed is a plant structure containing an embryo and stored nutrients within a protective coat (testa), formed from the ripened ovule after fertilization. Seeds are the defining reproductive feature of seed plants (spermatophytes), enabling them to dominate terrestrial ecosystems from forests to grasslands. Unlike spore-producing plants like ferns and mosses, seed plants use seeds for propagation, with angiosperms enclosing seeds in fruits and gymnosperms bearing them naked on cone scales.

## Timeline

- **~468 mya** — First land plants evolve, reproducing via spores
- **Late Devonian (416–358 mya)** — Earliest gymnosperms appear, first seed-bearing plants
- **Carboniferous (359–299 mya)** — Seed ferns evolve with cupule-enclosed ovules
- **1832** — de Candolle publishes seed storage guide in *Conservation des Graines*
- **1846** — de Candolle publishes seed viability studies
- **1897** — Jodin conducts seed hygrometric studies
- **1912** — Guppy publishes *Studies in Seeds and Fruits*
- **2005** — 2,000-year-old Judean date palm seed germinated from Masada
- **2021** — China adopts modern Seed Law

## Body

### Seed Formation and Plant Groups

Seeds form when the embryo sac within an ovule is fertilized by sperm from pollen, creating a zygote that develops into an embryo. This process defines seed plants (spermatophytes), which include gymnosperms and angiosperms. Gymnosperms ("naked seeds") lack ovaries and bear seeds on cone bracts, while angiosperms ("enclosed seeds") develop seeds within fruits derived from the ripened ovary. Other plants like ferns, mosses, and liverworts reproduce via spores and require water for propagation.

### Development and Anatomy

Angiosperm seeds contain three genetically distinct components: the embryo (from the zygote), the triploid endosperm (formed via double fertilization), and the maternal seed coat. Double fertilization involves one sperm fusing with the egg to form the zygote and another with the central cell to form the primary endosperm. The ovule structure includes the funicle (attachment stalk), nucellus (megaspore region), micropyle (pollen entry pore), and chalaza (opposite base). Ovule shapes include anatropous (curved), orthotropous (straight), campylotropous (tight C-curve), and amphitropous (partly inverted).

### Embryo Structure

The embryo consists of cotyledons (seed leaves), epicotyl (axis above cotyledons), plumule (shoot tip), hypocotyl (axis below cotyledons), and radicle (root tip). Monocotyledons have one cotyledon while dicotyledons have two. Monocots possess additional sheath structures: the coleoptile (covering plumule) and coleorhiza (covering radicle). In grasses like corn, the scutellum (shield-shaped cotyledon) absorbs nutrients from endosperm.

### Seed Coat Development

The seed coat forms from ovule integuments: the outer integument becomes the testa and the inner becomes the tegmen. Bitegmic seeds have both layers while unitegmic seeds have only one. The mature seed coat may contain an exotesta (outer layer), endotesta (inner layer of outer integument), exotegmen (outer layer of inner integument), and endotegmen (inner layer). Seeds may have appendages including arils (fleshy funicle outgrowths), elaiosomes (oily appendages for ant dispersal), wings, or hairs.

### Nutrient Storage

Seeds store nutrients primarily as endosperm in most monocots and endospermic dicots, or in cotyledons of non-endospermic (exalbuminous) dicots like beans and peas. Endosperm types include farinaceous (starch-rich), fleshy, oily, horny, and ruminated. All gymnosperm seeds are albuminous (containing endosperm). The aleurone layer surrounds endosperm and contains protein-rich aleurone grains.

### Dormancy and Germination

Seed dormancy prevents germination under suboptimal conditions and is classified as exogenous (external factors), endogenous (internal factors), combinational, or secondary. Five major types include morphological, physiological, morphophysiological, physical, and combinational dormancy. Germination requires seed viability, dormancy breaking, and proper environmental conditions (water, oxygen, temperature, light). The process involves three phases: water imbibition, lag phase, and radicle emergence.

### Dispersal Mechanisms

Seeds disperse via wind (anemochory), water (hydrochory), or animals (zoochory). Wind dispersal uses wings or hairs; water dispersal involves buoyant seeds; animal dispersal includes burrs with hooks, fleshy fruits eaten by animals, nuts cached by animals, and myrmecochory (ant dispersal via elaiosomes). Dehiscent fruits split open while indehiscent fruits remain closed.

### Size Variation

Seed size ranges dramatically from dust-like orchid seeds (0.81 μg, ~1 million per gram) to the coco de mer (up to 25 kg). Small seeds allow high production numbers while large seeds provide greater energy reserves. Orchid seeds lack endosperm and depend on mycorrhizal fungi for germination.

## Terms

- **Testa**: Outer seed coat layer derived from the outer integument
- **Endosperm**: Triploid nutrient tissue in angiosperms formed via double fertilization
- **Cotyledon**: Seed leaf that provides nutrients to the embryo; one in monocots, two in dicots
- **Hilum**: Scar on seed coat marking attachment point to funicle
- **Double fertilization**: Process in angiosperms where one sperm fertilizes egg and another fertilizes central cell to form endosperm
- **Dormancy**: State where viable seeds fail to germinate under optimal conditions
- **Anatropous**: Most common ovule shape, curved with micropyle near funicle
- **Bitegmic**: Seed with two integument layers forming testa and tegmen
- **Exalbuminous**: Seeds lacking endosperm at maturity, with nutrients stored in cotyledons
- **Myrmecochory**: Seed dispersal by ants via elaiosome appendages

## Debates and Open Questions

The origin of seed plants remains unsolved, though evidence increasingly points to a middle Devonian origin. The 2004 discovery of *Runcaria heinzelinii* in Givetian Belgium suggests an ancient origin, while the earliest definitive fossil seeds (*Elkinsia polymorpha*) date to the Late Devonian of West Virginia (~365 mya). The relative timing and mechanisms of seed size, shape, and dispersal evolution during the radiation of gymnosperms and angiosperms continue to be investigated. Additionally, the mechanisms underlying secondary dormancy and DNA repair during germination, particularly the role of DNA ligases and PARP enzymes, remain active areas of research.
