# Quran

## Overview  
The Quran is Islam’s central sacred text, believed by Muslims to be the literal, uncreated word of God (Allah) revealed verbatim to the Prophet Muhammad through the angel Gabriel. It is organized into 114 chapters (sūra) comprising individual verses (āyah), and functions both as a source of divine guidance and as the highest authority in Islamic law, theology, and daily practice. Beyond its religious role, the Quran is considered the finest example of classical Arabic literature and has profoundly shaped the Arabic language, Islamic civilization, and global culture.

## Timeline  

- **c. 610 CE** — First revelation to Muhammad in the Cave of Hira during Laylat al-Qadr
- **610–632 CE** — Incremental revelation over 23 years, concluding in 11 AH / 632 CE
- **632 CE** — Death of Muhammad; Quran not yet compiled into a single book
- **632–634 CE** — Compilation ordered by Caliph Abu Bakr under Zayd ibn Thabit
- **644–656 CE** — Caliph Uthman standardizes the text as the Uthmanic codex
- **650s CE** — Spread of Islam necessitates textual uniformity; conflicts over readings arise
- **8th century** — Earliest Quranic manuscripts, including Sanaa fragments, appear
- **9th century** — Doctrine of i'jāz (inimitability) emerges in Mu'tazila circles
- **10th century** — Ibn Mujāhid establishes seven canonical readings (qirā'āt)
- **1924** — Cairo edition becomes the standard printed text

## Body  

### Revelation and Prophetic Era  
Muslims believe the Quran was revealed orally to Muhammad through the angel Gabriel, beginning in 610 CE during his retreat to the Cave of Hira. The revelations continued intermittently over 23 years, ending shortly before his death in 632 CE. These revelations are categorized as either Meccan (two-thirds of the text) or Medinan, based on the location of Muhammad at the time of revelation. The Quran describes itself as a guide for humanity (2:185), emphasizing monotheism, moral conduct, and divine judgment. Its allusive style—referring to events without full narration—requires exegesis (tafsir) for interpretation.

### Compilation and Preservation  
Shortly after Muhammad’s death, the first compilation was undertaken under Caliph Abu Bakr, led by Zayd ibn Thabit, who gathered verses from palm leaves, bones, and the memories of companions. The text was later standardized under Caliph Uthman, who created the Uthmanic codex and distributed copies to major cities. This process aimed to resolve disputes over varying readings. However, debates persist regarding the extent of standardization and the suppression of alternative versions. Some scholars argue that the Quran evolved gradually, citing manuscript evidence like the Sanaa palimpsest, which reveals textual variants and suggests an evolving rather than fixed text.

### Structure and Content  
The Quran consists of 114 sūras arranged roughly by length, not chronology. Each sūra begins with the Bismillah ("In the name of God"), except the ninth. The text includes narratives of prophets, ethical teachings, legal injunctions, and eschatological warnings. Approximately one-third of the Quran deals with the afterlife and the Day of Judgment. Key themes include the unity of God (tawḥīd), the prophethood of Muhammad, and the importance of justice and compassion. The Quran also references figures from Jewish and Christian traditions, often adapting these stories to convey Islamic messages.

### Literary Style and Inimitability  
The Quran’s literary style is described as "rhymed prose," blending poetic rhythm with prose-like clarity. Its inimitability (i'jāz) is a core doctrine, asserting that no human speech can match its eloquence. This belief is reinforced by the tradition that Muhammad was illiterate (ummi), making the Quran’s literary excellence a miracle. The text employs various rhetorical devices, including repetition, parallelism, and self-referential statements. Some scholars interpret its fragmented structure as a deliberate literary technique to convey profound spiritual truths.

### Interpretation and Exegesis  
Tafsir, the exegesis of the Quran, is essential for understanding its deeper meanings. Classical tafsir divides verses into muhkam (clear) and mutashabih (ambiguous), with the latter requiring contextual and symbolic interpretation. Two main approaches exist: tafsir bi-al-ma'thur (based on transmitted traditions) and tafsir bi-al-ra'y (based on personal reasoning). Esoteric interpretations (ta'wil), particularly in Sufi traditions, explore the inner (batin) meanings of the text. Modern scholarship applies historical-critical methods, examining the Quran’s sources, composition, and relationship with earlier religious texts.

### Role in Worship and Law  
The Quran is central to Islamic worship, recited in daily prayers (salat) and during Ramadan. Proper recitation follows rules of tajwid, ensuring correct pronunciation and rhythm. It is also a source of Islamic law (sharia), with verses addressing governance, inheritance, marriage, and criminal justice. The Quran’s legal injunctions are interpreted through fiqh (jurisprudence), which varies across schools of thought. Rituals like fasting, charity (zakat), and pilgrimage (hajj) are rooted in Quranic teachings.

### Modern Scholarship and Criticism  
Academic studies of the Quran, known as Quranic studies, employ disciplines like philology, textual criticism, and historical analysis. Scholars debate the text’s compilation history, with some arguing for a more gradual process than traditional accounts suggest. Manuscript discoveries, such as those from Sanaa, reveal textual diversity and challenge claims of perfect preservation. Critics question the Quran’s origins, noting parallels with earlier religious texts and inconsistencies in transmission. Despite these debates, the Quran remains the authoritative text for Muslims, with its preservation seen as divinely guaranteed (Quran 15:9).

## Terms  

- **Sūrah**: A chapter of the Quran; there are 114 sūras.  
- **Āyah**: A verse of the Quran; the term also means "sign" or "miracle."  
- **Tafsir**: Exegesis or commentary explaining the Quran’s meaning.  
- **Tajwid**: Rules governing the proper pronunciation and recitation of the Quran.  
- **Qirā'āt**: Canonical variant readings of the Quran, transmitted through oral tradition.  
- **Hadith**: Oral traditions recording the sayings and actions of Muhammad, used alongside the Quran.  
- **Sharia**: Islamic law derived from the Quran and hadith.  
- **Fiqh**: Jurisprudence based on interpreting the Quran and hadith.  
- **I'jāz**: The doctrine of the Quran’s inimitability, asserting its miraculous literary quality.  
- **Muhammadiyya**: The belief that the Quran is the literal, uncreated word of God.  

## Debates and Open Questions  

### Compilation and Textual History  
Traditional Islamic narratives hold that the Quran was compiled under Abu Bakr and standardized under Uthman. However, some scholars argue that the text evolved over centuries, citing manuscript evidence like the Sanaa fragments, which show textual variants. The suppression of alternative codices, such as those attributed to Ibn Masud and Ubayy ibn Ka'b, raises questions about the extent of standardization.

### Ahruf and Qirā'āt  
The concept of ahruf (original readings) versus qirā'āt (canonical variants) remains debated. Some interpretations suggest that Uthman’s codex eliminated regional dialects, while others view the variants as divinely sanctioned. The relationship between these readings and the text’s stability is a subject of ongoing scholarly discussion.

### Inimitability vs. Literary Criticism  
While Muslims regard the Quran as inimitable (i'jāz), some Western scholars critique its literary coherence and note parallels with earlier texts. The doctrine of inimitability is challenged by textual variants and questions about the text’s historical development.

### Relationship with Earlier Scriptures  
The Quran’s connections to Jewish and Christian texts are widely acknowledged. Debates center on whether these references reflect direct borrowing, shared oral traditions, or independent revelation. The concept of tahrif (corruption of previous scriptures) is contested, with some scholars arguing for a more nuanced understanding of intertextuality.

### Modern Standardization  
The 1924 Cairo edition is the standard text used today, but its dominance has marginalized other readings, such as the Warsh version in North Africa. The lack of a critical edition based on manuscript evidence remains a point of contention among scholars seeking to reconstruct the Quran’s earliest form.
