# Mark Rothko

## Overview
Mark Rothko (1903–1970) was a Latvian-born American painter who became a central figure in abstract expressionism, though he rejected the label. He is best known for his "color field" paintings (1949–1970), featuring soft-edged rectangular regions of color intended to evoke basic human emotions—tragedy, ecstasy, doom—rather than aesthetic decoration. Rothko viewed art as a spiritual vehicle, influenced by Nietzsche’s *The Birth of Tragedy*, mythology, and Surrealism, seeking to fill a modern "spiritual void." His mature works demanded intimate, enveloping viewing conditions. Late in life, he executed major mural commissions (Seagram, Harvard, Rothko Chapel), but struggled with depression, health issues, and the commercialization of his work. He died by suicide in 1970; a subsequent landmark lawsuit (the "Rothko Case") recovered his estate from conflicted executors. His paintings now command record auction prices.

## Body

### Childhood and Migration
Rothko was born in Dvinsk, a town in the Pale of Settlement, to a Jewish intellectual family. His father, Jacob, a pharmacist and Marxist, provided a secular, political upbringing but later returned to Orthodox Judaism, sending young Marcus to *cheder* (religious school) to study Talmud. Fearing conscription of his elder sons into the Russian Army, Jacob emigrated to the U.S. in 1910; Marcus, his mother, and sister Sonia joined him in Portland, Oregon, in late 1913. Jacob died of colon cancer months later, leaving the family destitute. Marcus worked in a warehouse, accelerated through school, and graduated Lincoln High in 1921. He absorbed Portland’s radical labor culture (IWW, Emma Goldman, Bill Haywood), developing oratorical skills and a commitment to workers’ rights. A scholarship took him to Yale (1922), but finding it elitist and racist, he dropped out in 1923.

### Early Career and Artistic Formation
In New York (1923), seeing students sketch at the Art Students League sparked Rothko’s artistic vocation. He studied under Cubist Max Weber, who taught art as emotional/religious expression, and Arshile Gorky at Parsons. Early works (1924–1939) were representational, impressionistic urban scenes. Key influences: German Expressionists, Paul Klee, Georges Rouault, and especially Milton Avery, whose abstract nature paintings and professional example validated Rothko’s path. Rothko joined Avery’s circle (Gottlieb, Newman, Solman, Graham), vacationing at Lake George and Gloucester. In 1935, he co-founded "The Ten" to protest "literal painting." He taught children at the Brooklyn Jewish Center (1929–1950s) for income. His first solo shows (Portland 1933, Contemporary Arts Gallery NYC 1935) revealed a shift toward rich color fields beyond Avery.

### Transitional Period: Myth, Tragedy, and Surrealism (1940–1950)
Responding to WWII and a perceived dead end in American painting, Rothko turned to mythology to express universal tragedy. With Gottlieb and Newman, he studied Freud, Jung (collective unconscious, archetypes), Frazer’s *The Golden Bough*, and Nietzsche’s *The Birth of Tragedy*. Nietzsche’s view that Greek tragedy redeems mortal terror became Rothko’s core: art must relieve modern spiritual emptiness caused by mythology’s absence. He saw himself as a "mythmaker"; paintings titled *Antigone*, *Oedipus*, *The Furies*, *Gethsemane* juxtaposed barbaric violence and civilized passivity. Surrealism (Ernst, Miró, Dalí, Paalen) and Cubism provided formal vocabulary. In 1942, Rothko and Gottlieb issued a manifesto: "We favor the simple expression of the complex thought... We are for flat forms because they destroy illusion and reveal truth." A 1943 meeting with Clyfford Still, whose abstract color fields deeply impressed him, accelerated the break with Surrealism. *Slow Swirl at the Edge of the Sea* (1945) marks the shift: biomorphic forms in a swirling atmosphere, with a rigid rectangular background foreshadowing the mature style.

### Maturity: The "Classic" Color Field Paintings (1949–1970)
By 1949, "multiforms" (blurred color blocks, e.g., *No. 18*, 1948) resolved into the signature format: two or three soft-edged, vertically stacked rectangles of contrasting/complementary color on large vertical canvases. Rothko insisted on intimate viewing (as close as 18 inches) to envelop the viewer: "You are in it." Technique: thin binder-pigment layer on raw canvas, then heavily thinned oils applied fast and light, creating luminous, overlapping veils. He used egg, glue, acrylic resins, phenol formaldehyde, and other experimental mediums to control drying and layering; switched to acrylics in 1968 due to health. Early mature works used vibrant reds/yellows (energy, ecstasy); by mid-1950s, palette darkened to blues, greens, grays, blacks, reflecting, critics argued, personal darkness. Rothko denied being an abstractionist or colorist: color was "merely an instrument" for "basic human emotions." He refused to explain his work, insisting silence was "so accurate."

### Mural Commissions
**Seagram Murals (1958–1959):** Commissioned for the Four Seasons Restaurant in Mies van der Rohe/Philip Johnson’s Seagram Building. Rothko produced 40 dark red/brown vertical paintings. He later confessed his intent was to "ruin the appetite" of diners, making them feel trapped. Disgusted by the restaurant’s pretentious atmosphere, he refunded the commission and kept the paintings. They now reside at Tate Britain, Kawamura Memorial Museum (Japan), and National Gallery of Art (DC).  
**Harvard Murals (1962–1963):** Ten paintings for Holyoke Center penthouse. Lithol red pigment faded severely under sunlight; works removed by 1979. Restored 2014–2015 via color-projection lighting.  
**Rothko Chapel (1964–1970):** Non-denominational chapel in Houston, funded by John and Dominique de Menil. Rothko designed the octagonal, skylit space (inspired by Byzantine St. Maria Assunta) and 14 massive dark paintings (brown triptych, black triptychs, individual panels) to surround viewers in "impenetrable fortresses" of color. He supervised assistants due to failing health. Chapel dedicated 1971, after his death.

### Final Years, Suicide, and Estate Battle
Diagnosed with aortic aneurysm (1968), Rothko ignored medical advice (drinking, smoking, diet) but switched to smaller acrylics on paper. Marriage to Mell deteriorated; they separated New Year’s Day 1969. On February 25, 1970, he overdosed on barbiturates and cut his arm; no note. His "Black on Gray" late series is often linked to depression, though critics (Anfam, Grange) argue they continue lifelong themes. Shortly before death, Rothko and advisor Bernard Reis created a foundation for his work. Reis and co-executors (Morton Levine, Theodore Stamos) sold paintings to Marlborough Gallery at undervalued prices, splitting profits. Daughter Kate sued (1971); after 10+ years, courts found executors liable for negligence/conflict of interest, awarded $9.2M damages, returned 658 unsold paintings (half to estate, half to reconstituted Mark Rothko Foundation). Mell died six months later.

### Legacy
The Mark Rothko Foundation donated its holdings to 35 museums globally. David Anfam’s *Catalogue Raisonné* (1998) documents 836 canvases. Unpublished manuscript *The Artist’s Reality* (2004) outlines his philosophy. John Logan’s play *Red* (2009, Tony Award winner) dramatizes the Seagram episode. Monuments and the Mark Rothko Art Centre exist in Daugavpils. Major retrospectives continue (Fondation Louis Vuitton 2023, Palazzo Strozzi 2026). Auction prices remain among the highest for postwar art.

## Terms
- **Color field painting**: Abstract style characterized by large areas of flat, solid color; Rothko’s signature mode from 1949.
- **Multiforms**: Transitional works (1946–1949) with blurred, irregular color blocks, lacking figures or landscape, preceding the rectangular format.
- **The Ten**: Artist group (1935) including Rothko, Gottlieb, Bolotowsky, protesting equivalence of American painting with literal representation.
- **Cheder**: Traditional Jewish elementary school for religious study (Talmud, Hebrew).
- **Pale of Settlement**: Western region of Imperial Russia where Jewish residency was permitted.
- **Lithol red**: Organic red pigment used in Harvard Murals; fugitive (fades) under light exposure.
- **Rothko Case**: Landmark 1971–1975 lawsuit recovering Rothko’s estate from conflicted executors and Marlborough Gallery.
- **Abstract expressionism**: Post-WWII American art movement emphasizing spontaneous, gestural, or color-field abstraction; Rothko is associated but rejected the label.
- **The Birth of Tragedy**: Nietzsche’s 1872 work arguing Greek tragedy redeems existence; Rothko’s key philosophical influence.
- **Biomorphic abstraction**: Surrealist-influenced forms suggesting living organisms without literal representation; Rothko’s 1940s mode.

## Debates and open questions
- **Suicide vs. artistic continuity**: Whether the dark "Black on Gray" paintings are "pictorial suicide notes" (medical literature) or continuations of Rothko’s lifelong tragic/transcendent project (Anfam, Grange).
- **Seagram Murals motive**: Why Rothko accepted then repudiated a commission he knew was for a luxury restaurant; interpretations range from principled rejection of decoration to psychological "drama of trust and betrayal" (Breslin).
- **Spiritual vs. aesthetic experience**: Rothko insisted his work evoked religious emotion, not color relationships; critics debate whether the chapel paintings function as universal spiritual objects or remain tied to their Catholic origins.
- **Technique secrecy**: Rothko’s experimental paint mixtures (egg, glue, resins) were partly secret; conservation challenges persist, especially for Harvard and Chapel works.
- **Market vs. intent**: The explosion of auction prices ($186M in 2014) contrasts sharply with Rothko’s fear that collectors misunderstood his work’s purpose and his refusal to sell to those he deemed unworthy.
