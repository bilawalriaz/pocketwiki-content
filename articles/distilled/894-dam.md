# Dam

## Overview
A dam is a civil engineering structure that impounds water or raises the level of a water body, distinct from dikes or levees which line riverbanks. Dams are classified into four primary structural types—gravity, embankment, buttress, and arch—each transferring water loads differently: gravity dams rely on mass; embankment dams use broad earthworks; buttress dams employ a sloped face supported by triangular supports; and arch dams transfer horizontal thrust to valley walls. Dams serve critical societal functions, irrigating roughly 20% of global arable land (as of 2022) and providing about 14% of global electricity via hydropower (as of 2024). However, they fragment river ecosystems—only 23% of rivers flow freely to the ocean (as of 2019)—displace communities (e.g., 1.4 million for Three Gorges Dam), and alter sediment transport, driving a modern tension between water security and ecological restoration.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BCE** — Jawa Dam (Jordan) built; earliest known dam, an embankment structure 28 m thick and 5.5 m high.
- **c. 500 BCE** — Great Dam of Marib (Yemen) completed; 700 m long, 20 m high.
- **1st century CE** — Roman Empire begins building masonry gravity dams with vertical faces; pioneers waterproof cement.
- **c. 1350 CE** — Kurit Dam (Persia/Iran) built; world's first large thin arch dam, later raised to 64 m.
- **1776** — Charles-Augustin de Coulomb formulates soil stress theory, later applied to dam engineering.
- **1847** — François Zola designs first arch dam based on analytical stress calculations.
- **1936** — Hoover Dam (USA) completed; iconic concrete gravity dam.
- **1959** — Malpasset Dam (France) fails due to underground slip line, killing hundreds; Vajont Dam (Italy) completed, later overtopped by landslide wave (1963) killing >2,000.
- **1970** — Aswan High Dam (Egypt) completed; uses deep grout curtain (208 m) to seal sandy riverbed.
- **1975** — Banqiao/Shimantan dam failures (China) during Typhoon Nina; estimated 171,000–230,000 deaths.
- **2006** — Three Gorges Dam (China) completed; world's largest power station, displaced 1.4 million people.
- **2024** — EU Nature Restoration Law encourages removal of unneeded dams; >600 removed in Europe in 2025.

## Body

### Types and Structural Mechanics
**Embankment dams** are the most common type, built from compacted natural materials (rock, clay, sand, gravel, soil) forming a broad mound. Classified as rockfill or earthfill, they utilize local materials, reducing cost, and their wide base distributes load, allowing construction on softer soils. Their primary drawback is permeability; seepage mitigation requires internal impervious cores (clay, cement, asphalt), drainage blankets, or grout curtains. Uncontrolled seepage causes "piping"—progressive internal erosion leading to catastrophic breach. Design evolved from homogeneous earth (pre-16th century) to zoned construction (clay core, sloping soil shoulders, rock-faced slopes) and, in the 1970s, Concrete Face Rockfill Dams (CFRD) with sealed concrete slabs on the upstream face.

**Gravity dams** resist water pressure through sheer weight. Modern versions use concrete; historical ones used masonry. Cross-section is roughly triangular (base thickness 70–85% of height) with a vertical upstream face (inclination ≤0.05) and downstream slope (inclination 0.75–0.8). Stability requires the resultant force to fall within the middle third of the base (middle-third rule). They require bedrock foundations; soil would compress and crack the structure. "Uplift" pressure from water seeping beneath is mitigated by grout curtains and drainage galleries.

**Buttress dams** feature a sloped upstream face (inclination 0.2–1.67) supported by downstream triangular buttresses. They use less concrete than gravity dams but require complex formwork. The water load pushes the structure downward, enhancing stability. Smaller footprint reduces uplift risk. To accommodate differential movement between buttresses, the upstream face is segmented into "buttress heads" separated by flexible seals.

**Arch dams** are thin, curved concrete structures (base thickness < 50% height) transferring water load horizontally into valley walls. They require narrow valleys with strong, steep rock abutments. Shapes evolved from "constant radius" (cylindrical section) to "constant angle" (radius decreases toward base, adopted from 1914) and "double curvature" (domed, curved vertically and horizontally) for optimized strength. Concrete abutments are sometimes built to supplement weak valley walls.

**Composite and special structures** include arch-gravity dams (arch shape but gravity-thick base), multiple-arch dams (series of small arches between buttresses), and barrages (gated spillway integrated into structure). **Natural dams** form via rockslides (e.g., 2010 Attabad Lake, Pakistan; 1786 Dadu River, China, killing 100,000), volcanic activity (lava, lahars), or beaver activity (keystone species creating wetland habitats).

### Uses and Ancillary Structures
**Primary purposes** include irrigation (20% of arable land), hydropower (14% of global electricity; >80% of reservoir storage capacity used for hydro as of 2006), water supply (3,394 large dams in 2025), flood management (2,510 large dams in 2025, designed for statistical return periods like 1-in-100-year floods), navigation, recreation, and fish farming. **Pumped storage** dams cycle water between upper/lower reservoirs to balance grid demand (e.g., pumping at night, generating at peak). **Tailings dams** impound mining waste (often toxic), built on flat ground, raised incrementally, and lined to prevent contamination. **Cofferdams** are temporary enclosures for dry construction. **Weirs** are low, flow-regulating structures. **Saddle dams** seal low points in reservoir rims. **Underground dams** block groundwater flow in arid regions.

**Auxiliary structures** enable function: **Power plants** house turbines (Francis, Pelton, Kaplan) driven by water conveyed via penstocks; "head" (vertical drop) determines turbine choice. **Spillways** safely pass design floods (100–500 yr for small dams; 10,000 yr for large dams) using ogee profiles and energy dissipators. **Gates** (vertical lift, drum, radial) regulate reservoir level. **Outlets** (low-level tunnels/galleries) drain reservoirs for maintenance, sediment purge, or emergency drawdown. **Locks** (gated chambers) bypass navigation barriers. **Fish bypasses** (ladders, lifts, artificial streams) mitigate migration blockage.

### History: From Empiricism to Science
**Antiquity:** Jawa Dam (c. 3000 BCE) and Sadd el-Kafara (c. 2600 BCE, failed) in Egypt; Hittite dams (17th–13th c. BCE, Turkey); Sabaean dams culminating in Great Dam of Marib (c. 500 BCE, Yemen); Sunshu Ao's dam (c. 580 BCE, China); Sri Lankan reservoir dams (c. 370–300 BCE). **Roman Era (1st c. CE onward):** Masonry gravity dams with vertical faces; first use of hydraulic cement/pozzolana; Subiaco Dam (60 CE, 40 m tall); ~80 dams in Hispania (Proserpina Dam operational in 2026); Band-e Kaisar (Persia, Roman prisoners); Iron Gate (550 CE, arch-gravity). **Post-Classical/Middle Ages:** Sayama embankment (380 CE, Japan); Kurit Dam (c. 1350 CE, Persia, first large thin arch); European water wheels (Bazacle weir, 1170 CE); Bohemian fish ponds (15th–16th c.); Almansa Dam (1384, Spain, arch-gravity); Elche Dam (1640, Spain, first European true arch since Rome); Saint-Ferréol (1675, France, Midi Canal supply). **Industrial Revolution (late 18th c.):** Shift from empirical practice to science. Coulomb (1776) soil stress formula; Collin applied it to dams. Zola (1847) analytical arch design. DeSazilly established triangular gravity profile. Early scientific dams: Glencorse (1824, UK, clay core), Gouffre d'Enfer (1866, France, 60 m masonry), Mir Alam (1804, India, first large buttress), Parramatta (1856, Australia, thin arch test). **Modern Era (20th c.):** Post-WWII heavy machinery (bulldozers, scrapers) enabled massive projects: Hoover (1936), Three Gorges (2006), Tarbela (1976), Nurek (1980). Grout curtains allowed Aswan High Dam (1970) on deep Nile sands. Environmental opposition emerged regarding ecosystem impacts vs. "clean" hydro.

### Environmental and Socio-Economic Impacts
**Environmental:** Dams degrade downstream water quality/quantity (dry season), causing deforestation/desertification (Iran loses 2.2 billion m³/yr to evaporation; Aral Sea collapse linked to Soviet irrigation dams). Sediment trapping (half of global river sediment, ~8–16 km³/yr) shrinks deltas. Reservoir weight induces seismicity (Hoover, Nurek). Biodiversity loss: 53% of 7,369 fish species threatened with extirpation by 31,780 dams; Yangtze extinctions (Chinese paddlefish, baiji dolphin); invasive species thrive in reservoirs (zebra mussel, wels catfish). Salinity changes (Diama Dam, Senegal). Greenhouse gases: Concrete production emits 100–300 kg CO₂/m³; reservoir decomposition emits CO₂/CH₄. Cold-climate hydro emits 30–60x less CO₂/CH₄ than coal.

**Socio-economic:** Benefits: irrigation, water supply, hydro, recreation, industrial growth. Costs: Displacement (Aswan: 50,000 Nubians; Danjiangkou: 383,000; Three Gorges: 1.4M; Akosombo: 80,000, cultural disruption). Infrastructure modification. Waterborne diseases (schistosomiasis, malaria). Heritage loss (Philae/Abu Simbel relocated; Guaíra Falls inundated). **Water conflict:** Upstream dams on transboundary rivers (Turkey's GAP on Euphrates/Tigris vs. Syria/Iraq; India on Indus vs. Pakistan; China on Mekong vs. downstream nations; Ethiopia's GERD on Blue Nile vs. Egypt/Sudan) drive geopolitical tension.

### Design, Construction, and Operation
**Design Process:** Three stages—reconnaissance (site visit, geological/seismic/topographic data), feasibility (detailed geology, hydrology, hydraulics, environmental/flood impact, land acquisition, material sources), planning (detailed plans, schedule, cost estimates). **Technical Investigations:** Topographic (precise maps), Geological (foundation strength, faults, seismicity), Hydrological (watershed, precipitation, flood frequency, spillway capacity). **Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA)** required in many nations to assess/mitigate/compensate impacts. **Site/Type/Material Selection:** Driven by valley topography (narrow → gravity/arch; wide → embankment), geology (bedrock for gravity; strong abutments for arch), hydrology, material availability (local rock → rockfill; clay core needed), and spillway paths. Forces analyzed: hydrostatic, ice, sediment, thermal, uplift, flood, seismic, shrinkage, self-weight. **Aesthetics:** Curved forms (arch dams) often preferred; dams as civic monuments (Hoover, Bratsk, Three Gorges, Aswan).

**Construction:** River diversion via tunnels/channels and cofferdams (removed or incorporated). Foundation prep: strip to bedrock for concrete dams; embankments tolerate softer ground. **Grouting:** Consolidation (fill cracks) and curtain (deep borehole wall, 30–70% reservoir depth) to cut seepage. **Drainage:** Pipes, rock blankets, or internal galleries/shafts to control piping/uplift. **Placement:** Embankments: layered lifts (soil/rock from borrow areas), compacted, instrumented. Concrete: batch plant on site; placed in blocks (1.5–3 m high, 12–30 m wide); vibrated to remove voids; mass concrete heat managed by expansion joints (grouted post-cooling), refrigerated pipes, or Roller Compacted Concrete (RCC—less cement, less heat, immediate trafficability, lower cost).

**Operation:** Management ensures purpose fulfillment, safety, lifespan. **Monitoring:** Permanent instruments (tiltmeters, strain meters, piezometers, etc.) track stresses (water/ice/sediment load, seismic, foundation compression, settlement, thermal expansion). **Sedimentation:** Reduces capacity (~8–16 km³/yr trapped globally). Mitigation: watershed vegetation, terraces. **Dam Removal:** Increasingly practiced for safety, obsolescence, fish passage, sediment restoration. US: >1,200 removed (600+ 1996–2016); Elwha (2014–18) restored delta; Klamath (2020–24) restored salmon. Europe: >1M barriers (2021), 150,000+ obsolete; EU Nature Restoration Law (2024); 600+ removed in 2025, restoring 3,740 km rivers.

### Failure Modes and Lessons
Failure causes: **Arch dams** – abutment rock weakness, foundation erosion, shear at rock interface. **Gravity/Buttress** – overturning, sliding, cracking, earthquakes. **Embankment** – **piping** (internal erosion via seepage). Early high failure rates (19th/early 20th c., esp. embankments) dropped post-1900 (100 failures of >15 m dams by 1994), though consequences rose with scale. **Case studies:** Sadd el-Kafara (c. 2600 BCE, flood during construction). Malpasset (1959, France): underground slip line moved under reservoir weight, cracked arch. Vajont (1963, Italy): designers missed landslide-prone geology; massive slide displaced reservoir, 125 m wave overtopped dam (minor dam damage), >2,000 dead. Baldwin Hills (1963, US): cause undetermined (weak foundation, fault, oil field repressurization, cycling). Banqiao/Shimantan (1975, China): Typhoon Nina cascade failure, 171,000–230,000 dead.

### Society, Culture, and Regulation
**Wartime:** 20 documented attacks (1917–1993); Geneva Conventions Protocol I (1949) prohibits attacks causing severe civilian losses. **Regulation:** Most dam-building nations have statutes/regulations varying widely; some lack inspection agencies. Standards often reference "recognized rules of technology" rather than prescriptive specs. Jurisdiction varies (federal vs. state/province; e.g., Germany regulates at state level). **Art/Culture:** Soviet art glorified industrial dams (Brodsky, Klutsis). *The Dam Busters* (1955) depicts WWII RAF raid on Möhne Dam. *DamNation* (2014) advocates removal. Literature: *The Johnstown Flood* (McCullough, 1968); *The Monkey Wrench Gang* (Abbey, 1975). Music: Woody Guthrie's Columbia River songs (1941); Ethiopian pop songs funding GERD (2010s).

## Terms
- ****Embankment dam**** — A broad, mound-like barrier built from compacted natural materials (rock, clay, sand, gravel, soil); classified as rockfill or earthfill.
- ****Gravity dam**** — A massive concrete or masonry structure relying on its weight to resist water pressure; triangular cross-section requiring bedrock foundation.
- ****Buttress dam**** — A sloped upstream face supported by downstream triangular buttresses; uses less concrete than gravity dams but requires complex formwork.
- ****Arch dam**** — A thin, curved concrete dam transferring water load horizontally into valley walls; requires narrow valley with strong rock abutments.
- ****Piping**** — Progressive internal erosion in embankment dams where seepage forms an enlarging channel, leading to catastrophic breach.
- ****Grout curtain**** — A deep wall of injected grout (30–70% of reservoir depth) beneath and beside a dam to prevent under-seepage and uplift.
- ****Spillway**** — A controlled release structure for excess reservoir water, designed to pass design floods (e.g., 1-in-10,000-year event for large dams).
- ****Penstock**** — A pressurized conduit conveying water from the reservoir to the turbine in a hydropower plant.
- ****Head**** — The vertical distance between the upstream water surface and the turbine; determines hydraulic potential energy.
- ****Roller Compacted Concrete (RCC)**** — A drier concrete mix placed in thin lifts and compacted by rollers; generates less heat, requires less cement/formwork, speeds construction.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Climate mitigation vs. adaptation:** Whether hydropower's low-carbon electricity justifies its high methane/CO₂ emissions from reservoir decomposition and concrete production, and its vulnerability to drought-driven generation loss.
*   **Transboundary water rights:** Lack of binding international frameworks for upstream dam operations (e.g., GERD, Mekong, Euphrates/Tigris) creates persistent geopolitical instability.
*   **Sediment management vs. reservoir longevity:** Global trapping of 8–16 km³/yr of sediment reduces live storage and starves deltas; sustainable sediment bypass/flushing techniques remain technically difficult and rarely implemented at scale.
*   **Dam removal economics and ecology:** Balancing the high cost and sediment-release risks of removal against the ecological recovery of migratory fish (55% decline in Europe) and river connectivity; prioritization criteria for the >150,000 obsolete European barriers.
*   **Seismic safety of aging dams:** Many large dams (post-1900) were designed before modern seismic hazard assessment; reassessment of reservoir-induced seismicity and ground motion criteria for existing structures is ongoing.
*   **Pumped storage expansion vs. battery storage:** Whether new pumped-storage dams (requiring specific topography and high capital cost) remain competitive against rapidly falling battery costs for grid-scale energy storage.
