# George Boole

## Overview
George Boole (1815–1864) was an English mathematician and philosopher who revolutionized logic by transforming it from a philosophical study of language into a mathematical system. His development of Boolean algebra, which uses binary values and logical operators, provided the theoretical foundation for digital circuit design and modern computer science, making him a pivotal figure in the Information Age.

## Timeline
- **2 November 1815** — Born in Lincoln, Lincolnshire, England.
- **c. 1831** — Began teaching at age 16 to support his family.
- **1834** — Established his own school in Lincoln.
- **1840** — Published first paper in the *Cambridge Mathematical Journal*.
- **1844** — Won the Royal Society's first gold prize for mathematics.
- **1847** — Published *The Mathematical Analysis of Logic*, introducing symbolic logic.
- **1849** — Appointed first Professor of Mathematics at Queen's College, Cork.
- **1854** — Published *An Investigation of the Laws of Thought*, containing Boolean algebra.
- **1855** — Married Mary Everest.
- **1859** — Published *Treatise on Differential Equations*.
- **8 December 1864** — Died in Cork, Ireland, from pneumonia-induced pleural effusion.

## Biography and Early Life
Boole was the son of a shoemaker and received only a primary school education, supplemented by lessons from his father and self-study in Latin and modern languages. At 16, he became the family breadwinner, taking a teaching position. He established his own school at 19 and later ran a boarding school in Lincoln. Despite limited formal training, he mastered calculus through self-study with texts provided by local contacts and began publishing mathematical research in 1840.

## Academic Career and Major Works
In 1849, Boole became the first professor of mathematics at Queen's College, Cork (now University College Cork). His key works include *The Mathematical Analysis of Logic* (1847), which introduced symbolic logic, and *An Investigation of the Laws of Thought* (1854), which presented his mature system of Boolean algebra. He also authored systematic treatises on differential equations and the calculus of finite differences. His work on linear differential equations and the sum of residues of rational functions contributed to analysis.

## Development of Boolean Algebra
Boole's initial involvement in logic was prompted by a debate on quantification between Sir William Hamilton and Augustus De Morgan. He sought to systematize and extend Aristotelian logic using algebraic methods. In 1847, he developed Boolean algebra, where multiplication represents logical "and" and addition represents logical "or." A key innovation was the "principle of wholistic reference," later adopted in first-order logic. He defined the "universe of discourse" as the limited field of subjects in any discourse. An early challenge was the treatment of addition, which Boole conceived as a partial operation corresponding to the union of disjoint subsets.

## Legacy and Influence
Boole's work was expanded by scholars like William Stanley Jevons and Charles Sanders Peirce. In the 20th century, Claude Shannon and Victor Shestakov independently applied Boolean algebra to optimize electromechanical relay systems, forming the basis for digital circuit design and modern computers. Boole's ideas are fundamental to computer programming and the Information Age. He received honors including the Royal Society's gold prize, the Keith Medal, and honorary degrees from Dublin and Oxford. University College Cork celebrated his 200th birth anniversary in 2015.

## Personal Life
In 1855, Boole married Mary Everest, niece of George Everest. They had five daughters who became notable in various fields, including Alicia (a mathematician), Lucy (first female professor of chemistry in England), and Ethel (author of *The Gadfly*). Boole's religious views combined Christian theology with a belief in a "Supreme Intelligent Cause." He was influenced by mysticism and, according to his wife, by Indian logic.

## Terms
- **Boolean algebra**: A logical calculus of truth values or set membership, using binary variables and logical operators.
- **Universe of discourse**: The limited field of subjects or objects under consideration in a given logical discussion.
- **Principle of wholistic reference**: The idea that logical terms refer to the entire universe of discourse, not just specific instances.
- **Elective symbols**: Boole's term for symbols representing classes or sets in his logical system.
- **Invariant theory**: A branch of mathematics studying properties unchanged under transformations.
- **Differential equations**: Equations involving derivatives of a function, used to model change.
- **Calculus of finite differences**: A mathematical method for approximating derivatives using discrete differences.
- **Pleural effusion**: A condition involving fluid buildup around the lungs, which was the immediate cause of Boole's death.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The correct interpretation of addition (+) in Boolean algebra remains a point of historical discussion: whether it should represent non-exclusive "or," exclusive "or," or a partial operation.
- In 1921, economist John Maynard Keynes argued that Boole made a fundamental error in his definition of independence in probability theory, a critique later challenged by scholars like Theodore Hailperin and David Miller.
- The extent of influence from Indian logic and mysticism on Boole's work, as claimed by his wife Mary, is a subject of scholarly debate.
