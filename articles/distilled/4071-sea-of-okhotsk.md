# Sea of Okhotsk

## Overview
The Sea of Okhotsk is a marginal sea of the northwestern Pacific Ocean, bordered by Russia's Kamchatka Peninsula, the Kuril Islands, Japan's Hokkaido, and Sakhalin. It is a biologically productive and strategically important body of water, notable for its seasonal sea ice, role in global ocean circulation, and rich fisheries. The sea is almost entirely surrounded by Russian territory, with a long-standing territorial dispute with Japan over the southern Kuril Islands.

## Timeline
- **c. 1639–1645** — Russian explorers Vassili Poyarkov and Ivan Moskvitin become the first Europeans to visit the sea.
- **1643** — Dutch captain Maarten Gerritsz Vries charts parts of the coast but fails to identify Sakhalin and Hokkaido as islands.
- **1733** — The Second Kamchatka Expedition under Vitus Bering begins systematic mapping of the sea's coast.
- **1847** — Bowhead whales are first caught in the sea, initiating a major whaling period.
- **1855–1875** — The Kuril Islands are under Japanese administration.
- **1907–1949** — South Sakhalin is administered by Japan as Karafuto Prefecture.
- **1945** — The Soviet Union occupies South Sakhalin and the Kuril Islands after World War II.
- **1983** — The Soviet Union shoots down Korean Air Lines Flight 007 near the sea.
- **1991** — Foreign fishing fleets begin large-scale fishing in the central "Peanut Hole."
- **2013** — The UN subcommittee accepts Russia's claim to the Peanut Hole continental shelf.
- **2013** — The Sea of Okhotsk pollock fishery is certified by the Marine Stewardship Council.

## Body

### Geography and Climate
The Sea of Okhotsk covers 1,583,000 km² with a maximum depth of 3,372 m. It connects to the Sea of Japan via the La Pérouse Strait. It has a subarctic maritime climate and is the coldest sea in East Asia, being the southernmost sea in the Northern Hemisphere to freeze seasonally. Winter ice cover, driven by cold Siberian air, typically reaches 50–70% of the sea's surface. This ice formation expels salt, creating dense, oxygen-rich water that sinks and flows into the Pacific, ventilating the North Pacific Intermediate Water. The sea has warmed by up to 3°C since preindustrial times, inhibiting ice formation and shifting fish populations northward.

### Oceanography and Currents
The sea's circulation is generally cyclonic (counter-clockwise). Three dominant currents shape its dynamics: the warm West Kamchatka Current, the cold East Sakhalin Current (which carries nutrient-rich Amur River water south), and the warm, saline Soya Current from the Sea of Japan. A defining feature is the cold intermediate layer, a residual band of near-freezing water that persists beneath the warmer summer surface. The deep Kuril Basin in the south features anticyclonic circulation and eddies where these currents mix.

### Ecology and Fisheries
The sea is among the world's most biologically productive, supporting an estimated 17.2 million tonnes of epipelagic fish and squid biomass. Its productivity is fueled by riverine nutrients (especially iron from the Amur), upwelling, and seasonal mixing. Key commercial species include walleye pollock (Russia's most important fishery, yielding ~840,000 tonnes annually), Pacific herring, salmon, and red king crab (the world's most abundant population). The ecosystem supports 22 marine mammal species, including ice-associated seals and the endangered western gray whale, which feeds off northeastern Sakhalin.

### History and Geopolitics
The sea was historically inhabited by the Okhotsk culture and Ainu people. Russian exploration began in the 17th century, with the port of Okhotsk established as the first major settlement. The region saw intense whaling in the mid-19th century. During the Cold War, the sea was a Soviet ballistic missile submarine bastion and the site of U.S. cable-tapping operations. The "Peanut Hole," an area of international waters in the center, was subject to overfishing until a 2014 UN ruling extended Russia's continental shelf claim over it. Japan continues to claim the southern Kuril Islands, referred to as the Northern Territories.

### Resources and Hazards
The shelf holds significant oil and gas reserves, estimated at 3.5 billion tons of equivalent fuel. Exploration is active but hazardous; in 2011, the drilling rig *Kolskaya* sank in a storm, killing 53 people. The harsh conditions of the crab fishing industry are famously depicted in Japanese literature.

## Terms
- **Marginal sea:** A sea partially enclosed by islands, archipelagos, or peninsulas.
- **Brine rejection:** The process where salt is expelled from forming sea ice, increasing the salinity and density of the surrounding water.
- **Dicothermal layer:** A cold intermediate layer of water that remains near freezing beneath a seasonally warmed surface.
- **Cyclonic circulation:** A counter-clockwise flow of water or air in the Northern Hemisphere.
- **Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ):** A maritime zone where a state has special rights regarding exploration and use of marine resources.
- **Continental shelf:** The extended perimeter of a continent and associated coastal plain, over which a coastal state has sovereign rights for resource exploitation.
- **Pollock (Gadus chalcogrammus):** A commercially vital species of marine fish, also known as Alaska pollock.
- **Pinniped:** A widely distributed and diverse clade of carnivorous, fin-footed marine mammals (seals, sea lions, walruses).

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Territorial Dispute:** Japan claims the southern Kuril Islands (Northern Territories), which have been administered by Russia since 1945. This dispute prevents a formal peace treaty between the two nations.
- **Climate Change Impacts:** The rapid warming of the sea (three times the global mean) is reducing sea ice extent, altering ocean circulation, and causing significant shifts in fish populations, with major implications for regional fisheries and ecosystems.
- **Peanut Hole Governance:** While the 2014 UN ruling granted Russia continental shelf rights, the long-term management of this historically overfished area and its resources remains a point of international interest.
