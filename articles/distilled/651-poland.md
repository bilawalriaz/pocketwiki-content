# Poland

## Overview
Poland, officially the Republic of Poland, is a Central European country of over 38 million people, bounded by the Baltic Sea to the north and the Carpathian and Sudeten Mountains to the south. Its history spans a millennium of statehood, partition, independence, totalitarian rule, and democratic transition, making it a pivotal case study in European geopolitics, cultural resilience, and post-communist transformation. Today, it is a parliamentary republic, a developed market economy, and a key member of the EU, NATO, and other international institutions.

## Body

### Prehistory and Early Statehood
Human settlement in Poland dates to the Lower Paleolithic, with continuous habitation since the end of the Last Glacial Period (c. 10,000 BC). The Neolithic brought agricultural innovation, including early cheesemaking (5500 BC) and the Bronocice pot, depicting a possible wheeled vehicle (3400 BC). The Bronze and Iron Ages saw the rise of Lusatian culture and fortified settlements like Biskupin. By the early Middle Ages, West Slavic Polans dominated central Poland, giving the land its name. In 966, Mieszko I’s conversion to Christianity under the Roman Catholic Church marked the birth of the Polish state, formalized by the *Dagome iudex* (c. 991), which defined territorial boundaries and affirmed papal protection.

### Kingdom of Poland and the Jagiellonian Era
The Kingdom of Poland was officially crowned in 1025 under Bolesław I the Brave, who expanded territory into Lusatia, Moravia, and Kievan Rus’. The 11th–13th centuries were marked by fragmentation (1138) and external threats, including the Mongol invasion (1241) and the Teutonic Order’s incursions. The 14th century brought reunification under Władysław I the Short and the reign of Casimir III the Great, who strengthened institutions, founded the University of Kraków (1364), and imposed quarantine during the Black Death. In 1386, Jadwiga’s marriage to Władysław II Jagiełło initiated the Polish-Lithuanian union, creating a vast, multi-ethnic Commonwealth. The Battle of Grunwald (1410) decisively defeated the Teutonic Knights, and the Peace of Thorn (1466) established Prussian autonomy under Polish suzerainty.

### Golden Liberty and the Commonwealth
The 16th century, known as the Polish Golden Age, saw the consolidation of *Golden Liberty*—a noble-dominated political system codified by the Nihil novi act (1505), which transferred legislative power from the monarch to the Sejm. Religious tolerance flourished, attracting Jews, Protestants, and other minorities. The Union of Lublin (1569) formalized the Commonwealth, a federal state with an elective monarchy and a powerful nobility. However, internal weakness—manifest in the *liberum veto* (allowing any deputy to dissolve the Sejm)—and external pressures from Sweden, Russia, and the Ottomans led to decline. The Khmelnytsky Uprising (1648) and the Swedish Deluge (1655–1660) devastated the Commonwealth, though John III Sobieski’s victory at Vienna (1683) halted Ottoman expansion.

### Partitions and Insurrections
The late 18th century saw the Commonwealth’s collapse. The First Partition (1772) by Prussia, Russia, and Austria stripped Poland of territory. Stanisław II Augustus Poniatowski, backed by Catherine the Great, attempted reforms but faced resistance from the Bar Confederation (1768–1772). The adoption of the 3 May Constitution (1791)—Europe’s first modern constitution—provoked the Targowica Confederation and Russian intervention. The Second (1793) and Third (1795) Partitions erased Poland entirely. The Kościuszko Uprising (1794) failed to restore independence. Under Napoleonic rule, the Duchy of Warsaw (1807–1815) briefly revived Polish statehood. Subsequent uprisings—the November Uprising (1830–1831) and January Uprising (1863–1864)—were crushed, leading to intensified Russification and Germanization.

### Second Polish Republic and World War II
After WWI, the Treaty of Versailles (1919) restored Polish independence. The Second Polish Republic faced border conflicts, including the Polish-Soviet War (1919–1921), culminating in the decisive Battle of Warsaw (1920). The interwar period was marked by political instability, including the 1926 May Coup by Józef Piłsudski and the rise of the Sanacja regime. In September 1939, the Nazi German invasion of Poland on 1 September, followed by the Soviet invasion on 17 September, marked the beginning of World War II. The Holocaust claimed 3 million Polish Jews, and between 1.8 and 2.8 million ethnic Poles were killed, including up to 100,000 members of the Polish intelligentsia. The Home Army (Armia Krajowa) led resistance, notably during the 1944 Warsaw Uprising. Post-war border shifts expelled millions of Poles and Germans, reducing Poland’s territory by 20%.

### Communist Era and Transition
At the insistence of Joseph Stalin, the Yalta Conference sanctioned the formation of a new provisional pro-Communist coalition government in Moscow, which ignored the Polish government-in-exile based in London. In 1944, Stalin had made guarantees to Churchill and Roosevelt that he would maintain Poland's sovereignty and allow democratic elections to take place. However, upon achieving victory in 1945, the elections organised by the occupying Soviet authorities were falsified and were used to provide a veneer of legitimacy for Soviet hegemony over Polish affairs. The Soviet Union instituted a new communist government in Poland, analogous to much of the rest of the Eastern Bloc. The new communist government took control with the adoption of the Small Constitution on 19 February 1947. The Polish People's Republic (Polska Rzeczpospolita Ludowa) was officially proclaimed in 1952. The 1956 Gomułka era briefly liberalized, but repression resumed. The 1980 Solidarity trade union movement, led by Lech Wałęsa, challenged communist rule. Martial law (1981) failed to crush dissent, and the 1989 Round Table Agreement ushered in democratic elections. Lech Wałęsa, a Solidarity candidate, eventually won the presidency in 1990, and Poland transitioned to a market economy via shock therapy under Leszek Balcerowicz in the early 1990s.

### Third Polish Republic
Poland became a member of the Visegrád Group in 1991, and joined NATO in 1999. Poles then voted to join the European Union in a referendum in June 2003, with Poland becoming a full member on 1 May 2004. Poland joined the Schengen Area in 2007. The 2010 Smolensk crash killed President Lech Kaczyński. The Law and Justice Party (PiS) dominated 2015–2023, increasing EU friction. In 2023, Donald Tusk returned as PM after PiS lost its parliamentary majority. On 1 June 2025, The Law and Justice candidate for the 2025 Polish presidential election, Karol Nawrocki won the election, beating Civic Platform candidate Rafał Trzaskowski.

## Terms
- **Golden Liberty**: Noble-dominated political system in the Commonwealth, characterized by elective monarchy and the *liberum veto*.
- **Sejm**: Poland’s bicameral parliament, consisting of the Sejm (lower house) and Senate (upper house).
- **Teutonic Knights**: Germanic military order that invaded Prussia and clashed with Poland-Lithuania.
- **Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact**: 1939 Nazi-Soviet non-aggression pact that led to Poland’s invasion and partition.
- **Solidarity**: Independent trade union that led the anti-communist opposition in 1980s Poland.
- **Shock Therapy**: Rapid economic transition from planned to market economy, implemented by Leszek Balcerowicz in the early 1990s.
- **Voivodeship**: Poland’s 16 administrative provinces.
- **Polonisation**: Policy of assimilating non-Polish populations into Polish culture, applied in the Commonwealth.
- **Curzon Line**: Soviet-delineated border that became Poland’s eastern boundary after WWII.
- **Oder-Neisse Line**: Poland’s western border, established post-WWII, shifting territory westward.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Ethnic Composition**: The 2021 census reported 98.84% ethnic Poles, but debates persist over underreporting of minorities and the exclusion of undocumented migrants.
- **Judicial Independence**: International IDEA ranks Poland mid-range on democratic measures, citing concerns over judicial reforms under PiS that the EU deemed threats to rule of law.
- **Historical Memory**: Disputes over the 1943–1944 Wołyń Massacres, where up to 100,000 Poles were killed by the UPA, remain contentious in Polish-Ukrainian relations.
- **EU Integration vs. Sovereignty**: PiS’s Euroscepticism and conflicts with EU institutions over rule of law highlight ongoing tensions between integration and national autonomy.
- **Climate and Energy**: Poland’s reliance on coal (100 million tonnes annually) contrasts with its 2040 decarbonization goals, raising questions about feasibility and economic impact.
