# John III Sobieski

## Overview
John III Sobieski (1629–1696) was King of Poland and Grand Duke of Lithuania from 1674 to 1696. A skilled military commander, he is best known for leading the relief of Vienna from Ottoman siege in 1683, a victory that halted Ottoman expansion into Europe and cemented his legacy as a defender of Christendom. His reign marked a period of stabilization for the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth after decades of war, though he ultimately failed to secure the throne for his dynasty.

## Timeline

- **1648** — Returned from studies in Europe; volunteered for the army during the Khmelnytsky Uprising.
- **1665** — Married Marie Casimire d'Arquien; promoted to Crown Grand Marshal.
- **5 February 1668** — Appointed Grand Hetman of the Crown, the highest military rank.
- **11 November 1673** — Won a major victory over the Ottomans at the Battle of Khotyn (Chocim).
- **19 May 1674** — Elected King of Poland and Grand Duke of Lithuania.
- **2 February 1676** — Coronation as King John III.
- **1676** — Treaty of Żurawno signed with the Ottoman Empire.
- **31 March 1683** — Alliance treaty signed with Holy Roman Emperor Leopold I.
- **12 September 1683** — Led allied forces to victory at the Battle of Vienna.
- **17 June 1696** — Died of a heart attack in Wilanów; succeeded by Augustus II the Strong.

## Body

### Early Life and Military Career
Born into Polish nobility, Sobieski was educated at the Jagiellonian University and toured Europe, learning several languages. He entered military service in 1648 during the Khmelnytsky Uprising, where he and his brother Marek commanded cavalry units. After Marek's death in 1652, John rose through the ranks, fighting in the Russo-Polish War and the Swedish invasion known as "The Deluge." Initially capitulating to the Swedes in 1655, he soon returned to the Polish side. His diplomatic mission to the Ottoman Empire allowed him to study their military tactics. By 1668, he had achieved the rank of Grand Hetman of the Crown, the Commonwealth's highest military office.

### Rise to the Throne
Sobieski's political career was marked by factional struggles. He supported French candidates for the Polish throne and married a French noblewoman, aligning with the pro-French faction. Following the death of King Michał Korybut Wiśniowiecki in 1673, Sobieski's prestige from his victory at Khotyn made him the leading candidate. He was elected king in 1674 with near-universal support. His coronation was delayed until 1676 due to ongoing war with the Ottoman Empire. As king, he faced a devastated treasury and powerful magnates with foreign loyalties.

### Reign and the Battle of Vienna
Sobieski's reign focused on military reform and securing the southern border against the Ottomans. After a period of peace following the 1676 Treaty of Żurawno, he reorganized the army, adopting new formations and artillery tactics. His foreign policy shifted from a pro-French stance to an alliance with the Holy Roman Emperor Leopold I, formalized in 1683. This alliance was tested when the Ottoman Empire besieged Vienna. Sobieski led a combined force of about 65,000–76,000 men, including 22,000–27,000 Poles, against an Ottoman army of approximately 143,000. On 12 September 1683, he personally led a decisive cavalry charge that broke the Ottoman lines, lifting the siege. The victory was hailed across Europe, with the Pope calling him the "saviour of Western Christendom."

### Later Years and Legacy
The victory at Vienna did not bring decisive territorial gains for Poland within the Holy League. Subsequent campaigns against the Ottomans were prolonged and inconclusive, weakening Sobieski's position at home. He suffered from poor health and obesity in his later years and died in 1696. He failed to establish a lasting dynasty, as his sons did not succeed him. Remembered as a "hero king" and a patron of arts and sciences, his legacy is complex: a brilliant military leader who stabilized the Commonwealth but could not overcome its internal political weaknesses to secure its long-term future.

## Terms
- **Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth**: A dual state, a bi-confederation of Poland and Lithuania, that was one of the largest and most populous countries in 17th-century Europe.
- **Grand Hetman of the Crown**: The highest military commander in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth.
- **Szlachta**: The Polish nobility class, which held political power and elected the king.
- **Sejm**: The Polish parliament, a legislative assembly of the nobility.
- **Husaria (Winged Hussars)**: The elite heavy cavalry of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, famed for their decisive charge at Vienna.
- **Holy League**: A coalition of Christian states, championed by Pope Innocent XI, formed to oppose the Ottoman Empire after 1683.
- **Deluge**: The period of widespread destruction and foreign invasion of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, primarily by Sweden, from 1655 to 1660.
- **Treaty of Żurawno (1676)**: A peace treaty between the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth and the Ottoman Empire, ending a phase of their war.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Scholars debate the extent to which Sobieski's personal ambitions and his wife's influence shaped his foreign policy, particularly his shifting alliances between France and the Habsburgs.
- The long-term impact of the Battle of Vienna is contested: while a symbolic victory for Christendom, its practical benefits for the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth were limited, and some argue it overextended Sobieski's resources.
- His legacy is viewed ambivalently in modern contexts; for example, in 2024, the city of Vienna rejected a monument to him due to concerns about Islamophobia and anti-Turkish sentiment.
