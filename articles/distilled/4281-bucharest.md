# Bucharest

## Overview
Bucharest is the capital and largest city of Romania, serving as the nation's primary cultural, political, and economic hub. Situated on the Dâmbovița River, it is the 9th most populous city in the European Union by population within city limits. The city's history is marked by periods of rapid growth and significant destruction, notably during World War II and the communist era, and it is now experiencing a modern economic and cultural resurgence.

## Timeline
- **1459** — First mentioned as the 'Citadel of București', residence of Voivode Vlad the Impaler.
- **1698** — Becomes the permanent location of the Wallachian court.
- **1862** — Becomes the capital of the newly united Romanian United Principalities.
- **1881** — Becomes the political centre of the proclaimed Kingdom of Romania.
- **1916–1918** — Occupied by German forces during World War I.
- **1941** — Scene of the Legionnaires' rebellion and Bucharest pogrom.
- **1944** — Site of the royal coup that brought Romania into the Allied camp.
- **1965–1989** — Under Nicolae Ceaușescu, massive systematization demolishes historic areas for socialist architecture.
- **1989** — Romanian Revolution begins with protests in Bucharest, overthrowing the communist regime.
- **2016** — Historical city centre listed as "endangered" by the World Monuments Watch.

## Body

### History and Etymology
First recorded in 1459, Bucharest grew as a centre of Wallachian commerce and governance. Its name's origin is unverified; tradition links it to a founder named Bucur ('joy'), while other theories suggest derivations from beech forests or Ottoman names. The city became the permanent Wallachian court location in 1698 and later endured periods of foreign occupation and natural disasters. It became the national capital in 1862 and boomed after 1881, earning the nickname "Paris of the East" for its cosmopolitanism. The city suffered heavy damage in World War II. Under communist rule, particularly Nicolae Ceaușescu's systematization program, large historic quarters were demolished for socialist-style developments like the Palace of the Parliament. Since the 1989 revolution, Bucharest has undergone modernization and restoration.

### Geography and Climate
Bucharest lies on the Dâmbovița River in the Romanian Plain, traditionally considered to be built on seven hills. The city has an area of 226 km², with a humid continental climate featuring hot summers and cold, windy winters. Notable geographical features include several lakes in the north and the Văcărești Nature Park, a protected wetland dubbed the "Delta of Bucharest."

### Government and Administration
Bucharest has a unique administrative status, not part of any county. It is governed by a General Municipality, headed by a general mayor, and divided into six numbered sectors, each with its own mayor and council. The city is also part of the București–Ilfov development region for EU statistical purposes. Its judicial system includes sectoral courts, a municipal tribunal, and houses Romania's supreme and constitutional courts.

### Demographics and Economy
With a 2021 census population of 1,716,961, Bucharest is Romania's most populous city, though its population has declined slightly due to suburbanization. The city is the engine of the Romanian economy, generating about 24% of the national GDP. Its economy is centred on industry and services, with a strong presence in IT, finance, and retail. The Bucharest-Ilfov region has a GDP per capita significantly above the EU average.

### Transport
Bucharest is a major transport hub. Its public transport system includes the Bucharest Metro (five lines) and an extensive surface network of buses, trams, and trolleybuses. The main railway station, Gara de Nord, connects to national and international destinations. The city is served by two airports: Henri Coandă International (the busiest) and Aurel Vlaicu. A network of boulevards and ring roads handles road traffic, though congestion is a significant issue.

### Culture and Architecture
The city's architecture is a mix of historical styles, communist-era blocks, and contemporary buildings. Key landmarks include the Palace of the Parliament (the world's largest parliament building), the Romanian Athenaeum, and the Arcul de Triumf. Bucharest has a vibrant cultural scene with numerous museums, theatres, and music venues. It hosts major festivals like the George Enescu Festival. The city is also the seat of the Romanian Orthodox Patriarch and home to diverse religious communities.

## Terms
- **Systematization**: A communist-era urban planning policy involving the demolition of historic structures to build standardized, high-density housing and civic buildings.
- **Phanariotes**: Greek Christian administrators appointed by the Ottoman Empire to rule Wallachia and other Danubian Principalities from the 18th century until 1821.
- **Centrul Civic**: The "Civic Centre" built in the 1980s under Ceaușescu, replacing a large part of Bucharest's historic centre with monumental socialist-style buildings.
- **Blocuri**: High-density communist-era apartment blocks that house a large portion of Bucharest's population.
- **Hunger Circuses**: Circular retail complexes built during the Ceaușescu era, many of which were later converted into modern shopping malls.
- **Văcărești Nature Park**: A protected urban wetland area in southern Bucharest, formed from an abandoned communist-era project.
- **Gara de Nord**: Bucharest's main and busiest railway station, a hub for national and international rail connections.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The origin of the city's name "București" remains unverified, with multiple competing legends and etymologies.
- The quality of life in Bucharest is a subject of concern, with international surveys ranking it low among European capitals and local reports describing its urban management as "critical."
- The historical city centre's status as "endangered" highlights ongoing tensions between preservation and modern development.
- The impact of rapid suburbanization in surrounding Ilfov County on the city's infrastructure and social fabric is a continuing issue.
