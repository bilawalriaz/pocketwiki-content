# Ögedei Khan

## Overview
Ögedei Khan (c. 1186–1241) was the second Great Khan of the Mongol Empire, succeeding his father, Genghis Khan. He is pivotal for consolidating and massively expanding the empire across Asia and into Europe, while also establishing its first permanent capital and a more formalized bureaucracy. His reign marked the transition from a conquest-focused state to a more administratively complex empire.

## Timeline
- **c. 1186** — Born as the third son of Genghis Khan and Börte.
- **1219–1221** — Commands the siege of Urganch during the Mongol invasion of the Khwarazmian Empire.
- **1227** — Genghis Khan dies; Ögedei's brother Tolui acts as regent.
- **1229** — Elected Great Khan at a kurultai (assembly).
- **1230** — Launches a major campaign in Persia under General Chormaqan.
- **1231** — Initiates the Mongol invasions of Korea.
- **1234** — Completes the conquest of the Jin dynasty in northern China.
- **1235–1238** — Constructs the Mongol capital city, Karakorum.
- **1236–1242** — Mongol armies under Batu Khan and Subutai invade Europe, defeating Poland and Hungary.
- **1241** — Dies after a drinking bout; his death causes the Mongol armies in Europe to withdraw.

## Body

### Military Expansion
Ögedei continued his father's expansionist policies with relentless energy. In the Middle East, he sent General Chormaqan with 30,000–50,000 troops to subdue Persia and the Caucasus, completing the conquest of the Khwarazmian territories and subjugating Georgia and Armenia by 1240. In East Asia, he personally led campaigns to crush the Jin dynasty (1230–1234) and launched repeated invasions of Korea (beginning 1231), which ended with a temporary submission in 1241. His armies also skirmished with the Song dynasty and invaded the Indus Valley, besieging Lahore in 1241. Most famously, his reign saw the first major Mongol incursion into Europe (1236–1242), where Batu Khan's forces defeated the armies of Poland and Hungary. The Mongol advance halted in 1242, most likely due to Ögedei's death necessitating a return for the succession election, though logistical difficulties are also cited.

### Administration and Governance
Ögedei began the formal bureaucratization of the Mongol state. He worked with advisors from diverse backgrounds—including the Confucian official Yelü Chucai and the Muslim administrator Mahmud Yalavach—to develop systems of tax collection, regional administration, and trade. He established the *ortogh* system, where Mongol aristocrats invested in merchant partnerships. He divided the empire into administrative regions and created a post-road system (*Yam*) with relay stations every 25 miles. In 1234, he issued paper currency backed by silk reserves. He also codified laws, adding to his father's Great Yassa, and founded the capital city of Karakorum in the 1230s, which housed quarters for craftsmen and places of worship for Buddhists, Muslims, Taoists, and Christians.

### Character and Succession
Ögedei was described as charismatic, good-natured, intelligent, and pragmatic, preferring to listen to capable generals rather than act as a dictator. He was known for his alcoholism, which ultimately contributed to his death in 1241. Despite nominating his son Kuchu and later his grandson Shiremun as heirs, these designations were not binding. After a five-year regency by his widow Töregene, his son Güyük was elected khan in 1246, though the succession was contested by Batu Khan, leading to further instability.

## Terms
*   **Kurultai:** A traditional Mongol assembly of nobles and leaders convened to elect a new khan or decide major policies.
*   **Appanage:** A grant of territory or revenue given to a member of the ruling family.
*   **Great Yassa:** The secret legal code of the Mongol Empire established by Genghis Khan.
*   **Ortoq (ortogh):** A partnership system where Mongol nobles provided capital to merchants for trade ventures, sharing the profits.
*   **Yam:** The Mongol postal relay system, with stations providing fresh horses, food, and lodging for official messengers.
*   **Darughachi:** A Mongol governor or overseer appointed to administer a conquered territory.
*   **Paiza:** A tablet of authority given to Mongol officials, granting them the right to demand goods and services from civilians.
*   **Qubchuri:** A tax levied to supply goods for the imperial table and the *yam* system.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Cause of the European Withdrawal (1242):** The primary debate is whether the Mongol armies retreated from Europe solely because of Ögedei's death, as most sources indicate, or if logistical difficulties and the strength of European fortifications also played a significant role.
*   **The Oirat Incident:** The historicity of the account (from the Persian historian Juvayni) alleging that Ögedei ordered the mass rape of 4,000 Oirat girls is questioned. Scholars note it is the only such account and may be a later fabrication or misidentification, as other sources mention a forced requisition of women but not rape.
*   **Ögedei's Death:** While the standard account attributes his death to alcoholism after a drinking bout, an alternative anecdote from Juvayni suggests he died after a wolf he released turned on him, contradicting the more widely accepted story.
