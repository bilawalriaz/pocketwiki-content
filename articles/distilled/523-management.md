# Management

## Overview
Management is the administration of organizations—businesses, nonprofits, or government bodies—through the coordination of resources and human capital to achieve goals. It operates via a three-tier hierarchy: senior management sets strategy, middle management translates strategy into operations, and line management directs frontline employees. The discipline evolved from early industrial-era theories of efficiency into a formal academic field encompassing diverse branches like strategic, financial, and human resource management, increasingly informed by evidence-based practices.

## Timeline
- **1590s** — Term "management" enters English, meaning "act of managing by direction or manipulation."
- **1776** — Adam Smith publishes *The Wealth of Nations*, analyzing division of labor (pin factory: 200 to 48,000 pins/day).
- **Late 19th c.** — Salaried managers emerge as a distinct class; large corporations overshadow family businesses.
- **1911** — Frederick Winslow Taylor publishes *The Principles of Scientific Management*; J. Duncan writes first college management textbook.
- **1912** — Yoichi Ueno introduces Taylorism to Japan; becomes first consultant of "Japanese management style."
- **1916 (c.)** — Henri Fayol defines five functions of management: planning, organizing, commanding, coordinating, controlling.
- **1921** — Harvard Business School offers first Master of Business Administration (MBA) degree.
- **1946** — Peter Drucker publishes *Concept of the Corporation*, a foundational text of applied management.
- **1954** — Management by objectives systematized.
- **1984** — Theory of constraints introduced.
- **1986** — Six Sigma methodology developed.
- **2001** — Agile software development formally named, representing IT-driven management theory.

## Body

### Etymology and Definitions
The English verb *manage* derives from the 15th-century French *mesnager* (to hold the reins of a horse), Italian *maneggiare* (to handle tools or a horse), and Spanish *manejar*, all rooted in Latin *manus* (hand) and *agere* (to act). The noun "management" appeared in the 1590s; by 1739 it denoted a collective governing body (e.g., theater directors). Definitions vary: Henri Fayol defined management as forecasting, planning, organizing, commanding, coordinating, and controlling; Fredmund Malik as "the transformation of resources into utility"; Peter Drucker saw its basic task as marketing and innovation; Mary Parker Follett called it "the art of getting things done through people." Scholars note the difficulty of defining management without circularity ("management is what managers do") and debate whether the term equates solely to "business administration" or applies broadly to all organizations.

### Hierarchical Levels
Larger organizations typically employ a pyramid structure of three levels. **Senior management** (board of directors, CEO/president) sets strategic goals and policies, bearing fiduciary duties to shareholders; Fortune 500 directors spent ~4.4 hours/week on duties with median compensation of $212,512 (2010). **Middle management** (branch, regional, department managers) translates senior strategy into operational direction for frontline managers. **Line management** (supervisors, team leaders) oversees regular employees or volunteers, assigning tasks, ensuring quality/quantity, and channeling unresolved concerns upward; they are often considered part of the workforce rather than the management class.

### Training and Education
Management is taught globally at undergraduate (BBA, B.Com., B.P.A.) and graduate (MBA, specialized master’s) levels, housed in business schools or faculties of management. Higher education formalized managerial skills during the 20th-century "managerial revolution." Current good practices emphasize diversity, flexibility, retraining, "walking the shop floor," and securing "quick wins" (John Kotter’s "Short-Term Wins") for new managers. **Evidence-based management**—an emerging movement—bases decisions on three pillars: peer-reviewed research evidence, contextual practitioner judgment, and the preferences/values of those affected.

### Historical Development
**Early writing:** Adam Smith (1776) demonstrated division of labor’s productivity gains. **19th century:** Classical economists (Smith, John Stuart Mill) and innovators (Eli Whitney, James Watt, Matthew Boulton) developed standardization, quality control, cost accounting, and interchangeable parts; salaried managers rose to prominence as corporations displaced family firms. **20th century:** Personnel departments expanded rapidly (under 1 in 20 manufacturing firms had one in 1915; over one-third by 1929). Scientific management theories proliferated (Taylor, Gilbreths, Gantt). The Harvard MBA (1921) and comprehensive theories (Fayol, Alexander Church) standardized education. Sociological perspectives emerged (Weber’s bureaucracy, Mayo, Follett, Barnard). Operations research arose in the 1940s (Patrick Blackett). Later developments include management by objectives (1954), theory of constraints (1984), Six Sigma (1986), re-engineering (1990s), and agile (2001). **21st century:** Branches expanded to public administration, nonprofit management, and social entrepreneurship; workplace democracy (workers' self-management) gained advocacy.

### Nature of Work and Functions
In profitable organizations, management satisfies stakeholders: profit for shareholders, value for customers, employment for employees. In nonprofits, maintaining donor faith is paramount. Governance typically follows shareholder → board → senior management hierarchy. Per Fayol, management operates through five functions: **Planning** (deciding future actions), **Organizing** (allocating human/nonhuman resources), **Commanding/Leading** (directing people), **Coordinating** (structuring goal achievement), and **Controlling** (verifying progress against plans). Major branches include financial, human resource, cybernetics, IT, marketing, operations/production, and strategic management.

## Terms
- ****Senior management**** — Top-tier leaders (board, CEO) who set organizational strategy and policy; bear fiduciary duty.
- ****Middle management**** — Intermediary managers (branch, department heads) who translate strategy into operational directives.
- ****Line management**** — Frontline supervisors/team leaders who direct daily work of employees/volunteers.
- ****Scientific management**** — Early 20th-century theory (Taylor) applying scientific methods to optimize labor productivity.
- ****Evidence-based management**** — Decision-making informed by peer-reviewed research, practitioner judgment, and stakeholder values.
- ****Management by objectives (MBO)**** — Systematic approach (1954) aligning individual goals with organizational objectives.
- ****Six Sigma**** — Data-driven methodology (1986) for process improvement and defect reduction.
- ****Agile software development**** — Iterative, collaborative management framework for IT projects, formalized in 2001.
- ****Workers' self-management**** — Workplace democracy model distributing management functions among workers.
- ****Fayol’s five functions**** — Canonical management cycle: planning, organizing, commanding, coordinating, controlling.

## Debates and open questions
- **Definition scope:** Whether "management" equates to "business administration" (excluding nonprofits/public sector) or applies universally to all organized human activity.
- **Circularity problem:** The tautology "management is what managers do" reflects difficulty in defining the discipline independently of its practitioners.
- **Science vs. art:** Tension between early 20th-century attempts to place management on a "thoroughly scientific basis" (scientism) and views of management as philosophy or art (Follett).
- **Class and hierarchy:** Historical contrast of "management" vs. "labor"; contemporary debate over workplace democracy vs. command hierarchy.
- **Fad vs. rigor:** Proliferation of popularized systems (pop psychology) versus scientifically validated theories in late 20th century.
- **Ethical foundations:** Critiques from business ethics, critical management studies, and anti-corporate activism challenging core assumptions.
