# Hermitage Museum

## Overview
The State Hermitage Museum in Saint Petersburg, Russia, is one of the world's largest and most prestigious art and culture museums, holding the largest collection of paintings globally. Founded in 1764 as the private collection of Empress Catherine the Great, it evolved from the imperial family's secluded apartments into a public institution, symbolizing Russia's cultural ambition and its integration into European artistic traditions.

## Timeline
- **1764** — Empress Catherine the Great purchases 225 paintings from Berlin merchant Johann Ernst Gotzkowsky, founding the collection.
- **1764–1775** — Architect Yury Felten and Jean-Baptiste Vallin de la Mothe build the Small Hermitage extensions to house the growing collection.
- **1783–1787** — The Hermitage Theatre is built by Giacomo Quarenghi.
- **1787–1792** — Quarenghi constructs the Raphael Loggias wing.
- **1815** — Tsar Alexander I purchases 38 pictures from the heirs of Joséphine de Beauharnais.
- **1842–1851** — The New Hermitage, designed by Leo von Klenze, is constructed to house a public museum.
- **1852** — The New Hermitage opens to the public on 5 February.
- **1917** — After the October Revolution, the Imperial Hermitage and Winter Palace are merged as state museums.
- **1930–1934** — Over 2,000 artworks are sold abroad by the Soviet government.
- **1941–1944** — Collections are evacuated to Sverdlovsk during the Siege of Leningrad; the museum reopens in November 1944.
- **1948** — 316 Impressionist and modern works from nationalized collections are transferred to the Hermitage.
- **1994** — The museum reveals a secret trove of looted German Impressionist paintings.
- **2001–2008** — The Guggenheim Hermitage Museum operates in Las Vegas.
- **2004** — The Hermitage Amsterdam branch opens.

## Body

### Origins and Imperial Collection
The museum began as Catherine the Great's private collection, started in 1764 with paintings acquired from Gotzkowsky, which included works by Rembrandt, Rubens, and Hals. The "Hermitage" name referred to the Winter Palace's private apartments, a secluded refuge from court life. Catherine rapidly expanded the collection by purchasing major European collections, such as those of Heinrich von Brühl (1769), Pierre Crozat (1772), and Robert Walpole (1779). By her death, she had amassed over 4,000 paintings, establishing the Hermitage as a rival to Western European museums and a symbol of Russian enlightened patronage.

### 19th-Century Expansion and Public Opening
Under Tsars Alexander I and Nicholas I, the collection grew through acquisitions like the Joséphine de Beauharnais paintings (1815) and the Campana collection of antiquities (1861). Architect Leo von Klenze designed the New Hermitage building (1842–1851), which opened to the public in 1852. This period also saw the creation of major interior spaces, such as the Pavilion Hall, and the incorporation of collections like the Arsenal arms and armour (1885).

### Revolution, War, and Soviet Era
Following the 1917 Revolution, the imperial collections were nationalized and merged with the Winter Palace. The Soviet government sold over 2,000 artworks abroad between 1930 and 1934 to raise funds. During World War II, the collections were evacuated before the Siege of Leningrad, though the buildings were damaged. Post-war, the museum received Impressionist and modern art from nationalized private collections, such as those of Sergei Shchukin and Ivan Morozov.

### Modern Era and Global Presence
Since the 1990s, the Hermitage has expanded its physical footprint, adding the General Staff Building wing (1999) and opening domestic and international branches, including in Kazan (2005), Vyborg (2010), and formerly in Amsterdam (2004–2022) and Las Vegas (2001–2008). The museum has also addressed issues of provenance, revealing looted German artworks in 1994 and recovering stolen items in 2006. It remains a federal state property directed by Mikhail Piotrovsky since 1992.

## Terms
- **Hermitage**: Originally the private, secluded apartments of the Russian tsars in the Winter Palace, intended as a refuge from court obligations.
- **Numismatic collection**: A collection of coins, medals, and related objects, accounting for about one-third of the Hermitage's three million items.
- **Palace Embankment**: The waterfront area along the Neva River where the main Hermitage buildings are located.
- **Greek Revival**: An architectural style inspired by ancient Greek models, used by Leo von Klenze for the New Hermitage's ground floor interiors.
- **Lapidary**: The art of cutting or polishing precious stones, featured in 19th-century Russian works displayed in the museum.
- **Fauvists**: An early 20th-century group of artists (including Matisse) known for bold, non-naturalistic color, whose works are in the General Staff Building.
- **Soviet sale of Hermitage paintings**: The clandestine sale of over 2,000 artworks from the Hermitage collection abroad by the Soviet government between 1930 and 1934.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact number of paintings in Catherine's initial 1764 purchase from Gotzkowsky is debated, with sources listing either 225 or 317.
- The attribution of certain works, such as the *Benois Madonna* and *Madonna Litta*, is to Leonardo da Vinci or his school, not definitively to the master himself.
- The status and future of looted artworks held by the museum, such as the French Impressionist paintings revealed in 1994, remain a subject of international provenance research and potential claims.
