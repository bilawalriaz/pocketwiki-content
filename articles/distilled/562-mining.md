# Mining

## Overview
Mining is the extraction of valuable geological materials—such as metals, coal, gemstones, and industrial minerals—from the Earth. It is essential for obtaining resources that cannot be grown or synthesized artificially, underpinning modern infrastructure, energy systems, and technology. The industry has evolved from prehistoric surface gathering to a globalized sector dominated by multinational corporations employing advanced surface and underground techniques. However, mining generates significant environmental impacts, including habitat destruction, water contamination (notably acid mine drainage), and massive waste volumes (tailings). It also poses persistent occupational hazards and frequently drives social conflict, particularly regarding land rights, displacement of Indigenous communities, child labor, and the "resource curse" where mineral wealth correlates with poor economic development. As the global economy transitions from fossil fuels to renewable energy, demand for critical metals (copper, lithium, cobalt, nickel) is projected to skyrocket, intensifying pressure on ecosystems and vulnerable populations.

## Timeline
- **c. 41,000–43,000 BP** — Oldest known mine (Ngwenya Mine, Eswatini): Paleolithic humans mine hematite for ochre pigment.
- **c. 4000–3000 BC** — Neolithic flint mines operate at Grimes Graves (England) and Krzemionki (Poland).
- **2613–2494 BC** — Ancient Egypt dispatches expeditions to Wadi Maghareh for turquoise and copper; Nubian gold mines use fire-setting.
- **c. 500 BC** — Laurium silver mines (Greece) fund Athens; worked by >20,000 slaves using Bronze Age technology.
- **25 AD** — Romans develop sluicing/hydraulic mining in Spain (Las Médulas) using aqueducts for alluvial gold.
- **1465** — "Silver crisis" in Europe: shafts too deep for existing pump technology to dewater.
- **1627** — First recorded use of black powder (gunpowder) for rock blasting in Selmecbánya, Hungary.
- **1762** — First mining academy established in Selmecbánya (now Banská Štiavnica, Slovakia).
- **1872** — US General Mining Act encourages mining on federal lands; drives Westward Expansion.
- **1850s** — Australian gold rushes produce ~40% of world's gold; major mines (Mount Morgan, Broken Hill) established.
- **1906** — Courrières mine disaster (France) kills 1,099; Europe's worst mining accident.
- **1942** — Benxihu Colliery disaster (China) kills 1,549; world's deadliest mining accident.
- **1978** — US Mine Safety and Health Administration (MSHA) established; fatalities drop from 242 (1978) to 24 (2019).
- **1992** — World Bank pushes privatization of state mining via *The Strategy for African Mining*.
- **2007** — Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative (EITI) mainstreamed in World Bank partner countries.
- **2023** — Global coal extraction reaches 8.5 billion metric tons; projected metal demand (2022–2050) estimated at 7 billion tons.

## Body

### History of Mining
**Prehistory and Antiquity**  
The oldest archaeological mine is Ngwenya Mine (Eswatini), dated to 41,000–43,000 BP, where hematite was extracted for ochre. Neolithic flint mines (c. 4000–3000 BC) at Grimes Graves and Krzemionki used shafts and galleries. Ancient Egyptians mined malachite, turquoise, copper, and gold (Nubia) from the Old Kingdom onward, employing fire-setting and dry/wet washing for gold. In Europe, Laurium’s silver funded Athens (c. 500 BC), while Philip II of Macedon seized Mount Pangeo gold mines (357 BC) for coinage.

**Roman Innovations**  
Romans pioneered large-scale hydraulic mining. They built aqueducts to supply water for *hushing* (flushing overburden to expose veins), sluicing alluvial gold (Las Médulas, Spain), and powering machinery like reverse overshot water-wheels for dewatering (Rio Tinto, Spain). They followed veins underground using adits for drainage/ventilation and employed fire-setting with thermal quenching to fracture rock.

**Medieval Europe**  
Early Middle Ages focused on copper/iron via open pits. The 14th-century surge in armor/weapons demand spurred iron production. The 1465 silver crisis hit when pumps couldn't handle deeper floods. In England, a 1568 judicial decision and 1688 law restricted royal rights to gold/silver, enabling landlords to lease base metals/coal. German technicians and Dutch/English capital drove extraction (e.g., 4,000 foreigners at Keswick, 1642). Water mills powered crushing, hoisting, and ventilation. Black powder (1627) replaced fire-setting. Agricola’s *De re metallica* (1556) codified techniques, emphasizing water removal.

**Africa, Oceania, Americas**  
African iron metallurgy dates back >4,000 years; trans-Saharan gold trade (7th–14th c.) linked West Africa to Mediterranean. Post-19th century Great Trek, South African gold/diamonds drove politics. DRC leads African diamond production (12M carats, 2019). In Oceania, Australian/NZ gold/coal began 19th c.; Fiji’s Vatukoula boom (1934–39) raised output 100-fold. Prehistoric Americans mined Lake Superior copper (5,000+ BP) for continental trade. Spanish colonial extraction focused on Central/South American gold/silver. US mining expanded 19th c. (California Gold Rush, 1872 Mining Act), founding cities like Denver/Sacramento.

**Modernity**  
Early 20th c. US rushes (Montana, Utah, Arizona, Alaska) supplied copper for electrification. Canada lagged due to transport/capital limits. Australian gold rushes (1850s) yielded 40% world output; 1960s boom revived sector. 21st c. sees multinational dominance. Energy transition drives critical metal demand: 7B tons metals needed 2022–2050 (5B steel, 950M Al, 650M Cu, 170M graphite, 100M Ni). Metal extraction energy may soon surpass coal mining.

### Mine Development and Life Cycle
The cycle: **prospecting/exploration** → **resource estimation** → **pre-feasibility study** (theoretical economics) → **feasibility study** (financial/technical viability, commodity price risk) → **mine planning** (economically recoverable reserve, metallurgy, infrastructure, reclamation bonding) → **development** (access, plants, equipment) → **production** (while economical) → **reclamation** (restoration). Waste rock (often > ore volume) removal is a major cost; characterization is essential. Human factors (dust, noise, radiation, vibration, community psychology) are increasingly recognized as critical to success (Frank T. M. White’s "total environment of mining").

### Techniques
**Classification**  
- **Surface mining** (85% of US minerals, 98% metallic ores): open-pit, quarrying (sand/stone/clay), strip mining, mountaintop removal (coal), landfill mining.  
- **Sub-surface (underground)**: drift (horizontal), slope (diagonal), shaft (vertical). Methods: shrinkage stope (upward), longwall (mechanized shear), room-and-pillar (leaving roof supports), retreat mining (pulling pillars), block/sub-level caving.  
- **In-situ leaching**: dissolving soluble minerals (potash, uranium, copper) via injected fluids (acid/carbonate) without excavation.

**Explosives**  
Ammonium nitrate is most common. US used 22.3B kg (1990–99): 66.4% coal, 13.5% nonmetal, 10.4% metal. Queensland (1870–1920) accidents drove safety reforms.

**Specialized Surface Methods**  
- **High-wall mining**: remote cutter extracts coal from final pit wall where overburden is too deep; cycles sumping (undercut) and shearing.  
- **Placer deposits** (shallow, unconsolidated): mined via surface methods (sluices, trommels).

### Machines
Heavy machinery for exploration, overburden removal (bulldozers, trucks, shovels, draglines), rock breaking (drills, explosives), haulage (trams, lifts, huge trucks), and processing (crushers, mills, reactors, roasters). Placer mining uses hopper/trommel/sluice/jig gravity separation.

### Processing
**Mineral processing (dressing)**: mechanical crushing, grinding, washing to separate valuables from gangue. Placer: gravity (sluice boxes). Lode: crushing/pulverizing → mechanical/chemical separation.  
**Extractive metallurgy**: chemical reduction of oxides/sulfides to metal. Smelting (heat/reducing agent) or electrolytic reduction (aluminium).  
**Innovation**: Notre Dame (2018) developed container molecules to extract gold via chloroauric acid/solvent, eliminating water stripping and toxic sodium cyanide (used 125+ years); applicable to platinum, palladium, urban mining (e-waste/wastewater).

### Industry Structure and Economics
**Geography**: London (Anglo American HQ), Melbourne (BHP, Rio Tinto HQ). US dominated by coal/nonmetals. Africa: 40% gold, 60% cobalt, 90% PGM reserves. India significant. China: 95% rare earth production (2013).  
**Corporate Tiers**: Majors (>$500M revenue, self-finance major mines), Intermediates ($50–500M), Juniors (<$50M, equity-funded exploration). Canadian TSX Venture Exchange specializes in juniors.  
**World Bank Role**: Since 1955, ~$2B to 50 projects (reform, greenfield, processing). Pushed privatization (1992 *Strategy for African Mining*); CVRD privatized 1997. Codes promote foreign investment via tax holidays but criticized (Univ. Quebec) for falling "very short of permitting sustainable development"—the **resource curse**.  
**EITI (2007)**: Voluntary revenue transparency standard (govts/companies/civil society). Issues: artisanal mining inclusion, non-cash payments, revenue volatility, enforcement gaps.  
**Commodity Cycles**: 2000s supercycle drove aggressive mining; gold price spike increased Amazon deforestation 6x (2003–06 vs 2006–09) via artisanal mining.

### Safety
**Historical Disasters**: Courrières (1,099, 1906), Benxihu (1,549, 1942).  
**Modern Stats**: China officially ~5,000 deaths/yr (unofficial up to 20,000). US MSHA (1978) cut fatalities 242→24 (2019).  
**Hazards**: Rockdust → silicosis, asbestosis, pneumoconiosis. Gases → asphyxiation/explosion. Noise: NIOSH REL 85 dBA, MSHA PEL 90 dBA; 25% of exposed workers have hearing loss. Noisiest: stageloaders (84–102 dBA), auxiliary fans (84–120 dBA), roof bolters (92–103 dBA). Coal mines highest hearing loss risk.  
**Developing World**: DRC: artisanal cobalt miners (90% of output) work "medieval conditions" for $2/day; sulfuric acid exposure, contaminated water, schistosomiasis 27% (vs <13% general). South Africa: silica dust → TB >1,000/100k; crowded hostels drive recurrence (8/100 person-yrs).

### Environmental Effects
**Water Pollution**: Acid mine drainage (AMD) from sulfide oxidation releases arsenic, lead, mercury. Tailings dam failures (Marcopper 2M tons, Barrick 1M L cyanide 2015, Talvivaara Finland ecological collapse) and chronic runoff contaminate rivers/groundwater for decades post-closure. DRC cobalt: river water shows high Pb, Cr, Co, industrial acids; dark color, sludge.  
**Waste**: Tailings ratios: 99:1 (Cu), 200,000:1 (Au, 5.3 g/t). 3,500 impoundments (2000); 2–5 major failures/yr. Subaqueous disposal (STD) used in developing world; illegal in US/Canada. Waste classified sterile/mineralized/acid-generating; near-grade stockpiled for future. Abandoned mines: long-term AMD, biomagnification in food chains.  
**Land**: Habitat loss, soil contamination, cultural heritage destruction.

### Regulation and Enforcement
**Standards**: Strong regimes require EIA, management plans, closure bonds, monitoring. Developing nations often lack enforcement → "pollution havens."  
**Financial Levers**: Equator Principles, IFC standards, socially responsible investing criteria. Industry self-regulation via ICMM (formed from Global Mining Initiative, 1990s). ISO 14001 certifies environmental management (criticized as low rigor).  
**Land Rights**: Mineral rights often state-owned; surface rights private/customary. Communities lack control over subsurface, face displacement/inadequate compensation.  
**Enforcement Gaps**: South Africa (strong laws, weak enforcement/corruption). DRC (>50% exports unrecorded, 90% artisanal). Ghana (inadequate monitoring).

### Human Rights and Environmental Justice
**Environmental Justice**: Fair distribution of benefits/burdens; involvement in decisions. Mining impacts disproportionately hit low-income, Indigenous, marginalized groups (toxic exposure, displacement, weak legal recourse). Water stress: 25% world population faces "extremely high" stress; mining exacerbates scarcity.

**DRC Cobalt Crisis**: Artisanal/small-scale mining (ASM): hazardous conditions, tunnel collapses, child labor, $1–2/day. Kara (2023): wall collapses bury miners; clinics lack painkillers/antibiotics/x-rays. Nov 2024: makeshift bridge collapse kills 32. Families send children to mines to survive ("eat or school"). Protests repressed (deaths, evictions). Supply chain transparency initiatives (OECD due diligence) emerging.

**Child Labor**: DRC, South Africa, Philippines, Australia, Brazil. Children carry 25 kg sacks for food/shelter. Lawsuits vs Apple, Google, Microsoft, Tesla, Glencore (2019, 14 Congolese families: deaths, broken spines, forced labor).

**Indigenous Peoples**: Global Witness: ~1/3 of 227 environmental defenders killed (2020) were Indigenous (mining/logging/agribusiness/dams).  
- **DRC**: Forced evictions for industrial Cu/Co; compensation as low as $300. Govt complicity in trafficking/forced labor/sexual violence.  
- **South Africa**: 107,714 unpaid compensable claims (2017); Kabwe (Zambia) class action vs Anglo American for lead poisoning.  
- **Australia**: Bininj oppose sacred site damage; Indigenous Australians "not shared bounty equitably."  
- **Philippines**: Ifugao opposition led to Mountain Province mining ban; ancestral land rights ignored.  
- **Brazil**: 170+ tribes march against land rights rollback; UN urges Supreme Court to uphold rights. Gold mining: "waterbed effect" (displacement), need stronger gold trade laws.  
- **Uranium/US**: Navajo: sheep/plant contamination threatens ceremonies/diet; bioconcentration in food chain. 1987: ~50% Native Americans near uncontrolled toxic sites. "Toxic colonialism" targets high-poverty lands.  
- **Legal Gaps**: Zimbabwe Mines Act excludes communal land compensation. DRC child labor laws unenforced; US conflict-mineral law correlated with *increased* armed incidents at mines.

### Metal Reserves and Recycling
20th c. diversified metal use; China/India development + tech drive demand. Stocks shifting above ground (in-use). US per capita copper: 73 kg (1932) → 238 kg (1999). Recycling saves 95% energy for Al. But rates low: UNEP (2010) warns rare metals (mobile phones, EV batteries, fuel cells) face scarcity without dramatic recycling increases. Landfills now higher concentration than mines for Al (cans) and precious metals (e-waste); £360M recoverable from 4 sites (Cranfield study); 20 MJ/kg energy in waste. First landfill mine Tel Aviv (1953); little follow-up due to cheap primary ores.

## Terms
- ****Ore**** — A rock or mineral containing a valuable constituent that can be extracted and sold for profit.
- ****Gangue**** — Waste material surrounding the valuable mineral in an ore deposit.
- ****Tailings**** — Fine-grained waste slurry produced by ore milling; often stored in impoundments (dams).
- ****Acid Mine Drainage (AMD)**** — Acidic, metal-laden runoff formed when sulfide minerals are exposed to air and water; persists decades after closure.
- ****Placer Deposit**** — Valuable minerals concentrated in unconsolidated sediments (river gravels, beach sands) by gravity separation.
- ****Lode Deposit**** — Valuable minerals embedded in solid rock (veins, layers, disseminated grains).
- ****In-situ Leaching**** — Extraction by injecting solvents (water, acid, carbonate) to dissolve target minerals underground, then pumping solution to surface.
- ****Hushing**** — Roman hydraulic method: releasing a flood of water from reservoirs to strip overburden and expose ore veins.
- ****Fire-setting**** — Pre-explosive technique: heating rock face then quenching with water to induce thermal shock fracturing.
- ****Artisanal and Small-scale Mining (ASM)**** — Informal, labor-intensive mining by individuals/groups with minimal mechanization; often unregulated, high risk.
- ****Resource Curse**** — Paradox where countries with abundant natural resources exhibit slower economic growth and worse development outcomes.
- ****Environmental Justice**** — Fair distribution of environmental benefits/burdens and meaningful involvement of all communities in decisions.
- ****Biomagnification**** — Increasing concentration of contaminants (e.g., heavy metals) at higher trophic levels of a food chain.

## Debates and Open Questions
1. **Energy Transition vs. Extraction Impacts**: Projected 7B tons metal demand (2022–2050) for decarbonization will increase mining energy expenditure beyond coal; can "sustainable extraction" and circular economy (recycling, urban mining) scale fast enough to avoid replicating fossil fuel harms?
