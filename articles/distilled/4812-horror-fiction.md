# Horror fiction

## Overview
Horror is a genre of speculative fiction intended to disturb, frighten, or scare an audience. It is often divided into psychological and supernatural sub-genres. The threats in horror fiction frequently reflect broader cultural anxieties, making it a lens for societal fears.

## Timeline
- **Ancient** — Horror roots appear in folklore, religious texts (e.g., Book of Revelation), and classical tales (e.g., Pliny the Younger's ghost story).
- **Medieval** — Werewolf stories popular in French literature (e.g., Marie de France's "Bisclavret").
- **15th C.** — Cruel historical figures like Vlad III and Elizabeth Bathory inspire later vampire fiction.
- **1764** — Horace Walpole publishes *The Castle of Otranto*, founding the Gothic horror genre.
- **1818** — Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein* is published, a seminal work of science fiction and horror.
- **1897** — Bram Stoker's *Dracula* is published, creating an enduring horror icon.
- **Early 20th C.** — H. P. Lovecraft develops cosmic horror; specialist magazines like *Weird Tales* emerge.
- **1950s** — EC Comics' horror titles (e.g., *Tales From The Crypt*) satisfy demand for graphic imagery.
- **1959** — Robert Bloch's *Psycho*, inspired by real murders, becomes influential.
- **Late 1960s-70s** — A "horror boom" in publishing is triggered by novels like *Rosemary's Baby* and *The Exorcist*.
- **1970s-80s** — Stephen King becomes a dominant popular horror author.
- **21st C.** — Horror blends with other genres (e.g., *Pride and Prejudice and Zombies*) and expands into online and children's media.

## Body

### Origins and Gothic Foundations
Horror has ancient origins in folklore and religious traditions about death, evil, and the demonic. Early examples include classical ghost stories and medieval werewolf tales. The modern genre coalesced in the 18th century with the Gothic novel, pioneered by Horace Walpole's *The Castle of Otranto* (1764). This tradition, often featuring a woman menaced in a gloomy castle, was developed by writers like Ann Radcliffe and Matthew Lewis.

### 19th-Century Blossoming
The 19th century saw the Gothic tradition evolve into modern horror literature. Key works established enduring icons: Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein* (1818), John Polidori's "The Vampyre" (1819), and Bram Stoker's *Dracula* (1897). Authors like Edgar Allan Poe and Sheridan Le Fanu further defined the genre's psychological and supernatural elements.

### 20th-Century Proliferation and Popularization
The 20th century saw horror expand through cheap periodicals and specialist magazines like *Weird Tales*. H. P. Lovecraft popularized cosmic horror. The serial murderer became a recurring theme, influenced by real-life figures and later inspiring works like *Psycho* (1959). A major "horror boom" in publishing occurred in the late 1960s and 1970s, fueled by bestsellers like *The Exorcist*. Stephen King emerged as a dominant popular author. Horror also became a major force in cinema, from early films to 1970s slashers.

### 21st-Century Expansion
Contemporary horror often blends with other genres, such as urban fantasy and historical fiction. It has expanded into online publishing and "children's horror" (e.g., *Goosebumps*), though the impact on young audiences is under-researched. The genre continues to reflect modern anxieties.

### Characteristics and Theory
Horror provokes fear, disgust, or psychological disturbance. H. P. Lovecraft famously linked it to the "fear of the unknown." Theorist Noël Carroll posits that a horror "monster" must be both threatening and "impure," violating cultural categories. Scholars like Ann Radcliffe distinguish "terror" (dread before an event) from "horror" (revulsion after). Horror is theorized to provide a controlled thrill, allowing audiences to confront uncomfortable ideas and exercise caution.

## Terms
- **Gothic horror:** A sub-genre originating in the 18th century, often featuring gloomy settings, mystery, and supernatural elements.
- **Cosmic horror:** A sub-genre, associated with H. P. Lovecraft, emphasizing the terror of vast, unknowable, and indifferent cosmic forces.
- **Psychological horror:** A sub-genre that relies on mental and emotional states to create fear and unease.
- **Supernatural horror:** A sub-genre involving phenomena beyond scientific understanding, such as ghosts or demons.
- **Slasher film:** A horror film sub-genre typically featuring a killer murdering a group of people, often with a bladed weapon.
- **Splatter film:** A sub-genre of horror film focused on graphic violence and gore.
- **Numinous:** A concept from theologian Rudolf Otto, used in horror scholarship to describe an overwhelming, awe-inspiring, and terrifying religious or supernatural experience.
- **Terror vs. Horror:** A distinction made by Ann Radcliffe; terror is the dread felt before an event, while horror is the revulsion felt after.

## Debates and open questions
- **Why do people enjoy horror?** Theories include the "excitement" of a controlled thrill, the personality trait of "openness to experience," and the value of confronting uncomfortable ideas.
- **Metaphorical interpretations:** Horror works are often analyzed as metaphors for societal issues. For example, *Dracula* has been interpreted as a metaphor for repressed sexuality, corrupt aristocracy, or antisemitism.
- **Impact on children:** The appeal of "children's horror" and its internalized impact on young audiences is noted as under-researched and inconclusive.
