# Sultanate of Rum

## Overview
The Sultanate of Rum was a Turco-Persian, Sunni Muslim state founded by Seljuk Turks in Anatolia after the Battle of Manzikert (1071). It marks the beginning of Turkish history in Anatolia, serving as a bridge between Byzantine and Islamic cultures. The sultanate reached its peak in the 13th century before being weakened by the Crusades and ultimately destroyed by the Mongol invasion, leading to its fragmentation into Anatolian beyliks, including the Ottoman dynasty.

## Timeline
- **1071** — Battle of Manzikert; Seljuk Turks enter Anatolia.
- **1077** — Sultanate of Rum established by Suleiman ibn Qutalmish, seceding from the Seljuk Empire.
- **1097** — First Crusade defeats Kilij Arslan I, driving the sultanate to its capital at Konya.
- **1176** — Battle of Myriokephalon; Kilij Arslan II defeats Byzantine Emperor Manuel I.
- **1207** — Capture of Antalya by Kaykhusraw I, securing a key Mediterranean port.
- **1214** — Kaykaus I captures Sinop and makes the Empire of Trebizond a vassal.
- **1243** — Battle of Köse Dağ; Mongols defeat the Seljuks, making them vassals of the Ilkhanate.
- **1260** — Mongol victory at Aksaray ends tripartite rule; Anatolia fully subject to Möngke Khan.
- **1277** — Mamluk Sultan Baibars briefly defeats Mongols in Anatolia.
- **1308** — Last sultan, Mesud II, is murdered, ending the dynasty.

## Body

### Establishment and Early Conflicts
The sultanate was founded by Suleiman ibn Qutalmish, who gained control of Nicaea and other Byzantine cities in the 1070s. After his death, his son Kilij Arslan I established the state in south-central Anatolia with Konya as its capital. The sultanate faced the First Crusade (1096-99) and subsequent Crusades, with Seljuk rulers like Kilij Arslan I and Mesud I successfully defending their territories and expanding control over central Anatolia.

### Height of Power and Cultural Synthesis
Under sultans like Kaykhusraw I, Kaykaus I, and Kayqubad I (early 13th century), the sultanate reached its zenith. It captured key ports on the Mediterranean (Antalya, 1207) and Black Sea (Sinop, 1214) coasts, developed trade via caravanserai, and absorbed neighboring Turkish states. Culturally, a Turco-Persian synthesis emerged, with Persian becoming the administrative and literary language, while the military and nomadic populations used Turkic. The sultans adopted Persian names and patronized Persian art and architecture, creating a "second Iran" in Anatolia.

### Mongol Conquest and Decline
The sultanate was severely weakened by the Mongol invasion. After the 1243 Battle of Köse Dağ, it became an Ilkhanate vassal. Internal divisions followed Kaykhusraw II's death in 1246, with rule split among his sons. Mongol control tightened after 1256. The state fragmented into small beyliks (principalities) as central authority collapsed. The last sultan, Mesud II, was murdered in 1308, ending the dynasty, though Seljuk coins remained in use into the 14th century.

### Architecture and Society
The sultans sponsored Sunni Islamic architecture, building mosques, madrasas, and caravanserais (roadside inns) that blended Persian, Byzantine, and Armenian styles. These structures, like the Sultan Han caravanserai, facilitated trade and projected power. Society was multi-ethnic, including Greeks, Armenians, and Turks. The ruling elite employed ghulams (slave-soldiers from non-Muslim communities), a practice later adopted by the Ottomans.

## Terms
- **Beylik**: A Turkish principality or state, especially those that emerged after the Sultanate of Rum's collapse.
- **Caravanserai**: A roadside inn or trading post for caravans, providing shelter and defense.
- **Ghulam (pl. ghilmān)**: A slave-soldier, typically taken as a child from non-Muslim communities, who served in the military and court.
- **Ilkhanate**: The Mongol state that ruled Persia and parts of the Middle East after the Mongol conquests.
- **Madrasa**: An Islamic educational institution, often attached to a mosque.
- **Rum**: A term derived from "Rome," used by Seljuks to refer to Anatolia and its Byzantine-derived population.
- **Tımar**: A system of land grants in exchange for military service, inherited from Persian statecraft.
- **Turco-Persian**: A cultural synthesis blending Turkic and Persian elements in language, administration, and art.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent of Byzantine sabotage during the Second Crusade is debated; while some sources accuse Emperor Manuel I of hindering Crusaders, this is likely a fabrication by Western chroniclers.
- The precise role and integration of the native Byzantine Greek aristocracy within the Seljuk nobility and harem is a subject of scholarly interest.
- The degree to which the sultanate's cultural "Persianization" versus its Turkic military identity defined its society remains an area of study.
