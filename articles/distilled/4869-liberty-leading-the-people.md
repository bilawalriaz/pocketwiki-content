# Liberty Leading the People

## Overview
*Liberty Leading the People* is an 1830 oil painting by Eugène Delacroix that commemorates the July Revolution, which overthrew King Charles X. It is a defining work of the Romantic era, symbolizing the fight for freedom and the French Republic. The painting is celebrated for its powerful allegory and has become an enduring icon of revolution and national identity.

## Timeline
- **Autumn 1830** — Delacroix paints *Liberty Leading the People*.
- **1831** — First exhibited at the official Paris Salon.
- **1831** — Purchased by the French government for 3,000 francs.
- **c. 1832** — Removed from public display after the June Rebellion.
- **1848** — Briefly exhibited after the establishment of the Second Republic.
- **1855** — Exhibited again during the Second Empire.
- **1874** — Acquired by the Third Republic for the Louvre's collection.
- **1974–75** — Featured in a major touring exhibition in the U.S.
- **1999** — Transported to Tokyo via a specially equipped Airbus Beluga.
- **2012** — Moved to the Louvre-Lens museum.
- **2013** — Vandalized with an inscription; quickly restored.
- **2024** — Returns to display at the Louvre after a major restoration.

## Body

### Creation and Symbolism
Delacroix, a leading figure of French Romanticism, painted this work in the autumn of 1830. He described it as a "modern subject—a barricade," aiming to "paint for" his country. The central figure is a bare-breasted woman of the people, an allegorical goddess of Liberty who also personifies France as Marianne. She wears a Phrygian cap, a symbol of liberty from the 1789 Revolution. She leads a diverse group of fighters—bourgeoisie, students, and workers—over a barricade of fallen bodies, holding the tricolour flag. The painting is often mistakenly thought to depict the 1789 Revolution.

### Purchase and Controversial History
The French government bought the painting in 1831 to remind the new "citizen-king" Louis-Philippe of the revolution that brought him to power. However, its inflammatory political message led to its removal from public display after the June Rebellion of 1832. It was hidden for years, only being exhibited intermittently under later regimes. The Third Republic finally acquired it for the Louvre in 1874.

### Modern Conservation and Legacy
The painting has had a significant cultural legacy, influencing works like Victor Hugo's *Les Misérables* and the Statue of Liberty. In 2012, it was moved to the Louvre-Lens, where it was vandalized in 2013 but quickly restored. A major restoration in 2023–2024, involving on-site work due to its large size (2.99m x 3.62m), revealed hidden details under layers of varnish, including the original light grey color of Liberty's dress and a boot in the foreground.

## Terms
- **Romanticism**: An artistic and intellectual movement emphasizing emotion, individualism, and the glorification of the past and nature, reacting against Enlightenment rationalism.
- **Allegory**: A story, poem, or picture that can be interpreted to reveal a hidden meaning, typically a moral or political one.
- **Phrygian cap**: A soft, conical cap, historically associated with freed slaves in Rome, which became a symbol of liberty during the French Revolution.
- **Marianne**: A national personification of the French Republic, representing liberty, reason, and the nation itself.
- **Tricolour**: The French national flag with three vertical bands of blue, white, and red.
- **July Revolution**: The 1830 uprising that overthrew King Charles X and led to the reign of Louis-Philippe.
- **Paris Salon**: The official art exhibition of the Académie des Beaux-Arts in Paris, the most important art event in 19th-century France.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The identity of the man in the top hat is widely debated. While once thought to be a self-portrait of Delacroix, modern art historians refute this. Other suggestions include theatre director Étienne Arago or curator Frédéric Villot, but there is no consensus.
- The painting's exact political message has been interpreted differently over time. It was initially seen as a reminder to the new king, then as too revolutionary for public display, and later as a symbol of the Republic.
