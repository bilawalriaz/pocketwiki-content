# Chemical compound

## Overview
A chemical compound is a substance composed of identical molecules containing atoms from two or more different chemical elements bonded together in a fixed stoichiometric ratio. Unlike mixtures or pure elements (which may exist as diatomic or polyatomic molecules like O₂ or S₈), compounds have unique chemical structures and properties distinct from their constituent elements. They are classified into four major types—molecular, ionic, intermetallic, and coordination complexes—based on the bonding mechanism (covalent, ionic, metallic, or coordinate covalent bonds). Compounds can be transformed into new substances via chemical reactions that break and reform atomic bonds, and over 350,000 are registered globally with unique CAS identifiers.

## Timeline
- **1661** — Robert Boyle publishes *The Sceptical Chymist*, using "compound" in a modern sense and proposing "corpuscles" combine in various proportions to form diverse substances.
- **1724** — Isaac Watts publishes *Logick*, defining elements as simple substances that cannot be resolved into different kinds, and compounds as made of two or more simple substances.

## Body

### Historical Development of the Concept
Robert Boyle’s 1661 work *The Sceptical Chymist* marks the first use of "compound" with a meaning approximating the modern definition. Boyle distinguished "perfectly mixt bodies" (compounds) from elements, demonstrating that mercury could be recovered unchanged from reactions with aqua fortis (nitric acid) and sulfur, proving the components retain their identity in the compound. He theorized that "corpuscles" (atoms) of elements possess specific sizes and shapes, allowing them to combine in myriad proportions and connections to form an "almost incredible number" of distinct concretes (compounds). Over half a century later, Isaac Watts formalized the logical distinction in *Logick* (1724): elements are simple substances irreducible to other kinds, while compounds consist of two or more simple substances. Watts explicitly rejected the Aristotelian four elements and the Paracelsian three principles (salt, sulfur, mercury) in favor of experimentally derived definitions.

### Definitions and Composition
A chemical compound requires two or more different atom types in a fixed stoichiometric proportion, expressed by a chemical formula (e.g., H₂O for water). This fixed ratio allows conversion via chemical reaction into substances with fewer atoms. While pure elements like diatomic hydrogen (H₂) or octatomic sulfur (S₈) contain multiple atoms, they are not compounds because they lack different element types. Most compounds possess a unique CAS registry number. A significant boundary case involves **non-stoichiometric compounds** (e.g., palladium hydride PdHₓ, where 0.02 < x < 0.58), which exhibit reproducible but non-integral atomic ratios. Many crystalline solids like silicate minerals are often called non-stoichiometric compounds, though their variable composition often stems from trapped foreign elements or structural defects rather than true variable bonding; these substances constitute most of Earth's crust and mantle. Isotopic variation can also alter mass ratios without changing chemical identity.

### Types of Compounds
**Molecular compounds** are electrically neutral groups of atoms (molecules) held by **covalent bonds** (shared electrons). They are **heteronuclear** (different elements, e.g., H₂O) as opposed to homonuclear elemental molecules (e.g., O₂). A molecule is the smallest unit retaining the substance's properties. **Ionic compounds** consist of **cations** (positive ions) and **anions** (negative ions) held by **ionic bonds** (electrostatic forces). They form continuous three-dimensional crystalline networks rather than discrete molecules; examples include sodium chloride (Na⁺Cl⁻) and ammonium carbonate (NH₄⁺CO₃²⁻). Ionic compounds are typically hard, brittle, high-melting solids that insulate when solid but conduct electricity when molten or dissolved due to mobile ions. Those containing hydroxide (OH⁻) or oxide (O²⁻) are bases; others are salts. **Intermetallic compounds** are ordered solid-state alloys of two or more metals with metallic bonding, generally hard, brittle, and possessing good high-temperature strength. **Coordination complexes** feature a central metal **coordination centre** surrounded by bound **ligands** (molecules or ions) via **coordinate covalent bonds**; many transition metal compounds fall into this category.

### Bonding and Forces
Compound stability arises from several interaction types. **Covalent bonds** involve electron sharing between atoms of similar electronegativity (often nonmetals near each other on the periodic table), allowing both to achieve stable electron octets. **Ionic bonds** result from complete valence electron transfer from a metal (electron donor, forming a cation) to a nonmetal (electron acceptor, forming an anion), creating electrostatic attraction. **Metallic bonds** (in intermetallics) involve a delocalized "sea" of electrons. **Coordinate covalent bonds** (in complexes) occur when a ligand donates both electrons for a bond to the metal center. **Hydrogen bonding** is a strong dipole-dipole interaction between a hydrogen atom bonded to an electronegative atom (N, O, F) and another electronegative atom. **London dispersion forces** (a component of Van der Waals forces) are weak, temporary dipoles induced by electron fluctuations; they condense nonpolar substances into liquids and solids at low temperatures.

### Chemical Reactions
Compounds transform into new substances via chemical reactions involving bond breaking and reformation. A schematic double displacement reaction illustrates this: AB + CD → AD + CB. Here, bonds in reactants AB and CD break, and new bonds form to create products AD and CB. This process may involve interactions with other substances, energy changes, and the rearrangement of atoms into new fixed stoichiometric ratios.

## Terms
- ****Chemical compound**** — A substance of identical molecules containing atoms from two or more elements in a fixed ratio, held by chemical bonds.
- ****Stoichiometric proportion**** — A fixed, whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound (e.g., 2:1 in H₂O).
- ****Covalent bond**** — A bond formed by sharing electron pairs between atoms of similar electronegativity.
- ****Ionic bond**** — Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions formed by complete electron transfer.
- ****Cation / Anion**** — Positively charged ion (electron loser) / Negatively charged ion (electron gainer).
- ****Coordinate covalent bond**** — A covalent bond where both shared electrons originate from one atom (the ligand).
- ****Non-stoichiometric compound**** — A compound with a reproducible but non-integral, variable atomic ratio (e.g., PdHₓ).
- ****CAS number**** — A unique numerical identifier assigned to a chemical substance by the Chemical Abstracts Service.
- ****Ligand**** — An ion or molecule that binds to a central metal atom to form a coordination complex.
- ****London dispersion forces**** — Weak, temporary intermolecular forces caused by instantaneous electron dipoles.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Classification of non-stoichiometric substances:** Whether variable-composition solids (e.g., silicate minerals, palladium hydride) are true "chemical compounds" or merely "chemical substances" related to compounds via structural defects or trapped impurities.
*   **Nomenclature consistency:** Inconsistent usage differentiates "substances" (including non-stoichiometric examples) from "compounds" (requiring fixed ratios), leading to ambiguity in classifying crystalline materials that lack simple formulae.
