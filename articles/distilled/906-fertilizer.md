# Fertilizer

## Overview
Fertilizers are materials—natural or synthetic—applied to soil or plant tissues to supply essential nutrients for plant growth. They are central to modern agriculture, providing the macronutrients nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K), along with various micronutrients. The shift from organic sources like compost and manure to synthetic fertilizers, particularly after the development of the Haber-Bosch process in the early 20th century, enabled massive increases in crop yields and supported the global population boom. However, excessive use has led to environmental issues such as water pollution, soil acidification, and greenhouse gas emissions.

## Body

### Historical Development
Soil fertility management has been practiced since the dawn of agriculture. Early civilizations including the Egyptians, Romans, and Babylonians used minerals and manure to improve crop productivity. Scientific understanding advanced with Nicolas Théodore de Saussure and later Justus von Liebig, who identified key principles of plant nutrition. Von Liebig’s work laid the foundation for the modern fertilizer industry, though his ideas were refined by contemporaries like Carl Ludwig Sprenger and Hermann Hellriegel.

In 1837, John Bennet Lawes initiated systematic studies on the effects of different manures, leading to the creation of the artificial manure industry. His collaboration with Joseph Henry Gilbert resulted in decades of influential agricultural research. Around the same time, the Birkeland-Eyde process began fixing atmospheric nitrogen into nitric acid, marking the start of industrial-scale nitrogen fertilizer production.

### Industrial Production and Key Processes
The Haber-Bosch process, developed in the early 20th century, revolutionized fertilizer production by synthesizing ammonia from methane and atmospheric nitrogen. This ammonia serves as the base for all nitrogen fertilizers, including urea and ammonium nitrate. The Ostwald process further converts ammonia into nitric acid, which is used to produce nitrates.

After World War II, nitrogen production facilities originally built for explosives were repurposed for agricultural use, accelerating the adoption of synthetic fertilizers. By 2003, global nitrogen production reached 100 million tonnes annually. Phosphate fertilizers also expanded, sourced primarily from phosphate rock via acid treatment. Potassium fertilizers derive from potash minerals, often purified to remove impurities like sodium chloride.

### Nutrient Roles and Mechanisms
Plants require three primary macronutrients: nitrogen for leaf and stem growth, phosphorus for root and seed development, and potassium for water regulation and fruiting. Secondary nutrients include calcium, magnesium, and sulfur, while micronutrients such as iron, zinc, and boron are needed in trace amounts.

Nitrogen availability depends on biological fixation by bacteria or industrial synthesis. Phosphorus supports energy transfer through ATP and genetic material via DNA. Potassium regulates osmotic balance and enzyme activation. Some fertilizers modify soil structure to enhance water retention and aeration, complementing their nutritional role.

### Classification and Types
Fertilizers are categorized based on nutrient content and origin. Straight (single-nutrient) fertilizers include ammonium nitrate, urea, single superphosphate, and muriate of potash. Multinutrient formulations combine two or more elements—NP, NK, PK, or full NPK blends. These may be compound (chemically combined) or blended (physically mixed).

Organic fertilizers originate from living or decomposed matter—compost, manure, bone meal, blood meal, and biosolids. While lower in concentrated nutrients, they improve soil health and structure. In contrast, synthetic fertilizers offer precise nutrient ratios but lack organic matter benefits.

### Application Methods
Fertilizers are applied as solids (granules, prills) or liquids (aqueous solutions). Solid forms dominate (~90%) due to ease of transport and storage. Liquid applications allow faster uptake and uniform distribution, especially in fertigation systems where nutrients are dissolved in irrigation water.

Timing and dosage depend on crop needs, soil tests, and weather conditions. Fall-applied nitrogen for winter crops is often followed by spring topdressing. Catch crops like mustard help absorb residual nitrogen before leaching. Slow-release and controlled-release fertilizers extend nutrient availability, reducing waste and runoff.

Foliar sprays deliver nutrients directly to leaves, increasing efficiency for high-value crops. Additives like nitrification inhibitors slow nitrogen conversion, minimizing loss through leaching and volatilization.

### Environmental Impacts
Excessive fertilizer use contributes significantly to environmental degradation. Runoff containing nitrogen and phosphorus causes eutrophication in aquatic ecosystems, triggering algal blooms and dead zones. Nitrate contamination of groundwater poses risks to human health, notably "blue baby syndrome."

Soil acidification results from ammonium-based fertilizers releasing hydrogen ions, lowering pH and reducing nutrient availability. Liming counteracts this effect. Accumulation of heavy metals—including cadmium, fluoride, and uranium—from phosphate fertilizers threatens soil and food safety.

Synthetic nitrogen production accounts for approximately 5% of global greenhouse gas emissions. Nitrous oxide, a potent GHG with 296 times the warming potential of CO₂, arises during fertilizer application. Methane emissions increase in rice paddies treated with ammonium-based fertilizers.

### Policy and Sustainability Initiatives
Regulatory frameworks aim to reduce pollution. The EU’s Nitrates Directive limits nitrogen application rates. In the U.S., states monitor nonpoint source pollution from agricultural runoff. Subsidy reforms in China have encouraged more efficient fertilizer use among large farms.

Sustainable alternatives emphasize reduced tillage, buffer strips, cover crops, and integrated nutrient management. Regenerative practices seek to restore soil biology and reduce dependence on synthetic inputs. Emerging technologies explore renewable hydrogen sources for ammonia production, potentially decoupling fertilizer manufacturing from fossil fuels.

## Terms
- **NPK Rating**: Numerical label indicating percentages of nitrogen (N), phosphorus (as P₂O₅), and potassium (as K₂O) in a fertilizer.
- **Haber-Bosch Process**: Industrial method converting atmospheric nitrogen and hydrogen (from natural gas) into ammonia under high pressure and temperature.
- **Eutrophication**: Enrichment of water bodies with nutrients, leading to excessive plant growth and oxygen depletion.
- **Nitrification**: Microbial oxidation of ammonium to nitrite then nitrate, influencing nutrient cycling and potential losses.
- **Urease Inhibitor**: Chemical that slows urea hydrolysis, reducing ammonia volatilization and improving nitrogen use efficiency.
- **Controlled-Release Fertilizer (CRF)**: Granular product releasing nutrients gradually over time, matching plant demand.
- **Phosphogypsum**: Radioactive waste generated during phosphate fertilizer production, stored in stacks or landfills.
- **Catch Crop**: Fast-growing plants sown after harvest to absorb excess nutrients and prevent leaching.
- **Fertigation**: Application of dissolved fertilizers through irrigation systems for targeted nutrient delivery.
- **Luxury Consumption**: Uptake of nutrients beyond plant requirements, common with over-application.

## Debates and Open Questions
Several contentious issues surround fertilizer use and policy. The extent to which synthetic fertilizers contribute to trace mineral depletion in crops remains debated, with some attributing declines to high-yielding varieties rather than nutrient dilution alone. Whether organic farming can sustainably feed growing populations without synthetic inputs is hotly contested, particularly given yield gaps and land-use implications.

The long-term viability of phosphate reserves raises concerns about "peak phosphorus," though estimates vary widely depending on resource definitions and extraction technologies. Similarly, the environmental costs of nitrogen fertilizer—including emissions and biodiversity impacts—are weighed against its role in preventing hunger and supporting population growth.

Finally, the effectiveness of regulatory measures versus market-driven incentives in promoting sustainable practices continues to evolve, especially amid geopolitical disruptions affecting fertilizer supply chains and pricing.
