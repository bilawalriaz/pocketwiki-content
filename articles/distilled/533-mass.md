# Mass

## Overview
In physics, mass is an intrinsic, positive quantity measuring a body's resistance to acceleration (inertia) and the strength of its gravitational interaction. While historically conflated with weight—a force dependent on local gravity—mass is an invariant property of matter. Newton unified terrestrial and celestial mechanics by defining universal gravitational mass, showing that every object generates a gravitational field proportional to its mass. Einstein’s equivalence principle later established the identity of inertial and gravitational mass as a foundation of general relativity. In modern physics, mass arises from the coupling of elementary particles to the Higgs field (the Brout–Englert–Higgs mechanism), and special relativity reveals mass as a measure of energy content via *E=mc²*. The SI unit is the kilogram (kg), defined since 2019 by fixing the Planck constant, the speed of light, and the caesium hyperfine frequency.

## Timeline
- **Pre-history** — Humans recognize weight proportional to number of objects; balance scales compare masses via weight.
- **1609** — Kepler publishes three laws of planetary motion; Galileo discovers moons of Jupiter.
- **1638** — Galileo publishes *Two New Sciences*, establishing distance ∝ time² in free fall (constant acceleration).
- **1686–87** — Newton publishes *Principia*, defining mass as "quantity of matter" and formulating universal gravitation (*F = Gm₁m₂/r²*).
- **1797** — Cavendish experiment measures Earth's density/mass in traditional units.
- **1795/1889** — Kilogram defined as mass of 1 dm³ of water (1795); redefined by platinum–iridium International Prototype (IPK) (1889).
- **1905** — Einstein publishes special relativity; mass–energy equivalence (*E=mc²*) established.
- **1915** — Einstein formulates general relativity, incorporating the equivalence principle (inertial = gravitational mass).
- **1960s** — Standard Model developed; Higgs mechanism proposed to explain origin of elementary particle mass.
- **20 May 2019** — Kilogram redefined via fundamental constants (Planck constant, *c*, caesium frequency), retiring the IPK.

## Body

### Phenomena and Definitions
Mass manifests in three conceptually distinct phenomena, though experiments confirm their equivalence to one part in a trillion:
*   **Inertial mass**: Resistance to acceleration (*F = ma*).
*   **Active gravitational mass**: Strength of the gravitational field generated by an object.
*   **Passive gravitational mass**: Gravitational force exerted on an object in a known field (*F = Mg*).
Newton’s third law requires active and passive gravitational mass to be identical. The **equivalence principle** (Galilean/weak form) asserts the identity of inertial and passive gravitational mass, implying all objects fall at the same rate in a vacuum (universality of free-fall). The **strong equivalence principle** extends this: locally, a uniform gravitational field is indistinguishable from uniform acceleration.

### Weight vs. Mass
Weight is a force (measured in newtons); mass is an intrinsic property (kg). On Earth, *W = mg* (where *g* ≈ 9.80665 m/s²). A 50 kg mass weighs 491 N on Earth but only 81.5 N on the Moon. In free fall, weight is zero while mass remains unchanged. For non-gravitational accelerations (e.g., elevators, centrifuges), weight is *W = –ma*, where *a* is proper acceleration (deviation from free fall).

### Pre-Newtonian Concepts
Early humans used balance scales, recognizing weight ∝ number of objects (*Wₙ ∝ n*). The Romans standardized the *libra* (pound) and *uncia* (ounce) using carob seeds (1728 seeds = 1 pound; 144 = 1 ounce). Kepler (1609) described planetary orbits (elliptical, *T² ∝ a³*) but used "weight" for what Newton later named mass. Galileo (1638) experimentally established constant gravitational acceleration independent of mass (distance ∝ time²), using inclined planes and water clocks for timing.

### Newtonian Mass
Newton bridged Galileo’s free fall and Kepler’s orbits. He defined mass as "quantity of matter" (density × volume) and postulated **universal gravitational mass**: every object attracts every other with force *F = Gm₁m₂/r²*. This allowed two methods to measure gravitational mass: orbital mechanics (Kepler’s method) or surface gravity (*g = μ/R²*). Newton’s cannonball thought experiment linked projectile motion to orbital trajectories. The Cavendish experiment (1797) first measured Earth’s mass in traditional units (density 5.448× water).

### Inertial Mass and Operationalism
Ernst Mach and Percy Bridgman championed defining inertial mass operationally via collision: *m₁/m₂ = |a₂|/|a₁|* (using Newton’s 2nd and 3rd laws). This avoids defining "force" but faces difficulties: it ignores binding energy (e.g., proton mass in deuterium vs. free space differs by 0.239%) and requires multiple measurements to determine instantaneous acceleration (Poincaré’s "insurmountable flaw"). Mass also links momentum (*p = mv*) and kinetic energy (*K = ½mv²*).

### Units of Mass
The SI base unit is the **kilogram (kg)**. Since 2019, it is defined by fixing the Planck constant (*h*), the speed of light (*c*), and the caesium hyperfine frequency (Δν_Cs), replacing the platinum–iridium International Prototype (IPK, 1889–2019) which drifted over time.
Common non-SI units:
*   **Tonne (t)**: 1000 kg.
*   **Dalton (Da) / unified atomic mass unit (u)**: 1/12 mass of free carbon-12 atom ≈ 1.66×10⁻²⁷ kg.
*   **Electronvolt (eV)**: Energy unit used as mass via *E=mc²* (1 GeV/c² ≈ 1.78×10⁻²⁷ kg).
*   **Pound (lb)**: ≈ 0.45 kg.
*   **Planck mass**: ≈ 2.18×10⁻⁸ kg (derived from fundamental constants).
*   **Solar mass (M☉)**: ≈ 1.99×10³⁰ kg (astronomy).

### Relativity
**Special Relativity** distinguishes:
*   **Rest mass (invariant mass, *m₀*)**: Mass measured in the object's rest frame; invariant across all inertial frames.
*   **Relativistic mass (*m_rel = γm₀*)**: Total energy/*c²*; increases with velocity (*γ = 1/√(1−v²/c²)*). Relativistic mass has fallen into disuse; physicists prefer invariant mass and total energy (*E_total = γm₀c²*).
The energy–momentum relation: *m₀²c⁴ = E_total² − (|p|c)²*. Mass–energy equivalence means adding energy (e.g., heat) increases mass (melting 1 kg ice adds ~3.7 ng).
**General Relativity** adopts the equivalence principle as axiomatic. However, defining a global, invariant mass for a system is impossible due to the non-linearity of the Einstein field equations; gravitational field energy cannot be localized invariantly in the stress–energy tensor (pseudotensors are used for specific observers).

### Quantum Physics and the Standard Model
In quantum mechanics, mass *m* appears as a parameter in the Schrödinger equation (kinetic term *−ħ²/2m ∇²*) and the Dirac equation (*(−iγ^μ∂_μ + m)ψ = 0*). In the **Standard Model**, elementary particles are initially massless. The **Brout–Englert–Higgs mechanism** couples the fermion field *ψ* to the Higgs field *Φ*, replacing the mass term *mψ* with a Yukawa coupling *G_ψ ψ̄ Φ ψ*. The particle mass becomes *m = G_ψ v/√2* (where *v* is the Higgs vacuum expectation value), shifting the mystery to the unexplained coupling constants *G_ψ*.

### Tachyonic and Complex Mass
A **tachyonic field** has imaginary mass (*m² < 0*). This does not imply faster-than-light particles (causality is preserved); rather, it signals an instability. The system undergoes **tachyon condensation** (a phase transition/spontaneous symmetry breaking) to a stable vacuum where excitations have real, positive mass-squared. Examples: Higgs mechanism, ferromagnetism. Unstable particles are described by complex mass: real part = mass, imaginary part = decay rate (in natural units). If real and imaginary parts are comparable, the state is a scattering resonance, not a particle.

## Terms
- ****Inertial mass**** — Quantitative measure of a body's resistance to acceleration (*m = F/a*).
- ****Gravitational mass (active/passive)**** — Active: source strength of gravitational field; Passive: response to gravitational field (*F = Mg*).
- ****Equivalence principle**** — The postulate that inertial and gravitational mass are identical; foundation of general relativity.
- ****Rest mass (invariant mass)**** — Mass measured in the object's rest frame; Lorentz invariant.
- ****Relativistic mass**** — *γm₀*; total energy divided by *c²*; frame-dependent (largely deprecated).
- ****Mass–energy equivalence**** — *E = mc²*; mass is a measure of energy content; conserved together in closed systems.
- ****Dalton (Da) / unified atomic mass unit (u)**** — 1/12 the mass of a free carbon-12 atom; ≈ 1.66×10⁻²⁷ kg.
- ****Higgs mechanism (Brout–Englert–Higgs)**** — Process by which elementary particles acquire mass via coupling to the Higgs field.
- ****Tachyon condensation**** — Phase transition where a field with imaginary mass decays to a stable state with real mass, breaking symmetry.
- ****Planck mass**** — Fundamental unit of mass derived from *h*, *c*, and *G*; ≈ 2.18×10⁻⁸ kg.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Origin of mass values**: The Standard Model explains *how* particles acquire mass (Higgs mechanism) but not *why* the Yukawa coupling constants (*G_ψ*) have their specific values, which determine the observed mass spectrum.
*   **Quantum gravity**: General relativity cannot define a global, invariant mass for a system due to non-linearity; a quantum theory of gravity is needed to reconcile gravitational mass with quantum mass generation.
*   **Relativistic mass utility**: Pedagogical debate persists on whether the concept of velocity-dependent relativistic mass aids or hinders understanding, versus using only invariant mass and total energy.
*   **Tachyonic fields**: While mathematically consistent (causality preserved), the physical interpretation of imaginary mass and the nature of the tachyon condensation process remain active theoretical topics.
*   **Neutrino mass**: The Standard Model originally predicted massless neutrinos; observed oscillations imply non-zero mass, requiring an extension of the Higgs mechanism (e.g., seesaw mechanism) not yet confirmed.
