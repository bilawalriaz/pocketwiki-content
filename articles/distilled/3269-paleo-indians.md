# Paleo-Indians

## Overview
Paleo-Indians were the first peoples to inhabit the Americas, arriving during the late Pleistocene epoch. They are defined archaeologically by their lithic (stone) tool technology and their association with now-extinct megafauna (large animals). Their migration and subsequent adaptation represent the foundational chapter of human history in the Western Hemisphere, with ongoing debates centering on their exact timing, routes, and ecological impact.

## Timeline
- **c. 36,000 BP** — Genetic divergence of first Americans from Ancient East Asians.
- **45,000–12,000 BCE** — Beringia land bridge exists between Siberia and Alaska.
- **c. 16,500–13,500 BCE** — Ice-free corridors and coastal routes open for migration into the Americas.
- **c. 13,500 BCE** — Clovis culture appears in North America, noted for fluted projectile points.
- **c. 14,200–12,980 BP** — El Jobo points used in Venezuela; some of the earliest South American artifacts.
- **c. 11,000 BP** — Fish-tail points appear in Patagonia, South America.
- **c. 10,500–9,500 BCE** — Folsom tradition begins; bison becomes a primary hunted species on the Great Plains.
- **9,000–8,000 BCE** — End of the Paleoindian period; transition to the Archaic Period begins.

## Body

### Migration and Settlement
The traditional theory posits that Paleo-Indians migrated from Siberia into Alaska via the Beringia land bridge, which was exposed due to lowered sea levels during the Quaternary glaciation. From there, they followed herds of megafauna through ice-free corridors that developed between the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets from c. 16,500–13,500 BCE. An alternative model proposes a coastal migration route along the Pacific. The timing is heavily debated; while conventional estimates place human arrival in North America between 15,000 and 20,000 years ago, some evidence suggests people reached South America as early as 25,000 years ago. Sites like Monte Verde in Chile and Cooper's Ferry in Idaho predate the Clovis culture, indicating earlier and possibly multiple waves of migration.

### Culture and Subsistence
Paleo-Indians are primarily identified by their stone tools, especially fluted projectile points (spear points with a long groove). They lived in small, highly mobile bands of 20–60 people, moving frequently to follow seasonal resources. Their diet was rich in protein from hunting megafauna like mammoths, mastodons, and giant beavers, supplemented by fishing, nuts, and berries. The Clovis culture (c. 13,500 BCE) is a notable example, though whether Clovis people were specialist big-game hunters or had a mixed foraging strategy is disputed. As the climate warmed and megafauna declined, later Paleoindian groups like the Folsom tradition (c. 12,500–11,500 BP) focused more on bison.

### Regional Variations and Classification
Archaeologists classify Paleoindian cultures by their lithic technology and regional adaptations. In South America, distinct point types include the El Jobo (Venezuela), Fish-tail (southern cone), and Paijan (Peru/Ecuador). While some groups were territorial, like those at Monte Verde, others were highly mobile hunters. The widespread distribution of similar tool styles across vast distances indicates extensive trade networks or shared cultural practices among these early populations.

### Genetic Origins
Genetic studies show that the founding population of the Americas diverged from Ancient East Asians about 36,000 years ago. They encountered and mixed with a different Siberian population (Ancient North Eurasians) before migrating to Beringia, where they became isolated. This founding population then populated the Americas. Later migrations, such as those of Na-Dené and Inuit peoples, show distinct genetic markers, indicating separate, later waves of entry into the northern extremes.

## Terms
- **Paleo-Indian**: The term for the first peoples of the Americas during the lithic period, distinct from the Old World Paleolithic.
- **Lithic**: Pertaining to stone; in archaeology, it refers to tools made by chipping or flaking stone.
- **Fluted projectile point**: A spear point with a long groove ("flute") on one or both sides, characteristic of Paleoindian technology like Clovis points.
- **Megafauna**: Large animals, many now extinct, such as mammoths, mastodons, and giant sloths, that Paleoindians hunted.
- **Beringia**: The land bridge that connected Siberia and Alaska during periods of low sea level in the Pleistocene.
- **Clovis culture**: A widespread Paleoindian archaeological culture in North America (c. 13,500 BCE), identified by its distinctive fluted points.
- **Folsom tradition**: A Paleoindian culture (c. 12,500–11,500 BP) known for hunting bison and using finely crafted fluted points.
- **Archaic Period**: The period following the Paleoindian, characterized by a warmer climate, extinction of megafauna, and more regionally adapted, sedentary lifestyles.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Migration Timing and Routes**: The exact dates and whether the primary route was an ice-free corridor or the Pacific coast remain subjects of active research and debate.
- **Megafauna Extinction**: The cause of the Late Pleistocene megafauna extinctions is controversial. While human hunting is a proposed factor, many scholars argue for a combination of causes, including climatic change. A 2012 survey found 63% of archaeologists favored a "combination of factors."
- **Clovis First Hypothesis**: The long-held belief that the Clovis culture represented the first Americans has been challenged by numerous pre-Clovis sites across the Americas.
- **Number of Migration Waves**: Evidence suggests there were likely multiple waves of ancient settlers from Beringia to the American continent.
