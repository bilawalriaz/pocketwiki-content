# Machine

## Overview  
A machine is a thermodynamic system that uses power to apply forces and control movement to perform work. From ancient simple machines like the wedge and lever to modern robotic and molecular systems, machines transform input energy into useful output through mechanisms, actuators, and controllers. Understanding machines—how they evolved, how they function, and how they are classified—is central to engineering, biology, and the history of technology.

## Timeline  
- **Prehistoric** — Hand axe (wedge) and inclined plane used by humans
- **c. 3000 BC** — Shadoof and first crane machine in Mesopotamia
- **c. 2000 BC** — Lever used in ancient Egyptian technology
- **Early 2nd millennium BC** — Pulleys appear in Mesopotamia and Egypt
- **911–609 BC** — Screw invented in Neo-Assyrian Mesopotamia
- **c. 3rd century BC** — Archimedes studies lever, pulley, and screw
- **c. 10–75 AD** — Hero of Alexandria lists five mechanisms in *Mechanics*
- **9th century AD** — Windmills and programmable automata in the Islamic world
- **1551** — Taqi ad-Din Muhammad ibn Ma'ruf describes steam jack in Ottoman Egypt
- **1586** — Simon Stevin derives mechanical advantage of inclined plane
- **1600** — Galileo develops dynamic theory of simple machines
- **1782** — James Watt patents parallel motion linkage
- **1750–1850** — Industrial Revolution spreads machine-based manufacturing

## Body  

### Etymology and Definition  
The English word *machine* derives from Latin *machina*, via Greek *mekhane* ("contrivance"), rooted in *mekhos* ("means"). By the 17th century, it also meant a "scheme or plot," but the modern technical meaning—devices applying force and motion—was codified by John Harris in *Lexicon Technicum* (1704). *Engine*, a near-synonym, stems from Latin *ingenium* ("ingenuity").

### Simple Machines and Ancient Origins  
The six classic simple machines—lever, pulley, wheel and axle, wedge, inclined plane, and screw—are the foundational elements of mechanical systems. The hand axe, a wedge, is the earliest known force-transforming tool. The wheel and axle, lever, pulley, and screw all originated in the ancient Near East. The Egyptian pyramids employed the inclined plane, wedge, and lever. Greek philosophers like Archimedes analyzed the lever, pulley, and screw, discovering mechanical advantage, though their understanding was limited to statics. Hero of Alexandria later listed five mechanisms capable of setting loads in motion: lever, windlass, pulley, wedge, and screw.

### Renaissance to Industrial Revolution  
During the Renaissance, the study of simple machines shifted to dynamics and work. Galileo (1600) established that machines do not create energy but transform it. Leonardo da Vinci discovered friction laws, later rediscovered by Amontons and refined by Coulomb. The Industrial Revolution (1750–1850) marked a transition from manual to machine-based manufacturing, beginning in Britain with mechanized textiles, iron techniques, and coal use. James Watt’s 1782 parallel motion linkage enabled practical double-acting steam engines, powering locomotives, ships, and factories.

### Modern Machines and Systems  
Modern machines are complex systems comprising power sources, actuators, mechanisms, controllers, and interfaces. They include vehicles, appliances, industrial equipment, and robots. Mechanisms—gears, cams, linkages—are assemblies of machine elements like frames, bearings, springs, and fasteners. Controllers, from flyball governors to programmable logic controllers, regulate performance using sensors and feedback.

### Power Sources  
Machines are powered by human or animal effort, natural forces (wind, water), or energy conversion systems. Waterwheels and windmills provided early rotary motion. Steam engines (external combustion) and internal combustion engines (automotive, jet) use thermal cycles. Power plants generate electricity via steam turbines driven by fossil fuels or nuclear reactors. Electric motors, fluid power (hydraulic/pneumatic), and electrochemical sources (batteries, solar cells) are modern alternatives.

### Molecular and Computing Machines  
Biological molecules like myosin, kinesin, and dynein function as molecular machines, driving muscle contraction, intracellular transport, and ciliary motion. ATP synthase and ribosomes are nanomachines essential to energy production and gene expression. On the computational side, Charles Babbage’s 19th-century designs laid groundwork for mechanical calculators and computers. DNA-based nanostructures now mimic mechanical linkages at the molecular scale.

## Terms  
- **Mechanical advantage**: Ratio of output force to input force in a machine.  
- **Kinematic chain**: Series of interconnected links and joints forming a mechanism.  
- **Actuator**: Component that converts energy into motion.  
- **Joint**: Connection between machine parts allowing movement (e.g., revolute, prismatic).  
- **Degrees of freedom**: Number of independent movements a mechanism can perform.  
- **Cam and follower**: Mechanism where a shaped surface (cam) drives another part (follower).  
- **Linkage**: Assembly of links connected by joints to transmit motion.  
- **Flyball governor**: Feedback control device regulating engine speed.  
- **Flexure mechanism**: System using compliant elements to produce precise motion.  
- **Mobility**: Measure of a mechanism’s freedom of movement, per Chebychev–Grübler–Kurtzbach criterion.

## Debates and Open Questions  
The classification of simple machines remains debated; while traditionally six are listed, some scholars emphasize joint-based categorization over elemental devices. The boundary between machine and organism is blurred in molecular biology, where biological nanomachines rival engineered systems in complexity. Additionally, the definition of "machine" in computing—whether mechanical calculators or abstract models like Turing machines qualify—remains contested among historians and theorists.
