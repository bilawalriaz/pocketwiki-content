# Mark Antony

## Overview
Marcus Antonius (Mark Antony) was a Roman general and politician whose alliance with Julius Caesar, leadership in the civil wars that ended the Roman Republic, and fateful relationship with Cleopatra VII of Egypt made him a central figure in Rome's transformation into an empire. His rivalry with Octavian (later Augustus) culminated in a final civil war, leading to his defeat and suicide, which paved the way for the establishment of the Roman Empire under Augustus.

## Timeline
- **14 January 83 BC** — Born in Rome.
- **54 BC** — Joins Julius Caesar's staff for the conquest of Gaul.
- **49 BC** — As tribune, supports Caesar; flees to Caesar's camp, helping spark civil war.
- **44 BC** — Co-consul with Caesar; delivers funeral eulogy after Caesar's assassination.
- **43 BC** — Forms the Second Triumvirate with Octavian and Lepidus.
- **42 BC** — Defeats Brutus and Cassius at the Battle of Philippi.
- **41 BC** — Meets Cleopatra VII in Tarsus; begins their political and romantic alliance.
- **40 BC** — Marries Octavian's sister, Octavia, in the Treaty of Brundisium.
- **36 BC** — Fails in a major invasion of the Parthian Empire.
- **34 BC** — Makes the "Donations of Alexandria," granting territories to Cleopatra and their children.
- **31 BC** — Defeated by Octavian at the Battle of Actium.
- **1 August 30 BC** — Dies by suicide in Alexandria after Octavian's invasion of Egypt.

## Body

### Early Life and Career Under Caesar
Born into the plebeian gens Antonia, Antony's early life was marked by family scandal and personal dissipation. His military career began in 57 BC under Aulus Gabinius in the East. His association with Publius Clodius Pulcher led to a position on Julius Caesar's staff in 54 BC, where he proved an excellent commander during the Gallic Wars. As a tribune in 49 BC, Antony's support for Caesar was crucial; his flight from Rome after being blocked by the senate provided Caesar with a pretext to cross the Rubicon and begin civil war. Antony served as Caesar's master of horse (lieutenant) but proved an unpopular and violent administrator in Italy, leading to a temporary estrangement.

### The Assassination and the Second Triumvirate
After Caesar's assassination on the Ides of March (15 March) 44 BC, Antony, as the sole consul, seized the state treasury and positioned himself as Caesar's heir. He skillfully manipulated public opinion with a fiery funeral eulogy, turning the mob against the assassins. However, Caesar's will revealed his adoption of his great-nephew Octavian, creating a rival for leadership of the Caesarian faction. A power struggle ensued, with the senate initially backing Antony against Octavian. After military defeats and political maneuvering, the two, along with Marcus Aemilius Lepidus, formed the Second Triumvirate in November 43 BC. This legal three-man dictatorship immediately launched bloody proscriptions, killing thousands, including Cicero, to purge enemies and fund their war.

### War, the East, and Cleopatra
The Triumvirs defeated Caesar's assassins, Brutus and Cassius, at the Battle of Philippi in 42 BC. Antony then took control of Rome's eastern provinces. In 41 BC, he summoned Cleopatra VII, the ruler of Ptolemaic Egypt and a former lover of Caesar, to Tarsus. Their alliance became both political and romantic, producing three children. Antony used Egypt's vast wealth to fund his military campaigns, most notably a disastrous invasion of Parthia in 36 BC. His relationship with Cleopatra and his increasing adoption of Hellenistic royal customs alienated him from Rome and provided propaganda for his rival, Octavian.

### Final Conflict with Octavian and Death
The Triumvirate expired in 33 BC, and open conflict became inevitable. In 34 BC, Antony made the "Donations of Alexandria," publicly granting Roman territories to Cleopatra and their children and declaring Caesarion, Cleopatra's son by Julius Caesar, as Caesar's legitimate heir. This directly challenged Octavian's legitimacy as Caesar's adopted son. In 32 BC, the Roman senate, manipulated by Octavian, declared war not on Antony but on Cleopatra. The decisive naval Battle of Actium in 31 BC resulted in a crushing defeat for Antony and Cleopatra. They fled to Egypt, where, after Octavian's invasion in 30 BC, both committed suicide. With Antony dead, Octavian became the undisputed master of the Roman world, later taking the title Augustus to become the first Roman emperor.

## Terms
*   **Triumvirate:** A political alliance of three individuals. The Second Triumvirate (43–33 BC) was a legally constituted three-man dictatorship of Antony, Octavian, and Lepidus.
*   **Proscriptions:** Official lists of enemies of the state, published by the Triumvirs, whose members were outlawed, stripped of citizenship, and killed, with their property confiscated.
*   **Philhellene:** A "friend of Greek culture." Antony adopted this persona to win loyalty in the Greek East.
*   **Client Kingdom:** A nominally independent state that was under the political, military, and economic domination of Rome.
*   **Donations of Alexandria:** A 34 BC proclamation by Antony distributing Roman eastern territories to Cleopatra and their children, a key trigger for the final civil war.
*   **Battle of Actium:** The 31 BC naval battle where Octavian's forces decisively defeated Antony and Cleopatra, ending the civil war.
*   **Damnatio Memoriae:** A Roman punishment where a person's name and images were erased from public records and monuments to erase their memory.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Antony's Motives at the Lupercalia:** The source notes uncertainty over whether Antony's offer of a diadem to Caesar in 44 BC was a scripted test of public opinion, a spontaneous act to flatter or embarrass Caesar, or a plot by Caesar's enemies.
*   **The Authenticity of Antony's Will:** Octavian claimed to have found Antony's will, which named Caesarion as heir and requested burial in Alexandria. The source states that while some modern scholars suggest it was partially forged, "the ancient sources" do not describe it as such.
*   **Responsibility for the Parthian Defeat:** Ancient historians like Strabo and Plutarch blamed the Armenian king Artavasdes II for betraying Antony during his 36 BC campaign. However, the source notes that "modern sources note Antony's poor management" as a key factor.
