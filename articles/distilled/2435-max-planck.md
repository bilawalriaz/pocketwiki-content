# Max Planck

## Overview
Max Planck (1858–1947) was a German theoretical physicist whose work on black-body radiation led to the discovery of energy quanta, founding quantum theory and revolutionizing physics. His Planck constant is a fundamental constant of nature, and he was awarded the 1918 Nobel Prize in Physics. He also made major contributions to thermodynamics and was a leading figure in German science for decades.

## Timeline
- **23 April 1858** — Born in Kiel, Germany.
- **1874** — Enrolled at Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München.
- **1879** — Defended his doctoral thesis on the second law of thermodynamics.
- **1885** — Appointed associate professor of Theoretical Physics at Kiel University.
- **1889** — Named successor to Kirchhoff's position at Friedrich Wilhelm University, Berlin.
- **1894** — Turned attention to the problem of black-body radiation.
- **14 December 1900** — Presented his revised derivation of the black-body radiation law, introducing energy quantization (E=hν).
- **1905** — Among the first to recognize and promote Einstein's special theory of relativity.
- **1918** — Awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery of energy quanta.
- **1930** — Became President of the Kaiser Wilhelm Society (KWG).
- **23 January 1945** — His son Erwin was executed by the Nazi regime.
- **4 October 1947** — Died in Göttingen, Germany.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Planck came from an intellectual family of professors and judges. He showed early talent in mathematics and music. At the University of Munich, he studied under Philipp von Jolly, who discouraged him from theoretical physics, believing it was a "nearly fully matured science." Planck ignored this advice, moving to Berlin to study with Hermann von Helmholtz and Gustav Kirchhoff. He focused on thermodynamics, inspired by Rudolf Clausius's work on entropy, and completed both his doctorate and habilitation by 1880.

### Career and Thermodynamics
Planck became a professor in Berlin in 1889. His early research focused on entropy, which he defined as a measure of the irreversibility of a process. He pursued a phenomenological approach, skeptical of atomic theory, and generalized the second law of thermodynamics. Independently of Josiah Willard Gibbs, he derived laws for chemical equilibria. He also provided a theoretical foundation for electrolyte theory and the colligative properties of solutions.

### Discovery of the Quantum
In 1894, Planck began studying black-body radiation—the spectrum of light emitted by a perfect absorber. Existing theories (Wien's law and the Rayleigh-Jeans law) failed to match experimental data across all frequencies. In 1900, Planck derived a law that fit the data perfectly. To explain it, he made a revolutionary assumption in December 1900: energy is not continuous but emitted in discrete packets, or quanta, with energy E=hν, where h is the Planck constant and ν is the frequency. He called this "an act of despair," as it contradicted classical physics, but it became the foundation of quantum theory. He later described it as a discovery comparable to Newton's.

### Later Career and Political Turmoil
Planck was an early supporter of Einstein's relativity theory. During World War I, he endorsed the German cause and signed a nationalist manifesto. In the Weimar Republic, he led efforts to support science financially. When the Nazis rose to power in 1933, Planck, as president of the Kaiser Wilhelm Society, tried to maintain scientific work but failed to prevent the expulsion of Jewish scientists, including his friend Fritz Haber. He privately helped some Jewish scientists but avoided open conflict with the regime. His son Erwin was executed in 1945 for his role in the plot to assassinate Hitler. Planck died in 1947.

### Philosophy and Personal Life
Planck was a Lutheran who believed in a "conscious and intelligent spirit" as the matrix of all matter. He shifted from positivism to scientific realism, believing in an objective reality independent of observation. This made him a critic of the probabilistic Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics. He was an accomplished musician with absolute pitch, and his Berlin home was a cultural center where he often played music with Einstein.

## Terms
- **Entropy**: A thermodynamic quantity representing the unavailability of a system's thermal energy for conversion into mechanical work, often interpreted as a measure of disorder or irreversibility.
- **Black-body radiation**: The electromagnetic radiation emitted by a perfect absorber (a "black body") in thermal equilibrium, whose spectrum depends only on temperature.
- **Planck constant (h)**: A fundamental physical constant that is the quantum of electromagnetic action, linking the energy of a photon to its frequency.
- **Quantum (plural: quanta)**: The minimum amount of any physical entity (such as energy) involved in an interaction.
- **Photon**: A quantum of light or other electromagnetic radiation.
- **Privatdozent**: An academic title for a lecturer in a German university who is qualified to teach but does not hold a salaried professorship.
- **Kaiser Wilhelm Society (KWG)**: A German scientific organization founded in 1911, renamed the Max Planck Society in 1948.
- **Copenhagen interpretation**: A framework for understanding quantum mechanics that emphasizes the role of measurement and probability, which Planck and Einstein rejected.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Planck's philosophical shift**: Planck began as a positivist (following Ernst Mach) but became a scientific realist after discovering the quantum of action, leading to a public rift with Mach in 1908.
- **Reception of quantum theory**: Planck himself struggled to accept the full implications of his discovery, calling it "a purely formal assumption" and later trying unsuccessfully to reintegrate it into classical physics.
- **Copenhagen interpretation**: Planck, along with Einstein and Schrödinger, rejected the probabilistic Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics, preferring a deterministic, realist view.
- **Planck's quote on scientific progress**: He famously stated that new truths triumph not by convincing opponents but because opponents eventually die and a new generation grows up familiar with them. However, historical examples (like Darwin's theory) show this is not always the case.
