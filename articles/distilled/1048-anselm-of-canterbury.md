# Anselm of Canterbury

## Overview

Anselm of Canterbury (1033/4–1109) was an Italian Benedictine abbot, philosopher, and theologian who served as Archbishop of Canterbury from 1093 to 1109. He is widely regarded as the founder of Scholasticism and is famous for formulating the ontological argument for God's existence and the satisfaction theory of atonement. His tenure as archbishop was marked by significant conflicts with English monarchs over church reform and the Investiture Controversy, leading to two exiles.

## Body

### Early Life and Education

Anselm was born into a noble Lombard family in or around Aosta, a region then part of the post-Carolingian Kingdom of Burgundy. His father, Gundulph or Gundulf or Gondulphe, was a Lombard noble, probably one of Adelaide's Arduinici uncles or cousins; his mother, Ermenberge, was almost certainly the granddaughter of Conrad the Peaceful, related both to the Anselmid bishops of Aosta and to the heirs of Henry II. At age 15, Anselm felt the call to enter a monastery but, failing to obtain his father's consent, he was refused by the abbot. After his mother's death, probably at the birth of his sister Richera, Anselm's father repented. When Gundulph entered a monastery, Anselm, at age 23, left home with a single attendant, crossed the Alps, and wandered through Burgundy and France for three years. His countryman Lanfranc of Pavia was then prior of the Benedictine abbey of Bec in Normandy. Attracted by Lanfranc's reputation, Anselm reached Normandy in 1059. After spending some time in Avranches, he returned the next year. His father having died, he consulted with Lanfranc as to whether to return to his estates or to renounce them, becoming a hermit or a monk at Bec or Cluny. Given what he saw as his own conflict of interest, Lanfranc sent Anselm to Maurilius, the archbishop of Rouen, who convinced him to enter Bec as a novice at the age of 27.

### Abbot of Bec

In 1063, Duke William II summoned Lanfranc to serve as the abbot of his new abbey of St Stephen at Caen and the monks of Bec, despite the initial hesitation of some on account of his youth, elected Anselm prior. Under his leadership, Bec became the foremost seat of learning in Europe. During this period, he composed major works including the *Monologion* (1075–1076), which argued for the existence of God through reason alone, and the *Proslogion* (1077–1078), which introduced the ontological argument. He also wrote dialogues on truth, free will, and the fall of Satan, and defended Trinitarian doctrine against the heresy of Roscelin. After fifteen years, in 1078, Anselm was unanimously elected as Bec's abbot following the death of its founder, the warrior-monk Herluin. He was blessed as abbot by Gilbert d'Arques, Bishop of Évreux, on 22 February 1079.

### Archbishop of Canterbury and Conflicts with Monarchs

Appointed Archbishop of Canterbury in 1093, Anselm immediately clashed with King William II (Rufus) over church reform and royal control. William refused to allow Anselm to travel to Rome for his pallium, leading to tensions that culminated in Anselm's first exile in October 1097. During his exile, he participated in the Council of Bari (1098), defending Western doctrines against Greek bishops.

After William's death in 1100, Anselm returned under King Henry I, who initially pledged support for reform. However, conflicts resumed over lay investiture, leading to Anselm's second exile after March 1105. The Concordat of London in 1107 resolved the dispute, with Henry formally renouncing the right of English kings to invest bishops, though the agreement was seen as temporary.

### Philosophical and Theological Contributions

Anselm's works are characterized by their rational approach to Christian doctrine, seeking to demonstrate theological truths through reason. His principle of "fides quaerens intellectum" (faith seeking understanding) emphasized that belief precedes rational inquiry but must be supported by reason. His *Cur Deus Homo* (1095–1098) presented the satisfaction theory of atonement, arguing that Christ's sacrifice was necessary to satisfy divine justice for humanity's sin. His ontological argument, developed in the *Proslogion*, posited that the concept of a perfect being necessarily implies its existence.

### Legacy

Anselm was canonized and declared a Doctor of the Church by Pope Clement XI in 1720. His influence extended to later Scholastics like Thomas Aquinas and Duns Scotus. Modern scholarship debates his role as either a pious church leader or a shrewd political operator. His relics' location remains uncertain, and his legacy continues to be celebrated in various religious and academic institutions.

## Terms

- **Scholasticism**: A medieval philosophical and theological movement emphasizing reason and logical argumentation in the service of faith.
- **Ontological Argument**: An argument for God's existence based on the concept of a perfect being, asserting that existence is a necessary attribute of perfection.
- **Satisfaction Theory of Atonement**: The theological doctrine that Christ's death was necessary to satisfy divine justice for human sin.
- **Investiture Controversy**: A 11th-12th century conflict between the papacy and secular rulers over the appointment of church officials.
- **Fides Quaerens Intellectum**: Latin for "faith seeking understanding," Anselm's principle that faith precedes and guides rational inquiry.
- **Pallium**: A woolen band worn by archbishops, symbolizing their authority, traditionally conferred by the pope.
- **Gregorian Reform**: A series of 11th-century church reforms initiated under Pope Gregory VII, aimed at eliminating secular influence in church appointments.
- **Concordat of London**: The 1107 agreement between Anselm and Henry I, resolving disputes over lay investiture in England.
- **Monologion**: Anselm's 1075–1076 work arguing for God's existence through reason alone, without scriptural appeal.
- **Proslogion**: Anselm's 1077–1078 work containing the ontological argument, written as a direct address to God.

## Debates and Open Questions

Modern scholarship remains divided over Anselm's episcopal leadership. Some historians, like Fröhlich and Schmitt, argue that Anselm carefully managed his reputation as a devout scholar, minimizing worldly conflicts. Others, including Vaughn, contend that his image of "simple holiness and profound thinking" was a deliberate tool used by a skilled political operator. The traditional view, supported by Southern, portrays Anselm as a genuinely pious and reluctant church leader who "nursed a deep-seated horror of worldly advancement." These differing perspectives reflect broader questions about the relationship between religious ideals and political realities in medieval Europe.
