# Spanish colonization of the Americas

## Overview
The Spanish colonization of the Americas, initiated in 1493, was a transformative process of imperial expansion, demographic catastrophe, and cultural fusion. Driven by wealth, glory, and religious conversion, it established a vast empire that reshaped the Western Hemisphere's ecology, societies, and economies, laying the groundwork for modern Latin American nations.

## Timeline
- **1492** — Christopher Columbus makes first landfall in the Western Hemisphere.
- **1493** — Permanent Spanish settlement begins on Hispaniola.
- **1494** — Treaty of Tordesillas divides the New World between Spain and Portugal.
- **1519–1521** — Hernán Cortés conquers the Aztec Empire in central Mexico.
- **1532** — Francisco Pizarro captures the Inca emperor Atahualpa, beginning the conquest of Peru.
- **1535** — Viceroyalty of New Spain is established.
- **1542** — New Laws are enacted to protect indigenous peoples from exploitation.
- **1545** — The silver mountain of Cerro Rico de Potosí is discovered in Peru.
- **1571** — The Holy Office of the Inquisition is established in the Americas.
- **1717/1739** — Viceroyalty of New Granada is established (temporarily then permanently).
- **1776** — Viceroyalty of Río de la Plata is established.
- **1898** — Spain loses its last American colonies (Cuba, Puerto Rico) to the United States.

## Body

### Imperial Expansion and Conquest
Spanish expansion began under the Catholic Monarchs, Isabella I and Ferdinand II, motivated by "gold, glory, God." After the 1492 conquest of Granada, Columbus's voyages initiated settlement. The 1494 Treaty of Tordesillas formalized a division of the world with Portugal, though other European powers later ignored these claims. Conquests were led by an *adelantado* who financed the venture and recruited soldiers. Conquistadors like Hernán Cortés (Aztec Empire) and Francisco Pizarro (Inca Empire) exploited indigenous rivalries, forging crucial alliances key to their success.

### Caribbean and Early Settlement
Initial settlements on Hispaniola established patterns of exploitation. The Spanish used the *encomienda* system, granting settlers control over indigenous labor and tribute. This, combined with introduced diseases, caused a demographic catastrophe; the Taíno population was nearly wiped out within decades. As indigenous labor vanished, Spaniards began importing enslaved Africans. The first mainland settlements, like Cumaná (1501), were established, but the Caribbean remained the initial base for further exploration.

### Conquest of Mainland Empires
The conquests of Mexico (1519–1521) and Peru (1532) provided the wealth and territory Spain sought. Spanish forces, though small, leveraged alliances with subjugated peoples (e.g., Tlaxcalans against the Aztecs) and exploited civil wars (e.g., between Atahualpa and Huáscar). These conquests were followed by protracted campaigns to subdue surrounding regions. The discovery of massive silver deposits, particularly at Potosí (1545) and Zacatecas (1548), became the engine of the colonial economy and a major source of crown revenue.

### Governance and Administration
The Spanish Crown established a complex bureaucracy. Key institutions included the Council of the Indies (1524), which advised the monarch; the Casa de Contratación (1503), which regulated trade; and viceroyalties (New Spain, 1535; Peru, 1542) governed by viceroys as the monarch's direct representatives. Audiencias (high courts) served as judicial and administrative centers. At the local level, *cabildos* (town councils) governed Spanish towns, while the crown attempted to protect indigenous communities through laws like the New Laws (1542) and institutions like the *República de Indios*.

### Society and Demographics
The colonial population was stratified. At the top were *peninsulares* (Spanish-born) and *criollos* (American-born Spaniards). The largest group was indigenous peoples (*indios*), legally separated into the *República de Indios*. Enslaved and free Africans (*negros*) were also present. Intermixture produced a *casta* system of mixed-race categories, such as *mestizo* (Spanish and indigenous). The indigenous population suffered a catastrophic decline, estimated at 80% in the first century and a half, primarily from epidemic diseases like smallpox.

### Economy and Labor
The economy evolved from early reliance on indigenous tribute and *encomienda* labor to a system centered on silver mining and agriculture. Silver, processed using mercury in the *patio* process, was exported globally. Labor systems included the *mita* (rotational forced labor) in Andean mines and various forms of debt peonage. Agriculture and ranching developed to feed urban and mining populations, while export crops included sugar, cacao, and cochineal dye.

### The Catholic Church
Evangelization was a core justification for conquest. Early work was done by religious orders (Franciscans, Dominicans), but the crown later favored diocesan clergy under its control via the *Patronato Real* (royal patronage). The Jesuits were influential in frontier missions until their expulsion in 1767. The Inquisition was established to enforce orthodoxy but excluded indigenous peoples as neophytes.

### End of Empire
In the early 19th century, inspired by Enlightenment ideas and the Napoleonic Wars, Spanish American colonies fought for independence. Led by *criollo* elites like Simón Bolívar and José de San Martín, most colonies achieved independence by the 1820s. Spain retained Cuba and Puerto Rico until 1898, when they were ceded to the United States after the Spanish–American War.

## Terms
- **Adelantado**: A senior leader authorized by the crown to lead an expedition of discovery, conquest, and settlement.
- **Encomienda**: A grant of indigenous labor and tribute to a Spanish settler, theoretically in exchange for protection and Christian instruction.
- **Criollo**: A person of Spanish descent born in the Americas.
- **Peninsular**: A person born in Spain (the Iberian Peninsula).
- **Mestizo**: A person of mixed Spanish and indigenous ancestry.
- **Viceroyalty**: The largest administrative unit of the Spanish Empire, governed by a viceroy as the direct representative of the monarch.
- **Audiencia**: A high court that also served as a key administrative and executive body in its jurisdiction.
- **Patronato Real**: The royal patronage granted by the Pope to the Spanish Crown, giving it control over ecclesiastical appointments and affairs in its territories.
- **República de Indios**: The legal category separating indigenous communities from the Spanish population (*República de Españoles*).
- **Mita**: A system of rotational forced labor, adapted from Inca traditions, used especially in Andean silver mines.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The term "colonization" and the concept of "colonies" are contested by scholars, who debate whether these terms accurately describe the Spanish imperial relationship with its American territories.
- The demographic impact of colonization is debated, with estimates of pre-contact indigenous populations varying widely, affecting calculations of the scale of population decline.
- The environmental impact of colonial-era ranching, particularly sheep herding, is debated among historians.
