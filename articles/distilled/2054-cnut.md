# Cnut

## Overview
Cnut the Great (c. 995–1035) was a Danish prince who conquered England and later united it with Denmark and Norway, creating the North Sea Empire. His reign represents the peak of Viking political power, demonstrating how a Scandinavian ruler could successfully govern a major Christian kingdom through military prowess, diplomacy, and integration of English and Danish elites. He is remembered as a wise and effective king who restored stability to England after decades of Viking raids.

## Timeline
- **c. 995** — Birth of Cnut, son of Swein Forkbeard, King of Denmark
- **1013** — Swein Forkbeard invades England; Cnut left in charge of the fleet at Gainsborough
- **1014** — Swein dies; Cnut elected king by Vikings in England but expelled by restored Æthelred
- **1015** — Cnut launches major invasion of England with a large Scandinavian fleet
- **1016** — Æthelred dies; Edmund Ironside becomes king; decisive Battle of Assandun
- **1016** — Treaty of Alney: England divided; Cnut becomes king of all England after Edmund's death
- **1018** — Cnut becomes King of Denmark after his brother Harald's death
- **1026** — Victory at Battle of Helgeå against Norwegian-Swedish alliance
- **1027** — Cnut attends coronation of Holy Roman Emperor Conrad II in Rome
- **1028** — Cnut conquers Norway, becoming king of England, Denmark, and Norway
- **1031** — Scottish kings submit to Cnut
- **1035** — Cnut dies in Shaftesbury, England

## Body

### Conquest and Rule of England
Cnut's 1015 invasion force of about 10,000 men in 200 ships included Danes, Norwegians, Swedes, and Poles. After landing in Wessex, he received crucial defections, including Eadric Streona of Mercia and Thorkell the Tall. A fourteen-month campaign against Edmund Ironside culminated in the Battle of Assandun (October 1016), where Eadric Streona's treachery caused English defeat. The subsequent treaty divided England, but Edmund's death weeks later made Cnut sole king.

As king, Cnut eliminated potential rivals from the old dynasty, married Æthelred's widow Emma of Normandy, and collected a massive Danegeld of £82,500 to pay off his army. He reorganized England into four great earldoms (Wessex, Mercia, East Anglia, Northumbria), initially appointing Scandinavian followers but later trusting English nobles like Godwin. He issued law codes (I Cnut and II Cnut) and maintained Edgar's laws, presenting himself as a legitimate English monarch.

### North Sea Empire
After securing England, Cnut focused on Scandinavia. He became King of Denmark in 1018 and, after defeating a Norwegian-Swedish alliance at Helgeå (1026), conquered Norway in 1028. His empire included parts of Sweden and influence over Scotland (1031 submission) and the Irish Sea region. He controlled key maritime routes, with his influence extending to Dublin and the Scottish Isles.

Cnut's power rested on balancing English and Danish interests. He used English wealth to fund Scandinavian campaigns and Danish military strength to secure England. His 1027 journey to Rome, where he attended Emperor Conrad II's coronation, demonstrated his European stature. He negotiated reduced fees for English archbishops' pallia and better travel conditions for pilgrims.

### Church Relations and Legacy
Despite his ruthless rise, Cnut cultivated a pious image. He repaired churches damaged by Vikings, made lavish gifts to monasteries (Winchester, Evesham, Christ Church), and sent treasures to continental churches. His skalds composed poetry blending Christian themes with Norse mythology, comparing his rule to God's governance of heaven.

Cnut died in 1035, leaving an empire that fragmented immediately. His sons by two wives—Harold Harefoot (by Ælfgifu of Northampton) and Harthacnut (by Emma)—fought for succession. The North Sea Empire dissolved within a decade, but Cnut's reign showed that a Scandinavian ruler could effectively govern England, leaving a legacy of Anglo-Danish integration.

## Terms
- **North Sea Empire**: The collective name for Cnut's realms of England, Denmark, and Norway, united under his personal rule.
- **Danegeld**: A tax levied to raise money for tribute or military campaigns; Cnut collected £82,500 in 1018.
- **Earldom**: A major administrative division of England; Cnut replaced the Anglo-Saxon "ealdorman" title with the Scandinavian "earl."
- **Pallium**: A woolen band bestowed by the Pope on archbishops as a symbol of their authority; Cnut negotiated reduced fees for English archbishops.
- **Skald**: A court poet in Scandinavian tradition; Cnut employed several who composed verses praising his rule.
- **Witan**: The Anglo-Saxon council of advisors to the king; Cnut maintained this institution.
- **Jomsvikings**: A legendary order of Viking mercenaries; Thorkell the Tall, a Jomsviking chief, served Cnut.
- **Heregeld**: An annual tax for army maintenance; Cnut continued this Anglo-Saxon system.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Mother's identity**: Sources conflict—Norse sagas name her Gunhild (daughter of a Wendish king), while Continental sources suggest a Polish princess. Mainstream scholarship views her as semi-legendary or a composite.
- **Birth date**: Unknown; estimates range from c. 980 to 1000 based on conflicting evidence about his age during early campaigns.
- **Religious sincerity**: Scholars debate whether Cnut's piety was genuine devotion or political strategy to legitimize his rule over Christian England.
- **Empire's viability**: Historians question whether the North Sea Empire could have endured if Cnut's children had lived longer, potentially creating a permanent Anglo-Scandinavian union.
