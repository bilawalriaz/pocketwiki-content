# Constantine the Great

## Overview
Constantine I (272–337) was the first Roman emperor to convert to Christianity, fundamentally altering the empire's religious and political landscape. His reign marked the transition from classical antiquity to the Middle Ages, as he legalized Christianity, founded Constantinople as a new eastern capital, and reunified the empire after decades of civil war.

## Timeline
- **272** — Born in Naissus (modern Niš, Serbia) to Flavius Constantius and Helena.
- **306** — Proclaimed *augustus* (emperor) by his army at Eboracum (York) after his father's death.
- **312** — Defeated Maxentius at the Battle of the Milvian Bridge, becoming ruler of the West.
- **313** — Issued the Edict of Milan with Licinius, legalizing Christianity and all religions.
- **324** — Defeated Licinius, becoming sole emperor of the Roman Empire.
- **325** — Convened the First Council of Nicaea, producing the Nicene Creed.
- **330** — Officially dedicated Constantinople as the new eastern capital.
- **337** — Died at Achyron near Nicomedia; baptized on his deathbed.

## Body

### Rise to Power
Constantine was the son of Flavius Constantius, a *caesar* (junior emperor) in the Tetrarchy system established by Diocletian. After serving in the eastern provinces, he was proclaimed emperor by his father's troops in Britain in 306. He spent the next several years consolidating power in the West, fighting Germanic tribes along the Rhine and navigating the complex politics of the Tetrarchy. His legitimacy was initially contested, as he was granted only the title *caesar* rather than *augustus* by the senior emperor Galerius.

### Civil Wars and Unification
Constantine's path to sole rule involved defeating two major rivals. In 312, he marched into Italy and defeated Maxentius at the Milvian Bridge, a victory he attributed to the Christian God after reportedly seeing a vision of the Chi-Rho symbol. He then allied with Licinius, who ruled the East, but their partnership deteriorated into war. After victories at Adrianople, the Hellespont, and Chrysopolis in 324, Constantine defeated Licinius and became the undisputed ruler of the entire empire.

### Religious Transformation
Constantine's relationship with Christianity evolved throughout his life. He issued the Edict of Milan in 313, which ended persecution and restored Church property. He supported the Church financially, built major basilicas like Old St. Peter's and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, and convened the Council of Nicaea in 325 to settle doctrinal disputes, particularly Arianism. He promoted Christian clergy and symbols in public life but was not baptized until his deathbed, possibly to ensure absolution for sins committed during his reign.

### Administrative and Monetary Reforms
To strengthen the empire, Constantine separated civil and military authorities and reformed the bureaucracy. He introduced the *solidus*, a stable gold coin that became the standard for centuries, replacing debased currency. He also disbanded the Praetorian Guard and reorganized the army into mobile field units (*comitatenses*) and frontier garrisons (*limitanei*). In 326, he elevated many administrative positions to senatorial rank, reintegrating the old aristocracy into the government.

### Foundation of Constantinople
Recognizing the empire's shift eastward, Constantine rebuilt the Greek city of Byzantium as his new capital, Constantinople, dedicated in 330. Strategically located on the Bosphorus, it became a center of Christian learning and imperial power, serving as the capital of the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire for over a millennium.

### Later Years and Death
In his final years, Constantine campaigned against Goths and Sarmatians, reoccupying parts of the abandoned province of Dacia. He planned a major war against Persia but fell ill and died in 337. He was buried in Constantinople, and his empire was divided among his three sons.

## Terms
- **Tetrarchy**: A system of government established by Diocletian with two senior emperors (*augusti*) and two junior emperors (*caesars*).
- **Augustus**: The title for a senior Roman emperor.
- **Caesar**: The title for a junior emperor or designated heir in the Tetrarchy.
- **Edict of Milan**: A 313 decree that legalized Christianity and all religions in the Roman Empire.
- **Solidus**: A stable gold coin introduced by Constantine that became the standard currency for centuries.
- **Comitatenses**: Mobile field units of the Roman army under Constantine's reorganization.
- **Limitanei**: Frontier-garrison troops of the Roman army under Constantine's reorganization.
- **Labarum**: A military standard bearing the Chi-Rho symbol, adopted by Constantine.
- **Arianism**: A Christian doctrine, declared heretical at Nicaea, that held Christ was created by and subordinate to God the Father.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate the sincerity and timing of Constantine's conversion to Christianity. Some view him as a lifelong Christian who concealed his faith for political advantage, while others see him as a pragmatic politician who adopted Christianity for its unifying potential. The nature of his reported vision before the Milvian Bridge—whether a genuine religious experience or a political fabrication—remains contested. Additionally, the extent to which his religious policies were motivated by personal belief versus imperial statecraft is a central theme in modern historiography.
