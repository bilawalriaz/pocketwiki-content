# Nigerian Civil War

## Overview
The Nigerian Civil War (1967–1970) was a devastating conflict between the Nigerian federal government and the secessionist Republic of Biafra, primarily fought by the Igbo ethnic group. It resulted from deep-seated ethnic, political, and economic tensions exacerbated by colonial rule, leading to a brutal war marked by a catastrophic famine that killed up to two million civilians. The conflict highlighted the challenges of post-colonial nation-building in Africa and had a profound impact on international humanitarian response.

## Timeline
- **1 Oct 1960** — Nigeria gains independence from the United Kingdom.
- **15 Jan 1966** — Military coup led by Maj. Chukuma Kaduna Nzeogwu kills northern leaders.
- **29 Jul 1966** — Counter-coup by northern officers; Lt. Col. Yakubu Gowon becomes head of state.
- **Jun–Oct 1966** — Pogroms in Northern Nigeria kill an estimated 10,000–30,000 Igbos.
- **27 May 1967** — Gowon divides Nigeria into 12 states, splitting the Eastern Region.
- **30 May 1967** — Lt. Col. Odumegwu Ojukwu declares the independence of the Republic of Biafra.
- **6 Jul 1967** — Nigerian federal forces launch a "police action" to retake Biafra; war begins.
- **19 May 1968** — Nigerian forces capture Port Harcourt, a key Biafran city and oil hub.
- **Sep 1968** — Nigerian "final offensive" begins; blockade leads to mass starvation.
- **7 Jan 1970** — Nigerian "Operation Tail-Wind" final offensive begins.
- **13 Jan 1970** — Ojukwu flees to Ivory Coast; his deputy Philip Effiong surrenders.

## Body

### Background and Causes
The war's roots lie in the 1914 British amalgamation of the Northern and Southern Protectorates, which merged diverse ethnic and religious groups without addressing their differences. At independence in 1960, Nigeria was divided into three regions dominated by the Hausa-Fulani (North), Yoruba (West), and Igbo (East). Political power was structured around these regional/ethnic blocs, with the North holding a population majority and thus a majority of federal seats. Economic disparities grew, especially after oil was discovered in the Eastern Region in 1956. Tensions exploded with a military coup in January 1966, led mostly by Igbo officers, which killed northern political leaders. A counter-coup in July 1966 brought Lt. Col. Yakubu Gowon, a northern Christian, to power. This was followed by anti-Igbo pogroms in the North, killing tens of thousands and displacing millions to the Eastern Region. The Eastern leadership, led by Lt. Col. Odumegwu Ojukwu, concluded the federal government could not protect the Igbo, leading to secession.

### The War and Humanitarian Catastrophe
The war began on 6 July 1967. Nigerian forces, supported by the UK and Soviet Union, initially advanced but faced stiff Biafran resistance. A Biafran counter-offensive briefly captured the Mid-Western Region. By 1968, Nigerian forces had encircled most of Biafra, capturing key cities like Enugu and Port Harcourt. A federal blockade of the region led to a catastrophic famine. The Biafran government reported genocide, and images of starving children broadcast globally created a major humanitarian crisis. The Biafran airlift, organized by NGOs and private groups, attempted to break the blockade with food and medicine. The war saw the use of foreign mercenaries and international arms, with France and others covertly supporting Biafra. The conflict ended in January 1970 after a final Nigerian offensive, with Biafran forces surrendering.

### International Involvement and Legacy
The war was a proxy conflict. The UK and Soviet Union backed Nigeria to protect oil interests and influence, while France, Ivory Coast, and Gabon supported Biafra. The United States maintained official neutrality. The televised famine made the war a global cause célèbre, boosting the profile of NGOs and inspiring the formation of Médecins Sans Frontières (Doctors Without Borders). The war's legacy includes the political and economic marginalization of the Igbo people in Nigeria, the rise of Igbo nationalism, and ongoing secessionist movements like the Indigenous People of Biafra (IPOB). It also challenged pan-African unity and exposed the Organization of African Unity's limitations.

## Terms
- **Pogrom**: A massacre of a particular ethnic or religious group. The 1966 pogroms against Igbos in Northern Nigeria were a direct trigger for secession.
- **Secession**: The act of withdrawing formally from membership of a federation or body, especially a political state. Biafra's declaration of independence was an act of secession.
- **Blockade**: An act of sealing off a place to prevent goods or people from entering or leaving. The Nigerian federal blockade of Biafra was a key military strategy that caused mass starvation.
- **Kwashiorkor**: A form of severe protein malnutrition, a major cause of death during the Biafran famine.
- **Pan-Africanism**: A worldwide movement that aims to encourage and strengthen bonds of solidarity between all indigenous and diaspora people of African descent. The war highlighted its challenges.
- **Organisation of African Unity (OAU)**: A continental body established in 1963 to promote unity and solidarity among African states. It opposed Biafra's secession.
- **Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF)**: An international humanitarian medical non-governmental organization best known for its projects in conflict zones. It was founded in response to the Biafran War.
- **Nigerianisation**: A policy to replace foreign (primarily British) officials with Nigerians in government and the military after independence.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Genocide Debate**: Scholars and activists argue the war constituted genocide against the Igbo people, citing the 1966 pogroms and the deliberate starvation policy. Critics contend the Nigerian state's goal was to preserve the federation, not exterminate a group, and that the Igbo were not eliminated after the war.
- **Responsibility for the Famine**: While the Nigerian government's blockade was the primary cause, debates exist about the extent to which Biafran authorities also mismanaged resources and used the famine for propaganda.
- **Impact of Foreign Intervention**: The decisive role of British military support for Nigeria is widely acknowledged, but the scale and effectiveness of French and other support for Biafra remain debated.
- **Legacy of Marginalization**: There is ongoing debate about the extent to which the Igbo remain politically and economically marginalized in post-war Nigeria, and whether this constitutes a continuation of wartime persecution by other means.
