# Microeconomics

## Overview
Microeconomics is the branch of economics that analyzes how individuals and firms allocate scarce resources and interact in specific markets to determine relative prices. It contrasts with macroeconomics, which studies economy-wide aggregates like growth and unemployment. The field builds models—starting from assumptions of rational, utility-maximizing agents—to explain market mechanisms, identify conditions for efficient outcomes (Pareto optimality), and diagnose market failures such as externalities or public goods that create deadweight loss. Since the Lucas critique (1970s), modern macroeconomics has relied on these microfoundations to ground aggregate models in individual behavior.

## Timeline
- **1874** — Léon Walras publishes *Elements of Pure Economics*, developing general equilibrium theory.
- **1890** — Alfred Marshall publishes *Principles of Economics*, introducing partial equilibrium theory.
- **1933** — Ragnar Frisch distinguishes "micro-dynamic" and "macro-dynamic" analysis, originating the micro/macro split.
- **1941** — Pieter de Wolff uses the term "microeconomics" in print for the first time.
- **1953/1970** — Ronald Shephard develops duality theory, linking cost functions to production technology.
- **1970s** — Lucas critique prompts macroeconomics to adopt microfoundations based on individual optimization.

## Body

### Foundational Assumptions and Consumer Theory
Microeconomic theory typically begins with a **rational** agent: one possessing stable preferences that are **complete** (any two bundles can be ranked) and **transitive** (if A > B and B > C, then A > C). A technical assumption of **continuous** preferences ensures a **utility function** exists to represent these rankings; **local non-satiation (LNS)** guarantees the agent always prefers a marginally better bundle, preventing "thick" indifference curves. With a **budget constraint** (a bounded, closed subset of the consumption set), the **Utility Maximization Problem (UMP)** becomes a constrained optimization problem. The **Extreme Value Theorem** guarantees a solution exists, yielding a **Walrasian demand function** mapping prices and income to optimal quantities. An alternative approach, **revealed preference theory**, takes observed choices as primitive and derives preferences from them, avoiding the need to assume a utility function *a priori*.

### Production, Costs, and Value
**Production theory** studies the conversion of inputs (labor, capital, land) into outputs. The **cost-of-production theory of value** posits that price equals the sum of resource costs (factors of production plus taxation). In the short run, **total cost = fixed cost (FC) + variable cost (VC)**; FC (e.g., rent) does not vary with output, while VC (e.g., raw materials) does. Over longer horizons, more costs become variable. **Sunk costs** are incurred FC that cannot be recovered (e.g., pharmaceutical R&D). **Opportunity cost**—the value of the next-best alternative forgone—is the true economic cost of any action, independent of the number of other alternatives. **Duality theory** (Shephard, 1953/1970) formally links the cost function to the underlying production technology.

### Price Theory and Supply-Demand Mechanics
**Price theory** (associated with the Chicago School) uses the supply-demand framework to generate testable hypotheses about competitive equilibrium, emphasizing competition over strategic interaction (game theory). In a **perfectly competitive market** (many buyers/sellers, identical products, no price-setting power, perfect information), the **law of demand** (inverse price-quantity relationship driven by substitution and income effects) and **law of supply** (positive price-quantity relationship) intersect at **market equilibrium**. At this point, **marginal utility** (demand side) equals **marginal cost** (supply side), maximizing total surplus. **Elasticity** of supply differs in the short run (some inputs fixed) versus the long run (all inputs adjustable). The model extends to **factor markets** (e.g., labor), where wages equate labor demand (derived from product demand) and labor supply.

### Market Structures and Strategic Interaction
Market structure varies by number of firms, product differentiation, entry barriers, and competition type.
*   **Perfect competition:** Many firms, identical products, price takers, socially optimal output at minimum average cost.
*   **Monopolistic competition:** Many firms, differentiated products; higher costs than perfect competition but variety benefits consumers.
*   **Oligopoly:** Few dominant firms (e.g., cola, video games); strategic interdependence analyzed via **game theory** (Nash equilibrium, collusion/cartels). **Duopoly** is the two-firm special case.
*   **Monopoly:** Single seller; restricts output below social optimum to raise price, unless a **natural monopoly** exists where one firm minimizes cost due to economies of scale.
*   **Monopsony/Oligopsony:** One/few buyers, many sellers (e.g., single employer town); buyer exerts wage-setting power.
*   **Bilateral monopoly:** Single buyer faces single seller.

### Information Economics and Applied Fields
**Information economics** relaxes the "complete information" assumption. Information is easy to create/spread but hard to trust/control, leading to **asymmetric information** problems (adverse selection, moral hazard) that complicate standard theories. **Applied microeconomics** spans specialized fields: **Industrial organization** (firm entry/exit, innovation); **Labor economics** (wages, inequality, immigration); **Public economics** (tax/expenditure design); **Health/education/urban/financial economics**; **Law and economics** (efficiency of legal rules); and **Political economy** (institutions and policy outcomes).

## Terms
- ****Rationality**** — Preferences are complete (all bundles comparable) and transitive (consistent ranking).
- ****Utility Maximization Problem (UMP)**** — Constrained optimization: max utility subject to budget constraint; foundation of consumer theory.
- ****Walrasian Demand**** — The solution function of the UMP mapping prices/income to optimal consumption bundles.
- ****Local Non-Satiation (LNS)**** — For any bundle, a slightly better one exists arbitrarily close; ensures budget constraint binds.
- ****Opportunity Cost**** — Value of the next-best alternative forgone; the true economic cost of a choice.
- ****Sunk Cost**** — A fixed cost already incurred and unrecoverable; irrelevant for forward-looking decisions.
- ****Deadweight Loss**** — Loss of total surplus (consumer + producer) when market equilibrium is not Pareto efficient.
- ****Natural Monopoly**** — Industry where one firm can produce total output at lower cost than multiple firms due to economies of scale.
- ****Game Theory**** — Mathematical modeling of strategic interaction among rational agents; used for oligopolies, auctions, bargaining.
- ****Asymmetric Information**** — One party in a transaction possesses superior information, leading to market inefficiencies (adverse selection, moral hazard).

## Debates and open questions
*   **Microfoundations vs. Aggregation:** Whether macroeconomic models built strictly from optimizing micro-agents (post-Lucas critique) adequately capture coordination failures, heterogeneity, or bounded rationality observed in aggregate data.
*   **Market Failure Remedies:** The optimal policy response to externalities/public goods—direct government provision, Pigouvian taxes/subsidies, regulation, or creating "missing markets" (Coase theorem)—remains contested, especially regarding transaction costs and government failure.
*   **Behavioral Validity:** The standard assumptions of rationality, transitive preferences, and exponential discounting are challenged by behavioral economics (e.g., hyperbolic discounting, reference dependence), raising questions about the positive vs. normative scope of micro theory.
*   **Information and Complexity:** How to model the costs of acquiring/processing information (rational inattention) and the stability of equilibria when agents have heterogeneous beliefs or learning dynamics.
