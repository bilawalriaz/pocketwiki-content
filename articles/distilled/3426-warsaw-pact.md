# Warsaw Pact

## Overview
The Warsaw Pact was a collective defence treaty and military alliance formed in May 1955 by the Soviet Union and seven Eastern Bloc socialist republics. It served as the military counterpart to the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance (Comecon) and was established as a direct counterweight to the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) following West Germany's integration into NATO. Dominated by the Soviet Union, the Pact's primary function was to safeguard Soviet hegemony over Eastern Europe, with its only direct military actions being invasions of its own member states.

## Body

### Formation and Purpose
The Warsaw Pact was created in response to the rearmament and integration of West Germany into NATO. Soviet leaders, fearing a renewed German military threat, had previously proposed a pan-European security treaty or sought to join NATO itself, but these offers were rejected by Western powers. The Pact was signed on 14 May 1955 by the USSR, Albania, Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia, East Germany, Hungary, Poland, and Romania. It was the military complement to the economic Comecon and was intended to balance NATO's power. Despite its collective defence charter, the Pact was dominated by the Soviet Union, which controlled its military command structure.

### Cold War Operations and Internal Conflicts
For 36 years, NATO and the Warsaw Pact never directly fought each other. The conflict was ideological and fought through proxy wars. The Pact's largest military engagement was the 1968 invasion of Czechoslovakia to suppress the Prague Spring, in which all members participated except Romania and Albania. Albania withdrew from the Pact a month later. Romania, however, maintained a unique position of de facto independence from the early 1960s, refusing Soviet military integration and pursuing an independent foreign policy. The Soviet Union also used the Pact to enforce its will, as seen in the 1956 invasion of Hungary.

### Dissolution and Aftermath
The Pact began to unravel with the spread of the 1989 Revolutions in Eastern Europe, starting with Poland's Solidarity movement and the Pan-European Picnic. Communist governments fell across the bloc, and East Germany withdrew following German reunification in 1990. The remaining members formally disbanded the alliance on 25 February 1991. In the following decades, most former Warsaw Pact states joined NATO, while Russia and several post-Soviet states formed the Collective Security Treaty Organization (CSTO).

## Terms
*   **Warsaw Pact (WP):** A collective defence treaty and military alliance between the Soviet Union and seven Eastern Bloc states, active from 1955 to 1991.
*   **Comecon:** The Council for Mutual Economic Assistance, the economic organization for the Eastern Bloc states, for which the Warsaw Pact was the military complement.
*   **NATO:** The North Atlantic Treaty Organization, the Western military alliance formed in 1949 that the Warsaw Pact was created to counter.
*   **Satellite state:** A country that is formally independent but under heavy political, economic, and military influence or control by another country, in this case, the USSR.
*   **Brezhnev Doctrine:** The Soviet policy of intervening in socialist countries to prevent them from leaving the Soviet sphere of influence, used to justify the 1968 invasion of Czechoslovakia.
*   **Prague Spring:** A period of political liberalization in Czechoslovakia in 1968, which was ended by the Warsaw Pact invasion.
*   **Collective Security Treaty Organization (CSTO):** A Russia-led military alliance formed in 1992 by several post-Soviet states after the USSR's dissolution.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Was the Pact "superfluous"?** Given the USSR's existing political and military domination of Eastern Europe by 1955, some historians and NATO officials considered the formal treaty redundant, labeling it a "cardboard castle."
*   **Romania's Independence:** Scholars debate whether Romania's relationship with the Soviet Union is best described as "independent" or "autonomous," given its continued membership in the Pact and Comecon, though its actions consistently defied Soviet directives.
*   **Soviet Intentions:** The true nature of Soviet proposals to join NATO in 1954 remains debated—whether they were a serious attempt at collective security or a diplomatic maneuver to undermine Western unity.
