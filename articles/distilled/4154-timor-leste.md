# Timor-Leste

## Overview
Timor-Leste, or East Timor, is a young Southeast Asian nation comprising the eastern half of Timor island, the Oecusse exclave, and the islands of Atauro and Jaco. It is notable for its violent struggle for independence from Indonesia, which ended in 2002, making it the first new sovereign state of the 21st century. The country faces significant challenges in economic development, heavily reliant on dwindling oil revenues, while maintaining a unique cultural identity shaped by Austronesian, Melanesian, and Portuguese influences.

## Timeline
- **16th century** — Portuguese influence begins on the island of Timor.
- **1769** — Portuguese found the city of Dili, marking effective European occupation in the east.
- **1914** — Permanent Court of Arbitration establishes the border between Portuguese and Dutch Timor.
- **1942-1945** — Japanese occupation during WWII; guerrilla campaign (Battle of Timor) kills 40,000-70,000 Timorese.
- **1975** — Portugal withdraws; civil war erupts. Fretilin declares independence on 28 November.
- **1975** — Indonesia invades on 7 December.
- **1976** — Indonesia annexes East Timor as its 27th province.
- **1999** — UN-sponsored referendum leads to vote for independence. Pro-Indonesian militias launch violent campaign.
- **2002** — Timor-Leste becomes independent on 20 May; Xanana Gusmão elected first president.
- **2006** — Crisis of unrest forces 155,000 to flee; UN security forces intervene.
- **2012** — UN peacekeeping mission ends.
- **2022** — José Ramos-Horta elected president again. Timor-Leste participates in ASEAN summit as a full member.

## Body

### History and Independence
Timor was settled by Papuan and Austronesian peoples, developing a society of small polities under customary law. Portuguese colonial rule, established in the 16th century, was largely neglectful until the late 19th century. Following the 1974 Portuguese revolution, internal conflict led to a unilateral independence declaration by Fretilin in 1975. Indonesia invaded weeks later, fearing a communist state. The subsequent occupation (1975-1999) was marked by extreme violence, with a minimum of 102,800 conflict-related deaths. Resistance continued, and a 1999 UN referendum saw a clear vote for independence, triggering a punitive militia campaign. A UN administration (UNTAET) oversaw the transition to full sovereignty in 2002.

### Government and Politics
Timor-Leste is a semi-presidential republic. The president, directly elected for a five-year term, holds limited formal powers but significant informal influence. The prime minister, appointed by the president from the parliamentary majority, leads the government. The unicameral National Parliament is elected via a party-list system. Politics are dominated by personalities from the independence era, notably the Fretilin and CNRT parties. The country is divided into 14 municipalities, with the Oecusse exclave having special autonomous status. Despite political rhetoric, democratic institutions have functioned, with peaceful changes of government.

### Economy and Resources
The economy is a market system dependent on oil and gas exports, with the US dollar as currency. The Petroleum Fund, established in 2005, holds wealth from non-renewable resources but is at risk of exhaustion as reserves deplete. Over 80% of government spending comes from this fund. The country is poor, with over 40% living below the national poverty line. Agriculture, mainly subsistence farming and coffee exports, is underdeveloped. The private sector is small, and economic growth has been slow, averaging 2.5% annually from 2011-2021. The economy shows aspects of a "resource curse," with other sectors failing to develop.

### Demographics and Society
The population was 1,341,737 in the 2022 census, with a very young median age under 20. There is high ethnic and linguistic diversity, with 30 indigenous languages from Austronesian and Papuan families. Portuguese and Tetum are official languages, with English and Indonesian as working languages. The population is predominantly Roman Catholic (97.6% in 2022), a faith that grew dramatically during Indonesian rule as a form of resistance. Literacy has improved, but malnutrition and poverty remain widespread, particularly in rural areas.

### Culture
Timorese culture blends Austronesian and Melanesian traditions with Portuguese and Catholic influences. The concept of *lulik* (sacredness) is central, with community life organized around sacred houses (*Uma Lulik*). Traditional leaders retain influence in dispute resolution. Art forms include the handwoven *tais* textiles, with patterns specific to different communities. A strong oral tradition of poetry and history (*Lia nain*) persists. National identity, forged during resistance, continues to evolve in the post-independence era.

## Terms
- **Suco**: A traditional village or administrative unit, often linked to community identity and kinship.
- **Lulik**: A concept of sacredness central to traditional Timorese belief and practice.
- **Uma Lulik**: A sacred house, a physical and symbolic center of community life and identity.
- **Tais**: Traditional handwoven textiles with patterns specific to different communities.
- **Lia nain**: The oral tradition of reciting long stories or poetry, passing down traditional knowledge.
- **Petroleum Fund**: Timor-Leste's sovereign wealth fund, established in 2005 to manage oil and gas revenues.
- **Resource curse**: The paradox where countries with abundant natural resources often experience less economic growth and worse development outcomes than countries with fewer resources.
- **Mestiço**: A person of mixed Portuguese and local Timorese descent.
- **Lurai**: A traditional ruler of a kingdom in pre-colonial Timor.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The total number of conflict-related deaths during the Indonesian occupation (1974-1999) is difficult to determine, with estimates ranging from a minimum of 102,800 to as high as 200,000.
- The sustainability of the Petroleum Fund is a major concern, as withdrawals have exceeded sustainable levels almost every year since 2009, and the undeveloped Greater Sunrise field presents technical and political challenges.
- The implementation of decentralization remains debated, with administrative powers still largely centralized in Dili despite constitutional mentions of it.
- The long-term viability of the economy beyond oil revenues is uncertain, as non-oil sectors have failed to develop significantly.
