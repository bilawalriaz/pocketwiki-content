# Asthma

## Overview
Asthma is a common, chronic inflammatory disease of the airways characterized by variable, recurring symptoms—wheezing, coughing, chest tightness, and shortness of breath—and reversible airflow obstruction. It arises from a complex interplay of genetic susceptibility and environmental exposures (allergens, pollution, infections), resulting in airway hyperresponsiveness, mucus hypersecretion, and structural remodeling. While incurable, asthma is controllable through trigger avoidance and pharmacotherapy, primarily inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) with add-on therapies for severe disease. In 2023, asthma affected an estimated 363 million people globally and caused 442,000 deaths, with a disproportionate burden in low- and middle-income countries.

## Timeline
- **Ancient Egypt** — Condition recognized; treated with incense mixture (kyphi).
- **c. 450 BC** — Hippocrates names asthma as a specific respiratory problem (Greek *âsthma*, "panting").
- **12th century** — Maimonides writes *Treatise on Asthma*, emphasizing clean air and diet.
- **1905** — Epinephrine (adrenaline) first referenced as asthma treatment.
- **1930s–1950s** — Asthma classified as a "holy seven" psychosomatic illness; treated with psychoanalysis.
- **1940s** — Isoprenaline synthesized; first pure, selective β-agonist bronchodilator.
- **1950s** — Pressurized metered-dose inhaler (pMDI) developed for adrenaline/isoproterenol.
- **1960s** — Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) and selective short-acting beta agonists (SABA) enter wide use; β2-adrenergic receptors identified in lungs.
- **1970–1985** — Rise in asthma mortality reported in multiple developed nations (ages 5–34); major epidemic in New Zealand.
- **2023** — Global prevalence estimated at 363 million; 442,000 deaths reported.

## Body

### Signs, Symptoms, and Comorbidities
Asthma manifests as recurrent episodes of wheezing, dyspnea, chest tightness, and coughing, typically worse at night, early morning, or with exercise/cold air. Symptom frequency ranges from rare (trigger-specific) to persistent. Asthma frequently coexists with comorbidities that worsen severity and complicate treatment, including allergic rhinitis/conjunctivitis, COPD, bronchiectasis, obstructive sleep apnea, obesity, GERD, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and anxiety/depression.

### Classification: Syndrome, Severity, and Endotypes
Due to heterogeneous onset, symptoms, and treatment response, asthma is considered a **syndrome** rather than a single disease. Historical *extrinsic* (allergic) vs. *intrinsic* (non-allergic) classification has been replaced by:
*   **Severity:** Defined by medication intensity required to control symptoms (mild = as-needed; severe = high-dose ICS/LABA or uncontrolled despite maximal therapy). Assessed only *after* control is achieved.
*   **Control:** Symptom burden and future risk (exacerbations, lung function decline), measured via questionnaires, rescue inhaler use, spirometry, FeNO, and eosinophil counts.
*   **Endotypes (mechanisms):** **T2-high** (Type 2 inflammation: eosinophilia, high FeNO, allergy; driven by IL-4/5/13) includes early-onset allergic, late-onset eosinophilic, and aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD). **T2-low** (non-Type 2: neutrophilic, paucigranulocytic, obesity-related, smoking-related) mechanisms are less defined.
*   **Phenotypes (presentation):** Clinical subgroups within endotypes (e.g., occupational asthma splits into irritant-induced [T2-low] and sensitizer-induced [T2-high]).
*   **Asthma-COPD Overlap (ACO):** Lacks consistent definition; difficult to assign to either endotype.

### Exacerbations
Acute worsening (attacks) features increased symptoms, airflow decline (PEF/FEV1), accessory muscle use, tachycardia, and hypoxemia. Severity grades: *Mild* (≥20% PEF drop >2 days); *Moderate* (50% PEF drop, no accessory muscles); *Severe* (restlessness, speech difficulty, >50% PEF drop, hypoxemia); *Life-threatening* (lethargy, confusion). Triggers include viral infections, allergens, pollution, seasonal changes, and poor ICS adherence. "Brittle asthma" (historical term for recurrent severe attacks) is no longer a formal diagnostic category.

### Causes: Gene-Environment Interaction
Asthma develops when genetic susceptibility meets environmental triggers.
*   **Early-life risks:** Maternal obesity/stress/smoking/medications/C-section; childhood secondhand smoke, respiratory infections (RSV, rhinovirus), indoor mold, and air pollution (NO2, traffic, biomass, dust mites). Grandmaternal smoking increases grandchild risk via nicotine-induced epigenetic changes.
*   **Hygiene hypothesis:** Early microbial exposures may protect against atopy; evidence is mixed.
*   **Urban vs. rural:** Urban settings confer higher risk (pollution, stress, less green space); rural settings may offer protection (microbial diversity, activity).
*   **Allergens:** Sensitization triggers symptoms; early exposure effects on allergy development are debated (desensitization vs. increased risk). Food allergy/atopic dermatitis in childhood predicts asthma ("atopic march").
*   **Adult-onset:** Driven by genetics, obesity, smoking, urban environment, and occupational exposures (>400 asthmagens). Prevalence shifts from male-dominant (childhood) to female-dominant (adult).
*   **Genetics:** Highly polygenic; ~50%+ heritability. GWAS identify >200 loci (immune/epithelial genes: *IL33, TSLP, GATA3, MHC-II*). **17q12–21** (*ORMDL3/GSDMB*) is the strongest replicated locus for childhood-onset. Risk variants are regulatory, cell-type-specific, and interact with environment (viruses, pollution). Polygenic risk scores (PRS) stratify childhood-onset risk better than adult-onset. Significant genetic overlap exists with eczema, allergic rhinitis, and lung function traits.

### Pathophysiology
Chronic airway inflammation (eosinophils, neutrophils, Th2 cells, mast cells, epithelium, smooth muscle) is universal. Inflammation drives **airway remodeling**: smooth muscle hypertrophy, subepithelial fibrosis, angiogenesis, neural hyperplasia, and mucus gland hyperplasia. **Airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR)**—excessive narrowing to benign stimuli—results from smooth muscle contraction, edema, remodeling, mucus plugs, and neural hypersensitivity. AHR causes variable airflow limitation and is partially reversible with treatment.

### Diagnosis
No single test confirms asthma; diagnosis integrates characteristic symptom patterns (variability, triggers, personal/family atopy) with objective evidence of variable expiratory airflow limitation.
*   **Spirometry (Gold standard):** Pre/post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC. ≥12% and ≥200mL FEV1 increase supports asthma.
*   **Peak Expiratory Flow (PEF) variability:** >10% diurnal variation over 2 weeks suggests asthma.
*   **Bronchial Provocation:** Methacholine/histamine/mannitol/exercise challenge tests AHR. High sensitivity; low specificity (positive in rhinitis, COPD, etc.). Negative test off ICS helps rule out asthma.
*   **FeNO/Blood Eosinophils:** NICE guidelines (adults) use these first; elevated levels support T2-high asthma.
*   **Pediatrics (<5 yrs):** GINA requires: (1) >2 symptom episodes or wheeze + interval symptoms; (2) no alternative diagnosis; (3) response to asthma meds. NICE recommends empirical treatment trial.
*   **Differential:** Varies by age (children: foreign body, CF, immunodeficiency, vocal cord dysfunction; adults: heart failure, COPD, GERD, ILD, pulmonary embolism). Many mimics coexist with asthma.

### Prevention
Guideline-endorsed strategies: avoid broad-spectrum antibiotics in infancy; eliminate prenatal/postnatal tobacco smoke; correct maternal vitamin D deficiency; prefer vaginal delivery. Diet, breastfeeding, supplements, and early pet/allergen exposure lack sufficient evidence for recommendation.

### Management
Goal: Symptom control, exacerbation prevention, preserved lung function, minimal side effects. Core components: assessment/monitoring, patient education (action plan, inhaler technique), trigger reduction, pharmacotherapy.
*   **Lifestyle:** Smoking cessation critical. Occupational sensitizer avoidance. No routine recommendation for broad allergen avoidance (impractical). Exercise encouraged (pre-treat with SABA if trigger); pulmonary rehab for exercise intolerance. Weight loss for obesity. No evidence for dietary supplements, acupuncture, breathing exercises (adjunctive only), or manual therapies.
*   **Pharmacotherapy (Stepwise):**
    *   *Controllers (Daily):* **ICS** (cornerstone anti-inflammatory). **ICS/LABA** (preferred for ≥12 yrs; formoterol allows maintenance-and-reliever therapy/MART). **LTRA** (montelukast), **LAMA** (tiotropium mist), **azithromycin** as add-ons.
    *   *Relievers (As-needed):* **SABA** (salbutamol) or **ICS/formoterol** (preferred reliever in MART). *Historical SABA-only monotherapy abandoned due to exacerbation risk.*
    *   *Severe/Uncontrolled:* **Biologics** (anti-IgE, anti-IL5/5R, anti-IL4Rα, anti-TSLP) target T2-high pathways. **Allergen immunotherapy** (SCIT/SLIT) for sensitized patients. **Oral corticosteroids (OCS)** or **bronchial thermoplasty** as last resorts.
    *   *Pediatrics:* <5 yrs: 8–12 wk low-dose ICS trial + SABA. 6–11 yrs: Low-dose ICS maintenance + SABA (or ICS whenever SABA used); step up to ICS/LABA or add LTRA.
*   **Exacerbation Management:** Repeated SABA (salbutamol) + oral corticosteroids (5–7 days) + oxygen. Add ipratropium for moderate/severe. IV magnesium/heliox not routinely recommended (rescue only).
*   **Adherence:** Barriers include side-effect concerns, misinformation, cost, inhaler technique. Digital interventions show promise.

### Outlook (Prognosis)
Onset usually childhood. Half of young children with viral wheeze remit; persistent symptoms predict remodeling, reduced lung function, and adult persistence. Mortality rare but >1,000 deaths/day globally (GBD); higher in low-income regions (access gaps). **2019 Burden:** 21.6M DALYs (34th globally); 10.2M YLDs (24th leading cause of disability); slight male predominance in disability burden.

### Epidemiology
2023: ~363M cases, 442K deaths. Prevalence 1–29% by country. Higher in developed nations, but within them, higher in disadvantaged groups; in developing nations, higher in affluent groups. >80% mortality in low/middle-income countries. Childhood: boys > girls; Adults: women > men. Overall burden slightly higher in women.

### History
Ancient Egypt (kyphi) → Hippocrates (naming) → Maimonides (12th C, environment/diet) → 1905 Epinephrine → 1930s–50s Psychosomatic era → 1940s Isoprenaline → 1950s pMDI → 1960s ICS/SABA/β2-receptor discovery → 1970–85 Mortality epidemic (New Zealand notable) → Modern phenotype/endotype/biologic era.

### Society, Culture, and Economics
**Disparities:** US burden falls on racial/ethnic minorities and poor. Black/Puerto Rican children highest prevalence. Black mortality 2x White (2021). Redlined neighborhoods: 2.4x ED visit rates (pollution, structural factors). **Economics:** Reduced QoL (fatigue, sleep, activity, cognition) → missed school/work. 2013 US cost: $81.9B. Developing world: severe/uncontrolled (10–20% of patients) drive ~50% of costs. Global burden exceeds TB + HIV/AIDS combined.

## Terms
- ****Airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR)**** — Excessive bronchoconstriction in response to stimuli that are harmless in healthy airways; causes variable airflow limitation.
- ****Endotype**** — A disease subtype defined by a distinct molecular/mechanistic pathway (e.g., T2-high vs. T2-low asthma).
- ****Phenotype**** — Observable clinical characteristics of a disease (e.g., age of onset, symptom pattern, comorbidity profile).
- ****FEV1 / FVC**** — Forced Expiratory Volume in 1 second / Forced Vital Capacity; ratio (FEV1/FVC) diagnoses obstruction.
- ****FeNO**** — Fractional exhaled Nitric Oxide; non-invasive biomarker of Type 2 airway inflammation.
- ****ICS / LABA / LAMA / SABA**** — Inhaled Corticosteroid / Long-Acting Beta2-Agonist / Long-Acting Muscarinic Antagonist / Short-Acting Beta2-Agonist.
- ****MART**** — Maintenance and Reliever Therapy: single ICS/formoterol inhaler used for both daily control and as-needed relief.
- ****Remodeling**** — Structural airway changes (smooth muscle hypertrophy, fibrosis, angiogenesis, mucus gland hyperplasia) from chronic inflammation.
- ****Atopic march**** — Typical progression from infantile atopic dermatitis/food allergy to allergic rhinitis and asthma in childhood.
- ****Polygenic Risk Score (PRS)**** — Aggregate genetic risk score from GWAS variants; stratifies asthma susceptibility, especially childhood-onset.

## Debates and open questions
*   **T2-low endotype mechanisms:** Poorly defined; lack targeted biologics; neutrophilic, paucigranulocytic, and obesity-related pathways need elucidation.
*   **ACO definition:** No consensus diagnostic criteria; unclear if distinct entity or comorbidity; hampers classification and treatment.
*   **Hygiene hypothesis / early allergen exposure:** Conflicting data on whether early microbial/allergen exposure protects against or promotes atopy/asthma.
*   **Prevention evidence gaps:** No guideline-endorsed dietary, probiotic, or supplement strategies; vitamin D role in pregnancy needs confirmation.
*   **PRS clinical utility:** Polygenic scores stratify risk in research but lack validation for routine screening or treatment selection.
*   **Bronchial thermoplasty / OCS-sparing:** Long-term safety/efficacy of thermoplasty debated; optimal biologic sequencing and OCS withdrawal protocols in severe asthma unresolved.
*   **Global mortality disparity:** >80% deaths in LMICs; extent due to underdiagnosis, undertreatment (ICS access), pollution, or phenotype differences unclear.
