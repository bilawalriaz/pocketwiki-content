# Day

## Overview
A day is the period of one full rotation of Earth relative to the Sun, averaging 24 hours (86,400 seconds). This cycle drives circadian rhythms in organisms and underpins human calendars, which organize sequential days into weeks, months, and years. While the civil day conventionally begins at midnight, other traditions (e.g., Jewish, astronomical) start the day at sunset or noon. Precise timekeeping distinguishes between the variable *solar day* (Sun-to-Sun), the constant *SI day* (exactly 86,400 seconds), and the *sidereal day* (rotation relative to distant stars, ~23h 56m). Earth's rotation is gradually slowing due to tidal friction, necessitating occasional leap seconds in Coordinated Universal Time (UTC). There are currently about 365.2421875 solar days in one mean tropical year.

## Timeline
- **c. 4.5 Ga** — Earth forms; estimated day length ~6 hours.
- **19th century (mid)** — Railroads drive adoption of standard time zones based on central meridians.
- **1884** — International Meridian Conference resolves: "That the Conference expresses the hope that as soon as may be practicable the astronomical and nautical days will be arranged everywhere to begin at midnight."
- **1926** — Turkey adopts midnight-based (French) time system, replacing sunset-based (Turkish) system.
- **1929** — Most major countries have adopted time zones.
- **1972–2022** — 27 leap seconds inserted into UTC to account for rotational slowing (~1 every 2 years).
- **2015** — 40 time zones in use globally, referenced to UTC+00.

## Body

### Definitions and Reference Frames
The word "day" derives from Old English *dæġ* (Proto-Germanic *dagaz*). Several distinct definitions exist:
*   **Solar day (apparent):** Time for the Sun to return to the local meridian (culmination). Varies by ±7.9 seconds from 24 hours due to Earth's orbital eccentricity (Kepler's second law) and axial tilt. In recent decades, the average length has been about 86,400.002 seconds.
*   **Mean solar day:** Based on a fictitious "mean Sun" moving at constant speed along the celestial equator, removing annual variation. The length of the day defined by the Sun passing the local meridian is nearly constant (24 hours ± 30 seconds).
*   **Sidereal (stellar) day:** One full rotation relative to distant stars (~23h 56m 4.09s). Shorter than a solar day because Earth must rotate ~1° extra to "catch up" to the Sun after orbiting ~1° along its path. ~366.2422 sidereal days per tropical year vs. ~365.2422 solar days.
*   **SI day:** Defined as exactly 86,400 SI seconds (symbol *d*). Not an SI base unit; prior to 2026 it was described as accepted for use with SI.
*   **Civil day:** Legal convention, usually midnight-to-midnight, standardized within time zones (offsets from UTC). Daylight saving time creates one 23-hour and one 25-hour civil day annually in adopting regions.

### Variations in Length
Earth's rotation decelerates primarily due to **tidal deceleration** (Moon's gravitational friction), lengthening the mean solar day by ~2 milliseconds per century. This desynchronizes the SI second (atomic standard) from the rotational second, requiring **leap seconds** inserted into UTC by the IERS. Between 1972–2022, 27 positive leap seconds were added (making those days 86,401 seconds); negative leap seconds (86,399 seconds) are possible but unused. Geological estimates from coral growth rings (John W. Wells) show day length increasing from ~6 hours at formation to present, following a curve attributed to changing water volume (Arbab I. Arbab).

### Boundaries and Cultural Conventions
*   **Midnight (00:00):** Standard civil convention (ancient Romans, Chinese, modern ISO/International Meridian Conference 1884).
*   **Sunset/Nightfall:** Jewish religious calendar; medieval European *Florentine reckoning* (explaining "Eve" holidays like Christmas Eve).
*   **Sunrise:** Ancient Egypt.
*   **Noon:** Astronomy (historically), so a single night's observations share one date.
*   **Dawn:** Natural boundary for diurnal animals.

### Parts of the Day
*   **Daytime:** Sun above horizon (direct light). Averages ~7 minutes >12 hours due to Sun's angular diameter (~32') and atmospheric refraction (~34'), allowing light when Sun's center is ~50' below horizon.
*   **Twilight:** Indirect light (Sun below horizon). Subdivided by solar depression angle: *civil* (0–6°), *nautical* (6–12°), *astronomical* (12–18°). Dawn = morning twilight; dusk = evening twilight.
*   **Night:** Full darkness (Sun >18° below horizon).
*   **Cultural divisions:** E.g., Brazil uses four 6-hour blocks: *madrugada* (0–6), *manhã* (6–12), *tarde* (12–18), *noite* (18–24).

## Terms
- ****Solar day**** — Time between successive solar culminations (Sun at highest point); varies due to orbital eccentricity and axial tilt.
- ****Mean solar day**** — Average solar day length, defined by a fictitious "mean Sun" moving uniformly on the celestial equator.
- ****Sidereal day**** — One full Earth rotation relative to distant stars (~23h 56m 4.09s); used in astronomy.
- ****SI day**** — Exactly 86,400 SI seconds; the standard for computing and metrology (symbol *d*).
- ****Civil day**** — Legal day, typically midnight-to-midnight, governed by time zones (UTC offsets).
- ****Leap second**** — Extra second inserted into UTC (by IERS) to keep atomic time aligned with slowing Earth rotation.
- ****Tidal deceleration**** — Gradual slowing of Earth's rotation caused by Moon's gravitational friction; lengthens day ~2 ms/century.
- ****Circadian rhythm**** — Biological ~24-hour cycle in organisms driven by the day/night cycle.
- ****Florentine reckoning**** — Medieval system counting day from sunset; "two hours into the day" = two hours post-sunset.
- ****Nychthemeron**** — A full 24-hour cycle (Greek *nykt-* night + *hēmera* day).

## Debates and open questions
*   **Leap second future:** Whether to abolish leap seconds (decoupling civil time from Earth rotation) remains an active international metrological debate; the source notes their irregular placement due to non-tidal rotational factors.
*   **SI status:** The day's status as "accepted for use with SI" was slated to change in 2026; the long-term role of the day in the SI system is unresolved in the source.
*   **Geological model precision:** While coral rings confirm ancient shorter days, the exact curve of day-length increase (attributed to water volume changes by Arbab) involves modeling uncertainties.
