# Narration

## Overview
Narration is the use of commentary to convey a story, delivered by a narrator—a specific voice or character who presents the plot (the series of events) to an audience. It is a required element in all written stories but optional in other formats like film or games, where story can be told through dialogue or action. The choices made in narration, collectively called the narrative mode, determine who tells the story and how it is told, fundamentally shaping the audience's experience.

## Body

### Narrative Mode and Point of View
The narrative mode encompasses all choices a creator makes to develop narration, including point of view, tense, and technique. Narrative point of view is the storyteller's position relative to the story, often defined by grammatical person. A key distinction is between first-person (intradiegetic, where the narrator is part of the story) and third-person (extradiegetic, where the narrator is outside it). Literary theorist Boris Uspenskij identifies five planes for point of view: spatial, temporal, psychological, phraseological, and ideological. The ideological point of view, focusing on a narrator's worldview, is considered the most basic but hardest to formalize.

### First-, Second-, and Third-Person Narration
First-person narration uses "I" or "we," creating a close relationship between the narrator and reader. Second-person narration uses "you," creating distance and often irony; it is not a direct address to the reader but a stylistic choice, as in Italo Calvino's *If on a winter's night a traveler*. Third-person narration uses "he" or "she." It can be omniscient, where the narrator knows everything about the story world and characters' thoughts (common in 19th-century fiction), or limited, confined to one character's perspective. Some works use multiple points of view, alternating between characters to offer broader insight.

### Narrative Tense
The most common tense is past tense, where events occur before the narrator's present. Present tense depicts events as happening now, creating immediacy, as in *The Hunger Games*. Future tense is rare and often has a prophetic tone, with the narrator describing events yet to occur.

### Narrative Technique
Techniques include stream of consciousness, which attempts to replicate a character's internal thought processes, as in James Joyce's *Ulysses*. Another technique is the unreliable narrator, an untrustworthy narrator whose account may be biased or false, creating suspense or mystery, exemplified by Holden Caulfield in *The Catcher in the Rye*. The use of multiple narratives can frame stories within stories, a device seen in oral traditions like *One Thousand and One Nights*.

## Terms
- **Narration**: The conveyed story or commentary.
- **Narrator**: The voice or character delivering the story.
- **Plot**: The series of events in a story.
- **Narrative mode**: The set of choices (point of view, tense, technique) that shape narration.
- **Point of view**: The storyteller's position and perspective relative to the narrative.
- **Omniscient narrator**: A third-person narrator who knows all characters' thoughts and events.
- **Stream of consciousness**: A technique replicating a character's internal thought flow.
- **Unreliable narrator**: A narrator whose account is untrustworthy or biased.

## Debates and Open Questions
An ongoing debate concerns the precise definition of narrative point of view, with various theoretical approaches defining it through person, perspective, voice, consciousness, and focus. The ideological point of view is noted as particularly difficult to formalize, relying partly on intuitive analysis. The function and effect of multiple narrative perspectives are also subjects of scholarly interpretation, as they can shape the understanding of social identity and overarching narrative.
