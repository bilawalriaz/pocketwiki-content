# Mandolin

## Overview
The mandolin is a plucked string instrument from the lute family, most commonly featuring four courses of doubled strings tuned in unison. It is the soprano member of a family that includes larger instruments like the mandola and mandocello. Its design and playing traditions have evolved significantly, making it a versatile instrument central to various musical genres worldwide, from classical and folk to bluegrass.

## Timeline
- **17th-18th c.** — Mandolin predecessors like the gittern and mandore/mandola develop in Italy.
- **1850s** — The flatback mandolin style is developed in Europe.
- **Late 19th c.** — Orville Gibson pioneers the carved-top (archtop) mandolin in the United States.
- **Early 20th c.** — The "mandolin craze" makes it a popular fad instrument in the U.S. and Europe.
- **1902** — The Gibson Mandolin-Guitar Manufacturing Co. is founded.
- **1920s** — Gibson's Lloyd Loar designs influential F-5 model mandolins.
- **1930s** — The mandolin finds a new niche in American country, old-time, and bluegrass music.
- **1970s** — English luthier Stefan Sobell develops the large-bodied "Celtic" mandolin.
- **2000s-2010s** — Renewed popularity in Celtic and British folk traditions.

## Body

### Construction and Design
A mandolin's body acts as a resonator, attached to a neck. The three primary body styles are the **bowlback** (or roundback), constructed from curved wood strips; the **archtop**, with a carved, arched top and back; and the **flatback**, which uses a thin, braced sheet of wood like a guitar. Strings run from tuning machines on the neck to a tailpiece, passing over a floating bridge held in place by string tension. The vibration of the soundboard, which may have round, oval, or f-shaped sound holes, produces sound. Modern instruments typically use metal strings, requiring a stronger soundboard than earlier gut-strung models.

### Tuning and the Mandolin Family
The most common tuning for a mandolin's four courses is G3–D4–A4–E5, identical to a violin. The mandolin is the soprano member of a family defined by scale length and tuning. The **mandola** (or tenor mandola) is tuned a fifth below the mandolin. The **octave mandolin** (or mandola in Europe) is tuned an octave below. The **mandocello** is tuned an octave and a fifth below, and the **mandobass** is the bass member, often tuned in fourths like a double bass.

### Historical Development and Styles
The mandolin evolved from European lutes, with the Neapolitan and Lombard styles becoming widespread. The Neapolitan mandolin, with its bowl-shaped body, is prominent in European classical and traditional music. In the late 19th century, Orville Gibson's carved-top designs in the U.S. led to the archtop mandolin, which became central to American folk, old-time, and bluegrass music. Flat-backed mandolins are common in Irish, British, and Brazilian folk music. Design innovations have included experiments with double tops and backs to enhance volume and tone.

### Playing Traditions and Repertoire
The mandolin spread from Italy throughout Europe, often used in mandolin orchestras. In the early 20th century, virtuosi like Carlo Curti and Raffaele Calace popularized it as a solo instrument. While its classical repertoire is smaller than the violin's, composers like Vivaldi, Beethoven, and Mahler wrote for it. The instrument found a major new role in American country and bluegrass music in the 1930s. It is also used in Carnatic classical music in India and has seen renewed interest in Celtic folk traditions.

## Terms
- **Course:** A pair of strings tuned to the same pitch and played together.
- **Soundboard:** The top of the instrument's body, which vibrates to produce sound.
- **F-hole:** A sound hole shaped like a cursive 'f', common on archtop mandolins and violins.
- **Tremolo:** A technique of rapidly picking one or more pairs of strings to sustain a note.
- **Plectrum:** A pick used to pluck the strings.
- **Bowlback:** A mandolin style with a deep, rounded back made from curved wood strips.
- **Archtop:** A mandolin style with a carved, arched top and back.
- **Flatback:** A mandolin style with a flat, braced back like a guitar.
- **Scale length:** The vibrating length of the strings, determining the instrument's size and pitch range.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The interchangeability of terms like "octave mandolin" and "Irish bouzouki" in modern usage is noted, despite theoretical distinctions based on scale length.
- The relative merits and sound qualities of carved-top versus pressed-top (laminated) archtop mandolins are a point of discussion among players and luthiers.
- The historical perception of the mandolin as a "folk instrument" limited its inclusion in standard orchestras, a gap filled by dedicated mandolin orchestras.
