# Queen Victoria

## Overview
Queen Victoria (1819–1901) was the monarch of the United Kingdom from 1837 until her death, a reign of over 63 years that defined the Victorian era. Her period saw massive industrial, political, and imperial expansion, solidifying the British Empire and establishing the modern model of a constitutional monarchy centered on family morality.

## Timeline
- **24 May 1819** — Born at Kensington Palace, fifth in line to the throne.
- **20 June 1837** — Becomes Queen at age 18 after the death of King William IV.
- **10 February 1840** — Marries her first cousin, Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha.
- **1840–1882** — Survives at least seven assassination attempts.
- **14 December 1861** — Prince Albert dies; Victoria enters prolonged, deep mourning.
- **1 May 1876** — Granted the title Empress of India by Parliament.
- **20 June 1887** — Celebrates her Golden Jubilee, marking 50 years on the throne.
- **22 June 1897** — Diamond Jubilee celebrations honor 60 years of her reign.
- **22 January 1901** — Dies at Osborne House, aged 81; succeeded by her son Edward VII.

## Body

### Early Life and Accession
Victoria was the only child of Prince Edward, Duke of Kent, the fourth son of King George III. After her father and grandfather died in 1820, she was raised under the strict "Kensington System" by her mother and comptroller John Conroy, designed to make her dependent on them. She became heir presumptive after the deaths of her father's elder brothers. She ascended the throne at 18, immediately asserting her independence by dismissing Conroy and relying on Prime Minister Lord Melbourne.

### Marriage, Family, and Public Life
In 1840, Victoria married Prince Albert, who became her closest political adviser and confidant. They had nine children who married into royal families across Europe, earning her the nickname "grandmother of Europe." Her early reign was marked by political clashes, such as the "bedchamber crisis" with Prime Minister Robert Peel, and personal scandals like the Lady Flora Hastings affair. After Albert's death in 1861, she withdrew from public life for years, damaging the monarchy's popularity and fueling republicanism. Her popularity later recovered through events like her Golden and Diamond Jubilees.

### Imperial Role and Later Reign
Victoria became Empress of India in 1876, a title championed by Prime Minister Benjamin Disraeli. She supported the expansion of the British Empire, viewing it as a civilizing force. Her later years saw her as a symbolic figurehead, embodying imperial and family values. She outlived many of her children and grandchildren, including her son Leopold (who had haemophilia) and daughter Alice. She died in 1901, the last monarch of the House of Hanover.

## Terms
- **Kensington System**: An elaborate set of rules devised by Victoria's mother and John Conroy to isolate and control the young princess.
- **Heir Presumptive**: The person first in line to inherit the throne, but whose claim can be displaced by the birth of a closer heir.
- **Bedchamber Crisis (1839)**: A political standoff where Victoria refused Prime Minister Robert Peel's request to replace her Whig ladies-in-waiting with Tory ones.
- **Constitutional Monarchy**: A system where the monarch's powers are limited by law and convention, acting on the advice of elected ministers.
- **Haemophilia**: A genetic blood-clotting disorder that appeared in Victoria's descendants, likely arising from a spontaneous mutation.
- **Empress of India**: A title granted to Victoria in 1876, formalizing British sovereignty over the Indian subcontinent.
- **Victorian Era**: The period of Victoria's reign, characterized by industrial growth, strict social morality, and vast imperial expansion.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Political Influence**: Scholars debate the extent of Victoria's private political influence versus her public role as a symbolic figurehead, especially after the publication of her diaries and letters.
- **Haemophilia Origin**: The source of the haemophilia mutation in Victoria's lineage is debated; it is most likely a spontaneous mutation, possibly linked to her father's age at her conception, rather than evidence of illegitimacy.
- **Legacy of Mourning**: The impact of her prolonged seclusion after Albert's death on the development of the modern monarchy and public attitudes toward it remains a subject of analysis.
