# French and Indian War

## Overview
The French and Indian War (1754–1763) was a colonial conflict in North America between Great Britain and France, each allied with various Indigenous nations. It was the North American theater of the global Seven Years' War (1756–1763). The war resulted in the decisive British conquest of New France, fundamentally altering the balance of power in North America and setting the stage for the American Revolution.

## Timeline
- **1749** — Céloron's expedition reaffirms French claims in the Ohio Country.
- **1753** — George Washington is sent to warn the French to leave the Ohio Valley.
- **1754** — Battle of Jumonville Glen; Washington surrenders at Fort Necessity.
- **1755** — Braddock's expedition is defeated; Fort Beauséjour captured; Acadian deportation begins.
- **1756** — Britain formally declares war; French capture Fort Oswego.
- **1757** — French capture Fort William Henry after a siege.
- **1758** — British capture Fort Duquesne and Louisbourg; French win at Battle of Carillon.
- **1759** — British capture Quebec after the Battle of the Plains of Abraham.
- **1760** — British capture Montreal, effectively ending major combat in North America.
- **1763** — Treaty of Paris ends the war; France cedes Canada to Britain.

## Body

### Background and Early Conflict
Following the War of the Austrian Succession, tensions between Britain and France persisted in North America over territorial claims, particularly in the Ohio Country. French efforts to assert control, including Céloron's 1749 expedition and the construction of forts, alarmed British colonists and their Indigenous allies. The conflict escalated from skirmishes, such as the 1754 Battle of Jumonville Glen involving George Washington, into a full-scale war. Indigenous nations, including the Iroquois Confederacy, initially sought neutrality but were drawn into alliances based on trade and strategic interests.

### The Course of the War
The war saw early French successes, aided by effective woodland tactics and Indigenous alliances, culminating in British defeats like Braddock's 1755 expedition. The tide turned after 1757 when British Prime Minister William Pitt invested heavily in the colonial war effort. Between 1758 and 1760, a series of British campaigns captured key French strongholds: Fort Duquesne (1758), Louisbourg (1758), Quebec (1759), and finally Montreal (1760). The French, hampered by a British naval blockade and limited reinforcements, could not sustain their defense.

### Peace and Consequences
The 1763 Treaty of Paris formally ended the war. France ceded Canada and all territories east of the Mississippi River to Britain. In a separate treaty, France transferred Louisiana west of the Mississippi to Spain. Britain's victory made it the dominant colonial power in North America but left it with a massive debt. To manage its new territories and pacify Indigenous nations, Britain issued the Royal Proclamation of 1763, which restricted colonial settlement west of the Appalachians. This policy, along with new taxes to pay war debts, fueled colonial resentment and contributed to the American Revolution.

## Terms
- **Troupes de la marine**: French colonial regular troops who formed the core of New France's military defense.
- **Iroquois Confederacy**: A powerful alliance of six Indigenous nations in the Northeast that often sought to balance relations between the French and British.
- **Albany Congress (1754)**: A meeting of colonial representatives and Iroquois leaders to coordinate defense and diplomacy; its proposed plan of union was not adopted.
- **Pontiac's War (1763–1766)**: An Indigenous uprising against British rule in the Great Lakes region following the French defeat.
- **Royal Proclamation of 1763**: A British decree that organized new territories and reserved lands west of the Appalachians for Indigenous peoples.
- **Treaty of Paris (1763)**: The agreement that ended the Seven Years' War, transferring French Canada to Britain.
- **Acadian deportation**: The forced removal by the British of French-speaking Acadians from Nova Scotia, beginning in 1755.

## Debates and open questions
- The war's name is debated: "French and Indian War" is standard in the U.S., while Canadians and Europeans typically view it as part of the Seven Years' War. French Canadians may call it the "War of Conquest."
- The role and agency of Indigenous nations are central to modern scholarship, moving beyond viewing them as mere pawns of European powers.
- The long-term impact of the war on the origins of the American Revolution is a key historical discussion, particularly regarding British debt, taxation, and the removal of the French threat.
