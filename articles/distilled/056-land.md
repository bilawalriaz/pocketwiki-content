# Land

## Overview

Land is the solid terrestrial surface of Earth not submerged by ocean or other water, making up 29.2% of Earth's surface and including all continents and islands. It plays a crucial role in Earth's climate system through the carbon, nitrogen, and water cycles, and is the foundation of human civilization—supporting agriculture, habitation, and cultural identity. Land is formed through plate tectonics over millions of years, shaped by erosion, weathering, and climate, and is studied across physical geography, geology, ecology, and the social sciences.

## Body

### Formation and structure

Earth formed by accretion from a solar nebula, with assembly taking 10–20 million years. The atmosphere and oceans formed through volcanic outgassing and water delivered by asteroids, protoplanets, and comets. The crust formed when the molten outer layer cooled. Two models explain land mass formation: steady growth to present forms, or rapid early growth followed by long-term steady continental area. Continents form through plate tectonics, driven by heat loss from Earth's interior. The continental crust is thicker and less dense than oceanic crust, composed of igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rocks, with nearly 75% of continental surfaces covered by sedimentary rocks.

### Terrain and geomorphology

Land terrain varies from mountains to plains, with elevation ranging from −418 metres at the Dead Sea to 8,848 metres at Mount Everest's peak; mean land height is about 797 metres. Geomorphology studies processes shaping land: erosion transports material via wind, water, ice, and gravity, while weathering wears rock in place. Humans have caused erosion 10–40 times faster than normal, losing half the topsoil in the past 150 years. Plate tectonics, first hypothesized by Alfred Wegener in 1912, explains continental drift through moving tectonic plates meeting at boundaries, where earthquakes and volcanic activity are common.

### Climate and land cover

Land heats and cools faster than air or water, influencing climate through latitude, elevation, topography, reflectivity, and land use. Albedo—the measure of solar radiation reflected—varies by cover: forests reflect 10–15%, grasslands 15–20%, sandy deserts 25–40%. Land cover refers to material physically present on the surface; artificial surfaces account for about a third of a percent of all land. Worldwide there are approximately 16.7 million km² of cropland and 33.5 million km² of pastureland.

### Biomes and life

Five major land biomes exist: grasslands, forests, deserts, tundras, and freshwater. Deserts receive less than 25 centimetres of precipitation annually and cover about one-fifth of Earth's land. Forests are defined by the UN's FAO as land spanning more than 0.5 hectares with trees higher than 5 meters and canopy cover over 10%. Land plants (embryophytes) evolved from green algae, with 20–50% of oxygen produced by land vegetation. Terrestrial animals are not a monophyletic group—some lineages, like whales, evolved back to aquatic lifestyles.

### Human interaction

Land is "deeply intertwined with human development," determining "survival and wealth" particularly in developing countries. Humans have engaged in land activities for over 10,000 years, with global landscape transformation essentially complete by 3000 years ago. Agriculture supports over 95% of food production. Urban areas occupy less than 3% of Earth's land but drive land use change beyond city boundaries. In economics, land is a factor of production, leasable for rent. The tragedy of the commons describes how individual use of common land deteriorates it overall.

## Terms

- **Regolith**: Layer of rock, soil, and minerals forming the outer part of Earth's crust
- **Craton**: Relatively flat interior of a continent, contrasted with mountain ranges
- **Plate tectonics**: Theory that Earth's lithosphere divides into plates moving over the mantle
- **Albedo**: Measure of solar radiation reflected rather than absorbed by a surface
- **Orogenesis**: Formation of mountain belts through plate tectonics
- **Pedosphere**: Outermost layer of Earth's continental surface, composed of soil
- **Biome**: Area characterized by its vegetation, soil, climate, and wildlife
- **Desertification**: Land degradation in drylands where fertile areas become increasingly arid
- **Lebensraum**: "Living space"—geopolitical goal motivating Nazi Germany's territorial expansion
- **Tragedy of the commons**: Individual use of common spaces for personal gain, deteriorating the land overall

## Debates and open questions

Two competing models explain land mass formation: steady growth versus rapid early growth followed by long-term stable continental area, with the latter considered more likely. The prediction that warming soils will add carbon dioxide to the atmosphere through increased biological activity (a positive feedback) has been questioned based on recent knowledge of soil carbon turnover. The "Myth of the Flat Earth"—that medieval Europeans believed Earth was flat—is a misconception; most educated people in the Middle Ages accepted a spherical Earth based on observations of secondary effects and parallels with other planets.
