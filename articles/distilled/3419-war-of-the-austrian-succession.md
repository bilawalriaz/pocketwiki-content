# War of the Austrian Succession

## Overview
The War of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748) was a continent-wide conflict triggered by the disputed inheritance of the Habsburg monarchy after Emperor Charles VI's death. It reshaped the European balance of power, leading to Prussia's rise, the decline of the Dutch Republic, and a major diplomatic realignment that set the stage for the Seven Years' War.

- **26 Nov 1741** — French forces capture Prague.

- **17 May 1742** — Battle of Chotusitz; inconclusive Prussian victory.
- **Jun 1742** — Treaty of Breslau ends First Silesian War; Prussia keeps Silesia.
- **27 Jun 1743** — Battle of Dettingen; last time a British monarch led troops in battle.
- **15 Aug 1744** — Prussia launches Second Silesian War.
- **11 May 1745** — Battle of Fontenoy; French victory establishes dominance in the Netherlands.
- **25 Dec 1745** — Treaty of Dresden ends Second Silesian War; Austria accepts Prussian control of Silesia.
- **18 Oct 1748** — Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle ends the war.

## Body
### Background and Cause
The immediate cause was the death of Emperor Charles VI in 1740 and the inheritance of the Habsburg monarchy by his daughter, Maria Theresa. Charles had spent decades securing her succession via the 1713 Pragmatic Sanction, which confirmed female inheritance. However, his rivals—Bavaria, Saxony, and Prussia—refused to be bound by it. France, despite previously accepting the Sanction, backed Bavaria's claim. The legal disputes were largely pretexts for a geopolitical contest over the balance of power, exacerbated by the recent expansion of Habsburg, Prussian, and Bavarian power.

### The Silesian Wars and Central European Theatre
Prussia's King Frederick II invaded the wealthy province of Silesia in December 1740, exploiting Austria's military weakness. After the inconclusive Battle of Chotusitz in 1742, Austria agreed to the Treaty of Breslau, temporarily ceding Silesia to Prussia to focus on other fronts. Frederick re-entered the war in 1744 (the Second Silesian War) but was forced into a costly retreat. In 1745, Austria and Saxony formed the Treaty of Warsaw against Prussia, but Frederick won decisive victories at Hohenfriedberg and Kesseldorf. The resulting Treaty of Dresden (1745) confirmed Prussian control of Silesia, ending major fighting in Germany.

### The Low Countries and French Strategy
France's primary land objective was the Austrian Netherlands. After winning the Battle of Fontenoy in 1745, French forces under Marshal Saxe systematically captured key fortresses, including Tournai, Ghent, and Bruges. By 1747, they invaded Dutch Flanders, threatening the Dutch Republic itself. This prompted an Orangist revolution and the appointment of William IV as stadtholder. Despite French victories at Lauffeld and the capture of Maastricht, the British naval blockade and near-bankruptcy forced France to negotiate.

### Global Conflict and Naval Operations
The war merged with the War of Jenkins' Ear (1739) between Britain and Spain. Naval operations were marked by extensive privateering. In North America (King George's War), colonial militia captured the French fortress of Louisbourg in 1745. In India (First Carnatic War), French forces under Bourdonnais captured Madras in 1746, but the British later besieged Pondichéry unsuccessfully. At sea, British naval supremacy was cemented by victories like the Battle of Cape Finisterre (1747), which crippled French trade.

### The Peace of Aix-la-Chapelle
By 1748, France was financially exhausted due to the British naval blockade. The treaty, negotiated primarily between Britain and France, restored the *status quo ante bellum* in most territories. Maria Theresa was confirmed in her titles, but Austria was forced to accept the permanent loss of Silesia to Prussia. France recovered Louisbourg in Canada in exchange for returning Madras to Britain. The treaty satisfied few, was seen as an armistice, and directly led to the Diplomatic Revolution of 1756, where former enemies Austria and France allied against Prussia and Britain, precipitating the Seven Years' War.

## Terms
- **Pragmatic Sanction**: The 1713 edict issued by Emperor Charles VI to ensure his daughter Maria Theresa could inherit the Habsburg monarchy.
- **Salic Law**: A legal principle excluding women from inheriting property or titles, which complicated Maria Theresa's claim.
- **Barrier Fortresses**: A series of Dutch-held fortifications in the Austrian Netherlands, designed to guard against French expansion.
- **Cabinet War**: An 18th-century conflict fought by professional, state-funded armies for limited, dynastic objectives, with minimal civilian atrocities.
- **Diplomatic Revolution**: The 1756 realignment of European alliances, where Austria and France became allies against Prussia and Britain.
- **Privateering**: State-sanctioned piracy, where private ships (privateers) attacked enemy merchant vessels during wartime.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent to which the "Botta Conspiracy" (1743) in Russia was a genuine plot versus a fabrication by Frederick II to sow discord between Austria and Russia is disputed.
- The primary cause of the war is debated: was it fundamentally a legal dispute over succession, or a pretext for a pre-existing geopolitical struggle for European dominance?
- The long-term impact of the treaty is contested; while it restored peace, its failure to resolve underlying tensions made the subsequent Seven Years' War almost inevitable.
