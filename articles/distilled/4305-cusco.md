# Cusco

## Overview
Cusco, located in the Peruvian Andes, was the historic capital of the Inca Empire and remains a major cultural and tourist center. Its significance stems from its role as the political and religious heart of the Inca civilization, its layered history of Inca and Spanish colonial architecture, and its modern status as a gateway to Machu Picchu and a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Timeline
- **c. 900–1200 AD** — The Killke culture inhabits the region, constructing the fortress of Sacsayhuamán around 1100 AD.
- **12th century** — The city is founded by Manco Cápac as the capital of the Kingdom of Cusco.
- **13th–16th century** — Cusco becomes the capital of the Inca Empire (Tawantinsuyu).
- **1532** — The city is captured by generals of Atahualpa during the Inca Civil War.
- **1533** — Spanish conquistadors under Francisco Pizarro arrive and take control.
- **1534** — The Spanish officially re-establish the city on 23 March.
- **1650** — A major earthquake devastates the colonial city.
- **1781** — Indigenous leader Túpac Amaru II leads a rebellion and besieges the city.
- **1824** — After the Battle of Ayacucho, Cusco recognizes Peruvian independence.
- **1950** — Another major earthquake exposes ancient Inca walls beneath colonial structures.
- **1983** — UNESCO declares Cusco a World Heritage Site.
- **1993** — The Peruvian Constitution designates Cusco as the Historical Capital of Peru.

## Body

### Pre-Inca and Inca Foundations
The region was first settled by the Killke culture, who built the massive fortress of Sacsayhuamán. The Inca later expanded this site and established Cusco as their capital in the 12th century. According to legend, the city was rebuilt by the Sapa Inca Pachacuti, who transformed it into the center of the Tawantinsuyu empire. The city was planned in the shape of a puma and divided into four quarters corresponding to the empire's provinces. Inca architecture, characterized by precisely fitted stone masonry, formed the city's enduring foundation.

### Spanish Conquest and Colonial Era
The Spanish conquered Cusco in 1533, plundering its wealth, including the golden Temple of the Sun (Qurikancha). They built their colonial city directly atop Inca foundations, creating a unique architectural blend. Cusco became a center for Spanish colonization and the spread of Christianity, with the construction of cathedrals, convents, and mansions. The city's political importance waned after the capital was moved to Lima, but it remained a key regional hub. Major earthquakes in 1650 and 1950 damaged colonial structures but also revealed preserved Inca stonework beneath.

### Modern Era and Tourism
After independence, Cusco's political role diminished, but its cultural significance endured. The 1950 earthquake and subsequent restoration highlighted the resilience of Inca construction. In the late 20th century, the city embraced its heritage, leading to its 1983 UNESCO designation and its constitutional recognition as Peru's Historical Capital in 1993. Today, Cusco is Peru's premier tourist destination, hosting millions of visitors annually who come to explore its historic center and travel to nearby Inca sites like Machu Picchu.

## Terms
- **Sapa Inca**: The supreme ruler of the Inca Empire.
- **Tawantinsuyu**: The name for the Inca Empire, meaning "The Four Regions Together."
- **Coricancha (Qurikancha)**: The most important Inca temple, dedicated to the Sun God Inti, originally covered in gold.
- **Ayllu**: A traditional Andean community or lineage system.
- **Mestizaje**: The cultural and racial mixing of Indigenous and European (primarily Spanish) peoples.
- **Inti Raymi**: An ancient Inca festival honoring the Sun God, still celebrated annually in Cusco.
- **Cuzco School**: A colonial artistic movement blending European and Indigenous styles, known for its paintings.
- **Puma**: A sacred animal in Inca cosmology; the city of Cusco was reportedly planned in its shape.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact founding date of Cusco is debated, with sources placing it in the 12th century, though the region was inhabited earlier by the Killke culture.
- The traditional legend of the city's founding by Manco Cápac is a mythological account; archaeological evidence suggests a more gradual, organic growth of the settlement.
- The precise methods used by the Inca to quarry, transport, and fit the massive stones of their architecture remain undetermined.
