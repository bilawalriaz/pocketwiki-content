# Kumārajīva

## Overview
Kumārajīva (344–413 CE) was a Buddhist monk, scholar, and translator from the Kingdom of Kucha who became one of the most influential figures in Chinese Buddhism. His team's translations of Sanskrit texts into Chinese, known for their clarity and literary quality, revolutionized Buddhist terminology and philosophy in East Asia, and many remain in use today.

## Timeline
- **344 CE** — Born in the Kingdom of Kucha (present-day Xinjiang, China) to an Indian monk father and a Kuchean royal mother.
- **c. 351 CE (age 7)** — Mother Jīva joins a nunnery.
- **c. 353 CE (age 9)** — Begins studying Agamas and Sarvastivada Abhidharma in North India, Kashmir, and Kucha.
- **c. 363 CE (age 20)** — Receives full monastic ordination in Kucha; begins studying Madhyamaka philosophy.
- **379 CE** — His fame reaches China when the monk Seng Jun visits Kucha.
- **c. 384 CE (age ~40)** — Captured by the warlord Lü Guang and imprisoned for many years, during which he learns Chinese.
- **401 CE** — Brought to Chang'an by Emperor Yao Xing of the Later Qin dynasty.
- **401–413 CE** — Leads a court-sponsored translation team in Chang'an, translating about 35 sutras in 294 scrolls.
- **413 CE** — Dies in Chang'an.

## Body

### Early Life and Training
Kumārajīva was born in the Tocharian-speaking Kingdom of Kucha. His father, the Indian monk Kumārāyana, settled there after marrying the royal princess Jīva. Kumārajīva first studied the teachings of the Sarvāstivāda school, a major early Buddhist tradition emphasizing the existence of all dharmas (phenomena) across past, present, and future. He later converted to Mahāyāna Buddhism and studied the Mādhyamaka (Middle Way) philosophy of Nāgārjuna, which teaches the emptiness (śūnyatā) of all phenomena.

### Capture and Journey to China
In 379 CE, Chinese Emperor Fu Jian sent an army to bring Kumārajīva to China. However, the general Lü Guang captured Kumārajīva and held him hostage for about 17 years, during which he was forced to marry and give up his monastic vows. After the Later Qin dynasty defeated Lü Guang, Emperor Yao Xing brought Kumārajīva to the capital Chang'an in 401 CE, where he was honored as "National Preceptor."

### Translation Work and Method
In Chang'an, Kumārajīva led a large translation team, including disciples like Sengrui and Sengzhao. Their method was collaborative: Kumārajīva would read and explain a Sanskrit text, which the team would then discuss and render into Chinese through multiple revisions. This process was public, attended by monks and even the emperor. Their style prioritized conveying meaning smoothly over literal accuracy, overcoming the earlier *geyi* (concept-matching) system that used Daoist and Confucian terms. This approach produced clear, literary translations that introduced key Buddhist terminology into Chinese, such as *dà chéng* for Mahāyāna and *pú tí* for Bodhi (awakening).

### Translated Texts
The team translated numerous foundational Mahāyāna sutras, including the *Diamond Sutra*, *Lotus Sutra*, and *Vimalakīrti Nirdeśa Sūtra*. They also translated key Mādhyamaka treatises, such as Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (as the *Middle Treatise*), which became central to the East Asian Sanlun ("Three Treatise") school. Additionally, they translated meditation manuals and Abhidharma texts. Scholar Jan Nattier notes that Kumārajīva's translations appear shorter than later Sanskrit versions because he worked from earlier Indian manuscripts that had not yet been expanded.

### Philosophy and Original Works
Kumārajīva was an orthodox Mādhyamika who stressed the emptiness of all dharmas and interpreted early Buddhist texts through a Mahāyāna lens. He taught that the Buddha's teachings use skillful means (*upāya*) to guide beings, and that ultimate wisdom (*prajñā*) transcends conceptual thought. He authored a commentary on the *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* and, with his team, likely edited and abridged the *Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa* (Great Treatise on the Perfection of Wisdom), traditionally attributed to Nāgārjuna.

### Influence
Kumārajīva's translations became the standard in East Asian Buddhism, remaining preferred over later, more literal versions. His work corrected widespread Chinese misunderstandings of emptiness (*śūnyatā*) and not-self (*anatman*) that had been influenced by Neo-Taoist philosophy, establishing a proper Mādhyamaka foundation for Chinese Buddhist thought.

## Terms
- **Sarvāstivāda**: An early Buddhist school whose teachings Kumārajīva first studied.
- **Mahāyāna**: The "Greater Vehicle" of Buddhism, emphasizing the bodhisattva path and the emptiness of phenomena.
- **Mādhyamaka**: A Mahāyāna philosophical school founded by Nāgārjuna that teaches the middle way between eternalism and nihilism through the concept of emptiness.
- **Śūnyatā**: "Emptiness"; the Mādhyamaka doctrine that all phenomena lack inherent, independent existence.
- **Dharmas**: The basic constituents or phenomena of experience in Buddhist philosophy.
- **Geyi**: An early Chinese translation method that matched Buddhist concepts with native Daoist and Confucian terms.
- **Upāya**: "Skillful means"; the Buddha's use of adaptable teachings to guide different audiences.
- **Prajñā**: "Wisdom"; specifically, the transcendent insight into emptiness.
- **Sanlun**: The "Three Treatise" school in China, based on the Mādhyamaka texts translated by Kumārajīva's team.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The authenticity and extent of Kumārajīva's personal contributions to translations like the *Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa* are debated; scholars suggest his team, particularly Sengrui, may have significantly edited or abridged the texts.
- Scholars are divided on how many of the meditation texts attributed to Kumārajīva he actually worked on, though two (T. 613 and T. 614) are well-attested in early catalogs.
- The view that Kumārajīva's translations are "abbreviated" compared to later versions is considered erroneous by Jan Nattier, who argues he used earlier, shorter Indian source manuscripts.
