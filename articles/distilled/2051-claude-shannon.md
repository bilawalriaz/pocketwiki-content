# Claude Shannon

## Overview
Claude Shannon (1916–2001) was an American mathematician and engineer who founded information theory, laying the mathematical foundations for the digital age. His work in the 1940s provided the theoretical framework for digital circuits, cryptography, and data transmission, making him a pivotal figure in 20th-century science.

## Timeline
- **1936** — Graduated from University of Michigan with dual bachelor's degrees in electrical engineering and mathematics.
- **1937** — Completed his master's thesis at MIT, "A Symbolic Analysis of Relay and Switching Circuits," applying Boolean algebra to circuit design.
- **1940** — Earned a PhD in mathematics from MIT; began work at Bell Labs.
- **1945** — Prepared classified memorandum "A Mathematical Theory of Cryptography."
- **1948** — Published "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," founding information theory.
- **1949** — Published "Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems," a foundational paper in modern cryptography.
- **1950** — Built the Theseus learning machine, an early example of artificial intelligence.
- **1956** — Co-organized the Dartmouth workshop, considered the founding event of AI as a field.
- **1956–1978** — Served as a professor at MIT.
- **1985** — Awarded the Kyoto Prize.
- **2001** — Died on February 24.

## Body

### Digital Circuits and Computing
Shannon's 1937 master's thesis demonstrated that Boolean algebra could be used to design and simplify relay switching circuits. This work proved that electrical circuits could implement any logical numerical relationship, establishing the theoretical basis for all digital electronic circuits and computers. His approach transformed digital circuit design from an art into a science.

### Cryptography
During World War II, Shannon worked on cryptanalysis for U.S. national defense. His 1949 paper "Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems" is considered a turning point, marking the beginning of modern cryptography. He proved that the one-time pad is theoretically unbreakable and established that any unbreakable system must share its key characteristics. This work was foundational for symmetric-key cryptography, including later standards like DES and AES.

### Information Theory
Shannon's 1948 paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" introduced the concept of information entropy as a measure of uncertainty in a message. This paper is the blueprint for the digital era, enabling advancements from the compact disc to the Internet. He formally introduced the term "bit" and co-invented pulse-code modulation, which was essential for the shift from analog to digital telecommunications.

### Artificial Intelligence
Shannon made early contributions to AI. In 1950, he built the Theseus machine, a mechanical mouse that learned to navigate a maze through trial and error, demonstrating a foundational AI concept. He co-organized the 1956 Dartmouth workshop, which established AI as a field, and published influential papers on programming computers to play chess.

### Other Work and Legacy
Shannon estimated the game-tree complexity of chess (the "Shannon number") at approximately 10^120. He formulated "Shannon's maxim" in cryptography: "The enemy knows the system." He was also a successful investor. His achievements are widely considered on par with those of Einstein and Newton, and he is credited with creating the field of information theory and laying the foundations for the Information Age.

## Terms
- **Information Theory**: A mathematical framework for quantifying, storing, and communicating information, founded by Shannon.
- **Information Entropy**: A measure of the uncertainty or information content in a message, introduced by Shannon.
- **Bit**: The fundamental unit of information, representing a choice between two equally likely alternatives; term coined by Shannon.
- **Boolean Algebra**: A mathematical system of logic using binary variables, which Shannon applied to circuit design.
- **Pulse-Code Modulation (PCM)**: A method for digitally representing analog signals, co-invented by Shannon.
- **One-Time Pad**: An encryption technique proven by Shannon to be theoretically unbreakable if used correctly.
- **Shannon's Maxim**: The cryptographic principle that "The enemy knows the system."
- **Shannon Number**: Shannon's estimate of the game-tree complexity of chess, approximately 10^120.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The source notes Shannon's 1940 PhD thesis on genetics contained "important results" but remained "unpublished and relatively unknown until recently," suggesting ongoing scholarly rediscovery of his early work.
- Shannon's 1956 editorial "The Bandwagon" warned against the uncritical application of information theory, highlighting a tension between the theory's broad utility and its proper scientific boundaries.
