# Rocket

## Overview
A rocket is an elongated vehicle that generates thrust by expelling propellant at high speed, operating independently of atmospheric oxygen and thus functioning in the vacuum of space. Rockets enabled the Space Age and remain essential for satellite deployment, human spaceflight, and space exploration. Their development spans from 13th-century Chinese gunpowder weapons to modern multistage launch vehicles, driven by evolving propellant technologies and theoretical frameworks like the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation.

## Timeline
- **13th century**: Gunpowder rockets developed in Song Dynasty China; first multistage rocket described in *Huolongjing* (c. 1350)
- **Late 18th century**: Mysorean iron-cased rockets developed under Hyder Ali
- **1804**: Congreve rocket developed in Britain, based on Mysorean design
- **1813**: William Moore provides first mathematical treatment of rocket dynamics
- **1903**: Konstantin Tsiolkovsky develops foundational rocket theory
- **1926**: Robert Goddard launches first liquid-fuel rocket with supersonic nozzle
- **1928**: Soviet Union launches first solid-propellant rocket, which flew for approximately 1,300 metres
- **1944**: German V-2 becomes first human-made object in space, crossing the Kármán line on 20 June 1944
- **1969**: Apollo 11 lands on Moon using Saturn V rocket
- **1960s**: ICBMs and orbital rockets developed, particularly in the Soviet Union (Vostok, Soyuz, Proton) and the United States (e.g. the X-15)

## Body

### Historical Development
Gunpowder-powered rockets emerged in medieval China under the Song dynasty by the 13th century, with the *Huolongjing*, written by the Chinese artillery officer Jiao Yu in the mid-14th century, documenting the first known multistage rocket, the "fire-dragon issuing from the water." The Mongols spread this technology to the Middle East and Europe. Between 1270 and 1280, Hasan al-Rammah wrote *al-furusiyyah wa al-manasib al-harbiyya*, which included 22 gunpowder recipes for rockets. Roger Bacon mentioned firecrackers in his *Opus Majus* of 1267, and Konrad Kyeser described rockets in his military treatise *Bellifortis* around 1405. Giovanni Fontana created rocket-propelled animal figures in 1420. The Mysorean rockets of late 18th-century India introduced iron casings, dramatically improving range and reliability. British Congreve rockets (1804), developed by Sir William Congreve, extended effective range from 100 to 2,000 yards. The theoretical foundation was laid by Tsiolkovsky (1903), whose rocket equation remains central to spaceflight dynamics. William Leitch first proposed the concept of using rockets to enable human spaceflight in 1861.

### Modern Rocketry and Spaceflight
Robert Goddard's 1926 liquid-fuel rocket with a supersonic de Laval nozzle marked the birth of modern rocketry, increasing efficiency from 2% to 64%. In 1921, the Soviet research and development laboratory Gas Dynamics Laboratory began developing solid-propellant rockets, which resulted in the first launch in 1928, which flew for approximately 1,300 metres. These rockets were used in 1931 for the world's first successful use of rockets for jet-assisted takeoff of aircraft and became the prototypes for the Katyusha rocket launcher. The German V-2 program (1943-1944) produced the first artificial object in space by crossing the Kármán line with the vertical launch of MW 18014 on 20 June 1944. Post-WWII, Operation Paperclip brought German scientists like Wernher von Braun to the US. The Cold War accelerated development, producing ICBMs and orbital launch vehicles. The Saturn V enabled the Apollo Moon landings, demonstrating multistage rocket capability for achieving escape velocity.

### Design and Components
Rockets consist of propellant, tanks, engines, nozzles, and structural elements. Chemical rockets dominate high-power applications, using combinations of fuel and oxidizer—liquid (like RP-1/LOX), solid, hybrid, or monopropellant systems. The de Laval nozzle converts combustion chamber pressure into directed kinetic energy, producing thrust via Newton's third law. Multistaging allows rockets to shed dead weight during ascent, overcoming the exponential fuel requirements that prevent single-stage-to-orbit designs.

### Physics and Performance
Rocket performance depends on specific impulse (Isp)—the net impulse per unit propellant weight—and delta-v capacity, governed by the Tsiolkovsky equation: Δv = ve ln(m₀/m₁). High mass ratios (initial to final mass) are crucial for orbital insertion, requiring ~9.7 km/s delta-v. The Oberth effect makes high-speed burns more energy-efficient for interplanetary travel. Propulsive efficiency peaks when vehicle speed matches exhaust velocity, explaining why rockets excel at hypersonic speeds but are inefficient for subsonic aircraft.

### Applications
Beyond spaceflight, rockets serve military roles (missiles, ICBMs), scientific research (sounding rockets, atmospheric studies), emergency systems (ejection seats, launch escape systems), and hobby activities (model rockets). The Space Shuttle exemplified practical application, achieving ~16% energy efficiency despite carrying 100,000 kg to orbit. Private companies emerged in the 2010s, introducing cost pressures through reusable designs and mass production.

## Terms
- **Specific impulse (Isp)**: Net impulse per unit weight of propellant expelled; key performance metric
- **Delta-v**: Total change in velocity a rocket can achieve; determines mission capability
- **Mass ratio**: Initial mass divided by final mass; higher ratios enable greater delta-v
- **De Laval nozzle**: Convergent-divergent nozzle that accelerates gases to supersonic speeds
- **Staging**: Jettisoning of empty tanks and engines during flight to improve efficiency
- **Thrust-to-weight ratio**: Engine thrust divided by vehicle weight; determines acceleration capability
- **Propellant**: Mass stored and ejected to produce thrust; includes fuel and oxidizer
- **Oberth effect**: Greater energy gain from burns performed at higher velocities

## Debates and Open Questions
The feasibility of single-stage-to-orbit (SSTO) vehicles remains debated, as exponential fuel requirements make practical payloads negligible without staging. Reusability versus expendability continues as an economic question, with reusable systems requiring higher upfront investment but lower per-launch costs. The balance between liquid and solid propellants for different applications involves trade-offs in performance, storage, and complexity. Additionally, the environmental impact of large-scale rocket launches and the sustainability of space access remain emerging concerns.
