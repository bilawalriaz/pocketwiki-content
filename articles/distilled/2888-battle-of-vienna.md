# Battle of Vienna

## Overview
The Battle of Vienna (12 September 1683) was a decisive engagement where a Holy League army, led by Polish King John III Sobieski, relieved the city after a two-month Ottoman siege. It marked the turning point in the Ottoman–Habsburg wars, halting Ottoman expansion into Europe and leading to a major shift in the continent's balance of power.

## Timeline
- **14 July 1683** — Ottoman army, led by Grand Vizier Kara Mustafa Pasha, begins the siege of Vienna.
- **15 August 1683** — Polish King John III Sobieski departs Kraków with a relief army.
- **6 September 1683** — Sobieski's forces cross the Danube at Tulln to unite with Imperial troops.
- **8 September 1683** — Ottomans occupy the Burg ravelin, creating a critical breach in Vienna's defenses.
- **12 September 1683** — The Battle of Vienna is fought; Sobieski's cavalry charge breaks the Ottoman lines.
- **25 December 1683** — Kara Mustafa Pasha is executed in Belgrade for the defeat.
- **1686** — Imperial forces capture Buda, beginning the reconquest of Hungary.
- **1699** — Treaty of Karlowitz cedes most of Ottoman Hungary to the Habsburgs.

## Body

### Prelude and Siege
Capturing Vienna was a long-standing Ottoman strategic goal due to its control over Danube trade routes. After 20 years of peace, clashes in Hungary between Habsburg forces and Ottoman-backed rebels led by Imre Thököly provided the pretext for war. The Ottoman army, estimated between 90,000 and 300,000 men, besieged Vienna from 14 July. The city's garrison of 15,000, led by Ernst Rüdiger von Starhemberg, resisted. Ottoman sappers dug tunnels to breach the walls, but delays in the siege—partly due to the Ottomans' cautious approach and the defenders' counter-mining—allowed time for a relief force to assemble.

### The Relief and Battle
A Holy League army, funded by the Pope and various states, formed under Sobieski's command. He arrived with Polish forces, including the famed Winged Hussars, and united with Imperial troops under Charles V, Duke of Lorraine. On 12 September, the allied army attacked. The Imperial forces engaged the Ottoman left, while Sobieski's infantry advanced on the right. The battle culminated around 6:00 PM with the largest known cavalry charge in history: 18,000 horsemen, led by Sobieski, charged downhill, breaking the Ottoman lines. The exhausted and demoralized Ottoman army retreated, and Vienna was relieved.

### Aftermath and Significance
The defeat was catastrophic for the Ottomans, who lost tens of thousands of men. Kara Mustafa was executed. The victory enabled the Holy Roman Empire to conquer Hungary and parts of the Balkans over the next 16 years, culminating in the Treaty of Karlowitz (1699). It marked the end of Ottoman territorial expansion in Europe. The battle also had religious significance; Pope Innocent XI extended the Feast of the Holy Name of Mary to the entire Church in commemoration.

## Terms
- **Holy League**: The alliance of the Holy Roman Empire (led by the Habsburgs), the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, and other states against the Ottomans.
- **Grand Vizier**: The chief minister and commander of the Ottoman army; Kara Mustafa Pasha held this position.
- **Janissaries**: Elite infantry units of the Ottoman army, forming part of the Sultan's household troops.
- **Winged Hussars**: Polish heavy cavalry, renowned for their effectiveness and distinctive feathered armor.
- **Sappers**: Soldiers trained in digging tunnels (mines) to undermine and breach enemy fortifications.
- **Kuruc**: Hungarian rebels who fought against Habsburg rule, often with Ottoman support.
- **Treaty of Karlowitz (1699)**: The peace treaty that ended the Great Turkish War, forcing the Ottomans to cede large territories, including Hungary, to the Habsburgs.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Ottoman Army Size**: Estimates vary widely, from 90,000 to 300,000. Scholarly analysis suggests a strength of around 120,000 soldiers at the siege's start, declining significantly due to casualties, disease, and desertion.
- **Tatar Participation**: The role of the Crimean Tatar cavalry, who were tasked with guarding the Ottoman rear, is debated. Some sources indicate they were ineffective and did not fully engage the relief army.
- **Kara Mustafa's Strategy**: Some historians speculate the Grand Vizier avoided an all-out assault to capture Vienna intact with its riches, which may have delayed the siege and allowed the relief force to arrive.
