# Hugo Chávez

## Overview
Hugo Chávez was a Venezuelan military officer and politician who served as president from 1999 until his death in 2013. He fundamentally reshaped Venezuela's political and economic landscape through his "Bolivarian Revolution," creating a new constitution, implementing extensive social programs funded by oil revenues, and promoting a socialist ideology known as Chavismo. His presidency was marked by significant social spending that temporarily reduced poverty, but also by democratic backsliding, economic mismanagement, and growing authoritarianism, setting the stage for Venezuela's later crisis.

## Timeline
- **28 July 1954** — Born in Sabaneta, Barinas, Venezuela.
- **4 February 1992** — Leads a failed coup d'état against President Carlos Andrés Pérez; imprisoned.
- **1994** — Pardoned from prison; founds the Fifth Republic Movement (MVR) political party.
- **6 December 1998** — Elected president of Venezuela with 56.2% of the vote.
- **1999** — New constitution ratified, renaming the country the Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela.
- **11 April 2002** — Briefly ousted in a coup; restored to power two days later.
- **2003** — Launches the "Bolivarian Missions" social programs; creates currency controls (CADIVI).
- **15 August 2004** — Wins a recall referendum, retaining the presidency with 59% support.
- **2005** — Begins openly proclaiming the ideology of "socialism of the 21st century."
- **2007** — Forms the United Socialist Party of Venezuela (PSUV); a proposed constitutional expansion of powers is rejected in a referendum.
- **7 October 2012** — Wins a fourth presidential term with 55.1% of the vote.
- **5 March 2013** — Dies in Caracas at age 58 from cancer-related complications.

## Body

### Early Life and Military Career
Born into a middle-class family, Chávez studied at the Venezuelan Academy of Military Sciences. Influenced by leftist military leaders like Peru's Juan Velasco Alvarado and Panama's Omar Torrijos, he became disillusioned with Venezuela's political system. In the early 1980s, he founded the secretive Revolutionary Bolivarian Movement-200 (MBR-200), inspired by Simón Bolívar. After a failed coup attempt in 1992 against President Carlos Andrés Pérez, Chávez was imprisoned. His televised speech after surrender, ending with the phrase "por ahora" (for now), made him a popular figure among Venezuelans disillusioned with corruption.

### Political Rise and the 1998 Election
Pardoned in 1994, Chávez founded the Fifth Republic Movement (MVR) party. Capitalizing on widespread discontent with the traditional political parties, he won the 1998 presidential election with 56.2% of the vote. His victory marked a decisive break from the previous political order, which he blamed for the country's problems.

### Constitutional Reform and the Bolivarian Revolution
Upon taking office, Chávez moved quickly to rewrite the constitution. A new constitution was approved in 1999, extending the presidential term to six years, allowing immediate reelection, and renaming the country the Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela. It also granted the president greater powers and gave the military a role in national development. Using record-high oil revenues in the 2000s, his government launched the "Bolivarian Missions," social programs providing food, healthcare, and education. These programs led to temporary improvements in poverty reduction and social welfare, but their reliance on oil and state control created structural weaknesses.

### Political Crises and Consolidation of Power
Chávez's presidency was marked by intense political conflict. In 2002, he was briefly ousted in a coup before being restored to power. He then faced a two-month general strike at the state oil company PDVSA, which he countered by firing nearly 20,000 employees, damaging the industry's expertise. A 2004 recall referendum failed, with 59% voting to keep him in office. He consolidated power by forming the United Socialist Party of Venezuela (PSUV) in 2007 and winning a 2009 referendum that abolished presidential term limits.

### Economic Policy and Crisis
Chávez's economic policies included nationalizing key industries, implementing strict price controls on basic goods, and creating currency controls. While these measures initially funded social programs, they led to severe shortages, inflation, and a decline in domestic production. By the end of his presidency, the economy was faltering, with poverty and shortages increasing as oil prices declined.

### Human Rights and Democratic Backsliding
Chávez's government was criticized for suppressing press freedom, manipulating electoral laws, and arresting critics. Human rights organizations documented cases of torture, extrajudicial killings, and political persecution. The murder rate in Venezuela more than tripled during his tenure, and corruption remained widespread.

### Foreign Policy
Chávez pursued an anti-imperialist foreign policy, aligning Venezuela with Cuba, Bolivia, Ecuador, and Nicaragua. He was a vocal opponent of U.S. foreign policy and neoliberalism. He also promoted Latin American integration through initiatives like the Bolivarian Alliance for the Americas (ALBA) and the Union of South American Nations (UNASUR).

## Terms
- **Chavismo**: The political ideology and movement centered around Hugo Chávez's ideas, combining elements of Bolivarianism, socialism, and anti-imperialism.
- **Bolivarian Revolution**: The political and social movement led by Chávez aimed at transforming Venezuela into a more egalitarian, socialist society, inspired by Simón Bolívar.
- **Bolivarian Missions**: Social programs launched in 2003, funded by oil revenues, to provide healthcare, education, and food subsidies to the poor.
- **Fifth Republic Movement (MVR)**: The political party founded by Chávez in 1997 to support his presidential candidacy, later merged into the PSUV.
- **United Socialist Party of Venezuela (PSUV)**: The ruling political party formed in 2007 from the merger of pro-Chávez parties.
- **Puntofijo Pact**: A power-sharing agreement between Venezuela's main political parties from 1958 to 1999, which Chávez blamed for corruption and inequality.
- **PDVSA**: Petróleos de Venezuela S.A., the state-owned oil company, which Chávez used to fund social programs after purging its management.
- **CADIVI**: The currency control board created in 2003 to manage foreign exchange and control capital flight, leading to a black market for currency.
- **Dutch disease**: An economic phenomenon where a boom in one sector (like oil) harms other sectors of the economy, which affected Venezuela under Chávez.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Legacy of Social Programs**: Scholars debate whether the Bolivarian Missions created lasting improvements or merely temporary, superficial gains that masked deeper structural problems. Some argue the poverty reduction was real but fragile, while others contend it was unsustainable and ultimately reversed.
- **Democratic Backsliding**: There is significant debate over the extent to which Chávez dismantled Venezuela's democracy. While he won numerous elections, critics point to his undermining of checks and balances, press freedom, and judicial independence as evidence of authoritarianism.
- **Economic Responsibility**: Analysts debate whether Venezuela's economic crisis was caused primarily by Chávez's policies or by external factors like falling oil prices. Some argue his populist spending and nationalizations made the economy vulnerable, while others emphasize the role of global market forces.
- **Role in Regional Politics**: Chávez's influence on the Latin American "pink tide" is debated. Some see him as a key leader who inspired left-wing movements, while others argue his erratic policies and domestic failures limited his lasting impact.
