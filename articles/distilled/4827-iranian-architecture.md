# Iranian architecture

## Overview
Iranian architecture, spanning over 7,000 years from the Iranian plateau to parts of West Asia and Central Asia, is characterized by a continuous, recognizable style marked by a profound sense of scale, structural innovation in vaults and domes, and elaborate decorative programs. It represents a synthesis of pre-Islamic and Islamic traditions, evolving through successive empires while maintaining core principles of cosmic symbolism and harmony with the environment.

## Timeline
- **c. 5000 BC** — Earliest architectural remains on the Iranian plateau.
- **8th–6th centuries BCE** — Median Empire period; early monumental architecture.
- **6th–4th centuries BCE** — Achaemenid Empire; construction of Persepolis, Susa, and Pasargadae.
- **224–651 CE** — Sasanian Empire; development of large-scale domes and barrel vaults.
- **7th–9th centuries** — Early Islamic period; adaptation of Sasanian forms into mosques.
- **10th–11th centuries** — Emergence of distinct regional styles; use of baked brick and glazed tile.
- **11th–13th centuries** — Seljuk era; introduction of the four-iwan mosque plan and monumental tombs.
- **13th–14th centuries** — Ilkhanid period; construction of the massive Dome of Soltaniyeh.
- **14th–15th centuries** — Timurid era; refinement of vaulting and lavish tile decoration.
- **16th–18th centuries** — Safavid era; grand urban planning in Isfahan and perfection of tile mosaic.
- **18th–early 20th centuries** — Zand and Qajar dynasties; continuation of traditional forms with European influences.
- **1920s onward** — Contemporary period; modernist movements and revivalist trends.

## Body

### Fundamental Principles and Design
Traditional Iranian architecture maintains a continuous, unmistakable style despite political conflicts and invasions. Its guiding motif is cosmic symbolism, aiming to connect humanity with the powers of heaven. Key design elements include a marked feeling for scale, simple massive forms, high-arched portals, columns with bracket capitals, and recurrent plans like the columned porch (*talar*) and the dome on four arches. Available materials, primarily heavy clay and brick, dictated major forms, with mudbrick and fired brick being fundamental.

### Pre-Islamic Architecture
Pre-Islamic styles draw on millennia of development. The Achaemenids built on a grand scale, using imported artisans and materials for cities like Persepolis. The Parthians and Sasanians introduced new forms, including massive barrel-vaulted chambers and solid masonry domes, which heavily influenced later Islamic architecture. For example, the round plan of Abbasid Baghdad has Persian precedents like Firouzabad.

### Early Islamic Period (7th–9th Centuries)
Following the Arab-Muslim conquest, early Islamic architecture in Iran was heavily influenced by Byzantine and Sasanian traditions. Umayyad and later Abbasid architecture mixed these elements. The four-centred arch first appeared in the 9th century. Mosques typically had a hypostyle prayer hall and a courtyard. The Tarikhaneh Mosque in Damghan, with its Sassanid-like form, is a key surviving example from this period.

### Seljuk to Timurid Eras (11th–15th Centuries)
The Seljuk era (11th–13th centuries) was a classical age, introducing the four-iwan mosque plan, exemplified at the Jameh Mosque of Isfahan. Monumental tombs, like the Gonbad-e Qabus tower and the Samanid Mausoleum, emerged. The subsequent Ilkhanid and Timurid periods saw innovations in dome construction and decoration. The Ilkhanid Dome of Soltaniyeh (1302–1312) is a massive double-shelled dome. Timurid architecture, centered in Samarkand and Herat, refined vaulting techniques and used lavish tile mosaic and *banna'i* (geometric tile patterns).

### Safavid and Later Periods (16th–Early 20th Centuries)
The Safavid dynasty, particularly under Shah Abbas I, created a grand imperial style. In Isfahan, they developed a vast architectural ensemble around Naqsh-e Jahan Square, featuring mosques with single, large double-shelled domes covered in colorful tile mosaic. The *haft-rang* (seven-color) tile technique was perfected. Later Zand and Qajar dynasties continued traditional forms but introduced European elements like tall windows and formal staircases, as seen in Tehran's Golestan Palace.

### Persian Domes
The Sasanian Empire initiated large-scale domes in Iran. This tradition evolved through the Islamic period, with the Ilkhanids enabling taller structures via innovations like the double-shelled dome. The Safavids elevated dome design, covering exteriors with colorful tiles to create a distinctive skyline, as in Isfahan. The Dome of Soltaniyeh (50m high) remains the tallest masonry dome ever built.

## Terms
- **Iwan**: A rectangular hall or space, usually vaulted, walled on three sides, with one end entirely open.
- **Muqarnas**: A form of ornamented vaulting in Islamic architecture, often described as "honeycomb" or "stalactite" vaulting.
- **Hypostyle**: A roof or ceiling supported by columns, commonly used in early mosque prayer halls.
- **Mihrab**: A niche in the wall of a mosque that indicates the qibla (direction of Mecca).
- **Qibla**: The direction of the Kaaba in Mecca, towards which Muslims pray.
- **Talar**: A columned porch or portico, a recurring element from Achaemenid to modern times.
- **Banna'i**: A decorative technique using glazed bricks to create geometric patterns on building exteriors.
- **Tile mosaic**: A decorative technique where monochrome tiles of different colors are cut and fitted together to form patterns.
- **Double-shelled dome**: A dome construction with two layers, an inner and an outer shell, allowing for greater height and external decoration.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact chronology and original form of some early Islamic monuments, like the Tarikhaneh Mosque, are not well-documented and remain subjects of scholarly study.
- The precise functions of certain standalone towers and minarets from the Ghaznavid and Ghurid periods (e.g., the Minaret of Jam) are unclear.
- The extent of influence between Iranian architecture and neighboring traditions (e.g., Ottoman, Mughal) is an ongoing area of research, particularly regarding the transmission of styles like the Timurid aesthetic.
