# Bantu expansion

## Overview
The Bantu expansion was a major prehistoric migration of Proto-Bantu-speaking peoples from a nucleus in West-Central Africa across Central, Eastern, and Southern Africa between approximately 4000 BC and 500 AD. This movement fundamentally reshaped the continent's linguistic, cultural, and genetic landscape, as the expanding agriculturalists absorbed, displaced, or replaced pre-existing hunter-gatherer and pastoralist populations.

## Timeline
- **c. 70,000 years ago** — Central African Pygmies and Bantu speakers branch from a common ancestor.
- **c. 7000 BC** — Desertification of the Green Sahara may have pushed ancestral populations southward into West Africa.
- **c. 5000 BC** — Oldest pottery found in a Bantu-inhabited area (Shum Laka, Cameroon).
- **c. 4000 BC – 500 AD** — Main period of the Bantu expansion.
- **c. 2500–1200 BC** — Proto-Bantu peoples diverge into East and West Bantu groups.
- **c. 500 BC** — Western Stream reaches southern forest fringe; Eastern Stream reaches west of Lake Victoria.
- **c. 100 BC – 300 AD** — Bantu communities present on the coasts of Tanzania and Kenya.
- **c. 300 AD** — Bantu speakers reach the coast of modern KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa.
- **c. 500 AD** — Bantu speakers reach the modern Limpopo Province, South Africa.

## Body

### Origins and Theories
The expansion likely began in the highlands between Cameroon and Nigeria, with the Mambilla region identified as a possible homeland. Linguistic and genetic evidence points to a recent common origin for Bantu languages, a branch of the Atlantic-Congo family, in southern Cameroon. The expansion is traditionally linked to the development of agriculture, ceramics, and later iron use, which allowed exploitation of new ecological zones. A 2023 genetic study places the expansion's start in Western Africa around 4,000 years ago.

### Routes and Timeline
The expansion proceeded in two main streams. The **Western Stream** moved south along the Atlantic coast or Congo rivers, reaching central Angola by 500 BC. Progress was slow initially due to dense forest and lack of iron tools. The **Eastern Stream** moved east along the rainforest's northern edge or the Ubangi River, reaching the Lake Victoria region by 500 BC. From there, one group moved west to rejoin the Western Stream, while the other dispersed south and east across the continent.

### Pre-Expansion Demography
Before the expansion, Central, Southern, and Southeast Africa were inhabited by diverse groups: Pygmy foragers, Khoisan-speaking hunter-gatherers, Nilo-Saharan-speaking herders, and Cushitic-speaking pastoralists. The Bantu expansion pushed into these territories, leading to complex interactions.

### Interactions with Local Populations
Bantu speakers engaged in extensive cultural and physical contact with pre-existing groups, including intermarriage. Hunter-gatherer women often migrated into Bantu communities, while Bantu men moved into foraging groups. This contact is evidenced by shared technologies, rituals, and genes, such as the adoption of click sounds in some southern Bantu languages. While absorption was common, some displacement and possible population replacement occurred, though evidence is spotty.

## Terms
- **Proto-Bantu**: The reconstructed ancestral language from which modern Bantu languages descend.
- **Atlantic-Congo**: A major language family of Africa, of which Bantu is a branch.
- **Western Stream / Eastern Stream**: The two primary directional routes of the Bantu expansion.
- **Urewe pottery**: A type of early iron-age pottery associated with the Eastern Stream's arrival near Lake Victoria.
- **Khoisan**: A term for the diverse hunter-gatherer and pastoralist peoples of Southern Africa who spoke click languages.
- **Admixture**: The mixing of genes from previously isolated populations.
- **Haplogroup E1b1a1-M2**: The predominant paternal genetic lineage among Bantu-speaking peoples.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise cause and trigger for the expansion remain unclear; some scholars suggest it may have begun accidentally.
- There is debate over the correlation between archaeological evidence (like ceramics) and linguistic spread, with criticism that the link is sometimes assumed rather than proven.
- The extent of population replacement versus assimilation in specific regions, such as Angola, is uncertain due to a lack of clear archaeological evidence.
- The term "Bantu" itself is contested, with concerns it has been incorrectly expanded from a linguistic label to imply a unified culture, society, or race.
