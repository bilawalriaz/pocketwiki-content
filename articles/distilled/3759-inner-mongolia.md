# Inner Mongolia

## Overview
Inner Mongolia is an autonomous region in northern China, bordering Mongolia and Russia. It is China's third-largest administrative subdivision, known for its vast grasslands, significant Mongol population, and rich mineral resources. The region's history reflects a long interplay between nomadic and settled cultures, and its modern identity is shaped by economic development and ongoing ethnic and linguistic tensions.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Slab Grave culture, linked to Proto-Mongols, flourishes in the region.
- **340–295 BC** — King Wuling of Zhao expands into the area, building a wall in the Hetao region.
- **127 BC** — Han dynasty general Wei Qing reconquers the Hetao region from the Xiongnu.
- **1206** — Genghis Khan unifies the Mongol tribes, founding the Mongol Empire.
- **1271** — Kublai Khan establishes the Yuan dynasty, with a summer capital near present-day Dolonnor.
- **1635** — The Manchus gain control of Inner Mongolian tribes after the surrender of the Chakhar Mongols.
- **1911** — Outer Mongolia declares independence from the Qing dynasty.
- **1947** — The Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region is established by the Chinese Communists.
- **1966–1976** — During the Cultural Revolution, repressions target the Mongol population.
- **2020** — Protests erupt over government plans to phase out Mongolian-only medium teaching.

## Body

### Historical Development
The region's history is characterized by alternating control between Chinese dynasties and various nomadic groups, including the Xiongnu, Xianbei, Khitan, and Mongols. The Mongol Empire's rise in the 13th century brought the area under unified rule, followed by the Ming dynasty's reconstruction of the Great Wall along its southern border. The Qing dynasty (1644–1912) administered Inner Mongolia through a league-banner system and, despite initial prohibitions, encouraged Han Chinese settlement from the 18th century onward, altering the demographic balance.

### Modern Political History
After the fall of the Qing, the Republic of China reorganized the area into provinces. During World War II, parts were occupied by the Japanese puppet state Manchukuo, while Prince Demchugdongrub established the Mengjiang regime. Following the war, the Chinese Communists, with Soviet support, established the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region in 1947. Under the People's Republic of China, the region was expanded to its current elongated shape. The Cultural Revolution brought severe repression, disproportionately affecting Mongols. Since the 2000s, rapid economic growth from mining has been accompanied by environmental degradation and social tensions.

### Geography and Climate
Inner Mongolia is a vast plateau averaging 1,200 meters in altitude, featuring the Khingan Mountains in the north and the arid Gobi Desert in the west. Its elongated shape results in a four-season monsoon climate with long, cold winters and short, warm summers. The region is a major mining district with large reserves of coal, rare-earth elements, and other minerals. It faces significant environmental challenges, including desertification and sandstorms.

### Demographics and Culture
As of the 2020 census, Han Chinese constitute 78.7% of the population, while Mongols make up 17.7%, forming the largest Mongol population in the world. Intermarriage between Mongols and Han Chinese is common. The Mongolian language has seen periods of decline and revival; by law, public signage and government documents are bilingual. Traditional Mongolian culture, including nomadic herding and Tibetan Buddhism, remains influential, though urbanization and Han migration have altered traditional lifestyles.

### Economy
Inner Mongolia's economy is driven by mining (coal, rare-earth metals), energy production, and agriculture. It is a major coal producer and has the largest usable wind power capacity in China. Key industries include dairy processing (e.g., Yili, Mengniu), metallurgy, and chemicals. The region's GDP per capita ranks 8th among Chinese provinces, but growth has been uneven, contributing to ethnic tensions and environmental issues.

## Terms
- **Autonomous Region**: A provincial-level administrative division of China with constitutionally guaranteed rights to limited self-governance for ethnic minorities.
- **League (Meng)**: A historical administrative division in Inner Mongolia, equivalent to a prefecture, retained from the Qing dynasty banner system.
- **Banner (Qi)**: A county-level administrative division in Inner Mongolia, also a historical Mongol tribal unit under the Qing.
- **Hetao Region**: A fertile plain along the Yellow River in western Inner Mongolia, historically a key area for agriculture and conflict between nomads and farmers.
- **Mongolian Script**: The traditional vertical script used for writing Mongolian in Inner Mongolia, distinct from the Cyrillic alphabet used in the country of Mongolia.
- **Tibetan Buddhism (Yellow Buddhism)**: The dominant form of Buddhism in Inner Mongolia, also practiced by some Han Chinese.
- **Desertification**: The process by which fertile land becomes desert, a major environmental issue in parts of Inner Mongolia due to overgrazing and climate factors.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Ethnic Identity and Marginalization**: Many indigenous Mongolians feel increasingly marginalized due to Han Chinese migration, economic policies, and cultural assimilation pressures, leading to social unrest.
- **Language Policy**: The 2020 government plan to replace Mongolian with Chinese as the medium of instruction in some subjects sparked significant protests, highlighting tensions over cultural preservation versus national integration.
- **Environmental vs. Economic Development**: The rapid expansion of mining and industry has driven growth but caused severe pollution and grassland degradation, raising questions about sustainable development.
