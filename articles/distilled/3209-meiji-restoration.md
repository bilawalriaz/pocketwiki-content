# Meiji Restoration

## Overview
The Meiji Restoration was a political revolution in 1868 that restored practical imperial rule to Japan under Emperor Meiji, ending the Tokugawa shogunate's feudal system. It triggered Japan's rapid modernization and westernization, transforming it from an isolated feudal society into a centralized industrial state and a major world power, with profound consequences for East Asia.

## Timeline
- **1853** — Commodore Matthew Perry's expedition arrives, demanding Japan open to trade.
- **1854** — Convention of Kanagawa signed, ending Japan's isolation policy (*sakoku*).
- **1858** — Treaty of Amity and Commerce (unequal treaty) signed with the United States.
- **1860** — Shogunal regent Ii Naosuke is assassinated by *shishi* loyalists.
- **1863** — Chōshū Domain fires on foreign ships; British bombard Kagoshima.
- **1866** — Satsuma-Chōshū Alliance is formed against the Tokugawa shogunate.
- **1867** — Shōgun Tokugawa Yoshinobu resigns, returning political power to the Emperor.
- **1868** — Emperor Meiji declares the restoration of imperial rule (January 3).
- **1868-69** — Boshin War: Imperial forces defeat Tokugawa remnants.
- **1869** — Charter Oath issued, outlining goals for a new government.
- **1871** — Domains (*han*) are abolished and replaced with prefectures.
- **1873** — Nationwide conscription law establishes a modern imperial army.
- **1876** — Samurai stipends are commuted to bonds; samurai class is effectively abolished.
- **1889** — The Meiji Constitution is promulgated.

## Body

### Origins and Background
The Restoration arose from crises within the Tokugawa shogunate's feudal system. A rigid social hierarchy placed the Emperor and Court at the top, followed by the shōgun, daimyō (domain lords), and samurai, with farmers, artisans, and merchants below. The shōgun's authority was built on land control, but economic strains, including samurai poverty and merchant class growth, eroded this structure. Ideologically, the nativist *kokugaku* (national learning) movement and the Mito School's *sonnō jōi* ("revere the Emperor, expel the barbarian") slogan challenged shogunal legitimacy. Foreign pressure, beginning with Perry's 1853 expedition and culminating in unequal treaties, exposed the shogunate's weakness and ignited factional conflict between pro- and anti-foreign factions among samurai and domains.

### The Fall of the Shogunate
The unequal treaties sparked political violence and the rise of *shishi* ("men of spirit"), lower-ranking samurai loyal to the Emperor. After the shogunate's failed punitive expeditions against Chōshū Domain, the anti-Tokugawa Satsuma-Chōshū Alliance formed in 1866. In 1867, Shōgun Tokugawa Yoshinobu attempted to preempt revolution by resigning his administrative powers to the Emperor. However, Satsuma and Chōshū leaders, including Saigō Takamori and Ōkubo Toshimichi, staged a coup on January 3, 1868, formally declaring the restoration of imperial rule. The ensuing Boshin War (1868-69) saw the defeat of Tokugawa forces, ending the shogunate.

### Imperial Reforms and Modernization
The new Meiji government, guided by the 1868 Charter Oath, pursued radical centralization and modernization. It abolished the feudal domain system in 1871, reorganizing Japan into prefectures under central control. The samurai class was dismantled through stipend commutation (1876) and the 1873 conscription law, which created a modern national army. These reforms sparked samurai rebellions, like the 1877 Satsuma Rebellion, which was crushed by the new conscript army. The government adopted the slogan *fukoku kyōhei* ("rich nation, strong army"), promoting industrialization, a national railway system, and Western technology, funded by high land taxes on farmers. A national education system and a standard language were established to forge a unified national identity.

## Terms
- **Bakufu**: The "tent government" of the Tokugawa shogunate, ruled by the shōgun.
- **Daimyō**: Feudal lords who ruled over domains (*han*) during the Edo period.
- **Sakoku**: The national isolation policy of the Tokugawa shogunate, restricting foreign trade and contact.
- **Shishi**: "Men of spirit," lower and middle-ranking samurai who were radical loyalists to the Emperor.
- **Sonnō jōi**: "Revere the Emperor, expel the barbarian," an anti-foreign, pro-Imperial ideology.
- **Fukoku kyōhei**: "Enrich the country, strengthen the military," the modernizing slogan of the Meiji government.
- **Han**: The feudal domains ruled by daimyō, abolished in 1871.
- **Unequal Treaties**: Unfavorable treaties forced on Japan by Western powers after 1854, which granted extraterritoriality and fixed low tariffs.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source highlights scholarly debate on the nature of the Restoration's leadership. Historian W.G. Beasley argues that the victory was fundamentally that of the *shishi* and progressive daimyō, who initially sought only to displace the Tokugawa, not dismantle feudal institutions. The need for centralization and national unity then drove further reforms. Another point of analysis is the role of foreign pressure: while the unequal treaties destabilized the shogunate, the anti-Tokugawa alliance ultimately shifted from the xenophobic *jōi* policy to the pragmatic *fukoku kyōhei* after military failures against Western powers demonstrated the need for modern technology.
