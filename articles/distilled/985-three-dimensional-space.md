# Three-dimensional space

## Overview

Three-dimensional (3D) Euclidean space, denoted ℝ³, is the mathematical model of physical space in which every point is specified by three coordinates. It is foundational to geometry, physics, engineering, and computer graphics because it captures the spatial relationships humans experience. Beyond the classical Euclidean model, more general 3-manifolds—spaces that locally resemble ℝ³—extend these ideas into topology and relativity.

## Timeline

- **c. 350 BCE** — Aristotle asserts three dimensions as the basis of physical magnitude
- **c. 300 BCE** — Euclid's *Elements*, Books XI–XIII, systematize 3D geometry
- **1637** — Descartes publishes *La Géométrie*, introducing Cartesian coordinates
- **1670s** — Newton develops polar coordinate systems
- **1750s–1790s** — Euler and Monge advance differential geometry of curves and surfaces
- **1843** — Hamilton introduces quaternions, coining "scalar" and "vector"
- **1880s** — Gibbs formalizes dot and cross products; Grassmann and Peano develop abstract vector spaces

## Body

### Coordinate Systems and Analytic Geometry

Three-dimensional space is most commonly represented using Cartesian coordinates: three mutually perpendicular axes (x, y, z) intersecting at an origin. Any point is given by an ordered triple (x, y, z) of real numbers. Alternative systems include cylindrical and spherical coordinates, each suited to specific symmetries. Analytic geometry, pioneered by Descartes, translates geometric problems into algebraic equations via these coordinate systems.

### Lines, Planes, and Spatial Relationships

Two distinct points determine a line; three non-collinear points determine a plane. Two lines in 3D may intersect, be parallel, or be skew (neither intersecting nor coplanar). Two planes either intersect in a line or are parallel. A hyperplane in 3D is a plane, described by a single linear equation. Varignon’s theorem states that the midpoints of any quadrilateral in ℝ³ form a parallelogram, hence are coplanar.

### Spheres, Balls, and Surfaces

A sphere (2-sphere) is the set of points at fixed distance r from a center; the enclosed solid is a ball (3-ball). Volume is V = (4/3)πr³; surface area is A = 4πr². A 3-sphere arises as the boundary of a 4-ball. Surfaces of revolution are generated by rotating a plane curve about a fixed axis. Quadric surfaces—ellipsoids, hyperboloids, paraboloids, cones—are defined by second-degree equations in x, y, z.

### Polytopes

There are nine regular polytopes in 3D: five convex Platonic solids (tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron, icosahedron) and four nonconvex Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra.

### Linear Algebra and Vector Operations

Three-dimensional space is fundamentally tied to linear independence: three vectors are independent if none lies in the plane of the other two. The dot product A · B = |A||B|cos θ measures projection and angle; its self-product gives vector magnitude. The cross product A × B yields a vector perpendicular to both, with magnitude |A||B|sin θ. Together, (ℝ³, ×) forms a Lie algebra isomorphic to 𝔰𝔬(3), the algebra of 3D rotations.

### Calculus in 3D

Vector calculus studies fields and operators in ℝ³. The gradient ∇f points in the direction of greatest increase of f. Divergence ∇ · F measures net flux from a point; curl ∇ × F measures rotational tendency. Integrals include line integrals (over curves), surface integrals (over surfaces), and volume integrals (over regions). Key theorems connect these: the fundamental theorem of line integrals, Stokes’ theorem (relating surface curl to boundary circulation), and the divergence theorem (relating volume divergence to boundary flux).

### Topological and Abstract Perspectives

Topologically, 3D space is distinguished by properties like knotting—at least three dimensions are required to tie a knot. Abstractly, 3D space can be modeled as a vector space V over ℝ (without a preferred basis), an affine space E(3) (without a preferred origin), or an inner product space (with notions of length and angle). These abstractions clarify physical symmetries: rotational invariance favors basis-free descriptions, while translational invariance favors origin-free ones.

### Finite Geometry

Finite analogs of 3D space exist in Galois geometry. For example, PG(3,2) uses the Fano plane as its 2D subspaces, and for any finite field GF(q), a projective space PG(3,q) of three dimensions can be constructed.

## Terms

- **3-manifold**: A space that locally resembles ℝ³ but may have different global topology.
- **Cartesian coordinates**: A system using three perpendicular axes to specify point positions via ordered triples.
- **Cross product**: A binary operation on two vectors in ℝ³ yielding a third vector perpendicular to both.
- **Dot product**: A scalar product of two vectors equaling |A||B|cos θ, measuring projection and angle.
- **Quadric surface**: A surface defined by a second-degree equation in three variables.
- **Skew lines**: Lines in 3D that neither intersect nor lie in a common plane.
- **Surface of revolution**: A surface generated by rotating a plane curve about a fixed axis.
- **Vector field**: A function assigning a vector to each point in space.
- **Lie algebra**: An algebraic structure modeling infinitesimal symmetries; (ℝ³, ×) ≅ 𝔰𝔬(3).
- **Affine space**: A geometric structure lacking a fixed origin, preserving translational symmetry.

## Debates and Open Questions

The nature of physical space remains debated: while classical physics treats 3D Euclidean space as fundamental, general relativity models spacetime as a curved 4D manifold. Whether 3D space is globally Euclidean or a more complex 3-manifold is an open empirical question. In mathematics, the classification of 3-manifolds—resolved by Perelman’s proof of the Poincaré conjecture—remains a deep area of study, with questions about geometric structures and their uniqueness still active.
