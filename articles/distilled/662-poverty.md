# Poverty

## Overview  
Poverty is a condition in which individuals or communities lack the financial resources and essentials for a basic standard of living. It has diverse environmental, legal, social, economic, and political causes and effects, and is measured in two main ways: **absolute poverty** (income below what is needed for basic needs like food, shelter, and clothing) and **relative poverty** (inability to meet minimum living standards compared to others in the same society). Poverty perpetuates cycles across generations and regions, influencing health, education, shelter, and economic participation. International efforts, such as the UN's Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), aim to reduce poverty globally, though debates persist over measurement methods, causes, and effective interventions.

## Body  

### Definitions and Measurement  

The term *poverty* originates from Latin *paupertas*, meaning lack of resources. The **United Nations** defines it as a denial of choices and opportunities, violating human dignity. The **World Bank** describes it as deprivation in well-being, including low income, poor health, and limited access to services. The **European Union** uses **relative poverty**, measuring those whose resources fall below the minimum acceptable standard in their country.  

Two primary metrics exist:  
- **Absolute poverty**: A fixed threshold based on basic needs, often measured using purchasing power parity (PPP). The World Bank’s $1.90/day line is widely used but criticized for being too low or arbitrary.  
- **Relative poverty**: Income below a percentage of median earnings—commonly 60% in OECD/EU contexts. It reflects social exclusion rather than material deprivation alone.  

Other measures include **secondary poverty** (caused by substance abuse or poor financial decisions) and **relational poverty** (lack of meaningful human connections).

### Global Prevalence and Trends  

As of 2019, 85% of the global population lived on less than $30/day (PPP), with 10% surviving on under $1.90/day. Conflict-affected regions host over 40% of the poor. While global extreme poverty declined from 37% in 1990 to 9.6% in 2015, progress has slowed since 2015, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa. China lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty through economic reforms, while parts of Africa saw increases in both poverty rates and numbers. The COVID-19 pandemic reversed some gains, with Oxfam estimating five billion people became poorer since 2020.

### Causes and Cycles  

Poverty stems from structural inequalities, weak governance, conflict, and historical factors like colonialism. Neoliberal policies emphasize private investment and market liberalization, whereas socialist approaches advocate redistribution. Cultural narratives often blame the poor for their circumstances, despite evidence showing most are trapped by systemic barriers. Dynastic politics and corruption further entrench poverty in many nations.

Cycles of poverty operate at individual, community, and global levels. Poor health limits educational attainment, reducing future income potential. Discrimination based on gender, race, disability, or caste exacerbates vulnerability. Women and children bear disproportionate burdens, with girls facing early marriage and restricted access to education.

### Impacts on Health, Education, and Society  

Poverty severely affects physical and mental health. One-third of global deaths annually stem from poverty-related causes. Malnutrition contributes to half of all child mortality cases. Mental health issues arise from chronic stress, trauma, and cognitive strain due to financial insecurity. Children in poverty show delayed cognitive development and lower academic performance.

Education gaps widen inequality. Low-income students face higher dropout rates and reduced access to quality schooling. Structural barriers include unsafe learning environments, inadequate infrastructure, and lack of parental support. Conditional cash transfer programs have shown success in improving enrollment and nutrition outcomes.

Housing instability and homelessness increase exposure to violence and disease. Slum dwellers experience conditions worse than rural populations. Gentrification displaces vulnerable groups without addressing root causes.

### Poverty Reduction Strategies  

Strategies focus on increasing access to basic needs or boosting personal income:

#### Increasing Access to Basic Needs  
- **Technology**: Agricultural innovations like fertilizers and irrigation boosted food production post-Industrial Revolution. Medical advances improved maternal and child health outcomes.  
- **State Funding**: Universal healthcare reduces costs; targeted subsidies for essentials help the poor directly. However, corruption diverts funds, and debt servicing constrains public spending.  
- **Infrastructure**: Roads connect remote areas to markets and services. Microfranchising distributes goods efficiently in underserved regions.  
- **Family Planning**: Education and reproductive health services slow population growth, easing resource pressures.

#### Increasing Personal Income  
- **Income Grants**: Universal basic income (UBI) and negative income taxes provide direct financial support. Cash transfers improve consumption, schooling, and nutrition.  
- **Economic Freedoms**: Secure land rights empower rural populations. Market access and migration remittances boost incomes.  
- **Financial Services**: Mobile banking and postal savings systems serve the unbanked. Microcredit shows mixed results, with concerns about exploitative interest rates.  
- **Education & Training**: Free schooling and vocational training enhance employability. School feeding programs and deworming initiatives yield high returns on investment.

### Institutions and Ethics  

Key institutions include:
- **Intergovernmental Organizations**: The UN coordinates global goals via the SDGs. The World Bank and IMF fund development projects but impose structural adjustment conditions.  
- **NGOs**: Effective locally but face coordination challenges and donor dependency.  
- **For-Profit Entities**: The "poverty industrial complex" raises ethical concerns about profit motives overriding service delivery.

Ethically, poverty is viewed as a human rights violation. Environmental justice movements highlight disproportionate climate impacts on the Global South. Spiritual traditions sometimes embrace voluntary poverty as a path to enlightenment or moral purity.

## Terms  

1. **Absolute Poverty**: Income insufficient to meet basic needs like food, water, shelter, and clothing.  
2. **Relative Poverty**: Inability to achieve a standard of living typical in a given society.  
3. **Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)**: Exchange rate accounting for price differences across countries.  
4. **Secondary Poverty**: Poverty caused by behavioral factors like addiction or poor financial choices.  
5. **Relational Poverty**: Lack of meaningful social relationships contributing to marginalization.  
6. **Gini Coefficient**: Measure of income inequality within a population.  
7. **Microfinance**: Financial services targeting low-income individuals, including microloans and savings accounts.  
8. **Structural Adjustment Programs (SAPs)**: Policy reforms imposed by international lenders requiring fiscal austerity and privatization.  
9. **Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)**: UN framework aiming to end poverty, protect the planet, and ensure prosperity by 2030.  
10. **Post-Traumatic Poverty Disorder (PTPD)**: Psychological trauma experienced by those who have escaped poverty fearing relapse.

## Debates and Open Questions  

Several contentious issues surround poverty measurement and policy:
- Is the World Bank’s $1.90/day threshold realistic? Critics argue it underestimates true deprivation, proposing higher lines like $7.40/day.  
- Should developed nations prioritize relative or absolute measures? Relative poverty better captures inequality in wealthy societies.  
- Do neoliberal reforms effectively reduce poverty, or do they deepen disparities? Evidence varies by region and implementation.  
- Can technology and globalization alone lift people out of poverty, or must structural changes accompany them?  
- How should cultural values influence anti-poverty strategies? Some argue values shape economic behavior; others emphasize opportunity structures.  
- What role does climate change play in perpetuating poverty? Projections suggest millions more could fall into extreme poverty by 2030.  
- Are cash transfers more effective than in-kind aid? Studies favor direct transfers, but political resistance remains strong.  
- Should debt relief and land reform be central to poverty reduction? Advocates cite transformative potential; skeptics question feasibility.
