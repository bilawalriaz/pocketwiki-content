# History of Southeast Asia

## Overview
Southeast Asia's history is defined by its two distinct sub-regions—Mainland (Indochina) and Maritime (Insular)—and its position at the crossroads of major Indian Ocean and South China Sea trade routes. This geography fostered a patchwork of diverse cultures and polities, from early agrarian kingdoms to powerful thalassocracies (sea-based states), which selectively adopted and adapted influences from India, China, and later Europe, culminating in modern nation-states within the ASEAN framework.

## Timeline
- **c. 1.5 million years ago** — *Homo erectus* present in the region (e.g., Java Man).
- **c. 50,000–70,000 BCE** — Earliest *Homo sapiens* presence in Mainland and Maritime Southeast Asia.
- **c. 3,500 BCE** — Austronesian expansion begins from Taiwan, colonizing Island Southeast Asia.
- **c. 2,000 BCE** — Earliest copper use at Ban Chiang (Thailand) and Phùng Nguyên culture (Vietnam).
- **c. 1,500 BCE** — Austronesians establish the first Indian Ocean maritime trade network with India/Sri Lanka.
- **c. 500 BCE** — Sophisticated bronze industry (Đông Sơn culture); first Agrarian Kingdoms (e.g., Funan, Văn Lang).
- **Early centuries CE** — "Indianisation" begins; selective adoption of Hindu/Buddhist statecraft and culture.
- **5th–13th centuries CE** — Buddhism flourishes; Srivijaya (Sumatra) becomes a major maritime and Buddhist power.
- **13th century** — First Islamic societies emerge; rise of Majapahit (Java) and Khmer Empire (Cambodia).
- **1405–1433** — Chinese treasure voyages under Zheng He visit Southeast Asia.
- **1511** — Portuguese conquer Malacca, beginning the era of European colonialism.
- **1945–1984** — Post-WWII decolonization; end of European rule with Brunei's independence.

## Body

### Prehistory and Early Migrations
The region was inhabited by *Homo erectus* from about 1.5 million years ago. Modern *Homo sapiens* arrived by 50,000–70,000 BCE. From around 25,000 BCE, East Asian-related populations expanded south from the mainland. The Neolithic saw major migrations: Austronesian speakers from Taiwan colonized the islands using advanced seafaring technology, while Austroasiatic speakers populated the mainland via river systems. Early agricultural societies cultivating millet and wet-rice emerged by c. 1700 BCE.

### Bronze Age and Early Kingdoms
Copper and bronze production began independently around 2,000 BCE. By 500 BCE, the Đông Sơn culture produced sophisticated bronze drums. This technological development supported the first centralized Agrarian Kingdoms, such as Funan on the Mekong and Văn Lang in the Red River Delta, which relied on rice surplus and coastal trade.

### Indianisation and Maritime Trade
From around 500 BCE, Southeast Asia's position at the crossroads of Indian and Chinese maritime routes stimulated economic and cultural growth. Local rulers selectively adopted Indian Hindu concepts of statecraft (e.g., *devarāja* or god-king) and religion to legitimize power. This process, termed "Indianisation," led to the rise of Indianised kingdoms like Funan, Champa, and early Javanese states. The Austronesian maritime trade network, established by 1500 BCE, later evolved into the Maritime Silk Road.

### Spread of Religions
Buddhism arrived alongside Hinduism but gained broader popular appeal. It flourished between the 5th and 13th centuries, with Mahayana Buddhism prominent in Srivijaya and Java (e.g., Borobudur temple). Later, Theravada Buddhism was introduced from Sri Lanka, becoming dominant in mainland kingdoms like Pagan (Burma) and Sukhothai (Thailand). Islam arrived via traders as early as the 8th century but became widespread by the 13th century, spreading peacefully through trade networks and syncretizing with existing beliefs.

### Medieval Empires and Chinese Contact
The Srivijaya thalassocracy dominated maritime trade until the 13th century. On the mainland, the Khmer Empire (9th–15th centuries) and the Toungoo Empire (16th century) were major powers. China's influence was primarily through the tributary system and sporadic trade, not deep acculturation. The Ming dynasty's treasure voyages (1405–1433) sought to expand Chinese influence but were later discontinued.

### European Colonisation and the Modern Era
European colonialism began with the Portuguese capture of Malacca in 1511, followed by the Dutch, Spanish, British, and French. By 1913, nearly all the region was under colonial rule, except Thailand. Colonialism introduced commercial agriculture, new demographics, and modern state institutions, which fueled nationalist movements. After WWII, rapid decolonization led to independence for most nations. The Cold War saw conflicts like the Vietnam War. Contemporary Southeast Asia is characterized by economic growth and regional integration through ASEAN, founded in 1967.

## Terms
- **Thalassocracy**: A state whose power is primarily based on the sea and maritime trade.
- **Indianisation**: The process by which Southeast Asian polities selectively adopted Indian Hindu-Buddhist elements of statecraft, religion, and culture.
- **Devarāja**: A Hindu concept of divine kingship, where the ruler is seen as a god or god-king, used to legitimize authority.
- **Hoabinhian**: A stone tool industry and cultural tradition of hunter-gatherers in Southeast Asia from around 10,000 BCE.
- **Austronesian expansion**: The prehistoric migration of Austronesian-speaking peoples from Taiwan, colonizing Island Southeast Asia and the Pacific.
- **Maritime Silk Road**: The sea-based trade network connecting China, Southeast Asia, India, the Middle East, and Africa, flourishing from about the 2nd century BCE.
- **Sundaland**: The vast lowlands of the Sunda Shelf, exposed during glacial periods, which allowed human migration between mainland and island Southeast Asia.
- **ASEAN**: The Association of Southeast Asian Nations, a regional intergovernmental organization founded in 1967 to promote cooperation.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact nature, process, and extent of Indian influence on Southeast Asian civilizations is fiercely debated. Scholars question whether Indian merchants, Brahmins, or local initiative drove the process, and how deep the cultural adoption went.
- The routes and primary agents for the introduction of Islam into Southeast Asia are debated, with hypotheses pointing to direct Arab traders, Indian Muslim merchants, or even Chinese connections via the South China Sea.
- The true character and extent of control exerted by empires like Majapahit over their claimed territories remain unclear.
