# Kinkaku-ji

## Overview
Kinkaku-ji, officially Rokuon-ji, is a Zen Buddhist temple in Kyoto, Japan, famous for its Golden Pavilion (Kinkaku), a reliquary hall whose top two stories are covered in gold leaf. Originally a 14th-century shōgun's villa, it was converted into a temple and is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Its iconic pavilion, destroyed by arson in 1950 and meticulously rebuilt in 1955, is a masterpiece of Japanese architecture and garden design, symbolizing the Muromachi period's aesthetic ideals.

## Timeline
- **1397** — Shōgun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu purchases the Kitayama-dai villa and begins transforming it into the Kinkaku-ji complex.
- **1399** — The original Golden Pavilion is believed to have been constructed.
- **1467–1477** — During the Ōnin War, all buildings in the complex except the pavilion are burned down.
- **2 July 1950** — The pavilion is burned down by novice monk Hayashi Shoken.
- **1955** — The current pavilion structure is rebuilt.
- **1986–1987** — The gold leaf on the reconstructed pavilion is replaced.

## Body

### History and Significance
The site was originally a villa for the statesman Saionji Kintsune. In 1397, the powerful shōgun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu acquired it and converted it into the Kinkaku-ji complex. After his death, his son transformed it into a Zen temple per his wishes. The pavilion's gold leaf was symbolically intended to purify negative thoughts about death, while its visual excess was characteristic of the Muromachi period. The complex survived the Ōnin War (1467–1477), which destroyed other buildings. The most significant modern event was the 1950 arson by a novice monk, an act fictionalized in Yukio Mishima's novel *The Temple of the Golden Pavilion*. The pavilion was rebuilt in 1955, with its gold leaf replaced in the 1980s.

### Architectural Design
The three-story Golden Pavilion is a *shariden* (reliquary hall) housing Buddha's ashes. It uniquely incorporates three distinct architectural styles, one per floor. The first floor, the Chamber of Dharma Waters, is in the *shinden-zukuri* style of Heian aristocracy, using natural wood and plaster. The second floor, the Tower of Sound Waves, is in the *buke-zukuri* style of warrior aristocrats and contains a Buddha Hall. The third floor, the Cupola of the Ultimate, is built in traditional Chinese *Zen* style (*zenshū-butsuden-zukuri*). The building is topped with a bronze phoenix ornament.

### Garden Design
The pavilion is set in a Japanese strolling garden (*kaiyū-shiki-teien*) that employs the principle of "borrowed scenery" (*shakkei*), integrating the surrounding landscape. It extends over the Mirror Pond (*Kyōko-chi*), which reflects the building and contains ten islands. The garden's rock compositions and plant arrangements are designed to evoke famous scenes from Chinese and Japanese literature. The entire complex was built to illustrate the Western Paradise of the Buddha Amida, creating a harmony between heaven and earth. This garden is a prime example of Muromachi period design, which emphasized a minimalistic, scaled-down recreation of larger landscapes around a central structure.

## Terms
- **Shariden (舎利殿):** A reliquary hall that houses relics of the Buddha, such as ashes.
- **Shinden-zukuri (寝殿造):** An architectural style of the Heian period, characterized by a main hall with flanking wings and verandas, typical of aristocratic residences.
- **Buke-zukuri (武家造):** An architectural style associated with the samurai or warrior class.
- **Zenshū-butsuden-zukuri (禅宗仏殿造):** A Zen Buddhist temple architectural style, often featuring Chinese influences.
- **Kaiyū-shiki-teien (回遊式庭園):** A Japanese strolling garden designed for walking around a central pond or feature.
- **Shakkei (借景):** "Borrowed scenery," a design technique that incorporates background landscape (e.g., mountains) into the composition of a garden.
- **Hōō (鳳凰):** A mythical phoenix, a common decorative motif in East Asian art and architecture.

## Debates and Open Questions
There is scholarly debate about the extent of the gold leaf used on the original 14th-century pavilion. The 1955 reconstruction is considered a close copy, but some question whether the original had such an extensive gold-leaf coating.
