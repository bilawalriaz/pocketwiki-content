# Japanese art

## Overview
Japanese art encompasses a vast range of media and styles, from ancient pottery to contemporary anime. Its history is defined by cycles of absorbing foreign influences—primarily from China and Korea—and periods of isolation that fostered indigenous development. This dynamic interplay has produced a unique aesthetic tradition that has significantly influenced global art, particularly in the 19th and 20th centuries.

## Timeline
- **c. 10,500–c. 300 BCE** — Jōmon period; production of cord-marked pottery and dogū figurines.
- **c. 300 BCE–c. 300 CE** — Yayoi period; introduction of wet-rice cultivation, bronze bells, and wheel-thrown pottery.
- **c. 300–710 CE** — Kofun period; characterized by keyhole-shaped tombs and haniwa clay figures.
- **552/538 CE** — Official introduction of Buddhism to Japan, spurring continental artistic influence.
- **794–1185** — Heian period; development of indigenous Yamato-e painting and the e-maki narrative scroll.
- **1185–1333** — Kamakura period; rise of warrior class and realistic sculpture by the Kei school.
- **1338–1573** — Muromachi period; dominance of Zen Buddhism and Chinese-inspired ink wash painting.
- **1573–1603** — Azuchi-Momoyama period; monumental screen painting by the Kanō school.
- **1603–1867** — Edo period; flourishing of ukiyo-e woodblock prints and secular arts under Tokugawa rule.
- **1868–1912** — Meiji period; rapid modernization and influx of Western styles; technical peak in decorative arts.
- **1945–present** — Postwar period; emergence of avant-garde movements and global influence of manga and anime.

## Body

### Prehistoric and Early Historic Art
The earliest complex art was produced by the Jōmon people, known for their lavishly decorated pottery and clay figurines (dogū). The subsequent Yayoi and Kofun periods saw the introduction of new technologies like bronze casting and the development of tomb culture. The first major influx of continental culture arrived with Buddhism in the 6th and 7th centuries, leading to the creation of Japan's earliest sculptures and wooden temple architecture, such as at Hōryū-ji.

### Classical and Medieval Periods (Heian to Muromachi)
The Heian period saw a turn toward indigenous expression, with the rise of Yamato-e painting and the narrative handscroll (e-maki). The Fujiwara era's Pure Land Buddhism inspired Amida halls like the Phoenix Hall. Power shifted to warriors in the Kamakura period, spurring realistic sculpture. The Muromachi period was dominated by Zen Buddhism, which influenced monochromatic ink wash painting and the development of the tea ceremony aesthetic.

### Early Modern Period (Azuchi-Momoyama to Edo)
The Azuchi-Momoyama period featured bold, monumental screen painting by the Kanō school. The Edo period's isolationist policies fostered a vibrant popular culture, epitomized by ukiyo-e woodblock prints depicting the "floating world." This era also saw the refinement of lacquerware (maki-e) and the rise of the Rinpa school's decorative style. Porcelain production began after Korean potters were brought to Japan.

### Modern and Contemporary Art (Meiji to Present)
The Meiji Restoration opened Japan to Western influence, creating a dichotomy between yōga (Western-style painting) and Nihonga (Japanese-style painting). Technical mastery in decorative arts like cloisonné enamel and metalwork reached its peak for export. Post-WWII, Japanese art moved from socialist realism to a vibrant avant-garde in the 1960s, including Gutai and Mono-ha. Contemporary art is globally influential, with artists like Takashi Murakami and Yayoi Kusama, and the worldwide spread of manga and anime aesthetics.

## Terms
*   **Ukiyo-e:** A genre of woodblock prints and paintings from the Edo period, depicting the "floating world" of pleasure districts, kabuki actors, and landscapes.
*   **Yamato-e:** A classical Japanese painting style characterized by indigenous subject matter, decorative use of color, and narrative scrolls (e-maki).
*   **Maki-e:** A lacquer decoration technique sprinkling gold or silver dust onto wet lacquer, prominent in Edo and Meiji periods.
*   **Dogū:** Small clay figurines from the Jōmon period, often with exaggerated features, possibly used in rituals.
*   **Haniwa:** Terracotta clay figures placed on Kofun period tombs, depicting houses, animals, and people.
*   **Wabi-sabi:** A core aesthetic centered on the acceptance of transience and imperfection, finding beauty in the rustic, modest, and weathered.
*   **Shibui:** An aesthetic of simple, subtle, and unobtrusive beauty.
*   **Mono-ha:** A 1960s-70s art movement that explored the relationships between natural and industrial materials in their unaltered states.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The exact date and nature of Buddhism's introduction to Japan (552 or 538 CE) is debated among scholars.
*   The role of Korea in transmitting Buddhism and continental culture to Japan is a subject of historical discussion, with some scholars viewing it as an active agent rather than a mere transmitter.
*   The interpretation of Jōmon dogū figurines—whether they are fertility objects, shamanic tools, or representations of deities—remains uncertain.
