# Ghalib

## Overview

Mirza Ghalib (1797–1869) was a Mughal-era poet and letter writer who composed in both Persian and Urdu during the twilight of imperial rule and the rise of British colonial dominance in India. Renowned for his ghazals—lyric poems exploring love, philosophy, and existential anguish—Ghalib expanded the form beyond romantic lamentation into meditations on the human condition. Despite his literary stature, he lived in near-constant poverty, relying on royal patronage and the charity of friends. His legacy endures across South Asia and among diasporic communities, celebrated not only for his poetry but also for his innovative, conversational prose style in letter-writing.

## Body

### Early Life and Family Background

Born into a family of declining Mughal nobility, Ghalib’s lineage traced back to Seljuk Turks who had migrated from Samarkand during the reign of Ahmad Shah (1748–54). His paternal grandfather, Mirza Qoqan Baig, was a Seljuq Turk and descendant of Sultan Berkyaruq. His father, Mirza Abdullah Baig, served the Nawab of Lucknow and the Nizam of Hyderabad before dying in a battle in 1803 in Alwar when Ghalib was a little over 5 years old. Raised by his uncle, Ghalib lost him too in 1806. These early losses shaped his worldview, fostering themes of isolation and existential struggle that permeate his work. At 13, he married Umrao Begum, daughter of Nawab Ilahi Bakhsh, a union he later described as a second imprisonment. None of his seven children survived infancy, deepening his sense of personal tragedy.

### Literary Career and Language

Ghalib began composing poetry at 11, writing primarily in Urdu despite valuing Persian more highly. During his time, “Hindi” and “Urdu” were synonymous, and he often referred to his language as “Hindi,” titling one work *Ode-e-Hindi*. A pivotal influence came at 14 when Abdus Samad, a Persian convert, stayed with his family for two years, teaching him Persian, Arabic, philosophy, and logic. His original pen name was Asad, drawn from his given name Asadullah Khan; at some point early in his poetic career he adopted the pen-name of Ghalib. Though his Persian Divan is at least five times longer than his Urdu Divan, his fame rests on his Urdu ghazals. Before Ghalib, the ghazal was largely an expression of anguished love; he transformed it into a vehicle for philosophical inquiry and social commentary.

### Court Patronage and Titles

In 1850, Emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar granted Ghalib the titles *Dabir-ul-Mulk* (“Secretary of State”) and *Najm-ud-Daula* (“Star of the State”), symbolically integrating him into Delhi’s nobility. By 1854, he became the emperor’s poetic tutor, tutor to Prince Fakhr-ud Din Mirza, and royal historian of the Mughal Court. However, these honors did not translate into financial stability. Ghalib relied on modest salaries, government pensions, and credit, never achieving the security he sought. After the decline of the Mughal Empire and the rise of the British Raj, despite his many attempts, Ghalib could never get the full pension restored.

### Letters and Prose Innovation

Ghalib was a masterful letter writer whose informal tone revolutionized Urdu prose. Unlike the ornate epistolary style of earlier writers, he wrote as if speaking directly to the reader, using everyday language and humor. His letters chronicled the collapse of Mughal Delhi—describing vanished bazaars, razed havelis, and a city reduced to a military camp. Scholars argue that his letters alone could secure his place in Urdu literature. Ralph Russell translated them into English in *The Oxford Ghalib*.

### Religious Views and Skepticism

While Ghalib identified with Shia Islam and composed numerous naats (praise poems) for Prophet Muhammad, he was critical of orthodox clergy. He emphasized spiritual seeking over ritualism, mocking the *ulema* for their narrow focus on legal minutiae. In his Persian masnavi *Abr-i-Guhar Baar*, he questioned the appeal of paradise without earthly pleasures like friendship, intoxication, and longing. His skepticism extended to religious authority, as seen in his rebuke of clerics who “cannot hear the music of his secrets.”

### The 1857 Revolt and Colonial Encounter

During the Sepoy Mutiny, Ghalib was arrested and interrogated by British forces. When asked if he was Muslim, he replied sardonically, “Half?”—a response reflecting both his syncretic beliefs and defiance. The British suspended his pension, prompting another trip to Calcutta to appeal to the Governor-General. Though unsuccessful, the journey enriched his literary perspective, exposing him to Calcutta’s liberal intellectual circles. There, he wrote Persian masnavis like *Chiragh-e Dair* and *Bad-e-Mukhalif*, and expressed deep affection for the city in letters and poetry.

### Legacy and Cultural Impact

Ghalib died in 1869, buried near Nizamuddin Auliya. His home in Chandni Chowk is now a museum. His ghazals have been immortalized by classical and contemporary musicians, including Jagjit Singh, Mehdi Hassan, and Abida Parveen. His life has inspired numerous films, plays, and TV serials, notably the 1988 Doordarshan series starring Naseeruddin Shah, whose soundtrack became a cultural milestone. Stage adaptations, such as *Ghalib in New Delhi*, continue to draw audiences. In 2017, Google honored him with a doodle on his 220th birthday.

## Terms

- **Ghazal**: A poetic form consisting of independent couplets united by a refrain and radif (repeated word or phrase), traditionally expressing themes of love and separation.
- **Takhallus**: A poet’s pen name, adopted as part of their identity in literary works.
- **Na’at**: Poetic praise of the Prophet Muhammad, common in Islamic literary traditions.
- **Masnavi**: A narrative poem written in rhyming couplets, often didactic or mystical in content.
- **Qasida**: An ode or elegy in classical Arabic and Persian poetry, typically formal and panegyric in nature.
- **Mushaira**: A poetic symposium where poets recite their works aloud, fostering competitive and collaborative exchange.
- **Shayari**: The art of poetic composition, especially in Urdu and Persian, encompassing themes of emotion, philosophy, and society.
- **Divan**: A collected volume of a poet’s works, particularly ghazals and qasidas.
- **Sharh**: A detailed commentary or exegesis of a classical text, often used to explain complex poetic meanings.
- **Ustad**: A title denoting mastery or seniority, often applied to teachers or court poets.

## Debates and Open Questions

Scholars debate whether Ghalib’s Persian or Urdu works better represent his genius. While he personally favored Persian and claimed superiority for his Persian compositions, his enduring popularity stems from his Urdu ghazals. Critics also question the extent to which his religious skepticism was genuine versus literary device. Some interpret his critiques of orthodoxy as playful irreverence, while others see them as sincere challenges to dogma. Additionally, the authenticity and interpretation of his letters remain contested, with debates over editorial choices in published editions and translations. Finally, the historiography of his relationship with Sir Syed Ahmad Khan—particularly Ghalib’s harsh critique of the *Ai’n-e-Akbari*—continues to generate discussion about their ideological divergence and mutual influence.
