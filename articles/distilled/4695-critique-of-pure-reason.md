# Critique of Pure Reason

## Overview
Immanuel Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) is a foundational work of modern philosophy that seeks to determine the limits and scope of metaphysics. It argues that human knowledge is structured by the mind's innate faculties, which makes certain types of knowledge possible while rendering traditional metaphysical questions about God, the soul, and the world as a whole unknowable through pure reason alone. The work revolutionized philosophy by shifting the focus from objects to the conditions of human cognition.

## Timeline
- **1781** — First edition of *Critique of Pure Reason* published.
- **1782** — First review (by Garve/Feder) published, sparking controversy.
- **1783** — Kant publishes *Prolegomena to Any Future Metaphysics* to clarify his views.
- **1787** — Second, revised edition of the *Critique* published.
- **1788** — Publication of Kant's *Critique of Practical Reason*.
- **1790** — Publication of Kant's *Critique of Judgment*.

## Body

### Central Problem and Background
Kant's project was prompted by the skepticism of David Hume, who argued that concepts like cause and effect could not be derived from experience. Kant sought to answer Hume by asking: "How are synthetic a priori judgments possible?" He defines **a priori** knowledge as necessary and universal, independent of experience, and **synthetic** judgments as those where the predicate adds new information not contained in the subject concept (e.g., "All bodies are heavy"). Kant contends that mathematics, natural science, and metaphysics all rely on such synthetic a priori knowledge, which requires a new philosophical foundation.

### Transcendental Idealism and the Forms of Intuition
Kant proposes a "Copernican revolution" in philosophy: instead of assuming our knowledge conforms to objects, we must suppose objects conform to our knowledge. He argues that space and time are not properties of things-in-themselves but are **a priori forms of intuition**—the subjective conditions through which we perceive all objects. This is the core of his **transcendental idealism**: we can only know objects as they appear to us (phenomena), not as they are in themselves (noumena). This framework makes mathematical knowledge possible, as geometry and arithmetic are grounded in the pure intuitions of space and time.

### The Understanding and the Categories
To have knowledge, intuitions must be thought through concepts. Kant identifies twelve pure concepts of the understanding, or **categories** (e.g., causality, substance), which are derived from the logical forms of judgment. In the "Transcendental Deduction," he argues these categories are necessary conditions for any possible experience, as they are the rules by which the mind synthesizes sensory data into a coherent world of objects. The "Schematism" explains how these abstract categories are applied to sensory experience through time-determined rules.

### The Limits of Reason and Dialectical Illusion
In the "Transcendental Dialectic," Kant critiques the misuse of reason when it tries to apply categories beyond possible experience. He exposes the **paralogisms** (fallacious inferences about the soul), the **antinomies** (contradictory but equally valid arguments about the world's finitude or freedom), and the **ideal of pure reason** (the idea of God). He refutes traditional proofs for God's existence (ontological, cosmological, physico-theological), arguing they are invalid attempts to gain knowledge of the supersensible. Reason's proper use is limited to the empirical realm; its ideas of God, freedom, and immortality have only a practical, moral significance.

### Structure and Method
The *Critique* is divided into the "Doctrine of Elements" (Transcendental Aesthetic and Logic) and the "Doctrine of Method." The latter outlines the proper discipline of reason, distinguishing it from mathematical proof and establishing that philosophy cannot achieve dogmatic certainty about metaphysical topics. Kant concludes that while speculative reason cannot prove God's existence, practical reason postulates it as a necessary condition for morality.

## Terms
- **A priori / A posteriori**: Knowledge independent of experience (a priori) versus knowledge derived from experience (a posteriori).
- **Analytic / Synthetic**: Judgments where the predicate is contained in the subject concept (analytic) versus those where it adds new information (synthetic).
- **Transcendental idealism**: The doctrine that we know objects only as they appear to us (phenomena), structured by our mind's forms of intuition and categories, not as they are in themselves (noumena).
- **Intuition (Anschauung)**: The immediate representation of an object; for humans, all intuition is sensible and structured by the a priori forms of space and time.
- **Category**: One of the twelve pure concepts of the understanding (e.g., causality, substance) that structure all thought about objects.
- **Thing-in-itself (Ding an sich)**: The object as it exists independently of our perception and cognitive faculties; unknowable to us.
- **Phenomenon / Noumenon**: The object as it appears to us (phenomenon) versus the object as it might be in itself (noumenon).
- **Transcendental deduction**: Kant's argument that the categories are necessary conditions for the possibility of experience.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise nature and validity of Kant's arguments for the a priori status of space and time remain heavily debated.
- The interpretation of the "Transcendental Deduction"—how exactly the categories are shown to apply to experience—is a central and contested issue in Kant scholarship.
- The relationship between the unknowable thing-in-itself and the world of appearance, and whether this leads to an incoherent dualism, is a persistent criticism.
- The role and coherence of the "Refutation of Idealism" added in the second edition, intended to distinguish Kant's view from subjective idealism, is debated.
- The extent to which Kant's system successfully avoids the skepticism he sought to overcome is an ongoing philosophical question.
