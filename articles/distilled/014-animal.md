# Animal

## Overview

Animals are multicellular, eukaryotic organisms in the biological kingdom Animalia. They consume organic material, breathe oxygen, have muscle cells, reproduce sexually, and grow from a hollow sphere of cells called a blastula during embryonic development. With few exceptions, animals can move, and they form a clade—meaning they arose from a single common ancestor. Over 1.5 million living animal species have been described, with estimates of up to 7.77 million total species on Earth. The scientific study of animals is zoology; the study of animal behaviour is ethology.

## Timeline

- **~1.7–1.2 billion years ago** — Sediment-filled tubes resembling worm burrows appear in rocks; animal origin disputed
- **~650 Mya** — 24-Isopropylcholestane (24-ipc), likely from sponges, appears in rocks; common ancestor of all living animals estimated
- **Late Cryogenian** — Animals first appear in the fossil record
- **~665 Mya** — Possible early sponge fossils in the Trezona Formation, South Australia
- **557–562 Mya** — *Auroralumina attenboroughii*, earliest known Ediacaran crown-group cnidarian
- **Ediacaran period** — First body fossils of animals appear (*Charnia*, *Spriggina*); cholesterol in *Dickinsonia* confirms animal nature
- **~539 Mya** — Cambrian explosion begins; nearly all modern animal phyla appear as marine species
- **485.4 Mya** — Ordovician radiation; most animal classes appear
- **510–471 Mya** — Arthropod lineages colonize land alongside land plants
- **~375 Mya** — Lobe-finned fish *Tiktaalik* moves onto land in the late Devonian

## Body

### Etymology and Definition

The word *animal* comes from the Latin noun *animal*, derived from *animalis* meaning "having breath or soul." The biological definition includes all members of the kingdom Animalia. In colloquial usage, "animal" often refers only to nonhuman animals. *Metazoa* derives from Greek *meta* ("after") and *zōia* ("animals"); a metazoan is any member of the group Metazoa.

### Characteristics

Animals share several features with other living things: they are eukaryotic, multicellular, and aerobic, as are plants and fungi. Unlike plants and algae, animals cannot produce their own food—a feature shared with fungi. Animals ingest organic material and digest it internally.

Structurally, animals are set apart by cells surrounded by an extracellular matrix composed of collagen and elastic glycoproteins, motility during at least part of their life cycle, and a blastula stage during embryonic development. Typically, there is an internal digestive chamber with either one opening (in Ctenophora, Cnidaria, and flatworms) or two openings (in most bilaterians).

Development in Bilateria and Cnidaria is controlled by Hox genes, which signal when and where to develop structures such as body segments and limbs. The extracellular matrix forms a flexible framework allowing cells to move and reorganize into specialized tissues and organs; it may be calcified into shells, bones, and spicules. In contrast, cells of other multicellular organisms are held in place by cell walls and develop by progressive growth.

Nearly all animals use sexual reproduction. They produce haploid gametes by meiosis; smaller motile gametes are spermatozoa, larger non-motile ones are ova. These fuse into zygotes, which develop via mitosis into a blastula. In sponges, blastula larvae swim to new locations; in most other groups, the blastula invaginates to form a gastrula with two germ layers (ectoderm and endoderm), and usually a third (mesoderm). Inbreeding depression from mating with close relatives has driven evolution of mechanisms to avoid close inbreeding. Some animals reproduce asexually via fragmentation, budding (as in Hydra), or parthenogenesis (fertile eggs without mating, as in aphids).

### Ecology

Animals are categorized by trophic levels: carnivores (including piscivores, insectivores, ovivores), herbivores (folivores, frugivores, granivores, nectarivores, etc.), omnivores, fungivores, scavengers/detritivores, and parasites. Interactions form complex food webs. Predation drives an evolutionary arms race between predator and prey, leading to antagonistic coevolution. Almost all multicellular predators are animals. Some consumers use multiple methods—parasitoid wasp larvae feed on living host tissues, while adults consume nectar.

Most animals rely on biomass produced by plants and phytoplankton through photosynthesis. Herbivores eat plants directly; carnivores acquire nutrients indirectly. Animals oxidize carbohydrates, lipids, proteins in cellular respiration. Of 550 gigatons of carbon biomass on Earth, animals account for 2 gigatons. Some benthic animals near hydrothermal vents consume organic matter produced by chemosynthesis. Animals occupy virtually all habitats, but few survive above 50 °C or in the most extreme cold deserts of continental Antarctica.

### Diversity

The blue whale is the largest animal ever, weighing up to 190 tonnes and measuring 33.6 metres. The largest extant terrestrial animal is the African bush elephant (up to 12.25 tonnes). Titanosaur sauropods like *Argentinosaurus* may have weighed 73 tonnes. Some Myxozoa never grow larger than 20 μm; *Myxobolus szekeli* is no more than 8.5 μm when fully grown. Estimates of total species vary wildly—nematode estimates range from 10,000–20,000 to 100 million described or predicted species.

### Evolutionary Origin

Evidence of animals dates to the Cryogenian. 24-ipc in rocks from ~650 Mya likely originates from sponges based on molecular clock estimates. The first body fossils appear in the Ediacaran; the discovery of animal lipid cholesterol in *Dickinsonia* fossils confirms their animal nature. Animals likely originated under low-oxygen conditions capable of anaerobic respiration, later becoming fully dependent on oxygen. Many phyla first appear in the Cambrian explosion (~539 Mya) in beds like the Burgess Shale. The apparent suddenness may be a fossil record artefact, supported by *Auroralumina attenboroughii* (557–562 Mya). Some palaeontologists suggest animals appeared as early as 1 billion years ago; Tonian trace fossils may indicate triploblastic worm-like animals, but similar tracks are produced by the protist *Gromia sphaerica*.

### Phylogeny

Animals are monophyletic and sister to choanoflagellates (forming Choanozoa). The animal clade originated by 650 Mya, possibly as much as 800 Mya. The base of the animal tree is debated: Porifera (sponges) were long assumed sister to all others, but Ctenophora may hold that position. Sponges lack true tissues and nerve cells; comb jellies have distinct tissues but no organs, with nerves that developed independently. Cnidaria are radially symmetric, diploblastic (two germ layers), with a single-opening digestive chamber. Placozoans have no symmetry and no permanent digestive chamber. Bilateria—some 29 phyla and over a million species—are triploblastic with bilateral symmetry, distinct organs, and a two-opening digestive chamber. Most belong to protostomes or deuterostomes, distinguished by embryonic development patterns (spiral vs. radial cleavage; mouth vs. anus forming first). Xenacoelomorpha's position is contested.

### History of Classification

Aristotle divided animals into those with blood and those without. Carl Linnaeus created the first hierarchical classification in 1758 (*Systema Naturae*). Jean-Baptiste Lamarck expanded this into phyla by 1809. Georges Cuvier (1817) used comparative anatomy to group animals into four *embranchements*. In 1874, Ernst Haeckel divided the kingdom into Metazoa (multicellular) and Protozoa (single-celled, later moved to Protista). Modern classification relies on molecular phylogenetics.

### In Human Culture

Humans exploit many animal species for food (meat, eggs, dairy), materials (leather, fur, wool), as pets, and as working animals. Dogs were the first domesticated animal. Animals appear in cave art, mythology, religion, literature, heraldry, politics, and sports. The Western and Chinese zodiacs are based on animals. Animals serve as experimental models (e.g., *Drosophila melanogaster*), have been used to create vaccines since the 18th century, and provide medicines such as trabectedin.

## Terms

- **Blastula**: Hollow sphere of cells formed during embryonic development
- **Clade**: Group arising from a single common ancestor
- **Eukaryotic**: Cells with a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles
- **Gametes**: Haploid reproductive cells (spermatozoa and ova)
- **Germ layers**: Ectoderm, endoderm, and mesoderm—tissue layers that differentiate into organs
- **Hox genes**: Genes controlling development of body segments and limbs
- **Molecular phylogenetics**: Using genetic data to demonstrate evolutionary relationships
- **Parthenogenesis**: Asexual reproduction where fertile eggs develop without mating
- **Trophic level**: Position in the food chain based on feeding behaviour

## Debates and Open Questions

- Whether Porifera or Ctenophora is sister to all other animals remains contested; molecular phylogenetics supports both hypotheses
- The position of Xenacoelomorpha within Bilateria is uncertain—whether they are the basalmost bilaterians or more closely related to Ambulacraria
- Whether Tonian trace fossils (1 gya) indicate early animal evolution or were produced by protists like *Gromia sphaerica*
- Whether the Cambrian explosion's apparent suddenness is real or an artefact of the fossil record
- Whether sediment-filled tubes in 1.2–1.7 gya rocks have animal origins or are water-escape structures
- The collective geomorphic influence of animals on Earth's surface remains largely understudied
