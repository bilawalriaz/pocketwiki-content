# Marco Polo

## Overview
Marco Polo (c. 1254 – 8 January 1324) was a Venetian merchant and explorer whose 24-year journey along the Silk Road (1271–1295) produced *The Travels of Marco Polo*, the first comprehensive European account of the Mongol Empire and Yuan China. His detailed observations on geography, administration, paper money, and technology provided a factual baseline that contrasted with earlier "barbarian" narratives, directly inspiring later explorers such as Christopher Columbus and influencing European cartography, including the Fra Mauro map. While debates persist regarding his exact official rank and specific omissions (notably the Great Wall), modern scholarship validates his presence in China through precise economic and administrative details absent from other contemporary sources.

## Timeline
- **c. 1254** — Born in Venice; father Niccolò and uncle Maffeo absent on trading voyage.
- **1260–1269** — Niccolò and Maffeo travel to Constantinople, liquidate assets, meet Kublai Khan in Dadu (Beijing), and return to Venice bearing a papal request for 100 Christian scholars.
- **1271** — Marco (age 17), Niccolò, and Maffeo depart Venice for Asia via Acre and Hormuz, carrying papal letters and holy oil for Kublai Khan.
- **c. 1275** — Arrive at Kublai Khan’s summer palace in Shangdu; Marco enters Khan’s service as a foreign emissary.
- **c. 1275–1291** — Marco serves 17 years on diplomatic missions across China, Southeast Asia (Myanmar, Vietnam, Indonesia, Sri Lanka), and India.
- **1291–1293** — Polos escort Mongol princess Kököchin to Persia by sea (14 junks); only 18 of 600 passengers survive. Arghun Khan dies; Kököchin marries his son Ghazan.
- **1295** — Polos return to Venice after 24 years and ~15,000 miles, wealth converted to gemstones.
- **1296** — Marco captured by Genoese in a skirmish off the Anatolian coast; imprisoned in Genoa.
- **1298–1299** — Dictates travels to cellmate Rustichello da Pisa, producing *Devisement du Monde* (Franco-Venetian).
- **1299** — Released; returns to Venice, becomes wealthy merchant, marries Donata Badoèr (1300), fathers three daughters.
- **8–9 Jan 1324** — Dies in Venice; buried in the church of San Lorenzo.

## Body

### Family Origin and Early Life
Marco Polo was born c. 1254 in Venice to Niccolò Polo, a wealthy merchant trading in the Near East. Niccolò and his brother Maffeo departed on a voyage before Marco’s birth; in 1260, anticipating political upheaval in Constantinople (capital of the Latin Empire), they liquidated assets into jewels and travelled east, eventually meeting Kublai Khan, founder of the Yuan dynasty. Marco’s mother died during their absence; he was raised by an aunt and uncle, receiving a mercantile education in foreign currency, appraisal, and cargo handling, but little Latin. In 1269, the brothers returned to Venice, meeting 15-year-old Marco for the first time and learning that Kublai Khan had requested 100 Christian scholars and oil from the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem.

### Journey to the Mongol Court (1271–1275)
In 1271, during the sede vacante following Pope Clement IV’s death, the Polos sailed to Acre. There, Archdeacon Tedaldo Visconti—soon elected Pope Gregory X—entrusted them with papal letters for Kublai Khan and two Dominican friars as legates. Unable to find seaworthy ships at Hormuz, they traversed the Silk Road overland. En route, they survived a bandit ambush during a sandstorm. After three and a half years, they reached Shangdu (c. 1275), where Kublai Khan, impressed by Marco’s intelligence and linguistic ability (he knew four languages), appointed him a foreign emissary.

### Service Under Kublai Khan (1275–1291)
Marco served 17 years as an emissary, undertaking diplomatic missions throughout the empire: to southern and eastern Chinese provinces, Myanmar, Vietnam, Indonesia, Sri Lanka, and India. He entertained the Khan with reports on distant lands. The Polos became indispensable; Kublai repeatedly refused their requests to leave, fearing for their safety if he died and enemies targeted his foreign favorites. Marco’s account details imperial administration, salt monopolies, paper currency (noting regional variations like cowry shells in Yunnan), and nautical technology such as watertight bulkheads—details later corroborated by archaeology and Chinese records.

### Return via the Princess Kököchin Mission (1291–1295)
In 1291, Kublai agreed to let the Polos return, tasking them with escorting the Mongol princess Kököchin to marry Arghun Khan of Persia. They departed Zaitun (Quanzhou) on a fleet of 14 junks. The two-year voyage was catastrophic: of 600 passengers, only 18 survived, including all three Polos. Arriving in Persia c. 1293, they found Arghun dead; Kököchin married his son Ghazan. The Polos then travelled overland via Trebizond (Trabzon) and Constantinople, reaching Venice in 1295 with fortunes in gemstones.

### Captivity, Dictation, and *The Travels*
Venice was at war with Genoa. Marco armed a galley but was captured in a 1296 skirmish off the Anatolian coast (not the 1298 Battle of Curzola). Imprisoned in Genoa, he dictated his memoirs to Rustichello da Pisa, a professional romance writer. Rustichello composed *Devisement du Monde* in Franco-Venetian (1298–1299), likely structuring it as a merchant’s handbook on weights, measures, and distances. The text blends Polo’s observations with Rustichello’s literary flourishes—e.g., the Arthurian-style court introduction—and omits Polo’s astronomical observations (a comet in 1293 recorded by Pietro d’Abano). No authoritative manuscript exists; ~150 copies in various languages diverge significantly due to copying errors and translation layers. The Dominican Order endorsed the work: Francesco Pipino translated it into Latin in 1302, affirming Polo’s veracity, partly to support potential Christian-Mongol alliance talks.

### Later Life and Death
Released in August 1299, Marco returned to Venice. His family purchased a palazzo in *contrada San Giovanni Crisostomo* (Corte del Milion) using eastern profits. He became a wealthy merchant, financing expeditions but never leaving Venetian territories. His father Niccolò died before 1300. In 1300, he married Donata Badoèr; they had three daughters (Fantina, Bellela, Moreta). A 2022 discovery revealed an earlier daughter, Agnese (b. 1295/99 – d. 1319). Marco appeared in tax records (1305), inherited his father’s estates (1319), and purchased his wife’s family property (1321). Bedridden in 1323, he dictated a will on 8–9 January 1324 (Venetian law complicates the exact date), freeing a Tartar servant Peter, bequeathing assets to family, religious orders, guilds, and forgiving debts. He was buried at San Lorenzo.

### The Text: Manuscripts, Translation, and Rustichello’s Role
*The Travels* survives in ~150 manuscripts, none authoritative. The oldest ("F") is Old French with Italian influence; Luigi Foscolo Benedetto considered it the base text. Key variants include Ramusio’s 1559 Italian edition ("R"), a 15th-century Latin Toledo manuscript ("Z"), and Francesco Pipino’s 1302 Latin translation, which spread the work across Europe. The first English edition (John Frampton, 1579) derived from a Castilian translation. Modern editions vary: Moule and Pelliot (1938) used a Latin manuscript 50% longer than others; Latham (1958) blended texts for readability; Kinoshita (2016) prioritized the "F" manuscript as a product of Eurasian literary culture. Rustichello’s hand is evident in the "leisurely, conversational style," verbatim lifts from his Arthurian romances, and the insertion of "marvellous" elements expected by medieval readers.

### Authenticity Debates and Scholarly Consensus
**Omissions:** Critics (notably Frances Wood, 1995) cite Polo’s silence on the Great Wall, tea, chopsticks, Chinese characters, and footbinding as evidence he never reached China. Defenders (Stephen G. Haw, Igor de Rachewiltz, Morris Rossabi) counter: the Great Wall familiar today is Ming (post-1368); Yuan rulers controlled both sides and had no need for it; contemporaries like Ibn Battuta found no trace of it; footbinding was rare among Mongols and not yet widespread among Chinese; tea and chopsticks were omitted by other travellers. Polo’s description of women’s "dainty walk" may reference footbinding.

**Exaggerations:** Polo’s claim to have governed Yangzhou for three years (*seignorie*) is contested. No Chinese source names him; David Morgan suspects exaggeration (possibly by Rustichello). Peng Hai (2010s) identifies Polo with "Boluo," a courtier arrested in 1274 for a traffic violation, released by the Emperor, and transferred to Ningxia—dates aligning with Polo’s first mission. Paul Pelliot suggested Polo managed the Yangzhou salt monopoly, a significant post. Haw argues Polo never claimed high rank (e.g., *darughachi*), only the esteemed role of emissary (*keshig* guard member). The claim that the Polos designed mangonels for the 1273 Siege of Xiangyang is anachronistic (they arrived later) and likely a later insertion; Chinese sources credit engineers from Baghdad.

**Errors:** Polo cites 24 arches on the Marco Polo Bridge (actual: 11 or 13), 12 gates for Khanbaliq (actual: 11), five-masted ships for the Japan invasions (archaeology shows three masts), and conflates the 1274 and 1281 invasions.

**Appropriation:** Wood argues Polo retold the Kököchin voyage from Persian sources. De Rachewiltz refutes this: Polo’s account reconciles Chinese and Persian sources by explaining why only one of three envoys (Coja/Khoja) survived to be recorded by Rashid-al-Din; the Persian source postdates Polo (1310–11), so he could not have copied it.

**Modern Validation:** Hans Ulrich Vogel (2012) demonstrated Polo’s unique, accurate detail on Chinese paper money (shapes, seals, denominations, regional variations), salt production, and revenues—data absent from Arab/Persian sources and confirmed by Yuan documents and archaeology. Mark Elvin concludes the "case as a whole had now been closed": the work is essentially authentic, a serious though not infallible witness.

## Terms
- ****Silk Road**** — Network of overland trade routes connecting Europe, the Middle East, Central Asia, and China, used by the Polos for their outbound and return journeys.
- ****Yuan dynasty**** — Mongol-ruled imperial dynasty of China (1271–1368) founded by Kublai Khan; the polity Marco Polo served.
- ****Kublai Khan**** — Grand Khan of the Mongol Empire (r. 1260–1294) and founder of the Yuan dynasty; Polo’s patron for 17 years.
- ****Cathay**** — Medieval European name for northern China, derived from the Khitan people; used throughout Polo’s narrative.
- ****Franco-Venetian**** — A literary *koiné* blending Old French and Venetian Italian, used by Rustichello da Pisa to write the original *Devisement du Monde*.
- ****Rustichello da Pisa**** — Professional romance writer and Polo’s cellmate in Genoa; co-author who structured and stylized the oral dictation.
- ****Kököchin**** — Mongol princess escorted by the Polos from China to Persia (1291–1293) to marry Arghun Khan; married his son Ghazan after Arghun’s death.
- ****Paper money (Chao)**** — Fiat currency issued by the Yuan dynasty; Polo described its manufacture, denominations, seals, and regional usage (e.g., cowry shells in Yunnan) with unique accuracy.
- ****Darughachi**** — Mongol term for a high-ranking governor or commissioner leading a *tumen* (10,000 troops); Polo never claimed this rank.
- ****Keshig**** — The Mongol imperial guard, numbering up to 14,000; Haw suggests Polo may have held a position within this corps as an emissary.

## Debates and open questions
- **Did Polo govern Yangzhou?** The text claims he "ruled" (*seignorie*) the city for three years; Ramusio’s version says he was a temporary substitute. No Chinese record confirms a European governor; the "Boluo" identification (Peng Hai) remains debated.
- **Source of "marvellous" content:** To what extent do legends, the Arthurian-style introduction, and exotic marvels reflect Rustichello’s literary conventions versus Polo’s oral reporting?
- **Manuscript stemma:** With ~150 divergent manuscripts and no autograph, reconstructing the "original" text remains an open problem of textual criticism; key passages (e.g., the Xiangyang mangonels) appear only in some branches.
- **Extent of Polo’s itinerary:** While his presence in Yuan territory is widely accepted, the precise scope of his travel in southern China versus reliance on secondhand reports for peripheral regions (e.g., Japan, Java) is unresolved.
- **Astronomical omission:** Polo described a comet (1293) to Pietro d’Abano, recorded in the *Conciliator*, but this observation is absent from *The Travels*—raising questions about editorial selection or Rustichello’s filtering.
