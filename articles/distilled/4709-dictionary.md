# Dictionary

## Overview
A dictionary is a lexicographical reference work that lists words or lexemes from a language's lexicon, typically with definitions, pronunciations, and other information. It is a fundamental tool for understanding language, with a history spanning over four millennia, evolving from ancient bilingual wordlists to modern digital and online resources. The study of dictionaries, known as lexicography, examines their creation, structure, and use.

## Timeline
- **c. 2300 BCE** — Oldest known dictionaries: bilingual Sumerian–Akkadian cuneiform tablets from Ebla.
- **c. 3rd century BCE** — Earliest surviving monolingual dictionary: Chinese *Erya*.
- **1604** — First purely English alphabetical dictionary: *A Table Alphabeticall* by Robert Cawdrey.
- **1755** — Samuel Johnson's *A Dictionary of the English Language* becomes the English-language standard for over 150 years.
- **1828** — Noah Webster publishes *An American Dictionary of the English Language*, establishing American English spelling norms.
- **1884** — Oxford University Press begins publishing the *Oxford English Dictionary* (OED) in fascicles.
- **1928** — First complete ten-volume edition of the OED is released.
- **1961** — Publication of *Webster's Third New International Dictionary* sparks controversy over its descriptive approach.
- **1969** — *The American Heritage Dictionary of the English Language* is published, the first to use corpus linguistics.

## Body

### Definition and Types
A dictionary is a listing of words, often arranged alphabetically, providing information such as definitions, etymologies, and pronunciations. A broad distinction exists between **general dictionaries**, which aim to cover a language's lexicon comprehensively, and **specialized dictionaries**, which focus on terms within a specific field (e.g., law, medicine). In theory, general dictionaries are **semasiological** (mapping word to definition), while specialized ones are **onomasiological** (mapping concept to term), though both approaches are used in practice. Other types include bilingual (translation) dictionaries, thesauri (synonym dictionaries), and rhyming dictionaries.

### Prescriptive vs. Descriptive Approaches
Dictionaries can follow a **prescriptive** philosophy, reflecting what is considered "correct" language use, or a **descriptive** philosophy, recording actual usage. Noah Webster's 1828 dictionary was prescriptive, altering spellings to forge a distinct American English identity. Modern large-scale dictionaries like the OED and *Webster's Third* are primarily descriptive, though they may include usage notes on terms considered vulgar or nonstandard. The choice of approach can influence language use, as dictionaries are often accepted as authorities.

### Historical Development
The oldest dictionaries were bilingual wordlists from ancient Mesopotamia (c. 2300 BCE). Monolingual dictionaries emerged later, with the Chinese *Erya* (c. 3rd century BCE). In medieval Europe, glossaries and alphabetical lexicons like the *Catholicon* (1287) were common. The modern era of English lexicography began with Robert Cawdrey's *A Table Alphabeticall* (1604), but Samuel Johnson's *Dictionary* (1755) was the first to combine comprehensive definitions with textual references and alphabetical order, setting a standard for 150 years. The *Oxford English Dictionary*, begun in 1884, introduced historical principles, tracing word development over time.

### English and American Dictionaries
Early English dictionaries were glossaries of foreign words. Johnson's *Dictionary* (1755) was a landmark for reliability. In America, Noah Webster's dictionaries (starting 1806) deliberately introduced spelling reforms (e.g., "color" for "colour") and included American terms. The *Oxford English Dictionary* (OED), with its first complete edition in 1928, remains the most comprehensive historical dictionary of English. The 1961 *Webster's Third* controversy over its descriptive stance led to the creation of *The American Heritage Dictionary* (1969), which incorporated corpus linguistics and usage notes.

### Modern Forms and Specialized Uses
Dictionaries have evolved from books to electronic and online formats. Specialized dictionaries are categorized as multi-field (e.g., business), single-field (e.g., law), or sub-field (e.g., constitutional law). **Defining dictionaries** provide a core glossary of simple meanings for learners. **Historical dictionaries** describe word development over time using citations. Dictionaries for **natural language processing (NLP)** are designed for computer use, often featuring complex, multilingual networks of data rather than linear entries.

## Terms
- **Lexicography**: The systematic study and practice of compiling dictionaries.
- **Lexeme**: The fundamental unit of the lexicon, such as a word or phrase, considered as a whole.
- **Semasiological**: A dictionary approach that starts with a word and lists its meanings.
- **Onomasiological**: A dictionary approach that starts with a concept and lists the words used to express it.
- **Prescriptive**: A dictionary philosophy that prescribes or recommends "correct" language usage.
- **Descriptive**: A dictionary philosophy that describes actual language usage as found in records.
- **Corpus linguistics**: The analysis of language as it appears in large collections (corpora) of real-world text.
- **Natural Language Processing (NLP)**: A field of computer science focused on enabling computers to understand and process human language.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The debate between **prescriptive** and **descriptive** lexicography continues, with questions about a dictionary's role in guiding versus recording language use.
- There is no consensus on whether **lexicology** (the study of words) and **terminology** (the study of terms in specialized fields) are distinct disciplines.
- The influence of dictionaries on language is contested; while they can affect usage through their authority, ultimately, language is shaped by its speakers. As noted, dictionaries are "artificial repositories, put together well after the languages they define."
