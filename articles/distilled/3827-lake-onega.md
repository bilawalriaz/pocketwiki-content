# Lake Onega

## Overview
Lake Onega is the second-largest lake in Europe, located in northwestern Russia. It is a significant freshwater body formed by glacial activity, notable for its cultural heritage sites like the Kizhi Pogost and Onega petroglyphs, both UNESCO World Heritage Sites. The lake serves as a crucial hub for regional transport, industry, and ecology.

## Timeline
- **c. 12,000 years ago** — Lake formed by glacial-tectonic activity at the end of the last Ice Age.
- **4th–2nd millennia BC** — Onega petroglyphs (rock engravings) created on the eastern shore.
- **1350** — Dormition Monastery founded on Cape Muromsky.
- **1495** — First known mention of Kondopoga.
- **1703** — Petrozavodsk founded by Peter I.
- **1774** — Uspenskaya (Assumption) Church built in Kondopoga.
- **1818–1852** — Onega Canal constructed along the southern shore.
- **1927–1938** — Nizhnesvirskaya hydroelectric plant built.
- **1938–1952** — Verkhnesvirskaya hydroelectric plant completed, raising lake level by 0.5 m.
- **1990** — Kizhi Pogost inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **2021** — Onega petroglyphs inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Body

### Geological Origin and Topography
Lake Onega is of glacial-tectonic origin, a remnant of a larger Ice Age water body formed about 12,000 years ago. It covers a surface area of 9,891 km², making it the second-largest lake in Europe. The lake is deep, with an average depth of 30 m and a maximum of 127 m in the northern part. Its shoreline is varied: southern banks are low and continuous, while northern banks are rocky and rugged, creating an outline resembling a giant crayfish. The water level is stabilized by hydropower plants, varying only 0.9–1.5 m annually.

### Hydrography and Islands
The lake's catchment area drains via 58 rivers, with the Svir River as its sole outlet, connecting it to Lake Ladoga and the Baltic Sea. It contains about 1,650 islands, the largest being Big Klimenetsky. The most famous is Kizhi, home to a historical complex of 89 wooden structures, including the UNESCO-listed Kizhi Pogost. The lake is part of major waterways, including the White Sea–Baltic Canal and the Volga–Baltic Waterway.

### Ecology and Economy
The lake supports diverse flora and fauna, including relict species like lamprey, and about 47 fish species. Commercial fishing is significant, targeting species like European cisco and pike-perch. The basin is a source of granite and marble, with metallurgy concentrated around Petrozavodsk. Pollution from industrial cities like Petrozavodsk, Kondopoga, and Medvezhyegorsk is a growing concern, with sewage and oil pollution accumulating in the lake. Navigation is vital, with cargo traffic of 10–12 million tonnes per year.

### Cultural Heritage
The lake's shores host major historical sites. Petrozavodsk, founded in 1703, features Neoclassical architecture. Kondopoga was known for its tall wooden church, destroyed in 2018. The Onega petroglyphs, dating to the 4th–2nd millennia BC, consist of about 1,200 rock engravings depicting animals, people, and geometric shapes. The Dormition Monastery, founded in 1350, is another key monument.

## Terms
- **Glacial-tectonic origin**: Lake formation caused by the combined effects of glacial erosion and tectonic movements.
- **Petroglyphs**: Ancient rock engravings or carvings.
- **Pogost**: A historical Russian term for a churchyard or a complex of religious buildings.
- **Catchment area**: The area of land where all water drains into a common outlet, like a lake or river.
- **Hydroelectric plant**: A facility that generates electricity using the energy of falling or flowing water.
- **Relict**: A species that has survived from an earlier geological period.
- **Salinity**: The concentration of dissolved salts in water.
