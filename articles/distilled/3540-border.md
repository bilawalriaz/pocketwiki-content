# Border

## Overview
Borders are geographical or political boundaries that separate territories, regulating the movement of people, goods, and ideas. They are fundamental to the organization of states, economies, and societies, influencing everything from national security and trade to cultural identity and conflict. Understanding borders involves examining their historical evolution, diverse classifications, and the complex political and economic dynamics they create.

## Timeline
- **c. 11th century** — China's Song dynasty creates a defensive forest as a landscape border.
- **1922** — Saudi Arabian–Iraqi and Saudi Arabian–Kuwaiti neutral zones are established.
- **1945** — The Korean Peninsula is divided, later leading to a demilitarized zone at the 38th parallel.
- **1970** — The Saudi Arabian–Kuwaiti neutral zone ceases to exist.
- **1985** — The Schengen Agreement is signed, leading to open internal borders in Europe.
- **1990** — The border between North and South Yemen becomes defunct upon unification.
- **1991** — The border between East and West Germany becomes a relic border.
- **1991** — The former British Somaliland declares independence, claiming the former colonial line as its border.
- **1990s** — A renaissance in the academic study of borders begins.
- **2000s** — The U.S. significantly increases border control measures on its northern and southern borders.

## Body

### History and Evolution
In the pre-modern world, the term "border" was vague, often referring to a general borderland rather than a precise line. Government control typically diminished with distance from the capital, making remote borderlands attractive to outlaws. Historically, many borders were not clear lines but contested "marchlands." Modern borders are now generally clearly defined and demarcated, replacing these older, ambiguous zones.

### Classification of Borders
Borders are classified by their origin and form. **Political borders** are imposed by human agency, often following physical features. **Natural borders** are geographical obstacles like oceans, rivers (often along the *thalweg*, or deepest channel), lakes, forests, and mountain ranges. Their significance has decreased with technological advances. **Landscape borders** are human-engineered features, like defensive forests, that are demarcated by treaty but not by fences. **Geometric borders** are straight lines, such as the 49th parallel forming part of the Canada–U.S. border, common in former colonial regions. **Fiat boundaries** are any human-demarcated lines not based on physical features, like electoral districts. **Relic borders** are former boundaries still visible in the landscape, like the inner German border. **Lines of control** are militarized buffer zones, like the India-Pakistan ceasefire line. **Maritime borders** define jurisdiction over ocean resources. **Airspace borders** extend vertically, generally up to the Kármán line (100 km altitude), separating national airspace from outer space.

### Regulation and Control
Borders vary from completely open to heavily fortified. **Open borders**, like those in the Schengen Area, allow free movement of people. **Regulated borders** require checks on passports, visas, and customs. Many countries fortify borders with fences and barriers to control smuggling and migration. **Demilitarized zones (DMZs)**, like the Korean DMZ, prohibit military activity and can become accidental wildlife preserves. Border control can extend inland, as in the U.S., where jurisdiction reaches 100 miles from any boundary.

### Economic and Political Impacts
Borders create economic anomalies. They can foster smuggling or legitimate border trade, and may cluster services like alcohol sales or Special Economic Zones (SEZs). States regulate cross-border trade to collect tariffs. Politically, borders require legal paperwork for crossing and may involve quarantine for animals. They can be established by agreement, imposition, or inheritance. Border conflicts arise from incompatible territorial claims, often leading to fortifications and buffer zones. Historic walls like the Great Wall of China and Hadrian's Wall served military functions, while others, like the Maginot Line, were designed to prevent access.

### Cooperation and Study
Modern initiatives like the European Union foster **cross-border regions** to address local problems like transport and environment. The academic study of borders has grown since the 1990s, countering theories of a borderless world. **Border art** is a contemporary practice exploring issues of identity, surveillance, and division, often created by artists who are themselves "border crossers."

## Terms
- **Border**: A geographical or political boundary separating territories.
- **Frontier**: An open-ended border, often identifying an expanding borderland.
- **Marchlands**: Historical intervening areas claimed and fought over by both sides of a border.
- **Thalweg**: The deepest line along a river, often used to define a precise border.
- **Fiat boundary**: A human-demarcated boundary that does not track a physical feature.
- **Relic border**: A former boundary no longer a legal border but still visible in the landscape.
- **Line of Control (LoC)**: A militarized buffer border between nations, not recognized as an official international border.
- **Demilitarized Zone (DMZ)**: A border area where military activity is prohibited by agreement.
- **Special Economic Zone (SEZ)**: An area with economic regulations different from the rest of a country, often near borders or ports.
