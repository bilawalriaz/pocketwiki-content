# Tasman Sea

## Overview
The Tasman Sea is a marginal sea of the South Pacific Ocean, situated between Australia and New Zealand. It is a significant body of water for regional climate, ocean currents, and human history, serving as both a barrier and a connector between the two nations.

## Timeline
- **c. 85–55 million years ago** — The Tasman Sea's midocean ridge develops as Australia and Zealandia break apart from Gondwana.
- **1642** — Dutch explorer Abel Janszoon Tasman becomes the first known European to cross the sea.
- **1770s** — British explorer Lieutenant James Cook extensively navigates the Tasman Sea.
- **1876** — The first telegraph cable connecting Australia and New Zealand is laid across the sea.
- **January 1928** — Moncrieff and Hood disappear attempting the first trans-Tasman flight.
- **1928** — Charles Kingsford Smith and Charles Ulm complete the first successful flight over the sea.
- **1963** — The Commonwealth Pacific Cable, New Zealand's first international telephone cable, makes the telegraph obsolete.
- **1977** — Colin Quincey becomes the first person to row solo across the Tasman Sea.
- **2010** — Shaun Quincey completes the next successful solo rowing crossing.

## Body

### Geography and Extent
The Tasman Sea is approximately 2,250 km (1,400 mi) wide and covers an area of 2,300,000 km² (890,000 sq mi). Its maximum depth is 5,943 m (19,498 ft). The International Hydrographic Organization defines its precise boundaries, which extend from the Australian coast to New Zealand and include passages like Cook Strait and Foveaux Strait. The sea floor is primarily composed of globigerina ooze, with zones of pteropod and siliceous ooze in specific areas.

### Oceanography and Currents
The dominant circulation feature is the East Australian Current, a warm current that transports heat from the tropics southward. A branch of this current, the Tasman Front, flows eastward toward the north of New Zealand. Its path is influenced by westerly winds and the New Zealand landmass. Other currents include the East Auckland Current and the East Cape Current. South of Tasmania, the East Australian Current interacts with the Subantarctic Front of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current.

### Climate and Ecology
The sea's climate is characterized by frequent westerly winds, particularly in the south. During the southern winter (April–October), southwest winds are common. Research, such as that by the RV Tangaroa, has documented significant biodiversity, including 500 fish species and 1,300 invertebrate species. Fossils, like a tooth from the extinct megalodon shark, have also been found.

### Human History and Exploration
The sea is named after Abel Tasman, who crossed it in 1642. It was later navigated by James Cook in the 1770s. The Māori name for the sea is Te Moana-a-Rehua. Human attempts to cross it include the first telegraph cable in 1876, the first successful flight in 1928, and solo rowing crossings beginning in 1977. Colloquially, it is known as "The Ditch."

## Terms
- **Marginal sea:** A sea partially enclosed by islands, archipelagos, or peninsulas.
- **Midocean ridge:** An underwater mountain system formed by plate tectonics, marking where tectonic plates diverge.
- **Globigerina ooze:** A type of deep-sea sediment composed of the calcium carbonate shells of microscopic plankton.
- **East Australian Current:** A powerful, warm ocean current flowing southward along the east coast of Australia.
- **Tasman Front:** An eastward-flowing ocean current that branches from the East Australian Current.
- **Zealandia:** A largely submerged continent, of which New Zealand is the above-water part.
- **Gondwana:** An ancient supercontinent that included present-day Australia, Antarctica, and other landmasses.
- **Subtropical Front:** A boundary in the ocean where warmer subtropical waters meet cooler subantarctic waters.

## Debates and open questions
The exact etymology of the colloquial term "The Ditch" for the Tasman Sea is uncertain.
