# History of Iraq

## Overview
Iraq, in West Asia, largely corresponds to ancient Mesopotamia, the "cradle of civilization." Its history spans from prehistoric Neanderthal settlements to the modern republic, marked by the rise and fall of empires, the birth of writing and urban life, and recent conflicts. Understanding Iraq's past is essential for grasping human civilization's foundations and the modern Middle East's complex geopolitics.

## Timeline
- **c. 65,000–35,000 BC** — Neanderthal culture present in northern Iraq (Shanidar Cave).
- **c. 10,000 BC** — Neolithic Revolution begins; agriculture and animal domestication emerge.
- **c. 3500–3000 BC** — Sumerian civilization emerges; first writing system develops.
- **2335–2124 BC** — Akkadian Empire founded by Sargon, first Mesopotamian empire.
- **1792–1750 BC** — Hammurabi rules Babylon, creating a short-lived Babylonian Empire.
- **911–609 BC** — Neo-Assyrian Empire dominates the Ancient Near East.
- **626–539 BC** — Neo-Babylonian Empire flourishes under Nebuchadnezzar II.
- **539 BC** — Cyrus the Great conquers Babylon; Mesopotamia joins the Achaemenid Persian Empire.
- **636–638 AD** — Islamic conquest of Mesopotamia following the Battle of al-Qadisiyyah.
- **762 AD** — Baghdad founded as capital of the Abbasid Caliphate.
- **1258** — Mongol sack of Baghdad ends the Abbasid Caliphate.
- **1533–1918** — Most of modern Iraq is under Ottoman rule.
- **1921** — British establish the Kingdom of Iraq under King Faisal I.
- **1932** — Iraq gains formal independence from British Mandate.
- **1958** — Monarchy overthrown; Iraq becomes a republic.
- **1979–2003** — Saddam Hussein rules Iraq.
- **1990–1991** — Iraq invades Kuwait; Gulf War follows.
- **2003** — U.S.-led invasion topples Saddam Hussein's government.
- **2014–2017** — War against the Islamic State (ISIL).

## Body

### Prehistory and Ancient Mesopotamia
Northern Iraq was home to Neanderthals between 65,000 and 35,000 BC. Around 10,000 BC, the region became a center for the Neolithic Revolution, where agriculture and animal breeding first appeared. By the mid-6th millennium BC, the Ubaid period saw settled villages emerge. This led to the rise of Sumer, the world's earliest civilization, in the late 4th millennium BC. Sumerians developed cuneiform, the wheel, city-states, and early mathematics, astronomy, and law.

### Bronze Age Empires
Sumerian city-states were united under the Akkadian Empire (2335–2124 BC), founded by Sargon. After its collapse, the Ur III dynasty briefly reunified the region. The Old Babylonian Empire, most famous under Hammurabi (r. 1792–1750 BC), followed. The Middle Assyrian Empire (1365–1020 BC) made Assyria a major power. The Bronze Age ended with widespread collapse and migration.

### Iron Age and the Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian Empires
The Neo-Assyrian Empire (911–609 BC) became the dominant Near Eastern force, ruling from Egypt to Iran. It was succeeded by the Neo-Babylonian Empire (626–539 BC), whose greatest king, Nebuchadnezzar II, oversaw a cultural renaissance. This era ended when Cyrus the Great of Persia conquered Babylon in 539 BC.

### Classical Antiquity and Islamic Conquest
After Persian rule, the region was conquered by Alexander the Great in 331 BC and later became part of the Seleucid Empire. It then passed through Parthian and Roman control before being ruled by the Sassanid Persian Empire from the 3rd century AD. Arab tribes migrated into the area during this time. The Rashidun Caliphate conquered the Sassanids in the 7th century, bringing Islam and Arabic to the region.

### Abbasid Caliphate and Mongol Invasion
The Abbasid Caliphate made Baghdad its capital in 762, inaugurating the Islamic Golden Age. Baghdad became a global center for science and culture. This period ended with the Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258, which destroyed the Abbasid Caliphate and devastated the city.

### Ottoman Rule and British Mandate
From the 16th century, most of Iraq was part of the Ottoman Empire, often administered as the Baghdad Eyalet. Ottoman rule ended after World War I. The British established the Kingdom of Iraq in 1921 under a Hashemite monarchy, granting independence in 1932.

### Modern Iraq: Monarchy, Republic, and Conflict
The monarchy was overthrown in a 1958 coup, establishing a republic. Political instability followed, with multiple coups. The Ba'ath Party took power in 1968, and Saddam Hussein became president in 1979. His rule included the Iran-Iraq War (1980–1988) and the invasion of Kuwait in 1990, leading to the Gulf War and sanctions. The 2003 U.S.-led invasion toppled Saddam's government, leading to occupation, sectarian civil war, and the rise of ISIL. ISIL was militarily defeated in Iraq in 2017, but a low-level insurgency continues. Recent years have seen political protests and governmental challenges.

## Terms
- **Mesopotamia**: The historical region between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, largely within modern Iraq, known as the "cradle of civilization."
- **Cuneiform**: The earliest known writing system, developed by the Sumerians using wedge-shaped marks on clay tablets.
- **Sumer**: The world's earliest civilization, emerging in southern Mesopotamia in the late 4th millennium BC.
- **Akkadian Empire**: The first empire in Mesopotamia, founded by Sargon of Akkad in the 24th century BC.
- **Neo-Assyrian Empire**: A powerful Iron Age empire (911–609 BC) that dominated the Ancient Near East.
- **Abbasid Caliphate**: The third caliphate to succeed the Prophet Muhammad, ruling from Baghdad during the Islamic Golden Age (8th–13th centuries).
- **Ba'ath Party**: A secular Arab nationalist party that ruled Iraq from 1968 to 2003 under Ahmed Hassan al-Bakr and Saddam Hussein.
- **Islamic State (ISIL/ISIS)**: A radical Islamist militant group that controlled large parts of Iraq and Syria before being militarily defeated in Iraq in 2017.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact cause of the Akkadian Empire's collapse in the 22nd century BC is debated, with theories including invasion, internal revolt, or climate change.
- Historians dispute whether Baghdad's decline after the Mongol invasion was primarily due to destruction of irrigation infrastructure or long-term soil salination.
- The effects of UN sanctions on Iraq (1990–2003) are contested; while widely believed to have caused a major rise in child mortality, recent research suggests Iraqi government data on this was fabricated.
- The primary justification for the 2003 U.S. invasion—the presence of weapons of mass destruction—remains contentious, as no such stockpiles were found.
