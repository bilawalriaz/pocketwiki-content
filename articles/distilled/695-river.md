# River

## Overview  
A river is a natural channel carrying freshwater downhill toward an ocean, lake, or another river, driven by gravity and sustained by the water cycle. Rivers shape landscapes through erosion and sediment transport, support diverse ecosystems, and have been central to human civilization for water, transport, agriculture, and industry. Today, they face threats from dams, pollution, and climate change, prompting restoration and conservation efforts.

## Timeline  
- **c. 5500–3500 BCE** — First river-valley civilizations emerge (Tigris–Euphrates, Nile, Indus)
- **c. 3000 BCE** — Canals cut in Egypt; shadoof used for irrigation
- **c. 2500 BCE** — Sadd el-Kafara dam built on the Nile
- **7th century CE** — Spanish Muslims use water wheels and mills
- **1300s** — Watermills widespread in Europe (10,000+ in England)
- **1800s** — Urban rivers like Sunswick Creek covered for development
- **1970s** — Global dam construction peaks
- **2024** — Swimming permitted again in the Seine after pollution cleanup

## Body  

### Definition and Hydrology  
A river is a natural flow of freshwater moving downhill under gravity toward a lower body of water. The study of water movement is hydrology; landscape effects are geomorphology. Rivers originate from precipitation—rainfall, snowmelt, glacier melt, or groundwater seepage—collected in headwaters within a drainage basin (watershed). Drainage divides, such as the Continental Divide, separate basins. Some rivers are intermittent (flowing seasonally) or subterranean (flowing through caves, e.g., karst systems).

### Flow and Landscape Shaping  
Rivers flow downhill, eroding rock and soil to form valleys, canyons, and waterfalls. They transport sediment (alluvium), sorting particles by size: heavy material deposits near the source, fine silt travels farther. Rivers rarely run in a straight direction, instead preferring to bend or meander. Meandering occurs when obstacles redirect flow, building sediment on one bank and eroding the other. Floodplains form in flat areas, where periodic flooding deposits nutrient-rich sediment. Sediment yield—the amount of material removed per unit area—is monitored to assess ecosystem health and erosion rates.

### Classification  
Stream order ranks river branching complexity. The Strahler system assigns 1st-order to headwater streams; merging two 1st-order streams creates a 2nd-order, and so on. Order correlates with basin size and channel length.

### Ecology  
River ecosystems depend on channel width, flow velocity, and shading. The River Continuum Concept divides organisms into shredders (consume leaf litter), grazers/scrapers (eat algae), collectors (process detritus), and predators. The Flood Pulse Concept emphasizes seasonal flooding in riparian zones, where vegetation stabilizes banks and filters nutrients. Fish Zonation links species diversity to river discharge: larger rivers support more species. Anadromous fish (e.g., salmon) migrate from sea to spawn; catadromous fish (e.g., eels) move from rivers to sea.

### Human Uses  
Rivers provide water, transport, food, and energy. Engineering includes dams (for hydroelectricity and reservoirs), levees (flood control), dikes (channel straightening), and channelization (shortening meanders). The Mississippi River produced 400 million tons of sediment per year. Due to the construction of reservoirs, sediment buildup in man-made levees, and the removal of natural banks replaced with revetments, this sediment output has been reduced by 60%. Dams are very common worldwide, with at least 75,000 higher than 6 feet (1.8 m) in the U.S. Globally, reservoirs created by dams cover 193,500 square miles (501,000 km2). Dam-building reached a peak in the 1970s, when between two or three dams were completed every day, and has since begun to decline. Urban rivers suffer from impermeable surfaces, stormwater runoff, and historical sewage discharge, though treatment improvements have restored some water quality.

### History  
Pre-industrial societies relied on rivers for irrigation, transport, and mills. The mechanical shadoof began to be used to raise the elevation of water, and Roman aqueducts exemplify early water management. Water wheels powered medieval mills, later replaced by steam. A medieval watermill could do the work of 30–60 human workers. Industrialization increased river use for hydropower and shipping. The Mississippi and its 40%-of-the-U.S. basin became a major cargo route. One of the first large canals was the Canal du Midi, connecting rivers within France to create a path from the Atlantic Ocean to the Mediterranean Sea. Modern cities seek to restore urban rivers for recreation and ecology, as seen in Munich’s Isar restoration. Swimming was banned in the Seine for over 100 years due to concerns about pollution and the spread of E. coli, until cleanup efforts to allow its use in the 2024 Summer Olympics.

### Politics  
Rivers often form borders—23% of international boundaries follow large rivers. Disputes arise over upstream diversions and pollution. The Rio Grande is managed by the International Boundary and Water Commission; Egypt secures Nile water via Aswan Dam agreements. Up to 60% of national freshwater may cross borders, requiring treaties.

### Religion and Mythology  
Rivers symbolize life, fertility, and destruction. Greek myth features the Styx and Lethe; Genesis describes four rivers from Eden. Flood myths appear globally (Gilgamesh, Norse, Aboriginal). Sacred rivers include the Ganges (Hinduism), Nile (Egyptian gods), and Ogun (Yoruba). Ritual bathing and cremation practices persist along these waters.

### Threats  
Dams block fish migration and trap sediment, degrading habitats. Pollution from PFAS, fertilizers, and sewage creates dead zones. Climate change alters flow timing, reduces snowpack, and raises water temperatures. Conservation includes wetland protection, dam removal, and pollutant limits.

### Extraterrestrial Rivers  
Mars shows ancient river networks and deltas dating to 3.7 billion years ago, indicating past precipitation and a water cycle. Titan’s methane/ethane rivers resemble Earth’s fluvial systems, offering analogs for studying erosion without human influence.

## Terms  
- **Drainage basin**: Land area where all precipitation flows to a common outlet.  
- **Alluvium**: Sediment deposited by rivers, especially in floodplains and deltas.  
- **Meander**: A bend in a river formed by erosion and deposition cycles.  
- **Anadromous**: Fish that migrate from saltwater to freshwater to spawn (e.g., salmon).  
- **Catadromous**: Fish that migrate from freshwater to saltwater to spawn (e.g., eels).  
- **Stream order**: A numerical classification of river branching hierarchy (Strahler system).  
- **Riparian zone**: Area adjacent to a river, characterized by water-dependent vegetation.  
- **Channelization**: Engineering rivers into straighter, artificial channels.  
- **Endorheic basin**: A closed drainage basin with no outlet to the ocean.  
- **Flumen**: In planetary geology, a channel on Titan that may carry liquid methane.

## Debates and Open Questions  
- Whether climate change or extreme flooding caused the abandonment of ancient river-valley civilizations remains debated.  
- Effectiveness of fish ladders in restoring salmon migration past dams is contested.  
- Optimal levels of dam removal versus retention for balancing energy needs and ecosystem health are actively discussed.  
- The extent and duration of river flow on early Mars is uncertain, with ongoing analysis of sedimentary evidence.
