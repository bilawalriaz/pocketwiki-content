# Crisis of the Third Century

## Overview
The Crisis of the Third Century (235–284 AD) was a period of near-collapse for the Roman Empire, marked by constant civil war, foreign invasions, economic breakdown, and plague. It was a transformative era that ended the classical Principate system of government and set the stage for the late antique empire of Diocletian and Constantine, fundamentally altering Roman society, economy, and military structure.

## Timeline
- **235** — Assassination of Emperor Severus Alexander; accession of Maximinus Thrax.
- **238** — Year of the Six Emperors; revolt of Gordian I and II.
- **251** — Plague of Cyprian begins, causing massive population loss.
- **260** — Emperor Valerian is captured by the Sasanian Empire.
- **260** — Gallic Empire breaks away in the west.
- **267** — Palmyrene Empire breaks away in the east.
- **268/269** — Emperor Claudius II Gothicus defeats the Goths at the Battle of Naissus.
- **270–275** — Reign of Aurelian, who reunites the empire.
- **284** — Accession of Diocletian, who reforms the empire and ends the crisis.

## Body

### Origins and Political Instability
The crisis was triggered by the assassination of Severus Alexander in 235, which shattered the imperial succession. The ensuing decades saw over 50 claimants to the throne, mostly military commanders ("barracks emperors") who relied on their armies for power. This constant civil war prevented effective defense of the frontiers and drained the treasury. The empire fragmented into three competing states by 268: the central Roman Empire, the Gallic Empire in the west, and the Palmyrene Empire in the east.

### External Pressures and Plague
Civil war left the borders vulnerable. Germanic tribes like the Goths, Vandals, and Alamanni raided across the Rhine and Danube, while the resurgent Sasanian Empire threatened the east. Climate change and sea-level rise displaced northern tribes, pushing them into Roman territory. Compounding these invasions was the Plague of Cyprian (c. 249–262), which devastated the population, weakened the army, and disrupted agriculture.

### Economic Collapse and Social Change
To pay the ever-larger bribes needed to secure army loyalty, emperors debased the currency, leading to hyperinflation. The old denarius became worthless. This, combined with unsafe trade routes, caused the empire's vast internal trade network to break down. Large estates became self-sufficient, producing goods locally for barter. Many small farmers and urban poor lost their freedom, becoming *coloni* (tenant farmers tied to the land), a precursor to medieval serfdom. The balance of power shifted from cities to rural landowners.

### Recovery and Transformation
The turning point came with Claudius II Gothicus's victory over the Goths at Naissus (268/269). His successor, Aurelian (r. 270–275), reunified the empire by defeating the breakaway states and reformed the coinage. However, true stability was only achieved by Diocletian (r. 284–305). He ended the cycle of usurpation by establishing the Tetrarchy (rule of four), reformed the administration, military, and tax system, and moved the empire's center of gravity eastward. His reign marked the end of the Principate and the beginning of the Dominate, where emperors ruled as absolute "lords" (*domini*).

## Terms
- **Barracks Emperor**: An emperor who rose to power solely through military support, often lacking political experience or hereditary claim.
- **Coloni**: A class of tenant farmers in the later Roman Empire who were legally bound to the land they worked, a precursor to medieval serfdom.
- **Debasement**: The practice of reducing the precious metal content in coinage, which led to severe inflation during the crisis.
- **Foederati**: Barbarian groups given land within the empire in exchange for military service, whose influence grew as Roman manpower declined.
- **Gallic Empire**: A breakaway state (260–274) comprising Gaul, Britannia, and briefly Hispania.
- **Palmyrene Empire**: A breakaway state (267–273) centered on Palmyra, controlling Syria, Palestine, and Egypt.
- **Plague of Cyprian**: A devastating pandemic (c. 249–262) that severely reduced the empire's population and military strength.
- **Principate**: The early phase of Roman imperial rule (27 BC–284 AD) where emperors maintained a facade of republican government, using the title *princeps* ("first citizen").
- **Tetrarchy**: The system of government established by Diocletian in 293, dividing the empire among two senior emperors (*Augusti*) and two junior emperors (*Caesares*).

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise nature and origin of the Plague of Cyprian (possibly smallpox) is debated.
- The extent of economic collapse is contested; recent research suggests regional variation, with some provinces like Egypt and Africa remaining prosperous.
- The degree to which climate change directly caused barbarian migrations is a subject of ongoing scholarly discussion.
