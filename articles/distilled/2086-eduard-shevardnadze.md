# Eduard Shevardnadze

## Overview
Eduard Shevardnadze (1928–2014) was a pivotal Soviet and Georgian politician whose career spanned the Cold War's end and the birth of independent Georgia. As Soviet Foreign Minister (1985–1991), he was a key architect of the reforms that dissolved the USSR. He later led Georgia through civil war and separatist conflicts, but his presidency was ultimately ended by the Rose Revolution in 2003.

## Timeline
- **25 Jan 1928** — Born in Mamati, Georgian SSR.
- **1948** — Joins the Georgian Communist Party.
- **1972** — Becomes First Secretary of the Georgian Communist Party, de facto leader of Georgia.
- **1985** — Appointed Soviet Minister of Foreign Affairs by Mikhail Gorbachev.
- **Dec 1990** — Resigns as Soviet Foreign Minister, warning "Dictatorship is coming."
- **Nov 1991** — Briefly returns as Soviet Foreign Minister; resigns with Gorbachev in Dec.
- **Mar 1992** — Returns to Georgia, appointed Chairperson of the State Council.
- **Oct 1993** — Signs treaty for Georgia to join the Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS).
- **Nov 1995** — Elected President of Georgia with 70% of the vote.
- **Nov 1999** — Signs Georgia's accession to the Council of Europe.
- **2 Nov 2003** — Parliamentary election widely denounced as fraudulent.
- **23 Nov 2003** — Resigns as President following the Rose Revolution protests.
- **7 Jul 2014** — Dies in Tbilisi at age 86.

## Body

### Early Career and Rise in Soviet Georgia
Shevardnadze began his political rise in the late 1940s within the Komsomol (Communist youth league). His ascent was marked by a focus on anti-corruption campaigns. After a demotion in 1961, he returned to charge a Tbilisi official with corruption, which led to his appointment as head of the Georgian Ministry of Internal Affairs. His successful anti-corruption drive, supported by the Soviet government, culminated in his appointment as First Secretary of the Georgian Communist Party in 1972, replacing the corrupt Vasil Mzhavanadze.

### Leader of Soviet Georgia (1972–1985)
As First Secretary, Shevardnadze tackled Georgia's rampant corruption and economic stagnation. He implemented economic reforms, including the "Abasha experiment" in agriculture, which decentralized decision-making and spurred growth uncommon in the stagnating Soviet Union. He also navigated rising Georgian nationalism, notably compromising during the 1978 demonstrations to keep Georgian as the sole official language. His loyalty to successive Soviet leaders and his reformist reputation led to his appointment as Soviet Foreign Minister in 1985.

### Soviet Foreign Minister (1985–1991)
Appointed by Mikhail Gorbachev, Shevardnadze became the face of Soviet reforms like *glasnost* (openness) and *perestroika* (restructuring). He was instrumental in ending the Cold War, negotiating nuclear arms treaties with the U.S., facilitating the reunification of Germany, and withdrawing Soviet forces from Eastern Europe. He resigned in December 1990 over the growing influence of hardliners, but briefly returned after the failed 1991 coup before the USSR's dissolution.

### Leader of Independent Georgia (1992–2003)
After the Soviet collapse, Shevardnadze was invited to lead Georgia following the 1992 coup against President Zviad Gamsakhurdia. He faced immediate crises: the Georgian Civil War (1993) and wars in the separatist regions of Abkhazia and South Ossetia. To end the civil war, he joined the Russian-led CIS, securing Russian military assistance but deepening Georgia's dependence. He survived multiple assassination attempts. His rule saw large-scale privatization but was plagued by rampant corruption and nepotism among his inner circle. He pursued a pro-Western foreign policy, signing a partnership with NATO and declaring an ambition to join in 2002.

### Downfall and Legacy
Allegations of electoral fraud in the November 2003 parliamentary election sparked the Rose Revolution. Mass protests forced Shevardnadze to resign on 23 November 2003 to avoid bloodshed. He spent his final years in obscurity. His legacy is complex: he is credited with helping end the Cold War and guiding Georgia's early independence, but also criticized for failing to curb corruption and for presiding over the loss of Georgian territory.

## Terms
*   **First Secretary:** The de facto leader of a Soviet republic, heading its Communist Party.
*   **Perestroika:** Gorbachev's policy of political and economic restructuring in the USSR.
*   **Glasnost:** Gorbachev's policy of increased openness and transparency in government.
*   **Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS):** A regional organization formed after the USSR's dissolution, comprising former Soviet republics.
*   **Rose Revolution:** The 2003 non-violent change of power in Georgia triggered by protests against electoral fraud.
*   **Competitive authoritarianism:** A political system where democratic institutions exist but are systematically tilted in favor of incumbents.
*   **Komsomol:** The Communist Union of Youth, the youth wing of the Communist Party.

## Debates and Open questions
*   **Effectiveness of anti-corruption campaigns:** While Shevardnadze's early campaigns in Georgia were celebrated, his rule as president was marked by systemic corruption, raising questions about his commitment or ability to tackle it at the highest levels.
*   **Balancing nationalism and centralism:** His stance against "national narrow-mindedness" in Soviet Georgia contrasted with his later role as a Georgian nationalist leader, highlighting the complex political navigation required in the multi-ethnic Soviet and post-Soviet space.
*   **Legacy in state-building:** Scholars debate whether his presidency established fragile state institutions or merely diffused power among informal, competing centers, contributing to later instability.
