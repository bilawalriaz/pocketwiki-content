# Bosnia and Herzegovina

## Overview
Bosnia and Herzegovina is a Southeast European country on the Balkan Peninsula, known for its complex history of shifting empires and ethnic diversity. Its modern identity was forged through centuries of Ottoman and Austro-Hungarian rule, culminating in a devastating war in the 1990s that established its current decentralized political structure. The country is a developing, upper-middle-income economy and a candidate for both EU and NATO membership.

## Timeline
- **c. 1463** — The Kingdom of Bosnia is conquered by the Ottoman Empire.
- **1878** — The Congress of Berlin places Bosnia and Herzegovina under Austro-Hungarian administration.
- **1908** — Austria-Hungary formally annexes Bosnia and Herzegovina.
- **1914** — Archduke Franz Ferdinand is assassinated in Sarajevo, triggering World War I.
- **1941-1945** — During WWII, the region is part of the Independent State of Croatia (NDH), a Nazi puppet state.
- **1945** — Bosnia and Herzegovina becomes a constituent republic of the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia.
- **1992-1995** — The Bosnian War follows a declaration of independence from Yugoslavia.
- **1995** — The Dayton Agreement ends the war, establishing the current state structure.
- **2022** — Bosnia and Herzegovina is recognized as an EU candidate country.

## Body

### History and Formation
The region has been inhabited since the Paleolithic era, with significant Neolithic cultures like Butmir and Kakanj. It was populated by Illyrian and Celtic tribes before Roman conquest in the 1st century BCE. The ancestors of modern South Slavs arrived between the 6th and 9th centuries. The first independent Bosnian polity, the Banate of Bosnia, emerged in the 12th century and evolved into the powerful Kingdom of Bosnia by the 14th century. The Ottoman conquest in 1463 introduced Islam and began a four-century rule that reshaped the region's demographics and culture. After the 1878 Congress of Berlin, Austria-Hungary administered and later annexed the territory, promoting modernization while attempting to foster a distinct Bosnian identity. Following World War I, Bosnia became part of the Kingdom of Yugoslavia. In World War II, it was incorporated into the Nazi-puppet Independent State of Croatia, where horrific atrocities were committed against Serbs, Jews, and Roma. Post-war, it was a republic within communist Yugoslavia, experiencing relative peace and prosperity until the federation's collapse.

### The Bosnian War and Aftermath
Following multi-party elections in 1990, ethnic tensions over independence escalated. Bosnian Serb leaders established their own assembly and entity, while a referendum for independence, boycotted by most Serbs, passed overwhelmingly. Bosnia and Herzegovina declared independence in March 1992, leading to a war primarily between Bosnian Serb forces (backed by Serbia) and the Bosnian government army, with a later sub-conflict involving Bosnian Croats. The war was characterized by ethnic cleansing, concentration camps, and genocide, most notably the Srebrenica massacre of over 8,000 Bosniak men and boys in 1995. The 1995 Dayton Agreement ended the war by creating a highly decentralized state composed of two main entities: the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina (Bosniak-Croat) and Republika Srpska (Serb), along with the Brčko District.

### Politics and Government
The Dayton Agreement established a complex, multi-layered political system. Executive power is held by a tripartite presidency (one Bosniak, one Serb, one Croat) and a Council of Ministers. Legislative power resides in a bicameral Parliamentary Assembly. The country is largely decentralized, with most authority resting in the two entities and, within the Federation, in ten cantons. The Office of the High Representative, an international position, holds significant authority to oversee the peace agreement. The country's three main ethnic groups—Bosniaks (50.1%), Serbs (30.8%), and Croats (15.5%)—are recognized as "constituent peoples," with guaranteed power-sharing.

### Economy and Society
Bosnia and Herzegovina is a developing, upper-middle-income economy recovering from the war, which caused massive material damage. Key industries include manufacturing, agriculture, and a growing tourism sector. The country faces challenges like high unemployment and a significant "grey economy." Its population is approximately 3.4 million, though demographic experts debate this figure due to post-war migration. The three standard languages—Bosnian, Serbian, and Croatian—are mutually intelligible and serve as markers of ethnic identity. The country is religiously diverse, with Muslims forming the slight majority (50.7%), followed by Orthodox Christians (30.7%) and Catholics (15.2%).

## Terms
*   **Dayton Agreement:** The 1995 peace accord that ended the Bosnian War and established the country's current decentralized political structure and two main entities.
*   **Constituent Peoples:** The three ethnic groups (Bosniaks, Serbs, Croats) recognized by the Dayton Agreement as having guaranteed political representation and power-sharing.
*   **High Representative:** An international official tasked with overseeing the implementation of the Dayton Agreement, holding significant executive and legislative authority.
*   **Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina:** One of the two main political entities, primarily inhabited by Bosniaks and Croats.
*   **Republika Srpska:** One of the two main political entities, primarily inhabited by Serbs.
*   **Brčko District:** A self-governing, multi-ethnic unit in northern Bosnia, nominally under joint control of both entities.
*   **Bosniak:** An ethnic group, primarily Muslim, who identify as Bosnian.
*   **Stećci:** Medieval tombstones found in Bosnia and Herzegovina, a UNESCO World Heritage site.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Census Data:** The results of the 2013 census are contested by Republika Srpska officials, who dispute the methodology for counting non-permanent residents, leading to debates about the country's true population size and ethnic composition.
*   **Political Structure:** The Dayton Agreement's complex, ethnic-based power-sharing system is often criticized for entrenching ethnic divisions and causing political gridlock, leading to ongoing debates about necessary reforms for EU integration.
*   **National Identity:** The use of three separate official languages (Bosnian, Serbian, Croatian) that are mutually intelligible is seen by some scholars as a symbolic assertion of national identity over pragmatic unity.
