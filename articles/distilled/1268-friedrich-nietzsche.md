# Friedrich Nietzsche

## Overview
Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900) was a German philosopher, cultural critic, and philologist whose radical critique of Western morality, religion, and metaphysics profoundly shaped modern intellectual history. Beginning as a classical philologist appointed to the University of Basel at age 24, he resigned due to chronic illness and spent his productive decade as an independent writer. His philosophy centers on the "death of God"—the collapse of absolute values—and the resulting crisis of nihilism. He responded with concepts like the *will to power* (the fundamental drive of life), *perspectivism* (the rejection of objective truth), the *Übermensch* (creator of new values), and *eternal recurrence* (the ultimate test of life-affirmation). After a mental collapse in 1889, he lived in incapacitation until his death. His sister Elisabeth subsequently manipulated his unpublished notes to align with German nationalism and antisemitism, associating his work with Nazism; mid-20th-century scholars like Walter Kaufmann restored his reputation, establishing him as a foundational figure for existentialism, postmodernism, and post-structuralism.

- **1882** — Meets Lou Andreas-Salomé; proposes marriage thrice, rejected; writes first part of *The Gay Science*.

- **1889–1900** — Incapacitated; cared for by mother, then sister Elisabeth in Weimar.
- **25 Aug 1900** — Dies of pneumonia/stroke; buried in Röcken.
- **1901** — Elisabeth publishes *The Will to Power* (compiled from notebooks; later deemed a forgery).
- **1950s–60s** — Kaufmann, Hollingdale, Bataille lead scholarly rehabilitation; Nietzsche becomes central to continental philosophy.

## Body

### Biography and Intellectual Formation
Nietzsche was born into a Lutheran pastor’s family in Röcken. His father died in 1849 (brain disease), followed by his younger brother; the family moved to Naumburg. At Schulpforta (1858–1864), he excelled in classical languages but rebelled against the conservative milieu, reading the "un-German" poet Friedrich Hölderlin and the blasphemous Ernst Ortlepp, who may have introduced him to Wagner. At the University of Bonn (1864), he briefly studied theology but lost his faith, influenced by David Strauss’s *Life of Jesus* and Ludwig Feuerbach’s *The Essence of Christianity* (arguing humans created God). He switched to philology under Friedrich Ritschl, following him to Leipzig (1865). There he read Schopenhauer (decisive for his philosophical turn), Lange’s *History of Materialism* (introducing Kant, Darwin, and materialism), and served in the military until a riding accident in 1868 ended his service. He met Wagner later that year.

### Basel Professorship and Break with Wagner (1869–1879)
Ritschl secured Nietzsche a Basel chair at 24, before doctorate or habilitation (honorary doctorate from Leipzig). He renounced Prussian citizenship, remaining stateless. He served as a medical orderly in the Franco-Prussian War (1870–71), witnessing trauma and contracting severe illnesses. At Basel, he befriended Franz Overbeck (lifelong friend) and Jacob Burckhardt, and entered Wagner’s intimate circle at Tribschen. *The Birth of Tragedy* (1872) abandoned philological method for speculative philosophy, arguing Greek tragedy fused Apollonian (order) and Dionysian (chaos) impulses—a fusion lost since Euripides/Socrates introduced rationalism. The philological establishment, led by Ulrich von Wilamowitz-Moellendorff, savaged the book. Nietzsche’s *Untimely Meditations* (1873–76) critiqued German culture. Disillusioned by the 1876 Bayreuth Festival’s banality and Wagner’s German nationalism, he broke with Wagner. *Human, All Too Human* (1878) adopted an aphoristic, scientific style influenced by Afrikan Spir, rejecting Schopenhauerian pessimism. Declining health (migraines, blindness, indigestion) forced his resignation in 1879.

### Independent Philosopher and Productive Decade (1879–1888)
Living on a Basel pension, Nietzsche migrated seasonally (Sils Maria in summer; Genoa, Rapallo, Turin, Nice in winter) seeking health. He used a typewriter (Hansen Writing Ball) as eyesight failed; his former student Peter Gast became secretary and proofreader. He published a book yearly: *The Gay Science* (1882, "God is dead"), *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* (1883–85, *Übermensch*, eternal recurrence), *Beyond Good and Evil* (1886), *On the Genealogy of Morality* (1887). In 1882, he met Lou Andreas-Salomé via Malwida von Meysenbug and Paul Rée; he proposed marriage thrice, she refused. The trio’s planned academic commune collapsed; Nietzsche blamed Salomé, Rée, and his sister Elisabeth (who sabotaged the relationship). He fled to Rapallo, writing *Zarathustra* Part I in ten days. In 1886, he broke with publisher Schmeitzner over antisemitism, self-publishing *Beyond Good and Evil* and reissuing earlier works with new prefaces. He read Dostoevsky (1887), feeling kinship, and corresponded with Brandes (who lectured on Nietzsche in Copenhagen, 1888). In his final productive year (1888), he completed five works, including the autobiographical *Ecce Homo* ("Why I Am So Wise"), showing increasing grandiosity.

### Collapse, Death, and Posthumous Fate
On 3 January 1889 in Turin, Nietzsche collapsed after a public disturbance (legend: embracing a beaten horse). He sent delusional letters (*Wahnbriefe*) signed "Dionysus" and "the Crucified One," claiming divine identity and commanding political violence. Overbeck retrieved him to a Basel clinic; his mother moved him to Jena (Otto Binswanger) then Naumburg. Overbeck and Gast withheld *The Antichrist* and *Ecce Homo* for radical content. After his mother’s death (1897), Elisabeth returned from Paraguay (husband Bernhard Förster, an antisemite, had suicided) and seized control of the manuscripts. She compiled *The Will to Power* (1901) from disordered notebooks, distorting Nietzsche’s anti-antisemitic views to fit her nationalist ideology. Scholars (Mazzino Montinari) later called it a forgery. Nietzsche died in 1900 after strokes and pneumonia. Twentieth-century scholars (Kaufmann, Hollingdale, Bataille) disentangled his thought from Nazi appropriation, establishing critical editions.

### Citizenship, Nationality, and Ethnicity
Nietzsche was born a Prussian citizen in the German Confederation; he renounced citizenship in 1869, dying stateless. He is universally termed a "German philosopher" by language and culture. Late in life, he claimed Polish noble ancestry (Radwan coat of arms, surname "Nicki"), writing: "My ancestors were Polish noblemen... I am a pure-blooded Polish nobleman." Most scholars (Hans von Müller, Max Oehler, Colli and Montinari) reject this as "pure invention" or "mistaken belief"; the name Nietzsche derives from *Nikolaus* via German *Nitsche/Nitzke*. Hypotheses for his claim include a "campaign against Germany" (Hollingdale) or satirical parody of autobiography (Nicholas D. More).

### Relationships and Sexuality
Nietzsche never married. He proposed to Lou Salomé three times (rejected). Paul Deussen recounted a 1865 brothel visit in Cologne where Nietzsche fled in horror. Joachim Köhler argued Nietzsche was homosexual, citing syphilis (possibly from a male brothel in Genoa, per Freud/Otto Binswanger) and a romantic bond with Paul Rée. Most scholars (Allan Megill) find the evidence "very weak," projecting modern categories onto 19th-century friendship. Nigel Rodgers and Mel Thompson attribute his celibacy to chronic illness. Nietzsche expressed affection for women (e.g., Cosima Wagner). His philosophy’s relation to his psychology remains debated.

## Philosophy

### Apollonian and Dionysian
Introduced in *The Birth of Tragedy*, this dialectic derives from Greek gods Apollo (harmony, clarity, logic, individuation) and Dionysus (disorder, intoxication, ecstasy, unity). Nietzsche argued Athenian tragedy achieved a fusion of these *Kunsttriebe* (artistic impulses), allowing spectators to gaze into suffering (Dionysian abyss) and joyously affirm life via Apollonian form. He distinguished his Dionysus from the Orphic tradition (later corruption: god of suffering/redemption, fostering guilt and world-denial). Socrates and Euripides continued this Orphic trajectory, replacing instinct/myth with rationalism/dialectic, killing tragedy. The "Primordial Unity" experienced in tragedy is a frenzy of strength where art transforms reality into a mirror of the creator’s power. Without Apollonian form, Dionysian chaos lacks coherence; without Dionysian vitality, Apollonian form is sterile. This influenced Ruth Benedict (*Patterns of Culture*), Carl Jung, Michel Foucault, and Mark Rothko.

### Death of God and Nihilism
"God is dead" (*The Gay Science*) signifies the metaphorical death of the Abrahamic God as the foundation of Western meaning/value due to science and secularization. Nietzsche saw Christian morality as an antidote to primal nihilism (despair of meaninglessness), providing objective values and a suprasensory goal. Its collapse triggers *passive nihilism* (Schopenhauer’s "Western Buddhism": will to nothingness, denial of will to reduce suffering). Nietzsche called this a "will to nothingness"—still a form of willing. He viewed nihilism as a personal crisis: "I praise... its arrival... one of the greatest crises... Whether man recovers... is a question of his strength!" Overcoming nihilism is the precondition for a thriving culture. Heidegger read this as the death of metaphysics. Scholars debate parallels with Buddhism (Nishitani, Parkes) vs. Nietzsche’s defense of egoism (Milne), linking him instead to Heraclitus, Spinoza, Goethe.

### Eternal Return
A hypothetical physical concept (no supernatural reincarnation): the universe recurs identically across infinite time/space. First posed as a question in *The Gay Science* §341 and *Zarathustra* ("Of the Vision and the Riddle"). Nietzsche called it the "heaviest weight" (*das schwerste Gewicht*); affirming it requires *amor fati* (love of fate). It counters Schopenhauer’s denial of the will-to-live. Heidegger stressed the *burden of the question itself* as the "thought of thoughts." Alexander Nehamas outlines three readings: (1) fatalistic identity; (2) conditional cosmology; (3) "If my life were to recur, it could recur only in identical fashion"—independent of physics, grounded in self-constitution through action. It negates a "history of salvation."

### Perspectivism
The death of God entails no universal perspective; objective truth is incoherent. Knowledge is contingent, conditional, relative to fluid perspectives/interests. Rules (philosophy, science) require constant reassessment per individual circumstances. In *Zarathustra*, "a table of values hangs above every great person"; greatness lies in the *act of valuing*, not the content. "A thousand goals have there been... only the yoke for the thousand necks is still lacking: the one goal is lacking." Max Weber and Heidegger absorbed this: objectivity is possible only *after* a perspective/value is established. Nietzsche attacked Kant’s "thing-in-itself" and Descartes’ *cogito ergo sum* as unfalsifiable dogmas. Alasdair MacIntyre credited him with exposing the psychological motives (subjective will) behind claims of objectivity in Kant and Hume.

### Slave Revolt in Morals (*Genealogy of Morality*)
Nietzsche traces a shift from aristocratic "master morality" (good/bad) to "slave morality" (good/evil).
- **Master morality** (Homeric Greece): "Good" = noble, powerful, healthy, happy; "Bad" = weak, poor, sick (contempt/pity).
- **Slave morality** (Jewish/Christian origin): Born of *ressentiment*. Inverts values: "Good" = meek, charitable, other-worldly; "Evil" = worldly, powerful, selfish. Weakness is relabeled "meekness" (choice). Requires afterlife for justice (masters punished, slaves rewarded).
Nietzsche saw slave morality as the source of European nihilism: earthly life deemed unfair, justified only by heaven; loss of faith yields despair. Modern Europeans are "motley," torn between both. He called exceptional people to follow their "inner law" ("Become what you are" – Pindar), not universal morality (good for masses). Walter Kaufmann argued Nietzsche described these historically, not prescriptively; yet Nietzsche called master morality "a higher order of values... that say Yes to life" and waged "revaluation of all values" for a "philosophy of the future." In *Daybreak*, he launched a "Campaign against Morality," calling himself an "immoralist," attacking Christianity, Kantianism, utilitarianism. He condemned Christian pity (*Mitleid*) as strength-depleting, contagious suffering. He attacked Judaism as the priestly source of slave morality but explicitly condemned antisemitism, praising Jews for Christ, Spinoza, and preserving Greek philosophy. He called modern antisemitism "despicable," rooted in nationalism and jealousy.

### Übermensch
Introduced in *Zarathustra*’s Prologue: "Man is something that shall be overcome." The *Übermensch* creates new values, transcending good/evil and the "herd." Man is a "rope stretched between the animal and the Übermensch—a rope over an abyss... a bridge and not a goal." Contrasted with the "last man" (egalitarian modernity/democracy): apathetic, passionless, comfort-seeking, unable to dream—rendering the *Übermensch* impossible. Willing eternal recurrence is a necessary step for the *Übermensch* to create values untainted by asceticism ("spirit of gravity"); only the *Übermensch* has strength to affirm all of life, including failure. The Nazis literalized the metaphor into racial superiority. Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche manipulated manuscripts to support this. Nietzsche explicitly opposed antisemitism, pan-Germanism, and nationalism; he broke with his publisher (1886) and Wagner (1888) over these. In an 1887 letter to Theodor Fritsch, he mocked leading antisemites (Wagner, Paul de Lagarde, Houston Chamberlain). However, Franz Overbeck recalled Nietzsche’s private remarks on Jews could exceed antisemitism in severity, linking his anti-Christianity to anti-Semitism.

### Will to Power
The fundamental drive explaining behavior—superior to "struggle for existence" (Darwin) or "will to live" (Schopenhauer). Self-conservation is a *consequence* of exerting strength outward. Happiness (utilitarianism) is not an aim but a byproduct of overcoming resistance. Nietzsche speculated (non-final) that the physical world, like organic life, operates by will-to-power dynamics: forces, not stable atoms (rejecting atomism). He drew on Ruđer Bošković (matter as interplay of forces). One study defines it as "the principle of the synthesis of forces." He rejected inexorable natural laws, positing power relations between bodies. Scholars debate whether this constitutes a new metaphysics (contradicting his anti-metaphysics). Beyond *Beyond Good and Evil* §36, it appears mainly in unpublished notes. A 1888 instruction to his landlord to burn certain notes is cited as evidence he abandoned the project; however, Huang (2019) notes only 11 of 1067 aphorisms in *The Will to Power* came from those notes, which focused on morality critique.

## Terms
- ****Apollonian**** — Principle of order, harmony, clarity, logic, and individuation (associated with Apollo); provides form in art.
- ****Dionysian**** — Principle of chaos, intoxication, emotion, ecstasy, and unity (associated with Dionysus); provides vitality in art.
- ****Perspectivism**** — Epistemological view that all knowledge is contingent on specific perspectives/interests; no objective, universal "view from nowhere" exists.
- ****Master Morality**** — Aristocratic value system: "Good" = noble, strong, life-affirming; "Bad" = weak, contemptible (pre-moral, Homeric).
- ****Slave Morality**** — Reactive value system born of *ressentiment*: inverts master values; "Good" = meek, suffering, other-worldly; "Evil" = powerful, worldly.
- ****Ressentiment**** — Creative, reactive hatred of the powerless toward the powerful, generating new values by denying the existing hierarchy.
- ****Übermensch** (Overman/Superman)** — Creator of new values who overcomes nihilism and herd morality; "meaning of the earth"; a bridge, not a goal.
- ****Eternal Recurrence**** — Hypothetical cosmological idea: the universe repeats identically infinitely; the ultimate test of life-affirmation (*amor fati*).
- ****Will to Power**** — Fundamental metaphysical/biological drive: life as expansion, domination, and synthesis of forces; not mere survival.
- ****Amor Fati**** — "Love of fate"; the embrace of one's entire life, including suffering, as necessary and desirable.

## Debates and open questions
- **Nature of Nietzsche’s madness**: Original diagnosis (tertiary syphilis) challenged by later hypotheses: manic-depressive psychosis with vascular dementia (Cybulska), right-sided meningioma (Sax), frontotemporal dementia (Orth/Trimble), CADASIL (hereditary stroke disorder), mercury poisoning. Relation to philosophy debated (Bataille: poetic necessity; Girard: mimetic rivalry with Wagner).
- **Status of *The Will to Power***: Elisabeth’s compilation long treated as magnum opus; post-1960 consensus (Montinari) deems it a forgery not reflecting Nietzsche’s intent. Debate persists on value of *Nachlass* (posthumous notes) vs. published works.
- **Political appropriation**: Nazis selectively used *Übermensch* and "will to power" for racial ideology. Scholars (Kaufmann, Hollingdale, Bataille) vs. earlier interpreters (Baeumler) on whether Nietzsche’s aristocratic/anti-egalitarian rhetoric *enabled* fascism or was fundamentally incompatible (explicit anti-antisemitism, anti-nationalism).
- **Antisemitism vs. Anti-Judaism**: Nietzsche attacked ancient Jewish priesthood as source of slave morality but condemned modern antisemitism
