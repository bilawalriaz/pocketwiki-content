# Nicomachean Ethics

## Overview
The *Nicomachean Ethics* is Aristotle's foundational work on ethics, defining it as the practical science of achieving the highest human good, or *eudaimonia* (flourishing). It argues this good is achieved through cultivating virtues—stable character traits found as a mean between extremes—guided by practical wisdom. The text is essential for understanding Aristotelian ethics and profoundly influenced medieval philosophy and modern virtue ethics.

## Timeline
- **c. 353 BCE** — A reference to the Third Sacred War provides a *terminus post quem* for part of the work.
- **c. 335–322 BCE** — Traditional dating places composition during Aristotle's final period in Athens.
- **After 322 BCE** — Aristotle's library passes to Theophrastus, then to Neleus of Scepsis.
- **c. 100 BCE** — Apellicon recovers and restores degraded Aristotelian texts in Athens.
- **86 BCE** — Sulla seizes the library and transfers it to Rome.
- **1st century BCE** — Andronicus of Rhodes edits the first complete edition of Aristotle's works.
- **Early 2nd century CE** — Aspasius writes the earliest extant commentary on the *Ethics*.
- **12th century** — The work is rediscovered in the West, alongside commentaries by Averroes.
- **13th century** — Thomas Aquinas integrates Aristotelian ethics with Christian theology.
- **1958** — G.E.M. Anscombe's essay "Modern Moral Philosophy" sparks the modern virtue ethics revival.

## Body

### Title, Background, and Method
The title likely refers to Aristotle's son or father, both named Nicomachus. The work may be lecture notes rather than a polished publication. Parts overlap with Aristotle's *Eudemian Ethics* (Books V-VII are identical to *Eudemian* IV-VI), leading to debate about their relationship. The text's history is complex, passing through various libraries and editors before reaching its current form.

Aristotle's method is practical, not theoretical. He begins with the opinions of "people of good upbringing" and works toward a more rigorous understanding, acknowledging that ethics deals with "things that are so for the most part" and cannot demand mathematical exactness.

### The Highest Good: Eudaimonia
Aristotle argues all actions aim at some good, and the highest, most complete good for humans is *eudaimonia*—often translated as "happiness" or "flourishing." This is not a passive state but an activity (*energeia*) of the soul in accordance with virtue (*aretē*). He identifies the uniquely human function (*ergon*) as life guided by reason (*logos*). Therefore, *eudaimonia* is "the active exercise of his soul's faculties in conformity with excellence or virtue." It requires a complete lifetime and some external goods (e.g., friends, family).

### Moral Virtue and the Mean
Moral virtues (e.g., courage, temperance) are developed through habit and practice, not innate teaching. They are stable dispositions (*hexeis*) of character involving conscious choice. Each virtue is a "mean" (*mesótēs*) between two vices: a vice of excess and a vice of deficiency. For example, courage is the mean between rashness (excess) and cowardice (deficiency). The virtuous person chooses virtuous actions knowingly, for their own sake, and from a stable character.

### Specific Virtues and Justice
Aristotle details specific virtues. Courage is the mean regarding fear and confidence. Temperance (*sōphrosunē*) is the mean concerning bodily pleasures, especially those of touch and taste. Other virtues include generosity (with money), magnificence (with large-scale spending), and magnanimity ("greatness of soul"), which requires excellence in all virtues.

Justice (*dikaiosunē*) is a complete virtue concerning our relations with others. It is divided into distributive justice (fair allocation of goods) and rectificatory justice (correcting unfair transactions). Aristotle also discusses *epieikeia* (equity), a corrective to the rigidity of literal justice.

### Intellectual Virtues and Practical Wisdom
The rational soul has intellectual virtues. The most important for ethics is *phronesis* (practical wisdom or prudence), the ability to deliberate well about what is good and beneficial for oneself and for humans in general. It involves knowledge of both universal principles and particular situations. *Phronesis* is necessary to identify the virtuous mean and to translate knowledge into right action.

### Impediments to Virtue: Incontinence and Pleasure
Aristotle addresses *akrasia* (incontinence or weakness of will), where one acts against one's better judgment due to passion or desire. He reconciles this with Socrates' claim that no one does wrong willingly by suggesting the incontinent person's knowledge is temporarily overwhelmed by appetite. He distinguishes this from vice, where wrong action is chosen. Pleasure is not bad but accompanies virtuous activity; excessive bodily pleasure is the problem.

### Friendship (*Philia*)
Books VIII-IX are devoted to friendship, which is necessary for *eudaimonia*. Aristotle identifies three types: friendships of utility, of pleasure, and of virtue. The last is the perfect form, based on mutual recognition of and desire for the other's good character. Such friendships are rare, enduring, and require equality of virtue.

### Contemplation and the Highest Happiness
In Book X, Aristotle concludes the highest happiness is the activity of contemplation (*theōria*), which exercises the most divine part of us—the intellect (*nous*). This life is the most self-sufficient and pleasant. However, since humans are not gods, the life of practical virtue is also a form of happiness, secondary but necessary. He ends by stressing ethical theory must be put into practice through education and good laws, leading into his *Politics*.

## Terms
*   **Eudaimonia**: The highest human good, often translated as "happiness" or "flourishing"; an activity of the soul in accordance with virtue.
*   **Aretē**: Excellence or virtue; a stable disposition of character that enables a thing to perform its function well.
*   **Mesótēs**: The doctrine of the mean; virtue lies at a mean between two opposing vices of excess and deficiency.
*   **Hexis**: A stable disposition or state of character, like a virtue or a vice.
*   **Phronesis**: Practical wisdom; the intellectual virtue of deliberating well about action.
*   **Ergon**: Function or work; the characteristic activity of a being that defines its good.
*   **Energeia**: Activity or being-at-work; a complete, ongoing activity as opposed to a potentiality.
*   **Logos**: Reason, speech, or account; the uniquely human capacity for rational thought.
*   **Akrasia**: Incontinence or weakness of will; acting against one's better judgment.
*   **Philia**: Friendship or affection; a bond essential to human flourishing.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Relationship to the *Eudemian Ethics***: The cause of the identical books (V-VII of *NE* = IV-VI of *EE*) is debated—lost books, editorial compilation, or other explanations.
*   **Dating and Composition**: No consensus exists; the work may be a compilation of lecture notes from different periods.
*   **The Role of Contemplation**: The conclusion that the highest happiness is contemplative seems to conflict with the earlier emphasis on practical virtue and political life, creating a tension in the work's structure.
*   **The Scope of Justice**: How justice, described as a "complete virtue," relates to the other specific virtues discussed as "means" is not fully resolved.
*   **Modern Applicability**: The virtue ethics revival (e.g., Anscombe, MacIntyre) debates whether Aristotle's teleological framework can be updated for modern philosophy, which has largely abandoned his concept of a human *telos*.
