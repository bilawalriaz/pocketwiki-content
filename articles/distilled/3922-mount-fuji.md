# Mount Fuji

## Overview
Mount Fuji is an active stratovolcano and the highest mountain in Japan, standing at 3,776.24 m. It is a major cultural icon, a sacred site in Shinto, and a UNESCO World Heritage Site, renowned for its symmetrical cone and historical significance.

## Timeline
- **c. 100,000 years ago** — Formation of "Old Fuji" begins over older volcanic structures.
- **c. 10,000 years ago** — Formation of the modern "New Fuji" cone starts.
- **1707–1708** — The Hōei eruption, the last recorded eruption, forms a new crater and Mount Hōei peak.
- **1832** — Tatsu Takayama becomes the first woman on record to summit.
- **1860** — Sir Rutherford Alcock makes the first ascent by a foreigner.
- **1872** — Japanese government edict abolishes the ban on women climbing the mountain.
- **2004** — The staffed summit weather station closes after 72 years.
- **2013** — Mount Fuji is inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Cultural Site.
- **2024** — Yamanashi prefecture imposes a mandatory fee and daily hiker limit on the Yoshida trail.

## Body
### Etymology and Names
The origin of the name "Fuji" is debated. Theories link it to Japanese words for "immortal," "without equal," or "never-ending," or to the Ainu word for "fire" (though this is rejected by modern linguists). Other hypotheses suggest roots in Old Japanese for "fire master" or a connection to wisteria. In English, it is commonly called Mount Fuji or Fujiyama, while Japanese speakers use "Fuji-san," where "san" is the Sino-Japanese reading for "mountain," not an honorific.

### Geology and Formation
Mount Fuji sits at a triple junction of three tectonic plates, leading to volcanic activity. Its formation occurred in four phases: an ancient andesite core (Sen-komitake), a basalt layer (Komitake Fuji), "Old Fuji" (c. 100,000 years ago), and the modern "New Fuji" (c. 10,000 years ago). The last eruption was the Hōei eruption in 1707–1708. As of 2002, it was classified as active with a low eruption risk. Following the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake, some models suggested increased magma chamber pressure, but this remains speculative.

### Cultural and Religious Significance
Mount Fuji is one of Japan's "Three Holy Mountains." In Shinto, it is associated with the goddess Konohanasakuya-hime. Historically, it was a site for Shugendō worship and climbing pilgrimages. The Fuji-kō cult, founded in the Edo period, venerated the mountain as a female deity and encouraged ascents for spiritual rebirth. It has been a central subject in Japanese art, notably in Hokusai's woodblock prints.

### Access and Climbing
Approximately 300,000 people climbed Mount Fuji in 2009, primarily between July and August. There are four major routes to the summit, with the Yoshida route being the most popular due to its facilities. Climbers typically start at the fifth station and ascend overnight to view the sunrise (*goraikō*). Concerns over overtourism have led to new regulations, including a mandatory fee and daily hiker limits on the Yoshida trail starting in 2024.

## Terms
- **Stratovolcano**: A conical volcano built up by many layers of hardened lava and tephra.
- **Tectonic plates**: Large pieces of Earth's lithosphere whose movement causes geological activity.
- **Hōei eruption**: The last major eruption of Mount Fuji, occurring in 1707–1708.
- **Shugendō**: A syncretic Japanese religion blending mountain worship, Buddhism, and Shinto.
- **Fuji-kō**: An Edo-period religious cult centered on Mount Fuji.
- **Goraikō**: The Japanese term for the "arrival of light," referring to the sunrise viewed from the summit.
- **Yoshida route**: The most popular climbing path to the summit of Mount Fuji.
- **Aokigahara**: A forest at the northwest base of Mount Fuji, known for its folklore and high suicide rate.

## Debates and open questions
- The etymology of the name "Fuji" remains unresolved, with multiple competing linguistic hypotheses.
- The current eruptive risk is debated; while classified as low, post-2011 earthquake models suggesting increased magma pressure are considered speculative and unverifiable.
- The impact of overtourism and the effectiveness of new management measures like hiker limits and fees are ongoing concerns.
