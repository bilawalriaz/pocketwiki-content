# Kādambarī

## Overview
Kādambarī is a major Sanskrit romantic novel composed by Bāṇabhaṭṭa in the first half of the 7th century CE. It is celebrated for its intricate plot, nested narratives, and exploration of love and reincarnation. The work was left unfinished by Bāṇa and completed by his son, Bhūṣaṇabhaṭṭa, making it a significant example of the *kāvya* (ornate literary) tradition and a key text for understanding early Indian prose romance.

## Timeline
- **c. 606–647 CE** — Bāṇabhaṭta composes the *Pūrvabhāga* (first part) of *Kādambarī* during the reign of his patron, King Harṣavardhana.
- **7th century CE** — Bāṇa dies, leaving the novel incomplete.
- **7th century CE** — Bāṇa's son, Bhūṣaṇabhaṭṭa, completes the *Uttarabhāga* (latter part) according to his father's plan.
- **1896** — C. M. Ridding publishes an English translation.
- **1913** — P. V. Kane edits the *Uttarabhāga*.
- **1928** — M. R. Kale publishes a Sanskrit text with English translation.
- **1922** — The novel is adapted into the silent film *Mahashweta Kadambari*.
- **1944** — It is adapted into the Hindi film *Kadambari*.
- **2009** — David Smith publishes a translation in the Clay Sanskrit Library.
- **2010** — Padmini Rajappa publishes a Penguin translation.

## Body
### Authorship and Structure
The novel was substantially composed by Bāṇabhaṭṭa, who also wrote the biography *Harṣacarita*. His connection to King Harṣavardhana allows for a reliable dating of his life. The work is conventionally divided into two parts: the *Pūrvabhāga* by Bāṇa and the *Uttarabhāga* by his son. An alternate tradition names the son as Pulindabhaṭṭa. The narrative is framed within a succession of nested stories, beginning with a parrot recounting a tale told by the sage Jābali.

### Plot and Narrative Technique
The central plot concerns the love and eventual union between the hero Candrāpīḍa and the heroine Kādambarī. The story begins with King Śūdraka receiving a parrot, Vaśampāyana, who narrates the main tale. The narrative reveals that Candrāpīḍa is the reincarnation of the Moon God, and his friend Vaśampāyana is the reincarnation of the ascetic Pūṇḍarīka. Their love stories are intertwined across lifetimes, involving curses and divine interventions. The heroine, Kādambarī, does not appear until past the midpoint, and the plot features multiple subplots and characters appearing in various incarnations as humans, demigods, and animals.

### Themes and Source
The novel explores themes of love, desire, renunciation, and the consequences of curses. Its plot is likely adapted from the story of King Sumanas in Gunādhya's *Bṛhatkathā*, a lost work in the Paishācī language, which also appears in Somadeva's *Kathāsaritsāgara*. The intricate structure, with characters relating sub-stories to one another, is a hallmark of its sophisticated literary style.

## Terms
- **Kāvya**: A genre of ornate, classical Sanskrit literature characterized by elaborate style and poetic devices.
- **Pūrvabhāga**: The "earlier part" of the novel, written by Bāṇabhaṭṭa.
- **Uttarabhāga**: The "latter part" of the novel, completed by Bhūṣaṇabhaṭṭa.
- **Gandharva**: A class of demigods or celestial musicians in Hindu mythology.
- **Kinnara**: A race of demigods, often depicted as half-human, half-animal.
- **Chandala**: A term for a person from a specific social group, often associated with forest-dwellers or outcastes.
- **Veena**: A traditional Indian stringed instrument, a lute.
- **Pundarika**: A type of lotus; also the name of a character.
- **Harshacharita**: A biography of King Harṣavardhana written by Bāṇabhaṭṭa.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact identity of Bāṇa's son is debated, with traditions naming him as either Bhūṣaṇabhaṭṭa or Pulindabhaṭṭa.
- The degree to which the *Kādambarī* plot is adapted from the lost *Bṛhatkathā* is a matter of scholarly conjecture, as the source text is extinct.
- The narrative's complex structure and the delayed appearance of the heroine have led to discussions about its compositional unity and the relationship between the two parts.
