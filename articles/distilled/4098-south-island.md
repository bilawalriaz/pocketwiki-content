# South Island

## Overview
The South Island (Te Waipounamu) is New Zealand's largest island by area, home to 24% of the nation's population. It is defined by the dramatic Southern Alps, a resource-rich economy based on tourism and primary industries, and a history of Māori settlement, European colonisation, and gold rushes that shaped its demographic and cultural landscape.

## Timeline
- **c. 1288–1300 CE** — First Polynesian settlement at Wairau Bar.
- **1642** — Dutch explorer Abel Tasman makes first European contact.
- **1769–70** — British Captain James Cook charts the coastline.
- **1827–1839** — Musket Wars between Ngāi Tahu and Ngāti Toa.
- **1840** — Treaty of Waitangi signed; British sovereignty declared.
- **1843** — Wairau Affray, the only conflict of the New Zealand Wars in the South Island.
- **1861** — Gold discovered at Gabriel's Gully, sparking the Otago gold rush.
- **1864–1867** — West Coast gold rush.
- **1907** — "Middle Island" name officially discontinued in favour of "South Island".
- **1965** — HVDC Inter-Island link opens, enabling power transfer to the North Island.
- **2013** — "South Island" and "Te Waipounamu" officially gazetted as dual names.
- **2016** — Kaikōura earthquake causes major infrastructure damage.

## Body

### Naming and Identity
The island has long been known in English as the "South Island." Its Māori name, Te Waipounamu ("the waters of pounamu"), was officially gazetted as an alternative name in 2013. Historically, it was also called "Middle Island" or "New Munster." In Māori mythology, the island is sometimes Te Waka-a-Māui ("Māui's Canoe") or Te Waka o Aoraki, referencing the legend of a canoe that became the land.

### Early History and European Contact
Archaeological evidence at Wairau Bar indicates settlement from around 1288–1300 CE. The major iwi (tribes) of the pre-European era were Waitaha, Kāti Māmoe, and Ngāi Tahu, who gradually migrated south and absorbed the others. European contact began with Abel Tasman in 1642 and James Cook in 1769. From the 1780s, sealers and whalers established the first sustained contact. The 1820s–1830s saw the Musket Wars, with Ngāti Toa under Te Rauparaha attacking Ngāi Tahu settlements before peace was established by 1839.

### European Settlement and Gold Rushes
Permanent European settlement began in the 1820s. Following the Treaty of Waitangi (1840), organised settlements like Nelson, Otago, and Canterbury were established. The 1860s gold rushes in Otago and on the West Coast caused a population boom, attracting thousands of miners, including significant Chinese immigration. During this period, the South Island held the majority of New Zealand's European population and wealth. However, a subsequent "drift to the north" saw the North Island become the more populous region by the early 20th century.

### Geography and Geology
The island is dominated by the Southern Alps, a mountain range created by the collision of the Pacific and Indo-Australian tectonic plates along the Alpine Fault. This fault line, with rapid sideways and upward movement, causes frequent earthquakes. The landscape was heavily shaped by glaciation, creating features like Fiordland's 15 maritime fiords, glacial lakes, and the Canterbury Plains from eroded gravel. The climate varies from extremely wet on the West Coast to semi-arid in inland basins.

### Economy and Infrastructure
The economy relies on tourism, agriculture, and energy generation. The South Island produces most of New Zealand's hydroelectricity, with major schemes on the Waitaki, Clutha, and Manapouri rivers. Transport networks include state highways, railways like the TranzAlpine, and ferry services across Cook Strait. Christchurch is the main international gateway.

### Demographics and Culture
The population is 1,256,700 (June 2025), predominantly New Zealand European (Pākehā), with significant Māori and other ethnic communities. The island has a distinct cultural identity, reflected in art, literature (e.g., Booker Prize-winning novels *The Bone People* and *The Luminaries*), and a regional English accent known as the "Southland burr." Nine of New Zealand's thirteen national parks are located here, including the Te Wāhipounamu World Heritage Site.

## Terms
- **Iwi**: A Māori tribe or extended kinship group.
- **Pā**: A fortified Māori village or settlement.
- **Pounamu**: Greenstone or jade, a culturally significant material.
- **Alpine Fault**: A major geological fault line forming the boundary between the Pacific and Indo-Australian plates.
- **Orographic rainfall**: Precipitation caused by moist air being forced to rise over mountains.
- **Fiord**: A long, narrow, deep inlet of the sea between high cliffs, formed by glacial erosion. (Spelled "fiord" in New Zealand).
- **Loess**: A windblown sediment of silt and clay.
- **Tenure review**: A process under the Crown Pastoral Land Act 1998 to determine the future of high-country pastoral leases.
- **HVDC Inter-Island link**: A high-voltage direct current transmission cable connecting the North and South Islands' electricity grids.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Population Shift**: The long-term "drift to the north" saw the South Island's share of the national population decline from a majority to about 24%, though recent migration patterns (2018-2023) show a net inflow from the North Island.
- **High-Country Land Use**: The tenure review process for pastoral leases has been controversial, with debates over conservation versus freehold title and concerns about foreign ownership.
- **Independence Movements**: Periodic secessionist movements have advocated for a separate South Island colony or greater self-determination, though none have gained mainstream political traction.
