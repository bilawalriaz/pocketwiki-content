# Catherine de' Medici

## Overview
Catherine de' Medici (1519–1589) was Queen of France by marriage and, as mother to three successive kings, the de facto ruler during a period of intense civil and religious conflict. Her decades of influence, navigating the French Wars of Religion between Catholics and Huguenots, were marked by shifting policies of compromise and repression, and she is remembered for both her political tenacity and her role in the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre.

## Timeline
- **13 April 1519** — Born in Florence to Lorenzo de' Medici and Madeleine de La Tour d'Auvergne.
- **28 October 1519** — Marries Henry, Duke of Orléans (future Henry II), in Marseille.
- **10 June 1549** — Crowned Queen of France after Henry II's accession.
- **10 July 1559** — Henry II dies from a jousting accident; Catherine becomes queen mother.
- **5 December 1560** — Francis II dies; Catherine becomes regent for Charles IX.
- **1 March 1562** — Massacre of Vassy sparks the French Wars of Religion.
- **23 August 1572** — St. Bartholomew's Day massacre begins in Paris.
- **30 May 1574** — Charles IX dies; Henry III becomes king.
- **23 December 1588** — Henry III orders the assassination of the Duke of Guise.
- **5 January 1589** — Catherine dies at Blois.

## Body

### Early Life and Marriage
Catherine was the only child of Lorenzo de' Medici, Duke of Urbino, and died within a month of her birth. Raised in Florence, she was briefly held hostage during the 1527 overthrow of the Medici. In 1533, at age 14, she married Henry, second son of King Francis I, in a match arranged by her cousin, Pope Clement VII. The marriage was politically advantageous for the Medici but initially childless, and Henry openly favored his mistress, Diane de Poitiers. Catherine only secured her position after producing an heir in 1544, eventually bearing ten children.

### Queen Mother and Regent
Henry II's death in 1559 thrust Catherine into power. During the brief reign of her son Francis II, the powerful Guise family dominated the court. After Francis's death in 1560, Catherine became regent for the 10-year-old Charles IX, wielding sweeping powers. She initially pursued moderate policies, attempting to mediate between Catholics and Huguenots (Protestant Calvinists), but the Massacre of Vassy in 1562 ignited decades of civil war. Her authority was constantly challenged by the Guise faction and Huguenot leaders.

### The Wars of Religion and the St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre
Catherine's policies oscillated between compromise and repression as she struggled to control the factions. A turning point came after the Huguenot "Surprise of Meaux" in 1567, after which she abandoned conciliation. In 1572, she orchestrated the marriage of her daughter Margaret to the Huguenot leader Henry of Navarre to unite the factions. Following an assassination attempt on the Huguenot Admiral Coligny, Catherine and the king ordered a preemptive strike against Huguenot leaders gathered in Paris. This decision led to the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre, in which thousands were killed, permanently staining her reputation.

### Later Years and Death
Catherine continued as chief advisor to her third son, Henry III, but her influence waned as the Catholic League, led by the Guise family, gained power. In 1588, Henry III had the Duke of Guise assassinated, an act that horrified Catherine and further destabilized the kingdom. She died in January 1589, likely from pleurisy, and her son Henry III was assassinated seven months later, ending the Valois dynasty.

### Patron of the Arts
Catherine used lavish artistic patronage to bolster the prestige of the declining monarchy. She commissioned major architectural works, including the Tuileries Palace, and supported painters like Antoine Caron. Her court festivals helped develop the *ballet de cour*, a precursor to modern ballet. The legend that she introduced Italian cuisine and utensils to France is discredited by food historians.

## Terms
- **Huguenots:** French Protestants following the Calvinist tradition during the 16th and 17th centuries.
- **Guise Faction:** A powerful and militant Catholic noble family that opposed the monarchy and Huguenots, seeking to establish its own dynasty.
- **Regent:** A person appointed to administer a state because the monarch is a minor, absent, or incapacitated.
- **Edict of Saint-Germain (1562):** A decree issued by Catherine that granted limited toleration to Huguenots, which was soon violated.
- **St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre (1572):** A wave of Catholic mob violence against Huguenots in Paris and other cities, beginning on 24 August.
- **Catholic League:** A major political faction formed by ultra-Catholic nobles to combat Protestantism and influence the French crown.
- **Salic Law:** The French legal tradition that excluded women from inheriting the throne.
- **Ballet de cour:** A form of courtly entertainment combining dance, music, and poetry, which evolved into ballet.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Responsibility for the Massacre:** Historians debate the extent of Catherine's personal culpability in the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre, with some arguing she was the primary instigator and others suggesting she was reacting to a crisis beyond her control.
- **Effectiveness of Her Rule:** Scholars assess whether her policies were pragmatic attempts to preserve the Valois monarchy in impossible circumstances or whether her vacillation exacerbated the civil wars.
- **Legacy and Reputation:** Her historical image is contested, ranging from a "wicked Italian queen" of Protestant propaganda to a shrewd stateswoman who kept the state functioning during decades of anarchy.
