# Mongol conquests

## Overview
The Mongol conquests of the 13th and 14th centuries created the largest contiguous empire in history, stretching from the Pacific to Central Europe. Led initially by Genghis Khan, the Mongol military machine revolutionized steppe warfare, enabling rapid expansion that reshaped the political and demographic landscape of Eurasia. The empire's fragmentation after 1260 led to successor states that continued to influence regions for centuries.

## Timeline
- **c. 1162** — Birth of Temüjin (future Genghis Khan).
- **1206** — Temüjin unifies Mongol tribes and is proclaimed Genghis Khan, founding the Mongol Empire.
- **1209** — First full-scale Mongol invasion of Western Xia; it becomes a vassal state.
- **1211–1215** — Initial Mongol invasions of the Jin dynasty in northern China; capture of Zhongdu (Beijing) in 1215.
- **1218–1223** — Conquest of the Qara Khitai and the Khwarazmian Empire in Central Asia.
- **1226–1227** — Final Mongol conquest and destruction of Western Xia.
- **1237–1240** — Mongol invasion and devastation of Kievan Rus' and Volga Bulgaria.
- **1241** — Mongol victory at the Battle of Mohi in Hungary; death of Ögedei Khan leads to withdrawal from Europe.
- **1258** — Mongol sack of Baghdad, ending the Abbasid Caliphate.
- **1260** — Battle of Ain Jalut: Mamluks of Egypt inflict first major defeat on Mongols, halting their advance in the Middle East.
- **1271–1294** — Reign of Kublai Khan, founder of the Yuan dynasty in China.
- **1276** — Mongol capture of the Song dynasty capital, Lin'an (Hangzhou).

## Body

### Background and Unification
The Mongol tribe migrated to the Mongolian Plateau in the late 10th century. In the 12th century, they were overshadowed by rival tribes like the Naimans and Kerait. Temüjin, born c. 1162, rose to power through a series of alliances and conflicts. After defeating rivals like Jamukha and the Tatars, and subjugating the Kerait and Naimans, he unified the Mongol tribes. In 1206, he was proclaimed Genghis Khan, establishing the Mongol Empire.

### Military Organization and Tactics
The Mongol military overcame traditional nomadic weaknesses through strict discipline and organization. Genghis Khan reorganized soldiers into decimal units (tumens, mingghans, etc.) to break old tribal loyalties. Every adult male was a soldier, skilled in horse archery from childhood. They used composite bows with ranges up to 530 meters, and each soldier maintained 5-6 horses for mobility. Campaigns were meticulously planned, often starting in winter, and used tactics like feigned retreats, encirclement, and psychological warfare. Initially poor at siege warfare, they incorporated foreign engineers to master trebuchets, sapping, and other techniques, enabling the conquest of fortified cities.

### Conquest of China
The Mongols first targeted the Western Xia state in 1209, forcing it into vassalage. After the Xia later rebelled, Genghis Khan destroyed the state in 1226–27. The larger Jin dynasty in northern China was invaded from 1211. Despite being outnumbered, Mongol armies plundered the countryside, captured key passes, and besieged the capital, Zhongdu, which fell in 1215. After Genghis's death, his successors completed the conquest. The Southern Song dynasty fell to Kublai Khan's forces in 1276, with final resistance ending in 1279, establishing the Yuan dynasty.

### Expansion into Central Asia and the Middle East
Genghis Khan's conquests destroyed the Qara Khitai and the Khwarazmian Empire, depopulating large areas of Central Asia. His successors continued westward. The Mongols invaded and destroyed Volga Bulgaria and Kievan Rus' (1237–40), and later invaded Poland and Hungary. In the Middle East, Hulagu Khan sacked Baghdad in 1258, ending the Abbasid Caliphate. The Mongol advance was halted at the Battle of Ain Jalut in 1260 by the Egyptian Mamluks, marking their first major military defeat.

### Other Regions
Mongol invasions reached into South Asia, occupying parts of the northwest but failing to conquer Delhi. In Southeast Asia, the Yuan dynasty invaded Burma (1277–87) and Vietnam, but failed to hold territory due to local resistance and environment. The Mongols also conquered Tibet, Sakhalin, and made Korea (Goryeo) a vassal state. In North Asia, they subjugated Siberian tribes, organizing them into administrative units.

## Terms
- **Kurultai**: A traditional Mongol assembly of nobles to elect a khan and plan campaigns.
- **Tumen**: A decimal military unit of 10,000 soldiers.
- **Composite Bow**: A powerful, recurved bow made of wood, horn, and sinew, central to Mongol warfare.
- **Keshig**: The Mongol imperial guard, which also served as a training academy for officers.
- **Feigned Retreat**: A tactic where forces pretend to flee to lure an enemy into a trap.
- **Vassal State**: A state that submits to a superior power in exchange for retaining some autonomy.
- **Decimal System**: A military organization method dividing soldiers into units of 10, 100, 1,000, and 10,000.
- **Yuan Dynasty**: The Mongol-led dynasty that ruled China from 1271 to 1368.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact reasons for Genghis Khan's escalation from raiding to full-scale invasion of Western Xia in 1209 are unclear.
- The total Mongol manpower during Genghis Khan's lifetime is debated, with estimates between 50,000 and 75,000 soldiers.
- The function of the *qara'ul/qaraghul* military formation remains uncertain.
- The role of Mongol shamans in influencing battles through weather is recorded in accounts but is subject to scholarly interpretation.
