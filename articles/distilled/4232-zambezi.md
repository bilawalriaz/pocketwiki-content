# Zambezi

## Overview
The Zambezi is Africa's fourth-longest river and its largest east-flowing river, draining a 1.39 million km² basin across six countries before emptying into the Indian Ocean. It is vital for regional hydroelectric power, agriculture, and biodiversity, but its ecology has been significantly altered by major dams and faces ongoing environmental pressures.

## Timeline
- **c. Late Pliocene/Pleistocene (>2 million years ago)** — Upper Zambezi originally flowed south to the Limpopo; later captured by the east-flowing middle Zambezi, creating the first Victoria Falls.
- **January 1498** — Vasco da Gama becomes the first European to encounter the river, naming a delta channel "Rio dos Bons Sinais."
- **1511 & 1513** — Portuguese explorer António Fernandes makes the first recorded inland visits to the Zambezi region.
- **1851–1853** — David Livingstone conducts the first recorded European exploration of the upper Zambezi.
- **1855** — Livingstone discovers Victoria Falls.
- **1858–1860** — Livingstone and John Kirk ascend the river to the falls and trace the Shire River to Lake Malawi.
- **1884** — Frederick Stanley Arnot identifies the source of the Zambezi.
- **1905** — Victoria Falls Bridge is completed.
- **1959** — Kariba Dam is completed, creating one of the world's largest man-made lakes.
- **1974** — Cahora Bassa Dam is completed in Mozambique.
- **2004** — Katima Mulilo Bridge opens, completing the Trans-Caprivi Highway.
- **2021** — Kazungula Bridge opens, connecting Zambia and Botswana.

## Body

### Course and Geography
The river rises in a marshy dambo in Zambia at ~1,524 m elevation. It flows southwest into Angola, then south back into Zambia, where it develops the vast Barotse Floodplain. After receiving major tributaries like the Kabompo and Kafue, it plunges over Victoria Falls, the boundary between the upper and middle Zambezi. The middle section flows through the Batoka Gorge and into Lake Kariba. Below the Luangwa River confluence, it enters Mozambique, passes through Lake Cahora Bassa, and flows 650 km to its delta. The delta, now half its former width due to dams, splits into several distributaries, with the Chinde channel used for navigation.

### Hydrology and Dams
The river's discharge is dominated by northern tributaries, which contribute about 65% of its flow. Before major dams, it had a pronounced flood regime with a mean April maximum of 6,700 m³/s at the delta. The Kariba (1959) and Cahora Bassa (1974) dams have drastically altered this pattern, reducing seasonal flooding and trapping sediment. This has shrunk the Zambezi Delta, disrupted fish and wildlife breeding, and reduced prawn catches. The Cahora Bassa Dam's rapid filling in one season caused a 40% reduction in mangrove coverage and increased coastal erosion.

### Ecology and Wildlife
The Zambezi supports abundant wildlife, including hippopotamuses, Nile crocodiles, and diverse birdlife. Its floodplains and riverine woodlands are habitats for buffalo, elephants, and other large mammals. The river hosts hundreds of fish species, including cichlids, catfish, and tigerfish. The Zambezi Delta's seasonally flooded grasslands and mangroves form a critical ecoregion. However, dam-regulated flows, pollution from untreated sewage, and overfishing threaten these ecosystems. Epizootic ulcerative syndrome (EUS) caused a major fish kill in 2007.

### History and Etymology
The name "Zambezi" likely derives from the Bisa people, a trading group living near its source. Early Portuguese records called it the "Cuama River." David Livingstone recorded the local Lozi name "Leeambye," meaning "large river." Exploration was slow after Livingstone's expeditions, with the source not identified until 1884. The river's course was known to medieval geographers via Arab information, linked to the legendary Empire of Monomotapa.

### Economy and Transport
The river valley's population (~32 million) is largely dependent on agriculture and fishing. The dams provide hydroelectric power to Zambia, Zimbabwe, Mozambique, and South Africa. Tourism is significant, especially at Victoria Falls and Lake Kariba. The Zambezi has never been a major long-distance transport route due to rapids, but numerous bridges now cross it, including the historic Victoria Falls Bridge (1905) and the modern Kazungula Bridge (2021).

## Terms
- **Dambo**: A marshy, seasonally waterlogged grassland depression common in central Africa.
- **Miombo woodland**: A type of tropical and subtropical grasslands, savannas, and shrublands biome characterized by trees of the genera *Brachystegia* and *Julbernardia*.
- **Floodplain**: A flat area of land next to a river that experiences seasonal flooding.
- **Epeirogenic movements**: Large-scale, slow vertical movements of the Earth's crust that uplift or depress broad areas.
- **Graben**: A depressed block of the Earth's crust bordered by parallel faults, forming a valley.
- **Eutrophication**: Excessive richness of nutrients in a body of water, frequently due to runoff from the land, which causes a dense growth of plant life and death of animal life from lack of oxygen.
- **Transfrontier Conservation Area**: A protected area that spans the boundaries of two or more countries.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise etymology of "Zambezi" is debated, with theories linking it to the Bisa people or the Bantu word for "fish."
- The ecological and social impacts of the Kariba and Cahora Bassa dams, particularly regarding altered flood regimes and sediment flow, remain subjects of ongoing study and management challenges.
- The effectiveness of transboundary conservation initiatives like the Kavango–Zambezi Transfrontier Conservation Area is contingent on cooperation among riparian states and overcoming obstacles like landmine contamination.
