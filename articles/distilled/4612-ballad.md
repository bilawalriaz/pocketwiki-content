# Ballad

## Overview
A ballad is a narrative verse form, often set to music, that was a dominant form of popular poetry and song in England, Ireland, and Scotland from the Late Middle Ages through the 19th century. Its evolution from oral folk tradition to printed broadside and literary form reflects broader social and technological changes, and its structure and themes have influenced music globally.

## Timeline
- **13th c.** — Earliest recognizable English ballad, "Judas," appears in a manuscript.
- **Late 14th c.** — Ballads about Robin Hood are being sung, as indicated by William Langland's *Piers Plowman*.
- **c. 1495** — Wynkyn de Worde prints the oldest detailed Robin Hood ballad collection.
- **16th c.** — Development of cheap print leads to the mass production of broadside ballads.
- **1728** — John Gay's *The Beggar's Opera*, the first and most successful ballad opera, is performed.
- **1798** — William Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge publish *Lyrical Ballads*.
- **1802-03** — Walter Scott publishes *Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border*.
- **Late 19th c.** — Francis James Child publishes *The English and Scottish Popular Ballads*, classifying 305 ballads.
- **1879-80** — The Victorian magazine *The Pearl* publishes bawdy pornographic ballads.
- **1928** — Kurt Weill and Bertolt Brecht's *The Threepenny Opera*, a reworking of *The Beggar's Opera*, debuts.

## Body

### Origins and Form
Ballads derive from the medieval French *chanson balladée* ("dancing songs") and were influenced by Scandinavian storytelling traditions and German Minnelieder. The earliest English example is "Judas" (13th century). While originally couplets with refrains for dance, the classic form became the **ballad stanza**: a quatrain (four-line stanza) with alternating iambic tetrameter (8 syllables) and trimeter (6 syllables), typically with an ABCB or ABAB rhyme scheme. However, there is considerable variation in length, lines, and rhyme, making a strict definition difficult. Southern and Eastern European forms, like Spanish *romances*, differ significantly. Ballads were passed down orally, with no known author or single correct version, until 18th-century collectors like Bishop Thomas Percy began publishing them.

### Composition and Transmission
Scholars debate ballad origins. **Communalists** (e.g., Brothers Grimm) argue ballads are collective compositions, while **individualists** (e.g., Cecil Sharp) posit a single original author. Modern scholarship notes an interchange between oral and written forms. Transmission was a process of re-composition; Romantic figures like Walter Scott saw it as a potential "degeneration" from a pure original, requiring editorial "correction."

### Classification and Major Types
European ballads are classified into three major groups:
1.  **Traditional Ballads:** The oldest form, originating with medieval minstrels. They deal with themes like the supernatural, tragedy, love, and legend (e.g., Robin Hood). Key collectors include Francis James Child (England/Scotland) and Svend Grundtvig (Denmark).
2.  **Broadside Ballads:** Printed on single sheets from the 16th century, they were a cheap, mass-market product covering news, love, religion, and satire. They were sold in huge numbers, with over 400,000 sold annually in England by the 1660s.
3.  **Literary Ballads:** Composed by educated poets (e.g., Wordsworth, Coleridge, Keats, Goethe) from the late 18th century, who imitated the folk style. Later examples include works by Rudyard Kipling and Oscar Wilde.

### Ballad Opera and Global Spread
In the 18th century, **ballad operas** emerged as English stage entertainment using popular folk tunes and satirical, lower-class subjects, exemplified by *The Beggar's Opera* (1728). This form influenced later light opera and musicals. The ballad spread globally: **American ballads** include both European-derived songs and native narratives; **blues ballads** fused Anglo-American and Afro-American styles; and **bush ballads** became a distinct Australian form focused on outback life, with "Waltzing Matilda" as its most famous example.

### Modern Evolution
In the late 19th century, the term "ballad" began to mean a slow, sentimental love song, originating in the Tin Pan Alley music industry. This modern usage now encompasses "pop ballads," "rock ballads," and "power ballads" in contemporary music.

## Terms
- **Ballad Stanza:** A four-line stanza (quatrain) with alternating iambic tetrameter and trimeter lines, typically rhyming the second and fourth lines.
- **Iambic Tetrameter:** A metrical line of four iambic feet (an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed syllable), totaling eight syllables.
- **Iambic Trimeter:** A metrical line of three iambic feet, totaling six syllables.
- **Broadside Ballad:** A ballad printed on a large, single sheet of paper, sold cheaply from the 16th century onward.
- **Chapbook:** A small, cheap book made from folded and cut broadside sheets.
- **Communalism:** The theory that ballads originated as collective, anonymous compositions of a folk community.
- **Individualism:** The theory that ballads originated from a single, identifiable author.
- **Ballad Opera:** An 18th-century English stage genre combining spoken dialogue with short songs set to popular folk tunes.
- **Bush Ballad:** An Australian ballad form dealing with life in the rural outback.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Communal vs. Individual Origin:** The central scholarly debate is whether traditional ballads originated from communal folk composition or from individual authors, with variants seen as either natural evolution or corruption.
- **Classification:** There have been "many different and contradictory attempts to classify traditional ballads by theme," indicating no universally accepted system exists.
- **Transmission and Purity:** The process of oral transmission is debated—whether it represents a "degeneration" from an original pure form (as argued by Walter Scott) or a dynamic process of re-composition and interchange with written forms.
