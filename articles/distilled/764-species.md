# Species

## Overview
A species is the fundamental unit of biological classification and biodiversity, traditionally defined as a group of organisms capable of interbreeding and producing fertile offspring. However, this and other definitions face persistent challenges due to hybridization, asexual reproduction, and evolutionary change, creating the "species problem"—the difficulty of finding a universal definition. Despite theoretical difficulties, the concept remains essential for taxonomy, conservation, and scientific communication, with over 8 million eukaryotic species estimated to exist, only about 14% described by 2011.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC**: Aristotle distinguishes γένος (kind) and εἶδος (form), influencing later taxonomy
- **1686**: John Ray proposes first biological species definition based on seed propagation
- **1735–1758**: Carl Linnaeus establishes binomial nomenclature and taxonomic hierarchy
- **1809**: Lamarck describes transmutation of species in *Zoological Philosophy*
- **1858**: Darwin and Wallace present evolution by natural selection
- **1859**: Darwin publishes *On the Origin of Species*
- **1913**: Pseudomonas avellanae species categorized using genomic algorithms
- **1942**: Ernst Mayr formulates biological species concept emphasizing reproductive isolation
- **1951**: George Gaylord Simpson proposes evolutionary species concept
- **2006**: 16S rRNA similarity threshold narrowed to 98.7% for bacterial species
- **2013**: Average nucleotide identity (ANI) method applied to bacterial taxonomy
- **2020**: Whole-genome ANI used to categorize all sequenced bacteria and archaea

## Body

### Definition and Concepts
Biologists have developed numerous species concepts, with R.L. Mayden recording ~24 and John Wilkins identifying 26, grouped into seven kinds: agamospecies (asexual), biospecies (reproductively isolated sexual), ecospecies (ecological niches), evolutionary species (lineage-based), genetic species (gene pool), morphospecies (phenotype), and taxonomic species (taxonomist-determined). The biological species concept (BSC), Mayr's 1942 definition, dominates textbooks but struggles with asexual organisms, fossils, and hybridization.

### Typological and Morphological Approaches
Early taxonomists like Linnaeus relied on morphology, defining typological species by fixed properties. Modern field guides and iNaturalist still use this approach. Sokal, Crovello, and Sneath (1970s) introduced phenetic species, using numerical similarity measures across multiple traits. However, phenotypic variation doesn't always indicate species boundaries—for example, a four-winged Drosophila from a two-winged mother isn't a different species.

### Genetic and Molecular Methods
DNA barcoding uses single loci (e.g., mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase for eukaryotes, 16S rRNA for bacteria) for species identification. The Barcode of Life Data System contains sequences from over 190,000 species. Multilocus comparison improves resolution over single-locus methods, while whole-genome comparison via average nucleotide identity (ANI) provides definitive results. An "ANI gap" at 85–95% suggests a genetic boundary for prokaryotic species.

### Phylogenetic and Cladistic Species
Cladistic species are "the smallest aggregation of populations diagnosable by unique character states." This approach applies to both sexual and asexual organisms but can lead to taxonomic inflation—splitting existing species based on minor differences. Critics argue this dilutes conservation resources, while defenders claim it reflects true evolutionary diversity.

### Evolutionary and Ecological Concepts
Simpson's 1951 evolutionary species concept defines entities maintaining identity through time with independent evolutionary fates. Ecological species are adapted to specific niches, forming discrete clusters due to resource partitioning. Genetic species emphasize genetic isolation over reproductive isolation, established through DNA sequencing, karyotypes, or allozymes.

### Special Cases and Challenges
**Chronospecies**: Paleontologists use morphology and histology to identify sequential species through anagenesis (gradual evolution without branching). **Viral quasispecies**: Rapidly mutating viruses form genotype groups governed by mutation-selection balance, inapplicable to traditional species concepts. **Microspecies**: Organisms with minimal genetic variation form complexes (e.g., dandelions with 200+ microspecies in Britain). **Ring species**: Adjacent populations interbreed, but distant ends cannot, challenging reproductive isolation concepts.

### The Species Problem
Darwin noted the "vague and arbitrary" distinction between species and varieties. Modern challenges include: asexual organisms (bacteria, parthenogens), extinct forms (no breeding tests), hybridization (fertile hybrids permit gene flow), and horizontal gene transfer (HGT) disrupting vertical inheritance. Discordance between molecular and morphological data creates two categories: one morphology/multiple lineages (cryptic species) and one lineage/multiple morphologies (phenotypic plasticity).

### Taxonomy and Naming
Scientific names use binomial nomenclature (genus + specific epithet). Type specimens anchor descriptions in permanent repositories. Abbreviations include "sp." (singular), "spp." (plural), "cf." (confirmation needed), "nr." (near), and "aff." (affine). Online databases provide identifiers: NCBI taxid, KEGG codes, UniProt mnemonics, ITIS numbers, and LSIDs.

### Lumping, Splitting, and Circumscription
Taxonomists are "lumpers" (broad species concepts) or "splitters" (narrow concepts). Synonymy occurs when multiple names apply to one species—the older name takes priority. Species boundaries (circumscription) aren't governed by nomenclature codes, though the PhyloCode differs. Terms "sensu stricto" (narrow sense) and "sensus lato" (broad sense) qualify usage.

### Speciation and Change
Speciation occurs through reproductive isolation, most easily via allopatric speciation (geographic separation). The Bateson-Dobzhansky-Muller model explains hybrid incompatibility selection. Phyletic speciation involves gradual transformation without branching. Horizontal gene transfer enables gene exchange between species, particularly in bacteria via plasmid exchange. Extinction affects over 99% of Earth's species, with five major mass extinction events.

### Practical Implications
Species identification difficulties threaten research validity. Phylogenetic concepts report 48% more species than nonphylogenetic approaches, causing "taxonomic inflation." Conservation laws create conflicts—e.g., northern spotted owl hybridization with unprotected species. Alternative biodiversity measures like phylogenetic diversity have been proposed since species counts may not reflect evolutionary distinctness.

## Terms
- **Binomial nomenclature**: Two-part naming system (genus + specific epithet)
- **Chronospecies**: Fossil species identified through sequential morphological change
- **Horizontal gene transfer (HGT)**: Gene exchange between unrelated organisms
- **Phenetic species**: Groups defined by overall phenotypic similarity
- **Quasispecies**: Mutant viral genotype clouds governed by mutation-selection balance
- **Reproductive isolation**: Mechanism preventing interbreeding between species
- **Sensu stricto/lato**: Taxonomic qualifiers meaning "in the narrow/broad sense"
- **Speciation**: Process by which populations become distinct species
- **Taxonomic inflation**: Over-splitting of species leading to increased counts
- **Type specimen**: Reference specimen anchoring a species description

## Debates and Open Questions
The fundamental species problem remains unresolved: no single concept applies universally. Key debates include whether to prioritize reproductive isolation (BSC) or diagnosable differences (cladistic), how to handle asexual organisms and HGT, and whether "taxonomic inflation" from splitting practices genuinely reflects biodiversity or creates conservation challenges. The "cynical species concept" suggests species are whatever qualified biologists designate, raising questions about objectivity versus utility in taxonomy.
