# Merovingian dynasty

## Overview
The Merovingian dynasty was the ruling family of the Franks from the mid-5th century until 751. They were the first major Germanic dynasty to rule a unified kingdom in Western Europe after the fall of the Roman Empire, establishing a realm that encompassed most of Gaul and parts of Germania. Their rule laid the political and territorial foundations for what would later become France and Germany.

## Timeline
- **c. 450s** — Merovingians first appear as "Kings of the Franks" in the Roman army of northern Gaul.
- **481** — Death of Childeric I, the first well-known Merovingian king.
- **486** — Clovis I defeats Syagrius, a Roman military leader, in the Franco-Roman War.
- **496** — Clovis I converts to Nicene Christianity.
- **507** — Clovis I defeats the Visigoths at the Battle of Vouillé.
- **509** — The Franks and northern Gallo-Romans are united under Merovingian rule.
- **511** — Death of Clovis I; his kingdom is divided among his four sons.
- **534** — The Franks conquer the Burgundians.
- **613** — Clotaire II reunites the entire Frankish realm under one ruler.
- **687** — Pepin of Herstal (Pepin the Middle) triumphs at the Battle of Tertry, becoming the political ruler of the Frankish kingdom.
- **732** — Charles Martel leads the Franks to victory against the Moors at the Battle of Tours.
- **751** — Pepin the Short deposes the last Merovingian king, Childeric III, initiating the Carolingian dynasty.

## Body

### Origins and Identity
The dynasty's name derives from the Frankish King Merovech, father of Childeric I. Unlike some other Germanic royal lines, the Merovingians did not claim divine descent. Their kings were distinguished by their long hair; a Merovingian whose hair was cut could not rule, and rivals could be removed from succession by being tonsured and sent to a monastery.

### Expansion and Division
Under Clovis I, the Merovingians united the Franks, converted to Nicene Christianity, and conquered most of Gaul. The kingdom was treated as a single entity divisible among heirs. After Clovis's death in 511, it was frequently divided among his sons and grandsons, leading to internal warfare. The main divisions were Austrasia, Neustria, Burgundy, and Aquitaine. Despite divisions, the realm was conceived as a single entity, and periods of reunification occurred, most notably under Clotaire II in 613.

### Weakening Royal Power and the Rise of Mayors
From the 7th century, royal power weakened while the aristocracy gained strength. The king's chief official, the mayor of the palace, increasingly wielded actual power. Kings became ceremonial figures, later termed *rois fainéants* ("do-nothing kings"). In 687, Pepin of Herstal, Mayor of Austrasia, became the de facto ruler after the Battle of Tertry. His son, Charles Martel, continued this trend, ruling without a king in his final years.

### Government and Law
The Merovingian king redistributed conquered wealth and land among his followers. The kingdom was a form of patrimony, divided equally among heirs upon a king's death. Counts (*comites*) were appointed for defense and administration. Law was not universal; it was applied based on a person's ethnic origin, such as the *Lex Salica* for Salian Franks. Legal officials called *rachimburgs* memorized legal precedents, as creating new law was not part of the tradition.

### Religion and Culture
Christianity, introduced through contact with Gallo-Roman culture, was pragmatically used by the dynasty. Monasteries and bishoprics were awarded to supporters, and family members were appointed as abbots. The period produced hagiographies (saints' lives) that defined sanctity and controlled popular devotion. The Frankish language was likely forgotten rapidly, with Vulgar Latin remaining the spoken language in most regions.

### Historiography and Sources
Key contemporary sources include Gregory of Tours' *Decem Libri Historiarum* (covering to 594) and the *Chronicle of Fredegar* (covering 584–641, extended to 768). The *Liber Historiae Francorum* is another important narrative source. Archaeological evidence, such as the 1653 discovery of Childeric I's tomb, provides crucial information about material culture.

## Terms
- **Merovingian**: The ruling dynasty of the Franks from c. 450 to 751, named after King Merovech.
- **Nicene Christianity**: The mainstream form of Christianity that adheres to the Nicene Creed, as opposed to Arian Christianity favored by other Germanic tribes.
- **Mayor of the Palace**: The highest-ranking official under the Merovingian king, who increasingly held actual political power.
- **Rois fainéants**: French term meaning "do-nothing kings," used to describe the later, ceremonial Merovingian monarchs.
- **Lex Salica (Salic Law)**: The legal code of the Salian Franks, first codified c. 511.
- **Rachimburgs**: Merovingian legal officials who memorized and applied legal precedents.
- **Fisc**: The royal domain, whose income was expected to support the king.
- **Hagiography**: Literature concerning the lives of saints, a major form of Merovingian writing.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The legendary origin of the Merovingians from a sea-beast (quinotaur) is debated. It is now commonly seen as an etymological explanation for the name Merovech ("sea-bull") rather than evidence of a claimed supernatural lineage.
- The extent and nature of the transition from spoken Frankish to Vulgar Latin in Merovingian Gaul is debated, with some evidence suggesting Germanic was used as a second language by officials into the 9th century.
- The characterization of later Merovingians as entirely powerless *rois fainéants* is an oversimplification, as only the last two kings were truly inactive.
