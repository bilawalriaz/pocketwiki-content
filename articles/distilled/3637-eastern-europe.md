# Eastern Europe

## Overview
Eastern Europe is a subregion of the European continent defined by a complex and often debated mix of geopolitical, geographical, cultural, and historical factors. Its significance lies in its role as a cultural crossroads, shaped by traditions of the Slavs and Eastern Christianity, and as a geopolitical entity, most notably defined during the Cold War by the Iron Curtain. The term itself is considered "fuzzy" and sometimes pejorative, with its boundaries shifting over time.

## Timeline
- **1054** — The East–West Schism formally divides Eastern Orthodox and Western Catholic Christianity.
- **15th c.** — The Ottoman Empire conquers the Byzantine Empire, the center of Eastern Orthodoxy.
- **1861** — Serfdom is abolished in Russia with the Emancipation reform.
- **1919** — The Treaty of Versailles validates the creation of new nation-states in Eastern Europe after WWI.
- **1947–1991** — The Cold War period, during which "Eastern Europe" is synonymous with the Soviet-dominated Eastern Bloc.
- **1989** — The fall of the Iron Curtain begins the end of communist rule in the region.
- **1990** — German reunification absorbs the German Democratic Republic (East Germany).
- **1991** — The Soviet Union, COMECON, and the Warsaw Pact are dissolved.
- **1993** — Czechoslovakia peacefully separates into the Czech Republic and Slovakia.
- **2004–2013** — Multiple Eastern European countries, from Poland to Croatia, join the European Union.

## Body

### Definitions and Boundaries
The definition of Eastern Europe is imprecise and debated. Geographically, its eastern boundary is the Ural Mountains, Ural River, and Caucasus Mountains. The western boundary is historical and cultural, not geographical. Narrow definitions include only Belarus, Russia, and Ukraine, while broader ones add Moldova, Romania, the Balkans, the Baltic states, and the Caucasus. The term gained a clear geopolitical meaning during the Cold War (1947–1991), when it referred to the communist states of the Eastern Bloc under Soviet influence.

### Cultural and Religious Influences
A major cultural unifier for much of the region is Eastern Orthodoxy, which spread after the 1054 schism. Countries with dominant Orthodox churches include Belarus, Bulgaria, Georgia, Greece, Moldova, Romania, Russia, Serbia, and Ukraine. However, this is not a strict rule; Greece is overwhelmingly Orthodox but is rarely classified as Eastern Europe due to stronger Mediterranean influences. Other significant religious influences include Eastern Catholicism and Eastern Protestantism.

### Historical Development
The region's history includes ancient kingdoms later incorporated into Persian empires. A key early division was the cultural and linguistic split between the Greek-speaking east and Latin-speaking west of the Roman Empire. The medieval period saw Mongol invasions and the Ostsiedlung, a process of German settlement and economic development. Serfdom was prevalent until the 19th century, peaking in the 17th-18th centuries. Industrialization lagged behind Western Europe until after 1870.

### 20th Century to Present
World War I led to the breakup of empires and the creation of new states like Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Yugoslavia. World War II devastated the region as a major battlefield. After 1945, Soviet control imposed communist governments, forming the Eastern Bloc. The Cold War ended with the 1989 revolutions. Since then, many nations have transitioned to democracy and market economies, with several joining the European Union. The post-1989 period involved constitutional reforms and economic stabilization, though challenges like high debt persisted in some countries.

## Terms
- **Iron Curtain**: The political, military, and ideological barrier erected by the Soviet Union after WWII to seal off itself and its dependent eastern and central European allies from open contact with the West.
- **Eastern Bloc**: The group of communist states in Central and Eastern Europe, generally aligned with and under the military and political control of the Soviet Union during the Cold War.
- **East–West Schism**: The break of communion and theology between the Eastern (Orthodox) and Western (Catholic) Christian churches in 1054.
- **Ostsiedlung**: The medieval process of German settlement and colonization in parts of Central and Eastern Europe.
- **Serfdom**: A status of agricultural workers in the region, resembling slavery, where peasants were bound to the land owned by a lord.
- **Visegrád Group**: A cultural and political alliance of four Central European countries: the Czech Republic, Hungary, Poland, and Slovakia.
- **EuroVoc**: A multilingual thesaurus maintained by the EU that classifies countries, including a "Central and Eastern Europe" category.
- **Comecon**: The Council for Mutual Economic Assistance, an economic organization of communist states aligned with the Soviet Union.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise geographical and cultural boundaries of Eastern Europe are constantly debated and lack consensus among scholars.
- The term is sometimes considered pejorative due to stereotypes of inferiority compared to Western Europe, leading to the use of alternatives like "Central and Eastern Europe."
- The classification of specific countries is contested; for example, the Baltic states are placed in Northern Europe by some organizations and Eastern Europe by others, while Greece is rarely included despite its Orthodox heritage.
- The status of the South Caucasus nations (Armenia, Azerbaijan, Georgia) is ambiguous, as they are geographically in Western Asia but often included in definitions of Eastern Europe due to political and cultural ties.
