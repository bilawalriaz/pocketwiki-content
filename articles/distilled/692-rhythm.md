# Rhythm

## Overview
Rhythm is the regulated succession of strong and weak elements in time, derived from the Greek *rhythmos* ("regular recurring motion"). It structures cyclical phenomena across timescales from microseconds to years. In the performing arts, rhythm organizes musical sounds and silences, dance steps, and poetic meter; it also appears in visual patterns such as architectural façades. Anthropologically, the human capacity for *entrainment*—synchronizing vocalizations and movements to a shared pulse—is unique among primates and may have evolved to foster collective identity (e.g., battle trance, ritual). Cognitively, rhythm perception depends on a hierarchy of pulses (beat, division, multiple levels) and the memorization of repeating patterns short enough to anticipate. The interaction of rhythm and tempo is context-dependent: the brain prefers the simplest representation of temporal data (Kolmogorov complexity), so melodic redundancy allows rhythmic recognition despite tempo fluctuations.

## Timeline
- **Early hominid evolution** — Development of rhythm sense and entrainment proposed as adaptation for collective defense (battle trance).
- **1927** — "Counter rhythm" first recognized in OED as "a subordinate rhythm acting as a counterbalance to the main rhythm."
- **1930s** — Henry Cowell composes multi-periodic rhythms; collaborates with Leon Theremin to invent the rhythmicon (first electronic rhythm machine).
- **1976** — Maury Yeston defines "attack point rhythm" (composite rhythm) as the absolute surface of articulated movement.
- **20th century** — Western composers (Stravinsky, Bartók, Glass, Reich, Messiaen, Cage, Nancarrow, Young) expand rhythmic complexity: odd meters, phasing, additive rhythm, irrational rhythms, drones, player-piano studies.
- **Late 20th c.** — Scholars (Yeston, Lerdahl, Jackendoff, Toussaint, Rothstein, Lester, Mazzola, Krebber) establish rhythm and meter as a major research area.

## Body

### Definitions and distinctions
Rhythm is the grouping of unaccented beats around an accented one; it requires distinction between elements (strong/weak, long/short, sound/silence). It is distinguished from **pulse** (the underlying series of identical periodic stimuli), **meter** (the hierarchical organization of pulses into measures), and **beat** (the tactus, or perceived fundamental pulse to which listeners entrain). A **rhythmic unit** aligns with the metric level (metric, intrametric, contrametric/syncopated, extrametric); a **rhythmic gesture** does not occupy a full pulse period and is classified by its beginning (thetic, anacrustic, initial rest) and ending (strong, weak, upbeat).

### Temporal hierarchy and tempo
Musical time spans five scales (Moravcsik): *supershort* (single wave cycle, 30–10,000 Hz, perceived as pitch); *short* (~1 second, 40–240 bpm, the beat range—faster becomes a drone, slower seems unconnected); *medium* (few seconds, defines rhythmic units, motifs, phrases); *long* (many seconds to minutes, phrases into melodies/sections); *very long* (minutes to hours, whole compositions). Tempo (beats per minute) is inversely related to rhythmic-unit duration. Roads’ nine-scale model maps similarly but extends to geological and digital timescales.

### Rhythm–tempo interaction
Perception of rhythm depends on tempo and vice versa. Tangian’s analysis of Moussorgsky’s “Promenade” shows the same rhythm can be heard as written or as a faster repetition of a simpler pattern, depending on melodic context. The **principle of correlative perception** (minimizing Kolmogorov complexity) selects the simpler representation: without pitch, the literal rhythm is simpler; with melodic repetition, the “repeat at double tempo” representation is simpler. Redundant musical support (melody, harmony, accompaniment) makes rhythmic recognition robust to tempo deviations (e.g., Scriabin’s 5.5× tempo span). In non-redundant additive-rhythm traditions (Bulgarian, Turkish), minor tempo shifts are heard as rhythmic changes, not tempo variations.

### Metric structure and classification
**Prosody** (study of rhythm, stress, pitch in speech) gives poetry its “foot” (lift/tap of the foot) and music its **meter**. Three broad classes: *metrical (divisive) rhythm*—time values as multiples/fractions of the beat, regular accents (Western norm); *measured (additive) rhythm*—time values as multiples of a unit but accents irregular within the cycle; *free rhythm*—basic pulse but no regular grouping (e.g., Christian chant). **Ametric** music lacks meter entirely (some graphic scores, Honkyoku for shakuhachi). *Senza misura* instructs playing without a beat.

### Composite and counter rhythm
**Composite rhythm** (or *attack point rhythm*) is the resultant pattern of all sounding parts; in common-practice music it usually confirms the meter. **Counter rhythm** (OED 1927) is a subordinate rhythm counterbalancing the main one; usage has declined since the 1970s.

### African traditions
Griot oral tradition. Babatunde Olatunji (1927–2003) codified hand-drum teaching with six vocal syllables (“Goon, Doon, Go, Do, Pa, Ta”) for three sounds × two hands. West African music relies on **polyrhythm** (simultaneous different rhythms) and **cross-rhythm** (dominant vs. competing rhythms), underpinned by moral values, repetition at distant intervals, and call-and-response. Drum talk translates proverbs/lineages; musicians are judged by how well they uphold community values and stimulate participation.

### Indian tradition
Oral transmission; tabla players speak complex patterns (bols) before playing. **Tala** is the cyclic rhythmic framework structuring an entire composition. Sheila Chandra demonstrated vocalized rhythmic patterns in performance.

### Western art music (20th century onward)
Stravinsky, Bartók, Glass, Reich: odd meters, phasing, additive rhythm. Messiaen and pupils: irrational rhythms, New Complexity, disrupting regular beat. Cage: irregular rhythms highlight pitch relationships. Young: drones eliminate beat. Cowell: multiple periodic rhythms; rhythmicon (1930s). Nancarrow: player-piano studies.

### Linguistics
Rhythm (isochrony) is one of three prosodic components (with stress, intonation). Languages: **syllable-timed** (Spanish, Cantonese—equal time per syllable), **mora-timed**, **stress-timed** (English, Mandarin—equal intervals between stressed syllables). Narmour’s prosodic rules: *additive* (same duration), *cumulative* (short→long, closure), *countercumulative* (long→short, tension). Middleton notes this fails to capture syncopation; proposes *transformation*.

## Terms
- **Entrainment** — Synchronization of vocalizations or movements to a shared external pulse; uniquely human among primates.
- **Pulse (tactus)** — The perceived fundamental beat—a series of identical periodic stimuli—to which listeners tap or dance.
- **Meter** — Hierarchical organization of pulses into regular groups (measures) with recurring accents.
- **Syncopation** — Accenting a weak beat or off-beat, de-emphasizing the expected downbeat.
- **Polyrhythm** — Simultaneous sounding of two or more independent rhythms, often in different time signatures (polymeter).
- **Additive rhythm** — Measured rhythm where time values are multiples of a unit but accents do not recur regularly within the cycle.
- **Composite rhythm** — The overall rhythmic articulation resulting from all voices/parts sounding together (attack point rhythm).
- **Tala** — In Indian classical music, the cyclic rhythmic pattern that structures an entire composition.
- **Isochrony** — In linguistics, the rhythmic aspect of prosody; equal timing of syllables, moras, or stress intervals.
- **Kolmogorov complexity** — Measure of data complexity; the brain prefers the simplest (shortest) representation of rhythmic-temporal data.

## Debates and open questions
- **Animal rhythm cognition**: Oliver Sacks asserts no animal can be trained to synchronize movement to an auditory beat; pet owners and circus trainers dispute this, but cues may be visual/tactile.
- **Notation for African music**: Outsiders debate staff notation’s appropriateness; African scholars (Kyagambiddwa, Kongo) largely accept its conventions for transcription and debate.
- **Origin of human rhythm**: Jordania’s hypothesis (rhythm evolved for battle trance/collective defense) vs. possible roots in courtship ritual.
- **Rhythm–tempo modeling**: Whether the correlative-perception/simplicity principle fully explains context-dependent rhythm perception across cultures and complexities.
- **Linguistic rhythm classification**: Narmour’s additive/cumulative/countercumulative categories cannot account for syncopation; transformation-based models are proposed but not settled.
