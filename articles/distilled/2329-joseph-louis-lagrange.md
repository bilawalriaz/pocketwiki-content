# Joseph-Louis Lagrange

## Overview
Joseph-Louis Lagrange (1736–1813) was an Italian-born mathematician and physicist who became a French citizen. He is a central figure in the history of mathematics and physics, best known for reformulating classical mechanics into a powerful analytical framework and for foundational contributions to calculus, number theory, and algebra. His work bridged the era of Newton and the 19th-century development of mathematical physics.

## Body

### Early Life and Career in Turin
Lagrange was born in Turin to a family of Italian and French descent. He initially studied law but became fascinated with mathematics at age 17 after reading a paper by Edmond Halley. He was self-taught and quickly became accomplished. In 1755, he was appointed to teach calculus at the Royal Military Academy, becoming the first to teach calculus in an engineering school. In 1757, he co-founded the Turin Academy of Sciences, whose publications, the *Miscellanea Taurinensia*, contained his early major works on the calculus of variations, sound theory, and mechanics.

### Work in Berlin (1766–1787)
After Euler left Berlin, Lagrange was persuaded by King Frederick the Great to succeed him as director of mathematics at the Prussian Academy. He spent over twenty years there, producing a vast output of papers and his seminal work, *Mécanique analytique*. His research during this period was prolific and ranged across algebra, number theory, and astronomy. He won multiple prizes from the French Academy of Sciences for work on lunar libration, Jupiter's satellites, and the three-body problem, for which he discovered the special-case solutions now called Lagrangian points.

### Scientific Contributions
Lagrange's most famous achievement is the reformulation of Newtonian mechanics into Lagrangian mechanics, presented in *Mécanique analytique*. This approach uses generalized coordinates and the principle of least action to derive equations of motion from kinetic and potential energy, simplifying complex problems. He was a founder of the calculus of variations, deriving the Euler–Lagrange equations and the method of Lagrange multipliers for handling constraints. In algebra, his work on the theory of equations and resolvents anticipated Galois theory. In number theory, he proved that every natural number is a sum of four squares and was the first European to prove that Pell's equation has a nontrivial solution for any non-square natural number.

### Life in France (1787–1813)
Lagrange moved to Paris in 1787, where he initially fell into a depression. The French Revolution stirred him from his lethargy. Despite the Reign of Terror, he was protected and exempted from a decree ordering foreigners to leave France. He was instrumental in the development of the metric system, influencing the choice of the metre and kilogram. He became the first professor of analysis at the newly founded École Polytechnique in 1794, where his lectures formed the basis for his treatise *Théorie des fonctions analytiques*. He later fully embraced the use of infinitesimals. He was honored by Napoleon, made a Count of the Empire, and buried in the Panthéon.

## Terms
- **Calculus of Variations**: A field of mathematical analysis used to find functions that optimize certain quantities, foundational to Lagrangian mechanics.
- **Lagrangian Mechanics**: A reformulation of classical mechanics that uses generalized coordinates and energy functions (kinetic and potential) to derive equations of motion.
- **Generalized Coordinates**: A set of parameters that uniquely define the configuration of a system relative to a reference frame, with a number equal to the system's degrees of freedom.
- **Lagrange Multipliers**: A strategy for finding the local maxima and minima of a function subject to equality constraints.
- **Variation of Parameters**: A method for solving nonhomogeneous differential equations.
- **Lagrangian Points**: Five positions in an orbital configuration where a small object can maintain a stable position relative to two larger orbiting bodies.
- **Pell's Equation**: A Diophantine equation of the form x² − ny² = 1, where n is a non-square positive integer.
- **Wilson's Theorem**: A theorem in number theory stating that a natural number n > 1 is prime if and only if (n − 1)! + 1 is divisible by n.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The source notes a "chivalric view" that Euler withheld a paper to let Lagrange claim priority in the calculus of variations, but states this "has been disputed."
- Lagrange's algebraic proof of Taylor's theorem in *Théorie des fonctions analytiques* is noted as having "validity... open to question."
- His lectures at the École Polytechnique were described as "almost perfect in form and matter," yet he was considered an unsuccessful teacher with students unable to appreciate him.
