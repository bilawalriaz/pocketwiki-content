# Investiture of the Gods

## Overview
*Investiture of the Gods* (c. 1567–1619) is a 16th-century Chinese novel and a foundational work of the *shenmo* (gods and demons) genre. It mythologizes the historical fall of the Shang dynasty and the rise of the Zhou dynasty, weaving together history, folklore, and Taoist, Buddhist, and Confucian elements. Its narrative of divine intervention in human affairs and its list of 365 deified figures have profoundly shaped Chinese folk religion and popular culture.

## Body

### Plot and Setting
The novel is a romanticized retelling of the overthrow of the tyrannical Shang king Di Xin by Ji Fa (King Wu of Zhou). The conflict escalates into a cosmic war, with both sides aided by a vast cast of deities, immortals, demons, and humans with magical abilities. The story integrates elements from Chinese mythology, folk religion, and the three teachings (Taoism, Buddhism, Confucianism). The central conflict is framed as a "killing calamity" in the immortal world, which aligns with the mortal dynastic change. The three Taoist sects—Chan, Jie, and the Western (Buddhist) sect—jointly establish the *Fengshen Bang* (List of Deification) to canonize 365 beings as gods.

### Key Characters and Conflicts
The narrative follows several intertwined threads. The vixen spirit Daji corrupts King Di Xin, leading to the persecution of loyal ministers like Bi Gan. Jiang Ziya, a Taoist from Mount Kunlun, is the strategist for the Zhou, tasked with executing the *Fengshen Bang*. Heroes like Nezha, Yang Jian, and Huang Feihu defect from or fight against the Shang. The war features epic magical battles, such as the breaking of the "10 Absolute Formations" and the "Zhuxian and Wanxian Formations," involving major Taoist figures like Yuanshi Tianzun and Tongtian Jiaozhu. The novel concludes with the Zhou victory, Di Xin's death, and Jiang Ziya's investiture of the gods.

### Authorship and Historical Basis
Authorship is debated, with Xu Zhonglin, Lu Xixing, and Li Yunxiang as the most commonly attributed writers. The earliest printed edition credits Xu Zhonglin. The novel was compiled during the Ming dynasty from earlier works, including the Song-Yuan storyteller's prompt-book *Pinghua of King Wu's Expedition against Zhou*. While a fantasy, it adapts events from historical texts like the *Records of the Grand Historian*, such as Di Xin's indulgence in wine and women and his death by self-immolation.

### Impact and Influence
The novel has had a profound impact on Chinese folk religion, popularizing the worship of figures like Zhao Gongming and shaping perceptions of the heavenly court. Its list of deities was adapted for local rituals, such as the *Taiping Qingjiao* ceremonies. Scholars interpret its fictional conflict between the Chan and Jie sects as an allegory for Ming-dynasty religious rivalries or as a "pro-Daoist, anti-Buddhist" stance. Its themes of overthrowing a tyrant also resonated with anti-authoritarian sentiments, and its descriptions of magical combat influenced later events like the Boxer Rebellion.

## Terms
- **Shenmo (gods and demons):** A genre of Chinese fiction dealing with supernatural beings.
- **Fengshen Bang (List of Deification):** A register of 365 deities to be canonized, central to the novel's plot.
- **Chan Sect (阐教):** The orthodox Taoist sect in the novel, led by Yuanshi Tianzun.
- **Jie Sect (截教):** The rival Taoist sect in the novel, led by Tongtian Jiaozhu, often representing folk beliefs.
- **Jiang Ziya (姜子牙):** The Taoist strategist for the Zhou, tasked with executing the deification.
- **Di Xin (帝辛):** The last, tyrannical king of the Shang dynasty.
- **Daji (妲己):** A vixen spirit disguised as a concubine who corrupts Di Xin.
- **Nezha (哪吒):** A powerful young deity and warrior who sides with the Zhou.
- **Fengshentai (封神台):** The Platform of Gods where Jiang Ziya confers divine titles.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Authorship:** The primary debate concerns whether the novel was written by Xu Zhonglin, Lu Xixing, or Li Yunxiang, with evidence supporting different candidates.
- **Religious Allegory:** Scholars debate whether the fictional Chan-Jie sect conflict is an allegory for real Ming-dynasty religious rivalries between schools like Quanzhen and Zhengyi.
- **Political Interpretation:** Some interpret the novel's portrayal of overthrowing a tyrant as containing subtle anti-authoritarian or revolutionary ideas, influenced by the philosophy of Mencius.
