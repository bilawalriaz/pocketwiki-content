# Harp

## Overview
The harp is a stringed instrument with strings angled to its soundboard, plucked with the fingers. Among the oldest known instruments, its evolution from simple arched forms to complex pedal mechanisms reflects technological and musical development. It holds significant cultural value, especially in Ireland.

## Timeline
- **c. 2500 BCE** — Earliest harps and lyres found in Sumer, excavated from Ur.
- **Mid-3rd millennium BCE** — Oldest depictions of pillar-less arched harps in Egyptian tomb paintings.
- **c. 1900 BCE** — Arched harps in Iraq-Iran region replaced by angular harps.
- **770–476 BCE** — Chinese konghou harp documented during Spring and Autumn period.
- **8th–9th centuries CE** — Triangular frame harps with a pillar appear in Pictish stones and French manuscripts.
- **16th century** — Triple harp originates in Italy.
- **1697** — Jakob Hochbrucker of Bavaria introduces the first pedal mechanism.
- **1811** — Sébastien Erard patents the "double action" pedal system.
- **19th century** — Pedal harp gradually enters the classical orchestra.
- **Early 19th century** — Modern Celtic harp begins to appear in Ireland.
- **1929** — Harp enters musical comedy films with Arthur "Harpo" Marx.

## Body

### Origins and Early History
The harp's history dates to at least 3000 BCE in Asia, Africa, and Europe. The earliest known examples are from Sumer (c. 2500 BCE). Ancient Egyptian tomb paintings show arched harps without a forepillar, resembling a hunter's bow. In West Asia, arched harps were replaced by angular harps around 1900 BCE. The instrument spread to South Asia, depicted on Indus seals and described in Tamil Sangam literature (as early as 200 BCE), and to East Asia, with the Chinese konghou documented from the 7th–5th centuries BCE.

### Development in Europe
European harps evolved to include a "pillar," a third structural member supporting the neck and soundbox, creating a triangular frame. This design is seen in 8th-century Pictish stones. To play more complex, chromatic music, mechanisms were developed. By the Baroque period, more strings were added. In 17th-century Germany, manually turned hooks raised a string's pitch by a half step. This led to the single-action pedal harp in the 18th century, with pedals connected to these hooks. The double-action pedal harp, patented by Sébastien Erard in 1811, allowed strings to be raised by one or two half steps, making the harp fully chromatic and facilitating its entry into the classical orchestra.

### Harps in the Americas and Africa
Harp traditions in the Americas derive from Baroque harps brought by Spanish colonists. Strong traditions exist in Mexico, the Andean region, Venezuela, and Paraguay. The Paraguayan harp is the national instrument, known for its 36 strings and wide soundbox. The Andean harp is large with a resonant bass. In Venezuela, distinct traditions include the *arpa llanera* (for joropo music) and the *arpa central*. In Africa, many harps are bow or angle harps without a forepillar. Instruments like the West African kora are sometimes classified as "spike harps" or "harp lutes" due to their bridge construction.

### Modern Harp Types
The modern **concert harp** is a pedal harp, a technologically advanced instrument with seven pedals that make it chromatic. It became a standard orchestral instrument in the 19th century. **Folk or lever harps** are mid-size instruments, often with nylon strings and optional levers to change pitch, and are broadly called "Celtic harps." The **triple harp**, a multi-course instrument with three rows of strings, is traditional in Wales. **Chromatic-strung harps**, like the cross-strung harp, achieve chromaticity by adding extra rows of strings instead of using pedals. **Electric harps** use piezo-electric sensors for amplification.

### Structure and Mechanism
All harps have a neck, resonator (body), and strings. **Frame harps** have a pillar to support the neck; **open harps** (like arch or bow harps) do not. Strings, historically made of sinew or gut and now often nylon or metal, are secured at the neck with a tuning peg and at the soundboard. Pitch is determined by string length, tension, and weight. On pedal harps, the hollow pillar encloses rods connected to pedals, which move small pegs to change a string's vibrating length and pitch.

### Symbolism
The harp is a potent political symbol, especially in Ireland, where it has been used since at least the 6th century. It appeared on Irish coinage from 1542 and on the Royal Standard of King James VI and I in 1603. Since 1922, the Irish government has used a left-facing harp based on the Trinity College Harp as its state symbol. The harp is also used in corporate logos (e.g., Guinness, Ryanair) and sporting emblems (e.g., Finn Harps F.C., Hibernian F.C.). In Christianity, it symbolizes the sacred and heavenly.

## Terms
- **Forepillar/Pillar**: The vertical structural member in a frame harp that supports the neck against string tension.
- **Soundboard/Sounding board**: The flat, resonant surface of the harp's body that vibrates to project sound when strings are plucked.
- **Pedal harp**: A large, chromatic harp with foot-controlled pedals that alter the pitch of all strings of one note name.
- **Lever harp**: A smaller harp with individual levers on the neck that raise the pitch of single strings by a semitone.
- **Chromatic**: Able to play all twelve pitches of the Western musical scale (sharps and flats).
- **Diatonic**: Relating to a musical scale with seven notes per octave, like a major or minor scale.
- **Arched harp**: An early harp form with a curved neck, resembling a bow, and no pillar.
- **Angular harp**: A harp form with a sharp angle between the neck and resonator, which could be vertical or horizontal.
- **Multi-course harp**: A harp with more than one row of strings, such as a double or triple harp.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact identification of the ancient Israelite **kinnor** is unclear; it is generally translated as "harp" or "lyre" but its precise form is debated.
- The origin of the harp as a political symbol of Ireland is unknown, though it has been present since at least the 6th century.
- The classification of some African instruments, like the kora, is debated; they are sometimes called "spike harps" or "harp lutes" rather than true harps due to their bridge construction.
