# Thermodynamics

## Overview
Thermodynamics is the branch of physics that studies heat, work, temperature, and energy, and their relationships to matter and radiation. It is governed by four laws that provide a quantitative framework, later explained microscopically through statistical mechanics. Originating from efforts to improve steam engine efficiency in the 19th century, thermodynamics has become foundational across physics, chemistry, engineering, biology, and even economics.

## Timeline
- **1650** — Otto von Guericke builds the first vacuum pump, challenging the notion that "nature abhors a vacuum."
- **1656** — Robert Boyle and Robert Hooke build an air pump; Boyle’s Law (pressure-volume inverse relation) emerges.
- **1679** — Denis Papin invents the steam digester, a precursor to the piston engine.
- **1697** — Thomas Savery builds the first steam engine based on Papin’s designs.
- **1712** — Thomas Newcomen builds an improved steam engine.
- **1760s** — Joseph Black develops concepts of heat capacity and latent heat; James Watt improves steam engine efficiency.
- **1824** — Sadi Carnot publishes *Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire*, founding modern thermodynamics.
- **1850** — Rudolf Clausius states the second law of thermodynamics.
- **1854** — Lord Kelvin formulates a concise definition of thermodynamics.
- **1859** — William Rankine publishes the first thermodynamics textbook.
- **1865** — Clausius introduces the concept of entropy.
- **1873–76** — J. Willard Gibbs publishes foundational papers on chemical thermodynamics.

## Body

### Historical Development
Thermodynamics arose from practical needs to enhance steam engine performance. Otto von Guericke’s vacuum experiments (1650) and Robert Boyle’s air pump studies (1656) laid early groundwork. Denis Papin’s steam digester (1679) and subsequent engines by Savery (1697) and Newcomen (1712) demonstrated practical applications. Joseph Black’s work on heat capacity and latent heat, combined with James Watt’s engine improvements, provided essential theoretical foundations. Sadi Carnot’s 1824 treatise established the theoretical basis for heat engines and the Carnot cycle.

### Laws of Thermodynamics
The **zeroth law** establishes thermal equilibrium as an equivalence relation, enabling temperature measurement. The **first law** expresses energy conservation: ΔU = Q − W, where internal energy change equals heat added minus work done. The **second law** introduces entropy, stating heat flows spontaneously from hot to cold and defining the direction of natural processes. The **third law** asserts that as temperature approaches absolute zero, entropy approaches a minimum, providing an absolute reference for entropy measurement.

### Branches of Thermodynamics
**Classical thermodynamics** describes equilibrium systems using macroscopic properties. **Statistical mechanics** (or statistical thermodynamics) links microscopic particle behavior to macroscopic phenomena. **Chemical thermodynamics** applies thermodynamic principles to chemical reactions and phase changes. **Equilibrium thermodynamics** studies energy and matter transfer in systems at balance. **Non-equilibrium thermodynamics** extends these concepts to systems far from equilibrium, though many natural systems remain beyond current macroscopic methods.

### System Models and Processes
A thermodynamic system is a defined region of the universe separated from its surroundings by boundaries that may be fixed, movable, real, or imaginary. Systems are classified as open, closed, or isolated based on what crosses their boundaries. Thermodynamic processes—such as adiabatic (no heat exchange), isothermal (constant temperature), isobaric (constant pressure), and isochoric (constant volume)—describe how systems evolve between states. State variables (intensive or extensive) define system conditions, and equations of state relate these variables.

### Potentials and Conjugate Variables
Thermodynamic potentials—internal energy (U), enthalpy (H), Helmholtz free energy (F), and Gibbs free energy (G)—quantify stored energy under different constraints. These are derived via Legendre transformations. Conjugate variable pairs (pressure–volume, temperature–entropy, chemical potential–particle number) represent energy transfer mechanisms, where one variable acts as a generalized force and the other as displacement.

### Axiomatic and Applied Thermodynamics
Axiomatic thermodynamics, pioneered by Constantin Carathéodory in 1909, formulates the subject using rigorous mathematical axioms based on adiabatic accessibility. Modern applications span engineering, biochemistry, materials science, meteorology, and even economics, demonstrating thermodynamics’ broad relevance.

## Terms
- **Entropy**: A measure of disorder or randomness in a system; central to the second law.
- **Internal Energy (U)**: Total energy contained within a thermodynamic system.
- **Equation of State**: A relation between state variables (e.g., ideal gas law).
- **Thermodynamic Equilibrium**: A state where macroscopic flows cease and properties are uniform.
- **Reversible Process**: An idealized process that proceeds infinitely slowly through equilibrium states.
- **Adiabatic Process**: A process with no heat exchange between system and surroundings.
- **Legendre Transformation**: A mathematical method used to derive thermodynamic potentials.
- **Thermodynamic Potential**: A quantity representing stored energy available for work under specific constraints.
- **Conjugate Variables**: Pairs of thermodynamic properties whose product gives energy transferred.
- **Absolute Zero**: The theoretical temperature where all molecular motion ceases (0 K, −273.15°C).

## Debates and Open Questions
- Whether the term "thermodynamics" was coined by James Joule (1858) remains debated; Joule used "perfect thermo-dynamic engine" instead.
- The exact attribution of the term’s coinage is contested, with claims pointing to William Thomson and William Rankine (1854).
- Non-equilibrium thermodynamics lacks general principles for predicting approach rates to equilibrium, especially in systems far from equilibrium.
- The interpretation of entropy—as disorder, information, or a statistical measure—remains philosophically debated.
- Quantum thermodynamics explores thermodynamic behavior at quantum scales, raising questions about the applicability of classical laws.
