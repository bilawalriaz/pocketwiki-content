# Music of China

## Overview
The music of China is a vast collection of distinct traditions, primarily from the Han ethnic group but also from numerous other ethnic minorities. It encompasses ancient classical forms, indigenous folk music, and modern genres influenced by Western culture. This musical heritage is a core part of Chinese cultural identity, reflecting a continuous development from ancient cosmological concepts to contemporary globalized forms.

## Timeline
- **c. 7000–9000 BC** — Archaeological evidence of bone flutes (Jiahu) and clay instruments (Xun).
- **1122–257 BC** — Zhou dynasty establishes formal court music (yayue) and a pentatonic scale system.
- **221–207 BC** — Qin dynasty establishes the Imperial Music Bureau.
- **618–907 AD** — Tang dynasty sees a major flowering of music, especially for the qin.
- **1601** — Jesuit Matteo Ricci presents a harpsichord to the Ming court.
- **1910s–1920s** — New Culture Movement spurs interest in Western music and notation.
- **1920s** — The shidaiqu (Chinese-Western fusion pop) genre emerges in Shanghai.
- **1942** — Yan'an Forum launches campaigns to adapt folk music for revolutionary goals.
- **1949** — People's Republic of China established; revolutionary music is promoted.
- **1980s** — Cui Jian pioneers Chinese rock; state control limits its mainstream reach.
- **2000s–present** — Rise of digital music markets, hip-hop, and electronic dance music (EDM).

## Body

### Early History and Philosophy
Archaeological finds show music culture in China from at least 9,000 years ago. By the Zhou dynasty, a formal system of court music called **yayue** ("elegant music") was established, conceived as a cosmological manifestation of nature's sounds within a yin-yang order. Philosophers debated music's role: Confucius saw "correct" music as morally uplifting and essential for a stable state, while Mozi condemned it as an extravagant indulgence. Folk songs were collected by emperors, and many are preserved in the Confucian Classic of Poetry.

### Imperial China and Foreign Influence
The Imperial Music Bureau, expanded under the Han dynasty, supervised court and military music. Over centuries, Chinese music absorbed influences from Central Asia and India, introducing instruments like the **pipa** and new scales. The first major documented musical tradition is for the **qin** during the Tang dynasty. European music arrived in 1601 with a harpsichord, and Western influence grew significantly during the late Qing dynasty.

### Republican Era and Modernization
The early 20th century saw a major shift with the New Culture Movement, which promoted Western musical notation and conservatory training. The **shidaiqu** genre fused Chinese and Western popular music. After 1942, the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) adapted folk music into revolutionary songs to educate the populace. Composers like Xian Xinghai, known for the *Yellow River Cantata*, were prominent.

### Post-Revolution and State Control
After 1949, the CCP denounced popular music as "yellow music" (pornography). During the Cultural Revolution, only revolutionary music was permitted, with the "Eight Model Dramas" promoted under Jiang Qing's supervision. After 1989, a new rock scene emerged, led by Cui Jian, but state control of media limited its mainstream success. Today, rock is centered in Beijing and Shanghai with limited societal influence.

### Traditional Music and Instruments
Traditional Chinese music is melodic, not harmonic, and often uses a **pentatonic scale**. Instruments are classified by material into eight categories (**bayin**). Key instruments include the **guzheng** (zither), **erhu** (bowed string), **dizi** (flute), and the revered **qin**. Chinese opera is a comprehensive art form integrating music, dance, and martial arts, with major regional styles like Peking Opera and Kunqu.

### Regional and Ethnic Music
China's ethnic minorities have distinct musical traditions. Examples include the **On Ikki Muqam** suites of the Uyghurs in Xinjiang, the long and short tunes of the Mongolians, and the Buddhist chanting traditions of Tibet. Folk music varies greatly by region, from the **sizhu** (silk and bamboo) ensembles of the Jiangnan area to the **Nanyin** ballads of Fujian.

### Modern Popular Music
Modern Chinese pop began with **shidaiqu**. After 1949, the industry moved to Hong Kong and Taiwan, leading to **cantopop** and **mandopop**. Economic reforms in the late 1970s brought this music back to the mainland. Today, China has a large digital music market, with popular genres including hip-hop and electronic dance music (EDM).

### Rock and Heavy Metal
Chinese rock was pioneered by Cui Jian in the late 1980s. Bands like Black Panther and Tang Dynasty (the first Chinese heavy metal band) followed. Punk and post-punk scenes developed in the 1990s and 2000s, with bands like P.K. 14 and Carsick Cars gaining international recognition.

## Terms
- **Yayue**: "Elegant music," the formal court and ceremonial music system established in the Zhou dynasty.
- **Pentatonic scale**: A musical scale with five notes per octave, fundamental to traditional Chinese music.
- **Bayin**: The traditional eight-category classification system for musical instruments based on material (e.g., bamboo, silk, metal).
- **Qin**: A plucked seven-string zither, one of the most revered instruments in the Chinese literati tradition.
- **Pipa**: A four-stringed Chinese lute, believed to have been introduced from Central Asia in the 6th century.
- **Shidaiqu**: A genre of Chinese popular music from the 1920s-40s that fused Chinese melodies with Western jazz and pop.
- **Guoyue / Minyue**: Terms for "national music" or "people's music," referring to compositions for traditional Chinese instruments.
- **Chinese orchestra**: A modern ensemble modeled on the Western symphony orchestra but using Chinese instruments.
- **On Ikki Muqam**: A complex suite of twelve sections, a major musical tradition of the Uyghur people in Xinjiang.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent to which modern "traditional" Chinese music, shaped by 20th-century Western-influenced reforms (like equal temperament tuning and new instrument designs), accurately reflects ancient practices is a point of scholarly discussion.
- The balance between state promotion of traditional culture and the control of contemporary popular expression remains a dynamic tension in China's musical landscape.
