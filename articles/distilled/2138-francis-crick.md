# Francis Crick

## Overview
Francis Crick (1916–2004) was an English molecular biologist, biophysicist, and neuroscientist. He is most famous for co-discovering the double helix structure of DNA with James Watson in 1953, a breakthrough that revealed how genetic information is stored and copied, laying the foundation for modern molecular biology and genetics.

## Timeline
- **8 June 1916** — Born in Northampton, England.
- **1937** — Earned a BSc from University College London.
- **1947** — Began studying biology at Cambridge, transitioning from physics.
- **1951** — Started working with James Watson at the Cavendish Laboratory.
- **25 April 1953** — Watson and Crick's paper on the DNA double helix is published in *Nature*.
- **1954** — Completed his PhD thesis on X-ray diffraction of proteins.
- **1958** — Published influential paper outlining the "central dogma" of molecular biology.
- **1962** — Awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (with Watson and Wilkins).
- **1976** — Moved to the Salk Institute in California, shifting focus to neuroscience.
- **28 July 2004** — Died of colon cancer in La Jolla, California.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Crick was born in 1916 and raised in Northampton. He showed an early interest in science, moving away from religious belief by age 12. After attending Mill Hill School on a scholarship, he studied physics at University College London. World War II interrupted his PhD research in physics, and he worked for the Admiralty on mine design. Post-war, influenced by Erwin Schrödinger's *What Is Life?*, he switched to biology, joining the Cavendish Laboratory at Cambridge in 1949.

### Discovery of the DNA Structure
At Cambridge, Crick learned X-ray crystallography and collaborated with James Watson. Their goal was to determine the structure of DNA, competing with Linus Pauling. They used crucial, unpublished X-ray diffraction data from Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins at King's College London, including "Photo 51," which indicated a helical structure. Combined with Erwin Chargaff's rules (A pairs with T, G pairs with C), Watson and Crick built a model of DNA as a double helix with antiparallel backbones and complementary base pairs. Their model was published in *Nature* on 25 April 1953.

### Later Career in Molecular Biology and Neuroscience
After the DNA discovery, Crick focused on the genetic code and protein synthesis. He proposed the "central dogma" of molecular biology: genetic information flows from DNA to RNA to protein, and not in reverse. He also theorized about adaptor molecules (later identified as tRNA). In 1976, he moved to the Salk Institute in California, where he turned his attention to neuroscience. He collaborated with Christof Koch to study the neural basis of consciousness, focusing on visual awareness and proposing that consciousness involves short-term memory processes.

### Personal Views and Legacy
Crick was a vocal humanist and atheist, critical of organized religion. He advocated for scientific skepticism and evolution education. His work earned him numerous honors, including the Nobel Prize, the Royal Medal, and the Order of Merit. The Francis Crick Institute, a major biomedical research center in London, is named in his honor.

## Terms
- **Double helix**: The twisted-ladder molecular structure of DNA, with two strands of nucleotides connected by base pairs.
- **Central dogma**: Crick's principle stating that genetic information flows from DNA to RNA to protein, and not back from protein to nucleic acid.
- **X-ray crystallography**: A technique using X-ray diffraction patterns to determine the atomic and molecular structure of a crystal.
- **Chargaff's rules**: The observation that in DNA, the amount of adenine (A) equals thymine (T), and guanine (G) equals cytosine (C).
- **Antiparallel**: The arrangement of the two DNA strands, which run in opposite directions (5' to 3' and 3' to 5').
- **Adaptor molecules**: Crick's term for molecules (later found to be tRNA) that match nucleotide sequences in mRNA to specific amino acids during protein synthesis.
- **Directed panspermia**: A hypothesis proposed by Crick and Leslie Orgel suggesting that life on Earth may have been intentionally seeded by intelligent extraterrestrial beings.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Use of Rosalind Franklin's data**: A major controversy concerns whether Watson and Crick appropriately used Franklin's unpublished X-ray diffraction data and "Photo 51" without her knowledge or consent. Franklin's contributions were not fully recognized during her lifetime.
- **The "central dogma" terminology**: Crick later acknowledged that his use of the word "dogma" was a misnomer, as he intended it as a hypothesis, not an unchallengeable belief.
- **Consciousness**: Crick's later work on the neural correlates of consciousness remains an active and complex field of study, with his "astonishing hypothesis" that the mind is a product of brain activity still being explored.
