# Solar System

## Overview

The Solar System is the gravitationally bound system of the Sun and all objects that orbit it, including eight planets, dwarf planets, moons, asteroids, and comets. It formed about 4.6 billion years ago from the collapse of a molecular cloud and is located within the Milky Way Galaxy. The system is dominated by the Sun, which contains 99.86% of its total mass, and its study has shaped our understanding of planetary formation, habitability, and our place in the galaxy.

## Body

### Formation and Evolution

The Solar System formed at least 4.568 billion years ago from the gravitational collapse of a region within a large molecular cloud, likely several light-years across and probably birthing several stars. As the pre-solar nebula collapsed, conservation of angular momentum caused it to rotate faster and flatten into a protoplanetary disc roughly 200 AU in diameter with a hot protostar at the center. Planets formed by accretion, where dust and gas gravitationally attracted each other, coalescing into larger bodies; hundreds of protoplanets either merged, were destroyed, or were ejected.

In the inner Solar System, heat exceeded the boiling point of hydrocarbon molecules for the first million years, creating the soot line boundary and leading to low carbon content in inner planets. Beyond the frost line at about five AU, volatile icy compounds remained solid, allowing giant planets to grow massive enough to capture hydrogen and helium atmospheres. The Nice model proposes that gravitational encounters caused the gas giants to migrate, leading to dynamical instability; the grand tack hypothesis suggests Jupiter's final inward migration dispersed much of the asteroid belt, causing the Late Heavy Bombardment.

The Sun's main-sequence phase will last about 10 billion years total. Roughly 5 billion years from now, hydrogen in the core will be exhausted, the Sun will expand into a red giant about 260 times its current diameter, and is expected to vaporize Mercury and Venus while rendering Earth and Mars uninhabitable. The Sun will then eject its outer layers, leaving a dense white dwarf half the original mass but Earth-sized.

### Composition and Structure

The Sun contains 99.86% of the system's known mass; the four giant planets account for 99% of the remaining mass, with Jupiter and Saturn comprising more than 90%. All other objects together comprise less than 0.002% of total mass. A composition gradient exists: objects closer to the Sun are composed of elements with high melting points, while distant objects contain materials with lower melting points, with the frost line boundary at roughly five times Earth's distance from the Sun.

The inner Solar System contains the terrestrial planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars) and the asteroid belt. The outer Solar System contains the gas giants (Jupiter, Saturn) and ice giants (Uranus, Neptune), plus the Kuiper belt. Beyond Neptune lies the trans-Neptunian region, sometimes called the "third zone," containing the Kuiper belt, scattered disc, and extreme trans-Neptunian objects.

### Planets and Moons

The four terrestrial planets have dense, rocky compositions with few or no moons. Mercury is the smallest planet with extreme temperature swings from −170 °C to 420 °C. Venus has a carbon dioxide atmosphere ninety times denser than Earth's sea level, with surface temperatures over 400 °C due to greenhouse gases. Earth is the only known place with life and surface liquid water, with an atmosphere of 78% nitrogen and 21% oxygen. Mars has a thin carbon dioxide atmosphere and two tiny moons, Phobos and Deimos.

The four giant planets collectively make up 99% of mass orbiting the Sun. Jupiter, the largest, has 115 confirmed satellites as of 2026, including the Galilean moons. Saturn has 293 confirmed satellites and a distinctive visible ring system. Uranus uniquely orbits on its side with an axial tilt greater than 90°. Neptune, the furthest planet, has 16 confirmed satellites including Triton, which is geologically active with nitrogen geysers.

### Small Bodies and Distant Regions

The asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter contains tens of thousands of objects over one kilometer in diameter, with total mass unlikely more than a thousandth of Earth's. Ceres is the only dwarf planet there. The Kuiper belt extends between 30 and 50 AU, containing over 100,000 objects larger than 50 km, including Pluto, Haumea, Makemake, and Quaoar. The scattered disc extends to near 500 AU and contains Eris and Gonggong. Extreme trans-Neptunian objects like Sedna have orbits reaching hundreds of AU. The theorized Oort cloud, source of long-period comets, extends from potentially ~2,000 AU to up to ~200,000 AU.

### Habitability and Comparison with Other Systems

The habitable zone is conventionally located in the inner Solar System around Earth, where liquid water can exist on surfaces. Magnetic fields partially shield planets from cosmic rays. The Solar System is an "ordered" system (planet masses increase with distance), as are 37% of observed systems; "similar" systems comprise 59% and "mixed" just 4%. The Solar System lacks super-Earths and planets interior to Mercury's orbit, and has unusually small terrestrial and large gas giants with no intermediate sizes.

### Discovery and Exploration

Nicolaus Copernicus developed the first mathematically predictive heliocentric system. Kepler produced the Rudolphine Tables using elliptical orbits and Tycho Brahe's data. Galileo and Simon Marius discovered Jupiter's four satellites; Huygens discovered Titan and Saturn's ring shape. Halley realized comets return regularly, and the 1769 transit of Venus allowed calculation of the Earth–Sun distance within 0.8% of modern value. Bessel's 1838 stellar parallax measurement provided first direct proof of heliocentrism. By 1989, all eight planets had been visited by space probes. Humans landed on the Moon during the Apollo program and plan to return with Artemis.

## Terms

- **AU (astronomical unit)**: The Earth–Sun distance, used for measuring Solar System scales
- **Frost line**: Boundary beyond which volatile icy compounds remain solid, at about 5 AU
- **Protoplanetary disc**: Flattened rotating disk of gas and dust from which planets form
- **Main-sequence star**: A star fusing hydrogen into helium in its core, like the Sun
- **Heliosphere**: Region of space dominated by the Sun's magnetic field and solar wind
- **Dwarf planet**: Object of planetary mass that does not dominate its orbit
- **Kuiper belt**: Ring of icy debris beyond Neptune, from 30 to 50 AU
- **Oort cloud**: Theorized spherical shell of icy objects, source of long-period comets
- **Solar wind**: Continuous stream of charged particles emitted by the Sun
- **Hill sphere**: Region where the Sun's gravitational influence dominates over the galaxy

## Debates and Open Questions

Whether the Solar System is embedded in the Local Interstellar Cloud or just outside its edge remains unclear. The exact causes of the planets' nearly circular orbits compared to other systems are undetermined, with attempts to explain it via detection bias or planetary interactions. Statistical clustering in extreme trans-Neptunian object orbits may indicate a hypothetical Planet Nine, but could also result from observational biases or coincidence. The actual shape of the heliosphere's outer edge remains unknown. The Shiva hypothesis controversially suggests the Solar System's galactic position could explain periodic extinction events on Earth.
