# Forbidden City

## Overview
The Forbidden City is the world's largest preserved imperial palace complex, located in central Beijing. It served as the ceremonial and political center of China for over 500 years, housing 24 emperors of the Ming and Qing dynasties from 1420 to 1924. Its architecture and layout are a physical manifestation of traditional Chinese philosophy and imperial power, and it now houses the Palace Museum.

## Timeline
- **1406–1420** — Construction of the Forbidden City under the Yongle Emperor.
- **1420** — Becomes the imperial palace and seat of the Ming dynasty.
- **1644** — Captured by rebel forces; later occupied by the Manchus, establishing the Qing dynasty.
- **1860** — Occupied by Anglo-French forces during the Second Opium War.
- **1900** — Briefly occupied by foreign forces during the Boxer Rebellion.
- **1912** — Ceases to be the political center after the abdication of Emperor Puyi.
- **1924** — Puyi is evicted from the palace.
- **1925** — The Palace Museum is established.
- **1933** — National treasures are evacuated due to Japanese invasion.
- **1987** — Designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **2012–present** — Undergoes major restoration; sees over 14 million visitors annually.

## Body
### Construction and History
The complex was built from 1406 to 1420 when the Yongle Emperor moved the capital to Beijing. It required over a million workers and materials like precious *Phoebe zhennan* wood and specially baked "golden bricks." It was the seat of the Ming dynasty until 1644, when it was captured by rebels and then the Manchu-led Qing dynasty. The Qing rulers altered building names to emphasize "harmony" and introduced shamanist elements. The palace was occupied by foreign forces in 1860 and 1900. After the last emperor, Puyi, was evicted in 1924, the Palace Museum was founded in 1925. During the 20th century, parts of its collection were evacuated to Taiwan, forming the core of the National Palace Museum in Taipei.

### Layout and Structure
The Forbidden City is a rectangular complex measuring 961 m north-south and 753 m east-west, with 980 surviving buildings containing 8,886 rooms. It is surrounded by a 7.9 m high wall and a 52 m wide moat. The complex is divided into the **Outer Court** for ceremonial purposes and the **Inner Court** as the imperial residence. The Outer Court's focus is the three great halls on a marble terrace: the Hall of Supreme Harmony (the largest wooden structure in China), the Hall of Central Harmony, and the Hall of Preserving Harmony. The Inner Court centers on the Palaces of Heavenly Purity, Union, and Earthly Tranquility. The emperor's actual living quarters shifted to the Hall of Mental Cultivation in the Qing dynasty. The Six Eastern and Six Western Palaces were residences for imperial consorts.

### Symbolism and Architecture
The design meticulously reflects philosophical principles. Yellow glazed tiles symbolize the emperor. The main halls are grouped in threes (representing Heaven), while residences are in clusters of six (representing Earth). The number of roof statuettes indicates a building's status; the Hall of Supreme Harmony uniquely has ten. The layout follows the *Classic of Rites*, placing ancestral temples in front and residences in the back. The complex's central axis is the historic axis of Beijing.

### Collections and Modern Era
The Palace Museum holds over 1.86 million artifacts from the imperial collections, including ceramics, paintings, bronzes, jades, and mechanical timepieces. A major restoration project aims to open 76% of the palace to the public. The Forbidden City remains a vital cultural symbol for the Chinese nation.

## Terms
- **Outer Court (Wàicháo):** The southern section of the Forbidden City used for state ceremonies and rituals.
- **Inner Court (Nèitíng):** The northern section that served as the residence and daily living quarters for the emperor and his family.
- **Hall of Supreme Harmony:** The largest and most important hall in the Outer Court, used for major ceremonies like enthronements.
- **Palace Museum (Gùgōng Bówùyùan):** The museum established in 1925 that administers the Forbidden City and its collections.
- **Golden Bricks (jīnzhuān):** Specially baked paving bricks from Suzhou used in the floors of major halls.
- **Imperial Way:** The central stone-flagged path along the main axis, traditionally reserved for the emperor.
- **Phoebe zhennan (nánmù):** A precious wood used in the palace's construction.
- **Zijincheng:** The Chinese name for the Forbidden City, meaning "Purple Forbidden City," linking it to the celestial pole star.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The total number of rooms is traditionally claimed to be 9,999, but modern surveys indicate 8,886, making the exact count a point of clarification.
- The precise alignment of the Forbidden City's central axis is slightly off true north; researchers believe it was designed during the Yuan dynasty to align with Shangdu, another Yuan capital.
