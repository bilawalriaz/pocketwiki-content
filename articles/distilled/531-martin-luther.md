# Martin Luther

## Overview
Martin Luther (1483–1546) was a German theologian and former Augustinian friar whose challenge to Roman Catholic doctrine and practice ignited the Protestant Reformation. His core theological breakthrough—justification by faith alone (*sola fide*) through God's grace, rather than by human merit or sacramental works—undermined papal authority and the sacramental system, establishing the foundation of Lutheranism. Luther's translation of the Bible into vernacular German democratized scriptural access, standardized the German language, and influenced subsequent vernacular translations. While his reforms reshaped Western Christianity and culture, his later virulent anti-Jewish writings provided rhetorical ammunition for centuries of German antisemitism, including exploitation by the Nazi regime.

## Timeline
- **10 Nov 1483** — Born in Eisleben, County of Mansfeld, Holy Roman Empire.
- **1505** — Enters St. Augustine's Monastery in Erfurt after a thunderstorm vow; ordained priest 1507.
- **1508–1512** — Studies and teaches at University of Wittenberg; receives Doctor of Theology (1512).
- **31 Oct 1517** — Posts Ninety-five Theses disputing indulgences; rapid spread via printing press.
- **1520** — Publishes three major treatises: *To the Christian Nobility*, *Babylonian Captivity*, *Freedom of a Christian*.
- **3 Jan 1521** — Excommunicated by Pope Leo X (*Decet Romanum Pontificem*).
- **Apr 1521** — Refuses to recant at Diet of Worms; declared outlaw by Emperor Charles V (Edict of Worms).
- **1521–1522** — In protective custody at Wartburg Castle; translates New Testament into German.
- **1522** — Returns to Wittenberg; restores order via Invocavit Sermons against radical reformers.
- **1525** — Marries Katharina von Bora (former nun); publishes *On the Bondage of the Will* vs. Erasmus; condemns Peasants' War.
- **1529** — Publishes Large and Small Catechisms; Marburg Colloquy fails to unite with Zwingli on Eucharist.
- **1530** — Augsburg Confession presented (Luther at Veste Coburg); Schmalkaldic League formed 1531.
- **1534** — Complete Luther Bible (Old and New Testaments) published.
- **1543** — Publishes *On the Jews and Their Lies*, advocating expulsion and destruction of synagogues.
- **18 Feb 1546** — Dies in Eisleben; buried in Schlosskirche, Wittenberg.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Luther was born to Hans and Margarethe Luder in Eisleben; the family moved to Mansfeld where his father managed copper mines. Hans intended Martin for law, sending him to Latin schools (Mansfeld, Magdeburg, Eisenach) focused on the *trivium* (grammar, logic, rhetoric), then to the University of Erfurt (BA 1502, MA 1505). Luther found scholastic philosophy—especially Aristotle’s emphasis on reason—unsatisfying for knowing God, concluding divine revelation (Scripture) was the only path to theological certainty. A 1505 thunderstorm terror prompted a vow to St. Anne to become a monk; he entered the Augustinian Erfurt convent, disappointing his father.

### Monastic Crisis and Academic Rise
As a friar, Luther practiced extreme asceticism but suffered *Anfechtung* (spiritual despair), viewing Christ as a judge rather than savior. Superior Johann von Staupitz redirected him to academic theology. Ordained 1507, Luther transferred to the new University of Wittenberg (1508), earning doctorates in biblical studies and the *Sentences* of Peter Lombard, succeeding Staupitz as chair of theology (1512). Lecturing on Psalms, Romans, and Galatians (1510–1520), using Erasmus’s Greek New Testament, he developed his doctrine of justification: God declares sinners righteous solely by grace through faith in Christ (*sola fide, sola gratia*), imputing Christ’s alien righteousness to the believer (monergism), not infusing righteousness via cooperation (synergism).

### The Indulgence Controversy and Break with Rome
In 1517, Dominican Johann Tetzel sold plenary indulgences (remission of temporal punishment) to fund St. Peter’s Basilica and Archbishop Albert of Mainz’s debts. Luther’s Ninety-five Theses (31 Oct 1517), sent to Albert, disputed the theology of indulgences and papal authority over purgatory. The theses spread rapidly in German translation. Papal response escalated: heresy trial (Sylvester Mazzolini), interrogation by Cardinal Cajetan at Augsburg (1518)—where Luther denied papal infallibility and supremacy over Scripture—and the Leipzig Disputation (1519) with Johann Eck, who linked Luther to the condemned Jan Hus. Luther burned the papal bull *Exsurge Domine* (1520) and was excommunicated (3 Jan 1521). At the Diet of Worms (Apr 1521), under safe conduct, he refused to recant unless convinced by Scripture and reason, declaring conscience captive to God’s Word. The Edict of Worms declared him an outlaw.

### Wartburg, Radicalism, and the Peasants' War
Frederick the Wise staged Luther’s "kidnapping" to Wartburg Castle (1521–22). There he translated the New Testament (1522) and wrote against private masses, compulsory confession, and monastic vows. In Wittenberg, Andreas Karlstadt and the "Zwickau prophets" enacted radical reforms (iconoclasm, adult baptism). Luther returned secretly (Mar 1522), preaching the Invocavit Sermons urging reform by the Word, not violence. He suppressed the radicals but faced wider revolt: the German Peasants' War (1524–25). Though sympathetic to grievances (Twelve Articles), Luther condemned the rebellion as devilish violation of temporal authority (*Against the Murderous, Thieving Hordes of Peasants*), urging princes to "smite, slay, and stab" rebels. The defeat at Frankenhausen (1525) ended the revolutionary phase; Luther’s Reformation thereafter aligned with territorial princes.

### Building the Evangelical Church
Luther organized the Saxon church under Elector John the Steadfast, accepting state supervision (visitation) over his ideal of congregational autonomy. He produced the German Mass (1526)—retaining structure but offering communion in both kinds, German hymns, and optional ceremony—and the Large and Small Catechisms (1529) for clergy and laity, covering the Decalogue, Creed, Lord’s Prayer, Baptism, and the Lord’s Supper. The catechisms presented the Trinity personally, not abstractly. At the Marburg Colloquy (1529), Luther and Zwingli agreed on 14 articles but split on the Eucharist: Luther insisted on the Real Presence via *sacramental union* (Christ’s ubiquitous body in the bread/wine), while Zwingli held a symbolic view. This prevented Protestant political unity (Schmalkaldic League excluded Swiss).

### Bible Translation and Hymnody
Luther’s New Testament (1522) and complete Bible (1534, with collaborators) used Saxon chancellery German, accessible north and south. It shaped modern High German, influenced the Tyndale/King James line, and carried anti-papal woodcuts and prefaces. He excluded the Johannine Comma (1 John 5:7) as a forgery. As a hymnodist, Luther wrote ~36 hymns (e.g., "A Mighty Fortress," "From Heaven Above"), fusing high art and folk styles for congregational singing in worship, school, and home. His hymns became catechetical tools and inspired composers like J.S. Bach.

### Later Controversies
**Antinomianism (1537–40):** Johannes Agricola taught the Law (Ten Commandments) need not be preached to Christians. Luther countered that the Law’s "second use" (convicting sin) and "third use" (guiding Christian life) remain essential; removing the Law implies Christians are sinless.
**Philip of Hesse’s Bigamy (1539–40):** Luther reluctantly advised the Landgrave to marry secretly rather than divorce, damaging Luther’s reputation when the scandal broke.
**Antisemitism (1543):** After failed conversion efforts, Luther published *On the Jews and Their Lies*, denouncing Jews as "the devil’s people," urging synagogue burning, property seizure, and expulsion. He did not advocate murder, but historians debate whether his "sharp mercy" rhetoric sanctioned violence and influenced later Nazi ideology.
**Islam:** Luther viewed the Ottomans as God’s scourge against the papal Antichrist; he supported secular defense but opposed "Holy War," urging spiritual resistance via prayer. He criticized the Qur’an but opposed its censorship.

### Theology of the Soul and Death
Contra Calvin and Melanchthon, Luther held the soul "sleeps" peacefully after death (psychopannychism), rejecting purgatory and conscious torment for the saints, while affirming personal identity and resurrection. He interpreted Genesis passages on "waking" souls as referring to dream-interrupted sleep in this life.

### Final Years
Luther suffered Ménière’s disease, kidney stones, arthritis, and angina. His health worsened his irascibility. His last sermon (Eisleben, 15 Feb 1546) urged Jewish expulsion. He died 18 Feb 1546 in Eisleben, negotiating a mining settlement for his siblings. His final note: "We are beggars. This is true."

## Terms
- ****Justification**** — God’s forensic act of declaring a sinner righteous solely by grace through faith in Christ, imputing Christ’s alien righteousness (monergism), not by human merit or infused righteousness (synergism).
- ****Indulgence**** — A papal grant remitting the temporal punishment due for sins already forgiven; sale of plenary indulgences triggered Luther’s protest.
- ****Sola Fide / Sola Gratia**** — "Faith alone / Grace alone": Luther’s shorthand for justification apart from works.
- ****Sacramental Union**** — Luther’s Eucharistic doctrine: Christ’s true body and blood are really present "in, with, and under" the bread and wine via the ubiquity of Christ’s glorified human nature.
- ****Anfechtung**** — Spiritual trial/despair; Luther’s monastic experience of terror before God’s judgment.
- ****Monergism**** — Salvation effected by God alone (contra *synergism*, human cooperation with grace).
- ****Catechism**** — Instructional summary of doctrine (Decalogue, Creed, Lord’s Prayer, Sacraments) in question-answer format for memorization and understanding.
- ****Antinomianism**** — View that Christians are freed from obligation to the moral Law (Ten Commandments); Luther opposed this as confusing Law’s convicting and guiding uses.
- ****Psychopannychism**** — "Soul sleep": the dead rest unconsciously until resurrection; Luther’s view vs. Calvinist immediate glorification.
- ****Two Kingdoms**** — Luther’s doctrine: God rules the spiritual kingdom (church, gospel) and temporal kingdom (state, law) distinctly; Christians owe obedience to both.

## Debates and open questions
- **Nailing of the Theses:** Whether Luther physically posted the Ninety-five Theses on the Castle Church door (per Melanchthon) or only mailed them (per modern critics like Krämer et al.).
- **"Here I stand" at Worms:** The famous peroration appears only in later printed versions, not contemporary transcripts; scholars dispute its authenticity.
- **Antisemitism and Nazism:** Whether Luther’s theological anti-Judaism (religious, conversionist) directly fed racial Nazi antisemitism, or whether Nazis opportunistically appropriated a marginalized corpus ignored in the 18th–19th centuries (Wallmann, Bainton vs. Probst, Goldhagen).
- **Peasants' War Responsibility:** Whether Luther’s harsh polemic (*Against the Murderous Hordes*) incited princely slaughter or merely recognized the rebellion’s threat to civil order.
- **Philip of Hesse Bigamy:** Whether Luther’s private pastoral concession (secret bigamy > public divorce) constituted a moral failure or a pragmatic *casuistry* misjudged politically.
- **Faith and Reason:** Whether Luther was a fideist (reason "enemy of faith") or sought to demarcate distinct epistemological spheres (reason for worldly knowledge, faith for revelation).
- **Eucharistic Ubiquity:** Whether Luther’s *sacramental union* (Christ’s human nature ubiquitous) coheres with Chalcedonian Christology or risks Eutychianism (confusion of natures).
