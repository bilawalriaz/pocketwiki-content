# Second Bulgarian Empire

## Overview
The Second Bulgarian Empire (1185–1422) was a medieval Balkan state, successor to the First Bulgarian Empire. At its height under Tsars Kaloyan and Ivan Asen II, it dominated the Balkans, defeating the Byzantine and Latin Empires. Its decline stemmed from internal strife, feudal fragmentation, and invasions by Mongols, Hungarians, Serbs, and finally the Ottomans, who conquered it in the late 14th century.

## Timeline
- **1185** — Brothers Theodore and Asen lead a rebellion, re-establishing Bulgarian independence from Byzantium.
- **1197-1207** — Reign of Tsar Kaloyan; defeats the Latin Empire at Adrianople (1205) and gains papal recognition.
- **1218-1241** — Reign of Tsar Ivan Asen II; empire reaches peak power after the Battle of Klokotnitsa (1230).
- **1241** — Death of Ivan Asen II begins a long period of decline and instability.
- **1277-1280** — Popular uprising led by Ivaylo briefly disrupts the nobility and defeats Mongol and Byzantine forces.
- **1300** — Theodore Svetoslav ends Mongol interference, stabilizing the empire for a recovery period.
- **1331-1371** — Reign of Ivan Alexander; final cultural flourishing but also the first major Ottoman attacks.
- **1371** — Death of Ivan Alexander leaves the empire divided between his sons Ivan Shishman (Tarnovo) and Ivan Sratsimir (Vidin).
- **1393** — Ottomans capture the capital Tarnovo after a three-month siege.
- **1396** — Ottomans capture Vidin, effectively ending the medieval Bulgarian state.

## Body

### Rebellion and Rise
Founded in 1185 by aristocrat brothers Theodore (crowned Peter IV) and Asen, the empire rallied Bulgarians against Byzantine rule after a tax dispute. They declared Tarnovo the capital and secured Cuman tribal support. After initial setbacks, they won a decisive victory at the Tryavna Pass (1190), capturing the Byzantine imperial treasury. Ivan Asen I consolidated power but was murdered in 1196. His youngest brother, Kaloyan, succeeded him, securing a papal coronation in 1204 that made him "King" in a purely political union with Rome. He then defeated the Latin Empire at Adrianople (1205), capturing Emperor Baldwin I and making Bulgaria the preeminent Balkan power.

### Peak and Decline
Kaloyan's murder in 1207 led to instability under Boril. Stability was restored by Ivan Asen II, who reclaimed territories and defeated the Despotate of Epiros at Klokotnitsa (1230), extending Bulgarian influence from the Adriatic to the Black Sea. After his death in 1241, the empire declined due to weak rulers, civil wars, and invasions. A major peasant uprising led by Ivaylo (1277–1280) briefly disrupted this decline but did not reverse it. A temporary stabilization occurred under Theodore Svetoslav (r. 1300–1322), who ended Mongol interference and restored some prosperity.

### Fall to the Ottomans
The 14th century saw a final cultural flourishing but also fatal divisions. Emperor Ivan Alexander (r. 1331–1371) faced early Ottoman raids and, after his death, left the empire split between his sons Ivan Shishman in Tarnovo and Ivan Sratsimir in Vidin. The brothers failed to unite against the common threat. After the Ottoman victory at Chernomen (1371), they conquered Thrace and forced Ivan Shishman into vassalage. Renewed invasions captured Sofia (1382) and, after a siege, Tarnovo (1393). Ivan Shishman was executed in 1395, and Vidin fell in 1396. Resistance continued until 1422.

### Administration and Society
The state was a hereditary monarchy ruled by a Tsar, whose absolute power depended on personal ability. Administration followed Byzantine models, with titles like *great logothete* (first minister). The empire was divided into provinces governed by appointed dukes. Society comprised clergy, nobility (*bolyars*), and peasants. Feudal fragmentation increased in the 14th century, with nobles gaining semi-independent control.

### Military and Economy
The army, commanded by the Tsar and a *velik voivoda*, was highly mobile, using Balkan terrain for defense. It relied on light cavalry, including Cuman and later Mongol mercenaries. The economy was based on agriculture, mining, and crafts. Trade flourished, with exports like honey, wax, and silk. Coin minting peaked under Ivan Alexander but declined with central authority.

### Religion and Culture
The state prioritized restoring the autocephalous Bulgarian Patriarchate. After Constantinople's fall (1204), Tarnovo became an Eastern Orthodox center. The union with Rome (1205–1235) was a political tool. In the 14th century, the mystical Hesychast movement became dominant, promoted by figures like Theodosius of Tarnovo and Patriarch Euthymius. The Tarnovo Literary School, founded by Euthymius, produced linguistic reform and rich literature. After the Ottoman conquest, Bulgarian scholars and texts spread to Serbia, Wallachia, and Russia, significantly influencing their cultures.

## Terms
- **Tsar**: Bulgarian title for Emperor, originating in the First Bulgarian Empire.
- **Bolyar**: Member of the Bulgarian nobility, subdivided into greater and lesser ranks.
- **Hesychasm**: A mystical tradition of prayer in Eastern Orthodox Christianity that flourished in 14th-century Bulgaria.
- **Tarnovo Artistic School**: A distinct school of painting and architecture (13th-14th centuries) characterized by realism and decorative ornamentation.
- **Pronoia**: A grant of land and revenue from the state in exchange for military service, a Byzantine concept adopted in Bulgaria.
- **Cuman**: A Turkic nomadic people from the steppes north of the Danube who provided crucial military support to the Bulgarian rebels.
- **Latin Empire**: The state established by the leaders of the Fourth Crusade after they conquered Constantinople in 1204.
- **Despotate of Epiros**: A Byzantine successor state in the western Balkans that was a major rival to Bulgaria in the early 13th century.
- **Hesychast**: A follower of the Hesychast tradition of mystical prayer.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact nature and extent of the union between Bulgaria and the Papacy under Kaloyan is debated; it was primarily a political alliance, but its practical implications for the Bulgarian Church are discussed.
- The degree of autonomy and the precise relationship between the Bulgarian Patriarchate and the Ecumenical Patriarchate of Constantinople, especially after the Council of Lampsacus (1235), remains a point of scholarly discussion.
- The causes and social dynamics of the popular uprising led by Ivaylo (1277–1280) are analyzed as both a social revolt and a military response to foreign threats.
