# Rafael Trujillo

## Overview
Rafael Leónidas Trujillo Molina was a Dominican military officer and dictator who ruled the Dominican Republic from 1930 until his assassination in 1961. His 31-year regime, known as the "Age of Trujillo," was one of the longest and most brutal non-royal dictatorships in the world, characterized by a pervasive personality cult, state terrorism, and the systematic elimination of opposition. Trujillo's rule profoundly shaped the Dominican Republic's politics, economy, and international relations, leaving a legacy of both stability and terror.

## Timeline
- **24 October 1891** — Born in San Cristóbal, Dominican Republic.
- **1918** — Joins the newly created National Guard during the U.S. occupation.
- **3 March 1930** — Becomes head of the army and police after a coup, following a deal with rebel leader Rafael Estrella Ureña.
- **16 August 1930** — Inaugurated as president after a fraudulent election, assuming dictatorial powers.
- **October 1937** — Orders the Parsley Massacre, killing an estimated 17,000 to 35,000 Haitians.
- **1940** — Signs the Hull–Trujillo Treaty, ending U.S. control over Dominican customs revenues.
- **24 June 1960** — Regime attempts to assassinate Venezuelan President Rómulo Betancourt, leading to OAS sanctions.
- **25 November 1960** — The Mirabal sisters, prominent opponents, are murdered.
- **30 May 1961** — Assassinated by a group of conspirators.
- **November 1961** — The Trujillo family is pressured into exile.
- **1966** — The Dominican Republic stabilizes under a multi-party system.

## Body

### Rise to Power and Consolidation
Trujillo's political ascent began during the U.S. occupation of the Dominican Republic (1916-1924). He joined the U.S.-created National Guard in 1918 and rose through the ranks with American support. By 1924, he was commander of the National Police. In 1930, he exploited a coup against President Horacio Vásquez by secretly allying with the rebel leader, allowing the capital to fall while he remained neutral. This maneuver secured him the presidency in a sham election. He immediately established a one-party state with the Dominican Party, where membership was mandatory and enforced by his security forces.

### The Trujillo Regime: Cult and Control
Trujillo's rule was defined by an extreme personality cult. The capital was renamed Ciudad Trujillo, and his image and slogans like "Dios y Trujillo" (God and Trujillo) were omnipresent. He maintained power through a vast network of repression, primarily via the Servicio de Inteligencia Militar (SIM), a secret police force. The SIM carried out murders, disappearances, and torture both domestically and abroad, targeting real and perceived enemies. Estimates of deaths under his regime range from 25,000 to over 50,000. His family amassed enormous wealth by monopolizing key industries like salt, sugar, and tobacco.

### Foreign Policy and International Isolation
Trujillo pursued a foreign policy that mixed cooperation with the United States and interference in neighboring states. He signed the Hull–Trujillo Treaty in 1940, regaining financial sovereignty. However, his regime's brutality, especially the 1937 Parsley Massacre of Haitians and later actions like the attempted assassination of Venezuelan President Rómulo Betancourt in 1960, led to international condemnation. The Organization of American States (OAS) imposed sanctions after the Betancourt incident. The murder of the Mirabal sisters in 1960 further eroded his international standing, particularly with the U.S.

### Assassination and Aftermath
On 30 May 1961, Trujillo was assassinated by a group of conspirators. His son, Ramfis, temporarily seized control and brutally hunted down the plotters. However, the Trujillo family's grip on power weakened. Under pressure from the military and the threat of U.S. intervention, the family was forced into exile by November 1961. The assassination triggered a period of civil strife, culminating in the Dominican Civil War and a U.S.-OAS intervention in 1965. The country eventually transitioned to a multi-party democracy in 1966.

## Terms
*   **Generalissimo**: The highest military rank, which Trujillo held from 1930, making him the de facto most powerful figure even when not president.
*   **SIM (Servicio de Inteligencia Militar)**: The Military Intelligence Service, Trujillo's secret police responsible for internal repression and international operations.
*   **Personality Cult**: An extreme public image of a leader as an infallible, god-like figure, enforced through propaganda, symbols, and mandatory adulation.
*   **Parsley Massacre (La Masacre del Perejil)**: The 1937 mass killing of Haitians and Dominicans of Haitian descent along the border, ordered by Trujillo.
*   **Hull–Trujillo Treaty**: A 1940 agreement where the U.S. relinquished control over Dominican customs revenues, restoring the country's financial sovereignty.
*   **OAS (Organization of American States)**: An international organization that imposed diplomatic and economic sanctions on the Trujillo regime after the 1960 Betancourt assassination attempt.
*   **Figurehead President**: A nominal head of state who holds no real power, a role Trujillo used to maintain a facade of normalcy while ruling as generalissimo.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Extent of U.S. Involvement in Assassination**: The CIA's role is debated. While a 1975 report claimed "no active part," it acknowledged supplying weapons to the plotters and having "quite extensive Agency involvement." Internal documents suggest the U.S. moved to make Trujillo "expendable" as it rallied against Castro in Cuba.
*   **Legacy Evaluation**: Trujillo remains polarizing. Supporters credit him with bringing stability, economic growth, and doubling life expectancy. Critics emphasize the regime's unparalleled brutality, nepotism, corruption, and the xenophobic violence of the Parsley Massacre.
*   **Scale of Repression**: Precise death tolls are uncertain. Estimates for overall regime killings range from 25,000 to over 50,000, while the Parsley Massacre alone is estimated to have killed between 17,000 and 35,000 people.
