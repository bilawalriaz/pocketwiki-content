# Southeast Asia

## Overview
Southeast Asia is a geographically and culturally diverse region of Asia, encompassing approximately 4.5 million km² and home to roughly 676 million people. It lies between the Indian subcontinent, East Asia, and Oceania, and is divided into Mainland Southeast Asia and Maritime Southeast Asia. The region has been shaped by major historical forces including Indianization, the spread of Islam, European colonization, and decolonization after World War II. Today, it is politically unified through the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), established for regional cooperation.

## Body

### Geography and Geology
Southeast Asia spans about 4.5 million km², making up 9.8% of Asia’s landmass. It is geologically active, situated at the convergence of the Sunda and Philippine Sea plates, resulting in frequent earthquakes and volcanic eruptions. The region includes both Mainland Southeast Asia (entirely in the Northern Hemisphere) and Maritime Southeast Asia (partly in the Southern Hemisphere). Notable geographical features include the Mekong River, the South China Sea, and mountainous regions like those in New Guinea. The Wallace Line demarcates the boundary between Asian and Australasian fauna.

### Prehistory and Early Migrations
The region was inhabited by Homo erectus as early as 1.5 million years ago. Modern humans arrived between 50,000 and 70,000 BC, bringing with them advanced tools and rock art found in Sulawesi and Borneo. During periods of lower sea levels, Sundaland connected much of western Indonesia to the Malay Peninsula. Genetic studies suggest that Basal-East Eurasian populations originated in Mainland Southeast Asia around 50,000 BC, contributing to the ancestry of both Oceanian and East Asian populations.

### Hindu and Buddhist Kingdoms
From c. 400 BCE, Indian cultural influence spread throughout Southeast Asia, leading to the rise of Hindu and Buddhist kingdoms. The Pyu city-states in Myanmar and the Funan confederation in the Mekong Delta were among the earliest Indianized states. The Srivijaya Empire dominated maritime trade from the 7th to 13th centuries, while the Khmer Empire reached its height between the 11th and 13th centuries, constructing monumental cities like Angkor. Theravada Buddhism became prominent in mainland Southeast Asia, while Mahayana Buddhism took root in maritime regions.

### Spread of Islam
Islam began spreading into Southeast Asia from the 8th century via trade networks, gaining momentum by the 13th century. The conversion of rulers in Kedah (1136), Samudera Pasai (1267), and Malacca (15th century) accelerated Islamic influence. The Malacca Sultanate became a center of Islamic learning and commerce. Trade played a crucial role, with Muslim merchants from Arabia, Persia, India, and China integrating into local societies. By the 16th century, Islam had become a dominant force in much of Maritime Southeast Asia.

### Colonial Era and Decolonization
European powers arrived in the 16th century, initially for trade but eventually establishing colonial control. The Portuguese captured Malacca in 1511, the Spanish colonized the Philippines, the Dutch controlled the East Indies, and the British expanded in Burma and Malaya. Thailand remained independent. World War II disrupted colonial rule when Japan occupied much of the region. Post-war decolonization led to independence movements, culminating in the formation of ASEAN in 1967 to promote regional stability and integration.

### Contemporary Issues
Today, Southeast Asia faces challenges such as territorial disputes in the South China Sea, economic disparities, and environmental threats like deforestation and climate change. ASEAN serves as a platform for diplomatic dialogue and economic cooperation. Countries vary widely in governance, from democracies to authoritarian regimes, and in economic development, from emerging markets like Indonesia to high-income economies like Singapore.

## Terms
- **Sunda Plate**: A tectonic plate underlying most of Southeast Asia, excluding northern Indochina and the Philippines.
- **Wallace Line**: A biogeographical boundary separating Asian and Australasian species, running between Borneo and Sulawesi.
- **Indianization**: The process by which Indian culture, religion, and political systems influenced Southeast Asian societies starting around 400 BCE.
- **Austronesian Expansion**: A prehistoric migration beginning around 5,000 years ago, spreading Austronesian languages and seafaring culture from Taiwan across the Pacific and Indian Oceans.
- **Sundaland**: A Pleistocene landmass connecting mainland Southeast Asia with western Indonesian islands during periods of low sea levels.
- **Monsoon**: Seasonal wind patterns affecting rainfall and agriculture in Southeast Asia.
- **ASEAN**: The Association of Southeast Asian Nations, founded in 1967, promoting economic, political, and cultural cooperation among 11 member states.
- **Chola Invasion**: A 1025 CE naval expedition by the Chola Empire that weakened the Srivijaya Empire in Sumatra.
- **Negritos**: Indigenous peoples of Southeast Asia, characterized by dark skin and short stature, considered among the earliest inhabitants.
- **Coral Triangle**: A marine region encompassing Indonesia, the Philippines, and Papua New Guinea, known for exceptional biodiversity.

## Debates and Open Questions
Several unresolved issues persist regarding Southeast Asia’s past and future. The exact timing and routes of early human migrations remain debated, particularly concerning the extent of gene flow between Mainland and Maritime Southeast Asia. Scholars continue to discuss the relative roles of trade versus conquest in the spread of Islam, with some emphasizing peaceful conversion and others highlighting political motivations. The classification of certain territories—such as whether Taiwan, Hong Kong, or parts of southern China should be included in broader definitions of Southeast Asia—is contested. Additionally, the effectiveness of ASEAN in addressing regional security concerns, especially the South China Sea disputes, remains a topic of scholarly debate. Environmental degradation and climate change present urgent challenges, with ongoing discussions about balancing economic growth with conservation efforts.
