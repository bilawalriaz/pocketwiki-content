# Sappho

## Overview
Sappho (c. 630 – c. 570 BC) was an aristocratic Greek poet from Lesbos, celebrated in antiquity as the "Tenth Muse" for her lyric poetry composed to be sung with musical accompaniment. Though she likely produced around 10,000 lines, only approximately 650 survive, mostly as fragments preserved on papyrus or quoted by later authors; the *Ode to Aphrodite* is the sole certainly complete poem. Her work is distinguished by its clarity, vivid imagery, emotional immediacy, and pioneering use of the first-person "lyric I" to explore desire, jealousy, and female experience. Sappho’s poetry was canonized by Alexandrian scholars among the Nine Lyric Poets and profoundly influenced later Greek and Roman writers, including Catullus and Horace. In modern times, she is an iconic figure for female literary authority and homoerotic desire—the terms *sapphic* and *lesbian* derive from her name and home island—though the nature of her sexuality and the social context of her performances (whether educational, religious, or sympotic) remain subjects of intense scholarly debate.

## Timeline
- **c. 630 BC** — Traditional birth date on Lesbos (Eresos or Mytilene); David Campbell suggests c. 630 BC, Franco Ferrari c. 650–640 BC.
- **c. 600 BC** — Exiled to Sicily (per Parian Chronicle, between 604–595 BC), likely due to aristocratic factional strife on Lesbos.
- **c. 570 BC** — Approximate end of poetic activity (Gregory Hutchinson).
- **3rd–2nd c. BC** — Alexandrian scholars (Aristophanes of Byzantium, possibly Aristarchus of Samothrace) produce a critical edition in at least eight books, organized by metre.
- **c. 200 BC** — Earliest surviving manuscripts (potsherds, papyri) date to this period.
- **Late 3rd–early 2nd c. BC** — *Papyrus Oxyrhynchus 1800* records Sappho "accused by some of being... a woman-lover," the earliest explicit reference to homoeroticism.
- **1st c. BC** — Catullus adapts Sapphic stanza and fragments (esp. fr. 31) into Latin literature; Sappho becomes a stock character in Roman comedy and art.
- **10th c. AD** — *Suda* encyclopedia records biography, including spurious husband Kerkylas of Andros and claim she was "slanderously accused" of relations with female pupils.
- **1508** — Aldus Manutius prints Dionysius of Halicarnassus, including the *Ode to Aphrodite* (fr. 1), making Sappho widely accessible in print.
- **1879** — First modern papyrus fragment discovered at Fayum; Grenfell and Hunt later excavate Oxyrhynchus, yielding hundreds of fragments.
- **2004** — Discovery of the "Tithonus poem" (fr. 58) and a new fragment on Cologne papyrus.
- **2014** — Publication of "Brothers Poem" and fragments of nine poems (five known with new readings, four previously unknown).

## Body

### Ancient Sources and Biographical Uncertainty
Modern knowledge of Sappho derives from her fragmentary poetry—the only contemporary source—and later ancient testimonies. The earliest biography dates to the late 2nd or early 3rd century AD, roughly eight centuries after her death; the *Suda* (10th century) is the next major source. Earlier references exist, notably Herodotus (5th century BC) on her brother Charaxos and the courtesan Rhodopis. Ancient biographers inferred life details from her poems, assuming first-person narration was autobiographical, and from local traditions possibly contaminated by Athenian comedy. Since the 19th century, scholars have grown increasingly skeptical of biographical readings of lyric poetry; Mary Lefkowitz argues almost nothing can be known, while most hold that ancient testimonies contain kernels of truth but require extreme caution.

### Life and Family
Sappho was born into an aristocratic Lesbian family in the late 7th century BC. Her father’s name is uncertain (ten variants recorded; Scamandronymus most common); her mother may have been Cleïs. She had three brothers: Charaxos, Larichos (praised by Athenaeus as a cupbearer for noble families), and Eurygios. The 2014 "Brothers Poem" names Charaxos and Larichos. Herodotus reports Charaxos ransomed the Egyptian courtesan Rhodopis, prompting a rebuking poem from Sappho. She may have had a daughter, Cleïs, mentioned in fragments 98 and 132, though scholars debate whether *pais* (child/youthful beloved) and *agapata* (beloved) denote a daughter or a younger lover. The *Suda* claims she married Kerkylas of Andros, but the name appears to be a comic invention (*kerkos* = "penis"; *aner* = "man"). The Parian Chronicle records exile to Sicily c. 600 BC, likely tied to political conflicts involving the tyrant Myrsilus and her contemporary Alcaeus. The legend of her suicide by leaping from the Leucadian cliffs for the ferryman Phaon is considered ahistorical, possibly originating from comic poets or a misreading of a mythological poem, and was later used to assert her heterosexuality.

### Works: Transmission, Survival, and Loss
Sappho composed roughly 10,000 lines in Aeolic dialect across lyric, elegiac, and iambic forms (the latter two attested only in the *Suda*; three extant epigrams are Hellenistic imitations). It is unclear if she wrote poems down for posterity or as performance aids. Athenian publishers produced copies in the 5th century BC; Alexandrian scholars later created a standard edition in at least eight books organized by metre (Book 1: Sapphic stanzas, alphabetical). After the codex replaced the papyrus scroll (c. 3rd–4th century AD), her works were not systematically recopied, likely due to waning demand and the difficulty of her Aeolic dialect (noted as "strange" by Apuleius in the 2nd century AD). By the 12th century, John Tzetzes declared her works destroyed by time. Renaissance legends blamed the Church (e.g., Gregory Nazianzen, Pope Gregory VII), but the loss was primarily material and cultural. Only ~650 lines survive: one complete poem (*Ode to Aphrodite*), ~10 substantial fragments, and many single-word citations. Major sources are quotations in ancient authors and Oxyrhynchus papyri (excavated from 1890s onward). Key modern discoveries: 2004 (Tithonus poem), 2014 (Brothers Poem, nine poems total).

### Style and Performance
Sappho worked within a developed Lesbian tradition (Terpander, mythic Orpheus/Arion) using Aeolic metre, distinct for its fixed syllable count. She pioneered the "lyric I," shifting from the epic pose of divine conduit to individual subjectivity exploring desire, jealousy, and love. Her style features clarity, sharp imagery, direct quotation for immediacy, wordplay (e.g., fr. 96 adapting Homer’s "rosy-fingered Dawn" to "rose-fingered moon"), and hyperbole. She evokes an aristocratic *habrosyne* (luxury/refinement), referencing Lydia, golden cups, and "finer things" (Tithonus poem), contrasting with later "austere" classical style (Page duBois). Performance contexts are debated: the best-preserved fragments (e.g., *Ode to Aphrodite*) suggest solo monody, which she pioneered with Alcaeus, but André Lardinois argues for choral performance. Leslie Kurke notes the "private, informal" nature of major fragments lacks specific occasion markers, facilitating reperformance. She also composed wedding songs (*epithalamia*), cult hymns, narrative, and invective.

### Music
Poems were sung, but original music is lost; notation likely post-dated her. Aristoxenus credited her with the mixolydian mode (sorrowful, used in tragedy), though invention is unlikely. She presumably varied modes. Early performance likely used one note per syllable (melismata developed later). Accompaniment included *pektis* (Lydian harp), lyre, and *barbitos* (deep-pitched lyre), which she is iconically associated with (depicted on 5th-century vases). The *aulos* (double pipe) appears in fr. 44 for a Trojan wedding, not her own songs. Later commentators wrongly credited her with inventing the plectrum.

### Social Context: Teacher, Priestess, or Symposiast?
No single context fits all fragments. The 19th-century "schoolmistress" theory (Wilamowitz) aimed to "explain away" homoeroticism; Denys Page (1959) found "no trace of any formal... relationship" or academy. Campbell (1967) allowed a "literary coterie" but no formal post. The earliest source for teaching is Ovid (6 centuries later). Mid-20th century scholars proposed ritual education/choral training for girls (Lardinois finds this most plausible). The "priestess of Aphrodite" theory lacks evidence despite her hymns. Recent proposals place her in women’s symposia or writing for men’s symposia, but no external evidence exists for archaic women’s symposia, and cultic/family poems fit poorly. Yatromanolakis argues no single context covers all poems; Neri suggests she composed in multiple roles (pedagogic, friendly circle).

### Sexuality: The "Great Sappho Question"
In Athenian comedy, Sappho was caricatured as promiscuously *heterosexual*. The earliest explicit homoerotic accusation appears in *P. Oxy. 1800* (Hellenistic): "accused by some of being... a woman-lover." The *Suda* (10th c.) calls this "slander." Early modern translators heterosexualized her (Philips 1711 made the *Ode*’s object male; Verri 1781 read fr. 31 as love for Phaon). Welcker (1816) deemed her feelings "idealistic and non-sensual"; Müller called fr. 31 "friendly affection." By 1970, Devereux saw "proof positive of lesbianism." Today, most accept her poetry "clearly celebrates *eros* between women" (Boehringer). However, late 20th-century scholars (Most, Lardinois, duBois) question the category "lesbian" for antiquity: Sappho "would have had no idea what people mean" (Most); the debate is "nonsensical" (Lardinois) or "obfuscating" (duBois). Foucault-influenced scholars reject projecting modern sexuality; others argue lesbianism is transhistorical. Mueller suggests reading her poetry as "queer" regardless of the undecidable biographical label.

### Legacy
**Ancient:** Canonized as "Tenth Muse" (Dioscorides, 3rd c. BC) and "The Poetess" alongside Homer. Included in the Alexandrian Nine Lyric Poets. Solon allegedly wished to learn a song before dying (Aelian). Influenced Plato (*Phaedrus* echoes fr. 31), Hellenistic poets (Nossis as inheritor, Theocritus, Erinna, Callimachus), and Roman literature: Catullus adopted the Sapphic stanza, named his lover "Lesbia," and translated fr. 31 (Cat. 51); fr. 31 echoed in Lucretius, Plautus, Virgil, Horace, Juvenal, Statius. She starred in at least six comedies (promiscuous heterosexual) and was the most depicted poet on Attic vases (never singing). From the 4th c. BC, the Phaon suicide myth dominated (Menander, Ovid *Heroides* 15, art). Horace called her *mascula Sappho*; by the 3rd c. AD, Aelian posited two Sapphos (poetess vs. courtesan) to split literary fame from moral infamy.

**Modern:** Works lost by Middle Ages; rediscovered via printed quotations (Aldus 1508, Estienne 1566). First English translation 1652 (Hall). Bergk’s 1854 edition standard until papyrus finds (Lobel-Page 1955). Revered as "extraordinary" since the 9th century; Boccaccio and Christine de Pizan celebrated her as a learned lady. Renaissance *La Pléiade* imitated Sapphic stanzas and female-voiced love poetry. Romantics (Tennyson, Housman) and Imagists (Pound, H.D., Aldington) drew heavily on fragments. Composers set her to music (Saint-Saëns, Xenakis, Antoniou) and dramatized the Phaon myth (Mayr, Gounod, Pacini, Glanville-Hicks). 19th-century debate on sexuality (Welcker vs. Decadents Baudelaire/Swinburne) shifted to lesbian iconography (Michael Field, Amy Levy; "patron saint of lesbians" by 1900). Feminists (Hemans, Landon, Norton) claimed her as progenitor and "New Woman" model. 2004 and 2014 discoveries generated global media attention ("trouvaille of a lifetime," "greatest for 92 years").

## Terms
- ****Lyric poetry**** — Poetry composed to be sung to the accompaniment of a lyre (or other instruments), typically expressing personal emotion; distinct from epic or choral forms.
- ****Sapphic stanza**** — A metrical form of four lines (three long, one short *adonic*), believed in antiquity to be invented by Sappho; adopted by Catullus and Horace in Latin.
- ****Aeolic dialect**** — The Greek dialect of Lesbos and nearby coast; Sappho’s use of it made her poetry difficult for later Attic-standard readers, contributing to its loss.
- ****Lyric "I"**** — The first-person poetic voice presenting a specific individual’s subjective experience, pioneered by Sappho in contrast to the epic narrator as divine conduit.
- ****Monody**** — Solo song with a single melodic line; Sappho and Alcaeus pioneered this departure from multi-part choral performance.
- ****Mixolydian mode**** — A musical mode reported by Aristoxenus as used by Sappho, considered sorrowful and later standard in Greek tragedy.
- ****Barbitos**** — A deep-pitched, lyre-like string instrument of Lydian origin, closely associated with Sappho in poetry and vase painting.
- ****Habrosyne**** — A Greek concept of luxury, refinement, and elegance; a hallmark of the "élite" poetic tradition Sappho embodies (e.g., "I love the finer things").
- ****Epithalamium**** — A wedding song; Sappho’s choral *epithalamia* survive better than her cult hymns.
- ****Oxyrhynchus**** — Egyptian site where Grenfell and Hunt excavated a massive rubbish dump (late 19th c.), yielding the largest cache of Sappho papyri.

## Debates and open questions
- **Biographical reliability:** To what extent do her poems reflect autobiography vs. persona? (Lefkowitz: near-zero; majority: cautious kernels of truth).
- **Performance context:** Was she a teacher/choral trainer, priestess, symposiast, or leader of a female circle? No single role explains all fragments (Yatromanolakis); multiple contexts likely (Neri).
- **Sexuality categorization:** Does "lesbian" apply to an archaic Greek figure? Foucaultians say no (anachronistic category); essentialists say yes (transhistorical desire); queer theorists (Mueller) read the poetry as queer regardless of label.
- **Alexandrian edition structure:** Were there eight or nine books? Page conjectures a ninth; Yatromanolakis doubts it (no ancient source mentions a ninth).
- **Family relations:** Was Cleïs her daughter or beloved? (Hallett argues *agapata* implies daughter; others note *pais* can mean youthful male beloved).
- **Cause of loss:** Primarily material (codex transition, dialect difficulty) vs. moral suppression (Renaissance legend).
