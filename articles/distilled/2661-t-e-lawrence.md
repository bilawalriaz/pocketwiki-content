# T. E. Lawrence

## Overview
T.E. Lawrence (1888–1935) was a British archaeologist, army officer, and writer who became an international celebrity as a liaison to Arab forces during the Arab Revolt against the Ottoman Empire in World War I. His vivid self-account, *Seven Pillars of Wisdom*, and sensationalized media coverage cemented his legendary status as "Lawrence of Arabia," making him a complex symbol of British involvement in the modern Middle East.

## Timeline
- **16 Aug 1888** — Born in Tremadog, Wales, as the illegitimate son of Sir Thomas Chapman and Sarah Junner.
- **1907–1910** — Reads history at Jesus College, Oxford, graduating with First Class Honours.
- **1910–1914** — Works as an archaeologist at Carchemish in Ottoman Syria.
- **Oct 1914** — Joins the British Army as an interpreter; posted to the Arab Bureau in Cairo.
- **Oct 1916** — Sent to the Hejaz on an intelligence mission; meets Emir Faisal.
- **Jul 1917** — Leads the successful overland attack to capture the port of Aqaba.
- **Oct 1918** — Enters Damascus after its capture, helping establish a provisional Arab government.
- **1919–1920** — Attends the Paris Peace Conference as an adviser to Faisal's delegation.
- **1922–1935** — Retreats from public life, serving as an enlisted man in the RAF and Royal Tank Corps.
- **1926** — Publishes *Seven Pillars of Wisdom*.
- **19 May 1935** — Dies from injuries sustained in a motorcycle crash in Dorset.

## Body

### Early Life and Archaeology
Born the second of five illegitimate sons, Lawrence's family settled in Oxford in 1896. He attended the City of Oxford High School for Boys and developed a keen interest in the medieval past. He read history at Jesus College, Oxford, producing a thesis on crusader military architecture. From 1910 to 1914, he worked as an archaeologist at Carchemish in Syria, gaining crucial knowledge of the Middle East. In early 1914, he and Leonard Woolley conducted a covert military survey of the Negev desert for the British War Office.

### World War I and the Arab Revolt
After the war began, Lawrence joined the Arab Bureau in Cairo. He became a key advocate for supporting an Arab uprising against the Ottomans, led by Sharif Hussein of Mecca. In 1916, he was sent to the Hejaz, where he identified Emir Faisal as the revolt's most capable leader and became his primary British liaison. Lawrence helped devise a strategy of guerrilla warfare, attacking Ottoman railways and supply lines, notably the Hejaz Railway. His most famous military feat was the 1917 capture of Aqaba via a surprise overland attack. He participated in numerous raids and was promoted to lieutenant colonel. The revolt culminated in the capture of Damascus in October 1918, where Lawrence helped establish a short-lived Arab government under Faisal.

### Post-War Life and Retreat from Fame
Lawrence attended the Paris Peace Conference (1919) as part of Faisal's delegation but was disillusioned by the subsequent French and British division of the Middle East, which contradicted promises of Arab independence. The sensationalized lectures of American journalist Lowell Thomas had already made him a global celebrity, a fame he deeply resented. In 1922, he retreated from public life, enlisting in the Royal Air Force under the name "John Hume Ross." After being exposed, he enlisted in the Royal Tank Corps as "T.E. Shaw" before rejoining the RAF in 1925. He served until 1935, finding anonymity in the ranks and contributing to the development of high-speed air-sea rescue boats.

### Writings and Legacy
Lawrence's major work is *Seven Pillars of Wisdom* (1926), a richly detailed, subjective account of the Arab Revolt blending memoir, history, and strategic analysis. He published an abridged version, *Revolt in the Desert* (1927), as a best-seller, donating profits to charity. His posthumously published memoir *The Mint* offers a gritty portrayal of RAF enlisted life. His public image was shaped by Lowell Thomas's romanticized shows and later by David Lean's epic 1962 film *Lawrence of Arabia*. His life and writings have been the subject of intense scholarly debate regarding his truthfulness, motivations, and psychological state.

## Terms
- **Arab Bureau**: A British military intelligence unit in Cairo during WWI, focused on Arab affairs.
- **Guerrilla Warfare**: A form of irregular warfare where small, mobile groups use tactics like raids and ambushes against a larger, conventional army.
- **Hejaz Railway**: An Ottoman railway line from Damascus to Medina, a primary target for Lawrence's guerrilla attacks to disrupt enemy supply lines.
- **Liaison**: An officer who acts as a link between different military units or allied forces, in this case between the British and Arab armies.
- **Seven Pillars of Wisdom**: Lawrence's autobiographical account of the Arab Revolt, considered a classic of 20th-century literature.
- **Sykes–Picot Agreement**: A secret 1916 pact between Britain and France to divide Ottoman territories in the Middle East, contradicting promises made to Arab leaders.
- **Arab Revolt (1916–1918)**: An armed uprising by Arab forces against Ottoman rule, supported by Britain, which played a significant role in the Middle Eastern theatre of WWI.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Veracity of *Seven Pillars***: Scholars debate the extent of embellishment in Lawrence's account. While some claims have been disproven by his own notes, the overall reliability of his narrative remains contested.
- **The Dera'a Incident**: Lawrence's account of being captured, beaten, and sexually assaulted at Dera'a in 1917 is questioned by some historians. While no independent testimony exists, many biographers find it credible and believe it had profound psychological effects on him.
- **Sexuality and Masochism**: Lawrence's sexuality is a subject of extensive biographical speculation. He denied personal sexual experience, but evidence of masochism (arranged beatings) is well-established, with theories linking it to childhood experiences or war trauma.
- **The Aldington Controversy**: Richard Aldington's 1955 biography launched a sustained attack on Lawrence's character, accusing him of chronic lying and Francophobia. While many of Aldington's specific claims have been refuted, the controversy forced a more critical reassessment of the Lawrence legend.
