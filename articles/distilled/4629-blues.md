# Blues

## Overview
Blues is a foundational American music genre and form that originated among African Americans in the Deep South around the 1860s. It evolved from African musical traditions, spirituals, and work songs, expressing the hardships and culture of Black communities. Its characteristic structures, melodies, and emotional expression have profoundly influenced jazz, rhythm and blues, rock and roll, and popular music worldwide.

## Timeline
- **c. 1860s** — Blues origins begin among African Americans in the Deep South after the end of slavery.
- **1908** — First publication of blues sheet music.
- **1912** — Hart Wand's "Dallas Blues" becomes the first copyrighted blues composition.
- **1920** — Mamie Smith records "Crazy Blues," the first blues recording by an African-American singer.
- **1920s-30s** — Classic female blues singers (Ma Rainey, Bessie Smith) become major stars; country blues is recorded.
- **1940s** — Post-WWII migration leads to the rise of electric urban blues in cities like Chicago.
- **1950s** — Chicago electric blues (Muddy Waters, Howlin' Wolf) influences early rock and roll.
- **1960s** — British blues revival and American blues rock emerge, renewing interest in traditional blues.
- **1980s-present** — Resurgence of interest in traditional and contemporary blues styles, including "soul blues."

## Body

### Origins and Etymology
Blues emerged in the post-emancipation American South (1860s-1890s), a period coinciding with the transition from slavery to sharecropping. Its roots are in African musical traditions, particularly from the Sahel region, which favored stringed instruments and melismatic singing. Key African-derived elements include the call-and-response format, blue notes (flattened thirds, fifths, or sevenths), and the use of instruments like the banjo. The term "blues" likely derives from "blue devils," a phrase for melancholy, and was used in print to describe a sad state of mind by the mid-19th century.

### Lyrical and Musical Form
Early blues lyrics often formed loose narratives about personal and social hardships. The most common lyrical structure, the AAB pattern, was standardized in the early 20th century: a line is sung, repeated, and followed by a concluding line. Musically, the twelve-bar blues is the most common form, using a repeating chord progression (I-IV-V) in 4/4 time. Melodies draw on the minor pentatonic scale with blue notes, while rhythms feature shuffle patterns and a backbeat, creating a characteristic "groove."

### Early History and Pre-War Blues
The first blues recordings date from the 1920s, categorized as rural country blues and more polished urban blues. Country blues was often solo or duo acoustic music with regional styles like Delta blues (slide guitar) and Piedmont blues (ragtime fingerpicking). Urban blues featured classic female singers like Ma Rainey and Bessie Smith, who performed with bands in theaters and on "race records." Male urban performers like T-Bone Walker and boogie-woogie pianists also gained popularity.

### Post-WWII Electric Blues and Influence
The Great Migration of African Americans to northern cities after World War II led to the development of electric blues. Chicago became a central hub, with artists like Muddy Waters and Howlin' Wolf using amplified guitars, harmonica, and full bands. This style, recorded by labels like Chess Records, directly influenced the birth of rock and roll. In the 1960s, British bands like the Rolling Stones and American artists like the Paul Butterfield Blues Band sparked a blues revival, blending blues with rock to create blues rock.

### Contemporary Blues
Since the 1980s, blues has seen a resurgence in both traditional and contemporary forms. Artists like Stevie Ray Vaughan and Robert Cray brought blues to mainstream audiences in the 1980s. The genre continues to evolve, with contemporary performers exploring soul blues and blues rock, supported by dedicated labels, festivals, and awards like the Blues Music Awards.

## Terms
- **Blues Scale**: A musical scale characterized by the inclusion of "blue notes," typically the flattened third, fifth, and seventh degrees of a major scale.
- **Twelve-Bar Blues**: The most common chord progression in blues music, consisting of three chords (I, IV, V) played over a 12-measure sequence.
- **Call-and-Response**: A musical pattern where a phrase is answered by another phrase, rooted in African and African-American traditions.
- **Blue Notes**: Notes sung or played at a slightly lower pitch than the standard major scale for expressive, melancholic effect.
- **Shuffle**: A rhythmic pattern in blues and jazz where the beat is divided into triplets, creating a "swinging" feel.
- **Juke Joint**: An informal establishment featuring music, dancing, and gambling, important in the early development of blues.
- **Race Records**: A term used by the U.S. recording industry in the early 20th century to market music by and for African Americans.
- **Slide Guitar**: A guitar technique where a slide (bottleneck, knife blade) is used along the strings to create a gliding, vocal-like effect.
- **Boogie-Woogie**: A style of blues piano playing characterized by a repetitive, driving bass pattern in the left hand.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars debate the precise African origins of the blues, with some pointing to the Sahel region and Islamic musical influences, while others note the absence of certain African elements like polyrhythm and drumming. The social and economic reasons for the blues' emergence in the post-emancipation South are not fully known, though it is linked to the new freedoms and hardships of sharecropping. The genre's boundaries were fluid in its early years, with no clear division between blues and country music except for the performer's ethnicity.
