# Arnolfini Portrait

## Overview
The Arnolfini Portrait (1434) by Jan van Eyck is a seminal Early Netherlandish oil painting depicting a wealthy Italian merchant and his wife in a Bruges interior. It is celebrated for its revolutionary realism, complex symbolism, and technical mastery of oil glazes, making it a cornerstone of Western art history. The painting's precise meaning—whether a marriage record, a legal document, or a memorial—remains a subject of intense scholarly debate.

## Timeline
- **1434** — Painting created and dated by Jan van Eyck in Bruges.
- **Before 1516** — Acquired by Don Diego de Guevara, a Spanish courtier.
- **1516** — Given to Margaret of Austria; listed in her Mechelen inventory.
- **1530** — Inherited by Mary of Hungary; later passed to Philip II of Spain.
- **1813** — Likely taken during the Battle of Vitoria; later acquired by Colonel James Hay.
- **1842** — Purchased by the National Gallery, London, for £600.

## Body

### Technique and Style
Van Eyck used oil paint, applying multiple thin, translucent glazes to achieve intense color and luminous realism. The wet-in-wet (alla prima) technique allowed for subtle blending of light and shadow, creating three-dimensional forms. His meticulous rendering of textures—from fur to brass—and his sophisticated use of light from the window to define space were unprecedented. The painting's illusionism is heightened by details like the convex mirror, which reflects the room and two figures, possibly including the artist.

### Subject and Setting
The painting shows a man and a woman, believed to be Italian merchants from Lucca resident in Bruges, in an upstairs room that likely served as a reception area. Their rich, fur-lined clothing signifies wealth, as do the brass chandelier, oranges, and Oriental carpet. The room's details, like the single lit candle and the cherry tree outside, are rich with potential symbolic meaning. The famous inscription on the wall, "Johannes de eyck fuit hic 1434," acts as a signature and witness statement.

### Identity and Occasion
The subjects were long identified as Giovanni di Arrigo Arnolfini and his wife Jeanne Cenami, but archival research shows they married in 1447, after the painting's date. The man is now thought to be either Giovanni di Arrigo or his cousin Giovanni di Nicolao Arnolfini. The woman may be an undocumented first wife of Giovanni di Arrigo, a second wife of Giovanni di Nicolao, or possibly Giovanni di Nicolao's first wife, Costanza Trenta, who died by 1433. This has led to theories that the painting could be a memorial portrait.

### Scholarly Interpretations
Erwin Panofsky famously argued the painting is a unique form of marriage contract, with the signature and mirror figures serving as legal witnesses. He also proposed a system of "disguised symbolism" where everyday objects carry hidden meanings (e.g., the dog for fidelity, the single candle for the all-seeing eye of God). While the marriage contract theory is not universally accepted, Panofsky's symbolic analysis has been highly influential. Other scholars, like Edwin Hall, see a betrothal, while Margaret Carroll suggests it depicts a married couple formalizing a legal mandate for the wife to conduct business. Lorne Campbell of the National Gallery sees little need for special narrative content, viewing it as a straightforward, if elaborate, portrait.

## Terms
- **Oil glazes:** Thin, translucent layers of oil paint applied over an underpainting to build up depth, color, and luminosity.
- **Alla prima (wet-in-wet):** A painting technique where layers of wet paint are applied to previous wet layers, allowing for blending on the canvas.
- **Pentimenti:** Visible traces of an artist's alterations to a painting during its creation, often seen in underdrawings.
- **Disguised symbolism:** A theory, advanced by Panofsky, that objects in Early Netherlandish paintings have hidden religious or moral meanings disguised as realistic details.
- **Morganatic marriage:** A marriage between people of unequal social rank, which prevents the lower-ranking spouse and children from inheriting titles or property.
- **Fidem levare:** A gesture of oath-taking, where a hand is raised.
- **Fides manualis:** A gesture of consent, where hands are joined.
- **Pattens:** Overshoes worn to protect indoor footwear from outdoor elements.

## Debates and Open Questions
The central debate concerns the painting's purpose and the identity of its subjects. Key questions include:
1.  **What is the occasion?** Is it a marriage, a betrothal, a legal contract for business authority, or a memorial portrait for a deceased wife?
2.  **Who are the sitters?** The specific Arnolfini and his wife remain uncertain, with the memorial theory hinging on the identification of the woman as the deceased Costanza Trenta.
3.  **What is the role of symbolism?** Scholars debate the extent and nature of "disguised symbolism." Panofsky saw pervasive hidden meanings; Bedaux argued such symbolism cannot be proven if it aligns with contemporary reality; Harbison advocates for a "multivalent reading" where objects have multiple associations.
4.  **Is the woman pregnant?** Art historians generally agree her pose reflects contemporary fashion and ideals of fertility, not pregnancy, though the memorial theory gives new weight to the possibility of a symbolic reference to childbirth.
