# Saint Peter

## Overview
Saint Peter (born Simon bar Jonah, c. 1 BC – AD 64/68) was a Galilean fisherman who became one of Jesus’s Twelve Apostles and the most prominent leader of the early Christian church. The New Testament portrays him as the spokesman for the disciples, the first to confess Jesus as the Messiah, and the first witness to the resurrection. Catholic and Orthodox traditions regard him as the first bishop of Rome (pope) and bishop of Antioch, founding the churches of both cities. He is traditionally held to have been martyred by crucifixion in Rome under Emperor Nero (c. AD 64–68), requesting to be crucified upside down. While all ancient churches venerate him as a major saint, they disagree sharply on the authority of his successors: Catholics see him as the first pope with universal jurisdiction; Eastern Orthodox grant his successors a "primacy of honor" only; Protestants reject a papal connection entirely. Modern scholarship debates the historicity of his Roman episcopate and the authorship of the two New Testament epistles bearing his name.

## Timeline
- **c. 1 BC** — Born Simon bar Jonah in Bethsaida, Galilee.
- **c. AD 27–30** — Called by Jesus; renamed Cephas (Aramaic) / Petros (Greek), meaning "rock"; becomes leading disciple.
- **c. AD 30** — Denies Jesus three times during Passion; first to witness risen Christ (per 1 Cor 15); restored by Jesus (John 21).
- **c. AD 30–44** — Leads Jerusalem church; preaches to Gentiles after vision (Acts 10); imprisoned by Herod Agrippa, escapes.
- **c. AD 49** — Incident at Antioch: Paul rebukes Peter for separating from Gentile believers (Gal 2:11–14).
- **c. AD 42–67 (traditional)** — Travels to Rome; founds church with Paul; serves as bishop for 25 years (per Jerome/Eusebius).
- **AD 64/68** — Martyred in Rome under Nero; crucified upside down on Vatican Hill; buried nearby.
- **c. AD 180** — Irenaeus lists Linus as Peter’s successor in Rome.
- **4th century** — Constantine builds Old St. Peter’s Basilica over traditional burial site.
- **1968** — Pope Paul VI announces bones found under basilica altar likely Peter’s relics.

## Body

### Names and Etymologies
Peter’s original name was **Simon** (Greek *Simōn*), a Hellenized form of Hebrew *Shimon*; in two passages he is called **Simeon**, reflecting a Jewish custom of pairing a patriarchal name with a similar-sounding Greek one. Jesus gave him the Aramaic nickname **Cephas** (*Kepha*, "rock/stone"), rendered in Greek as **Petros** (*Πέτρος*), a masculinized form of *petra* ("rock"). The combined name "Simon Peter" appears 19 times in the New Testament. The precise nuance of *Kepha* is debated: it may mean "rock," "crag," "stone," or, as some Catholic theologians argue, "precious stone" denoting a distinguished person, though the latter lacks secure Aramaic attestation. In Syriac documents he is called Simon Cephas.

### Biographical Sources and Accounts
Sources fall into three tiers: (1) New Testament writings (Pauline epistles, Acts, Gospels, Petrine epistles); (2) New Testament apocrypha (Gospel of Peter, Acts of Peter, etc.), deemed late pseudepigrapha with minimal historical value; (3) Apostolic and Church Fathers (Clement of Rome, Ignatius, Irenaeus, Eusebius).

Peter was a Jewish fisherman from Bethsaida, son of Jonah/John, brother of Andrew. The Synoptics record Jesus healing his mother-in-law at Capernaum, implying he was married (1 Cor 9:5 corroborates). He worked in a fishing partnership with James and John. In Luke, Jesus uses Peter’s boat to preach, then miraculously fills their nets, prompting Peter to follow him. In John, Andrew brings Simon to Jesus, who immediately names him Cephas.

Key episodes: Peter’s confession at Caesarea Philippi ("You are the Christ") elicits the "rock" declaration (Matt 16:18). He walks on water briefly but sinks when faith wavers (Matt 14). At the Last Supper he refuses foot-washing, then demands total cleansing. In Gethsemane he cuts off the high priest’s servant Malchus’s ear (John 18:10); Jesus heals it. He denies Jesus thrice before the cockcrow, weeping bitterly. Post-resurrection, he is the first male witness (1 Cor 15:5; Luke 24:34), enters the empty tomb first (John 20:6), and receives a threefold restoration by Jesus (John 21:15–17). In Acts, he leads the Jerusalem church, heals, preaches to Cornelius (opening the Gentile mission), is imprisoned by Herod Agrippa (AD 42–44) and freed by an angel, then departs to "another place."

### First Leader of the Early Church
The Gospels and Acts portray Peter as the foremost apostle. He is always listed first among the Twelve and forms an inner triad with James and John (witnesses to Jairus’s daughter, Transfiguration, Gethsemane). He acts as spokesman, conducts Matthias’s election, and his voice decides the Gentile controversy at the Jerusalem Council (Acts 15). However, he is soon eclipsed by **James the Just** ("brother of the Lord"), who becomes bishop of Jerusalem. Gerd Lüdemann attributes this to James’s conservative Jewish-law faction prevailing over Peter’s liberal stance; James D. G. Dunn argues it reflects Peter’s missionary absences. Dunn calls Peter a "bridge-man" (*pontifex maximus*) between Paul (apostle to Gentiles) and James (apostle to Jews). Eusebius cites Clement of Alexandria: the three pillars—Peter, James, John—chose James as bishop of Jerusalem.

### The "Rock" Dialogue (Matthew 16:13–19)
Jesus asks, "Who do you say I am?" Peter answers, "You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God." Jesus replies: "Blessed are you, Simon son of Jonah… you are **Petros**, and on this **petra** I will build my church… I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven." In Aramaic the same word *kepha* underlies both name and rock. Interpretations diverge:
- **Catholic**: Peter personally is the rock; the keys confer supreme pastoral authority and papal primacy.
- **Eastern Orthodox**: The rock is Peter’s confession of faith (or the apostolic college), not his person; *petra* grammatically denotes a "small detachment of the massive ledge."
- **Protestant**: *Petros* (masculine) meant "pebble" in Attic Greek vs. *petra* "boulder"; the rock is Christ or Peter’s faith. Counter-arguments note Koiné Greek shows no such distinction (Apollonius Rhodius uses *petros* for a huge boulder), and Aramaic *kepha* is identical in both clauses.
- **Patristic**: Tertullian, Cyprian, Augustine, and Jerome saw a connection to Peter’s primacy; the Catechism of the Catholic Church holds a "double meaning" (Peter personally and his faith).

### Apostolic Succession
Catholic and Orthodox sources root apostolic succession in Peter’s leadership. Irenaeus (c. 180) names Linus as Peter’s successor in Rome; Tertullian says Clement was ordained by Peter. Scholars consensus: *episkopos* and *presbyteros* were interchangeable in the 1st century; a single "monarchical" bishop in Rome likely emerged only in the mid-2nd century or later.

### Antioch and Corinth
**Antioch**: Galatians 2:11 records Peter visiting Antioch, where Paul rebukes him for withdrawing from Gentile table-fellowship. Tradition (Origen, Eusebius) makes him first Patriarch of Antioch. *Liber Pontificalis* (9th c.) says he served seven years, possibly leaving family there. Clementine literature (4th c., preserving earlier traditions) gives an itinerary from Caesarea Maritima to Antioch, naming 12–16 followers and recording ordinations of Zacchaeus (Caesarea) and Maro (Tripolis) en route, plus a debate with Simon Magus.
**Corinth**: 1 Corinthians hints at a "Cephas" party. Dionysius of Corinth (c. 170) writes that Peter and Paul founded both the Church of Rome and the Church of Corinth, taught together in Italy, and were martyred simultaneously.

### Connection to Rome
**Papacy**: The Catholic Church teaches the pope is Peter’s successor. Irenaeus lists Linus, Anacletus, Clement, etc., as successive bishops of Rome. Eusebius confirms Linus succeeded Peter. Tertullian states Clement was ordained by Peter.
**New Testament evidence**: No explicit mention of Peter in Rome. 1 Peter 5:13 greets from "Babylon," widely interpreted as a cryptogram for Rome (common in Jewish/Christian literature post-AD 70). Romans 16 (c. AD 57) greets 50 Romans but not Peter; Acts 28 (AD 60–62) records Paul’s Roman imprisonment without mentioning Peter.
**Church Fathers**: Ignatius of Antioch (c. 107) refers to Peter and Paul admonishing Romans. Irenaeus (c. 180) calls them founders of the Roman church. Clement of Alexandria (c. 190) says Peter preached publicly in Rome. Origen and Eusebius state Peter founded Antioch, then went to Rome, presiding there until death. Lactantius (c. 318) places Peter’s arrival under Nero.
**Simon Magus**: Eusebius says Peter pursued Simon Magus to Rome, where Simon was worshipped as a god until Peter destroyed him. Jerome dates Peter’s arrival to Claudius’s second year (AD 42), giving a 25-year episcopate ending in Nero’s 14th year (AD 67–68). The *Actus Vercellenses* (7th c.) sets the confrontation in Rome.

### Death and Burial
**Crucifixion**: John 21:18–19 hints at crucifixion ("stretch out your hands"). Tradition places death during Nero’s persecution after the Great Fire (AD 64), on Vatican Hill. *Acts of Peter* (2nd c.) says he was crucified head-down at his own request, deeming himself unworthy to die like Jesus. The "Quo vadis?" legend (Peter fleeing Rome, meeting Jesus returning to be crucified again) derives from this apocryphon. Tertullian (c. 200) and Origen (c. 230) attest crucifixion in Rome; Jerome specifies AD 67–68. *Liber Pontificalis* gives 25-year tenure but inconsistently dates Linus’s succession to AD 56. A 2025 paper suggests intra-community violence as cause.
**Burial**: Caius (c. 198) points to "trophies" (monuments) of Peter on the Vatican and Paul on the Ostian Way. Jerome says Peter was buried on the Vatican near the triumphal way. Constantine built Old St. Peter’s Basilica (early 4th c.) over the believed grave, despite difficult terrain.
**Relics**: Pope Vitalian (665) sent filings from Peter’s chains to Northumbria. The skull is claimed at the Archbasilica of St. John Lateran (9th c.+). Bones found under the high altar in 1950 were identified in the 1960s as a 1st-century male, c. 61 years old; Pope Paul VI (1968) declared them likely Peter’s. Pope Francis displayed fragments in 2013 and gifted nine to Patriarch Bartholomew in 2019.
**Scholarly views**: Most Catholic and many secular scholars accept Peter’s martyrdom in Rome under Nero. Otto Zwierlein (2009, 2013) argues *no* reliable literary or archaeological evidence places Peter in Rome; Timothy Barnes calls this a "nadir in historical criticism." Zwierlein questions the authenticity and dating of 1 Clement and Ignatius’s letter to the Romans. The head-down crucifixion is plausible given Roman practice (Josephus), though death would not be by suffocation.

### Rome as Babylon
1 Peter 5:13: "She who is in Babylon… sends greetings." Eusebius cites Clement of Alexandria and Papias: Peter composed 1 Peter in Rome, calling it "Babylon." Most scholars accept the metaphor for pagan Rome (brutality, idolatry), though some argue for the Egyptian Babylon (near Cairo) given Mark’s Egyptian ties. Craig Koester: "the whore [of Babylon] is Rome, yet more than Rome"—the imperial world alienated from God. The historical Babylon was deserted (Strabo). Scholarship largely rejects Petrine authorship of both epistles due to polished Greek and historical anachronisms.

### Feast Days
- **29 June**: Solemnity of Saints Peter and Paul (Roman Martyrology, Eastern Orthodox, Anglican). Augustine: "their martyrdom occurred on different days, they were one."
- **22 February**: Chair of Saint Peter (Roman Rite).
- **18 November**: Dedication of the Basilicas of Peter and Paul.
- **Orthodox**: 16 January (Veneration of Chains), 29 June (Feast), 30 June (Synaxis of the Twelve Apostles). Every Thursday dedicated to the Apostles.
- Pre-1960 Roman Calendar included 18 January (Chair at Rome) and 1 August (St. Peter in Chains).

### Primacy of Peter
**Catholic**: Peter is the first pope. Basis: John 21:15–17 ("Feed my sheep" = universal jurisdiction); Matthew 10:2 (*protos* = primacy in foundation); Matthew 16:18–19 (rock, keys, binding/loosing). The Fisherman’s Ring and papal keys symbolize this commission, paralleling Eliakim in Isaiah 22. The Peshitta (Syriac) uses *kepha* for both name and rock. Catechism admits "double meaning" (Peter and his faith). Protestant objections focus on *petros*/*petra* distinction; Catholic replies note Koiné Greek usage and Aramaic identity. Lutheran historian cites 1870 Vatican Council: cardinals split 17 (Peter), 16 (Christ), 8 (apostolic college), 44 (Peter’s faith), rest (whole church)—yet Rome teaches Peter is the rock.

**Eastern Orthodox**: Peter and Paul are "Preeminent Apostles" (*Coryphaeus* = choir-director). Peter led at Jerusalem but had no "princely" authority. At the Council of Jerusalem, James decided; Peter spoke. The rock is the confession of faith (feminine demonstrative *tautē* in Greek). The 2007 Ravenna Document acknowledges Rome as *protos* (first among patriarchs) *if* papacy unites with Orthodoxy, but disagrees on prerogatives.

**Syriac Orthodox**: Ephrem, Aphrahat, Maruthas affirm Peter’s unique office. Jesus is *Kepha* (messianic rock); giving Simon the name *Kepha* shares Christ’s person and office. Aphrahat: receiving another’s name confers their rights. Peter is the foundation stone, the faithful witness.

**New Apostolic Church**: Peter as first Chief Apostle.

**Latter-day Saints**: Peter was first leader post-resurrection; apostolic succession accepted, papal succession rejected. Joseph Smith recorded Peter appearing in 1829 to restore priesthood keys. Bruce R. McConkie: the "rock" is revelation.

### Non-Christian Views
- **Judaism**: Old tradition says rabbis sent Peter to separate Christianity from Judaism; he remained a practicing Jew, authored the *Nishmas* prayer.
- **Islam**: Qur’an calls disciples "helpers"; exegesis names Peter. Tradition: one of three sent to Antioch. Twelver Shia see Peter as parallel to Ali (vicegerent to prophet); imamate fulfills 12 princes of Ishmael, final Imam Mahdi’s mother a descendant of Peter.
- **Bahá’í**: Upholds Peter’s primacy; "the Rock" = Peter’s belief in Christ as Son of God, foundation of God’s Law. Bahá’u’lláh addresses Peter as "The Rock" crying out the Father’s arrival.
- **Ossetian mythology**: *Dan-* prefix + Peter → Donbettyr, god of waters, patron of fish/fishermen.
- **Andean medicine**: San Pedro cactus (*Echinopsis pachanoi*) named for Peter holding keys to heaven; effects let users "reach heaven while on earth." Declared Peruvian cultural heritage (2022).

### Writings
**New Testament Epistles**: 1 Peter and 2 Peter. Both show polished Greek unexpected for an Aramaic fisherman. Majority of scholars reject Petrine authorship of both. Daniel Wallace notes the consensus is "settled negatively," though he dissents for 2 Peter. 1 Peter may use a secretary (Silvanus, 5:12), though Ehrman argues Silvanus was the carrier. 1 Peter addresses Christians in Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, Bithynia; persecution may reflect Domitian (81–96) rather than Nero (confined to Rome). 2 Peter shares material with Jude; dated as late as c. 150 by some; canonical acceptance only in 4th century (Syriac Orthodox: 6th century). Jerome attributed style differences to different interpreters.

**Mark**: Tradition (Papias via Eusebius, Clement of Alexandria, Irenaeus) holds Mark was Peter’s interpreter, writing down his preaching non-chronologically. Gospel is anonymous; authorship debated.

**Pseudepigrapha/Apocrypha**: Gospel of Peter (partially Docetic), Acts of Peter, Acts of Peter and Andrew/Paul/Twelve, Gnostic Apocalypse of Peter, Letter of Peter to Philip (Nag Hammadi), Apocalypse of Peter (accepted by many until 4th c.), Preaching of Peter, *Epistula Petri* (preface to Clementine literature).

**Non-canonical sayings**: Gospel of Thomas (Peter compares Jesus to "just messenger"; asks Jesus to dismiss Mary), Apocalypse of Peter (dialogue on fig tree), Gospel of Mary (Peter jealous of Mary Magdalene; Levi rebukes his temper), Fayyum Fragment (3rd c., predicts denial), fragmentary Gospel of Peter (Peter and Andrew return to fishing after empty tomb).

### Iconography
Earliest portrait: 4th century (discovered 2010). Standard type: old, thick-set, short beard, white/balding hair, "slightly combative" face. Contrasts with Paul (bald, long beard, dark hair, thin). Anglo-Saxon art omits beard. Attribute: one or two large keys (from early 8th c.), persistent through Renaissance. By 15th c., Western art shows bald crown; Orthodox icons retain hair. Peter usually stands nearest Christ in narrative scenes; heads saintly groups on God’s right. Common scenes: cutting off Malchus’s ear, martyrdom (inverted cross), prison rescue, hearing the cock crow (Counter-Reformation symbol of repentance/Confession). The cartoon trope of Peter as literal gatekeeper of heaven is absent from traditional art.

## Terms
- ****Cephas / Kepha**** — Aramaic nickname given by Jesus to Simon, meaning "rock" or "stone"; Hellenized as *Petros* (Peter).
- ****Petros / Petra**** — Greek words: *Petros* (masculine proper name "Peter"); *petra* (feminine noun "rock, cliff"). Central to Matthew 16:18 interpretation.
- ****Apostolic Succession**** — Doctrine that bishops are spiritual successors of the apostles; Catholics and Orthodox root Roman primacy in Peter’s succession.
- ****Monarchical Episcopate**** — Single bishop governing a local church; scholars date its emergence in Rome to mid-2nd century or later.
- ****Babylon (1 Peter 5:13)**** — Cryptogram for Rome in early Christian literature; alternatively, Egyptian Babylon or metaphor for pagan imperial power.
- ****Pseudepigrapha**** — Writings falsely attributed to a biblical figure; e.g., Gospel of Peter, Acts of Peter.
- ****Primacy of Honor**** — Eastern Orthodox view: Peter’s successors hold first place in dignity (*protos*) but not universal jurisdiction.
- ****Quo Vadis**** — Latin "Where are you going?"; from *Acts of Peter*: fleeing Peter meets Jesus returning to Rome to be crucified again.
