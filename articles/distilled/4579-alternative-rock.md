# Alternative rock

## Overview
Alternative rock is a genre of rock music that originated from the independent music underground of the 1970s and evolved into a mainstream commercial force in the 1990s. It is defined by its initial rejection of mainstream commercialism, diverse musical styles, and lyrical focus on social and personal concerns. The genre's mainstream breakthrough, led by grunge and Britpop, reshaped the music industry and popular culture, though it has since diversified into numerous subgenres.

## Timeline
- **1967** — The Velvet Underground & Nico album is released, a key precursor to alternative rock.
- **1979** — The term "alternative" is first used to describe non-mainstream rock music.
- **1980** — The UK's first national "Indie Chart" is published, promoting independent labels.
- **1983** — R.E.M.'s debut album *Murmur* enters the Top 40, a breakthrough for American college rock.
- **1988** — *Billboard* creates the "Alternative Songs" chart, reflecting the genre's growing radio presence.
- **1991** — Nirvana's "Smells Like Teen Spirit" is released, instigating the grunge phenomenon.
- **1991** — The first Lollapalooza festival tour unites the alternative rock community.
- **1995** — The "Battle of Britpop" occurs between Oasis and Blur, symbolizing the movement's peak.
- **1997** — Radiohead's *OK Computer* is released, influencing the post-Britpop era.
- **2000s** — Emo and post-punk revival bands achieve mainstream multi-platinum success.
- **2010s** — Indie rock becomes the dominant term for modern alternative music in the U.S.

## Body

### Origins and Etymology
Alternative rock emerged from the punk and post-punk scenes of the late 1970s. The term "alternative" was first used in 1979 and gained traction in the 1980s, replacing earlier labels like "college rock" and "indie." It originally described intentionally non-mainstream acts distributed through independent labels, college radio, and fanzines. The genre's identity was rooted in a DIY (do-it-yourself) ethos and a rejection of the corporate music industry's commercialism.

### The 1980s Underground
Throughout the 1980s, alternative rock remained largely an underground phenomenon in both the U.S. and UK. In America, bands like R.E.M., Hüsker Dü, and the Replacements diversified from punk into more melodic styles, laying the groundwork for future success. In the UK, The Smiths became a seminal band, influencing the indie scene with jangly guitars and English-centric lyrics. Key subgenres developed, including gothic rock, shoegaze (characterized by overwhelming, distorted sound), and the Madchester scene, which blended indie pop with dance rhythms.

### Mainstream Breakthrough in the 1990s
The early 1990s saw alternative rock explode into the mainstream. Nirvana's 1991 album *Nevermind* popularized grunge—a sludgy, punk-metal hybrid from Seattle—and established the commercial viability of alternative rock. This success led major labels to sign alternative bands, creating a cultural shift away from 1980s hair metal. In the UK, Britpop emerged as a reaction to grunge, with bands like Oasis and Blur celebrating British guitar pop and dominating the charts. Concurrently, indie rock solidified as a distinct, often lo-fi, movement that resisted mainstream co-option.

### Diversification in the 2000s and Beyond
Following the peak of grunge and Britpop, the genre diversified. Post-grunge bands like Foo Fighters and Nickelback commercialized the grunge sound. Emo evolved from an underground style into a mainstream phenomenon with multi-platinum acts like My Chemical Romance. The 2000s also saw a garage rock and post-punk revival led by bands like The Strokes and The White Stripes. In the 2010s, the term "indie rock" largely replaced "alternative" in the U.S., and the genre continued to fuse with electronic, hip-hop, and pop elements, as seen in acts like Twenty One Pilots.

## Terms
- **DIY Ethic:** A core principle of alternative rock emphasizing self-reliance and independence from major corporate structures, often involving self-produced music and independent label distribution.
- **Grunge:** A 1990s subgenre originating in Seattle that fused punk rock and heavy metal, characterized by sludgy guitar sounds and themes of apathy and social alienation.
- **Britpop:** A 1990s UK movement reacting against American grunge, drawing inspiration from 1960s British Invasion and 1970s glam rock, with a focus on British cultural identity.
- **Shoegaze:** A late-1980s UK subgenre named for performers' tendency to stare at effects pedals, creating a dense, distorted "wash of sound" with obscured vocals.
- **Indie Rock:** Originally a term for independently distributed music, it evolved into a distinct genre in the 1990s, often characterized by a rejection of mainstream commercialism and a more melodic, less aggressive sound than grunge.
- **Post-Punk:** A late-1970s genre evolving from punk that incorporated more experimental and electronic elements, serving as a direct precursor to many alternative styles.
- **College Rock:** A term used in the 1980s for alternative music popular on U.S. college radio stations, which were crucial for breaking new artists.
- **Emo:** A genre originating from 1980s hardcore punk, known for its emotionally intense, confessional lyrics, which achieved mainstream success in the 2000s.

## Debates and Open Questions
The definition of "alternative rock" is inherently paradoxical and debated. The term describes music that is both "fiercely iconoclastic, anticommercial, and antimainstream" and, simultaneously, a marketable category used by the music industry to sell products. This tension was acute in the 1990s, when the genre's mainstream success was celebrated as a revolution and denounced as a "gigantic sellout." Scholars also debate the precise boundaries of the genre, as its musical styles are incredibly diverse and have continuously evolved, making it "ever harder to define."
