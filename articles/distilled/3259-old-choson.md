# Old Chosŏn

## Overview
Old Chosŏn (Gojoseon) was the first kingdom on the Korean Peninsula, traditionally founded by the mythical Tan'gun in 2333 BCE. It represents the foundational state in Korean history, marking the transition from prehistoric societies to more centralized political entities. Its history is a blend of mythology and recorded events, involving interactions with Chinese states and culminating in its conquest by the Han dynasty in 108 BCE.

## Timeline
- **c. 2333 BCE** — Traditional founding of Old Chosŏn by Tan'gun, as recorded in the *Samguk yusa*.
- **1122 BCE** — According to Chinese and later Korean sources, Kija (Jizi) arrives and founds Kija Chosŏn.
- **4th century BCE** — Old Chosŏn develops into a significant state, capable of waging war against the Yan state.
- **c. 300 BCE** — Old Chosŏn loses territory to Yan and likely relocates its capital to the Pyongyang region.
- **194 BCE** — Wi Man, a refugee from Yan, usurps the throne, establishing Wiman Joseon.
- **109 BCE** — Han dynasty, under Emperor Wu, invades Wiman Joseon.
- **108 BCE** — Han forces conquer Wiman Joseon and establish the Four Commanderies of Han.
- **313 CE** — The last Han commandery, Nakrang, is destroyed by Goguryeo, ending direct Chinese control.

## Body

### Founding Myths and Historiography
The origins of Old Chosŏn are explained through three primary myths: Tan'gun, Kija, and Wi Man. The Tan'gun myth, recorded in the 13th-century *Samguk yusa*, describes the kingdom's founding by a heavenly prince and a bear-woman. While considered mythological, it is interpreted as reflecting early sociocultural development and has been central to Korean national identity. The Kija myth, from Chinese sources, claims a Shang dynasty sage founded a kingdom in 1122 BCE. Its historical existence is heavily debated; many modern scholars view it as a later conflation or a symbol of early Chinese cultural influence. The Wi Man narrative is more historically grounded, describing a usurpation in 194 BCE by a Yan refugee, which is corroborated by Chinese records.

### State Formation and Territory
Early Chinese texts mention Old Chosŏn as a region trading with the state of Qi. Its exact location is debated, with traditional views placing it in the northern Korean Peninsula, while recent South Korean scholarship expands its territory into Manchuria. By the 4th century BCE, it had evolved into a confederated kingdom, the most advanced polity in the peninsula. It engaged in conflict with the Yan state, losing significant territory around 300 BCE. The kingdom's capital was Wanggeom-seong (modern Pyongyang) from at least the 2nd century BCE.

### Downfall and Legacy
In 194 BCE, Wi Man usurped the throne, founding Wiman Joseon. In 109 BCE, the Han dynasty invaded, ostensibly due to diplomatic insults and concerns over an alliance with the Xiongnu. After a difficult campaign, Han conquered the kingdom in 108 BCE and established four commanderies. Old Chosŏn disintegrated, giving rise to successor states like Buyeo, Okjeo, and Dongye. Goguryeo and Baekje emerged from Buyeo. The last Han commandery fell in 313 CE, after which the region was absorbed by Goguryeo.

### Culture and Archaeology
Old Chosŏn's culture is marked by the Mumun pottery period (1500–300 BCE) and the adoption of bronze technology around 1000 BCE. A distinctive "mandolin-shaped" bronze dagger culture flourished, indicating a unique regional identity. Megalithic dolmen tombs, used for elite burials, are numerous in Korea and Manchuria. Iron technology was introduced from Yan around 300 BCE. Archaeological evidence, such as walled-town states and elaborate tombs, points to increasing social stratification and a complex society.

## Terms
- **Gojoseon (Old Chosŏn):** The first kingdom on the Korean Peninsula, its name distinguished from the later Joseon dynasty by the prefix "Go" (old).
- **Tan'gun:** The mythical founder of Old Chosŏn, born of a heavenly prince and a bear-woman; a central figure in Korean foundation mythology.
- **Kija (Jizi):** A sage from China's Shang dynasty who, according to Chinese records, founded Kija Chosŏn in 1122 BCE; his historical role is debated.
- **Wi Man:** A refugee from the Chinese state of Yan who usurped the Old Chosŏn throne in 194 BCE, establishing Wiman Joseon.
- **Wiman Joseon:** The state established by Wi Man, which was conquered by the Han dynasty in 108 BCE.
- **Four Commanderies of Han:** Administrative divisions established by the Han dynasty in the former territory of Old Chosŏn after its conquest.
- **Dolmen (Goindol):** A type of megalithic tomb consisting of a large stone slab supported by upright stones, common in the Korean Peninsula and Manchuria.
- **Mandolin-shaped dagger (Bipahyeong donggeom):** A distinctive type of bronze sword characteristic of Old Chosŏn's material culture.
- **Samhan:** Three confederacies (Mahan, Jinhan, Byeonhan) in the southern Korean Peninsula that emerged after the fall of Old Chosŏn.

## Debates and Open Questions
Scholars fiercely debate the historical existence and location of Kija Chosŏn, with many modern experts dismissing it as a fabrication or symbolic narrative. The geographical center of Old Chosŏn is contested, with traditional views placing it in the northern Korean Peninsula versus newer theories situating its early core in Manchuria. The nature of Old Chosŏn's political structure—whether it was a unified state or a loose confederation—remains unclear. Furthermore, the Tan'gun myth is interpreted variously as a native Korean product, a synthesis of tribal integration, or a later nationalist construct.
