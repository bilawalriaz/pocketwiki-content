# Italian literature

## Overview
Italian literature encompasses works written in Italian or by Italians, often in regional dialects related to modern Italian. Beginning in the 12th century with vernacular literary use, it evolved through major periods like the Renaissance, which perfected the language and produced globally influential authors. This tradition is significant for shaping Italian national identity, contributing to world literature, and reflecting Italy's political and social transformations.

## Timeline
- **12th century** — Vernacular use begins; *Ritmo laurenziano* is the first extant document.
- **c. 1230** — Sicilian School emerges, creating the first standard Italian literary language.
- **14th–15th c.** — Renaissance humanism develops; Dante, Petrarch, and Boccaccio establish foundational works.
- **15th century** — Lorenzo de' Medici champions Florentine influence; drama develops significantly.
- **16th century** — High Renaissance perfects Italian language; Machiavelli and Guicciardini found modern history.
- **1690** — Academy of Arcadia founded to restore literature via ancient simplicity.
- **1789** — French Revolution inspires patriotic/classicist literature, inaugurated by Beccaria's *Dei delitti e delle pene*.
- **1818** — Romantic movement's organ, *Conciliatore*, established in Milan.
- **1827** — Manzoni publishes *I promessi sposi*, a masterpiece of realism and Risorgimento symbol.
- **1848** — Pre-revolutionary literary movement represented by Giuseppe Giusti and Vincenzo Gioberti.
- **1926** — Grazia Deledda wins Nobel Prize in Literature, first Italian woman to do so.
- **1980** — Umberto Eco achieves international success with *Il nome della Rosa*.

## Body

### Early Vernacular and Medieval Foundations
Italian literature originated in the 12th century with vernacular texts like the *Ritmo laurenziano*. The earliest tradition was Occitan, brought by troubadours (*trovatori*) patronized by northern aristocracy. The Sicilian School (c. 1230), under Frederick II, produced the first standard Italian literary language, adapting Provençal forms and inventing the sonnet. Religious literature, including Francis of Assisi's *Cantico del Sole* and Jacopone da Todi's mystical poetry, also emerged. In Tuscany, the *Dolce Stil Novo* ("Sweet New Style") movement, led by Guido Guinizelli and later Dante Alighieri, shifted focus to inner, metaphysical love.

### The 14th Century: The Roots of the Renaissance
The 14th century was dominated by three towering figures. Dante Alighieri's *Divine Comedy* is a theological and political allegory of the afterlife, blending classical and Christian traditions. Petrarch, the first humanist, perfected the sonnet in his *Canzoniere*, focusing on psychological introspection. Giovanni Boccaccio's *Decameron* established the novella as an art form, depicting contemporary society with realism. This period also saw the rise of chronicles, political poetry, and imitation of these masters.

### The 15th and 16th Centuries: Renaissance and High Renaissance
Renaissance humanism, emphasizing classical study and eloquence, flourished in Florence under the Medici. Lorenzo de' Medici and Poliziano blended classical and popular styles. Epic poetry was revitalized by Luigi Pulci's *Morgante Maggiore* and Matteo Boiardo's *Orlando innamorato*. The 16th century perfected the Italian language and style. Baldassare Castiglione's *Il Cortegiano* defined the ideal courtier. Niccolò Machiavelli and Francesco Guicciardini pioneered modern historical and political science. Ludovico Ariosto's *Orlando furioso* and Torquato Tasso's *Gerusalemme liberata* became the era's great epic poems. Pietro Bembo influential codified Tuscan as the literary standard.

### The 17th Century: Baroque and Secentismo
Following foreign domination, the Baroque period (*Secentismo*) valued ingenuity (*ingegno*) and elaborate conceits (*concetti*). Giambattista Marino's *L'Adone* epitomized this style, prioritizing metaphor and sensory appeal. The Accademia dell'Arcadia (1690) later reacted against this excess, advocating a return to classical simplicity. The period also saw the development of the Baroque novel, theatre, and satire, alongside philosophical works like Tommaso Campanella's *La città del sole*.

### The 18th Century: Enlightenment and Reform
The Enlightenment brought historical and social inquiry. Giambattista Vico's *Scienza nuova* investigated historical laws, while Cesare Beccaria's *Dei delitti e delle pene* advocated penal reform. Giuseppe Parini satirized aristocratic society in *Il giorno*. The latter half of the century, inspired by the French Revolution, saw the rise of patriotic classicism. Vittorio Alfieri's tragedies championed liberty, followed by Vincenzo Monti and Ugo Foscolo, whose *Le sepolcri* became a patriotic anthem.

### The 19th Century: Romanticism and Risorgimento
The Romantic movement, centered on the journal *Conciliatore*, sought historical and moral truth (*il vero storico*). Alessandro Manzoni's *I promessi sposi* is the movement's masterpiece, using realism to explore Italian history and society. Giacomo Leopardi became the era's greatest lyric poet, expressing profound philosophical pessimism. The Risorgimento (Italian unification) was fueled by patriotic literature from writers like Giuseppe Giusti and Vincenzo Gioberti.

### The 20th Century and Beyond
After the Risorgimento, literature moved away from political themes. The *Scapigliatura* and *Verismo* (realism), led by Giovanni Verga, opposed Romanticism. Early 20th-century writers like Italo Svevo and Luigi Pirandello explored psychological and existential themes. Neorealism, flourishing after WWII, depicted social realities through authors like Alberto Moravia, Primo Levi, and Pier Paolo Pasolini. Later, Italo Calvino and Umberto Eco gained international acclaim for postmodern and philosophical fiction. The Nobel Prize in Literature has been awarded to six Italian-language authors.

## Terms
- **Vernacular**: The native language of a country or region, as opposed to a literary or sacred language. In Italy, this refers to the various dialects that evolved into modern Italian.
- **Dolce Stil Novo**: ("Sweet New Style") A 13th-century Italian literary movement that used refined language to explore themes of idealized, metaphysical love.
- **Humanism**: A Renaissance intellectual movement that emphasized the study of classical antiquity, human potential, and secular subjects.
- **Secentismo**: The Italian Baroque literary style of the 17th century, characterized by elaborate conceits, complex metaphors, and a focus on ingenuity.
- **Arcadia**: A 17th-century literary academy founded to promote a return to the perceived simplicity and pastoral ideals of ancient Greek poetry.
- **Risorgimento**: ("Resurgence") The 19th-century political and social movement for Italian unification.
- **Verismo**: ("Realism") A late 19th-century Italian literary movement that depicted contemporary life with gritty, often pessimistic realism.
- **Neorealism**: A post-WWII Italian literary and cinematic movement that focused on the difficult economic and moral conditions of everyday life.
- **Petrarchism**: The literary practice of imitating the style and themes of the 14th-century poet Petrarch, particularly in love poetry.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise classification of early vernacular texts like the *Ritmo laurenziano* is debated, as they lack uniform stylistic or linguistic traits.
- Scholars debate whether Torquato Tasso belongs to the High Renaissance or is a transitional figure to the Baroque period due to his use of elaborate metaphors.
- The extent to which the metaphorical freedom of Marinism represents a new way of perceiving reality versus an intellectual game remains critically debated.
- The relationship and relative importance of the two post-Risorgimento trends, *Scapigliatura* and *Verismo*, which both opposed Romanticism, is a subject of analysis.
