# History of Antarctica

## Overview
Antarctica is Earth's southernmost continent, a vast, ice-covered landmass central to global climate systems and a unique zone of international scientific cooperation. Its history is a narrative of evolving human understanding, from theoretical "Great Southern Land" to a continent of strategic and scientific importance, ultimately governed by a treaty that sets it aside for peace and research.

## Timeline
- **2nd century AD** — Marinus of Tyre coins the term "Antarctic."
- **1487** — Bartolomeu Dias rounds the Cape of Good Hope, first encountering Antarctic cold.
- **1773** — James Cook's expedition crosses the Antarctic Circle for the first time.
- **19 February 1819** — William Smith discovers the South Shetland Islands.
- **27/28 January 1820** — First generally recognized sighting of the Antarctic continent by Bellingshausen and Lazarev.
- **14 December 1911** — Roald Amundsen's expedition becomes the first to reach the South Pole.
- **17 January 1912** — Robert Falcon Scott's expedition reaches the South Pole, 33 days after Amundsen.
- **1957–1958** — International Geophysical Year (IGY) fosters major international scientific cooperation.
- **1 December 1959** — The Antarctic Treaty is signed, establishing Antarctica as a scientific preserve.
- **7 January 1978** — Emilio Marcos de Palma is born, the first baby born on the continent.
- **1991** — The Madrid Protocol bans mineral extraction for 50 years.

## Body

### Early Exploration and the Myth of Terra Australis
For centuries, European geographers theorized a vast southern continent, *Terra Australis Incognita* ("Unknown Southern Land"), to balance the known northern landmasses. Early voyages around the Cape of Good Hope and Cape Horn in the 15th and 16th centuries proved that if such a land existed, it was a separate continent. In 1773, James Cook became the first to cross the Antarctic Circle, though he did not sight the continent itself. His voyages effectively disproved the myth of a habitable, temperate southern continent, concluding that any land farther south was inaccessible and of no economic value.

### Discovery and the Heroic Age
The first confirmed land south of 60°S was the South Shetland Islands, sighted by William Smith in 1819. This triggered a rush of sealing expeditions. The first generally accepted sighting of the Antarctic continent occurred in January 1820 by the Russian expedition of Bellingshausen and Lazarev, with a British expedition under Edward Bransfield sighting the Trinity Peninsula days later. The "Heroic Age of Antarctic Exploration" (c. 1897–1922) saw intense international effort, culminating in the race to the South Pole. Norwegian Roald Amundsen first reached the Pole on 14 December 1911. British explorer Robert Falcon Scott arrived 33 days later; he and his four companions perished on the return journey. The era is considered to have closed with Ernest Shackleton's Shackleton–Rowett Expedition (1921–22).

### The Mechanical Age and Overland Crossings
After Shackleton's death, exploration became increasingly technology-based. Aircraft were first used extensively in the 1920s and 1930s, with pioneers like Richard Evelyn Byrd overflying the South Pole. The first overland crossing of the continent was completed in 1958 during the Commonwealth Trans-Antarctic Expedition, led by Vivian Fuchs with Edmund Hillary leading the support party. This period saw the establishment of the first permanent bases, such as Mawson, McMurdo, and the Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station.

### Political Claims and the Antarctic Treaty
Multiple nations asserted territorial claims based on discovery, proximity, or historical inheritance from Spain. Britain, France, Norway, Australia, New Zealand, Chile, and Argentina all claimed sectors, with significant overlaps, particularly around the Antarctic Peninsula. These claims led to geopolitical friction, including incidents like the 1952 Hope Bay clash. The International Geophysical Year (1957–58) provided a framework for cooperation, leading to the Antarctic Treaty System in 1959. Signed by 12 nations, the treaty set aside sovereignty disputes, banned military activity, and designated the continent as a natural reserve devoted to peace and science.

## Terms
- **Terra Australis Incognita**: The hypothetical, vast southern continent long believed by European geographers to exist to balance the northern landmasses.
- **Antarctic Circle**: The parallel of latitude at approximately 66°33'S, south of which the sun does not set on the December solstice and does not rise on the June solstice.
- **Heroic Age of Antarctic Exploration**: A period from the late 19th century to the early 20th century characterized by expeditions relying on man-hauling, dogs, and limited technology.
- **Antarctic Convergence**: A biological boundary in the Southern Ocean where cold, northward-flowing Antarctic waters sink beneath warmer sub-Antarctic waters, creating a distinct ecosystem.
- **Ross Ice Shelf**: A massive, floating ice shelf in the Ross Sea, first encountered by James Clark Ross in 1841.
- **Uti possidetis juris**: A principle of international law meaning "as you possess under law," used by Chile and Argentina to claim Antarctic territory based on historical Spanish colonial boundaries.
- **Antarctic Treaty System**: The set of international agreements, centered on the 1959 Antarctic Treaty, that governs international relations with respect to Antarctica.
- **Madrid Protocol**: The 1991 Protocol on Environmental Protection to the Antarctic Treaty, which designates Antarctica as a natural reserve and bans mineral resource activities.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Priority of Discovery**: The first sighting of the Antarctic continent is disputed. Russia credits Bellingshausen and Lazarev (27/28 January 1820), while Britain promotes Edward Bransfield's sighting of the Trinity Peninsula on 30 January 1820. The United States has historically claimed priority for Charles Wilkes in 1840, though this is contested.
- **Territorial Sovereignty**: Seven nations maintain active territorial claims, with significant overlaps. These claims are legally "frozen" under the Antarctic Treaty but not renounced, leaving the continent's ultimate political status unresolved.
- **Early Human Contact**: A 2021 research paper suggested early Polynesians may have been the first people to see Antarctica, but this theory has been widely dismissed by scholars as improbable and lacking credible evidence.
