# Second Italo-Ethiopian War

## Overview
The Second Italo-Ethiopian War (1935–1936) was a colonial war of aggression waged by Fascist Italy against the Ethiopian Empire. It was the largest colonial campaign in history and a major military success for Italy, but it also strained its economy, discredited the League of Nations, and pushed Italy into an alliance with Nazi Germany. The conflict is marked by Italy's widespread use of chemical weapons and significant civilian casualties.

## Timeline
- **3 October 1935** — Italy invades Ethiopia from Eritrea and Somalia without a declaration of war.
- **18 November 1935** — The League of Nations imposes weak economic sanctions on Italy.
- **December 1935** — Ethiopian "Christmas Offensive" temporarily halts the Italian advance.
- **28 December 1935** — Mussolini authorizes the use of poison gas on a "vast scale."
- **31 March 1936** — Italians win the decisive Battle of Maychew, destroying organized Ethiopian resistance.
- **2 May 1936** — Emperor Haile Selassie flees into exile.
- **5 May 1936** — Italian forces enter Addis Ababa.
- **9 May 1936** — Mussolini proclaims the Italian Empire.
- **19 February 1937** — Assassination attempt on Marshal Graziani triggers the Addis Ababa massacre.
- **5 May 1941** — Haile Selassie re-enters Addis Ababa after Allied liberation.

## Body

### Background and Outbreak
Italy sought to avenge its defeat at the 1896 Battle of Adwa and expand its East African colonies. Tensions escalated after the 1934 Walwal Incident, a border clash in the Ogaden. Diplomatic efforts failed; Britain and France, prioritizing Italy as an ally against Germany, offered tacit approval. On 3 October 1935, 200,000 Italian soldiers invaded from Eritrea, with a smaller force attacking from Somalia.

### Military Campaign
The Italian advance from the north was initially slow, leading Mussolini to replace commander Emilio De Bono with Pietro Badoglio. After Ethiopian counterattacks in late 1935, Badoglio authorized the systematic use of mustard gas, violating the 1925 Geneva Protocol. Italian forces, equipped with tanks, aircraft, and colonial troops, won decisive battles at Amba Aradam, Tembien, and Shire. The final Ethiopian offensive was crushed at Maychew on 31 March 1936. Addis Ababa fell on 5 May, and Italy annexed Ethiopia, forming Italian East Africa.

### Occupation and Resistance
Italian rule was brutal and costly. A 1937 assassination attempt on Viceroy Rodolfo Graziani led to the Addis Ababa massacre, killing an estimated 19,200 people. Despite public works, the occupation faced persistent Ethiopian guerrilla resistance. The war drained Italy's budget and, combined with international condemnation, ended the Stresa Front and pushed Italy closer to Nazi Germany.

### War Crimes and Casualties
Italian forces used 300–500 tons of mustard gas against military and civilian targets, including Red Cross units. Deliberate attacks on ambulances and hospitals were reported. Ethiopian forces used banned dum-dum bullets and mutilated prisoners. Casualty figures are disputed: Italian losses are estimated at 20,000–30,000 (including later occupation), while Ethiopian deaths from combat, reprisals, and famine range from 250,000 to over 760,000.

## Terms
- **Mustard Gas**: A chemical warfare agent used by Italy to suppress resistance, causing severe burns and long-term health effects.
- **Ascari**: Indigenous soldiers recruited from Italian colonies (Eritrea, Somalia, Libya) to serve in the Italian army.
- **Ras**: A title for a Ethiopian nobleman or military commander.
- **League of Nations**: An international organization founded after WWI to maintain peace; its failure to stop the invasion undermined its credibility.
- **Stresa Front**: A 1935 agreement between Italy, France, and Britain to oppose German rearmament; it collapsed after the war.
- **Patriots**: Ethiopian guerrilla fighters who resisted Italian occupation after 1936.
- **Yekatit 12**: The Ge'ez calendar date (19 February 1937) for the Addis Ababa massacre.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Genocide**: Some historians describe the Italian campaign, particularly the use of chemical weapons and mass reprisals, as genocidal in intent or effect.
- **Impact of Chemical Weapons**: Historians debate whether mustard gas was the decisive factor in Italy's victory or primarily a tool of terror with limited tactical impact.
- **Casualty Figures**: Ethiopian government claims of over 760,000 deaths are considered exaggerated by some scholars, while Italian figures are seen as significant underestimates.
- **International Responsibility**: The war is a key case study in the failure of collective security, highlighting the appeasement policies of Britain and France.
