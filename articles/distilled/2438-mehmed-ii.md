# Mehmed II

## Overview
Mehmed II (1432–1481), known as "the Conqueror," was the Ottoman sultan who ended the Byzantine Empire by capturing Constantinople in 1453. His reign marked the transformation of the Ottoman Empire into a major world power, with significant territorial expansion, administrative centralization, and cultural patronage that blended Eastern and Western traditions.

- **3 May 1481** — Dies near Istanbul, possibly poisoned

## Body

### Early Life and First Reign
Mehmed was born in Edirne to Sultan Murad II and Hüma Hatun. At age 11, he was sent to Amasya to govern and gain experience, following Ottoman custom. His Islamic education, particularly under mentors Molla Gürâni and Akshamsaddin, shaped his worldview and reinforced his duty to conquer Constantinople. He first became sultan in 1444 at age 12 when his father abdicated. During this first reign, he faced a Crusade led by John Hunyadi after Hungary violated peace treaties. When Mehmed requested his father's return to command the army, Murad II reclaimed the throne in 1446, and Mehmed served as governor of Manisa until Murad's death in 1451.

### Conquest of Constantinople
Upon his second accession in 1451, Mehmed strengthened the Ottoman navy and built Rumelihisarı fortress to control the Bosphorus. In 1453, at age 21, he besieged Constantinople with 80,000–200,000 troops, over 70 large cannons (including Orban's bombard), and 320 vessels. After a 57-day siege, the city fell on 29 May 1453. Mehmed claimed the title "Caesar of Rome," arguing Constantinople had been the Roman capital since 330 AD. This claim was recognized by the Eastern Orthodox Church but rejected by most European monarchs. He moved the Ottoman capital to Constantinople and began transforming it into a thriving imperial center.

### Territorial Expansion
Mehmed systematically conquered remaining Byzantine territories: the Morea (1458–1460) and Trebizond (1461). He annexed Serbia (1459) after campaigns in 1454–1459, and Bosnia (1463). His wars with Venice (1463–1479) resulted in Ottoman gains including Negroponte and Shkodra. In Anatolia, he reunified Turkish beyliks, defeating the Karamanids and the Aq Qoyunlu at the Battle of Otlukbeli (1473). He also subdued Wallachia after conflicts with Vlad III Dracula, and fought Moldavia (1475–1476), though Stephen the Great inflicted a major defeat at Vaslui. Albania fell after Skanderbeg's death in 1468, with Shkodra captured in 1478–1479.

### Administration and Culture
Mehmed centralized Ottoman government through a kanunname (1477–1481) that codified officials' roles and salaries. He replaced aristocratic divan members with devshirme (Christian-converted) officials loyal to him, and secluded the sultan from public access. He established the millet system, granting religious communities autonomy under their leaders. Constantinople was repopulated through forced migrations (sürgün) and became a multicultural capital with approximately 60% Muslim, 20% Christian, and 10% Jewish population by 1478.

Mehmed patronized both Islamic and Western culture, inviting Italian Renaissance artists like Gentile Bellini and Greek scholars. He built the Fatih Mosque complex with eight madrasas, Topkapı Palace, and collected manuscripts in multiple languages. He preserved Christian relics and art, including Hagia Sophia mosaics, while also commissioning Christian-themed paintings. His interest in classical literature and history served diplomatic and legitimacy purposes.

### Family and Succession
Mehmed had at least eight consorts and four sons, including future sultan Bayezid II. He formally legalized fratricide to prevent civil wars like the Ottoman Interregnum, stating brothers could be killed "for the common benefit of the people." This practice continued until the 17th century.

## Terms
- **Devshirme**: System of recruiting Christian boys (ages 8–20) from Balkan provinces, converting them to Islam, and training them for military or administrative service.
- **Millet**: Autonomous religious community within the Ottoman Empire, granted legal and fiscal self-governance under its religious leader.
- **Kanunname**: Legal code issued by Mehmed II (1477–1481) that formalized government structure, official roles, salaries, and protocols.
- **Sürgün**: Forced population transfers to repopulate Constantinople after 1453, bringing Muslims, Christians, and Jews from across the empire.
- **Ghazi**: Warrior for the Islamic faith; Mehmed emphasized this role in justifying conquests.
- **Ulama**: Muslim religious scholars and teachers; Mehmed centralized their employment under the state.
- **Divan**: Ottoman imperial council; Mehmed transformed it from aristocratic to bureaucratic composition.
- **Ethnarch/Milletbashi**: Religious leader of a millet community, like the Orthodox Patriarch, who held civil authority over co-religionists.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Caesar of Rome claim**: While recognized by the Orthodox Church, Mehmed's claim to Roman imperial succession was rejected by Western Europe and remains historically contested.
- **Millet system origins**: Older scholarship credited Mehmed with creating the millet system, but recent research suggests this formalized later (18th–19th centuries), though some autonomy existed earlier.
- **Christian art collection**: Historians debate whether Mehmed's collection of Christian relics and art was for diplomatic bargaining or expressed sincere personal interest.
- **Death cause**: Substantial circumstantial evidence suggests Mehmed was poisoned, possibly by his son Bayezid II, though this remains unproven.
- **Troy visit**: The story of Mehmed visiting Troy to "avenge the Trojans" is considered fictitious by historians, with no Ottoman sources supporting it.
