# Frankenstein

## Overview
*Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus* is an 1818 Gothic novel by Mary Shelley. It tells the story of Victor Frankenstein, a scientist who creates a sapient creature, and explores themes of creation, responsibility, and isolation. The novel is a foundational work of both Gothic literature and science fiction, having profoundly influenced popular culture and literary discourse on the ethics of scientific ambition.

## Timeline
- **1815** — Mary Shelley travels through Europe, visiting areas near Frankenstein Castle.
- **Summer 1816** — At Lord Byron's villa, the group proposes writing ghost stories; Shelley has her "waking dream" that inspires *Frankenstein*.
- **1816–1817** — Shelley writes the novel, beginning in Bath and completing it in 1817.
- **1 January 1818** — The first edition is published anonymously in London.
- **1821** — A French translation appears, the first to credit Mary Shelley as author.
- **1823** — A second English edition is published, crediting Shelley on the title page.
- **31 October 1831** — A heavily revised, single-volume "popular" edition is published under Shelley's name.

## Body

### Composition and Inspiration
Mary Shelley began writing *Frankenstein* in the summer of 1816, during a stay at Lord Byron's villa near Lake Geneva. The group, confined indoors by poor weather, decided to each write a ghost story. Shelley was inspired by a "waking dream" of a scientist creating life, prompted by conversations about galvanism (the use of electricity in biology) and the nature of life. She expanded the initial short story idea into a novel with encouragement from her husband, Percy Bysshe Shelley. The novel was published anonymously in 1818, with a preface written by Percy Shelley.

### Plot Summary
Victor Frankenstein, obsessed with alchemy and modern science, creates a humanoid creature from body parts. Repulsed by his creation, he abandons it. The creature, initially benevolent, learns language and society by observing a family but is rejected by humans due to his appearance. After being shot while saving a girl, he turns against humanity and murders Victor's brother, framing the family servant. The creature demands Victor create a female companion. Victor begins but destroys the unfinished female, fearing the consequences. In revenge, the creature murders Victor's friend, wife, and father. Victor pursues the creature to the Arctic, where he dies after recounting his story to Captain Robert Walton. The creature then departs, planning to end his own life.

### The Creature and the Name "Frankenstein"
In Shelley's novel, the creature is never named "Frankenstein"; he is referred to as "monster," "creature," or "fiend." The name "Frankenstein" belongs to his creator, Victor. The common misnomer, where the creature itself is called Frankenstein, became widespread after early film adaptations, notably James Whale's 1931 movie. The creature is compared to both Adam from *Paradise Lost* and the fallen angel Lucifer, highlighting his isolation and rejection.

### Literary Influences and Themes
Shelley's work was influenced by her parents' writings, John Milton's *Paradise Lost*, and Samuel Taylor Coleridge's *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner*. Scientific ideas from figures like Erasmus Darwin and Luigi Galvani are reflected in the novel. Personal tragedies, including the death of her first child and her mother, influenced themes of loss, guilt, and parental responsibility. The novel critiques unchecked ambition and explores the consequences of "playing God."

### Publication and Reception
The 1818 first edition was published anonymously in 500 copies. Critical reviews were mixed; some praised its imagination, while others condemned it. The novel gained popularity through theatrical adaptations, beginning with Richard Brinsley Peake's 1823 play *Presumption; or, the Fate of Frankenstein*. The 1831 revised edition, with a new preface and stylistic changes, is the most widely read version today. Scholars debate the merits of the original 1818 text versus the revised 1831 version.

## Terms
- **Galvanism**: A late 18th/early 19th-century scientific concept involving the use of electrical current to stimulate muscle contraction, which influenced ideas about reanimation.
- **Sapient**: Intelligent or having the qualities of a human being, used to describe the creature's capacity for thought and language.
- **Gothic novel**: A literary genre featuring horror, death, and the supernatural, often set in dark, mysterious settings.
- **Romantic movement**: An artistic and literary movement emphasizing emotion, individualism, and the sublime power of nature, which influenced Shelley's work.
- **Prometheus**: A Titan from Greek mythology who created humans and stole fire for them; the novel's subtitle references this figure.
- **Paradise Lost**: An epic poem by John Milton about the biblical story of the Fall of Man, which the creature reads and identifies with.
- **Year Without a Summer**: The global climate anomaly of 1816, caused by the eruption of Mount Tambora, which set the scene for the novel's creation.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Textual Authority**: Scholars debate whether the 1818 or 1831 edition is the definitive text. The 1831 version includes significant revisions, a new preface, and a more moralizing tone.
- **Source of the Name "Frankenstein"**: Mary Shelley claimed the name came from a dream. Scholars have proposed alternative sources, including Frankenstein Castle in Germany, which she may have visited.
- **Scientific Inspiration**: The extent to which Shelley was directly influenced by specific scientists like Giovanni Aldini or Johann Konrad Dippel is debated, as she does not mention them in her notes.
- **The Creature's Nature**: Interpretations of the creature vary, from a sympathetic figure to a symbol of uncontrolled scientific ambition or a reflection of Shelley's personal traumas.
