# Jean Racine

## Overview
Jean-Baptiste Racine (1639–1699) stands alongside Molière and Corneille as one of the three great playwrights of 17th-century France. A master of the French alexandrine (a twelve-syllable verse line), he wrote nine secular tragedies, one comedy (*Les Plaideurs*), and two religious plays (*Esther*, *Athalie*) for a girls' school. His work defines French neoclassical tragedy: it observes the strict "unities" of time, place, and action, restricts vocabulary to roughly 2,500 words, and eliminates onstage violence in favor of psychological crisis. Racine replaced the Greek model of divine fate with a Jansenist-influenced vision of hereditary, physiological passion—love as a fatal illness that destroys dignity and reason. Though idolized under Louis XIV as the embodiment of *l'esprit français*, he was later dismissed by Romantics as artificial "courtly drama"; modern criticism has restored his reputation for psychological realism and structural rigor.

## Timeline
- **22 Dec 1639** — Born in La Ferté-Milon, Picardy.
- **1641–1643** — Orphaned; raised by grandparents, then grandmother at Port-Royal convent (1649).
- **1650s** — Educated at Petites écoles de Port-Royal (Jansenist); excels in Greek/Latin classics.
- **1663** — Gains praise from critic Nicolas Boileau; enters Paris theatrical circles.
- **20 Jun 1664** — *La Thébaïde* premiered by Molière's troupe at Théâtre du Palais-Royal.
- **1665** — *Alexandre le Grand* staged by Molière, then secretly moved to rival Hôtel de Bourgogne; breaks with Molière and seduces actress Thérèse du Parc.
- **1667** — *Andromaque* establishes his dominance; breaks ties with Port-Royal.
- **1668** — Sole comedy *Les Plaideurs* produced.
- **1669–1677** — Major secular tragedies: *Britannicus*, *Bérénice*, *Bajazet*, *Mithridate*, *Iphigénie*, *Phèdre*.
- **1672** — Elected to Académie française.
- **1674** — Boileau's *Art Poétique* declares Racine superior to Corneille.
- **1677** — *Phèdre* rivals Pradon's version; Racine quits commercial theater (scandal, conscience, or claque).
- **1679** — Court scandal; marries Catherine de Romanet; returns to Jansenist devotion.
- **1677–1699** — Royal historiographer; court offices (treasurer of France 1674, ordinary gentleman 1690, secretary 1696).
- **1689, 1691** — Returns to theater at Madame de Maintenon's request: *Esther*, *Athalie* for Saint-Cyr pupils.
- **21 Apr 1699** — Dies of liver cancer; buried at Port-Royal, moved to Saint-Étienne-du-Mont (1710).

## Body

### Education and Jansenist Formation
Racine’s childhood at Port-Royal, a center of **Jansenism** (a Catholic theology emphasizing original sin, divine grace, and predestination, condemned as heretical by the Pope and French bishops), shaped his tragic vision. The Petites écoles provided a rigorous classical education in Greek and Roman literature, supplying the mythological material for his plays. Though he later broke with the community and denied Jansenist influence, critics link his portrayal of hereditary corruption and irresistible passion to the sect’s fatalism.

### Theatrical Debut and Rivalries
Racine abandoned law for poetry, winning Boileau’s patronage. His first tragedy, *La Thébaïde* (1664), and *Alexandre le Grand* (1665) were staged by Molière’s troupe. When *Alexandre* succeeded, Racine secretly moved it to the Hôtel de Bourgogne, renowned for tragedy, betraying Molière and seducing his star actress, Thérèse du Parc. This secured the Hôtel de Bourgogne as his permanent company. He competed directly with Pierre and Thomas Corneille and faced rival versions of the same myths (e.g., Pradon’s *Phèdre* 1677), whose success—engineered by a **claque** (paid applauders)—contributed to his 1677 retirement.

### Neoclassical Form: The Unities and Alexandrine
Racine observed the **three unities** (time, place, action) more strictly than the Greeks or Corneille. He confined action to the final 24 hours of a prolonged crisis, used a single vague setting (e.g., "a room in Pyrrhus’s palace"), and excluded subplots and comic relief. His **alexandrine** verse—12 syllables with a medial caesura—is praised for "diamond-edge" precision, "hard, electric rage," and a restricted vocabulary of ~2,500 words that excludes quotidian terms. This formal austerity concentrates attention on psychological flux: fluctuating states of mind mount to a crisis (usually Act IV) and resolve swiftly.

### Tragic Vision: Love as Physiological Disorder
Racine replaced Greek *hamartia* (unwitting error) with a **flaw of character**: an atavistic, hereditary passion. Love is a "fatal illness" with alternating calm and crisis, fundamentally selfish and destructive. The **eternal triangle**—two young lovers thwarted by a queen’s unrequited love—structures most plays (*Phèdre*, *Bajazet*, *Britannicus*). The queen (Hermione, Roxane, Phèdre) is the most delineated role; she knows her passion is monstrous yet cannot master it. **Anagnorisis** (recognition) occurs early (Phèdre knows her guilt from Act I), not at the end. The **confidant** (Narcisse, Œnone) externalizes the hero’s inner conflict and occasionally drives action (Narcisse poisons Britannicus; Œnone accuses Hippolyte).

### Treatment of Sources
In secular plays, Racine freely rewrote Greek and Roman myth to serve modern psychology. He revived Euripides’ dead Astyanax in *Andromaque* to preserve Andromaque’s fidelity to Hector while maintaining tension over her son’s life. In *Iphigénie*, he borrowed Ériphile from Pausanias to avoid Artemis’s miraculous intervention. In *Phèdre*, he imported Aricie from Virgil to introduce sexual jealousy absent in Euripides and Seneca. In religious plays (*Esther*, *Athalie*), he adhered closely to the Old Testament, though he aged Joas from seven to nine/ten via the Septuagint and granted Joad prophetic powers from a Chronicles verse about his son.

### Court Career and Late Religious Plays
After 1677, Racine became royal historiographer, joined the Académie, and accumulated court sinecures. Louis XIV supported his widow and seven children. At Madame de Maintenon’s request, he wrote *Esther* (1689) and *Athalie* (1691) for the Maison royale de Saint-Louis at Saint-Cyr. These "moral fables" use biblical subjects but retain his secular dramaturgy: psychological intensity, alexandrine verse, and the unity of time.

### Critical Reception Across Centuries
- **17th c.**: Divided audiences vs. critics; Boileau’s *Art Poétique* (1674) crowned him over Corneille. Under Louis XIV, he became the state-sanctioned model of *l'esprit français*, stifling innovation.
- **19th c.**: Schiller and Goethe dismissed him as *höfisches Drama* (courtly drama), constrained by etiquette. French conservatives clung to him; Baudelaire and Flaubert revolted. British critics preferred Shakespeare.
- **20th c.**: Critics (e.g., Artaud) attacked his "psychological theater" for lacking violent action; others (Proust, Tadié) reclaimed his universality and realist depth.
- **21st c.**: Widely performed and translated (Lowell, Wilbur, Hughes, Argent); influences writers like A.S. Byatt.

## Terms
- ****Alexandrine**** — The standard French heroic verse line: 12 syllables divided by a caesura after the 6th syllable.
- ****Jansenism**** — A 17th-century Catholic movement (condemned as heretical) stressing original sin, predestination, and the necessity of efficacious grace; centered at Port-Royal.
- ****Three Unities**** — Neoclassical rules derived from Aristotle’s *Poetics*: unity of action (one main plot), unity of time (≤24 hours), unity of place (single locale).
- ****Hamartia**** — Aristotle’s term for the tragic hero’s error or flaw; in Racine, a hereditary character defect (monstrous passion) rather than an unwitting act.
- ****Anagnorisis**** — The moment of tragic recognition; in Racine, often occurs early and lucidly (Phèdre), not at the climax.
- ****Claque**** — An organized group paid to applaud or disrupt a performance; Pradon’s *Phèdre* succeeded partly through one.
- ****Confidant**** — A stock character (nurse, tutor, servant) who receives the protagonist’s monologues, externalizing inner conflict.
- ****Récit**** — A long narrative speech reporting offstage action (battles, deaths), required by the unity of place.
- ****Höfisches Drama**** — German term ("courtly drama") used by Schiller/Goethe to denote tragedy constrained by court etiquette and convention.
- ****L'esprit français**** — The 17th-century French national self-image of cultural superiority, embodied in Racine’s "perfect" tragedies.

## Debates and open questions
- **Jansenist influence**: Racine denied it; scholars dispute whether his fatalism derives from Port-Royal theology or a Christianized reading of Euripides/Seneca.
- **Cause of 1677 retirement**: Competing explanations—qualms of conscience (Warren Lewis), humiliation by Pradon’s claque, or the 1679 court scandal—remain unresolved.
- **Universality vs. period piece**: 19th-century Germans and British saw only courtly artifice; 20th-century critics argued for timeless psychological realism.
- **Translatability**: The linguistic density of the alexandrine is widely held to be untranslatable, yet major poets (Lowell, Hughes, Argent) continue to attempt it.
- **Historical veracity**: Contemporaries attacked *Britannicus* and *Mithridate* for inaccuracy; Racine countered that his rivals were worse offenders and that tragedy requires poetic, not historical, truth.
