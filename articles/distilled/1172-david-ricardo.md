# David Ricardo

## Overview
David Ricardo (1772–1823) was a British classical economist whose theories on value, rent, and international trade fundamentally shaped 19th-century economics. Born into a wealthy Sephardic Jewish family in London, he was disowned at 21 for eloping with a Quaker and converting to Unitarianism, then built a fortune in government securities before entering Parliament as a reformer. His most enduring contributions include the labor theory of value, the theory of rent, and the principle of comparative advantage, which argued that even mutually beneficial trade exists when nations specialize according to relative efficiencies.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Ricardo was the third surviving child of Abraham Israel Ricardo, a successful stockbroker, and Abigail Delvalle, from a respected Sephardic Jewish family recently arrived from the Dutch Republic. At 14, he began working with his father, but at 21, he eloped with Priscilla Anne Wilkinson, a Quaker, and converted to Unitarianism. This religious conversion led to complete estrangement from his family—his father disowned him and his mother never spoke to him again. With support from the banking house Lubbocks and Forster, Ricardo established himself independently and made his fortune financing government borrowing during the Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars.

### Parliamentary Record
Elected MP for Portarlington in 1818 after purchasing the seat for £4,000, Ricardo championed liberal reforms including free trade, parliamentary reform, and criminal law reform. He consistently opposed the Corn Laws, viewing them as barriers that raised food prices, increased wages, reduced profits, and hindered economic growth. His friend John Louis Mallett noted Ricardo's unwavering conviction, describing him as meeting every subject "with a mind made up, and opinions in the nature of mathematical truths," though Mallett doubted his disregard for practical experience. Ricardo voted for repeal of the Blasphemous and Seditious Libels Act, supported inquiry into the Peterloo Massacre, and advocated abolition of the death penalty for forgery.

### Economic Theory

**Value Theory**: Ricardo refined Adam Smith's labor theory of value, arguing that commodity value depends on the quantity of labor necessary for production, not on compensation paid to workers. He critiqued Smith's narrow focus on labor alone, demonstrating that even when labor is the sole factor, differences in worker skill and tools create value disparities. This led George Stigler to characterize Ricardo's theory as a "93% labor theory of value."

**Rent Theory**: Ricardo defined rent as the difference in produce obtained from employing equal quantities of capital and labor on different qualities of land. As population grows and superior land becomes fully cultivated, cultivation extends to poorer soils, diminishing returns. Landowners of more fertile land charge rent to compensate for this differential productivity. Ricardo argued this rent represented individual benefit but was a "paper monetary return" to society.

**Profit Theory**: In his Theory of Profit, Ricardo posited that as real wages increase, real profits decrease because revenue from manufactured goods is split between profits and wages. He stated: "Profits depend on high or low wages, wages on the price of necessaries, and the price of necessaries chiefly on the price of food."

### International Trade
Ricardo's theory of comparative advantage challenged mercantilist notions that trade aimed to accumulate gold and silver. He argued nations should specialize in industries where they have greatest efficiency relative to their own alternative uses of resources, not necessarily where they hold absolute advantages. Using his famous England-Portugal example with "four magic numbers," he demonstrated that even when one country is more efficient in producing all goods, mutual benefit arises through specialization. However, Ricardo recognized the theory applied only when capital was immobile between nations.

### Banking and Monetary Policy
Ricardo advocated for central bank autonomy as the sole issuer of money, proposing that a fixed ratio of gold and Treasury bills would secure liquidity. He argued that government should not issue paper money directly but borrow from the Bank of England, maintaining that currency augmentation or diminution should not depend solely on issuer will. His *Plan for the Establishment of a National Bank* (published posthumously in 1824) outlined these principles.

### Technological Change
Ricardo expressed concern about machinery's short-term impact on laborers, writing in 1821 that he was "convinced that the substitution of machinery for human labour, is often very injurious to the interests of the class of labourers." He acknowledged that working-class fears about machinery were not "founded on prejudice and error, but conformable to correct principles of political economy."

## Terms
- **Comparative Advantage**: The principle that nations should specialize in producing goods where they have the greatest relative efficiency, enabling mutually beneficial trade even when one nation is superior in all areas.
- **Labor Theory of Value**: The doctrine that the value of a commodity is determined by the quantity of labor necessary for its production.
- **Rent (Economic)**: The differential payment made for the use of land based on its relative productivity compared to other parcels.
- **Corn Laws**: British tariffs on imported grain and agricultural products, which Ricardo opposed as harmful to economic growth.
- **Ricardian Equivalence**: The proposition that government financing methods (taxes vs. debt) have no economic effect because taxpayers save to pay expected future taxes.
- **Laissez-faire**: Economic policy advocating minimal government intervention in markets.
- **Mercantilism**: Economic theory promoting trade surpluses and bullion accumulation as national wealth measures.
- **High Sheriff**: A ceremonial county official in England and Wales, a position Ricardo held in Gloucestershire (1818–19).

## Debates and Open Questions
Ricardo's theories have faced significant criticism. Joan Robinson argued that Portugal's experience under free trade demonstrated economic underdevelopment rather than mutual benefit, contending that Ricardo's model assumed static equilibrium with full employment and no trade imbalances. Utsa Patnaik identified a logical fallacy in assuming both countries produce the same goods, noting that developed and underdeveloped nations often trade goods not producible domestically. Critics also point out that Ricardo's assumption of continuous, absolute production ignores external disruptions like natural disasters that could cripple specialized economies. Modern economists consider the traditional interpretation of Ricardo's "four magic numbers" overly simplistic, with the "new interpretation" (rediscovered by Roy J. Ruffin in 2002 and elaborated by Andrea Maneschi in 2004) offering a more flexible reading based on production possibility sets rather than simple labor ratios. Additionally, contemporary extensions incorporate intermediate goods and unemployment, addressing deficiencies in original Ricardian models that assumed labor as the sole input.
