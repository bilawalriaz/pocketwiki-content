# History of communication

## Overview
The history of communication technologies traces the evolution of human methods for exchanging information, from speech to the internet. This progression is deeply intertwined with shifts in political and economic systems, as new tools for recording and transmitting ideas have consistently reshaped power structures and social organization. The development moved from ephemeral oral traditions to durable inscriptions, enabling increasingly complex societies and global networks.

## Timeline
- **c. 100,000 BCE** — Origin of human speech.
- **c. 30,000 BCE** — First symbols used for communication, including cave paintings.
- **c. 10,000–12,000 BCE** — Development of petroglyphs (rock carvings).
- **c. 9000 BCE** — Use of pictograms (illustrative symbols) begins.
- **c. 5000 BCE** — Pictograms evolve into ideograms (symbols for abstract ideas).
- **Late 4th millennium BCE** — First writing systems (e.g., cuneiform, hieroglyphs) emerge.
- **c. 2000 BCE** — First pure alphabets (abjads) appear in Ancient Egypt.
- **105 CE** — Invention of paper by Cai Lun.
- **1440** — Johannes Gutenberg invents the printing press with movable type.
- **1837** — Samuel Morse builds the first telegraph lines.
- **1876** — Alexander Graham Bell exhibits the first electric telephone.
- **1969** — First hosts of ARPANET, the internet's ancestor, are connected.

## Body

### From Speech to Symbols
Human communication began with speech approximately 100,000 BCE. The limitations of speech spurred the development of symbols around 30,000 BCE to improve the range and longevity of information. The earliest known symbols are cave paintings, with the oldest found in Chauvet Cave (c. 30,000 BCE). These evolved into petroglyphs (rock carvings) by about 10,000–12,000 BCE. The next step was pictograms (c. 9000 BCE), which, unlike petroglyphs, could tell a story by being ordered chronologically. Pictograms evolved into ideograms, which could represent abstract concepts like "sadness" or "day," not just physical objects.

### The Invention of Writing
Writing systems emerged in the late 5th millennium BCE, contemporary with the Bronze Age. The oldest are logographic (based on symbols for words or morphemes), including Sumerian cuneiform and Egyptian hieroglyphs. Cuneiform evolved from pictographs to a wedge-shaped script that included phonetic elements by 2800 BCE, becoming a general-purpose system. The Chinese script may have developed independently around the 16th century BCE. The first true alphabets (abjads), mapping symbols to single phonemes, appeared in Egypt around 2000 BCE. This Semitic alphabet spread and became the ancestor of nearly all modern alphabets, including the Greek alphabet, which adapted it by the 8th century BCE to better represent its spoken language.

### Printing and Mass Communication
The invention of printing technology revolutionized information dissemination. The Chinese developed wooden block movable type printing by 1305. Johannes Gutenberg's 1440 invention of a printing press with metal movable type was a pivotal innovation in Europe. Later advances included paper from wood pulp (1844) and the photocopier (1938). These technologies enabled mass communication, fundamentally changing access to information.

### Telecommunication and the Digital Age
Telecommunication—the transmission of signals over a distance—began with ancient methods like smoke signals and drums. The first electrical systems appeared in the 1830s. Samuel Morse's telegraph (1837) and code were revolutionary. The telephone followed in 1876. The 20th century saw rapid advances: radio broadcasting began in 1920, television signals were first transmitted in 1925, and the first geosynchronous communications satellite was launched in 1963. The digital era was marked by the first programmable computer (ENIAC, 1946), the first email (1965), and the connection of ARPANET hosts in 1969. The World Wide Web prototype was built in 1989 and released to the public in 1993, leading to the rise of search engines, social media, and smartphones.

## Terms
- **Logographic**: A writing system where symbols represent words or morphemes (e.g., Chinese characters).
- **Syllabic**: A writing system where symbols represent syllables (e.g., Japanese kana).
- **Alphabetic (Abjad)**: A writing system where symbols primarily represent consonant phonemes (e.g., the Phoenician alphabet).
- **Cuneiform**: An ancient writing system using wedge-shaped marks on clay tablets, developed by the Sumerians.
- **Petroglyph**: An image created by removing part of a rock surface by incising, picking, or carving.
- **Pictogram**: A symbol representing a concept or object by illustration.
- **Ideogram**: A graphical symbol that represents an idea or concept, not a specific word.
- **Telegraph**: A system for transmitting messages over a distance using coded electrical signals.
- **ARPANET**: The early packet-switching network and the first network to implement the TCP/IP protocol suite, forming the technical foundation of the internet.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The status of the Indus Valley script as a writing system is questioned by a few scholars.
- The exact nature and origin of the early "alphabetic" system developed in central Egypt around 1700 BCE remain open to interpretation, as these early writings cannot be read.
- There is scholarly debate regarding the earliest uses of the Greek alphabet due to the changes made to adapt the West-Semitic alphabet.
