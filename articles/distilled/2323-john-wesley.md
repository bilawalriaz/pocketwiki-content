# John Wesley

## Overview
John Wesley (1703–1791) was an English cleric and theologian who, with his brother Charles, founded Methodism, a revival movement within the Church of England. His emphasis on personal salvation, social holiness, and disciplined discipleship created a global Christian tradition that now numbers over 75 million adherents. Wesley's life and teachings continue to shape Protestant theology and practice.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Wesley was the 15th child of Samuel and Susanna Wesley, both from clerical families. His upbringing was highly disciplined, with daily scripture memorization and spiritual instruction. A rectory fire in 1709, from which he was rescued as a child, became part of his personal legend. He was educated at Charterhouse School and Christ Church, Oxford, where he was ordained a deacon in 1725 and a priest in 1728. At Oxford, he led the "Holy Club," a group focused on rigorous study and devout living, which earned the derisive nickname "Methodists" for their methodical habits.

### The Georgia Mission and Conversion
In 1735, Wesley sailed to Savannah, Georgia, as a missionary. The voyage introduced him to Moravian pietists, whose calm faith during a storm deeply impressed him. His High Church ministry in Georgia was controversial and ended in personal disappointment after a failed romance and legal disputes. He returned to England in 1737, spiritually depressed. On 24 May 1738, at a Moravian meeting on Aldersgate Street, he felt his heart "strangely warmed" while hearing Martin Luther's preface to Romans, an experience he later identified as his evangelical conversion.

### Founding Methodism
After his conversion, Wesley initially worked with Moravians but broke with them in 1739 over their doctrine of quietism. He began preaching outdoors, following the example of his friend George Whitefield, to reach people ignored by parish churches. He formed small societies for accountability and discipleship, appointing itinerant lay preachers—both men and women—to lead them. Despite persecution from clergy and mobs, the movement grew rapidly. Wesley established a connectional structure of societies, circuits, and annual conferences, creating the organizational foundation of Methodism.

### Theology and Advocacy
Wesley's theology, often summarized by the "Wesleyan Quadrilateral," prioritized Scripture, tradition, reason, and experience. He championed Arminianism, arguing against Calvinist predestination for the doctrine of prevenient grace, which enables all people to accept salvation. His most distinctive doctrine was "entire sanctification," the belief that believers could achieve a state of perfect love for God and neighbor in this life. He was a vocal abolitionist, calling slavery "the sum of all villainies," and cautiously supported women preachers after 1771. He remained an Anglican priest throughout his life, though his 1784 ordinations for America effectively created a separate Methodist church there.

### Personal Life and Legacy
Wesley traveled extensively, preaching over 40,000 sermons and covering 250,000 miles. He wrote prolifically on theology, music, and medicine. He married unhappily in 1751 and died poor in 1791, leaving behind a movement of 135,000 members. His teachings on personal and social holiness continue to influence Methodism and the broader Holiness movement.

## Terms
- **Methodism**: A revival movement within the Church of England, founded by John Wesley, emphasizing personal salvation, disciplined discipleship, and social holiness.
- **Holy Club**: A religious society at Oxford led by Wesley, known for its methodical devotional practices, which gave Methodism its name.
- **Aldersgate Experience**: Wesley's pivotal conversion on 24 May 1738, when he felt his heart "strangely warmed" by faith in Christ.
- **Arminianism**: A theological view, championed by Wesley, that emphasizes free will and the possibility of salvation for all, opposing Calvinist predestination.
- **Prevenient Grace**: A core Wesleyan doctrine stating that God's grace precedes human decision, enabling all people to respond to salvation.
- **Entire Sanctification**: Wesley's teaching that believers can achieve a state of perfect love for God and neighbor, free from willful sin, in this life.
- **Itinerancy**: The practice of appointing preachers to travel regularly between different circuits, a key organizational principle of early Methodism.
- **Wesleyan Quadrilateral**: A theological method attributed to Wesley, using Scripture, tradition, reason, and experience as sources for doctrine, with Scripture as primary.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Relationship with the Church of England**: Wesley insisted Methodists remain within the Anglican Church, but his ordinations for America in 1784 effectively created a separate denomination, a tension his brother Charles strongly opposed.
- **Doctrine of Perfection**: Wesley's teaching on "entire sanctification" or "Christian perfection" has been debated within and outside Methodism, with some critics calling it a form of "sinless perfectionism" that Wesley himself tried to avoid.
- **Later Views on Universal Salvation**: Some scholars suggest Wesley may have leaned toward universal salvation late in life, based on a 1787 letter endorsing a universalist work, though this interpretation is disputed.
- **Women's Preaching**: While Wesley eventually allowed women to preach, he imposed strict guidelines, and the full inclusion of women in Methodist ministry remained a gradual and contested process.
