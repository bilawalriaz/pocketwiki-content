# Cyrus the Great

## Overview
Cyrus II of Persia (c. 600–530 BC), known as Cyrus the Great, founded the Achaemenid Empire by overthrowing the Median Empire and conquering Lydia and the Neo-Babylonian Empire. At its height under his rule, the empire stretched from Asia Minor to the Indus River, becoming the largest the world had yet seen. The Achaemenid Empire's greatest territorial extent was achieved under Darius the Great, whose rule stretched from Southeast Europe and Northeast Africa in the west to the Indus Valley in the east. Cyrus is renowned for a policy of religious and cultural tolerance, exemplified by his liberation of the Jewish people from Babylonian captivity and his support for rebuilding the Temple in Jerusalem. He established a centralized administration using the satrapy system, allowing regional autonomy under royal oversight. His death in 530 BC during a Central Asian campaign is recorded in conflicting ancient accounts. Cyrus’s legacy as a model ruler influenced figures from Alexander the Great to Thomas Jefferson, and his tomb at Pasargadae remains a UNESCO World Heritage site.

## Timeline
- **c. 600–599 BC** — Birth of Cyrus II to Cambyses I (King of Anshan) and Mandane (daughter of Median King Astyages).
- **559 BC** — Cyrus succeeds his father Cambyses I as King of Anshan, remaining a vassal of the Median Empire.
- **553–550 BC** — War with Median King Astyages; Cyrus revolts, wins support of Median general Harpagus, and captures Ecbatana, overthrowing the Median Empire.
- **c. 546 BC** — Conquest of Lydia: Cyrus defeats King Croesus at the Battle of Thymbra, captures Sardis, and incorporates Asia Minor.
- **545–540 BC** — Eastern campaigns: Cyrus campaigns in Bactria, Sogdia, Saka, and other eastern provinces.
- **533 BC** — Cyrus crosses the Hindu Kush mountains and collects tribute from Indus Valley cities.
- **540 BC** — Capture of Elam and its capital Susa.
- **Oct 539 BC** — Battle of Opis; Persian forces rout Babylonian army. Sippar falls without resistance.
- **12 Oct 539 BC** — Persian troops enter Babylon by diverting the Euphrates; Nabonidus captured.
- **29 Oct 539 BC** — Cyrus enters Babylon; issues Cyrus Cylinder, proclaiming titles and restoration policies.
- **Dec 530 BC** — Death of Cyrus: Herodotus and Ctesias record death in battle against Massagetae or Derbices near Syr Darya; Xenophon claims he died peacefully at Pasargadae. Succeeded by son Cambyses II.

## Body

### Dynastic Background and Titles
The Achaemenid dynasty traced its lineage to Achaemenes (Haxāmaniš), who founded the state of Parsumash in southwestern Iran. His successor Teispes took the title "King of Anshan." Teispes’s son Cyrus I was succeeded by Cambyses I (r. 600–559 BC), Cyrus the Great’s father. Cyrus’s regal titles evolved with his conquests: the Nabonidus Chronicle notes his shift from "King of Anshan" to "King of Persia" between his Median war and his Lydian campaign. His full titulary eventually included Great King, King of Persia, Anshan, Media, Babylon, Sumer and Akkad, and King of the Four Corners of the World. Scholarly debate exists regarding his dynastic legitimacy; while the Behistun Inscription and Herodotus present him as an Achaemenid, M. Waters argues his family was of Teispid/Anshanite origin, unrelated to Darius the Great’s line.

### Early Life, Family, and Mythology
Cyrus was born c. 600–599 BC. He married Cassandane, an Achaemenian and daughter of Pharnaspes. They had two sons, Cambyses II and Bardiya, and three daughters, Atossa, Artystone, and Roxane. Cyrus and Cassandane were known to love each other deeply; after her death (c. 538 BC), Cyrus insisted on public mourning throughout the kingdom, and the Nabonidus Chronicle records Babylonia mourned for six days. Herodotus provides a mythological birth narrative: Median King Astyages dreamed his daughter Mandane’s offspring would overthrow him, so he ordered the infant Cyrus killed. The herdsman Mithradates spared him, substituting his own stillborn son. At age ten, Cyrus’s royal behavior during a game revealed his identity; Astyages punished the herdsman Harpagus by serving him his own son at a banquet, then sent Cyrus to his biological parents in Persia. Strabo claims Cyrus was originally named Agradates, adopting "Cyrus" (possibly from the river near Pasargadae or his grandfather Cyrus I) upon reuniting with his family.

### Rise to Power: Conquest of Media
In 559 BC, Cyrus inherited the throne of Anshan as a Median vassal. Around 553 BC, Astyages attacked Cyrus ("King of Anshan"). The Median general Harpagus, resentful of Astyages’s earlier cruelty, defected to Cyrus with part of the army. The Nabonidus Chronicle confirms a conflict lasting at least three years (553–550 BC), ending with the capture of Ecbatana and Astyages. Cyrus spared his grandfather and married his daughter Amytis, pacifying vassals like the Bactrians, Parthians, and Saka. He incorporated Sogdia during campaigns of 546–539 BC. This victory united the twin Achaemenid kingdoms of Parsa and Anshan into Persia proper.

### Conquest of Lydia and Asia Minor
Between the Median overthrow (550 BC) and the fall of Babylon (539 BC), Cyrus conquered Lydia. King Croesus attacked the Persian city of Pteria; Cyrus marched against him. The Battle of Pteria was a stalemate; Croesus retreated to Sardis. Cyrus pursued, besieging Sardis before allies could arrive. On Harpagus’s advice, Cyrus deployed dromedaries (camels) to panic the Lydian cavalry, winning the Battle of Thymbra (546 BC). Croesus was captured; Herodotus says Cyrus spared him as an advisor, but the Nabonidus Chronicle may imply execution. Cyrus left the Lydian Pactyas to transport Croesus’s treasury, but Pactyas revolted. Cyrus’s generals Mazares and then Harpagus suppressed the revolt and conquered Lycia, Aeolia, and Caria by 542 BC, using earthworks to breach city walls.

### Eastern Campaigns and Central Asia
After Lydia, Cyrus campaigned east (c. 545–540 BC). He failed to conquer Gedrosia (likely taken later by Darius I). He then attacked Bactria, Arachosia, Sogdia, Saka, Chorasmia, and Margiana. In 533 BC, he crossed the Hindu Kush, collecting tribute from Indus Valley cities, establishing vassal states in western India. He returned west due to unrest in Babylon.

### Conquest of the Neo-Babylonian Empire
By 540 BC, Cyrus held Elam and Susa. The Nabonidus Chronicle records Nabonidus gathering cult statues into Babylon, signaling war. In October 539 BC, Cyrus won the Battle of Opis on the Tigris. Sippar surrendered on 10 October; Nabonidus fled. On 12 October, General Gubaru entered Babylon without resistance—Herodotus claims the Persians diverted the Euphrates via a basin to march up the riverbed. Nabonidus surrendered; Cyrus entered Babylon on 29 October. He proclaimed his titles via the Cyrus Cylinder, deposited in the Esagila temple of Marduk. The Cylinder denounces Nabonidus as impious, credits Marduk for Cyrus’s victory, and records the repatriation of displaced peoples and restoration of temples. Cyrus’s empire now stretched from Asia Minor to the Indus River.

### Death and Burial
Cyrus died around December 530 BC. Accounts conflict:
*   **Herodotus:** Killed in a fierce battle against the Massagetae (Queen Tomyris) after a failed ruse involving wine. Tomyris decapitated him and dipped his head in blood.
*   **Ctesias:** Died fighting the Derbices, aided by Scythians, Indians, and war elephants northeast of the Syr Darya headwaters.
*   **Xenophon (Cyropaedia):** Died peacefully at his capital, Pasargadae.
*   **Berossus:** Died warring against the Dahae archers northwest of the Syr Darya.
Cuneiform evidence confirms death c. December 530 BC and Cambyses II’s succession. Cyrus was likely buried in a limestone tomb at Pasargadae (built c. 540–530 BC), described by Strabo and Arrian based on Aristobulus’s eyewitness account for Alexander the Great. The tomb held a golden coffin, fine carpets, and tapestries. Alexander visited the tomb, was horrified by its looting after his invasion, ordered its restoration, and admired Cyrus via Xenophon’s *Cyropaedia*. The tomb survives as a UNESCO World Heritage site.

### Administration and Politics
**Satrapy System:** Cyrus governed a multi-state empire from four capitals (Pasargadae, Babylon, Susa, Ecbatana). He organized conquered lands into satrapies (administrative provinces). A *satrap* (governor) administered the region; a *general* handled military recruitment and order; a *state secretary* kept records. Both general and secretary reported to the satrap and the central government. Darius the Great later expanded this system.
**Military Strategy:** The empire relied on tribute and conscripts. Cyrus created an organized army including the Immortals unit, consisting of 10,000 highly trained soldiers. His model of a vast, multi-ethnic superstate under a single central administration endured for centuries, adopted by the Seleucids, Parthians, and Sasanians.
**Infrastructure:** Cyrus developed Pasargadae with palaces, a royal park, and the "Paradisia" gardens—four-quartered walled gardens with 1,000+ meters of carved limestone irrigation channels. He established an innovative postal system using relay stations (*Chapar Khaneh*).
**Currency:** After conquering Lydia (547 BC), Cyrus adopted the Lydian *Croeseid* (the world’s first gold coin) as the empire’s standard, retaining the lion-and-bull design but reducing weight to 8.06 grams from the original 10.7 grams to meet demand for a larger population.

### Human Rights and the Cyrus Cylinder
The Cyrus Cylinder (discovered 1879, in the British Museum) is a clay foundation deposit in Akkadian cuneiform from 539 BC. It portrays Cyrus as Marduk’s chosen ruler, denounces Nabonidus, and records reforms: improving Babylonian lives, repatriating displaced peoples, and restoring temples. The Shah of Iran (1970s) and the UN (1971) promoted it as the "first human rights charter." Historians generally view it as a traditional Mesopotamian royal reform decree, not a modern rights document. Neil MacGregor (British Museum) calls it "the first attempt we know about running a society... with different nationalities and faiths."

### Religion and Philosophy
**Zoroastrianism:** It is debated if Cyrus was a Zoroastrian or if Zoroastrianism became the state religion later. Evidence for early influence includes family names and parallels between Isaiah’s description of Yahweh (influenced by Persian propaganda) and the Gathas’ Ahura Mazda. Counter-arguments cite Cyrus’s religious tolerance and the uncertain dating of the prophet Zoroaster.
**Tolerance:** Cyrus permitted freedom of religion, restored Babylonian god statues to sanctuaries, and funded temples of various faiths. Whether this was a novel policy or a continuation of Babylonian/Assyrian practice is disputed.
**Personal Faith:** The Cyrus Cylinder frames Cyrus’s rule around Marduk’s will. Inscriptions show he honored Marduk and local cults (Esangil, Ezida). Funerary customs at his tomb suggest a privileged cult for the Iranian god Mithra; the Pasargadae bas-relief figure may depict Mithra.
**Liberation of the Jews:** The Hebrew Bible (2 Chronicles, Ezra, Isaiah) credits Cyrus with ending the Babylonian captivity (c. 538 BC), permitting the return to Zion (Judah/Yehud), and commissioning the Second Temple (completed 515 BC under Darius I). Isaiah 45 calls Cyrus "messiah" (anointed), the only non-Jew so designated. Josephus (*Antiquities* 11.1) claims Cyrus was motivated by reading Isaiah’s prophecy naming him 140 years prior. Modern scholars debate the historicity of a specific "decree" versus a general repatriation policy; Grabbe argues the return was a "trickle" of perhaps 30,000 people over decades. Leith suggests Cyrus used religious decrees to co-opt strategic groups near Egypt.
**Quranic Theory:** Since the 19th century, some Muslim scholars (Syed Ahmad Khan, Abul Kalam Azad) have identified Cyrus with *Dhu al-Qarnayn* (Quran 18:83–98), a figure who travels east/west and builds a barrier against Gog and Magog. Western scholars generally favor identifying *Dhu al-Qarnayn* with Alexander the Great.

## Terms
- ****Achaemenid Empire**** — The first Persian Empire (c. 550–330 BC), founded by Cyrus the Great, spanning from the Balkans to the Indus Valley.
- ****Satrapy**** — An administrative province of the Achaemenid Empire governed by a satrap; the system allowed regional autonomy under central oversight.
- ****Satrap**** — The governor of a satrapy, often a vassal king, responsible for administration, taxation, and justice.
- ****Cyrus Cylinder**** — A clay cylinder inscribed in Akkadian cuneiform (539 BC), deposited in Babylon’s Esagila temple, recording Cyrus’s conquest, religious restoration, and repatriation policies.
- ****Immortals**** — An elite Achaemenid military unit of 10,000 highly trained soldiers, maintained at constant strength.
- ****Croeseid**** — The world’s first gold coin, minted by Lydian King Croesus; adopted and standardized by Cyrus after conquering Lydia (547 BC).
- ****Paradisia**** — The four-quartered walled gardens at Pasargadae with advanced limestone irrigation channels; a model for later Persian and Islamic garden design.
- ****Chapar Khaneh**** — Relay stations forming the Achaemenid postal system, enabling rapid communication across the empire.
- ****Massagetae**** — A nomadic Eastern Iranian tribal confederation (related to Scythians) living near the Syr Darya; Cyrus died campaigning against them per Herodotus.
- ****Dhu al-Qarnayn**** — "The Two-Horned One," a Quranic figure (18:83–98) identified by some Muslim scholars as Cyrus the Great, but traditionally linked to Alexander the Great in Western scholarship.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Dynastic Legitimacy:** Whether Cyrus was a true Achaemenid (per Behistun/Herodotus) or a Teispid/Anshanite ruler unrelated to Darius’s line (M. Waters).
*   **Death Narrative:** The cause, location, and circumstances of Cyrus’s death (battle vs. natural causes; Massagetae vs. Derbices vs. Dahae; Herodotus vs. Ctesias vs. Xenophon vs. Berossus).
*   **Zoroastrian Affiliation:** Whether Cyrus practiced Zoroastrianism, and whether the religion was a state creed during his reign or only later under Darius.
*   **Cyrus Cylinder as "Human Rights Charter":** Whether the cylinder represents a unique declaration of universal rights (Shah/UN view) or a standard Mesopotamian royal reform inscription (mainstream historians).
*   **Jewish Return Historicity:** Whether Cyrus issued a specific decree (Ezra 1) or a general policy; whether the return was a mass event or a slow "trickle" (Grabbe); the authenticity of the biblical text vs. propagandist idiom (Briend/Davies).
*   **Quranic Identification:** Whether *Dhu al-Qarnayn* refers to Cyrus, Alexander the Great, or a pre-Islamic Arabian king.
