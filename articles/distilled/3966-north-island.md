# North Island

## Overview
The North Island (Te Ika-a-Māui) is one of New Zealand's two main islands, separated from the South Island by Cook Strait. It is the country's most populous island, holding 76% of New Zealand's residents and 78% of its national GDP, making it the demographic and economic core of the nation.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Last Glacial Period ends; rising sea levels eventually separate the North and South Islands.
- **Pre-colonial** — Māori mythology describes the island as the great fish of the demigod Māui.
- **1770** — Captain James Cook's expedition circumnavigates New Zealand, recording Māori names for the island.
- **19th century** — The island is sometimes mapped as "New Ulster."
- **1860s** — Conclusion of the Otago gold rush begins a long-term "Northern drift" of New Zealand's population.
- **2009** — The New Zealand Geographic Board finds the island has no official name.
- **October 2013** — The board officially names the island "North Island" or "Te Ika-a-Māui."

## Body

### Naming and Identity
The island has long been known in English as the North Island. Its official Māori name, Te Ika-a-Māui ("the fish of Māui"), was formally adopted in 2013 after a public consultation found it was the most common name in Māori oral tradition. Another Māori name, Aotearoa, was historically used for the North Island but now refers to New Zealand as a whole. In standard usage, the definite article "the" is used in prose (e.g., "in the North Island") but omitted in maps and headings.

### Māori Mythology
According to Māori tradition, the North Island is the great fish caught by the demigod Māui. Māui and his brothers fished from their canoe, which became the South Island. After Māui hauled the fish from the sea, his brothers fought over it and chopped it up, creating the island's mountains and valleys. This myth is reflected in tribal names like Muaūpoko ("front of the head") and Muriwhenua ("backland"), which denote location and seniority within the "fish."

### Geography and Volcanology
The North Island covers 113,729 km². During the Last Glacial Period, it was connected to the South Island by a coastal plain; rising seas separated them about 7,000 years ago. The island features significant volcanic activity, including the Auckland Volcanic Field, Mount Ruapehu, Mount Taranaki, and the North Island Volcanic Plateau. Major geographic features include Lake Taupō, the Waikato and Whanganui rivers, and numerous bays and peninsulas.

### Demographics and Culture
As of June 2025, the North Island's population is 4,067,400. Growth is driven by natural increase and international migration, though internal migration has recently flowed toward the South Island. At the 2023 census, the largest ethnic groups were European (63.1%), Māori (19.8%), and Asian (19.3%). Māori form the majority in three districts: Kawerau, Ōpōtiki, and Wairoa. The island contains 30 urban areas with populations over 10,000, including Auckland and the capital, Wellington.

### Economy and Governance
The North Island's sub-national GDP was NZ$282.355 billion in 2021, representing 78% of New Zealand's total. It is served by major airports, with Auckland being the busiest. The island is divided into nine local government regions, including Northland, Auckland, Waikato, and Wellington.

## Terms
- **Te Ika-a-Māui**: The official Māori name for the North Island, meaning "the fish of Māui."
- **Cook Strait**: The body of water separating the North and South Islands.
- **Northern drift**: The long-term trend of New Zealand's population shifting toward the North Island.
- **Natural increase**: Population growth from births minus deaths.
- **Golden Triangle**: The region enclosed by Auckland, Hamilton, and Tauranga, containing nearly half of New Zealand's population and economic activity.
- **Pākehā**: A Māori term for New Zealanders of European descent.

## Debates and Open Questions
The 2013 official naming resolved a formal debate about the island's lack of an official name. A historical naming debate involved the use of "Aotearoa" for the North Island versus the entire country. The ongoing demographic trend of "Northern drift" is noted, though its pace has slowed and internal migration patterns have recently reversed.
