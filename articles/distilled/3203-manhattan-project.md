# Manhattan Project

## Overview
The Manhattan Project was the secret World War II research and development program that produced the first nuclear weapons. Led by the United States with British and Canadian collaboration, it employed over 129,000 people at its peak and cost nearly $2 billion. Its success, culminating in the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, ended the war and ushered in the Atomic Age, fundamentally altering global politics and warfare.

- **Jun 1941** — Office of Scientific Research and Development (OSRD) created, absorbing uranium research.
- **Oct 1941** — President Roosevelt approves accelerating the atomic program.
- **Jun 1942** — Army Corps of Engineers assigned to manage the project; Manhattan District established.
- **Sep 1942** — Major General Leslie Groves takes command.
- **Dec 1942** — First self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction achieved (Chicago Pile-1).
- **Aug 1943** — Quebec Agreement formalizes US-UK-Canadian collaboration.
- **Nov 1943** — X-10 Graphite Reactor at Oak Ridge goes critical.
- **Sep 1944** — Hanford's first plutonium reactor (B Reactor) goes critical.
- **Jul 1945** — Trinity test: first nuclear device detonated in New Mexico.
- **Aug 1945** — Atomic bombs dropped on Hiroshima (Little Boy) and Nagasaki (Fat Man).
- **Jan 1947** — United States Atomic Energy Commission (AEC) assumes control from the Manhattan Project.

## Body

### Origins and Organization
The project began in response to fears that Nazi Germany would develop an atomic bomb first. Following the 1938 discovery of nuclear fission, scientists like Leo Szilard urged the U.S. to act. Early research was slow until British findings (the MAUD Committee) and physicist Mark Oliphant's intervention in 1941 convinced American leaders of the bomb's feasibility. The Army took over in 1942, creating the Manhattan District under Colonel James Marshall. In September 1942, General Leslie Groves was appointed to lead the project, providing aggressive management and securing top priority for resources. J. Robert Oppenheimer was chosen to direct the secret weapons design laboratory at Los Alamos, New Mexico.

### Production Sites and Materials
The project's primary goal was to produce sufficient fissile material: highly enriched uranium and plutonium. Over 80% of the budget funded massive production plants. Uranium enrichment occurred at the Clinton Engineer Works (Oak Ridge, Tennessee) using three parallel methods: electromagnetic separation (Y-12), gaseous diffusion (K-25), and thermal diffusion (S-50). Plutonium was produced in nuclear reactors at the Hanford Engineer Works (Washington), with chemical separation plants to extract it. These sites, along with research facilities like the Metallurgical Laboratory in Chicago, were built in remote areas, requiring the relocation of thousands of residents.

### Weapon Design and Testing
The Los Alamos Laboratory designed two bomb types. The simpler "Little Boy" was a gun-type weapon using enriched uranium. The more complex "Fat Man" was an implosion-type weapon using plutonium. The implosion design became critical after 1944, when Hanford-produced plutonium was found to contain plutonium-240, making it unsuitable for a gun-type bomb due to a high spontaneous fission rate. The first nuclear device, an implosion bomb codenamed "the gadget," was successfully tested in the Trinity explosion on July 16, 1945, at White Sands Proving Ground, New Mexico.

### Intelligence and Collaboration
The project gathered intelligence on the German nuclear program through the Alsos Mission, which concluded Germany was far from developing a bomb. Collaboration with the UK, formalized by the 1943 Quebec Agreement, brought British scientists to Los Alamos and other sites. Despite strict security and compartmentalization, Soviet spies, most notably Klaus Fuchs, penetrated the program, providing crucial information to the USSR.

### Use and Aftermath
The 509th Composite Group, equipped with modified B-29 bombers, delivered the weapons. Little Boy was dropped on Hiroshima on August 6, 1945, and Fat Man on Nagasaki on August 9, 1945. Japan surrendered on August 15. Post-war, the project conducted further tests (Operation Crossroads), developed new weapons, and laid the foundation for the national laboratory system and the nuclear navy. Control passed to the civilian Atomic Energy Commission in January 1947.

## Terms
- **Fissile Material**: A substance capable of sustaining a nuclear chain reaction (e.g., uranium-235, plutonium-239).
- **Implosion**: A weapon design where conventional explosives compress a subcritical sphere of fissile material into a supercritical state.
- **Gun-type**: A simpler weapon design where one subcritical piece of fissile material is fired into another to form a supercritical mass.
- **Critical Mass**: The minimum amount of fissile material needed to sustain a nuclear chain reaction.
- **Plutonium-240**: An isotope of plutonium with a high spontaneous fission rate, making reactor-bred plutonium unsuitable for gun-type weapons.
- **Compartmentalization**: A security policy where individuals know only what is necessary for their specific job.
- **Calutron**: An electromagnetic isotope separator developed at the University of California.
- **Gaseous Diffusion**: An enrichment process that separates isotopes by forcing a gas through a semi-permeable membrane.
- **Bismuth phosphate process**: The chemical method developed to separate plutonium from irradiated uranium in Hanford's separation plants.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Necessity of the bombings**: Historians debate whether the atomic bombings of Japan were militarily necessary to end the war or if alternatives, such as a demonstration or modified surrender terms, could have achieved surrender without the massive civilian casualties.
- **Soviet espionage impact**: The extent to which espionage accelerated the Soviet atomic bomb project is debated. Some argue it saved the USSR years of research, while others contend the Soviets spent significant time verifying the stolen data.
- **Environmental legacy**: The long-term environmental contamination from production sites like Hanford, where radioactive wastes were stored in temporary tanks that later leaked, remains a major cleanup challenge and subject of ongoing legal and political disputes.
