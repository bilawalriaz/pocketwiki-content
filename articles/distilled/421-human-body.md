# Human body

## Overview
The human body is the complete physical structure of a human being, organized hierarchically from chemical elements into cells, tissues, organs, and organ systems. It maintains homeostasis—stable internal conditions such as blood sugar, oxygen, and pH—through the coordinated function of systems including the circulatory, nervous, endocrine, and musculoskeletal systems. Study of the body spans anatomy (structure), physiology (function), histology (tissues), and embryology (development), informing medicine, art, and biology. Adult bodies contain roughly 30 trillion human cells and 38 trillion bacteria, are approximately 60% water by weight, and rely on DNA-directed protein synthesis for cellular function and repair.

## Timeline
- **c. 420 BCE** — Hippocrates initiates the study of human physiology in Ancient Greece.
- **384–322 BCE** — Aristotle applies critical thinking and emphasizes the relationship between structure and function.
- **2nd century CE** — Galen of Pergamum compiles classical anatomical knowledge used throughout the Middle Ages; first to use experiments to probe body functions.
- **1497–1558** — Jean Fernel introduces the term "physiology".
- **1514–1564** — Andreas Vesalius pioneers modern human anatomy via dissection; publishes *De humani corporis fabrica*.
- **1628** — William Harvey describes the circulatory system, combining observation with experiment.
- **1838** — Matthias Schleiden and Theodor Schwann establish cell theory: organisms are made of cells.
- **1813–1878** — Claude Bernard develops the concept of the *milieu interieur* (internal environment).
- **1871–1945** — Walter Cannon defines homeostasis as the regulation of the internal environment to a steady state.
- **20th century** — Knut Schmidt-Nielsen and George Bartholomew extend studies to comparative physiology and ecophysiology; evolutionary physiology emerges as a distinct subdiscipline.
- **Modern era** — Imaging technologies (MRI, CT, ultrasound) enable unprecedented detail in anatomical study.

## Body

### Composition
The human body is chemically composed primarily of hydrogen, oxygen, carbon, calcium, and phosphorus. An adult male body is roughly 60% water (about 42 liters), divided between intracellular fluid (23 L), interstitial fluid (8.4 L), and blood plasma (3.2 L). Electrolyte composition differs across compartments: sodium and chloride dominate extracellular fluid, while potassium and phosphates prevail intracellularly. At maturity, the body contains an estimated 30 trillion human cells and 38 trillion bacteria, plus commensal organisms on the skin. Cells reside in an extracellular matrix of proteins like collagen bathed in extracellular fluid. Each cell suffers tens of thousands of DNA damage events daily; unrepaired or misrepaired damage can cause mutations threatening cell viability.

### Genome and Cellular Function
Cellular function is directed by DNA housed in the nucleus. Segments of DNA are transcribed into RNA, which directs protein synthesis; proteins determine cell activity and gene expression, allowing self-regulation via production levels. Mature red blood cells lack a nucleus and DNA. Not all body structures are cellular; the extracellular matrix and fluids are acellular components.

### Tissues
Tissues are groups of cells performing a specialized function; their study is histology. Four main types exist: **epithelia** (lining cells covering surfaces and cavities, including endothelium lining blood vessels), **connective tissue** (support and structure), **nerve tissue** (signaling), and **muscle tissue** (contraction). Epithelia regulate passage, protect underlying structures, and serve as sensory surfaces.

### Organs
Organs are structured collections of tissues with specific functions (e.g., heart, lungs, liver). Most reside within body cavities: the thoracic cavity houses the heart and lungs (in pleural cavities), while the abdominal cavity contains the stomach, liver, spleen, and intestines. The skin is the major external organ.

#### Heart
Located in the thoracic cavity between the lungs, the heart is enclosed by the pericardium. It has two atria and two ventricles. The primary purpose of the atria is to allow uninterrupted venous blood flow to the heart during ventricular systole, allowing enough blood to enter the ventricles during atrial systole; consequently, the atria allow a cardiac output roughly 75% greater than would be possible without them. The purpose of the ventricles is to pump blood to the lungs through the right ventricle and to the rest of the body through the left ventricle. An electrical conduction system (sinoatrial node → atrioventricular node) coordinates contraction. Coronary artery disease—plaque buildup narrowing coronary arteries—is the leading global cause of death (16% of all deaths), risking myocardial infarction. Risk factors include obesity, smoking, high cholesterol, hypertension, inactivity, and diabetes. Primary heart cancer is exceedingly rare due to minimal cell division in mature cardiomyocytes.

#### Gallbladder
A pear-shaped organ posterior to the liver’s right lobe, the gallbladder stores bile from the liver (via the cystic and common hepatic ducts) and releases it into the duodenum via the common bile duct to emulsify fats. Blood supply is typically from the cystic artery (branch of the right hepatic artery). Gallstones (cholelithiasis) are common; obstruction causes biliary colic, nausea, and vomiting. Standard treatment is cholecystectomy. Gallstones increase risk of gallbladder cancer, which is rare but rapidly fatal if not caught early.

### Organ Systems
#### Circulatory System
Comprises the heart and blood vessels (arteries, veins, capillaries). Blood transports oxygen, nutrients, waste, hormones, and immune cells. Two circuits: **pulmonary** (heart ↔ lungs for gas exchange) and **systemic** (heart ↔ body). Blood cells originate in bone marrow and spleen.

#### Digestive System
Includes mouth, esophagus, stomach, small and large intestines, rectum, liver, pancreas, gallbladder, and salivary glands. Converts food into absorbable molecules (amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins, minerals). Peristalsis propels food. Digestion begins mechanically and enzymatically in the mouth; gastric acids in the stomach create chyme; the small intestine absorbs nutrients and water; the large intestine forms feces for rectal storage and anal expulsion.

#### Endocrine System
Principal glands: pituitary, thyroid, adrenals, pancreas, parathyroids, gonads. Nearly all tissues also secrete hormones. Hormones act as inter-system signals regulating diverse functions (e.g., blood pressure, metabolism, growth).

#### Immune System
Comprises white blood cells, thymus, lymph nodes, and lymph channels (shared with lymphatic system). Distinguishes self from non-self; neutralizes pathogens via antibodies, cytokines, toll-like receptors, and other specialized proteins.

#### Integumentary System
Skin, hair, nails, sweat glands, sebaceous glands. Provides containment, structural protection, and a major sensory interface.

#### Lymphatic System
Extracts, transports, and metabolizes lymph (interstitial fluid). Structurally and functionally analogous to the circulatory system but carries lymph instead of blood.

#### Musculoskeletal System
Skeleton (bones, ligaments, tendons, joints, cartilage) plus attached muscles. Provides structure and movement. Long bones contain marrow (hematopoiesis); all bones store calcium and phosphate. Subdivided into muscular and skeletal systems.

#### Nervous System
Neurons and glial cells form nerves, ganglia, gray matter, brain, and spinal cord. The brain governs thought, emotion, memory, sensory processing, and system control. Special senses: vision, hearing, taste, smell.
- **Structural division**: Central Nervous System (CNS: brain, spinal cord) organizes motion, processes sensory data, and mediates cognition; Peripheral Nervous System (PNS: nerves, ganglia outside CNS) gathers sensory input and directs motor output. Whether CNS directly generates consciousness is debated.
- **Functional division**: Somatic Nervous System (voluntary: speech, skeletal movement); Autonomic Nervous System (involuntary: digestion, blood pressure regulation).
- **Diseases**: Epilepsy (abnormal electrical activity → seizures); Multiple Sclerosis (autoimmune demyelination); Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis / Lou Gehrig’s disease (motor neuron degeneration).

#### Reproductive System
Produces germ cells and hormones; matures at puberty.
- **Female**: Ovaries, fallopian tubes, uterus, cervix, vulva. ~70,000 immature oocytes at birth → ~40,000 at puberty; no new oocytes produced. Menstruation and secondary sex characteristics begin at ages 9–13.
- **Male**: Testes (produce sperm continuously), epididymides, vasa deferentia, accessory glands, penis, scrotum.
- **Pathologies**: Polycystic ovary syndrome, testicular torsion, STIs (syphilis, HIV, chlamydia, HPV), and cancers of most reproductive organs.

#### Respiratory System
Nose, nasopharynx, trachea, lungs. Diaphragm contraction creates negative pressure, drawing air into alveoli (gas-exchange sacs surrounded by capillaries). Oxygen diffuses into blood; CO₂ and water vapor exit. Impediments cause disease: asthma (chronic inflammation → wheezing), pneumonia (alveolar infection, sometimes tuberculous), emphysema (alveolar wall destruction, often from smoking).

#### Urinary System
Two kidneys, two ureters, bladder, urethra. The kidneys filter about 150 quarts (170 liters) of blood daily via nephrons, but most of it is returned to the bloodstream with only 1–2 quarts (1–2 liters) ending up as urine containing urea, creatinine, excess ions, and water. Ureters use peristalsis to move urine to the bladder (capacity ~16 oz / 0.5 L). Bladder empties via urethra upon CNS signal. Diseases: kidney stones (crystallized urine solutes), urinary tract infections, renal failure (requiring dialysis or transplant), and cancers of bladder, kidneys, ureters, urethra.

### Anatomy
Human anatomy studies body shape and form. The body has four limbs, head, neck, and torso. Shape derives from a bony skeleton surrounded by adipose tissue, muscle, connective tissue, and organs. The vertebral column encloses the spinal cord (CNS pathway). Named structures include all major bones, muscles, and nerves (excluding variants like sesamoid bones). Blood circulates: deoxygenated blood → venae cavae → right heart → lungs → left heart → aorta → arteries → capillaries → veins → repeat. Blood is filtered at kidneys and liver. Major cavities: cranial (brain, protected by blood-brain barrier), pleural (lungs), abdominal (intestines, liver, spleen). Proportions vary by age, sex, and individual.

### Physiology
Human physiology examines mechanical, physical, bioelectrical, and biochemical functions in health. Systems interact to maintain homeostasis (stable blood sugar, oxygen, pH, temperature, blood pressure, posture, energy supply). The **neuroendocrine system** exemplifies integration: the nervous system transmits impulses/neurotransmitters to the brain; the endocrine system releases hormones (e.g., for blood pressure/volume regulation); together they regulate the internal environment.

### Development
Development begins at fertilization (sperm penetrates ovum). The zygote implants in the uterus, becoming an embryo then fetus until birth. Postnatal growth includes physical and psychological maturation, driven by genetic, hormonal, and environmental factors. Aging continues through childhood, adolescence, adulthood, and old age.

### Society and Culture
#### Professional Study
Health professionals learn via illustrations, models, and demonstrations. Medical/dental students add cadaver dissection. Anatomy, physiology, and biochemistry are core first-year medical sciences.

#### Depiction
In Western contexts, body depictions fall into information (science/education), art, and pornography. Ambiguous images risk misinterpretation; legal disputes often center on the art/erotica boundary.

#### History of Anatomy
Hippocratic Corpus (Ancient Greece) described skeleton/muscles. Galen (2nd century) synthesized classical texts. Vesalius (1514–1564) established modern anatomy via dissection (*De humani corporis fabrica*). Microscopy enabled histology. Modern imaging (MRI, CT, fluoroscopy, ultrasound) provides high-resolution in vivo detail.

#### History of Physiology
Hippocrates (~420 BCE) and Aristotle (384–322 BCE), who applied critical thinking and emphasis on the relationship between structure and function, founded the field. Galen (c. 129 – c. 216) was the first to use experiments to probe the body's functions. Fernel coined "physiology" (16th century). Harvey (1628) elucidated circulation. Cell theory (1838) and Bernard’s *milieu interieur* (19th century) led to Cannon’s homeostasis concept. In the 20th century, the physiologists Knut Schmidt-Nielsen and George Bartholomew extended their studies to comparative physiology and ecophysiology. Most recently, evolutionary physiology has become a distinct subdiscipline.

## Terms
- ****Homeostasis**** — The maintenance of a stable internal environment (e.g., blood sugar, oxygen, pH, temperature) through interacting organ systems.
- ****Histology**** — The microscopic study of tissues (groups of cells with a specialized function).
- ****Epithelia**** — Lining cells covering external surfaces, gastrointestinal tract, and internal cavities (endothelium); regulate transport, protect, and sense.
- ****Peristalsis**** — Rhythmic contraction and relaxation of smooth muscle that propels contents through tubular organs (e.g., GI tract, ureters).
- ****Neuroendocrine system**** — Functional integration of the nervous and endocrine systems to regulate the internal environment via nerve impulses and hormones.
- ****Milieu interieur**** — Claude Bernard’s term for the extracellular fluid environment surrounding cells, later linked to homeostasis.
- ****Alveoli**** — Microscopic air sacs in the lungs where gas exchange occurs across capillary walls.
- ****Nephron**** — The functional filtering unit of the kidney; removes waste, balances electrolytes, and forms urine.
- ****Sesamoid bones**** — Anatomical variant bones embedded in tendons (e.g., patella); not universally present or named.
- ****Cholecystectomy**** — Surgical removal of the gallbladder, standard treatment for symptomatic gallstones.

## Debates and open questions
- **Consciousness origin**: Whether the central nervous system directly gives rise to consciousness remains unresolved.
- **Cell counts**: Estimates of total human cells (~30 trillion) and bacteria (~38 trillion) are derived from organ-level summation and carry uncertainty.
- **Coronary artery disease primacy**: While identified as the leading global cause of death (16%), the relative contribution of risk factors (obesity, smoking, lipids, hypertension, inactivity, diabetes) varies by population.
- **Gallbladder cancer rarity vs. lethality**: The disease is uncommon but rapidly fatal if not diagnosed early; screening strategies for high-risk (gallstone) populations are not established in the source.
