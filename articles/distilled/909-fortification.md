# Fortification

## Overview
Fortification refers to military constructions designed to defend territories in warfare and establish control during peacetime. Derived from Latin *fortis* ("strong") and *facere* ("to make"), fortifications have evolved alongside military technology—from ancient mud-brick walls to modern underground bunkers. Their development reflects the constant arms race between attackers and defenders, with each innovation in weaponry prompting new defensive strategies.

## Body

### Early Uses and Regional Developments
Fortifications date back to the earliest settlements. In the Indus Valley, cities like Kot Diji and Harappa featured defensive walls, while Mohenjo-daro and Dholavira used stone and baked brick. In Europe, the Neolithic settlement of Sesklo (c. 6800 BC) and later Solnitsata (c. 4700 BC) in Bulgaria represent some of the earliest walled settlements. The "Great Wall of Brodgar" (c. 3200 BC) in Scotland, though possibly ritualistic, demonstrates early large-scale stone construction.

In the Near East, casemate walls—double walls with internal chambers—became common from the Middle Bronze Age through Iron Age II. These structures, found at sites like Ti'inik (Taanach), were eventually replaced by solid walls by the 9th century BC, likely due to improved siege technology by the Neo-Assyrian Empire.

### Roman and Medieval Fortifications
The Romans advanced fortification engineering with massive, mortar-bound stone walls. Hadrian’s Wall (AD 122) marked the northern frontier of Roman Britain, while the Aurelian Walls and Theodosian Walls protected Rome and Constantinople respectively. Roman forts (*castella*) and Greek *phrouria* served as garrisons guarding roads and borders.

Medieval Europe saw the rise of castles, evolving from earlier Roman forts and hill forts. Emerging in the 9th century under the Carolingians, castles became centers of feudal power. Towns grew around them, often protected by walls and ditches. The Renaissance brought new challenges: gunpowder weapons necessitated lower, thicker walls with angled bastions to deflect cannon fire. Star forts, exemplified by Fort Bourtange, incorporated geometric designs allowing interlocking fields of fire.

### Modern Era: Industrial Age to Present
The 19th century introduced explosive shells, rendering traditional star forts vulnerable. Polygonal fortifications emerged, featuring deep ditches cut into rock, underground tunnels, and minimal surface profiles. The ring fortress concept—forts spaced to support each other—was adopted in Prussia and France (e.g., Séré de Rivières system).

World War I entrenched trench warfare, but mobility returned in WWII. Permanent fortifications like the Maginot Line proved bypassable, emphasizing the importance of mobile defense. Post-WWII, nuclear weapons and ICBMs shifted focus to missile silos, submarines, and airpower. Modern fortifications include hardened aircraft shelters, underground bunkers (e.g., Cheyenne Mountain), and minefields.

Today, large-scale static fortifications are largely obsolete. Field fortifications—temporary positions using sandbags, gabions, or logs—dominate low-intensity conflicts. Military airfields rely on revetments and dispersal to protect assets from attack.

## Terms
- **Castrametation**: The traditional art of laying out military camps or constructing fortifications, especially as practiced by Roman legions.
- **Casemate wall**: A double wall with transverse partitions creating internal chambers, used for storage or reinforcement during sieges.
- **Oppidum**: A large fortified Celtic settlement, common in Central Europe during the late Iron Age.
- **Star fort**: A 16th-century fortification style characterized by angular bastions and geometric design to withstand cannon fire.
- **Polygonal fortification**: A 19th-century style featuring low-profile forts with deep ditches and underground passages, designed to resist explosive shells.
- **Ring fortress**: A defensive system where forts are placed in a ring around a city or strategic point, capable of mutual support.
- **Field fortification**: Temporary defensive structures built during active campaigns, typically using earth, logs, or sandbags.
- **Bastion**: A projecting part of a fortification, traditionally triangular, providing flanking fire along the curtain wall.
- **Enceinte**: The main defensive wall or perimeter of a fortification.
- **Caponier**: A passage or gallery in a ditch that allows defenders to fire along the length of the ditch.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact function of early walls like the "Great Wall of Brodgar" remains debated—whether purely defensive or also symbolic/ritualistic.
- The origins of casemate walls are contested; once attributed to the Hittites, archaeological evidence now shows earlier examples in the Levant.
- The effectiveness of traditional fortifications in colonial warfare is questioned, as small garrisons could hold out against indigenous forces but were vulnerable to coordinated modern attacks.
- The strategic value of permanent fortifications in the nuclear age is widely debated, given their vulnerability to overwhelming firepower.
- Whether the Maginot Line was strategically successful is disputed—it failed to prevent German invasion but succeeded in forcing Germany to attack through Belgium.
