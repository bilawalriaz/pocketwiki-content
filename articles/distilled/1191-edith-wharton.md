# Edith Wharton

## Overview

Edith Wharton (1862–1937) was an American novelist and designer whose insider's perspective on Gilded Age high society made her one of the most incisive chroniclers of upper-class American life. Drawing from her own privileged upbringing in New York's social elite, she critiqued the constraints and moral emptiness of her world through works like *The House of Mirth* and *The Age of Innocence*. Beyond fiction, Wharton was a prolific writer of travel literature, interior design treatises, and ghost stories, and she became a committed advocate for the French war effort during World War I, earning the Legion of Honour.

## Body

### Early Life and Social Formation

Edith Newbold Jones was born into one of New York’s most affluent families, with ancestral ties to the Rensselaer and Astor lineages. Her father, George Frederic Jones, amassed wealth in real estate, and the family’s saying—“keeping up with the Joneses”—is attributed to his branch. Raised in Manhattan and later spending winters in New York and summers in Newport, Rhode Island, Wharton was immersed in the rituals of high society. Her early education was conducted by tutors and governesses during extended European residences, where she developed fluency in French, German, and Italian. Despite her mother Lucretia’s strict prohibition against novels, Wharton voraciously read from her father’s library, absorbing classics, philosophy, and history. This intellectual self-education, combined with keen observation of social customs, laid the groundwork for her later literary career.

### Literary Beginnings and Debutante Years

Wharton began writing stories and poetry in childhood, though her mother’s disapproval discouraged early publication ambitions. At age 15, she published a translation under a pseudonym, and by 1878, a privately printed volume of her verse appeared. In 1879, she published a poem under a pseudonym in the *New York World*, followed by five anonymous poems in the *Atlantic Monthly* in 1880. However, between 1880 and 1890, she largely set aside writing to fulfill the expectations of a debutante, navigating courtships and social engagements. Her engagement to Henry Leyden Stevens ended abruptly in 1882, shortly before their planned wedding. These experiences informed her understanding of the limited agency available to women within elite society, a theme that would dominate her fiction.

### Marriage, Design, and Literary Emergence

On April 29, 1885, at the age of 23, Wharton married Edward Robbins (Teddy) Wharton, who was 12 years her senior, at the Trinity Chapel Complex in Manhattan. A man of similar social background who shared her passion for travel and aesthetics, Teddy Wharton accompanied her on extensive overseas trips, mostly visiting Italy. The couple settled in Newport and later built The Mount, their estate in Lenox, Massachusetts, which Wharton designed herself. Collaborating with designer Ogden Codman, she authored *The Decoration of Houses* (1897), an influential guide to domestic architecture and interior design. During this period, Wharton also began publishing short stories and essays, though her first novel did not appear until she was forty. Her marriage grew strained due to Teddy Wharton’s mental instability, leading to their divorce in 1913 after 28 years.

### World War I and Humanitarian Work

When World War I erupted, Wharton chose to remain in Paris despite the danger. She threw herself into humanitarian efforts, opening a workroom for unemployed women in August 1914, which soon expanded to employ 60 women. When the Germans invaded Belgium in the fall of 1914 and Paris was flooded with Belgian refugees, she helped to set up the American Hostels for Refugees, which provided shelter, meals, and clothes, and eventually created an employment agency to help them find work. She collected more than $100,000 on their behalf and organized the Children of Flanders Rescue Committee, sheltering nearly 900 refugees. Alongside friend Walter Berry, she traveled to the Western Front, documenting her experiences in *Fighting France* (1915). On April 18, 1916, President Raymond Poincaré appointed her Chevalier of the Legion of Honour in recognition of her dedication to the war effort. Her wartime dedication marked a turning point, solidifying her identity as both an American expatriate and a committed Francophile.

### Later Career and Legacy

After the war, Wharton settled permanently in France, continuing to write novels and travel narratives. During the post-war years, she divided her time between Hyères and Provence, where she finished *The Age of Innocence* in 1920. The novel won the Pulitzer Prize in 1921, making Wharton the first woman to receive the honor. Though the three fiction judges—Stuart Pratt Sherman, Robert Morss Lovett, and Hamlin Garland—voted to give the prize to Sinclair Lewis’s *Main Street*, Columbia University’s advisory board, led by President Nicholas Murray Butler, overturned their decision and awarded the prize to *The Age of Innocence*. Wharton was nominated for the Nobel Prize in Literature three times (1927, 1928, 1930). Her autobiography, *A Backward Glance* (1934), offered a selective account of her life, omitting details of her troubled marriage and affair with Morton Fullerton. She died of a stroke on August 11, 1937, at Le Pavillon Colombe, her 18th-century house on Rue de Montmorency in Saint-Brice-sous-Forêt, and was buried in the American Protestant section of the Cimetière des Gonards in Versailles.

## Terms

- **Gilded Age**: A period in late 19th-century America characterized by rapid industrialization, economic disparity, and ostentatious displays of wealth among the elite.
- **Debutante**: A young woman making her formal entrance into high society, often marked by a debutante ball.
- **Legion of Honour**: France’s highest civilian and military decoration, awarded for exceptional service to the nation.
- **Dramatic Irony**: A literary device where the audience knows more than the characters, creating tension or poignancy.
- **Social Mores**: The customs, values, and moral standards of a particular society.
- **Patronage System**: In Wharton’s era, wealthy families used social connections and financial support to advance careers and marriages.
- **Expatriate**: A person living outside their native country, often for extended periods.
- **Interior Design**: The art and practice of decorating and arranging indoor spaces, which Wharton practiced professionally.
- **Naturalist School**: A literary movement emphasizing determinism and scientific analysis of human behavior, often critical of sentimentalism.
- **Chevalier**: The fifth class of the Legion of Honour, awarded for distinguished service.

## Debates and Open Questions

One enduring debate concerns the Pulitzer Prize controversy of 1921: while the three fiction judges selected Sinclair Lewis’s *Main Street*, Columbia University’s advisory board, led by President Nicholas Murray Butler, awarded the prize to Wharton’s *The Age of Innocence*. This raises questions about editorial interference versus artistic merit in literary awards. Additionally, scholars continue to examine the extent to which Wharton’s personal relationships—particularly with her mother and Morton Fullerton—shaped her fictional portrayals of female agency and marital dissatisfaction. The omission of these details from her autobiography has sparked ongoing discussion about memory, self-representation, and the boundaries between life and art in her work.
