# Pacific Coast Ranges

## Overview
The Pacific Coast Ranges are a series of mountain ranges stretching along the West Coast of North America from Alaska to central Mexico. They are a major geographic and ecological feature, defining the continent's western edge and influencing regional climate, such as creating rain shadows that form arid deserts. Geologically, they are not a single unified range but a collection of distinct ranges formed by the accretion of terranes (fragments of crust) onto the North American plate over hundreds of millions of years.

## Timeline
- **c. 750 million years ago** — A rupture in the supercontinent Rodinia forms a passive margin in the eastern Pacific Northwest.
- **c. 200 million years ago** — The breakup of Pangaea begins the westward movement of the North American plate, creating an active margin.
- **c. 115 million years ago** — The accretion of the insular belt (Wrangellia Terrane) is completed.
- **c. 90–80 million years ago** — The subducting Farallon plate splits, forming the Kula Plate to the north.
- **c. 48 million years ago** — The Laramide Orogeny ends; accretion of the Siletzia terrane begins in the Pacific Northwest.
- **Miocene epoch** — Volcanic activity in the Cascadia subduction zone forms the modern Cascade Range.
- **c. 7–8 million years ago** — A change in North American Plate movement slows extension in the Basin and Range Province, and rifting begins in the Gulf of California.

## Body

### Definition and Extent
The Pacific Coast Ranges (officially the Pacific Mountain System in the U.S.) extend from Alaska to northern/central Mexico. They are part of the larger North American Cordillera but specifically comprise the "Western System," including ranges like the Saint Elias, Coast, Olympic, Cascade, California Coast, Transverse, Peninsular, and Sierra Madre Occidental. The term "Coast Range" is used more narrowly by the U.S. Geological Survey for ranges south of the Strait of Juan de Fuca, excluding the Sierra Nevada and Cascade Ranges.

### Geography and Climate
The ranges vary dramatically, from tidewater glaciers in Alaska to rugged, chaparral-covered mountains in California. The coastline often drops steeply into the sea, with complex fjords and islands in British Columbia and Alaska. Major coastal plains exist at river mouths, such as the Los Angeles Basin, where sediment is up to 6 miles (10 km) deep. From San Francisco Bay north, winter storms from the Gulf of Alaska bring heavy precipitation to western slopes, while in Southern California, the mountains create a rain shadow that produces arid deserts.

### Geology and Formation
The ranges are primarily composed of accreted terranes—crustal fragments added to the continent's margin. The breakup of Pangaea initiated westward plate movement, leading to the accretion of various terranes (e.g., the insular belt, Klamath Mountains, Guerrero super-terrane). The modern Cascade Range formed from volcanic activity in the Cascadia subduction zone following the accretion of the Siletzia terrane. The province is defined by geography, not geology; its ranges consist of rocks from different periods, from Precambrian to 10,000-year-old volcanic rock.

### Major Ranges and Icefields
The system includes numerous named ranges from the Kenai and Chugach Mountains in Alaska to the Sierra Madre Occidental in Mexico. The Pacific Coast Ranges host the world's largest temperate-latitude icefields, including the Harding, Juneau, and Stikine Icefields.

## Terms
- **Accreted Terranes:** Fragments of crustal material (like island arcs or oceanic plateaus) that were carried by tectonic plates and welded onto a continent's margin.
- **Active Margin:** A continental edge where tectonic plates converge, leading to earthquakes, volcanism, and mountain building (e.g., the West Coast of North America).
- **Batholith:** A large mass of intrusive igneous rock that forms from cooled magma deep in the Earth's crust.
- **Cordillera:** An extensive chain of mountains or mountain ranges, typically running parallel to a coast.
- **Fjord:** A long, narrow, deep inlet of the sea between high cliffs, formed by glacial erosion.
- **Laramide Orogeny:** A period of mountain building in western North America from about 80 to 35 million years ago.
- **Rain Shadow:** A dry area on the leeward (downwind) side of a mountain range, where descending air warms and dries, reducing precipitation.
- **Terrane:** A fault-bounded or distinct area of crustal material with a geological history different from surrounding areas.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise timing of the accretion of the insular belt is uncertain, though it was completed by at least 115 million years ago.
- The Pacific Coast Ranges province is defined by geography, not geology, leading to the inclusion of ranges with vastly different rock types and histories (e.g., the Mesozoic Peninsular Ranges vs. the Precambrian San Bernardino Mountains).
