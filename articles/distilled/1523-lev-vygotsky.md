# Lev Vygotsky

## Overview
Lev Vygotsky (1896–1934) was a Soviet psychologist who founded cultural-historical activity theory, arguing that human cognition is not biologically fixed but constructed through social interaction, language, and tool use. He introduced the zone of proximal development (ZPD)—the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can achieve with guidance—and identified play as the "leading activity" driving preschool development. His work was banned in the USSR from 1936 until after Stalin’s death in 1953, but posthumous translations made him a cornerstone of modern developmental and educational psychology.

## Timeline
- **November 17, 1896** — Born in Orsha, Russian Empire (now Belarus), to a middle-class Jewish family.
- **1913** — Admitted to Moscow University via a 3% Jewish quota lottery; studied law and attended Shanyavsky People's University.
- **January 1924** — Presented at the Second All-Russian Psychoneurological Congress in Petrograd; met Alexander Luria and moved to Moscow to join the Psychological Institute.
- **1925** — Completed dissertation *The Psychology of Art* (published 1960s); published *Pedagogical Psychology*; attended London congress on deaf education; hospitalized for tuberculosis until mid-1926.
- **1926–1930** — Led a research group (Luria, Leontiev, Zankov, et al.) investigating higher psychological functions via instrumental, developmental, and cultural-historical approaches.
- **December 1928** — Conflict with Institute director Kornilov curtailed the Vygotsky-Luria group’s experimental work.
- **1934** — Published *Thinking and Speech* posthumously; died of tuberculosis in Moscow on June 11, age 37.
- **1936** — Communist Party condemned "pedology"; Vygotsky’s work banned as heresy during Stalin’s Great Purge.
- **1956** — First major collection of Vygotsky’s texts published in the USSR after Stalin’s death.
- **1962** — English translation of *Thinking and Speech* (*Thought and Language*) spurred Western influence.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Vygotsky was born Lev Simkhovich Vygodsky in Orsha (Mogilev Governorate) and raised in Gomel. Home-schooled until 1911, he graduated with distinction from a private Jewish gymnasium. In 1913, he entered Moscow University via a restricted "Jewish Lottery" (3% quota). Though interested in humanities, parental pressure directed him to medical school; he transferred to law after one semester while auditing lectures at Shanyavsky Moscow City People's University. His patronymic and surname were later changed to Semyonovich Vygotsky for unclear reasons.

### Career and the Vygotsky Circle
After the 1924 Psychoneurological Congress, Alexander Luria secured Vygotsky a research fellowship at the Moscow Psychological Institute under Konstantin Kornilov. Vygotsky worked on the "crisis in psychology," arguing for a unified methodology aligned with the Marxian spirit rather than quote-mining Marx. From 1926–1930, he directed a collaborative program on higher psychological functions (voluntary attention, logical memory, decision-making) pursued through three lenses: the **instrumental approach** (tools as mediation aids), the **developmental approach** (ontogenetic acquisition), and the **cultural-historical approach** (sociocultural shaping of mediation). In December 1928, a conflict with Kornilov forced the group’s experimental work out of the Institute.

### Cultural-Historical Theory
Vygotsky proposed a paradigm shift: higher mental functions are not innate but have a cultural history. They originate as **interpsychological** processes (social relations between people) and become **intrapsychological** (individual mental functions) through **internalization**. He reversed the traditional view: "formerly it was thought that every child was capable of reflection... from the collision of such reflections, argument was generated. But the matter is actually something else. Studies show that reflection is generated from argument." This applies to all higher functions—voluntary attention, logical memory, concept formation.

### Cultural Mediation and Internalization
Mediation is the mechanism linking social and individual planes. Cultural tools (language, writing, numbering systems, artifacts) mediate action. **Internalization** transforms external, socially shared activities into internal mental processes. It involves two aspects: (1) mastering "knowing how" through socially guided practice (e.g., riding a bicycle), and (2) **appropriation**—adapting tools for personal ends (e.g., using a pencil creatively rather than copying). For the young child, speech and action are fused: "the path from him to things and from things to him goes through another person."

### Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) and Scaffolding
The **ZPD** is "the distance between the actual developmental level as determined by independent problem solving and the level of potential development as determined through problem solving under adult guidance or in collaboration with more capable peers." It provides a **prospective** (future-oriented) view of development, contrasting with retrospective assessments of independent performance. **Scaffolding** is the tailored support from a **more knowledgeable other (MKO)**—teacher, peer, book, or software—that enables the learner to operate at the ZPD’s upper limit. **Reciprocal teaching** (open dialogue) creates new learning opportunities. The ZPD concept extends beyond cognition to personality and other domains.

### Play as Leading Activity
Vygotsky identified play as the preschool child’s "leading activity"—the primary source of development. In play, children create an **imaginary situation** governed by **rules**, requiring them to act against immediate impulse (e.g., a stick becomes a horse). This develops **self-regulation**, **volition**, and the separation of meaning from objects. Play creates a ZPD: "in play a child always behaves beyond his average age, above his daily behavior; in play it is as though he were a head taller than himself."

### Thinking and Speech
In his final work, *Thinking and Speech* (1934), Vygotsky traced the genetic relationship between thought and language. **Inner speech** originates from **egocentric speech** (social speech directed to self), which Piaget viewed as disappearing; Vygotsky showed it **internalizes** to become verbal thought. Word meaning evolves: early on, the act is independent of the word; ultimately, "the word which becomes the act, the word which makes man's action free." Concept formation moves from **syncretic heaps** → **complexes** (associative chains) → **potential concepts** → **true concepts** (systematic, mediated by words).

### Defectology and Disability
Vygotsky reframed disability (defectology) as a **socio-cultural** rather than purely biological problem. A primary defect (e.g., blindness) causes secondary defects only through social exclusion from cultural mediation. Remediation requires restructuring the social environment and providing alternative cultural tools (e.g., Braille, sign language) to enable higher psychological functions. He headed the Medical-Pedagogical Station (1927–28) and edited *Issues of the Upbringing of Blind, Deaf and Mentally Retarded Children* (1924).

### Legacy in the Soviet Union and the West
Vygotsky’s death coincided with the 1936 pedology ban and Stalin’s purges; his work was suppressed until 1956. His students (Luria, Leontiev, Zaporozhets, El’konin, Gal’perin, Bozhovich, Zinchenko) built **activity theory** (Leontiev), step-by-step formation of mental actions (Gal’perin), and applications in memory, perception, play, and learning. In the US, Michael Cole championed Vygotsky after the 1962 translation; in the UK, Andy Clark linked scaffolding to the **extended mind thesis** (cognition relies on environmental structure).

## Terms
- ****Cultural-historical activity theory**** — Framework positing that higher mental functions originate in culturally mediated social activity and are internalized by the individual.
- ****Mediation**** — The use of cultural tools (signs, symbols, artifacts) as intermediaries between stimulus and response, transforming natural reactions into higher functions.
- ****Internalization**** — The process by which externally mediated, social actions are reconstructed as internal, individual mental processes.
- ****Appropriation**** — The learner’s active adaptation of a cultural tool for personal goals, modifying its standard use.
- ****Zone of proximal development (ZPD)**** — The gap between a learner’s independent performance level and their potential level with guidance from a more knowledgeable other.
- ****Scaffolding**** — Temporary, adjustable support provided by an MKO within the ZPD, withdrawn as competence increases.
- ****More knowledgeable other (MKO)**** — Any agent (adult, peer, book, software) that provides guidance within the learner’s ZPD.
- ****Leading activity**** — The dominant, development-driving activity of a given age period (e.g., play for preschoolers).
- ****Inner speech**** — Internalized, abbreviated, predicative speech that functions as verbal thought; originates from egocentric speech.
- ****Defectology**** — Vygotsky’s term for special education/rehabilitation, reconceptualized as addressing the socio-cultural consequences of biological impairment.

## Debates and open questions
- **Marxist alignment**: Vygotsky criticized "Marxist psychology" built on quote-mining, advocating a Marxist *methodology* instead. Scholars debate how consistently his theory embodies historical materialism versus idealist elements.
- **ZPD measurement**: The ZPD is a metaphor; operationalizing its bounds (especially the upper limit) and distinguishing it from simple assisted performance remains methodologically contested.
- **Piaget vs. Vygotsky on egocentric speech**: Piaget saw it as non-social and disappearing; Vygotsky saw it as social in origin and internalizing. Empirical resolution is incomplete.
- **Universality vs. cultural specificity**: The theory claims universal developmental mechanisms (mediation, internalization) but emphasizes culturally specific tools. The tension between universal stages and cultural variability is unresolved.
- **Textual integrity**: Key works (*The Psychology of Art*, *Historical Meaning of the Crisis in Psychology*) were published decades later with editorial interventions; the extent of distortion is debated.
- **Activity theory split**: Leontiev’s "activity theory" (object-oriented activity as unit of analysis) diverges from Vygotsky’s focus on mediation and semiotic processes; the relationship between the two frameworks is a live scholarly issue.
