# Silver

## Overview

Silver (Ag, atomic number 47) is a soft, white, lustrous transition metal with the highest electrical and thermal conductivity of any metal. Valued since antiquity as a precious and coinage metal, it has played central roles in monetary systems, jewellery, and decorative arts. Beyond these traditional uses, silver is critical in modern industry for photovoltaics, electrical contacts, catalysis, and antimicrobial applications. Its unique electron configuration ([Kr]4d¹⁰5s¹) underpins its singular physical and chemical properties, including exceptional reflectivity and ductility.

## Timeline

- **Prehistoric** — Native silver, gold, and copper used as early forms of money
- **c. 600 BC** — First silver coins minted in Lydia from electrum
- **7th century BC** — Greeks extract silver from galena; Athens mines at Laurium
- **1st–2nd century AD** — Roman silver production peaks at ~200 tonnes/year
- **15th century BC** — Silver more expensive than gold in Egypt
- **16th–18th century** — Spanish conquest shifts global silver production to Americas
- **19th century** — Primary production moves to North America (Canada, Mexico, US)
- **1970s–80s** — Poland emerges as major producer; production returns to Americas
- **2025** — World mine production estimated at 26,000 tonnes

## Body

### Physical and Chemical Properties

Silver belongs to group 11 of the periodic table alongside copper and gold, sharing the electron configuration [Kr]4d¹⁰5s¹. This single valence electron over a filled d-subshell accounts for silver's high electrical and thermal conductivity, low hardness, and high ductility. Unlike transition metals with incomplete d-shells, metallic silver lacks covalent bonding character, resulting in weak metallic bonds and exceptional malleability. Silver crystallizes in a face-centered cubic lattice with a coordination number of 12.

The metal exhibits brilliant white metallic luster and superior optical reflectivity compared to aluminum at wavelengths above ~450 nm. However, it does not react with air even at red heat, classifying it as a noble metal. Silver tarnishes in the presence of sulfur compounds, forming black silver sulfide (Ag₂S), and dissolves readily in nitric acid and concentrated sulfuric acid.

### Occurrence and Production

Silver occurs in nature as native silver, in alloys with gold and other metals, and in minerals such as acanthite (Ag₂S), argentite, and chlorargyrite (AgCl). The Earth's crust abundance is 0.08 ppm, similar to mercury. Most commercial silver is recovered as a byproduct of lead-zinc, copper, and gold mining.

Historically, extraction involved smelting followed by cupellation, where lead oxidizes to PbO and is absorbed, leaving molten silver. Modern production primarily uses electrolytic refining of copper, lead, and zinc, with silver collecting as "anode slime." Commercial fine silver is at least 99.9% pure, with higher purities available. In 2025, Mexico led global production at 6,300 tonnes, followed by Peru (3,600 t) and China (3,400 t).

### Isotopes

Natural silver consists of two stable isotopes: ¹⁰⁷Ag (51.839% abundance) and ¹⁰⁹Ag (48.161%). This nearly equal abundance is rare in the periodic table. Both isotopes form via stellar processes—the s-process (slow neutron capture) in stars and the r-process (rapid neutron capture) in supernovae. Twenty-eight radioisotopes have been characterized, with ¹⁰⁵Ag (t½ = 41.29 days) being the most stable. The ¹⁰⁷Pd–¹⁰⁷Ag decay system (t½ = 6.5 million years) is used to date early solar system materials, first identified in the Santa Clara meteorite in 1978.

### Chemistry and Compounds

Silver is relatively unreactive due to poor d-shell shielding of the 5s electron, placing it near the bottom of the electrochemical series (E⁰ = +0.799 V). Its chemistry is dominated by the +1 oxidation state, though +2 and rare +3 states exist. Silver compounds exhibit significant covalent character due to the small ionic size and high ionization energy (730.8 kJ/mol).

Key compounds include:
- **Oxides**: Ag₂O (dark brown, easily reduced), AgO (mixed I,III oxide)
- **Halides**: All four silver(I) halides known; AgCl, AgBr, AgI are photosensitive and used in traditional photography
- **Nitrate**: AgNO₃ (white, versatile precursor, formerly called "lunar caustic")
- **Carbonate**: Ag₂CO₃ (yellow, used for silver powder production)
- **Explosives**: Silver azide (AgN₃), silver fulminate (AgCNO), silver acetylide (Ag₂C₂)

### Applications

Silver's applications span multiple sectors:

**Electronics**: Highest electrical conductivity among metals makes it essential for conductors, RF connectors, printed circuits, and semiconductor devices. During WWII, 13,540 tons were used in uranium enrichment calutrons.

**Photography**: Silver halides in gelatin emulsions form latent images upon light exposure, developed to metallic silver. Digital imaging has significantly reduced demand.

**Jewellery and Silverware**: Sterling silver (92.5% Ag) alloyed with copper for durability. Tarnishes easily; restoration involves reducing Ag₂S back to metallic silver.

**Medicine**: Silver sulfadiazine and nanomaterials in wound dressings; silver ion coatings on catheters and breathing tubes reduce infections. Silver ions disrupt bacterial enzyme systems and DNA.

**Catalysis**: 15% silver on alumina catalyzes ethylene oxide production; silver gauze converts methanol to formaldehyde.

**Other Uses**: Nanoparticles in conductive inks; E174 food coloring; cloud seeding with silver iodide; water sanitization; silver-plated equipment for high-temperature chemistry.

### Monetary Use

Silver standards were widespread until the 20th century. Notable coins include the Greek drachma, Roman denarius, Islamic dirham, and Spanish dollar. Silver bullion carries ISO code XAG. Modern coin production involves casting, rolling, heat treatment, and high-speed minting (8,000 coins/hour).

### Symbolic Role

Silver appears in mythology as a "lesser" metal in Hesiod's ages of man and Ovid's metamorphoses. Folklore attributes mystical powers to silver—bullets effective against werewolves, poison detection, and fairy realm passage. The idiom "silver bullet" denotes highly effective solutions. The "thirty pieces of silver" references Judas's betrayal in Christian tradition.

## Terms

- **Native silver**: Silver occurring in pure elemental form in nature
- **Cupellation**: Extraction process where lead oxidizes to PbO, separating silver from lead alloys
- **Anode slime**: Precious metal residue collected during electrolytic refining
- **Sterling silver**: Alloy containing 92.5% silver and 7.5% other metals (usually copper)
- **Electrum**: Natural gold-silver alloy
- **Troy ounce**: Unit of mass (31.1034768 g) used for precious metals
- **Argyria**: Blue-gray skin pigmentation from silver accumulation in tissues
- **Oligodynamic effect**: Antimicrobial property of metals at low concentrations
- **Fineness**: Purity measure on a per-mille basis (e.g., 0.925 fine = 92.5% pure)
- **Parkes process**: Method for extracting silver from lead bullion using zinc

## Debates and Open Questions

Several areas remain debated:

1. **Etymology**: The Proto-Indo-European root *h₂erǵ- ('white/shining') for silver may have non-Indo-European origins, with some scholars proposing Paleo-Hispanic roots linked to Basque *zilar*.

2. **Marine toxicity**: While silver is highly toxic to many aquatic organisms, its full environmental impact in oceanic conditions remains unclear due to complex bioaccumulation patterns and transformation into non-reactive biological compounds.

3. **Colloidal silver safety**: Despite traditional use, clinical evidence does not support health claims for colloidal silver products, and the FDA has ruled them unsafe for disease treatment.

4. **Food additive safety**: The European Food Safety Authority (2025) reported insufficient data to conclude on the safety of E174 silver as a food additive.

5. **Bacterial resistance**: While silver ions are effective antimicrobials, bacteria can develop resistance, raising concerns about long-term efficacy in medical applications.
