# History

History is the systematic study of the human past, analyzing and interpreting evidence to construct narratives about what happened and why. As an academic discipline, it relies on primary and secondary sources, evaluated through source criticism, to build coherent accounts. Historians debate whether it is a social science, a humanity, or a hybrid, and whether its purpose is theoretical (uncovering truth) or practical (learning lessons). The field emerged in antiquity, professionalized in the 19th century, and now encompasses diverse branches by period, region, theme, and method.

## Timeline

- **c. 5th century BCE** — Herodotus publishes *Histories*, a foundational text of Western historical writing emphasizing rational inquiry.
- **Late 2nd millennium BCE** — Early precursors of Chinese historical writing emerge, later associated with Confucian philosophy and dynastic government.
- **629 CE** — A bureau for writing history is established in China during the Tang dynasty, institutionalizing historical compilation.
- **14th century** — The word "history" enters Middle English via Old French, meaning "story, tale."
- **c. 1500–1800** — Renaissance and early modern period: humanists use text criticism, secularization advances, printing press expands access to history.
- **19th century** — History professionalizes: Leopold von Ranke's source criticism becomes standard; university departments and journals are founded.
- **19th century** — Auguste Comte formulates positivism; Karl Marx proposes historical materialism, arguing economic forces drive change.
- **20th century** — Annales school emerges, using interdisciplinary methods to study long-term social and economic trends.
- **20th century** — Authoritarian regimes (Nazi Germany, Soviet Union, China) manipulate historical narratives for ideology.
- **20th century** — Postmodernism and postcolonialism challenge claims to single universal historical truth.

## Body

### Definition and Purpose

History as a discipline conceptualizes and describes what happened by collecting and analyzing evidence, covering not only how events developed but also why they happened and their contexts. In a different sense, "history" refers to the past events themselves, while a "history" (countable noun) is a text representing the past—a cultural product subject to reinterpretation as new evidence emerges. Some historians distinguish histories from chronicles: chronicles merely catalogue events chronologically, whereas histories aim at comprehensive understanding of causes, contexts, and consequences. History has primarily focused on written documents since writing's invention, leaving prehistory to fields like archaeology, though its scope broadened in the 20th century.

Historians debate whether history is a social science or part of the humanities. Like social scientists, historians formulate hypotheses and present evidence-based arguments; like humanists, they rely on subjective interpretation, storytelling, and cultural heritage. Some call it a hybrid discipline. History contrasts with pseudohistory, which deviates from historiographical standards by relying on disputed evidence, selectively ignoring genuine evidence, or distorting the record, often for ideological agendas.

Proposed purposes of history include: the pure discovery of truth (emphasizing disinterested inquiry); teaching lessons to avoid repeating mistakes; fostering awareness of human diversity across contexts; and promoting social cohesion through collective identity. Politicians have used history to justify the status quo or promote change; extreme cases include historical denialism, such as Holocaust denial, Armenian genocide denial, Nanjing Massacre denial, and Holodomor denial.

### Methods

The historical method covers collecting, evaluating, and synthesizing evidence. Research often starts with a question—descriptive, explanatory, or aimed at refuting or confirming theories. Historians rely on primary sources (originating during the studied period: official documents, letters, diaries, photographs, artefacts) and secondary sources (analyses of other sources). Whether a document is primary or secondary depends on its use. Archives, libraries, and museums preserve sources; online databases increasingly aid access.

Source criticism begins with external criticism (evaluating authenticity: when, where, by whom, and why a source was created, and whether it was modified) and proceeds to internal criticism (evaluating content: clarifying meaning, then assessing accuracy, comprehensiveness, and the author's ability and biases). Cross-referencing with other credible sources helps determine reliability.

Historical synthesis examines how isolated statements fit into a larger story, addressing not only what occurred but why and with what consequences. Tools include periodization (dividing time into periods organized around central themes, e.g., the three-age system: Stone, Bronze, Iron Ages) and examining silences (gaps where events left no evidential traces). Quantitative approaches analyze large datasets. Schools of thought include: positivism (empirical evidence for objective truths), postmodernism (rejecting grand narratives, highlighting subjective interpretation), Marxism (economic forces and class struggle), the Annales school (long-term social and economic trends, quantitative methods), and feminist history (gender roles and women's experiences).

### Areas of Study

**By period:** A traditional division is prehistory, ancient history, post-classical history, early modern history, and modern history. Prehistory covers human evolution (anatomically modern humans emerged about 200,000 years ago) and the shift from nomadic hunting-gathering to sedentary agriculture. Ancient history (beginning c. 3500 BCE in some regions) examines first civilizations in Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus Valley, China, and Peru, alongside the development of writing, urbanization, and early religions and philosophies. Post-classical history (from c. 500 CE) sees the spread of Buddhism, Christianity, and Islam, flourishing trade networks, and the Mongol Empire's dominance in the 13th–14th centuries. Early modern history (from c. 1500 CE) features European global expansion, the biological exchange with the Americas, and the Scientific Revolution. Modern history (from the late 18th century) covers the Industrial Revolution, colonial empires, globalization, social revolutions, and two world wars that undermined European dominance.

**By geographic location:** The history of Africa begins with human evolution, covers ancient Egypt (4th millennium BCE), later civilizations (Nubia, Axum, Carthage, Ghana, Mali, Songhay), the spread of Islam, the Atlantic slave trade, European colonization, and post-World War II independence. Asian history notes humans arriving c. 100,000 years ago, early civilizations, the birth of major religions, the Silk Road, Mongol Empire, colonial control, and post-independence modernization. European history traces Greek contributions, the Roman and Byzantine Empires, the medieval period (from the 5th-century fall of the Western Roman Empire), exploration, industrialization, world wars, and Cold War division. The Americas saw first humans c. 20,000–15,000 years ago, civilizations like Norte Chico, Maya, Olmec, Teotihuacan, Aztec, and Inca, then European arrival, disease-driven population decline, independence movements, and U.S. global dominance. Oceania's history begins with human arrival c. 60,000–50,000 years ago, followed by European contact, Western control, and post-war decolonization.

**By theme:** Political history studies power organization, including states, factions, parties, and international relations; it is among the oldest branches (Herodotus, Thucydides). Diplomatic history examines international relations and treaties; military history studies armed conflicts and warfare evolution. Economic history covers production, exchange, and consumption, often using quantitative methods. Social history investigates everyday life, experiences of social groups, or social problems; related fields include cultural, gender, and religious history. Intellectual history studies the evolution of ideas, including philosophy, science, and art history. Environmental history examines human-environment relations. Other thematic branches include constitutional, legal, urban, business, medical, and people's history.

**Others:** Quantitative and digital history rely on quantitative methods and digital media. Comparative history compares phenomena across times, regions, or cultures. Oral history uses oral reports—eyewitness accounts, hearsay, communal legends—reflecting personal memories. Counterfactual history explores alternative courses. Big History covers everything from the Big Bang to the present; world history examines human history as a whole. Macro-, meso-, and microhistory differ in scale. Public history presents history to the general public in museums, heritage sites, and popular media.

### Evolution of the Discipline

Before writing, historical knowledge was preserved through oral traditions. Early writings mixed facts with myth (e.g., the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, the *Odyssey*). Herodotus's *Histories* (5th century BCE) emphasized rational, evidence-based inquiry; Thucydides refined this with a focus on political and military developments. Roman historiography added moral judgements and used annalistic styles. Chinese tradition, with precursors from the late 2nd millennium BCE, emphasized verification and dynastic histories; Sima Qian's meticulous methods shaped standards. Indian narratives mixed facts with supernatural elements (e.g., the *Mahabharata*). Medieval European history was documented by clergy as chronicles, reinterpreted through a religious lens (Eusebius, Bede, Augustine). Islamic historiography emphasized chains of transmission; Al-Tabari wrote a comprehensive history, and Ibn Khaldun reflected on philosophical issues.

The Tang dynasty established a history bureau in 629 CE, producing Veritable Records; Song dynasty historians wrote in diverse genres. Japanese historiography emerged in the 8th century, influenced by China but with less critical source evaluation. The Renaissance shifted away from medieval religious outlooks; humanists used text criticism, contributing to secularization. The printing press expanded access; empiricism questioned universal truths; the Enlightenment applied rationalism and scepticism. In the 19th century, history professionalized under Ranke's influence; positivism and Marxism proposed general laws. The 20th century saw the Annales school, ideological manipulation by authoritarian regimes, and new approaches (history from below, microhistory, oral history, feminist history, postcolonialism, postmodernism).

### Related Fields

**Historiography** is the study of historical research methods and development—a "history of history." It examines standards of evidence, interpretative assumptions, and rhetorical presentation, identifying schools of thought and tracing the discipline's evolution.

**Philosophy of history** investigates theoretical foundations, exploring basic entities of historical phenomena (individuals vs. collectives), causal mechanisms (general laws vs. unique contingent factors), and large patterns (cyclic vs. linear, teleological). It overlaps with historiography but focuses on more general and evaluative questions. Historicism argues that understanding requires knowledge of unique history.

**Historical objectivity** is debated. Some argue all history is biased (e.g., G. M. Trevelyan, Keith Jenkins). Realists, empiricists, and reconstructionists see truth as recoverable through rigorous evidence evaluation. Perspectivists claim objectivity is relative to competing perspectives. Relativists say no historical knowledge can be proven objective. Postmodernists argue meaning is created through texts whose language "constitute our world as we perceive it." Neo-realists reemphasize empiricism while acknowledging subjective influences.

**Education:** History is part of most public school curricula. Early education fosters interest and identity; secondary education covers broader topics and research methodologies. Teachers use narratives, questions, discussions, and direct source work. Traditional methods emphasize memorizing facts; modern approaches seek active engagement and understanding of patterns. History education aims for historical literacy and cultural identity, but can spark "history wars" over curricula and biased treatments. Informal education occurs in museums, popular media, and oral traditions.

**Other fields:** History draws on archaeology (material culture), geology (environmental contexts), genetics (human origins and migration), anthropology (culture and behaviour), and historical linguistics (language development). History connects to politics (e.g., irredentist claims based on historical theories) and historical religions (e.g., Christianity centered on events surrounding Jesus Christ).

## Terms

- **Primary source**: A source originating during the studied period, offering direct evidence (documents, letters, artefacts).
- **Secondary source**: A source analyzing or interpreting information from other sources.
- **Source criticism**: The process of evaluating a source's authenticity (external) and content accuracy (internal).
- **Periodization**: Dividing time into periods organized around central themes or developments.
- **Positivism**: A school emphasizing empirical evidence to discover objective truths in history.
- **Annales school**: A 20th-century approach using interdisciplinary, quantitative methods to study long-term social and economic trends.
- **Historiography**: The study of historical research methods and the development of the discipline.
- **Historicism**: The view that understanding something requires knowledge of its unique history or evolution.
- **Pseudohistory**: Practices deviating from historiographical standards, distorting the record for ideological agendas.
- **Big History**: The branch covering everything from the Big Bang to the present, incorporating cosmology, geology, biology, and anthropology.

## Debates and open questions

Historians debate whether history is a social science, a humanity, or a hybrid. The purpose of history is contested: pure truth-seeking versus practical lessons for the present. The possibility of objective historical knowledge is a central philosophical debate—some argue all history is biased, while others maintain truth is reachable through rigorous methods. The precise definition of social history is disputed. Periodizations vary by region and theme (e.g., Chinese dynasties vs. pre-Columbian/colonial/post-colonial divisions for the Americas). The boundaries between thematic branches (political, economic, social, intellectual) are vague. History education sparks "history wars" over curricula and the biased treatment of controversial topics.
