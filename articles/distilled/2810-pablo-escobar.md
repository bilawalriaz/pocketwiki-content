# Pablo Escobar

## Overview
Pablo Escobar was a Colombian drug lord who founded and led the Medellín Cartel, which monopolized the cocaine trade into the United States during the 1980s and early 1990s. His immense wealth, violent tactics, and complex public image—simultaneously a brutal narcoterrorist and a populist benefactor—made him one of the most notorious criminals in history and a central figure in Colombia's violent conflict with drug trafficking.

## Timeline
- **1 December 1949** — Born in Rionegro, Colombia.
- **1976** — Arrested with 39 kg of cocaine; later released through bribery.
- **1976** — Founded the Medellín Cartel.
- **1982** — Elected as an alternate member of the Colombian Chamber of Representatives.
- **1984** — Minister of Justice Rodrigo Lara-Bonilla, who opposed him, was assassinated.
- **1989** — Presidential candidate Luis Carlos Galán, who had expelled Escobar from their party, was assassinated on his orders.
- **1989** — Avianca Flight 203 was bombed, killing all 107 aboard.
- **1991** — Surrendered to authorities and was confined in his self-built prison, La Catedral.
- **22 July 1992** — Escaped from La Catedral before a planned transfer to a standard jail.
- **2 December 1993** — Killed by Colombian National Police in Medellín.

## Body

### Early Life and Criminal Beginnings
Born into a poor family in Rionegro and raised in Medellín, Escobar briefly studied law but left due to lack of funds. He began his criminal career with small-scale theft, including stealing and reselling tombstones. He soon escalated to violent crime, forming a gang that stole cars and kidnapped people for ransom. His most notable early crime was the 1971 kidnapping and murder of businessman Diego Echavarria, for which he received a $50,000 ransom.

### Rise of the Medellín Cartel
In the mid-1970s, as the cocaine trade expanded in Colombia, Escobar founded the Medellín Cartel. Despite a 1976 arrest for drug trafficking, he secured release through bribery and intimidation, a strategy known as "silver or lead" (money or death). The cartel established smuggling routes from Peru, Bolivia, and Ecuador through Colombia to the United States. By the late 1970s, it was transporting thousands of kilograms of cocaine monthly. Escobar used his vast profits to build a criminal empire, including the lavish Hacienda Nápoles estate, and to bribe politicians at all levels of the Colombian government.

### Political Power and Escalating Violence
Escobar entered politics in the 1980s, winning a seat in Congress in 1982. His political career ended when Justice Minister Rodrigo Lara-Bonilla publicly accused him of criminal activity, leading to Lara-Bonilla's assassination in 1984. Escobar then waged war against the Colombian state to avoid extradition to the U.S., founding the Los Extraditables organization. This campaign included the 1985 Palace of Justice siege, which killed nearly half of the Supreme Court justices, and the assassinations of political rivals like Luis Carlos Galán in 1989. The bombing of Avianca Flight 203, intended to kill Galán's successor, drew direct U.S. intervention.

### Surrender, Imprisonment, and Death
Facing intense pressure, Escobar surrendered in 1991. He negotiated a sentence in his own luxurious prison, La Catedral, which featured a football pitch and a waterfall. Reports of his continued criminal activity led authorities to attempt a transfer in 1992, prompting his escape. A nationwide manhunt ensued, crippling the Medellín Cartel. On 2 December 1993, Colombian police, using U.S. technology to trace a phone call, located Escobar in Medellín. He was killed in a shootout, with debate persisting over whether he committed suicide or was shot by police.

### Aftermath and Legacy
Escobar's death led to the fragmentation of the Medellín Cartel and the rise of the rival Cali Cartel. His legacy is controversial; while condemned for his crimes, he is remembered by some in Colombia as a "Robin Hood" figure for building housing, hospitals, and sports facilities for the poor. His funeral was attended by over 25,000 people. His estate, Hacienda Nápoles, became a theme park, and his introduced hippos have since become an invasive species. His life has been extensively dramatized in film, television, and music.

## Terms
*   **Medellín Cartel:** The drug trafficking organization founded and led by Pablo Escobar that dominated the cocaine trade in the 1980s.
*   **Narcoterrorist:** A term describing Escobar's use of terrorism tactics (bombings, assassinations) to advance his drug trafficking interests and fight the state.
*   **"Silver or lead" (Plata o plomo):** Escobar's strategy of offering bribes ("silver") to public officials or threatening them with death ("lead") if they refused.
*   **Los Extraditables:** A group of Colombian drug traffickers, led by Escobar, that fought against the extradition of Colombians to the United States.
*   **La Catedral:** The luxurious, self-built prison where Escobar was confined from 1991 to 1992.
*   **Cali Cartel:** The main rival of the Medellín Cartel, which gained dominance in the cocaine trade after Escobar's death.
*   **Palace of Justice siege (1985):** A violent attack on Colombia's Supreme Court building by the M-19 guerrilla group, which Escobar financed, resulting in the deaths of 98 people, including 11 justices.
*   **Hacienda Nápoles:** Escobar's extravagant private estate, which featured a zoo and later became a theme park.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Cause of Death:** There is debate over whether Escobar was killed by police gunfire or committed suicide during the final confrontation.
*   **Political Influence:** Scholars debate the extent to which Escobar and other drug lords infiltrated and corrupted all levels of the Colombian government during the 1980s.
*   **Role in the Palace of Justice siege:** While Virginia Vallejo testified that Escobar financed the operation, the full extent of his involvement and the precise sequence of events remain subjects of investigation and legal proceedings.
*   **Public Image:** The "Robin Hood" narrative versus the reality of his extreme violence continues to be a point of contention in assessing his legacy in Colombia.
