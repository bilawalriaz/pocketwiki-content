# Dylan Thomas

## Overview
Dylan Marlais Thomas (1914–1953) was a Welsh poet and writer whose lyrical, musical verse and "play for voices" *Under Milk Wood* made him a cultural icon. Born in Swansea, he left school at 16 to work as a reporter while producing over 200 poems in notebooks, publishing his first collection, *18 Poems*, at age 20. He married Caitlin Macnamara in 1937; their volatile, alcohol-fueled relationship produced three children. Thomas struggled financially, supplementing meager literary earnings with BBC radio broadcasts, film scripts for the Ministry of Information, and lucrative but grueling reading tours of the United States. His reputation as a "roistering, drunken and doomed poet" was cemented by his death at 39 in New York City following a coma induced by pneumonia, medical mismanagement, and heavy drinking. While early criticism dismissed his work as obscure or verbose, later scholarship emphasizes his disciplined craftsmanship, unique modernist symbolism rooted in Welsh geography and biblical imagery, and status as a major 20th-century poet.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Thomas was born in the Uplands area of Swansea into a bilingual, chapel-going family; his father, a frustrated schoolteacher with a first-class English degree, named him after a figure in *The Mabinogion*. Childhood summers on the Llansteffan peninsula (Carmarthenshire), where 95% of residents spoke Welsh, immersed him in a rural, Welsh-speaking world of farms like Fernhill, later central to his poetry. He attended Mrs. Hole’s dame school and Swansea Grammar School (where his father taught), editing the school magazine but plagiarizing poems for publication. At 16, he left for the *South Wales Daily Post* (1931–32), then freelanced while filling five notebooks with verse. He also acted, wrote, and painted sets for the Swansea Little Theatre and the "Kardomah Gang" of local artists.

### London, Marriage, and Early Fame (1933–1939)
In London, the teenaged Thomas published signature poems—"The Force That Through the Green Fuse Drives the Flower," "Light breaks where no sun shines"—attracting T.S. Eliot, Stephen Spender, and Geoffrey Grigson. *18 Poems* (1934) and *Twenty-five Poems* (1936) won critical acclaim, though the former was a vanity publication. He refused to sign the New Apocalyptics' manifesto, calling them "intellectual muckpots," though they imitated him. Politically, he was radical left, pacifist, and anti-fascist, demonstrating against Oswald Mosley. In 1936 he met dancer Caitlin Macnamara (introduced by Augustus John); they married in 1937. The couple began a peripatetic existence, moving between Wales, England, and London, often fleeing creditors.

### Wartime, Film, and Broadcasting (1939–1949)
*The Map of Love* (1939) and *Portrait of the Artist as a Young Dog* (1940) sold poorly. An "unreliable lung" (chronic bronchitis/asthma) earned a C3 exemption from conscription. From 1941, Thomas wrote 28 film scripts for Strand Film Company (Ministry of Information), providing his first steady income; this cinematic technique influenced *Under Milk Wood*. The 1941 Swansea Blitz destroyed the Kardomah Café, inspiring the radio piece *Return Journey*. *Deaths and Entrances* (1946), containing war elegies like "A Refusal to Mourn the Death, by Fire, of a Child in London," was hailed as a major work. From 1945–49, Thomas made over 100 BBC broadcasts (poetry, talks, acting), becoming a national celebrity despite management's distrust of his drinking. A 1947 Society of Authors scholarship funded a trip to Italy; in 1949 he visited Prague, narrating an early *Under Milk Wood* draft.

### Laugharne and *Under Milk Wood* (1949–1953)
Margaret Taylor bought the Boat House in Laugharne (May 1949), where Thomas converted a cliff-side garage into a writing shed. Here he completed "Do not go gentle into that good night" (1951) and labored on *Under Milk Wood*, a "play for voices" set in the fictional Llareggub. The play evolved from *Quite Early One Morning* (1944) and New Quay sketches; Walford Davies notes New Quay was "crucial" for its character gallery. Thomas missed BBC deadlines; Douglas Cleverdon eventually produced it.

### American Tours and Death
Four US tours (1950, 1952, 1953, 1953) organized by John Brinnin brought fame and income but exacerbated his drinking and health. The 1952 tour (46 dates) yielded the Caedmon *A Child's Christmas in Wales* recording. *Collected Poems* (1952) won the Foyle Prize; Philip Toynbee called him "the greatest living poet in the English language." In Iran (Jan–Feb 1951) on a film assignment (possibly for British intelligence), he was appalled by poverty. During the third tour (April–June 1953), he performed *Under Milk Wood* at Harvard and New York, meeting Igor Stravinsky to discuss an opera ("The Opera that Never Was"). On the fourth tour (Oct 1953), he arrived in New York gravely ill with bronchitis, pneumonia, and emphysema. After collapsing at rehearsals, he was treated by Dr. Milton Feltenstein with cortisone and a triple dose of morphine sulphate (32.4 mg), precipitating a coma. Admitted to St. Vincent's Hospital (5 Nov), he was diagnosed with "acute alcoholic encephalopathy" but found to have extensive bilateral bronchopneumonia. He died 9 Nov 1953, aged 39. The pathologist cited pneumonia, brain swelling, and fatty liver (no cirrhosis).

### Aftermath and Legacy
John Brinnin’s *Dylan Thomas in America* (1955) fixed the "doomed poet" legend. Later biographers (Ferris, Tremlett, FitzGibbon) argue he was not an alcoholic but a binge drinker with periods of abstinence, and that medical neglect and environmental factors (NYC smog) killed him. Caitlin died in 1994, buried beside him. The Boat House is now a museum; the Dylan Thomas Prize (under 30) and Screenplay Award honor him. A 1982 plaque in Westminster Abbey’s Poets' Corner quotes *Fern Hill*.

## Poetry

### Poetic Style and Influences
Thomas rejected literary groups, forging a unique modernist poetic in the 1930s influenced by symbolism and surrealism. He replaced the discursive, propagandist voice of contemporaries with exuberant word-play, treating words as a craftsman treats wood or stone. His central theme is the unity of life: cycles of growth, sexuality, death, and rebirth linking generations. Imagery derives from physiology, the Bible, Welsh folklore, Freud, and the literary canon. Early verse features verbal density, alliteration, sprung rhythm, and internal rhyme (critics cite Gerard Manley Hopkins; Thomas denied it). He used fixed forms, notably the villanelle "Do not go gentle into that good night." Musicality defines mature poems like "Fern Hill" and "In Country Sleep." He credited nursery rhymes for his love of words' sound over sense. Prose works (*Portrait of the Artist as a Young Dog*, *Quite Early One Morning*) alternate poetic fantasy with realist narrative.

### Welsh Poet
Thomas denied Welsh bardic influence ("I can't read Welsh") and disliked Welsh nationalism ("Land of my fathers, and my fathers can keep it"—a line from his screenplay *The Three Weird Sisters*). Yet his imagination was rooted in Welsh geography: "a fanatically narrow groove" of hereditary descent. Critics debate formal Welsh metrics: Glyn Jones and Kenneth O. Morgan find *cynghanedd* (consonant harmony) accidental or absent; Constantine FitzGibbon asserts "No major English poet has ever been as Welsh as Dylan." Morgan argues his work echoes a changing Wales—rural/urban, chapel/profane, Welsh/English.

## Critical Reception
Thomas’s stature has been debated since his death. Seamus Heaney noted he became "as much a case history as a chapter in the history of poetry." Detractors (The Movement: Amis, Larkin) attacked verbal obscurity, metaphysical pretentiousness, and romantic rhapsodizing; Amis dismissed him as "frothing at the mouth with piss." Defenders (Robert Lowell, Kenneth Rexroth) praised his dazzling, enjoyable obscurity and disciplined redrafting. The Poetry Archive notes he was "drunk on language as well as whiskey" but a disciplined craftsman. Psychoanalytic readings (David Holbrook) were rejected as reductive by later theorists (Lacan, Kristeva) who see his materiality of language as a strength. Despite academic marginalization from the 1970s, public popularity remains high: 10th in a 2009 BBC poll, 50 *Desert Island Discs* selections, and widespread cultural permeation (music, film, literature).

## Terms
- ****C3 Category**** — British WWII medical classification exempting men from combat duty; Thomas received it due to chronic lung disease (bronchitis/asthma).
- ****Cynghanedd**** — Welsh poetic meter using consonant harmony and internal rhyme; debated whether Thomas used it consciously or accidentally.
- ****Kardomah Gang**** — Informal group of young writers, musicians, and artists (including Vernon Watkins, Daniel Jones, Bert Trick) meeting at Swansea's Kardomah Café c. 1930s.
- ****New Apocalyptics**** — 1930s English poetic group (Henry Treece, J.F. Hendry) embracing myth and surrealism; Thomas refused their manifesto but influenced them.
- ****Play for Voices**** — Thomas's term for *Under Milk Wood*; a radio drama relying on poetic language and vocal performance rather than stage action.
- ****Sprung Rhythm**** — Poetic meter (associated with Gerard Manley Hopkins) counting stresses per line regardless of syllables; detected in Thomas's early verse.
- ****Strand Film Company**** — London production company for which Thomas wrote 28 Ministry of Information scripts (1941–48), gaining cinematic technique and steady income.
- ****Villanelle**** — Fixed 19-line poetic form with two repeating rhymes and two refrains; used by Thomas for "Do not go gentle into that good night."
- ****Llareggub**** — Fictional Welsh town in *Under Milk Wood*; "bugger all" spelled backward. Early title for the play: *Llareggub. A Piece for Radio Perhaps*.
- ****Boat House**** — Thomas's final home in Laugharne (bought by Margaret Taylor, 1949); now a museum run by Carmarthenshire County Council.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Cause of Death**: Source cites pneumonia, brain swelling, fatty liver; debates persist on relative roles of alcohol, medical mismanagement (Feltenstein’s morphine overdose, delayed specialist care), NYC smog, and underlying lung disease.
- **Alcoholism vs. Binge Drinking**: Brinnin and popular legend label him an alcoholic; FitzGibbon, Ferris, Tremlett, and Sinclair argue for periodic heavy binges amid long abstinence, rejecting clinical alcoholism.
- **Welsh Identity**: Thomas denied bardic influence and nationalism, yet critics (FitzGibbon, Ackerman) root his work in Welsh geography and psyche; formal metrics (*cynghanedd*) remain contested.
- **Plagiarism**: Biographers note extensive juvenile plagiarism in school magazine and *Boy's Own Paper*; its impact on his early development vs. later originality is debated.
- **Critical Standing**: The Movement's rejection vs. enduring public popularity; recent scholarship (Goodby, New Centenary Edition) seeks to reclaim him as a major modernist, moving beyond the "doomed poet" myth.
