# Samuel Johnson

## Overview

Samuel Johnson (18 September [O.S. 7 September] 1709 – 13 December 1784) was an English writer and polymath whose prolific output as a poet, essayist, lexicographer, critic, and biographer established him as a central figure in 18th-century English literature. His *Dictionary of the English Language* (1755), containing 42,733 entries illustrated by roughly 114,000 literary quotations, was the pre-eminent English dictionary for 150 years and a landmark in lexicography. Johnson’s critical philosophy emphasized clarity, moral utility, and fidelity to common language over archaic decoration, shaping modern literary criticism and the art of biography. His life, marked by physical disabilities (scrofula scars, deafness, blindness, gout), probable Tourette syndrome, and severe depression ("the black dog"), was immortalized by James Boswell’s *Life of Samuel Johnson* (1791), widely considered the greatest biography in English.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Johnson was born above his father’s bookshop in Lichfield. A sickly infant, he survived scrofula (tuberculous cervical lymphadenitis), treated unsuccessfully by Queen Anne’s "royal touch" in 1712, leaving deep facial scars. He excelled at Lichfield Grammar School in Latin but exhibited tics and involuntary movements later identified as Tourette syndrome. Family poverty forced him to leave Pembroke College, Oxford, after 13 months without a degree (1729), though he later received honorary doctorates from Trinity College Dublin (1765) and Oxford (1775).

### Early Career and Marriage
After his father’s death (1731), Johnson failed as a school usher and undermaster. In 1735, he married Elizabeth "Tetty" Porter, a wealthy widow, and opened Edial Hall School, which failed within a year, costing Tetty much of her fortune. Biographer Robert DeMaria suggests Tourette syndrome made public teaching impossible, pushing Johnson toward "the invisible occupation of authorship." He moved to London in 1737 with former pupil David Garrick, writing prolifically for Edward Cave’s *Gentleman’s Magazine*. His poem *London* (1738) earned praise from Alexander Pope but little money. He befriended the poet Richard Savage, sharing Grub Street poverty; Savage’s death (1743) prompted Johnson’s pioneering *Life of Mr Richard Savage* (1744).

### The Dictionary (1746–1755)
In 1746, a consortium of publishers contracted Johnson for 1,500 guineas to produce an authoritative dictionary. He claimed he could finish in three years versus the French Academy’s 40 scholars taking 40 years ("the proportion of an Englishman to a Frenchman"). It took eight. Johnson worked at 17 Gough Square with six assistants, underlining quotations in borrowed books for copying onto slips. The *Plan* (1747) was patronized by Lord Chesterfield, who neglected the project; Johnson’s famous 1755 letter rebuked Chesterfield’s belated praise. Published April 1755, the folio contained 42,733 entries and ~114,000 quotations (Shakespeare, Milton, Dryden most cited). It sold for £4 10s but yielded no royalties. Though not the first English dictionary, it surpassed predecessors in literary authority and defined usage for 150 years until the *Oxford English Dictionary* (1928). During this period, Johnson also wrote *The Rambler* essays (1750–52), moral-religious pieces that gained popularity in volume form.

### Later Career: Shakespeare, The Club, and Rasselas
Johnson’s Shakespeare edition (proposed 1756, published 1765) corrected textual corruptions from careless editors and added explanatory notes. He argued drama should mirror life, rejecting classical unities. Delays prompted Churchill’s satire ("He for subscribers baits his hook / and takes your cash, but where's the book?"). A £300 pension (1762) secured independence. In 1763, he met James Boswell and founded The Club (Reynolds, Burke, Garrick, Goldsmith, later Smith, Gibbon). Friendship with brewer Henry Thrale and his wife Hester provided a stable home for 17 years. *Rasselas* (1759), written in a week to pay for his mother’s funeral and settle her debts, explores the futility of constant pleasure in the "Happy Valley"; it was instantly popular, translated into 14 languages.

### Final Works and Political Pamphlets
Johnson’s 1773 Scottish tour with Boswell produced *A Journey to the Western Islands of Scotland* (1775), critiquing James Macpherson’s *Ossian* poems as fabrications (no ancient Scots Gaelic manuscripts existed). In the 1770s, he published Tory pamphlets: *The False Alarm* (1770, anti-Wilkes), *Thoughts on Falkland’s Islands* (1771, anti-war), *The Patriot* (1774, against false patriotism), and *Taxation No Tyranny* (1775, defending Coercive Acts, attacking American slaveholders’ hypocrisy). His final major work, *Lives of the Poets* (1779–81), commissioned for 200 guineas, comprised 52 biographical-critical prefaces, insisting on accuracy over hagiography and including lesser poets.

### Final Years and Death
Henry Thrale’s death (1781) and Hester’s remarriage to Gabriel Piozzi disrupted Johnson’s life. The deaths of friends (Robert Levet, Thomas Lawrence, housekeeper Anna Williams) deepened his isolation. A stroke in June 1783 temporarily removed speech. He died 13 December 1784 after months of gout, bronchitis, and "mental anguish and delusions." Boswell recorded his final Latin words: *iam moriturus*. He was buried in Westminster Abbey.

## Literary Criticism
Johnson’s criticism was practical, aimed at helping readers understand literature. **Poetry**: He favored contemporary language and clear imagery over Miltonic blank verse (which bred bad imitations) and Gray’s obscure allusions. *London* is playful; *The Vanity of Human Wishes* (imitating Juvenal X) grapples with Christian ethics, arguing happiness comes through virtue. **Biography**: He rejected Plutarch’s moralizing hagiography, demanding accuracy, inclusion of negative traits, and significance for lesser lives (*Rambler* 60). He valued autobiography as least likely to distort. **Editing**: He insisted editors preserve the author’s text against "corrections" by careless printers. **Shakespeare**: He defended Shakespeare’s natural dialogue (colloquial, elliptical) against neoclassical rules but noted faults: moral looseness, vulgarity, careless plots. His notes clarified obscure passages using literary context. **Dictionary as Critical Tool**: The *Dictionary*’s quotations from Bacon, Hooker, Milton, Shakespeare, Spenser across natural science, philosophy, poetry, and theology enabled "exactness of criticism" by showing words in literary context.

## Views and Character
Johnson was a devout Anglican Tory. He opposed slavery morally (toasting "the next rebellion of the Negroes"), respected other Christian denominations, but despised Milton’s Puritanism. His rhetoric was forceful: he "refuted" Bishop Berkeley’s immaterialism by stomping a stone ("I refute it thus!"). Adam Smith called him the most book-learned man alive; Burke thought him a potential parliamentary giant. Boswell’s portrait emphasizes his later tavern years, obscuring earlier political activity. Johnson held anti-Scottish prejudices ("abuse the Scotch"), yet befriended Boswell. He hated the title "Dr. Johnson," imposed by Boswell’s usage.

## Health and Disability
Johnson suffered scrofula scars, deafness (one ear), blindness (one eye), gout, testicular cancer, emphysema, and cardiac failure (hypertension). He endured severe depression ("the black dog") from youth, once contemplating suicide. Hester Thrale noted his fear of madness, distinguishing him from Christopher Smart by his emotional containment. Posthumous diagnosis of **Tourette syndrome** (first proposed 1967) is widely accepted based on Boswell’s detailed accounts: tics (head twisting, knee rubbing, body rocking), vocalizations (half-whistle, clucking), doorway rituals, and exhaustion after disputes. Arthur K. Shapiro called him "the most notable example of a successful adaptation to life despite the liability of Tourette syndrome." Some scholars argue his compulsive energy fueled his literary output.

## Legacy
Johnson was a celebrity in life; his health and conversation were reported in journals. He revolutionized biography, spawning Boswell’s *Life* (1791)—called by Walter Jackson Bate "the most famous single work of biographical art"—and numerous rivals (Hawkins, Thrale, Murphy, Burney). Critical reputation fluctuated: Macaulay dismissed him as an idiot savant; Romantics opposed his Milton views. By the 20th century, G. Birkbeck Hill, T.S. Eliot, F.R. Leavis, and Yvor Winters elevated him as perhaps the only English critic deserving the title "great." Harold Bloom placed him unmatched in the Western canon. His insistence on examining literary language became a prevailing mid-20th-century critical mode. The Donald and Mary Hyde Collection at Harvard holds half his correspondence and manuscripts. Societies, conferences, a BBC prize (1999), a Royal Mail stamp (2009), a Google Doodle (2017), and a Church of England feast day (13 Dec) commemorate him.

## Terms

- ****Lexicographer**** — A compiler or writer of dictionaries; Johnson’s primary claim to this title is his 1755 *Dictionary*.
- ****Grub Street**** — A London street (Moorfields) inhabited by hack writers; Johnson defined it as the source of "any mean production." He and Savage worked there as anonymous jobbing writers.
- ****The Rambler**** — Johnson’s twice-weekly essay series (1750–52), 208 numbers, moral/religious tone, published anonymously; later collected in volumes.
- ****The Idler**** — Johnson’s weekly essay series (1758–60) in *The Universal Chronicle*, lighter than *The Rambler*, written partly to avoid finishing Shakespeare.
- ****The Club**** — A dining club founded 1763, meeting Mondays at the Turk’s Head, Soho; members included Reynolds, Burke, Garrick, Goldsmith, Boswell, later Adam Smith and Edward Gibbon.
- ****Rasselas**** — Philosophical novella (1759): Prince Rasselas escapes the "Happy Valley" (constant pleasure) to find suffering universal; returns without returning to the Valley.
- ****Lives of the Poets**** — Johnson’s final major work (1779–81), 52 biographical-critical prefaces to selected poets, commissioned by booksellers; insisted on accuracy over praise.
- ****Tourette syndrome**** — Neurodevelopmental disorder involving motor and vocal tics; posthumously diagnosed for Johnson based on Boswell’s descriptions of gestures, sounds, and rituals.
- ****The black dog**** — Johnson’s metaphor for his recurrent severe depression/melancholia, referenced in his late letters.
- ****Royal touch**** — The belief that a monarch’s touch could cure scrofula ("King’s Evil"); Queen Anne touched Johnson in 1712, unsuccessfully.

## Debates and open questions

*   **Tourette syndrome diagnosis**: While widely accepted since the 1967 report, the diagnosis relies entirely on 18th-century observations (Boswell, Thrale) rather than clinical examination. Some scholars debate whether tics were solely Tourette’s or compounded by OCD, rheumatic chorea, or habit.
*   **Boswell’s reliability**: Boswell’s *Life* shapes Johnson’s popular image but omits two major political periods (Walpole era, Seven Years’ War) and attacks Johnson’s views in pamphlets Boswell disagreed with (*The False Alarm*, *Taxation No Tyranny*). Critics (e.g., Donald Greene) question its status as objective biography versus hagiography.
*   **Political consistency**: Johnson’s early Jacobite sympathies vs. later Hanoverian acceptance; his opposition to slavery vs. defense of colonial taxation (*Taxation No Tyranny*); his anti-Scottish prejudice vs. Scottish biographer Boswell.
*   **Dictionary’s methodology**: Johnson’s etymologies were criticized even then (Macaulay: "wretched etymologist"); his reliance on literary quotations privileged "high" usage over spoken language, a tension in lexicography.
*   **Shakespeare edition’s textual theory**: Johnson aimed for the author’s final intention but lacked a coherent textual bibliography; later editors (Malone) refined his principles.
