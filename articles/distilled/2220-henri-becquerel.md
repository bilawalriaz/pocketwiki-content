# Henri Becquerel

## Overview
Antoine Henri Becquerel was a French experimental physicist whose accidental discovery of spontaneous radioactivity in 1896 fundamentally changed the understanding of matter and energy. This discovery, made while investigating phosphorescence, earned him a share of the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics and paved the way for nuclear physics and radiotherapy.

## Timeline
- **15 December 1852** — Born in Paris into a family of physicists.
- **1872–1877** — Studies engineering at École polytechnique and École des ponts et chaussées.
- **1888** — Receives D.Sc. from the University of Paris for a thesis on light polarization and phosphorescence.
- **1892** — Appointed Professor of Applied Physics at the Muséum national d'histoire naturelle.
- **1895** — Becomes a professor at École polytechnique.
- **Late 1895** — Wilhelm Röntgen discovers X-rays, sparking widespread scientific interest.
- **24 February 1896** — Reports initial results from experiments exposing phosphorescent uranium salts to sunlight.
- **1 March 1896** — Discovers that uranium salts emit penetrating rays without sunlight exposure, a phenomenon he later identifies as spontaneous radioactivity.
- **May 1896** — Concludes the radiation originates from the uranium itself, not from external excitation.
- **1900** — Measures beta particles, identifying them as high-speed electrons.
- **1901** — Observes that radium causes skin burns, leading to the development of radiotherapy.
- **1903** — Shares the Nobel Prize in Physics with Marie and Pierre Curie.
- **25 August 1908** — Dies in Le Croisic, likely from effects of radiation exposure.

## Body
### Education and Career
Born into a distinguished family of scientists, Becquerel received a rigorous education in engineering and physics. He held key academic and administrative positions, including Professor of Applied Physics at the Muséum national d'histoire naturelle and chief engineer in the Department of Roads and Bridges, before becoming a professor at the École polytechnique.

### Discovery of Radioactivity
Becquerel's discovery was serendipitous. Inspired by Röntgen's recent finding of X-rays, he hypothesized that phosphorescent materials, like uranium salts, might emit similar rays when exposed to sunlight. His initial experiments in February 1896 confirmed this, producing shadows on photographic plates. However, when he developed plates that had been stored in the dark due to overcast weather, he found the images were just as strong. This crucial observation led him to realize by May 1896 that the radiation was emitted spontaneously by the uranium itself, without any external energy source. This phenomenon was later named "radioactivity." His work prompted intense research, including the discovery of other radioactive elements like thorium, polonium, and radium by the Curies.

### Later Work and Legacy
Becquerel continued to study radioactivity, identifying beta particles as high-speed electrons and discovering the medical potential of radiation after being burned by radium. This led to the development of radiotherapy for cancer treatment. The SI unit of radioactivity, the becquerel (Bq), is named in his honor, as are lunar and Martian craters and a uranium mineral, becquerelite.

## Terms
*   **Radioactivity:** The spontaneous emission of radiation (such as alpha particles, beta particles, or gamma rays) by certain atomic nuclei.
*   **Phosphorescence:** The emission of light by a substance after it has absorbed light or other electromagnetic radiation, with a prolonged afterglow.
*   **X-rays:** A form of high-energy electromagnetic radiation discovered by Wilhelm Röntgen in 1895.
*   **Beta particles:** High-energy, high-speed electrons or positrons emitted by certain types of radioactive nuclei.
*   **Serendipity:** The occurrence of events by chance in a happy or beneficial way.
*   **Crookes tube:** An early experimental electrical discharge tube, used in the discovery of cathode rays and X-rays.
*   **Radiotherapy:** The treatment of disease, especially cancer, using radiation such as X-rays or gamma rays.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that the discovery of radioactivity was nearly made decades earlier. In 1857, Abel Niépce de Saint-Victor observed that uranium salts emitted radiation that could darken photographic plates, a fact later published by Becquerel's own father, Edmond Becquerel, in 1868. This highlights that scientific discovery often depends on the right context and interpretation, as the earlier observation was not pursued as a fundamental new phenomenon.
