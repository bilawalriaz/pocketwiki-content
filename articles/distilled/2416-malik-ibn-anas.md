# Malik ibn Anas

## Overview
Malik ibn Anas (c. 711–795) was a foundational Muslim scholar and jurist from Medina, best known as the eponym of the Maliki school of Islamic jurisprudence, one of the four major Sunni legal schools. His compilation of *al-Muwatta'* is one of the earliest surviving Islamic law books and a seminal hadith collection, shaping legal and theological thought across the Muslim world.

## Timeline
- **c. 711** — Born in Medina into the Banu Taym of Quraysh.
- **c. 711–795** — Studied under leading scholars of Medina, including Hisham ibn Urwa and Ja'far al-Sadiq.
- **Early Abbasid Caliphate** — Compiled *al-Muwatta'*, aiming to survey law based on Medinan consensus and practice.
- **Reign of Caliph al-Mansur** — Refused the caliph's request to impose his school as the sole legal authority, arguing for the mercy of scholarly diversity.
- **795** — Died in Medina at age 83 or 84; buried in Al-Baqi' cemetery.

## Body

### Life and Scholarship
Born in Medina, Malik was immersed in Islamic learning from youth, memorizing the Quran and studying under the city's leading scholars. He inherited knowledge from the Seven Jurists of Medina (*Fuqahā’ al-Sab‘ah*). His chain of narration, from the Prophet Muhammad through Ibn Umar and Nafi‘, was later deemed the "Golden Chain" (*Silsilat al-Dhahab*) for its authenticity. His premier work, *al-Muwatta'* ("The Smoothed Path"), was compiled to provide a standard of law based on the consensus and practice (*sunna*) of the Medinan community, addressing widespread differences of opinion.

### Theology and Jurisprudence
Malik's theology emphasized orthodox Sunni doctrines. He was a strict opponent of anthropomorphism (comparing God's attributes to human ones) and supported the beatific vision (believers seeing God in the afterlife). He defined faith (*iman*) as comprising both speech and works. In jurisprudence, he valued the *sunna* of Muhammad as essential for salvation, likening it to "Noah's Ark." He championed the practice of seeking blessings (*tabarruk*) through relics, such as the Prophet's pulpit, and supported intercession through the Prophet in supplication.

### Ethics and Legacy
Malik viewed differences of opinion among scholars as a divine mercy for the community. He famously refused caliphal attempts to impose a single legal school, fearing it would cause disaster. He emphasized intellectual humility, declaring "I do not know" to be the scholar's shield against error, and discouraged needless religious disputation. His school became normative across North Africa, al-Andalus, and parts of the Middle East. He was venerated by later Sunni scholars and mystics, with his student al-Shafi'i calling him "the star" among scholars.

## Terms
- **Muhaddith**: A scholar specializing in the study and authentication of hadith (sayings and actions of the Prophet Muhammad).
- **Eponym**: A person after whom something is named; Malik is the eponym of the Maliki school.
- **Sunna**: The established practices and traditions of the Prophet Muhammad, serving as a model for Muslim life.
- **Hadith**: A record of the traditions or sayings of the Prophet Muhammad, revered and forming part of the second source of Islamic law.
- **Al-Muwatta'**: Malik's compilation, meaning "The Smoothed Path," one of the earliest collections of Islamic law and hadith.
- **Anthropomorphism**: The attribution of human characteristics or form to God, which Malik opposed.
- **Beatific vision**: The orthodox Sunni belief that believers will see God in the afterlife.
- **Tabarruk**: The practice of seeking blessings (*baraka*) through veneration of relics or sacred objects.
- **Ijazah**: Certification and permission to teach Islamic knowledge, granted by a qualified scholar.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Authenticity of Traditions**: Some later scholars, like Ibn Hazm, doubted that prophecies foretelling Malik's birth referred to him, though the majority tradition accepted this identification.
- **Stance on Mysticism**: While Malik is recorded as holding early Sufis in high regard and combining inward and outward sciences, some weak traditions suggest he disapproved of group *dhikr* (remembrance of God). Scholars debate whether these reports reflect his actual views or criticisms of specific excesses.
- **Imposition of Legal School**: The historical account of Malik refusing the Abbasid caliph's attempt to impose the Maliki school as universal law raises questions about the early political dynamics of Islamic jurisprudence and the concept of scholarly diversity.
