# Moon landing

## Overview
A Moon landing is the arrival of a crewed or robotic spacecraft on the Moon. It represents a pinnacle of space exploration, driven initially by Cold War competition and now by scientific discovery and commercial ambition. The first successful crewed landing was Apollo 11 in 1969, with six crewed missions following by 1972. After a long hiatus, uncrewed landings resumed in 2013, with recent missions expanding to the lunar far side and south pole.

## Timeline
- **4 Oct 1957** — Soviet Union launches Sputnik 1, initiating the Space Race.
- **1959** — Luna 2 (USSR) makes the first hard lunar impact.
- **3 Feb 1966** — Luna 9 (USSR) achieves the first soft lunar landing.
- **20 Jul 1969** — Apollo 11 (USA) completes the first crewed Moon landing.
- **14 Dec 1972** — Apollo 17 (USA) is the last crewed lunar landing of the 20th century.
- **14 Dec 2013** — Chang'e 3 (China) ends a 37-year gap in soft lunar landings.
- **3 Jan 2019** — Chang'e 4 (China) makes the first landing on the lunar far side.
- **23 Aug 2023** — Chandrayaan-3 (India) lands near the lunar south pole.
- **19 Jan 2024** — SLIM (Japan) makes Japan the fifth nation to soft-land on the Moon.
- **22 Feb 2024** — Odysseus (USA) is the first successful commercial lunar lander.
- **2 Mar 2025** — Blue Ghost (USA) achieves the first fully successful commercial landing.

## Body

### Uncrewed Landings
The first lunar missions were uncrewed. The Soviet Union achieved the first hard impact with Luna 2 in 1959 and the first soft landing with Luna 9 in 1966. The U.S. followed with successful Surveyor soft landings. After the Apollo era, a 37-year gap in soft landings ended with China's Chang'e 3 in 2013. China has since landed four times, including the first far-side landing (Chang'e 4, 2019) and two sample-return missions. India (Chandrayaan-3, 2023) and Japan (SLIM, 2024) have also achieved soft landings. Commercial entities have recently entered this field; after several failures, Intuitive Machines' Odysseus (2024) and Firefly Aerospace's Blue Ghost (2025) successfully landed.

### Crewed Landings
All crewed lunar landings were conducted by NASA's Apollo program. Between July 1969 and December 1972, twelve astronauts walked on the Moon across six missions. Each mission used a Lunar Module for descent, with a third crew member remaining in orbit. The program was a direct response to early Soviet space successes, framed by President Kennedy as a demonstration of U.S. technological leadership during the Cold War. The political context was crucial; the U.S. accelerated its military and civilian space programs to counter Soviet achievements like Sputnik and Luna missions.

### Scientific and Political Background
Reaching the Moon requires a rocket to escape Earth's gravity. A spacecraft must then decelerate to land; a "soft landing" requires near-zero speed at contact, achieved with retrorockets. The political rivalry between the U.S. and USSR, known as the Space Race, was the primary driver for early lunar exploration. The Soviet Union's early firsts (first satellite, first lunar impact) shocked the U.S., leading to the creation of NASA and a massive investment in the Apollo program. The U.S. strategy evolved from direct ascent to a lunar orbit rendezvous profile, which was more efficient for crewed return missions.

### Transition to Lunar Orbit
Initial uncrewed landers used direct ascent. However, crewed missions required a return capability, making lunar orbit operations essential. By 1966, both superpowers shifted to lunar orbiters to map the surface and select landing sites. These orbiters discovered "mascons" (mass concentrations) beneath lunar maria, which could destabilize spacecraft orbits. The Soviet Zond program attempted crewed circumlunar loops but was plagued by failures. In contrast, NASA successfully tested its Apollo spacecraft in lunar orbit with Apollo 8 (1968) and Apollo 10 (1969), paving the way for the Apollo 11 landing.

## Terms
- **Hard landing:** A spacecraft impacts the lunar surface at high speed, often intentionally for probes not designed to survive.
- **Soft landing:** A controlled descent where the spacecraft touches down at negligible speed, essential for crewed missions and delicate instruments.
- **Retrorockets:** Rockets fired against the direction of travel to decelerate a spacecraft for landing.
- **Lunar orbit rendezvous:** A mission profile where a spacecraft enters lunar orbit, and a separate lander descends to the surface, improving fuel efficiency for return.
- **Mascons (mass concentrations):** Dense volumes of material beneath the lunar surface that can perturb spacecraft orbits.
- **Free return trajectory:** A spacecraft path that loops around the Moon and returns to Earth without needing to enter lunar orbit, used as a safety option on Apollo missions.
- **Space Race:** The Cold War competition between the U.S. and USSR for dominance in spaceflight capability.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The primary driver for the Apollo program was political (Cold War prestige) rather than purely scientific, a point of historical analysis.
- The Soviet lunar program was secretive, hiding mission failures by assigning public mission numbers only after success.
- The long hiatus in crewed lunar landings after 1972 is attributed to high costs and shifting political priorities.
- The recent surge in commercial and international uncrewed landings signals a new era, but the sustainability and scientific return of these missions are still being evaluated.
