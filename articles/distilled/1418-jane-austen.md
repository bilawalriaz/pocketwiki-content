# Jane Austen

## Overview
Jane Austen (1775–1817) was an English novelist whose six major works—Sense and Sensibility, Pride and Prejudice, Mansfield Park, Emma, Northanger Abbey, and Persuasion—critique the dependence of women on marriage for social standing and economic security among the landed gentry. Writing anonymously, she bridged 18th-century novels of sensibility and 19th-century literary realism through irony, free indirect speech, and a focus on everyday manners. Though moderately successful in her lifetime, her reputation grew posthumously, cementing her status as a foundational figure in English literature whose novels have remained continuously in print since 1833.

## Timeline
- **16 Dec 1775** — Born in Steventon, Hampshire, to Rev. George Austen and Cassandra Leigh.
- **1783–1786** — Brief formal schooling in Oxford, Southampton, and Reading; thereafter home-educated via family libraries.
- **1787–1793** — Writes *Juvenilia* (three notebooks of parodies, stories, and *The History of England*).
- **1795–1796** — Flirtation with neighbour Tom Lefroy; family intervenes, ending contact.
- **1796–1798** — Drafts *First Impressions* (later *Pride and Prejudice*), revises *Elinor and Marianne* (later *Sense and Sensibility*), begins *Susan* (later *Northanger Abbey*).
- **1797** — Father offers *First Impressions* to publisher Cadell; declined by return of post.
- **1800–1805** — Family moves to Bath after father’s retirement; writing productivity drops sharply.
- **1802** — Accepts then retracts marriage proposal from Harris Bigg-Wither.
- **1803** — Sells copyright of *Susan* to Crosby & Co. for £10; they advertise but never publish it.
- **1805** — Father dies; family faces financial precarity, moves to Southampton (1806).
- **1809** — Moves to Chawton cottage on brother Edward’s estate; stable, productive period begins.
- **Oct 1811** — *Sense and Sensibility* published "on commission" by Thomas Egerton; sells out by 1813.
- **Jan 1813** — *Pride and Prejudice* published (copyright sold to Egerton for £110); immediate success.
- **May 1814** — *Mansfield Park* published; ignored by reviewers but sells out in six months.
- **Dec 1815** — *Emma* published by John Murray; dedicated to Prince Regent at his librarian’s request.
- **1816** — Repurchases copyright of *Susan*; completes *Persuasion*; brother Henry’s bank fails.
- **18 July 1817** — Dies in Winchester at age 41; buried in Winchester Cathedral.
- **Dec 1817** — *Persuasion* and *Northanger Abbey* published posthumously with Henry’s "Biographical Note" revealing authorship.
- **1833** — Richard Bentley publishes collected *Standard Novels* edition; novels never go out of print again.

## Body

### Family, Childhood, and Education
Austen was born into a clerical family of modest means in Steventon. Her father, George Austen, held two livings worth £200 annually (supplemented by farming and boarding pupils), placing the family well below the typical gentry income of £1,000–£5,000. Her mother, Cassandra Leigh, came from a prominent family; an aunt, Jane Leigh-Perrot, was tried for grand larceny, and a brother, George, was fostered away due to developmental disabilities—details omitted from early family biographies. The household was intellectually open; private theatricals staged in the rectory barn cultivated Austen’s satirical gifts. After a near-fatal bout of typhus at boarding school (1783), she was educated at home, drawing on her father’s library and that of family friend Warren Hastings. She never lived outside her immediate family circle after 1786.

### Juvenilia and Early Manuscripts (1787–1798)
From age eleven, Austen wrote parodies of sentimental and Gothic conventions, compiled into three *Juvenilia* notebooks (90,000 words). *Love and Freindship* (1790) mocked novels of sensibility; *The History of England* (1791) parodied Oliver Goldsmith. Scholar Richard Jenkyns calls these works "boisterous" and "anarchic." By 1792 she began *Catharine or the Bower*, a precursor to *Northanger Abbey*. Between 1793–1795 she wrote *Lady Susan*, a sophisticated epistolary novella featuring a manipulative female protagonist, possibly modeled on her cousin Eliza de Feuillide. In 1796 she drafted *First Impressions*; her father’s submission to Cadell was rejected unseen. She then revised *Elinor and Marianne* from epistolary to third-person narration and began *Susan*, a Gothic satire, completing it c. 1799.

### Bath, Southampton, and Creative Silence (1800–1809)
George Austen’s sudden retirement moved the family to Bath in 1800. Austen’s productivity collapsed; she revised *Susan* but abandoned *The Watsons*, a bleak study of dependent women. Scholars debate whether this reflects depression (Tomalin) or simply a busier social life and frequent moves (Honan, Irvine). In 1802 she accepted a practical proposal from Harris Bigg-Wither, heir to local estates, but withdrew the next morning, later advising her niece never to marry without affection. Her father’s death in 1805 left the women dependent on brothers’ annuities. They lived in rented lodgings, Worthing, and Southampton until 1809. In 1809 Austen angrily demanded publication or return of *Susan* from Crosby; he refused, and she lacked funds to repurchase it until 1816.

### Chawton and Publication (1809–1817)
Edward Austen provided a cottage in Chawton village in 1809. Life was quiet; the family socialized little outside kin. Austen published four novels anonymously—standard practice for women to avoid the stigma of "literary lioness" status. *Sense and Sensibility* (1811) was sold "on commission" (author bears financial risk); Austen earned £140. *Pride and Prejudice* (1813) sold copyright for £110; had it been on commission, she would have earned £475. *Mansfield Park* (1814) sold out in six months, yielding her highest earnings. *Emma* (1815) moved to John Murray; its success was offset by a failed second edition of *Mansfield Park*. The Prince Regent’s librarian pressed her to dedicate *Emma* to him; she complied but satirized his suggestions in *Plan of a Novel*. In 1816 Henry Austen repurchased *Susan*; family finances collapsed when his bank failed.

### Illness and Death
Austen fell ill in early 1816. Most biographers follow Zachary Cope’s 1964 retrospective diagnosis of Addison’s disease; Hodgkin’s lymphoma is also proposed. She rewrote *Persuasion*’s ending (Aug 1816) and began *Sanditon* (Jan 1817), stopping after twelve chapters in March. Confined to bed by April, she was taken to Winchester in May and died 18 July 1817. Her epitaph praises her "extraordinary endowments of mind" but omits her authorship.

### Posthumous Reputation
Henry Austen’s 1817 "Biographical Note" first named her. Sales were steady but novels were out of print in England by the 1820s. Richard Bentley’s 1833 *Standard Novels* collected edition (illustrated by Ferdinand Pickering) began continuous publication. The 1869 *Memoir* by nephew James Edward Austen-Leigh created the "dear aunt Jane" legend, sparking "Austenolatry" and a counter-movement of "Janeites" claiming deeper appreciation. 19th-century critics (Scott, Whately) praised her realism; others (Charlotte Brontë, Mark Twain, D.H. Lawrence) dismissed her limited scope. Academic study began with George Pellew’s 1883 Harvard dissertation and R.W. Chapman’s 1923 scholarly edition. Post-WWII criticism diversified (feminist, postcolonial); Harold Bloom ranked her among the greatest Western writers (1994). In China, she was banned during the Cultural Revolution (1966–76) as "British bourgeois imperialist."

### Genre, Style, and Technique
Austen repudiated the "trashy sensationalism" of Gothic and sentimental fiction (Walter Scott’s phrase), returning the novel on a "slender thread" to Richardson and Fielding’s realistic study of manners. She pioneered extensive **free indirect speech**, blending narrator and character voices to present thoughts directly while retaining control. Her dialogue uses syntax and phrasing—not vocabulary—to signal social variant and mood; Elizabeth Bennet’s convoluted rejection of Darcy reveals wounded pride. Plots center on women’s economic dependence on marriage. Critics note her "ethical sensibility" fused with comedy: she uses irony to reconcile satirical bias with a sense of the good (Polhemus). F.R. Leavis and Ian Watt placed her in the "great tradition" of irony, realism, and satire.

### Adaptations
Adaptations began with family completions of fragments (19th century) and Rosina Filippi’s drawing-room dramas (1895). The 1940 MGM *Pride and Prejudice* (Olivier/Garson) began a film tradition; BBC serials (1970s/80s) emphasized fidelity. The 1995 boom—Ang Lee’s *Sense and Sensibility* (Emma Thompson’s Oscar-winning script) and BBC’s *Pride and Prejudice* (Jennifer Ehle/Colin Firth)—sparked ongoing adaptations across media, including *Bride and Prejudice* (2004), *Love & Friendship* (2016, from *Lady Susan*), and *Sanditon* (2019–23). Critic Robert Irvine notes American adaptations subtly downplay the rigid land-based class hierarchy central to Austen’s Regency England.

## Terms
- ****Landed gentry**** — British social class of landowners who could live entirely from rental income, below the peerage but above professionals and merchants.
- ****Novels of sensibility**** — 18th-century genre emphasizing emotional responsiveness, sentiment, and refined feeling; Austen parodied their excesses.
- ****Literary realism**** — 19th-century mode representing everyday life, manners, and social structures without idealization or romantic exaggeration.
- ****Free indirect speech**** — Narrative technique blending third-person narration with a character’s internal voice/thoughts; Austen was the first English novelist to use it extensively.
- ****Publishing on commission**** — Author pays production costs; publisher recoups costs plus 10% commission from sales, remitting balance to author. Author bears loss if sales fail.
- ****Selling the copyright**** — Author receives a one-time payment; publisher owns all future rights and profits. Austen used this for *Pride and Prejudice* (£110).
- ****Epistolary novel**** — Novel told through a series of documents (letters, diary entries); Austen’s early *Lady Susan* and first draft of *Sense and Sensibility* used this form.
- ****Juvenilia**** — Works produced in an author’s youth; Austen’s three notebooks (1787–1793) contain parodies, stories, and *The History of England*.
- ****Addison’s disease**** — Adrenal insufficiency causing fatigue, weight loss, low blood pressure; the leading retrospective diagnosis for Austen’s fatal illness (Cope, 1964).
- ****Standard Novels series**** — Richard Bentley’s affordable collected editions (1833) that established Austen’s continuous print presence and canonical status.

## Debates and open questions
- **Cause of death:** Addison’s disease (Cope, 1964) vs. Hodgkin’s lymphoma vs. other possibilities (tuberculosis, Brill-Zinsser disease); no autopsy was performed.
- **Bath productivity:** Was the 1800–1809 creative silence due to depression (Tomalin), disruption from socializing/moving (Honan, Irvine), or the trauma of the Bigg-Wither proposal and father’s death?
- **Tom Lefroy’s significance:** Was the 1795–96 flirtation a genuine romance thwarted by poverty, or did Austen satirize sentimental conventions in her letters (Halperin)?
- **Political stance:** Was Austen a conservative "Tory of the deepest dye" (Koppel) upholding hierarchy, or a feminist critiquing patriarchal economics (Johnson, Sandock)? Gadamerian view: both readings can be textually grounded.
- **Cassandra’s letter destruction:** Why did Cassandra burn most letters (1801–1804 especially)? To protect family reputation from Jane’s "acid" comments, or to shape the "good quiet Aunt Jane" legend?
- **French reception:** Why did pirated French translations (Montolieu, 1810s–20s) radically alter plots/characters? Was it translator incompetence or adaptation to French taste for "lush romantic fantasies" (Noel King)?
- **Sanditon’s direction:** Would the unfinished novel have developed its "energetic invalid" heroine Diana Parker into satire or sympathy? Austen mocked hypochondria yet described herself similarly days before stopping.
