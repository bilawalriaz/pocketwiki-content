# Mithridates VI Eupator

## Overview
Mithridates VI Eupator (135–63 BC) was the Hellenistic king of Pontus who became one of the Roman Republic's most formidable opponents. He sought to dominate Anatolia and the Black Sea region, waging three major wars against Rome to break its dominion over Asia and the Greek world. His reign marked a significant clash between an expanding Eastern kingdom and the rising power of Rome.

## Timeline
- **c. 135 BC** — Born in Sinope, Pontus.
- **c. 120 BC** — Father Mithridates V assassinated; Mithridates VI and brother become joint heirs under regency of mother Laodice VI.
- **116–113 BC** — Overthrows his mother and brother, becoming sole ruler of Pontus.
- **c. 108 BC** — Subjugates Colchis and the Bosporan Kingdom on the Black Sea.
- **89 BC** — Defeats Nicomedes IV of Bithynia and his Roman allies, overrunning Roman Asia.
- **88 BC** — Orders the massacre of Roman and Italian settlers in Asia Minor (the "Asiatic Vespers").
- **88–84 BC** — First Mithridatic War: Lucius Cornelius Sulla forces him out of Greece; a lenient peace is signed.
- **83–81 BC** — Second Mithridatic War: Mithridates defeats the Roman legate Murena.
- **73–63 BC** — Third Mithridatic War: After Bithynia is bequeathed to Rome, Mithridates invades but is ultimately defeated by Pompey.
- **63 BC** — Commits suicide in Crimea after a rebellion led by his son Pharnaces II.

## Body

### Rise to Power and Expansion
Mithridates VI ascended to the throne of Pontus as a teenager following his father's assassination. After overthrowing his regent mother, he pursued an aggressive expansionist policy. He first secured his kingdom's eastern flank by subjugating Colchis and the Bosporan Kingdom, defeating Scythian rivals. He then turned west, clashing with Nicomedes IV of Bithynia over control of Anatolia. When Rome intervened on Bithynia's behalf, conflict became inevitable.

### The Mithridatic Wars
The conflict erupted in 89 BC when Nicomedes IV, backed by Rome, invaded Pontus. Mithridates won a decisive victory and, exploiting Rome's preoccupation with the Social War, overran Roman Asia. He orchestrated the massacre of tens of thousands of Roman and Italian settlers, an event known as the Asiatic Vespers. Positioning himself as a champion of Hellenism against Roman "barbarism," he gained the support of many Greek cities, including Athens.

Rome's response came under Lucius Cornelius Sulla, who defeated Mithridates in Greece during the First Mithridatic War (88–84 BC). A hasty peace restored pre-war borders. The Second Mithridatic War (83–81 BC) was provoked when the Roman legate Murena attacked, but Mithridates prevailed. The final, protracted Third Mithridatic War (73–63 BC) began after Rome annexed Bithynia. Mithridates was initially routed by Lucullus but staged a comeback, only to be decisively defeated by Pompey at the Battle of the Lycus in 66 BC.

### Rulership and Legacy
Mithridates skillfully used Hellenism as a political tool, presenting himself as a protector of Greek culture against Rome. His court was cosmopolitan, and his coinage blended Greek and Persian motifs. His kingdom became a major intellectual and cultural center. His decades of resistance made him a legendary figure, though his ultimate failure led to the expansion of Roman power in the East.

### Personal Life and Mithridatism
Mithridates was a polyglot, reportedly speaking the languages of all the nations he ruled. He is famously associated with *mithridatism*—the practice of building immunity to poison by regularly ingesting sub-lethal doses. He developed complex antidotes, testing them on condemned criminals. His personal "universal antidote" (*mithridatium*) became a famous, if variable, pharmaceutical recipe in Western and Islamic medicine for centuries.

## Terms
- **Hellenistic ruler**: A king in the tradition of Alexander the Great's successors, blending Greek culture with local traditions.
- **Pontus**: A Hellenistic kingdom on the southern coast of the Black Sea, in northeastern Anatolia.
- **Asiatic Vespers**: The 88 BC massacre of Roman and Italian settlers in Asia Minor ordered by Mithridates.
- **Mithridatic Wars**: Three major conflicts (88–84 BC, 83–81 BC, 73–63 BC) between Mithridates VI of Pontus and the Roman Republic.
- **Mithridatism (Mithridatization)**: The practice of consuming small, non-lethal doses of poison to build immunity.
- **Mithridatium**: A complex antidote formula developed by Mithridates, which became a staple of ancient and medieval medicine.
- **Philhellenism**: Love of or admiration for Greek culture, which Mithridates used as a political tool.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact number of casualties in the Asiatic Vespers is debated, with ancient sources giving figures as high as 80,000.
- The precise composition and efficacy of Mithridates' personal antidote (*mithridatium*) are lost to history, leading to centuries of speculation and varied recipes.
- Scholars debate the sincerity of Mithridates' philhellenism versus its use as a calculated political strategy to rally support against Rome.
