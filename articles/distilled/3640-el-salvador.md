# El Salvador

## Overview
El Salvador is a small, densely populated sovereign nation in Central America, bordered by Guatemala and Honduras. Its history is marked by pre-Columbian civilizations, Spanish conquest, a turbulent post-independence period, a devastating civil war, and recent dramatic political and social shifts under President Nayib Bukele. The country's economy has transitioned from agricultural exports to a more diversified model, though it faces challenges with crime, corruption, and natural disasters.

## Timeline
- **c. 1200 BC** — Tazumal, a major Maya settlement, is first inhabited.
- **1524** — Spanish conquest of the Cuzcatlán region begins under Pedro de Alvarado.
- **1609** — The Captaincy General of Guatemala, including El Salvador, is established by Spain.
- **1821** — El Salvador gains independence from Spain and briefly joins the First Mexican Empire.
- **1823** — El Salvador becomes part of the Federal Republic of Central America.
- **1841** — El Salvador declares full sovereignty as an independent republic.
- **1932** — A peasant revolt is brutally suppressed in "La Matanza," killing tens of thousands.
- **1979-1992** — The Salvadoran Civil War is fought between the government and the FMLN.
- **1992** — The Chapultepec Peace Accords end the civil war.
- **2001** — El Salvador adopts the U.S. dollar as its official currency.
- **2019** — Nayib Bukele is elected president, breaking the two-party system.
- **2022** — A state of emergency is declared to combat gangs, leading to mass incarcerations.

## Body

### Pre-Columbian and Colonial Era
The territory was inhabited by Mesoamerican civilizations, including the Maya and later the Nahua-speaking Pipil people, who called their domain Cuzcatlán. Spanish conquistadors, led by Pedro de Alvarado, began the conquest in 1524, facing significant resistance. The region was incorporated into the Captaincy General of Guatemala in 1609. The colonial economy relied on indigo and cacao exports, with indigenous populations declining due to disease, forced labor, and land loss.

### Independence and Political Instability
El Salvador gained independence from Spain in 1821, was briefly annexed by Mexico, and then joined the Federal Republic of Central America until its dissolution in 1841. The late 19th and early 20th centuries were characterized by political instability, with frequent military coups and the rise of an oligarchy based on coffee exports. A brutal suppression of a 1932 indigenous and communist-led revolt, known as La Matanza, cemented military control for decades.

### Civil War and Peace Accords
Entrenched socioeconomic disparities and political repression culminated in the Salvadoran Civil War (1979-1992) between the military-led government and the Farabundo Martí National Liberation Front (FMLN), a left-wing guerrilla coalition. The conflict, marked by widespread human rights abuses, killed over 75,000 people. It ended with the 1992 Chapultepec Peace Accords, which demilitarized the opposition, reformed security forces, and established a multiparty democracy. The war triggered mass emigration to the United States.

### Post-War Politics and the Bukele Era
Post-war politics were dominated by the right-wing ARENA party and the left-wing FMLN. In 2019, Nayib Bukele of the GANA party won the presidency, breaking this duopoly. His administration has been defined by a severe crackdown on gangs, declaring a state of emergency in 2022 that led to the arrest of over 72,000 suspects and a dramatic drop in homicide rates. Bukele's party, Nuevas Ideas, won a supermajority in 2021, enabling constitutional changes, including the removal of presidential term limits. In 2021, El Salvador became the first country to adopt Bitcoin as legal tender, a status later reversed in 2025 under IMF pressure.

### Economy and Society
Historically dependent on agricultural exports like indigo and coffee, El Salvador has diversified into manufacturing and services. In 2001, it replaced its currency with the U.S. dollar. Remittances from Salvadorans abroad are a major source of income. The country is highly urbanized and has a young population. Spanish is the official language, and the majority of the population is Christian. El Salvador is geographically small and volcanically active, lying on the Pacific Ring of Fire.

## Terms
- **Cuzcatlán**: The largest indigenous domain in Salvadoran territory before European contact, inhabited by the Pipil people.
- **Captaincy General of Guatemala**: An administrative division of the Spanish Empire (created 1609) that governed Central America, including El Salvador, during the colonial period.
- **La Matanza**: The brutal 1932 government suppression of a peasant revolt, resulting in the deaths of tens of thousands, mostly indigenous Pipil peasants.
- **FMLN (Farabundo Martí National Liberation Front)**: A coalition of left-wing guerrilla groups that fought the government in the civil war and later became a major political party.
- **Chapultepec Peace Accords**: The 1992 agreements that ended the Salvadoran Civil War, mandating military reforms and establishing a multiparty system.
- **Remittances**: Funds sent home by Salvadorans living abroad, a critical component of the national economy.
- **Bitcoin Law**: Legislation passed in 2021 making Bitcoin legal tender in El Salvador, later repealed in 2025.
- **State of Emergency**: A measure declared in 2022 granting authorities broad powers to arrest suspected gang members, leading to mass incarcerations.

## Debates and Open questions
- The long-term economic and social impact of the 2022 crackdown and mass incarceration remains a subject of intense debate, with reports of human rights abuses.
- The effectiveness and consequences of adopting (and later repealing) Bitcoin as legal tender are still being assessed.
- The consolidation of power under President Bukele, including constitutional changes to allow re-election and extend terms, raises questions about the future of democratic institutions in El Salvador.
- The root causes of gang violence and the sustainability of the security-driven reduction in homicide rates are ongoing scholarly and policy discussions.
