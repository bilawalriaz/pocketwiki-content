# Great Lakes

## Overview
The Great Lakes—Superior, Michigan, Huron, Erie, and Ontario—are five interconnected freshwater lakes on the Canada–U.S. border. They form the largest group of freshwater lakes on Earth by surface area, containing about 21% of the world's surface fresh water. Formed by glacial retreat around 14,000 years ago, they are vital for transportation, ecology, and the economies of both nations.

## Timeline
- **c. 14,000 years ago** — The Great Lakes begin to form as retreating ice sheets expose basins that fill with meltwater.
- **1679** — The *Le Griffon*, the first known sailing ship on the upper Great Lakes, is launched.
- **1818** — The Rush–Bagot Treaty leads to the disarmament of naval vessels on the lakes.
- **1824-1825** — The Welland Canal and Erie Canal open, creating a water route from the Great Lakes to the Atlantic Ocean.
- **1848** — The Illinois and Michigan Canal opens, providing a direct water link between the Great Lakes and the Mississippi River.
- **1909** — The International Joint Commission is established to manage boundary water disputes.
- **1972** — The first U.S. Clean Water Act is passed, and the first Great Lakes Water Quality Agreement is signed.
- **1988** — The invasive zebra mussel is first discovered in the Great Lakes.
- **2005** — The Great Lakes–Saint Lawrence River Basin Water Resources Compact is signed to prevent water diversions.
- **2020** — Record high water levels are recorded on Lakes Superior, Erie, and Michigan–Huron.

## Body

### Geography and Formation
The five lakes form a single, naturally interconnected freshwater system within the Great Lakes Basin. Water flows from Lake Superior through Lakes Huron and Michigan, south to Erie, and north to Ontario, draining ultimately to the Atlantic Ocean via the Saint Lawrence River. The lakes are also linked to the Mississippi River basin through the Chicago Sanitary and Ship Canal. They began forming at the end of the Last Glacial Period as the Laurentide Ice Sheet retreated, leaving basins carved by glaciers that filled with meltwater. The foundational geology includes the 1.1-billion-year-old Midcontinent Rift and the 570-million-year-old Saint Lawrence rift.

### Hydrology and Climate
The lakes' water levels are primarily influenced by regional meteorology and climatology, not tides. Outflows from Lakes Superior and Ontario are regulated, while those from Michigan–Huron and Erie are not. The lakes have a humid continental climate and significantly moderate local weather. They are known for producing "lake-effect snow," where cold winds pick up moisture from the relatively warmer lake surfaces, depositing heavy, localized snowfall on downwind shores. This moderating effect also creates "fruit belts" along their shores, suitable for growing apples, cherries, and grapes.

### Ecology and Invasive Species
The Great Lakes ecosystem supports diverse flora and fauna, including deep-water species like the deepwater sculpin. Historically, the region was surrounded by forests, though logging and development have altered this. The lakes have faced severe ecological challenges. Overfishing depleted key species like whitefish by the late 19th century. Since the 19th century, an estimated 160 non-native species have been introduced, many becoming invasive. Major invaders include the sea lamprey, which decimated lake trout populations, and the zebra and quagga mussels, which filter vast amounts of water, disrupting the food web and clogging infrastructure. The invasive alewife became dominant by the 1960s, leading to large-scale stocking of salmon to control its population.

### Pollution and Conservation
Industrial and municipal pollution severely degraded water quality by the mid-20th century. The 1972 Clean Water Act and the Great Lakes Water Quality Agreement led to significant improvements by the 1980s, particularly in reducing untreated sewage and toxic discharges like PCBs and mercury. However, challenges persist. Phosphorus runoff causes recurring harmful algal blooms in Lake Erie. Combined sewer overflow events still release untreated effluent during heavy rains. Plastic pollution is a major issue, with an estimated 22 million pounds entering the lakes annually, breaking down into microplastics found in fish and drinking water. Conservation efforts, such as the Great Lakes Restoration Initiative, focus on cleanup, habitat restoration, and invasive species control.

### History and Economy
Native American populations inhabited the region for millennia. European exploration began in the 17th century, with the Great Lakes becoming crucial for the fur trade. The opening of canals in the 19th century spurred immigration, settlement, and the growth of major cities like Chicago, Detroit, and Toronto. The lakes became a vital corridor for shipping bulk goods like iron ore, coal, and grain. Today, more than 100 lake freighters operate on the system. Tourism and recreation, including a $4 billion annual fishing industry, are also major economic drivers. The lakes are prone to severe storms and have claimed hundreds of ships, including the SS *Edmund Fitzgerald* in 1975.

## Terms
*   **Great Lakes Basin:** The area of land where all water drains into the Great Lakes and their connecting rivers.
*   **Lake-effect snow:** Localized, heavy snowfall that occurs when cold, dry air passes over the warmer, moist air of a large lake.
*   **Invasive species:** Non-native organisms that spread widely and cause ecological or economic harm.
*   **Zebra mussel:** A small, invasive freshwater mussel that filters large volumes of water, disrupting ecosystems and clogging pipes.
*   **Combined sewer overflow (CSO):** A discharge of untreated sewage and stormwater into water bodies when a sewer system's capacity is exceeded by heavy rainfall.
*   **Microplastics:** Tiny plastic particles less than 5 mm in size, resulting from the breakdown of larger plastic debris or from sources like synthetic clothing fibers.
*   **Seawaymax:** The maximum size of ship that can fit through the locks of the St. Lawrence Seaway.
*   **International Joint Commission (IJC):** A binational organization established in 1909 to help the U.S. and Canada manage shared water resources and resolve disputes.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Official Status of Lake Michigan–Huron:** Hydrologically, Lakes Michigan and Huron are a single body of water. If officially recognized as one lake, "Lake Michigan–Huron" would be the world's largest freshwater lake by surface area, surpassing Lake Superior.
*   **Impact of Climate Change:** Long-term data shows a general decline in maximum annual ice cover, aligning with regional warming. The full impact on lake levels, ecosystems, and weather patterns like lake-effect snow is an ongoing area of study.
*   **Effectiveness of Invasive Species Control:** Despite decades of effort, new invasive species continue to arrive, and established ones like quagga mussels have caused drastic, unprecedented changes to lake food webs, such as the collapse of deepwater fish communities in Lake Huron.
*   **Future of Water Diversion:** While the Great Lakes Compact restricts diversions, concerns remain about potential future proposals to divert water to water-scarce regions, especially as climate patterns shift.
