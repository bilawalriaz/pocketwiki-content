# Vijayanagara Empire

## Overview
The Vijayanagara Empire (1336–1646) was a major Hindu empire in southern India, founded to resist northern Muslim invasions. It became a dominant power, especially under Krishnadevaraya in the early 16th century, and its legacy includes significant architectural, cultural, and administrative achievements. Its decline followed a catastrophic defeat in 1565.

## Timeline
- **1336** — Empire founded by Harihara I and Bukka Raya I of the Sangama dynasty.
- **1374** — Bukka Raya I gains control over Goa and the Tungabhadra-Krishna doab.
- **1407** — Deva Raya I enters a tribute treaty with the Bahmani Sultanate.
- **1509** — Krishnadevaraya of the Tuluva dynasty begins his reign, marking the empire's peak.
- **1520** — Krishnadevaraya captures Raichur from the Deccan sultanates.
- **1542–1543** — Vijayanagara Civil War following the death of Achyuta Deva Raya.
- **1565** — Defeat at the Battle of Talikota by a coalition of Deccan sultanates; capital Vijayanagara (Hampi) is sacked.
- **1572** — Tirumala Deva Raya of the Aravidu dynasty abdicates, dividing the remnant empire.
- **1646** — Final collapse of the Vijayanagara Empire's remnants.

## Body

### Origins and Early Expansion
The empire was established in 1336 by Harihara I and Bukka Raya I, likely as a successor to earlier Hindu kingdoms (Hoysala, Kakatiya, Yadava) that had fallen to the Delhi Sultanate. Its founding was a culmination of southern resistance to Muslim invasions. The original capital was at Anegondi, but it was moved to Vijayanagara (modern Hampi) during Bukka Raya I's reign for better defense. Early rulers expanded south of the Tungabhadra River and consolidated power against regional rivals.

### Peak Under Krishnadevaraya
The empire reached its zenith under Krishnadevaraya (r. 1509–1529). His armies were consistently victorious, gaining territory from the Deccan sultanates (including Raichur and Gulbarga) and the Gajapati Empire of Odisha. He strengthened and consolidated the empire, employing both Hindus and Muslims in his army. This period saw the completion of major monuments and a flourishing of arts and literature.

### Decline and Fall
After Krishnadevaraya, internal strife weakened the empire. A civil war (1542–1543) followed the death of Achyuta Deva Raya, leading to the de facto rule of Rama Raya. His interference in Deccan sultanate affairs and the use of Muslim generals made him unpopular. In 1565, a coalition of Deccan sultanates defeated the Vijayanagara army at the Battle of Talikota. Rama Raya was executed, and the capital was plundered and destroyed. The Aravidu dynasty attempted to reconstitute the empire from a new capital, but it gradually fragmented and ended by 1646.

### Governance and Economy
The empire's administration was hierarchical, with the emperor assisted by a council of ministers. Provinces (Rajya) were governed by commanders or royal family members. The economy was agrarian, with key crops including rice, cotton, and spices. It had a vibrant textile industry and extensive overseas trade with ports on both coasts, exporting goods like cotton, spices, and gems to regions including China, Persia, and East Africa. The state used a monetized system with gold, silver, copper, and brass coins.

### Society and Religion
Society was organized by caste, though occupational mobility existed. The rulers were tolerant of all religions, protecting Hinduism while also patronizing mosques and interacting with Christian and Jewish communities. The Bhakti (devotional) movement was prominent, with Haridasa saints spreading Vaishnava devotion through music and poetry. The empire's patronage enabled a golden age in Kannada, Telugu, Tamil, and Sanskrit literature.

### Architecture and Legacy
Vijayanagara architecture is a synthesis of earlier South Indian styles (Chalukya, Hoysala, Pandya, Chola). It is characterized by ornate pillared halls, monolithic sculptures, and the use of durable granite. The capital at Hampi is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The empire's legacy includes advancements in water management, irrigation, and the codification of Carnatic music.

## Terms
- **Sangama dynasty**: The founding dynasty of the Vijayanagara Empire (1336–1485).
- **Deccan sultanates**: The Muslim kingdoms (e.g., Bahmani, Bijapur, Golconda) north of the Tungabhadra River that were rivals of Vijayanagara.
- **Battle of Talikota (1565)**: The decisive defeat of the Vijayanagara Empire by a coalition of Deccan sultanates, leading to the sack of its capital.
- **Bhakti movement**: A Hindu devotional movement emphasizing personal devotion to a god, prominent during the Vijayanagara period.
- **Haridasas**: Vaishnava (Vishnu-worshipping) poet-saints who spread devotion through Kannada songs (Devaranamas).
- **Prabandha**: A style of Telugu literary composition, often narrative poetry.
- **Kalyanamantapa**: An ornate, pillared marriage hall, a hallmark of Vijayanagara temple architecture.
- **Varaha**: A gold coin, the highest denomination in the Vijayanagara monetary system.
- **Rajya**: A main province of the empire, administered by a commander or governor.
- **Aravidu dynasty**: The final ruling dynasty of the Vijayanagara Empire (1542–1646), established after the fall of Hampi.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The ethnolinguistic origins of the founders, Harihara I and Bukka Raya I, are debated: one theory holds they were Kannadigas from the Hoysala army, another that they were Telugu people from the Kakatiya kingdom.
- The role of the saint Vidyaranya in inspiring the empire's founding is noted in tradition but is considered uncertain by historians.
- The interpretation of the term "Hinduraya Suratrana" found in inscriptions is debated, with scholars disagreeing on whether it signifies the adoption of Islamic political traditions or simply means "protector among Hindu kings."
