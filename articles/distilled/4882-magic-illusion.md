# Magic (illusion)

## Overview
Magic is a performing art that entertains audiences through tricks, illusions, and seemingly impossible feats achieved by natural means. It is one of the oldest performing arts, distinguished from supernatural magic, and has evolved from fairground entertainment to a sophisticated theatrical form, adapting to new media like television and the internet.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — The "cups and balls" trick, one of the oldest recorded illusions, is first performed.
- **1584** — Reginald Scot publishes *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*, one of the first books to explain magic secrets.
- **1720s** — English showman Isaac Fawkes begins promoting his magic act in advertisements.
- **1845** — Jean Eugène Robert-Houdin opens a magic theatre in Paris, pioneering modern stage magic.
- **1873** — J.N. Maskelyne and Cooke establish their show at the Egyptian Hall in London.
- **1905** — The Magic Circle is formed in London to promote stage magic.
- **1970s** — Uri Geller's televised spoon-bending performances spark controversy over claims of supernatural power.
- **1997** — David Blaine's TV special *Street Magic* popularizes a new style of "guerilla" performance.
- **2008** — Penn Jillette discusses technology's role in magic's future in a TED Talk.
- **2020** — COVID-19 lockdowns cause a surge in online magic shows via video conferencing.

## Body

### History and Evolution
The term "magic" derives from the Greek *mageia*, originally referring to the rituals of Persian priests (*magoi*). For centuries, performers of illusion were associated with the occult. Early books like Scot's *Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584) sought to debunk supernatural claims and reveal trick methods. Until the 18th century, magic was common fairground entertainment. The art gained respectability and moved to theatres in the 19th century, led by pioneers like Jean Eugène Robert-Houdin, who opened a dedicated magic theatre in Paris in 1845. This transition was mirrored in London by John Henry Anderson. The late 19th and early 20th centuries, featuring stars like Harry Houdini and Howard Thurston, are known as the "Golden Age of Magic." The art form later adapted to television and, in the 21st century, to the internet and social media.

### Performance Styles and Techniques
Magic is categorized into various specialties. **Stage illusions** use large props and assistants for big audiences. **Close-up magic** uses everyday items like cards and coins for small groups. **Mentalism** creates the impression of mind-reading or prediction. **Escapology** involves escapes from restraints. Magicians employ techniques including sleight of hand (dexterous manipulation), misdirection (diverting attention), optical illusions, hidden compartments, and psychological suggestion. They traditionally guard their methods closely, sharing secrets only within the profession through formal and informal training.

### Categories of Effects
Magicians classify their tricks into core effect categories, though debate exists about the exact list. Common categories include: **Production** (making something appear), **Vanish** (making something disappear), **Transformation** (changing an object's state), **Restoration** (destroying and restoring an object), **Transposition** (two objects swapping places), **Teleportation** (an object moving instantly), **Escape**, **Levitation** (defying gravity), **Penetration** (solid through solid), and **Prediction**. Most routines combine several of these effects.

### Learning and Secrecy
Learning magic was historically secretive, with professionals guarding methods. Today, knowledge is shared through books, videos, and magic clubs like the International Brotherhood of Magicians and The Magic Circle. A core ethical debate exists: some performers, calling themselves "illusionists," insist on presenting their work as skillful deception, while others embrace the theatrical claim of "magic." This tension extends to the misuse of magic techniques by fraudulent mediums, faith healers, and con artists for personal gain outside performance contexts.

## Terms
- **Sleight of Hand**: Dexterous manipulation of objects to conceal secret actions.
- **Misdirection**: The technique of diverting an audience's attention from the method of a trick.
- **Mentalism**: A genre creating the illusion of psychic or mind-reading abilities.
- **Escapology**: The art of escaping from confinement or restraints.
- **Stage Illusion**: Large-scale magic performed for big audiences, often using assistants and big props.
- **Close-up Magic**: Intimate magic performed with everyday objects for a small audience.
- **Production**: A magic effect where an object appears from seemingly nowhere.
- **Vanish**: A magic effect where an object disappears.
- **Transformation**: A magic effect where an object changes into something else.
- **Penetration**: A magic effect where one solid object passes through another.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Ethical Presentation**: Should performers present their work as supernatural "magic" or as honest "illusion"? This divides the profession.
- **Categorization**: Magicians disagree on the exact number and definitions of core effect categories (e.g., whether "penetration" is a separate category).
- **Secrecy vs. Exposure**: The tradition of guarding methods clashes with modern pressures, as seen in the criticism of magicians like Justin Flom who reveal secrets publicly.
- **Misuse of Techniques**: The application of magic methods for fraud (e.g., by mediums or con artists) remains a persistent ethical and legal issue.
