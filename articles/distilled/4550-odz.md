# Łódź

## Overview
Łódź is a major city in central Poland, historically known as a 19th-century textile manufacturing hub often called the "Polish Manchester." Its identity was shaped by rapid industrialization, a multinational population, and the trauma of the Holocaust, after which it became a temporary seat of government and a renowned center for film and modern art.

## Timeline
- **1332** — First appears in a written record.
- **1423** — Granted town rights by King Władysław II Jagiełło.
- **1793** — Annexed by Prussia in the Second Partition of Poland.
- **1815** — Becomes part of Congress Poland (Russian client state).
- **1820** — Designated for centrally planned industrial development.
- **1850s–1890s** — Experiences explosive growth as a textile manufacturing center.
- **1931** — Muzeum Sztuki founded, Europe's first modern art museum.
- **1939–1945** — Under German occupation; the Łódź Ghetto is established.
- **1945** — Liberated; becomes Poland's temporary capital.
- **1948** — National Film School is opened.
- **1989** — Textile industry collapses, leading to economic decline.
- **2017** — Named a UNESCO City of Film.

## Body

### Early History and Industrialization
Łódź was a small, private settlement owned by Kuyavian bishops until the late 18th century. Following the Second Partition of Poland (1793), it was annexed by Prussia and later became part of Congress Poland under Russian rule. In 1820, the city was designated for planned industrial development, attracting German craftsmen and, later, a massive influx of migrants. The abolition of customs barriers with Russia in 1851 and the development of railways fueled a textile boom. Industrialists like Karl Wilhelm Scheibler and Izrael Poznański built vast factory complexes and palaces, creating a city of stark contrasts between wealth and poverty, as depicted in Władysław Reymont's novel *The Promised Land*.

### 20th Century: War and Transformation
During the interwar period, Łódź became a center for the Polish avant-garde and housed the pioneering Muzeum Sztuki. After the German invasion in 1939, the city was renamed Litzmannstadt and incorporated into Nazi Germany. Its large Jewish population was confined to the Łódź Ghetto, the second-largest in occupied Europe, from which over 200,000 people were systematically sent to extermination camps. Liberated in 1945, Łódź informally served as Poland's capital while Warsaw was rebuilt. Under the communist regime, industry was nationalized, and large panel-block housing estates were constructed.

### Contemporary City
The collapse of the textile industry after 1989 led to severe economic and demographic decline, with the population falling from a peak of ~854,000 in 1988 to become Poland's fourth-largest city. Since the 2010s, revitalization efforts have transformed former industrial sites like the Manufaktura complex into cultural and commercial hubs. Łódź is internationally renowned for its National Film School, which has produced directors like Roman Polański and Andrzej Wajda. The city's central location and transport links have attracted business service and logistics companies.

## Terms
- **Congress Poland:** A client state of the Russian Empire established in 1815, which included Łódź.
- **Textile Industry:** The core of Łódź's 19th-century economy, producing fabrics for export, especially to Russia.
- **Łódź Ghetto (Litzmannstadt Ghetto):** A walled district established by Nazi Germany in 1940 to imprison over 200,000 Jews before their deportation to extermination camps.
- **Muzeum Sztuki:** Founded in 1931, it was the first museum in Europe dedicated to modern art.
- **National Film School:** A prestigious academy opened in 1948, instrumental in shaping Polish cinema.
- **Manufaktura:** A large arts, shopping, and entertainment centre created from a 19th-century cotton factory complex.
- **Piotrkowska Street:** The city's main commercial and cultural artery, one of the longest commercial streets in the world.

## Debates and Open Questions
The origin of the city's name is debated; while *łódź* means "boat," theories also link it to the medieval village of Łodzia, the River Łódka, the willow tree (*łoza*), or the personal name Włodzisław. The city's future development is challenged by a continuing demographic crisis of depopulation and an aging population.
