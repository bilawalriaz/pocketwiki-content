# Guadeloupe

## Overview
Guadeloupe is a French overseas department and region in the Caribbean, consisting of six inhabited islands and numerous islets. As an integral part of France and the European Union, it holds a unique political and economic status, blending Caribbean geography and culture with French administration. Its history is marked by Indigenous settlement, European colonization, slavery, and ongoing social and economic challenges.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — Possible first settlement by Indigenous peoples.
- **c. 1400** — Kalina-Carib peoples displace the earlier Arawak population.
- **1493** — Christopher Columbus visits and names the island.
- **1635** — French colonists formally claim the island.
- **1650** — First African slaves arrive, establishing plantation agriculture.
- **1685** — The Code Noir institutionalizes slavery.
- **1763** — France regains Guadeloupe from Britain in exchange for Canada.
- **1802** — Slavery is re-established after a brief abolition.
- **1848** — Slavery is abolished in the French Empire.
- **1946** — Guadeloupe becomes an overseas department of France.
- **2007** — Saint Barthélemy and Saint Martin are detached as separate overseas collectivities.
- **2009** — A 44-day general strike highlights social and economic grievances.

## Body

### Etymology and Early History
The islands were originally called *Karukera* ("Island of Beautiful Waters") by the Arawak people. Christopher Columbus named them *Santa María de Guadalupe* in 1493 after a Spanish shrine. The French later adapted the name. Archaeological sites like Morel in Le Moule contain pre-Columbian artifacts dating from 400 BC to 1400 AD.

### Colonial Era and Slavery
Spanish colonization attempts failed. The French established control in 1635, leading to the decimation of Indigenous populations through disease and violence. The economy was built on plantation agriculture, first with tobacco and then sugar, relying on enslaved Africans who arrived from 1650. Slave resistance was immediate. The Code Noir (1685) formalized slavery. The islands changed hands between European powers during the 18th century, with Britain capturing them during the Seven Years' War. The French Revolution led to a brief abolition of slavery in 1794, but Napoleon's forces re-established it in 1802 after a fierce rebellion. Slavery was finally abolished in 1848, followed by the importation of indentured laborers from India.

### 20th Century to Present
Guadeloupe became an overseas department in 1946. Post-war tensions over social inequality and its relationship with France led to strikes and riots, such as the 1952 "Massacre of St Valentine." An independence movement emerged in the 1970s, prompting France to grant regional status in 1974 and greater autonomy in 1982. A 2003 referendum led to the separation of Saint Barthélemy and Saint Martin. A major 44-day strike in 2009 protested low wages, high living costs, and unemployment.

### Geography and Climate
Guadeloupe is an archipelago in the Lesser Antilles. The two main islands, Basse-Terre (volcanic and mountainous, home to the active La Grande Soufrière volcano) and Grande-Terre (flat and limestone-based), form a butterfly shape. The climate is tropical with a dry season (January–June) and a wet season (July–December). The islands are vulnerable to hurricanes, with the deadliest being the 1776 Pointe-à-Pitre hurricane.

### Demographics and Governance
The population was estimated at 378,561 in 2024, primarily of Afro-Caribbean descent, with minorities of European, Indian, Lebanese, Syrian, and Chinese origin. The capital is Basse-Terre, while the economic hub is Pointe-à-Pitre. As a French department, it is governed by elected departmental and regional councils and sends representatives to the French National Assembly and Senate. The official language is French, but Guadeloupean Creole is widely spoken.

### Economy and Culture
The economy relies on tourism, agriculture (bananas, sugar, rum), light industry, and public administration, with high unemployment, especially among youth. Culturally, Guadeloupe is a blend of African, European, and Indian influences, evident in its Creole language, music (zouk, gwo ka), cuisine, and traditional dress. The Catholic faith is predominant, but Hinduism and other religions are present.

## Terms
*   **Overseas department and region:** A territorial division of France with the same status as mainland departments, subject to French law and part of the EU.
*   **Code Noir:** A 1685 decree that defined the conditions of slavery in the French colonies.
*   **Creole:** A language that develops from a mixture of different languages; Guadeloupean Creole is French-based.
*   **Béké:** A term for the white Creole population of the French Antilles, historically the planter class.
*   **Zouk:** A popular music genre and dance style originating in the French Antilles in the late 1970s.
*   **Gwo ka:** A traditional Guadeloupean music and dance form with African roots, using percussion.
*   **ZNIEFF:** *Zone naturelle d'intérêt écologique, faunistique et floristique* – a French designation for areas of ecological importance.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The political status of Guadeloupe remains a subject of debate, with discussions ranging from greater autonomy to full independence.
*   The long-term social and economic impacts of the 2009 general strike and the persistent high unemployment rate are ongoing concerns.
*   The environmental legacy of colonial agriculture, including pesticide contamination (e.g., chlordecone), and the sustainability of tourism and water resources are critical challenges.
