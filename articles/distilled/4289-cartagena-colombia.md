# Cartagena, Colombia

## Overview
Cartagena de Indias is a major port city on Colombia's Caribbean coast, founded by Spain in 1533. Its strategic location made it a crucial hub for the Spanish Empire, serving as a primary port for exporting Bolivian silver and importing enslaved Africans. The city's extensive colonial fortifications, which defended it from numerous pirate attacks, are now a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and its history reflects the broader narrative of exploration, colonization, and independence in the Americas.

## Timeline
- **c. 4000 BC** — Earliest human settlement in the region (Puerto Hormiga Culture).
- **1 June 1533** — Founded by Spanish commander Pedro de Heredia.
- **1544** — First major pirate attack by Jean-François Roberval.
- **1586** — Sacked by Sir Francis Drake; Spain commissions a master defense plan.
- **1610** — The Holy Office of the Inquisition is established in the city.
- **1697** — Severe raid by Baron de Pointis and Jean Baptiste Ducasse.
- **1741** — Battle of Cartagena de Indias; Spanish defeat a large British armada.
- **11 November 1811** — Declaration of Independence from Spain.
- **1815-1816** — Siege and recapture by Spanish forces under Pablo Morillo.
- **1821** — Final liberation by patriot forces; Simón Bolívar grants the title "Heroic City."
- **1984** — Colonial walled city and fortress designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Body

### Pre-Columbian and Founding
The area around Cartagena Bay was inhabited from around 4000 BC, with the Puerto Hormiga Culture producing some of the Americas' earliest ceramics. By AD 1500, the region was home to Mocanae-speaking Carib tribes, with the Kalamari settlement of Calamarí being preeminent. Spanish explorer Pedro de Heredia entered the bay in 1533, founded the city on the site of the abandoned Calamarí village, and named it after Cartagena, Spain. His early rule involved extensive raids for gold from indigenous tombs.

### Colonial Era and Defense
Cartagena's importance grew as the main port for exporting silver from Potosí, making it a prime target for pirates and rival European powers. After devastating attacks by figures like Jean-François Roberval (1544) and Francis Drake (1586), Spain commissioned Italian engineer Bautista Antonelli to design a comprehensive fortification system. Construction of walls, forts, and the iconic Castillo San Felipe de Barajas (completed 1654) continued for two centuries, creating one of the most extensive defensive complexes in the Americas. The city also became a major center for the transatlantic slave trade under the Spanish asiento system.

### Independence and Declare
Following Napoleon's invasion of Spain, Cartagena declared independence on 11 November 1811. Spain responded with a "pacifying expedition" under Pablo Morillo, which besieged the city in 1815. A brutal siege led to mass death from starvation and disease, and after its fall, Morillo executed rebel leaders. The city was finally liberated in 1821 by patriot forces under Mariano Montilla, after which Simón Bolívar bestowed the title "Heroic City." The wars left the city depopulated and economically devastated, leading to a long period of decline.

### Modern City
Today, Cartagena is the capital of the Bolívar Department and Colombia's fifth-largest city. Its economy is driven by maritime industries, petrochemicals, and tourism centered on its well-preserved colonial walled city and beaches. The historic center, divided into the neighborhoods of San Sebastián, Santa Catalina, San Diego, and Getsemaní, showcases Spanish colonial architecture. The modern district of Bocagrande is known for its skyscrapers and tourist facilities.

## Terms
- **Asiento System**: The Spanish monopoly contract granting other European nations the license to trade enslaved Africans to its overseas territories.
- **Bocachica / Bocagrande**: The two main entrances ("Small Mouth" and "Big Mouth") to Cartagena Bay.
- **Castillo San Felipe de Barajas**: The largest Spanish fortress in the Americas, completed in 1654, which protected the city from landward attacks.
- **Holy Office of the Inquisition**: Established in Cartagena in 1610 to enforce Catholic orthodoxy, it punished heresy and other crimes until its abolition in 1811.
- **Mocanae**: A sub-family of the Carib language group spoken by indigenous tribes around Cartagena Bay at the time of Spanish contact.
- **Puerto Hormiga Culture**: An early formative culture (c. 4000 BC) in the region, noted for producing some of the oldest ceramics in the Americas.
- **Situado**: A royal subsidy; Cartagena received significant funds from this system between 1751 and 1810.
- **UNESCO World Heritage Site**: A location with cultural or natural significance protected under international treaty; Cartagena's colonial fortifications were designated in 1984.

## Debates and Open questions
- The exact timeline and cultural succession of pre-Columbian societies in the Cartagena Bay area, such as the relationship between the Puerto Hormiga and Monsú cultures, is based on archaeological hypothesis.
- The economic impact of the discontinued *Carrera de Indias* convoys and the Bourbon dynasty's reforms on Cartagena's 18th-century economy is a point of historical analysis.
- The long-term effects of the loss of the *situado* subsidy and the silting of the Canal del Dique on the city's post-independence decline are key factors in its 19th-century stagnation.
