# Helmuth von Moltke the Elder

## Overview
Helmuth von Moltke the Elder (1800–1891) was a Prussian Generalfeldmarschall and Chief of the General Staff for 30 years. He is regarded as the creator of a modern method of directing armies in the field, pioneering the use of railways for mobilization and supply, and developing decentralized command structures. His strategies were decisive in the wars that unified Germany under Prussian leadership.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Moltke was born into a military family and was educated at the Royal Danish Military Academy. He entered the Danish army but transferred to the Prussian Army in 1822, losing seniority. After graduating from the Prussian Military Academy, he served on the general staff and in various roles. In 1835, he traveled to the Ottoman Empire, where he served as an adviser to the Sultan, modernizing the army and conducting extensive surveys. He returned to Prussia in 1839, broken in health but decorated with the Pour le Mérite.

### Chief of the General Staff and Military Theory
Appointed Chief of the Prussian General Staff in 1857, Moltke implemented sweeping reforms. He modernized armaments, communication, staff training, and army mobilization. He pioneered the military use of railways, creating a Railway Section to manage troop movements. Moltke's theory of war, influenced by Carl von Clausewitz, emphasized strategy as a practical art of adapting means to ends. He developed the concept of "mission-type tactics," where commanders were given objectives and allowed initiative, recognizing that detailed control over large armies was impossible. His famous dictum was that "no plan of operations extends with certainty beyond the first encounter with the enemy's main strength."

### Wars of German Unification
Moltke's strategies were tested in three major wars. In the Second Schleswig War (1864), his initial plan was mismanaged, but he later helped direct the final operations. In the Austro-Prussian War (1866), he used railways for rapid mobilization and divided the Prussian army into three groups to achieve superiority at the decisive point. His strategy of maneuvering to cut enemy communications and then fighting defensively led to the decisive victory at the Battle of Königgrätz. In the Franco-Prussian War (1870–71), he planned a concentration of forces south of Mainz. His armies executed a "right wheel" maneuver, defeating French forces in detail at battles like Wörth, Spicheren, and Gravelotte, culminating in the encirclement and surrender of the French army at Sedan and the subsequent Siege of Paris.

### Later Life and Legacy
After the Franco-Prussian War, Moltke was made a Count and Field Marshal. He served in the Reichstag and supervised the official history of the war. He retired as Chief of the General Staff in 1888. His 90th birthday was a national holiday. He died in 1891 and was given a state funeral. He is the earliest-born person whose voice is preserved, via two wax cylinder recordings from 1889.

## Terms
- **Generalfeldmarschall**: The highest military rank in the Prussian and German armies.
- **Chief of the General Staff**: The principal military adviser and operational commander of the army.
- **Mission-type tactics (Auftragstaktik)**: A decentralized command method where subordinates are given a mission objective and allowed to use initiative to achieve it.
- **Offensive–defensive strategy**: A tactic of maneuvering to cut an enemy's lines of communication and then fighting from a defensive position.
- **Railway Section**: A department Moltke added to the military council to manage the strategic use of railways for mobilization and supply.
- **Pour le Mérite**: A prestigious Prussian military order.
- **Graf**: A German noble title equivalent to Count.

## Debates and open questions
- The article notes that Moltke's war plan for the Second Schleswig War was initially mismanaged, leading to the Danish army's escape.
- During the Austro-Prussian War, Moltke was not fully satisfied with the Battle of Königgrätz, as he failed to prevent the Austrian retreat.
- In the Franco-Prussian War, Moltke has been blamed for the costly final attack at the Battle of Gravelotte, though the article states this was ordered by the king.
