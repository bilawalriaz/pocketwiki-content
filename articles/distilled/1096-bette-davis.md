# Bette Davis

## Overview
Ruth Elizabeth "Bette" Davis (1908–1989) was an American actress whose six-decade career established her as one of Hollywood’s greatest stars. Noted for her willingness to play unsympathetic, sardonic characters and her intense, transformative acting style, she won two Academy Awards for Best Actress (*Dangerous*, 1935; *Jezebel*, 1938) and became the first person to earn ten Oscar nominations for acting. After a difficult start at Universal Studios, she flourished at Warner Bros. through the 1930s and 1940s, defining the "woman's picture" genre. A career slump in the late 1940s was reversed by *All About Eve* (1950), often cited as her finest performance. She later pioneered the "psycho-biddy" horror subgenre with *What Ever Happened to Baby Jane?* (1962) and transitioned to acclaimed television work, winning an Emmy in 1979. Davis co-founded the Hollywood Canteen for WWII servicemen, served as the first female president of the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences, and received the AFI Lifetime Achievement Award in 1977. Her personal life was marked by four marriages, estrangement from her daughter B.D. Hyman, and a final decade battling breast cancer and stroke recovery while continuing to act.

## Body

### Early Life and Theater Beginnings
Born in Lowell, Massachusetts, Davis was nicknamed "Betty" until she adopted the spelling "Bette" from Honoré de Balzac’s *La Cousine Bette*. After her parents separated in 1915, she attended boarding school before moving to New York City in 1921. A 1926 performance of Ibsen’s *The Wild Duck* starring Peg Entwistle inspired her to act. Rejected by Eva Le Gallienne’s theater school as "insincere," she joined George Cukor’s stock company in Rochester, earning her first paid role as a chorus girl. She made her Broadway debut in 1929 in *Broken Dishes*, followed by *Solid South*.

### Hollywood Arrival and Warner Bros. Breakthrough
Davis moved to Hollywood in 1930 for a Universal Studios screen test. Humiliated by studio head Carl Laemmle Jr.’s comment that she had "as much sex appeal as Slim Summerville," she languished in six unsuccessful films before her contract lapsed. Actor George Arliss intervened, casting her in Warner Bros.’ *The Man Who Played God* (1932), leading to an 18-year tenure at the studio. Her critical breakthrough came on loan to RKO as the "vicious and slatternly" Mildred Rogers in *Of Human Bondage* (1934). Though widely acclaimed—*Life* called it "probably the best performance ever recorded on the screen by a U.S. actress"—she was omitted from the Oscar ballot, prompting a write-in campaign that forced the Academy to reform its voting procedures. She won her first Oscar the next year for *Dangerous* (1935), which she called a "consolation prize" for the prior snub.

### Contract Battle and Peak Stardom
In 1936, frustrated by poor role assignments, Davis refused to work, demanding a salary raise and the right to make outside films. She fled to England to sign with Toplitz Films, but Warner Bros. won a British injunction; Justice Branson ruled she was in breach "for no discoverable reason except that she wanted more money," ordering her to pay $80,000 restitution. Paradoxically, the lawsuit marked the start of her peak. She won the Volpi Cup at Venice for *Marked Woman* (1937) and a second Oscar for *Jezebel* (1938), launching five consecutive Best Actress nominations (*Dark Victory*, *The Letter*, *The Little Foxes*, *Now, Voyager*). She became Warner’s top moneymaker, shaving her hairline for *The Private Lives of Elizabeth and Essex* (1939) and clashing with directors like William Wyler over character interpretation. Her 1941 presidency of the Academy ended in resignation after two months due to "brash manner and radical proposals."

### War Years and the Hollywood Canteen
Davis channeled her energy into the war effort, co-founding the Hollywood Canteen in 1942 with John Garfield. She served as president throughout WWII, providing food and entertainment to servicemen, later earning the Distinguished Civilian Service Medal (1983). Her film output included the romantic drama *Now, Voyager* (1942)—featuring the iconic two-cigarette lighting scene—and the comedy *The Man Who Came to Dinner* (1942). Personal tragedy struck in 1943 when her second husband, Arthur Farnsworth, died from a delayed skull fracture. Her grief manifested as erratic behavior on *Mr. Skeffington* (1944), though the film was a hit and earned another nomination.

### Post-War Decline and *All About Eve* Resurgence
After the war, Davis’s career faltered. She formed BD Productions for *A Stolen Life* (1946), her only self-produced film, and clashed with Warner over *Beyond the Forest* (1949), a critical disaster that prompted her release from the studio. Freelancing, she accepted the role of Margo Channing in *All About Eve* (1950), calling the script "the best she had ever read." Her performance—"syllable-perfect," per director Joseph L. Mankiewicz—won the Cannes Best Actress Award and an Oscar nomination, reviving her status. She married co-star Gary Merrill (her fourth husband) and adopted two children, though the marriage deteriorated amid alcohol abuse and domestic violence.

### Horror, Television, and Late-Career Honors
The 1950s brought few successes until *What Ever Happened to Baby Jane?* (1962), a Grand Guignol horror film co-starring Joan Crawford. Davis negotiated 10% of gross profits and delivered a psychotic performance as Jane Hudson, earning her tenth Oscar nomination and a BAFTA nod. The production was marred by on-set physical altercations with Crawford. Davis then pivoted to television, winning an Emmy for *Strangers: The Story of a Mother and Daughter* (1979) and earning nominations for *White Mama* (1980) and *Little Gloria... Happy at Last* (1982). In 1977, she became the first woman to receive the AFI Lifetime Achievement Award. Kim Carnes’s hit "Bette Davis Eyes" (1981) introduced her to a new generation.

### Illness, Memoir War, and Final Years
In 1983, a mastectomy for breast cancer triggered four strokes, paralyzing her left side and slurring her speech. She recovered partially through rigorous therapy but smoked 100 cigarettes daily until her death. In 1985, her daughter B.D. Hyman published *My Mother's Keeper*, depicting Davis as overbearing and alcoholic. Davis disinherited Hyman, supported by ex-husband Gary Merrill and son Michael Merrill. In her 1987 memoir *This 'n That*, Davis wrote, "I will never recover as completely from B.D.'s book as I have from the stroke." Her final screen role was *The Whales of August* (1987), playing a blind sister to Lillian Gish. She received the Kennedy Center Honors, France’s Legion of Honor, and Italy’s Campione d’Italia before dying in France in 1989. Her epitaph, suggested by Mankiewicz: "She did it the hard way."

## Terms
- **Pre-Code**: Era in Hollywood (1930–1934) before strict enforcement of the Motion Picture Production Code, allowing more risqué themes; *Ex-Lady* (1933) is an example.
- **Woman's Picture**: Industry term for films centered on female protagonists and emotional/romantic struggles, a genre Davis dominated in the 1930s–40s (e.g., *Now, Voyager*).
- **Hollywood Canteen**: WWII nightclub founded by Davis and John Garfield (1942) offering free food, dancing, and entertainment to servicemen, staffed by industry volunteers.
- **Write-in Vote (Oscars)**: Unique 1935 Academy allowance permitting voters to write names not on the official ballot; Davis placed third for *Of Human Bondage*, prompting rule changes.
- **Volpi Cup**: Best Actress award at the Venice Film Festival; Davis won in 1937 for *Marked Woman*.
- **Psycho-biddy / Grande Dame Guignol**: Subgenre of horror/thriller featuring aging, mentally unstable former stars; launched by *What Ever Happened to Baby Jane?* (1962).
- **AFI Life Achievement Award**: Annual honor from the American Film Institute; Davis was the first female recipient (1977).
- **Community Property Laws (1930s)**: Legal framework giving husbands control over wives' earnings; cited in Davis’s first marriage disputes with Harmon Nelson.
- **Grand Guignol**: Style of graphic, sensational horror theater; applied to *Baby Jane* for its shocking violence and psychological terror.
- **Epitaph**: "She did it the hard way," suggested by Joseph L. Mankiewicz after *All About Eve*, inscribed on Davis’s tomb at Forest Lawn.

## Debates and open questions
- **Write-in Oscar legitimacy**: The Academy’s 1935 acceptance of write-in votes for Davis (*Of Human Bondage*) remains a unique anomaly; she placed third, but the Academy does not count it as an official nomination, creating debate over whether she had 10 or 11 nominations.
- **Davis vs. Crawford feud**: The rivalry is documented, but its intensity is debated—Davis and Crawford spoke highly of each other’s talent publicly, yet engaged in physical altercations on *Baby Jane*; Crawford’s acceptance of Anne Bancroft’s Oscar (1963) is widely viewed as a deliberate slight.
- **B.D. Hyman’s memoir accuracy**: *My Mother's Keeper* (1985) depicted Davis as abusive and alcoholic; Davis, Merrill, and friends called it "out of context" and motivated by greed, while Hyman’s earlier *60 Minutes* interview had praised Davis’s parenting.
- **Acting style: mannerism vs. authenticity**: Critics polarized on whether Davis’s "clipped vocal style," "popping eyes," and cigarette choreography constituted brilliant characterization or self-caricature; Pauline Kael called her a "caricature of a harpy," while others praised her "shameless showing off" as compulsively watchable.
- **Scarlett O'Hara casting**: Davis actively pursued the *Gone with the Wind* role; Warner offered her in a package deal, but David O. Selznick rejected her as unsuitable. Whether she would have accepted the part opposite Errol Flynn (whom she opposed as Rhett Butler) is speculative.
- **Television vs. film legacy**: Davis’s Emmy-winning TV work (*Strangers*, *White Mama*) is often overshadowed by her film peak; scholars debate whether her small-screen roles extended her artistic range or merely sustained visibility during declining health.
