# Toyotomi Hideyoshi

## Overview
Toyotomi Hideyoshi (1537–1598) was a Japanese samurai and daimyō who rose from peasant origins to become the second "Great Unifier" of Japan. He completed the unification of the country after the death of his lord, Oda Nobunaga, and established a rigid social class system that shaped Japanese society for centuries. His legacy includes major castles, the confiscation of weapons from peasants, and the failed invasions of Korea, which weakened his regime and paved the way for the Tokugawa Shogunate.

## Timeline
- **1537** — Born in Nakamura, Owari Province to a peasant family.
- **1558** — Becomes an ashigaru (foot soldier) for the Oda clan under Oda Nobunaga.
- **1582** — After Nobunaga's assassination, defeats his killer at the Battle of Yamazaki and becomes his successor.
- **1585** — Appointed Imperial Regent (kampaku) and given the clan name Toyotomi.
- **1585** — Conquers Shikoku island.
- **1587** — Conquers Kyūshū island.
- **1588** — Launches a "sword hunt," confiscating weapons from peasants.
- **1590** — Completes Japan's unification by defeating the Hōjō clan at the Siege of Odawara.
- **1592** — Launches the first Japanese invasion of Korea.
- **1595** — Orders his nephew and heir, Toyotomi Hidetsugu, to commit suicide.
- **1598** — Dies at Fushimi Castle, leaving his young son Hideyori as successor.

## Body

### Rise to Power
Hideyoshi began his career as a low-ranking retainer for Oda Nobunaga, distinguishing himself through negotiation and military skill. He rose to become one of Nobunaga's most trusted generals. After Nobunaga was killed in the Honnō-ji Incident in 1582, Hideyoshi avenged him by defeating the assassin, Akechi Mitsuhide, at the Battle of Yamazaki. He then outmaneuvered other Oda retainers, notably defeating Shibata Katsuie at the Battle of Shizugatake in 1583. A subsequent stalemate with Tokugawa Ieyasu led to a political settlement where Ieyasu submitted to Hideyoshi's authority.

### Unification and Rule
To legitimize his power without becoming shōgun, Hideyoshi secured the highest court titles, including Imperial Regent (kampaku). He systematically unified Japan by conquering Shikoku (1585), Kyūshū (1587), and the Kantō region (1590). His rule was marked by major social reforms: a "sword hunt" disarmed peasants, and laws fixed samurai in castle towns, creating a rigid class structure. He also conducted nationwide land surveys for taxation. His construction of Osaka Castle and lavish cultural patronage, such as the tea ceremony, displayed his immense power.

### Korean Invasions and Death
Hideyoshi launched two large-scale invasions of Korea (1592 and 1597) with the ultimate goal of conquering Ming China. The first campaign saw initial success but was halted by Korean naval victories and Chinese intervention. The second campaign was a costly stalemate. These wars drained resources and prestige. Hideyoshi died in 1598, leaving his young son Hideyori under the care of a council of regents. This unstable succession allowed Tokugawa Ieyasu to seize power at the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600, ending the Toyotomi clan's dominance.

## Terms
- **Daimyō**: A powerful feudal lord in pre-modern Japan.
- **Ashigaru**: A low-ranking foot soldier, often a peasant conscripted for service.
- **Kampaku**: The Imperial Regent, the highest official position in the Japanese nobility.
- **Taikō**: The title for a retired Kampaku, which Hideyoshi used after stepping down.
- **Sengoku period**: A century-long era of civil war and social upheaval in Japan (c. 1467–1615).
- **Sword hunt**: Hideyoshi's 1588 decree confiscating weapons from peasants to prevent revolts.
- **Hachidokuniwari**: The "Eight Route Division" strategy for the invasion of Korea.
- **Council of Five Elders**: The group of powerful daimyō appointed by Hideyoshi to protect his heir.

## Debates and open questions
- The primary reason for the Toyotomi regime's downfall is debated, with Hideyoshi's loss of all adult heirs, leaving only a child, being a key factor.
- The exact motivations and strategic goals of the Korean invasions, particularly whether the conquest of China was a realistic objective, remain subjects of historical analysis.
- The extent to which Hideyoshi's personal inferiority complex, stemming from his peasant background, influenced his later policies and behavior is discussed by historians.
