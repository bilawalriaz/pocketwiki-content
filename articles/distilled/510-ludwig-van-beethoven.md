# Ludwig van Beethoven

## Overview
Ludwig van Beethoven (baptised 17 December 1770 – 26 March 1827) was a German composer, conductor, and pianist whose work bridged the Classical and Romantic eras. Born in Bonn, he moved to Vienna in 1792 to study with Joseph Haydn, quickly establishing himself as a virtuoso pianist and composer. His career is conventionally divided into three periods: an early Classical phase; a middle "heroic" period (c. 1802–1812) marked by expanded forms and emotional intensity; and a late period (1812–1827) characterized by profound intellectual depth, formal innovation, and the integration of fugue and choral forces. Despite progressive hearing loss beginning c. 1798—which ended his performing career and caused severe social isolation—he continued to compose masterpieces, including nine symphonies, 32 piano sonatas, 16 string quartets, the opera *Fidelio*, and the *Missa solemnis*. His Ninth Symphony (1824) was the first major choral symphony. He died in Vienna at age 56; his funeral drew an estimated 10,000 mourners.

## Body

### Early Life and Education (Bonn, 1770–1792)
Beethoven was born into a musical family in Bonn; his grandfather Ludwig was Kapellmeister at the Electoral court. His father Johann, a tenor, subjected him to harsh, intensive musical training from age five, hoping to promote him as a child prodigy akin to Mozart. Beethoven’s general education was neglected—he learned addition but never multiplication or division. In 1780–81, court organist Christian Gottlob Neefe became his most important teacher, instructing him in composition and J.S. Bach’s *Well-Tempered Clavier*. Neefe secured Beethoven a paid position as assistant court organist (1784). A crucial emotional refuge was the von Breuning family; Helene von Breuning acted as a "second mother," fostering his literary interests. Count Ferdinand von Waldstein became a patron, commissioning a ballet (WoO 1) and later facilitating Beethoven’s move to Vienna. After his mother’s death (1787) and his father’s descent into alcoholism (pension partially redirected to Ludwig in 1789), Beethoven played viola in the court orchestra, gaining practical experience with opera repertoire. He met Haydn in 1790 and 1792; Waldstein wrote prophetically that Beethoven would "receive Mozart's spirit from Haydn's hands."

### Vienna: Early Years and First Period (1792–1802)
Arriving in Vienna in November 1792, Beethoven studied counterpoint with Haydn, then Johann Albrechtsberger after Haydn’s departure for England. He also studied Italian vocal style with Antonio Salieri. He withheld works from publication to maximize impact, debuting publicly in 1795 with his own piano concertos. The Piano Trios Op. 1 (1795), dedicated to Prince Lichnowsky, were a financial success. He gained renown as a pianist, winning duels against Joseph Wölfl (1799) and Daniel Steibelt (1800). The *Pathétique* Sonata (Op. 13, 1799) marked a new emotional depth. He composed his first six string quartets (Op. 18, 1798–1800) and the Septet (Op. 20). The First Symphony premiered in 1800, the Second in 1803. By 1800, his music was in high demand. He taught piano to aristocratic pupils, including Josephine Brunsvik (whom he loved) and Carl Czerny. He dedicated the *Moonlight* Sonata (Op. 27 No. 2, 1802) to Julie Guicciardi. In 1802, his brother Kaspar began managing his business affairs, negotiating higher fees and pushing for arrangements of popular works.

### The "Heroic" Middle Period (1802–1812)
**Deafness:** Beethoven reported hearing loss beginning in 1798, accompanied by severe tinnitus. The cause was likely otosclerosis with auditory nerve degeneration. In April–October 1802, at Heiligenstadt, he wrote the unsent *Heiligenstadt Testament*, expressing suicidal despair but resolving to live for his art. He never became totally deaf; low tones and sudden loud sounds remained audible until the end.

**Heroic Style:** Returning from Heiligenstadt, Beethoven declared a "new way." The *Eroica* Symphony (No. 3, 1803–04), originally titled "Bonaparte," was unprecedented in scale and scope; he erased the dedication when Napoleon crowned himself Emperor. The Razumovsky quartets (Op. 59, 1806), *Waldstein* (Op. 53) and *Appassionata* (Op. 57) sonatas, Violin Concerto (1806), and Fifth Symphony (1808) share this expansive, dramatic language. E.T.A. Hoffmann (1810) hailed him as the supreme Romantic composer.

**Patronage and *Fidelio*:** Archduke Rudolf became his most important patron (14 dedications, including the *Archduke* Trio Op. 97). *Fidelio* (originally *Leonore*) premiered in 1805 to empty houses due to French occupation; it failed critically and financially. Beethoven revised it twice. A massive 1808 benefit concert premiered the Fifth and Sixth Symphonies but was under-rehearsed. In 1809, facing an offer from Jérôme Bonaparte, Rudolf, Kinsky, and Lobkowitz pledged a 4000-florin pension; only Rudolf paid reliably. The French bombardment of Vienna (1809) disrupted output.

**Goethe and the Immortal Beloved:** Commissioned for *Egmont* (1810), Beethoven met Goethe at Teplitz in 1812; mutual respect was tinged with personality clashes. At Teplitz in 1812, he wrote the unsent "Immortal Beloved" letter. Musicologist Maynard Solomon argues the recipient was Antonie Brentano; other candidates include Josephine Brunsvik and Julie Guicciardi. After 1812, no romantic liaisons are documented.

### Acclaim, Crisis, and Late Period (1813–1827)
**Family and Health Crises (1813–1819):** Beethoven’s output dropped sharply after 1812. He intervened in his brother Johann’s relationship (Johann married Therese Obermayer in 1812). His brother Kaspar died in 1815, triggering a bitter, years-long custody battle over nephew Karl against Kaspar’s widow Johanna. Beethoven won sole guardianship in 1816, lost it in 1818 (failing to prove noble birth), and regained it in 1820. His treatment of Karl was overbearing. A lengthy "inflammatory fever" (1816–17) and legal battles caused a compositional pause. He studied Bach, Handel, and Palestrina, renewed ties with Maelzel (endorsing the metronome), and composed the song cycle *An die ferne Geliebte* (Op. 98, 1816) and the *Hammerklavier* Sonata (Op. 106, 1818)—"Now I know how to write music." Conversation books became necessary for communication.

**Resurgence and Major Late Works (1819–1823):** Archduke Rudolf’s elevation to Archbishop (1819) prompted the *Missa solemnis* (Op. 123). Diabelli’s variation project sparked the 33 *Diabelli Variations* (Op. 120). Beethoven negotiated with publishers (Schlesinger, Peters, Schott) and accepted commissions from the London Philharmonic Society (Ninth Symphony) and Prince Galitzin (three string quartets). He completed the *Missa solemnis*, Ninth Symphony, and *Diabelli Variations* in 1823. The *Missa* was presented to Rudolf in March 1823 (inscribed "From the heart – may it return to the heart!").

**Final Years (1824–1827):** The Ninth Symphony premiered 7 May 1824 at the Kärntnertortheater; Beethoven beat time alongside conductor Michael Umlauf. The contralto Caroline Unger turned him to see the applause he could not hear. He completed the late quartets for Galitzin (Op. 127, 130, 131, 132, 135, and the *Große Fuge* Op. 133). Op. 131 (seven linked movements) he rated his "most perfect single work." Op. 135’s finale bears the motto "Muss es sein? – Es muss sein!" (Must it be? – It must be!). In August 1826, Karl attempted suicide; he recuperated with Beethoven and Johann at Gneixendorf. Beethoven completed Op. 135 and the replacement finale for Op. 130 there. Returning to Vienna in December 1826, he fell ill (dropsy, jaundice, cirrhosis). Multiple abdominal paracenteses were performed. He died 26 March 1827; Anselm Hüttenbrenner reported a thunderclap at the moment of death. Autopsy revealed severe liver damage and dilated auditory nerves. Funeral (29 March) drew ~10,000; he was buried in Währing, later moved to Vienna’s Zentralfriedhof (1888).

### Musical Style and the Three-Period Framework
The three-period division (early, middle, late), proposed as early as 1818, is standard but imperfect: it omits the Bonn years and obscures differential development across genres (piano sonatas show continuous progression; quartets fit the three-period model neatly). **Early (1792–c.1802):** Mastery of the "Viennese style" (Haydn/Mozart); expanded scale (four-movement sonatas, scherzi replacing minuets, extreme dynamics, chromatic harmony). Key works: Symphonies 1–2, Op. 18 quartets, first 20 piano sonatas (*Pathétique*). **Middle (c.1802–1812):** "Heroic" style—large-scale works expressing struggle (Symphonies 3–8, *Fidelio*, *Waldstein*, *Appassionata*, Violin Concerto, Razumovsky quartets). The term "heroic" is debated; works like the *Pastoral* Symphony (No. 6) do not fit. **Late (c.1810–1827):** Renewed study of early music (Gregorian chant, Palestrina, Bach, Handel). Integration of fugue, church modes, Baroque devices. Intellectual depth, formal innovation, personal expression. Key works: last five piano sonatas, *Diabelli Variations*, late cello sonatas, late quartets (*Große Fuge*), *Missa solemnis*, Ninth Symphony (choral finale). Schonberg: "a language compressed, cryptic and explosive, expressed in forms of his own devising."

### Instruments
Beethoven favored pianos by J.A. Stein, then Nannette Streicher (Stein’s daughter). He received an Érard (1803)—its resonance may have influenced the *Waldstein* Sonata—but abandoned it by 1810. A Broadwood grand (1818) disappointed him tonally; he sought modifications for volume. A Graf piano (1825) with quadruple strings and a resonator failed to overcome his deafness.

## Terms
- **Kapellmeister**: Music director of a court or chapel; Beethoven’s grandfather held this post in Bonn.
- **Opus number (Op.)**: A chronological catalog number assigned by a composer to a published work; Beethoven first used them for the Piano Trios Op. 1 (1795).
- **WoO (Werke ohne Opuszahl)**: "Works without opus number"; a catalog for Beethoven’s unpublished or early works (e.g., WoO 63, first publication).
- **Heiligenstadt Testament**: An unsent 1802 letter to his brothers expressing despair over deafness and resolve to live for art; discovered posthumously.
- **Conversation books**: Notebooks used from 1818 onward for Beethoven to communicate with visitors via writing; vital primary sources for his late life.
- **Song cycle**: A group of songs designed to be performed in sequence as a single entity; Beethoven’s *An die ferne Geliebte* (Op. 98, 1816) is the first major example.
- **Choral symphony**: A symphony incorporating vocal soloists and chorus; Beethoven’s Ninth (1824) was the first major instance.
- **Große Fuge (Great Fugue)**: Op. 133, originally the finale of String Quartet Op. 130; published separately as a massive, complex double fugue.
- **Missa solemnis**: Op. 123, a grand, concert-mass setting of the Latin Ordinary composed 1819–1823 for Archduke Rudolf’s installation.
- **Diabelli Variations**: Op. 120, 33 variations on a waltz by Anton Diabelli (1819–1823), considered a summit of variation form.

## Debates and open questions
- **Periodization**: The three-period model is conventional but criticized for omitting the Bonn years and imposing false boundaries on genres that evolved continuously (especially piano sonatas).
- **"Heroic" label**: Scholars debate whether "heroic" accurately describes the entire middle period; works like the *Pastoral* Symphony and Op. 78 Sonata lack heroic rhetoric.
- **Immortal Beloved identity**: The recipient of the 1812 letter remains unproven; leading candidates are Antonie Brentano (Solomon), Josephine Brunsvik, Julie Guicciardi, Therese Malfatti, and Anna Maria Erdődy.
- **Cause of deafness**: Likely otosclerosis with auditory nerve degeneration, but not definitively proven; lead poisoning and syphilis have been hypothesized but lack consensus.
- **Cause of death**: Cirrhosis/liver failure is primary (autopsy: severe liver damage); contributing factors include heavy alcohol use, lead exposure (recent hair analysis), and possible infectious hepatitis.
- **Karl’s guardianship**: Beethoven’s legal battle and overbearing treatment of his nephew Karl are viewed variously as noble devotion or destructive obsession; Karl’s 1826 suicide attempt underscores the tragedy.
- **Late style interpretation**: Whether the late works reflect spiritual transcendence, compositional necessity driven by deafness, or a deliberate "late style" project remains a central musicological question.
