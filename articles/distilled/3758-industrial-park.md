# Industrial park

## Overview
An industrial park is a zoned and planned area designed for industrial development, serving as a heavyweight counterpart to business or office parks. It functions as a strategic tool to harness economic agglomeration—the benefits firms gain from geographic concentration—to drive efficiency, innovation, and urban development. These parks are particularly vital in developing countries as instruments of industrial policy to attract investment, create jobs, and integrate into global markets.

## Timeline
- **Late 19th century** — Alfred Marshall develops the concept of industrial districts, emphasizing external economies from geographic concentration.
- **1990** — Michael Porter publishes *The Competitive Advantage of Nations*, introducing his cluster theory and the "diamond model."
- **1991** — Paul Krugman's work on new economic geography formalizes the concept of agglomeration.
- **Modern era** — Industrial parks become widely applied as industrial policy instruments in developing countries.

## Body

### Theoretical Foundations
Industrial parks are grounded in economic theories of agglomeration, industrial organization, and spatial economics. Alfred Marshall's late 19th-century notion of industrial districts highlighted how geographic concentration creates external economies: labor market pooling for specialized skills, access to specialized suppliers, and knowledge transfer through proximity. Michael Porter's cluster theory builds on this, focusing on how affiliated companies, suppliers, and institutions within a space can boost productivity and innovation through competition and cooperation. His "diamond model" combines firm strategy, demand conditions, and factor conditions to argue that agglomerations provide a global competitive advantage. New economic geography, exemplified by Paul Krugman, models agglomeration as a balance between inward forces (economies of scale, knowledge flows) and outward forces (congestion, land costs), with industrial parks formalizing this balance.

### Purpose and Benefits
Industrial parks are typically located on city outskirts with strong transportation links (road, rail, port). Their primary purpose is to concentrate dedicated infrastructure—such as high-power electricity, large-volume water supplies, and advanced communications—to reduce per-business costs. This integrated infrastructure attracts new businesses and allows for localized environmental controls, separating industrial activity from urban areas to mitigate social and environmental impacts. In developing countries, parks are key policy tools to attract foreign direct investment (FDI), promote export-led industrialization, and bridge institutional and infrastructural gaps.

### Benchmarking and Criticism
The performance of industrial parks is often benchmarked against criteria including investment, environmental protection, social responsibility, and governance (ESG), as operator costs directly affect tenant competitiveness. However, parks vary widely in quality. Many small communities establish parks with only basic utilities and highway access, lacking public transportation. In developing countries like India, challenges include a shortage of skilled labor and the clustering of incompatible industries (e.g., pharmaceuticals with heavy engineering), which can harm quality-focused sectors.

### Variations
Specialized parks exist, such as biotechnology industrial parks (or eco-industrial clusters). In land-scarce cities like Singapore and Hong Kong, "flatted factories" provide multi-level industrial space with cargo lifts and road access per floor. In Geneva, Switzerland, industrial areas are being transformed into mixed-use zones with ground-floor services for workers due to limited land reserves.

## Terms
- **Agglomeration**: The concentration of firms and people in a specific location, leading to economic benefits.
- **Cluster**: A geographic concentration of interconnected companies, suppliers, and institutions in a particular field (Michael Porter).
- **Diamond Model**: Porter's framework linking firm strategy, demand conditions, factor conditions, and related industries to explain competitive advantage.
- **External Economies**: Benefits that firms enjoy from being located near each other, such as shared labor pools and knowledge spillovers.
- **Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)**: Investment made by a company or individual in one country into business interests in another country.
- **Industrial District**: Marshall's concept of a geographic concentration of specialized small firms benefiting from external economies.
- **New Economic Geography**: A field of economics that models the spatial distribution of economic activity, including agglomeration forces (e.g., Paul Krugman).
- **Zoning**: The legal regulation of land use, designating specific areas for industrial, residential, or commercial purposes.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source material highlights ongoing scholarly debate about the optimal balance between organic cluster emergence and planned growth in industrial park design. It also notes that the effectiveness of parks as policy instruments depends critically on complementary secondary policies regarding sustainability, governance, and design, suggesting an open question about the specific conditions that make them successful.
