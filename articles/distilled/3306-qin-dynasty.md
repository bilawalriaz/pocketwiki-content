# Qin dynasty

## Overview
The Qin dynasty (221–206 BC) was the first imperial dynasty of China, unifying the warring states into a centralized empire. Though it lasted only 14 years, its administrative, legal, and cultural reforms laid the foundation for the Chinese imperial system that endured until 1911. Its legacy is defined by both its transformative innovations and its reputation for harsh, legalist rule.

## Timeline
- **c. 9th century BC** — Feizi granted rule over Qin settlement; state of Qin established.
- **770 BC** — Duke Xiang of Qin escorts Zhou court east, establishing Qin as a major vassal state.
- **359–338 BC** — Shang Yang implements major administrative and military reforms in Qin.
- **230 BC** — Qin begins wars of unification, conquering Han.
- **221 BC** — King Zheng completes unification, declares himself Qin Shi Huang (First Emperor).
- **214–210 BC** — Southern expansion against Baiyue peoples; northern campaign against Xiongnu.
- **210 BC** — Death of Qin Shi Huang; succession crisis begins.
- **209 BC** — Popular revolts break out across the empire.
- **207 BC** — Ziying, last Qin ruler, surrenders to rebel leader Liu Bang.
- **206 BC** — Xianyang destroyed; end of Qin dynasty.
- **202 BC** — Liu Bang founds Han dynasty after Chu–Han Contention.

## Body

### Rise and Unification
The state of Qin originated as a minor Zhou fief in the 9th century BC. Its power grew significantly in the 4th century BC due to reforms by the statesman Shang Yang, who created a strong, centralized state with a large army supported by a stable economy. Qin's core territory in the Guanzhong region provided fertile farmland and a strategic, mountain-protected stronghold. During the Warring States period, Qin developed a superior military that employed ruthless tactics, contrasting with the more ceremonial warfare of other states. After internal setbacks, an expansionist policy under Prime Minister Fan Sui (from 266 BC) led to the conquest of the six rival states between 230 and 221 BC.

### Imperial Rule and Reforms
Following unification in 221 BC, Emperor Qin Shi Huang established a highly centralized, bureaucratic government. The empire was divided into 36 commanderies administered by officials, ending aristocratic rule. A vast array of standardization reforms were implemented, including currency (the banliang coin), weights, measures, and the writing system (consolidated into small seal script). The state directly administered the peasant majority, enabling massive construction projects like the Great Wall, a national road system, and the emperor's mausoleum guarded by the Terracotta Army. The Qin also pursued southward expansion into Baiyue territories and a northern campaign against the Xiongnu nomads.

### Collapse
Qin Shi Huang's death in 210 BC triggered a power struggle. Chief eunuch Zhao Gao and Prime Minister Li Si manipulated the succession, placing the inept Qin Er Shi on the throne. Their internal conflicts and the emperor's misrule—including heavy taxes, forced labor, and suppression of dissent—sparked widespread popular revolts. After Zhao Gao forced Qin Er Shi to commit suicide, the last ruler, Ziying, surrendered to rebel forces in 207 BC. The dynasty's collapse led to the Chu–Han Contention, from which Liu Bang emerged victorious to found the Han dynasty.

### Society and Economy
Over 90% of the population were peasants engaged in cereal agriculture (millet, wheat, barley). The state promoted maximizing cultivated land and used a household registration system for direct administration. Merchants were viewed with suspicion and legally restricted. Qin law, while administrative and focused on theft, included harsh punishments like hard labor for public works, though evidence suggests some use of pardons and fines. The state's capacity to mobilize labor and transform the environment is noted as a key event in East Asian environmental history.

## Terms
- **Legalism**: A philosophy of government emphasizing strict laws, centralized power, and harsh punishments, which heavily influenced Qin rule.
- **Warring States period**: The era (c. 475–221 BC) of intense conflict among seven major states preceding Qin unification.
- **Mandate of Heaven**: The Zhou-era concept that a ruler's authority was granted by divine approval, a claim Qin rulers did not initially make.
- **Commandery**: A top-level administrative division of the Qin empire, replacing the old feudal states.
- **Small seal script**: The standardized writing system consolidated under Qin Shi Huang to unify the empire's script.
- **Banliang**: The standardized round bronze coin introduced by Qin to replace regional currencies.
- **Corvée**: Unpaid labor owed to the state, often used for large construction projects.
- **Censorate**: A supervisory system introduced to monitor and check the powers of government officials.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The veracity of the "burning of books and burying of scholars" events (213–212 BC) is disputed by some modern scholars, as the earliest account is from the Shiji (c. 91 BC).
- The extent of standardization under Qin Shi Huang is debated; while regional script variations were reduced, variant characters remained frequent among scribes, suggesting the idea of a strict, uniform standard may be a later Han-era notion.
- The etymology of "China" from "Qin" is questioned by scholars who note the Sanskrit term "Sina" appears centuries before the Qin dynasty's founding.
