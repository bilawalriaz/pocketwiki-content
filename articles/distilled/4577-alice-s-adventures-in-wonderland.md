# Alice's Adventures in Wonderland

## Overview
*Alice's Adventures in Wonderland* is an 1865 English children's novel by Lewis Carroll, telling the story of a girl named Alice who falls into a fantasy world of anthropomorphic creatures. It is a seminal work of literary nonsense that helped end the era of didactic (moralistic) children's literature, inaugurating an age focused on delight and entertainment. Its narrative, characters, and imagery have had a profound and lasting influence on popular culture and literature worldwide.

## Timeline
- **4 July 1862** — Carroll first tells the story to Alice Liddell and her sisters during a boat trip.
- **1863** — Carroll begins planning a print edition and meets publisher Alexander Macmillan.
- **26 November 1864** — Carroll gives Alice Liddell the handwritten manuscript, *Alice's Adventures Under Ground*.
- **1865** — First published edition (Macmillan) is printed but withdrawn due to Tenniel's dissatisfaction with quality.
- **November 1865** — The successful reprint, printed by Richard Clay, is published.
- **1871** — Sequel *Through the Looking-Glass* is published.
- **1886** — Facsimile of Carroll's original manuscript is published.
- **1890** — Abridged version for young children, *The Nursery "Alice"*, is published.
- **1903** — First screen adaptation, a British silent film, is released.
- **1907** — Copyright expires in the UK, entering the public domain.
- **1928** — Carroll's original manuscript is sold at auction for a world record price.
- **1960** — Martin Gardner publishes the influential *The Annotated Alice*.

## Body

### Origin and Manuscript
The story was conceived on 4 July 1862 during a river trip with the Liddell sisters. Alice Liddell asked Carroll to write it down, and he produced a manuscript titled *Alice's Adventures Under Ground*, which he presented to her in 1864. For publication, Carroll expanded the text significantly and commissioned John Tenniel to create the definitive illustrations. The first print run in 1865 was halted and reprinted in 1865 due to Tenniel's objections to the printing quality.

### Plot Summary
Bored on a riverbank, Alice follows a White Rabbit down a rabbit hole into Wonderland. Her journey is marked by constant changes in size after consuming food and drink, leading to an identity crisis. She encounters a series of nonsensical characters and situations: a Caucus Race, a hookah-smoking Caterpillar, a grinning Cheshire Cat, and a chaotic Mad Hatter's Tea Party. Eventually, she reaches the garden of the Queen of Hearts, a tyrant who constantly orders beheadings. After a nonsensical trial, Alice scoffs at the authority of the playing-card court, causing them to attack her, and she awakens from her dream.

### Characters and Allusions
The characters are often interpreted as caricatures of real people from Carroll's life. The Dodo is thought to represent Carroll himself (a play on his stammered name, "Dodo-Dodgson"). The Lory and Eaglet represent Alice's sisters, Lorina and Edith. The Hatter may be based on Theophilus Carter, an Oxford furniture dealer. The Dormouse's story of three sisters—Elsie, Lacie, and Tillie—is an anagrammatic reference to the Liddell sisters.

### Themes and Style
The novel is a prime example of **literary nonsense**, embracing paradox and existential questions without resolution. It is rich in linguistic play, puns, and parodies of contemporary poems. Central themes include the subversion of logic and rules, with Wonderland operating on its own arbitrary, game-like structures. Eating and drinking are constant motifs, driving the plot through Alice's size changes and placing her in a shifting position within the "eat or be eaten" food chain. As a mathematician, Carroll may have used the story to satirize new mathematical concepts of his time.

### Illustrations and Publication
John Tenniel's 42 wood-engraved illustrations are the definitive visual interpretation. The book was a publishing sensation, never going out of print and being translated into 174 languages. Carroll financed the initial print run, giving him editorial control. The first edition's red binding was chosen to appeal to children.

## Terms
- **Literary nonsense**: A genre of writing that uses logical contradictions and absurdity for humorous or artistic effect.
- **Didacticism**: A literary style that aims to instruct or moralize, which *Alice* helped displace in children's literature.
- **Roman à clef**: A novel in which real people or events are depicted under the guise of fiction.
- **Anthropomorphic**: Attributing human characteristics to animals or objects.
- **Concrete poetry**: Poetry where the visual arrangement of words is part of the meaning (e.g., "The Mouse's Tale").
- **Parody**: An imitation of a particular writer, artist, or genre with deliberate exaggeration for comic effect.
- **Non-Euclidean geometry**: A mathematical system that rejects Euclid's parallel postulate, a concept developing in Carroll's time.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Scholars debate the extent to which the character of Alice was based on the real Alice Liddell.
- There is disagreement about whether the story was conceived entirely on the "golden afternoon" of 4 July 1862 or developed over a longer period.
- Interpretations of the book's symbolism are varied; for example, Morton N. Cohen reads it as a *roman à clef* of Carroll's life, while Jan Susina argues the character has a tenuous relationship with Liddell.
- The precise nature and extent of mathematical satire in the book remain a subject of scholarly analysis.
