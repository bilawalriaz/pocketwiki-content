# Video

## Overview
Video is an electronic medium for recording, copying, playback, transmission, and display of moving visual images, with or without audio. Initially developed for live transmission using mechanical and later cathode-ray tube systems, video evolved from analog magnetic tape recording to digital formats that dominate today. Digital video enables efficient compression, storage, editing, and distribution across broadcast, physical media, and internet platforms, profoundly influencing media production, communication, entertainment, education, and global information dissemination.

## Timeline
- **1884** — Nipkow disk mechanical video scanner patented
- **1951** — First practical video tape recorder (VTR) developed by Ampex team led by Charles Ginsburg
- **1956** — VTRs sold commercially for ~US$50,000; tapes cost US$300 per hour
- **1971** — Sony introduces consumer videocassette recorder (VCR) decks and tapes
- **1997** — DVD commercially introduced
- **2006** — Blu-ray Disc introduced
- **2013** — Digital cameras surpass film cameras in Hollywood usage

## Body

### Analog Video
Video originated from 19th-century facsimile systems. Mechanical scanners like the Nipkow disk were patented in 1884, but practical systems required decades of development. Unlike film, which records sequential photographic images, video encodes images electronically into analog or digital signals. Initially live-only, video used CRT systems where electron beams scanned photoconductive plates to produce brightness-proportional voltage signals. Charles Ginsburg's Ampex team developed the first practical VTR in 1951, capturing live camera signals onto magnetic videotape. By 1971, Sony brought VCR technology to consumers.

### Digital Video
Digital video offers higher quality and lower costs than analog. Following DVD (1997) and Blu-ray (2006) adoption, videotape sales declined. Computer and smartphone advances enabled inexpensive digital capture, storage, editing, and transmission, reducing production costs and enabling tapeless workflows. Digital broadcasting transitions are relegating analog to legacy status. High-resolution cameras with improved dynamic range and color gamuts have converged digital video with film technology, with digital cameras surpassing film in Hollywood by 2013.

### Frame Rate and Scanning
Frame rate—the number of still images per second—ranges from 6–8 fps in early mechanical cameras to 120+ fps in modern professional equipment. PAL/SECAM specify 25 fps; NTSC specifies 29.97 fps; film uses 24 fps. The minimum for persistence of vision (moving image illusion) is ~16 fps. Video can be interlaced or progressive. Progressive scan updates all lines sequentially per frame, yielding optimal spatial resolution. Interlacing, invented to reduce CRT flicker without increasing frame rate, splits each frame into odd and even fields. NTSC, PAL, and SECAM are interlaced formats (e.g., 576i50). Displaying interlaced content on progressive devices degrades resolution unless deinterlacing is applied, though this cannot match native progressive quality.

### Aspect Ratio
Aspect ratio is the proportional relationship between video width and height. Traditional TV uses 4:3 (1.33:1); HDTV uses 16:9 (1.78:1); the Academy film ratio is 1.375:1. Digital video pixels often have non-square aspect ratios (e.g., PAL/NTSC CCIR 601). The 720×480 raster uses thin pixels on 4:3 displays and fat pixels on 16:9 displays. Mobile phone usage has driven vertical video growth, rising from 5% of viewing in 2010 to 29% in 2015, with vertical ads watched nine times more frequently than landscape ads.

### Color Model and Depth
Color models map encoded values to visible colors: YIQ (NTSC), YUV (PAL), YDbDr (SECAM), YCbCr (digital video). Color depth—the number of distinct colors per pixel—depends on bits per pixel. Chroma subsampling (e.g., 4:4:4, 4:2:2, 4:2:0) reduces data by averaging chrominance across pixel blocks while preserving luminance, exploiting human eye sensitivity differences. This reduces chrominance data by 50% (4:2:2) or 75% (4:2:0) without reducing displayable color values.

### Quality and Compression
Video quality is measured via formal metrics like peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) or subjective assessment (ITU-T BT.500). The Double Stimulus Impairment Scale (DSIS) compares unimpaired reference video with impaired versions using expert ratings. Uncompressed video offers maximum quality but high data rates. Compression reduces spatial redundancy (intraframe, related to image compression) and temporal redundancy (interframe, including motion compensation). Modern standards include MPEG-2 (DVD, Blu-ray, satellite) and MPEG-4 (AVCHD, mobile 3GP, Internet).

### Stereoscopy
Stereoscopic 3D uses dual channels (right/left eye) viewed via polarized filters on projectors with matching glasses, anaglyph 3D (color-coded overlay with red/cyan glasses), or alternating frames synchronized with LCD shutter glasses (common in VR, halving effective frame rate).

### Formats and Media
Transmission formats specify connectors, protocols, refresh rates, resolutions, and color spaces. Recording formats span analog (composite, S-Video, component) and digital (SDI, DVI, HDMI, DisplayPort). Transport media include wireless/cable broadcast, coaxial SDI, and network streaming (MPEG transport stream, SMPTE 2022/2110). Digital television standards include ATSC (US/Canada/Mexico/Korea), DVB (Europe), ISDB (Japan), ISDB-Tb (Brazil/Argentina), and DMB (Korea). Analog standards include NTSC, PAL, SECAM, and obsolete systems. Recording formats evolved from VTRs to digital tape, optical discs (Laserdisc, DVD, Blu-ray), and file-based storage. Video codecs (encoder/decoder) compress/decompress digital video using lossy standards.

## Terms
- **Frame rate**: Number of still images displayed per second; determines motion smoothness and persistence of vision threshold (~16 fps).
- **Interlacing**: Scanning method splitting each frame into odd/even fields to reduce flicker without increasing frame rate; denoted by "i" (e.g., 576i50).
- **Progressive scan**: Display method updating all scan lines sequentially per frame for optimal spatial resolution.
- **Aspect ratio**: Proportional relationship between video width and height (e.g., 4:3, 16:9).
- **Chroma subsampling**: Technique reducing color data by averaging chrominance across pixel blocks (e.g., 4:2:0) while preserving luminance.
- **Codec**: Software/hardware compressing and decompressing digital video; a portmanteau of encoder and decoder.
- **Deinterlacing**: Process converting interlaced video to progressive format for display on progressive-scan devices.
- **Persistence of vision**: Phenomenon where rapid image succession creates the illusion of continuous motion (~16 fps minimum).
- **Blanking interval**: Margin surrounding visible frame content containing metadata and synchronization information.
- **Lossy compression**: Compression discarding some original data, reducing quality upon decompression (e.g., MPEG-2, MPEG-4).

## Debates and Open Questions
The transition from analog to digital video continues globally, with analog becoming legacy technology. The choice between digital and film in Hollywood was resolved by 2013 when digital cameras surpassed film usage. Vertical video adoption reflects changing mobile consumption patterns. Deinterlacing remains imperfect for displaying interlaced content on progressive devices. Chroma subsampling balances data reduction against color detail fidelity. The superiority of digital over film for high-resolution, dynamic range, and color gamut applications is now established in professional production.
