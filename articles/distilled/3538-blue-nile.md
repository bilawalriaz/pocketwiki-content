# Blue Nile

## Overview
The Blue Nile is one of the two major tributaries of the Nile River, originating at Lake Tana in Ethiopia. It is the primary source of the Nile's water and fertile silt, contributing over 85% of the river's flow during the rainy season. Its course through deep gorges has made it a historically significant and challenging river, central to the water politics and economies of Ethiopia, Sudan, and Egypt.

## Timeline
- **c. 11th century** — Ethiopian control of the Blue Nile basin becomes a diplomatic tool in relations with Egypt.
- **1565** — João Bermudes provides the first European description of the Blue Nile Falls (Tis Abay).
- **21 April 1618** — Pedro Páez becomes the first European known to have seen the Blue Nile's source.
- **1821** — Frédéric Cailliaud's attempt to trace the river from its confluence is thwarted by the canyon.
- **1902-1905** — W.W. Macmillan and B.H. Jenssen make early, unsuccessful attempts to explore the canyon reach.
- **1925-1933** — Robert Cheesman maps the upper course from the adjacent highlands.
- **1968** — A British-Ethiopian team completes the first full descent of the river from Lake Tana.
- **1970** — Completion of the Aswan High Dam in Egypt ends the Nile's annual floods.
- **1999** — Virginia Morell and Nevada Wier raft the entire river to Sudan.
- **28 April 2004** — Pasquale Scaturro and Gordon Brown complete the first known navigation of the entire Blue Nile.
- **November 2012** — Ethiopia begins construction of the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam.
- **February 2022** — Electricity generation begins at the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam.

## Body

### Course and Hydrology
The Blue Nile originates at Gish Abay in Ethiopia, where it is called the Abay River. It flows for approximately 1,450 km (900 mi), with 800 km within Ethiopia, through a series of deep gorges in the Ethiopian Highlands. In Sudan, it travels about 650 km before joining the White Nile at Khartoum to form the Nile. Its flow peaks from June to September, supplying 80–86% of the Nile's water during this period. The river carries fertile silt eroded from the highlands, which historically caused the Nile's annual floods in Egypt.

### Economic and Political Significance
The Blue Nile is vital to the economies of Sudan and Egypt. In Sudan, dams like Roseires and Sennar provide 80% of the country's hydropower and irrigate the Gezira Scheme, a major agricultural area. Egypt depends on the river for over 85% of its Nile water. The construction of Ethiopia's Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam, a 6000-megawatt hydroelectric project, began in 2012 and has raised concerns in Sudan and Egypt about potential water reduction.

### Historical Exploration and Diplomacy
Control of the Blue Nile has been a key element in Ethiopian-Egyptian relations since the 11th century. Ethiopian emperors used the threat of diverting the river as a diplomatic lever. European exploration of the river was long hindered by its impassable canyon. The first full descent was achieved in 1968, and the first complete navigation by kayak occurred in 2004. The river's source was identified by Europeans in the early 17th century.

## Terms
- **Tributary**: A river that flows into a larger river.
- **Confluence**: The point where two rivers meet and merge.
- **Sediment/Silt**: Solid material, like fertile soil, carried and deposited by a river.
- **Hydropower**: Electricity generated from the energy of falling or flowing water.
- **Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD)**: A large hydroelectric dam under construction on the Blue Nile in Ethiopia.
- **Gezira Scheme**: A major agricultural irrigation project in Sudan between the Blue and White Niles.
- **Canyon/Gorge**: A deep, narrow valley with steep sides, carved by a river.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary ongoing debate concerns the filling and operation of the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam. Sudan and Egypt have voiced concerns over potential reductions in their water supply, while Ethiopia views the dam as essential for its economic development. The historical interdependence between Ethiopia and Egypt, where Ethiopia controlled the water source and Egypt held religious authority, is a key context for modern water politics.
