# Jean-Baptiste Lamarck

## Overview
Jean-Baptiste Lamarck (1744–1829) was a French naturalist who proposed one of the first comprehensive theories of biological evolution. Though his proposed mechanism for evolution was largely rejected, his work was foundational in establishing evolution as a scientific concept and in classifying invertebrates.

## Timeline
- **1744** — Born in Bazentin, France.
- **c. 1761** — Fought in the Seven Years' War; promoted for bravery.
- **1778** — Published *Flore françoise*; gained membership in the French Academy of Sciences.
- **1793** — Appointed professor of zoology at the Muséum national d'Histoire naturelle.
- **1801** — Published *Système des animaux sans vertèbres*, coining the term "invertebrates."
- **1802** — Published *Hydrogéologie*, one of the first to use the term "biology" in its modern sense.
- **1809** — Published *Philosophie zoologique*, outlining his theory of evolution.
- **1815–22** — Published *Histoire naturelle des animaux sans vertèbres* in seven volumes.
- **1829** — Died in Paris in poverty.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Lamarck was born into an impoverished aristocratic family with a military tradition. After serving with distinction in the Seven Years' War, he turned to natural history. His major botanical work, *Flore françoise* (1778), brought him prominence and led to his appointment at the Jardin des Plantes (later the Muséum national d'Histoire naturelle), where he became a professor of zoology in 1793.

### Contributions to Classification
Lamarck was a pioneering taxonomist, especially for invertebrates. In his 1801 *Système des animaux sans vertèbres*, he coined the term "invertebrates" and redefined major groups, separating arachnids from insects and crustaceans into their own classes. He is remembered as a taxonomist of considerable stature, particularly in malacology (the study of mollusks).

### Theory of Evolution
Lamarck developed the first truly cohesive theory of biological evolution, which he presented in lectures and published works, most notably *Philosophie zoologique* (1809). His theory proposed two main forces: a "complexifying force" that drove organisms up a ladder of complexity, and an "adaptive force" that shaped them to their environment through use and disuse of characteristics.

The complexifying force (*Le pouvoir de la vie*) was a tendency for organisms to become more complex, driven by internal fluids and based on his pre-Lavoisier, alchemical view of elements. The adaptive force (*L'influence des circonstances*) operated through two laws: 1) Use and disuse: organs strengthen with frequent use and deteriorate with disuse. 2) Inheritance of acquired characteristics: changes acquired during an organism's lifetime can be passed to offspring. This second law is the core of what is now called "Lamarckism" or soft inheritance.

### Later Life and Legacy
Lamarck's evolutionary ideas were controversial and opposed by contemporaries like Georges Cuvier. He died in poverty, and his body was lost. His theory was later eclipsed by Darwin's natural selection, though Darwin acknowledged Lamarck's role in promoting the idea of evolution by natural law. In the 20th century, August Weismann's theory of germ plasm seemed to refute Lamarckism. However, modern epigenetics—the study of heritable changes in gene expression not involving DNA sequence changes—has sparked debate about whether Lamarckian-like processes might occur, though no known example relates to use and disuse of organs.

## Terms
- **Invertebrates**: Animals without a backbone; a term coined by Lamarck.
- **Biology**: The study of life; Lamarck was one of the first to use the term in its modern sense.
- **Lamarckism**: The theory that organisms can pass on characteristics acquired during their lifetime to their offspring; also called soft inheritance or use/disuse theory.
- **Use and disuse**: Lamarck's first law: organs strengthen with frequent use and weaken with disuse.
- **Inheritance of acquired characteristics**: Lamarck's second law: traits acquired during an organism's lifetime can be inherited by its offspring.
- **Complexifying force**: Lamarck's proposed internal drive for organisms to evolve toward greater complexity.
- **Adaptive force**: Lamarck's proposed mechanism by which organisms adapt to their environment through use and disuse.
- **Epigenetics**: The study of heritable changes in gene expression that do not involve changes to the DNA sequence itself.
- **Essentialism**: The philosophical belief that species have fixed, unchanging essences; a view Lamarck initially held but later abandoned.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Validity of Lamarckism**: Lamarck's specific mechanism of inheritance of acquired characteristics via use and disuse was rejected by mainstream biology after Weismann's work. However, the discovery of epigenetic inheritance has led some scientists to argue for a "neolamarckist" view, though this remains debated and no known epigenetic mechanism is linked to use and disuse.
- **Lamarck's Materialism**: Scholars debate the extent of Lamarck's materialism. He was a deist who referred to God as the "sublime author of nature," but his explanations for evolution relied solely on natural, physical processes without recourse to supernatural intervention.
- **Historical Influence**: While Lamarck's theory was not accepted in his time, historians like Stephen Jay Gould argue he was the "primary evolutionary theorist" whose structured framework set the tone for subsequent evolutionary thought.
