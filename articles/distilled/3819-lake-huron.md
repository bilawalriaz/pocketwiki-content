# Lake Huron

## Overview
Lake Huron is one of North America's five Great Lakes, shared by Ontario, Canada, and Michigan, USA. It is hydrologically connected to Lake Michigan, forming the world's largest freshwater lake by surface area. The lake's ecology has been dramatically altered by invasive species, and its history includes significant Native American settlement and European exploration.

## Body

### Geography and Hydrology
Lake Huron is the second-largest Great Lake by surface area (23,000 sq mi) and the third-largest freshwater lake on Earth. It is connected to Lake Michigan by the five-mile-wide Straits of Mackinac; together they form Lake Michigan–Huron, the largest freshwater lake by area globally. The lake's surface is 577 feet above sea level, with an average depth of 195 feet and a maximum depth of 750 feet. Its main inlet is the St. Marys River from Lake Superior, and its main outlet is the St. Clair River toward Lake Erie. Water flows through Lake Huron faster than other Great Lakes, with a retention time of 22 years.

### Geology and Formation
Lake Huron was formed by melting glaciers at the end of the last ice age. Before glaciation, the area was a low-lying depression drained by ancient rivers. A key geological feature is the submerged Alpena-Amberley Ridge, which was exposed as a land bridge about 9,000 years ago when water levels were ~100 meters lower. This ridge was used by Paleo-Indians for hunting, as evidenced by at least 60 stone constructions discovered since 2008.

### History and Human Use
Before European contact, the region supported large Native American towns, with archaeological evidence of a settlement housing 4,000–6,000 people. French explorers called the lake "La Mer Douce" (the fresh-water sea). The name "Lake Huron" derives from the indigenous Huron (Wyandot) people. The lake has a long history of shipwrecks, with over a thousand recorded. The first European ship lost on the Great Lakes was the *Le Griffon* in 1679. The devastating Great Lakes Storm of 1913 sank 10 ships and killed 235 on the lake.

### Ecology
Lake Huron's ecology has undergone drastic change. Its native deepwater fish community was dominated by lake trout, which were virtually extirpated by 1950 due to overfishing and invasive sea lamprey. Invasive alewife and rainbow smelt also became abundant. Non-native Pacific salmon were stocked from the 1960s. A profound food web change occurred in 2003, linked to newer invasive species like zebra and quagga mussels. This led to a collapse in the demersal fish community by 2006 and reduced Chinook salmon catches. Some native species, like lake trout and walleye, have benefited from the subsequent disappearance of alewife.

## Terms
- **Retention time**: The average time a water molecule spends in a lake; Lake Huron's is 22 years.
- **Huronian glaciation**: A geological period named from evidence collected in the Lake Huron region.
- **Straits of Mackinac**: The five-mile-wide, 20-fathom-deep channel connecting Lake Huron and Lake Michigan.
- **Alpena-Amberley Ridge**: An ancient, submerged ridge beneath Lake Huron, once a land bridge used by Paleo-Indians.
- **Bathymetric maps**: Maps that show the underwater depth and features of a lake or ocean floor.
- **Extirpated**: Locally extinct; a species no longer present in a specific area.
- **Demersal fish**: Bottom-dwelling fish species.
- **Zooplankton**: Tiny animals that drift in water and are a key part of the aquatic food web.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact location of the wreck of the *Le Griffon* is disputed, with competing claims from Manitoulin Island and Russell Island in Georgian Bay.
- The precise causes of the profound 2003 food web change and the ongoing collapse of the demersal fish community are linked to invasive species but remain an active area of study.
