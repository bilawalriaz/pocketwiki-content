# Phrygia

## Overview
Phrygia was an ancient kingdom and region in west-central Anatolia (modern Turkey), significant for its powerful Iron Age empire, its distinct Indo-European language, and its influential culture that shaped Greek religion and music. Its history spans from legendary migrations and a peak of power in the 8th century BC through centuries as a province under successive empires, eventually assimilating into the Byzantine world.

## Timeline
- **c. 12th century BC** — Possible Phrygian migration into Anatolia following the Hittite Empire's collapse.
- **c. 720–695 BC** — Peak of Phrygian power under King Midas, rivaling Assyria and Urartu.
- **c. 695 BC** — Cimmerians sack the Phrygian capital, Gordium.
- **540s BC** — Phrygia conquered by Cyrus the Great, becoming a Persian satrapy.
- **333 BC** — Alexander the Great passes through Gordium and cuts the Gordian Knot.
- **301 BC** — Battle of Ipsus fought in Phrygia after Alexander's death.
- **188 BC** — Southern Phrygia comes under control of the Attalids of Pergamon.
- **133 BC** — Phrygia passes to the Roman Empire.
- **2nd century AD** — The Christian heresy of Montanism (the "Phrygian heresy") arises.
- **1071–13th century** — Phrygia overrun by Turks after the Battle of Manzikert.
- **1453** — The name "Phrygia" passes out of use after the Ottoman conquest of Byzantium.

## Body

### Origins and Language
Ancient tradition held that the Phrygians migrated from the Balkans, where they were known as Bryges. Their language, Phrygian, was an Indo-European language that survived until the 6th century AD. It was not part of the Anatolian language family (like Hittite) and is now considered a centum language most closely related to Greek, supporting a European origin. This linguistic link, along with shared myths, suggests a proto-Graeco-Phrygian stage. Some scholars, however, propose a later migration during the Bronze Age collapse or even an Anatolian origin.

### The Phrygian Kingdom
Phrygia's power peaked in the late 8th century BC under King Midas (possibly the "Mita" of Assyrian records). The kingdom dominated western and central Anatolia, engaging in conflict and later diplomacy with Assyria. The capital, Gordium, flourished with writing and distinctive pottery. This era ended when Cimmerian invaders sacked Gordium around 695 BC, leading to Midas's reported suicide. Phrygia then became subject to Lydia, Persia, Alexander the Great, and his Hellenistic successors.

### Culture and Religion
Phrygian religion was polytheistic and centered on the mountain goddess Matar Kubeleya (later Cybele), worshipped with ecstatic rites by priests called Corybantes. Other deities included the storm god Tiws (a variant of Zeus) and the moon god Mas. Phrygian music, especially the "Phrygian mode," was influential in ancient Greece, associated with emotional intensity and ritual ecstasy. The Phrygian cap became an iconic symbol in classical art.

### Later History and Legacy
After Alexander, northern Phrygia was overrun by Celts (becoming Galatia), while the south passed to Pergamon and then Rome. The Romans divided the region into provinces. Christianity arrived early, and the 2nd-century Montanist heresy originated here. Phrygians gradually became Christian and Greek-speaking, assimilating into the Byzantine Empire. The region was conquered by Turks in the 11th–13th centuries, and the name "Phrygia" faded from use.

## Terms
- **Satrapy:** A province of the ancient Persian Empire, governed by a satrap.
- **Centum language:** A branch of Indo-European languages (including Greek and Phrygian) characterized by certain sound changes, as opposed to the satem branch (like Armenian).
- **Matar Kubeleya:** The Phrygian "Mother of the Mountain Peaks," a goddess later known as Cybele.
- **Corybantes:** Priests of Cybele, known for ecstatic, rhythmic dancing.
- **Phrygian mode:** An ancient Greek musical scale considered warlike and emotionally intense.
- **Gordian Knot:** A legendary knot tied by King Gordius, famously cut by Alexander the Great.
- **Cimmerians:** A nomadic people who invaded Anatolia in the 7th century BC, destroying Phrygian power.
- **Montanism:** A 2nd-century Christian prophetic movement originating in Phrygia, emphasizing ecstasy and new prophecy.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Origins:** The timing and nature of Phrygian migration from the Balkans are debated, with theories ranging from a Bronze Age move to a later arrival after the Hittite collapse.
- **Language Relationship:** While Greek is now seen as Phrygian's closest relative, the exact nature of their relationship and the existence of a common proto-language are subjects of ongoing research.
- **Relation to Armenians:** Herodotus claimed Armenians were Phrygian colonists, but this is contested due to linguistic (Phrygian is centum, Armenian is satem) and archaeological discrepancies.
- **Ecstatic Cults:** The idea that Phrygia was the origin of ecstatic cults in Greece is now largely rejected by scholars as a Greek and Roman projection onto a perceived "oriental" land.
