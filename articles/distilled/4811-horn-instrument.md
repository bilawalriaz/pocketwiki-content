# Horn (instrument)

## Overview
A horn is a family of musical instruments characterized by a tube, usually metal and often curved, with a narrow end for blowing and a wide, flared end for sound emission. Unlike cylindrical-bore instruments like the trumpet, the horn's bore gradually widens, making it conical. This design, originating from animal horns, evolved through centuries of technological innovation—from natural horns using crooks and hand-stopping to modern valved instruments—fundamentally shaping its role in orchestral, military, and popular music.

## Timeline
- **10th century BC** — Earliest metal horns (lurer) appear in Scandinavia.
- **14th century** — First written records of hunting-horn signals.
- **c. 1570** — Earliest surviving tightly spiralled hunting horn by Valentin Springer.
- **1667** — First known hooped hunting horn (trompe de chasse) made by Starck in Nuremberg.
- **1713** — Horns become regular members of continental orchestras.
- **c. 1753** — Anton Joseph Hampel creates the Inventionshorn with sliding crooks.
- **c. 1750** — Hampel develops hand-stopping technique.
- **1818** — Rotary valves introduced by Stölzel and Blümel.
- **1845** — Adolphe Sax patents the saxhorn family.
- **1853** — Wagner tuba invented for Richard Wagner's *Der Ring des Nibelungen*.

## Body

### Origins and Early Development
Horns originated from actual animal horns, a practice preserved in the Jewish shofar. Metal emulation began by the 10th century BC with Scandinavian lurer, curved bronze horns often found in pairs. The Romans, credited by some to the Etruscans, used horns like the cornu. By the 17th century, hunting horns evolved to solve the problem of playing on horseback, leading to the crescent-shaped trompe and the tightly coiled cor à plusieurs tours. The modern, single-hoop hunting horn (trompe de chasse) emerged around 1667, with a non-removable mouthpiece and a soldered bell for stability.

### Technical Evolution and Orchestral Adoption
Early horns could only play notes from the harmonic series of a single key. To change keys, players used crooks—insertable tubing sections. The first surviving crooked horn dates to 1721. This innovation led to the horn's adoption into orchestras by the early 18th century. A major advancement was the Inventionshorn (c. 1753), which used internal sliding crooks, allowing the left hand to secure the instrument while the right hand operated the bell. Hand-stopping, developed around 1750, allowed players to alter pitch by inserting the right hand into the bell, enabling chromatic playing. The introduction of rotary valves in 1818 (and later piston valves) finally made the horn fully chromatic, though adoption was slow due to tradition and initial unreliability.

### Modern Varieties and Design
The German horn, with its rotary valves and larger bore, is the standard orchestral instrument, most commonly a double horn in F/B♭. The French horn is distinguished by its narrower bore and piston valves. The Vienna horn uses a unique Pumpenvalve and closely mimics the natural horn's size and sound. Other variants include the mellophone and marching horn, designed for forward projection in marching bands, and the Wagner tuba, a rare instrument with a vertical bell written for by composers like Wagner and Bruckner. The saxhorn family, developed in the 1840s, revolutionized military and brass bands.

## Terms
- **Bore**: The internal diameter of the instrument's tubing; a conical bore gradually increases in width, unlike a cylindrical bore.
- **Crook**: A detachable section of tubing inserted to change the instrument's fundamental key and pitch.
- **Hand-stopping**: A technique where the player inserts the right hand into the bell to alter the pitch, enabling chromatic notes.
- **Harmonic series**: The set of notes naturally produced by a brass instrument from a single length of tubing, based on lip tension.
- **Inventionshorn**: A type of natural horn with internal sliding crooks, developed c. 1753 to facilitate key changes.
- **Natural horn**: A valveless horn that can only play the harmonic series of one key at a time, often using crooks and hand-stopping.
- **Rotary valve**: A valve mechanism that rotates to redirect air into additional tubing, common on German horns.
- **Piston valve**: A valve that moves vertically to redirect air, common on French horns and saxhorns.
- **Trompe de chasse**: The French hunting horn, a single-hoop instrument that became the ancestor of the modern orchestral horn.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact use and playing manner of ancient instruments like the lurer is unknown due to a lack of written histories from their makers.
- There is debate over the literal interpretation of early hunting-horn notation (e.g., calls notated on a single pitch), as a monotonic reading would make many calls indistinguishable.
- The precise origin of the hooped hunting horn is debated; while the first known example was made in Germany in 1667, the form became especially associated with France.
