# Ja'far al-Sadiq

## Overview
Ja'far al-Sadiq (c. 702–765) was the sixth and last agreed-upon Imam for both Twelver and Ismaili Shia Muslims. A pivotal scholar, jurist, and hadith transmitter, he is the eponymous founder of the Ja'fari school of Islamic jurisprudence and a revered figure in Sunni Islam and Sufism. His long life and teachings during the transition from the Umayyad to the Abbasid Caliphate were crucial in establishing Shia Islam as a distinct intellectual and theological tradition.

## Timeline
- **c. 702** — Born in Medina to the fifth Shia Imam, Muhammad al-Baqir, and Umm Farwa (a descendant of Abu Bakr).
- **c. 733** — Becomes the sixth Shia Imam after the death of his father, Muhammad al-Baqir.
- **740s-750s** — Teaches a large body of students in Medina, including future Sunni jurists Abu Hanifa and Malik ibn Anas.
- **750** — The Abbasid Caliphate overthrows the Umayyads. Al-Sadiq maintains political quietism.
- **762** — His house is burned on the orders of the Abbasid caliph al-Mansur; he survives.
- **765** — Dies in Medina, reportedly poisoned at the instigation of al-Mansur.
- **765+** — His followers split over succession, leading to the formation of the Twelver and Ismaili branches.

## Body

### Life and Political Context
Al-Sadiq spent most of his 64-year life in Medina. He witnessed the fall of the Umayyad Caliphate and the rise of the Abbasids, who initially sought Shia support but later persecuted them. He adopted a policy of quietism, refusing to join revolts led by relatives like Zayd ibn Ali or support the Abbasid revolution. He viewed the Imamate as a spiritual, divinely designated authority separate from the political caliphate. This stance, combined with the doctrine of *taqiya* (dissimulation under persecution), allowed him to teach freely during periods of weak central authority.

### Imamate and Succession
Al-Sadiq elaborated the Shia doctrine of the Imamate, holding that the Imam is a divinely designated (*nass*) descendant of Muhammad through Ali and Fatima, possessing infallible (*isma*) knowledge. His death triggered a major schism. The largest group followed his younger son, Musa al-Kazim, becoming the Twelvers. Another group followed the lineage of his eldest son, Isma'il (who predeceased him), forming the Ismailis. Other factions briefly followed other sons or believed al-Sadiq would return as the Mahdi.

### Scholarly and Jurisprudential Legacy
Al-Sadiq is the eponymous founder of the Ja'fari school of law, the primary school of Twelver Shia jurisprudence. He taught that God's law should be understood through the Imam's guidance, not solely through human reasoning like analogical deduction (*qiyas*). He emphasized a devolved legal authority, instructing followers to refer to scholars who transmitted the Imams' hadiths. His teachings form the core of Shia theology, including concepts of free will (a middle path between predestination and absolute human agency) and Quranic exegesis (*ta'wil*), which seeks inner meanings.

### Reverence Across Traditions
Al-Sadiq is respected in Sunni Islam as a reliable hadith transmitter and jurist, with major figures like Malik ibn Anas citing him. He holds a prominent place in Sufi lineages as a master of the spiritual path (*tariqat*). His vast number of recorded traditions—more than those of the other Shia Imams combined—made his teachings a source for diverse early Islamic intellectual movements, though this also complicates efforts to ascertain his precise views.

## Terms
- **Imamate**: In Shia Islam, the divinely ordained leadership of the Muslim community, held by a specific lineage of descendants from Muhammad through Ali and Fatima.
- **Nass**: The divinely inspired designation of each Shia Imam by the previous one.
- **Ismah**: The doctrine of the infallibility and sinlessness of the Shia Imams.
- **Taqiya**: Religious dissimulation, or the concealing of one's faith under persecution.
- **Ja'fari school**: The school of Islamic jurisprudence founded on the teachings of Ja'far al-Sadiq, followed by Twelver Shia Muslims.
- **Hadith**: A record of the traditions or sayings of the Prophet Muhammad and the Shia Imams, revered as a source of religious law and moral guidance.
- **Umayyad Caliphate**: The second of the four major Islamic caliphates (661–750 CE), overthrown by the Abbasids.
- **Abbasid Caliphate**: The third Islamic caliphate (750–1258 CE), which succeeded the Umayyads.
- **Mahdi**: In Shia Islam, a messianic figure, a descendant of Muhammad, who will appear at the end of time to establish justice.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Authenticity of Works**: Many religious and scientific texts are attributed to al-Sadiq, but scholars generally regard them as inauthentic, suggesting he taught orally and left writing to his students.
- **Views on Sufism**: While early Sufi writers claim him as a spiritual forebear, some Shia scholars dismiss these links as later attempts to appropriate his authority for Sufi teachings.
- **Nature of His Teachings**: The wide range of contradictory views ascribed to him across different Islamic factions (Shia, Sunni, Sufi, etc.) makes it difficult for historians to reconstruct his precise, original teachings.
- **Succession Crisis**: The exact reasons for the split between the Twelver and Ismaili factions after his death, and the roles of his various sons, remain subjects of historical analysis.
