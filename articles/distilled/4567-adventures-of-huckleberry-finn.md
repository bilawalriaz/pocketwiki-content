# Adventures of Huckleberry Finn

## Overview
*Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* is a picaresque novel by Mark Twain, first published in the UK in December 1884 and the US in February 1885. It is a direct sequel to *The Adventures of Tom Sawyer* and is widely considered among the Great American Novels. The work is celebrated for its pioneering use of vernacular English and its scathing satire on racism and societal hypocrisy in the antebellum South, though it has also been a perennial source of controversy for its language and racial depictions.

## Timeline
- **1876–1883** — Mark Twain composes the novel in pen on notepaper, revising extensively.
- **December 10, 1884** — First published in Canada and the United Kingdom.
- **February 18, 1885** — First published in the United States.
- **1885** — The Concord Public Library bans the book for being "coarse" and "trash."
- **1885** — The manuscript is donated to the Buffalo Public Library.
- **1905** — The Brooklyn Public Library bans the book for "bad word choice."
- **1935** — T.S. Eliot and Ernest Hemingway praise the novel, cementing its classic status.
- **1991** — The missing first half of the original manuscript is rediscovered.
- **2011** — NewSouth Books publishes an edition replacing the racial slur "nigger" with "slave."
- **2016** — A Virginia public school district removes the novel due to racial slurs.

## Body
### Plot and Characters
The novel is narrated by Huckleberry "Huck" Finn, a boy of about thirteen or fourteen. After receiving a fortune, he is taken in by the Widow Douglas and her sister Miss Watson, who try to "sivilize" him. To escape his abusive, alcoholic father, Huck fakes his own murder and flees to Jackson's Island, where he reunites with Miss Watson's slave, Jim, who has run away to avoid being sold. They travel down the Mississippi River on a raft, seeking freedom in the free state of Illinois. Their journey is episodic, featuring encounters with con men (the Duke and the King), a violent family feud (the Grangerfords and Shepherdsons), and various scams. The climax occurs when Jim is sold to the Phelps family. Huck and Tom Sawyer devise an elaborate, theatrical plan to free him, only to learn at the end that Miss Watson had already freed Jim in her will. Huck declares he will "light out for the Territory" to avoid being adopted.

### Themes and Literary Significance
The novel explores themes of race, identity, freedom, and the conflict between societal norms and individual conscience. Huck's moral journey is central; he struggles with the "deformed conscience" of a racist society but ultimately makes a choice based on his friendship with Jim, believing he will "go to hell" for helping a runaway slave. Twain described the novel as a story where "a sound heart and a deformed conscience come into collision." The book is noted for its "deeply felt portrayal of boyhood" and its use of local color regionalism. It was a pivotal work in American literature, making vernacular language a legitimate tool for serious prose and dismantling "decadent Romanticism."

### Critical Reception and Controversy
From its release, the novel was controversial. Early criticism focused on its "coarseness" and "inelegant" language. Later, and more persistently, criticism centered on its racial content. While many scholars argue the novel is a powerful anti-racist satire that humanizes Jim, others contend it relies on minstrel-show stereotypes and confirms racist tropes. The frequent use of the racial slur "nigger" has led to repeated challenges, bans, and expurgated editions in schools and libraries. Critics like Ernest Hemingway praised the novel's first part but dismissed the ending as "cheating," while others, like Ralph Ellison, defended its structural and moral necessity.

## Terms
- **Picaresque novel:** A genre of prose fiction that depicts the adventures of a roguish hero of low social class who lives by his wits in a corrupt society.
- **Vernacular English:** The language or dialect spoken by the ordinary people in a particular region.
- **Local color regionalism:** A style of fiction that emphasizes the unique features of a particular locale, including its customs, dialect, and landscape.
- **Antebellum:** Occurring or existing before a war, specifically the American Civil War (1861–1865).
- **Satire:** The use of humor, irony, exaggeration, or ridicule to expose and criticize people's stupidity or vices.
- **Coming-of-age story:** A genre of literature that focuses on a protagonist's growth from youth to adulthood.
- **Minstrel show:** A form of entertainment popular in the 19th century that involved white performers in blackface caricaturing African Americans.
- **Expurgated edition:** A version of a text from which material considered offensive has been removed.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Is the novel racist or anti-racist?** Scholars are divided. Some argue Twain uses satire to attack racism by humanizing Jim and exposing societal hypocrisy. Others contend the novel's use of racial slurs and stereotypical humor ultimately reinforces the racist attitudes of its era.
- **The significance of the ending:** A major scholarly debate concerns the final chapters, where Tom Sawyer orchestrates an elaborate rescue of the already-free Jim. Critics like Hemingway saw this as a decline into farce, while defenders like Ellison argue it is essential for the novel's moral and symbolic structure.
- **Appropriateness for education:** The novel remains one of the most frequently challenged books in U.S. schools. The core question is whether its literary value outweighs the harm caused by its offensive language and whether it can be taught effectively without causing pain to students.
