# Population transfer in the Soviet Union

## Overview
From 1930 to 1952, the Soviet government under Joseph Stalin conducted a series of forced population transfers, deporting millions of people classified as "enemies of the people," entire ethnic groups, and labor forces to remote, underpopulated regions. These actions, directed by the NKVD under Lavrentiy Beria, aimed to consolidate control, punish perceived disloyalty, and reshape the ethnic map of the USSR. The deportations, which affected at least 6 million people, are widely considered crimes against humanity and ethnic persecution, with some, like those of the Crimean Tatars and Chechens, officially recognized as genocides.

## Timeline
- **1930–1931** — Dekulakization: 1.8 million "kulaks" (affluent farmers) deported to Siberia and Central Asia.
- **1937** — First mass ethnic deportation: 171,781 Soviet Koreans forcibly moved from the Far East to Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan.
- **1939–1941** — 1.45 million people deported from annexed eastern Poland (Kresy).
- **1940–1953** — Over 200,000 people deported from the Baltic states (Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania).
- **1941–1949** — Approximately 3.3 million ethnic minorities deported to Siberia and Central Asia during and after WWII.
- **1943–1944** — Deportation of entire nationalities from the Caucasus and Crimea, including Karachays, Kalmyks, Chechens, Ingush, Balkars, and Crimean Tatars.
- **18 May 1944** — Mass deportation of nearly all Crimean Tatars to Uzbekistan begins.
- **1944–1953** — Deportation of various groups from the Black Sea region, including Kurds, Greeks, Bulgarians, and Armenians.
- **1945–1948** — Expulsion of Germans from Eastern Europe; an estimated 300,000–360,000 perish due to Soviet actions and broader expulsions.
- **1945–1952** — 4.2 million Soviet citizens displaced by WWII are repatriated, often under coercion.
- **1956** — Nikita Khrushchev condemns the deportations in his "Secret Speech."
- **1991** — Russian SFSR law denounces all mass deportations as "Stalin's policy of defamation and genocide."

## Body

### Deportation of Social Groups
The first major deportations targeted the "kulak" class during collectivization. Despite the term's historical meaning of affluent farmers, many were labeled kulaks for resisting collectivization. Soviet archives document 1,803,392 people sent to labor colonies in 1930–31, with 389,521 reported deaths from 1932–1940. Estimates of the total deported range from 1.68 million to as high as 15 million by 1937. These deportations continued until the early 1950s, with the last major action against Lithuanian kulaks in 1951.

### Ethnic Operations
From the 1930s, deportations shifted from class-based to ethnic-based targeting. The 1937 deportation of Koreans set the precedent for mass transfers of entire nationalities. Between 1935 and 1938, at least ten nationalities were deported. During WWII, this escalated dramatically. Entire groups deemed disloyal, such as the Chechens, Ingush, Crimean Tatars, and Volga Germans, were forcibly relocated. Justifications cited mass treason and collaboration with Germany, though evidence for widespread collaboration is often disputed. The deportations profoundly altered the ethnic composition of the Soviet Union.

### Western Annexations and Post-War Expulsions
Following the 1939 Molotribent Pact, the USSR annexed eastern Poland and the Baltic states, deporting over 1.45 million from Poland and over 200,000 from the Baltics. After WWII, the Soviet Union expelled Germans from annexed territories like Kaliningrad (formerly East Prussia) and conducted population exchanges with Poland. These actions, along with the expulsion of Germans from Eastern Europe, resulted in hundreds of thousands of deaths.

### Repatriation and Rehabilitation
After WWII, 4.2 million Soviet citizens were repatriated from Allied-controlled territories. While 80% returned voluntarily, many faced suspicion, forced labor, or prosecution. Rehabilitation began under Khrushchev, who condemned the deportations in 1956. Some groups, like the Chechens and Ingush, were allowed to return and have their autonomy restored in 1957–58. However, others, including the Crimean Tatars and Volga Germans, were not granted the right to return until much later, if at all. A 1991 Russian law formally recognized the deportations as genocide.

## Terms
- **Kulak**: A term for relatively affluent peasants in the Russian Empire; in the Soviet context, it was broadly applied to farmers resisting collectivization.
- **NKVD**: The People's Commissariat for Internal Affairs, the Soviet secret police responsible for organizing the deportations.
- **Dekulakization**: The Soviet campaign of mass deportation and persecution of kulaks as a class.
- **Special Settlers**: A legal category for deported populations who were not prisoners but were subjected to forced settlement and labor.
- **Repatriation**: The process of returning people to their country of origin, in this case, the forced return of Soviet citizens after WWII.
- **Primordialism**: A view that ethnic groups possess inherent, permanent traits; used in debates to argue deportations were based on racialized views of disloyalty.
- **Soviet Xenophobia**: A theory that deportations were driven by an ideological fear of foreign influence, not ethnic animus.

## Debates and Open Questions
Historians debate whether the deportations constituted genocide. Some, like Raphael Lemkin (who coined the term), and the parliaments of Ukraine and Canada, classify the deportation of Crimean Tatars as genocide. The European Parliament recognized the deportation of Chechens and Ingush as genocide in 2004. Others, like Alexander Statiev, argue the deaths resulted from poor planning and wartime conditions, not a conscious intent to exterminate, labeling them "Soviet assimilation of unwanted nations."

A key scholarly debate concerns motivation. Terry Martin's "Soviet xenophobia" theory posits that deportations were ideologically driven by a fear of foreign contamination, with ethnicity serving as a proxy for suspected political disloyalty. In contrast, historians like Jon K. Chang contend the deportations were fundamentally based on ethnicity and racial prejudice, pointing to the loyalty of many deported minorities in Soviet intelligence operations as evidence against the disloyalty rationale.
