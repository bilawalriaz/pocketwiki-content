# Seljuk Empire

## Overview
The Seljuk Empire (1037–1308) was a high medieval, Turco-Persian empire that united the fractured eastern Islamic world, stretching from Anatolia to Central Asia. It played a pivotal role in the Crusades, facilitated the Turkification of Anatolia, and fostered a golden age of Persianate culture and Sunni Islamic scholarship before its fragmentation and eventual conquest by the Khwarazmians and Mongols.

## Timeline
- **985** — Seljuk, founder of the dynasty, converts to Islam near the Syr Darya.
- **1037** — Tughril and Chaghri found the empire after capturing Merv and Nishapur.
- **1040** — Decisive Seljuk victory over the Ghaznavids at the Battle of Dandanaqan.
- **1055** — Tughril enters Baghdad, ending Buyid influence and becoming protector of the Abbasid Caliph.
- **1071** — Alp Arslan defeats the Byzantines at the Battle of Manzikert, opening Anatolia to Turkic settlement.
- **1092** — Death of Malik-Shah I triggers a civil war and the empire's fragmentation.
- **1096-1099** — The First Crusade passes through Seljuk lands; Crusaders capture Jerusalem in 1099.
- **1141** — Ahmad Sanjar is defeated by the Qara-Khitai at the Battle of Qatwan, losing eastern territories.
- **1153** — Sanjar is captured by rebellious Oghuz Turks, leading to the empire's collapse in the east.
- **1194** — The last Great Seljuk sultan, Toghrul III, is defeated by the Khwarazmian Empire.
- **1308** — The Sultanate of Rum, the last Seljuk state, falls.

## Body

### Founding and Expansion
The Seljuk dynasty originated from the Qiniq tribe of Oghuz Turks. After converting to Islam in 985, they migrated into Transoxiana and Khorasan. Led by Tughril and his brother Chaghri, they defeated the Ghaznavids at Dandanaqan (1040), establishing a Persianate state. Tughril entered Baghdad in 1055, becoming the temporal protector of the Abbasid Caliph and filling the power vacuum left by the Buyids. His successor, Alp Arslan, expanded west, defeating the Byzantines at Manzikert (1071) and authorizing Turkoman generals to establish principalities (beyliks) across Anatolia.

### Golden Age and Internal Structure
Under Malik-Shah I (r. 1072–1092) and his powerful vizier Nizam al-Mulk, the empire reached its zenith, bordering China and the Byzantines. The state was organized as a "family federation," with autonomous appanages ruled by relatives. Nizam al-Mulk established the iqta' military system and the Nizamiyya madrasas, promoting Sunni orthodoxy. The capital moved to Isfahan. This era is considered the empire's golden age, marked by administrative sophistication and cultural patronage.

### Fragmentation and the Crusades
Malik-Shah's death in 1092 sparked a civil war among his sons and brother, fracturing the empire into competing sultanates and atabegs. This disunity hampered a coordinated response to the First Crusade (1096–1099), though the Seljuks initially defeated the People's Crusade. The Second Crusade (1147–1149) was triggered by the Zengid capture of Edessa. The empire's eastern half suffered a catastrophic blow at the Battle of Qatwan (1141), losing vassalage over the Kara-Khanids to the Qara-Khitai.

### Decline and Fall
Ahmad Sanjar's defeat and capture by Oghuz rebels (1153) led to the collapse of central authority in the east. The empire splintered into successor states like the Sultanate of Rum and the Kerman Seljuk Sultanate. In the west, the Zengids and later Saladin's Ayyubids supplanted Seljuk influence. The Great Seljuk line ended when Toghrul III was defeated by the Khwarazmian Empire in 1194. The Sultanate of Rum in Anatolia survived until the Mongol invasions of the 1260s, after which its territory fragmented into beyliks, including the future Ottoman state.

### Culture and Religion
The Seljuks were highly Persianized, adopting Persian as the language of administration and courtly culture while maintaining Turkic among themselves. They were devout Sunnis who promoted the sect through the construction of madrasas and mosques, countering Shia influence. Their architecture introduced the four-iwan mosque plan and elaborate brickwork. They patronized arts like ceramics (pioneering the Mina'i technique), metalwork, and manuscript illumination, blending Turkic, Persian, and Islamic traditions.

## Terms
- **Iqta'**: A system of land grants given to military officers in lieu of salary, reorganized by Nizam al-Mulk to support a standing army.
- **Atabeg**: A title for a guardian or regent, often ruling a province autonomously while nominally loyal to the sultan.
- **Beylik**: A principality or state ruled by a bey, a title used for the Turkoman lords who carved out domains in Anatolia.
- **Mamluk**: Slave soldiers, often of Turkic origin, who formed a professional military force dependent on the sultan rather than tribal loyalties.
- **Madrasa**: An Islamic educational institution, often with attached mosques, promoted by the Seljuks to spread Sunni orthodoxy.
- **Four-iwan plan**: A mosque layout featuring four vaulted halls opening onto a central courtyard, a key Seljuk architectural innovation.
- **Muqarnas**: A form of three-dimensional geometric decoration used in vaults and domes, creating a honeycomb-like effect.
- **Persianate**: A society culturally influenced by Persian language, literature, and customs, as adopted by the Seljuks.
- **Tughra**: A calligraphic monogram or seal of a sultan, one of the few Turkic public symbols used.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact nature of the Seljuk tribal organization and the balance of power between the sultan, family members, and Turkoman generals is debated.
- The extent to which the Seljuks were "pious defenders of Islam" versus patrons of occult sciences (astrology, alchemy) in their later period is challenged by some scholarship.
- The origins and development of the muqarnas architectural form—whether it began under the Seljuks or concurrently in North Africa— remain uncertain.
