# Jean Renoir

## Overview
Jean Renoir (1894–1979) was a French filmmaker, actor, producer, and author widely regarded as one of the greatest directors in cinema history. The son of Impressionist painter Pierre-Auguste Renoir, he transitioned from silent films to become a defining voice of 1930s French cinema, creating humanist masterpieces like *La Grande Illusion* (1937) and *The Rules of the Game* (1939) that explored class, war, and the complexity of human motives. Forced to flee to Hollywood during World War II, he later returned to Europe and India, pioneering color filmmaking and television techniques while maintaining a career-long focus on the "beauty in our common humanity." His influence extends through the French New Wave and directors globally, earning him a Lifetime Achievement Academy Award in 1975.

## Body

### Early Life and Artistic Formation
Renoir was raised in an artistic household; his father’s financial success allowed a privileged but restless education at boarding schools from which he frequently ran away. A pivotal figure was Gabrielle Renard, his nanny and mother’s cousin, who introduced him to Guignol puppet shows and early motion pictures, teaching him to "see the face behind the mask and the fraud behind the flourishes." World War I interrupted his youth: he served in the cavalry, then as a reconnaissance pilot after a bullet wound left him with a permanent limp. During convalescence, he discovered cinema, watching Chaplin and D.W. Griffith. After the war, he briefly attempted ceramic art at his father’s suggestion but pivoted to film, partly to advance the acting career of his first wife, Catherine Hessling, his father’s last model. He cited Erich von Stroheim as a key early inspiration.

Renoir was the uncle of cinematographer Claude Renoir (1913–1993), the son of his elder brother Pierre. With Claude, he made *The River* (1951), the first color film shot in India.

### The 1930s: Poetic Realism and Political Engagement
Renoir’s maturity coincided with the advent of sound. His early talkies, *On purge bébé* and *La Chienne* (1931), displayed a growing mastery of ensemble dynamics. *Boudu Saved from Drowning* (1932) satirized bourgeois pretensions, while *Toni* (1935)—shot on location with non-actors—presaged the Italian Neorealism and French New Wave movements. By mid-decade, Renoir aligned with the Popular Front, a left-wing coalition. Films like *The Crime of Monsieur Lange* (1935), *Life Belongs to Us* (1936), and *La Marseillaise* (1938) reflected this politics, blending collective action with individual frailty.

His peak came with two masterpieces. *La Grande Illusion* (1937), a World War I prisoner-of-war drama starring Jean Gabin and Erich von Stroheim, examined class solidarity transcending national borders. It won Best Artistic Ensemble at Venice, became the first foreign-language film nominated for Best Picture, and was banned by the Nazis and Fascist Italy. *The Rules of the Game* (1939), a satirical ensemble piece set during a hunting weekend, exposed the "awful thing" that "everyone has his reasons." Renoir played Octave, the connective thread across social strata. The film was savaged at its premiere, banned by the pre-war French government (suspicious of Renoir’s pacifism and Communist sympathies), and its original negative later destroyed in an Allied bombing raid. It was reconstructed in the 1950s and subsequently canonized.

### Hollywood Exile and Return to Europe
Following the 1940 German invasion, Renoir fled to the U.S. with Dido Freire (later his second wife), sharing a cabin with Antoine de Saint-Exupéry. Hollywood proved frustrating: *Swamp Water* (1941) was competent, but *The Amazing Mrs. Holliday* saw him replaced as director. He found footing with the anti-Nazi *This Land Is Mine* (1943) and achieved his greatest U.S. success with *The Southerner* (1945), earning a Directing Oscar nomination. However, *Diary of a Chambermaid* (1946) and *The Woman on the Beach* (1947)—the latter heavily reshot after poor previews—were critical failures, ending his American phase. He became a naturalized U.S. citizen during this period.

Renoir traveled to India in 1949 to shoot *The River* (1951), his first color film. A meditation on nature and coming-of-age in colonial India, it won the Venice International Prize. Back in Europe, he embraced color for a trilogy of musical comedies on theater (*The Golden Coach*, 1953), politics (*French Cancan*, 1954), and commerce (*Elena and Her Men*, 1956). He simultaneously produced stage plays, including his own *Orvet*.

### Late Innovation and Literary Turn
In the late 1950s, Renoir adapted live television techniques—multi-camera setups, faster shooting—for *Picnic on the Grass* (1959), filmed at his father’s Cagnes-sur-Mer estate, and *The Testament of Doctor Cordelier* (1959), shot on Paris streets. *The Elusive Corporal* (1962) returned to WWII POW themes, exploring the tension between the desire for freedom and the need for security. As film financing dried up, Renoir turned to writing: a memoir of his father, *Renoir, My Father* (1962); the novel *The Notebooks of Captain Georges* (1966); and his autobiography, *My Life and My Films* (1974). His final film, *The Little Theatre of Jean Renoir* (1970), comprised three avant-garde shorts. He spent his last years in Beverly Hills, dying of a heart attack in 1979.

## Terms
- ****Auteur**** — A filmmaker whose personal influence and artistic control over a movie are so great that they are regarded as the author of the film; Renoir was one of the first to be recognized as such.
- ****Poetic Realism**** — A 1930s French film style blending realistic settings with lyrical, often fatalistic storytelling; Renoir was a leading practitioner.
- ****Popular Front**** — A coalition of left-wing parties in 1930s France; Renoir’s mid-decade films reflected its anti-fascist, pro-worker politics.
- ****Deep Focus**** — A cinematographic technique keeping foreground, middle-ground, and background simultaneously in sharp focus; Renoir used it to capture ensemble action within a single shot.
- ****Guignol**** — Traditional French puppet theater originating in Lyon; Renoir’s childhood exposure via nanny Gabrielle Renard shaped his view of performance and social masks.
- ****French New Wave**** — Late 1950s/60s movement (Truffaut, Godard, etc.) emphasizing personal authorship and location shooting; Renoir’s *Toni* and humanism were key precursors.
- ****Ensemble Cast**** — A narrative structure distributing focus across multiple principal characters rather than a single protagonist; central to *The Rules of the Game* and *La Grande Illusion*.
- ****Humanist Cinema**** — An informal genre prioritizing empathy, moral complexity, and the dignity of ordinary people; critics position Renoir as its paramount model.

## Debates and open questions
- **Political commitment vs. artistic detachment:** Critics debate whether Renoir’s Popular Front films constitute genuine political cinema or a humanism that transcends ideology. His pacifism and Communist sympathies drew government suspicion, yet *The Rules of the Game* critiques all classes equally.
- **Hollywood period assessment:** Scholars disagree on the merit of Renoir’s American films. *The Southerner* is widely praised, but *The Woman on the Beach* and *Diary of a Chambermaid* are often dismissed as compromised studio products, though some argue they adapt his style to genre constraints.
- **Restoration of *The Rules of the Game*:** The 1950s reconstruction by Jean Gaborit and Jacques Durand, with Renoir’s cooperation, is considered near-complete, but the destruction of the original negative leaves uncertainty about the exact 1939 premiere cut.
- **Late style: decline or evolution?**: The television-influenced films of 1959 and the avant-garde *Little Theatre* (1970) are viewed either as innovative adaptations to new media/budget constraints or as a falling off from his 1930s peak.
- **Influence lineage:** While Renoir’s impact on the French New Wave (Truffaut named his company after *The Golden Coach*) and directors like Altman and Scorsese is acknowledged, the specific mechanisms—whether visual style, narrative structure, or ethical stance—remain a subject of critical analysis.
