# North Sea

## Overview
The North Sea is a shallow, epicontinental sea on the European continental shelf, bordered by the UK, Norway, Denmark, Germany, the Netherlands, Belgium, and France. It is a vital hub for global shipping, fisheries, and energy resources, and has been a central stage for European geopolitical and military conflicts from the Viking Age to the World Wars. Today, it is a major source of oil, gas, and renewable wind energy, but faces significant environmental pressures from human activity.

## Timeline
- **c. 8150–6000 BCE** — Storegga Slides cause a major tsunami in the North Sea.
- **43 CE** — Roman conquest of Britain increases North Sea shipping and trade.
- **793** — Viking Age begins with the attack on Lindisfarne; Vikings dominate the North Sea for ~250 years.
- **13th century** — Hanseatic League begins to control most North Sea trade.
- **1652–1673** — Anglo-Dutch Wars fought for control of North Sea trade routes.
- **1904** — Dogger Bank incident: Russian fleet fires on British fishing boats.
- **1916** — Battle of Jutland, the largest naval battle of World War I, fought in the North Sea.
- **1953** — North Sea Flood kills over 2,000 people, prompting major coastal defense upgrades.
- **1969** — Ekofisk oil field discovered, marking the start of large-scale offshore oil and gas exploitation.
- **2002** — Horns Rev 1, one of the world's first large-scale offshore wind farms, is completed.

## Body

### Geography and Hydrology
Over 970 km long and 580 km wide, with a mean depth of 90 metres, the North Sea connects to the Atlantic via the English Channel and to the Norwegian Sea. It lies mostly on the continental shelf, except for the deep Norwegian trench. Key features include the shallow Dogger Bank fishing ground and areas like the Long Forties. Water circulation is anti-clockwise, driven by Atlantic currents. Average summer and winter temperatures are 17°C and 6°C, with salinity averaging 34–35 g/L.

### Coasts and Coastal Management
Coastlines vary dramatically: Norway has deep fjords, while southern coasts are low-lying and marshy. Coastal management has a long history, with artificial dwelling hills built as early as 500 BC and connected dike lines emerging around 1200 AD. Modern defenses, like those in the Netherlands where 27% of land is below sea level, use reinforced dikes and dune planting. Storm surges are a persistent threat, notably the 1953 flood and the 1228 flood that killed over 100,000 people.

### Geology and Nature
The North Sea basin has existed for millions of years, with its precursor sea growing and shrinking with sea-level changes. During glacial periods, it was often dry land known as Doggerland. The sea is biologically rich, supporting over 230 fish species, including commercially vital cod, herring, and mackerel. It is also home to marine mammals like seals and porpoises, and millions of seabirds. However, heavy industrialization has led to biodiversity loss, including the extinction of grey whales and walruses in the region.

### History and Geopolitics
The North Sea has been a conduit for migration, trade, and conflict. After the Roman era, Germanic tribes migrated across it. The Vikings used it as a base for raids and trade. From the Middle Ages, control of its trade routes was contested by the Hanseatic League, the Dutch Republic, and Britain. In the 20th century, it was the primary theatre for naval warfare in both World Wars, with Britain's fleet blockading Germany. Post-WWII, its military significance declined as it became surrounded by NATO members, but its economic importance soared with the discovery of oil and gas.

### Economy and Resources
The North Sea is a major economic zone. Its oil and gas fields, like Ekofisk and Troll, are crucial to European energy security, with Brent Crude serving as a global price benchmark. Fishing accounts for over 5% of international commercial catch, though overfishing threatens stocks. The sea is also a global shipping hub, with ports like Rotterdam among the world's busiest. Since the 1990s, it has become a leader in offshore wind energy, hosting massive projects like the London Array and the planned Dogger Bank wind farm.

### Critical Maritime Infrastructure and Environment
The seabed hosts a dense network of critical infrastructure: ~71,000 km of subsea cables, pipelines for oil and gas, and offshore wind farms. This infrastructure is vital for energy and communications but is vulnerable to accidental damage from anchors or fishing, and deliberate sabotage. Environmental issues are severe, including pollution, eutrophication, and habitat loss from trawling. International agreements like the OSPAR Convention and the MARPOL Accords aim to protect the marine environment.

## Terms
- **Epicontinental sea**: A shallow sea that lies on a continental shelf.
- **Continental shelf**: The extended perimeter of a continent and associated coastal plain, covered by relatively shallow sea.
- **Moraine**: An accumulation of unconsolidated glacial debris.
- **Amphidromic system**: A pattern of tidal circulation around a point of zero tidal range.
- **Doggerland**: A landmass that connected Great Britain to continental Europe during periods of lower sea level.
- **Eutrophication**: Excessive richness of nutrients in a body of water, frequently due to runoff, causing dense plant growth and oxygen depletion.
- **OSPAR Commission**: The body that manages the OSPAR Convention for the protection of the marine environment of the North-East Atlantic.
- **Critical Maritime Infrastructure (CMI)**: Offshore and subsea assets (e.g., cables, pipelines, wind farms) whose disruption could have significant societal and economic effects.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise boundaries and management of fishing rights and mineral resources in the North Sea have been subjects of ongoing international negotiation and legal judgment, such as the 1969 ICJ ruling on the continental shelf.
- The environmental impact of expanding offshore wind farms versus their benefits for decarbonization is a subject of ongoing study and public concern.
- The vulnerability of critical subsea infrastructure to deliberate attack, especially in the context of geopolitical tensions, is a growing security concern for North Sea states.
