# Herod the Great

## Overview
Herod the Great (c. 72–4/1 BCE) was a Roman client king of Judea whose reign marked the end of the Hasmonean dynasty and the beginning of the Herodian dynasty. He is remembered for massive building projects, including the expansion of the Second Temple, and for a rule that combined political acumen with notorious brutality. His legacy is polarizing, viewed as both a great builder and a tyrant by ancient and modern historians.

## Timeline
- **c. 72 BCE** — Born in Idumea to Antipater the Idumaean and Cypros, a Nabatean princess.
- **c. 47 BCE** — Appointed governor of Galilee by Rome.
- **40 BCE** — Named King of the Jews by the Roman Senate.
- **37/36 BCE** — Captures Jerusalem, ending Hasmonean rule and beginning his sole reign.
- **35 BCE** — Orders the drowning of his brother-in-law, High Priest Aristobulus III.
- **31 BCE** — Defeats Antigonus with Roman support; executes him.
- **29 BCE** — Executes his Hasmonean wife, Mariamne I.
- **c. 20 BCE** — Begins massive expansion of the Second Temple in Jerusalem.
- **9 BCE** — Inaugurates the new city and harbor of Caesarea Maritima.
- **7 BCE** — Executes his sons Alexander and Aristobulus for treason.
- **4 or 1 BCE** — Dies after a painful illness; kingdom divided among his sons.

## Biography and Rise to Power
Herod was of Idumean (Edomite) and Nabatean Arab descent, though his family had been forcibly converted to Judaism. He rose to power through his father Antipater's alliance with Rome, serving as governor of Galilee. After the Parthian-backed Antigonus seized the Judean throne in 40 BCE, Herod fled to Rome, where the Senate unexpectedly appointed him king. With Roman military support, he conquered Jerusalem in 37 or 36 BCE, overthrowing the Hasmonean dynasty and establishing his own rule.

## Reign in Judea
Herod's rule was characterized by a constant balancing act between serving Roman interests and managing his Jewish subjects. He maintained power through a network of spies, a large personal bodyguard, and the ruthless elimination of threats, including members of his own family like his wife Mariamne I and his sons. His relationship with Rome was crucial; after Mark Antony's defeat, he successfully secured the favor of Octavian (Augustus). His reign saw immense building projects, most notably the expansion of the Second Temple, which aimed to gain Jewish support but was funded by heavy taxation. His introduction of pagan elements and disregard for some Jewish sensibilities fueled resentment, which erupted into riots after his death.

## Architectural Achievements
Herod was a prolific builder. His most famous project was the expansion of the Second Temple in Jerusalem, which doubled the size of the Temple Mount platform using advanced engineering. The Western Wall is a remnant of this retaining wall. He also built the port city of Caesarea Maritima with innovative hydraulic cement, and constructed fortresses like Masada and Herodium. These projects served both to display his power and to provide employment, though they strained the kingdom's finances.

## New Testament References and Death
The Gospel of Matthew describes Herod ordering the Massacre of the Innocents to kill the infant Jesus, an event most modern scholars dismiss as a literary device not corroborated by other sources. Herod died in Jericho after a severe illness. According to Josephus, he attempted suicide and, fearing no one would mourn him, ordered prominent men to be executed upon his death to ensure public grief—a command not carried out. The date of his death is debated, with traditional scholarship placing it in 4 BCE and more recent arguments favoring 1 BCE.

## Successors and Tomb
Augustus divided Herod's kingdom among three sons: Herod Archelaus (ethnarch of Judea), Herod Antipas (tetrarch of Galilee), and Philip (tetrarch of northern territories). Archelaus was later deposed by Rome. Herod's tomb was discovered in 2007 at Herodium, though its identification is debated by some archaeologists.

## Terms
- **Client King:** A ruler who governs a territory under the authority and protection of a larger empire (in this case, Rome).
- **Hasmonean Dynasty:** The Jewish royal dynasty that ruled Judea before Herod's conquest.
- **Tetrarch:** A ruler of a fourth part of a region; a title given to Herod's sons for their territories.
- **Ethnarch:** A ruler of a people or nation, a title slightly lower than king.
- **Sanhedrin:** The supreme Jewish council and tribunal in Judea.
- **Mikveh:** A ritual bath used for Jewish purification.
- **Hydraulic Cement:** A specialized concrete that sets underwater, used by Herod in harbor construction.
- **Toparchy:** A small district or province under a local ruler.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Date of Death:** The traditional date is 4 BCE, based on the reign lengths of his sons. Recent scholarship argues for 1 BCE, citing coin evidence and chronological issues with the siege of Jerusalem.
- **Historicity of the Massacre of the Innocents:** The event is only recorded in Matthew and is widely considered by scholars to be a theological narrative rather than a historical fact.
- **Identification of Herod's Tomb:** While the discovery at Herodium is widely accepted, some archaeologists argue the tomb is too modest to be Herod's.
- **Ethnicity and Religious Identity:** Scholars debate the extent of Herod's Arab versus Jewish identity and the sincerity of his Jewish observance, given his Idumean background and pagan building projects.
