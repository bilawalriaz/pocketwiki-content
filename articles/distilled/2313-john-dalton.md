# John Dalton

## Overview
John Dalton (1766–1844) was an English scientist whose work established the foundations of modern atomic theory and stoichiometric chemistry. He proposed that each element consists of identical atoms of characteristic weight, and that compounds form when atoms combine in fixed whole-number ratios. His quantitative framework transformed chemistry from a qualitative to a mathematical science and remains the basis of modern chemical thought. He was also a pioneering meteorologist and the first to scientifically describe color blindness.

- **27 July 1844** — Died in Manchester, England.

## Body

### Meteorology and Gas Laws
Dalton's early scientific work was in meteorology, influenced by Quaker Elihu Robinson. He began a daily weather diary in 1787, making over 200,000 observations. He also measured mountain heights using barometers. His "Experimental Essays" (1801) explored the constitution of mixed gases and vapor pressure. He enunciated the law that all elastic fluids expand equally by heat (later known as Gay-Lussac's Law). His work on gas absorption led to his law of partial pressures, which states that the total pressure of a mixture of gases is the sum of the pressures each gas would exert alone.

### Color Blindness
In 1794, Dalton presented a paper on his own deficient color perception, which he and his brother shared. He theorized it was caused by discoloration of the eyeball's liquid medium. Although his theory was incorrect, his research was foundational. A 1995 examination of his preserved eyeball confirmed he had deuteranopia, a congenital red-green color blindness. The condition is still called "Daltonism" in several languages.

### Atomic Theory
Dalton's most important contribution was his atomic theory, published in *A New System of Chemical Philosophy* (1808). The theory posits that elements are made of identical, indivisible atoms; atoms of different elements have different weights; and compounds form from atoms combining in simple whole-number ratios. To calculate relative atomic weights, he used a "rule of greatest simplicity," assuming the simplest possible formulas for compounds (e.g., water as OH, ammonia as NH). While some formulas were incorrect, the core principles survived. His theory provided the first method for calculating relative atomic weights, a crucial innovation uncontested by predecessors like Bryan Higgins.

### Relative Atomic Weights and Notation
Dalton published the first table of relative atomic weights for six elements (hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, carbon, sulfur, phosphorus), using hydrogen as the standard (weight = 1). He developed a system of circular symbols to represent atoms and molecules, which he used to depict 21 elements and 17 simple molecules. He later resisted the more convenient chemical notation devised by Jöns Jacob Berzelius.

### Experimental Approach and Legacy
Dalton was known as a "coarse experimenter" who relied on his intellect over precise instruments, though modern replications confirm his skill. He was independent, often distrusting others' results. His work earned him election to the Royal Society (1822) and a government pension. He died in 1844 after a series of strokes, receiving a civic funeral. His legacy includes the dalton (Da) unit of atomic mass, numerous institutions named after him, and the enduring framework of atomic theory.

## Terms
- **Atomic theory**: The scientific theory that matter is composed of discrete units called atoms, which combine in fixed ratios to form compounds.
- **Stoichiometry**: The calculation of reactants and products in chemical reactions based on the conservation of mass and fixed proportions.
- **Law of partial pressures**: Dalton's Law stating that the total pressure exerted by a mixture of non-reacting gases is equal to the sum of the partial pressures of individual gases.
- **Relative atomic weight**: The ratio of the average mass of atoms of an element to 1/12 the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
- **Deuteranopia**: A type of congenital red-green color blindness where the medium-wavelength (green) photopsin is missing.
- **Rule of greatest simplicity**: Dalton's assumption that the simplest possible atomic ratios form compounds, used to deduce molecular formulas.
- **Caloric**: A historical, incorrect theory of heat as a self-repellent fluid.
- **Binary compound**: A compound composed of two atoms (one of each element) in its simplest form, per Dalton's classification.

## Debates and Open questions
- The origin of Dalton's atomic theory is debated. It may have been suggested by his research on gas composition or by the ideas of Irish chemist Bryan Higgins, though Dalton's method for calculating atomic weights was his unique contribution.
- A study of his notebooks concluded his theory arose from studying the physical properties of gases, not from explaining the law of multiple proportions. Another study suggests it was developed to reconcile analytical data on nitric acid.
- Dalton's "rule of greatest simplicity" was an unproven assumption necessary for his calculations, leading to incorrect formulas for water and ammonia.
