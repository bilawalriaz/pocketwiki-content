# Mahatma Gandhi

## Overview
Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (1869–1948) was an Indian lawyer, anti-colonial nationalist, and political ethicist who pioneered *satyagraha* (nonviolent resistance) as a mass political tool. After developing his philosophy during 21 years in South Africa fighting racial discrimination, he returned to India in 1915 and led the Indian National Congress in a decades-long campaign for *swaraj* (self-rule). His strategy combined civil disobedience—such as the 1930 Salt March—with constructive programmes addressing poverty, untouchability, and Hindu-Muslim unity. Gandhi’s insistence on religious pluralism clashed with the Muslim League’s demand for a separate Pakistan; the 1947 partition triggered horrific communal violence, which he tried to halt through fasting. He was assassinated in 1948 by a Hindu nationalist who viewed his protection of Muslims as betrayal. Gandhi remains a global symbol of nonviolent protest, honored in India as the "Father of the Nation."

## Timeline
- **2 Oct 1869** — Born in Porbandar, Gujarat, British India.
- **1888–1891** — Studies law at Inner Temple, London; called to the bar.
- **1893–1914** — Lives in South Africa; develops *satyagraha* fighting anti-Indian laws; founds Natal Indian Congress (1894) and *Indian Opinion* (1903); leads ambulance corps in Boer War (1900) and Bambatha Rebellion (1906).
- **1906** — First mass *satyagraha* against Transvaal Asiatic Registration Act (Black Act).
- **1915** — Returns to India at Gopal Krishna Gokhale’s request; joins Indian National Congress.
- **1917–1918** — Leads Champaran (indigo peasants) and Kheda (tax relief) agitations; first major Indian successes.
- **1919–1922** — Non-cooperation Movement; supports Khilafat Movement; arrested 1922, sentenced to six years.
- **1930** — Salt March (12 Mar–6 Apr); 388 km march to Dandi to defy salt tax; ~60,000 imprisoned.
- **1931** — Gandhi–Irwin Pact; attends Second Round Table Conference in London.
- **1932** — Fasts unto death against Communal Award; Poona Pact reserves seats for untouchables within Hindu electorate.
- **1942** — Launches Quit India Movement ("Do or Die"); arrested; released 1944 for health.
- **15 Aug 1947** — Indian independence and partition; Gandhi fasts in Calcutta to stop riots.
- **30 Jan 1948** — Assassinated in Delhi by Nathuram Godse, a Hindu nationalist.

## Body

### Early Life and London Years
Born into a Gujarati Hindu Modh Bania family, Gandhi was the son of Karamchand Uttamchand Gandhi, *dewan* (chief minister) of Porbandar state. His mother, Putlibai, a devout Pranami Vaishnava, deeply influenced his religious eclecticism. Married at 13 to Kasturbai (Kasturba), he left for London in 1888 after vowing to abstain from meat, alcohol, and women. At University College and Inner Temple, he overcame shyness through a public speaking group, encountered the London Vegetarian Society and Theosophists, and read the *Bhagavad Gita* seriously for the first time. Called to the bar in 1891, he returned to India but failed to establish a law practice due to an inability to cross-examine witnesses.

### South Africa: The Crucible of Satyagraha (1893–1914)
A one-year contract for Dada Abdullah in Natal became a 21-year residence. Immediate racial humiliations—ejection from a first-class train compartment at Pietermaritzburg, beatings, court orders to remove his turban—shifted his self-conception from "Briton first, Indian second" to a fighter for Indian dignity. He founded the Natal Indian Congress (1894) to oppose disfranchisement. During the Boer War (1900) he organized the Natal Indian Ambulance Corps to disprove stereotypes of Hindu effeminacy. The 1906 Transvaal Asiatic Registration Act (requiring fingerprinting and passes) prompted the first mass *satyagraha* campaign; Gandhi defined *satyagraha* as "devotion to truth" and nonviolent non-cooperation. He also served as a stretcher-bearer during the 1906 Bambatha Rebellion, though his early writings sometimes echoed racial hierarchies distinguishing Indians from Africans. By 1910 he established Tolstoy Farm near Johannesburg as a communal laboratory for simple living and resistance.

### Return to India and Early Campaigns (1915–1922)
Mentored by moderate Congress leader Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Gandhi transformed liberal constitutionalism into a mass movement rooted in Indian cultural idioms. His first victories were the 1917 Champaran agitation (Bihar indigo peasants forced to grow a dying cash crop) and the 1918 Kheda tax revolt (Gujarat famine relief), where Vallabhbhai Patel emerged as a key lieutenant. In 1919 he backed the Khilafat Movement (Sunni Muslim defence of the Ottoman Caliph) to forge Hindu-Muslim unity, but the alliance collapsed after the 1922 Chauri Chaura violence led him to suspend the Non-cooperation Movement. He was arrested for sedition and served two years of a six-year sentence.

### Civil Disobedience and the Salt March (1930–1934)
After pressing for dominion status, Gandhi launched the 1930 Salt Satyagraha, marching 388 km from Ahmedabad to Dandi to make salt illegally. The campaign drew global attention; the brutal suppression of protesters at Dharasana salt works and the imprisonment of ~60,000 Indians pressured the British. The Gandhi–Irwin Pact (1931) freed prisoners and sent him as sole Congress representative to the Second Round Table Conference in London, where he opposed separate electorates for minorities as divisive. Back in India, he fasted unto death in 1932 against the Communal Award granting untouchables a separate electorate; the resulting Poona Pact (with B.R. Ambedkar) secured reserved seats within a joint Hindu electorate. Gandhi resigned from Congress in 1934 to avoid stifling internal debate.

### World War II, Quit India, and Partition (1939–1947)
Gandhi opposed Indian participation in WWII without a freedom pledge. The 1942 Quit India Movement ("Do or Die") triggered mass arrests (Gandhi held 1942–44) and violent unrest. Meanwhile, Muhammad Ali Jinnah’s Muslim League, cooperating with the British, advanced the Pakistan demand. Gandhi’s 1944 talks with Jinnah failed. The 1946 Direct Action Day sparked Calcutta killings and a cycle of retaliatory violence. Viceroy Wavell and later Mountbatten accepted partition; Gandhi opposed it but participated in final negotiations. Independence on 15 August 1947 brought 10–12 million refugees and 500,000+ deaths. Gandhi fasted in Calcutta and Delhi to quell riots, earning the enmity of Hindu extremists.

### Principles: Truth, Nonviolence, and Brahmacharya
Gandhi’s ethics synthesized Jain vows (*satya*, *ahimsa*, *brahmacharya*, *asteya*, *aparigraha*) with Hindu *Advaita Vedanta* (non-dualism: "Truth is God"). *Satyagraha* ("soul force") politicized *ahimsa*: not passive but active resistance accepting suffering to convert the opponent. He insisted nonviolence required the *capacity* for violence (hence his 1918 recruitment drive), preferring violence to cowardice. *Brahmacharya* (celibacy/self-restraint) became a public experimental vow from 1906; after Kasturba’s death (1944) he tested it by sleeping naked with young women associates (including grandniece Manu), drawing criticism from colleagues and family. He also advocated prohibition and *swadeshi* (boycott of foreign goods, especially British cloth).

### Literary Output
A prolific writer in Gujarati, Hindi, and English, Gandhi edited *Indian Opinion* (South Africa), *Young India*, *Navajivan*, and *Harijan*. *Hind Swaraj* (1909) became the independence movement’s "intellectual blueprint." His autobiography, *The Story of My Experiments with Truth*, details his ethical evolution. The *Collected Works* span ~50,000 pages in 100 volumes.

### Death and Legacy
Assassinated 30 January 1948 by Nathuram Godse (Hindu Mahasabha/RSS links). Over a million attended the funeral; ashes immersed at multiple sites globally. Commemorated as Gandhi Jayanti (2 Oct) and International Day of Nonviolence. Inspired Martin Luther King Jr., Nelson Mandela, and countless movements.

## Terms
- **Satyagraha**: "Devotion to truth"; Gandhi’s method of nonviolent non-cooperation and civil disobedience grounded in the conviction that moral force ("soul force") can confront injustice without hatred.
- **Ahimsa**: Nonviolence; the Jain/Hindu principle of causing no harm to living beings in thought, word, or deed, elevated by Gandhi to a political strategy.
- **Swaraj**: Self-rule; Gandhi’s term for independence, implying not just political sovereignty but individual and communal self-discipline.
- **Brahmacharya**: Celibacy/continence; one of the five great vows, extended by Gandhi to dietary and sensory control as spiritual discipline.
- **Swadeshi**: "Of one’s own country"; economic boycott of foreign (especially British) goods and institutions to foster self-reliance.
- **Untouchability**: The caste-based practice of ostracizing Dalits (formerly "untouchables"); Gandhi campaigned against it, calling them *Harijans* ("children of God").
- **Khilafat Movement** (1919–1924): Indian Muslim campaign to preserve the Ottoman Caliphate; Gandhi supported it to cement Hindu-Muslim unity.
- **Communal Award** (1932): British decree granting separate electorates to minorities, including untouchables; Gandhi’s fast forced the Poona Pact compromise.
- **Quit India Movement** (1942): Mass civil disobedience demanding immediate British withdrawal; slogan "Do or Die."
- **Partition** (1947): Division of British India into Hindu-majority India and Muslim-majority Pakistan (West and East), accompanied by massive violence and displacement.

## Debates and open questions
- **Consistency of nonviolence**: Critics (including his private secretary) note the tension between *ahimsa* and his 1918 WWI combatant recruitment drive; Gandhi argued true nonviolence requires the *capacity* for violence.
- **Race views in South Africa**: Early statements (e.g., 1896 "raw Kaffir" remark, 1895 "Aryan stock" argument) suggest Gandhi initially accepted racial hierarchies distinguishing Indians from Black Africans; scholars debate whether he transcended this or remained complicit.
- **Brahmacharya experiments**: The nature and ethics of his post-1944 celibacy tests with young women (including teenagers) remain contested—viewed by some as spiritual discipline, by others as abuse of power.
- **Partition responsibility**: Gandhi opposed partition but participated in negotiations; Stanley Wolpert notes he never approved the final plan. Debate continues on whether his insistence on unity or his tactics inadvertently enabled partition.
- **Ambedkar vs. Gandhi on caste**: Ambedkar dismissed Gandhi’s *Harijan* approach as paternalistic; Gandhi’s 1932 fast against separate electorates forced the Poona Pact, which Ambedkar later called coercive.
- **Churchill’s critique**: Winston Churchill’s vitriolic attacks ("seditious Middle Temple lawyer," "Hindu Mussolini") shaped British and Western perceptions; historians assess their impact on policy and Gandhi’s global image.
