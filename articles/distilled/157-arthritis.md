# Arthritis

## Overview

Arthritis is a general medical term for a disorder in which the smooth cartilaginous layer lining a joint is lost, causing bone to grind on bone during movement. Symptoms generally include joint pain and stiffness, and may include redness, warmth, swelling, and decreased range of motion; some types also affect other organs like the skin. The most common forms are osteoarthritis (wear-and-tear, typically in weight-bearing joints) and rheumatoid arthritis (an autoimmune disorder), with other types including gout, lupus, spondyloarthritis, and septic arthritis. There is no known cure, but treatment ranges from rest, exercise, and medications to joint replacement surgery; arthritis is a leading cause of disability, affecting over 20% of US adults.

## Body

### Classification

Arthritis encompasses several diseases where joint pain is the most prominent symptom, including hemarthrosis, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, gout and pseudogout, septic arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, Still's disease, and psoriatic arthritis. Joint pain can also be a symptom of other diseases without arthritis itself, such as psoriasis, reactive arthritis, Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, hepatitis, Lyme disease, celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, sarcoidosis, systemic lupus erythematosus, and many others. An undifferentiated arthritis does not fit into well-known clinical categories and may be an early stage of a definite rheumatic disease.

### Signs and symptoms

Pain of varying severity is common in most types of arthritis. Other symptoms include swelling, joint stiffness, redness, and aching around the joint. Arthritic disorders like lupus and rheumatoid arthritis can affect other organs, leading to inability to use the hand or walk, stiffness in one or more joints, rash or itch, malaise and fatigue, weight loss, poor sleep, muscle aches, tenderness, and difficulty moving the joint.

### Causes

Causes differ by type. Osteoarthritis results from damage to joint cartilage from prior injury or long-term wear-and-tear, causing bone-to-bone contact and grinding; inflammation and swelling may add to the extent and pain if cartilage is severely damaged. In rheumatoid arthritis, the immune system attacks the lining of the joint capsule, causing inflammation and swelling. Gout is caused by excessive uric acid production, depositing urate crystals in joints, particularly extremities like toes; urate levels may rise from purine-rich foods or body factors affecting urate clearance, a topic remaining under study. Other types include ankylosing spondylitis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, and reactive arthritis.

### Risk factors

Common risk factors increase the chance of developing arthritis later in adulthood; some are modifiable, others not. For osteoarthritis, risk factors include obesity, prior joint injury, type of joint, and muscle strength. The strongest risk factors for inflammatory arthritis (such as rheumatoid arthritis and lupus arthritis) are female sex, family history, age, obesity, prior joint damage, and exposure to tobacco smoke. Smoking is linked to increased susceptibility, particularly for rheumatoid arthritis.

### Diagnosis

Diagnosis is made by clinical examination from an appropriate health professional, supported by radiologic imaging and blood tests depending on the suspected type. Pain patterns vary: rheumatoid arthritis is generally worse in the morning with stiffness lasting over 30 minutes, while osteoarthritis pain initially relates to activity and becomes more constant over time. Key features include rate of onset, pattern of joint involvement, symmetry, early morning stiffness, tenderness, locking with inactivity, aggravating and relieving factors, and systemic symptoms. Physical examination may include observing joints, evaluating gait, and examining skin. Screening blood tests include rheumatoid factor, antinuclear factor (ANF), extractable nuclear antigen, and specific antibodies; elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) or C-reactive protein (CRP) indicates inflammation. Anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies and rheumatoid factor (RF) are common tests for rheumatoid arthritis. Imaging includes X-rays, computed tomography (CT), positron emission tomography (PET), bone scanning, and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA).

**Osteoarthritis (OA)** is the most common form, affecting humans and animals like dogs, cats, and horses. It results from cartilage breakdown causing bones to rub and erode each other. Symptoms begin with minor pain during activity and can progress to pain at rest. OA typically affects weight-bearing joints (back, knee, hip) and is more common in the elderly, with increased age the strongest predictor, likely due to declining chondrocytes (cartilage cells) maintaining cartilage. Over 30% of women have some degree of OA by age 65. X-ray findings include joint space narrowing, bone spurs, sclerosis, and bone cysts.

**Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)** is a disorder where the immune system attacks body tissues, specifically articular cartilage at bone ends, and many other body parts. RA often affects fingers, wrists, knees, and elbows symmetrically, and can cause severe progressive deformity within years if untreated. It usually onsets earlier than OA, commonly affecting people aged 20 and above; in children it can present with skin rash, fever, pain, disability, and limitations in daily activities. Inflammation of the synovium (joint capsule lining) is a main trigger of bone erosion, caused partly by pro-inflammatory cytokines and receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa B ligand (RANKL), a cell surface protein in Th17 cells and osteoblasts; osteoclast activity can be induced by osteoblasts through the RANK/RANKL mechanism.

**Lupus** is an autoimmune collagen vascular disorder that can present with severe arthritis; about 90% of patients have musculoskeletal involvement. Symptoms can mimic rheumatoid arthritis, with fingers, wrist, and knee most affected. Other features include skin rash, extreme photosensitivity, hair loss, kidney problems, and shortness of breath from lung scarring.

**Gout** usually affects one joint early on, then many over time, most commonly the big toe, knee, and/or fingers. Flares cause swelling, warmth, redness, and significant pain. Management uses anti-inflammatories (NSAIDs, colchicine, glucocorticoids); between flares, patients take medications decreasing uric acid production (allopurinol, febuxostat) or increasing elimination (probenecid). Gout is associated with excessive alcohol and red meat intake; a diet rich in fiber, vegetables, and whole grains while limiting alcohol and fatty foods is recommended. Pseudogout, an uncommon form, is caused by calcium pyrophosphate crystals; no targeted treatments exist, and management aims at decreasing inflammation.

**Other types**: Infectious (septic) arthritis occurs when bacteria spread through the bloodstream from elsewhere, causing chills, fever, and joint pain; it must be diagnosed and treated rapidly to prevent irreversible joint damage. Only about 1% of cases are viral; SARS-CoV-2 (causing COVID-19) tends to cause reactive arthritis rather than local septic arthritis. Psoriatic arthritis develops from psoriasis; most individuals first develop skin symptoms (scaly patches, itchiness), then joint symptoms. It can go into remission but has no cure; treatment uses immune suppressive medications. A small percentage develop a severely destructive form affecting hand joints, sometimes causing permanent disability.

### Treatment

There is no known cure. Options vary by type and include physical therapy, exercise and diet, orthopedic bracing, and oral and topical medications; joint replacement surgery may repair damage, restore function, or relieve pain.

**Physical therapy**: Exercise of the affected joint can noticeably improve long-term pain relief and is encouraged to maintain joint and overall health. Physical and occupational therapy significantly improve function, decrease pain, and delay surgical intervention; exercise prescribed by a physical therapist is more effective than medications for knee osteoarthritis. Exercise focuses on muscle strength, endurance, and flexibility, sometimes balance training. Assistive technology aids reduce physical barriers; devices can be customized or bought commercially.

**Medications**: Treatment begins with medications having fewest side effects, adding others if insufficient. For osteoarthritis, first-line treatment is acetaminophen (paracetamol); for inflammatory arthritis, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) like ibuprofen. Topical NSAIDs may have better safety profiles than oral ones; intra-articular corticosteroid injections may be considered for severe OA. For rheumatoid arthritis, treatments range from corticosteroids to monoclonal antibodies given intravenously, including disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs)—specifically csDMARDs, TNF biologics, and tsDMARDs. DMARDs slow RA progression by initiating an adaptive immune response, partly via CD4+ T helper (Th) cells, specifically Th17 cells, which produce inflammatory cytokines like interleukin-17 (IL-17).

**Surgery**: Surgical interventions have been used since the 1950s; the primary option is joint replacement (arthroplasty), commonly for shoulder, hip, and knee. Arthroscopic surgery for knee osteoarthritis provides no additional benefit compared to optimized physical and medical therapy. Joint replacement can last 15–30 years; patients can resume activities like swimming, tennis, and golf.

**Adaptive aids**: People with hand arthritis struggle with activities of daily living (ADLs) like turning keys or opening jars. Adaptive aids/assistive devices (ADs) help but are generally costlier than conventional products; 3-D printed aids released as open source hardware reduce costs. The vast majority of arthritis patients need and use them.

**Alternative medicine**: Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) for knee osteoarthritis requires further research. Low level laser therapy may relieve pain and stiffness with tentative evidence. Pulsed electromagnetic field therapy (PEMFT) has tentative evidence for improved functioning but not pain; the FDA has not approved it for arthritis, though Health Canada licenses PEMF devices for arthritic pain.

### Epidemiology

Arthritis is predominantly a disease of the elderly, but children can be affected. It is more common in women than men at all ages and affects all races and cultures. In the US (2013–2015), 54.4 million (22.7%) adults had doctor-diagnosed arthritis, and 23.7 million (43.5% of those) had arthritis-attributable activity limitation (AAAL); this number is expected to increase with an aging population. Adults with comorbidities—heart disease, diabetes, obesity—had higher prevalence (49.3%, 47.1%, 30.6% respectively). Disability from musculoskeletal disorders increased 45% from 1990 to 2010; osteoarthritis is the fastest increasing major health condition. African data are lacking; a systematic review found rheumatoid arthritis prevalence from 0.07% (rural South Africa) to 2.5% (urban South Africa), osteoarthritis up to 55.1% (urban South Africa) and 29.5–82.7% in South Africans aged 65+, knee osteoarthritis highest at 33.1% (rural South Africa), ankylosing spondylitis 0.1%, psoriatic arthritis 4.4%, gout 0.7%, and juvenile idiopathic arthritis 0.3%.

### History

Evidence of osteoarthritis and potentially inflammatory arthritis has been discovered in dinosaurs. The first known traces of human arthritis date to c. 4500 BC, frequently referred to as the most common ailment of prehistoric peoples; it was noted in skeletal remains of Native Americans in Tennessee and parts of Olathe, Kansas. Evidence spans from Ötzi the mummy (c. 3000 BC) to Egyptian mummies (c. 2590 BC). In 1703, William Musgrave published *De arthritide symptomatica*. Augustin Jacob Landré-Beauvais first described rheumatoid arthritis symptoms; though his classification as a relative of gout was inaccurate, his dissertation encouraged further study. John Charnley completed the first hip replacement in the 1960s.

### Society and culture

Arthritis is the most common cause of disability in the United States; over 20 million individuals have severe daily functional limitations. Absenteeism and frequent physician visits are common; some become homebound. In 2013, arthritis-attributable medical care costs and earnings losses totaled an estimated $303.5 billion.

### Terminology

The term derives from *arthr-* (Ancient Greek: ἄρθρον, 'joint') and *-itis* (-ῖτις, 'pertaining to'), the latter suffix associated with inflammation. The plural form *arthritides* denotes the collective group of arthritis-like conditions.

## Terms

- **Osteoarthritis (OA)**: Most common arthritis form; cartilage breakdown from wear-and-tear causing bone-on-bone contact, typically in weight-bearing joints.
- **Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)**: Autoimmune disorder where the immune system attacks joint capsule lining, often symmetrically in hands and feet.
- **Gout**: Arthritis from excessive uric acid forming urate crystals in joints, often the big toe.
- **Septic arthritis**: Infectious arthritis caused by bacteria spreading through the bloodstream, requiring rapid treatment.
- **DMARDs**: Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs that slow rheumatoid arthritis progression; includes csDMARDs, TNF biologics, and tsDMARDs.
- **Synovium**: Lining of the joint capsule; its inflammation triggers bone erosion in rheumatoid arthritis.
- **RANKL**: Receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa B ligand, a cell surface protein in Th17 cells and osteoblasts that induces osteoclast activity.
- **Arthroplasty**: Joint replacement surgery, the primary surgical treatment for arthritis.
- **Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)**: Blood test indicating inflammation, often elevated in rheumatoid arthritis.
- **Arthritides**: Plural form of arthritis, denoting the collective group of arthritis-like conditions.

## Debates and open questions

- The factors affecting urate clearance from the blood in gout remain under study.
- Whether transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) is effective for knee osteoarthritis pain requires further research.
- Evidence for low level laser therapy and pulsed electromagnetic field therapy (PEMFT) is tentative; PEMFT has evidence for improved functioning but not pain, and the FDA has not approved it for arthritis.
- Data on arthritis prevalence in Africa are lacking and underestimated, with many studies of moderate or poor quality.
