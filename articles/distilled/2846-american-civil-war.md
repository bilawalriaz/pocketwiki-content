# American Civil War

## Overview
The American Civil War (1861-1865) was a conflict between the United States ("the Union") and the secessionist Confederate States of America ("the Confederacy"). Fought primarily over slavery and states' rights, the Southern states seceded to preserve the institution of slavery. The war ended with a Union victory, the abolition of slavery, and the preservation of the United States as one nation.

- **Jun 19, 1865** — Juneteenth: Emancipation Proclamation enforced in Texas.
- **Aug 20, 1866** — President Johnson officially declares the war ended.

## Body

### Origins and Secession
The war's origins were rooted in the Southern states' desire to preserve slavery. Historians overwhelmingly agree slavery was the central cause for secession. The election of Abraham Lincoln, who opposed slavery's expansion, triggered the secession of seven Deep South states between December 1860 and February 1861. These states formed the Confederate States of America. The primary political battle leading to secession was over whether slavery would expand into western territories. The North's primary goal in rejecting secession was to preserve the Union.

### Outbreak of War
The war began on April 12, 1861, when Confederate forces bombarded Fort Sumter in South Carolina. Lincoln's call for 75,000 militiamen after the attack prompted four more states (Virginia, Tennessee, Arkansas, North Carolina) to secede. The border states of Maryland, Delaware, Missouri, and Kentucky remained in the Union, though with divided loyalties. Lincoln used federal power to maintain control in Maryland, including suspending habeas corpus.

### Military Campaigns and Strategy
The war was fought across multiple theaters. In the Eastern Theater, the Union's Army of the Potomac and the Confederacy's Army of Northern Virginia fought a series of bloody, inconclusive battles until the Union victory at Gettysburg in July 1863. In the Western Theater, Union forces under Ulysses S. Grant won key victories at Forts Henry and Donelson, Shiloh, and Vicksburg, splitting the Confederacy by controlling the Mississippi River.

The Union implemented the "Anaconda Plan," a naval blockade to strangle the Southern economy. In 1864, Grant, now commanding all Union armies, began a war of attrition against Lee in Virginia while General William Tecumseh Sherman conducted a "March to the Sea" through Georgia, destroying Confederate infrastructure. The war ended with Lee's surrender at Appomattox Court House on April 9, 1865.

### Emancipation and African American Soldiers
Abolishing slavery was not an initial Union war goal but became one as the conflict progressed. The Emancipation Proclamation, issued by Lincoln on January 1, 1863, declared slaves in rebel states to be free. It enabled the recruitment of African American soldiers; about 190,000 served in the Union Army, providing a crucial manpower advantage. The war culminated in the passage of the Thirteenth Amendment, which permanently abolished slavery.

### Home Fronts and Society
Both sides mobilized their populations through conscription, which was unpopular and led to riots and evasion. Women played vital roles as nurses, spies, and workers on the home front, taking on traditionally male jobs. The Southern economy collapsed due to the Union blockade, the loss of enslaved labor, and the destruction of infrastructure. The Union economy, by contrast, was bolstered by industrialization and government financing.

### End of the War and Aftermath
Following Lee's surrender, other Confederate forces surrendered through May and June 1865. President Lincoln was assassinated on April 14, 1865. The war left an estimated 698,000 soldiers dead and the South devastated. The period of Reconstruction (1865-1877) followed, aiming to rebuild the South, reintegrate it into the Union, and establish civil rights for freed slaves through the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments.

## Terms
- **Union**: The United States of America, the federal government that fought to preserve the nation.
- **Confederacy**: The Confederate States of America, the government formed by 11 seceding Southern states.
- **Secession**: The act of withdrawing formally from membership of a federation or body, especially a political state.
- **Emancipation Proclamation**: An executive order issued by President Lincoln on January 1, 1863, that declared the freedom of all enslaved people in Confederate-held territory.
- **Anaconda Plan**: The Union's overall war strategy, which included a naval blockade of Southern ports and control of the Mississippi River to split the Confederacy.
- **Border States**: Slave states that did not secede from the Union: Delaware, Kentucky, Maryland, and Missouri.
- **Ironclad**: A warship protected by thick iron or steel armor plates, exemplified by the USS Monitor and CSS Virginia.
- **Total War**: A military strategy that aims to destroy an enemy's ability to wage war by targeting its industry, infrastructure, and civilian population, as seen in Sherman's March.
- **Reconstruction**: The period (1865-1877) following the war during which the former Confederate states were reintegrated into the Union and laws were passed to establish the rights of freed slaves.
- **Lost Cause**: A pseudo-historical ideology that seeks to present the Confederate cause as just and heroic while minimizing the centrality of slavery to the conflict.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Could the Confederacy have won?** Most historians argue Confederate victory was possible but unlikely due to the Union's advantages in population and industry. The South's best strategy was to fight a defensive war to exhaust Northern will, but political and military setbacks, particularly after 1863, made this untenable.
- **The "Lost Cause" Myth:** This ideology, which denies slavery was the principal cause of secession, is disproven by historical evidence, including the seceding states' own declarations. Historians note it was a rationalization created to vindicate the Confederate cause and excuse postwar racism.
- **Impact of Emancipation:** Scholars debate the timing and motivations behind Lincoln's decision to make emancipation a war goal, weighing military necessity against moral conviction and political strategy.
