# Lewis Carroll

## Overview
Charles Lutwidge Dodgson (1832–1898), writing as Lewis Carroll, was a Victorian polymath: a Christ Church, Oxford mathematician and Anglican deacon who authored *Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland* (1865) and *Through the Looking-Glass* (1871), defining the genre of literary nonsense. His fiction grew from his facility with logic, wordplay, and fantasy, while his academic work contributed to geometry, matrix algebra, mathematical logic (notably the Method of Trees), and voting theory (Dodgson’s method). He was also a pioneering photographer (1856–1880), producing ~3,000 wet-collodion images, many of children, and an inventor of puzzles (the word-ladder “Doublets”), games, and devices (nyctograph, stamp case). His life is shadowed by missing diary volumes, a still-unexplained 1863 break with the Liddell family, and modern debates over his relationships with children and the nature of his sexuality, which scholars variously attribute to Victorian aesthetics, repressed desire, or biographical myth-making.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Dodgson was born into a conservative, high-church Anglican family of northern English clergy and army officers. His father, a mathematically gifted double-first graduate of Christ Church, became a country parson and later Archdeacon of Richmond, instilling Tractarian (Anglo-Catholic) views. Dodgson, the eldest boy of 11, showed precocious intellect (reading *The Pilgrim’s Progress* at seven) but developed a lifelong stammer (“hesitation”) and suffered childhood illnesses: deafness in one ear from fever, whooping cough at 17 leaving a weak chest, and later a knee injury. Educated at home, then Richmond School and Rugby (1846–49), he was scholastically brilliant but socially miserable, later recalling that “no earthly considerations would induce me to go through my three years again.” At Rugby he defended younger boys against bullies, earning a reputation for using his fists “in defence of a righteous cause.” He matriculated at Christ Church in 1850; two days into residence his mother died of “inflammation of the brain” (meningitis or stroke). His academic career oscillated between effortless brilliance (First in Moderations 1852, First in Finals 1854, top of the list) and procrastination (failing a scholarship exam 1855 through “inability to apply himself”). Nevertheless, his talent secured the Mathematical Lectureship in 1855, which he held until 1881.

### Character, Health, and Social World
Dodgson stood ~6 ft (1.83 m), slender, with curly brown hair and blue-grey eyes; later described as asymmetrical and stiff in gait. His stammer persisted throughout life; the popular notion that he spoke fluently only with children is unsupported—many children remembered the stammer, many adults did not notice it. He caricatured himself as the Dodo (play on “Do-do-dgson”), though whether this references his stammer is speculative. Despite the impediment, he was an engaging entertainer: a passable singer, mimic, storyteller, and charades player. In the 1850s–60s he entered the Pre-Raphaelite circle, befriending John Ruskin (1857), Dante Gabriel Rossetti and family (c. 1863), William Holman Hunt, John Everett Millais, and Arthur Hughes. He also knew fairy-tale author George MacDonald, whose children’s enthusiasm for the *Alice* manuscript prompted Dodgson to seek publication. Politically and religiously conservative (Tory, “stiffly conservative in political, theological, social theory”), he was ordained deacon in 1861 but declined priesthood, citing a profound sense of sin and unworthiness recorded in his diaries. He expressed orthodox Christian faith in private letters (“I owe all to Him who loved me, and died on the Cross of Calvary”) while doubting he was fully a “High Churchman.” He joined the Society for Psychical Research and accepted “thought reading” as real.

### Literature: The Alice Books and Beyond
Dodgson wrote from childhood, contributing to the family magazine *Mischmasch* and publishing humorous verse in *The Comic Times*, *The Train*, and others. In March 1856 he debuted the pseudonym “Lewis Carroll” (Latin *Carolus Ludovicus* → “Carroll Lewis” → reversed) chosen by editor Edmund Yates. The *Alice* books originated from his friendship with the family of Dean Henry Liddell (arrived 1856), especially the three sisters Lorina, Edith, and Alice. On 4 July 1862 he improvised the story on a rowing trip to Godstow; Alice Liddell begged him to write it down. He delivered the illustrated manuscript *Alice’s Adventures Under Ground* in November 1864. George MacDonald’s children’s reception encouraged publication; Macmillan accepted it in 1863. *Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland* appeared in 1865 with Sir John Tenniel’s illustrations. The sequel, *Through the Looking-Glass* (1871, title page misdated 1872), has a darker mood, possibly reflecting his father’s 1868 death and ensuing depression. Both works embed logic puzzles, acrostics (Alice Liddell’s name spelled out in the final poem of *Looking-Glass*), and satire on contemporary mathematics. *The Hunting of the Snark* (1876), a nonsense epic poem illustrated by Henry Holiday, sold 17 editions by 1908. *Sylvie and Bruno* (1889, 1893), a two-world fantasy satirising English academia, sold only 13,000 copies and was critically dismissed for its “babytalk” and intricacy. His 1895 article “What the Tortoise Said to Achilles” in *Mind* formulated a philosophical regress argument on deductive reasoning, still discussed (reprinted 1995 with Simon Blackburn’s commentary).

### Photography (1856–1880)
Influenced by his uncle Skeffington Lutwidge and Oxford friend Reginald Southey, Dodgson mastered the wet-collodion process, establishing a rooftop studio on Tom Quad. He created ~3,000 images; fewer than 1,000 survive ( ~60% deliberately destroyed). Roger Taylor and Edward Wakeling’s catalogue shows just over half of surviving prints depict young girls; 30 show nude or semi-nude children, taken with a parent present, often in the Liddell garden for natural light. He also photographed men, women, boys, landscapes, skeletons, dolls, and notable sitters: Millais, Ellen Terry, Rossetti, Julia Margaret Cameron, Michael Faraday, Lord Salisbury, Alfred Tennyson. Photography gave him entrée to higher social circles. He ceased abruptly in 1880 because studio maintenance was too time-consuming; commercial photographers had adopted the faster dry-plate process. Popular taste shifted toward Modernism, away from his staged, often blurred or hand-altered compositions.

### Inventions and Games
Dodgson invented the “Wonderland Postage-Stamp Case” (1889), a cloth folder with denomination slots and a pamphlet on letter-writing. The *nyctograph* (gridded card with a 16-symbol alphabet) allowed note-taking in the dark without striking a light. He devised an early Scrabble precursor, the word-ladder “Doublets” (1879, e.g., CAT→COT→DOT→DOG), published weekly in *Vanity Fair* for two years. Other inventions: a day-of-week calculator, typewriter margin-justifier, tricycle steering device, fairer tennis tournament elimination rules, postal money-order improvements, betting and division rules, a liqueur-measuring scale for Senior Common Room, double-sided adhesive strips, a bedside reading aid for invalids, and at least two cryptographic ciphers. In political theory he proposed Dodgson’s method (Condorcet voting) and an 1884 proportional-representation system with single transferable votes and multi-member districts (liquid democracy).

### Mathematical Work
As Mathematical Lecturer, Dodgson published nearly a dozen books under his real name in geometry, linear/matrix algebra, mathematical logic, and recreational mathematics. He produced the first printed proof of the Rouché–Capelli theorem (linear algebra). His *Dodgson condensation* method for evaluating determinants led Robbins and Rumsey to the alternating-sign-matrix conjecture (now theorem). In *Symbolic Logic Part II* (posthumous) he introduced the Method of Trees, the earliest modern truth-tree system. Rediscovered ciphers and his “Memoria Technica” (1990s) reveal sophisticated mathematical ideas in his recreational puzzles.

### Correspondence and Later Life
Dodgson logged 98,721 letters in a custom register and published *Eight or Nine Wise Words about Letter-Writing* (1890). His routine changed little after fame: he taught until 1881, remained in Christ Church residence, attended the 1886 West End musical *Alice in Wonderland*, and made only one foreign trip (ecclesiastical visit to Russia, 1867, with Rev. Henry Liddon, recording a “Russian Journal”). In his early sixties synovitis increasingly immobilized him, sometimes confining him to bed for months. He died of pneumonia following influenza on 14 January 1898 at his sisters’ home “The Chestnuts,” Guildford, and was buried at Mount Cemetery, Guildford.

### Controversies and Mysteries
**Missing Diaries:** At least four of 13 diary volumes and ~7 pages are missing (period 1853–1863, ages 21–31). Most scholars suspect family excision to protect the family name. The missing page for 27 June 1863 is central to the “break” with the Liddells. A “cut pages in diary” document (written posthumously by nephew Philip Dodgson Jacques from aunts’ accounts) states Mrs. Liddell told Dodgson of gossip concerning him and the governess and “Ina” (likely Lorina Liddell, Alice’s older sister or mother). This implies the break was not about Alice. A 1930 letter from younger Lorina to Alice recalls her mother spoke to Dodgson about his “too affectionate” manner toward Alice as she grew older, offending him so he ceased visits.

**Sexual Conduct Speculation:** Since the 1940s biographers (Cohen, Thomas, Bakewell) have speculated on an “erotic” element in Dodgson’s child friendships and nude photography. Cohen argues his “sexual energies sought unconventional outlets” and that he may have wanted to marry 11-year-old Alice, causing the 1863 break. Hudson and Green stop short of “paedophile” but note his passion for small girls and disinterest in adults. Catherine Robson calls him “the Victorian era’s most famous (or infamous) girl lover.” Will Brooker counters that 20th-century perception is heavily Freudian, reflecting modern attitudes toward childhood and celebrity more than Victorian reality. Hugues Lebailly situates Dodgson’s child nudes within the “Victorian Child Cult,” where such images expressed innocence and appeared on Christmas cards; mainstream photographers (Rejlander, Cameron) routinely made them. Lebailly argues biographers err by viewing this through 20th/21st-century eyes. Karoline Leach (“the Carroll Myth”) notes diaries reveal strong interest in adult women (married and single), relationships considered scandalous then; family suppression of this evidence created a false impression of exclusive interest in children. She also notes many “child-friends” were in their late teens or twenties, and traces the “friendships end at 14” claim to Langford Reed’s 1932 biography.

**BBC Documentary (2015):** *The Secret World of Lewis Carroll* presented a full-frontal nude photograph of adolescent Lorina Liddell from the Musée Cantini, Marseille, attributed in pencil to “L. Carroll” (unknown hand). Scholars (Edward Wakeling, Jenny Woolf) challenged its provenance: the photo surfaced in the 1970s, lacks Dodgson’s catalogue number, shows irregular trimming, and depicts a maturity Dodgson never photographed; the Liddells would not have permitted it. The BBC Trust ruled the documentary cannot be re-shown in its current form for failing to inform participants of the photo during filming.

**Ordination Refusal:** Expected to become priest within four years of his master’s degree, Dodgson was ordained deacon (22 Dec 1861) but appealed to Dean Liddell to avoid priesthood. Liddell initially threatened expulsion but relented overnight. No conclusive evidence explains why. Suggestions include stammer (though he later preached), Bishop Wilberforce’s anti-theatre stance (Dodgson loved theatre), admiration for heterodox theologian F.D. Maurice, interest in theosophy, and an overwhelming sense of sin/unworthiness (“vile and worthless” sinner) recorded in diaries.

**Medical Conditions:** In 1880 he recorded his first migraine with aura (“moving fortifications”). “Alice in Wonderland syndrome” (micropsia/macropsia) is named for the book’s size-changes, but no evidence links it to Dodgson’s experience. He had two loss-of-consciousness attacks diagnosed as “epileptiform” seizures by Drs Morshead, Brooks, Stedman; one left him with a bloody nose and prolonged malaise. Neurologist Yvonne Hart (2010) concluded he “very likely had migraine and may have had epilepsy,” but doubted an epilepsy diagnosis without further data.

## Terms
- ****Studentship**** — A permanent fellowship at Christ Church, Oxford, requiring ordination as deacon (and normally priest) within a set period; provided Dodgson’s financial security.
- ****High-church / Anglo-Catholic / Tractarian**** — Anglican tradition emphasizing ritual, apostolic succession, and Catholic continuity; associated with John Henry Newman’s Oxford Movement.
- ****Wet-collodion process**** — 1850s photographic technique requiring glass plates to be coated, sensitized, exposed, and developed while still wet; demanded a portable darkroom and long exposures.
- ****Doublets (word ladder)**** — Puzzle invented by Dodgson (1879): transform one word into another by changing one letter at a time, each step a valid word (e.g., CAT→COT→DOT→DOG).
- ****Nyctograph**** — Dodgson’s writing tablet: a gridded card with a 16-symbol alphabet allowing note-taking in darkness without light.
- ****Dodgson condensation**** — Algorithm for computing determinants by iteratively condensing a matrix; led to the alternating-sign-matrix theorem.
- ****Method of Trees**** — Truth-tree (semantic tableau) system for testing logical validity, introduced in *Symbolic Logic Part II*; earliest modern use.
- ****Dodgson’s method**** — Voting rule: elect the candidate who would win all pairwise contests (Condorcet winner) with minimal preference changes; a contribution to social choice theory.
- ****Alice in Wonderland syndrome**** — Neurological condition (micropsia/macropsia) causing perceived size distortion; named after Carroll’s book, not confirmed as his own experience.
- ****“Cut pages in diary” document**** — Posthumous summary by nephew Philip Dodgson Jacques (from aunts’ accounts) of excised diary material, notably 27 June 1863, suggesting the Liddell break concerned gossip about the governess and “Ina,” not Alice.

## Debates and open questions
- **Cause of the 1863 Liddell break:** The missing diary page (27 June 1863) and conflicting secondary accounts (Jacques summary vs. Lorina Liddell’s 1930 letter) leave the reason—proposal to Alice, gossip about governess/Ina, maternal objection to affection toward Alice—unresolved.
- **Nature of Dodgson’s interest in children:** Whether his nude photography and emotional attachments reflect Victorian aesthetic innocence (“Child Cult”), repressed paedophilia, or a biographical myth constructed by family suppression of his adult relationships remains contested.
- **Authenticity of the Cantini photograph:** The full-frontal nude attributed to “L. Carroll” lacks Dodgson’s catalogue number, shows anomalous trimming, and depicts a maturity inconsistent with his known work; its attribution is unverified and disputed by scholars.
- **Reason for refusing priesthood:** Stammer, theological heterodoxy (Maurice, theosophy), Bishop Wilberforce’s anti-theatre views, and acute sense of sin are all proposed; no primary source confirms any single cause.
- **Medical diagnosis:** Migraine with aura is well-documented (1880 diary); epilepsy is suggested by two “epileptiform” attacks but lacks confirmatory evidence; temporal lobe epilepsy has been hypothesized to explain *Alice*’s experiences but remains speculative.
- **Extent of family censorship:** Dodgson’s nieces removed references to adult women from diary manuscripts while retaining child references, shaping the historical record; the full original diaries are lost.
