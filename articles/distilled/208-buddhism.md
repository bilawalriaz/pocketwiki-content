# Buddhism

## Overview
Buddhism (Buddhadharma) is an Indian religion and philosophy originating in the 5th century BCE from the teachings of the Buddha, a śramaṇa teacher who attained awakening (bodhi) in the eastern Gangetic plain. It is the world's fourth-largest religion, with approximately 324 million followers (4.1% of the global population) as of 2020. The tradition teaches that existence is characterized by dukkha (unsatisfactoriness/suffering) driven by craving (taṇhā) and ignorance (avidyā), trapping beings in saṃsāra, the cyclic process of rebirth. Liberation (nirvāṇa) is achieved by following the Middle Way—avoiding extremes of asceticism and indulgence—through ethics (śīla), meditation (samādhi), and wisdom (prajñā). The canon is vast and multilingual (Pali, Sanskrit, Tibetan, Chinese). Two major extant branches are Theravāda ("School of the Elders"), emphasizing arhatship and the Pali Canon, and Mahāyāna ("Great Vehicle"), emphasizing the bodhisattva path and Mahāyāna sūtras. Vajrayāna ("Indestructible Vehicle"), incorporating esoteric tantric techniques, is often classified within Mahāyāna. Buddhism spread across Asia via the Silk Road, declined in India by the 12th century CE, and reached the West in the 19th–20th centuries.

## Timeline
- **c. 5th century BCE** — Life of the Buddha; founding of the Sangha in the eastern Gangetic plain.
- **c. 3rd century BCE** — Emperor Ashoka (Maurya Empire) patronizes Buddhism; sends missions to Sri Lanka and Central Asia; first major schisms (Sthavira/Mahasanghika).
- **c. 1st century BCE – 2nd century CE** — Composition of earliest Mahāyāna sūtras (e.g., Prajñāpāramitā); Buddhism enters China via Silk Road/Kushan Empire.
- **c. 1st–5th century CE** — Pali Canon written down in Sri Lanka; rise of major Indian monastic universities (Nalanda, Vikramashila).
- **c. 4th–6th century CE** — Gupta period; flourishing of Mahāyāna philosophy (Madhyamaka, Yogācāra) and early Tantra/Vajrayana.
- **c. 7th–8th century CE** — Esoteric Buddhism (Vajrayana) established in Tibet; Chinese Esoteric Buddhism and Chan (Zen) flourish in Tang China.
- **c. 8th–12th century CE** — Pāla Empire supports Buddhism in India; Islamic conquests begin destroying Indian Buddhist institutions.
- **c. 1200 CE** — Near disappearance of Buddhism from the Indian subcontinent.
- **13th–14th century CE** — Theravāda becomes dominant in Southeast Asia (Burma, Thailand, Cambodia, Laos).
- **19th–20th century CE** — Western academic study begins; Asian immigration and conversion establish Buddhism in the West; Buddhist modernism and Navayana movements arise.

## Body

### The Buddha and Early Community
The historical Buddha (Gautama/Siddhartha) was a śramaṇa from the Shakya clan (oligarchic republic) in the Ganges Plain, living c. 5th century BCE. Early texts (e.g., *Ariyapariyesanā-sutta*) describe his renunciation motivated by dukkha (suffering/unsatisfactoriness of impermanent existence). He studied meditation under Āḷāra Kālāma and Uddaka Rāmaputta, practiced extreme asceticism, then attained awakening (bodhi) under the Bodhi Tree at Bodh Gaya through dhyāna (meditative absorption). He realized the Four Noble Truths, dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda), and the Middle Way. He founded the Sangha (monastic order) and taught for 45 years, dying at 80 in Kushinagar (parinirvāṇa). His teachings were orally transmitted; the first council (historicity debated) aimed to preserve them. By c. 100 CE, schisms over Vinaya (monastic code) and doctrine produced numerous schools (e.g., Theravāda, Sarvāstivāda, Mahasanghika), each with their own Tripiṭaka (Three Baskets: Vinaya, Sutta, Abhidhamma).

### Core Worldview: Dukkha, Causality, and Liberation
**Four Noble Truths:** 1) Life in saṃsāra is dukkha (intrinsically unsatisfactory due to impermanence). 2) Dukkha arises with taṇhā (craving/thirst). 3) Dukkha ceases with the cessation of taṇhā (nirodha). 4) The path to cessation is the Noble Eightfold Path (Right View, Intention, Speech, Action, Livelihood, Effort, Mindfulness, Concentration), grouped into wisdom (prajñā), ethics (śīla), and meditation (samādhi).

**Three Marks of Existence:** All conditioned phenomena are *anicca* (impermanent), *dukkha* (unsatisfactory), and *anattā* (non-self—no permanent soul/essence). Some schools add a fourth: nirvāṇa is peace (śānti).

**Saṃsāra and Rebirth:** Saṃsāra is the cyclic wandering through six realms (three good: heavenly, demi-god, human; three bad: animal, hungry ghost, hell). Rebirth occurs without a permanent soul (anattā); the mechanism is a continuum of consciousness (vijñāna) or an inexpressible personality (pudgala), driven by karma.

**Karma:** Intentional actions (cetanā) of body, speech, and mind plant "seeds" in the unconscious receptacle (ālaya) that ripen in this or future lives, determining rebirth quality. Unlike Jainism, intent is essential. Merit transfer (dāna to Sangha, dedication to ancestors) is a later Mahāyāna feature, absent in early texts.

**Dependent Origination (Pratītyasamutpāda):** All phenomena arise dependently on conditions; nothing is independent except nirvāṇa. The Twelve Nidānas trace the causal chain: ignorance → karmic formations → consciousness → name-and-form → six senses → contact → feeling → craving → grasping → becoming → birth → aging/death. Breaking this chain yields liberation.

**Emptiness (Śūnyatā):** In early Buddhism, the five aggregates (form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness) are "empty of Self." In Mahāyāna Madhyamaka (Nagarjuna), all phenomena lack *svabhāva* (intrinsic nature/own-being), refuting essentialist views. Yogācāra posits "consciousness-only" (vijñaptimātratā): we perceive only mental impressions, not external objects.

**The Three Jewels (Triratna):** Refuge in Buddha (teacher/awakened one), Dharma (teaching/law/raft to cross over), and Sangha (monastic community preserving Dharma; also *āryasaṅgha*, the noble ones who have attained awakening). Theravāda sees Buddha as a human teacher; Mahāyāna sees Buddhas (Amitābha, Vairocana) as transcendent, eternal beings accessible in pure lands.

### Mahāyāna Distinctives
Mahāyāna ("Great Vehicle") emerged c. 1st century BCE/CE as a set of ideals for bodhisattvas (beings seeking Buddhahood for all), not a separate Vinaya. Key doctrines: **Bodhisattva Path** (universal vs. Theravāda's optional/heroic path); **Buddha-nature (Tathāgatagarbha)**—all beings possess the potential for Buddhahood (controversial vis-à-vis anattā); **Madhyamaka** (emptiness of intrinsic nature); **Yogācāra** (consciousness-only/idealism); **Pure Land** devotion to Amitābha for rebirth in Sukhavati. Mahāyāna accepts early Āgamas but privileges Mahāyāna sūtras as definitive for "greater capacity" beings.

### Vajrayāna / Tantra
Developing in India c. 4th–8th century CE (Gupta/Pāla eras), Vajrayāna ("Indestructible Vehicle") employs tantric methods (mantra, mudra, mandala, deity yoga, subtle body yoga) for rapid attainment. It requires initiation (abhiṣeka) and guru devotion. *Anuttarayogatantra* (Highest Yoga Tantra) has Generation Stage (visualizing oneself as deity/mandala) and Completion Stage (subtle body practices). It draws on a "pan-Indian religious substrate" and shows mutual influence with Śaiva Tantra. Established in Tibet (8th century onward) and Japan (Shingon).

### Paths and Practices
**Ethics (Śīla):** Based on non-harming (ahiṃsa). Laypeople observe Five Precepts (no killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech, intoxicants). Monastics follow Vinaya (e.g., 227 Pātimokkha rules in Theravāda). Mahāyāna adds Bodhisattva Precepts.
**Meditation:** *Samatha* (calm/concentration → samādhi, four dhyānas/jhānas, formless attainments) and *Vipassanā* (insight into impermanence, non-self, dependent origination). Debate exists on sequence (calm then insight, insight then calm, or simultaneous). **Brahmavihāras:** Loving-kindness (mettā), compassion (karuṇā), empathetic joy (muditā), equanimity (upekkhā).
**Mindfulness (Sati/Smṛti):** Non-forgetting awareness of present experience; cultivated via *Satipaṭṭhāna* (four establishments: body, feelings, mind, dhammas) and *Ānāpānasati* (mindfulness of breathing).
**Wisdom (Prajñā):** Insight into three marks, dependent origination, karma; antidote to ignorance (avidyā).
**Devotion:** *Śraddhā* (faith/confidence), *pūjā* (offerings), chanting (sutras, mantras like *nianfo* or *Om mani padme hum*), pilgrimage, stupa veneration. Merit-making supports better rebirth; lay focus historically differs from monastic nirvāṇa-goal.

### Textual History
Oral tradition in Prakrits (Pali, Gāndhārī). First written c. 1st century BCE in Sri Lanka (Pali Canon/Theravāda). **Early Buddhist Texts (EBTs):** Four Pali Nikāyas + Chinese Āgamas (parallels). **Tripiṭakas:** School-specific canons (Vinaya, Sutta, Abhidhamma). **Mahāyāna Sūtras:** Composed 1st c. BCE–2nd c. CE (Prajñāpāramitā, Lotus, Avataṃsaka, Tathāgatagarbha); translated into Chinese (Lokakṣema, 2nd c. CE). **Śāstras:** Philosophical treatises (Nagarjuna’s *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*, Vasubandhu’s *Abhidharmakośa*). **Tantras:** Esoteric texts (8th c. onward), using mantra, deity yoga, antinomian practices. Chinese Canon (2184 texts), Tibetan Canon (1108 Buddha-word + 3461 commentaries). Dunhuang cache (1900) yielded 40,000+ manuscripts.

### Historical Trajectory
**India:** Ashoka (3rd c. BCE) made Buddhism imperial religion; spread to Sri Lanka (Theravāda) and Central Asia. Post-Ashoka: sectarian proliferation (18+ schools). Kushans (1st–3rd c. CE) patronized Sarvāstivāda/Gandhāra; Silk Road transmission to China. Gupta/Pāla eras (4th–12th c.): Nalanda/Vikramashila universities; Mahāyāna philosophy; Vajrayana rise. Decline from 7th c. due to Hindu revival (Vaishnavism/Śaivism), loss of patronage, Islamic invasions (10th–12th c.); extinct in India by c. 1200.
**Sri Lanka & Southeast Asia:** Theravāda arrived 3rd c. BCE; became dominant in Burma, Thailand, Cambodia, Laos (13th–14th c.).
**East Asia:** Entered China 1st–2nd c. CE (An Shigao, Lokakṣema). Tang (618–907): Chan (Zen), Pure Land, Esoteric. Song (960–1279): Chan dominance, canon printing. Spread to Korea (4th c.), Japan (6th–7th c.: Tendai, Shingon, later Zen, Pure Land, Nichiren), Vietnam (1st–2nd c.).
**Tibet & Himalayas:** 7th c. royal adoption; 8th c. Padmasambhava/Śāntarakṣita established Vajrayana (Nyingma); later Sarma schools (Kagyu, Sakya, Gelug). Mongolian adoption (13th c.).
**Modern Era:** Colonialism/Christian missions prompted Buddhist modernism (Olcott, Dharmapala: "scientific/rational Buddhism"). 1893 World Parliament of Religions introduced Buddhism to West. 20th c.: Asian immigration, counterculture interest, institutional growth (Suzuki, Thích Nhất Hạnh, Dalai Lama). Persecution in Communist states (Tibet, Mongolia, China Cultural Revolution); revival post-1970s. Navayana (Ambedkar, 1956): Dalit mass conversion rejecting karma/rebirth/monasticism for social equality. Secular Buddhism, Won Buddhism, Soka Gakkai, Dhammakaya movements. Sexual abuse scandals in Zen/Tibetan groups.

### Demographics (2020)
~324 million (4.1% world population); only major religion declining 2010–2020 (-5%, aging/low fertility/disaffiliation). Largest populations: Thailand (64M, 94%), China (42M, mostly Mahāyāna). Dominant in Thailand, Cambodia, Myanmar, Sri Lanka, Bhutan, Laos, Mongolia, Japan, Singapore, Vietnam. Mahāyāna >50% of Buddhists; Theravāda ~36%. Growth mainly in India (Navayana/Dalit conversions) and West (converts).

## Terms
- ****Anattā (Anātman)**** — Non-self; the doctrine that no permanent, unchanging soul or essence exists in any phenomenon.
- ****Bodhisattva**** — A being who vows to attain Buddhahood for the benefit of all sentient beings; central ideal in Mahāyāna.
- ****Dependent Origination (Pratītyasamutpāda)**** — The principle that all phenomena arise in dependence on causes and conditions; the causal framework of saṃsāra.
- ****Dhyāna (Jhāna)**** — Meditative absorption; states of unified, concentrated awareness (four rūpa-jhānas, four arūpa-jhānas).
- ****Dukkha**** — Unsatisfactoriness, suffering, stress; the intrinsic flaw of conditioned existence due to impermanence.
- ****Karma (Kamma)**** — Intentional action (cetanā) that plants karmic seeds determining future rebirth and experience.
- ****Nirvāṇa (Nibbāna)**** — "Blowing out" of greed, hatred, delusion; liberation from saṃsāra; the unconditioned.
- ****Saṃsāra**** — The cyclic wandering through six realms of rebirth, driven by karma and ignorance.
- ****Śūnyatā**** — Emptiness; lack of intrinsic nature (svabhāva) in all phenomena (Madhyamaka); emptiness of Self (early/Theravāda).
- ****Tripiṭaka**** — "Three Baskets": Vinaya (monastic code), Sutta/Sūtra (discourses), Abhidhamma/Abhidharma (scholastic analysis).

## Debates and open questions
- **Historical Buddha:** Exact dates (6th vs. 5th c. BCE), social background (kshatriya vs. oligarchic Shakya), and authenticity of specific biographical details remain uncertain.
- **Early Doctrine Recovery:** Scholars dispute whether a coherent "earliest Buddhism" can be reconstructed (positions: substantial authenticity vs. radical skepticism vs. cautious optimism). Key disputes: centrality of karma, role of insight vs. jhāna, formulation of Four Noble Truths.
- **Mahāyāna Origins:** Whether it began as a textual cult, forest ascetic movement, or lay movement; the relationship between Mahāyāna sūtras and the historical Buddha.
- **Anattā vs. Tathāgatagarbha:** Whether Buddha-nature doctrine (innate Buddhahood/Self) contradicts core non-self teaching; traditional resolution: "Self" in Tathāgatagarbha means "not-self."
- **Vajrayāna Origins:** Extent of borrowing from Śaiva Tantra (Sanderson: direct borrowing; Davidson: mutual influence/uncertain chronology).
- **Meditation Theory:** Whether *samatha* and *vipassanā* represent two distinct paths (La Vallee Poussin) or complementary aspects (Analayo, Gethin).
- **Vegetarianism:** Whether the Buddha permitted meat (early texts: "threefold pure" rule) or mandated vegetarianism (Mahāyāna sūtras); ongoing debate.
- **Modern Identity:** Whether Buddhism is a "religion" (theistic definition problem), philosophy, or psychotherapy; tensions between traditional Asian Buddhism (karma/rebirth/merit) and Western convert Buddhism (secular/psychological focus).
