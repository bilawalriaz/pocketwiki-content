# Mulla Sadra

## Overview
Mullā Ṣadrā (c. 1571/2 – c. 1635/40) was an Iranian Twelver Shi'i philosopher, mystic, and theologian who is considered the most influential philosopher in the Muslim world in the last four centuries. He created a major synthesis of earlier Islamic philosophical traditions into a new system called Transcendent Theosophy, fundamentally shifting Islamic philosophy from an essentialist to an existentialist framework.

## Timeline
- **c. 1571/2** — Born in Shiraz, Iran, to a notable family.
- **1591** — Moved to Qazvin to pursue formal education.
- **1597** — Moved to Isfahan to continue studies under teachers like Mir Damad.
- **After studies** — Faced condemnation for unorthodox views; retired to Kahak near Qom for contemplation.
- **1612** — Invited back to Shiraz to teach at the Khan School.
- **c. 1605–1630** — Gradually composed his masterwork, *The Four Journeys*.
- **Died** — Died in Basra after the Hajj; buried in Najaf, Iraq.
- **19th century** — His influence revived during the Qajar period in Iran.
- **1979** — After the Islamic Revolution, his works became central to Iranian seminary and university curricula.
- **1994** — Sadra Islamic Philosophy Research Institute founded in Tehran.

## Body

### Philosophical Synthesis
Mullā Ṣadrā's primary achievement was synthesizing Avicennism, Suhrawardi's Illuminationist philosophy, Ibn Arabi's Sufi metaphysics, and Ash'ari theology into a Twelver Shi'i framework. His masterwork, *The Transcendent Philosophy of the Four Journeys of the Intellect*, presents this unified system. He aimed to reinterpret Peripatetic philosophy to prove the Unity of Existence, a core Sufi concept.

### Existentialism and the Primacy of Existence
Ṣadrā introduced a form of existentialism where "existence precedes the essence." He argued that existence is the primary, singular reality, while essences (quiddities) are secondary and variable, determined by their "intensity" of being. This framework solved key theological problems: it affirmed God's absolute authority and knowledge of all particulars (including evil) without making God responsible for sin, thereby reconciling divine judgment with human free will. His existentialism is cosmological, focused on God's relationship to the universe, rather than on individual human experience.

### Substantial Motion and Causation
A central doctrine is "substantial motion" (*al-harakat al-jawhariyyah*), which posits that everything in nature, including the fundamental substance of things, is in a constant state of change and transformation due to the "self-flow" of being. This contrasts with Aristotle and Avicenna, who limited change to accidental categories. For Ṣadrā, all contingent beings require a cause, leading to a First Cause (God) who is both the beginning and end of the creative act. This causal framework explains how God can judge reality by perceiving the intensity of existence itself, not by being tainted by the particulars of sin.

### Truth and Legacy
Ṣadrā held a metaphysical theory of truth: a statement is true if it corresponds to mind-independent reality. His legacy was preserved in Iran, especially during the Qajar period, and expanded significantly after the 1979 Islamic Revolution. His works are now taught in Iranian seminaries and universities. His influence also spread to Iraq, South Asia (where his works were studied for centuries), and modern academic circles worldwide.

## Terms
- **Transcendent Theosophy (al-hikmah al-muta’āliyah):** Mullā Ṣadrā's philosophical system, synthesizing multiple Islamic intellectual traditions.
- **Existentialism:** In Ṣadrā's philosophy, the doctrine that existence is primary and precedes essence, applied to a cosmological and theological context.
- **Essence (Quiddity):** The "whatness" or defining characteristics of a thing, which Ṣadrā saw as secondary to its existence.
- **Substantial Motion (al-harakat al-jawhariyyah):** The theory that the fundamental substance of all things is in constant, inherent change.
- **Unity of Existence:** A Sufi concept Ṣadrā sought to prove, asserting that all existence is ultimately one reality, originating from God.
- **Peripatetic Philosophy:** The Aristotelian-influenced Islamic philosophy of thinkers like al-Farabi and Avicenna, which Ṣadrā reinterpreted.
- **Illuminationist (Ishraqi) Philosophy:** The school of Suhrawardi, emphasizing mystical insight and light metaphysics, which Ṣadrā incorporated.
- **‘Ālim:** A scholar of Islamic sciences.
- **Kalam:** Islamic speculative theology.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise nature and relationship of Ṣadrā's existentialism to Western existentialism is a point of scholarly discussion, with the source noting they are not readily comparable.
- The problem of how God can judge sin without being tainted by it was a central philosophical question for centuries; Ṣadrā's solution using the primacy of existence is presented as a major resolution.
- The theory of the "unity of the intellect and the intelligible" describes a complex, non-arithmetical union in the act of knowledge, which remains a profound and debated philosophical concept.
