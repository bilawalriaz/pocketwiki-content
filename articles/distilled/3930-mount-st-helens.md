# Mount St. Helens

## Overview
Mount St. Helens is an active stratovolcano in Washington state, part of the Cascade Volcanic Arc. It is most famous for its catastrophic 1980 eruption, the most economically destructive volcanic event in U.S. history, which killed 57 people and dramatically altered the mountain's landscape. The volcano remains closely monitored due to its potential for future, possibly larger, eruptions.

## Timeline
- **c. 37,600 years ago** — Volcano begins forming during the Ape Canyon eruptive stage.
- **c. 1900 BC** — Largest known Holocene eruption deposits the Yn tephra.
- **c. 1480** — Kalama period begins with an eruption several times larger than 1980.
- **1800** — Goat Rocks eruptive period begins; first period with oral/written records.
- **May 19, 1792** — First recorded European sighting by George Vancouver's expedition.
- **March 20, 1980** — A magnitude 4.2 earthquake signals the start of the modern eruptive period.
- **May 18, 1980** — A magnitude 5.1 earthquake triggers a massive lateral eruption, killing 57 people.
- **1982** — Mount St. Helens National Volcanic Monument is established.
- **2004–2008** — A new lava dome grows in the crater; continuous eruption ends in 2008.
- **May 14, 2023** — A mudslide destroys the Spirit Lake Outlet Bridge, cutting off access.

## Body

### Geology and Eruptive History
Mount St. Helens is part of the Cascade Volcanic Arc, formed by the subduction of the Juan de Fuca Plate beneath the North American Plate. It is geologically young, having formed within the last 40,000 years. Its eruptive history is divided into ancestral stages (Ape Canyon, Cougar, Swift Creek) and the modern Spirit Lake stage (since c. 2500 BC). The 1980 eruption was a Plinian event rated a 5 on the Volcanic Explosivity Index. A magnitude 5.1 earthquake triggered the collapse of the north face, creating the largest debris avalanche in recorded history (0.6 cubic miles). This lateral blast reduced the summit elevation by about 1,300 feet and left a horseshoe-shaped crater. The eruption released 24 megatons of thermal energy, ejected over 0.67 cubic miles of material, and sent an ash plume 12–16 miles high. Following 1980, activity continued with dome-building until 2008. Geologists predict future eruptions may be more destructive due to the configuration of the lava domes.

### Ecology and Human Impact
The 1980 eruption devastated terrestrial ecosystems, flattening vegetation over 230 square miles. However, aquatic ecosystems benefited from ashfall, and most lakes recovered within six years. The event advanced the ecological concept of "biological legacies"—survivors (organisms, organic debris) that influence post-disturbance recovery. Large mammals were decimated but eventually migrated back; mountain goat numbers recovered to 152 by 2015. Human impacts included 57 deaths, destroyed infrastructure (200 homes, 185 miles of highway), and $100 million in agricultural damage. The eruption also had positive effects, such as increased apple and wheat yields and the creation of the tourist gemstone helenite. The Mount St. Helens National Volcanic Monument was established in 1982 to protect the area and allow natural recovery.

### Indigenous Significance and Human History
The volcano is sacred to the Cowlitz, Klickitat, and Yakama tribes, who know it as Lawetlat'la ("the smoker") or Loowit. It features in their creation stories, such as the Bridge of the Gods tale, where the mountain is the transformed maiden Loowit. European explorers first recorded it in 1792. The first authenticated eruption report was in 1835. The 1980 eruption's death toll was lower than it might have been because it occurred on a Sunday. The volcano is now a popular climbing destination, with a permit system in place. A new glacier, Crater Glacier, formed in the post-1980 crater and has grown rapidly, now surrounding the new lava domes.

## Terms
- **Stratovolcano**: A conical volcano built up by many layers of hardened lava, tephra, pumice, and volcanic ash.
- **Subduction**: The geological process where one tectonic plate sinks beneath another into the Earth's mantle.
- **Plinian eruption**: A highly explosive volcanic eruption characterized by a sustained column of gas and ash reaching high into the stratosphere.
- **Debris avalanche**: A massive, fast-moving landslide of rock, soil, and ice triggered by a volcanic eruption or earthquake.
- **Lahar**: A volcanic mudflow or debris flow composed of a slurry of pyroclastic material, rocky debris, and water.
- **Tephra**: Fragmented material ejected into the air during a volcanic eruption, ranging from fine ash to large blocks.
- **Pyroclastic flow**: A fast-moving current of hot gas and volcanic matter that moves away from a volcano.
- **Biological legacy**: Organisms, organic debris, or biotic patterns that survive a catastrophic disturbance and influence ecosystem recovery.
- **Holocene epoch**: The current geological epoch, spanning approximately the last 11,700 years of Earth's history.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact configuration and interaction of the two magma chambers beneath the volcano, and whether the lower chamber is shared with Mount Adams, are subjects of ongoing study.
- The potential scale and impact of future eruptions are uncertain, though they are predicted to be more destructive than 1980 due to the current dome structure.
- The long-term ecological recovery and the full role of biological legacies in the post-1980 landscape continue to be researched.
