# Francisco Morazán

## Overview
José Francisco Morazán was a Central American liberal statesman and military leader who served as president of the Federal Republic of Central America three times between 1829 and 1839. He is remembered as a visionary who attempted to unify and modernize Central America through progressive reforms, but his efforts ultimately failed due to bitter conservative opposition, leading to the federation's dissolution and his own execution.

## Timeline
- **3 October 1792** — Born in Tegucigalpa, Captaincy General of Guatemala.
- **15 September 1821** — Central America declares independence from Spain; Morazán joins a militia opposing annexation to Mexico.
- **11 November 1827** — Wins the Battle of La Trinidad, defeating federal forces and becoming Head of State of Honduras.
- **16 September 1830** — Inaugurated as President of the Federal Republic of Central America for his first term.
- **14 February 1835** — Sworn in for his second presidential term after winning new elections.
- **February 1837** — A cholera epidemic in Guatemala sparks a conservative-led indigenous revolt led by Rafael Carrera.
- **1 February 1839** — Completes his second presidential term; the federation begins to collapse.
- **18 March 1840** — Suffers a decisive defeat in Guatemala, ending hopes of restoring the federation.
- **13 April 1842** — Takes control of Costa Rica after overthrowing Braulio Carrillo.
- **15 September 1842** — Executed by firing squad in San José, Costa Rica.

## Body

### Early Life and Military Rise
Born to a criollo (Spaniard born in the New World) father and a Central American mother, Morazán faced educational barriers due to colonial discrimination against non-peninsulares. He joined a militia opposing Central America's annexation to Mexico in 1821. His military prominence began with his victory at the Battle of La Trinidad in 1827, which made him the liberal leader of Honduras and a key figure in the region's politics.

### Liberal Reforms and the Federation
As president, Morazán enacted sweeping liberal reforms aimed at dismantling Spanish colonial institutions. These included freedom of the press, speech, and religion; secularizing marriage; abolishing tithes; and separating church from state. These policies, tested first in Guatemala, struck at the power of the clergy and the conservative oligarchy, creating powerful enemies and fueling a bitter conflict between liberals and conservatives.

### Civil War and the Federation's Collapse
Morazán's reforms and the liberal-conservative divide led to chronic instability. A cholera epidemic in 1837 was exploited by conservative clergy to incite an indigenous revolt led by Rafael Carrera. Although Morazán repeatedly defeated Carrera's forces, he could not achieve a lasting pacification. By 1838, Honduras, Nicaragua, and Costa Rica had left the union, and the Federal Republic was effectively dead.

### Final Years and Execution
After the federation's collapse, Morazán served as Head of State in El Salvador and later Costa Rica. In 1842, he briefly took power in Costa Rica to restore the federation but faced a popular uprising. After a prolonged siege, he was captured and executed. His death marked the definitive end of the dream of a unified Central American state.

## Terms
- **Caudillo**: A military and political strongman, common in 19th-century Latin America.
- **Criollo**: A person of Spanish descent born in the Americas, often part of the local elite.
- **Federal Republic of Central America**: The union of the Central American states from 1823 to 1838/1841.
- **Liberalism**: A political ideology favoring secularism, individual rights, and free trade, opposing traditional church and aristocratic power.
- **Conservatism**: A political ideology seeking to preserve traditional social hierarchies, church authority, and existing property relations.
- **Tithing**: A traditional tax of one-tenth of produce paid to support the church, which Morazán abolished.
- **Secular marriage**: Marriage as a civil contract recognized by the state, not solely a religious sacrament.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The primary cause of the federation's collapse is debated: was it the inherent weakness of the federal system, the success of conservative opposition, or the failure of liberal reforms to gain popular support?
- Historians assess whether Morazán's liberal reforms were too radical and too fast for the social and political realities of the time, thereby provoking inevitable backlash.
- The role of the Catholic Church in actively undermining the liberal government, particularly by using the cholera epidemic to incite rebellion, is a central point of analysis.
