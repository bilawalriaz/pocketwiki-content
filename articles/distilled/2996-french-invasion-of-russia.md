# French invasion of Russia

## Overview
The French invasion of Russia (1812) was a catastrophic military campaign led by Napoleon Bonaparte to force Tsar Alexander I to comply with the Continental System, a trade blockade against Britain. It is considered one of history's most devastating military endeavors, resulting in the near-total destruction of Napoleon's Grande Armée and shattering his reputation of invincibility, which directly led to his eventual defeat.

## Timeline
- **24 June 1812** — Napoleon's Grande Armée crosses the Neman River into Russia.
- **August 1812** — French capture Smolensk after a two-day battle, but the main Russian armies escape.
- **20 August 1812** — Mikhail Kutuzov replaces Barclay de Tolly as Russian commander-in-chief.
- **7 September 1812** — Battle of Borodino: a bloody, indecisive battle west of Moscow.
- **14 September 1812** — Napoleon enters Moscow, which is deserted and later set ablaze.
- **19 October 1812** — Napoleon begins the retreat from Moscow after five weeks of occupation.
- **24 October 1812** — Battle of Maloyaroslavets forces the French back onto the devastated Smolensk road.
- **November 1812** — Severe winter weather sets in, causing massive French losses during the retreat.
- **5 December 1812** — Napoleon abandons the army and returns to Paris.
- **14 December 1812** — The remnants of the Grande Armée leave Russian territory.

## Body

### Background and Causes
The campaign was triggered by Russia's withdrawal from Napoleon's Continental System, a blockade designed to cripple Britain's economy. Russia's exit, due to economic strain, left Napoleon without his primary foreign policy tool. Additional tensions included the French annexation of Western Galicia, which Russia viewed as a threat, and Napoleon's failed marriage alliance with the Russian Tsar.

### The Invasion and Advance
Napoleon's multinational Grande Armée, numbering nearly half a million men, advanced rapidly into Russia. The Russian armies, under Barclay de Tolly and later Kutuzov, employed a strategy of strategic retreat and attrition, avoiding a decisive general engagement. This forced the French into an increasingly overstretched supply line. The first major battle occurred at Smolensk in August, but the Russians escaped. The campaign's pivotal battle, Borodino, was a bloody stalemate that failed to destroy the Russian army.

### Occupation of Moscow and Retreat
After Borodino, Kutuzov abandoned Moscow to preserve his army. Napoleon occupied the city on 14 September, only to find it deserted and soon engulfed in fires set by the Russians. After waiting in vain for a peace offer, Napoleon ordered a retreat in late October. The retreat became a disaster as the French were forced back onto the same devastated route they had advanced on. The onset of a severe Russian winter, combined with constant harassment by Russian forces and peasants, led to catastrophic losses from cold, starvation, and disease.

### Logistics and Losses
The campaign highlighted the critical failure of Napoleon's logistics. His army, accustomed to living off the land in more fertile Europe, could not be sustained in Russia's sparsely populated and agriculturally poor regions. Poor roads, extreme weather, and the vast distances overwhelmed the supply system. Of the initial force of over 450,000, only about 120,000 men (excluding deserters) survived. Russian military losses were also severe, but their field army remained intact. The catastrophic French defeat shattered Napoleon's aura of invincibility and encouraged Austria and Prussia to switch sides, leading to the War of the Sixth Coalition.

## Terms
- **Continental System**: Napoleon's foreign policy tool, a blockade aimed at crippling the British economy by closing European ports to British trade.
- **Grande Armée**: The name given to the French Imperial Army and its allies during the Napoleonic Wars.
- **Scorched Earth**: A military strategy of destroying anything that might be useful to the enemy, including food supplies and shelter, as the Russians employed during the retreat.
- **Rasputitsa**: The Russian term for the season of muddy or quibbly roads that hampered movement, especially in autumn and spring.
- **Cossacks**: Semi-nomadic, Slavic-speaking people from the steppes of Southern Russia and Ukraine, renowned as skilled cavalry and irregular fighters.
- **Attrition Warfare**: A military strategy of wearing down an opponent's strength through continuous losses in personnel and materiel, as practiced by the Russian commanders.
- **Berezina**: The river where the Grande Armée suffered heavy losses during a chaotic crossing in late November 1812.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Primary Cause of Defeat**: Historians debate whether the campaign was doomed by logistical overreach, the Russian strategy of attrition, or the severity of the winter. The source notes that most losses occurred before the severe cold set in, suggesting logistics and disease were primary factors.
- **Kutuzov's Strategy**: Kutuzov's decision to abandon Moscow and avoid a decisive battle after Borodino was controversial at the time and remains debated. Critics argued it was overly cautious, while supporters credit it with preserving the army that ultimately won the war.
- **Historiographical Bias**: The source highlights that much Western historiography has relied on French and German sources, often ignoring Russian perspectives and portraying Russia as a backward, "Asiatic" force defeated by geography and climate rather than by Russian military and popular resistance.
