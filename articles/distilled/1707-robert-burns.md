# Robert Burns

## Overview
Robert Burns (1759–1796) was a Scottish poet and lyricist widely regarded as the national poet of Scotland. Writing in both Scots and English, he pioneered the Romantic movement by blending vernacular language with classical literary knowledge, producing enduring works such as "Auld Lang Syne," "Tam o' Shanter," and "A Man's a Man for A' That." Born a tenant farmer’s son in Ayrshire, Burns endured poverty and hard labour, which damaged his health but fueled his empathy for the common person. His 1786 *Kilmarnock volume* brought immediate fame, leading to a celebrated stay in Edinburgh where he mingled with the literati and collected folk songs for *The Scots Musical Museum*. Later, as an exciseman in Dumfriesshire, he balanced government service with radical sympathies for the American and French Revolutions, joining a Volunteer Corps to prove loyalty. He died at 37, likely from a rheumatic heart condition exacerbated by infection. Posthumously, he became a global cultural icon, inspiring liberation movements in Russia, influencing writers from Wordsworth to Bob Dylan, and generating a worldwide tradition of Burns Suppers.

## Body

### Early Life and Education in Ayrshire
Robert Burns was born in a cottage built by his father in Alloway, South Ayrshire. His father, William Burnes, was a self-educated tenant farmer who provided his children with a surprisingly broad education—reading, writing, arithmetic, geography, history, and a *Manual of Christian Belief*—supplemented by the tutor John Murdoch, who taught Latin, French, and mathematics. Despite this, the family’s poverty forced Burns into full-time farm labour by age 15 at Mount Oliphant, work he later blamed for his "weakened constitution" and premature aging. At 15, during harvest 1774, Nelly Kilpatrick inspired his first poem, "O, Once I Lov'd A Bonnie Lass." A brief period of study at Kirkoswald in 1775 introduced him to Peggy Thompson, subject of two early songs.

### Tarbolton, Freemasonry, and the Turn to Poetry
In 1777 the family moved to Lochlea farm near Tarbolton. Here Burns integrated into community life: he joined a dancing school (against his father's wishes), formed the Tarbolton Bachelors' Club with his brother Gilbert, and was initiated into St David’s Masonic Lodge in 1781. A failed apprenticeship as a flax-dresser in Irvine ended when the shop burned down during New Year celebrations. Back at Lochlea, he befriended Richard Brown, who encouraged his poetic ambitions. Burns began a commonplace book in 1783 while his father fought a successful Court of Session case against their landlord, dying a fortnight after victory in January 1784.

### Mauchline, Jamaica, and the Kilmarnock Volume
The brothers struggled to keep Lochlea, moving to Mossgiel Farm near Mauchline in 1785. Burns met Jean Armour, one of the "Belles of Mauchline," who became pregnant with twins in March 1786. Simultaneously, Elizabeth Paton, his mother’s servant, bore his first child. Facing financial ruin and Armour’s father’s opposition to their marriage, Burns accepted a job as a book-keeper (assistant overseer of enslaved people) on a Jamaican plantation owned by Patrick Douglas. To raise passage money, he published *Poems, Chiefly in the Scottish Dialect* by subscription in Kilmarnock on 31 July 1786. The volume—containing "To a Mouse," "Halloween," and "The Cotter's Saturday Night"—sold out instantly. A letter of praise from Dr. Thomas Blacklock persuaded Burns to cancel his emigration and ride to Edinburgh for a second edition.

### Edinburgh: Celebrity and Song Collection
Arriving in November 1786, Burns was lionized by the Edinburgh literati (Dugald Stewart, Henry Blair) and aristocracy. The 1787 Edinburgh edition, frontispieced by Alexander Nasmyth’s portrait, earned him £400. He formed lifelong friendships with Lord Glencairn and Frances Dunlop. He pursued Agnes McLehose ("Clarinda") in a passionate epistolary romance, but turned to her servant Jenny Clow (who bore him a son) and Margaret Cameron when she resisted a physical affair. Crucially, he met James Johnson, publisher of *The Scots Musical Museum*, to which Burns contributed roughly 200 of the 600 songs across its six volumes (1787–1803), preserving and refining Scottish folk melodies. He also began contributing to George Thomson’s *A Select Collection of Original Scottish Airs*, where his lyrics were set by composers including Haydn and Beethoven.

### Dumfriesshire: Exciseman, Radical, and Master Lyricist
Burns returned to Jean Armour, marrying her in March 1788 and leasing Ellisland Farm in Dumfriesshire. He trained as an exciseman (gauger), a demanding role involving long rides and detailed bookkeeping to enforce tax laws. At Ellisland he wrote his narrative masterpiece "Tam o' Shanter" (1790). Farming failed, and in 1791 he moved to Dumfries town, abandoning agriculture for full-time excise work and songwriting. He contributed over 100 lyrics to Thomson and Johnson, describing his method as matching poetic sentiment to a tune, then composing outdoors in harmony with nature before refining at his desk. His politics grew overtly radical: he sympathized with the French Revolution and the Society of the Friends of the People, writing "A Man's a Man for A' That" (based on Thomas Paine’s *Rights of Man*) and "Parcel of Rogues to the Nation." To protect his government post, he joined the Royal Dumfries Volunteers in 1795, serving diligently while continuing to write anonymous revolutionary verse.

### Death, Posthumous Controversy, and Descendants
Burns died on 21 July 1796, aged 37, likely from endocarditis stemming from a long-standing rheumatic heart condition (possibly since age 21) aggravated by a bacterial infection from a tooth abscess. Rumours of alcoholism, promoted by his first biographer James Currie, are considered overstated by modern scholars. He received a military funeral with his Volunteer Corps uniform on the coffin. In 1834, during the reopening of the mausoleum for his widow’s burial, a phrenologist group exhumed his body and removed the skull to create plaster casts; six casts are known to exist in Scottish museums. Jean Armour secured a pension for their surviving children via a 1798 collected works edition. Through five surviving children (of 12 born), Burns has over 900 living descendants as of 2019.

### Literary Style and Themes
Burns’s style combines spontaneity, directness, and sincerity, ranging from tender lyricism ("A Red, Red Rose") to bawdy humour ("The Merry Muses of Caledonia") and sharp satire ("Holy Willie’s Prayer," "The Holy Fair"). He wrote fluently in Scots, Scottish English, and standard English—often mixing registers in a single work ("Love and Liberty"). His themes encompass republicanism, Radicalism, Scottish patriotism, anticlericalism, class inequality, gender roles, poverty, sexuality, and the conviviality of folk culture (whisky, song, carousing). Biographer Robert Crawford has hypothesized manic depression ("blue devilism") based on emotional extremes in the poems and handwriting analysis, though the National Trust for Scotland considers evidence insufficient.

### Global Influence
**Britain:** Classified as a proto-Romantic, Burns influenced Wordsworth, Coleridge, and Shelley. His Scots predecessors were Allan Ramsay and Robert Fergusson. Later, Hugh MacDiarmid fought the "sentimental cult" (the "heaven-taught ploughman" myth) that Burns’s Edinburgh admirers created.
**Canada:** Patron poet of Scottish Canadians; "Robbie Burns Day" celebrated nationwide; hybrid "Gung Haggis Fat Choy" festival in Vancouver.
**United States:** Lincoln composed a toast for a Burns celebration (1864). John Steinbeck took *Of Mice and Men* from "To a Mouse." Bob Dylan cited "A Red, Red Rose" as his greatest lyrical inspiration (2008). J.D. Salinger used a misreading of "Comin' Thro' the Rye" for *The Catcher in the Rye*.
**Russia:** Translated by Samuil Marshak (1924, 600,000+ copies), Burns became the "people's poet" in Imperial and Soviet Russia, endorsed by the Communist regime for his egalitarianism ("A Man's a Man for A' That"). A USSR commemorative stamp was issued in 1956; he remains popular post-Soviet Union.

### Honours and Material Legacy
**Landmarks:** Birthplace cottage (Alloway) is the Robert Burns Birthplace Museum (National Trust for Scotland). Ellisland Farm and Dumfries House are preserved. Statues stand globally (Alloway, Leith, Dumfries, Atlanta, Boston, Quincy, Dunedin, Camperdown). Towns named Burns exist in New York and Oregon.
**Currency/Stamps:** USSR first commemorative stamp (1956). Royal Mail issues in 1966, 1996, 2009. Pictured on Clydesdale Bank £5 (1971–2009) and £10 (2009–present); Bank of Scotland £5 features Brig o' Doon (2007). Royal Mint £2 coin (2009).
**Portraits:** Nasmyth’s 1787 portrait (Scottish National Portrait Gallery) is the definitive image. A lost 1803 Henry Raeburn portrait was rediscovered in 2025 and displayed at the National Portrait Gallery in 2026 before transfer to Alloway.
**Burns Suppers:** Held globally on 25 January (Burns Night), exceeding St. Andrew's Day in Scottish observance. Format: Selkirk Grace, piping/cutting haggis with "Address to a Haggis," toasts (including "Immortal Memory" and "Toast to the Lassies"), concluding with "Auld Lang Syne." First supper held by The Mother Club, Greenock, 1802.
**Other:** Mercury crater named "Burns" (1985). Honorary Chartered Surveyor (2012). Robert Burns Academy opened in Cumnock (2020).

## Terms
- ****Scots language**** — A Germanic language variety spoken in Lowland Scotland and parts of Ulster, distinct from Scottish English; Burns's primary poetic medium.
- ****Kilmarnock volume**** — Burns's first published collection (1786), *Poems, Chiefly in the Scottish Dialect*, printed by John Wilson in Kilmarnock; launched his fame.
- ****Exciseman (gauger)**** — A government officer responsible for enforcing excise taxes (on alcohol, malt, etc.); Burns held this post 1788–1796 to secure income.
- ****The Scots Musical Museum**** — A six-volume collection (1787–1803) of Scottish folk songs edited by James Johnson; Burns contributed ~200 songs and significant editorial work.
- ****George Thomson**** — Edinburgh publisher of *A Select Collection of Original Scottish Airs*; commissioned Burns lyrics and classical arrangements by Haydn/Beethoven.
- ****Phrenology**** — A discredited 19th-century pseudo-science claiming personality traits could be determined by skull shape; motivated the 1834 exhumation of Burns's skull.
- ****Proto-Romantic**** — A literary precursor to Romanticism; Burns valued emotion, nature, and vernacular language before the movement fully formed.
- ****Selkirk Grace**** — A short Scots prayer attributed to Burns ("Some hae meat and canna eat..."), traditionally recited at Burns Suppers before the haggis.
- ****Royal Dumfries Volunteers**** — A British Volunteer Corps unit Burns joined in 1795 (rank: private) to demonstrate loyalty to the Crown amid suspicion of his radical politics.
- ****Clarinda**** — Pseudonym for Agnes McLehose, with whom Burns conducted a passionate but unconsummated epistolary affair in Edinburgh (1787–1791).

## Debates and open questions
*   **Jamaica plantation role:** Historians debate Burns’s complicity in slavery. He accepted a post as "book-keeper" (assistant overseer) in 1786 but never went. His 1792 poem "The Slave's Lament" is attributed to him but disputed; he signed no abolitionist petitions. Scholar Lisa Williams notes his "strange silence" on chattel slavery compared to peers.
*   **Cause of death:** Traditional accounts cite rheumatic heart disease (possibly from childhood) exacerbated by a dental abscess infection. James Currie’s biography emphasized alcoholism ("intemperance"), but modern medical historians argue hard labour and infection were primary, with alcohol rumours overstated.
*   **Mental health:** Biographer Robert Crawford argues for bipolar disorder ("manic depression") based on poetic extremes and handwriting analysis; Burns called his lows "blue devilism." The National Trust for Scotland rejects the diagnosis as unsupported by sufficient evidence.
*   **Mary Campbell ("Highland Mary"):** The nature of their 1786 relationship is conjectural. Burns wrote she "plighted their troth" over the Water of Fail (a traditional marriage rite) and planned to emigrate together; she died of typhus months later.
*   **Number of skull casts:** Following the 1834 phrenological exhumation, it is unknown how many plaster casts were made by plasterer James Fraser; six are documented in museums, but some may be copies of an original.
*   **Authorship of "The Merry Muses of Caledonia":** This collection of bawdy songs bears a title not chosen by Burns; the extent of his authorship versus collection/adaptation of existing folk material remains unclear.
