# Second Congo War

## Overview
The Second Congo War (1998–2003) was a devastating multi-nation conflict in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), often called "Africa's World War." It began when Congolese President Laurent-Désiré Kabila turned against his former Rwandan and Ugandan allies, who had helped him seize power. The war drew in nine African nations and numerous armed groups, causing an estimated 5.4 million deaths—primarily from disease and malnutrition—making it the deadliest conflict since World War II. Its legacy of violence and instability continues in eastern Congo today.

## Timeline
- **2 August 1998** — Second Congo War begins as Banyamulenge rebels, backed by Rwanda, revolt in Goma.
- **19 August 1998** — Zimbabwe, Angola, and Namibia intervene to support the Kabila government.
- **18 January 1999** — A ceasefire is agreed in Windhoek, but fighting continues.
- **July 1999** — The Lusaka Ceasefire Agreement is signed by six warring nations.
- **16 January 2001** — President Laurent-Désiré Kabila is assassinated; his son Joseph succeeds him.
- **April 2001** — A UN panel reports on the illegal exploitation of Congolese resources.
- **19 April 2002** — The Sun City Agreement is signed, outlining a framework for peace.
- **30 July 2002** — Rwanda and the DRC sign the Pretoria Accord for peace and withdrawal.
- **17 December 2002** — The Global and All-Inclusive Agreement is signed, marking the formal end of the war.
- **18 July 2003** — The Transitional Government of the DRC is established.

## Background
### First Congo War
The conflict's roots lie in the First Congo War (1996–97). Rwanda, concerned about Hutu militias (ex-FAR and Interahamwe) in eastern Zaire planning attacks, backed the Tutsi-led Banyamulenge. These militias had fled there after the 1994 Rwandan genocide. The Tutsi-dominated Rwandan Patriotic Front (RPF) armed the Banyamulenge, while Zaire's Mobutu regime was too weak to stop it.

### Kabila's March to Kinshasa
With support from Rwanda, Uganda, and Angola, Laurent-Désiré Kabila's Alliance of Democratic Forces for the Liberation of Congo (ADFLC) advanced westward. Kabila, a long-time opponent of Mobutu, provided political credibility. His forces, largely Tutsi veterans, met little resistance from Mobutu's crumbling army. Kabila took Kinshasa on 17 May 1997, renamed the country the Democratic Republic of Congo, and became president.

### Unwelcome Support from Other African Nations
After taking power, Kabila faced the challenge of governing and the presence of his foreign backers. The visible Rwandan military in the capital angered many Congolese. Tensions peaked when Kabila dismissed his Rwandan chief of staff and, on 30 July 1998, ordered all Rwandan and Ugandan forces to leave. This alarmed the Banyamulenge in eastern Congo and directly triggered the Second Congo War.

## Course of the War
### 1998–1999
On 2 August 1998, the Banyamulenge revolted in Goma, backed by Rwanda. The Rally for Congolese Democracy (RCD) rebel group was formed. Rwanda and Uganda launched a bold offensive, hijacking planes to seize the government base at Kitona on the Atlantic coast. By late August, rebels held key infrastructure and threatened Kinshasa. Kabila rallied a coalition of African nations to his defense. In September 1998, Zimbabwean and Angolan forces intervened, saving the government and pushing rebels back from the capital, creating a military stalemate. A ceasefire was agreed in January 1999 but failed.

### 1999–2000
Internal divisions within the RCD led to a split in April 1999. The Lusaka Ceasefire Agreement was signed in July 1999 by the six warring countries, but implementation stalled. Tensions between backers Uganda and Rwanda escalated into direct clashes between their armies in Kisangani in August 1999 and again in 2000. The UN authorized a monitoring force (MONUC) in February 2000, but fighting continued.

### 2001
On 16 January 2001, President Laurent-Désiré Kabila was assassinated by one of his bodyguards. His son, Joseph Kabila, was swiftly sworn in as president. In April, a UN panel accused Rwanda, Uganda, and Zimbabwe of systematically exploiting Congolese resources. Joseph Kabila proved a more adept negotiator, and his government's position stabilized.

### 2002
By 2002, Rwanda's position weakened due to rebel fatigue and internal mutinies. Under South African mediation, a series of peace agreements were reached. The Sun City Agreement (April) provided a framework for a unified government. The Pretoria Accord (July) secured Rwanda's withdrawal, which was completed by October. The Global and All-Inclusive Agreement (December) formally ended the war and established a transitional government.

## Terms
- **Banyamulenge**: An ethnic Tutsi group in eastern Congo whose treatment was a key trigger for both Congo wars.
- **Interahamwe**: Hutu militias responsible for the 1994 Rwandan genocide, who later operated from eastern Congo.
- **Rally for Congolese Democracy (RCD)**: A Tutsi-led rebel group backed by Rwanda that fought the Kabila government.
- **Movement for the Liberation of Congo (MLC)**: A rebel group backed exclusively by Uganda.
- **MONUC**: The United Nations Organization Mission in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, deployed to monitor the ceasefire.
- **Conflict minerals**: Resources like coltan, cassiterite, and gold whose trade funded armed groups and prolonged the war.
- **Kadogo**: A term for child soldiers, many of whom were involved in the assassination of Laurent-Désiré Kabila.
- **Effacer le tableau**: A campaign of extermination against Pygmy civilians by the MLC in 2002–2003.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Rwanda's Motives**: Scholars debate whether Rwanda's intervention was primarily to protect the Banyamulenge Tutsi or a smokescreen for regional ambitions and resource exploitation.
- **Death Toll**: The estimated 5.4 million deaths is contested. The Human Security Report Project argues the figure is inflated and should be revised to around 860,000 excess deaths, while the original authors maintain their methodology is sound.
- **Assassination of Kabila**: While a disgruntled child soldier is confirmed as the shooter, theories persist about the involvement of external actors like Rwanda, Angola, or others, though no proof exists.
- **International Complicity**: The UN has accused Uganda and Rwanda of illegally exploiting Congolese resources. Furthermore, institutions like the IMF and World Bank have been criticized for providing debt relief to these nations during the conflict, potentially facilitating the looting.
