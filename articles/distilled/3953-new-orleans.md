# New Orleans

## Overview
New Orleans is a major U.S. port city on the Mississippi River in Louisiana, renowned for its unique cultural blend of French, Spanish, African, and American influences. Founded in 1718, it has been a critical commercial hub and a center for distinctive music, cuisine, and festivals like Mardi Gras. Its history is marked by colonial struggles, rapid antebellum growth, and profound challenges from flooding, racial segregation, and the catastrophic impact of Hurricane Katrina in 2005.

## Body

### Colonial Foundations and Early Growth
New Orleans was founded in 1718 by the French Mississippi Company. It became the capital of French Louisiana before being transferred to Spain in 1763. Spanish rule lasted until 1800, when France regained control, only to sell the territory to the United States in the 1803 Louisiana Purchase. The city's early economy relied on trade and the labor of enslaved Africans, whose arrival in the 1720s began shaping a distinct Afro-Creole culture. The 1809 migration of refugees from the Haitian Revolution doubled the city's population and solidified its Francophone character.

### Antebellum Prosperity and Civil War
Following the Louisiana Purchase, New Orleans grew rapidly, becoming the wealthiest and third-most populous U.S. city by 1840. Its port was central to the domestic slave trade and the export of cotton and sugar. The city was occupied by Union forces in 1862, sparing it major wartime destruction. During Reconstruction, New Orleans had a racially integrated government and school system, but white supremacist violence, culminating in the 1874 Battle of Liberty Place, helped end federal oversight by 1877.

### Jim Crow, Decline, and Civil Rights
Post-Reconstruction, Louisiana enacted Jim Crow laws. The 1896 *Plessy v. Ferguson* case, which upheld "separate but equal" segregation, originated from a challenge by New Orleans's free people of color. The city's relative economic and population rank declined in the 20th century as trade shifted to rail and highways. It became a focal point of the Civil Rights Movement, with events like the 1960 school desegregation crisis involving Ruby Bridges.

### Hurricane Katrina and Recovery
New Orleans has always been vulnerable to flooding due to its low elevation and proximity to water. On August 29, 2005, Hurricane Katrina made landfall, causing the federally built levee system to fail and flooding over 80% of the city. The disaster killed more than 1,300 people and displaced hundreds of thousands, leading to a population decline of over 50%. Recovery has been slow and uneven, with concerns about gentrification and the displacement of long-term residents, particularly Black and low-income communities. The city's population had recovered to about 80% of its pre-Katrina level by 2015.

### Culture and Economy
New Orleans's economy is driven by its port, tourism, and conventions. The city is world-famous for its cuisine (e.g., gumbo, beignets), music (birthplace of jazz), and festivals. Its architectural heritage, especially in the French Quarter, reflects its colonial past. The city is also known for its distinctive dialects and religious diversity, including Catholicism, Protestantism, and Louisiana Voodoo.

## Terms
- **Creole**: Refers to descendants of the original French and Spanish colonists, and later, a distinct culture blending European, African, and Native American influences.
- **Gens de couleur libres**: Free people of color in colonial Louisiana, often of mixed race, who formed a distinct social class.
- **Levee**: An embankment built to prevent the overflow of a river.
- **Mardi Gras**: The final day of the Carnival season before Lent, celebrated with parades and festivals.
- **Subsidence**: The gradual sinking of land, a major issue for New Orleans due to its soft soil and drainage practices.
- **Voodoo**: A syncretic religion blending West African spiritual traditions with Catholicism, prominent in New Orleans.
- **Yat**: A distinctive local dialect of English, similar to accents found in New York City.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise rate and impact of land subsidence in the New Orleans area are debated, with studies offering varying conclusions on its severity and causes.
- The long-term viability of the city's flood protection system is questioned, as levees cannot provide absolute protection against extreme events, especially with rising sea levels.
- The social and demographic impacts of post-Katrina recovery are contested, with ongoing concerns about gentrification, displacement, and racial equity.
