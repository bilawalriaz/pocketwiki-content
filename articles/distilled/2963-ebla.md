# Ebla

## Overview
Ebla was a major ancient Semitic city-state in Syria, flourishing from the Early to Middle Bronze Age (c. 3500–1600 BC). Its discovery, particularly the Ebla tablets, revolutionized understanding of the ancient Near East by proving the Levant was a center of civilization equal to Egypt and Mesopotamia. The city experienced three distinct kingdoms, each destroyed and rebuilt, and was a vital hub for international trade and administration.

## Timeline
- **c. 3500 BC** — First settled as a small agricultural community (Mardikh I).
- **c. 3000–2300 BC** — First Kingdom (Mardikh II) period; Ebla becomes a dominant Syrian power.
- **c. 2500–2350 BC** — "Age of the Archives"; Ebla at its peak under kings like Irkab-Damu and Isar-Damu.
- **c. 2300 BC** — First destruction of Ebla, ending the archive period.
- **2300–2000 BC** — Second Kingdom (Mardikh IIB2); rebuilt under a new dynasty, later a vassal of Ur III.
- **c. 2000 BC** — Second destruction of Ebla.
- **c. 2000–1600 BC** — Third Kingdom (Mardikh III); rebuilt by Amorites, becomes a vassal of Yamhad (Aleppo).
- **c. 1600 BC** — Final destruction by the Hittite king Mursili I.
- **1964** — Italian archaeologists begin excavations at Tell Mardikh.
- **1968** — Statue of King Ibbit-Lim found, identifying the site as ancient Ebla.
- **1974–1976** — Discovery of the Ebla tablets archive in Palace G.
- **2011–2020** — Site damaged during the Syrian Civil War; used as a military observation point.

## Body

### The Three Kingdoms of Ebla
Ebla's history is divided into three major periods. The **First Kingdom** (c. 3000–2300 BC) was a powerful, expansionist state controlling much of northern Syria. It maintained a vast trade network and left behind the famous Ebla tablets. This kingdom ended with a major destruction c. 2300 BC. The **Second Kingdom** (c. 2300–2000 BC) was a continuation under a new dynasty, eventually becoming a vassal of the Ur III empire before its own destruction. The **Third Kingdom** (c. 2000–1600 BC) was rebuilt by Amorite settlers. It flourished as a trade center but was a vassal of the kingdom of Yamhad until its final destruction by the Hittites.

### Society, Language, and Culture
Ebla's population during its peak was estimated at 40,000 in the capital and over 200,000 in the kingdom. The people spoke **Eblaite**, a Semitic language with features of both East and West Semitic. Society was notable for the high status of women, who received equal pay and held important positions. The city's architecture, with palaces open to courtyards, contrasted with the more citadel-like Mesopotamian models. Religious festivals were elaborate, and the pantheon was primarily North-Western Semitic, featuring deities unique to Ebla like Kura and Hadabal.

### Economy and Trade
Ebla's prosperity was built on a redistributive economy controlled by the palace, which managed large herds of sheep, goats, and cattle. Its wealth came from extensive international trade, exporting timber and textiles. Artifacts found at Ebla include goods from Sumer, Cyprus, Egypt, and as far as Afghanistan. The city was a commercial rival to Mari and maintained trade routes to Mesopotamia and the Mediterranean coast.

### The Ebla Tablets and Library
The discovery of about 20,000 cuneiform tablets (c. 2500–2350 BC) was monumental. Written in Sumerian and Eblaite, they form the oldest known library, showing sophisticated arrangement by subject. The tablets provide detailed records of economic transactions, diplomatic correspondence, and administrative practices, offering unparalleled insight into 3rd millennium BC Levantine society.

### Religion and Royal Ideology
Ebla was polytheistic. The First Kingdom's pantheon included unique deities like the city's patron god Kura and his consort Barama, alongside others like Hadad and the goddess Ishara, who was more prominent than Ishtar. The dead kings were also worshiped. In the Third Kingdom, under Amorite influence, Hadad and Ishtar became the most important deities, and unique Eblaite gods disappeared.

## Terms
- **Tell**: An artificial mound formed by the accumulated debris of human settlement over millennia.
- **Cuneiform**: An ancient system of writing using wedge-shaped marks on clay tablets.
- **Eblaite**: The Semitic language spoken in Ebla, attested in the city's tablets.
- **Amorite**: A Semitic people who migrated into the Levant and Mesopotamia in the late 3rd millennium BC.
- **Vassal**: A state or ruler that is subordinate to and pays tribute to a more powerful state.
- **Redistributive economy**: An economic system where a central authority (like a palace) collects goods and resources and then distributes them to the population.
- **Chora**: The core economic hinterland directly controlled by and supporting a capital city.
- **Hypogeum**: An underground tomb or vault.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Cause of the First Destruction (c. 2300 BC)**: Theories include conquest by Akkadian kings Sargon or Naram-Sin, revenge by the city of Mari, or a natural catastrophe. There is no scholarly consensus.
- **Nature of the Second Kingdom**: The relationship between Ebla and the Ur III empire is unclear; it was likely a vassal, but the exact terms are unknown.
- **Biblical Connections**: Early claims of links between the Ebla tablets and the Bible (e.g., references to Yahweh or Sodom) are now widely discredited by the academic consensus.
- **Chronology**: The absolute chronology of the archive period is uncertain, though a relative chronology for its 50-year span has been established.
