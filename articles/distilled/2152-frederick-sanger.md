# Frederick Sanger

## Overview
Frederick Sanger (1918–2013) was a British biochemist who won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry twice. His work determined the precise structures of proteins and DNA, providing the foundational techniques for molecular biology that enabled the sequencing of the human genome.

## Timeline
- **13 August 1918** — Born in Rendcomb, Gloucestershire, England.
- **1940** — Began PhD at Cambridge University on amino acid metabolism.
- **1951-1952** — Determined the complete amino acid sequences of insulin's A and B chains.
- **1958** — Awarded first Nobel Prize in Chemistry for protein sequencing work.
- **1962** — Moved to the newly built Laboratory of Molecular Biology in Cambridge.
- **1964** — Discovered formylmethionine tRNA, which initiates protein synthesis in bacteria.
- **1975** — Published the "Plus and Minus" DNA sequencing technique.
- **1977** — Introduced the "dideoxy" chain-termination method for DNA sequencing.
- **1980** — Awarded second Nobel Prize in Chemistry (shared with Walter Gilbert and Paul Berg).
- **1983** — Retired from research.
- **4 October 1993** — Opened the Sanger Centre (now Sanger Institute), named in his honour.
- **19 November 2013** — Died in Cambridge.

## Body

### Sequencing Insulin
Sanger's first major breakthrough was determining the complete amino acid sequence of the two polypeptide chains of bovine insulin. This proved that proteins have a unique, defined chemical structure, overturning the assumption that they were amorphous. He developed a method using 1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (Sanger's reagent) to label the N-terminal amino acid of a chain. He then partially hydrolyzed the protein into smaller peptides, separated them using two-dimensional paper chromatography and electrophoresis to create a "fingerprint," and identified each fragment. By assembling these overlapping fragments, he deduced the full sequence. This discovery was foundational for the central dogma of molecular biology, showing how DNA codes for specific proteins.

### Sequencing RNA and DNA
After moving to the Laboratory of Molecular Biology, Sanger turned to nucleic acids. He first worked on RNA, discovering the initiator tRNA in bacteria and sequencing the 5S ribosomal RNA from *E. coli*. He then developed methods for DNA sequencing. His initial "Plus and Minus" technique (1975) used DNA polymerase and radiolabeled nucleotides to generate fragments, allowing him to sequence the first complete DNA genome, bacteriophage φX174. In 1977, he introduced the revolutionary "dideoxy" chain-termination method. This technique used modified nucleotides (dideoxynucleotides) to terminate DNA chain elongation at specific bases, creating fragments of different lengths that could be separated by size. This method, known as the Sanger method, enabled rapid and accurate sequencing of long DNA stretches and was ultimately used to sequence the entire human genome.

### Legacy and Personal Life
Sanger supervised over ten PhD students, two of whom—Rodney Porter and Elizabeth Blackburn—also won Nobel Prizes. He retired in 1983 but agreed to have the Sanger Centre (now the Sanger Institute) named after him in 1992; it became a leading global centre for genomics. Raised a Quaker, he later became an agnostic. He was known for his modesty, declining a knighthood and describing himself as "just a chap who messed about in a lab."

## Terms
- **Amino acid sequence**: The specific linear order of amino acids in a protein chain, which determines its structure and function.
- **Polypeptide chain**: A linear organic polymer consisting of a large number of amino-acid residues bonded together in a chain, forming part of (or the whole of) a protein molecule.
- **N-terminal**: The start of a protein or polypeptide chain, terminated by an amino acid with a free amine group (-NH2).
- **Fingerprinting**: A technique for separating and identifying peptide fragments from a protein to deduce its sequence.
- **Dideoxy chain-termination method (Sanger method)**: A DNA sequencing technique that uses dideoxynucleotides to terminate DNA strand synthesis, producing fragments of varying lengths that are separated to determine the sequence.
- **DNA polymerase I**: An enzyme that synthesizes DNA molecules from nucleotide building blocks.
- **Bacteriophage φX174**: A virus that infects bacteria; its genome was the first fully sequenced DNA-based genome.
- **Formylmethionine tRNA**: A specific transfer RNA molecule that initiates protein synthesis in bacteria by carrying the first amino acid.
