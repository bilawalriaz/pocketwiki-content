# Niels Bohr

## Overview
Niels Bohr (1885–1962) was a Danish theoretical physicist whose work on atomic structure and quantum theory fundamentally shaped modern physics. He developed the Bohr model of the atom, introduced the principle of complementarity, and founded the Copenhagen school of quantum mechanics. Beyond science, he was a philosopher and advocate for international scientific cooperation, particularly regarding nuclear energy.

## Timeline
- **7 October 1885** — Born in Copenhagen, Denmark.
- **1911** — Completed Ph.D. thesis on electron theory of metals.
- **1913** — Published his "trilogy" of papers proposing the Bohr model of the atom.
- **1921** — Founded the Institute of Theoretical Physics (now Niels Bohr Institute) in Copenhagen.
- **1922** — Awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics for his work on atomic structure.
- **1927** — Formulated the principle of complementarity; participated in the Como Conference.
- **1939** — Correctly identified uranium-235 as the isotope responsible for fission with thermal neutrons.
- **September 1941** — Met with Werner Heisenberg in Copenhagen, a meeting of enduring historical speculation.
- **September 1943** — Fled Nazi-occupied Denmark to Sweden, then to Britain.
- **1943–1945** — Worked with the British Tube Alloys project and visited the Manhattan Project.
- **1957** — Became first chairman of the Nordic Institute for Theoretical Physics (NORDITA).
- **18 November 1962** — Died in Copenhagen.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Bohr was born in Copenhagen to a professor of physiology and the daughter of a banker. He studied physics at the University of Copenhagen under Christian Christiansen. In 1905, he won a gold medal competition for his work on measuring surface tension, improving upon Lord Rayleigh's method. He completed his master's thesis in 1909 and his Ph.D. in 1911 on the electron theory of metals, concluding that classical electron theory could not fully explain the magnetic properties of metals.

### The Bohr Model and Early Quantum Theory
In 1911, Bohr traveled to England, meeting Ernest Rutherford, whose nuclear model of the atom became the foundation for Bohr's work. In 1913, Bohr published his "trilogy" of papers, adapting Rutherford's model with Max Planck's quantum theory. He proposed that electrons orbit the nucleus in discrete, quantized "stationary states" and can jump between orbits, emitting or absorbing specific quanta of energy. This model successfully derived the Balmer series for hydrogen spectral lines and explained the Pickering series as due to ionized helium. While superseded by later quantum mechanics, the model's principles remain valid and it is still widely taught.

### The Copenhagen Institute and Later Atomic Work
Bohr founded the Institute of Theoretical Physics in 1920, which became a global center for quantum mechanics. He mentored physicists like Hans Kramers and Werner Heisenberg. In 1924, using the Pauli exclusion principle, Bohr predicted the chemical properties of the then-undiscovered element 72, which was subsequently found and named hafnium. In 1927, he conceived the principle of complementarity, stating that quantum objects can exhibit mutually exclusive properties (like wave or particle behavior) depending on the experimental context. This idea, along with his work on the correspondence principle, was central to the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics.

### World War II and the Manhattan Project
Bohr helped refugee scholars escape Nazism in the 1930s. After the German occupation of Denmark, his 1941 meeting with Heisenberg, who led the German nuclear project, remains historically debated. In 1943, Bohr escaped to Sweden and then Britain, where he joined the Allied nuclear effort. He worked with the Manhattan Project, visiting Los Alamos, and recognized early that nuclear weapons would transform international relations. He advocated for sharing information with the Soviet Union to foster post-war cooperation, a position rejected by Churchill and Roosevelt. After the war, he called for international control of nuclear energy.

### Philosophy and Later Life
Bohr was deeply influenced by philosophy, particularly the work of Søren Kierkegaard and Immanuel Kant. He argued that quantum phenomena must be described using classical concepts like space and time to be communicable. His principle of complementarity was both a physical and philosophical framework. After the war, he was instrumental in establishing CERN and the Nordic Institute for Theoretical Physics. He received the first Atoms for Peace Award in 1957.

## Terms
- **Bor Model:** An early model of the atom where electrons orbit the nucleus in specific, quantized energy levels.
- **Complementarity:** Bohr's principle that quantum objects can have contradictory properties (e.g., wave and particle) that cannot be observed simultaneously.
- **Correspondence Principle:** The idea that quantum mechanical behavior must approximate classical physics at large quantum numbers.
- **Old Quantum Theory:** The pre-1925 quantum theory incorporating Planck's quantum and Bohr's atomic model.
- **Stationary States:** The discrete, stable orbits of electrons in the Bohr model where they do not radiate energy.
- **Copenhagen Interpretation:** The dominant interpretation of quantum mechanics developed by Bohr, Heisenberg, and others in Copenhagen.
- **Liquid Drop Model:** Bohr's 1936 model of the atomic nucleus, treating it as a deformable drop of nuclear fluid.
- **Tube Alloys:** The codename for the British nuclear weapons research project during WWII.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **The Bohr-Heisenberg Meeting (1941):** The content and intent of their private conversation remain disputed, with differing accounts from both men and historical speculation about whether Heisenberg sought to warn Bohr or discuss moral responsibilities.
- **Bohr's Philosophical Stance:** Scholars debate whether Bohr was an anti-realist, instrumentalist, or a different kind of realist regarding the quantum world. His famous quote about "no quantum world" is attributed second-hand and disputed.
- **Influence of Kierkegaard:** The extent to which the philosopher Kierkegaard influenced Bohr's scientific and philosophical thought is debated among biographers.
- **Role in Danish Rescue (1943):** Historians differ on whether Bohr's actions in Sweden were a decisive factor in the subsequent mass rescue of Danish Jews.
