# Jane Addams

## Overview
Jane Addams (1860–1935) was a pioneering American social reformer, activist, and philosopher who co-founded Hull House, a settlement house that provided social services to immigrant communities in Chicago. She was a leader in the Progressive Era, advocating for social justice, women's suffrage, and world peace, and became the first American woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize in 1931. Her work fundamentally shaped the fields of social work and sociology, emphasizing democratic cooperation and addressing systemic issues like poverty, child labor, and public health.

## Timeline
- **1860** — Born in Cedarville, Illinois.
- **1881** — Graduated from Rockford Female Seminary; father died.
- **1889** — Co-founded Hull House in Chicago with Ellen Gates Starr.
- **1910** — Awarded honorary M.A. from Yale, first woman to receive one.
- **1912** — Co-founded the Juvenile Protective Association; published *A New Conscience and Ancient Evil*.
- **1915** — Chaired the International Congress of Women at The Hague; co-founded the Women's International League for Peace and Freedom (WILPF).
- **1920** — Co-founded the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU).
- **1931** — Awarded the Nobel Peace Prize (shared with Nicholas Murray Butler).
- **1935** — Died in Chicago; buried in Cedarville, Illinois.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born into a prosperous family, Addams was influenced by her father, a state senator and supporter of Abraham Lincoln. After her mother's early death, she was raised by siblings. A childhood illness caused lifelong health issues. She attended Rockford Female Seminary, graduating in 1881, where she was influenced by writers like Thomas Carlyle and Leo Tolstoy. Her father's death left her financially independent but directionless; a failed attempt at medical school and a European tour followed. She was inspired by the settlement house model after visiting Toynbee Hall in London in 1888.

### Hull House and Settlement Work
In 1889, Addams and Ellen Gates Starr co-founded Hull House in a run-down mansion. It became a center for social services, research, and cultural activities for Chicago's immigrant population. Hull House offered a night school, art gallery, gymnasium, and other facilities, serving about 2,000 people weekly. Residents conducted studies on housing, health, and labor, leading to reforms like the first juvenile court law and factory inspection laws. Addams emphasized "civic housekeeping," arguing women's domestic skills should inform public policy on issues like sanitation and public health. The settlement's art program aimed to foster independent thinking and cultural exchange.

### Social Reform and Advocacy
Addams linked social reform to democratic ideals, working with philosopher John Dewey to redefine democracy through civic activism. She supported labor unions, women's suffrage, and immigrant rights. Hull House became a training ground for social workers and a hub for reform campaigns, including the "garbage wars" to improve sanitation. She co-founded the Juvenile Protective Association in 1901 to address child welfare. Her writings, such as *The Spirit of Youth and the City Streets* (1909), highlighted the need for urban recreation. She also supported eugenics and prohibition, viewing them as tools to combat social ills.

### Peace Activism and Pacifism
Addams was a leading pacifist, opposing U.S. imperialism after the Spanish-American War. During World War I, she chaired the International Congress of Women at The Hague in 1915, advocating for mediation. She co-founded the WILPF and served as its president, promoting "positive peace" that integrated social justice. Her pacifism drew criticism during the war, but she persisted, linking peace to women's roles and democratic values. Her philosophy, termed "peaceweaving," emphasized building relationships to address root causes of conflict. She received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1931 for her efforts.

### Personal Life and Legacy
Addams had romantic relationships with women, including Starr and Mary Rozet Smith, with whom she shared a home for 40 years. She was a secular humanist, though her work was influenced by social Christianity. She taught at the University of Chicago Extension and was a charter member of the American Sociological Society. Her legacy includes founding Hull House, advancing social work, and influencing sociology. She is memorialized through institutions like the Jane Addams College of Social Work and the Jane Addams Memorial Tollway.

## Terms
- **Settlement house**: A community center in an urban area where volunteers live and provide social services to the poor, aiming to bridge class divides.
- **Progressive Era**: A period of social activism and political reform in the U.S. from the 1890s to the 1920s, addressing issues like industrialization and urbanization.
- **Social work**: A profession focused on helping individuals and communities enhance well-being, often through advocacy and direct service.
- **Pacifism**: Opposition to war and violence, advocating for peaceful conflict resolution.
- **Pragmatism**: A philosophical tradition emphasizing practical consequences and real-world effects as the basis for knowledge and action.
- **Civic housekeeping**: Addams's concept that women's domestic skills should be applied to public policy and community welfare.
- **Peaceweaving**: A metaphor for building peace through relationships and addressing social justice, as theorized by Addams.
- **Eugenics**: A now-discredited movement advocating for selective breeding to improve human genetic traits, often tied to social reform in the early 20th century.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Secular vs. Religious Motives**: Scholars debate whether Addams's work was primarily driven by secular humanism or social Christianity, with evidence supporting both influences.
- **Eugenics Support**: Her advocacy for eugenics is controversial, as it conflicts with her later legacy of social justice, raising questions about her views on race and disability.
- **Pacifism's Impact**: Historians assess whether her pacifism during World War I weakened the U.S. position or advanced long-term peace ideals.
- **Legacy in Sociology**: There is ongoing discussion about her marginalization in early sociology and efforts to reclaim her contributions to the field.
