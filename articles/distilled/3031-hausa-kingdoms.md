# Hausa Kingdoms

## Overview
The Hausa Kingdoms (Masarautun Hausa) were a collection of states in the region between the Niger River and Lake Chad (modern northern Nigeria), ruled by the Hausa people before the Fulani jihads of the early 19th century. They were significant trading centers that connected the Western and Eastern Sudanic empires, and their history is defined by urbanization, the spread of Islam, and eventual conquest by the Sokoto Caliphate.

## Timeline
- **c. 9th century** — Hausa states possibly first mentioned by Ya'qubi.
- **1349–85** — King Yaji I of Kano converts to Islam; conquers Rano.
- **15th century** — Bornu expands west, vassalizing Hausa states.
- **1463–99** — Muhammad Rumfa of Kano builds mosques and madrassahs.
- **1549–66** — Ibrahim Maje institutes Islamic marriage law in Katsina.
- **18th century** — States become economically and politically exhausted.
- **By 1808** — Hausa states conquered by Usuman dan Fodio and incorporated into the Sokoto Caliphate.

## Body

### Origins and Mythology
Hausa oral tradition centers on the Bayajidda legend, which describes a Baghdadi prince who kills a snake (Sarki) in a well at Daura and marries the queen, Daurama. This marriage ended the region's matriarchal rule. Their descendants are said to have founded the Hausa Bakwai (seven "true" states) and the Hausa Banza ("bastard" or illegitimate) states. The seven "true" states were Daura, Kano, Katsina, Zazzau, Gobir, Rano, and Biram. Daura is considered the "mother city."

### Rise and Urbanization
Early Hausa authority was based on control of agricultural land and kinship. Urbanization led to fortified capital cities becoming centers of power. The states developed specialized economic roles: Kano and Rano were textile centers ("kings of indigo"), Katsina and Daura were trade hubs ("kings of the market"), Zazzau supplied slave labor ("king of the slaves"), and Gobir defended the northern frontier ("king of war"). Immigration from the Sahel and Sudan, including merchants, clerics, and pastoralists, was constant.

### Islam and Trade
Islam spread to Hausaland from the north and east. The first ruler to convert was likely Yaji I of Kano (r. 1349–85), influenced by Soninke Wangara traders. Subsequent rulers, like Muhammad Rumfa of Kano (r. 1463–99), built mosques and brought in scholars, turning Kano and Katsina into centers of Islamic learning. Islam facilitated trade networks and provided legal and administrative support through the Ulama class. However, a divide persisted between the Muslim urban elite and rural communities practicing traditional or syncretic religion.

### Fall to the Sokoto Caliphate
By the 18th century, the Hausa states were weakened by internal wars, heavy taxation, and famines. In the early 19th century, a Fulani-led jihad, citing social injustices and religious syncretism, conquered the states. By 1808, they were incorporated into the Hausa-Fulani Sokoto Caliphate under Usuman dan Fodio.

## Terms
- **Hausa Bakwai**: The seven "true" states founded by the descendants of Bayajidda.
- **Hausa Banza**: The "bastard" or illegitimate states, considered inferior to the Hausa Bakwai.
- **Sarki**: The Hausa word for "king" or ruler.
- **Ulama**: A class of Muslim scholars and clerics who provided legal and administrative services.
- **Gandu**: A farm unit operated by two or more families, headed by a legal representative (maigida).
- **Talakawa**: The commoners in Hausa society.
- **Sarkin noma**: "Kings of farming," early chiefs responsible for agricultural rituals.
- **Sabon gari**: Quarters or new towns founded by Hausa traders in other regions.

## Debates and Open questions
- The exact date of the foundation of the Hausa kingdoms is unknown.
- It is debated whether the 15th-century vassalage by Bornu included all Hausa states or only Kano and Biram.
- The extent of Leo Africanus's personal visit to Hausaland is uncertain, though he described its political and economic state.
