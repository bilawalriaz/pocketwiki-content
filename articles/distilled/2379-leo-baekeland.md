# Leo Baekeland

## Overview
Leo Hendrik Baekeland (1863–1944) was a Belgian chemist who became a pivotal figure in American industry. He is best known for inventing Velox photographic paper and, most significantly, Bakelite, the first fully synthetic plastic. His work with Bakelite launched the modern plastics industry, earning him the title "The Father of the Plastics Industry."

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born in Ghent to a cobbler and a housemaid, Baekeland excelled academically. He earned a PhD *maxima cum laude* from Ghent University at age 21 and briefly taught before becoming an associate professor of chemistry in 1889.

### Move to America and Velox
In 1889, a travel scholarship brought Baekeland to the U.S., where he was encouraged to stay by a Columbia professor. After working for a photographic company, he established himself as a consultant. He then focused on creating a photographic paper for artificial light enlargements. After two years, he perfected "Velox," the first commercially successful paper of its kind. In 1899, he sold the rights to George Eastman for $750,000, securing his financial independence.

### Transition and Electrochemistry
The sale of Velox included a non-compete clause preventing Baekeland from photographic research for 20 years. He used his wealth to build a private laboratory and pursued new fields. After studying electrochemistry in Germany, he helped develop an improved electrolytic cell for the chloralkali process, contributing to the founding of the Hooker Chemical Company.

### Invention of Bakelite
Seeking a new profitable field, Baekeland investigated synthetic resins. He systematically studied the reactions between phenol (carbolic acid) and formaldehyde, building on earlier, unsuccessful work by chemists like Adolf von Baeyer. His goal was to create a synthetic shellac substitute, which he called "Novolak." Continuing his research, he discovered that by controlling temperature and pressure, he could produce a hard, moldable, and insoluble plastic. He named this material Bakelite. Its process patent was filed in 1907. Bakelite was the first plastic that retained its shape after being heated, making it ideal for electrical insulators, radios, and telephones, and marking the beginning of the plastics age.

### Later Life and Legacy
Baekeland became a professor at Columbia in 1917 and a U.S. citizen in 1919. In 1922, his company merged into the Bakelite Corporation following favorable patent litigation. He received major awards, including the Perkin Medal (1916) and Franklin Medal (1940). He sold his company to Union Carbide in 1939 and retired. At his death in 1944, global Bakelite production was approximately 175,000 tons, used in over 15,000 products. He held more than 100 patents.

## Terms
- **Bakelite**: The first synthetic plastic, invented by Baekeland. It is a thermosetting phenol-formaldehyde resin, valued for its electrical insulation and heat resistance.
- **Velox**: The first commercially successful photographic paper, invented by Baekeland, which allowed enlargements to be printed by artificial light.
- **Phenol (carbolic acid)**: A key chemical compound used in the synthesis of Bakelite.
- **Formaldehyde**: A key chemical compound used in the synthesis of Bakelite.
- **Novolak**: An early, soluble phenol-formaldehyde resin created by Baekeland as a synthetic shellac substitute; it was not a major commercial success.
- **Thermosetting plastic**: A plastic that irreversibly cures (sets) under heat and pressure, like Bakelite, and cannot be remelted.
- **Chloralkali process**: An industrial process for producing chlorine and sodium hydroxide, which Baekeland helped improve with a better electrolytic cell.
- **Polymeric**: A term for molecules composed of many repeated subunits; Baekeland recognized natural resins and fibers as polymeric.
