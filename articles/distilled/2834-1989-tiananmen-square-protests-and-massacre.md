# 1989 Tiananmen Square protests and massacre

## Overview
The 1989 Tiananmen Square protests were student-led demonstrations in Beijing demanding democratic reforms, press freedom, and an end to corruption. Beginning as mourning for reformist leader Hu Yaobang, the movement escalated into a nationwide challenge to the Chinese Communist Party's authority. The government's violent military crackdown on June 4, 1989, caused significant civilian casualties, halted political liberalization, and drew enduring international condemnation.

## Timeline
- **15 April 1989** — Death of former CCP General Secretary Hu Yaobang sparks initial student gatherings.
- **26 April 1989** — *People's Daily* editorial brands protests a "counter-revolutionary rebellion," angering students.
- **13 May 1989** — Students begin a hunger strike in Tiananmen Square, days before Soviet leader Gorbachev's visit.
- **19 May 1989** — Zhao Ziyang makes his last public appearance, urging students to end their hunger strike.
- **20 May 1989** — Chinese government declares martial law and mobilizes troops to Beijing.
- **3–4 June 1989** — Military advances into Beijing, engaging in bloody clashes with protesters along Chang'an Avenue.
- **4 June 1989** — Troops clear Tiananmen Square in the early morning hours.
- **5 June 1989** — "Tank Man" incident occurs; a lone protester blocks a column of tanks.
- **9 June 1989** — Deng Xiaoping publicly praises the military action and condemns the protests.
- **13 June 1989** — Beijing police issue arrest warrants for 21 most-wanted student leaders.

## Body

### Origins and Background
The protests emerged from a decade of economic reform under Deng Xiaoping, which dismantled the Mao-era "iron rice bowl" (guaranteed lifetime employment) and created social dislocation. Rapid growth brought inflation, corruption, and a legitimacy crisis for the Communist Party. Intellectuals and students, facing a poor job market and inspired by the "New Enlightenment" movement, increasingly called for political liberalization. Hu Yaobang's death on April 15, 1989, provided the catalyst for students to gather and voice grievances.

### Escalation and Government Division
The movement grew from mourning into organized protest, with students drafting the "Seven Demands." The government response was divided between reformers led by General Secretary Zhao Ziyang, who favored dialogue, and hardliners led by Premier Li Peng. A pivotal moment came on April 26, when a hardline *People's Daily* editorial condemned the protests, backfiring by galvanizing more support. By May, a student hunger strike and Gorbachev's impending visit escalated the crisis. Zhao's conciliatory speeches were undermined by hardliners, and on May 17, Deng Xiaoping decided to impose martial law. Zhao's final public appearance on May 19, urging students to end their strike, marked his political end.

### Military Crackdown and Aftermath
After initial attempts to enter the city were blocked by citizens, the military launched a violent advance on June 3. Troops used live ammunition to clear barricades along Chang'an Avenue, with the heaviest fighting and casualties in the Muxidi area. In the early hours of June 4, soldiers cleared Tiananmen Square, destroying the "Goddess of Democracy" statue. The iconic "Tank Man" photograph was taken on June 5. The government reported about 300 deaths, while other estimates ranged into the thousands. In the aftermath, the CCP purged reformist officials, including Zhao Ziyang, arrested thousands, and launched a propaganda campaign justifying the crackdown as necessary to end "political turmoil."

### Long-Term Impact and Censorship
The suppression ended a period of political opening and accelerated a shift toward market-oriented reforms under strong state control. Internationally, China faced arms embargoes from the U.S. and EU and temporary isolation. Domestically, the event became one of the most censored topics in China. The government's official narrative condemns the protests as a "riot" and credits the crackdown with ensuring stability for economic growth. Public commemoration is forbidden, and the "Tank Man" image is blocked. The events continue to shape China's political landscape, limiting political expression while the economy has grown dramatically.

## Terms
- **Iron Rice Bowl**: The Mao-era system of guaranteed lifetime employment and social benefits in state-owned enterprises, dismantled during economic reforms.
- **Boluan Fanzheng**: Deng Xiaoping's policy of "bringing order out of chaos" to correct the mistakes of the Cultural Revolution and launch economic reforms.
- **New Enlightenment**: A movement among Chinese intellectuals in the 1980s promoting liberal values like democracy, humanism, and Western-style reforms.
- **26 April Editorial**: A *People's Daily* editorial branding the student movement a "counter-revolutionary rebellion," escalating tensions.
- **Operation Yellowbird**: A covert operation organized from Hong Kong to help dissident leaders escape China after the crackdown.
- **Goddess of Democracy**: A 10-meter-tall statue erected by protesters in Tiananmen Square, destroyed by the military on June 4.
- **Tank Man**: An unidentified protester who stood before a column of tanks on June 5, 1989, in an iconic act of defiance.
- **Tiananmen Mothers**: A victims' advocacy group founded by Ding Zilin and Zhang Xianling to seek accountability for those killed.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Death Toll**: Official figures cite around 300 dead, while independent estimates range from several hundred to several thousand. The exact number remains disputed due to government suppression.
- **Violence in the Square**: Foreign journalists and diplomats present reported no mass killing *inside* Tiananmen Square during the final clearance. The vast majority of violence and deaths occurred on Beijing's streets, particularly along Chang'an Avenue.
- **International Involvement**: The Chinese government alleges the protests were instigated by Western powers as a "color revolution." This claim is rejected by most independent scholars and participants.
- **Legacy and Reassessment**: Calls for the government to officially reassess its verdict have been made by figures like Zhao Ziyang's aide Bao Tong, but the CCP maintains the crackdown was necessary and correct.
