# Arthur Schopenhauer

## Overview
Arthur Schopenhauer (1788–1860) was a German philosopher best known for *The World as Will and Representation* (1818), which posits that the phenomenal world is driven by a blind, irrational will. Building on Kant’s transcendental idealism, he rejected German idealism and integrated insights from Indian philosophy, especially the Upanishads and Buddhism. His philosophy of pessimism profoundly influenced later thinkers, artists, and scientists, despite limited recognition during his lifetime.

## Timeline
- **1788** — Born in Danzig
- **1802–1805** — Merchant apprenticeship; father’s death in 1805
- **1809** — Enrolls at University of Göttingen
- **1811** — Moves to University of Berlin
- **1813** — Completes *On the Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason*
- **1818** — Publishes *The World as Will and Representation*
- **1820** — Briefly lectures at University of Berlin
- **1840** — Publishes *The Two Fundamental Problems of Ethics*
- **1851** — Publishes *Parerga and Paralipomena*, gains wider attention
- **1860** — Dies in Frankfurt am Main

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born into a wealthy merchant family in Danzig, Schopenhauer received a practical education but showed little interest in commerce. After his father’s suspected suicide in 1805, he pursued philosophy, studying under figures like Johann Gottlieb Fichte and Friedrich Schleiermacher at the University of Berlin. He quickly rejected their ideas, gravitating instead toward Kant, Plato, and Eastern texts introduced by Friedrich Majer.

### Core Philosophy
Schopenhauer extended Kant’s transcendental idealism, arguing that the world is fundamentally shaped by human perception (*representation*) and driven by an irrational, blind force he called the *will*. This will, the thing-in-itself, manifests as endless striving, causing suffering. Liberation comes through ascetic denial of the will, a concept reinforced by his study of Hindu and Buddhist thought.

### Aesthetics and Ethics
Art, particularly music, offers temporary respite from the will by allowing contemplation of eternal Ideas. Morally, Schopenhauer emphasized compassion (*Mitleid*) as the basis of ethics, seeing all beings as manifestations of the same will. He advocated asceticism and quietism, rejecting materialism and affirming the unity of existence.

### Later Life and Legacy
Despite initial neglect, Schopenhauer gained posthumous influence through *Parerga and Paralipomena* (1851). His ideas shaped Nietzsche, Wagner, Tolstoy, and Einstein, and resonated in literature and psychology. He remained critical of Hegel and German idealism, viewing them as obfuscations of truth.

## Terms
- **Will (Wille)**: The blind, irrational essence of all being; the inner nature of the world.
- **Representation (Vorstellung)**: The phenomenal world as perceived by subjects, structured by space, time, and causality.
- **Thing-in-itself (Ding an sich)**: The true nature of reality, unknowable except as will.
- **Principle of Sufficient Reason**: The law that everything has a reason or cause.
- **Principium Individuationis**: The principle that creates the appearance of individuality; illusory in the will’s totality.
- **Asceticism**: Denial of the will to live, leading to liberation from suffering.
- **Compassion (Mitleid)**: The foundation of morality; recognition of shared suffering.
- **Metaphysical Voluntarism**: The doctrine that will, not reason, is the fundamental reality.

## Debates and Open Questions
Schopenhauer’s relationship with Buddhism is debated: while he acknowledged similarities with the Four Noble Truths, scholars question whether his philosophy truly aligns with Buddhist doctrine. His views on women, race, and Judaism remain controversial, reflecting 19th-century biases. Whether he should be classified as an antinatalist is contested—his metaphysics suggest existence transcends individual birth and death, complicating moral judgments about procreation. His influence on existentialism, psychoanalysis, and quantum physics continues to be explored.
