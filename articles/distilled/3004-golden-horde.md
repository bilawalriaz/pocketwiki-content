# Golden Horde

## Overview
The Golden Horde, or Ulus of Jochi, was a Mongol and later Turkicized khanate established in the 13th century from the northwestern part of the Mongol Empire. It became a functionally independent state after the empire's division in 1259, replacing the earlier Cuman–Kipchak confederation. The khanate was a major political and military power in Eastern Europe and Central Asia for over two centuries, profoundly influencing the political development of Russia and the surrounding regions through its system of tribute and suzerainty.

## Timeline
- **c. 1225** — Jochi receives lands around the Irtysh River from Genghis Khan.
- **1236–1242** — Batu Khan and Subutai lead the Mongol invasion of Europe, conquering Volga Bulgaria, Russian principalities, and raiding Hungary and Poland.
- **1242** — Batu establishes his capital on the lower Volga, consolidating the Horde's power.
- **1259** — After the division of the Mongol Empire, the Golden Horde becomes functionally independent.
- **1257–1266** — Reign of Berke Khan, the first Muslim ruler of the Horde, who allies with the Mamluks and fights the Ilkhanate.
- **1313–1341** — Reign of Özbeg Khan, who adopts Islam as the state religion, marking the Horde's peak.
- **1346–1347** — The Black Death spreads from the Horde's siege of Caffa to Europe.
- **1359–1381** — The "Great Troubles," a period of intense political instability and civil war.
- **1381–1395** — Tokhtamysh briefly reunites the Horde and sacks Moscow, but is later defeated by Timur.
- **1480** — The Great Stand on the Ugra River formally ends Mongol suzerainty over Moscow.
- **1502** — The Crimean Khanate sacks the Horde's capital, ending the Great Horde.

## Body

### Origins and Expansion
The Golden Horde originated from the appanage of Jochi, eldest son of Genghis Khan. After Jochi's death in 1225, his son Batu Khan led a massive western campaign (1236–1242) that conquered the Volga Bulgars, Russian principalities, and reached into Central Europe. Following the death of the Great Khan Ögedei in 1241, Batu withdrew and consolidated his power on the lower Volga, establishing a capital at Sarai. The Horde's authority over the Russian principalities was formalized through a system of tribute collection and the granting of patents (yarliqs) to princes.

### The Golden Age and Islamization
Under Batu and his successors, the Horde became a major Eurasian power. A pivotal shift occurred under Berke Khan (r. 1257–1266), who converted to Islam and allied with the Mamluk Sultanate against the rival Ilkhanate. The Horde's power and cultural prestige peaked under Özbeg Khan (r. 1313–1341), who made Islam the state religion, oversaw a flourishing trade network, and maintained a vast army. The capital, Sarai, grew into one of the world's largest cities. However, the Black Death in the 1340s severely damaged the Horde's economy and population.

### Decline and Disintegration
Following the assassination of Jani Beg in 1357, the Horde entered a prolonged period of civil war known as the "Great Troubles." Power was contested by rival khans and powerful generals like Mamai. A brief reunification under Tokhtamysh (r. 1381–1395) was shattered by invasions from the Central Asian conqueror Timur, who destroyed the Horde's capital and trade centers. After Tokhtamysh's death, the Horde fragmented into numerous successor states, including the Khanates of Kazan, Crimea, Astrakhan, and Sibir, and the Nogai Horde. The Great Horde, the last direct remnant, was finally conquered by the Crimean Khanate in 1502.

### Administration and Relations with Russia
The Golden Horde ruled its vast, multi-ethnic domain through a system of indirect control. In Russia, Mongol-appointed officials (basqaqs) initially oversaw tribute collection and conscription. Russian princes were required to receive their throne patents from the khan in Sarai. Over time, the grand princes of Moscow, starting with Ivan I, gained the exclusive right to collect tribute from other Russian principalities, which allowed them to consolidate power. The Russian Orthodox Church was granted tax exemptions and flourished under Mongol protection. While the Horde's rule was often exploitative, it also facilitated the adoption of Mongol administrative practices, military techniques, and loanwords into Russian culture.

## Terms
- **Ulus**: A Mongol term for a tribal territory or appanage; the Golden Horde was the Ulus of Jochi.
- **Kurultai**: A traditional Mongol assembly of nobles to elect a khan or make major decisions.
- **Basqaq (or Baskak)**: A Mongol official responsible for overseeing tribute collection and administration in a conquered territory.
- **Yarliq**: A patent or decree issued by a khan, granting a prince the right to rule a territory.
- **Sarai**: The capital city of the Golden Horde, first Sarai Batu, later the larger Sarai Berke.
- **Tatar Yoke**: The period of Mongol suzerainty and tribute collection over the Russian principalities (c. 1240–1480).
- **Great Stand on the Ugra River (1480)**: A military standoff that marked the formal end of Mongol political authority over Moscow.
- **Timur (Tamerlane)**: The Central Asian conqueror whose invasions in the 1390s devastated the Golden Horde and accelerated its fragmentation.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The origin and meaning of the name "Golden Horde" is debated; it may derive from the golden color of the khan's tents or be a later Russian calque.
- The precise nature and extent of Mongol influence on Russian society, culture, and state formation is a subject of ongoing scholarly discussion, with views ranging from seeing it as purely destructive to acknowledging significant administrative and cultural transfers.
- The internal political dynamics of the Horde, especially the balance of power between the khan, powerful emirs like Nogai and Edigu, and the Chinggisid princes, are complex and not fully understood due to limited contemporary sources.
