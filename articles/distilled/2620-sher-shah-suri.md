# Sher Shah Suri

## Overview
Sher Shah Suri (c. 1472/1486–1545) was an Afghan ruler who founded the Sur Empire after defeating the Mughal Emperor Humayun. Despite a brief reign of only five years, he is celebrated as one of India's greatest administrators, whose military, economic, and infrastructure reforms laid the administrative foundations later perfected by the Mughal Emperor Akbar.

## Timeline
- **1472/1486** — Born Farid Khan in Sasaram, Bihar.
- **1497** — Appointed manager of his father's jagirs (estates), beginning his administrative career.
- **1528** — Enters service of the Sultan of Bihar after his father's death.
- **1530** — Becomes regent and de facto ruler of Bihar.
- **1538** — Defeats the Sultan of Bengal and begins his rise to imperial power.
- **1540** — Defeats Humayun at the Battle of Kannauj and is crowned Emperor of Hindustan.
- **1541** — Suppresses a revolt in Bengal and reorganizes its administration.
- **1542** — Conquers the Sultanate of Malwa.
- **1543** — Defeats the Rajput king of Marwar at the Battle of Sammel.
- **1545** — Dies from injuries sustained during the siege of Kalinjar Fort.

## Body

### Rise to Power
Born into a Pashtun family, Sher Shah's early life was marked by family strife. After managing his father's estates for two decades, he entered the service of the Sultan of Bihar. Following the Sultan's death in 1528, Sher Shah became the regent, consolidating power through military and administrative reforms. He skillfully navigated conflicts with the Lohani nobility and the Bengal Sultanate, using stratagem and diplomacy to expand his control.

### Conflict with the Mughals and Imperial Ascension
Sher Shah's ambition brought him into conflict with the Mughal Empire. After a series of campaigns against Bengal, he provoked a direct confrontation with Emperor Humayun. Sher Shah's strategic genius was evident in his victories at the Battle of Chausa (1539) and the Battle of Kannauj (1540), where he decisively routed the Mughal armies. These victories drove Humayun from India and allowed Sher Shah to establish the Sur Empire with its capital in Delhi.

### Reign as Emperor and Reforms
As emperor, Sher Shah implemented sweeping reforms. He introduced a tri-metallic currency system, standardizing the silver *rūpya* (rupee), gold *mohur*, and copper *paisa*. He reorganized the empire into provinces (*Iqtas*) and districts (*Sarkars*), each with clear administrative and revenue-collection roles. His most famous infrastructure project was the extension and improvement of the Grand Trunk Road, which facilitated trade and communication across Northern India. He also established an efficient postal system and built caravanserais for merchants.

### Military Campaigns and Death
Sher Shah's empire expanded through campaigns in Punjab, Malwa, Marwar, and Mewar. He was known for his strict military discipline, merit-based promotions, and innovative tactics. His reign ended in 1545 while besieging Kalinjar Fort, where he died from injuries caused by a gunpowder explosion. His death led to succession disputes and civil war, allowing the Mughals to eventually reclaim power.

## Terms
- **Jagir**: A revenue assignment or estate granted to a noble or military officer in lieu of a cash salary.
- **Iqta**: A province or territory of the empire, often administered by a military governor.
- **Sarkar**: A district within an Iqta, overseen by civil and revenue officers.
- **Rūpya**: A silver coin of standard weight (178 grains) introduced by Sher Shah, the precursor to the modern rupee.
- **Grand Trunk Road**: A historic major road connecting the Indian subcontinent, which Sher Shah extended and improved.
- **Shiqdar**: A chief civil and military officer in a Sarkar or Pargana, responsible for law and order.
- **Munsif**: A revenue and judicial officer in a Sarkar or Pargana.
- **Dagh system**: A branding system for horses used to maintain a standing army and prevent fraud.

## Debates and Open Questions
Historians debate Sher Shah's religious policy. Some argue he was intolerant, citing his wars against Rajputs and the massacre at Raisen as *jihad*. Others contend he was generally tolerant, noting his fair treatment of Hindus in administration and justice, and that his policies aimed for Islamic supremacy without displacing Hinduism. The exact size of his armies is also questioned, with contemporary figures likely being exaggerated.
