# Oceania

## Overview

Oceania is a geographical region encompassing Australasia, Melanesia, Micronesia, and Polynesia, and is also described as a continent with mainland Australia as its continental landmass. Spanning the Eastern and Western hemispheres, it has a land area of about 9,000,000 square kilometres and a population of around 46.3 million as of 2024, making it the smallest and second-least populated continental land area after Antarctica. The region has a diverse mix of economies, from highly developed markets in Australia, French Polynesia, Hawaii, New Caledonia, and New Zealand to much less developed ones in Kiribati, Papua New Guinea, Tuvalu, Vanuatu, and Western New Guinea. First settled over 60,000 years ago and explored by Europeans from the 16th century, Oceania's history includes major colonial alteration and significant Pacific theatre action in both World Wars.

## Body

### Etymology and Definition

The term Oceania derives from the French *océanie*, coined in 1812 by geographer Conrad Malte-Brun from the Greek *Okeanòs* (ocean). Malte-Brun earlier coined the expression *Terres océaniques* ('Oceanic lands') c. 1804, from which French cartographer Adrien-Hubert Brué derived the shorter *Océanie* in 1814. The term is used because, unlike other continental groupings, the ocean links the parts of the region together.

Definitions of Oceania vary widely. The broadest encompasses islands between mainland Asia and the Americas, while more popular definitions exclude Indonesia, the Philippines, Taiwan, Japan, and islands near the Americas. In the 19th century, geographers divided Oceania into racially based subdivisions: Australasia, Malesia (the Malay Archipelago), Melanesia, Micronesia, and Polynesia. In 1973, anthropologists Roger Green and Andrew Pawley proposed the terms Near Oceania (New Guinea, Bismarck Archipelago, Solomon Islands except Santa Cruz) and Remote Oceania to replace the outdated categories, which many scholars have adopted since the early 1990s.

### Boundaries

Islands at the geographic extremes include the Bonin Islands (Japan), Hawaii (US), Clipperton Island (France), Rapa Nui/Easter Island (Chile), and Macquarie Island (Australia). The United Nations has used its own geopolitical definition since 1947, comprising Australasia, Melanesia, Micronesia, and Polynesia, excluding Hawaii (which was part of the original 1947 definition but became a US state in 1959), Easter Island, and the Bonin Islands. The CIA World Factbook uses the name "Australia and Oceania" with a similar definition. Historical interpretations varied enormously: some included the Aleutian Islands, Japanese archipelago, Taiwan, and even the Galápagos Islands, while others excluded the Malay Archipelago as belonging to Asia. The 1998 *Encyclopedia of Earth and Physical Sciences* notes that disagreement persists over whether Indonesia, the Philippines, and Taiwan should be included.

### Colonisation

Indigenous Australians migrated from Africa c. 70,000 years ago and arrived in Australia c. 50,000 years ago, reaching Tasmania c. 40,000 years ago via a land bridge. The original inhabitants of Melanesia were likely ancestors of present-day Papuan-speaking people. Micronesia was settled several millennia ago, with the earliest archaeological traces found on Saipan dated to 1500 BCE. Polynesians are a subset of the sea-migrating Austronesian people, with origins traced to Taiwan; by roughly 1400 BCE, Lapita Peoples appeared in the Bismarck Archipelago. Easter Island was discovered and settled around 1200 CE, according to recent radiocarbon dating.

### European Exploration and Settlement

Portuguese navigators between 1512 and 1526 reached the Maluku Islands, Timor, Tanimbar Islands, Caroline Islands, and west Papua New Guinea. The Magellan-Elcano expedition (1519–1522) achieved the first circumnavigation. Abel Tasman discovered Van Diemen's Land (Tasmania), New Zealand (1642), and Fiji. James Cook made first contact with Aboriginal Australians in 1770. Britain established colonies in Australia (1788), New Zealand (1840), and Fiji (1872). France declared Tahiti a protectorate in 1842 and took possession of New Caledonia in 1853. Germany established colonies in New Guinea (1884) and Samoa (1900). The US expanded into the Pacific beginning with Baker Island and Howland Island in 1857, with Hawaii becoming a US territory in 1898.

### Modern History

New Zealand forces occupied German Samoa in August 1914, and Australian forces attacked German New Guinea in September 1914. The attack on Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941 led to US entry into World War II. The Japanese invaded New Guinea and the Solomon Islands but were turned back at the Battle of the Coral Sea and the Kokoda Track campaign before being defeated in 1945. Australia and New Zealand became dominions in the 20th century, adopting the Statute of Westminster Act in 1942 and 1947 respectively. French Polynesia was renamed in 1957, Hawaii became a US state in 1959, and Fiji and Tonga became independent in 1970. The South Pacific Forum was founded in 1971, becoming the Pacific Islands Forum in 2000.

### Geography, Geology, and Environment

Oceanian islands are of four types: continental islands, high islands (volcanic), coral reefs, and uplifted coral platforms. The Pacific Plate, at 103 million square kilometres, is the largest tectonic plate and contains the hot spot forming the Hawaiian Islands. New Zealand's geology features volcanic activity and earthquakes due to its position on the boundary of the Australian and Pacific Plates. Australia is the lowest, flattest, and oldest landmass on Earth with no active volcanism. The region contains Earth's third-largest remaining tropical rainforest, covering much of New Guinea. Primary forest decreased by 17,000 hectares per year in 1990–2000, 25,600 ha/year in 2000–2015, and 20,200 ha/year in 2015–2025. Australia's fauna is notable for the scarcity of native placental mammals and dominance of marsupials. Australia, New Zealand, New Guinea, Wallacea, and Pacific islands collectively possess 42% of the world's parrot species.

### Politics and Economy

Australia is a federal parliamentary constitutional monarchy with Charles III as King of Australia. New Zealand is a constitutional monarchy with a parliamentary democracy and uncodified constitution. Most Oceanian countries are parliamentary democracies. Australia and New Zealand are the only highly developed independent nations in the region, with Australia's economy by far the largest. The Australian Securities Exchange in Sydney is the largest stock exchange in Australia and the South Pacific. The overwhelming majority of people in Pacific islands work in the service industry, including tourism. The main produce is copra (coconut), with timber, beef, palm oil, cocoa, sugar, and ginger also grown. Tourism is a large source of income for Pacific island nations; Fiji attracts almost half a million tourists annually.

### Demographics and Culture

The predominant religion is Christianity (73%), with 92% in Melanesia, 93% in Micronesia, and 96% in Polynesia identifying as Christian. Fiji has the highest percentage of Hindus in Oceania at 29.7%. Native languages fall into three groups: Austronesian, Aboriginal Australian (including Pama–Nyungan), and Papuan languages. Oceania was settled by two major waves of migration: Australo-Melanesians c. 40–80 thousand years ago, and Austronesian speakers approximately 3.5 thousand years ago. The rock art of Aboriginal Australians is the longest continuously practiced artistic tradition in the world, dating as far back as 60,000 years. Rugby union is the national sport of New Zealand, Samoa, Fiji, and Tonga; cricket is the most popular sport in Australia.

## Terms

- **Australasia**: Subregion comprising Australia, New Zealand, and neighbouring Pacific islands
- **Melanesia**: Southwestern subregion including New Guinea, Bismarck Archipelago, Solomon Islands, Vanuatu, Fiji, and New Caledonia
- **Micronesia**: Subregion north of the equator including Mariana Islands, Caroline Islands, Marshall Islands, and Kiribati
- **Polynesia**: Subregion stretching from Hawaii to New Zealand, including Samoa, Tonga, Cook Islands, and Easter Island
- **Lapita Peoples**: Austronesian culture named for its pottery tradition, appearing in the Bismarck Archipelago c. 1400 BCE
- **Near Oceania / Remote Oceania**: Terms proposed in 1973 by Green and Pawley to replace Melanesia/Micronesia/Polynesia categories
- **Marsupials**: Mammals that raise young in a pouch, dominant in Australia's fauna
- **Austronesian language family**: Large language family including Malay, Gilbertese, Fijian, Māori, and Hawaiʻian
- **Copra**: Dried coconut kernel, the main produce of the Pacific islands
- **EEZ (Exclusive Economic Zone)**: Maritime zone granting a state special rights over resources

## Debates and open questions

Definitions of Oceania vary considerably, with ongoing disagreement over whether Indonesia, the Philippines, Taiwan, Japan, and the Aleutian Islands should be included. The 1998 *Encyclopedia of Earth and Physical Sciences* notes that "the term has also come under scrutiny by many geographers," with some insisting Oceania encompasses even the cold Aleutian Islands and Japan. The 2011 *Oxford Handbook of World History* describes Oceania as "often treated as a secondary topic in world history, appearing at the end of global narratives as a marginal region." The *Oxford Handbook of Prehistoric Oceania* (2018) questions its own restrictive definition: "Why not include Australia, for example, or even too broad, for what does Highland New Guinea have to do with Hawaiʻi?" Micronesian settlement origins remain debated due to difficulties with archaeological excavations, with much evidence based on linguistic analysis. The dating of Easter Island's settlement is also contested, with recent radiocarbon dates suggesting discovery as recently as 1200 CE.
