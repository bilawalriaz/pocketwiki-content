# Tokugawa Ieyasu

## Overview
Tokugawa Ieyasu (1543–1616) was a Japanese samurai and daimyo who founded the Tokugawa shogunate, establishing a regime that ruled Japan for over 260 years. As the third of Japan's "Great Unifiers," he consolidated power after the deaths of his predecessors, Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi, culminating in victory at the Battle of Sekigahara (1600). His rule initiated the long Edo period of peace and isolation.

## Timeline
- **1543** — Born as Matsudaira Takechiyo in Okazaki Castle.
- **1549** — Father dies; taken as a hostage by the Imagawa clan.
- **1560** — Imagawa Yoshimoto killed at Battle of Okehazama; Ieyasu reclaims his seat and allies with Oda Nobunaga.
- **1567** — Adopts the surname "Tokugawa."
- **1575** — Victory at Battle of Nagashino with Nobunaga against the Takeda clan.
- **1579** — Orders execution of his wife and forces his eldest son to commit seppuku.
- **1582** — Nobunaga's death; Ieyasu survives a perilous journey back to Mikawa.
- **1584** — Battles Toyotomi Hideyoshi at Komaki and Nagakute; later submits and becomes a vassal.
- **1590** — Relocates to the Kantō region, building his power base at Edo.
- **1600** — Wins the Battle of Sekigahara, becoming the de facto ruler.
- **1603** — Appointed shōgun by the Emperor.
- **1605** — Abdicates as shōgun but retains power as retired shōgun (ōgosho).
- **1614-15** — Destroys the Toyotomi clan in the Siege of Osaka.
- **1616** — Dies; posthumously deified as Tōshō Daigongen.

## Body

### Early Life and Service under Imagawa
Born Matsudaira Takechiyo, Ieyasu was the son of a minor daimyo in Mikawa Province. His childhood was marked by instability; at age five, he was abducted by the rival Oda clan before being sent as a hostage to the powerful Imagawa clan at age nine. He served the Imagawa, fighting against the Oda, until the clan's leader was killed in 1560. Seizing the opportunity, Ieyasu reclaimed his ancestral castle, asserted independence, and formed a secret alliance with Oda Nobunaga.

### Alliance with Nobunaga and Expansion
Ieyasu formally allied with Nobunaga in the Kiyosu Alliance (1562). He spent the next two decades consolidating his home province of Mikawa, suppressing the militant Ikkō-ikki Buddhist movement, and expanding eastward into Tōtōmi and Suruga provinces. He fought alongside Nobunaga against rival clans like the Asakura-Azai and endured a major defeat by the Takeda clan at Mikatagahara (1573). Following Nobunaga's death in 1582, Ieyasu navigated a dangerous journey back to his territory and then expanded into former Takeda lands during the Tenshō-Jingo War.

### Vassalage to Hideyoshi and the Move to Kantō
After a brief conflict with Toyotomi Hideyoshi, Ieyasu submitted and became his most senior vassal. In 1590, Hideyoshi relocated Ieyasu and his entire clan to the vast but underdeveloped Kantō region, centered on the fishing village of Edo. This move, though risky, allowed Ieyasu to build a powerful, loyal domain away from Hideyoshi's direct control. He became one of Hideyoshi's Five Elders (regents) and, after Hideyoshi's death in 1598, emerged as the most powerful figure in the coalition.

### Rise to Shogun and the Tokugawa Regime
Ieyasu's power was challenged by Ishida Mitsunari, leading to the decisive Battle of Sekigahara in 1600, where Ieyasu's Eastern Army triumphed. He redistributed lands to reward allies and punish enemies, creating a clear hierarchy of *fudai* (hereditary) and *tozama* (outside) daimyo. Appointed shōgun in 1603, he established the Tokugawa shogunate. He abdicated in 1605 but remained the effective ruler as *ōgosho*, supervising the construction of Edo Castle and implementing the *bakuhan* system—a framework of graded rewards and punishments to control the daimyo and ensure peace.

### Final Years and Consolidation
Ieyasu's final years were spent eliminating the last threat to his rule: the Toyotomi clan. He used pretexts to wage the Siege of Osaka (1614–15), destroying Hideyori and extinguishing the Toyotomi line. He issued laws to control the daimyo (*Buke shohatto*) and the imperial court (*Kuge shohatto*). Initially tolerant of Christianity, he grew suspicious of foreign influence, leading to the expulsion of missionaries and the start of Japan's isolationist *sakoku* policy. He died in 1616, having secured the Tokugawa shogunate's dominance for centuries.

## Terms
- **Daimyo:** A powerful feudal lord who controlled large territories and commanded private armies of samurai.
- **Shōgun:** The military dictator of Japan, nominally appointed by the Emperor but holding actual governing power.
- **Bakuhan system:** The political system of the Tokugawa shogunate, combining centralized shogunal authority (*baku*) with the domain system of the daimyo (*han*).
- **Fudai daimyo:** Hereditary vassals of the Tokugawa who were allies before the Battle of Sekigahara; they held key government positions.
- **Tozama daimyo:** "Outside" lords who submitted to Tokugawa authority only after Sekigahara; they were excluded from central government.
- **Ōgosho:** A retired shōgun who retained de facto control over the government.
- **Sakoku:** The policy of national isolationism enforced by the Tokugawa shogunate, severely limiting foreign trade and contact.
- **Ikkō-ikki:** A militant faction of peasants and monks from the Jōdo Shinshū Buddhist sect who opposed feudal rule.
- **Koku:** A unit of rice volume used to measure a daimyo's wealth and the size of their domain.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact cause of his father Hirotada's death in 1549 is debated; a popular theory of assassination by vassals bribed by the Oda is considered unreliable by modern scholarship, with natural illness being a likely alternative.
- The circumstances of the "Nobuyasu Incident" (1579), where Ieyasu ordered his wife and heir to die, are complex. Theories range from a conspiracy with the Takeda clan to internal factional conflict within the Tokugawa house between the "Hamamatsu" and "Okazaki" factions.
- Historians debate whether Ieyasu's relocation to Kantō in 1590 was a reluctant submission to Hideyoshi's order or a strategic opportunity he embraced to build an independent power base.
- The characterization of Ieyasu in later historiography, particularly after the Meiji Restoration, often portrayed him negatively as a "cunning old man," a view modern scholars like Hamada Koichiro have sought to re-evaluate.
