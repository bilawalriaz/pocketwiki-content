# Capetian dynasty

## Overview
The Capetian dynasty is one of the oldest and most influential royal houses in European history. Founded by Hugh Capet in 987, it ruled France almost continuously until 1848 and, through its cadet branches, established monarchies across Europe, including in Spain and Luxembourg. Its enduring legacy is the gradual centralization of the French state and a remarkable pattern of dynastic survival and expansion.

## Timeline
- **987** — Hugh Capet is elected King of France, founding the Capetian dynasty.
- **c. 1180–1223** — Reign of Philip II Augustus, who solidifies Capetian power and drops the practice of associate kingship.
- **1328** — Death of Charles IV ends the senior Capetian line; Philip VI of the House of Valois, a cadet branch, becomes king.
- **1589** — Henry IV of the House of Bourbon, another cadet branch, becomes king after the Wars of Religion.
- **1792** — The French monarchy is abolished during the Revolution; Louis XVI is executed in 1793.
- **1814** — The Bourbon monarchy is restored after Napoleon's defeat.
- **1830** — The July Revolution replaces Charles X with Louis Philippe I of the House of Orléans, the last Capetian monarch in France.
- **1700–present** — Cadet Bourbon branches reign in Spain and Luxembourg.

## Body

### Origins and the "Capetian Miracle"
The dynasty's name derives from its founder, Hugh Capet, though the meaning of "Capet" is unknown. The "Capetian miracle" refers to the dynasty's ability to secure and hold the French crown through a unique strategy. By associating their eldest sons with them in the kingship during their lifetimes, the early Capetians established hereditary succession, transforming an elective monarchy into a sacral one. This orderly father-to-son succession, maintained from Hugh Capet until the 14th-century crisis, provided a crucial political advantage over rivals plagued by succession disputes, allowing the initially weak Capetians to steadily expand their power from the Île-de-France to rule all of France.

### Expansion and Cadet Branches
The dynasty's power grew through alliances with the Church and active participation in the Crusades. However, the alliance with the papacy suffered after the Aragonese Crusade, and Philip IV later brought the papacy under French control. A key turning point was the adoption of Salic law during the Hundred Years' War, which restricted the French throne to male-line descendants. This law caused the crown to pass from the senior Capetian line to the cadet House of Valois in 1328, and later to the House of Bourbon in 1589. Younger sons were given appanages (estates), directing their ambitions abroad rather than at the French throne. Consequently, Capetian cadet branches established monarchies in Portugal, Spain, Naples, Sicily, Hungary, Poland, and elsewhere.

### Modern Legacy
The French monarchy was abolished in 1792 and restored in 1814, but finally ended in 1848. Today, the senior legitimate member of the dynasty is Louis Alphonse, Duke of Anjou, a Legitimist pretender to the French throne. The only reigning Capetian monarchs are King Felipe VI of Spain and Grand Duke Guillaume V of Luxembourg (since 2025), both from the Bourbon branch. Numerous other pretenders represent exiled dynasties in Brazil, France, Portugal, and Italy.

## Terms
- **Agnatic**: Descent through the male line only, as required by Salic law for the French succession.
- **Appanage**: An estate or territory granted by a monarch to a younger son or brother to maintain their rank.
- **Cadet branch**: A line of descent from a dynasty that is not the senior-most line.
- **Demesne**: Land directly owned and administered by a lord or monarch.
- **Salic law**: An ancient Frankish legal tradition re-established in the 14th century that excluded women from inheriting the French throne.
- **Associate kingship**: The early Capetian practice of crowning the eldest son during the reigning king's lifetime to ensure smooth succession.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that the meaning of Hugh Capet's nickname "Capet" is unknown, with folk etymology linking it to "cape" and other suggestions connecting it to the Latin *caput* ("head"). The exact political and social mechanisms that allowed the initially weak Capetians to outmaneuver more powerful rivals like the Anglo-Normans over centuries is a subject of historical analysis, often centered on the strategic value of their stable succession.
