# Kathasaritsagara

## Overview
The *Kathāsaritsāgara* ("Ocean of the Streams of Stories") is a monumental 11th-century Sanskrit collection of Indian legends and folk tales. Compiled by Somadeva of Kashmir, it is a vital source for understanding Indian narrative literature, preserving hundreds of stories within a complex frame narrative. Its tales have traveled widely, influencing literature from the Middle East to modern authors like Salman Rushdie.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC (legendary)** — The sage Guṇāḍhya is said to have composed the original *Bṛhatkathā* in the Paiśācī language.
- **8th-9th century CE** — Budhasvāmin of Nepal composes the Sanskrit *Bṛhatkathāślokasaṃgraha*, a verse epitome of the lost original.
- **c. 1063-1081 CE** — Somadeva compiles the *Kathāsaritsāgara* for Queen Sūryavatī of Kashmir.
- **c. 1060s CE** — Kṣemendra writes the *Bṛhatkathāmañjarī*, another Sanskrit summary of the *Bṛhatkathā*.
- **1418/20-1470 CE** — A Persian translation, *Bahr al-asmar*, is made in Kashmir under Sultan Zayn al-‘Abidin (now lost).
- **Late 16th century** — Mustafa Khaliqdad ‘Abbasi creates a Persian adaptation, *Darya-yi asmar*, during Akbar's reign.
- **1824** — H. H. Wilson publishes the first European summary of the work.
- **1839-1862** — Hermann Brockhaus publishes the first critical edition of the Sanskrit text.
- **1880-1884** — C. H. Tawney produces the first complete English translation.
- **1889** — The Nirṇaya-Sāgara Press (Bombay) publishes the influential edition by Durgāprasāda.
- **1924-1928** — N. M. Penzer reissues Tawney's translation in an expanded 10-volume edition.
- **2007-2009** — Sir James Mallinson begins a new English translation for the Clay Sanskrit Library (2 of 7 vols. published).

## Body

### Origin and Structure
Somadeva states his work is an abridgment of the lost *Bṛhatkathā* ("Great Narrative") by Guṇāḍhya, written in the obscure Paiśācī language. The *Kathāsaritsāgara* consists of 18 books (*lambhakas*) containing 124 chapters (*taraṅgas*) and approximately 22,000 verses (*ślokas*). Its frame story follows the adventures of Prince Naravahanadatta, son of King Udayana of Kosambi, and his quest to marry many women and become king of the Vidyadharas (a class of supernatural beings). Over 350 tales are woven around this central plot.

### The Frame Narrative
The collection begins with a divine origin story: the tales were originally told by the god Shiva to his wife Parvati. An eavesdropping attendant, Pushpadanta, is cursed to be reborn on Earth. He eventually recites the stories to a *piśāca* (a type of goblin), fulfilling the conditions to return to heaven. Pushpadanta and his friend are reborn as the Brahmins Vararuchi and Guṇāḍhya. Guṇāḍhya learns the stories from the *piśāca* and writes them in his own blood, as he has no ink. After a king initially rejects the work, Guṇāḍhya burns most of it, but the surviving portion is later recovered and translated from Paiśācī into Sanskrit.

### Content and Themes
The books loosely follow Naravahanadatta's life, from the adventures of his father Udayana to his own marriages and eventual coronation as Vidyadhara emperor. However, the primary substance is the multitude of embedded stories. These tales cover a vast range: romantic adventures, moral fables, mythological legends, and stories of trickery and revenge. Notably, Book 10 contains the entire *Pañcatantra*, and Book 12 includes an early version of the *Vetālapañcaviṃśati* (Twenty-five Tales of a Vampire). The work is celebrated for its elegant style, humor, and vivid character sketches.

### Manuscript Traditions and Translations
Two other major Sanskrit recensions of the *Bṛhatkathā* exist: Kṣemendra's *Bṛhatkathāmañjarī* and Budhasvāmin's *Bṛhatkathāślokasaṃgraha*. Scholars believe all three draw from a common, expanded Kashmiri prose version of the lost Paiśācī original, not directly from Guṇāḍhya. The *Kathāsaritsāgara* was translated into Persian twice: once in the 15th century (now lost) and again in the late 16th century as *Darya-yi asmar*. The first complete English translation was by C. H. Tawney (1880-84), later expanded by N. M. Penzer. A modern translation by Sir James Mallinson began in 2007.

## Terms
- **Lambhaka**: A "book" or major section of the *Kathāsaritsāgara*; there are 18.
- **Taraṅga**: A "wave" or chapter within a *lambhaka*; there are 124 in total.
- **Śloka**: A Sanskrit verse form consisting of two half-verses of 16 syllables each.
- **Vidyadhara**: A class of supernatural beings or "heavenly spirits" with magical powers, residing in the Himalayas.
- **Piśāca**: A type of goblin or flesh-eating demon in Hindu mythology.
- **Bṛhatkathā**: The "Great Narrative," the lost 1st-century CE original by Guṇāḍhya in the Paiśācī language, from which the *Kathāsaritsāgara* is derived.
- **Paiśācī**: A poorly understood, possibly Dravidian or Indo-Aryan, language or dialect in which the original *Bṛhatkathā* was written.
- **Frame narrative**: A literary technique where a main story serves as a framework for a series of shorter, embedded tales.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Relationship to the *Bṛhatkathā***: Scholars debate whether Somadeva and Kṣemendra worked from the same text and how closely their versions reflect Guṇāḍhya's original Paiśācī work, which is lost. The available Sanskrit recensions are all significantly different.
- **Historicity of Guṇāḍhya**: While compared to epic poets like Vyasa, the historical existence and exact period of Guṇāḍhya are uncertain.
- **Transmission of Tales**: The work is a key node in the global transmission of Indian stories. Its relationship to other collections like the *Pañcatantra* and the *One Thousand and One Nights* is a subject of ongoing study, with evidence suggesting it may preserve earlier versions of some tales.
