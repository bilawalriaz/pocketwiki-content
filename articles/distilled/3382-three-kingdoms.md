# Three Kingdoms

## Overview
The Three Kingdoms period (220–280 AD) was an era of tripartite division in China following the collapse of the Han dynasty. It was a time of significant warfare, population decline, and political fragmentation, but also of technological innovation and cultural development. The period's instability and legendary figures have made it one of the most romanticized eras in Chinese history.

## Timeline
- **184** — Yellow Turban Rebellion begins, weakening Han authority.
- **189** — Dong Zhuo seizes control of the capital after Emperor Ling's death.
- **200** — Battle of Guandu: Cao Cao defeats Yuan Shao, securing northern China.
- **208** — Battle of Red Cliffs: Allied forces of Liu Bei and Sun Quan defeat Cao Cao, preventing unification.
- **220** — Cao Pi forces Emperor Xian to abdicate, establishing the state of Cao Wei.
- **221** — Liu Bei proclaims himself emperor, founding Shu Han.
- **229** — Sun Quan proclaims himself emperor, establishing Eastern Wu.
- **234** — Death of Zhuge Liang during a northern campaign against Wei.
- **263** — Wei conquers Shu Han.
- **266** — Sima Yan usurps the throne from Cao Wei, establishing the Jin dynasty.
- **280** — Jin conquers Eastern Wu, reunifying China and ending the period.

## Body

### Prelude and Collapse of the Han
The Eastern Han dynasty declined due to political infighting between imperial relatives and eunuchs, compounded by natural disasters. The Yellow Turban Rebellion (184) was a massive Taoist-led uprising that, though defeated, led to the decentralization of power. In 189, the warlord Dong Zhuo seized the capital, deposing the emperor and triggering a coalition against him. His subsequent assassination led to further chaos, with regional warlords like Cao Cao, Yuan Shao, and Sun Jian vying for control. By 196, Cao Cao had secured the puppet Emperor Xian, giving him legal authority to command other warlords.

### Formation of the Three Kingdoms
Cao Cao's victory at the Battle of Guandu (200) established his dominance in the north. In the south, Sun Quan consolidated power along the Yangtze. The pivotal Battle of Red Cliffs (208) saw a temporary alliance between Liu Bei and Sun Quan defeat Cao Cao's larger force, ensuring the survival of two southern powers. Following this, Liu Bei seized Yi Province (modern Sichuan) in 214. After Cao Cao's death in 220, his son Cao Pi forced the Han emperor to abdicate, founding Cao Wei. Liu Bei and Sun Quan soon declared themselves emperors of Shu Han and Eastern Wu, respectively, formalizing the tripartite division.

### The Three States in Balance
The period from 220 to 263 was relatively stable. **Shu Han**, under the regent Zhuge Liang, launched several northern campaigns against Wei but was hampered by supply issues. **Eastern Wu**, under Sun Quan, focused on developing its southern economy, integrating local tribes, and engaging in maritime trade. **Cao Wei** was the most powerful state, controlling the populous north. It managed its northern frontiers against nomadic groups like the Xianbei and successfully defended against Wu's attacks on the Huai River region. Internally, the Sima family gradually rose to prominence within Wei's court.

### Decline and Reunification
Shu Han declined after Zhuge Liang's death, suffering from internal corruption. In 263, Wei forces under Deng Ai executed a daring flanking maneuver, leading to Shu's surrender. In 266, Sima Yan, grandson of the powerful general Sima Yi, usurped the Wei throne and established the Jin dynasty. Eastern Wu, now ruled by the tyrannical Sun Hao, was the last holdout. In 280, a multi-pronged Jin invasion, including a fleet sailing down the Yangtze, overwhelmed Wu's defenses. The surrender of Sun Hao reunified China under Jin, ending the Three Kingdoms era.

### Impact and Legacy
The period saw a catastrophic population decline due to warfare, famine, and plague. A 280 AD census recorded only about 16 million people, a fraction of the late Han population. Economically, the breakdown of central authority led to the rise of self-sufficient manorial estates. Culturally, the era produced notable poets like Cao Zhi and was later immortalized in the 14th-century historical novel *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*, which heavily shaped its popular legacy.

## Terms
- **Mandate of Heaven**: The divine right to rule, claimed by each of the three kingdoms to legitimize their succession from the Han.
- **Tuntian**: Military agricultural colonies established by Cao Cao to support his army through state-managed farming.
- **Battle of Red Cliffs (208)**: A decisive naval battle where allied forces of Liu Bei and Sun Quan defeated Cao Cao, preventing the unification of China and cementing the north-south division.
- **Regent**: A person appointed to administer the state because the ruler is a minor, absent, or incapacitated (e.g., Zhuge Liang for Shu Han).
- **Records of the Three Kingdoms**: The standard historical text of the period, compiled by Chen Shou in the 3rd century AD.
- **Romance of the Three Kingdoms**: A 14th-century historical novel by Luo Guanzhong that dramatizes the period and is its most famous cultural adaptation.
- **Shanyue**: Indigenous tribes of the southeast, subjugated and integrated into the population and army of Eastern Wu.
- **Jiange**: A strategically vital mountain pass in Shu Han, whose defense was critical against northern invasions.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Legitimacy**: Historians have long debated which state was the legitimate successor to the Han. Traditional historiography, following Chen Shou, often favored Cao Wei (as the successor to the Jin dynasty). Later Neo-Confucian scholars like Zhu Xi argued for Shu Han, based on Liu Bei's blood relation to the Han imperial family.
- **Population Figures**: The census data from the period is considered an undercount. The dramatic population drop is attributed not only to warfare but also to mass migration, famine, disease, and the existence of large populations outside state control (e.g., refugees, bandits).
- **Periodization**: While 220–280 is the standard timeframe, some historians argue for starting the period earlier (e.g., with the Yellow Turban Rebellion in 184) to include the warlord chaos that directly led to the tripartite division.
