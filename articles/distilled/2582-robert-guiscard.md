# Robert Guiscard

## Overview
Robert Guiscard (c. 1015–1085) was a Norman adventurer who, through conquest and alliance, built a powerful duchy in southern Italy and conquered Sicily from Muslim rule. His campaigns fundamentally shifted the region's political and religious landscape, establishing Norman dominance and expanding Latin Christianity, while his conflicts with the Byzantine Empire and alliance with the Papacy shaped Mediterranean politics for generations.

## Timeline
- **c. 1015** — Born in Normandy, sixth son of Tancred de Hauteville.
- **1047** — Arrives in southern Italy with a small following.
- **1053** — Fights for the Normans at the Battle of Civitate.
- **1057** — Succeeds his brother Humphrey as Count of Apulia and Calabria.
- **1059** — Treaty of Melfi: Swears fealty to Pope Nicholas II, who invests him as Duke of Apulia and Calabria and Lord of Sicily.
- **1061** — Conquers Messina, beginning the Norman conquest of Sicily.
- **1071** — Captures Bari, ending Byzantine rule in southern Italy.
- **1076** — Conquers the Principality of Salerno.
- **1081** — Defeats Byzantine Emperor Alexios I at the Battle of Dyrrachium.
- **1084** — Sacks Rome to rescue Pope Gregory VII from Emperor Henry IV.
- **17 July 1085** — Dies of fever in Cephalonia.

## Body

### Rise to Power
Robert arrived in Italy in 1047 to find lands scarce. He initially served as a mercenary and robber before receiving the fortress of Scribla from his brother Drogo, the Count of Apulia. He expanded his power through marriage and military service, fighting at the Battle of Civitate in 1053. Upon his brother Humphrey's death in 1057, Robert succeeded as Count, bypassing an elder half-brother. He secured his position through a strategic marriage to the Lombard princess Sikelgaita and, most importantly, the 1059 Treaty of Melfi with Pope Nicholas II. This treaty, made during the Investiture Controversy, granted Robert papal legitimacy for his conquests in exchange for his fealty.

### Conquest of Southern Italy and Sicily
With papal backing, Robert launched systematic campaigns. He first fully subjugated Calabria from the Byzantines. Then, with his brother Roger, he began the conquest of Sicily, taking Messina in 1061. The campaign was prolonged; a 1064 expedition failed due to a tarantula infestation at the campsite. Progress continued, and in 1071 Robert created the County of Sicily for Roger. Palermo fell in 1072, and Muslim resistance ended with the fall of Noto in 1091. Concurrently, Robert besieged and captured Bari in 1071, expelling the Byzantines from Italy. He then absorbed the Lombard principalities of Salerno (1076) and Benevento (1078), the latter he later returned to the Papacy.

### Conflict with the Byzantine Empire
In his final years, Robert attacked the Byzantine Empire itself, using a pretender as a pretext. He defeated Emperor Alexios I at Dyrrachium in 1081 and occupied Corfu and Durazzo. He was forced to return to Italy in 1083 to rescue Pope Gregory VII from Emperor Henry IV, sacking Rome in the process. He returned to the Balkans but died of fever in Cephalonia in 1085. His conquests were largely lost to the Byzantines after his death.

### Legacy and Religion
Robert's conquests brought Latin Christianity to areas previously under the Byzantine rite. He founded churches and monasteries, such as the Norman monastery at Sant'Eufemia Lamezia. His alliance with the Papacy, though sometimes strained, was a cornerstone of his power. Popes preferred a strong Catholic Norman to Byzantine or Imperial rule. This papal-Norman alliance, solidified after the Great Schism of 1054, created a formidable opposition to the Eastern Empire. He was succeeded as Duke by his son Roger Borsa, while his disinherited eldest son, Bohemond, later became a leader of the First Crusade.

## Terms
- **Investiture Controversy**: A conflict between the Papacy and Holy Roman Empire over the right to appoint church officials, which led the Pope to ally with the Normans.
- **Treaty of Melfi (1059)**: Agreement where Robert Guiscard swore fealty to Pope Nicholas II, receiving papal legitimacy for his titles and future conquests.
- **Consanguinity**: The state of being related by blood; Robert's first marriage was annulled on these grounds.
- **Byzantine rite**: The Eastern Christian liturgical tradition, which was historically followed in southern Italy and Sicily before the Norman conquest.
- **Latin Christianity**: The Western Christian tradition, centered on the Pope in Rome, which Robert Guiscard promoted in his conquered territories.
- **Lombards**: A Germanic people who ruled various principalities in southern Italy and were initially allies, then rivals, of the Normans.
- **Saracen**: A term used in medieval European sources to refer to Muslims, particularly those ruling Sicily and North Africa.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact size of Robert's armies is debated, with sources giving wildly different numbers (e.g., 1,300 knights vs. 10,000 troops for the 1081 expedition).
- The primary motivation for his attack on the Byzantine Empire is unclear; it was launched under the pretext of supporting a false claimant, but may have been driven by ambition or a perceived right through a broken betrothal.
- The long duration and setbacks of the Sicilian conquest (e.g., the failed 1064 expedition) highlight the challenges of the campaign, which is sometimes oversimplified as a straightforward victory.
