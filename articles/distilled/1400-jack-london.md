# Jack London

## Overview
Jack London (1876–1916) was an American novelist, journalist, and activist who became one of the first U.S. authors to achieve international celebrity and significant wealth through commercial fiction. Born John Griffith Chaney in San Francisco, his illegitimacy, working-class upbringing, and brutal experiences as a laborer, oyster pirate, hobo, and Klondike gold seeker forged a worldview blending socialism, social Darwinism, and a drive for individual success. His Klondike suffering—scurvy, tooth loss, and near-starvation—provided the raw material for his most famous works, *The Call of the Wild* (1903) and *White Fang* (1906), while his political evolution produced the dystopian *The Iron Heel* (1908) and the autobiographical *Martin Eden* (1909). His non-fiction exposé *The People of the Abyss* (1903), based on undercover investigation in London's East End, stands as a major work on poverty. London viewed writing as a business to fund his "Beauty Ranch" in Sonoma County, a sustainable agriculture project that failed economically. His legacy is complicated by his advocacy of eugenics and "yellow peril" racism, contrasted with empathetic portrayals of Indigenous and Asian characters. He died at 40 from uremia aggravated by alcoholism and morphine use; suicide rumors persist but are rejected by modern scholars.

## Body

### Family and Formative Trauma
London’s origins were shrouded in secrecy and trauma. His mother, Flora Wellman, daughter of a Pennsylvania canal builder, lived with astrologer William Chaney in San Francisco. According to Flora’s 1875 account in the *San Francisco Chronicle*, Chaney demanded an abortion; when she refused, he denied paternity. Flora attempted suicide by gunshot, survived, and gave birth in January 1876. The infant was wet-nursed by Virginia "Jennie" Prentiss, a formerly enslaved neighbor who became London’s primary source of childhood affection. In late 1876, Flora married John London, a disabled Civil War veteran, giving the boy his surname. The family settled in Oakland, where London attended grade school but was largely self-educated. At 21, while at Berkeley, London discovered newspaper accounts of his mother’s suicide attempt and wrote to Chaney. Chaney replied that he was impotent, accused Flora of relations with other men, and denied the abortion demand. Devastated, London quit Berkeley and left for the Klondike.

### Apprenticeship: Labor, Crime, and the Road
London’s adolescence was a catalog of industrial exploitation and adventure. At 13, he worked 12–18 hour days at Hickmott’s Cannery. Borrowing from Prentiss, he bought a sloop, the *Razzle-Dazzle*, and became an "oyster pirate" stealing from private beds in San Francisco Bay. After his boat wrecked, he joined the California Fish Patrol. In 1893, he shipped out on the *Sophie Sutherland* for a seven-month sealing voyage to Japan. Returning to the 1893 depression, he labored in a jute mill and a power plant, joined Coxey’s Army of the unemployed, and rode the rails as a hobo. In 1894, he served 30 days in Buffalo’s Erie County Penitentiary for vagrancy, an experience he called "unprintable horrors" in *The Road*. Back in Oakland, he attended high school, publishing his first piece, "Typhoon off the Coast of Japan," in the school magazine. He studied at Heinold’s First and Last Chance Saloon, where owner John Heinold lent him tuition for Berkeley. There he met sailors like Captain Alexander McLean, the model for Wolf Larsen in *The Sea-Wolf*.

### The Klondike Crucible
On July 12, 1897, London and his brother-in-law Captain Shepard joined the Klondike Gold Rush. The physical toll was severe: malnutrition caused scurvy, swelling his gums and costing him his four front teeth; hip and leg pain became chronic; facial marks remained for life. Father William Judge, "The Saint of Dawson," provided shelter and care. London’s landlords were the Bond brothers, educated mining engineers whose father was a wealthy Republican investor; London sparred politically with them. The experience radicalized him: he concluded the only escape from the work "trap" was to "sell his brains" via writing. It also yielded "To Build a Fire" (1902, revised 1908), widely considered his masterpiece, contrasting a 1902 "tame" version with a sunny ending against the 1908 brutal classic where the protagonist freezes to death.

### Literary Breakthrough and *The Crowd*
Returning in 1898, London faced rejection until *The Black Cat* paid $40 for "A Thousand Deaths"—"the first money I ever received for a story." New printing technologies had created a mass market for magazines, and London treated writing as a business. By 1900 he earned $2,500 (approx. $97,000 today). *The Call of the Wild* (1903) sold to *The Saturday Evening Post* for $750; Macmillan’s promotion made it a sensation. In 1903, he published *The People of the Abyss*, a non-fiction exposé of his undercover investigation in London's East End, a major work dealing with socialism and workers' rights. In Oakland and Piedmont, London became the center of "The Crowd," a Bohemian circle meeting at Xavier Martinez’s home (Sundays) and London’s (Wednesdays). Members included poets George Sterling and Ambrose Bierce, artist Arnold Genthe, and writer Anna Strunsky. London and Strunsky co-authored *The Kempton-Wace Letters* (1903), an epistolary debate on love versus eugenic mating.

### Marriages: "Mother-Girl" and "Mate-Woman"
London married Bessie Maddern on April 7, 1900, the day *The Son of the Wolf* published. Both admitted it was a friendship match for "sturdy children," not love. They had two daughters, Joan (1901) and Bessie "Becky" (1902). The marriage strained; London complained to friends that Bessie’s "purity" (code, per biographer Stasz, for fear of venereal disease from his affairs) made her intolerant. He left in July 1903; divorce finalized November 1904. In 1905, he married Charmian Kittredge, five years his senior, introduced by her aunt Netta Eames in 1900. Their affair began in 1903 while London recovered from a buggy accident at Wake Robin resort. Kingman calls Charmian "Jack’s soul-mate." She accompanied him on the *Snark* cruise (1907–1909) and extended Hawaii stays. They attempted children; one died at birth, another miscarried. London contrasted the "Mother-Girl" (Bessie) and "Mate-Woman" (Charmian) in his fiction and letters.

### War Correspondent and Bohemian Club
In early 1904, Hearst sent London to cover the Russo-Japanese War. He arrived in Yokohama January 25, was arrested in Shimonoseki (released via Ambassador Lloyd Griscom), arrested again in Korea near the Manchurian border, then embedded with the Imperial Japanese Army at the Battle of the Yalu. Frustrated by censorship, he sought transfer to the Russian Army but was arrested a third time for assaulting Japanese assistants he accused of stealing horse fodder. President Theodore Roosevelt intervened personally; London left in June 1904. That August, he attended "Summer High Jinks" at Bohemian Grove with George Sterling, elected to honorary membership in the Bohemian Club alongside John Muir, Frank Norris, and Ambrose Bierce. He later wrote *The Acorn Planter* (1916) for the Grove Plays, though it was never performed.

### Beauty Ranch: Agrarian Dream and Economic Failure
In 1905, London bought a 1,000-acre ranch in Glen Ellen, Sonoma County, declaring, "Next to my wife, the ranch is the dearest thing in the world to me." He studied agricultural manuals, built California’s first concrete silo, hired Italian and Chinese stonemasons, and aimed to adapt Asian sustainable practices. He wrote to fund acreage: "I write a book for no other reason than to add three or four hundred acres to my magnificent estate." The ranch failed economically. Sympathetic biographer Stasz cites bad luck and being ahead of his time; critic Kevin Starr calls London a bad manager, absent six months a year (1910–1916), impaired by alcoholism, whose workers viewed the operation as a "rich man’s hobby." In 1913, the 15,000-square-foot Wolf House mansion burned down two weeks before move-in, a $80,000 loss ($2.87M today). The ruins and ranch are now Jack London State Historic Park.

### Animal Activism
London witnessed circus animal cruelty, prompting forewords in *Jerry of the Islands* and *Michael, Brother of Jerry* urging public awareness. In 1918 (posthumously), the Massachusetts SPCA and American Humane Education Society founded the Jack London Club to protest trained animal acts. Member pressure led Ringling–Barnum and Bailey to temporarily cease such acts in 1925.

### Death and the Suicide Debate
London died November 22, 1916, on a sleeping porch at his ranch. He suffered dysentery, late-stage alcoholism, uremia, and tropical infections (including yaws) from the *Snark* voyage. He was in extreme pain, taking morphine and opium (then over-the-counter). The death certificate cites uremia following acute renal colic. Older sources claimed suicide due to morphine use and fictional suicides in *John Barleycorn* (youthful drowning attempt), *Martin Eden* (protagonist drowns himself), and *The Little Lady of the Big House* (physician-assisted morphine death). Biographer Clarice Stasz and most modern scholars (including Russ Kingman) reject suicide, attributing death to uremia aggravated by accidental morphine overdose. Ashes were buried near Wolf House; Charmian joined him in 1955.

### Plagiarism Accusations
London’s prolific output and methods—"expression... is far easier than invention"—invited charges. He bought plots from Sinclair Lewis and used newspaper clippings. In 1901, his "Moon-Face" and Frank Norris’s "The Passing of Cock-eye Blacklock" shared a newspaper source. Egerton Ryerson Young claimed *The Call of the Wild* derived from *My Dogs in the Northland* (1902); London acknowledged the source and claimed to have thanked Young. In 1906, the *New York World* printed "deadly parallels" between "Love of Life" and a nonfiction article by Augustus Biddle and J.K. Macdonald; London pled guilty to "identity of time and situation." The most serious charge involved *The Iron Heel* Chapter 7 ("The Bishop’s Vision"), nearly identical to Frank Harris’s 1901 essay "The Bishop of London and Public Morality." London claimed he clipped a newspaper reprint believing it a genuine speech; Harris demanded 1/60th of royalties.

### Political Views: Socialism and Its Limits
London’s socialism arose from experience, not theory. In "How I Became a Socialist," he described his individualism "hammered out" by seeing people at the "bottom of the social pit." He joined the Socialist Labor Party (1896), spoke in Oakland City Hall Park (arrested 1897), and joined the Socialist Party of America (1901). He ran for Oakland mayor in 1901 (245 votes) and 1905 (981 votes), lectured nationally (1906), and published *War of the Classes* (1905) and *Revolution, and other Essays* (1906). He sympathized with the IWW ("Wobblies") but rejected sabotage; met Big Bill Haywood in 1912. At the ranch, he grew ambivalent, complaining of "inefficient Italian labourers." In 1916, he resigned from the Glen Ellen Socialist chapter. Kevin Starr calls this "post-socialist" boredom with class struggle. George Orwell detected a "Fascist strain": love of violence, belief in "natural aristocracy," animal-worship, and exaltation of the primitive.

### Race, "Yellow Peril," and Eugenics
London shared Californian fears of Asian immigration, terming it "the yellow peril" (essay, 1904). His 1910 story "The Unparalleled Invasion" depicts China’s demographic conquest of the world (1976–1987), met by Western biological warfare. He admitted this "postulate is itself a product of Western race-egotism." Conversely, stories like "The Mexican," "The Chinago," and "Koolau the Leper" (based on Kaluaikoolau’s 1893 revolt) portray Mexican, Asian, and Hawaiian characters empathetically. He admired Japanese customs from his war correspondence. In a 1913 letter, he advocated socialism as the "actual application... of the theory of the Brotherhood of Man" to end race prejudice. Shortly after boxer Jack Johnson was crowned the first Black world heavyweight champion in 1908, London pleaded for a white candidate to come forward to defeat Johnson; the term "great white hope" is often attributed to him (though pre-existing). London supported eugenics, writing the future "belongs to eugenics." *Before Adam* (1906–07) and *The Kempton-Wace Letters* reflect pro-eugenic themes. *The Valley of the Moon* emphasizes "real Americans" (Anglo-Saxon), while *Little Lady of the Big House* nuances this: superior individuals exist in all races; encouraging the best to mate improves populations. Hawaii’s intermarriage challenged his views: "little Hawaii... is making a better demonstration than the United States."

### Literary Assessment: Short Stories vs. Novels
Critic Dale L. Walker argues London’s "true métier was the short story" (under 7,500 words), where his "flood of images" and "narrative gift" were "constrained and freed." "To Build a Fire" exemplifies his growth from a 1902 children’s version to the 1908 masterpiece. Klondike stories ("All Gold Canyon," "The Law of Life," "Love of Life," "An Odyssey of the North") explore survival, morality, and mixed ancestry. Boxing stories ("A Piece of Steak," "The Mexican") address aging labor and ethnic prejudice. Science fiction tales include "The Unparalleled Invasion" (germ warfare), "Golia" (energy weapon), "The Shadow and the Flash" (invisibility), and "The Red One" (extraterrestrial object, influenced by Jung). Nineteen original collections appeared in his life. Novels are considered flawed: *The Star Rover* (1915) and *Smoke Bellew* are linked stories; *John Barleycorn* is "synoptic episodes." Ambrose Bierce praised *The Sea-Wolf*’s Wolf Larsen as "among the greatest of things" but condemned the "awful" love element. *The Iron Heel* anticipates *Nineteen Eighty-Four* as soft sci-fi dystopia. *The Call of the Wild* is hailed as a "beautiful prose poem" (Maxwell Geismar), belonging with *Walden* and *Huckleberry Finn* (Franklin Walker), and a "mordant parable... his masterpiece" (E.L. Doctorow).

### The "Credo" and "The Scab" Diatribe
A "Jack London Credo" was quoted by literary executor Irving Shepard in an introduction to a 1956 collection of London stories. The words Shepard quoted were from a story in the *San Francisco Bulletin*, December 2, 1916, by journalist Ernest J. Hopkins, who visited the ranch just weeks before London's death. Stasz notes only the first line is safely attributable; journalists then were "unreliable or even sheer inventions." However, the final three sentences—"I’d rather sing one wild song and burst my heart with it, than live a thousand years watching my digestion and being afraid of the wet..."—appear in London’s hand in suffragette Vida Goldstein’s autograph book and are authenticated by *The Oxford Dictionary of American Quotations* and NPR. The passage appears in Ian Fleming’s *You Only Live Twice* (1964) and the film *No Time to Die* (2021) as James Bond’s philosophy. A virulent anti-scab diatribe ("After God had finished the rattlesnake... he made a scab") is frequently attributed to London, cited by Justice Thurgood Marshall in *Letter Carriers v. Austin* (1974) as "generally attributed to author Jack London." It does not appear in his collected writings. London published a 1903 speech titled "The Scab" in *War of the Classes* (1905) which offers a structural analysis: a scab gives more for less, hurting fellow workers, but is compelled by a "tooth-and-nail society" where capital and labor quarrel over surplus value.

## Terms
- **Oyster pirate**: One who illegally harvested oysters from privately leased beds in San Francisco Bay; London’s first entrepreneurial venture (c. 1889).
- **Coxey’s Army**: 1894 protest march of unemployed workers led by Jacob Coxey; London joined, beginning his tramping years.
- **Scurvy**: Vitamin C deficiency causing gum swelling, tooth loss, and muscle pain; London contracted it in the Klondike (1897–98), losing four front teeth.
- **The Crowd**: Bohemian literary circle in Oakland/Piedmont (formed post-1898), including George Sterling, Ambrose Bierce, Anna Strunsky, and Xavier Martinez; met Sundays at Martinez’s, Wednesdays at London’s.
- **"Mother-Girl" / "Mate-Woman"**: London’s archetypes contrasting domestic purity (Bessie Maddern) with adventurous, sexual partnership (Charmian Kittredge); central to *The Kempton-Wace Letters*.
- **Beauty Ranch**: London’s 1,000-acre Sonoma County property (1905–1916), intended as a sustainable agriculture showcase and funded by writing
