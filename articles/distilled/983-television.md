# Television

## Overview
Television (TV) is a telecommunication medium that transmits moving images and sound, serving as a dominant mass medium for advertising, entertainment, news, and sports. Emerging from 19th-century mechanical experiments, it evolved through electronic, color, and digital eras into today’s internet-connected smart displays. Its development reshaped global culture, commerce, and daily life, making it one of the most influential technologies of the 20th and 21st centuries.

## Timeline
- **1900** — Term "television" coined by Constantin Perskyi at International Congress of Electricity in Paris
- **1925** — John Logie Baird demonstrates first public televised moving silhouette images in London
- **1926** — Baird transmits first true public television demonstration of a moving face in motion
- **1927** — Philo Farnsworth transmits first all-electronic image using image dissector camera tube
- **1928** — First transatlantic television signal sent by Baird between London and New York
- **1936** — BBC begins world's first regular high-definition television service from Alexandra Palace
- **1941** — First paid television advertisement aired in U.S. on WNBT before a baseball game

- **1998** — Flat-panel LCD TVs surpass CRT sales globally
- **2000s** — Digital television transition begins worldwide
- **2010s** — Smart TVs become dominant form of television set

## Body

### Mechanical Television
Television originated in mechanical systems using spinning disks for image scanning. Alexander Bain introduced facsimile transmission (1843–1846), followed by Paul Nipkow’s 1884 patent of the Nipkow disk—a spiral-patterned spinning disk that scanned images line by line. Early demonstrations included Georges Rignoux and A. Fournier’s 1909 live image transmission using selenium cells, and Boris Rosing and Vladimir Zworykin’s 1911 mechanical mirror-drum scanner system. John Logie Baird achieved the first public demonstration of moving silhouette images in 1925 and the first true television demonstration of a moving face in 1926. His system used synchronized Nipkow disks with thallium sulfide photocells and neon lamps for display, achieving 30 scan lines. By 1936, Baird’s mechanical system reached 240 lines resolution on BBC telecasts, though it relied on film scanning rather than direct scene capture. Mechanical television remained dominant through the 1930s despite low resolution, ending in 1939.

### Electronic Television
Electronic television replaced mechanical systems using cathode-ray tubes (CRTs) for both scanning and display. J.J. Thomson’s 1897 cathode ray deflection experiments laid groundwork for Ferdinand Braun’s 1907 Braun tube—the first practical CRT. Vladimir Zworykin developed early electronic camera tubes at Westinghouse starting in 1923, though his 1925 demonstration showed poor results. Philo Farnsworth independently developed the image dissector camera tube, transmitting his first image—a straight line—in 1927 and live human images by 1929. Kálmán Tihanyi’s 1926 charge-storage principle revolutionized camera tube sensitivity, later incorporated into RCA’s Iconoscope (1931). The EMI team under Isaac Shoenberg improved upon this with the super-Emitron (1934), achieving ten to fifteen times greater sensitivity. The world’s first regular high-definition electronic service began in Britain in 1936 using EMI’s Emitron cameras.

### Color Television
Color television development began alongside black-and-white systems. John Logie Baird demonstrated the first color transmission in 1928 using three-color mechanical scanning discs. The CBS field-sequential color system, introduced by Peter Goldmark in 1939, used a spinning color filter wheel but was incompatible with existing sets. Wartime production halts delayed commercialization. RCA’s compatible color system, approved by the National Television Systems Committee, encoded color information separately from brightness to conserve bandwidth while maintaining backward compatibility with black-and-white receivers. The first color broadcast aired in 1954, but widespread adoption didn’t occur until the mid-1960s when networks transitioned to color programming.

### Digital Television
Digital television (DTV) transmits audio and video through digitally processed multiplexed signals, enabling multiple programs per channel via data compression. Development became feasible in the 1990s with discrete cosine transform (DCT) compression technology. The FCC mandated in 1990 that the new ATV standard provide genuine HDTV with at least double existing resolution, support simulcasting, and allow new design principles. A key debate centered on interlaced versus progressive scanning—interlaced suited older CRT displays but progressive better matched newer LCD screens and computer integration. The transition to digital began in the late 2000s, with analog shutdown deadlines set globally for the 2010s.

### Smart Television
Smart TVs integrate internet connectivity and Web 2.0 features, representing convergence between computers, set-top boxes, and traditional televisions. These devices run pre-loaded operating systems and support streaming services like Netflix, YouTube TV, and Amazon Prime Video. Unlike Internet TV or IPTV, smart TV refers specifically to the hardware platform. Major manufacturers announced the production of smart TVs only for middle-end and high-end TVs in 2015, and they became dominant by the late 2010s. The rise of streaming services fundamentally changed content consumption patterns, with 28% of U.S. adults citing streaming services as their main means for watching television by 2017.

### Display Technologies
Television display technology evolved from mechanical disks to CRTs, then to flat-panel alternatives. CRTs dominated until the late 1990s when LCDs (liquid crystal displays) using cold cathode fluorescent lamps gained prominence. LED-backlit LCDs emerged in the 2000s, becoming the most widely produced display type by the mid-2010s. OLED (organic light-emitting diode) displays, which don’t require backlights and offer superior contrast, began replacing LEDs. Other technologies included plasma displays (for large screens), DLP (digital light processing) projectors, and various experimental systems. Resolution standards progressed from standard-definition (480i/576i) to high-definition (720p, 1080i, 1080p) and ultra-high-definition (4K, 8K).

### Broadcast Systems
Television signals distribute through terrestrial, cable, satellite, and internet methods. Terrestrial broadcasting uses high-powered transmitters to send signals to individual receivers, regulated by government licensing. Cable television delivers programming via coaxial or fiber-optic cables to subscribers, originating from community antennas in areas with poor over-the-air reception. Satellite television relays signals from communication satellites via parabolic dishes and low-noise block downconverters. Internet television distributes content through streaming, with traditional providers launching services like Sling TV (2015) and YouTube TV (2017).

### Content and Social Impact
Television programming spans genres from expensive dramas to low-cost reality shows, funded through advertising, subscriptions, licensing fees, or government support. The first paid advertisement aired in 1941 for Bulova watches. Television’s broad reach makes it a powerful advertising medium, with rates determined by Nielsen ratings and daypart scheduling. Socially, television has profoundly influenced public opinion, education, and cultural norms. However, concerns include media violence effects, cognitive impacts on children, and environmental issues from electronic waste containing lead and mercury. Religious groups like Jehovah’s Witnesses and some Christian denominations oppose television use, viewing it as an “occasion of sin.”

## Terms
- **Nipkow disk**: Spinning disk with spiral hole pattern used for mechanical image scanning
- **Cathode-ray tube (CRT)**: Vacuum tube using electron beams to illuminate phosphor-coated screens
- **Image dissector**: Electronic camera tube converting optical images directly into electrical signals
- **Charge storage**: Principle allowing camera tubes to accumulate photoelectrons during scanning cycles
- **Interlaced scanning**: Technique scanning even then odd lines alternately to reduce bandwidth
- **Progressive scanning**: Method scanning all lines sequentially in a single pass
- **Simulcast**: Broadcasting same content simultaneously on multiple channels or formats
- **Smart TV**: Television with integrated internet connectivity and web applications
- **Backwards compatible**: System allowing new technology to work with older equipment
- **Flat-panel display**: Thin, lightweight screens including LCD, LED, OLED, and plasma technologies

## Debates and Open Questions
The transition from analog to digital television raised questions about scanning methods—interlaced versus progressive—with consumer electronics favoring interlaced for higher resolution while computer industries preferred progressive for better compatibility with digital systems. The future of OLED versus LED display dominance remains uncertain as manufacturing scales. Environmental concerns about electronic waste from discarded televisions containing hazardous materials continue to challenge the industry. The long-term viability of traditional broadcast television versus internet streaming platforms represents an ongoing structural shift in how content reaches audiences.
