# Internal structure of Earth

## Overview
Earth's internal structure consists of chemically and mechanically distinct layers that vary in composition, pressure, and flow behavior with depth. Understanding these layers relies on seismic wave analysis, gravity and magnetic field measurements, and high-pressure experiments. The structure is key to explaining plate tectonics, the geomagnetic field, and planetary differentiation.

## Timeline
- **c. 4.6 billion years ago** – Planetary differentiation separates iron-nickel core from silicate mantle and crust.
- **4.4 billion years ago** – Oldest known mineral grains indicate a solid crust had formed.
- **1936** – Inge Lehmann discovers the solid inner core using seismic wave data.
- **1940 onward** – Francis Birch and others develop geophysics based on chondrite meteorite analogs.

## Body

### Global Properties
Earth's mass is approximately 6×10²⁴ kg, yielding an average density of 5.515 g/cm³. These values are derived from gravitational force measurements and orbital satellite observations.

### Layers
Earth's layers are defined either compositionally or mechanically. Compositionally, they are the silicate crust, the iron-magnesium mantle, and the iron-nickel core. Mechanically, they include the rigid lithosphere, ductile asthenosphere, mesosphere (lower mantle), liquid outer core, and solid inner core. The two classification schemes overlap only partially and are not typically intermixed.

### Crust and Lithosphere
The crust ranges from 5 km (oceanic) to 70 km (continental) thick. Oceanic crust is dense and mafic; continental crust is less dense and felsic. Rocks are categorized as sial (aluminum silicate) or sima (magnesium silicate). The Mohorovičić (Moho) discontinuity marks a seismic velocity jump from basalt-like to peridotite-like velocities, reflecting a density change. The oldest crustal minerals date to 4.4 billion years.

### Mantle
The mantle extends to 2,890 km depth, comprising 83.7% of Earth’s volume. It is divided into upper and lower sections by a transition zone, with the D″ layer at its base. Though solid, mantle rock flows slowly due to extreme heat and pressure. Convection here drives plate tectonics. Heat sources include radioactive decay and residual formation energy. Viscosity increases with depth, ranging from 10²¹ to 10²⁴ pascal-second.

### Core
The liquid outer core spans from 2,890 km to 5,150 km depth, composed mainly of iron and nickel. The solid inner core, discovered by Lehmann in 1936, has a radius of ~1,220 km. The core formed during early differentiation, where dense materials sank inward. Laboratory experiments using diamond anvils suggest the inner core contains large iron crystals aligned north-south. Dynamo theory attributes Earth’s magnetic field to convection in the outer core modulated by the Coriolis effect. The inner core stabilizes this field but cannot retain a permanent magnetization due to high temperatures. Core freezing is estimated to occur in ~91 billion years.

### Seismology
Seismic waves from earthquakes reveal internal layering. Shear waves cannot travel through the liquid outer core, while compressional waves refract and reflect at boundaries due to velocity contrasts governed by Snell’s law.

## Terms
- **Lithosphere**: Rigid outer shell including crust and uppermost mantle.
- **Asthenosphere**: Ductile upper mantle layer beneath the lithosphere where tectonic plates move.
- **Mesosphere**: Lower mantle layer where high pressure solidifies rock; also called subasthenospheric mantle.
- **Mohorovičić discontinuity (Moho)**: Boundary between crust and mantle marked by a seismic velocity increase.
- **Conrad discontinuity**: Boundary within the crust separating sial and sima layers; may be indistinct.
- **D″ layer**: Lowest part of the mantle adjacent to the core-mantle boundary.
- **Planetary differentiation**: Process where denser materials sink inward during planetary formation.
- **Dynamo theory**: Explanation for Earth’s magnetic field via convection and rotation in the outer core.
- **Snell's law**: Principle governing seismic wave refraction at layer boundaries.
- **Chondrite model**: Assumption that Earth’s composition resembles ordinary chondrite meteorites.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Inner core state**: Some high-pressure laser studies suggest the inner core may be a plasma with solid-like density rather than a true solid.
- **Core composition**: Whether silicon is the dominant light element in the core remains debated; enstatite chondrite models offer alternative compositions.
- **Crystal structure**: Whether the inner core consists of a single giant crystal or polycrystalline aggregates is unresolved.
- **Cooling rate**: Estimates of core solidification timing vary, with uncertainty around heat loss mechanisms.
