# Bank

## Overview
A bank is a financial institution that accepts deposits from the public, creates demand deposits, and makes loans. By operating under fractional-reserve banking—holding liquid assets equal to only a portion of current liabilities—banks create new money when they issue loans. They are critical to financial stability and economic function, performing payment processing, credit intermediation, and maturity transformation (borrowing short-term to lend long-term). Consequently, they are heavily regulated, subject to capital requirements like the Basel Accords, and supervised by central banks. Modern banking emerged in 14th-century Renaissance Italy, evolving from ancient credit practices and goldsmith banking in 17th-century London.

## Timeline
- **4th–3rd millennium BCE** — Archaic banking activity begins in ancient world.
- **12th–early 14th century** — Knights Templar operate early private banking network for pilgrims in Europe.
- **1397** — Giovanni di Bicci de' Medici founds the Medici Bank in Florence.
- **1401** — Consell de Cent establishes Taula de canvi de Barcelona, earliest known state deposit bank.
- **1472** — Banca Monte dei Paschi di Siena founded (oldest existing retail bank).
- **1590** — Berenberg Bank founded (oldest existing merchant bank).
- **17th–18th century** — Fractional-reserve banking and banknotes emerge via London goldsmiths.
- **1695** — Bank of England originates permanent issue of banknotes.
- **1728** — Royal Bank of Scotland establishes first overdraft facility.
- **19th century** — Ordinary deposits legally recognized as loans (mutuum) to the bank; Lubbock's Bank establishes London clearing house.
- **1875** — Rothschilds finance British government purchase of Suez Canal shares, pioneering international finance.
- **20th century** — Telecommunications and computing enable dramatic growth in bank size and geographic spread.
- **2008** — Global financial crisis triggers major bank failures and regulatory reform (e.g., CoCos).
- **March 2023** — Global banking crisis: three US bank failures and collapse/shutdown of several major global banks.

## Body

### Core Activities and Business Models
Banks act as payment agents (checking accounts, ACH, wire transfers, ATMs, EFTPOS) and intermediaries: they borrow via deposits (current, term) and debt securities, and lend via advances, installment loans, and securities investment. Revenue derives primarily from the **interest spread**—the difference between interest paid on deposits and charged on loans—supplemented by transaction fees and financial advice. Since the Gramm–Leach–Bliley Act (US), banks merge commercial, investment, and insurance functions for cross-selling. Risk-based pricing charges higher rates to riskier borrowers. Payment cards (debit, credit, prepaid, smart) generate interest, cardholder fees, and merchant transaction fees.

### Channels and Products
Access channels include physical branches, ATMs, mail, online, mobile, telephone, video banking, relationship managers, and direct selling agents. Retail products cover deposits (savings, CDs, money market, IRAs), cards (ATM, debit, credit), loans (mortgage, personal), and payment systems (NEFT, RTGS). Business/commercial banking offers loans, capital raising (equity/debt/hybrids), revolving credit, risk management (FX, derivatives), cash management, and securities services.

### Historical Evolution
**Archaic/Medieval:** Activity traces to 4th millennium BCE. In medieval Europe, Knights Templar (12th–14th century) issued receipts for deposits, enabling remote withdrawal. Renaissance Italian city-states (Florence, Genoa, Barcelona) birthed modern banking; families like Bardi, Peruzzi, and Medici (1397) established international branches. The Taula de canvi (1401) and Bank of Saint George (1407) were early state/chartered banks.
**Early Modern (17th–19th Century):** London goldsmiths stored gold, issued receipts, and began lending against deposits, creating fractional-reserve banking and assignable promissory notes (early banknotes). The Bank of England (1695) standardized banknote issuance. The Royal Bank of Scotland invented the overdraft (1728). Lubbock's Bank created a clearing house (early 19th century). By the 19th century, deposits were legally *mutuum* (loans to the bank). The Rothschilds scaled international finance (1875 Suez Canal).
**Modern (20th–21st Century):** Telecom/computing drove consolidation and geographic expansion. The 2008 crisis caused massive failures, profit collapse (85% drop for top 1,000 banks), and new capital instruments (Contingent Convertible Bonds/CoCos). The 2023 crisis saw rapid failures due to liquidity shortages and insolvency.

### Capital, Risk, and Regulation
Banks manage **credit risk** (borrower default), **liquidity risk** (asset sale speed), **market risk** (portfolio value decline), **operational risk** (process failure), **reputational risk**, and **macroeconomic risk**. Capital (equity, retained earnings, subordinated debt) buffers losses. The **Basel Accords** set international risk-weighted capital standards. Post-2008, regulators mandated **CoCos**—hybrid securities that convert to equity or write down when capital falls below a threshold, absorbing losses.
Regulation requires a special license (minimum capital, fit-and-proper owners, approved business plan). Regulators (often central banks) participate in the market. In the US, oversight is fragmented (FDIC, Fed, OCC, state agencies), creating coordination challenges. Banking law implies contractual duties: honoring checks up to balance/overdraft, collecting deposits, secrecy (with exceptions), combining accounts, and lien rights on deposits.

### Economic Functions
1.  **Money Issue:** Banknotes and current accounts act as money (negotiable, repayable on demand, valued at par).
2.  **Netting/Settlement:** Interbank clearing offsets inward/outward payments, economizing reserves and reducing cross-border settlement costs.
3.  **Credit Quality Improvement:** Banks lend to average borrowers but are high-quality borrowers themselves; diversification and capital absorb losses.
4.  **Maturity Transformation:** Borrowing short (demand deposits) and lending long (term loans), managed via reserves, marketable securities, and wholesale funding.
5.  **Money Creation/Destruction:** In fractional-reserve systems, new loans create money; principal repayment destroys it.

### Crises and Industry Structure
Systemic crises stem from liquidity risk (bank runs), credit risk (non-repayment), and interest rate risk (spread compression). Historic examples: Great Depression runs, US Savings & Loan crisis (1980s–90s), Japanese crisis (1990s), sub-prime crisis (2000s), 2023 global crisis.
**Size (2008–09):** Top 1,000 banks held $96.4T assets (EU 56%, Asia 14%, US 13%). US has most institutions (5,330 in 2015); China’s top four banks had >67,000 branches (2009).
**M&A:** ~28,800 deals (1985–2018), total value ~$5.17T, peaking in 1999 and 2007 (~$460B each).
**Types:** Commercial, community, community development, land development, credit unions/co-ops, postal savings, private, offshore, savings, ethical, direct/internet-only, informal. **Investment banks** underwrite securities, advise on M&A, trade, and make markets. **Merchant banks** provide equity capital. **Universal banks** (financial services companies) combine activities, often including insurance (**bancassurance**). **Central banks** (government-owned) supervise, set rates, provide liquidity, act as lender of last resort. **Islamic banks** avoid interest (riba), earning profit via markup/fees.

### Challenges (Focus: United States)
**Regulatory Burden:** Multiple overlapping regulators (FDIC, Fed, OCC, states) with changing rules; FFIEC (1979) improved consistency but consolidation reduced examiner staff/budgets, leading to less hands-on oversight and potential missed problems.
**Economic Environment:** Managing interest rate spreads amid low loan rates, deposit competition, and volatility. Rising rates help spreads but unpredictably affect borrowers.
**Asset Quality:** Loans are primary assets; declining quality (exacerbated by lax underwriting during "good times," reduced oversight, and cut training) threatens solvency.
**Structural:** Aging management/boards; shareholder pressure for earnings; legacy IT systems (decades old, costly to maintain/integrate).
**Competition & Disintermediation:** Money market funds and direct market instruments (Treasuries, corporate debt) draw deposits away. Competition varies from competitive to oligopoly (measured by Lerner index/Boone indicator); higher competition raises savings rates, lowers mortgage rates.
**Fossil Fuel Financing:** Large developed-nation banks continue heavy financing of fossil fuels post-Paris Agreement, drawing criticism.

### Globalization
Telecom/financial tech (e.g., Bloomberg) reduced barriers, enabling cross-border services. However, banking remains less globalized than other industries: foreign-owned banks hold <10% market share in most nations. Local banks are preferred for small business/individual loans (soft information); large corps access global banks. Citigroup and HSBC attempted global retail networks (1980s) but retreated (Citi 2021, HSBC 2022–23) as the anticipated "global consumer" did not materialize.

### Accounts and Deposits
**Accounting (GAAP):** Credit accounts (Revenue, Equity, Liabilities) increase via credit; Debit accounts (Assets, Expenses) increase via debit. Customer deposits are credits (bank liability); withdrawals are debits. Positive balance = credit balance on statement; overdraft = debit balance.
**Brokered Deposits:** Large sums placed by deposit brokers seeking best rates ("hot money"). High reliance correlates with risk-taking and failure (2008 failed banks avg. 4x brokered deposits vs. average). Regulation opposed by banks as a funding source for deposit-poor areas.
**Custodial Accounts:** Assets held for third parties (e.g., client funds pre-conversion/transfer).

## Terms
- ****Fractional-reserve banking**** — System where banks hold liquid assets equal to only a fraction of current liabilities, lending the remainder to create money.
- ****Demand deposit**** — Funds held in an account from which the depositor can withdraw at any time without notice (e.g., checking/current account).
- ****Interest spread**** — Difference between interest rate charged on loans and interest rate paid on deposits/funding sources; primary profit driver.
- ****Maturity transformation**** — Borrowing short-term (deposits) to fund long-term loans; core banking function requiring liquidity management.
- ****Mutuum**** — Legal classification of a bank deposit as a loan to the bank, obligating the bank to return an equivalent sum, not the specific money.
- ****Basel Accords**** — International regulatory frameworks setting minimum capital requirements and risk-weighted asset standards for banks.
- ****Contingent Convertible Bonds (CoCos)**** — Hybrid capital securities that automatically absorb losses (convert to equity or write down) when issuer's capital falls below a trigger level.
- ****Bancassurance**** — Distribution of insurance products by a bank; portmanteau of *banque* (bank) and *assurance* (insurance).
- ****Brokered deposits**** — Large deposits placed by third-party brokers on behalf of investors, often rate-sensitive ("hot money") and linked to higher bank risk.
- ****Lender of last resort**** — Central bank function providing emergency liquidity to solvent but illiquid banks during systemic crises.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Regulatory Architecture (US):** Whether fragmented, multi-agency oversight (FDIC, Fed, OCC, states) creates gaps, inconsistency, and examiner burnout versus the benefit of specialized supervision.
*   **Brokered Deposits:** Whether they are a vital funding channel for growing communities or a destabilizing "hot money" source encouraging excessive risk-taking (evidenced by 2008 and 1980s S&L crises).
*   **Global Retail Banking Viability:** Whether a sustainable "global consumer" segment exists to justify cross-border retail networks, given the retreat of Citigroup and HSBC after decades of investment.
*   **Fossil Fuel Financing:** Tension between profitability/continuing to finance fossil fuels and alignment with Paris Agreement climate goals; pressure for mandatory transition plans.
*   **Definition of Banking:** Legal definitions remain check-centric; theorists argue statutes must broaden to cover institutions conducting current accounts and enabling third-party payments without checks (EFTPOS, internet banking).
*   **Shadow Banking/Disintermediation:** Ongoing migration of funds to non-bank financial intermediaries (money market funds, capital markets) challenges traditional deposit-funded models and regulatory perimeters.
