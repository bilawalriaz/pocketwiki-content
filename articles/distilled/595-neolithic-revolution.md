# Neolithic Revolution

## Overview

The Neolithic Revolution, also called the First Agricultural Revolution, was the wide-scale transition of human cultures in Afro-Eurasia from hunting and gathering to agriculture and settlement, beginning around 11,700 years ago after the last Ice Age. This shift enabled population growth, surplus food production, and the eventual rise of complex societies, though it also reduced dietary diversity and often worsened health outcomes.

## Timeline

- **~11,700 BP** — End of last Ice Age; start of Holocene epoch
- **~11,000 BP** — Domestication begins in Fertile Crescent
- **~10,000–8,000 BCE** — Agriculture emerges in Fertile Crescent
- **~9,000 BCE** — Barley domesticated in Near East
- **~8,000 BCE** — Agriculture appears in East Asia (millet)
- **~7,000 BCE** — Agriculture reaches Europe via Anatolia
- **~6,500 BP** — Permanent farming in Nile Valley
- **~5,000 BP** — Austronesian expansion spreads rice cultivation

## Body

### Background and Transition

Prehistoric hunter-gatherers were highly mobile, living in small groups with temporary shelters and seasonal diets. In contrast, agriculture allowed for permanent settlements and supported larger populations through surplus food production. This sedentary lifestyle encouraged division of labor, trade, and eventually complex societies. However, reliance on staple crops often led to nutritional deficiencies and increased disease due to poor sanitation and proximity to domesticated animals.

### Agricultural Transition

The term "Neolithic Revolution" was coined by V. Gordon Childe in 1936 to describe the profound societal changes brought by agriculture. The transition began between 10,000 and 8,000 BCE in the Fertile Crescent, and around 8000 BCE in Papua New Guinea. It involved not just farming but also animal domestication, pottery, polished stone tools, and rectangular houses. The process was neither uniform nor linear, varying significantly across regions.

### Domestication

#### Crops

Around 9000 BP, humans began selectively breeding cereal grasses like emmer, einkorn, and barley. Plants with traits such as seed retention and larger seeds were favored. Daniel Zohary identified "founder crops" including wheat, barley, rye, flax, peas, chickpeas, bitter vetch, and lentils. Domestication likely occurred in a small number of events per species, spreading from the Levant into Europe. Early attempts sometimes failed—rye was abandoned in Anatolia before being successfully domesticated in Europe. At sites like Gilgal I, archaeologists found large seed caches dating to ~11,000 years ago, indicating early cultivation. Granaries developed to store surplus grain, enabling population growth and specialization.

#### Livestock

As sedentary life became more efficient, animals were brought closer to settlements. Dogs were among the first domesticated animals (~15,000 years ago in East Asia), followed by sheep, goats, cattle, and pigs. West Asia was a primary source for many domesticated species. As the Middle East became drier, farmers migrated east-west, spreading crops and animals along similar climates. This axis facilitated rapid diffusion across Eurasia, unlike the north-south axis of Africa, which hindered temperate crop spread.

### Centers of Agricultural Origin

#### West Asia

Agriculture first appeared in West Asia ~10,000–9,000 years ago. The region domesticated einkorn wheat, emmer wheat, barley, lentils, peas, bitter vetch, chickpeas, and flax. Domestication was gradual, preceded by millennia of pre-domestication cultivation. Sites like Ohalo II (~23,000 years old) show early cereal harvesting tools. Multiple regions within the Fertile Crescent may have independently developed cereal domestication.

#### East Asia

Northern China saw domestication of foxtail and broomcorn millet ~8,000 years ago, associated with cultures like Houli and Peiligang. Soybeans were domesticated ~4,500 years ago. Southern China focused on rice cultivation between 13,500 and 8,200 years ago, with two proposed centers: lower Yangtze (pre-Austronesian) and middle Yangtze (Hmong-Mien). Millet and rice cultures met ~9,000–7,000 BP, forming a cultivation corridor. Austronesian expansion from Taiwan (~5,000 BP) spread rice and millet throughout Island Southeast Asia and Oceania.

#### Africa

Three independent agricultural centers emerged: Ethiopian highlands (coffee, khat, ensete, teff, finger millet), Sahel (sorghum, pearl millet), and West Africa (African rice, yams, oil palm). The Nile Valley's agriculture likely derived from Fertile Crescent migration. Bananas and plantains were re-domesticated in Africa ~5,000 years ago. The Bantu expansion (1st millennium BCE–CE) spread agriculture to Central and Southern Africa.

#### Americas

Maize, beans, and squash were domesticated in Mesoamerica starting ~7,000 BCE. Potatoes and manioc originated in South America. Eastern North American societies domesticated sunflower and goosefoot ~2,500 BCE. Sedentary village life based on farming developed later in central Mexico during the second millennium BCE.

#### New Guinea

Kuk Swamp shows taro and yam cultivation dating to 11,000 BP, with bananas and sugarcane by ~6,950 BCE. Taro may be the world’s earliest cultivated crop, introduced to the Solomon Islands ~28,000 years ago. This region likely contributed to the spread of Trans-New Guinea languages.

### Spread of Agriculture

#### Europe

Neolithic material culture spread from the Levant through Anatolia into Europe ~8,500–6,000 BP, taking roughly 2,500 years to reach Britain. All European Neolithic sites contain Southwest Asian crops and animals; no independent animal domestication occurred. Carbon-14 dating shows a steady spread at ~1 km/year. Mitochondrial DNA analysis confirms significant human migration accompanied agricultural diffusion, likely via maritime routes through Cyprus and the Aegean.

#### South Asia

Mehrgarh (Pakistan) is the earliest South Asian Neolithic site (~6500–5500 BCE), showing wheat, barley, cattle, sheep, and goats. Local domestication of barley and zebu cattle occurred, while wheat likely came from the Near East. Cultural similarities with Mesopotamian sites suggest strong connections. Eastward spread occurred at ~0.65 km/year.

### Causes

Several theories explain the origins of agriculture, none mutually exclusive:
- **Oasis Theory** (Pumpelly, Childe): Climate drying forced communities into oases, leading to animal domestication. Lacks strong climate support.
- **Hilly Flanks Hypothesis** (Braidwood): Agriculture began in Taurus/Zagros mountains with fertile land and wild cereals.
- **Feasting Model** (Hayden): Elites drove agriculture to support large feasts and display power.
- **Demographic Theories** (Sauer, Binford, Flannery): Population pressure exceeded carrying capacity, necessitating food production.
- **Evolutionary/Intentionality Theory** (Rindos): Agriculture evolved as mutual adaptation between humans and plants.
- **Climate Stability** (Richerson, Boyd, Bettinger): Stable Holocene climate enabled reliable farming.
- **Natural Environments** (Grienin): Agriculture arose in specific favorable environments.
- **Long Development** (Moore, Hole): Gradual evolution over millennia in the Levant, possibly beginning in the Epipaleolithic.

### Consequences

#### Social Change

Agriculture enabled denser populations, surplus accumulation, and social stratification. Surpluses supported elites who controlled resources and decision-making. Specialization increased, and larger societies adopted diverse governance models. Gender inequality may have intensified, as suggested by shifts in religious symbolism from revered female figures (Venus of Lespugue) to dominated ones (Circe, Charybdis).

#### Secondary Products Revolution

Andrew Sherratt proposed a second phase where animals were used for products beyond meat: milk, wool, hides, manure, and traction. This innovation enabled intensive farming, nomadic pastoralism, and camel domestication, but also contributed to desertification through overgrazing.

#### Diet and Health

Neolithic diets were higher in carbohydrates but lower in fiber, protein, and micronutrients. This led to dental caries, slower childhood growth, increased body fat, and shorter stature. Europeans lost ~13 cm in average height post-transition, not recovering until the 20th century. Disease spread more rapidly in sedentary communities; zoonotic diseases like influenza, smallpox, and Salmonella emerged. Natural selection favored disease resistance in populations with long animal contact, explaining disparities in immunity among global populations.

## Terms

- **Neolithic Revolution**: The transition from hunting-gathering to agriculture and settlement.
- **Domestication**: The genetic modification of plants/animals through selective breeding for human use.
- **Founder Crops**: The first domesticated plant species, including wheat, barley, legumes, and flax.
- **Secondary Products Revolution**: The later use of domesticated animals for milk, wool, labor, and other non-meat products.
- **Broad Spectrum Revolution**: The hypothesized expansion of dietary range during early agriculture.
- **Paleolithic**: The Old Stone Age, characterized by hunting and gathering.
- **Holocene**: The current geological epoch, beginning ~11,700 years ago after the last Ice Age.
- **Fertile Crescent**: A region in the Middle East where agriculture first developed.
- **Austronesian Expansion**: The migration of Austronesian-speaking peoples spreading rice cultivation across the Pacific and Indian Oceans.
- **Zoonosis**: Diseases that jump from animals to humans.

## Debates and Open Questions

Scholars debate whether the Neolithic Revolution was driven primarily by environmental change, demographic pressure, or cultural innovation. The sequence and relationship between key developments—agriculture, sedentism, pottery, and animal domestication—vary by region, challenging universal models. Some argue for independent invention in multiple centers, while others emphasize diffusion. The health impacts remain contested: while some regions show severe nutritional decline, others (like Southeast Asia) experienced less detrimental effects. The role of climate stability versus human agency in triggering agriculture is still debated, as is the extent to which population movements versus cultural exchange drove its spread.
