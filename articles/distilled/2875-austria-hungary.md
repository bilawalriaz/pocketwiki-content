# Austria-Hungary

## Overview
Austria-Hungary was a dual monarchy in Central Europe that existed from 1867 to 1918. It was a multi-national empire formed by the Compromise of 1867, which united the Austrian Empire and the Kingdom of Hungary under a single monarch. As a major European power, its dissolution after World War I redrew the map of Europe, creating several new nation-states.

## Timeline
- **1866** — Austria-Hungary is defeated in the Austro-Prussian War, leading to its exclusion from German affairs.
- **1867** — The Austro-Hungarian Compromise creates the dual monarchy under Emperor Franz Joseph I.
- **1878** — Austria-Hungary occupies Bosnia and Herzegovina following the Congress of Berlin.
- **1908** — Austria-Hungary formally annexes Bosnia and Herzegovina, triggering the Bosnian crisis.
- **28 June 1914** — Archduke Franz Ferdinand is assassinated in Sarajevo, precipitating World War I.
- **28 July 1914** — Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia, beginning its involvement in World War I.
- **3 November 1918** — The Armistice of Villa Giusti ends hostilities on the Italian front.
- **31 October 1918** — Hungary's Aster Revolution begins; the union with Austria is terminated.
- **11 November 1918** — Emperor Karl I relinquishes participation in state affairs.
- **12 November 1918** — The Republic of German-Austria is proclaimed.
- **16 November 1918** — The Hungarian Democratic Republic is proclaimed.

## Body

### Establishment and Structure
Austria-Hungary was formed by the Compromise of 1867, which turned the Habsburg domains into a real union between two sovereign states: Cisleithania (the Austrian lands) and Transleithania (the Kingdom of Hungary). A single monarch, Franz Joseph I, ruled as both Emperor of Austria and Apostolic King of Hungary. The two states shared common ministries for foreign affairs, defense, and finance, but maintained separate parliaments, governments, and most other state functions. The Kingdom of Croatia-Slavonia was an autonomous region under the Hungarian crown. After 1878, Bosnia and Herzegovina was under joint Austro-Hungarian administration until its full annexation in 1908.

### Foreign Policy and Balkan Tensions
After being excluded from Germany and Italy, Austria-Hungary turned its focus to the Balkans. Foreign Minister Gyula Andrássy pursued a policy of opposing Russian expansion and blocking Serbian ambitions. Following the Russo-Turkish War of 1877–78, the Congress of Berlin (1878) awarded Austria-Hungary the occupation of Bosnia and Herzegovina. To counter Russian and French interests, Austria-Hungary formed the Triple Alliance with Germany and Italy in 1882. The 1908 annexation of Bosnia and Herzegovina, orchestrated by Foreign Minister Alois Lexa von Aehrenthal, provoked a major international crisis and strengthened opposition from Serbia and Russia.

### World War I and Dissolution
The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in 1914 led Austria-Hungary to issue an ultimatum to Serbia, and subsequently declare war, triggering World War I. The empire fought on the Central Powers side but suffered severe military defeats, particularly against Russia and Italy. The multi-ethnic army faced low morale and supply shortages, and by 1916, Austria-Hungary was effectively a military satellite of Germany. The war caused severe economic strain and food shortages on the home front. In 1918, as defeat loomed, nationalist movements declared independence. Emperor Karl I's attempt to federalize the empire failed. Hungary terminated the union on 31 October 1918, and Karl relinquished power on 11 November, leading to the proclamation of successor republics in Austria and Hungary.

### Demographics and Nationality Issues
Austria-Hungary was a multi-ethnic state where language was a primary marker of identity. The Austrian half recognized equal rights for all races and languages in principle, while Hungary's 1868 Nationalities Law granted minorities individual but not communal rights. In practice, both governments pursued policies of Magyarization and Germanization, leading to intense political disputes, especially in Bohemia between Czech and German speakers. The empire had a significant Jewish population, which faced some antisemitic political movements but generally experienced a period of relative security and advancement.

### Economy and Infrastructure
The economy was predominantly rural but modernized after 1867. It developed a significant machine-building industry and the fourth-largest railway network in Europe. Economic growth was uneven, with western areas like Vienna and Prague more industrialized, while the eastern Kingdom of Hungary became a major agricultural exporter. The empire had a central bank, the Austro-Hungarian Bank, and advanced telecommunications, including the world's second underground railway in Budapest (1896).

## Terms
- **Dual Monarchy**: The political union of the Austrian Empire and the Kingdom of Hungary under a single monarch, established by the Compromise of 1867.
- **Cisleithania**: The informal name for the northern and western Austrian lands of the dual monarchy, officially "The Kingdoms and Lands Represented in the Imperial Council."
- **Transleithania**: The informal name for the lands of the Hungarian Crown, including Hungary and Croatia-Slavonia.
- **Compromise of 1867**: The agreement that created the dual monarchy, granting co-equal status to Austria and Hungary in shared affairs like foreign policy and defense.
- **k. u. k. (kaiserlich und königlich)**: The abbreviation for "Imperial and Royal," used for institutions common to both halves of the monarchy, such as the army and foreign ministry.
- **Bosnian Crisis**: The 1908–09 international crisis triggered by Austria-Hungary's annexation of Bosnia and Herzegovina, which heightened tensions with Serbia and Russia.
- **Triple Alliance**: The 1882 defensive alliance between Austria-Hungary, Germany, and Italy.
- **Magyarization**: The policy of promoting Hungarian language and culture within the Kingdom of Hungary, often at the expense of minority cultures.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The extent to which Austria-Hungary's multi-ethnic structure was an inherent weakness versus a product of specific political failures and wartime pressures.
- The viability of proposed reforms like "trialism" (adding a South Slav component) as a potential solution to nationalist tensions before the war.
- The relative responsibility of Austria-Hungary versus other Great Powers in the outbreak of World War I.
- The long-term impact of the empire's dissolution on the political and ethnic conflicts of 20th-century Central and Eastern Europe.
