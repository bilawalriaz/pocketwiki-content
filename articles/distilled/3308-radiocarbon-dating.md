# Radiocarbon dating

## Overview
Radiocarbon dating is a method for determining the age of organic materials by measuring the decay of the radioactive isotope carbon-14 (¹⁴C). Developed by Willard Libby in the late 1940s, it revolutionized archaeology and geology by providing a universal chronological framework, allowing for the dating of events up to approximately 50,000 years ago and enabling comparisons across vast distances.

## Timeline
- **1939** — Martin Kamen and Samuel Ruben synthesize ¹⁴C and discover its long half-life.
- **1940s** — Serge Korff predicts ¹⁴C creation in the atmosphere; Willard Libby conceives the dating method.
- **1949** — Libby and James Arnold publish first test results on samples of known age (e.g., Egyptian tombs).
- **1960** — Willard Libby receives the Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
- **1960s** — First calibration curves developed using tree-ring data (dendrochronology).
- **1970s** — Liquid scintillation counting becomes a common measurement technology.
- **Late 1970s** — Accelerator Mass Spectrometry (AMS) emerges as a more precise and sensitive alternative to beta counting.
- **1998** — First IntCal calibration curve published, standardizing calendar age conversion.
- **2020** — Latest IntCal20 and SHCal20 calibration curves released, incorporating new data sources.

## Body

### Principle and Discovery
Radiocarbon (¹⁴C) is created in the upper atmosphere when cosmic rays interact with nitrogen. It combines with oxygen to form radioactive carbon dioxide, which enters the biosphere through photosynthesis. Organisms maintain a constant ¹⁴C ratio with the atmosphere while alive. Upon death, carbon exchange stops, and the ¹⁴C decays at a known rate (half-life ≈ 5,730 years). Measuring the remaining ¹⁴C allows calculation of the time since death. Libby's method was validated by dating samples with independent historical ages, such as Egyptian royal tombs.

### Measurement Technologies
Two primary methods exist. **Beta counting** detects the beta radiation emitted by decaying ¹⁴C atoms in a sample. **Accelerator Mass Spectrometry (AMS)** directly counts ¹⁴C atoms, allowing for analysis of much smaller samples (e.g., individual seeds) and providing faster results. AMS is now the preferred method due to its higher precision and sensitivity.

### Calibration and Corrections
Raw radiocarbon ages ("conventional radiocarbon age") assume a constant atmospheric ¹⁴C level, which is incorrect. Calibration curves, built from independently dated records like tree rings, convert radiocarbon years to calendar years. Key corrections account for:
*   **Atmospheric Variation:** Historical fluctuations in atmospheric ¹⁴C, including the "Suess effect" (dilution from fossil fuel burning) and the "bomb pulse" (increase from nuclear testing).
*   **Isotopic Fractionation:** Natural variation in ¹⁴C uptake by different organisms, corrected using stable carbon isotope (δ¹³C) measurements.
*   **Reservoir Effects:** Differences in ¹⁴C levels between the atmosphere and other carbon reservoirs, such as the ocean (marine effect) or freshwater (hard water effect), which can make samples appear older.

### Archaeological Application and Impact
Radiocarbon dating provides absolute dates for organic materials found in archaeological contexts. Interpretation requires careful consideration of **association** (the relationship between the dated sample and the artifact of interest) and potential issues like the **"old wood" problem** (dating wood from a tree's inner rings, which are older than its felling). Its impact is termed the "radiocarbon revolution," as it enabled the construction of a global prehistoric timeline, challenged diffusionist theories of cultural spread, and shifted archaeological focus toward questions of human behavior and local innovation.

## Terms
*   **Radiocarbon (¹⁴C):** A radioactive isotope of carbon created in the atmosphere, used as the basis for dating.
*   **Half-life:** The time required for half of the radioactive atoms in a sample to decay. For ¹⁴C, it is about 5,730 years.
*   **Calibration Curve:** A graph (e.g., IntCal) that converts a radiocarbon age into a more accurate calendar age by accounting for historical variations in atmospheric ¹⁴C.
*   **Accelerator Mass Spectrometry (AMS):** A technique that directly counts ¹⁴C atoms in a sample, allowing for analysis of very small specimens.
*   **Isotopic Fractionation:** The natural process where organisms preferentially absorb lighter carbon isotopes (¹²C over ¹³C and ¹⁴C), requiring correction in dating.
*   **Reservoir Effect:** A discrepancy in ¹⁴C age caused by a sample originating from a carbon reservoir (like the ocean) that is not in equilibrium with the atmosphere.
*   **Conventional Radiocarbon Age:** An uncalibrated age calculated using the "Libby half-life" of 5,568 years for consistency with early research.
*   **BP (Before Present):** A time convention where "present" is defined as the year 1950.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Scroll Contamination:** The authenticity of radiocarbon dates for the Dead Sea Scrolls has been debated due to potential contamination from modern materials (e.g., castor oil) used in their conservation.
*   **Neanderthal Dates:** Some researchers suggest that many published radiocarbon dates for Neanderthal artifacts are inaccurately young due to contamination by "young carbon."
*   **Calibration Precision:** While calibration curves are continually refined, uncertainties remain, especially for periods with plateaus or rapid fluctuations in atmospheric ¹⁴C, leading to multiple possible calendar date ranges for a single radiocarbon measurement.
