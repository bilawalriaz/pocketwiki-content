# Almoravid dynasty

## Overview
The Almoravid dynasty was a Berber Muslim empire that controlled the western Maghreb (North Africa) and al-Andalus (Muslim Iberia) from the mid-11th to the mid-12th century. Emerging from Saharan nomadic tribes, they united the region under a strict interpretation of Maliki Islam, halted the advance of Christian kingdoms in Iberia for a time, and fostered significant cultural and architectural exchange between Africa and Europe. Their empire ultimately fell to the rival Almohad movement.

## Timeline
- **c. 1040** — Yahya ibn Ibrahim meets scholar Abu Imran al-Fasi, seeking a Maliki teacher for his people.
- **1054–55** — Almoravids capture the key trade cities of Sijilmasa and Awdaghust.
- **1058–59** — Spiritual leader Abdallah ibn Yasin is killed in battle against the Barghawata.
- **c. 1070** — Abu Bakr ibn Umar founds the new capital, Marrakesh.
- **1086** — Yusuf ibn Tashfin defeats Castilian King Alfonso VI at the Battle of Sagrajas.
- **1090–94** — Almoravids conquer the Taifa kingdoms of al-Andalus, including Seville and Valencia.
- **1118** — Aragonese King Alfonso I captures Zaragoza, a major Almoravid setback.
- **1120s** — Ibn Tumart founds the rival Almohad movement in the Maghreb.
- **1147** — The Almohads capture Marrakesh, ending the Almoravid dynasty.

## Body

### Origins and Early Conquests
The Almoravids originated from the Lamtuna, Gudala, and Massufa—nomadic Sanhaja Berber tribes in the Sahara. Their movement began as a religious reform led by the puritan scholar Abdallah ibn Yasin, who preached strict adherence to Maliki Islamic law and rejected tribalism. In the 1050s, they conquered the trans-Saharan trade hubs of Sijilmasa and Awdaghust, gaining control of vital gold routes. After internal divisions, the empire split: Abu Bakr ibn Umar led the southern Saharan territories, while his cousin Yusuf ibn Tashfin governed the expanding northern realm in the Maghreb.

### Expansion and the Almoravid State
Yusuf ibn Tashfin consolidated Almoravid control over Morocco, founding Marrakesh as the capital around 1070. He organized the western Maghreb into provinces and formally acknowledged the Abbasid Caliph in Baghdad, taking the title *Amir al-Muslimīn* ("Prince of the Muslims"). The Almoravids intervened in al-Andalus at the invitation of Taifa kings threatened by Christian kingdoms. Their victory at the Battle of Sagrajas (1086) temporarily halted Christian advances. Subsequently, they deposed the Taifa rulers and directly governed al-Andalus, uniting it with the Maghreb politically for the first time.

### Decline and Fall
Almoravid power began to wane after the loss of Zaragoza to Aragon in 1118. Their legitimacy eroded as they failed to protect al-Andalus from Christian advances and were forced to reintroduce non-canonical taxes. The decisive blow came from the Almohad movement, a religious reform founded by Ibn Tumart in the 1120s that challenged Almoravid orthodoxy. The Almohads, led by Abd al-Mu'min, progressively conquered Almoravid territories. The last Almoravid ruler, Ishaq ibn Ali, was killed when the Almohads captured Marrakesh in 1147.

### Culture and Architecture
The Almoravid period fostered a unified Andalusi-Maghribi artistic style. They patronized architecture that blended local traditions with influences from al-Andalus, introducing elements like *muqarnas* (honeycomb vaulting) to the west. Key monuments include the Almoravid Qubba in Marrakesh and the ribbed dome of the Great Mosque of Tlemcen. They also produced fine textiles, ceramics, and illuminated manuscripts, with the Maghrebi cursive script becoming standardized. Their initial puritanism softened over time, leading to the adoption of more luxurious artistic forms.

## Terms
- **Almoravid**: From Arabic *al-Murābiṭūn*, meaning "those from the ribats" (fortified religious houses), referring to their origins as a religious reform movement.
- **Sanhaja**: A major Berber tribal confederation in the Sahara from which the Almoravid ruling elite emerged.
- **Maliki school**: A conservative Sunni Islamic school of jurisprudence that formed the basis of Almoravid religious law.
- **Taifas**: The independent Muslim kingdoms that emerged in al-Andalus after the collapse of the Caliphate of Córdoba.
- **Paria**: Tribute paid by Taifa rulers to Christian kingdoms in exchange for military protection.
- **Ribat**: A fortified monastery or religious house, central to the early Almoravid identity.
- **Muqarnas**: An architectural ornament resembling honeycomb or stalactites, used in vaulting and decoration.
- **Almohad**: The successor dynasty to the Almoravids, founded by Ibn Tumart as a rival religious reform movement.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The nature and extent of Almoravid military conquest in the Ghana Empire (c. 1076–77) is heavily debated. Some scholars argue it was a full military invasion, while others, citing archaeological evidence, suggest it was more a gradual political and religious influence.
- The exact reasons for the adoption of the name "Almoravid" are uncertain, with multiple historical traditions offering different explanations.
- The degree to which Almoravid art and architecture represented a break from, or a continuation of, earlier Andalusi and Maghrebi styles is a subject of scholarly discussion.
