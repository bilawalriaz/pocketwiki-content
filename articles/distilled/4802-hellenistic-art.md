# Hellenistic art

## Overview
Hellenistic art (c. 323–30 BC) marks the period following Alexander the Great's death, when Greek culture spread across a vast area from Egypt to Central Asia. This era saw art shift from the idealized forms of Classical Greece toward greater emotional expression, realism, and diversity, influenced by new royal patrons and a cosmopolitan society. It laid crucial groundwork for Roman and later Western art traditions.

## Timeline
- **323 BC** — Death of Alexander the Great; his empire divides into Hellenistic kingdoms.
- **c. 300–250 BC** — Construction of the Temple of Apollo at Didyma begins.
- **285–246 BC** — Ptolemy II sponsors the Propylon of the Sanctuary of the Great Gods on Samothrace.
- **269–197 BC** — Attalus I commissions votive groups commemorating victories over Gauls.
- **c. 200–180 BC** — Construction of the Great Altar of Pergamon under Eumenes II.
- **197–159 BC** — Height of Pergamene Baroque sculpture.
- **156–153 BC** — Brief revival in sculpture noted by Pliny.
- **146 BC** — Roman conquest of the Greek mainland.
- **30 BC** — Roman conquest of Ptolemaic Egypt; end of the Hellenistic period.

## Body

### Architecture
Hellenistic architecture moved away from Classical city-state models toward vast urban plans and monumental complexes under royal patronage. The Doric temple was largely abandoned. Innovations included buildings that conformed to natural terrain and the proliferation of theaters and parks. A "gigantism" is seen in projects like the Temple of Apollo at Didyma, with its colossal Ionic columns.

**Alexandria** developed a distinctive "baroque" style with ornate, broken pediments and heavy use of Corinthian capitals, which later influenced Roman architecture. **Pergamon** exemplifies Hellenistic planning, with its acropolis complex fanned across a hillside, featuring a monumental altar and a theater for 10,000. Excavations at **Olynthus** reveal well-preserved homes focused on practical comfort and private courtyards, often decorated with mosaics.

### Sculpture
Sculpture became more naturalistic and expressive, depicting extremes of emotion, suffering, and everyday subjects. Portraiture flourished, showing individuals of all ages without idealization. The period saw the rise of private art collecting and the production of standardized statues for new cities across the Greek world.

Key styles include the **"Baroque"** (e.g., the multi-figure *Laocoön Group*), characterized by dramatic poses and intense emotion, and the **"Rococo"** (e.g., *Invitation to the Dance*), which favored playful, lighthearted motifs. The **Pergamene Baroque** school, seen in the *Great Altar* friezes, pushed dramatic expression to its peak. The **Neo-Attic** style later emerged as a reaction, returning to Classical forms, often for Roman copies.

### Paintings and Mosaics
No panel paintings survive, but mosaics and wall paintings (like those at **Vergina**) show Hellenistic innovations: landscape backgrounds, three-dimensional perspective, and chiaroscuro (light/shade). **Sacro-idyllic** scenes combined sacred and pastoral themes.

**Mosaics** evolved from pebble techniques to intricate works using tesserae (small stone/glass cubes). Techniques like *opus vermiculatum* (fine, detailed work) and *opus tessellatum* (larger tiles) were used. The **Alexander Mosaic** (from Pompeii) is a famous copy of a lost painting. Master mosaicists like **Sosos of Pergamon** created trompe-l'œil (optical illusion) effects. The mosaics of **Delos** represent the zenith of the art form.

### Pottery and Minor Arts
Fine painted pottery declined as metalware became more popular. Vase forms became simpler, often black with minimal decoration. New types included **Megarian ware** (mold-made relief vases) and **West Slope Ware** (painted slip on black). Regional styles like **Apulian** and **Centuripe ware** (with post-fire polychrome) persisted.

**Terracotta figurines** became hugely popular, from elegant **Tanagra** figures to "grotesques" depicting deformities. **Metalwork** excelled in virtuoso pieces like the *Derveni Krater*. The period saw the invention of **glass-blowing** and the rise of **cameo** carving. **Coinage** increasingly featured portraits.

## Terms
- **Diadochi**: Alexander the Great's generals who founded successor kingdoms.
- **Koine Greek**: The common dialect of Greek that spread during the Hellenistic period.
- **Baroque (style)**: In Hellenistic art, a style emphasizing dramatic, exaggerated poses and intense emotion.
- **Rococo (style)**: A lighter, more playful artistic trend contrasting with Baroque drama.
- **Tesserae**: Small, cut cubes of stone, glass, or other material used to make mosaics.
- **Opus vermiculatum**: A mosaic technique using very fine tesserae to create detailed, painterly images.
- **Chiaroscuro**: The use of strong contrasts between light and dark to model three-dimensional forms.
- **Sacro-idyllic**: A landscape style blending sacred and pastoral, dreamlike elements.
- **Neo-Attic**: A later Hellenistic style that revived Classical forms, often for Roman copies.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The characterization of Hellenistic art as "decadent" following the Classical "Golden Age" is a modern (18th-century) view now challenged by scholarship.
- The relationship between the "Baroque" and "Rococo" trends is debated; some see them as simultaneous opposites, others as sequential developments.
- The origins of tessellated mosaics are unclear; there is no direct evidence they evolved from earlier pebble mosaics.
- The function of "grotesque" terracotta figurines is uncertain—were they medical models, caricatures, or something else?
