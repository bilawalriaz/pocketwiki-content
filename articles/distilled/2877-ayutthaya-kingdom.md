# Ayutthaya Kingdom

## Overview
The Ayutthaya Kingdom (1351–1767) was a major Thai kingdom in Southeast Asia, centered on the island city of Ayutthaya in the Chao Phraya River valley. It is considered the precursor of modern Thailand. For over four centuries, it evolved from a maritime confederation into one of the great powers of Asia, a major international trade hub, and a center of Siamese culture, before its destruction by Burma in 1767.

## Timeline
- **c. 1351** — Uthong V (Ramathibodi I) founds the Ayutthaya Kingdom, merging four port polities.
- **1431** — Ayutthaya attacks Angkor, marking a shift toward a hinterland state.
- **1455** — King Borommatrailokkanat promulgates the Palatine Law, centralizing government.
- **1569** — Ayutthaya falls to the Toungoo dynasty of Burma, becoming a vassal state.
- **1584** — King Naresuan proclaims independence from Burma.
- **1605** — Naresuan dies; his brother Ekathotsarot succeeds, beginning a period of peace and commerce.
- **1629** — Prasat Thong usurps the throne, ending the Sukhothai dynasty line.
- **1657–1688** — Reign of King Narai, marked by Persian and French influence and the 1686 embassy to Louis XIV.
- **1688** — The Siamese revolution: Phetracha stages a coup, kills Narai's heir, and expels most Europeans.
- **1765–1767** — The Konbaung dynasty of Burma besieges and destroys Ayutthaya.
- **1767** — The city falls; Phraya Taksin later establishes a new capital at Thonburi.

## Body

### Origins and Founding
The kingdom emerged from a merger of four port polities in the Lower Chao Phraya Valley: Lopburi (Lavo), Suphanburi (Suphannabhum), Ayodhya, and Phetchaburi (Phrip Phri). The conventional founding date of 1351 marks when Uthong V united these polities and relocated the capital to an island at the confluence of the Chao Phraya, Lopburi, and Pa Sak rivers. The name Ayutthaya derives from Ayodhya, the holy city of the Hindu epic Ramayana. Archaeological evidence indicates the site was a substantial town before 1351.

### Early Maritime Dominance and Transition
For its first two centuries, Ayutthaya was a maritime-oriented confederation, conducting raids and demanding tribute from Malay principalities down to Sumatra. Its political integrity was maintained through familial connections under the mandala system, where city rulers swore allegiance to the king but retained privileges. In the 15th and 16th centuries, the kingdom transitioned to a hinterland state, absorbing the Northern Cities and shifting focus to inland trade routes with China. King Borommatrailokkanat (r. 1448–1488) centralized the government with the Chatusadom system and established the sakdina social hierarchy.

### Warfare and Independence
From the mid-16th century, Ayutthaya faced repeated invasions from Burma's Taungoo dynasty. It was a Burmese vassal from 1569 to 1584. King Naresuan restored independence and later expanded Siamese territory. The period after 1600 saw a decline in large-scale warfare, allowing for a prolonged era of peace and commerce.

### Golden Age and International Trade
The 17th and 18th centuries were a "golden age" of Siamese culture and international trade. Ayutthaya became a middleman for trade between global empires, including Ming/Qing China, the Mughal Empire, and European powers. The reign of Narai (1657–1688) was notable for Persian and French influence. After the 1688 revolution expelled most Europeans, trade with China grew dramatically, introducing capitalism and stimulating the economy. This period saw the rise of a wealthy commoner class and the decline of traditional labor control systems.

### Fall and Aftermath
Internal weaknesses, including chronic succession struggles and a failing corvée system, undermined the kingdom's defense. The militaristic Konbaung dynasty of Burma invaded in 1759–1760 and again in 1765–1767. After a 14-month siege, Ayutthaya fell in April 1767 and was completely destroyed. The capital was later re-established at Thonburi by Phraya Taksin, who reunified Siam within a few years.

## Terms
- **Mandala system**: A political model common in Southeast Asia where a central ruler held hegemony over a network of semi-autonomous city-states bound by loyalty and tribute.
- **Sakdina system**: A social hierarchy codified in the 15th century where the king granted control over land and the labor of commoners (phrai) to officials in exchange for service.
- **Phrai**: Commoners or serfs registered for corvée labor and military service under the sakdina system.
- **Chatusadom**: The "Four Pillars" system of central government with two prime ministers (Samuha Nayok and Samuha Kalahom) and four ministries.
- **Uparaja**: A viceroy or prince, usually the king's senior son or brother, a position that often led to succession conflicts.
- **Ramakien**: Thailand's national epic, adapted from the Hindu Ramayana, which heavily influenced Ayutthayan art, drama, and the concept of divine kingship.
- **Prang**: A towering, corn-cob-shaped Buddhist temple structure representing Mount Meru, characteristic of Ayutthayan architecture.
- **Corvée system**: A labor system requiring commoners to provide unpaid service to the state or their assigned lord.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Origins and Founding**: The identity and origins of the founder, Uthong V, are disputed, with chronicles offering multiple conflicting legends. Scholars also debate whether the 1351 date marks a true foundation or a relocation of an already significant settlement.
- **Nature of Expansion**: Traditional narratives emphasize territorial conquest, while modern scholarship suggests Ayutthaya expanded primarily through political merger and consolidation of existing city-states.
- **Decline and "General Crisis"**: The hypothesis that Ayutthaya declined after the 1688 expulsion of Europeans has been largely refuted by recent studies. Historians like Lieberman, Baker, and Phongpaichit argue the 18th century was a period of economic vitality driven by Chinese trade, not decline.
- **Fall in 1767**: The extent of destruction and depopulation is debated, with some historians suggesting the kingdom's social and economic structures had already collapsed internally before the walls fell.
