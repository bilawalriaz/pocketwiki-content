# Thomas Hobbes

## Overview
Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679) was an English philosopher and political theorist, a founder of modern political philosophy. His 1651 masterpiece, *Leviathan*, presents a foundational theory of the social contract, arguing that an absolute sovereign is necessary to prevent the chaos of a "state of nature." His work, shaped by the English Civil War, profoundly influenced the understanding of political structure and human nature.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Hobbes was born prematurely in 1588. After his father left following a fight, he was raised by his wealthy uncle. He was educated at local schools and then at Magdalen Hall, Oxford, where he studied scholastic logic and mathematics. In 1608, he incorporated his B.A. at Cambridge and began a lifelong connection with the Cavendish family as a tutor. A grand tour of Europe (1610–1615) exposed him to scientific methods, contrasting with his Oxford scholasticism. His first major scholarly work was a 1628 English translation of Thucydides.

### Intellectual Development and Exile
After returning to England in 1637, Hobbes wrote *The Elements of Law* (1640) amid rising political tensions. Fearing disfavor after the Long Parliament assembled, he fled to Paris in November 1640. There, he engaged with philosophical circles, critiqued Descartes, and completed *De Cive* (1641), the third section of his planned philosophical system. The English Civil War (1642–1651) revitalized his political interests, leading to the wider distribution of *De Cive* and the composition of his masterwork.

### *Leviathan* and Political Theory
Hobbes's political theory, most fully expressed in *Leviathan* (1651), was a response to civil war. He argued that without government, humans exist in a "state of nature"—a "war of all against all" where life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." To escape this, individuals enter a social contract, surrendering their natural rights to an absolute sovereign in exchange for security and order. This sovereign's power is indivisible and cannot be resisted, as it derives from the people's own authorization. Hobbes rejected Aristotle's view that humans are naturally suited for political life, instead seeing the state as a human invention for convenience and survival.

### Later Life and Controversies
After returning to London in 1651, Hobbes lived under the protection of the new government. He published the remaining parts of his philosophical system: *De Corpore* (1655) and *De Homine* (1658). Following the Restoration of Charles II in 1660, Hobbes received a pension but faced accusations of atheism. A 1666 bill against atheism targeted *Leviathan*, and he was thereafter barred from publishing on human conduct in England. He spent his final years with the Cavendish family, producing translations of Homer and an autobiography. He died in 1679.

## Terms
- **Social Contract Theory**: The idea that individuals consent to surrender some freedoms to a ruler or government in exchange for protection and maintenance of social order.
- **State of Nature**: A hypothetical condition of humanity without government or laws, which Hobbes characterized as a state of perpetual conflict.
- **Leviathan**: The title of Hobbes's 1651 book and his metaphor for the all-powerful sovereign state created by the social contract.
- **Absolute Sovereign**: The undivided and supreme authority in a state, whose power is necessary to prevent civil war and whose commands must be obeyed.
- **De Cive**: Latin for "On the Citizen," the third and first-published section of Hobbes's philosophical trilogy, focusing on civil philosophy.
- **De Corpore**: Latin for "On the Body," the first section of Hobbes's trilogy, dealing with physics and the nature of bodies.
- **De Homine**: Latin for "On Man," the final section of the trilogy, focusing on human nature and optics.
- **Hobbism**: A term used to describe Hobbes's philosophy, often used pejoratively by his contemporaries to denote materialism and atheism.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Religious Views**: Hobbes's exact religious beliefs are debated. He was accused of atheism, but he defended himself and argued for a corporeal God. Scholars disagree on whether he was a secret atheist, a deist, or an unorthodox Christian.
- **Influence of the Civil War**: While the war provided the immediate context for *Leviathan*, scholars note that many core elements of his political thought were already present in *The Elements of Law* (1640), suggesting his methodology was largely pre-formed.
- **The Nature of the Sovereign**: There is debate over whether Hobbes's absolute sovereign is a single person, an assembly, or a democratic body, and whether his theory ultimately supports monarchy or is compatible with other forms of government.
