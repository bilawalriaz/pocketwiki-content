# Masonry

## Overview

Masonry is the ancient craft of building structures using units such as stone, brick, concrete blocks, or glass, bound together with mortar. It is one of the oldest construction methods, predating written history, and remains widely used for both structural and non-structural applications. Its durability, fire resistance, and thermal mass make it valuable in modern construction, though its weight and poor tensile performance require careful design, especially in seismic zones.

## Timeline

- **Prehistoric** — Masonry predates written history; early structures rely on self-weight for stability
- **c. 2600 BC** — Egyptian pyramids constructed using stone masonry
- **Roman era** — Roman aqueducts demonstrate advanced masonry engineering
- **Medieval period** — Gothic cathedrals showcase refined stone masonry techniques
- **Mid-20th century onward** — Steel-reinforced masonry elements introduced for modern tall, light buildings

## Body

### Materials and Techniques

Masonry units include bricks, building stone (granite, marble, limestone), cast stone, concrete blocks, glass blocks, and adobe. The durability of masonry depends heavily on material quality, mortar, workmanship, and assembly patterns. A mason (or bricklayer) constructs masonry; it is classified as a construction trade.

### Structural and Non-Structural Applications

Masonry serves both structural roles—walls, columns, beams, foundations, load-bearing arches—and non-structural ones such as fireplaces, chimneys, and veneer systems. Brick and concrete block are most common in industrialized nations and may be load-bearing or non-load-bearing. Hollow-core concrete blocks offer high compressive strength and can be filled with concrete or rebar to improve tensile and lateral resistance.

### Advantages and Limitations

Masonry increases thermal mass, is non-combustible, and resists projectile impact from storms. However, freeze-thaw cycles can degrade it, it is heavy and requires stable foundations, and it is labor-intensive compared to concrete. Masonry walls rely on weight for stability and perform poorly in earthquakes due to weak lateral resistance and minimal connection between units.

### Dry Set and Stonework

Dry set masonry relies on friction between interlocking blocks, sometimes enhanced by surface grooves, and may omit mortar entirely. Stonework uses dressed stones (ashlar masonry) or irregular stones (rubble masonry), laid coursed or uncoursed. Slipform stonemasonry combines reinforced concrete with rubble stone faces. Natural and manufactured stone veneers are applied over concrete or CMU walls for appearance.

### Brick Construction

Solid brick walls consist of multiple wythes of stretcher and header bricks bound in courses. Bond patterns—common, English, and Flemish—vary in strength and insulation. Staggered bonds are stronger and less crack-prone. Modern trends favor rustic appearances using burnt, salvaged, or artificially aged bricks. Serpentine (crinkle-crankle) walls follow curved paths, offering greater stability and sometimes allowing single-wythe unreinforced construction.

### Concrete Masonry Units (CMUs)

CMUs include cinder blocks, concrete blocks, and hollow tiles. They are larger than bricks, faster to lay, and have low water absorption. Voids can be filled with concrete and rebar for reinforcement, particularly at corners and openings. Bond beams and ladder reinforcement enhance lateral and tensile strength. Architectural CMUs offer textured finishes (split-face, ground-face, scored) and color options. Glazed CMUs provide smooth, impervious surfaces.

### Specialty Forms

Glass blocks provide translucent or clear vision. Masonry veneer walls use brick or stone facing attached to a structurally independent wall via brick ties, with an air gap and drainage. Gabions are wire baskets filled with stone, used for retaining walls; they are flexible and well-drained but limited by wire corrosion. Masonry provides passive fire protection, withstanding temperatures up to 1,000 °F (538 °C) for up to four hours and containing fires 93% of the time.

### Mechanical Behavior

Masonry is a highly compressed material with a large strength differential between compression and tension. Loads do not distribute evenly as in elastic materials but concentrate along stiff load paths, requiring specialized modeling approaches.

## Terms

- **Wythe**: A vertical layer of masonry units, such as brick or block, in a wall.
- **Course**: A horizontal row of masonry units laid in a single mortar joint.
- **Ashlar masonry**: Stonework using dressed, shaped stones cut to regular sizes.
- **Rubble masonry**: Stonework using irregularly shaped stones.
- **Bond**: The pattern in which masonry units are laid, such as English or Flemish bond.
- **CMU (Concrete Masonry Unit)**: A standard concrete block, including cinder and hollow tile variants.
- **Brick tie**: A metal strip connecting a masonry veneer to a structural wall.
- **Bond beam**: A reinforced horizontal element tying masonry walls together, often at the top course.
- **Gabion**: A wire basket filled with stone, used as a flexible retaining structure.
- **Endothermic effect**: The ability of masonry materials to absorb heat through chemical or physical processes, slowing fire spread.

## Debates and Open Questions

The seismic vulnerability of unreinforced masonry remains a major concern, with ongoing research into retrofit methods and reinforcement strategies. The balance between aesthetic rusticity and structural uniformity in brickwork continues to evolve. Additionally, the long-term durability of gabions in corrosive environments depends on wire material selection, which remains a subject of engineering evaluation.
