# Panama Canal

## Overview
The Panama Canal is an 82-kilometer artificial waterway in Panama that connects the Caribbean Sea (Atlantic Ocean) with the Pacific Ocean. It is a critical conduit for global maritime trade, significantly reducing travel time and distance by allowing ships to avoid the lengthy and hazardous route around the southern tip of South America. Its construction was one of the largest and most difficult engineering projects ever undertaken, and its operation has decisively influenced global trade patterns and economic growth.

## Timeline
- **1513** — Spanish conquistador Vasco Núñez de Balboa crosses the Isthmus of Panama, noting the possibility of a canal.
- **1881** — France, led by Ferdinand de Lesseps, begins construction of a sea-level canal.
- **1889** — French effort goes bankrupt after spending US$287 million and losing over 22,000 workers to disease and accidents. |
- **1903** — With U.S. support, Panama declares independence from Colombia. The Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty grants the U.S. rights to build and control the canal.
- **1904** — The United States formally takes control of the canal project.
- **1914** — The canal opens on August 15 with the passage of the SS *Ancon*.
- **1977** — The Torrijos–Carter Treaties are signed, providing for the gradual transfer of canal control to Panama.
- **1999** — The Panamanian government assumes full control of the canal.
- **2006** — A national referendum approves a $5.25 billion expansion project. |
- **2016** — The expanded canal with a third set of locks opens for commercial traffic on June 26.

## Body

### Early Proposals and French Attempt
The idea of a canal across Panama dates to the 16th century. In 1881, a French company led by Ferdinand de Lesseps began construction, aiming for a sea-level canal like the Suez Canal. The project was plagued by immense engineering challenges, including the Chagres River's flooding and the Culebra Cut's unstable geology. Tropical diseases, primarily yellow fever and malaria, killed an estimated 22,000 workers. The effort collapsed financially in 1889.

### U.S. Acquisition and Construction
The United States, under President Theodore Roosevelt, took over the project in 1904 after purchasing the French assets and rights. The U.S. adopted a lock-and-lake design, championed by chief engineer John Frank Stevens, which used the dammed Chagres River to create the artificial Gatun Lake. A massive sanitation campaign led by Colonel William C. Gorgas nearly eliminated mosquito-borne diseases. The canal was completed in 1914 under chief engineer George Washington Goethals, at a cost of almost $500 million.

### Operation, Expansion, and Transfer
The U.S. controlled the canal and the surrounding Panama Canal Zone until the Torrijos–Carter Treaties mandated the transfer of control to Panama, which was completed in 1999. The Panama Canal Authority (ACP) now manages the waterway. To accommodate larger "Neopanamax" ships and increase capacity, a major expansion was completed in 2016, adding a third, wider lane of locks. The canal is a vital revenue source for Panama but faces challenges from drought-induced low water levels in Gatun Lake, its primary freshwater source.

## Terms
*   **Panamax:** The maximum size of ship that can transit the original locks, defined by a width of 33.5 meters (110 ft).
*   **Neopanamax:** The larger size of ship that can transit the expanded canal's new locks, with a beam up to 49 meters (160 ft) and draft up to 15 meters (49 ft).
*   **Gatun Lake:** An artificial freshwater lake, created by damming the Chagres River, that forms a key part of the canal's transit route and provides water for the locks.
*   **Culebra Cut (Gaillard Cut):** The 12.5-kilometer channel excavated through the continental divide to connect Gatun Lake to the Pacific-side locks.
*   **Locks:** Enclosed chambers with gates that raise and lower ships between sea level and the level of Gatun Lake.
*   **PC/UMS ton:** Panama Canal/Universal Measurement System ton, a unit of volume (100 cubic feet) used to calculate tolls for most vessels.
*   **TEU:** Twenty-foot Equivalent Unit, a standard unit of cargo capacity based on the volume of a 20-foot-long intermodal container.
*   **Torrijos–Carter Treaties:** The 1977 agreements that provided for the gradual transfer of the Panama Canal from U.S. to Panamanian control.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Water Scarcity:** The canal's operation depends on freshwater from Gatun Lake. Severe droughts, potentially linked to climate change, have forced restrictions on ship transits, threatening the canal's reliability and revenue.
*   **Geopolitical Sovereignty:** The canal's history is marked by foreign control and intervention. Recent comments by U.S. political figures suggesting a reassertion of control have been firmly rejected by Panama, reaffirming the canal as Panamanian "inalienable patrimony."
*   **Environmental Impact:** The canal's construction and operation have caused deforestation, facilitated the spread of invasive species between oceans, and contributed to water and air pollution.
*   **Competition:** The canal faces potential competition from alternative routes, including the Suez Canal, the proposed Nicaragua Canal (now largely defunct), and the Interoceanic Corridor of the Isthmus of Tehuantepec in Mexico.
