# Chalcolithic

## Overview
The Chalcolithic, also known as the Copper Age or Eneolithic, is an archaeological period defined by the increasing use of smelted copper tools and weapons, though stone remained the dominant material. It marks a critical technological transition between the Neolithic (New Stone Age) and the Bronze Age, representing humanity's first steps into metallurgy. The period occurred at different times across the globe and was absent in some regions, highlighting the non-linear nature of technological adoption.

## Timeline
- **c. 6500 BC** — Cold-working (hammering) of near-pure copper begins, e.g., in North America's Great Lakes Old Copper complex.
- **c. 5500 BC** — Oldest securely dated evidence of copper-making found at Prokuplje, Serbia.
- **c. 5000 BC** — World's oldest securely dated evidence of high-temperature copper smelting at Belovode, Serbia.
- **c. 4650 BC** — Early tin bronze foil discovered at Pločnik, Serbia, indicating independent European bronze development.
- **c. 4200 BC** — Arsenical copper/bronze production documented at Norşuntepe and Değirmentepe in eastern Turkey.
- **5th–3rd millennium BC** — Transition from Copper Age to Bronze Age occurs in Europe and the Ancient Near East.
- **c. 2500–2200 BC** — Chalcolithic period in Britain, marked by first copper/gold objects and Bell Beaker culture immigration.
- **3000–2500 BC** — Independent copper smelting develops in the Aïr Mountains, Niger.

## Body

### Terminology and Definition
The period has multiple names due to evolving scholarly definitions. The term "Copper Age" was proposed by Ferenc Pulszky in the 1870s to insert a phase between the Stone and Bronze Ages. John Evans later distinguished a transitional "Copper Age" from the Bronze Age proper. The name "Eneolithic" (from Italian *eneo-litica*) was introduced in 1884, and "Chalcolithic" (from Greek *khalkos* for copper and *lithos* for stone) was adopted around 1900 to avoid confusion. Today, all three terms are used synonymously.

### Technological and Social Change
The Chalcolithic is characterized by the shift from using native copper to smelting it from ore. This new technology did not immediately replace stone tools but led to changes in craft specialization. In regions like the Tehran Plain, the introduction of copper working is linked to a decline in the quality and aesthetic variation of stone tools, suggesting the collapse of Neolithic specialized production networks and a shift to household-based manufacturing.

### Regional Developments
Metallurgy may have emerged first in the Near East, with early lead and copper smelting documented by the 7th–6th millennia BC. In Europe, copper artifacts appear from 4000 BC, with major smelting centers in Serbia. The Beaker culture is associated with spreading copper and bronze technologies across the continent. In South Asia, copper use is evident from 7000 BC at Mehrgarh, with distinct Chalcolithic farming cultures (e.g., Ahar, Malwa) flourishing later. The Americas saw independent copper working, notably the Old Copper complex in the Great Lakes (6500–1000 BC), though evidence for smelting is disputed. In East Asia, copper artifacts appear by the 5th millennium BC but saw limited early use.

## Terms
- **Chalcolithic**: From Greek *khalkos* (copper) and *lithos* (stone); a synonym for the Copper Age.
- **Cold Working**: Shaping metal by hammering without heating, an early copper-working technique.
- **Smelting**: Extracting metal from ore by heating it to high temperatures, a key Chalcolithic innovation.
- **Eneolithic**: Another term for the Copper Age, derived from Italian *eneo-litica*.
- **Bell Beaker Culture**: A European archaeological culture (c. 2800–1800 BC) associated with spreading copper metallurgy.
- **Arsenical Copper/Bronze**: An alloy of copper with arsenic, an early alternative to tin bronze.
- **Funnel Beaker Culture**: A Neolithic culture in northern Europe (c. 4300–2800 BC) that used copper artifacts.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The origins and spread of copper smelting are debated: was it invented independently in multiple regions (e.g., Europe and the Near East) or diffused from a single source?
- The extent to which the Chalcolithic represents a distinct "age" versus a transitional phase is contested, as seen in Britain where some archaeologists do not recognize a formal Chalcolithic period due to small-scale production.
- The social and economic impact of early metallurgy is debated, particularly whether it caused the collapse of specialized Neolithic craft networks or was integrated into existing systems.
