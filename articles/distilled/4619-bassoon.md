# Bassoon

## Overview
The bassoon is a woodwind instrument that plays in the tenor and bass ranges, known for its distinctive tone, wide range, and versatility. It is a non-transposing instrument with a conical bore, typically made of wood, and is a staple of orchestral, concert band, and chamber music. Its complex fingering system and unique sound production make it a challenging but expressive instrument.

## Timeline
- **Mid-16th century** — The dulcian, the bassoon's forerunner, is available in multiple sizes.
- **c. 1650s** — Martin Hotteterre is credited with developing the four-section baroque bassoon.
- **1680s** — Earliest extant bassoons by Johann Christoph Denner and Richard Haka appear.
- **c. 1700** — A fourth key (G♯) is added to the baroque bassoon.
- **First half of 18th century** — A fifth key (low E♭) is added.
- **1823** — Carl Almenräder publishes a treatise on improving the bassoon.
- **1831** — Almenräder and Johann Adam Heckel start a bassoon factory.
- **19th century** — The modern bassoon in its primary forms (Heckel and Buffet systems) emerges.
- **1913** — Igor Stravinsky's *The Rite of Spring* features a famous high-register bassoon solo.
- **1960s** — Giles Brindley develops the experimental "logical bassoon."

## Body

### Characteristics and Construction
The bassoon's range begins at B♭1 and extends over three octaves to roughly G5, though writing rarely exceeds C5 or D5. It is a non-transposing instrument, meaning written notes sound at their actual pitch. The instrument disassembles into six main pieces: the reed, bocal (a crooked metal tube), wing joint, boot (which folds over), bass joint, and bell. Its conical bore has a total sounding length of 2.54 m (8 ft 4 in), despite an overall height of 1.34 m (4 ft 5 in). Modern bassoons are typically made of maple, though student models may use polypropylene or ebonite.

### The Double Reed
Sound is produced by blowing air through a double reed made of *Arundo donax* cane, causing it to vibrate. Reed-making is a centuries-old art; advanced players often make and customize their own reeds. The process involves splitting cane, gouging, shaping, folding, binding with wire, and sealing with thread. The reed's final shape and adjustments significantly affect playability and tone.

### History and Development
The bassoon evolved from the dulcian, a 16th-century instrument with a similar double reed and conical bore. The modern bassoon was likely developed in the 1650s by Martin Hotteterre, who created a four-section design for greater accuracy. By 1700, it had four keys, and a fifth was added in the early 18th century. In the 19th century, increased demands for volume and virtuosity led to refinement. Carl Almenräder and Johann Adam Heckel developed the 17-key Heckel (German) system, which became the global standard. The Buffet (French) system, with a narrower bore and different keywork, is primarily used in France and Belgium.

### Use in Ensembles
Before 1760, the dulcian reinforced bass lines in wind consorts and opera orchestras. In the Classical era, composers like Mozart and Haydn exploited the bassoon's expressive and virtuosic capabilities. The Romantic era saw it used for both supportive bass lines and lyrical solos, with composers like Berlioz and Wagner writing for three or four bassoons. In the 20th century, its role expanded further, with Stravinsky's *The Rite of Spring* using its high register. The bassoon is also infrequently used in jazz and popular music, appearing in works by artists like Smokey Robinson and in film scores.

### Technique and Fingering
The bassoon is held diagonally and supported by a seat strap or harness. Its fingering system is complex, with the left thumb operating nine keys and the right thumb operating four. A unique technique called "flicking" involves momentarily pressing high keys to ensure clean slurs. The "whisper key" is held for low notes to prevent cracking. Embouchure (lip position and pressure) and breath control are critical for tone and intonation. Extended techniques include multiphonics, flutter-tonguing, and circular breathing. A "low A extension" can be added to the bell to reach notes below B♭1, as required by some composers like Wagner and Mahler.

## Terms
- **Bassoon**: A woodwind instrument in the tenor and bass ranges, known for its double reed and conical bore.
- **Dulcian**: The 16th-century forerunner of the bassoon, with a similar double reed and conical bore.
- **Heckel (German) system**: The dominant modern bassoon design, with 17 keys and a wide, singing tone.
- **Buffet (French) system**: An alternative bassoon design with a narrower bore and 22 keys, offering a more vocal quality.
- **Double reed**: A pair of cane blades vibrated together to produce sound, used in bassoons and oboes.
- **Non-transposing instrument**: An instrument where written notes correspond to the actual pitches sounded.
- **Flicking**: A bassoon technique where the left thumb momentarily presses high keys to ensure clean slurs.
- **Whisper key**: A key on the bassoon held for low notes to prevent them from cracking into a higher octave.
- **Embouchure**: The way a player applies their lips, facial muscles, and teeth to the mouthpiece or reed to control tone.
- **Extended techniques**: Unconventional playing methods, such as multiphonics or flutter-tonguing, used for special effects.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact origins of the bassoon are debated; while Martin Hotteterre is often credited, some historians believe it was developed by several French craftsmen in the 1650s.
- The relative merits of the Heckel and Buffet systems are a matter of preference and regional tradition, with each having distinct tonal and technical characteristics.
- The use of the bassoon in jazz and popular music remains infrequent, and its role in these genres is not firmly established.
