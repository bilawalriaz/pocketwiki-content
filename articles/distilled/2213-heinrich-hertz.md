# Heinrich Hertz

## Overview
Heinrich Hertz (1857–1894) was a German physicist whose experimental work conclusively proved the existence of electromagnetic waves, confirming James Clerk Maxwell's theoretical equations. His discovery laid the foundation for all modern wireless communication, from radio to Wi-Fi, and he also made significant contributions to contact mechanics and the photoelectric effect.

## Timeline
- **22 February 1857** — Born in Hamburg, Germany.
- **1880** — Obtained Ph.D. from the University of Berlin.
- **1885** — Became a full professor at the University of Karlsruhe.
- **1886** — Married Elisabeth Doll; began experiments leading to the proof of electromagnetic waves.
- **1887** — Published observations on the photoelectric effect and electromagnetic waves.
- **1888** — Published papers demonstrating transverse electromagnetic waves traveling at the speed of light.
- **1889** — Became professor of physics at the University of Bonn.
- **1 January 1894** — Died in Bonn, aged 36.
- **1930** — The SI unit for frequency, the hertz (Hz), was named in his honor.

## Body

### Proof of Electromagnetic Waves
James Clerk Maxwell's 1864 theory predicted electromagnetic waves but had not been experimentally proven. Hertz, while experimenting with electrical coils in 1886, noticed induced sparks, giving him an idea for an apparatus. Using a dipole antenna as a transmitter and a resonant loop as a receiver, he generated and detected what are now called radio waves. Between 1887 and 1889, he conducted a series of experiments, measuring the waves' properties—such as reflection, polarization, and velocity—and proved they traveled at the speed of light. This confirmed Maxwell's theory and established that light is a form of electromagnetic radiation. Hertz did not foresee the practical applications of his discovery, which soon led to the development of radio communication by others like Guglielmo Marconi.

### Other Scientific Contributions
Hertz's work extended beyond electromagnetic waves. In 1883, he conducted early experiments on cathode rays, though his interpretation of their electrical neutrality was later corrected by J.J. Thomson. He also helped establish the photoelectric effect in 1887, observing that ultraviolet light could cause a charged object to lose its charge more readily, though he did not explain the phenomenon. Earlier, in 1881-1882, he published foundational work on contact mechanics, analyzing how elastic solids behave under load. This theory, while neglecting adhesion, became a cornerstone for later models in nanotechnology and tribology. He also described the "Hertzian cone," a fracture pattern in brittle materials.

### Philosophy and Legacy
Hertz's 1894 book, *The Principles of Mechanics*, presented a new formulation of mechanics based on space, time, and mass, arguing against the Newtonian concept of force. This work influenced philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein's "picture theory" of language. Despite his early death, Hertz's legacy is immense. The unit of frequency, the hertz (Hz), is named for him. His family faced persecution under the Nazi regime due to their Jewish ancestry, but his scientific impact endures through numerous institutes, medals, and the fundamental role of his discovery in modern technology.

## Terms
- **Electromagnetic waves**: Waves consisting of oscillating electric and magnetic fields that propagate through space, as predicted by Maxwell's equations.
- **Dipole antenna**: An antenna made of two straight conductors, used here to transmit electromagnetic waves.
- **Photoelectric effect**: The emission of electrons from a material when light shines on it, observed by Hertz but explained later by Einstein.
- **Contact mechanics**: The study of the deformation of solids that touch each other at one or more points.
- **Hertzian cone**: A conical fracture pattern produced in brittle materials by the transmission of stress waves from a point of impact.
- **Hertz (Hz)**: The SI unit of frequency, defined as one cycle per second, named in his honor.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that Hertz's early work on cathode rays was misinterpreted due to experimental conditions, a point clarified later by J.J. Thomson. His contact mechanics theory was later refined by models (JKR and DMT) that incorporated adhesion, which Hertz had neglected. The exact cause of his fatal illness was only identified decades after his death as likely granulomatosis with polyangiitis.
