# Nouakchott

## Overview
Nouakchott is the capital and largest city of Mauritania, serving as the nation's administrative and economic hub. Founded in 1958 as a planned city for the newly independent nation, it transformed from a small fishing village into a major Saharan metropolis due to rapid, often unplanned growth driven by drought and desertification. Its development highlights the challenges of rapid urbanization in an arid environment.

## Timeline
- **Pre-1958** — Nouakchott is a small fortified fishing village (ksar).
- **March 1958** — Construction begins to enlarge the village into a capital for independent Mauritania.
- **28 November 1960** — Mauritania gains independence; Nouakchott is the new capital.
- **1970s** — Severe drought triggers massive migration to the city, causing explosive, unplanned growth.
- **1976** — The city is attacked twice by the Polisario Front during the Western Sahara conflict.
- **1986** — A Chinese-built deepwater port opens.
- **1990** — Nouakchott is designated a region of Mauritania.
- **2009** — A government slum resettlement program begins.
- **2012** — The Sheikh Zayed solar power plant, considered Africa's largest, is completed.
- **2014** — The region is split into three administrative regions (wilayat).
- **2016** — Nouakchott–Oumtounsy International Airport opens, replacing the old airport.
- **2023** — The city's population is estimated at nearly 1.5 million.

## Body

### Founding and Early Growth
Nouakchott was selected as the capital by Mauritania's first president, Moktar Ould Daddah, to symbolize modernity and national unity, avoiding existing cities tied to specific ethnic or regional identities. Construction began in 1958 on a site chosen for its central location. The city was initially planned for 15,000 people, with a grid-like central business district. By the 1970s, new districts like the Cinquième Quartier had surpassed the original ksar in importance.

### Rapid Urbanization and Challenges
From the 1970s, Nouakchott experienced massive, unconstrained growth as hundreds of thousands fled rural drought and desertification. This influx led to the development of extensive slum areas, such as the kebbe, where cement buildings were often constructed overnight to avoid demolition. By the mid-1980s, the population was estimated between 400,000 and 500,000. The city is largely flat, with some areas below sea level, and faces daily threats from advancing sand dunes.

### Geography and Climate
Located on the Atlantic coast of the Sahara, Nouakchott's coastline features shifting sandbanks and areas of quicksand. The city is built around the tree-lined Avenue Gamal Abdel Nasser. It has a hot desert climate (Köppen: BWh) with hot days and cool winter nights, averaging 95 mm of rainfall annually. Climate change projections suggest that by 2050, its climate may resemble that of Khartoum.

### Government and Demographics
Nouakchott is divided into three administrative regions (wilayat), each containing three departments (moughataa). A directly elected regional council was established in 2018. The city's population was only 20,000 in 1969 but grew to 958,399 in the 2013 census. A government slum resettlement program, which began in 2009, relocated families to planned housing and improved services for over 181,000 people.

### Economy and Infrastructure
As the center of the Mauritanian economy, Nouakchott hosts three-quarters of the country's service sector enterprises. Its economy is heavily informal. Key infrastructure includes a deepwater port (expanded beyond its original 500,000-ton capacity) and the Nouakchott–Oumtounsy International Airport. Major roads connect the city to other parts of the country, and a tramway project was presented in 2022.

### Education and Culture
The city is home to the University of Nouakchott Al Aasriya (opened 1981) and other higher education institutions. Cultural attractions include the National Museum of Mauritania and the National Library. Mosques are extremely common, with the Saudi Mosque and Masjid Ibn Abbas being particularly notable. Nouakchott also hosts ten of the fourteen teams in the Mauritanian Super D1 football league.

## Terms
- **Ksar**: A fortified village, the traditional form of settlement in the region.
- **Desertification**: The process by which fertile land becomes desert, typically as a result of drought, deforestation, or inappropriate agriculture.
- **Kebbe**: A shanty town area in Nouakchott formed by displaced people, characterized by rapidly constructed cement buildings.
- **Wilaya**: An administrative region of Mauritania, led by a governor appointed by the central government.
- **Moughataa**: A department, a subdivision of a wilaya.
- **DWT (Deadweight Tonnage)**: A measure of how much weight a ship can safely carry, used to indicate a port's cargo capacity.
- **RCP 4.5**: Representative Concentration Pathway 4.5, a moderate climate change scenario where global warming reaches ~2.5–3 °C by 2100.
- **Informal economy**: Economic activity that is not taxed or monitored by the government, often involving unregistered businesses and transactions.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Population Estimates**: Official census figures (e.g., 134,000 in 1977, 393,325 in 1988) are considered probable undercounts due to the presence of a nomadic population and rapid, unplanned growth.
- **Slum Resettlement Success**: While the World Bank reported substantial success in improving services for over 181,000 people, initial concerns from aid agencies questioned whether necessary infrastructure could be put in place in receiving neighborhoods.
- **Climate Vulnerability**: The city's low-lying areas and threat from advancing sand dunes present ongoing environmental challenges, exacerbated by climate change projections.
