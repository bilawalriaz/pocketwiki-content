# Indus River

## Overview
The Indus is a major transboundary river of South and Central Asia, vital for the ecology, agriculture, and economies of the regions it flows through, particularly Pakistan. Its basin is home to ancient civilizations and remains a critical, yet contested, water source in the modern era.

## Timeline
- **c. 50 Million years ago** — Indian plate collides with Eurasian plate along the Indus-Yarlung suture zone.
- **c. 3300 BC** — Major cities of the Indus Valley Civilisation, like Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, are established.
- **2nd millennium BC** — The Punjab region is mentioned in the Rigveda as "Sapta Sindhu" and in the Avesta as "Hapta Həndu".
- **c. 515 BC** — Persian King Darius I sends Greek explorer Scylax of Caryanda to explore the Indus.
- **1819** — The Rann of Kutch earthquake causes the Indus to shift its course westwards.
- **1850** — The British East India Company introduces modern irrigation canals.
- **1960** — The Indus Waters Treaty is signed between India and Pakistan.
- **2010** — Severe monsoon flooding devastates large areas of Pakistan along the Indus.
- **2011** — Satellite images show the Indus re-entering India and feeding the Rann of Kutch after heavy rains.
- **2025** — India suspends the Indus Waters Treaty following a terrorist attack in Kashmir.

## Body

### Course and Geography
The Indus River rises in Tibet as the Sengge Zangbo ("Lion's Mouth") and flows northwest through Ladakh and Gilgit-Baltistan. It carves deep gorges near the Nanga Parbat massif before bending south into the Punjab plains. Its principal tributaries include the Zanskar, Shyok, Kabul, and the five rivers of Punjab (Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, Sutlej), which form the Panjnad River before joining the Indus. The river ends in a large delta in Sindh, Pakistan, and is one of the few rivers to exhibit a tidal bore. Its flow is seasonal, fed by Himalayan and Karakoram glaciers and snowmelt, and it swells during the monsoon.

### Geology and Formation
The Indus is an antecedent river, meaning it existed before the Himalayas and entrenched itself as the mountains rose. Its headwaters flow along the Indus-Yarlung suture zone, marking the ancient collision of the Indian and Eurasian plates. The river feeds the Indus submarine fan, the second-largest sediment body on Earth, primarily sourced from the Karakoram and Himalayan mountains. Geological evidence suggests the Indus was not connected to the Punjab rivers until about five million years ago, when they were captured from their eastward flow into the Ganga.

### Historical Significance
The river basin was the cradle of the Indus Valley Civilisation (c. 3300 BC), a major Bronze Age urban culture. It is referenced in ancient texts like the Rigveda and Avesta. The word "India" derives from the river's name. Historically, the Indus Valley served as a gateway for invasions and cultural exchanges, from the Persian Empire under Darius I and Alexander the Great's campaign to later Muslim and Mughal conquests.

### Economy and Modern Infrastructure
The Indus is the backbone of Pakistan's agriculture, especially in the Punjab and Sindh provinces, sustaining irrigation for crops like cotton, wheat, and sugarcane. The Indus Basin Irrigation System is the world's largest contiguous irrigation network. Major infrastructure includes the Tarbela and Mangla dams, and several barrages (e.g., Sukkur, Kotri). The 1960 Indus Waters Treaty governs water sharing with India, though it was suspended by India in 2025.

### Ecology and Wildlife
The river supports diverse ecosystems, from temperate forests to arid plains. It is home to the endangered Indus River dolphin, two otter species, and over 180 freshwater fish species, including 22 endemic ones. The Indus delta is a critical ecological region, though its health is threatened by reduced freshwater flow and silt deposition due to dams and irrigation.

### Modern Issues
Key challenges include the India-Pakistan water dispute, pollution from industrial and agricultural runoff, and the impacts of climate change. Glacial retreat in the Himalayas threatens long-term water security. The 2010 and 2011 floods caused massive destruction, highlighting the river's volatility. Large-scale water diversion has led to soil salinization and reduced flow to the delta, harming its ecosystem.

## Terms
- **Antecedent river**: A river that existed before the current landscape (like a mountain range) and maintained its course as the land changed.
- **Indus Valley Civilisation**: A major Bronze Age urban civilization (c. 3300-1300 BC) in the northwestern Indian subcontinent.
- **Indus Waters Treaty (IWT)**: A 1960 water-sharing treaty between India and Pakistan governing the use of the Indus system's rivers.
- **Panjnad River**: A river formed by the confluence of the five major tributaries of the Indus in Punjab (Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, Sutlej).
- **Sengge Zangbo**: The Tibetan name for the Indus River, meaning "Lion's Mouth".
- **Tidal bore**: A strong tide that pushes up a river against the direction of the current.
- **Submarine fan**: A large, fan-shaped deposit of sediment on the ocean floor, formed by material carried by a river.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Exact Source**: The traditional source is the Sênggê Kanbab spring, but alternative sources further upstream or a small lake northeast of Mount Kailash have been proposed.
- **River Length**: The length is variously cited as 2,880 km or 3,180 km, with a 2011 remeasurement suggesting ~3,600 km.
- **Future Water Security**: There are "nasty fears" that climate change-induced glacial melt could severely reduce the Indus's flow, with potentially catastrophic consequences for the region's population.
- **Treaty Stability**: The 2025 suspension of the Indus Waters Treaty by India introduces major uncertainty into the water-sharing arrangement between the two nuclear-armed nations.
