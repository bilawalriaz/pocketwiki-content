# Spring and Autumn period

## Overview
The Spring and Autumn period (c. 770–c. 481 BCE) was the first half of the Eastern Zhou dynasty, marked by the decline of Zhou royal authority and the rise of autonomous, warring states. It is a foundational era for Chinese philosophy, interstate diplomacy, and the transition from a feudal to a more bureaucratic political system, ending with the partition of the state of Jin.

## Timeline
- **771 BCE** — Quanrong invasion destroys Western Zhou capital; court moves east to Luoyi, beginning Eastern Zhou.
- **722 BCE** — Start of the *Spring and Autumn Annals* chronicle for the state of Lu.
- **707 BCE** — Duke Zhuang of Zheng defeats Zhou royal forces, first open defiance of the king.
- **685–643 BCE** — Duke Huan of Qi, with advisor Guan Zhong, becomes the first recognized hegemon.
- **632 BCE** — Duke Wen of Jin defeats Chu at Battle of Chengpu, becomes hegemon.
- **588 BCE** — Jin's army is split into six divisions controlled by noble families (the "Six Ministers").
- **584 BCE** — State of Wu enters Zhou politics, allied with Jin against Chu.
- **506–496 BCE** — King Helü of Wu, advised by Sun Tzu, conquers Chu's capital Ying.
- **497–453 BCE** — Civil war in Jin ends with its partition into Han, Wei, and Zhao, marking the start of the Warring States period.

## Body

### Background and Early Period (771–685 BCE)
The period began after the Western Zhou capital was sacked in 771 BCE. The relocated Zhou court held nominal power but controlled only a small territory. The old feudal (*fengjian*) system of royal relatives governing fiefdoms became irrelevant as these fiefdoms grew into independent states. A hierarchical alliance system emerged where the Zhou king granted the title of hegemon (*bà*) to the most powerful state's leader, who was obligated to protect weaker states and the Zhou from "barbarian" groups. The state of Zheng was the first to openly defy the Zhou king in 707 BCE.

### The Age of Hegemons (685–591 BCE)
Powerful states competed for the hegemonic title, which provided legitimacy to lead alliances and intervene in other states' affairs. Duke Huan of Qi (r. 685–643) was the first hegemon, using centralized reforms and the slogan "Revere the King, Expel the Barbarians." After Qi's decline, hegemony shifted among states like Jin, Song, and Chu. The southern state of Chu, considered semi-barbarian, expanded northward and its rulers claimed the title of "king," defying Zhou tradition. This era saw constant warfare and diplomacy among the major powers: Qin, Jin, Qi, and Chu.

### Late Period and Internal Strife (591–453 BCE)
As interstate conflict continued, internal power struggles intensified. In Jin, the duke's authority was usurped by six powerful aristocratic families (the "Six Ministers") in 588 BCE. Similar aristocratic takeovers occurred in other states. New powers emerged on the periphery: the state of Wu, allied with Jin, attacked Chu in 506 BCE, and later the state of Yue defeated Wu. The period's end is marked by the partition of Jin (497–453 BCE) among three ministerial families (Han, Wei, Zhao), which, along with the usurpation of Qi by the Tian family, began the Warring States period.

### Culture and Society
The era saw the systematization of noble ranks (e.g., *gong*, *hou*, *bo*, *zi*) into a coherent political hierarchy. A new class of lower-tier aristocrats, the *shi*, emerged. Key philosophical figures like Confucius, Laozi, and Sun Tzu were active, though their ideas were likely written down later. The *Spring and Autumn Annals*, a chronicle of Lu, is the standard historical source for the period. The *Zuo Zhuan* provides detailed accounts of diplomacy and warfare.

## Terms
- **Hegemon (霸, bà):** A powerful state leader granted authority by the Zhou king to lead alliances and maintain order.
- **Fengjian:** The early Zhou feudal system where royal relatives controlled fiefdoms; it became irrelevant as states grew autonomous.
- **Spring and Autumn Annals:** The official chronicle of the state of Lu (722–481 BCE), the primary source for the period.
- **Six Ministers:** The six noble families (Zhi, Zhao, Han, Wei, Fan, Zhonghang) that usurped power in Jin from 588 BCE.
- **Shi (士):** A class of lower-tier aristocrats or gentlemen who served as officials and scholars.
- **Mandate of Heaven:** The Zhou king's divine right to rule, which retained ritual prestige even as real power waned.
- **Zun wang rang yi:** The political slogan "Revere the King, Expel the Barbarians," used to justify hegemonic power.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **The Five Hegemons:** Traditional lists vary. One includes Duke Huan of Qi, Duke Xiang of Song, Duke Wen of Jin, Duke Mu of Qin, and King Zhuang of Chu. Another replaces Song and Qin with King Fuchai of Wu and King Goujian of Yue. Duke Mu of Qin and King Fuchai of Wu are sometimes excluded as they were not officially recognized by the Zhou king.
- **Authorship of the Classics:** Tradition credits Confucius with editing the Five Classics, including the *Spring and Autumn Annals*. Modern scholarship considers it unlikely one person produced all five, and the transmitted versions derive from later edits by Liu Xin.
- **Periodization:** The end date is debated; some sources use 403 BCE (the formal partition of Jin) or 453 BCE (the end of the Jin civil war) instead of 481 BCE.
