# Al-Andalus

## Overview
Al-Andalus refers to the Muslim-ruled territories on the Iberian Peninsula from 711 to 1492. It was a major center of cultural, scientific, and economic exchange between the Islamic and Christian worlds, and its legacy profoundly influenced the European Renaissance. The period ended with the completion of the Christian Reconquista and the fall of the Emirate of Granada.

## Timeline
- **711** — Tariq ibn-Ziyad leads the Muslim conquest of the Visigothic Kingdom, beginning the era of al-Andalus.
- **756** — Abd al-Rahman I establishes the independent Umayyad Emirate of Córdoba.
- **929** — Abd al-Rahman III declares himself Caliph, founding the Caliphate of Córdoba.
- **1031** — The Caliphate of Córdoba collapses, fragmenting into the taifa kingdoms.
- **1085** — Christian capture of Toledo prompts taifa rulers to seek Almoravid intervention.
- **1147** — The Almohad Caliphate overthrows the Almoravids and takes control of al-Andalus.
- **1212** — Christian coalition defeats the Almohads at the Battle of Las Navas de Tolosa.
- **1238** — The Nasrid Emirate of Granada is established, the last Muslim state in Iberia.
- **1492** — Muhammad XI (Boabdil) surrenders Granada to the Catholic Monarchs, ending Muslim rule.

## Body

### Political History
Al-Andalus began as a province of the Umayyad Caliphate after the 711 conquest. Internal revolts, particularly the Berber Revolt of 740, weakened central authority and allowed the Kingdom of Asturias to expand northward. In 756, the exiled Umayyad prince Abd al-Rahman I established an independent emirate in Córdoba, which reached its peak as a powerful caliphate under Abd al-Rahman III (r. 929–961). After the caliphate's collapse in 1031, al-Andalus fragmented into competing taifa kingdoms. These were eventually united under Berber empires from North Africa—the Almoravids (from 1085) and the Almohads (from 1147)—who intervened to halt Christian advances. Following the Almohad defeat at Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212, Christian kingdoms rapidly conquered most of the peninsula, leaving only the Nasrid Emirate of Granada. This final Muslim state survived as a tributary until its surrender in 1492.

### Science and Philosophy
Al-Andalus was a major intellectual hub. In medicine, Abu al-Qasim al-Zahrawi (d. 1013) authored a comprehensive encyclopedia, *Kitab al-tasrif*, which included influential surgical illustrations. The astronomer al-Zarqali (d. 1087) accurately calculated the motion of the solar apogee. Philosophers like Averroes (Ibn Rushd, d. 1198) and Ibn Tufail (d. 1185) critiqued Ptolemaic astronomy and promoted Aristotelian models. Their works, along with those of Jewish philosopher Maimonides (though he worked outside al-Andalus), were translated into Latin and significantly influenced medieval European thought and the Renaissance.

### Society and Culture
Society was stratified, with Arabs at the top, followed by Berbers, Muladies (Iberian converts to Islam), Mozarabs (Christians under Muslim rule), and Jews. While periods of tolerance existed, particularly under the Caliphate of Córdoba, non-Muslims paid the *jizya* tax and faced intermittent persecution, especially under the Almohads. Arabic became the dominant language, though Romance dialects (Mozarabic) persisted. Andalusian poetry innovated the strophic *muwaššaḥ* form, which influenced European troubadour lyrics. Architecture flourished, from the Great Mosque of Córdoba to the Alhambra palace in Granada, characterized by intricate geometric patterns, calligraphy, and water features. Agricultural advances, including new irrigation techniques and crops like sugarcane and citrus, transformed Iberian farming.

## Terms
- **Taifa**: A small, independent Muslim-ruled kingdom that emerged after the collapse of the Caliphate of Córdoba in 1031.
- **Reconquista**: The centuries-long process by which Christian kingdoms in northern Iberia gradually conquered Muslim-held territories.
- **Muladi (Muwallad)**: An Iberian native who converted to Islam.
- **Mozarab**: A Christian living under Muslim rule in al-Andalus, often adopting Arabic cultural traits while maintaining their faith.
- **Jizya**: A poll tax levied on non-Muslim adult males in an Islamic state in exchange for protection.
- **Ahl al-dhimma**: The "protected people," referring to non-Muslims (primarily Christians and Jews) living under Islamic rule.
- **Muqarnas**: A form of ornamented vaulting in Islamic architecture, resembling stalactites.
- **Acequia**: An irrigation channel, a technique introduced or expanded during Muslim rule.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The etymology of "al-Andalus" is debated. Traditional derivation from the "Vandals" is challenged by alternative theories linking it to "Atlantis," a Gothic term, or a pre-Roman substrate.
- The nature and degree of tolerance between religious communities in al-Andalus is a subject of scholarly debate, with varying interpretations of the treatment of *dhimmis* (protected non-Muslims).
- The extent to which irrigation systems in al-Andalus built upon or were independent of older Roman infrastructure is debated among historians.
