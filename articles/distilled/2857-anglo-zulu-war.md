# Anglo-Zulu War

## Overview
The Anglo-Zulu War (January–July 1879) was a conflict between the British Empire and the Zulu Kingdom in present-day South Africa. It was initiated by British High Commissioner Sir Bartle Frere to remove the Zulu as an obstacle to his plan for a British-led confederation in southern Africa, which aimed to secure cheap labour for mines and plantations. The war is notable for the shocking early Zulu victory at Isandlwana and the celebrated British defence at Rorke's Drift, ultimately ending with a British victory that dismantled the Zulu Kingdom.

## Body
### Background and Causes
By the 1870s, the British Empire sought to consolidate control over southern Africa. Lord Carnarvon, Secretary of State for the Colonies, proposed a confederation modeled on Canada, which would create a ruling white minority over a black majority to provide cheap labour. Sir Bartle Frere was appointed High Commissioner in 1877 to implement this plan. The independent Zulu Kingdom and Boer republics were seen as obstacles. Frere, along with Theophilus Shepstone (who had annexed the Transvaal in 1877), portrayed King Cetshwayo's Zulu army as a threat to regional peace. Despite the reluctance of the British government, which feared overextension, Frere manufactured a *casus belli* by exaggerating border incidents. In December 1878, he issued an ultimatum with demands—including the disbandment of the Zulu army and the acceptance of a British resident—that Cetshwayo could not accept without losing his throne.

### The Zulu Kingdom
The Zulu Kingdom had been built by Shaka Zulu through conquest. After Shaka's assassination in 1828, the kingdom faced conflict with migrating Boers, culminating in a major Zulu defeat at the Battle of Blood River (1838). Subsequent kings maintained complex relations with both Boers and British. Cetshwayo became king in 1872, reviving Shaka's military methods. His army was a militia armed primarily with the *iklwa* (thrusting spear) and cowhide shields, with limited and poor-quality firearms. Border disputes with the Transvaal, particularly over the Utrecht strip, were a persistent source of tension, with a British commission in 1878 ruling largely in the Zulus' favour.

### The War
The British invasion began on 11 January 1879 with three columns. On 22 January, the main Zulu army outmanoeuvred and annihilated the British centre column at the Battle of Isandlwana, a catastrophic defeat for the British. That night, a Zulu force attacked the border post at Rorke's Drift, but was repelled by a small garrison in a famous defence. The first invasion stalled, with other British columns besieged at Eshowe or forced to withdraw. The British regrouped and launched a second invasion in June 1879. After victories at Kambula and Gingindlovu, they advanced cautiously. On 4 July, the British decisively defeated the Zulu army at the Battle of Ulundi.

### Aftermath
Following Ulundi, the Zulu army dispersed and King Cetshwayo was captured in August. The British dismantled the kingdom, dividing it into thirteen chiefdoms under compliant leaders to prevent future unity. This led to internal strife and civil war. Cetshwayo was briefly restored in 1883 but was soon overthrown and died in exile. The Zulu Kingdom was formally annexed by Britain in 1887. The war's high British casualties dispelled notions of colonial invincibility and contributed to the defeat of Benjamin Disraeli's government in 1880.

## Terms
*   **Confederation Plan:** A British political scheme to unite southern African states under imperial control, creating a white-ruled dominion.
*   **Casus belli:** An act or event that provokes or justifies war.
*   **Impi:** A Zulu regiment or army unit.
*   **Iklwa:** A short, broad-bladed Zulu thrusting spear.
*   **Induna:** A Zulu officer or headman.
*   **Ultimatum:** A final set of demands, the rejection of which results in severe consequences, such as war.
*   **Protectorate:** A state that is controlled and protected by another.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Frere's Motives:** Scholars debate whether Frere acted with reckless ambition to force a war his superiors in London did not want, or if he genuinely believed a preemptive strike was necessary for colonial security.
*   **Cetshwayo's Strategy:** There is discussion about whether Cetshwayo sought war or was primarily defensive, aiming to preserve his kingdom's independence against British encroachment.
*   **Impact of Rorke's Drift:** The battle's fame, amplified by Victorian propaganda and later films, is often questioned as disproportionate to its strategic significance, serving more to distract from the disaster at Isandlwana.
