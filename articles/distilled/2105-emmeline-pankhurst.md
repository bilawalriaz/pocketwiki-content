# Emmeline Pankhurst

## Overview
Emmeline Pankhurst (1858–1928) was a British political activist who founded the Women's Social and Political Union (WSPU) and led its militant campaign for women's suffrage. Her leadership, marked by escalating tactics from protest to property destruction, was instrumental in securing the vote for women in 1918, though her methods remain historically debated.

## Timeline
- **15 July 1858** — Born in Manchester to politically active parents.
- **1879** — Marries barrister Richard Pankhurst, a women's suffrage advocate.
- **1889** — Co-founds the Women's Franchise League, advocating for all women's suffrage.
- **1893** — Joins the Independent Labour Party (ILP).
- **1894** — Elected as a Poor Law Guardian, witnessing harsh workhouse conditions.
- **1898** — Richard Pankhurst dies, leaving her with debt and new responsibilities.
- **10 Oct 1903** — Founds the Women's Social and Political Union (WSPU) with the motto "deeds, not words."
- **1905** — WSPU adopts militant tactics; daughter Christabel is arrested.
- **1908** — First imprisonment for obstruction; WSPU protests grow.
- **1912** — WSPU adopts arson and bombing as tactics; Pankhurst is imprisoned and force-fed.
- **1913** — Family rifts deepen; daughters Sylvia and Adela leave the WSPU.
- **Aug 1914** — Suspends suffrage militancy to support the British war effort in WWI.
- **1918** — Representation of the People Act grants votes to women over 30.
- **1917-18** — Transforms the WSPU into the Women's Party.
- **1926** — Joins the Conservative Party.
- **14 June 1928** — Dies, weeks before the Equal Franchise Act grants equal voting rights.

## Body

### Early Life and Political Awakening
Born into a family with a history of political agitation, Pankhurst was introduced to women's suffrage at age 14. She married Richard Pankhurst, a barrister and suffrage supporter, in 1879. After his death in 1898, she worked as a Poor Law Guardian, where the dire conditions in Manchester's workhouses radicalized her further. She became active in the ILP but grew frustrated with its lack of focus on women's suffrage.

### Founding the WSPU and Militant Campaign
Believing moderate tactics had failed, Pankhurst founded the all-female WSPU in 1903 to pursue direct action. The group's militancy escalated from heckling and window-smashing to hunger strikes and, later, arson and bombings. Pankhurst and other members were repeatedly imprisoned, where they endured force-feeding. The government responded with the "Cat and Mouse Act," temporarily releasing hunger-striking prisoners. The WSPU's tactics created a schism with more moderate suffragists and led to internal family conflicts, with her daughters Sylvia and Adela eventually leaving the organization.

### World War I and Political Shift
With the outbreak of WWI in 1914, Pankhurst and her daughter Christabel halted militant activities to support the war effort, organizing rallies and encouraging women to enter the workforce. This patriotic turn alienated pacifist members, including her other daughters. After the war, she transformed the WSPU into the Women's Party, which advocated for women's equality but also promoted anti-communism and the British Empire. In 1926, she joined the Conservative Party and was selected as a parliamentary candidate in 1927, a move that surprised many given her radical past.

### Legacy and Death
Pankhurst died on 14 June 1928, just weeks before the Equal Franchise Act granted voting rights to all women over 21. Her legacy is complex: she is celebrated as a pivotal figure in winning women's suffrage, but her autocratic leadership style and the violent tactics of the WSPU remain subjects of historical debate. A statue in her honor was unveiled in Victoria Tower Gardens in 1930.

## Terms
- **Suffragette**: A term coined by the press for militant women's suffrage activists, later adopted by the WSPU.
- **WSPU (Women's Social and Political Union)**: The militant organization founded by Pankhurst in 1903, dedicated to "deeds, not words."
- **Militancy**: The use of direct action, including property destruction and civil disobedience, to achieve political goals.
- **Cat and Mouse Act (1913)**: A law allowing the temporary release of hunger-striking prisoners to avoid their death in custody.
- **Force-feeding**: The traumatic practice of feeding hunger-striking prisoners via tubes, used against suffragettes.
- **Conciliation Bill**: A series of proposed laws in 1910-11 that would have granted limited women's suffrage, which the WSPU initially supported.
- **Representation of the People Act 1918**: The law that first granted voting rights to women over 30 who met property qualifications.
- **Equal Franchise Act 1928**: The law that granted voting rights to all women over 21 on equal terms with men.

## Debates and Open Questions
Historians disagree on whether the WSPU's militancy helped or hindered the suffrage movement. Some argue it raised public awareness and pressured the government, while others contend it alienated potential supporters and delayed progress. Pankhurst's political shift from socialist to conservative, her autocratic leadership of the WSPU, and her support for the British Empire and eugenic ideas also remain points of scholarly critique and debate.
