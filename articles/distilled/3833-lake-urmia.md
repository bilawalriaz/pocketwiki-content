# Lake Urmia

## Overview
Lake Urmia is an endorheic (closed-basin) salt lake in northwestern Iran, historically one of the largest saltwater lakes in the world. It is a critical ecological and cultural landmark, but has suffered dramatic shrinkage due to drought, damming, and water diversion, leading to severe environmental and social consequences. Its fluctuating fate illustrates the complex interplay between climate, human intervention, and regional politics.

## Timeline
- **c. 6000 BC** — Earliest settlement at Yanik Tepe on the lake's east shore.
- **9th century BCE** — First historical mention in Assyrian records from the reign of Shalmaneser III.
- **1970s** — Highway project across the lake initiated, later abandoned.
- **2008** — Urmia Lake Bridge completed, dividing the lake and restricting water flow.
- **Late 2017** — Lake shrinks to 10% of its former size due to drought and water management.
- **2019** — Dry spell broken; lake begins refilling from rain and water diversion.
- **Early 2020s** — Renewed acute drop in water levels due to drought and mismanagement.
- **September 2025** — NASA imagery shows the lake has almost entirely dried up.
- **Early 2026** — Massive rainfall and dam releases cause water levels to rise to a five-year high.

## Body

### Names and History
The lake's name has multiple proposed etymologies, including from Neo-Aramaic for "city of water" and Old Persian for "glittering." It is known locally as Daryâče-ye Orumiye in Persian and Kaputan tsov ("blue sea") in Armenian. Archaeological sites like Yanik Tepe and Se Girdan kurgans show human presence dating back to the 4th millennium BC. Assyrian records from the 9th century BCE mention peoples in the area, including the ancestors of the Medes and Persians.

### Chemistry and Infrastructure
Lake Urmia's water is hypersaline, with sodium and chloride concentrations about four times that of seawater. The 2008 Urmia Lake Bridge and causeway divided the lake, leaving only a 1.5 km gap that severely restricts water exchange between the north and south sections. This infrastructure, combined with drought, has increased salinity, particularly in the northern half, and accelerated the drying of the lake bed.

### Ecology and Environmental Crisis
The lake is a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve and Ramsar site, hosting diverse flora and fauna, including 226 bird species and the endemic brine shrimp *Artemia urmiana*, a key food source for migratory birds like flamingos. Severe shrinkage and increased salinity have devastated this ecosystem, eliminating fish populations and threatening the brine shrimp. Restoration efforts, including international projects and domestic programs like the Urmia Lake Restoration Program (ULRP), have aimed to improve water management and reduce agricultural consumption. Despite these efforts, the lake has repeatedly neared complete desiccation, most recently in 2025.

### Social and Political Response
The lake's decline has sparked significant public protest in Iran, despite restrictions on public assembly. Demonstrations in 2011, including at football matches, protested government inaction and proposed water transfers. The environmental crisis has drawn international attention, with recovery plans involving substantial funding and cooperation with organizations like the UNDP and FAO.

## Terms
- **Endorheic**: A closed drainage basin that retains water and allows no outflow to other external bodies of water.
- **Hypersaline**: Having a very high concentration of dissolved salts, significantly higher than seawater.
- **Artemia**: A genus of aquatic crustaceans (brine shrimp) that thrive in high-salinity environments.
- **Kurgan**: A type of tumulus or burial mound, common in ancient Eurasian cultures.
- **Ramsar site**: A wetland of international importance designated under the Ramsar Convention.
- **Biosphere Reserve**: An international designation by UNESCO for areas balancing conservation with sustainable use.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary debate centers on the relative causes of the lake's shrinkage: is it primarily due to natural climate cycles and drought, or is it predominantly the result of human activities like dam construction, river diversion, and agricultural water pumping? The effectiveness and management of large-scale restoration programs, such as the ULRP, are also subjects of ongoing scrutiny and discussion regarding their implementation and impact.
