# Leopold von Ranke

## Overview
Leopold von Ranke (1795–1886) was a German historian who founded modern source-based history by establishing the first historical seminar at the University of Berlin and mandating rigorous archival research using primary documents. He introduced standards of empiricism and narrative history focused on international politics (*Außenpolitik*), insisting history be written "as it actually happened" (*wie es eigentlich gewesen*). His work professionalized the discipline in the 19th century, though his Lutheran conviction that divine providence guided history—particularly the rise of the German Empire—drew criticism for theological bias. Later scholars, including E. H. Carr and Fernand Braudel, challenged his empiricism as naive for ignoring the historian’s role in selecting facts.

## Timeline
- **21 Dec 1795** — Born in Wiehe, Electorate of Saxony, into a family of Lutheran pastors and lawyers.
- **1814–1817** — Studies classics and Lutheran theology at Leipzig University; masters philology.
- **1817–1825** — Teaches classics at Friedrichs Gymnasium, Frankfurt an der Oder; turns toward history.
- **1824** — Publishes *Histories of the Latin and Teutonic Peoples (1494–1514)*, pioneering wide use of primary sources (diaries, dispatches, eyewitness accounts).
- **1825** — Appointed professor at University of Berlin; establishes first historical seminar teaching source criticism.
- **1829** — Publishes *The Serbian Revolution* using material from eyewitness Vuk Karadžić.
- **1832–1836** — Founds and edits *Historische-Politische Zeitschrift*, advocating conservative loyalty to the Prussian state.
- **1834–1836** — Publishes *The Popes of Rome, Their Church and State (16th–17th c.)*; coins term "Counter-Reformation."
- **1841** — Appointed Historiographer Royal to the Prussian court.
- **1843** — Marries Clarissa Helena Graves; they have three sons and one daughter.
- **1847–1848** — Publishes *Memoirs of the House of Brandenburg*, offending Prussian nationalists by portraying Prussia as a typical medium-sized state.
- **1852–1861** — Publishes *French History Mainly in the 16th and 17th Centuries* (5 vols.), praised for impartiality.
- **1854** — Lectures to future King Maximilian II of Bavaria: "every age is next to God," rejecting teleological progress.
- **1854–1857** — Publishes *History of the Reformation in Germany* using 96 volumes of ambassadorial correspondence.
- **1859–1867** — Publishes *History of England Principally in the 16th and 17th Centuries* (6 vols., expanded to 9 vols. by 1884).
- **1865** — Ennobled (addition of "von").
- **1871** — Retires; wife dies; becomes half-blind, relies on assistants.
- **1880** — Begins six-volume *World History*; reaches only 12th century before death.
- **23 May 1886** — Dies in Berlin at age 90; assistants complete *World History* to 1453 using his notes.

## Body

### Early Formation and Intellectual Foundations
Ranke’s upbringing in a Lutheran clerical family and education at Schulpforta and Leipzig University grounded him in philology, ancient languages, and theology. At Leipzig, he studied under Johann Gottfried Jakob Hermann and immersed himself in Thucydides, Livy, Niebuhr, Kant, and the German Idealists. He rejected contemporary history books as mere fact-collections, preferring the narrative art of ancient historians. His turn to history during his schoolmaster years (1817–1825) was driven by a desire for professionalized methodology and a theological conviction that God’s hand could be discerned in historical processes.

### Professionalization and the Berlin Seminar
Ranke’s 1824 debut work demonstrated an unprecedented range of primary sources—memoirs, diplomatic dispatches, government documents—moving beyond literary texts to mundane archival material. Impressed, the Prussian minister of education appointed him to the University of Berlin in 1825. There he instituted the **historical seminar**, a laboratory-style classroom where students learned *Heuristik* (source discovery) and *Quellenkritik* (source criticism) by analyzing actual documents. He dispatched students to newly opened archives across Europe (notably the Venetian Archives, accessed via Friedrich von Gentz and Klemens von Metternich) and built a research network that made Berlin the center of historical scholarship. Ranke aligned with Friedrich Carl von Savigny’s emphasis on the particularity of historical epochs against Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel’s universal teleology, arguing history could not be reduced to a single concept.

### Major Works and the Principle of *Wie es eigentlich gewesen*
Ranke’s output defined 19th-century political and diplomatic history. *The Popes of Rome* (1834–36), written without Vatican access but using Venetian and Roman private papers, coined "Counter-Reformation" and earned praise from Lord Acton for balance. His *French History* (1852–61) and *History of England* (1859–84) extended his reputation for impartiality. In his 1854 lectures, he articulated his anti-teleological creed: "every age is next to God"—each epoch is unique, equal before God, and must be understood on its own terms, not as a step toward modernity. His famous dictum *wie es eigentlich gewesen* ("how it actually happened") has been debated: some read it as a call for pure fact-recording; others (following Georg Iggers) argue *eigentlich* means "essentially," urging the historian to find the divine "Holy hieroglyph" behind the particulars.

### Conservative Politics and Theological Historiography
Ranke’s *Historische-Politische Zeitschrift* (1832–36) attacked liberalism and the French Revolution, arguing each state receives a unique moral character from God; Prussians should fulfill their state’s "idea." His *Memoirs of the House of Brandenburg* (1847–48) offended nationalists by treating Prussia as a normal German state rather than a destined great power. In *Zur orientalischen Frage* (1850s), he framed the Eastern Question as a religious conflict requiring Christian intervention for Ottoman Christians. For Ranke, Christianity was the unsurpassable moral apex; history revealed providence, not human progress.

### Later Years and Posthumous Legacy
After retiring in 1871, Ranke continued writing on German history (Wallenstein, Hardenberg, Frederick William IV) and began a universal *World History* (1880), incomplete at his death. Half-blind after his wife’s death, he dictated to assistants, reflecting that a historian must be old because "great events complete their course before his eyes." His 25,000-volume library was purchased by Syracuse University. His methods dominated Western historiography until the mid-20th century, when E. H. Carr denounced his empiricism as naive (historians choose facts) and Fernand Braudel replaced event-history with *histoire problème* (structural analysis). Walter Benjamin called Ranke’s dictum "the strongest narcotic of the [19th] century."

## Terms
- ****Primary source (empiricism)**** — Original, first-hand evidence (diaries, dispatches, eyewitness accounts) created at the time under study; Ranke made these the exclusive basis of historical writing.
- ****Historical seminar**** — A university teaching method pioneered by Ranke where students practice source criticism (*Quellenkritik*) and archival research (*Heuristik*) collaboratively under professor guidance.
- ****Quellenkritik (source criticism)**** — The systematic evaluation of a document’s authenticity, authorship, bias, and reliability before using it as historical evidence.
- ****Wie es eigentlich gewesen**** — Ranke’s guiding principle: "how it actually happened" (or "how it essentially was"); debated as either pure factual reconstruction or discernment of the epoch’s inner essence.
- ****Counter-Reformation**** — Term coined by Ranke (1834–36) for the Catholic Church’s 16th-century response to the Protestant Reformation, encompassing reform and reaction.
- ****Außenpolitik**** — "Foreign policy"; Ranke’s focus on international relations and diplomacy as the proper subject of political history.
- ****Teleological history**** — The view (associated with Hegel) that history unfolds toward a predetermined end or goal; Ranke rejected this, insisting each age is complete in itself.
- ****Göttingen school of history**** — Late-18th-century scholars (e.g., Schlözer, Gatterer) who emphasized critical source analysis and statistics; Ranke built on their methods.
- ****Historiographer Royal**** — Official court historian appointment; Ranke received this from the Prussian king in 1841.
- ****Holy hieroglyph**** — Ranke’s metaphor for the trace of divine providence in historical particulars, which the historian must seek while taking "pleasure in the particular."

## Debates and open questions
- **Meaning of *wie es eigentlich gewesen***: Does it demand value-free fact collection (positivist reading) or empathetic reconstruction of an epoch’s essential idea/God’s intent (idealist/romantic reading)?
- **Theology vs. empiricism**: Can Ranke’s Lutheran providentialism (history as manifestation of God’s will) be separated from his source-critical method, or does it predetermine his narrative choices (e.g., Prussia’s divine mission, Christianity’s moral superiority)?
- **Political conservatism**: Did his state-centric, anti-liberal framework (each state has a God-given "idea") compromise his claimed objectivity, particularly in *Memoirs of the House of Brandenburg*?
- **Legacy of "rankian" empiricism**: 20th-century critics (Carr, Braudel, Benjamin) argue his focus on political/diplomatic events and "facts" ignores structural forces, economic geography, and the historian’s inevitable subjectivity in selecting evidence. Is his method salvageable as a component of modern practice, or is it fundamentally outdated?
