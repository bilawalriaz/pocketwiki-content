# Terrorism

## Overview  
Terrorism is the use of violence against non-combatants to achieve political or ideological aims, often leveraging fear as a strategic tool. The term originated during the French Revolution’s Reign of Terror but gained global prominence in the 1970s. Despite its widespread use, no universally accepted legal definition exists due to its politically charged nature. Modern terrorism includes both non-state and state actors, with religious extremism increasingly dominating since 9/11.

## Timeline  
- **Late 18th century** — Term "terrorism" coined during French Revolution’s Reign of Terror
- **1858–1881** — Irish Republican Brotherhood initiates Fenian dynamite campaign
- **1878** — Narodnaya Volya founded in Russia; pioneers use of dynamite
- **1892–1894** — France enters Ère des attentats; rise of lone-wolf and mass terrorism
- **1970s** — Global attention to terrorism via PLO, IRA, ETA, Red Army Faction
- **1980s onward** — Surge in suicide attacks; typified by 9/11 (2001)
- **2001** — September 11 attacks mark new epoch in global terrorism
- **2013** — 80% of global terrorism deaths occur in Iraq, Afghanistan, Pakistan, Nigeria, Syria
- **2018** — UN adopts Code of Conduct Towards Achieving a World Free of Terrorism

## Body  

### Etymology and Definition  
The term "terrorism" derives from the French Revolution’s Reign of Terror under the Jacobins, led by Maximilien Robespierre, who declared, “Terror is nothing other than justice, prompt, severe, inflexible.” Edmund Burke later denounced the Jacobins for unleashing “hell-hounds called Terrorists.” The term resurged internationally in the 1970s due to groups like the PLO, IRA, and ETA. No universal definition exists; legal systems vary widely. U.S. Code §2331 defines it as acts intended to intimidate civilians or influence government policy. Scholars like Alex Schmid proposed “peacetime equivalents of war crimes,” but it was rejected. Over 109 definitions existed by 2006.

### Historical Evolution  
Early scholarship viewed terrorism as a 19th-century phenomenon until David C. Rapoport’s 1984 article highlighted its ancient roots in religious movements like the Thugs, Assassins, and Sicarii Zealots. Bruce Hoffman expanded this into the “New Terrorism” model, emphasizing religious over political motives. The late 19th century saw the shift from person-based to location-based attacks, exemplified by the Ère des attentats in France. Key incidents like the Liceu bombing and Café Terminus attack introduced mass, indiscriminate violence.

### Types and Motivations  
Terrorism varies by ideology: left-wing, right-wing, nationalist, religious, or state-sponsored. The U.S. Law Enforcement Assistant Administration classified it into categories including political, non-political, anonymous, quasi-, and state terrorism. Motivations include self-determination, ethnonationalism, environmentalism, supremacism, and religious imperatives. Personal factors include identity, exposure to violence, financial incentives, mental health issues, and social isolation. Lone wolves are often driven by religious beliefs (43%) or mental health disorders (32%).

### Tactics and Targets  
Terrorists target densely populated areas—transport hubs, malls, tourist sites—to maximize fear and media coverage. Stabbing attacks have reemerged in the 21st century, particularly among Palestinian and Islamic State militants. Media spectacle is central; groups exploit mass media and the internet to amplify messages. The 1968 El Al Flight 426 hijacking marked a turning point, combining civilian hostages with symbolic targeting for global attention.

### Perpetrators and Funding  
Perpetrators range from individuals to states. Non-state actors include Shining Path, IRA, FARC, and Hamas. State sponsors like the Soviet Union and Iran fund groups such as Hezbollah and Hamas. Funding sources include “revolutionary taxes,” kidnapping, smuggling, and fraud. The Financial Action Task Force monitors terrorist financing since 2001.

### Counterterrorism and Impact  
Responses include targeted laws, surveillance, military action, and diplomatic efforts. The U.S. employs two models: the Bush Doctrine (military focus) and the law enforcement model (cooperation and domestic security). Ending terrorist groups often involves negotiation, repression, or decapitation. Audrey Kurth Cronin identified six pathways: leader killing, political integration, goal achievement, public disillusionment, brute-force defeat, or reorientation.

### State and State-Sponsored Terrorism  
State terrorism involves governments using terror tactics, such as massacres or genocides. Critics cite WWII bombings and Cold War interventions. Kofi Annan argued state actions should be judged under existing international law rather than labeled “terrorism.” State-sponsored terrorism involves funding or harboring groups, though states rarely acknowledge this openly.

### Pejorative Use and Debate  
“Terrorism” is a pejorative term applied subjectively. “One man’s terrorist is another man’s freedom fighter” reflects this bias. Groups like the ANC and Menachem Begin were once labeled terrorists but later recognized as legitimate leaders. Media outlets often avoid the term to maintain neutrality. Legal frameworks struggle to distinguish activism from terrorism, especially in conflicts involving national liberation movements.

## Terms  
- **Asymmetric warfare**: Conflict where opposing forces differ significantly in power, with weaker parties using unconventional tactics.  
- **Decapitation**: Strategy of capturing or killing a group’s leader to dismantle its operations.  
- **Lone wolf**: Individual acting alone, often motivated by ideology or personal grievances.  
- **New Terrorism**: Model emphasizing religious motives and indiscriminate violence, developed by Bruce Hoffman.  
- **Propaganda of the deed**: Tactic of using dramatic acts to inspire supporters and draw attention.  
- **Quasi-terrorism**: Acts resembling terrorism but lacking intent to induce mass fear.  
- **Revolutionary tax**: Euphemism for protection money extorted by militant groups.  
- **Sacred terror**: Religious terrorism rooted in divine mandate, as described by Rapoport.  
- **State terrorism**: Government use of terror tactics against civilians for political control.  
- **Terrorism studies**: Academic field analyzing causes, impacts, and prevention of terrorism.

## Debates and Open Questions  
- **Definition**: No consensus on a universal legal definition due to political and moral subjectivity.  
- **Religious vs. secular motives**: Whether religious terrorism is fundamentally different from secular forms.  
- **Effectiveness**: Does terrorism achieve its political goals, or does it backfire?  
- **State responsibility**: Should state actions ever be classified as terrorism under international law?  
- **Counterterrorism ethics**: Balancing civil liberties with security measures in democratic societies.  
- **Root causes**: To what extent do poverty, mental illness, or ideology drive individuals to terrorism?  
- **Media role**: Does media coverage inadvertently encourage terrorism by amplifying messages?
