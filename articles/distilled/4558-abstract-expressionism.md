# Abstract expressionism

## Overview
Abstract expressionism was the first major American art movement to achieve international influence, emerging in New York City after World War II. It marked a shift from the social realism of the 1930s, combining emotional intensity with radical abstraction, and established New York as the new center of the Western art world, a role previously held by Paris.

## Timeline
- **1919** — The term "abstract expressionism" first used in Germany to describe German Expressionism.
- **1929** — Alfred Barr uses the term to describe works by Wassily Kandinsky.
- **1940s** — European modernist artists and critics flee to New York, bringing Surrealist and other avant-garde influences.
- **1943** — Barnett Newman lists key artists in the new movement, including Pollock, Rothko, and Gottlieb.
- **1946** — Art critic Robert Coates first applies the term "abstract expressionism" to American art.
- **1948** — Barnett Newman has his first solo exhibition.
- **1950-53** — Willem de Kooning paints his controversial *Woman* series.
- **1951** — The Ninth Street Show, a landmark exhibition of abstract expressionists, is held in New York.
- **1952** — Harold Rosenberg coins the term "action painting."
- **1953** — Helen Frankenthaler creates *Mountains and Sea*, a seminal stain painting.
- **1958** — Mark Tobey becomes the first American painter since Whistler to win the top prize at the Venice Biennale.
- **Early 1960s** — The cultural dominance of abstract expressionism in the U.S. begins to diminish.

## Body

### Origins and Influences
Abstract expressionism emerged from the convergence of European avant-garde ideas and a new generation of American artists in wartime New York. Surrealism was a key predecessor, with its emphasis on spontaneous and subconscious creation methods. Artists like Jackson Pollock drew on techniques from Surrealists André Masson and Max Ernst. The movement's name reflects its synthesis of the emotional intensity of German Expressionism with the anti-figurative aesthetics of European schools like Futurism, the Bauhaus, and Synthetic Cubism. Educators like Hans Hofmann were crucial in transmitting these modernist legacies to American artists.

### Style and Major Directions
The movement encompassed diverse styles, unified by large-scale canvases and an "all-over" compositional approach. It is broadly divided into two main directions. **Action painting**, a term coined by critic Harold Rosenberg, emphasized the canvas as an "arena in which to act," where the physical process of painting—the gesture, drip, and splash—was the subject. Key figures include Jackson Pollock, Willem de Kooning, and Franz Kline. **Color field painting**, championed by critic Clement Greenberg, focused on large, flat areas of color to create a unified, monolithic image. Artists like Mark Rothko, Barnett Newman, and Clyfford Still sought to eliminate superfluous rhetoric and present abstraction as an end in itself. Some artists, like Hans Hofmann and Robert Motherwell, worked in both modes.

### Critical Promotion and Cold War Context
The movement's rise was significantly aided by influential critics. Clement Greenberg promoted abstract expressionism, and Pollock in particular, as the culmination of modernist painting's pursuit of purity. Harold Rosenberg's writing defined the existential drama of action painting. The movement's cultural dominance during the 1950s coincided with the Cold War. Some historians argue the CIA covertly promoted abstract expressionism as a symbol of American freedom and individualism, contrasting with Soviet socialist realism. This view is contested by other scholars who question the extent and impact of such intervention.

### Legacy and Consequences
By the early 1960s, abstract expressionism's dominance waned, giving way to reactions against its emphasis on individualism and abstraction. Movements like Pop art and Minimalism emerged in direct response. However, its influence was profound and lasting, paving the way for Lyrical Abstraction, Neo-expressionism, and other late 20th-century movements. It established a new paradigm for artistic creation, where the process and the artist's inner expression became paramount.

## Terms
- **Abstract Expressionism:** An American post-WWII art movement characterized by large canvases, spontaneous or subconscious creation, and an emphasis on the act of painting itself.
- **Action Painting:** A style within abstract expressionism where the canvas is seen as an arena for the physical act of painting, emphasizing gesture and process.
- **Color Field Painting:** A style within abstract expressionism that uses large, flat areas of color to create a unified, monolithic image, often with spiritual or emotional resonance.
- **New York School:** The group of abstract expressionist artists, critics, and poets centered in New York City in the 1940s and 1950s.
- **Stain Painting:** A technique where thinned paint is poured or stained directly onto raw canvas, pioneered by artists like Helen Frankenthaler.
- **All-over Composition:** A compositional approach where the entire canvas is treated with equal importance, without a central focal point.
- **Surrealism:** An early 20th-century avant-garde movement that emphasized the unconscious mind, automatism, and dream imagery, a key influence on abstract expressionism.
- **Social Realism:** The dominant American art style of the 1930s, often depicting social and political themes, which abstract expressionism rebelled against.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Why did it gain mainstream acceptance in the 1950s?** One theory suggests that in the politically conservative McCarthy era, abstract art was seen as apolitical and therefore safe, unlike the overt social protest of earlier realism.
- **The role of the CIA:** A significant historical debate centers on whether the CIA covertly promoted abstract expressionism as a tool of Cold War cultural propaganda to symbolize American freedom. Some historians argue this was a major factor, while others contend the evidence is decontextualized or overstated.
- **Critical interpretation:** A fundamental divide existed between critics like Clement Greenberg, who focused on the formal "objectness" of the paintings, and Harold Rosenberg, who emphasized the existential "event" of their creation.
