# Napoleon

## Overview
Napoleon Bonaparte (1769–1821) rose from Corsican obscurity to become Emperor of the French, dominating Europe through a combination of military genius, administrative reform, and ruthless political consolidation. He stabilized post-revolutionary France, codified its laws in the Napoleonic Code, and exported revolutionary principles—equality before the law, meritocracy, secular administration—across the continent. His conquests dismantled the Holy Roman Empire and catalyzed German and Italian unification. However, his relentless expansionism triggered a cycle of coalitions that devastated Europe, caused an estimated six million deaths, and ended in catastrophic defeats in Russia (1812) and at Leipzig (1813) and Waterloo (1815). His legacy remains deeply contested: an enlightened modernizer who emancipated Jews and abolished feudalism, yet an authoritarian who censored the press, reinstated slavery, curtailed women’s rights, and ruled through plebiscites and propaganda.

## Body

### Early Life and Military Ascent
Born in Corsica one year after its cession to France, Napoleon was educated at French military schools (Brienne, École militaire), where his mathematical aptitude and intense application distinguished him. Commissioned in 1785, he embraced the Revolution, but his Corsican nationalism clashed with Pasquale Paoli, forcing his family to flee to France in 1793. His artillery command at the **Siege of Toulon** (1793) brought him to the attention of the Robespierres, earning a brigadier generalship. After a brief imprisonment following Robespierre’s fall, he saved the National Convention on **13 Vendémiaire** (5 October 1795) by using cannon against royalist insurgents, securing command of the Army of Italy.

### The Italian and Egyptian Campaigns (1796–1799)
In Italy, Napoleon demonstrated the operational speed and artillery concentration that defined his warfare. He knocked Piedmont out of the war in two weeks, then defeated four Austrian relief attempts at Mantua, culminating at **Rivoli** (January 1797). He forced the Treaty of Campo Formio, looted Venice, and extracted vast wealth. Returning a hero, he proposed an expedition to Egypt to threaten British India. The campaign (1798–99) yielded scientific triumphs (Rosetta Stone) but strategic failure: Nelson destroyed his fleet at the **Battle of the Nile**, his Syrian expedition failed at Acre, and plague decimated his army. Learning of French defeats in Europe, he abandoned his army and returned to Paris.

### The Consulate and Domestic Reform (1799–1804)
The **Coup of 18 Brumaire** (November 1799) installed Napoleon as First Consul under the Constitution of Year VIII, which concentrated power in his hands while maintaining republican forms. He centralized administration via **prefects**, created the **Council of State** to draft laws, established the **Banque de France**, and introduced the **metric system**. The **Concordat of 1801** reconciled the regime with the Catholic Church, while the **Organic Articles** asserted state control over the clergy. The **Napoleonic Code** (1804) entrenched equality before the law, property rights, and the abolition of feudalism, but restricted divorce and reduced women’s rights. He founded the **Legion of Honour** (1802) and the **lycée** system to create a meritocratic elite. A plebiscite made him Consul for Life (1802); another confirmed the hereditary Empire (1804).

### The Empire at War: 1805–1809
The breakdown of the **Treaty of Amiens** (1803) resumed war with Britain. Napoleon assembled the **Grande Armée** at Boulogne but abandoned the invasion after Villeneuve’s failure at Trafalgar. He pivoted east, destroying an Austrian army at **Ulm** and the Austro-Russian army at **Austerlitz** (December 1805), dissolving the Holy Roman Empire. In 1806, he crushed Prussia at **Jena–Auerstedt**, created the **Confederation of the Rhine**, and launched the **Continental System** (Berlin Decree) to blockade Britain. After a bloody stalemate at **Eylau**, he defeated Russia at **Friedland** (June 1807), leading to the **Treaties of Tilsit**, which divided Europe between France and Russia and humiliated Prussia.

### The Spanish Ulcer and Austrian Resurgence (1808–1809)
Napoleon’s intervention in Spain—deposing the Bourbons and installing his brother Joseph—triggered the **Peninsular War**. Popular uprising, British intervention under Wellington, and brutal guerrilla warfare tied down 300,000 French troops. Simultaneously, Austria attacked in 1809. Napoleon won at **Eckmühl**, suffered a rare setback at **Aspern-Essling**, then prevailed at **Wagram**. The **Treaty of Schönbrunn** stripped Austria of territory and population. To secure an heir and an Austrian alliance, he divorced Joséphine and married Marie Louise (1810); their son, the King of Rome, was born in 1811.

### The Russian Catastrophe and the Sixth Coalition (1812–1814)
Tensions with Russia over the Continental System and Poland led to the **invasion of Russia** (June 1812) with a multinational force of ~450,000. The Russians retreated, burning Moscow after the bloody **Battle of Borodino** (7 September). Napoleon occupied a burning city for six weeks; Alexander refused to negotiate. The winter retreat destroyed the Grande Armée: only ~75,000 crossed the Niemen back. Prussia and Sweden joined Russia; Austria joined in August 1813. Napoleon won at Lützen, Bautzen, and Dresden, but was decisively defeated at **Leipzig** (October 1813), the largest battle in Europe prior to WWI. The allies invaded France, captured Paris, and forced Napoleon’s abdication (April 1814). He was exiled to **Elba** as sovereign.

### The Hundred Days and Final Exile (1815)
Escaping Elba in February 1815, Napoleon marched on Paris, rallying troops sent to arrest him. Louis XVIII fled; the powers at Vienna declared him an outlaw and formed the **Seventh Coalition**. Napoleon invaded Belgium, aiming to defeat Wellington and Blücher separately. At **Waterloo** (18 June), Wellington held until Blücher’s arrival crushed the French right. Napoleon abdicated again (22 June), surrendered to the British, and was exiled to **Saint Helena**, where he died in 1821.

### Religion and Minorities
Napoleon viewed religion instrumentally: “I made myself a Catholic to win the Vendée, a Moslem for Egypt.” The **Concordat of 1801** recognized Catholicism as the majority faith while confirming revolutionary seizure of Church lands. He annexed the Papal States (1809), arrested Pope Pius VII (1809–14), and excommunicated him. He extended civil rights to Protestants and Jews, abolishing ghettos and convening a **Sanhedrin** (1807) to reconcile Jewish law with the state, though discrimination persisted. He abolished the Spanish Inquisition in conquered territories.

### Personality and Image
Contemporaries noted his intelligence, memory, capacity for work, and battlefield presence (Wellington: “worth 40,000 men”). He could inspire loyalty and charm, but also humiliate subordinates and fly into rages. Historians debate whether his ambition served France or himself; whether he was a pragmatic realist who became a megalomaniac after 1807; and whether his Corsican outsider status drove a compensatory need for glory. The “Napoleon complex” (short-man aggression) is a myth: at ~168 cm (5'6"), he was average height. British propaganda caricatured him as “Little Boney,” a bogeyman tyrant.

### Reforms: Administration, Law, Education, Warfare
- **Administration**: Prefects replaced local autonomy; Council of State drafted laws; Banque de France stabilized currency.
- **Law**: Seven codes culminated in the **Napoleonic Code**, exported by conquest, influencing Latin America, Louisiana, Quebec, and Europe.
- **Education**: Centralized **Imperial University** (1808) controlled curriculum; **lycées** and **grandes écoles** produced bureaucrats and officers; primary education remained religious/communal; female education was domestic and religious.
- **Warfare**: Corps system, mobile artillery reserves, merit promotion, and offensive concentration defined **Napoleonic warfare**. Clausewitz called him a genius; Connelly argued his tactics defied analysis. War became more total, decisive, and politically transformative.

### Long-Term Influence Outside France
The Napoleonic Code spread revolutionary legal principles globally. Feudalism, manorialism, and guilds were abolished in conquered lands; church courts were subordinated; civil equality was proclaimed. The **Confederation of the Rhine** (1806) simplified the Holy Roman Empire’s 300+ states into ~40, catalyzing German nationalism and eventual unification (1871). In Italy, Napoleonic rule sparked the *Risorgimento*. In Spanish America, the ousting of the Bourbons created a power vacuum filled by Bolívar and San Martín, launching independence movements. Poland honors him in its national anthem for the Duchy of Warsaw.

### Memory and Evaluation
Napoleon’s reputation polarized immediately. Royalists saw a usurper; liberals, a betrayer of the Revolution; romantics, a Promethean genius. **Propaganda**—censorship, controlled art, the *Memorial of Saint Helena* (1822)—crafted a legend of a visionary European unifier. The legend fueled opposition to the Bourbon Restoration (1815–30) and enabled Napoleon III’s Second Empire (1852–70). Modern historians reject the Hitler comparison (no genocide) but condemn the reinstatement of slavery, the Haitian expedition, civilian repression, looting of art, and the ~6 million dead in the Napoleonic Wars. Debates persist: enlightened modernizer vs. authoritarian warmonger; military genius vs. lucky gambler; heir of the Revolution vs. its gravedigger.

## Terms
- ****Grande Armée**** — Napoleon’s principal field army (1805–1812), organized into self-sufficient corps with integrated artillery and cavalry reserves.
- ****Continental System**** — Economic blockade (Berlin Decree, 1806) forbidding European trade with Britain; widely evaded, it hurt France as much as Britain.
- ****Napoleonic Code**** — Civil code (1804) establishing equality before the law, secular property rights, and abolition of feudal privileges; restricted women’s rights.
- ****Concordat of 1801**** — Agreement with Pope Pius VII recognizing Catholicism as France’s majority religion; state gained control of bishop appointments and clergy salaries.
- ****Peninsular War**** — Conflict (1808–1814) in Spain and Portugal triggered by Napoleon’s deposition of the Bourbons; “the Spanish ulcer” drained French resources.
- ****Hundred Days**** — Period (20 March – 22 June 1815) between Napoleon’s return from Elba and his second abdication after Waterloo.
- ****Confederation of the Rhine**** — League of German states (1806) created by Napoleon as a French client buffer; ended the Holy Roman Empire.
- ****Sanhedrin**** — Assembly of Jewish notables convened by Napoleon (1807) to adapt Mosaic law to French civil statutes.
- ****Corps system**** — Military innovation: large, combined-arms formations (infantry, cavalry, artillery) capable of independent operation and rapid concentration.
- ****Plebiscite**** — Direct vote used by Napoleon to legitimize constitutional changes (Consul for Life, Emperor, Constitution); results often inflated.

## Debates and open questions
- **Agency vs. Structure**: Did Napoleon create his opportunities, or did the Revolution’s vacuum and France’s demographic/military advantages make his rise inevitable?
- **Military Genius**: Was his success due to tactical innovation (corps system, artillery concentration) or superior French revolutionary organization and opponent incompetence?
- **Revolutionary Heir or Betrayer**: Did the Code and abolition of feudalism fulfill the Revolution, or did the Empire, censorship, and hereditary nobility betray it?
- **Slavery and Race**: How to weigh his 1794 abolition (by the Convention) against his 1802 reinstatement, the Haitian expedition, and racial bans in France?
- **The Russian Campaign**: Was the invasion a rational response to the Continental System’s failure, or hubristic overreach driven by belief in his “star”?
- **Waterloo**: Could Napoleon have won with better staff work (Grouchy’s failure), or was the coalition’s numerical and strategic superiority decisive?
- **Long-term Balance**: Do the legal/administrative exports outweigh the ~6 million war dead and the reactionary settlement at Vienna (1815)?
