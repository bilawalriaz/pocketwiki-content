# Notre-Dame de Paris

## Overview
Notre-Dame de Paris is a medieval Catholic cathedral on the Île de la Cité in Paris, France. It is a masterpiece of French Gothic architecture, renowned for its pioneering use of the flying buttress, its vast stained-glass rose windows, and its extensive sculptural program. As the cathedral church of the Archdiocese of Paris and a symbol of the French nation, its history reflects centuries of religious, political, and cultural change, including periods of construction, revolutionary desecration, major restoration, and a devastating 2019 fire followed by a meticulous reconstruction.

## Timeline
- **1163** — Construction begins under Bishop Maurice de Sully.
- **c. 1182** — Choir completed and high altar consecrated.
- **c. 1200** — Nave with flying buttresses completed.
- **1250–1260** — North transept and rose window completed.
- **1793** — Cathedral converted into a "Temple of Reason" during the French Revolution.
- **1804** — Napoleon crowns himself Emperor at Notre-Dame.
- **1844–1864** — Major restoration by Eugène Viollet-le-Duc and Jean-Baptiste Lassus.
- **1944** — Liberation of Paris celebrated with a Mass at the cathedral.
- **2019** — A fire destroys the roof and flèche (spire).
- **2024** — Cathedral reopens after extensive reconstruction.

## Body

### Construction and Gothic Innovation
Construction began in 1163 on the site of earlier churches, including a 4th-century Cathedral of Saint Étienne. Bishop Maurice de Sully initiated the project in the Gothic style, which had recently been inaugurated at Saint-Denis. The choir was completed by 1182, followed by the nave, which incorporated the revolutionary flying buttress. This structural innovation transferred the roof's weight outward to external supports, allowing for thinner walls, greater height, and larger windows. The west façade and its two towers were largely completed by the mid-13th century. The transepts were later remodeled in the Rayonnant style, featuring the spectacular north and south rose windows.

### Revolution, Restoration, and National Symbol
The cathedral suffered severe damage during the French Revolution, when it was desecrated, stripped of metals, and temporarily rededicated as a "Temple of Reason." Napoleon Bonaparte restored it to Catholic use and crowned himself there in 1804. By the 19th century, it was in disrepair, prompting Victor Hugo's 1831 novel *The Hunchback of Notre-Dame* to rally support for its preservation. A major restoration (1844–1864) under Viollet-le-Duc rebuilt much of the decoration, including a new flèche. The cathedral became a symbol of the French state, hosting national events like the 1944 Liberation of Paris celebration.

### The 2019 Fire and Reconstruction
On 15 April 2019, a fire destroyed the oak roof and the 19th-century flèche. The main structure, including the stone vaulting, towers, and most stained glass, survived. A massive, internationally supported reconstruction effort began, focusing on historical accuracy. A new oak flèche, based on Viollet-le-Duc's designs, was installed in 2023. The interior was cleaned, revealing the original blonde stone beneath centuries of grime. The cathedral reopened in December 2024.

### Architecture, Art, and Furnishings
Notre-Dame's west façade is adorned with sculptures illustrating biblical stories, including the Last Judgment tympanum. The cathedral's three great rose windows—the west (c. 1225), north (c. 1250), and south (c. 1260)—are iconic features. The south rose, gifted by Louis IX, contains 94 medallions depicting biblical scenes. The Great Organ, with over 8,000 pipes, was built by François Thierry in the 18th century and restored by Aristide Cavaillé-Coll in the 19th century. The cathedral possesses important relics, including the Crown of Thorns.

## Terms
- **Flying Buttress:** An external, arched support that transfers the weight of a roof or vault away from a wall, allowing for thinner walls and larger windows.
- **Rib Vault:** A ceiling construction technique using a framework of intersecting stone ribs to support the roof, a key feature of Gothic architecture.
- **Rose Window:** A large, circular stained-glass window, typically with a radiating, flower-like design.
- **Flèche:** A slender, pointed spire, typically located over the crossing of a cathedral's transepts.
- **Rayonnant Style:** A phase of French Gothic architecture (c. 1240–1350) characterized by a focus on two-dimensional surfaces and the repetition of motifs, especially in window tracery.
- **Cathedra:** The official throne of a bishop, symbolizing his authority; its presence defines a cathedral.
- **Tympanum:** The semi-circular or triangular decorative wall surface over an entrance, bounded by a lintel and arch, often filled with sculpture.
- **Grisaille:** A style of stained glass using mainly monochrome grey or brown tones, often to simulate stone tracery.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Original Design of Buttresses:** Art historian Andrew Tallon argued, based on laser scans, that flying buttresses were part of the cathedral's original 12th-century design, not a later addition.
- **Post-Fire Restoration Philosophy:** The 2019 fire sparked debate about restoration versus innovation. While a law mandated preserving the historic character, proposals for contemporary elements, like new stained-glass windows by Claire Tabouret, have been controversial among preservationists.
- **Ownership and Maintenance:** Since 1905, the French state owns Notre-Dame, while the Catholic Church has exclusive rights to use it. The state is responsible for maintenance, a model that applies to all French cathedrals.
