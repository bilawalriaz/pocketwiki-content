# Child

## Overview
A child is biologically defined as a human between birth and puberty, though legal definitions typically extend to the age of majority (often 18, per the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, ratified by 192 of 194 nations). Historically, the concept of childhood as a distinct, protected phase emerged in Europe during the 17th–18th centuries, influenced by Enlightenment thinkers like John Locke (tabula rasa) and Jean-Jacques Rousseau (childhood as sanctuary), contrasting with earlier views of children as miniature adults. Industrialization exposed the gap between romantic ideals and the reality of child labor, spurring Factory Acts and compulsory schooling. Modern childhood is characterized by institutionalized education, play as a recognized right, and distinct developmental stages, though cultural attitudes and legal responsibilities vary globally.

## Body

### Biological, Legal, and Social Definitions
Biologically, a child exists between infancy and puberty. Legally, the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989) defines a child as anyone under 18 unless majority is attained earlier; this is ratified by 192 of 194 member countries. However, national laws vary: Singapore defines a child as under 14 (age of majority 21), while U.S. Immigration Law uses under 21. Some English definitions include the fetus. In many cultures, adulthood begins after a rite of passage, which may not align with puberty. Children universally possess fewer rights and responsibilities than adults and are legally deemed unable to make serious decisions, requiring care by a responsible adult.

### Developmental Stages of Childhood
**Early childhood** follows infancy, beginning with toddlerhood (independent speech/steps) around age 1–3, continuing to age 5–6. The National Association for the Education of Young Children includes infancy in this stage. Children learn through observation, experimentation, and communication; adults supervise to foster autonomy. Strong emotional bonds form with caregivers, and preschool/kindergarten initiates social life.

**Middle childhood** (approx. ages 7–10) is marked by school entry, presenting new settings that reveal mental disorders (autism, dyslexia, ADHD) requiring specialized plans (Individualized Education Plans, response to intervention). Children develop socially and mentally, gaining independence through chores, responsible decisions, and peer/parental shaping. Social play facilitates learning through observation and teaching.

**Late childhood (Preadolescence)** spans roughly ages 9–12, ending with puberty onset (menarche, spermarche, peak height velocity, typically ages 11–14). Preadolescents hold a more realistic worldview than younger children, described as the "most sensible stage." They develop self-identity, independence, and a critical view of authority figures, feeling distinct from the family unit.

### Adolescence
Adolescence spans puberty onset to legal adulthood, roughly teenage years (13–19), though puberty begins earlier (10–11 for girls, 11–12 for boys). Biologically post-childhood, adolescents remain legal children in many contexts—lacking full rights and subject to compulsory schooling. The transition brings physical, psychological, and behavioral changes; the endpoint varies by country and function.

### History of Childhood
**Renaissance to Enlightenment:** Artistic depictions of children surged during the European Renaissance without immediately altering social attitudes. Philippe Ariès (1960) argued the concept of childhood emerged in the 1600s, though Nicholas Orme contends it existed in the medieval period. John Locke’s *tabula rasa* (mind as blank slate) established the parental duty to instill correct notions. The rise of a Protestant commercial middle class (Dutch Republic, England) created a family ideology centered on child-rearing; Puritanism emphasized children's spiritual welfare.

**Romanticism and Industrialization:** The 18th-century Enlightenment and Romantic period birthed the modern notion of childhood autonomy. Rousseau’s *Emile* (1762) idealized childhood as a sanctuary. Reynolds’ portraiture (e.g., *The Age of Innocence*, 1788) visualized innocence. William Wordsworth and Henry Vaughan (17th century) rooted childhood in divinity and pastoral purity, contrasting Calvinist views of infant depravity. Industrialization (from 1760) created a stark divergence: British children labored in factories, mines, and as chimney sweeps. The contradiction between middle-class ideals and working-class reality drove the Factory Acts (from 1830s), aided by Charles Dickens’ exposés.

**Modern Concepts (Late 19th Century–Present):** The Victorian era cemented the sanctity of the child and family centrality. Children’s literature flourished (*Alice in Wonderland*, 1865). Compulsory schooling removed children from work. The market economy enabled childhood as a time of fun (factory dolls, organized sports). The Boy Scouts (1908) aimed to build character. Ariès’ *Centuries of Childhood* (1960) and George Boas’ *The Cult of Childhood* (1966) spurred historical scholarship. Modern Western childhood is viewed as a time of happiness, play, and learning, with "loss of innocence" (e.g., *To Kill a Mockingbird*, *Lord of the Flies*) marking the transition to adulthood; Peter Pan symbolizes eternal childhood.

### Healthy Childhoods
**Children's Health:** Encompasses physical, mental, and social well-being. Requires nutrition, sleep, exercise, and safety. Malnutrition, prevalent in regions, links to diarrhea, pneumonia, and malaria.

**Child Protection:** UNICEF defines this as preventing/responding to violence, exploitation, and abuse (commercial sexual exploitation, trafficking, child labor, female genital mutilation/cutting, child marriage). The Convention on the Rights of the Child protects fundamental rights.

**Play:** Essential for cognitive, physical, social, and emotional development (empathy, creativity, conflict resolution). Recognized by the UN Commission on Human Rights as a right. Undirected play builds group skills, negotiation, and self-advocacy; adult-controlled play diminishes these benefits. "Playful Structure" combines informal and formal learning. Environmental inequities hinder play for poor children: less social support, authoritarian parenting, and limited access to books/computers.

**Street Culture:** The cumulative culture created by children (ages 7–12), strongest in urban working-class districts where unsupervised street play occurs. Children imbue urban spaces (kerbs, wastelands) with imaginative meaning and designate meeting spots. Indoor distractions (video games, TV) threaten its vitality.

### Geographies of Childhood
This field examines how adult society perceives childhood and how adult attitudes/environments shape children's lives. **Nature Deficit Disorder** (Richard Louv, 2005) describes the trend (US/Canada) of reduced outdoor play due to electronic media (avg. 44 hours/week for US children), correlating with behavioral problems and declining National Park visits. Media (e.g., Disney films) accelerates nature disconnection by deemphasizing natural views.

### Age of Responsibility
The age for societal responsibility (marriage, voting, criminal culpability) has shifted. Roman times and the Church regarded children as not culpable. In the 19th century, children under seven were deemed incapable of crime; those seven and older faced adult penalties (whipping, branding, hanging, adult prison). Minimum employment, marriage, and military service ages vary internationally and remain disputed.

### Education
Education imparts knowledge, reasoning, and preparation for mature life. Article 13 of the UN International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (1966) recognizes a universal right. Schooling is compulsory in most places, though homeschooling/e-learning are alternatives. Barriers persist: UNICEF (2011) counted 57 million children out of school; >20% of African children never attend/finish primary school. Warfare prevents 28 million from education (risk of sexual violence, school attacks). Poverty, child labor, social attitudes, and distance also exclude children.

### Attitudes Toward Children
**Cultural Variation:** A 1988 European study found Italy most child-centric, Netherlands least, with Austria, UK, Ireland, and West Germany intermediate.

**Child Marriage:** 2013 rates for girls under 18: Niger 75%, Central African Republic/Chad 68%, Bangladesh 66%, India 47%. UNICEF 2019: Sub-Saharan Africa 37%, South Asia 30%, Latin America/Caribbean 25%, Middle East/North Africa 18%, Eastern Europe/Central Asia 11%. Boys also affected: 4.5% globally married before 18 (Central African Republic highest at 27.9%).

**Fertility:** Pre-contraception, women had many children. Global total fertility rate (2017): 2.37 children/woman (~80M added yearly). Completed cohort fertility at 50 (CCF50) correlates most heavily with women's education: 5–8 children for uneducated women, <2 for those with 12+ years education.

### Issues
**Emergencies and Conflicts:** As of 2010, ~13 million children displaced by armed conflict. Disasters yield PTSD rates of 3–87% (natural) and 15–50% (chronic conflict) in children aged 0–8. Consistent care for healthy development is disrupted.

**Child Abuse and Labor:** Protection targets exploitation: child labor, trafficking, sexual abuse (prostitution, pornography), military use, illegal adoptions. Key instruments: Worst Forms of Child Labour Convention; Minimum Age Convention (1973); Optional Protocols on Sale of Children, Armed Conflict; Hague Adoption Convention; Council of Europe Convention on Sexual Exploitation.

### Health
**Child Mortality:** In early 17th-century England, ~2/3 of children died before age four. The Industrial Revolution dramatically increased life expectancy. Global under-five deaths fell from 12.6M (1990) to 6.6M (2012). Infant mortality dropped from 90 to 48 per 1,000 live births (1990–2012). Highest rates remain in sub-Saharan Africa (98 per 1,000), over double the global average.

## Terms
- ****Age of Majority**** — The legal threshold of adulthood; the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child defines a child as under 18 unless this is attained earlier.
- ****Tabula Rasa**** — John Locke’s theory that the mind at birth is a "blank slate," implying parents must imbue the child with correct notions.
- ****Preadolescence**** — Developmental stage (approx. ages 9–12) following early childhood and preceding adolescence, ending with major puberty onset (menarche, spermarche, peak height velocity).
- ****Nature Deficit Disorder**** — Term coined by Richard Louv (2005) describing behavioral problems resulting from reduced outdoor play and increased electronic media use (avg. 44 hrs/week for US children).
- ****Factory Acts**** — British legislation (from 1830s) mitigating child labor exploitation in factories and mines, driven by reformers and Charles Dickens' exposés.
- ****Individualized Education Plan (IEP)**** — Specialized plan to help children with disabilities (e.g., autism, dyslexia, ADHD) identified during middle childhood schooling.
- ****Child Marriage**** — Marriage of a person under 18; prevalent for girls (e.g., 75% in Niger, 2013) and boys (4.5% globally).
- ****Completed Cohort Fertility at 50 (CCF50)**** — Demographic measure of total children per woman, heavily dependent on female education level (5–8 for uneducated, <2 for 12+ years education).
- ****Street Culture**** — Cumulative culture invented by children (ages 7–12) in urban working-class districts, imbuing public spaces with imaginative meaning; threatened by indoor media.
- ****Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989)**** — UN treaty ratified by 192/194 nations defining child as under 18 and protecting fundamental rights (protection, education, health, play).

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Historical Emergence of Childhood:** Philippe Ariès argues childhood is a modern social construction (post-1600s); Nicholas Orme and others argue a distinct concept existed in the medieval period.
*   **Age Boundaries:** Legal definitions of "child" vary significantly (UN: <18; Singapore: <14; US Immigration: <21), creating tension between biological maturity (puberty) and legal status.
*   **Criminal Responsibility:** The age of culpability has shifted from Roman/Church immunity to 19th-century thresholds (age 7) to modern variable standards; international dispute persists on minimum ages for military service and marriage.
*   **Play vs. Structure:** Tension exists between the developmental necessity of undirected, child-driven play (a UN-recognized right) and adult-controlled "playful structure" or pressured, hurried upbringing that limits its benefits.
*   **Nature Disconnection:** The reality of "Nature Deficit Disorder" (correlated with 44 hrs/week screen time and declining park visits) versus the cultural de-emphasis of nature in media.
*   **Global Education Access:** Despite recognized rights, 57M children (2011) remain out of school due to warfare (28M), poverty, labor, and distance; >20% of African children never complete primary education.
*   **Child Mortality Disparities:** While global rates have halved (1990–2012), sub-Saharan Africa retains an infant mortality rate (98/1,000) over double the world average.
