# Abolitionism

## Overview
Abolitionism was the political movement to end slavery and liberate enslaved individuals worldwide, gaining major momentum in the Western world during the late 18th and 19th centuries. It involved legal reforms, slave revolts, and international activism, ultimately leading to the global outlawing of slavery, though modern forms of forced labor and trafficking persist.

## Timeline
- **1315** — France issues a decree proclaiming that any slave setting foot on French soil should be freed.
- **1542** — Spain passes the New Laws, abolishing the enslavement of indigenous people in its colonies.
- **1772** — The Somersett case in England rules that slavery does not exist under English common law.
- **1777** — Vermont becomes the first U.S. state to abolish slavery.
- **1791–1804** — The Haitian Revolution leads to the first successful slave revolt and the creation of an independent, slavery-free nation.
- **1807** — The British Parliament passes the Slave Trade Act, making the slave trade illegal throughout the British Empire.
- **1833** — The Slavery Abolition Act is passed in the British Empire, freeing slaves in most colonies by 1838.
- **1848** — France abolishes slavery in its remaining colonies under the Second Republic.
- **1865** — The 13th Amendment to the U.S. Constitution is ratified, abolishing slavery in the United States.
- **1888** — Brazil becomes the last country in the Americas to outlaw slavery.
- **1926** — The League of Nations adopts the Slavery Convention, an international treaty to eradicate slavery.
- **1948** — The United Nations' Universal Declaration of Human Rights declares slavery illegal.
- **1981** — Mauritania becomes the last country to officially abolish slavery by presidential decree.

## Body

### Early and Colonial Abolition
Early abolition efforts were often limited to metropolitan territories. In France, a 1315 decree declared "France signifies freedom," but slavery persisted in colonies. Spain's 1542 New Laws first abolished indigenous slavery in European colonies. In Japan, chattel slavery was abolished by Toyotomi Hideyoshi in 1590. The first nation to self-liberate from slavery was Haiti, following a revolution (1791–1804) led by formerly enslaved people like Toussaint L'Ouverture.

### The British Abolitionist Movement
The British movement was catalyzed by the 1772 Somersett case, which established that slavery was not recognized by English common law. The Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade formed in 1787, with figures like William Wilberforce leading parliamentary campaigns. The Slave Trade Act of 1807 ended the British slave trade, but slavery itself was abolished in the empire by the Slavery Abolition Act of 1833, which freed slaves by purchasing them from their masters.

### Abolition in the Americas
In the United States, northern states abolished slavery between 1777 and 1804, while it remained legal in the South. The abolitionist movement, led by figures like William Lloyd Garrison and Frederick Douglass, intensified in the 1830s. The Civil War (1861–1865) culminated in the Emancipation Proclamation (1863) and the 13th Amendment (1865), which ended slavery "except as a punishment for crime." In Latin America, slavery was abolished during independence movements in the early 19th century, with Brazil being the last to end it in 1888.

### International and 20th-Century Abolition
International efforts intensified in the 20th century. The League of Nations created commissions like the Temporary Slavery Commission (1924) and adopted the 1926 Slavery Convention. The United Nations' 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights explicitly banned slavery. Chattel slavery remained legal in parts of the Arab world until the 1960s and 1970s, with Mauritania being the last country to officially abolish it in 1981.

### Women and Abolitionism
Women were central to abolitionist rhetoric and activism. Abolitionists frequently highlighted the suffering of enslaved women and mothers. Female authors like Harriet Beecher Stowe and activists like the Grimké Sisters advocated for abolition. Enslaved women, such as Harriet Tubman, directly challenged the institution, while in places like Cuba and Brazil, enslaved women used urban proximity to courts and networks to argue for their freedom.

### Aftermath and Contemporary Issues
Abolition often led to complex social and economic consequences. Haiti was forced to pay a crippling "independence debt" to France. In the U.S., the 13th Amendment's exception for criminal punishment was exploited through systems like Black Codes and convict leasing. Today, while slavery is illegal globally, human trafficking and forced labor affect tens of millions, prompting modern abolitionist groups like Anti-Slavery International to continue the fight.

## Terms
- **Abolitionism:** The political movement to end slavery and liberate enslaved people.
- **Chattel slavery:** A system where one person has total ownership of another, treating them as property.
- **Manumission:** The act of an owner freeing their slaves.
- **Marronage:** The practice of enslaved people escaping to form independent communities.
- **Contraband:** A term used during the U.S. Civil War for escaped slaves who fled to Union lines.
- **Code Noir:** A 17th-century French decree that regulated slavery and the slave trade in its colonies.
- **Somersett case:** A 1772 English legal decision that ruled slavery was unsupported by common law in England.
- **13th Amendment:** The amendment to the U.S. Constitution that abolished slavery and involuntary servitude, except as punishment for a crime.
- **Slavery Convention (1926):** An international treaty under the League of Nations aimed at abolishing slavery worldwide.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact legal implications of the 1772 Somersett case remain unclear to lawyers, though it was widely interpreted as ending slavery in England.
- The narrative and scholarly literature around the Reconstruction Amendments (13th, 14th, 15th) in the U.S. is debated regarding their original aims and whether they constitute a full abolitionist vision.
- There is debate over whether the U.S. Constitution should be viewed as an abolitionist document, with some arguing it was fundamentally pro-slavery until the Reconstruction Amendments.
- The effectiveness of "stroke-of-the-pen" abolition laws is questioned, as they often failed to address deep-seated social, economic, and racial inequalities, leading to ongoing struggles like the Jim Crow system in the U.S.
