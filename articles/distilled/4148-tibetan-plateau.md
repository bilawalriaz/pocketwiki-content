# Tibetan Plateau

## Overview
The Tibetan Plateau, also called the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, is the world's largest and highest plateau, averaging over 4,500 meters in elevation. Known as "the Roof of the World," it is a critical geographical and ecological feature that acts as a "water tower" for Asia, feeding major rivers and influencing regional climate patterns like the monsoon. Its formation is tied to the collision of tectonic plates, and it remains a focus of scientific study due to its sensitivity to climate change.

## Timeline
- **c. 70 Mya** — India begins drifting north from Gondwana.
- **c. 55–50 Mya** — Indian and Eurasian plates collide, closing the Tethys Ocean and initiating plateau formation.
- **c. 50 Mya** — Early Tibet is a deep valley bounded by mountains, not a uniform flatland.
- **c. 25.5–21.6 Mya** — Plateau elevation falls by ~900 meters due to tectonic or erosional processes.
- **c. 21.6–20.4 Mya** — Plateau rises by 500 to 1,000 meters.
- **c. 14–8 Mya** — Plateau elevation approaches modern altitude.
- **c. 200,000–40,000 years ago** — Archaic Denisovans inhabit the plateau.
- **c. 30,000–40,000 years ago** — Earliest evidence of modern human occupation.
- **7th–9th century AD** — The Tibetan Empire exists on the plateau.
- **2009** — Former China Meteorological Administration head warns of rapid glacial retreat.
- **2015** — Researchers drill a 50-meter ice core from the Guliya glacier, recovering ancient viruses.

## Body

### Geography and Geology
The Tibetan Plateau is a vast, high-altitude arid steppe bordered by major mountain ranges, including the Himalayas to the south and the Kunlun Mountains to the north. It is the source of Asia's three longest rivers: the Yellow, Yangtze, and Mekong. Its geological history is defined by the collision of the Indo-Australian and Eurasian plates, which began around 55–50 million years ago. This collision crumpled light sediments from the closed Tethys Ocean, creating the Himalayas and uplifting the plateau. The plateau's elevation has fluctuated since its initial uplift, with its modern altitude reached approximately 14 to 8 million years ago. It is still rising at about 5 mm per year, though erosion mitigates the net gain.

### Environment and Ecosystems
The plateau supports diverse montane grassland ecosystems, from alpine tundra to monsoon-influenced shrublands. Species diversity is generally reduced due to high elevation and low precipitation. Notable fauna includes the Tibetan wolf, snow leopard, wild yak, and the high-altitude jumping spider, which lives above 6,500 meters. The region is divided into numerous ecoregions, such as the Central Tibetan Plateau alpine steppe and the Yarlung Tsangpo arid steppe, where most permanent human populations reside.

### Human History and Impact
Archaic Denisovans lived on the plateau from around 200,000 to 40,000 years ago, with modern humans arriving between 30,000 and 40,000 years ago. Tibetan culture adapted to the harsh environment, with nomadic pastoralism persisting as a key livelihood; pastoral nomads constitute about 40% of the ethnic Tibetan population. The plateau's massive elevation drives the strongest seasonal monsoon on Earth by creating large-scale temperature and pressure differences between the land and surrounding oceans.

### Glaciers and Climate Change
The Tibetan Plateau contains the world's third-largest store of ice, making it a vital "Third Pole" freshwater reserve. However, it is highly vulnerable to climate change. Temperatures are rising four times faster than elsewhere in China, causing rapid glacial retreat. Over the past five decades, 80% of glaciers have retreated, losing 4.5% of their combined area. This threatens long-term water supplies for major Asian rivers like the Indus and Ganges. Ice core research, such as from the Guliya glacier, provides a valuable record of past climates and biological samples.

## Terms
- **Orogeny**: A mountain-building process, especially one due to tectonic plate collision.
- **Peneplain**: A low-relief plain representing the final stage of erosion.
- **Permafrost**: Ground that remains frozen for two or more consecutive years.
- **Montane**: Relating to or inhabiting mountainous regions.
- **Steppe**: A large area of flat grassland without trees, typically in semi-arid regions.
- **Grabens**: Depressed blocks of the Earth's crust bordered by parallel faults.
- **Strike-slip fault**: A fracture in rock where movement is horizontal, parallel to the fault line.
- **Continuum model**: A geological model where deformation is distributed across a region, not concentrated on discrete blocks.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact timing of the initial collision between the Indian and Eurasian plates is debated, with estimates ranging around 55–50 Mya.
- The cause of the plateau's relatively low internal relief is debated: some argue it is an uplifted peneplain formed at low altitude, while others attribute it to erosion and infill at high elevations.
- The current tectonic behavior of the plateau is explained by two competing models: the block model (crust as discrete blocks with little internal deformation) and the continuum model (distributed deformation from crustal flow).
