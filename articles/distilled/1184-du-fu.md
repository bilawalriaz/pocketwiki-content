# Du Fu

## Overview
Du Fu (712–770) was a Tang dynasty poet and aspiring civil servant whose life was upended by the An Lushan Rebellion (755–763). Though he failed the imperial examinations and never achieved high office, the rebellion forced him into a itinerant existence that became the crucible for his art. He produced nearly 1,500 poems distinguished by their historical witness, moral compassion for the suffering of ordinary people, and technical mastery of the *lüshi* (regulated verse) form. Largely unrecognized in his lifetime, his reputation grew posthumously until he was canonized as the "Poet-Historian" and "Poet-Sage," embodying Confucian ideals of loyalty and benevolence. His influence on Chinese literature is comparable to Shakespeare’s in English, and he profoundly shaped Japanese literary culture from the Muromachi period onward.

## Body

### Early Life and Failed Ambitions
Du Fu was born into a scholarly family; his grandfather Du Shenyan was a noted poet under Empress Wu Zetian. He received a standard Confucian education aimed at the civil service but failed the *Jinshi* examination in 735, likely due to an overly dense prose style or lack of political connections. After his father’s death c. 740, he yielded his hereditary privilege of office to a half-brother. A pivotal moment came in 744 when he met Li Bai, the era’s reigning poetic star. The friendship provided Du Fu a living model of the reclusive poet-scholar life, though the relationship was lopsided—Du Fu wrote twelve poems to Li Bai, Li Bai only one in return. Du Fu attempted the exam again in 747, but the prime minister failed all candidates to eliminate rivals. He thereafter petitioned the emperor directly (751, 754, 755) and secured a minor post as Registrar in 755, which was immediately nullified by the outbreak of rebellion.

### The An Lushan Rebellion and Itinerant Years
The An Lushan Rebellion (Dec 755 – 763) devastated China; the census dropped from 52.9 million (754) to 16.9 million (764). This cataclysm made Du Fu a poet: displacement, famine, and the suffering of civilians became his enduring subjects. In 756, he was captured by rebels in Chang'an and witnessed the occupied capital, producing the famous "Spring View" (*Chūn wàng*). He escaped in 757, joined Emperor Suzong’s court, and was appointed Reminder—a ceremonial post he tried to use substantively, protesting the dismissal of his patron Fang Guan, which led to his brief arrest. After the capital’s recapture (Dec 757), his advice was ignored, and he was demoted to Commissioner of Education in Huazhou (758). Frustrated, he left in 759, spending six weeks in Qinzhou writing over sixty poems noted for their "grim simplicity."

### Chengdu: A Peaceful Interlude
In late 759, Du Fu travelled to Chengdu, where Prefect Pei Di and later Governor General Yan Wu (a former colleague) provided patronage and financial relief. For roughly five years, he lived in a thatched cottage (now a tourist site), experiencing his happiest, most peaceful period. Poems from this era are "light, often finely observed," depicting domestic tranquility and nature. He briefly fled a local rebellion in 762 but returned in 764 as advisor to Yan Wu during campaigns against the Tibetan Empire.

### Final Wandering and Late Mastery
In 765, Du Fu headed down the Yangtze toward his native Luoyang region, slowed by failing health (asthma, diabetes, tuberculosis, blindness, deafness). He stayed nearly two years (766–768) in Kuizhou at the Three Gorges entrance. This was his final great poetic flowering: he wrote ~400 poems in a dense, powerful late style while serving as unofficial secretary to Governor Bo Maolin. In March 768 he resumed travel, reaching Hunan province, where he died aboard ship in late 770. He was survived by his wife and two sons.

### Works: History, Morality, and Technique
**History.** Since the Song dynasty, critics have called Du Fu the "poet for history" (*shī shǐ*). His poems comment on military tactics, government failures, and advice to the emperor, while indirectly recording the era’s impact on ordinary people—data absent from official histories. His political stance was emotional, not calculative: a plea for unselfish duty that became the Confucian poetic ideal.

**Moral Engagement.** Dubbed "poet sage" (*shī shèng*), a counterpart to Confucius, Du Fu broadened poetry’s scope to include previously "unsuitable" topics: domestic life, calligraphy, painting, animals. His compassion included himself objectively, lending grandeur to the collective suffering by juxtaposing it with his "slightly comical triviality" (Hawkes). "The Song of the Wagons" (c. 750) exemplifies his early voice for the conscript soldier.

**Technical Excellence.** Critics use *jídàchéng* ("complete symphony," from Mencius on Confucius) to describe his range. He mastered all forms and registers, from colloquial to highly allusive. He is the supreme exponent of *lüshi* (regulated verse)—a strict eight-line form with tonal and parallel constraints—composing ~1,000 of his 1,500 extant poems in this mode. He used its required parallelisms for expressive depth, not mere ornament. He also wrote 18 poems on painting (e.g., "Song of a Painting for General Cao Ba"), the first Chinese poet to do so extensively, sparking enduring debates on art criticism. His style evolved "chameleon-like": derivative early work; grim simplicity in Qinzhou; light observation in Chengdu; dense visionary power in Kuizhou.

## Terms
- ****Jinshi** (進士)** — The highest imperial civil service examination degree in imperial China; Du Fu failed it twice (735, 747).
- ****An Lushan Rebellion** (安史之亂)** — A devastating revolt (755–763) led by General An Lushan against the Tang dynasty; caused massive population loss and displacement, defining Du Fu’s mature life and poetry.
- ****Lüshi** (律詩)** — "Regulated verse": an eight-line poetic form with strict tonal patterns, rhyme schemes, and mandatory parallelism in the middle two couplets; Du Fu’s signature form (~1,000 poems).
- ****Shī shǐ** (詩史)** — "Poet-Historian" / "Poetry as History": the critical epithet for Du Fu, acknowledging his poems as a primary historical record of the mid-Tang crisis.
- ****Shī shèng** (詩聖)** — "Poet-Sage": the epithet pairing Du Fu with Confucius as the moral exemplar of Chinese poetry.
- ****Jídàchéng** (集大成)** — "Complete symphony": a term from Mencius describing Confucius’s synthesis of tradition; applied by critics to Du Fu’s mastery of all poetic forms and registers.
- ****Kanshi** (漢詩)** — "Chinese poetry by Japanese poets"; the genre through which Du Fu’s influence permeated Japanese literature from the 9th century onward.
- ****Parallelism** (對仗)** — A structural requirement in regulated verse where corresponding words in two lines match in part of speech, meaning, and tone; Du Fu used it for expressive juxtaposition.
- ****Three Gorges** (三峽)** — The Yangtze River gorges where Du Fu spent his final productive years (766–768) in Kuizhou.
- ****Thatched Cottage** (草堂)** — Du Fu’s Chengdu residence, reconstructed as a memorial; symbol of his brief domestic peace and later literary pilgrimage site.

## Debates and open questions
- **Cause of 735 exam failure:** William Hung argues Du Fu’s prose was too dense and obscure; Eva Shan Chou suggests failure to cultivate capital connections.
- **Reason for leaving Huazhou (759):** Traditional ascription is famine; Hung argues frustration with the post is more likely.
- **Diabetes diagnosis:** The article claims Du Fu is the "first person in the historical record identified as a diabetic patient," but the textual evidence for this specific medical retrospective diagnosis is not detailed in the source.
- **Painting criticism controversy:** Du Fu’s negative commentary on the horse paintings of Han Gan "ignited a controversy that has persisted to the present day"; the source does not specify the exact critical parameters of this debate.
- **Translation fidelity:** Translators debate how to handle *lüshi* parallelisms (preserve strictly vs. conceal via enjambment) and dense allusions (annotate extensively vs. "translate out" or omit). No consensus approach exists.
