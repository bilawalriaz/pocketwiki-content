# Palmyra

## Overview
Palmyra was an ancient Semitic city in the Syrian desert that became a wealthy caravan hub and a powerful regional kingdom in the 3rd century CE. Its strategic location on trade routes between the Roman and Parthian empires allowed it to flourish culturally and economically, producing distinctive art and architecture. The city's brief period of independence under Queen Zenobia ended with its destruction by Rome, after which it declined into a minor settlement, its ruins later becoming a major archaeological site and UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Timeline
- **c. 2000 BC** — First historical mention in Assyrian records as "Tadmor."
- **64 BC** — Roman conquest of Syria; Palmyra left independent but within Rome's sphere.
- **14 AD** — Palmyra formally incorporated into the Roman province of Syria.
- **129 AD** — Emperor Hadrian visits and grants Palmyra the status of a free city.
- **213-216 AD** — Emperor Caracalla elevates Palmyra to a Roman colonia.
- **260 AD** — Odaenathus defeats the Sasanian emperor Shapur I, beginning Palmyra's rise to power.
- **267 AD** — Odaenathus is assassinated; his son Vaballathus and Queen Zenobia assume power.
- **270-272 AD** — Zenobia expands the Palmyrene Empire, conquering Egypt and parts of Anatolia.
- **272 AD** — Roman Emperor Aurelian defeats Zenobia at the Battle of Emesa.
- **273 AD** — Aurelian razes Palmyra to the ground after a rebellion.
- **634 AD** — Palmyra conquered by the Rashidun Caliphate.
- **1400 AD** — City destroyed by Timur (Tamerlane).
- **1932 AD** — Modern village of Tadmur relocated; ancient site cleared for excavation.
- **2015-2017 AD** — Islamic State (IS) occupies Palmyra, destroying major monuments; recaptured by Syrian Army.

## Body

### Etymology and Location
The city's earliest name, "Tadmor," appears in 18th-century BC Mari tablets. The etymology is debated, with the standard interpretation connecting it to the Semitic word for "date palm" (*tamar*). The Greek name "Palmyra," first recorded by Pliny the Elder, is likely a corruption or translation of "Tadmor." Palmyra is located in an oasis 215 km northeast of Damascus, surrounded by mountains and the Syrian Desert.

### Society and Culture
Palmyra's population was a mix of Amorites, Arameans, and Arabs, who developed a distinct Palmyrene identity. They spoke Palmyrene Aramaic but used Koine Greek for commerce. Society was initially organized around clans and four tribes, but by the 3rd century, aristocratic families dominated. Palmyrene culture fused local Semitic traditions with Greco-Roman influences, visible in its art, which featured frontal bust reliefs, and its architecture, like the Temple of Bel, which combined a Semitic sanctuary plan with Roman columns.

### Political History
Palmyra began as a caravan station, gaining autonomy under Rome. Its power peaked in the 260s CE under King Odaenathus, who defeated the Sasanian Persians and was granted imperial authority in the East. After his assassination, Queen Zenobia ruled for her son Vaballathus, expanding the Palmyrene Empire. In 272-273 CE, Emperor Aurelian crushed this rebellion, destroying the city. It was later rebuilt as a smaller Roman fortress under Diocletian.

### Economy
Palmyra's wealth came from controlling caravan trade between the Roman Empire and the East. Merchants established colonies along the Silk Road, exporting silk, spices, and other goods. The city taxed this trade, as recorded in the 137 CE Tariff Law. Agriculture, supported by an irrigation system including the Harbaqa Dam, supplemented but could not replace trade income.

### Religion and Later History
Palmyrenes worshipped a pantheon of Semitic, Mesopotamian, and Arab deities, with Bel as the chief god. The sun god Malakbel may have influenced the Roman cult of Sol Invictus. Christianity replaced paganism by the 4th century, and Islam followed the 7th-century Arab conquest. The city declined after destruction by Timur in 1400, becoming a small village until modern archaeological work began.

## Terms
- **Colonia**: A Roman colonial settlement, often granted to cities as a mark of honor and integration into the empire.
- **Boule**: The Greek term for a city council or senate, which in Palmyra was a gathering of tribal elders.
- **Clibanarii**: Heavily armored cavalry, a key component of the Palmyrene army.
- **Ginnaye**: Lesser Palmyrene deities, similar to tutelary spirits or the Roman *genius*, who guarded people and places.
- **Loculi**: Burial niches in Palmyrene tombs where the dead were placed at full length.
- **Agora**: A public gathering space; Palmyra's resembled an Eastern caravanserai more than a Greek civic hub.
- **Strategos**: A general or military commander appointed by the Palmyrene council.
- **Tariff Court**: The building in Palmyra's agora where merchants paid taxes on goods, as inscribed on the 137 CE Tariff Law slab.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Ethnic Identity**: Scholars debate whether the Palmyrenes considered themselves a distinct ethnicity, Arameans, or Arabs, with evidence supporting a unique Palmyrene identity.
- **Language Disappearance**: The reasons for the rapid decline of the Palmyrene Aramaic language after 273 CE are unclear, with theories ranging from Roman suppression to social reorganization.
- **Sol Invictus Connection**: The link between the Palmyrene sun god Malakbel and the Roman imperial cult of Sol Invictus promoted by Aurelian is inferred but not definitively proven.
- **Founding Legends**: The biblical association of Palmyra with King Solomon is considered a conflation with another city and does not match archaeological evidence.
