# Pig

## Overview
The domestic pig (Sus domesticus) is an omnivorous, domesticated mammal of the genus Sus, raised globally for meat (pork), leather, and increasingly as a model organism in medicine. It is one of the most numerous large mammals on Earth, with approximately 1.5 billion individuals slaughtered annually for food. Pigs were independently domesticated twice during the Neolithic—once in the Near East and once in China—and have since spread across the globe through human migration and trade, profoundly shaping agriculture, culture, and public health.

## Timeline
- **~11,400 BP** — Pigs present in Cyprus, indicating Near Eastern domestication
- **~8,000 BP** — Independent domestication begins in China
- **~8,500 BP** — Pigs arrive in Europe from the Near East
- **~4000 BP** — Austronesian peoples transport pigs to Island Southeast Asia and Oceania
- **16th century** — Spanish introduce pigs to the Americas during the Columbian Exchange
- **2021** — Genetically modified pig donates first organ to a human

## Body

### Taxonomy and Evolution
The domestic pig is classified either as a subspecies of the wild boar (Sus scrofa domesticus) or as a distinct species (Sus domesticus), depending on taxonomic authority. Carl Linnaeus named the wild boar Sus scrofa in 1758, while Johann Christian Polycarp Erxleben classified the domestic form separately in 1777. Phylogenetic analysis using mitochondrial DNA supports close relation to other Sus species.

### Domestication and Spread
Pigs were domesticated independently in the Near East (Tigris Basin) and China around 8,000 years ago. When Near Eastern pigs reached Europe, they interbred extensively with local wild boar, reducing Near Eastern genetic ancestry to less than 5% over 3,000 years while retaining domesticated traits. A third domestication event occurred in Europe via local wild boar. Austronesian seafarers carried pigs to Island Southeast Asia and Oceania around 4,000 years ago; these introduced pigs maintained a genetically distinct "Pacific Clade." During the 16th-century Columbian Exchange, Spanish explorers brought pigs to the Americas, where many escaped and became feral.

### Anatomy and Physiology
Adult pigs typically weigh 140–300 kg, though some breeds exceed this range. They have a specialized snout with a prenasal bone and cartilaginous disk for digging. Their dental formula is 3.1.4.3 / 3.1.4.3 (44 teeth total), with rear teeth adapted for crushing and male canines forming tusks. Pigs lack effective sweat glands and regulate temperature through wallowing and behavioral means. Their thermoneutral zone is 16–22 °C. They possess both apocrine and limited eccrine glands and are among four mammalian species with genetic mutations conferring snake venom resistance.

### Reproduction
Females reach sexual maturity at 3–12 months and cycle every 18–24 days if unbred. Gestation lasts 112–120 days. Sows build nests before farrowing, using twigs and leaves, triggered by hormonal changes (rising prolactin, falling progesterone). Piglets compete for anterior teats, establishing stable dominance hierarchies. Nursing involves complex vocal and sensory interactions, with milk ejection linked to oxytocin release. Medieval European sows farrowed once yearly; by the 19th century, twice yearly became common. Lifespan averages about 27 years.

### Behavior
Pigs live in small groups of ~8–10 individuals and seek physical contact through huddling. They walk ~4 km daily when free-ranging and feed continuously, unlike ruminants. Rooting is an instinctual behavior used for foraging and communication. Pigs demonstrate advanced intelligence comparable to dogs, including self-recognition in mirrors, maze-solving, and symbolic communication. They have panoramic vision (~310°), acute hearing, and a highly developed sense of smell exploited in truffle hunting.

### Agriculture and Economy
China leads global pork production (55 million tonnes in 2023), followed by the EU (22.8 million tonnes) and the US (12.5 million tonnes). Industrial farming dominates in developed nations, raising welfare concerns over practices like tail docking, castration without anesthesia, and gestation crates. Most US pigs receive ractopamine to promote muscle growth. Climate change poses risks through heat stress, particularly in European farms.

### Health and Disease
Pigs host numerous pathogens affecting productivity and human health, including African Swine Fever, Nipah virus, and porcine coronaviruses. Parasites like Taenia solium and Trichinella spiralis can transmit to humans via undercooked pork. Intensive farming contributes to antibiotic resistance and environmental pollution through waste lagoons containing nitrogen, phosphorus, heavy metals, and pharmaceuticals.

### Cultural Significance
Pigs feature prominently in art, literature, and religion since Neolithic times. Romans revered pork as the finest meat. Medieval European carnivals celebrated swine, especially in cities like Bologna and Nuremberg. Pork consumption is forbidden in Islam and Judaism, while pigs are sacred in some other traditions. Miniature breeds are sometimes kept as pets despite behavioral challenges.

## Terms
- **Tusk**: Continuously growing canine tooth in male pigs, sharpened by grinding.
- **Thermoneutral Zone**: Temperature range (16–22 °C) where pigs maintain body heat without extra energy expenditure.
- **Estrus**: Period of female fertility; lasts 2–3 days, recurring every 18–24 days if not bred.
- **Standing Heat**: Reflexive standing response in sows triggered by boar pheromones (androstenol).
- **Farrowing**: Process of giving birth to piglets.
- **Ractopamine**: Beta-agonist drug used in US swine production to increase muscle mass and weight gain.
- **Wallowing**: Behavior of coating the body in mud or water to aid thermoregulation and parasite control.
- **Pacific Clade**: Genetically distinct mitochondrial lineage of pigs introduced by Austronesian peoples to Oceania.
- **Charcuterie**: Cured and preserved meat products derived from pork, including ham, bacon, and sausages.
- **Galactose-alpha-1,3-galactose**: Carbohydrate antigen removed from donor pigs to prevent immune rejection in xenotransplantation.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Species vs. Subspecies Status**: Whether the domestic pig constitutes a separate species (Sus domesticus) or a subspecies of wild boar (Sus scrofa domesticus) remains contested among taxonomists.
- **Color Vision**: The extent of pigment-based color vision in pigs is debated, though retinal cone cells sensitive to blue and green wavelengths suggest some capacity.
- **Wallowing Function**: While traditionally viewed as thermoregulatory, wallowing may also serve sun protection, parasite control, and scent-marking functions.
- **Timing of Double Farrowing**: The shift from annual to biannual farrowing in European pigs during the 19th century lacks precise historical documentation.
- **Environmental Impact Trade-offs**: Though pork emits fewer greenhouse gases than beef, its overall sustainability relative to plant proteins and poultry continues to be evaluated.
