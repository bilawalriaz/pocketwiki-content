# James Chadwick

## Overview
Sir James Chadwick (1891–1974) was a British experimental physicist whose 1932 discovery of the neutron revolutionized nuclear physics and enabled the development of atomic weapons. His work provided the key to understanding nuclear structure and chain reactions, and he later played a critical leadership role in the Allied atomic bomb project during World War II.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Chadwick studied physics at the University of Manchester under Ernest Rutherford, graduating with First Class Honours in 1911. He earned his MSc in 1913 and, with an 1851 Research Fellowship, went to Berlin to study beta radiation under Hans Geiger. Using a Geiger counter, he showed beta radiation produced a continuous spectrum, not discrete lines. Interned in Germany during World War I, he conducted experiments in a camp laboratory. After the war, he followed Rutherford to the Cavendish Laboratory at Cambridge, earning his PhD in 1921.

### Career at Cambridge and Liverpool
At the Cavendish Laboratory, Chadwick served as Rutherford's assistant director of research for over a decade, helping train physicists like John Cockcroft and Mark Oliphant. He continued probing the nucleus, noting anomalies in the proton-electron model of nuclear structure. In 1935, he moved to the University of Liverpool, where he overhauled the physics laboratory and built a cyclotron, using part of his Nobel Prize money to fund it. He anticipated that neutrons and radioactive isotopes could be used in cancer treatment.

### Discovery of the Neutron
In 1932, Chadwick investigated puzzling radiation from beryllium bombarded by alpha particles, as reported by Walther Bothe and Herbert Becker. When Frédéric and Irène Joliot-Curie showed this radiation could knock protons from paraffin, Chadwick hypothesized it was a neutral particle—the neutron, long theorized by Rutherford. After two weeks of focused experiments using a simple apparatus, he confirmed its existence. His discovery resolved nuclear spin anomalies and explained how neutrons, uncharged and thus not repelled by nuclei, could penetrate atoms and enable nuclear reactions. He later measured the neutron's mass with Maurice Goldhaber, finding it slightly greater than a proton-electron pair.

### World War II and the Atomic Bomb
In 1939, Chadwick was asked to assess the feasibility of an atomic bomb. His research at Liverpool, under constant threat of bombing, helped confirm the small critical mass of uranium-235. In 1941, he wrote the final MAUD Report, which persuaded the U.S. government to launch a major bomb program. Following the 1943 Quebec Agreement, he led the British Mission to the Manhattan Project, earning the trust of General Leslie Groves. He had access to nearly all American facilities and helped integrate British scientists into the project. He witnessed the Trinity test in 1945 and later advocated for Britain's own nuclear weapons program.

### Later Life
After the war, Chadwick served as a scientific advisor to the UN Atomic Energy Commission. He returned to academia in 1948 as Master of Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge, where he worked to enhance its research reputation. He remained a taciturn and private figure, deeply affected by the moral weight of his work, and died in 1974.

## Terms
- **Neutron**: A subatomic particle with no electric charge and a mass slightly greater than a proton, found in the nucleus of atoms.
- **Cyclotron**: A type of particle accelerator that uses a magnetic field to accelerate charged particles in a spiral path, used to create nuclear reactions.
- **MAUD Report**: A 1941 British scientific report concluding that an atomic bomb was feasible, which directly influenced the U.S. to begin its bomb program.
- **Tube Alloys**: The codename for the British atomic bomb research project during World War II.
- **Manhattan Project**: The codename for the American-led effort to develop the first atomic bombs during World War II.
- **Critical mass**: The minimum amount of fissile material (like uranium-235) needed to sustain a nuclear chain reaction.
- **Fission**: The splitting of a heavy atomic nucleus into two lighter nuclei, releasing a large amount of energy.
- **Geiger counter**: An instrument for detecting and measuring ionizing radiation, developed by Hans Geiger.

## Debates and open questions
- The exact nature of the neutron remained unclear after its discovery; physicists like Niels Bohr and Werner Heisenberg debated whether it was a fundamental particle or a proton-electron composite.
- Precise measurements of the neutron's mass in 1933–34 yielded conflicting results from different laboratories before Chadwick and Goldhaber's method provided a more accurate value.
- Chadwick's initial caution about the atomic bomb's feasibility contrasted with later assessments, reflecting the evolving theoretical understanding of fission and chain reactions in the early 1940s.
