# Saxons

## Overview
The Saxons were a Germanic people of early medieval northern Germany whose name became synonymous with coastal raiders in Roman times and later with a powerful duchy within the Frankish Empire. Their history is central to the formation of medieval Europe, involving conflict with Rome, migration to Britain, and a protracted conquest and Christianization by Charlemagne that integrated them into the Carolingian and later Holy Roman Empires.

## Timeline
- **c. 2nd century AD** — Possible first mention as "Saxones" in Ptolemy's *Geographia* (disputed).
- **3rd–4th centuries** — "Saxons" appear as a term for coastal raiders attacking the Roman Empire from north of the Rhine.
- **409/410** — Britain is ravaged by Saxon invaders; Romano-British officials are expelled.
- **441–442** — British provinces are reported to be "reduced to Saxon rule."
- **c. 531** — Franks conquer the kingdom of Thuringia, a neighbor of the Saxons.
- **772–804** — Charlemagne's Saxon Wars result in the conquest and forced Christianization of the continental Saxons.
- **804** — Saxony is incorporated into the Carolingian Empire as a "stem duchy."
- **10th century** — Saxon dukes become kings and emperors of Germany (e.g., Otto I).
- **1180** — The Duchy of Saxony is divided after Duke Henry the Lion's defiance.
- **15th century** — The name "Saxony" is transferred to the eastern lands of the House of Wettin.

## Body

### Origins and Early Mentions
The name "Saxon" first appears in Roman records to describe coastal raiders from north of the Rhine, a term similar to the later "Viking." These raiders included Frisians, Angles, and Jutes. A possible earlier reference is in Ptolemy's 2nd-century work, which mentions a tribe called "Saxones" north of the Elbe, though some manuscripts read "Axones." Historians debate whether this is a genuine early mention or a scribal error.

### Conflict with Rome and Migration
From the 3rd century, Saxons were noted as raiders using boats. The Roman defensive system known as the *Litus Saxonicum* ("Saxon Shore") was built along the coasts of Britain and Gaul. In the 5th century, Saxon raiding contributed to the end of Roman rule in Britain, where they were later invited as *foederati* (allied troops) before revolting. The term "Saxon" at this time likely described a broad group of northern raiders rather than a single tribe.

### Merovingian Period and Early Frankish Conflicts
Before the 8th century, the continental Saxons were not politically unified and were likely ruled by regional "satraps." The Franks, particularly the Merovingian dynasty, fought numerous campaigns against them. In 556, the Frankish king Chlothar I ravaged Saxony and Thuringia. By the 7th century, Saxons were paying tribute to Frankish kings and were sometimes used as military allies.

### Charlemagne's Saxon Wars and Christianization
The Saxons were finally conquered by Charlemagne after a long series of annual campaigns (772–804). The wars aimed to subjugate and forcibly convert them to Christianity. Their sacred pillar, the *Irminsul*, was destroyed in 772. Despite promises of conversion, the Saxons repeatedly revolted when Charlemagne was distracted. The conflict ended with the Saxons renouncing their pagan customs and being incorporated into the Frankish Empire.

### The Duchy of Saxony and Later History
After conquest, Saxony became a Carolingian stem duchy. Its early dukes expanded eastward at the expense of Slavic peoples. In the 10th century, Saxon dukes became the first Holy Roman Emperors. The duchy was divided in 1180. Later, the name "Saxony" was transferred to the eastern lands of the House of Wettin, creating a distinction between the original "Lower Saxony" and the new "Upper Saxony."

### Language and Social Structure
The Old Saxon language, attested from the 8th century, is a North Sea Germanic language related to Old English. Society was rigidly divided into three castes: the noble *edhilingui*, the free *frilingi*, and the subject *lazzi*. The *Lex Saxonum* (Saxon law) regulated this society, forbidding intermarriage and setting high wergilds (payments for injury or death) for nobles. An annual council at Marklo involved all castes in lawmaking and war decisions, a system Charlemagne abolished.

### Religion
Saxon paganism included worship of deities like Thunaer (Thor), Wodan, and the ancestral god Saxnot. Practices included cremation, sacrifices, and veneration of sacred groves and the *Irminsul*. Christianization was led by English missionaries but was enforced violently under Charlemagne. Resistance persisted, notably in the 9th-century Stellinga revolt, which sought to restore old social and religious structures.

## Terms
- **Stem Duchy**: A major duchy within the Carolingian and later Holy Roman Empires, based on traditional tribal territories.
- **Satrap**: A regional ruler or governor; used to describe the pre-unification Saxon leaders.
- **Litus Saxonicum**: The "Saxon Shore," a Roman defensive system of forts along the coasts of Britain and Gaul.
- **Foederati**: Barbarian groups allied to the Roman Empire by treaty, often serving as troops.
- **Irminsul**: A sacred pillar or tree in Saxon paganism, destroyed by Charlemagne in 772.
- **Wergild**: A monetary compensation paid to the family of a victim for a crime, varying by social status.
- **Edhilingui/Frilingi/Lazzi**: The three main castes in Saxon society: nobles, freemen, and subject laborers.
- **Lex Saxonum**: The body of law governing the Saxons after their conquest by the Franks.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Ptolemy's Reference**: Scholars debate whether the "Saxones" in Ptolemy's 2nd-century work refer to the later Saxons or are a manuscript error for another tribe like the Aviones.
- **Early Identity**: It is unclear when "Saxon" shifted from a general term for northern raiders to a specific ethnic or political identity for the continental people.
- **Political Unity**: The continental Saxons likely lacked political unity before the 8th-century conflicts with the Franks, but the exact nature of their pre-conquest social organization is debated.
