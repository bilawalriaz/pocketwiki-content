# Sindh

## Overview
Sindh is a major province in southeastern Pakistan, historically significant as the cradle of the Indus Valley Civilization and the gateway for Islam into the Indian subcontinent. Its identity is deeply shaped by the Indus River, a distinct Sufi-influenced culture, and a complex history of successive empires and local dynasties. Today, it is Pakistan's second-most populous province and its economic hub, with Karachi as its capital and the nation's financial center.

## Timeline
- **c. 2500 BCE** — Mohenjo-daro, a major city of the Indus Valley Civilization, is built in Sindh.
- **518 BCE** — The Achaemenid Empire captures Sindh, establishing the satrapy of Hindush.
- **325 BCE** — Alexander the Great conquers parts of Sindh.
- **712 CE** — Muhammad bin Qasim conquers Sindh for the Umayyad Caliphate, introducing Islam.
- **9th century** — The semi-independent Habbari Emirate emerges in Sindh.
- **1335** — The Samma dynasty overthrows the Soomras, establishing the Sindh Sultanate.
- **Late 16th century** — The Mughal Empire conquers Sindh.
- **1783** — The Talpur dynasty begins its rule over Sindh.
- **1843** — The British East India Company annexes Sindh.
- **1936** — Sindh becomes a separate province within British India.
- **1947** — Sindh becomes part of the newly independent Dominion of Pakistan.
- **1970** — Sindh is restored as a province after a period in West Pakistan.

## Body

### Ancient and Medieval History
The region's settled history begins with the Indus Valley Civilization, exemplified by the city of Mohenjo-daro (c. 2500 BCE). After its decline, the area was known as Sindhu-Sauvīra. Sindh was subsequently conquered by a succession of empires: the Achaemenids, Macedonians, Mauryans, Indo-Greeks, Indo-Scythians, and Sasanians. Local rule re-emerged with the Rai and Chach dynasties. The Muslim conquest in 712 CE under the Umayyad Caliphate marked a pivotal shift, establishing a caliphal province and initiating the region's Islamic period. Later medieval rule included the semi-independent Habbari Emirate, followed by the Soomra, Samma, and Arghun dynasties, often under the suzerainty of the Delhi Sultanate.

### Mughal, British, and Colonial Rule
The Mughal Empire captured Sindh in the late 16th century. By the early 18th century, the indigenous Kalhora dynasty ruled as Mughal vassals, later succeeded by the Talpur dynasty in 1783. The British East India Company annexed Sindh in 1843, initially incorporating it into the Bombay Presidency. Grievances over neglect led to a movement for separation, which succeeded in 1936 when Sindh became a distinct province. During this period, Sindhi Muslims, influenced by the Khilafat Movement and local Sufi pirs, increasingly supported the Pakistan Movement, with Sindh becoming the first province to endorse the Lahore Resolution in 1940.

### Modern Province and Economy
Sindh became part of Pakistan in 1947. It was amalgamated into West Pakistan in 1955 but restored to provincehood in 1970. The province's economy is the second-largest in Pakistan, dominated by Karachi, the country's largest city and financial hub, which hosts major seaports like Port Qasim. The rest of the province has an agriculture-based economy, producing fruits and vegetables. Sindh is known as the "Gateway of Islam" and is renowned for its distinct culture, strongly influenced by Sufism, which is a key marker of identity for both Sindhi Muslims and Hindus. It is home to UNESCO World Heritage Sites like Mohenjo-daro and the Makli Necropolis.

### Demographics and Culture
Sindh had a population of 55.7 million in the 2023 census. Muslims constitute 90% of the population, with a strong Sufi ethos. The province has Pakistan's highest percentage of Hindus (8.8%), primarily in rural areas. Sindhi is the first language for 60% of the population, followed by Urdu. The culture is celebrated for its art, architecture, and crafts like Ajrak (block-printed shawls) and Sindhi caps. The annual Sindhi Topi Day promotes this heritage.

## Terms
- **Indus Valley Civilization**: A Bronze Age civilization (c. 3300–1300 BCE) that flourished along the Indus River, with major urban centers like Mohenjo-daro in Sindh.
- **Satrapy**: A province or administrative district of the ancient Persian Achaemenid Empire.
- **Sufism**: A mystical branch of Islam emphasizing inner spiritual experience, which has deeply influenced Sindhi culture and identity.
- **Ajrak**: A traditional block-printed shawl or textile from Sindh, featuring intricate geometric and floral patterns.
- **Wadera**: A feudal landlord or rural chieftain in Sindh, often wielding significant political and economic power.
- **Pir**: A Sufi spiritual guide or saint, whose descendants often hold social and political influence in Sindh.
- **Kalhora Dynasty**: A Sindhi Muslim dynasty that ruled Sindh from the early 18th century until 1783, initially as Mughal vassals.
- **Talpur Dynasty**: A Baloch dynasty that ruled Sindh from 1783 until the British annexation in 1843.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise location and extent of the ancient Achaemenid satrapy of "Hindush" is debated, with some scholars placing it in Sindh and others in the Punjab.
- The causes of the decline and abandonment of Mohenjo-daro and the Indus Valley Civilization are not fully settled, with theories including climate change, river shifts, and invasion.
- The exact delineations of territory and power between the Soomra dynasty and contemporary Ghaznavid/Ghurid rulers in 11th-12th century Sindh are not yet fully known.
