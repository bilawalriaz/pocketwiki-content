# Compass

## Overview
A compass is a navigational instrument that indicates direction relative to the Earth's magnetic or rotational poles. The magnetic compass—one of China's Four Great Inventions—originated as a divination tool in the Han dynasty (since c. 206 BC) and was adopted for navigation by the Song dynasty (11th century), reaching Europe by c. 1190. It operates by aligning a magnetized element with the horizontal component of Earth's magnetic field to point toward magnetic north. Because magnetic north diverges from true (geographic) north by a variable angle called **magnetic declination**, and local ferrous materials cause **deviation**, users must apply corrections for precise navigation. Modern variants include liquid-damped handheld models, gyrocompasses (which find true north via Earth's rotation), and GPS compasses (which calculate heading from satellite positions).

## Body

### Operating Principles
A magnetic compass aligns a magnetized needle or card with the horizontal component of Earth's magnetic field. The field exerts torque on the needle, pulling its north pole toward Earth's North magnetic pole (a magnetic south pole geographically). The needle mounts on a low-friction pivot—often a jewel bearing—allowing it to settle into equilibrium. Directions are expressed in degrees clockwise from north (0°), enabling azimuths or bearings. **Magnetic declination** (variation) is the angle between magnetic north and true north (direction to the Geographic North Pole); it varies by location and changes over time (**geomagnetic secular variation**). **Deviation** is error caused by local magnetic fields from ferrous metal or electric currents. Maps provide local declination; compasses may have adjustable declination or compensating magnets.

### Historical Development
**Natural Magnet:** Greek philosopher Thales (6th century BC) noted lodestone properties. The name *magnet* may come from lodestones found in Magnesia, Anatolia. The ancient Indian medical text *Sushruta Samhita* describes using magnetic properties of the lodestone to remove arrows embedded in a person's body. Chinese texts (*Guiguzi*, 4th century BC; *Lüshi Chunqiu*, 2nd century BC) describe magnetism and attraction.  
**Divination Device:** Han dynasty geomancers (2nd century BC) used a "south-pointing spoon" of lodestone on a bronze plate to align north–south for divination. *Lunheng* (20–100 AD) notes needle attraction. No magnetite spoon has been found in Han tombs.  
**Navigational Compass:** By 1088, Song dynasty Chinese used magnetized iron needles (struck with lodestone) for navigation, per Shen Kuo. Dry compasses appeared c. 1300 in Europe and the Islamic world. A 4th-century Indian iron-fish-in-oil compass is claimed but debated; evidence before the 11th century is considered weak.

### Modern Design and Construction
**Liquid-Filled Compasses:** Most modern compasses suspend a magnetized needle or dial in a liquid-filled capsule (lamp oil, mineral oil, alcohol). Liquid damps oscillation, stabilizes the needle, and allows underwater operation. Flexible diaphragms or capsule materials accommodate thermal expansion. Phosphorescent markings enable night reading.  
**Orienteering/Baseplate Compasses:** Feature a rotating capsule with magnetized needle, orienting "box" for aligning with magnetic north, transparent baseplate with map orienting lines and direction-of-travel (DOT) indicator, and a degree-marked bezel. Extras: map/romer scales, sighting mechanisms (mirror/prism), global needles (gimbal-mounted for hemisphere independence), rare-earth magnets for fast settling, adjustable declination, inclinometers.  
**Military Compasses:** U.S. M-1950 lensatic compass uses a magnetized card with electromagnetic induction damping (not liquid) and a deep-well design for global use up to 8° tilt. A needle lock reduces wear. Tritium (120 mCi) with phosphors provides self-luminous illumination (half-life ~12 years). Card compasses require a separate protractor for map bearings.  
**Marine Compasses:** Two+ magnets on a pivoting card read against a lubber line (fixed reference). Traditionally 32 rhumbs; modern use degrees. Gimbal-mounted in a binnacle to stay horizontal.  
**Construction:** A magnetic rod is made by rubbing iron with lodestone or aligning and striking/tempering (weaker). The rod pivots on a low-friction surface (cork in water historically; dry pivot c. 1300). Cards evolved from cardinal points to 24 points (China), 32 points (Europe), then 360° (civilian), 400 grads (19th century Europe), 6400 mils (NATO "millieme"), or 6000 mils (Soviet/Warsaw Pact). Needles are balanced for one of five magnetic zones to prevent dip-induced sticking; some use sliding counterweights ("riders") for multi-zone use.

### Errors and Limitations
**Magnetic Latitude:** Near magnetic poles, declination becomes extreme, the needle dips (magnetic inclination), and the compass drifts or sticks.  
**Local Interference:** Ferrous deposits, steel structures, engines, MRIs, and current-carrying conductors create fields that deflect the needle. Compensating magnets help but cannot eliminate all deviation.  
**Acceleration/Deceleration Errors:** In vehicles/aircraft, inertia tilts the needle, altering the indicated heading depending on hemisphere and force direction. Compensating magnets worsen this.  
**Turning Error:** Turning from east/west headings causes the compass to lag or lead.  
**Usage Precautions:** Must be held level; tilt causes casing contact and false readings. Avoid magnets, electronics (fields exceed Earth's 0.5 gauss), and magnetic rocks (e.g., magnetite). Test for interference by moving away and observing needle movement.

### Variants and Specialty Compasses
- **Thumb Compass:** Minimal/no degree markings; oversized needle for rapid map orientation in orienteering. Transparent base for map reading. Rare-earth magnets achieve ≤1 second settling.
- **Earth Inductor Compass:** Uses electromagnetic induction; Earth's field acts as induction field for a generator whose output varies with orientation.
- **Vertical Card Aircraft Compass:** Gear-driven dial from a shaft-mounted magnet; eddy-current damping cup reduces oscillation; reduces dipping errors.
- **Electronic (MEMS) Compasses:** Solid-state sensors (2–3 axis) measure Earth's field; microprocessor outputs digital/analog heading. In phones, clocks, etc.
- **Qibla Compass:** Indicates direction to Mecca for Muslim prayer.
- **Optical/Prismatic Compass:** Liquid-damped floating dial with integral optical sight for surveyors, cavers, foresters; reads to fractions of a degree; often tripod-mounted.
- **Trough Compass:** Rectangular box for land surveying with plane tables.
- **Luopan:** Feng shui compass.

### Non-Magnetic Compasses
**Gyrocompass:** Fast-spinning wheel (electrically powered) uses friction forces to exploit Earth's rotation, finding true north (rotational axis). Advantages: immune to ferromagnetic hulls; finds true north. Used on large ships; magnetic compass as backup. Electronic fluxgate compasses increasingly used on smaller vessels. Fluxgate electronic compasses can be calibrated automatically, and can also be programmed with the correct local compass variation so as to indicate the true heading.
**GPS Compass:** Two+ antennae + inertial motion unit (IMU) calculate positions to derive heading (0.02° accuracy, seconds startup). Determines true north; unaffected by magnetic perturbations. Cheaper, faster, better in polar regions, less vibration-sensitive than gyrocompasses. Depends on GPS satellites (vulnerable to jamming/solar storms). Single-antenna GPS receivers derive direction from sequential positions if moving. Gyrocompasses remain in military/submarine use where GPS/magnetic fail.

### Use and Navigation
**Basic Use:** Compass points to magnetic north (~1,000 miles from geographic North Pole). True north = magnetic bearing ± declination ± deviation. Casual users can ignore declination unless >20° variance. Recording distances and magnetic bearings allows return to start.
**Map and Compass (Terrain Association):** To take a true bearing: place compass edge on map connecting current location to destination; rotate orienting lines to align with map longitude/vertical margin (true north), ignoring needle; read bearing at DOT line. For magnetic bearing, adjust for declination (or use preset declination). To follow a course: set bearing, hold compass level, turn body until needle overlays orienting arrow, follow DOT arrow. To verify target: point DOT arrow at target, orient needle to orienting arrow, read magnetic bearing; convert to true if needed. Declination values from map or online.

## Terms
- ****Magnetic Declination (Variation)**** — Angle between magnetic north and true (geographic) north at a given location; varies spatially and temporally.
- ****Deviation**** — Compass error caused by local magnetic fields (ferrous metal, electric currents) in the immediate environment.
- ****Magnetic Inclination (Dip)**** — Vertical angle of Earth's magnetic field lines; causes needle to tilt up/down, especially near magnetic poles.
- ****Lubber Line**** — Fixed reference mark on a compass bowl or card indicating the vessel's heading.
- ****Rhumbs**** — Traditional 32 equally spaced points on a marine compass card (11.25° each).
- ****Gimbal**** — Pivoted support allowing a compass to remain horizontal despite vessel tilt.
- ****Binnacle**** — Housing for a ship's compass, often containing corrector magnets and iron balls.
- ****Millieme (Mil)**** — Angular unit ≈ 1/6400 of a circle (NATO); 1 mil subtends ~1 m at 1 km.
- ****MEMS Compass**** — Microelectromechanical systems compass using solid-state magnetic sensors and a microprocessor.
- ****Tritium Illumination**** — Radioluminescent lighting using tritium gas (half-life ~12 years) and phosphors; requires no external recharge.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Pre-11th Century Navigation:** Whether a 4th-century Southern Indian iron-fish-in-oil compass was used for navigation; most historians consider evidence before the Song dynasty (11th century) very weak.
- **Han Dynasty Spoon:** Literary references describe a "south-pointing spoon" for divination, but no physical magnetite spoon has been excavated from Han tombs.
- **Transmission Route:** Exact pathway of compass knowledge from China to the Islamic world and Europe (c. 1190–13th century) remains inferred rather than documented.
- **Polar Navigation Limits:** No magnetic compass works reliably near the magnetic poles; operational boundaries depend on instrument design and local field geometry.
