# Nagarjuna

## Overview
Nāgārjuna (c. 150–250 CE) was an Indian Buddhist philosopher and founder of the Madhyamaka ("Middle Way") school. He is considered the most important Buddhist philosopher after the Buddha, whose writings on emptiness (śūnyatā) became foundational for Mahayana Buddhism across Asia. His philosophy systematically deconstructs all metaphysical theories of inherent existence (svabhāva), arguing that all phenomena are dependently arisen and empty of independent nature.

## Timeline
- **c. 150–250 CE** — Conventional dating for Nāgārjuna's life
- **1st–2nd century CE** — Mahayana movement emerges as a minority within Buddhism
- **c. 2nd century CE** — Nāgārjuna possibly advises a Satavahana king in the Deccan Plateau
- **344–413 CE** — Kumārajīva produces earliest Chinese translations and biographical accounts of Nāgārjuna
- **11th century onwards** — Tibetan hagiographical accounts of Nāgārjuna begin appearing
- **6th century CE** — Bhavaviveka first introduces the term "Madhyamaka"

## Body

### Life and Historical Context
Very little is reliably known of Nāgārjuna's life. Most scholars place him in South India between the 1st and 3rd centuries CE. The earliest biographical sources, associated with the translator Kumārajīva (344–413), are sparse and hagiographical. Some scholars, notably Joseph Walser, argue Nāgārjuna was an advisor to a Satavahana king, possibly Yajña Śrī Śātakarṇi (c. second half of the 2nd century). Walser also suggests he likely wrote in a mixed monastery in the Andhra region where Mahāyānists were a minority.

Traditional accounts depict Nāgārjuna as a great revivalist who saved Mahayana during a period of decadence. Later Chinese and Tibetan hagiographies add miraculous elements, including retrieving Prajñāpāramitā sutras from the realm of nāgas (supernatural serpent beings). These traditions also associate him with alchemy, mantras, and esoteric Buddhism, though these attributions are historically unverifiable. The name "Nāgārjuna" was popular, leading to confusion with later figures, including an Ayurvedic writer and a Tantric Buddhist author.

### Core Philosophy: Emptiness and Dependent Origination
Nāgārjuna's central teaching is that all phenomena are empty (śūnya) of svabhāva (inherent existence, intrinsic nature). This is not nihilism but is synonymous with the Buddhist doctrine of dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda): things arise in dependence on causes and conditions, not by their own power. His philosophy is called Madhyamaka because it avoids the metaphysical extremes of eternalism (śāśvata) and annihilationism (uccheda).

In his masterwork, the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (MMK), Nāgārjuna dismantles substance theories across topics like causation, time, and personal identity. He uses the tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi)—a logical schema of four propositions—to negate all possible views on a subject, demonstrating their inherent contradictions. For example, in analyzing causation, he refutes that things arise from themselves, from others, from both, or from neither, showing that cause and effect are mutually interdependent and conceptually constructed.

### Epistemology and the Two Truths
Nāgārjuna critiques the Nyāya school's theory of valid cognition (pramāṇa). He argues that epistemic instruments (like perception) cannot be self-justifying, nor can they be validated by their objects without circularity. Thus, knowledge is conventionally effective but lacks ultimate, independent grounding.

This leads to his doctrine of two truths: conventional truth (saṃvṛtisatya) and ultimate truth (paramārtha satya). The ultimate truth is that all things, including emptiness itself, are empty. As scholar Mark Siderits summarizes, "the ultimate truth is that there is no ultimate truth." Conventional truths—language, concepts, everyday practices—are the only means to engage with the world, but they do not reflect an independent reality. Emptiness is a corrective tool to dissolve false views of svabhāva, not a metaphysical foundation.

### Ethics and the Bodhisattva Path
While known for philosophy, Nāgārjuna also wrote ethical works like the *Ratnāvalī* (Precious Garland). He outlines the bodhisattva path, distinguishing between the "Dharma of ascendance" (abhyudaya)—practices leading to good rebirths—and the "Dharma of transcendence" (naiḥśreyasa)—wisdom leading to nirvana. Faith and merit-making support the cultivation of wisdom. His ethics are grounded in emptiness, which dissolves the self/other divide and enables universal compassion. Virtuous actions are skillful means (upāya) that ultimately unravel all karma.

### Engagement with Other Traditions
Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka developed as an internal critique of Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika realism. He targeted their ontological categories (substances, qualities) and epistemological foundations, arguing they cannot sustain claims to intrinsic existence. This dialectical engagement forced refinements in both Buddhist and Brahmanical systems. He was also conversant with non-Mahayana Buddhist Abhidharma schools, often attacking their realist tendencies.

## Terms
- **Śūnyatā (Emptiness):** The doctrine that all phenomena lack inherent existence (svabhāva); synonymous with dependent origination.
- **Svabhāva (Inherent Existence):** An unchanging, independent substance or nature; the primary target of Nāgārjuna's critique.
- **Pratītyasamutpāda (Dependent Origination):** The principle that all phenomena arise in dependence on causes and conditions.
- **Madhyamaka (Middle Way):** The philosophical school founded by Nāgārjuna that avoids metaphysical extremes.
- **Pramāṇa (Valid Cognition):** Means of knowledge (e.g., perception, inference) in Indian epistemology.
- **Catuṣkoṭi (Tetralemma):** A logical schema of four propositions used to deconstruct views.
- **Saṃvṛtisatya (Conventional Truth):** The truth of everyday language and concepts.
- **Paramārtha Satya (Ultimate Truth):** The truth that all things are empty; ultimately, that there is no ultimate truth.
- **Bodhisattva:** One who seeks Buddhahood for the benefit of all beings.
- **Upāya (Skillful Means):** Compassionate actions that guide beings toward liberation.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Authenticity of Works:** There is lively controversy over which texts are genuinely by Nāgārjuna. The Chinese Canon contains over 22 attributed works, the Tibetan Tanjur over 140. Scholars like Christian Lindtner, David Burton, and Jan Westerhoff propose different "core" lists, with the MMK, *Vigrahavyāvartanī*, *Śūnyatāsaptati*, *Yuktiṣāṣṭika*, *Vaidalyaprakaraṇa*, and *Ratnāvalī* being most commonly accepted.
- **Interpretation of Emptiness:** Scholars debate whether Nāgārjuna is a semantic anti-dualist (Garfield, Siderits), a Kantian idealist (Murti), a nihilist, a relativist, or a pragmatist (Kalupahana).
- **Relationship to Earlier Buddhism:** Some scholars (e.g., Choong Mun-keat) argue Nāgārjuna defended a doctrine already present in early sources, while others see him as an innovator.
- **Influence and Connections:** Debates continue about possible influences from Greek Pyrrhonism (McEvilley, Beckwith) and the extent of his engagement with specific Buddhist sects (e.g., Mahāsāṃghikas).
