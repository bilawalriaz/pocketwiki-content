# Spanish flu

## Overview
The 1918–1920 flu pandemic, also known as the Great Influenza epidemic, was an exceptionally deadly global outbreak caused by the H1N1 subtype of the influenza A virus. It infected an estimated 500 million people, about one-third of the world's population, with death toll estimates ranging from 17 million to possibly 100 million, making it one of the deadliest pandemics in history. The pandemic occurred near the end of World War I, and its course and impact were deeply intertwined with the war's conditions and global movements.

## Timeline
- **March 1918** — Earliest probable cases documented in Haskell County, Kansas, USA.
- **April 1918** — Cases recorded in France, Germany, and the UK; virus reaches the Western Front.
- **August 1918** — A much deadlier second wave begins, spreading from Boston and Freetown.
- **October 1918** — The month with the highest fatality rate of the entire pandemic.
- **November 1918** — Pandemic activity begins to recede in many areas after the Armistice.
- **January 1919** — A significant third wave peaks in parts of Europe and the United States.
- **December 1919** — A fourth wave begins in Japan and later spreads to other regions.
- **Mid-1920** — The pandemic is largely considered over by the public and governments.

## Body

### Origins and Naming
The pandemic's geographic origin is indeterminate, with competing hypotheses pointing to the United States, Europe, or China. The name "Spanish flu" is a misnomer; it arose because neutral Spain's newspapers freely reported the outbreak, while wartime censors in belligerent nations suppressed news to maintain morale. This created the false impression that Spain was the epicenter. The disease was known by many other associative and descriptive names globally, such as "three-day fever," "purple death," and "the grip."

### Waves and Spread
The pandemic occurred in at least three major global waves. The first wave in early 1918 was relatively mild. The second wave, beginning in late 1918, was far more deadly and was exacerbated by World War I conditions: troop movements, overcrowding in camps and hospitals, malnourishment, and poor hygiene. These factors promoted bacterial superinfection, which killed most victims. A third wave in 1919 and a fourth in 1920 followed, with varying severity across regions. The virus spread rapidly via modern transportation and troop deployments.

### Epidemiology and Mortality
The virus had a basic reproduction number between 2 and 3. Unusually, it disproportionately killed young, healthy adults, possibly due to a "cytokine storm"—an overreaction of the immune system. Most deaths resulted from bacterial pneumonia following the viral infection. Global mortality estimates vary widely, but India was among the hardest-hit regions, with an estimated 12–17 million deaths. Mortality patterns showed significant geographic and social disparities, with higher rates in crowded urban areas and among lower social classes. Some communities, like American Samoa, avoided deaths entirely through effective quarantine.

### Public Health Response and Legacy
Responses included maritime quarantines, social distancing measures (closing schools and public venues), and the use of face masks, though their efficacy was debated. There were no antiviral drugs or antibiotics for secondary infections. Wartime censorship and misinformation hampered public awareness. The pandemic's long-term effects included reduced educational attainment and economic impacts for those exposed in utero. It is often called a "forgotten pandemic" because its toll was overshadowed by World War I and subsequent historical events.

## Terms
- **H1N1**: The subtype of influenza A virus that caused the 1918 pandemic.
- **Cytokine storm**: An overreaction of the immune system that can cause severe tissue damage and was a proposed cause of high mortality in young adults.
- **Bacterial superinfection**: A secondary bacterial infection (often pneumonia) that occurs after a viral infection and was the primary cause of death in many patients.
- **Basic reproduction number (R0)**: The average number of people one infected person will infect in a susceptible population; estimated at 2–3 for this virus.
- **Exonym**: A name for a disease or place used by people from another region or culture (e.g., "Spanish flu" used outside Spain).
- **Endonym**: A name for a disease or place used by people within that region or culture.
- **Phylogenetic analysis**: The study of evolutionary relationships among biological entities, used here to trace the virus's origin.
- **Heliotrope cyanosis**: A blue-violet discoloration of the skin, a severe symptom of the disease's progression.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Geographic Origin**: The precise starting point of the pandemic is debated, with leading hypotheses pointing to Haskell County, Kansas; the military camp at Étaples, France; or China.
- **High Young Adult Mortality**: The reason for the unusually high death rate among young adults is debated, with the cytokine storm theory being prominent but not universally accepted.
- **Mortality Estimates**: The total global death toll is highly contested, with estimates ranging from 17 million to over 100 million, reflecting challenges in historical data.
- **Role of Climate**: The influence of a severe climate anomaly (1914–1919) on the pandemic's severity and spread is a subject of ongoing research.
