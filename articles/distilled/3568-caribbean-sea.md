# Caribbean Sea

## Overview
The Caribbean Sea is a large, tropical sea of the North Atlantic Ocean, located between the Americas. It is a major geographical and geopolitical region, home to numerous islands and bordered by many countries. Its history, ecology, and economy are globally significant, though it faces serious environmental threats.

## Timeline
- **c. 160–180 million years ago** — Caribbean Sea begins forming during the Mesozoic Era.
- **1492** — Christopher Columbus sails into Caribbean waters, initiating European contact and colonization.
- **16th century** — European colonizers distinguish the Caribbean Sea as the "North Sea" from the Pacific "South Sea."
- **1902** — Severe eruption of Mount Pelée causes many casualties.
- **1986** — The Convention for the Protection and Development of the Marine Environment of the Wider Caribbean Region comes into effect.
- **1996** — Belize Barrier Reef is declared a World Heritage Site.
- **2005** — Unusually warm waters begin increasingly threatening coral reefs.
- **2010** — A 7.1-magnitude earthquake strikes Haiti.
- **2012** — A regional study detects heavy metals in Caribbean coastal sediments.

## Body

### Geography and Extent
The Caribbean Sea is one of the world's largest seas, covering about 2,754,000 km². Its deepest point is the Cayman Trough at 7,686 m. It is bounded by the Greater and Lesser Antilles, South America, and Central America. The International Hydrographic Organization defines its precise limits, noting that while Trinidad and Tobago and Barbados are on the continental shelf, they are considered part of the Atlantic Ocean.

### Geology
The sea lies on the Caribbean Plate and is separated from the ocean by island arcs. The younger Lesser Antilles arc was formed by the collision of the South American and Caribbean Plates and includes active volcanoes. The larger northern islands (Cuba, Hispaniola, Jamaica, Puerto Rico) lie on an older arc. The seafloor is divided into five basins by underwater ridges and contains two major oceanic trenches, making the area prone to earthquakes and tsunamis.

### Oceanography and Climate
The Caribbean's hydrology is highly homogeneous, with stable surface temperatures. It has experienced a cooling phase until 1974, followed by a warming phase. The climate is tropical, ranging from rainforest to savanna and arid zones. The region is frequently impacted by tropical cyclones, with a hurricane season from June to November.

### Ecology
The Caribbean hosts about 9% of the world's coral reefs, including the world's second-largest barrier reef, the Mesoamerican Barrier Reef. These reefs are vital ecosystems but are severely threatened by coral bleaching due to warm waters. The region has high biodiversity, with many endemic species of plants, fish, reptiles, amphibians, and birds. However, many species, such as sea turtles and certain iguanas, are endangered.

### Economy and Human Activity
Human activity has increased significantly since colonization. The sea is a major oil production area and supports a large fishing industry. Tourism, especially cruise travel and reef-based activities, is a major economic driver. However, human activity contributes to significant pollution, with only an estimated 10% of sewage from Central American and Caribbean island countries being properly treated as of 1993. The region is also famous for rum production.

## Terms
- **Caribbean Plate:** A tectonic plate on which the Caribbean Sea floor lies.
- **Island Arc:** A chain of volcanic islands formed by the collision of tectonic plates.
- **Coral Bleaching:** The loss of symbiotic algae (zooxanthellae) from coral tissues due to stress, often from warm water, causing the coral to turn white and potentially die.
- **Endemic:** A species native and restricted to a certain place.
- **Tropical Cyclone:** A rotating, organized system of clouds and thunderstorms that originates over tropical waters.
- **Upwelling:** The process where cold, nutrient-rich water rises from the ocean depths to the surface.
- **Zooxanthellae:** Microscopic algae living symbiotically within coral polyps, providing them with food and color.
