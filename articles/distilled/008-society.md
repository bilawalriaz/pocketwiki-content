# Society

## Overview

A society is a group of individuals involved in persistent social interaction, typically sharing a spatial or social territory, political authority, and cultural expectations. Societies are characterized by patterns of relationships between individuals who share a distinctive culture and institutions. Human societies are complex and highly cooperative, featuring specialization of labor via social roles, and are governed by societal norms—expectations around acceptable behavior. Societies vary by technology level and economic activity, and larger societies with food surpluses often exhibit stratification. Humans shape society, but society in turn shapes human beings.

## Body

### Etymology and usage

The term "society" refers to a large group living in an ordered community or to the "state of being with other people." It dates to at least 1513, originating from 12th-century French *societe* ("company"), from Latin *societas* ("fellowship," "alliance"), from *socius* ("comrade"). The word "social" derives from Latin *socii* ("allies"), particularly the Italian Socii states, who rebelled against Rome in the Social War of 91–87 BC.

### Conceptions in biology

Humans, bonobos, and chimpanzees are highly social animals, suggesting sociability is hardwired into human nature. Human society differs from chimp and bonobo groups in male parental roles, language use, labor specialization, and building multi-generational "nests" (camps, towns, cities). Some biologists, including E.O. Wilson, categorize humans as eusocial—placing them with ants at the highest sociability level—though others disagree. Social group living may have evolved through group selection in difficult physical environments.

### Conceptions in sociology

Western sociology has three dominant paradigms: functionalism, conflict theory, and symbolic interactionism. Karl Marx held that humans are intrinsically social beings who cannot survive except through social cooperation, and that people enter relations of production "independent of their will." Max Weber defined social action as action that, by virtue of subjective meanings, "takes account of the behavior of others."

**Functionalism** views individuals as working together like organs in a body, creating emergent behavior or "collective consciousness." Auguste Comte and Émile Durkheim believed society constitutes a separate "level" of reality, with individuals as transient occupants of stable social roles.

**Conflict theory** posits that groups interact on the basis of conflict rather than agreement. Marx conceived society as an economic "base" with a "superstructure" of government, family, religion, and culture; the base determines the superstructure, and societal change is driven by conflict between laborers and owners of the means of production.

**Symbolic interactionism** is a microsociological theory focusing on how individuals relate to society through shared language and symbols. In the latter half of the 20th century, theorists viewed society as socially constructed; Peter L. Berger described society as "dialectic"—created by humans, but this creation in turn molds humans.

**Non-Western views**: The Western emphasis has been criticized as Eurocentric. Syed Farid al-Attas cites Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406) and José Rizal (1861–1896). Khaldun saw society as having "meaningful configuration" with hidden causes; he distinguished nomadic life (high social cohesion, or *asabijja*, from kinship and shared defense) from sedentary life (secularization, decreased cohesion, luxury). Rizal argued that Filipino "indolence," used by the Spanish to justify colonization, was actually caused by colonial exploitation and policies discouraging farming.

### Types of societies

Sociologists classify societies by technology into pre-industrial, industrial, and post-industrial. Gerhard Lenski lists hunting and gathering, horticultural, agricultural, and industrial, plus specialized types (fishing, herding). Cultural evolution toward complexity is not unidirectional.

**Pre-industrial** societies use human and animal labor for food production. **Hunter-gatherers** collect wild plants and hunt animals, moving constantly; they form small bands or tribes (usually under 50 people), are relatively egalitarian, decide by consensus, and have no formal political offices—a chief is merely influential. Marshall Sahlins called them the "original affluent society," estimating adults work 3–5 hours daily; others cite high mortality and warfare, while proponents argue their well-being challenges the link between technology and progress. **Pastoral** societies rely on domesticated herds, live nomadically, and contain multiple communities (thousands of people) because open terrain enables political integration; they create food surpluses, specialized labor, and inequality. **Horticultural** societies grow crops in garden plots cleared from jungle or forest, using human labor and simple tools; they rotate plots, allowing permanent or semi-permanent villages, and develop specialized roles (craftspeople, shamans, traders). **Agrarian** societies use the plow (Lenski's distinction) to cultivate large areas, producing larger communities and trade towns. They show extreme class stratification based on landownership, with three contrasts: governing class vs. masses, urban vs. peasant, literate vs. illiterate. Landowners combine government, religious, and military institutions to justify ownership; slavery, serfdom, or peonage is common. Caste systems, as in South Asia, are associated with agrarian societies. Donald Brown suggests modern Western emphasis on personal liberty reacted against agrarian rigidity.

**Industrial** societies, emerging in the 18th-century Industrial Revolution, rely on externally powered machines for mass production. Labor shifts from primary industries (extracting raw materials) to processing raw materials. Industrialization brings population booms, urbanization, and often capitalism with high inequality but high social mobility. Working conditions are harsh, prompting labor unions. Industrial societies have increased power for warfare, government control via information technologies, and environmental impact.

**Post-industrial** societies are dominated by information and services rather than goods production. An **information society** involves significant creation, distribution, and integration of information; the concept dates to the 1930s but now applies to information technologies' effects on education, economy, health, government, warfare, and democracy, including new social forms in cyberspace. A **knowledge society** goes further, transforming information into resources for effective action rather than merely disseminating raw data.

### Characteristics

**Norms and roles**: Social norms are shared standards of acceptable behavior, informal or codified into laws. Social roles are norms and duties tied to social status; functionalists see individuals forming society by occupying roles, while symbolic interactionists see symbols navigating roles. Erving Goffman's dramaturgical lens treats roles as scripts governing interactions.

**Gender and kinship**: Gender roles divide norms, dress, behavior, rights, and power by sex; some argue they arise naturally from sex differences, but they have varied historically. Kinship organizes relationships by consanguinity (parent-child, descendants), affinity (marriage), and fictive ties (godparents, adoptive children). All societies have incest taboos; some have preferential marriage rules.

**Ethnicity**: Ethnic groups share traditions, ancestry, language, history, or culture. There is no generally accepted definition; humans can change group affiliation if advantageous. Ethnicity is separate from race (based on physical characteristics), though both are socially constructed. Ethnic identity is tied to the rise of the nation-state in the 19th–20th centuries.

**Government and politics**: Governments create laws and policies. Early political power distribution depended on fresh water, fertile soil, and climate; denser farming populations led to further governance development. As of 2022, 43% of governments were democracies, 35% autocracies, 22% mixed. The largest international organization is the United Nations with 193 member states.

**Trade and economics**: Trade, the voluntary exchange of goods and services, differentiates humans from other animals and may have given *Homo sapiens* an advantage over Neanderthals, who lacked long-distance trade networks. Early trade involved tool materials like obsidian; antiquity and medieval routes carried food and luxury goods (spice trade). Early economies used gift-giving more than barter; early money included cattle and cowrie shells, evolving to coins, paper, and electronic money. As of 2018, in China, Europe, and the US, the richest tenth hold over seven-tenths of total wealth.

**Conflict**: Whether war is innate or recent is debated. One view holds war evolved to eliminate competitors; humans kill at rates comparable to other primates (high adult killing, low infanticide). Another view holds war is recent, appearing with changing social conditions; evidence suggests warlike behavior became common only about 10,000 years ago. Phylogenetic analysis predicts 2% of human deaths from homicide, matching band societies, but rates vary—societies with legal systems and anti-violence attitudes have about 0.01% homicide.

## Terms

- **Societal norms**: Shared standards of acceptable behavior within a society, informal or codified into laws.
- **Eusocial**: Highest level of sociability in animal ethology; some biologists place humans with ants in this category.
- **Functionalism**: Sociological paradigm viewing society as organs working together to create collective consciousness.
- **Conflict theory**: Paradigm positing social interaction based on conflict between groups or classes.
- **Symbolic interactionism**: Microsociological theory focusing on shared language and symbols in individual-society relations.
- **Asabijja**: Ibn Khaldun's term for social cohesion, high in nomadic life, arising from kinship and shared defense.
- **Horticultural society**: Society growing crops in garden plots with human labor and simple tools, rotating plots.
- **Agrarian society**: Society using the plow to cultivate large areas, with rigid class stratification.
- **Kinship**: Culturally defined family relationships based on consanguinity, affinity, or fictive ties.
- **Information society**: Society where creation, distribution, and integration of information is a significant activity.

## Debates and open questions

- Whether humans are eusocial (Wilson) or not is disputed among biologists.
- Whether hunter-gatherers were the "original affluent society" (Sahlins) or suffered high mortality and warfare is contested.
- Whether war is an innate human characteristic or a recent phenomenon (appearing ~10,000 years ago) remains unsettled.
- Whether Western sociology's three paradigms adequately explain non-Western societies is criticized as Eurocentric.
- There is no generally accepted definition of what constitutes an ethnic group.
