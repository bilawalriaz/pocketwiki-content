# Niels Henrik Abel

## Overview
Niels Henrik Abel (1802–1829) was a Norwegian mathematician whose brief, impoverished life yielded foundational work in algebra and analysis. His most famous result is the first complete proof that the general quintic equation cannot be solved by radicals, a problem unresolved for over 250 years. He also pioneered the theory of elliptic functions and discovered a new class of functions now named after him, leaving a legacy that profoundly shaped modern mathematics.

## Timeline
- **5 August 1802** — Born prematurely in Nedstrand, Norway.
- **1815** — Enters the Cathedral School in Christiania (Oslo).
- **1818** — Teacher Bernt Michael Holmboe recognizes his talent and provides private lessons.
- **1821** — Enters the Royal Frederick University; begins work on the quintic equation.
- **1823** — Proves the impossibility of solving the general quintic by radicals (Abel–Ruffini theorem).
- **1824** — Publishes his proof in a short memoir.
- **1825** — Begins a European tour, visiting Berlin and befriending publisher August Crelle.
- **1826** — Submits his major theorem on addition of algebraic differentials to the French Academy; it is misplaced.
- **May 1827** — Returns to Norway after his tour is deemed a failure.
- **6 April 1829** — Dies of tuberculosis in Froland, Norway, at age 26.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born to a pastor father and a merchant's daughter, Abel's early home life was unstable; his father died in 1820 after a ruined political career and heavy drinking. At the Cathedral School, his mathematical genius was identified by teacher Bernt Michael Holmboe, who secured his education. Abel entered the university in 1821 already the most knowledgeable mathematician in Norway. He initially believed he had found a formula for the quintic equation, but after a challenge from Carl Ferdinand Degen, he discovered his error, which led him to prove the problem's impossibility.

### Career and European Tour
Supported by professors, Abel published his first articles in Norway. In 1824, he published his proof of the quintic's unsolvability, but in a compressed, difficult form to save costs. A government scholarship allowed a European tour in 1825. In Berlin, he collaborated with August Crelle, whose new journal became the primary outlet for Abel's work. He traveled through Europe, conducting research on elliptic and abelian functions. In Paris, he submitted his most important work—a theorem on the addition of algebraic differentials—to the French Academy, but it was forgotten until after his death. His tour ended without visiting Gauss or publishing in Paris, leading to the loss of his scholarship.

### Mathematical Contributions
Abel's work cleared obscurities in analysis and opened new fields. His proof of the quintic's unsolvability involved early concepts of group theory. He provided a rigorous proof of the binomial theorem for all numbers and laid the foundations for the theory of elliptic functions, revealing their double periodicity. He also discovered a new class of functions, now called abelian functions. Much of his work was published in Crelle's Journal, and his collected works were edited by Holmboe (1839) and later by Sylow and Lie (1881).

## Terms
- **Quintic equation**: A polynomial equation of the fifth degree.
- **Solving by radicals**: Expressing the roots of an equation using only arithmetic operations and root extractions.
- **Abel–Ruffini theorem**: Abel's proof that there is no general algebraic solution for quintic or higher-degree equations.
- **Elliptic functions**: Complex functions that are doubly periodic, generalizing trigonometric functions.
- **Abelian functions**: A class of functions generalizing elliptic functions to higher dimensions.
- **Crelle's Journal**: The first major journal dedicated to pure mathematics, founded by August Crelle.
- **Binomial theorem**: A formula for expanding expressions of the form (a+b)^n.
