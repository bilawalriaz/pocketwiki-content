# William Pitt the Younger

## Overview
William Pitt the Younger (1759–1806) was a British statesman who served as the last prime minister of Great Britain and the first of the United Kingdom. He is the youngest person ever to hold the office, becoming prime minister at age 24. His long premierships were dominated by the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars, during which he became a symbol of British resistance and financial resilience.

## Body

### Early Life and Entry into Politics
Born into a political family, Pitt was the son of former prime minister William Pitt the Elder. He was educated at home and then at Cambridge, where he befriended William Wilberforce. Called to the bar in 1780, he entered Parliament in 1781 through the pocket borough of Appleby, a system he later criticized. He quickly gained renown as a brilliant debater, initially aligning with Whigs like Charles James Fox to oppose the American War of Independence.

### Rise to Power and First Premiership (1783–1801)
After the collapse of the Fox-North Coalition, King George III appointed the 24-year-old Pitt prime minister in December 1783, despite a hostile Parliament. He survived a vote of no confidence and won a decisive majority in the 1784 general election, cementing his power. His first government focused on financial and administrative reform. He reduced the national debt, which had doubled after the American war, by introducing a sinking fund and new taxes, including Britain's first income tax in 1799. He reformed the East India Company with the India Act 1784 and established the penal colony of New South Wales in 1788.

### War with France and Ireland
Pitt initially sought to avoid war with Revolutionary France but was committed to it by 1793. His strategy relied on British naval power and funding continental allies. Domestically, he cracked down on radicalism with repressive laws like the Seditious Meetings Act. A costly and disastrous expedition to Saint-Domingue (1793–1798) ended in withdrawal after heavy losses to disease. In Ireland, the 1798 Rebellion, supported by France, convinced Pitt that the Irish Parliament was unworkable. He engineered the Acts of Union 1800, uniting Ireland with Great Britain, but his attempt to secure Catholic emancipation as part of the deal was blocked by King George III, leading to his resignation in 1801.

### Second Premiership and Death (1804–1806)
Pitt returned as prime minister in 1804 to lead Britain in the Napoleonic Wars. He formed the Third Coalition, but it collapsed after defeats at Ulm and Austerlitz. The British victory at Trafalgar (1805) secured naval supremacy. Pitt's government mobilized Britain's economic resources to fund the war, with the national debt soaring to £679 million by 1814. His health, long poor, declined under the strain. He died in 1806, leaving debts of £40,000, which Parliament paid.

## Terms
*   **Prime Minister:** The head of government in the United Kingdom. Pitt's long tenure helped define the modern powers of the office.
*   **Chancellor of the Exchequer:** The finance minister of the UK. Pitt held this role concurrently with the premiership.
*   **Rotten/Pocket Boroughs:** Parliamentary constituencies with very few voters, often controlled by a single patron, allowing for the sale of seats.
*   **Sinking Fund:** A fund set aside to accumulate money for paying off national debt.
*   **Acts of Union 1800:** Laws that merged the Kingdom of Great Britain and the Kingdom of Ireland into the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland.
*   **Catholic Emancipation:** The process of removing restrictions on Catholics in Britain and Ireland, which Pitt supported but failed to achieve.
*   **Third Coalition:** An alliance of Britain, Austria, Russia, and Sweden against Napoleonic France, formed in 1805.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Reason for Resignation (1801):** The traditional view is that Pitt resigned over the King's refusal to grant Catholic emancipation. Historian J-M Alter argues the true cause was Pitt's desire for peace with France; he engineered the Catholic question as a pretext to force pro-war cabinet members to resign, paving the way for his friend Addington to pursue peace.
*   **Assessment of His Legacy:** Historians debate his success. He is praised for stabilizing Britain's finances and leading the nation through war. However, his failures include the lack of parliamentary reform, the disastrous Saint-Domingue campaign, and the unfinished abolition of the slave trade, which biographer William Hague considers his greatest failure.
*   **Political Label:** Though often called a Tory, Pitt described himself as an "independent Whig" and opposed strict party systems. His "new Toryism" revived the Tory Party but did not fit neatly into contemporary political labels.
