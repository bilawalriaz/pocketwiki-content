# Jean Le Rond d'Alembert

## Overview
Jean Le Rond d'Alembert (1717–1783) was a central figure of the French Enlightenment, a mathematician, physicist, and philosopher whose work spanned from foundational mechanics to music theory. He is best known for co-editing the *Encyclopédie* with Denis Diderot and for key contributions to mathematics and physics, including the wave equation and d'Alembert's principle. His life and work exemplify the Enlightenment's drive to systematize knowledge and apply reason to all fields.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
D'Alembert was the natural son of writer Claudine Guérin de Tencin and artillery officer Louis-Camus Destouches. Abandoned as an infant, he was named after the church where he was found and raised by a glazier's wife, Madame Rousseau. His father secretly funded his education. He studied at the Jansenist Collège des Quatre-Nations, where he later rejected Cartesian principles like innate ideas and vortices. He briefly studied law and was nominated *avocat* in 1738.

### Mathematical and Scientific Career
D'Alembert's scientific career began in 1739 with a critique of a mathematics text. He was elected to the Académie des Sciences in 1741. His 1743 *Traité de dynamique* presented his own laws of motion. He later developed the wave equation (d'Alembert's equation) and its solution (d'Alembert's formula) while studying vibrating strings. In 1752, he described what is now called d'Alembert's paradox: that an inviscid, incompressible fluid exerts zero drag on a body. He also created a ratio test for series convergence.

### The *Encyclopédie* and Philosophy
In the late 1740s, d'Alembert was engaged as co-editor for mathematics and science of the *Encyclopédie*, a monumental project with Denis Diderot. He authored over a thousand articles, including the influential *Preliminary Discourse*. His work on the *Encyclopédie* led him to doubt materialism, aligning with idealist philosophy. He left the project in 1757 after an article caused a controversy with the clergy of Geneva.

### Music Theory
D'Alembert engaged with music theory starting in 1749 by reviewing Jean-Philippe Rameau's work. In 1752, he published *Éléments de musique*, aiming to popularize and simplify Rameau's theories, which posited music as a mathematical science. D'Alembert argued that three principles, not one, were needed to derive musical modes. His lack of musical training led to misunderstandings of Rameau's ideas, eventually causing a bitter dispute between them.

### Personal Life and Legacy
D'Alembert was a prominent figure in Parisian salons and lived with Julie de Lespinasse. He suffered from poor health and died of a urinary bladder illness. As a known unbeliever, he was buried in a common grave. In France, the fundamental theorem of algebra is named the d'Alembert–Gauss theorem. The d'Alembert operator is fundamental in modern physics. His incorrect probability argument in *Croix ou Pile* led to the "d'Alembert system" in gambling.

## Terms
- **Natural son**: A child born to unmarried parents.
- **Jansenist**: A member of a Catholic theological movement emphasizing original sin and human depravity.
- **Avocat**: A lawyer in the French legal system.
- **Wave equation (d'Alembert's equation)**: A partial differential equation describing the propagation of waves, such as sound or light.
- **D'Alembert's paradox**: The result in fluid dynamics that an inviscid, incompressible flow exerts zero drag on a body.
- **Ratio test**: A mathematical test to determine if an infinite series converges or diverges.
- **D'Alembert operator**: A differential operator used in wave equations and theoretical physics.
- **Martingale**: A betting strategy where the stake is adjusted based on previous outcomes.
- **Socinianism**: A nontrinitarian Christian theology.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The source notes that d'Alembert's proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra contained an error later corrected by Carl Friedrich Gauss.
- His interpretation and simplification of Rameau's music theory was contested by Rameau himself, leading to a public dispute.
- His argument in *Croix ou Pile* about coin-toss probability is famously incorrect.
