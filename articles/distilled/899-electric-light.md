# Electric light

## Overview
An electric light is a device that converts electricity into visible light, serving as the dominant form of artificial illumination. The technology evolved through three main categories: incandescent lamps (heating a filament), gas-discharge lamps (electric arc through gas), and LED lamps (electrons crossing a semiconductor band gap). Efficiency has risen dramatically since 19th-century arc lamps and early bulbs, culminating in a 21st-century phase-out of incandescent bulbs driven by regulation and consumer preference for LEDs. Beyond illumination, electric lights enable applications in heat generation, medical therapy, horticulture, and serve as a universal cultural symbol for sudden inspiration.

## Timeline
- **1799–1800** — Alessandro Volta invents the voltaic pile (first electric battery), enabling incandescence experiments.
- **1802** — Vasily Petrov develops the first persistent electric arc.
- **1806** — Humphry Davy demonstrates a practical arc light.
- **1840** — Warren de la Rue creates an early bulb with a platinum coil in a vacuum tube; too costly for commerce.
- **1846** — William Greener patents an early electric lamp (specification 11076).
- **Late 1870s–1880s** — Joseph Swan (UK) and Thomas Edison (US) independently develop commercial incandescent lamps; later merge into Edison and Swan Electric Light Company.
- **1910** — Georges Claude introduces the first neon light.
- **1912** — William D. Coolidge patents the tungsten filament, becoming the incandescent standard.
- **1934** — Arthur Compton reports on British fluorescent experiments; George E. Inman’s team builds a prototype fluorescent lamp at GE’s Nela Park.
- **1962** — First practical LED demonstrated (deep red only, used for indicators).
- **1994** — Shuji Nakamura demonstrates the first high-brightness blue LED, enabling white LEDs via phosphor conversion.
- **2009** — Philips introduces first LED lamps designed to replace standard 60 W Edison-screw incandescent bulbs.
- **2019** — US electricity usage falls for at least five straight years, partly due to LED replacement of incandescents.

## Body

### Historical Development
Electric lighting originated with Volta’s battery (1799–1800), which allowed heating wires to incandescence. Davy’s 1806 arc light demonstration and Petrov’s 1802 persistent arc established the gas-discharge principle. Early incandescent experiments, like de la Rue’s 1840 platinum vacuum bulb, proved the concept but failed commercially due to material costs. The late 1870s and 1880s saw intense rivalry between Swan and Edison; Edison’s thinner filaments and superior vacuum yielded a viable product, leading to a merger that dominated the market until tungsten filaments (Coolidge, 1912) standardized the technology. Discharge lighting advanced with Claude’s neon (1910) and the 1934 fluorescent prototype at GE, spurred by Compton’s report on British work. LEDs emerged in 1962 as red indicators; Nakamura’s 1994 blue LED enabled white light via phosphor conversion, triggering the 21st-century market shift. By 2019, US electricity demand had dropped for five consecutive years, attributed largely to this transition.

### Incandescent and Halogen Lamps
Modern incandescent bulbs use a coiled tungsten filament sealed in a glass globe with vacuum or inert gas (argon). At 2,000–3,300 K, the filament glows with a continuous spectrum but converts only 2–5% of energy into visible light; the rest becomes heat, which burdens cooling in warm climates but aids heating in cold ones (e.g., heat lamps). Halogen lamps are a high-temperature variant requiring fused silica or aluminosilicate envelopes (often double-walled for safety). They operate above 200 °C, offering higher efficacy, longer life, constant light output, and compact filaments for precise optical control, but pose higher burn/fire risks and shatter if contaminated by fingerprints.

### Fluorescent and Discharge Lamps
Fluorescent lamps pass current through low-pressure mercury vapor or argon, generating ultraviolet photons that strike a phosphor coating to emit visible light. They achieve 50–100 lumens per watt—several times incandescent efficacy—using one-quarter to one-third the power for equivalent light. They require a ballast to regulate current, raising fixture cost but lowering lifetime energy expense. Compact fluorescents (CFLs) retrofit incandescent sockets but contain mercury, classifying them as hazardous waste requiring recycling. Broader discharge lamps use gases like neon, xenon, sodium, or metal halides between metal electrodes. They operate on the same arc principle as carbon arcs but require igniters (for high-voltage starting) and ballasts to limit current. Low-pressure sodium lamps are the most efficient source, emitting near-monochromatic orange-yellow light favored by astronomers for filterable light pollution, but restricted to outdoor use due to poor color rendering.

### LED Lamps
Light-emitting diodes (LEDs) produce light via electrons crossing a semiconductor band gap. Popular as indicators since the 1970s, efficacy gains in the 2000s enabled lighting uses (vehicle lights, flashlights, general illumination). Advantages include low power consumption, minimal heat, instant on/off, color stability (for single-color diodes), and long life (indicator LEDs up to 100,000 hours; lighting LEDs shorter due to harder driving). Lifetime is highly temperature-dependent: elevated internal temperatures drastically reduce lifespan. Lasers have been adapted as an alternative for highly focused illumination.

### Carbon Arc Lamps
The first practical electric light (Davy, c. 1805), carbon arcs use two carbon rods in open air with a current-limiting ballast. Touching and separating the rods strikes an arc, creating a white-hot plasma. They offer higher efficacy than filaments but require constant rod adjustment due to erosion, emit significant UV, need ventilation indoors, and demand shielding from direct view. They dominated large-scale/street lighting from the 1870s until incandescent supersession in the early 20th century, persisting in projectors, stage lighting, and searchlights until post-WWII.

### Characteristics and Standards
Lamps are standardized by shape codes and socket types (e.g., "A19/A60 E26/E27" for common Edison-screw bulbs). Key comparison metrics: luminous flux (lumens), power (watts), luminous efficacy (lumens/watt), color temperature (kelvins), and color rendering index (CRI). Rated life is median hours until 50% failure (for LEDs, until 50% reach 70% lumen depreciation). Production tolerances cause wide variance; the Phoebus cartel (1900s) deliberately shortened lifespans as planned obsolescence. Frequent switching cycles reduce life, especially for CFLs.

### Uses and Impacts
Artificial light makes cities visible from space; outdoor lighting grew 3–6% annually in the late 20th century, creating light pollution affecting 80% of the global population and harming wildlife. Lamps serve as heat sources (incubators, infrared heaters, Easy-Bake Ovens), medical tools (light therapy for vitamin D, skin conditions, seasonal affective disorder, neonatal jaundice via blue light), and horticultural grow lights for indoor hydroponics and aquatic plants.

### Cultural Symbolism
In Western culture, an illuminated light bulb above a head signifies sudden inspiration. A stylized bulb serves as the logo of the Turkish AK Party.

## Terms
- ****Incandescent lamp**** — Light produced by heating a filament (typically tungsten) to white-hot temperatures via electric current.
- ****Gas-discharge lamp**** — Light produced by an electric arc passing through a gas (e.g., fluorescent, neon, sodium).
- ****LED (Light-emitting diode)**** — Solid-state device emitting light when electrons cross a semiconductor band gap.
- ****Ballast**** — Electrical device that regulates current through a discharge lamp; required because lamp resistance drops after arc strike.
- ****Luminous efficacy**** — Ratio of visible light output (lumens) to power input (watts); measures energy efficiency.
- ****Color temperature**** — Hue of light expressed in kelvins (K); lower values are "warm" (yellowish), higher are "cool" (bluish).
- ****CRI (Color Rendering Index)**** — Measure of how accurately a light source reveals colors compared to a natural reference.
- ****Phosphor**** — Coating inside fluorescent tubes that converts ultraviolet photons into visible light.
- ****Tungsten filament**** — Standard incandescent filament material (patented 1912) with high melting point enabling efficient operation.
- ****Band gap**** — Energy difference in a semiconductor that determines the wavelength (color) of light emitted by an LED.

## Debates and open questions
- **Phase-out policies**: The global incandescent ban (early 21st century) balances government regulation against consumer preference; economic impacts (EU estimated 5–10 billion euro gain, 15 billion tonnes CO₂ saved) are weighed against upfront costs and light-quality concerns.
- **LED lifetime definition**: Industry defines LED life as time until 50% of units suffer 70% lumen depreciation, which differs from traditional median-failure metrics and complicates consumer comparison.
- **Light pollution vs. utility**: Outdoor lighting growth (3–6%/year late 20th century) conflicts with astronomical research and ecological health; low-pressure sodium is favored by astronomers for filterability but rejected for general use due to monochromatic output.
- **Mercury in fluorescents**: CFLs save energy but introduce hazardous waste streams; recycling mandates vary by jurisdiction, creating disposal compliance gaps.
- **Thermal management for LEDs**: High internal temperatures drastically shorten LED life, creating a design tension between compact form factors, high output, and longevity.
