# White Nile

## Overview
The White Nile is the longer but less voluminous of the two major tributaries of the Nile River, the other being the Blue Nile. Its name derives from the pale color of its water, caused by clay sediment. It is a vital waterway for North and East Africa, with its course passing through multiple countries and featuring significant geographical and historical landmarks.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BC** — The most remote headwaters of the Nile are located in the Great Lakes region of Africa.
- **28–29 April 1994** — 250,000 Rwandan refugees cross the bridge at Rusumo Falls into Tanzania in 24 hours.
- **1963** — Karuma Bridge is built in Uganda.
- **1898** — The Fashoda Incident occurs at Kodok, marking an end to the Scramble for Africa.
- **2006** — The southern Sudanese state of Bahr al-Jabal is renamed Central Equatoria.
- **2009** — Uganda announces plans for a 750-megawatt hydropower project near Karuma Bridge.

## Body

### Course and Headwaters
The White Nile's most distant source is debated, with candidates being the Ruvyironza River in Burundi or the Nyabarongo River in Rwanda. These rivers meet near Rusumo Falls to form the Kagera River, which flows into Lake Victoria. From Lake Victoria's outlet at Jinja, Uganda, the river is called the Victoria Nile. It flows via Lake Kyoga to Lake Albert, passing over Bujagali Falls and through Murchison Falls National Park, where it is compressed into a seven-meter-wide passage at Murchison Falls before entering Lake Albert.

### The Albert Nile and Mountain Nile
From Lake Albert, the river is called the Albert Nile, which separates Uganda's West Nile sub-region. Upon entering South Sudan near Nimule, it becomes the Mountain Nile (or Bahr al-Jabal). This section passes through rapids and the vast Sudd swamp before reaching Lake No. At Lake No, it merges with the Bahr el Ghazal to form the White Nile in its strict sense.

### The White Nile Proper and Confluence
From Lake No, the White Nile flows east for 120 km to the mouth of the Sobat River, a stretch filled with swamps and lagoons. The Sobat adds significant sediment, contributing to the river's pale color. From Malakal, South Sudan, it flows north into Sudan, passing Kodok (site of the 1898 Fashoda Incident). The river lends its name to Sudan's White Nile state before joining the Blue Nile at Khartoum to form the main Nile.

### Navigation and Infrastructure
The White Nile is navigable from Lake Albert to Khartoum via the Jebel Aulia Dam, though the stretch between Juba and Uganda requires upgrades. Hydropower projects, such as the planned Karuma Power Station, are significant infrastructure developments along its course.

## Terms
- **White Nile**: The longer, less voluminous tributary of the Nile, named for its pale, sediment-laden water.
- **Blue Nile**: The larger, more voluminous tributary of the Nile, which originates in Ethiopia.
- **Sudd**: A vast swamp in South Sudan through which the Mountain Nile flows.
- **Lake No**: The lake in South Sudan where the Mountain Nile and Bahr el Ghazal merge to form the White Nile.
- **Bahr al-Jabal**: Arabic for "Mountain River," another name for the Mountain Nile section in South Sudan.
- **Bahr el Ghazal**: A major tributary that joins the Mountain Nile at Lake No.
- **Victoria Nile**: The name for the White Nile between Lake Victoria and Lake Albert.
- **Albert Nile**: The name for the river between Lake Albert and the South Sudan border.
- **Fashoda Incident**: An 1898 diplomatic standoff between Britain and France at Kodok, ending the Scramble for Africa.

## Debates and Open Questions
The most remote source of the Nile is debated, with the Ruvyironza in Burundi and the Nyabarongo in Rwanda as primary candidates. The exact point where the "White Nile" begins is also a matter of definition, with some considering it to start only at the confluence at Lake No.
