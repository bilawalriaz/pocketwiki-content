# Herman Melville

## Overview

Herman Melville (1819–1891) was an American novelist and poet whose career spanned the American Renaissance and beyond. Born in New York City, the third child of a prosperous merchant whose death in 1832 left the family in dire financial straits, Melville went to sea as a young man, experiences that shaped his early novels like *Typee* (1846) and *Moby-Dick* (1851). Despite initial success, his later works were poorly received, leading him to abandon fiction for poetry. Rediscovered in the early 20th century, Melville is now regarded as one of America’s greatest writers, with *Moby-Dick* hailed as a Great American Novel.

## Body

### Early Life and Education

Melville was born in New York City, on August 1, 1819, the third of eight children to Allan Melvill and Maria (Gansevoort) Melvill. His father, a prosperous merchant, died on January 28, 1832, when Herman was 12, leaving the family in dire financial straits. The family’s subsequent poverty forced young Melville to leave school and work as a clerk. His education began when he was six, including New York Male High School, Columbia Grammar and Preparatory School, and multiple periods at The Albany Academy, where classical studies left a lasting impact on his writing. His mother, Maria Gansevoort, was committed to her family's more strict and biblically oriented Dutch Reformed version of the Calvinist creed, instilling strong religious influences that would later inform his themes of morality and existential struggle.

### Years at Sea (1839–1844)

At age 20, Melville signed aboard the merchant ship *St. Lawrence* as a "boy" (a green hand), followed by the whaler *Acushnet* in 1841. These voyages provided material for his early novels. After jumping ship at Nuku Hiva Bay in the Marquesas Islands, he lived in or near the Taipi Valley, experiences that became the basis for *Typee* and its sequel *Omoo*. His time at sea fostered a deep skepticism toward authority and a fascination with non-Western cultures, themes central to his work.

### Rise to Fame and Literary Ambition (1845–1851)

Returning home, Melville capitalized on his adventures by publishing *Typee* (1846) and *Omoo* (1847), both bestsellers. Their success allowed him to marry Elizabeth "Lizzie" Knapp Shaw in 1847. Encouraged by literary figures like Nathaniel Hawthorne—whom he deeply admired—he wrote *Moby-Dick* (1851), a complex psychological and philosophical novel. Though initially unsuccessful, it is now considered a masterpiece of American literature.

### Decline and Shift to Poetry (1852–1891)

Following the failure of *Pierre* (1852) and *The Confidence-Man* (1857), Melville turned away from fiction. He became a customs inspector in 1866 and held the post for 19 years, during which he published several poetry collections, including *Battle-Pieces and Aspects of the War* (1866) and the epic *Clarel* (1876). Personal tragedies, including the death of his son Malcolm in 1867, marked this period. He died from cardiac dilation in 1891.

### Posthumous Recognition and Revival

Interest in Melville resurged beginning in 1917 with Carl Van Doren's article, coinciding with the centennial of his birth in 1919. Scholars like Raymond Weaver championed his work, and the rediscovery of *Billy Budd*—whose pages were stored in a family breadbox until 1919 when Melville's granddaughter gave them to Weaver, who edited it and published it in a new collected edition in 1924—further elevated his reputation. The Melville Society, founded in 1945, and the Northwestern-Newberry Edition of The Writings of Herman Melville solidified his place in American literary canon.

## Terms

- **American Renaissance**: A period in mid-19th-century American literature marked by major works from authors like Hawthorne, Melville, Whitman, and Twain.
- **Great American Novel**: A term coined by John William De Forest in 1868, referring to a novel that captures the national character and experience.
- **Romance (literary genre)**: A genre emphasizing emotion, imagination, and often supernatural or exotic elements, distinct from realism.
- **Polyphonic**: A literary technique involving multiple voices or perspectives within a single narrative.
- **Hebraism**: Stylistic features borrowed from Hebrew language and biblical texts, such as parallelism and genitive constructions.
- **Lyceum movement**: A 19th-century American educational and cultural initiative promoting public lectures and debates.
- **Metaphysical estrangement**: A sense of alienation or disconnection from traditional beliefs and structures, often explored in Melville’s work.
- **Homosocial**: Relating to close emotional bonds between members of the same sex, frequently depicted in Melville’s male-dominated narratives.
- **Court-martial**: A military tribunal convened to try offenses under military law, notably featured in *Billy Budd*.
- **Epic poem**: A long narrative poem exploring heroic or grand themes, such as *Clarel*.

## Debates and Open Questions

Several scholarly debates surround Melville’s life and work:

- **Intentions behind *Billy Budd***: Critics debate whether Melville intended the novella as a critique of legal rigidity or moral ambiguity. Some see Captain Vere as embodying unjust legalism, while others argue he represents necessary order.
- **Homoerotic subtext**: Scholars like Warren Rosenberg argue that Melville deliberately explored themes of sexuality and gender, particularly in *Moby-Dick* and *Billy Budd*, though interpretations vary widely.
- **Religious meaning in *Moby-Dick***: Whether Ahab symbolizes defiance of God or human hubris remains contested. Some view the whale as a symbol of divine mystery, others as nature itself.
- **Autobiographical readings**: Many early biographers interpreted Melville’s fiction through a Freudian lens, seeing characters as projections of his psyche. Modern critics question this approach, advocating instead for textual analysis over biographical speculation.
- **Poetry vs. Prose**: While *Moby-Dick* dominates popular perception, some scholars argue Melville’s poetry—especially *Clarel*—represents his mature artistic vision, challenging the hierarchy that privileges his novels.
