# Friedrich Wöhler

## Overview
Friedrich Wöhler (1800–1882) was a German chemist whose work bridged inorganic and organic chemistry. He is most famous for synthesizing urea from inorganic materials, a discovery that challenged the prevailing theory of vitalism and helped establish the modern field of organic chemistry. His career also included isolating several elements and pioneering new laboratory teaching methods.

## Timeline
- **31 July 1800** — Born in Eschersheim, Germany.
- **1823** — Earned doctorate in medicine from Heidelberg University.
- **1823–1824** — Studied under Jöns Jacob Berzelius in Stockholm.
- **1827** — Isolated pure aluminium powder using an improved method.
- **1828** — First to isolate pure beryllium and yttrium; synthesized urea from ammonium cyanate (Wöhler synthesis).
- **1832** — Published research on the benzoyl radical with Justus Liebig, establishing the concept of compound radicals.
- **1836** — Became Professor of Chemistry at the University of Göttingen, a position he held for 46 years.
- **1854** — Elected a Fellow of the Royal Society of London.
- **23 September 1882** — Died in Göttingen, Germany.

## Body

### Inorganic Chemistry and Element Isolation
Wöhler investigated over twenty-five elements. He refined Hans Christian Ørsted's method for isolating aluminium, using potassium metal to reduce aluminium chloride and producing pure aluminium powder in 1827. In 1828, he was the first to isolate pure metallic beryllium and yttrium by heating their anhydrous chlorides with potassium. He also determined that a sample believed to be pure titanium was a mixture, and he isolated crystalline forms of boron and silicon (with Sainte Claire Deville and Heinrich Buff, respectively). His work included preparing the first samples of silane (SiH₄) and boron nitride.

### Organic Chemistry and the Wöhler Synthesis
Wöhler's most famous contribution was his 1828 synthesis of urea, an organic compound found in urine, by heating ammonium cyanate, an inorganic salt. This reaction, known as the Wöhler synthesis, contradicted the theory of vitalism, which held that organic compounds could only be produced by living organisms due to a "vital force." While his work was a major blow to vitalism, its exact role in overturning the theory is debated, as it also demonstrated the concept of chemical isomerism—where two compounds have the same atoms but different structures and properties.

### Collaboration and Compound Radicals
In 1832, Wöhler collaborated with Justus Liebig to analyze the oil of bitter almonds. Their work proved that a group of atoms (C₇H₅O) could act as a single unit, or functional group, which they called the benzoyl radical. This established the concept of compound radicals, a foundational idea in organic chemistry that explained how groups of atoms behave as single entities in reactions.

### Educational Reform
As a professor at Göttingen for 46 years, Wöhler trained about 8,000 students. He revolutionized chemistry education by emphasizing hands-on laboratory work and involving students directly in his research. These practices, unusual at the time, became standard in universities worldwide.

## Terms
- **Vitalism**: The obsolete theory that living organisms are animated by a non-physical "vital force" that allows them to produce organic compounds.
- **Wöhler Synthesis**: The chemical reaction (1828) in which urea is synthesized from ammonium cyanate, demonstrating that organic compounds can be made from inorganic precursors.
- **Compound Radical**: A group of atoms within a molecule that behaves as a single, stable unit in chemical reactions.
- **Chemical Isomerism**: The phenomenon where two or more compounds have the same chemical formula but different structural arrangements and properties.
- **Functional Group**: A specific group of atoms within a molecule that is responsible for the characteristic chemical reactions of that molecule.
- **Aluminium**: A silvery-white, lightweight metal. Wöhler's improved isolation method made it available for further study.
- **Beryllium**: A lightweight, hard metal. Wöhler was the first to isolate it in pure form.
- **Yttrium**: A silvery-white metal used in various applications. Wöhler isolated it in pure form.

## Debates and open questions
The source notes that Wöhler's role in definitively overturning vitalism has been exaggerated over time. His synthesis was initially celebrated as a refutation, but historians like Hermann Kopp emphasized it more as a demonstration of isomerism. Furthermore, modern chemistry understands that cyanate is not purely inorganic, as it is formed in metabolic pathways, meaning Wöhler's reaction was not a strict conversion from inorganic to organic matter. The extent to which his work single-handedly ended the vitalism debate remains a point of historical discussion.
