# Audience

## Overview
An audience is a group of people who engage with a work of art, performance, or media. The concept is central to understanding how communication and art function, as audiences are not passive recipients but active participants who shape the meaning and success of a work through their reception, feedback, and participation. The study of audiences, known as audience theory, is a key part of media and communication studies.

## Body

### Types of Audience
Audiences can be categorized as either particular (real) or theoretical (imagined). **Particular audiences** are actual groups of people. An **immediate audience** is physically present with a speaker, like a live theatre crowd, and provides direct feedback like applause. A **mediated audience** consumes content through technology like television or the internet, separating them from the creator in time and space; their size is measured via ratings and polls.

**Theoretical audiences** are constructs used for analysis or creation. A rhetor may address an **audience of self** during deliberation. A **universal audience** is an imagined, diverse group used as an ethical test for an argument's validity. An **ideal audience** is the rhetor's intended target for persuasion. An **implied audience** is inferred by a reader or critic from the text itself. The internet has further blurred these lines, allowing individuals to be both audience members and creators simultaneously.

### Audience Participation
Audience participation involves active engagement from the audience, often breaking the "fourth wall" in performances. This is common in British pantomime, stand-up comedy, and shows like *Blue Man Group*. Participation can range from simple call-and-response to complex interactions, such as voting on a musical's plot outcome in *The Mystery of Edwin Drood*.

Brands increasingly use "crowd activation" to foster engagement, providing objects like branded light-up hats to involve audiences in events like the Super Bowl halftime show. Iconic examples of participation culture include the prop-throwing and heckling associated with *The Rocky Horror Picture Show* screenings.

### Faux Participation
Some media simulates audience participation without direct viewer input. The television series *Mystery Science Theater 3000* features characters who heckle bad movies, modeling a form of critical audience engagement for the viewer. Similarly, online recappers and reviewers, such as those on the site Television Without Pity, adopt the persona of a fellow audience member sharing in the experience.

## Terms
- **Audience:** A group of people who participate in or encounter a work of art, performance, or media.
- **Immediate Audience:** A real, face-to-face audience present during a live rhetorical event.
- **Mediated Audience:** A real audience that consumes content through a medium like television or the internet, separated from the creator.
- **Universal Audience:** An imagined, diverse audience used as a test for the ethical and logical validity of an argument.
- **Implied Audience:** The audience constructed and addressed within a text, inferred by a reader or critic.
- **Audience Participation:** Active engagement by the audience in a performance, often breaking the fourth wall.
- **Crowd Activation:** A marketing term for using audience participation to create engagement between a brand and consumers.
- **Faux Participation:** Media that simulates or models audience participation without requiring direct input from the actual viewer.

## Debates and Open questions
The concept of a **universal audience** is criticized for being idealistic and potentially hindering effective persuasion with specific, real-world audiences. The rise of the internet has fundamentally challenged traditional audience models by enabling "citizen journalism" and blurring the line between creator and consumer, as noted by journalists Jeff Jarvis and Tom Curley. This shift raises ongoing questions about control, engagement, and the evolving role of the audience in the digital age.
