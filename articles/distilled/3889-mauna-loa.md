# Mauna Loa

## Overview
Mauna Loa is Earth's largest active volcano by mass and volume, forming part of the Island of Hawaiʻi. It is a shield volcano created by the Hawaii hotspot, with a history of frequent, fluid lava eruptions that have built its massive structure over hundreds of thousands of years. Its activity poses significant hazards to populated areas, making it a key site for volcanological study and monitoring.

## Timeline
- **c. 400,000 years ago** — Mauna Loa emerges above sea level from submarine eruptions.
- **100,000–200,000 years ago** — A massive slump collapse occurs on the volcano's west flank.
- **c. 1,000–1,500 years ago** — The summit caldera, Mokuʻāweoweo, forms.
- **1843** — The first well-documented historical eruption occurs.
- **1868** — A major eruption coincides with the largest recorded earthquake in Hawaii's history (magnitude 8).
- **1872–1877** — A long-lasting summit eruption lasts approximately 1,200 days.
- **1926** — An eruption destroys the village of Hoʻōpūloa.
- **1935** — A bombing operation attempts to divert lava flows threatening Hilo.
- **1942** — An eruption occurs during WWII blackout, prompting a press gag order.
- **1950** — The largest rift eruption in modern history occurs, destroying the village of Hoʻokena-mauka.
- **1984** — Eruption brings lava flows within 6 km (4 mi) of Hilo.
- **2022** — The most recent eruption begins on November 27 and ends on December 13.

## Body

### Geology and Formation
Mauna Loa is one of five volcanoes on the Island of Hawaiʻi, formed as the Pacific Plate moved over the stationary Hawaii hotspot. It is a classic shield volcano with gentle slopes, built by fluid, silica-poor lava. Rising 9,170 m (30,085 ft) from its seafloor base, it is taller from base to summit than Mount Everest is from sea level. Its summit caldera, Mokuʻāweoweo, is flanked by two prominent rift zones: the southwest rift and the northeast rift. The volcano has a shallow magma chamber about 4.7 km (3 mi) deep. It interacts complexly with neighboring volcanoes, particularly Kīlauea, which sits on its southeastern flank and was once considered a satellite.

### Eruptive History
Mauna Loa has likely been erupting for at least 700,000 years. Its growth has been rapid, with 40% of its surface covered by lavas less than 1,000 years old. Eruptions typically follow a pattern: activity begins at the summit and may later shift to the rift zones. Historical eruptions are usually effusive, producing fast-moving lava flows. Notable events include the voluminous 1872–1877 summit eruption and the rapid 1950 rift eruption that reached the ocean in hours. The volcano exhibits a cyclical pattern of summit-dominant and rift-dominant activity phases, each lasting centuries.

### Hazards and Monitoring
As a Decade Volcano, Mauna Loa is monitored intensively by the Hawaiian Volcano Observatory (HVO), established in 1912. The primary hazards are lava flows, which can inundate property and infrastructure, as seen in 1926 and 1950. A greater, though less frequent, hazard is a catastrophic flank collapse, which could trigger massive tsunamis. Modern monitoring uses seismic networks, GPS, tiltmeters, and gas detectors to forecast eruptions, which are often preceded by years of increased seismic activity and ground deformation.

### Human History and Observatories
Ancient Hawaiians revered Mauna Loa and built trails like the ʻAinapō Trail to its summit for religious purposes. European attempts to summit the volcano began in the late 18th century, with the first confirmed ascent by Archibald Menzies in 1794. The U.S. Exploring Expedition led by Charles Wilkes conducted extensive scientific work at the summit in 1840–41. Today, the volcano hosts important scientific facilities, including the Mauna Loa Observatory for atmospheric monitoring and the Mauna Loa Solar Observatory.

## Terms
- **Shield volcano**: A broad, gently sloping volcano built by the eruption of low-viscosity lava that can flow over great distances.
- **Hawaii hotspot**: A stationary plume of hot mantle material that creates the Hawaiian Island chain as the Pacific Plate moves over it.
- **Rift zone**: A linear area of weakness on a volcano where magma intrudes and erupts, often forming a chain of vents.
- **Caldera**: A large depression formed when a volcano collapses into the void left after a magma chamber empties during an eruption.
- **Effusive eruption**: An eruption characterized by the outpouring of fluid lava without significant explosive activity.
- **Decade Volcanoes**: A list of 16 volcanoes identified by the IAVCEI for focused study due to their history of large, destructive eruptions and proximity to population centers.
- **Magma chamber**: A reservoir of molten rock beneath a volcano's surface.
- **Flank collapse**: The catastrophic failure of a volcano's side, often resulting in massive landslides and potential tsunamis.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The effectiveness of the 1935 bombing operation to divert lava flows from Hilo is heavily disputed by volcanologists.
- The cause of a period of near-inactivity at Mauna Loa between about 7,000 and 6,000 years ago is unknown and not observed at other Hawaiian volcanoes.
- The precise nature and mechanics of the interaction between Mauna Loa's and Kīlauea's magma systems, including whether pulses from one can trigger eruptions at the other, remains an area of study.
