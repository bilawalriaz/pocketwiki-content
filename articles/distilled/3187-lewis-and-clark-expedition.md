# Lewis and Clark Expedition

## Overview
The Lewis and Clark Expedition (1804–1806), officially the Corps of Discovery, was the first American overland journey to the Pacific coast and back. Commissioned by President Thomas Jefferson after the Louisiana Purchase, its primary goals were to find a practical water route to the Pacific, assert U.S. sovereignty over the new territory, and document its geography, resources, and Native American nations. The expedition's success established an American claim to the Pacific Northwest and produced invaluable scientific and ethnographic records.

## Body

### Motivations and Preparations
President Jefferson's chief aim was to find "the most direct and practicable water communication across this continent, for the purposes of commerce." He also sought to declare U.S. sovereignty over lands inhabited by Native American tribes and to secure accurate knowledge of the Louisiana Purchase's resources. Scientific research, while a secondary objective, was important for strengthening the U.S. claim to the Pacific Northwest. Jefferson selected his personal secretary, Meriwether Lewis, who then invited William Clark to co-lead. Lewis received specialized training in Philadelphia in medicine, astronomy, and specimen preservation. The expedition was equipped with advanced weapons like the Girandoni air rifle, scientific instruments, and "Indian Peace Medals" for diplomacy.

### The Journey West
The Corps, comprising about 45 men including York, an enslaved man owned by Clark, traveled up the Missouri River. They faced tense negotiations with the Lakota (Sioux) near present-day South Dakota. They built Fort Mandan for the first winter, where they hired Charbonneau and met his Shoshone wife, Sacagawea. Following the Missouri to its source, they crossed the Continental Divide at Lemhi Pass. Using canoes, they descended the Clearwater, Snake, and Columbia rivers, finally reaching the Pacific Ocean in November 1805. They built Fort Clatsop for the winter, where Lewis focused on documenting the region's botany.

### The Return and Native American Relations
The return journey began in March 1806. The Corps split to explore more territory, leading to a violent encounter between Lewis's group and Blackfeet warriors, resulting in two Blackfeet deaths. Clark's group had horses stolen by the Crow. The reunited party reached St. Louis in September 1806. A primary objective was to establish relations with Native American tribes, which they did with over two dozen nations. They provided gifts and demonstrated military strength to assert U.S. authority, though encounters ranged from friendly assistance to near-conflict, as with the Lakota.

### Key Figures and Scientific Work
Sacagawea, a Shoshone woman, served as an interpreter and her presence with an infant signaled the expedition's peaceful intent. York, Clark's enslaved servant, was crucial for labor and impressed Native Americans who had never seen a Black man. The expedition documented over 200 new plant and animal species and produced about 140 maps, filling in the main outlines of the Pacific Northwest's geography. They did not find a continuous water route but identified a viable overland trail from the Missouri headwaters to the Columbia River.

## Terms
- **Corps of Discovery**: The official name for the Lewis and Clark Expedition party.
- **Louisiana Purchase**: The 1803 acquisition by the U.S. of the Louisiana Territory from France.
- **Continental Divide**: The principal mountain ridge separating river systems that drain into the Pacific Ocean from those draining into the Atlantic.
- **Keelboat**: A large, flat-bottomed boat used by the expedition for the first leg of the journey up the Missouri River.
- **Pirogue**: A lightweight canoe used by the expedition.
- **Indian Peace Medals**: Silver medals bearing Jefferson's portrait, distributed to Native American leaders as symbols of friendship and U.S. sovereignty.
- **Girandoni air rifle**: A powerful, repeating air rifle carried by the expedition to demonstrate advanced technology.
- **Fort Mandan**: The expedition's winter camp (1804–05) in present-day North Dakota.
- **Fort Clatsop**: The expedition's winter camp (1805–06) near the Pacific coast in present-day Oregon.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact size of the Corps of Discovery is debated, with accounts varying but believed to be as many as 45 members.
- The role and contributions of Sacagawea are often exaggerated in popular culture; scholars note she was important as an interpreter and in diplomatic relations, but not the guide for the expedition.
- The last years of York's life are disputed; one account suggests he lived as a chief with Native Americans in Wyoming in the 1830s.
- The expedition's journals were not fully published until the 20th century, meaning many of their scientific discoveries were unknowingly rediscovered later.
