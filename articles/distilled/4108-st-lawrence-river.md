# St. Lawrence River

## Overview
The St. Lawrence River is a major international waterway connecting the Great Lakes to the Atlantic Ocean. It is a vital drainage system for the continent, a historic transportation route for Indigenous peoples and European colonizers, and remains a critical shipping corridor today.

## Timeline
- **c. 13,000–10,000 years ago** — The Champlain Sea, a precursor to the river, forms and then shrinks due to post-glacial rebound.
- **11th century** — Norse explorers reach the Gulf of St. Lawrence.
- **1535** — Jacques Cartier sails up the river, naming the gulf after Saint Lawrence on his feast day (August 10).
- **1604** — Samuel de Champlain records the name "Saint-Laurent" on a map, establishing the modern name.
- **1759** — British forces use the river to attack Quebec City during the Seven Years' War.
- **1809** — John Molson launches the first steamboat, the *Accommodation*, on the river.
- **1825** — The Lachine Canal opens, allowing ships to bypass the Lachine Rapids.
- **1942–1944** — The Battle of the St. Lawrence sees German U-boats attack shipping in the river and gulf during WWII.
- **1959** — The St. Lawrence Seaway is officially opened, allowing ocean vessels to reach the Great Lakes.
- **1970s** — The "Save the River" ecological campaign successfully opposes planned development.

## Body

### Formation and Geography
The river formed as the ancient Champlain Sea receded following the Last Glacial Maximum. It begins at the outflow of Lake Ontario and flows 1,197 km (744 mi) to the Gulf of St. Lawrence, often cited as the world's largest estuary. The total length from its farthest headwater (North River in Minnesota) is 3,058 km (1,900 mi). Its drainage basin covers 1,344,200 km², including the Great Lakes, and spans parts of eight U.S. states and two Canadian provinces. The river is seismically active due to the Saint Lawrence rift system.

### Indigenous and European History
The river was a primary transportation route for numerous Indigenous nations, including the Innu, Mi'kmaq, Abenaki, and Haudenosaunee. French explorers, beginning with Cartier and Champlain, used it as the main route to explore the continental interior. Control of the river was strategically crucial in the Seven Years' War, leading to the British conquest of New France. The first steamboat service began in 1809, and the Lachine Canal (1825) was the first major improvement for navigation.

### Modern Development and Ecology
The St. Lawrence Seaway, a system of locks and canals, was completed in 1959 to enable ocean-going vessels to reach the Great Lakes. The river supports significant biodiversity, including 13 cetacean species like the blue whale and beluga. Industrial activity, particularly from aluminum and automotive plants in the 20th century, introduced toxic pollutants like PCBs and dioxins. A successful ecological campaign in the 1970s helped protect the river from further development.

## Terms
- **Estuary**: The tidal mouth of a large river, where the sea meets freshwater. The St. Lawrence estuary is often called the world's largest.
- **Discharge**: The volume of water flowing through a river channel per unit of time. The St. Lawrence has the second-highest discharge in North America.
- **Watershed**: The area of land that drains all precipitation into a common outlet, like a river. The St. Lawrence watershed covers over 1.3 million km².
- **Seaway**: A navigable channel through shallow water. The St. Lawrence Seaway is a system of locks and canals for ocean vessels.
- **Rift system**: A linear zone where the Earth's crust is being pulled apart. The Saint Lawrence rift system makes the valley seismically active.
- **Cetacean**: A marine mammal of the order Cetacea, which includes whales, dolphins, and porpoises. Thirteen species frequent the St. Lawrence.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source of the St. Lawrence River is debated; while the North River in Minnesota is considered the hydrological source, the water system changes names frequently as it crosses many lakes. The classification of the St. Lawrence Valley is also physiographically debated: the U.S. considers it part of the Appalachian division, while Canada views it as a distinct St. Lawrence Lowlands division.
