# New Guinea

## Overview
New Guinea, the world's second-largest island in Melanesia, is a region of immense biological and cultural diversity. It hosts the third-largest remaining rainforest and the highest plant biodiversity of any island. Politically divided between Indonesia (west) and Papua New Guinea (east), its history features early human settlement, complex indigenous societies, and a colonial past shaping its modern geopolitics.

## Timeline
- **c. 60,000–50,000 BCE** — First human inhabitants arrive, likely from Southeast Asia via land bridges.
- **c. 6000 BCE** — Sugarcane cultivation begins; evidence of early highland agriculture and irrigation.
- **c. 3,500 years ago** — Austronesian peoples arrive, colonizing coastal areas and offshore islands.
- **1526–27** — Portuguese explorer Jorge de Menezes makes first known European contact.
- **1545** — Spanish explorer Íñigo Ortíz de Retes names the island "Nueva Guinea."
- **1828** — The Netherlands formally claims the western half as Dutch New Guinea.
- **1884** — Germany claims northeastern New Guinea; Britain administers southeastern New Guinea.
- **1902** — Australia begins administering the Territory of Papua; Dutch posts established in the west.
- **1942–45** — Japanese invasion during WWII; key battles fought in Australian-administered territories.
- **1963** — Indonesia takes control of the western half from the UN, renaming it West Irian.
- **1975** — Papua New Guinea gains full independence from Australia.
- **2001** — Indonesia passes a Law on Special Autonomy for Papua; the province is renamed from Irian Jaya.
- **2022** — Indonesia splits Papua Province into four new provinces, creating six total in the western half.

## Body

### Names and Etymology
"Papua" was used before Western contact; its etymology is debated, with theories linking it to the Tidore language meaning "not united" or to a Malay word for "frizzly-haired." The name "New Guinea" was coined by Íñigo Ortíz de Retes in 1545 due to a perceived resemblance to people of the Guinea region in Africa. The Dutch later called it *Nieuw Guinea*. In the 20th century, the Indonesian government promoted "Irian," derived from Biak language myths, but this name was replaced by "Papua" in 2001.

### Geography and Ecology
Geologically, New Guinea is part of the Australian continental shelf and was connected to Australia as the landmass Sahul during Pleistocene low sea levels. The island is dominated by the east-west New Guinea Highlands, including Puncak Jaya (4,884 m), Oceania's highest point. It features vast lowland rainforests, wetlands, and some of the world's largest mangrove forests. Rivers like the Sepik and Fly carry enormous water volumes. Biologically, it is part of the Australasian realm, sharing fauna like marsupials with Australia, but its flora has strong Asian affinities. It is a global biodiversity hotspot, with an estimated 5–10% of the world's species, including over 1,000 new species discovered between 1998 and 2008.

### Human History and Culture
Archaeological evidence indicates continuous habitation since at least 50,000 BCE. The Papuan people descended from these early inhabitants, who developed one of the earliest known agricultural systems, with highland irrigation dating back at least 10,000 years. Around 3,500 years ago, Austronesian-speaking peoples arrived, settling coastal areas and introducing pigs and dogs. This long history has resulted in extraordinary cultural diversity, with nearly a thousand tribal groups and over 1,000 distinct languages, making New Guinea the most linguistically diverse area in the world. Traditional societies are often organized around agriculture, pig-based trade, and inter-village relations that have historically included warfare.

### Colonial and Modern History
European colonization began in the 16th century, with formal claims by the Netherlands (west), Germany (northeast), and Britain (southeast) in the 19th century. After World War I, Australia administered the former German and British territories. The western half remained under Dutch control until 1963, when it was transferred to Indonesia following a UN-brokered agreement. A controversial 1969 referendum, the "Act of Free Choice," formalized integration with Indonesia, sparking ongoing resistance and conflict. Papua New Guinea gained independence from Australia in 1975. In the Indonesian provinces, a Special Autonomy law was passed in 2001, but its implementation is debated. In 2022, Indonesia further subdivided the western region into six provinces.

## Terms
- **Sahul:** The combined landmass of Australia, New Guinea, and Tasmania during Pleistocene low sea levels.
- **Melanesia:** A subregion of Oceania in the southwestern Pacific, including New Guinea and nearby island groups.
- **Papuan languages:** A diverse category for non-Austronesian languages spoken in New Guinea, most unrelated.
- **Austronesian languages:** A language family spread across Southeast Asia and the Pacific; in New Guinea, spoken mainly in coastal and island areas.
- **Bird's Head Peninsula:** The large peninsula in northwest New Guinea, also known as the Vogelkop or Doberai Peninsula.
- **New Guinea Highlands:** The mountainous spine running east-west across the island, home to ancient agricultural societies.
- **Act of Free Choice:** The 1969 UN-supervised referendum in which hand-picked Papuan elders voted for integration with Indonesia.
- **Organisasi Papua Merdeka (OPM):** The Free Papua Movement, an organization formed in 1965 that has resisted Indonesian rule.
- **Special Autonomy:** A legal framework established in 2001 to grant greater self-governance to Papua within Indonesia.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Date of first settlement:** While evidence indicates human presence by 50,000 BCE, proposals for settlement as early as 60,000 years ago are debated.
- **Etymology of "Papua":** The origin of the name is unclear, with competing theories from Tidore, Malay, and Biak languages.
- **Implementation of Special Autonomy:** Critics argue the Indonesian government has been reluctant to issue necessary regulations, causing the policy to "fail" in practice.
- **Human cost of conflict:** Estimates of Papuan deaths due to government-sponsored violence since Indonesian integration vary widely, from over 100,000 to roughly 500,000.
