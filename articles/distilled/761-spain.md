# Spain

## Overview

Spain, officially the Kingdom of Spain, is a Southern and Western European nation occupying most of the Iberian Peninsula, with additional territories in North Africa, the Atlantic, and the Mediterranean. It is the largest country in Southern Europe, the fourth-most populous EU member, and a developed parliamentary democracy under a constitutional monarchy. Its history—from pre-Roman Iberian cultures through Roman Hispania, Muslim Al-Andalus, the Reconquista, and the rise and fall of a global empire—has profoundly shaped its language, culture, and institutions. Today, Spain is a major global economy, a leading tourist destination, and a culturally influential nation with deep regional identities and linguistic diversity.

## Body

### Prehistory and Ancient Peoples

Archaeological evidence at Atapuerca shows hominid habitation in the Iberian Peninsula dating back 1.3 million years. Modern humans arrived around 35,000 BCE, leaving behind cave paintings such as those in Altamira. The Iberian Peninsula served as a refugium during the last ice age, repopulating northern Europe afterward. Before Roman conquest, the region was home to Iberians, Celts, Lusitanians, and Tartessians, with Phoenician, Carthaginian, and Greek trading settlements along the coasts. The Phoenician colony of Gadir (Cádiz) was particularly influential. Carthaginian expansion under Hamilcar Barca in the late 3rd century BCE laid groundwork for later Roman involvement.

### Roman Hispania and the Visigothic Kingdom

Between 210 and 205 BCE, Rome captured Carthaginian colonies during the Second Punic War, beginning a two-century process of conquest completed by the 1st century BCE. Roman rule lasted over six centuries, unified by law, Latin language, and roads. Hispania became a granary exporting gold, wool, olive oil, and wine. Notable figures born there include emperors Hadrian and Trajan, and philosopher Seneca. Christianity arrived in the 1st century CE. After the Western Roman Empire's collapse, Germanic tribes—including the Visigoths, Suebi, and Vandals—entered the peninsula. The Visigoths eventually unified most of Hispania under their kingdom centered on Toledo.

### Muslim Era and the Reconquista

In 711–718 CE, the Umayyad Caliphate conquered nearly all of the Iberian Peninsula, establishing Al-Andalus with Córdoba as its capital. The Visigothic Kingdom collapsed, and only northern Christian kingdoms survived in the mountainous regions. The Kingdom of Asturias-León became the nucleus of Christian resistance. Over centuries, Christian kingdoms gradually advanced southward in the Reconquista. The muladíes (Iberian Muslims) formed the majority of Al-Andalus by the 10th century. The Caliphate of Córdoba fragmented into Taifa kingdoms in the 11th century. The capture of Toledo in 1085 shifted power decisively toward Christian kingdoms. The Almoravids and Almohads briefly reunited Muslim territories with stricter Islamic rule. The Nasrid Kingdom of Granada, the last Muslim stronghold, fell in 1492 after the Granada War.

### Spanish Empire and the Age of Discovery

The dynastic union of Castile and Aragon in 1469 under Isabella I and Ferdinand II marked the de facto unification of Spain. In 1492, Jews were expelled, and Columbus reached the Americas, initiating European exploration and conquest. The Spanish Inquisition enforced Catholic orthodoxy, and Islam was outlawed by 1527. The Habsburg period (1516–1700) saw Spain become a dominant world power under Charles V and Philip II, with vast overseas territories. The Spanish Golden Age flourished culturally. However, continuous warfare—including the Dutch Revolt, Thirty Years' War, and Anglo-Spanish conflicts—drained resources. The 18th century brought Bourbon centralization through the Nueva Planta decrees, strengthening royal authority and administrative uniformity.

### Liberalism, Civil War, and Dictatorship

The Napoleonic invasion sparked the Peninsular War (1808–1814), devastating Spain and triggering independence movements in the Americas. The 19th century was marked by liberal-absolutist conflicts and the Carlist Wars. The Spanish-American War of 1898 resulted in the loss of Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines. The 20th century began with industrialization and labor movements, but political instability led to the Second Republic (1931) and then the Spanish Civil War (1936–1939). Francoist forces, supported by Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy, won in 1939. Francisco Franco ruled as a dictator until his death in 1975, promoting National Catholicism and a unitary Spanish identity.

### Restoration of Democracy and Modern Spain

Following Franco's death, King Juan Carlos I oversaw a peaceful transition to democracy. The 1978 Constitution established Spain as a parliamentary monarchy with 17 autonomous Communities. Spain joined NATO in 1982 and the European Economic Community in 1986. The 2008 financial crisis caused severe unemployment and social unrest. The 2017 Catalan independence referendum led to a constitutional crisis. Pedro Sánchez became Prime Minister in 2018 via a no-confidence vote and formed Spain's first coalition government in 2019. Recent challenges include managing migration, regional autonomy disputes, and economic recovery post-pandemic.

## Terms

- **Al-Andalus**: The Muslim-ruled portion of the Iberian Peninsula from 711 to 1492.
- **Reconquista**: The centuries-long Christian kingdoms' gradual reconquest of the Iberian Peninsula from Muslim rule.
- **Muladíes**: Muslims of ethnic Iberian origin in Al-Andalus.
- **Taifa**: Small independent Muslim kingdoms that emerged after the collapse of the Caliphate of Córdoba in the 11th century.
- **Nueva Planta decrees**: 18th-century Bourbon reforms centralizing Spanish authority and abolishing regional privileges.
- **Autonomous Communities**: The 17 self-governing regions established by the 1978 Constitution.
- **La Movida Madrileña**: A cultural movement in post-Franco Spain emphasizing freedom and artistic expression.
- **Francoist Spain**: The authoritarian dictatorship of Francisco Franco (1939–1975).
- **Cortes Generales**: The bicameral parliament of Spain, consisting of the Congress of Deputies and the Senate.
- **Plazas de soberanía**: Small Spanish territories in North Africa, including Ceuta and Melilla.

## Debates and Open Questions

Several scholarly debates persist regarding Spanish history and identity. The etymology of "Hispania" remains uncertain, with theories ranging from Phoenician origins ("land of rabbits") to pre-Indo-European roots. The nature of the Reconquista is debated—some scholars view it as a religious war, while others emphasize political and economic motivations. The extent of Muslim integration and conversion in Al-Andalus continues to be studied, particularly regarding the role of muladíes. The degree of continuity between Roman, Visigothic, and later Christian institutions is contested. In contemporary politics, the balance between regional autonomy and national unity remains contentious, especially in Catalonia and the Basque Country. The interpretation of Franco's legacy and the adequacy of Spain's transitional justice mechanisms are ongoing topics of discussion. The classification of Spain's languages and the enforcement of co-official status also generate debate. Additionally, the economic impact of the 2008 crisis and the effectiveness of subsequent reforms are regularly analyzed. The role of the Spanish monarchy in the democratic system and its future are subjects of public discourse. Finally, Spain's approach to immigration and integration, particularly from Latin America and North Africa, continues to evolve and provoke discussion.
