# Nicolas Léonard Sadi Carnot

## Overview
Nicolas Léonard Sadi Carnot (1796–1832) was a French military engineer and physicist whose 1824 book, *Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire*, established the first successful theory of the maximum efficiency of heat engines. Though largely ignored in his lifetime, his work became the foundation for the second law of thermodynamics, leading to his recognition as the "father of thermodynamics."

## Body

### Life and Career
Sadi Carnot was the son of Lazare Carnot, a prominent mathematician and military commander. His family faced political difficulties after the fall of Napoleon in 1815. Carnot was educated at the École polytechnique and became an officer in the French Army's engineering corps. His military career stagnated, and he eventually left the army in 1828 to pursue private intellectual interests. He was a bachelor who died in relative obscurity.

### *Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire*
Carnot's 1824 book sought to answer fundamental questions about the limits of heat engine efficiency. At the time, steam engines were vital to industry but lacked a scientific theory of heat. Carnot accepted the prevailing "caloric" theory, which viewed heat as a weightless fluid. He introduced an idealized engine cycle (the Carnot cycle) consisting of isothermal and adiabatic expansions and compressions. He argued that this reversible cycle represents the most efficient possible engine, as any irreversible process (like heat conduction between different temperatures) wastes potential work. His central conclusion was that the maximum work obtainable from heat depends only on the temperatures of the hot and cold reservoirs, not on the working substance.

### Reception and Legacy
Carnot's book received little notice until Émile Clapeyron's 1834 commentary. This later attracted the attention of William Thomson and Rudolf Clausius. Thomson used Carnot's principles to define an absolute thermodynamic temperature scale. Clausius modified Carnot's arguments to be compatible with the conservation of energy, leading him to define the concept of entropy and formulate the second law of thermodynamics. Carnot's work did not lead to immediate practical engine improvements but provided the theoretical framework for later developments, such as the diesel engine. Unpublished notes from 1832 show Carnot had later rejected the caloric theory and accepted the mechanical equivalent of heat, but he died before publishing these ideas.

## Terms
- **Carnot cycle**: An idealized, reversible sequence of four processes (two isothermal, two adiabatic) that defines the maximum theoretical efficiency for a heat engine operating between two temperatures.
- **Caloric**: The obsolete theory that heat is a weightless, invisible fluid that flows between bodies.
- **Isothermal**: A process occurring at a constant temperature.
- **Adiabatic**: A process in which no heat is transferred into or out of a system.
- **Entropy**: A thermodynamic property that quantifies the unavailability of a system's thermal energy for conversion into mechanical work, formally defined later by Clausius.
- **Second law of thermodynamics**: A fundamental principle stating that the total entropy of an isolated system can never decrease over time, implying limits on the efficiency of heat engines.
- **Phase transition**: The transformation of a substance from one state of matter to another (e.g., liquid to vapor) at a constant temperature and pressure.

## Debates and open questions
- The precise circumstances and cause of Carnot's mental illness and death are obscured by the actions of his brother, who destroyed many personal papers.
- Carnot's unpublished 1832 notes show he had moved beyond the caloric theory, but it is debated whether he recognized the full implications for his earlier work or why he did not publish these new ideas.
