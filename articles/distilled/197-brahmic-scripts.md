# Brahmic scripts

## Overview
Brahmic scripts (Indic scripts) are a family of **abugidas**—writing systems where each consonant carries an inherent vowel (usually short *ə*) that is modified or suppressed by diacritics. All descend from the **Brahmi script**, attested from the 3rd century BCE under Emperor Ashoka. The family split early into **Northern** (via Gupta) and **Southern** (via Kadamba, Pallava, Vatteluttu) branches, spreading across South, Southeast, and parts of Central and East Asia through trade, Buddhism, and cultural diffusion ("Indianization"). They underlie scripts for Indo-Aryan, Dravidian, Tibeto-Burman, Austroasiatic, Austronesian, Tai, Turkic, and Mongolic languages, and provided the tabular order (gojūon) for Japanese kana.

## Timeline
- **3rd century BCE** — Brahmi script clearly attested in Ashokan edicts.
- **c. 2nd century BCE** — Tamil-Brahmi inscriptions appear in South India.
- **4th–6th century CE** — Gupta script flourishes; becomes primary ancestor of Northern Brahmic scripts.
- **7th–8th century CE** — Major medieval scripts emerge: Nagari, Siddham, Sharada (North); Kadamba, Pallava, Vatteluttu (South).
- **8th century CE** — Regional scripts have diverged across South and Southeast Asia.
- **Medieval period** — Scripts spread to Southeast Asia via trading ports; initially used for Sanskrit, later adapted for local languages.
- **Present** — 100+ Brahmic scripts encoded in Unicode (v. 17.0).

## Body

### Origins and Early Divergence
Brahmi is the ultimate ancestor, firmly dated to the 3rd century BCE through Ashoka’s imperial edicts. By the time of the earliest surviving epigraphy, Brahmi had already split into **Northern** and **Southern** regional variants. Northern Brahmi evolved into the **Gupta script** (4th–6th century CE), which became the fountainhead for most North and Central Indian scripts. Southern Brahmi gave rise to **Kadamba**, **Pallava**, and **Vatteluttu** scripts by the early centuries CE, which in turn diversified into the scripts of South India and Southeast Asia.

### Northern Brahmic Development
From Gupta emerged a cascade of cursives during the medieval period. By the 7th–8th century, **Nagari**, **Siddham**, and **Sharada** were established. **Siddham** became the primary vehicle for Buddhist sutras; its calligraphic tradition survives in Japan, and its tabular presentation is believed to be the source of the *gojūon* ordering of Japanese kana. Nagari evolved into **Devanagari** and numerous descendants (Gujarati, Modi, Kaithi, Bengali–Assamese, Odia, Tirhuta, Newar, Ranjana, etc.). Other Northern branches include the **Sharada/Landa** group (Gurmukhi, Khojki, Khudabadi, Takri), **Tibetan** and its derivatives (ʼPhags-pa, Zanabazar, Soyombo), and the **Tocharian** (Slanting Brahmi) script of Central Asia.

### Southern Brahmic Development and Southeast Asian Spread
Southern scripts spread peacefully via **Indianization**—the diffusion of Indian learning, religion, and trade. At Southeast Asian ports, early inscriptions (c. early centuries CE) used Indian languages in Indian-derived scripts; later, these scripts were adapted for local languages (Mon, Burmese, Khmer, Thai, Lao, Cham, Javanese, Balinese, Batak, Bugis, Philippine scripts, etc.). The **Pallava** script was especially influential, giving rise to **Grantha**, **Tamil**, **Malayalam**, and the scripts of insular Southeast Asia (Kawi, Javanese, Balinese, Batak, Lontara, Baybayin, etc.). **Vatteluttu** spawned scripts in Kerala and Sri Lanka (Sinhala). The **Kadamba** line fed into **Telugu-Kannada**, which split into modern Telugu and Kannada.

### Structural Characteristics (Abugida Mechanics)
- **Inherent vowel**: Each consonant letter implies a short *ə* (schwa); in Bengali, Assamese, Odia it has shifted to /ɔ/.
- **Virama/halanta**: A diacritic suppressing the inherent vowel, creating a bare consonant.
- **Vowel signs (mātrās)**: Each vowel has an independent form (word-initial) and dependent forms that attach left, right, above, below, or both sides of the base consonant.
- **Conjuncts (ligatures)**: Consonant clusters (up to four in Devanagari) fuse into ligatures; special combining forms exist for *r*.
- **Nasalization & aspiration**: Marked by dedicated signs (anusvāra, visarga, etc.) on the dependent vowel.
- **Alphabetical order (varṇamālā)**: Phonetically systematic—vowels first, then consonants grouped by articulation: velars, palatals, retroflexes, dentals, labials, approximants, sibilants, others. Each stop series contains four phonation values (voiceless/voiced × unaspirated/aspirated) plus a nasal.

### Unicode Encoding
As of Unicode 17.0, all major historical and modern Brahmic scripts have been assigned code blocks, enabling digital interchange across the family.

## Terms
- ****Abugida**** — A segmental writing system where a base consonant carries an inherent vowel modified by diacritics.
- ****Brahmi**** — The ancestral script (3rd c. BCE) of all Brahmic families, used for Ashokan edicts.
- ****Gupta script**** — 4th–6th c. CE Northern Brahmi cursive; primary ancestor of North Indian scripts.
- ****Virama / Halanta**** — Diacritic that cancels a consonant’s inherent vowel.
- ****Mātrā**** — Dependent vowel sign attached to a consonant base.
- ****Conjunct / Ligature**** — Fused glyph representing a consonant cluster without intervening vowels.
- ****Varṇamālā**** — The phonetically ordered alphabet: vowels → stops by place → sonorants → sibilants.
- ****Indianization**** — Peaceful spread of Indian scripts, religion, and culture to Southeast Asia via trade.
- ****Siddham**** — 7th–8th c. Buddhist script; source of Japanese kana ordering and calligraphic tradition.
- ****Gojūon**** — The tabular “fifty-sound” ordering of Japanese kana, derived from Indic script charts.

## Debates and open questions
- **Ultimate origin of Brahmi**: Whether Brahmi derives from Aramaic/Imperial Aramaic, Kharosthi, or an indigenous prototype remains debated; the article notes only its clear attestation from Ashoka.
- **Precise transmission to Japan**: The pathway by which Siddham’s tabular order became Japanese *gojūon* is described as “believed to be descended” but not definitively proven.
- **Classification of marginal scripts**: Several listed scripts (e.g., Proto-Tai, Khom Thai, Marchung, Pungs-chen) are marked with queries or have uncertain genealogical placement.
- **Dating of Southern divergence**: Tamil-Brahmi (2nd c. BCE) is the earliest Southern epigraphy, but the exact timing of the Kadamba/Pallava/Vatteluttu split is not fixed in the source.
