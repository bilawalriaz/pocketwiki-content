# Charlie Chaplin

## Overview
Charlie Chaplin (1889–1977) was an English comic actor, filmmaker, and composer who became a global icon through his silent-film persona, the Tramp—a dignified, shabby-gentleman vagrant whose struggles blended slapstick with pathos. Rising from extreme London poverty and workhouse childhoods, he entered film in 1914 at Keystone Studios, quickly gaining creative control and co-founding United Artists (1919) to secure total independence. He wrote, directed, produced, edited, and scored nearly all his features, including *The Kid* (1921), *The Gold Rush* (1925), *City Lights* (1931), *Modern Times* (1936), and *The Great Dictator* (1940). His perfectionist, improvisational method—shooting vast footage without a finished script—yielded films noted for visual elegance, social commentary, and autobiographical resonance. Political controversies, a paternity suit, and FBI pressure forced his 1952 exile to Switzerland, where he completed his final films. He received an Honorary Academy Award in 1972 and a knighthood in 1975, and is widely regarded as one of cinema’s most influential artists.

## Body

### Early Life and Stage Apprenticeship
Chaplin’s childhood in Kennington, South London, was defined by destitution. His father, a singer, abandoned the family c. 1891 and died of cirrhosis in 1901. His mother, Hannah, suffered psychosis from syphilis and malnutrition, cycling through Cane Hill asylum from 1898 until her death in 1928. Chaplin entered Lambeth Workhouse at seven, then Norwood Schools for destitute children. At 14, he briefly lived alone while his mother was institutionalised. He began performing at five, joining the Eight Lancashire Lads clog-dancing troupe (1899–1900). Dissatisfied with dancing, he pursued comedy, securing roles in *Jim, a Romance of Cockayne* (1903) and a celebrated three-year run as Billy in *Sherlock Holmes* (1903–1906). After a failed solo act, his half-brother Sydney secured him a trial with Fred Karno’s prestigious comedy company in 1908. Karno became his "training ground": Chaplin learned to vary comic pace, mix pathos with slapstick, and developed the "Inebriate Swell" drunk routine. He headed Karno’s North American vaudeville tours (1910–1913), where he was hailed as a premier pantomimist.

### Film Breakthrough and the Tramp (1914–1917)
Signed by Keystone’s Mack Sennett in September 1913, Chaplin debuted in *Making a Living* (2 Feb 1914). For his second film, he assembled the Tramp costume—baggy pants, tight coat, small hat, large shoes, toothbrush moustache—to create contradictions that "made me feel the person he was." The character debuted publicly in *Kid Auto Races at Venice* (7 Feb 1914). After clashing with directors, Chaplin won the right to direct, starting with *Caught in the Rain* (4 May 1914). He directed nearly all his Keystone shorts (approx. one per week), introducing a slower, character-driven comedy that built a massive fanbase. At Essanay (Dec 1914–Dec 1915), he formed a stock company, recruited leading lady Edna Purviance, and deepened the Tramp’s emotional range, notably in *The Tramp* (Apr 1915) and *The Bank* (sad ending). By 1915, "Chaplinitis" made him the film industry’s first international star. At Mutual (1916–1917), earning $670,000/year, he built his own Los Angeles studio and produced elaborate two-reelers (*The Immigrant*, *Easy Street*, *The Adventurer*) considered his finest short work, though he grew frustrated by the four-week release schedule.

### Independence and Silent Features (1918–1936)
Seeking quality over quantity, Chaplin signed with First National (Jun 1917, $1 million) and built Charlie Chaplin Studios (completed Jan 1918). *A Dog’s Life* (Apr 1918) treated the Tramp as a "Pierrot"; *Shoulder Arms* (Oct 1918) risked war comedy successfully. Frustrated by First National’s resistance to his quality demands and merger rumours, he co-founded United Artists (1919) with Fairbanks, Pickford, and Griffith, giving artists full control. His first feature, *The Kid* (1921, 68 min), merged comedy and drama around poverty and parent-child separation, becoming a global hit. *A Woman of Paris* (1923), a restrained drama starring Purviance without Chaplin, failed commercially but influenced "sophisticated comedy." *The Gold Rush* (1925), inspired by the Klondike and Donner Party, cost nearly $1M, used 600 extras in the Truckee mountains, and grossed $5M US; Chaplin called it the film he wanted to be remembered by. *The Circus* (1928) was marred by a traumatic divorce from teenage wife Lita Grey ($600,000 settlement, largest ever) but won a special Academy Award.

Resisting sound, Chaplin produced *City Lights* (1931) as a silent film with his own composed score. The 21-month shoot nearly broke him, but the film succeeded critically and financially ($3M+), with its closing scene hailed as cinema’s highest acting moment. After a 16-month world tour (1931–1932)—during which he narrowly escaped assassination in Japan’s May 15 Incident—he made *Modern Times* (1936), a satire on industrial capitalism and the Great Depression. It used sound effects and a gibberish song (the Tramp’s only voice) but no dialogue, marking his first overt political realism.

### Sound Films, Controversy, and Exile (1940–1952)
*The Great Dictator* (1940), Chaplin’s first full talkie, satirised Hitler (Adenoid Hynkel) and featured a Jewish Barber. The five-minute final speech, delivered directly to camera against fascism, was praised by Churchill and Roosevelt but identified by scholars as the moment politics became inseparable from his star image, triggering a popularity decline. The 1940s brought escalating scandals: a paternity suit by Joan Barry (1943–44), an FBI-orchestrated Mann Act prosecution (acquitted), and marriage to 18-year-old Oona O’Neill (1943), daughter of playwright Eugene O’Neill—a union that lasted until his death and produced eight children. *Monsieur Verdoux* (1947), a black comedy about a wife-murdering bank clerk critiquing capitalism and war, was booed in the US and failed commercially. Amid Cold War hysteria, Chaplin was publicly accused of communism; the FBI investigated, HUAC subpoenaed him, and Representative John E. Rankin called for his deportation. *Limelight* (1952), an autobiographical, melancholy film about a faded comedian, premiered in London. En route, the US revoked his re-entry permit, citing political and moral grounds. Chaplin chose not to return, settling in Switzerland.

### European Years and Late Recognition (1953–1977)
At Manoir de Ban (Corsier-sur-Vevey), Chaplin sold his US studio and United Artists stock. *A King in New York* (1957), shot at Shepperton Studios, satirised McCarthyism, consumerism, and widescreen cinema; banned from US release until 1973. He spent his final decades re-editing, scoring, and re-releasing his silent features (*The Chaplin Revue*, 1959). Political winds shifted: *The New York Times* advocated his return (1962); he received honorary doctorates from Oxford and Durham (1962); a New York retrospective (1963) drew rave reviews. His memoir *My Autobiography* (1964) became a bestseller. His last film, *A Countess from Hong Kong* (1967, Technicolor, widescreen, starring Brando/Loren), failed critically and commercially. Health declined after strokes in the late 1960s; a planned film *The Freak* for daughter Victoria was abandoned. In 1972, the Academy awarded him an Honorary Oscar for "incalculable effect... making motion pictures the art form of this century," prompting a 12-minute standing ovation. He was knighted (KBE) in 1975. He died on Christmas Day 1977, aged 88. His coffin was stolen in 1978 and recovered weeks later.

### Filmmaking Method and Style
Chaplin never worked from a completed script until *The Great Dictator*. Early films began with a vague premise ("Charlie enters a health spa"); he built sets and improvised gags on film with his stock company, allowing narrative to emerge through "metamorphoses and permutations" (Robinson). From *A Woman of Paris* onward he prepared plots, but every film through *Modern Times* underwent radical restructuring. His perfectionism—funded by his financial independence—produced extreme shooting ratios: 53 takes per finished take in *The Kid*; 40,000 feet for the 20-minute *The Immigrant*. He acted out all roles for actors to imitate, personally edited all footage, and controlled every element, making him an early *auteur* (Sarris). Visually, his films are simple and stage-like; he believed "action is the main thing" and the camera should not intrude.

Comically, Chaplin slowed slapstick’s pace, exhausting each scene’s potential. Humour arose from the Tramp’s attitude—lifting his hat to a tree he bumped—rather than the bump itself. His "quirky mannerisms," "serious demeanour in slapstick," surreal object transformations, and puppet-like physicality (askew derby, drooping shoulders, geometrical walk) defined the style. Themes centred on the Tramp surviving a hostile world with dignity, defying authority, representing the underprivileged as "everyman turned heroic saviour" (Robinson, Louvish). Pathos—laughter and tears—stemmed from personal failure, social strictures, economic disaster, and nature. Autobiographical threads ran throughout: *The Kid* reflects workhouse trauma; *Limelight* mirrors his parents; *A King in New York* echoes his exile. Social commentary evolved from sympathetic underdog portrayals to overt politics: *Modern Times* (factory conditions), *The Great Dictator* (anti-fascism), *Monsieur Verdoux* (anti-war/capitalism), *A King in New York* (anti-McCarthyism).

### Music
Self-taught on piano, violin, and cello, Chaplin composed synchronised orchestral scores from *City Lights* (1931) onward, later scoring all silent re-releases. Unable to read notation, he collaborated with arrangers (David Raksin, Raymond Rasch, Eric James), singing or playing improvised themes that were developed in close collaboration; "not a note... was placed there without his assent" (Vance). Three songs became popular standards: "Smile" (*Modern Times*, hit for Nat King Cole 1954), "Terry’s Theme" (*Limelight*, "Eternally" by Jimmy Young 1952), and "This Is My Song" (*A Countess from Hong Kong*, Petula Clark, #1 UK 1967). He won a competitive Oscar for Best Original Score (*Limelight*) in 1973.

## Terms
- **Tramp**: Chaplin’s iconic screen persona—a shabby but dignified vagrant in baggy pants, tight coat, small bowler hat, large shoes, toothbrush moustache, and bamboo cane—whose gentlemanly manners and resilience amid poverty blended slapstick with pathos.
- **Slapstick**: A comedy style relying on exaggerated physical action (falls, chases, blows); Chaplin refined it by slowing the pace, focusing on character reaction, and integrating emotional depth.
- **Pathos**: The quality of evoking pity or sadness; Chaplin systematically combined it with comedy, creating "laughter and tears" through the Tramp’s dignity in suffering.
- **United Artists**: Distribution company co-founded in 1919 by Chaplin, Douglas Fairbanks, Mary Pickford, and D.W. Griffith, allowing artists to finance and control their own films—a revolutionary model for creative independence.
- **Stock company**: A regular ensemble of actors (e.g., Edna Purviance, Eric Campbell, Henry Bergman) Chaplin reused across films, enabling efficient improvisation and consistent character dynamics.
- **Auteur**: A filmmaker who exercises total authorial control over a film (writing, directing, editing, producing); Chaplin is identified as an early exemplar due to his complete personal oversight.
- **Mann Act**: US federal law (1910) prohibiting interstate transport of women for "immoral purposes"; the FBI used it to prosecute Chaplin in 1944 (he was acquitted) as part of a smear campaign.
- **House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC)**: Congressional committee (1938–1975) investigating alleged communist influence; Chaplin was subpoenaed but never testified, though his political associations fueled deportation calls.
- **Manoir de Ban**: Chaplin’s 35-acre estate in Corsier-sur-Vevey, Switzerland, purchased in 1953 after his US exile; now the "Chaplin’s World" museum.
- **Mutual period (1916–1917)**: Chaplin’s $670,000/year contract producing 12 two-reelers at his own studio; widely regarded by scholars as his creative peak for short-form comedy.

## Debates and open questions
- **Political allegiance**: Chaplin denied being a communist, calling himself a "peacemonger," but his advocacy for a Second Front (1942), support for Soviet–American friendship groups, and friendships with suspected communists led to FBI investigation and 1952 exile. Declassified British archives (2003) reveal George Orwell secretly listed him as a "secret communist" in 1949. The extent of his party membership or ideological commitment remains debated.
- **Paternity of Carol Ann Barry**: Blood tests excluded Chaplin, but California law at the time barred such evidence; the court declared him the father (1944). The scientific vs. legal determination remains a point of contention.
- **Authorship of film scores**: Critics have argued Chaplin’s arrangers (Raksin, Rasch, James) deserve primary credit. Raksin insisted Chaplin provided the "musical imperative" and approved every note; the collaborative process blurs authorship lines.
- **Artistic merit of late films**: *A King in New York* (1957) and *A Countess from Hong Kong* (1967) received mixed contemporary reviews. Scholars debate whether they represent a decline or a misunderstood evolution in satire and style.
- **Cinematic modernity**: Donald McCaffrey argues Chaplin’s stage-like visual simplicity shows he "never completely understood film as a medium"; others (Kamin) counter that his comedic longevity proves a sophisticated grasp of cinematic space and timing.
- **Birth details**: No official birth certificate exists; Chaplin believed he was born at East Street, Walworth. Paternal Romani heritage is speculated but unverified ("Without a birth certificate, no one will ever know the truth").
