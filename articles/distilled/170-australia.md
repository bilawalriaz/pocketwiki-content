# Australia

## Overview

Australia, officially the Commonwealth of Australia, is a country comprising the mainland of the Australian continent, the island of Tasmania, and numerous smaller islands. It is the sixth-largest country in the world by land area (7,688,287 km²), the world's flattest and driest inhabited continent, and a megadiverse country with a wide variety of landscapes and climates. A parliamentary democracy with a constitutional monarchy and a federation of six states and ten territories, Australia has a highly urbanised population of almost 29 million concentrated on the eastern seaboard, a highly developed economy, and one of the highest per capita incomes globally.

## Timeline

- **50,000–65,000 years ago** — Ancestors of Aboriginal Australians begin arriving from Southeast Asia during the Last Glacial Period
- **1606** — Dutch ship Duyfken, captained by Willem Janszoon, makes the first documented European landing in Australia
- **1770** — Captain James Cook maps the east coast, names it "New South Wales," and claims it for Great Britain
- **26 January 1788** — First Fleet arrives at Sydney Cove; the date later becomes Australia's national day
- **1855–1890** — Six colonies individually gain responsible government
- **1 January 1901** — Colonies federate, forming the Commonwealth of Australia
- **1914–1918** — Australia joins the Allies in World War I; ~60,000 of 324,000 who served overseas are killed
- **1939–1945** — Australia fights in World War II; Britain's defeat in Singapore (1942) shifts Australia's principal alliance to the US
- **1942** — Statute of Westminster Adoption Act (backdated to 1939) confirms federal legislative independence from the UK
- **1986** — Australia Acts sever remaining constitutional ties with the UK while maintaining the monarch as Queen of Australia
- **1999** — Constitutional referendum: 55% of voters reject abolishing the monarchy and becoming a republic

## Body

### Etymology

The name "Australia" derives from the Latin *Terra Australis Incognita* ('unknown southern land'), a name used for a hypothetical southern continent since ancient times. Dutch visitors in the 17th century called the continent New Holland. Explorer Matthew Flinders circumnavigated the continent in 1803 and popularised the name Australia, though his 1814 published account used Terra Australis. Governor Lachlan Macquarie officially recommended the name Australia in December 1817; the British Admiralty adopted it in 1824, and Parliament used it in legislation in 1828. The name "Commonwealth of Australia" was formalised in the Commonwealth of Australia Constitution Act 1900 (UK). Colloquial names include "Oz," "Straya," and "Down Under."

### Indigenous prehistory

Indigenous Australians comprise two broad groups: Aboriginal Australians (mainland and many islands, including Tasmania) and Torres Strait Islanders (a distinct Melanesian people). Human habitation began 50,000 to 65,000 years ago via land bridges and short sea crossings from Southeast Asia. The Madjedbebe rock shelter in Arnhem Land is possibly the oldest human site; the oldest human remains, at Lake Mungo, date to approximately 42,000 years ago. At first European contact, Aboriginal Australians spoke at least 250 distinct languages, with pre-settlement population estimates ranging from 300,000 to 3 million. Cultures were deeply connected to land, with The Dreaming maintained through oral tradition, songs, dance, and paintings. Some groups practised fire-stick farming, fish farming, and built semi-permanent shelters. Torres Strait Islanders settled their islands at least 2,500 years ago, were seafarers, and by the 1300s had villages. By the mid-18th century, northern Aboriginal groups had contact and trade with Makassan trepangers from present-day Indonesia.

### European exploration and colonisation

In 1606, Willem Janszoon's Duyfken made the first documented European landing; later that year Luís Vaz de Torres sailed through Torres Strait. Abel Tasman reached Van Diemen's Land in 1642 and mapped the north coast in 1644; the Dutch named the continent New Holland. In 1770, Captain James Cook mapped and claimed the east coast as New South Wales. In 1786, Britain announced a penal colony; the First Fleet under Captain Arthur Phillip arrived at Sydney Cove on 26 January 1788. Most early settlers were convicts assigned as labourers to free settlers. The 1808 Rum Rebellion led to a temporary military junta. The indigenous population declined for 150 years mainly due to infectious disease; no treaties were signed, and frontier conflicts killed tens of thousands of Indigenous people and thousands of settlers, with many historians arguing acts of genocide occurred.

### Colonial expansion

Settlement spread to Van Diemen's Land (1803), across the Blue Mountains (1813), and to King George Sound (1827), extending the British claim to the whole continent. The Swan River Colony (1829) became Western Australia. Separate colonies were carved from New South Wales: Tasmania (1825), South Australia (1836), Victoria (1851), and Queensland (1859). South Australia and Victoria were free colonies (never accepted convicts); the convict system was abolished in eastern colonies by the 1850s, while Western Australia accepted convicts from 1850 to 1868. The six colonies gained responsible government between 1855 and 1890. Gold rushes from the early 1850s brought migrants from China, North America, and continental Europe, and led to the 1854 Eureka Rebellion. The 1860s saw blackbirding (coerced South Sea Islander labour). From 1886, colonial governments began removing Aboriginal children from families under forced assimilation policies.

### Federation to the World Wars

Federation occurred on 1 January 1901 after a decade of planning and referendums. Australia was a self-governing dominion within the British Empire, a founding member of the League of Nations (1920) and the United Nations (1945). The Statute of Westminster 1931 ended UK legislative power over Australia at the federal level without consent; Australia adopted it in 1942, backdated to 1939. The Australian Capital Territory was formed in 1911 for the future capital Canberra (Melbourne served as temporary capital 1901–1927); the Northern Territory transferred to the Commonwealth in 1911. Australia administered Papua (from 1905) and New Guinea (from 1920), unified in 1949 and independent in 1975. In World War I, ~60,000 of 324,000 Australians serving overseas were killed; the 1915 Gallipoli campaign is commemorated on Anzac Day. In World War II, Australia fought in Pacific, European, and Mediterranean theatres; Britain's 1942 Singapore defeat and Japanese attacks led to a shift to the US as principal ally, formalised in the 1951 ANZUS treaty.

### Post-war and contemporary eras

Post-war, Australia saw rising living standards and a large European immigration wave ("New Australians"), justified by "populate or perish"; the White Australia policy was gradually relaxed from the 1960s. Australia participated in the Korean War, Malayan Emergency, and Vietnam War (1962–1973). The 1967 referendum gave the federal government power to legislate for Aboriginal Australians and included them in the census. The High Court's Mabo v Queensland (No 2) recognised native title, ruling Australia was not *terra nullius* at European settlement. Following the 1973 end of the White Australia policy, large non-European (mostly Asian) immigration transformed demography and culture. The 1986 Australia Acts severed remaining constitutional ties with the UK. Australia joined the US in the Afghanistan War (2001–2021) and Iraq War (2003–2009); China became its largest trading partner. During the COVID-19 pandemic, lockdowns were implemented from March 2020 and gradually eased after 2021 vaccine rollout; in October 2023, COVID-19 was declared no longer a communicable disease incident of national significance.

### Geography, geology, climate, and biodiversity

Mainland Australia lies between latitudes 9° and 44° south, surrounded by the Indian and Pacific oceans. The Great Barrier Reef, the world's largest coral reef, extends over 2,300 km off the north-east coast. Most of Australia is arid or semi-arid; the mainland's average height is 325 m (vs. 870 m for all continents). Mount Kosciuszko (2,228 m) is the mainland's highest peak. The Murray-Darling is the major river system; Australia's rivers have the lowest discharge into the sea of any continent. About 95% of the population lives within 100 km of the coast. Australia separated from Antarctica about 35 million years ago; rising seas separated it from New Guinea ~8,000 years ago and Tasmania ~6,000 years ago. The Australian plate moves north-northeast ~6–7 cm/year; the 1989 Newcastle earthquake killed 13. The oldest zircon crystals on Earth (4.4 billion years) were found in Western Australia. The climate ranges from wet tropical to arid, influenced by the El Niño–Southern Oscillation, Indian Ocean Dipole, and Southern Annular Mode. Climate change has caused a 1.5 °C temperature rise since 1910. Australia is one of 17 megadiverse countries: ~94% of amphibians, 93% of reptiles and flowering plants, 69% of mammals, and 46% of birds are endemic. About 70% of species are estimated undiscovered. Australia has lost more mammal species than any other continent over the past two centuries.

### Government, economy, and demographics

Australia combines UK elements (fused executive, constitutional monarchy, strong party discipline) with US elements (federalism, written constitution, strong bicameralism). The Parliament comprises the monarch, Senate (76 senators), and House of Representatives (150 members). Following elections on 3 May 2025, Anthony Albanese is prime minister; Charles III is King of Australia, represented by the governor-general. Voting is compulsory. The two major political groups are the Labor Party (centre-left) and the Coalition of Liberal and National parties (centre-right). The economy is the world's twelfth-largest by nominal terms, with the second-highest wealth per adult (2021). China accounts for roughly 40% of exports. Defence spending was 1.9% of GDP in 2022. Australia has no single federal charter of rights, though the ACT, Victoria, and Queensland have state-based ones. The population (27,724,744 as of September 2025) is highly urbanised (67% in capital city areas in 2018). Immigrants account for 30% of the population, the highest proportion among major Western nations. English is the de facto official language; at the 2021 census, 38.9% identified as having no religion and 43.9% as Christian. Life expectancy is 83 years. Education is compulsory from about age 5 to about 16; Australia has the highest ratio of international students per head of population in the world.

### Culture

Australian culture reflects Indigenous traditions, British and Irish heritage, and post-1945 multicultural immigration. Many Australians identify egalitarianism, mateship, irreverence, and lack of formality as national traits. Australia has over 100,000 Aboriginal rock art sites; the Heidelberg School was the first "distinctively Australian" Western art movement. Writers Henry Lawson and Banjo Paterson captured bush life; Paterson's "Waltzing Matilda" (1895) is regarded as the unofficial national anthem. Patrick White won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1973. The Story of the Kelly Gang (1906) was the world's first feature-length narrative film; the Australian New Wave of the 1970s brought international success. Cuisine includes bush tucker, British-influenced dishes (meat pie, fish and chips), and post-war fusion (flat white, avocado toast). Australia has participated in every Summer Olympics and Commonwealth Games, hosted the Olympics twice (1956, 2000; Brisbane set for 2032), and won the men's Cricket World Cup a record six times.

## Terms

- **Terra nullius**: Latin for 'land belonging to no one'; the High Court ruled in Mabo (1992) that Australia was not terra nullius at European settlement.
- **The Dreaming**: Sacred era in Aboriginal mythology in which ancestral totemic spirit beings formed creation and established laws, structures, and ceremonies.
- **ANZUS**: Mutual security alliance between Australia, New Zealand, and the United States, maintained since 1951.
- **White Australia policy**: Immigration restrictions strengthened after Federation (1901) and gradually relaxed from the 1960s, ending in 1973.
- **Native title**: Pre-colonial land interests recognised in law for the first time in Mabo v Queensland (No 2).
- **Responsible government**: System where the executive is accountable to parliament; gained by each colony between 1855 and 1890.
- **Megadiverse country**: One of 17 countries harbouring the majority of Earth's species; Australia's long isolation makes much of its biota unique.
- **Blackbirding**: Practice from the 1860s where South Sea Islanders were coerced or abducted into indentured labour, mainly by Queensland colonists.
- **Medicare**: Universal national health insurance scheme, introduced 1975, made universal under that name in 1981, nominally funded by a 2% income tax surcharge.
- **ANZAC**: Australian and New Zealand Army Corps; the 1915 Gallipoli defeat is seen as a "baptism of fire" forging national identity, commemorated on Anzac Day.

## Debates and open questions

- It is uncertain how many waves of immigration contributed to the ancestors of modern Aboriginal Australians.
- Estimates of the Aboriginal population before British settlement range from 300,000 to 3 million.
- Pre-colonial Aboriginal practices have been variously characterised as "hunter-gatherer," "agricultural," "natural cultivation," and "intensification."
- Many historians argue frontier conflicts included acts of genocide by settlers.
- There is ongoing scientific debate over the role of human activity versus climate change in the extinction of Australian megafauna about 46,000 years ago.
- The precise scope of the governor-general's reserve powers is unclear; their most notable exercise was the dismissal of the Whitlam government in the 1975 constitutional crisis.
- What "Australian values" mean, and whether Australians uphold them, has been debated since before Federation.
- A 2011–2012 report found 44% of the population lacks high literacy and numeracy competence, interpreted by some as lacking "skills needed for everyday life."
