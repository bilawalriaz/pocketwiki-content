# Djembe

## Overview
The djembe is a rope-tuned, goblet-shaped drum from West Africa, played with bare hands. Originating with the Mandinka people, it is renowned for its wide range of sounds and its central role in traditional ensembles for dance, ceremony, and community gathering. Its global spread in the late 20th century, driven by national ballets and emigrating master drummers, has made it one of the most recognized African instruments worldwide.

## Timeline
- **c. 1230 CE** — The Mali Empire, associated with the traditional distribution of the djembe, is established.
- **1952** — Fodéba Keïta founds Les Ballets Africains, introducing the djembe to European audiences.
- **1958** — Guinea gains independence; Les Ballets Africains becomes the first national ballet.
- **1960s** — Ladji Camara begins teaching djembe in the United States.
- **1984** — Death of Sékou Touré; funding for Guinean ballets declines, leading to emigration of master drummers.
- **1991** — The documentary *Djemebefola* is released, bringing the djembe to a wide international audience.
- **1998** — Mamoudou Conde begins exploring female djembe players in ballets; the documentary *Mögöbalu* is released.
- **2000s** — Modern mounting systems and commercially produced djembes become widespread; djembe tourism grows.
- **2004** — The all-female group Amazones (later Nimbaya!) first tours the US.
- **2007** — The film *The Visitor* features the djembe, increasing its mainstream recognition.

## Body

### Origin and Traditional Role
The djembe is associated with the Mandinka blacksmith caste (Numu), though its use is not hereditary. Its history likely spans several centuries, possibly predating the Mali Empire. Traditionally, the djembe is played only by men, often in an ensemble with dunun bass drums. The music is participatory, creating rhythm for dance, song, or work, with a lead drummer improvising to accentuate dancers' movements.

### Global Spread and Modern History
Before the 1950s, the djembe was known only locally. Its international exposure began with Guinea's national ballets in the late 1950s, which toured globally. After funding declined in the 1980s, master drummers like Mamady Keïta and Famoudou Konaté emigrated to the West, establishing teaching and performance communities. This diaspora, along with documentaries, recordings, and tourism, fueled global interest. The instrument has since been adopted by Western artists and manufacturers, leading to commercial production and a growing market for educational materials.

### Sound and Technique
The djembe is exceptionally loud for its size. Players produce three basic sounds: a low-pitched bass, a medium-pitched tone, and a high-pitched slap, achieved by varying hand position and contact area. Acoustically, it is a Helmholtz resonator, where the bass pitch is determined by the shell's shape, while tone and slap pitches rise with skin tension. Skilled players can produce many additional sounds for solo effects.

### Construction and Tuning
A traditional djembe is carved from a single log of dense hardwood (e.g., lenke, djalla) and headed with rawhide, most commonly goatskin. The modern mounting system uses steel rings and synthetic rope to apply high tension, replacing older cowhide systems. Tuning is done by tightening the rope, often using a "Mali weave" pattern to create twists. Decorations can include metal rattles (sege sege), rope wraps, and carvings, though elaborate carvings are a more recent trend driven by Western demand.

### Changing Traditions
Traditional gender roles are evolving. While historically a male instrument, female djembefolas have gained prominence, notably through the all-female ballet group Nimbaya!. Commercial production has also increased, with instruments made outside West Africa using substitute woods or synthetic materials, differing from traditional hand-carved drums.

## Terms
- **Djembefola**: A person who plays the djembe; the term does not imply a specific skill level.
- **Dunun**: A family of rope-tuned, cylindrical bass drums that traditionally accompany the djembe.
- **Numu**: The Mandinka caste of blacksmiths, associated with the origin of the djembe.
- **Helmholtz resonator**: An acoustic system where sound is produced by air vibrating in a cavity; the djembe's bass frequency is determined by its shell shape.
- **Mali weave**: A rope-tuning technique that creates twists in the vertical ropes to increase skin tension.
- **Sege sege / Sesse**: Metal rattles attached to a djembe for decoration and added sound.
- **Nyama**: A spiritual energy believed by the Malinké to run through all things, influencing the choice of wood for djembes.
- **Accompaniment pattern**: A recurring rhythmic figure played by most instruments in a djembe ensemble, over which a soloist improvises.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact age of the djembe is unclear due to a lack of written records; it may predate or postdate the Mali Empire (c. 1230 CE).
- There is no firm consensus on whether the benefits of the three-ring mounting system (introduced in the 2000s) justify its extra weight and complexity.
- The impact of djembe tourism and Western demand is debated, with concerns about the commodification and change of traditional djembe culture.
