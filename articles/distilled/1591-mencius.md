# Mencius

## Overview
Mencius (c. 371–289 BC), born Meng Ke, was a Confucian philosopher honored as the "Second Sage" for his role as the principal interpreter of Confucius’s thought. Active during the Warring States period, he traveled among feudal states advising rulers and taught at the Jixia Academy in Qi. His eponymous text, the *Mencius*—recorded as dialogues rather than aphorisms—was later canonized as one of the Four Books central to Neo-Confucian orthodoxy. Mencius is best known for arguing that human nature is innately good, possessing "Four Beginnings" (sprouts of benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and wisdom) that require cultivation through education and a benevolent political environment. He asserted that rulers who fail to provide humane governance forfeit the Mandate of Heaven and may be legitimately overthrown, redefining the ruler as a steward subordinate to the people’s welfare.

## Timeline
- **c. 371 BC** — Born Meng Ke in the state of Zou (modern Zoucheng, Shandong).
- **c. 4th c. BC** — Studied under Zisi (Confucius’s grandson); began itinerant career advising rulers.
- **319–312 BC** — Served as official and scholar at the Jixia Academy in the state of Qi.
- **c. 312 BC** — Observed three-year mourning leave for mother’s death; subsequently retired from public life.
- **c. 289 BC** — Died; buried in the Cemetery of Mencius near Zoucheng.
- **1130–1200** — Zhu Xi establishes the *Mencius* as one of the Four Books of Neo-Confucianism.
- **1711** — François Noël publishes the first full Latin edition of the *Mencius* in Prague.
- **2008** — First Mencius Institute established in Xuzhou, China.
- **2016** — First overseas Mencius Institute opens at Universiti Tunku Abdul Rahman, Malaysia.

## Body

### Life and Legendary Mother
Mencius was born in Zou to the Meng clan; his father died early, leaving his mother, Meng Mu (née Zhang), to raise him. She is celebrated in the *Biographies of Exemplary Women* by Liu Xiang for three formative interventions. First, she moved their home three times—from a cemetery (where Mencius mimicked burial rites), to a market (where he mimicked merchants), to a school (where he mimicked scholars)—establishing the idiom *mengmu sanqian* on the importance of environment. Second, when Mencius played truant, she cut her weaving on the loom to demonstrate that abandoning a task midway ruins the work, inspiring his diligence. Third, when Mencius sought to divorce his wife for a breach of etiquette (sitting improperly), his mother cited the *Book of Rites* to prove Mencius himself had violated ritual by failing to announce his entry, forcing him to acknowledge his error.

### Human Nature and the Four Beginnings
Mencius’s central doctrine is that human nature (*xing*) is inherently good (*shan*). He argues that evil arises from environmental corruption or the failure to cultivate innate potentials, not from nature itself. To demonstrate this, he cites the "child at the well" thought experiment: any witness spontaneously feels alarm and compassion, not for reward or reputation. This feeling of commiseration is the **beginning of benevolence (*ren*)**; the feeling of shame and dislike is the **beginning of righteousness (*yi*)**; the feeling of deference is the **beginning of propriety (*li*)**; and the feeling of right and wrong is the **beginning of wisdom (*zhi*)**. These "Four Beginnings" (or "Four Sprouts," *siduan*) are as natural as four limbs. Mencius positions himself between Xunzi (who held nature is bad and requires artificial ritual correction) and Taoists (who held nature is effortlessly good and needs no cultivation): the sprouts are innate but require active "extension" and education to mature into full virtues.

### Education and Destiny
Mencius viewed education not as memorization but as "seeking the lost mind"—recovering and expanding the innate moral sprouts. He famously warned, "One who believes all in the *Book of Documents* would be better off without the *Book*," advocating critical interrogation of texts for internal consistency and empirical probability. Regarding **Destiny (*ming*)**, Mencius distinguished it from fatalism: Destiny is the unforeseen, constructive path that arises when one acts in accordance with virtue; it cannot be forced by intellect. "One who understands Destiny will not stand beneath a tottering wall" signifies that moral agency requires prudence, not passive resignation. Cultivating the Way is like maintaining a path: "Unused pathways are covered with weeds."

### Politics and Economics
Mencius elevated the people (*min*) above the ruler in the hierarchy of political legitimacy. A ruler who governs tyrannically loses the status of a true king and becomes a mere "villain" (*zei*); overthrowing such a figure is not regicide but the execution of a criminal ("I have merely heard of killing a villain Zhou, but I have not heard of murdering [him as] the ruler"). The ruler is a steward who must practice benevolent government (*renzheng*)—ensuring the people have "permanent property" (secure livelihood) to support constant virtue—before expecting loyalty. Policy prescriptions include: low, progressive land taxes (only supplemental revenue); free trade and zero import tariffs (taxing merchants hurts villagers); state prevention of monopolies but otherwise non-interference in markets; and resource management aligned with natural cycles of replenishment, prioritizing posterity over profit. Mencius distinguished "superior men" (who pursue righteousness) from "inferior men" (who pursue profit), arguing that a profit-driven state undermines the moral foundation of society.

### Influence and Reception
Mencius’s synthesis of Confucian ethics with a theory of innate goodness became the orthodox interpretation for later Chinese philosophy, particularly the Neo-Confucians of the Song dynasty. Zhu Xi (1130–1200) enshrined the *Mencius* as one of the Four Books, the core curriculum for civil service examinations for centuries. Early Jesuit missionaries (e.g., Matteo Ricci) neglected the text, viewing Neo-Confucianism as contaminated by Buddhism and Taoism, and mistranslating Mencius’s critique of self-centeredness as a condemnation of clerical celibacy. François Noël, recognizing Zhu Xi’s commentary as a native development of Confucianism, published the first complete Latin edition in 1711, though its influence remained largely confined to Central and Eastern Europe.

## Terms
- ****Second Sage (亞聖, *Yàshèng*)**** — Honorific title for Mencius, placing him second only to Confucius in the Confucian tradition.
- ****Four Beginnings / Four Sprouts (四端, *Sìduān*)**** — The innate moral germs—commiseration (benevolence), shame (righteousness), deference (propriety), and right/wrong discernment (wisdom)—which Mencius argues all humans possess.
- ****Human Nature is Good (性善, *Xìngshàn*)**** — Mencius’s signature doctrine: moral virtue is intrinsic to human biology/psychology; evil results from environmental damage or neglect of cultivation.
- ****Benevolent Government (仁政, *Rénzhèng*)**** — Rule that secures the people’s material welfare ("permanent property") as the necessary condition for moral development and political legitimacy.
- ****Destiny (命, *Mìng*)**** — The Heaven-ordained, unforeseen path that unfolds when one acts virtuously; distinct from fatalism, it requires active moral effort and prudence.
- ****Permanent Property (恆產, *Héngchǎn*)**** — Stable, sufficient livelihood (land or occupation) that allows commoners to maintain constant virtue; a prerequisite for a well-ordered society.
- ****Jixia Academy (稷下學宮, *Jìxià Xuégōng*)**** — State-sponsored intellectual center in Qi (c. 319–312 BC) where Mencius served as a scholar-official.
- ****Mengmu Sanqian (孟母三遷)**** — "Mencius’s mother moves three times"; idiom signifying the critical role of environment in moral education.
- ****Four Books (四書, *Sìshū*)**** — Canonical Neo-Confucian curriculum (Great Learning, Doctrine of the Mean, Analects, Mencius) fixed by Zhu Xi in the 12th century.
- ****Villain / Lone Man (賊, *Zéi*)**** — Mencius’s term for a ruler who violates benevolence, thereby forfeiting the title "king" and the protection due a sovereign.

## Debates and open questions
- **Innate goodness vs. innate badness:** The core pre-Qin debate between Mencius (nature is good; cultivation = extension) and Xunzi (nature is bad; cultivation = transformation via ritual/artifice). The text notes Mencius explicitly positions himself between Xunzi and Taoists (who denied the need for cultivation).
- **Regicide vs. tyrannicide:** Whether Mencius’s statement on King Zhou of Shang constitutes a general right of rebellion or a narrow, retrospective judgment on a unique historical tyrant remains a major interpretive dispute in Confucian political theory.
- **Mencius’s authorship and textual layers:** The *Mencius* comprises long dialogues likely compiled by disciples; the extent to which specific arguments reflect the historical Mencius versus later Warring States or Han editors is unresolved in the source.
- **Jesuit reception and mistranslation:** The early modern European dismissal of the *Mencius* as "Buddhist/Taoist contamination" and the specific mistranslation regarding celibacy raise questions about how cross-cultural hermeneutics shaped the Western understanding of Confucianism for centuries.
