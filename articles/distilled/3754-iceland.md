# Iceland

## Overview
Iceland is a Nordic island nation in the North Atlantic, situated on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge between the Eurasian and North American tectonic plates. Its unique geology, featuring active volcanoes and geothermal energy, has shaped its history and modern economy. Culturally and politically linked to Europe, it is the world's most sparsely populated country and a highly developed nation with a strong social welfare system.

## Timeline
- **c. 870** — Garðar Svavarsson becomes the first Norseman to circumnavigate Iceland.
- **874** — Ingólfr Arnarson becomes the first permanent settler, founding Reykjavík.
- **930** — The Althing, one of the world's oldest parliaments, is established.
- **1262–1264** — The Old Covenant ends the Commonwealth period; Iceland comes under Norwegian rule.
- **1397** — Iceland becomes part of the Kalmar Union, later under Danish rule.
- **1550** — Lutheranism is imposed by force; the last Catholic bishop is executed.
- **1814** — The Treaty of Kiel cedes Iceland from Norway to Denmark.
- **1918** — The Danish–Icelandic Act of Union establishes Iceland as a sovereign state in personal union with Denmark.
- **1944** — Iceland becomes a republic following a referendum, ending ties with Denmark.
- **1949** — Iceland becomes a founding member of NATO.
- **1980** — Vigdís Finnbogadóttir is elected the world's first democratically elected female head of state.
- **2008** — A major financial crisis collapses Iceland's banking system.

## Body

### Settlement and Commonwealth
Permanent Norse settlement began in 874 AD. By 930, the Althing was founded as a legislative and judicial assembly for the independent Commonwealth. The population was of Norse and Gaelic origin. Christianity was adopted around 999–1000. Internal strife during the Age of the Sturlungs (c. 1220–1264) led to the signing of the Old Covenant, bringing Iceland under Norwegian rule.

### Foreign Rule and Independence Movement
Iceland passed from Norwegian to Danish control with the Kalmar Union (1397). The Reformation was enforced in 1550. Centuries of harsh trade restrictions, natural disasters, and epidemics (like the Black Death) caused population decline. A national consciousness inspired by European nationalism emerged in the 19th century, leading to a gradual home rule movement. The Danish–Icelandic Act of Union (1918) granted full sovereignty, though Denmark retained some foreign affairs control.

### The Modern Republic
During World War II, Iceland's strategic location led to a British, then U.S., occupation. In 1944, a referendum established the Republic of Iceland. The post-war era saw economic growth from fisheries and Marshall Plan aid. Iceland joined NATO in 1949 and hosted the 1986 Reagan–Gorbachev summit. The economy diversified after joining the European Economic Area in 1994, but a severe financial crisis struck in 2008. The country has since recovered, with tourism becoming a major industry.

### Geography and Geology
Iceland is the world's only large landmass on a mid-ocean ridge above sea level, sitting on the divergent boundary of the Eurasian and North American plates. This causes high volcanic and geothermal activity, with about 30 active volcanic systems. The interior is a volcanic plateau of sand, lava fields, and glaciers. The climate is subarctic, moderated by the Gulf Stream. The only native land mammal at settlement was the Arctic fox; all other mammals were introduced.

### Politics and Society
Iceland is a parliamentary republic with a ceremonial president and a powerful prime minister. The Althing, re-established in 1845, is arguably the world's oldest parliament. It has a high level of democracy, gender equality, and press freedom. The country has no standing army but a coast guard. It is a member of NATO and the EEA but not the EU. The population is small (~395,000), with over 70% living in the southwest.

### Economy and Energy
Iceland has a market economy with low taxes and a high trade union membership. Historically dependent on fishing, the economy now relies on tourism, renewable energy, and services. It is the world's largest electricity producer per capita, with ~85% of primary energy from geothermal and hydropower. The 2008 financial crisis led to a severe contraction, but the economy has since stabilized and grown.

## Terms
- **Althing**: The national parliament of Iceland, founded in 930 and re-established in 1845, making it one of the world's oldest legislative assemblies.
- **Commonwealth**: The period of Icelandic self-governance (c. 930–1262) under the Althing, before submission to Norwegian rule.
- **Kalmar Union**: A personal union that from 1397 to 1523 united the kingdoms of Denmark, Norway, and Sweden under a single monarch.
- **Patronymic**: A naming system where a person's surname is derived from their father's (or mother's) first name, e.g., Jónsson ("son of Jón").
- **Mid-Atlantic Ridge**: A divergent tectonic plate boundary in the Atlantic Ocean where new oceanic crust is formed; Iceland is a rare subaerial portion of it.
- **Geothermal energy**: Heat energy generated and stored in the Earth's crust, widely harnessed in Iceland for electricity and heating.
- **Cod Wars**: A series of four confrontations (1952–1976) between Iceland and the United Kingdom over fishing rights and Iceland's extension of its exclusive economic zone.
- **EEA (European Economic Area)**: An agreement allowing Iceland, along with EU member states, to participate in the EU's single market for goods, services, capital, and people.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **EU Membership**: A long-standing debate. Iceland applied in 2009 but the process was halted. A referendum on resuming negotiations is scheduled for August 2026, with public opinion divided, often citing concerns over control of natural resources like fisheries.
- **Presidential Powers**: The extent of the Icelandic president's political power is not legally clear; the constitution grants some powers, but tradition and other provisions suggest a more ceremonial role.
- **Environmental Impact**: Despite its renewable energy, Iceland has high per capita CO2 emissions from transport and aluminium smelting. The collapse of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) is considered a national security risk.
