# Mount Kosciuszko

## Overview
Mount Kosciuszko is the highest mountain on mainland Australia, standing at 2,228 metres (7,310 ft) in the Snowy Mountains of New South Wales. It is a significant natural landmark and a popular destination for recreation, with a history of exploration and naming that involves both European settlers and Indigenous Australians.

## Timeline
- **1840** — Polish explorer Paweł Edmund Strzelecki, with guides, reaches the summit and names it Mount Kosciuszko after a Polish general.
- **1846** — Surveyor Townsend traverses the adjacent peak, which would later be named after him.
- **1885** — Explorer Robert von Lendenfeld, using an erroneous map, reaches Mount Townsend believing it to be Kosciuszko and names the higher, neighbouring peak Mount Townsend.
- **1940** — Cartographer B. T. Dowd's study confirms Strzelecki's original naming was correct.
- **1977** — The road from Charlotte Pass to near the summit is closed to public motor vehicles for environmental reasons.
- **1997** — The Geographical Names Board of New South Wales officially adopts the spelling "Mount Kosciuszko."
- **2019** — A proposal for the dual name "Kunama Namadgi" is submitted to the Geographical Names Board.

## Body

### Etymology and Naming Confusion
The mountain was named by Polish explorer Paweł Edmund Strzelecki in 1840 after Tadeusz Kościuszko, a Polish-Lithuanian military leader, due to a perceived resemblance to a mound in Kraków. A cartographical error on Victorian maps later transposed the names of Mount Kosciuszko and the adjacent, slightly lower Mount Townsend. This error caused confusion, notably when explorer Robert von Lendenfeld in 1885 reached Mount Townsend believing it was the highest peak. The confusion was officially resolved in 1940, confirming Strzelecki's original naming. The spelling was standardized to "Kosciuszko" in 1997.

### Aboriginal Names and Dual Naming
The Ngarigo people have names associated with the mountains. Names like *Jagungal* and *Tar-gan-gil* are recorded for Mount Townsend, meaning "bogong moth." In 2019, a proposal was submitted for the dual name *Kunama Namadgi* for Mount Kosciuszko, meaning "snow" and "mountain." This proposal is disputed within the Indigenous community, with some elders stating the name is not from their language and is offensive in some dialects.

### Geography and Access
The mountain was formed by geologic uplift and features eroded granite boulders at its summit. It is accessible via several walking tracks. The most popular routes are from Charlotte Pass (7.6 km walk) and Thredbo (via chairlift, 5 km walk), both leading to Rawson Pass before a final 1.4 km ascent to the summit. The Hannel's Spur Track is a more challenging 15.5 km route with a 1,800 m vertical ascent, following the path of Strzelecki's original climb. A public toilet at Rawson Pass caters to over 100,000 summer visitors.

### Recreation and Context
The mountain is the easiest of the "Seven Summits" (the highest peaks on each continent) to climb. It is the centerpiece of Kosciuszko National Park, which contains major ski resorts like Thredbo and Perisher. The annual Coast to Kosciuszko ultramarathon finishes at the summit. While it is the highest on mainland Australia, higher peaks exist in Australian external territories, such as Mawson Peak on Heard Island and Dome Argus in the Antarctic Territory.

## Terms
- **Topographic isolation**: The distance from a summit to the nearest point of equal or higher elevation.
- **Aestivate**: To pass the summer in a state of dormancy, like the bogong moths on the mountain.
- **Geologic uplift**: The process by which the Earth's crust is raised, forming mountains.
- **Granite intrusions**: Bodies of igneous rock that formed from magma cooling beneath the Earth's surface.
- **Seven Summits**: The highest mountains on each of the seven continents.
- **Dual naming**: The practice of officially recognizing both a traditional Indigenous name and a colonial name for a place.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary debate concerns the proposed dual name *Kunama Namadgi*. Proponents, like the Toomaroombah Kunama Namadgi Indigenous Corporation, state it is the traditional Ngarigo name. Opponents, including some Indigenous elders, dispute its authenticity and meaning, arguing it is not from their language and is offensive. This reflects broader discussions about the process and criteria for officially recognizing Indigenous place names.
