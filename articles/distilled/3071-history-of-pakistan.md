# History of Pakistan

## Overview
The region that became Pakistan is a crossroads of South Asia, Central Asia, and the Iranian plateau. Its history is a tapestry of ancient civilizations, successive empires, and cultural synthesis, culminating in the creation of a modern nation-state in 1947. The story is defined by the rise and fall of major powers, the fusion of diverse cultures, and the political struggle for a separate Muslim homeland.

## Timeline
- **c. 7000–5500 BCE** — Neolithic site of Mehrgarh shows early farming and herding.
- **c. 3300–1300 BCE** — Indus Valley Civilization flourishes, with major urban centers like Mohenjo-daro and Harappa.
- **c. 1500–500 BCE** — Vedic Period begins with Indo-Aryan migrations and the composition of the Vedas.
- **518 BCE** — Achaemenid Empire under Darius I conquers the Indus Valley.
- **326 BCE** — Alexander the Great campaigns through the region, defeating local tribes.
- **322–185 BCE** — Mauryan Empire, under Ashoka, controls much of the subcontinent, promoting Buddhism.
- **c. 1st–3rd centuries CE** — Kushan Empire, a major patron of Buddhism and the Silk Road, dominates the region.
- **712 CE** — Arab general Muhammad ibn al-Qasim conquers Sindh, establishing Islamic rule.
- **1001–1027 CE** — Mahmud of Ghazni launches repeated invasions from Afghanistan into Punjab and Sindh.
- **1526–1857** — Mughal Empire rules most of South Asia, leaving a significant architectural and cultural legacy.
- **1843–1849** — British East India Company conquers Sindh and Punjab.
- **1906** — All-India Muslim League is founded to protect Muslim political interests.
- **1940** — Lahore Resolution demands independent Muslim states in northwestern and eastern India.
- **1947** — British India is partitioned; Pakistan gains independence on August 14.

## Body

### Ancient and Classical Civilizations
Early habitation is evidenced by the Soanian culture (c. 774,000–11,700 BCE). The Neolithic site of Mehrgarh (7000–2600 BCE) marks the dawn of village life, transitioning into the urban Indus Valley Civilization (c. 3300–1300 BCE). This Bronze Age civilization featured advanced city planning and trade. Following its decline, the Vedic Period (c. 1500–500 BCE) saw the arrival of Indo-Aryans and the composition of the Vedas. The region was then incorporated into successive empires: the Achaemenid Persian Empire (from 518 BCE), Alexander the Great's Macedonian Empire (326 BCE), and the Mauryan Empire (322–185 BCE), which promoted Buddhism under Emperor Ashoka.

### Empires and Cultural Synthesis
The classical era saw kingdoms blending Hellenistic, Central Asian, and Indian influences. The Indo-Greek Kingdom (c. 155–10 BCE) fostered Gandharan art, depicting the Buddha in a Greco-Roman style. This was followed by the Indo-Scythian, Indo-Parthian, and the expansive Kushan Empire (1st–3rd centuries CE), which linked Indian Ocean trade with the Silk Road and patronized Buddhism. The Alchon Huns (5th–6th centuries CE) disrupted the region before its incorporation into the Gupta Empire. The Arab conquest of Sindh in 712 CE introduced Islam, which gradually spread, especially in the south, while Hindu and Buddhist populations remained numerous in the north.

### Medieval Sultanates and the Mughal Empire
From the 11th century, Turkic and Afghan dynasties invaded and ruled parts of the region. The Ghaznavid dynasty (997–1187 CE) and the Ghurid dynasty (12th century) expanded Islamic rule eastward, laying the foundation for the Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526). This series of Turkic dynasties ruled from Delhi, fostering Indo-Islamic culture and the Urdu language. The Mughal Empire (1526–1857), founded by Babur, unified much of the subcontinent. Under emperors like Akbar, it was known for administrative efficiency and religious tolerance. Its decline in the 18th century led to the rise of regional powers, including the Sikh Empire in Punjab (1799–1849).

### Colonial Period and the Pakistan Movement
The British East India Company conquered Sindh (1843) and Punjab (1849), followed by direct British Crown rule after 1857. The All-India Muslim League was founded in 1906 to safeguard Muslim interests. The idea of a separate Muslim state was articulated by Muhammad Iqbal in 1930, and the name "Pakistan" was coined in 1933. The Muslim League, under Muhammad Ali Jinnah, gained overwhelming Muslim support, culminating in the 1940 Lahore Resolution demanding independent states. Despite British attempts to maintain a united India, escalating communal violence and the failure of the Cabinet Mission Plan led to partition. Pakistan was created on August 14, 1947, amidst massive population displacement and violence.

## Terms
- **Indus Valley Civilization:** A Bronze Age urban civilization (c. 3300–1300 BCE) in the Indus River basin, known for advanced city planning and drainage systems.
- **Vedic Period:** The era (c. 1500–500 BCE) following Indo-Aryan migrations, during which the Vedas were composed, forming the foundation of Hinduism.
- **Gandharan Art:** A style from the Gandhara region (modern Pakistan/Afghanistan) blending Hellenistic and Buddhist influences, notably depicting the Buddha in human form.
- **Delhi Sultanate:** A series of Muslim dynasties (1206–1526) that ruled from Delhi, marking the establishment of Islamic political power in much of North India.
- **Mughal Empire:** A Muslim empire (1526–1857) that ruled most of the Indian subcontinent, known for its administrative system and architectural achievements like the Taj Mahal.
- **Two-Nation Theory:** The ideology, promoted by the Muslim League, that Hindus and Muslims are two distinct nations with separate cultures and destinies, justifying the demand for Pakistan.
- **Lahore Resolution:** The 1940 Muslim League resolution that formally demanded independent states for Muslims in the northwestern and eastern zones of British India.
- **Partition of India:** The 1947 division of British India into the independent dominions of India and Pakistan, accompanied by massive communal violence and population transfer.

## Debates and Open questions
- The reasons for the decline and collapse of the Indus Valley Civilization around 1700 BCE remain unknown, with theories including climate change and aridification.
- The extent and nature of the Indo-Aryan migration into the subcontinent during the Vedic Period is a subject of ongoing scholarly debate.
- The motivations behind the British decision to advance the date of independence and partition from June 1948 to August 1947 are debated, with factors including the deteriorating law and order situation and Lord Mountbatten's personal timeline.
- The scale of casualties during the 1947 Partition violence is disputed, with estimates ranging from 200,000 to 2,000,000 deaths.
