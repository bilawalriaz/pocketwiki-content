# William Harvey

## Overview
William Harvey (1578–1657) was an English physician whose meticulous experiments and quantitative reasoning revolutionized medicine by proving the circulation of blood. His work, *De Motu Cordis* (1628), overturned centuries of Galenic dogma, establishing the heart as a pump and demonstrating a closed circulatory system, a foundational concept in modern physiology.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Harvey's education began in Folkestone and Canterbury before he entered Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge, in 1593. He graduated B.A. in 1597 and then traveled to Italy, where he studied at the University of Padua. There, he was influenced by his teacher Hieronymus Fabricius and his work on venous valves. Harvey graduated M.D. from Padua in 1602 at age 24, receiving high praise for his skill and learning.

### Medical Career and Royal Service
After returning to England, Harvey obtained an M.D. from Cambridge and established himself in London. He became a Fellow of the Royal College of Physicians in 1607 and married Elizabeth Browne. In 1609, he became the physician at St Bartholomew's Hospital, a post he held for most of his life. His career advanced with his appointment as Lumleian lecturer in 1615, where he taught anatomy using detailed notes and dissections. He later served as physician to both King James I and King Charles I, using royal hunting expeditions to study deer anatomy. During the English Civil War, he supported the king, lost his papers to a mob, and was made Warden of Merton College, Oxford.

### *De Motu Cordis* and the Discovery of Circulation
Harvey's seminal work, *Exercitatio Anatomica de Motu Cordis et Sanguinis in Animalibus*, was published in Frankfurt in 1628. Using experiments on live and dead animals, and a famous ligature demonstration on a human arm, he systematically dismantled Galenic theory. He argued that the heart's contractions (systole) actively pump blood, not just generate heat. A key proof was quantitative: he calculated that the heart pumps far more blood in a half-hour than the body contains, making the Galenic idea of continuous blood production by the liver impossible. He concluded blood circulates in a closed loop, propelled by the heart, with veins returning it to the heart via one-way valves. His theory faced significant resistance, taking about twenty years to gain acceptance.

### Later Work and Legacy
Harvey's other major work, *On Animal Generation* (1651), advanced embryology with the doctrine *omne vivum ex ovo* ("all life comes from the egg"), challenging spontaneous generation. He retired after the Civil War, living with his brothers. He died in 1657, likely from a cerebral hemorrhage, and left funds to the Royal College of Physicians. His legacy is commemorated in numerous institutions, including the annual Harveian Oration.

## Terms
- **Pulmonary circulation**: The circuit where blood is pumped from the heart to the lungs for oxygenation and back to the heart.
- **Systemic circulation**: The circuit where oxygenated blood is pumped from the heart to the rest of the body and deoxygenated blood returns to the heart.
- **Galenic theory**: The ancient medical model, based on Galen's writings, which held that blood was continuously produced by the liver and consumed by the body, with separate venous and arterial systems.
- **Lumleian lecturer**: A prestigious anatomical teaching position at the Royal College of Physicians, established in 1582.
- **De Motu Cordis**: Harvey's 1628 publication, *On the Motion of the Heart and Blood*, which detailed his theory of blood circulation.
- **Systole**: The phase of the heartbeat when the heart muscle contracts and pumps blood from the chambers into the arteries.
- **Diastole**: The phase of the heartbeat when the heart muscle relaxes and allows the chambers to fill with blood.
- **Capillaries**: The tiny blood vessels that connect arteries to veins, allowing exchange between blood and tissues (Harvey inferred their existence but did not directly observe them).
- **Omne vivum ex ovo**: Latin for "all life comes from the egg," a principle Harvey championed in his work on generation.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Priority of discovery**: While Harvey provided the first complete and detailed description of circulation, precursors like Michael Servetus, Realdo Colombo, and Jacques Dubois had described parts of the pulmonary circuit. The article notes Ibn al-Nafis also provided an earlier model.
- **Resistance to acceptance**: Harvey's theory directly contradicted the deeply entrenched Galenic paradigm, leading to significant professional backlash. Some physicians "would rather err with Galen than proclaim the truth with Harvey."
- **Observation vs. inference**: Harvey's discovery relied heavily on inference and quantitative reasoning, as he lacked the microscopes needed to directly observe capillaries. He wrote that blood "permeates the pores" in the flesh.
