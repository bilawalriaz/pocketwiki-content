# Nervous system

## Overview  
The nervous system is the animal organ system responsible for detecting environmental changes and coordinating responses by transmitting electrochemical signals between specialized cells. It evolved from simple cell-to-cell signaling in sponges to complex centralized brains and peripheral networks in bilaterians, enabling advanced perception, behavior, and cognition. Neuroscience studies its structure, function, development, and disorders, with applications in medicine and biology.

## Timeline  
- **~600 MYA** — Nervous tissue first appears in early animals
- **Ediacaran (~550 MYA)** — Bilaterian body plan with centralized nerve cord emerges
- **1906** — Sherrington publishes *The Integrative Action of the Nervous System*
- **1943** — McCulloch & Pitts propose artificial neuron model
- **1973** — Long-term potentiation (LTP) discovered
- **1990s** — Genetic mechanisms of neural induction resolved

## Body  

### Structure and Organization  
The nervous system consists of nervous tissue, defined by neurons—cells capable of rapid electrical signaling via synapses—and glial cells, which provide metabolic support, insulation (myelin), and immune defense. In vertebrates, the central nervous system (CNS) includes the brain and spinal cord, protected by meninges and the skull/vertebrae; the peripheral nervous system (PNS) comprises nerves connecting the CNS to the body. The PNS splits into the somatic system (voluntary control of skin/muscles) and the autonomic system (involuntary control of organs/glands), further divided into sympathetic (emergency response), parasympathetic (rest/digest), and enteric (gut) subsystems.

### Evolution and Comparative Anatomy  
Nervous systems vary widely across animals. Sponges lack neurons but possess calcium-wave signaling and postsynaptic-like structures. Radially symmetric animals like jellyfish have diffuse nerve nets. Bilaterians—most animals—share a segmented nerve cord with ganglia and a frontal brain, originating in the Ediacaran. Protostomes (e.g., insects) have ventral nerve cords; deuterostomes (e.g., vertebrates) have dorsal cords, reflecting an evolutionary body-axis inversion. Worms like *C. elegans* have fully mapped connectomes, while arthropods and molluscs exhibit complex ganglia and identified neurons such as the squid giant axon.

### Function  
Neurons communicate via electrical synapses (direct connections) or chemical synapses (neurotransmitter release into synaptic clefts). Signals propagate as action potentials along axons, triggering neurotransmitter release that binds to postsynaptic receptors, causing excitation, inhibition, or modulation. Synaptic plasticity—including long-term potentiation (LTP) via NMDA receptors—underlies learning and memory. Neural circuits process sensory input, integrate information, and generate motor outputs. Reflex arcs provide rapid responses, while higher-order processing in the brain enables perception, decision-making, and behavior.

### Development  
Vertebrate nervous systems develop from the ectoderm: the neural plate folds into the neural tube (CNS) and neural crest (PNS). Neural induction requires inhibition of BMP signaling by molecules like Noggin and Chordin. Neuroblasts differentiate into neurons and glia, guided by neurotrophins. Early embryonic patterning establishes the basic body plan, with segmental ganglia and brain vesicles forming along the neural tube.

### Pathology  
CNS damage is often severe due to limited regeneration and scar tissue formation. PNS nerves can regenerate slowly. Disorders include diabetic neuropathy, multiple sclerosis (demyelination), ALS, and trauma-induced injuries. The blood-brain barrier protects the CNS but complicates drug delivery. Idiopathic neuropathies lack identifiable causes. Neurological practice integrates neuroscience and psychiatry for diagnosis and treatment.

## Terms  
- **Neuron**: Specialized cell transmitting electrochemical signals via synapses.  
- **Glial cell**: Supportive cell providing structure, nutrients, myelination, and immune defense.  
- **Synapse**: Junction where neurons communicate via neurotransmitters or direct electrical coupling.  
- **Action potential**: Rapid electrical impulse propagating along axons.  
- **Neurotransmitter**: Chemical messenger released at synapses to affect target cells.  
- **Myelin**: Fatty sheath insulating axons, speeding signal conduction.  
- **Neurulation**: Embryonic process forming the neural tube and neural crest.  
- **Long-term potentiation (LTP)**: Persistent strengthening of synapses underlying memory.  
- **Central pattern generator**: Neural circuit producing rhythmic outputs without sensory input.  
- **Command neuron**: Identified neuron capable of triggering specific behaviors.  

## Debates and Open Questions  
- **Mirror neurons**: Their role in cognition, imitation, and language remains speculative, with insufficient evidence for broad claims.  
- **Command neuron concept**: Controversial due to context-dependent activation rather than universal behavioral control.  
- **Neural induction mechanisms**: While BMP inhibition is key, the exact roles of FGF and other signals vary across species.  
- **CNS regeneration**: Scar tissue limits recovery; mechanisms for promoting repair are under investigation.  
- **Spatiotemporal control of neurogenesis**: How neuroblasts migrate and integrate into circuits is still being unraveled.
