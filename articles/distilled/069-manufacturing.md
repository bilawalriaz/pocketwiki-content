# Manufacturing

## Overview
Manufacturing is the creation or production of goods using equipment, labor, machines, tools, and chemical or biological processing. It is the essence of the secondary sector of the economy, transforming raw materials from the primary sector into finished goods on a large scale. These goods may be sold to other manufacturers for more complex products or distributed via the tertiary sector to end users through wholesalers and retailers. Manufacturing engineering designs and optimizes the manufacturing process, beginning with product design and materials specification.

## Etymology
The Modern English word *manufacture* is likely derived from the Middle French *manufacture* ("process of making"), which itself originates from the Classical Latin *manū* ("hand") and the Middle French *facture* ("making"). Alternatively, the English word may have been independently formed from the earlier English *manufacture* ("made by human hands") and *fracture*. Its earliest usage in English was recorded in the mid-16th century to refer to the making of products by hand.

## Body

### Prehistory and ancient history
Human ancestors manufactured stone tools long before Homo sapiens emerged about 200,000 years ago. The Oldowan "industry" dates to at least 2.3 million years ago, with the earliest direct evidence of tool usage found in Ethiopia within the Great Rift Valley, dating back to 2.5 million years ago. Stone tools were made by striking a hard stone "core" with a hammerstone, producing sharp edges for choppers and scrapers. The Middle Paleolithic (c. 300,000 years ago) introduced prepared-core techniques; the Upper Paleolithic (c. 40,000 years ago) developed pressure flaking. Neolithic peoples manufactured polished tools from flint, jade, jadeite, and greenstone.

Copper smelting likely originated when pottery kilns reached sufficiently high temperatures. Smelting arsenic-rich ores yielded arsenical bronze, work-hardenable for tools. True tin bronze was delayed because tin deposits are globally rare. Bronze improved on stone due to strength, ductility, and castability in molds, advancing shipbuilding with bronze nails replacing cord-lashed hull boards. The Iron Age is conventionally defined by the widespread manufacturing of weapons and tools using iron and steel rather than bronze; iron smelting required hot-working and specially designed furnaces. The discovery's place and time remain unknown, partly because nickel-containing ores are hard to distinguish from meteoritic iron.

Ancient civilizations produced many technologies from manufacturing advances. Mesopotamians invented the wheel and axle mechanism, first appearing with the potter's wheel. Egyptian papyrus and pottery were mass-produced and exported across the Mediterranean. Early Egyptian construction used bricks of clay, sand, silt, and other minerals.

### Medieval and early modern
The Middle Ages brought new inventions and economic growth. Papermaking, a 2nd-century Chinese technology, reached the Middle East when Chinese papermakers were captured in the 8th century, then spread to Europe via the Umayyad conquest of Hispania. A paper mill was established in Sicily in the 12th century. Lynn Townsend White Jr. credited the spinning wheel with increasing rag supply, leading to cheap paper and aiding printing's development. The blast furnace came into widespread use in France in the mid-15th century due to cannon casting, though China had used it since the 4th century BC. The stocking frame (1598) increased knitter output from 100 to 1000 knots per minute.

### First and Second Industrial Revolutions
The Industrial Revolution (1760–1830s) transitioned Europe and the United States from hand production to machines, new chemical manufacturing, iron production, steam and water power, machine tools, and mechanized factories. It caused unprecedented population growth. Textiles dominated in employment, output value, and capital. Mechanized spinning began in Britain in the 1780s, with steam power and iron production growing rapidly after 1800. Mechanized textiles spread to continental Europe and the US in the early 19th century.

A recession from the late 1830s to early 1840s occurred as early innovations matured. New technologies like locomotives, steamships, hot blast iron smelting, and the electrical telegraph were introduced in the 1840s–1850s but were insufficient for high growth. Rapid growth resumed after 1870 with the Second Industrial Revolution: new steel processes, mass production, assembly lines, electrical grids, and advanced machine tools. Incandescent light bulbs became practical in the late 1870s, enabling second and third shift work. Shoe production mechanized mid-19th century; sewing machines and reapers were mass-produced mid-to-late 19th century; bicycles from the 1880s.

### Modern manufacturing
Factory electrification, beginning in the 1890s with practical DC and AC motors, was fastest between 1900 and 1930, aided by central station utilities and falling electricity prices (1914–1917). Electric motors offered flexibility and less maintenance than line shafts, with many factories seeing 30% output increases. The Ball Brothers Glass Manufacturing Company electrified its mason jar plant around 1900: glass blowing machines replaced 210 craftsmen, electric trucks handled 150 dozen bottles versus 6 dozen by hand, and an electric overhead crane replaced 36 laborers.

Mass production was popularized in the late 1910s–1920s by Henry Ford's Ford Motor Company, combining electric motors with chain or sequential production. Ford used special-purpose machine tools like multiple spindle drill presses and a multiple head milling machine that machined 15 engine blocks simultaneously. Model T production used 32,000 machine tools.

Lean manufacturing (just-in-time) was developed in Japan in the 1930s, reducing production times and supplier/customer response times. It was introduced in Australia in the 1950s by British Motor Corporation at its Victoria Park plant in Sydney, later migrating to Toyota. Western awareness came in 1977 via two English-language articles, one calling it the "Ohno system" after Taiichi Ohno. Implementations began in 1980 and multiplied across US industry. World-class manufacturing is a term promoted by Richard J. Schonberger, though initially used by R. H. Hayes and Steven C. Wheelwright.

### Manufacturing strategy
Traditional manufacturing strategy assesses performance along five dimensions: cost, quality, dependability, flexibility, and innovation. Wickham Skinner, "the father of manufacturing strategy," adopted "focus," arguing a business cannot excel on all five dimensions and must select one or two competitive priorities—leading to the "trade-offs" theory. Elizabeth Haas wrote in 1987 about delivering value via "lower prices, greater service responsiveness or higher quality." The trade-off theory has been debated: Colin New noted by 1991 it was "somewhat fashionable" to abandon it, while Skinner wrote in 1992 that enthusiasm for manufacturing strategy concepts had been higher, with interest "bursting out all over." Terry Hill noted manufacturing is often seen as less strategic than marketing or finance. Ken Platts and Mike Gregory devised a manufacturing audit scheme, popularized in *Competitive Manufacturing* (1998), supported by the UK's Department of Trade and Industry. Two main control strategies exist: push manufacturing (forecast-driven, emphasizing batch processing) and pull manufacturing (replenishment-driven, connected to the next stage's work demands).

### Industrial policy
Manufacturing supports national infrastructure and defense, but most processes involve social and environmental costs. Hazardous waste cleanup may outweigh product benefits, and hazardous materials risk worker health. Efforts address these via efficiency, waste reduction, industrial symbiosis, and eliminating harmful chemicals. Developed countries regulate with labor and environmental laws, pollution taxes, and tort/product liability. These protections may be absent in the third world, driving relocation of manufacturing to developing economies with lower production costs.

Financially, manufacturing aims for cost benefits per unit, reducing product prices and securing profit margins. In the US, NIOSH identifies manufacturing as a priority industry under the National Occupational Research Agenda. Local economic plans, like Leeds City Council's, address manufacturing location and labor needs. Heavy equipment moves during upgrades or relocations require specialized logistics—rigging, tilt-and-load transport, forklifts, oversize-load permitting—with standards from the Specialized Carriers & Rigging Association.

Surveys examine cross-national variations in manufacturing growth, competitiveness, and attractiveness to foreign investors. On June 26, 2009, GE CEO Jeff Immelt called for the US to increase manufacturing employment to 20% of the workforce, noting excessive outsourcing. US manufacturing performs poorly compared to other high-wage countries; 3.2 million—one in six—US manufacturing jobs disappeared between 2000 and 2007. In the UK, EEF has called for rebalancing the economy away from financial services.

### Major manufacturing nations
According to UNIDO, China was the top manufacturer in 2023, producing 28.7% of global output, followed by the US, Germany, Japan, and India. UNIDO's Competitive Industrial Performance (CIP) Index combines gross output with high-tech capability and world economic impact; Germany topped the 2020 index, followed by China, South Korea, the US, and Japan. In 2023, US manufacturing was 10.70% of national output, employing 8.41% of the workforce, with $2.5 trillion in output. Germany's 2023 output reached $844.93 billion (up 12.25% from 2022), employing about 5.5 million people (20.8% of the workforce). The World Bank also publishes a list of the top 50 countries by total value of manufacturing output in U.S. dollars.

## Terms
- **Secondary sector**: The part of the economy that transforms raw materials into finished goods.
- **Primary sector**: The part of the economy that extracts raw materials.
- **Tertiary sector**: The service sector, including distribution to end users.
- **Oldowan industry**: The earliest known stone tool-making method, dating to at least 2.3 million years ago.
- **Prepared-core technique**: A Middle Paleolithic method for rapidly forming multiple blades from a single stone core.
- **Pressure flaking**: A Upper Paleolithic technique using a wood, bone, or antler punch to finely shape stone.
- **Lean manufacturing (just-in-time)**: A production method reducing times within production and from suppliers/customers, developed in Japan in the 1930s.
- **Push manufacturing**: Manufacturing according to forecast, emphasizing batch processing and lot sizes.
- **Pull manufacturing**: Manufacturing focused on replenishment, connected to the next stage's work demands.
- **Competitive Industrial Performance (CIP) Index**: UNIDO's measure combining gross manufacturing output with high-tech capability and world economic impact.

## Debates and open questions
- The place and time of iron smelting's discovery are unknown, partly because nickel-containing ores are difficult to distinguish from hot-worked meteoritic iron.
- The "trade-offs" theory in manufacturing strategy—that a business cannot excel on all five performance dimensions—has been debated and questioned; by 1991 it was "somewhat fashionable" to abandon it, though Skinner noted renewed enthusiasm in 1992.
- Whether manufacturing is seen as less "strategic" than marketing and finance, with manufacturing managers making only reactive contributions to strategy discussions.
