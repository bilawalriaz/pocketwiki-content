# Eastern Front (World War I)

## Overview
The Eastern Front of World War I was the vast theater between the Central Powers (Germany, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire, Bulgaria) and the Entente (primarily Russia, later Romania). Unlike the static Western Front, it featured dynamic movement, flanking maneuvers, and the occupation of over 100,000 square miles. Its outcome, culminating in Russia's exit from the war, was a decisive Central Powers victory that reshaped Eastern Europe but could not prevent their defeat in the West.

## Timeline
- **28 Jul 1914** — Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia, triggering the conflict.
- **17 Aug 1914** — Russia invades East Prussia and Galicia, beginning major hostilities.
- **Aug 1914** — German victory at Battle of Tannenberg halts the Russian invasion of East Prussia.
- **Sep 1914** — Russian victory in Battle of Galicia secures most of the region.
- **May 1915** — Central Powers launch the successful Gorlice–Tarnów Offensive, forcing a major Russian retreat.
- **18 Apr 1916** — Russian forces capture Trabzon on the Ottoman front.
- **Jun 1916** — Brusilov Offensive inflicts massive losses on Austria-Hungary, the largest Entente victory of the war.
- **Aug 1916** — Romania enters the war on the Entente side.
- **Dec 1916** — Central Powers capture Bucharest; Romania loses two-thirds of its territory.
- **Mar 1917** — February Revolution in Russia leads to Tsar Nicholas II's abdication.
- **Jul 1917** — Kerensky Offensive, Russia's last major attack, ends in failure.
- **Nov 1917** — Bolsheviks seize power in the October Revolution.
- **Mar 1918** — Treaty of Brest-Litovsk takes Russia out of the war.
- **Nov 1918** — Armistice ends the war; Treaty of Brest-Litovsk is annulled.

## Body
### Background and War Plans
The war began with Austria-Hungary's ultimatum to Serbia, backed by Germany. Russia, bound by treaty to Serbia, ordered full mobilization, which Germany interpreted as an act of war. Pre-war planning shaped the conflict: Germany's Schlieffen Plan aimed to quickly defeat France before turning east, while Russia's Schedule 19A committed it to an immediate offensive against both Germany and Austria-Hungary to support France. Austria-Hungary, facing internal nationalist pressures, sought to crush Serbia but misaligned its strategy with Germany's.

### 1914–1915: Initial Offensives and the Great Retreat
Russia's 1914 offensives achieved mixed results: a disastrous defeat in East Prussia (Tannenberg) was offset by a major victory in Galicia against Austria-Hungary. In 1915, Germany shifted focus east, launching the Gorlice–Tarnów Offensive. This triggered the "Great Retreat," where Russia abandoned Poland, Lithuania, and parts of Belarus and Ukraine, losing over 300,000 square kilometers. The retreat was caused by critical shortages of artillery and ammunition, systemic command failures, and corruption, though Russia's vast territory prevented a total collapse. By late 1915, a stable front line formed from Riga to Tarnopol.

### 1916: Brusilov Offensive and Romanian Entry
To relieve pressure on France at Verdun, Russia launched the Brusilov Offensive in June 1916. Using innovative tactics, it became the war's largest Entente victory, inflicting over a million casualties on Austria-Hungary and forcing Germany to divert divisions from the West. The offensive's success persuaded Romania to join the Entente in August. However, the Central Powers quickly counterattacked, overrunning Romania by December and capturing its vital Ploiești oilfields.

### 1917: Revolution and Collapse
The February Revolution, sparked by food shortages and war weariness, toppled the Tsar. The Provisional Government continued the war, but the failed Kerensky Offensive (July 1917) and growing troop discipline issues weakened the army. The Bolsheviks' October Revolution promised peace. Though the army largely remained at the front until early 1918, the Bolsheviks began dissolving it while negotiating with the Central Powers.

### 1918: Treaty and Aftermath
After a German offensive (Operation Faustschlag) forced their hand, the new Soviet government signed the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk in March 1918, ceding vast territories (including Ukraine, Poland, and the Baltic states) and exiting the war. This allowed Germany to transfer forces west, but its final offensive failed. The treaty was annulled by the November Armistice. Romania, which had signed a separate peace in May 1918, re-entered the war on the Allied side in November.

## Terms
*   **Schlieffen Plan:** Germany's pre-war strategy to avoid a two-front war by quickly defeating France before turning its full force against the slower-mobilizing Russian army.
*   **Schedule 19A:** Russia's modified war plan, adopted in 1912, committing to an immediate offensive against both Germany and Austria-Hungary to support France.
*   **Gorlice–Tarnów Offensive:** A major 1915 Central Powers offensive that broke through Russian lines in Galicia, leading to the "Great Retreat" and the loss of vast Russian-held territory.
*   **Brusilov Offensive:** A 1916 Russian offensive against Austria-Hungary, noted for its innovative tactics and scale, which inflicted catastrophic losses and became the Entente's greatest victory on the Eastern Front.
*   **February Revolution:** The 1917 revolution in Russia that overthrew Tsar Nicholas II and established a Provisional Government, which continued the war.
*   **October Revolution:** The 1917 Bolshevik coup that overthrew the Provisional Government, with a platform to end the war immediately.
*   **Treaty of Brest-Litovsk:** The March 1918 peace treaty between the Central Powers and Soviet Russia, which took Russia out of the war at the cost of huge territorial concessions.
*   **Kerensky Offensive:** The last major Russian offensive of the war, launched in July 1917, which ended in a disastrous defeat and contributed to the army's collapse.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Russian Army Strength in 1917:** Historians debate the condition of the Russian army after 1916. Some, like General Brusilov and historian Sergei Oldenburg, argue it was well-supplied, numerically superior, and capable of fighting defensively, suggesting its collapse was primarily due to political revolution rather than military defeat.
*   **Impact of the Eastern Front on the West:** The failure of Germany's 1918 spring offensive in the West is directly linked to the fact that significant German cavalry and resources remained tied down in the east even after Russia's exit from the war.
*   **Casualty Figures:** Precise casualty numbers are debated due to poor statistics. Estimates for total Russian losses range from 7.9 to 9 million, while Central Powers losses on the Eastern Front are estimated between 5.1 and 6 million, with some sources suggesting they were higher than on the Western Front.
