# Mountain

## Overview
A mountain is an elevated landform with steep sides and significant exposed bedrock, generally higher than a hill though no universal definition exists. Mountains form through tectonic forces—volcanism, crustal folding, and fault-block uplift—acting over millions of years, and are subsequently shaped by erosion. High elevations create colder, distinct climates that drive altitudinal zonation of ecosystems and limit human habitation; roughly 24% of Earth's land mass is mountainous. Mountains serve as critical water towers for over half of humanity, host specialized economies (mining, tourism), and hold cultural significance, while facing acute threats from climate change such as accelerating glacial loss and slope instability.

## Timeline
- **1947** — Leslie Holdridge develops the biotemperature concept to classify mountain ecology.
- **1970s** — United States Board on Geographic Names abandons its 1,000 ft (305 m) technical definition of a mountain.
- **1983** — Nanda Devi (Himalayas) closed to climbers due to sacred status.
- **20th–21st century** — Accelerating ice loss observed in mountain glaciers and ice caps globally.
- **Present** — Mountains cover 24% of Earth's land mass; 140 million people live above 2,500 m.

## Body

### Definition and Classification
No single definition of a mountain is universally accepted; criteria include elevation, volume, relief, steepness, and continuity. The Oxford English Dictionary emphasizes an "impressive or notable" altitude relative to surroundings. Local usage varies: some authorities use 600 m (1,969 ft) as the hill-mountain threshold, while the UK and Ireland officially define a mountain as a summit ≥ 2,000 ft (610 m), sometimes adding a 300 m (984 ft) prominence requirement. The US Geological Survey states the terms lack technical definitions in the US. The UN Environment Programme classifies "mountainous environment" into seven classes based on elevation, slope, and local relief range (e.g., Class 1: > 4,500 m; Class 4: 1,500–2,500 m with > 2° slope). Under this system, mountains cover 33% of Eurasia, 24% of North America, 19% of South America, and 14% of Africa.

### Geology and Formation
Three main mountain types arise from plate tectonics. **Volcanic mountains** form at subduction zones, mid-ocean ridges, or hotspots where magma reaches the surface (e.g., Mount Fuji, stratovolcanoes) or solidifies below ground as dome mountains (e.g., Navajo Mountain). **Fold mountains** occur when continental plates collide, shortening the crust along thrust faults and overthickening it; because continental crust floats on the denser mantle (isostasy), crustal roots extend deep downward to balance surface uplift (e.g., Balkan Mountains, Jura Mountains). **Block mountains** (horsts) rise along faults where crustal blocks tilt or lift relative to dropped blocks (graben), typical of extensional settings like East Africa, the Vosges/Rhine valley, and the Basin and Range Province. **Erosion** by water, wind, ice, and gravity sculpts uplifted areas, making mountain surfaces younger than their constituent rocks; glacial erosion creates pyramidal peaks, arêtes, and cirques, while plateau mountains (e.g., the Catskills) form from dissected uplifted plateaus.

### Climate and Climate Change
Mountain climate cools with elevation due to adiabatic expansion of rising air. The dry adiabatic lapse rate is ~9.8 °C/km; the moist rate (with condensation) is ~5.5 °C/km. Roughly, ascending 100 m equals moving 80 km poleward. Precipitation shifts to snow at height, and winds increase. Holdridge’s biotemperature (mean temperature with sub-zero values set to 0 °C) captures ecological limits; permanent snow peaks have biotemperatures < 1.5 °C. Mountain environments are highly sensitive to anthropogenic climate change, undergoing unprecedented shifts in 10,000 years. Highlands warm faster than adjacent lowlands (though globally the signal is mixed), precipitation increases lag lowlands, and models disagree on regional trends. Accelerating glacier/permafrost melt destabilizes slopes, increasing landslides. Altered river discharge threatens water security for nearly half of mountain areas that supply essential water to urban and semiarid regions (e.g., central Asia). Alpine ecosystems, often confined to small niches as cold refugia, face direct climate stress and indirect soil instability.

### Ecology: Altitudinal Zonation and Sky Islands
Colder, often wetter conditions at elevation create distinct **altitudinal zonation**—bands of relatively constant climate hosting adapted biota. In dry regions, orographic precipitation enhances zonation. Zones typically progress from alpine tundra above treeline, to subalpine needleleaf forests, to montane forests (needleleaf in temperate zones, broadleaf rainforest in tropics). Because conditions above and below a zone are inhospitable, populations can become isolated on **"sky islands"**, creating unique, fragmented ecological systems.

### Mountains and Humans
**Physiological limits:** The highest permanently tolerable altitude is ~5,950 m; above 8,000 m ("death zone") oxygen is insufficient for human life (summits of Everest and K2). **Settlement and economy:** Only 7% of land lies above 2,500 m, hosting 140 million people (20–30 million above 3,000 m), half concentrated in the Andes, Central Asia, and Africa. Communities above 4,000 m are rare and specialized (e.g., La Rinconada, Peru, a gold-mining town at 5,100 m); El Alto, Bolivia (4,150 m, ~1 million) is a diverse exception. Traditional agriculture carries high crop-failure risk; mining and tourism (parks, ski resorts) are major economic drivers. ~80% of mountain people live below the poverty line. **Water resources:** Most rivers originate in mountains; snow storage regulates downstream flow. Over half of humanity depends on mountain water. **Geopolitics:** Mountains frequently serve as natural boundaries. **Cultural significance:** Mountains are sacred in many traditions: Mount Olympus (Greek gods), Mount Fuji (Shinto/Buddhist), Mount Kailash (Hinduism, Bon, Buddhism, Jainism), Mount Brandon (Irish Catholic pilgrimage), Nanda Devi (Hindu goddesses, closed 1983), Mount Ararat (Noah’s Ark), and Alpine summit crosses.

### Superlatives
Heights are usually measured from sea level: **Mount Everest** (Himalayas) is highest at 8,848 m (29,029 ft); ≥100 peaks exceed 7,200 m, all in central/southern Asia. **Tallest from base:** **Mauna Kea** (Hawaii) rises ~10,203 m from the Pacific floor (4,207 m above sea level). **Largest by volume/area:** **Mauna Loa** (shield volcano, ~75,000 km³, 5,200 km² base). **Largest non-shield volcano:** **Mount Kilimanjaro** (635 km², 4,793 km³). **Largest non-volcanic mountain:** **Mount Logan** (311 km² base). **Farthest from Earth’s center:** **Chimborazo** (Ecuador) or **Huascarán** (Peru), due to equatorial bulge, despite being >2 km lower than Everest.

## Terms
- **Altitudinal zonation**: Layering of distinct ecosystems along elevation bands of roughly constant climate.
- **Adiabatic lapse rate**: Rate of temperature decrease with altitude due to expansion of rising air (~9.8 °C/km dry; ~5.5 °C/km moist).
- **Biotemperature**: Mean temperature with all sub-zero values set to 0 °C, used to define ecological limits (Holdridge, 1947).
- **Block mountain (horst)**: Uplifted crustal block bounded by faults; adjacent dropped blocks form graben/rift valleys.
- **Death zone**: Altitudes above ~8,000 m where atmospheric oxygen is insufficient to sustain human life.
- **Fold mountain**: Mountain formed by crustal shortening and overthickening at convergent plate boundaries, balanced by deep crustal roots (isostasy).
- **Isostasy**: Gravitational equilibrium where buoyant continental crust floats on denser mantle; surface uplift requires a larger downward root.
- **Sky island**: Isolated mountain habitat separated by inhospitable lowlands, hosting unique endemic species.
- **Stratovolcano**: Steep, conical volcano built by alternating layers of lava and pyroclastic deposits.
- **Topographical prominence**: Vertical distance between a summit and the lowest contour line encircling it and no higher summit.

## Debates and open questions
- **Definition**: No universal quantitative definition exists; thresholds vary by country, authority, and context (elevation vs. prominence vs. local relief).
- **Climate change attribution**: Whether highlands warm faster than lowlands globally remains unresolved; regional precipitation projections are highly uncertain.
- **Tallest mountain metric**: "Highest" (sea level) vs. "tallest" (base-to-peak) vs. "farthest from Earth’s center" yield different answers (Everest, Mauna Kea, Chimborazo/Huascarán).
- **Ecological response**: Velocity and direction of species migration under rapid warming; persistence of sky-island endemics.
- **Water security**: Quantifying future river discharge changes for downstream populations dependent on glacial/snow melt.
