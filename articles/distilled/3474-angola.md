# Angola

## Overview
Angola is a large, resource-rich country on Africa's southwestern coast. Its modern history is defined by centuries of Portuguese colonialism, a brutal civil war fueled by Cold War rivalries, and a post-conflict economic boom driven by oil, which has created immense wealth alongside deep inequality.

## Timeline
- **1483** — Portuguese explorer Diogo Cão reaches the area; relations established with the Kingdom of Kongo.
- **1575** — Paulo Dias de Novais founds Luanda (São Paulo de Loanda).
- **1665** — Portuguese kill King António I of Kongo at the Battle of Mbwila, crippling the kingdom.
- **1836** — Slave trade abolished in Angola (enforcement remains weak).
- **1884–85** — Berlin Conference sets Angola's colonial borders.
- **1961** — Armed struggle for independence begins.
- **1975** — Angola gains independence from Portugal; civil war immediately erupts.
- **2002** — Civil war ends after the death of UNITA leader Jonas Savimbi.
- **2017** — José Eduardo dos Santos steps down after 38 years; João Lourenço becomes president.
- **2024** — Angola withdraws from OPEC following a production quota dispute.

## Body

### Pre-colonial and Colonial Era
Before Portuguese arrival, the region was populated by Khoi and San hunter-gatherers, later displaced by Bantu migrations. By the 13th century, states like the Kingdom of Kongo, Ndongo, and Matamba had formed. Portuguese colonisation began in the 16th century, with the slave trade becoming the dominant economic activity, devastating the Kingdom of Kongo's society and economy. Portugal's control over the interior remained limited until the late 19th and early 20th centuries, facing resistance from groups like the Mbunda and Kwanyama.

### Independence Struggle and Civil War
The war for independence (1961–1974) was fought by three rival nationalist movements: the MPLA, FNLA, and UNITA. After Portugal's 1974 revolution, these factions vied for power, leading to a devastating civil war upon independence in 1975. The conflict became a Cold War proxy war, with the MPLA backed by the Soviet Union and Cuba, and UNITA and the FNLA supported by the United States and South Africa. The MPLA established a Marxist-Leninist one-party state. A 1992 election led to renewed war after UNITA rejected the results. The conflict finally ended in 2002 after Savimbi's death.

### Post-War Politics and Economy
Since 2002, Angola has been a dominant-party republic under the MPLA. The economy has grown rapidly, driven by oil and diamonds, making it one of the world's fastest-growing. However, wealth is highly concentrated, leading to extreme inequality, poverty, and poor social indicators like low life expectancy. China is a major trade and investment partner. The government has faced criticism for corruption and authoritarianism, though some political and economic reforms have occurred under President Lourenço.

## Terms
- **Lusophone**: A Portuguese-speaking country or community.
- **Bantu expansion**: The historical migration of Bantu-speaking peoples across central and southern Africa.
- **Kingdom of Kongo**: A powerful pre-colonial state in the region, with which the Portuguese first interacted.
- **Atlantic slave trade**: The forced transportation of enslaved Africans to the Americas, which dominated Angola's colonial economy.
- **MPLA**: Popular Movement for the Liberation of Angola, the ruling party since independence.
- **UNITA**: National Union for the Total Independence of Angola, the main insurgent group during the civil war.
- **Sonangol**: The state-owned oil conglomerate that controls Angola's petroleum industry.
- **OPEC**: Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, which Angola joined in 2006 and left in 2024.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source highlights ongoing debates about Angola's governance and development. Key questions include the sustainability of its oil-dependent economy, the effectiveness of anti-corruption efforts, and the depth of democratic reforms. The future of the separatist conflict in the Cabinda exclave remains unresolved. Scholars also debate the legacy of the civil war and whether the post-2002 political system truly represents a break from authoritarian rule.
