# Sergei Eisenstein

## Overview
Sergei Eisenstein (1898–1948) was a Soviet film director, screenwriter, editor, and theorist, widely considered one of the greatest filmmakers of all time. He pioneered the theory and practice of montage—the editing technique of juxtaposing shots to generate meaning and emotion—and is best known for the silent films *Strike* (1925), *Battleship Potemkin* (1925), and *October* (1928), as well as the historical epics *Alexander Nevsky* (1938) and *Ivan the Terrible* (1945/1958). His work shaped Soviet montage theory and influenced filmmakers globally, though his career was repeatedly constrained by Soviet political pressures.

## Timeline
- **1898** — Born in Riga, Governorate of Livonia, Russian Empire (present-day Latvia), to a middle-class family.
- **1918** — Left the Petrograd Institute of Civil Engineering to join the Red Army in the Russian Civil War.
- **1920** — Moved to Moscow, began theatre work with Proletkult, an experimental Soviet artistic institution.
- **1923** — Wrote "The Montage of Attractions" and made his first film, *Glumov's Diary*.
- **1925** — Released *Strike* and *Battleship Potemkin*; the latter won international acclaim.
- **1928** — Released *October*; left the USSR for a European tour with collaborators Grigori Aleksandrov and Eduard Tisse.
- **1930** — Signed a contract with Paramount Pictures; project failed, then traveled to Mexico to film *¡Que viva México!*, which was left unfinished.
- **1938** — Released *Alexander Nevsky*, his first sound film, winning the Order of Lenin and Stalin Prize.
- **1944–1946** — Completed *Ivan the Terrible*, Part I (released 1944) and Part II (completed 1946, unreleased until 1958).
- **1948** — Died of a heart attack on 11 February, aged 50; ashes buried in Novodevichy Cemetery, Moscow.

## Body

### Early life and education
Eisenstein was born on 22 January [O.S. 10 January] 1898 in Riga, to a middle-class family. His father, architect Mikhail Osipovich Eisenstein, was born into a Jewish family but converted to the Russian Orthodox Church before marriage; his mother, Julia Ivanovna Konetskaya, came from a Russian Orthodox merchant family. After his parents' divorce, Julia moved to France, and Eisenstein was raised Orthodox Christian but later became an atheist. He studied architecture and engineering at the Petrograd Institute of Civil Engineering, but left in 1918 to join the Red Army, where he provided propaganda and was exposed to Kabuki theatre and Japanese, learning some 300 kanji characters, which he cited as influencing his pictorial development.

### From theatre to cinema
In Moscow from 1920, Eisenstein worked for Proletkult, designing for Vsevolod Meyerhold. His 1923 essay "The Montage of Attractions" launched his theoretical career. His first full-length feature, *Strike* (1925), was followed by *Battleship Potemkin* (1925), which earned worldwide critical acclaim. This allowed him to direct *October* (1928) for the tenth anniversary of the October Revolution. While foreign critics praised his work, his focus on structural issues—camera angles, crowd movements, montage—drew criticism from the Soviet film community, forcing public self-criticism and commitments to conform to socialist realism, the state-mandated artistic doctrine.

### Travels and American projects
In autumn 1928, Eisenstein toured Europe with Aleksandrov and Tisse, officially to study sound film. In 1930, Paramount offered him a contract; he proposed projects like a biography of arms dealer Basil Zaharoff and a film of *Sutter's Gold*, but failed to impress producers. His script for *An American Tragedy* was rejected, and anti-communist attacks led to the contract's nullification in October 1930. Through Charlie Chaplin, he met Upton Sinclair, who funded a Mexican film project via the "Mexican Film Trust." Eisenstein signed a contract in November 1930, but filming exceeded its schedule and budget. Under pressure from Stalin, who called him a deserter, Sinclair shut down production; customs officials later found Eisenstein's erotic drawings in luggage, and his visa expired. The footage was edited into films like *Thunder Over Mexico* (1933–1934) without Eisenstein's involvement.

### Return to the Soviet Union
Eisenstein's Mexican failure led to depression and a hospital stay in 1933. He returned to teaching at the State Institute of Cinematography. Assigned *Bezhin Meadow* in 1935, he again exceeded budgets and deadlines; the project was cancelled. His career was saved when Stalin blamed the industry executives, leading to the execution of Boris Shumyatsky, the film industry head. Eisenstein then made *Alexander Nevsky* (1938), a biopic of the medieval prince's victory at the Battle of the Ice, with music by Sergei Prokofiev; it served as an allegorical warning against Nazi Germany and won the Order of Lenin and Stalin Prize. During World War II, he was evacuated to Alma-Ata, where he planned *Ivan the Terrible*; Part I won Stalin's approval, but Part II was criticized and unreleased until 1958, and Part III's footage was confiscated and mostly destroyed.

### Film theory and teaching
Eisenstein was among the earliest film theorists, building on Lev Kuleshov's work but differing on montage's principles. He argued that the "collision" of shots, not just their "linkage," could manipulate emotions and create metaphors. He developed five "methods of montage": metric, rhythmic, tonal, overtonal, and intellectual. His books *Film Form* and *The Film Sense* explain these ideas. At VGIK, he taught directing, photography, and editing, using literature like Balzac's *Le Père Goriot* for exercises. His early films used untrained actors and groups as characters, addressing class conflict rather than individuals.

### Personal life and death
Contemporaries largely believed Eisenstein was gay; he denied this to friend Marie Seton, citing "bisexual tendency in the intellectual dimension." In 1934, seven months after homosexuality became a criminal offense, he married filmmaker Pera Attacheva. He suffered a heart attack in 1946 and died of a second on 11 February 1948. His sketchbooks, including over 500 erotic drawings withheld from the state archive, have been exhibited since the late 1990s.

## Terms
- **Montage**: Editing technique of juxtaposing shots to generate meaning and emotion; Eisenstein's core theory.
- **Proletkult**: Experimental Soviet artistic institution aiming to create a revolutionary working-class aesthetic.
- **Socialist realism**: State-mandated Soviet artistic doctrine requiring realistic, politically conformist representation.
- **Metric, rhythmic, tonal, overtonal, intellectual montage**: Eisenstein's five methods of editing, from simple timing to abstract ideological collision.
- **Battle of the Ice**: 1242 victory of Alexander Nevsky over Germanic invaders, subject of his 1938 film.
- **VGIK**: State Institute of Cinematography, where Eisenstein taught and wrote curricula.
- **¡Que viva México!**: Unfinished Mexican film project (1930–1932), later edited by others into several films.

## Debates and open questions
- **Sexuality**: Eisenstein's homosexuality is debated; he denied it, but contemporaries and later scholars widely believed he was gay, and a film on the topic faced difficulties in Russia.
- **Mexican film**: The extent of footage shot (estimates range from 170,000 to over 250,000 lineal feet) and who was responsible for its failure remain contested; Eisenstein blamed production supervisor Hunter Kimbrough.
- **Bezhin Meadow**: The film's cancellation and reconstruction in the 1960s from storyboards raise questions about what was lost and why it failed.
