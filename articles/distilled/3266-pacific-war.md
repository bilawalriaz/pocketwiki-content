# Pacific War

## Overview
The Pacific War (1941–1945) was the largest theater of World War II, fought between Japan and the Allies across East and Southeast Asia, the Pacific and Indian Oceans, and Oceania. It began with Japan's surprise attack on Pearl Harbor and ended with Japan's surrender following atomic bombings and the Soviet invasion of Manchuria. The war's outcome reshaped the global order, leading to the end of Japan's empire and the beginning of the Cold War in Asia.

## Timeline
- **7 Jul 1937** — Marco Polo Bridge Incident sparks full-scale war between Japan and China.
- **7–8 Dec 1941** — Japan attacks Pearl Harbor, the Philippines, Malaya, and other Allied territories.
- **Jun 1942** — Battle of Midway; Japanese naval power is critically weakened.
- **Aug 1942–Feb 1943** — Guadalcanal campaign; first major Allied offensive and Japanese withdrawal.
- **Jun 1943** — Operation Cartwheel begins, aiming to isolate the Japanese base at Rabaul.
- **Nov 1943–Feb 1944** — Gilbert and Marshall Islands campaign.
- **Jun 1944** — Battle of the Philippine Sea; Japanese carrier air power is destroyed.
- **Oct 1944** — Battle of Leyte Gulf; the Imperial Japanese Navy is effectively destroyed as a fighting force.
- **Feb–Mar 1945** — Battle of Iwo Jima.
- **Apr–Jun 1945** — Battle of Okinawa, the largest and bloodiest battle of the Pacific War.
- **6 & 9 Aug 1945** — U.S. atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki.
- **9 Aug 1945** — Soviet Union invades Manchuria.
- **2 Sep 1945** — Japan signs the Instrument of Surrender, ending World War II.

## Body

### Origins and Japanese Offensives (1931–1942)
Conflict began with Japan's invasion of Manchuria in 1931, escalating to full-scale war with China in 1937. Tensions with Western powers rose after Japan occupied French Indochina in 1940, leading to an Allied oil embargo. Japan's "Southern Plan" aimed to seize resource-rich territories in Southeast Asia. On 7 December 1941, Japan launched surprise attacks on Pearl Harbor, the Philippines, Malaya, and other targets, bringing the U.S. and British Empire into the war. Over the next six months, Japan conquered a vast perimeter including the Dutch East Indies, Burma, and numerous Pacific islands, achieving its initial strategic objectives.

### The Turning Point (1942)
The Battle of the Coral Sea (May 1942) halted a Japanese advance toward Port Moresby, New Guinea. The decisive turning point came at the Battle of Midway (June 1942), where U.S. forces sank four Japanese fleet carriers, crippling Japan's offensive naval capability. The costly Guadalcanal campaign (August 1942–February 1943) marked the first major Allied offensive and a strategic Japanese withdrawal, shifting the initiative to the Allies.

### Allied Offensives and Japanese Collapse (1943–1945)
The Allies adopted an "island-hopping" strategy, bypassing strongholds to seize key airfields and naval bases. Major campaigns included the Gilbert and Marshall Islands (1943–44), the Marianas (1944), and the Philippines (1944–45). The Battle of Leyte Gulf (October 1944) destroyed the Imperial Japanese Navy's surface fleet. In 1945, bloody battles for Iwo Jima and Okinawa brought Allied forces within bombing range of the Japanese home islands. Concurrently, Allied submarines strangled Japan's supply lines, and strategic firebombing devastated its cities.

### The China and Burma Theaters
The Second Sino-Japanese War (1937–1945) was a massive, grinding conflict. By 1941, it had become a stalemate, though Japan launched major offensives like Operation Ichi-Go (1944). In Burma, initial Japanese conquests were reversed by Allied forces, culminating in the recapture of Rangoon in May 1945.

### End of the War
Faced with atomic bombings, the Soviet invasion of Manchuria (9 August 1945), and a naval blockade, Japan announced its surrender on 15 August 1945. The formal surrender was signed on 2 September 1945. Japan lost its empire and was occupied by the Allies until 1952.

## Terms
- **Island-hopping**: An Allied strategy of capturing key islands while bypassing and isolating heavily defended Japanese positions.
- **Kamikaze**: Japanese suicide attacks, primarily by aircraft, used in the war's final stages to inflict maximum casualties on Allied naval forces.
- **Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere**: Japan's concept for a bloc of Asian nations led by Japan and free of Western powers, used to justify its expansion.
- **Three Alls Policy**: A Japanese counterinsurgency strategy in China—"Kill all, Burn all, Loot all"—responsible for millions of civilian deaths.
- **ABDACOM**: The short-lived American-British-Dutch-Australian Command, the first Allied supreme command in Southeast Asia (1942).
- **Tokyo Express**: The Allied nickname for Japanese naval transport missions to resupply troops on Guadalcanal at night.
- **Operation Downfall**: The planned Allied invasion of the Japanese home islands, which was rendered unnecessary by Japan's surrender.
- **Fukkaku tactics**: Japanese defensive tactics involving honeycombed fortifications in island terrain, used at Peleliu, Iwo Jima, and Okinawa.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Necessity of the atomic bombings**: Historians debate whether the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki were necessary to force Japan's surrender or if a naval blockade and conventional bombing would have sufficed, potentially avoiding the massive casualties projected for an invasion.
- **Strategic value of Iwo Jima**: The high American casualties at Iwo Jima (over 26,000) have led to ongoing debate about whether the island's capture was strategically worth the cost, given its limited use for B-29 operations.
- **Soviet entry's impact**: The significance of the Soviet invasion of Manchuria in precipitating Japan's surrender is debated, with some arguing it was a decisive factor and others viewing the atomic bombs as the primary cause.
- **Casualty figures**: Precise death tolls, especially for China and civilian populations across Asia, remain subject to scholarly debate due to incomplete records and differing methodologies.
