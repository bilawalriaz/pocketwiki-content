# Billy Wilder

## Overview
Billy Wilder (1906–2002) was an Austrian-born, Jewish-American filmmaker whose five-decade Hollywood career established him as one of cinema’s most versatile writer-directors. Fleeing Nazi Germany, he arrived in Hollywood in 1934 and mastered genres from film noir (*Double Indemnity*, *Sunset Boulevard*) to screwball comedy (*Some Like It Hot*, *The Apartment*), often pushing the boundaries of the Production Code. He won six Academy Awards from 21 nominations—including rare triple wins for Best Picture, Director, and Screenplay for *The Lost Weekend* (1945) and *The Apartment* (1960)—and his films are celebrated for tight plotting, memorable dialogue, and a belief that writing should dictate visual style.

## Timeline
- **June 22, 1906** — Born Samuel Wilder in Sucha, Galicia, Austria-Hungary, to a Polish-Jewish family.
- **1926** — Moves to Berlin after interviewing Paul Whiteman in Vienna; works as journalist and taxi dancer.
- **1929–1933** — Writes 12 German films, including *People on Sunday* (1930), a landmark of the *Neue Sachlichkeit* (New Objectivity) movement.
- **1933** — Flees to Paris after Hitler’s rise; makes directorial debut with *Mauvaise Graine* (1934).
- **1934** — Arrives in Hollywood; becomes naturalized U.S. citizen in 1939.
- **1939** — First major hit: co-writes *Ninotchka* with Charles Brackett; first Oscar nomination.
- **1944** — Directs *Double Indemnity* (co-written with Raymond Chandler), defining film noir conventions and challenging the Hays Code.
- **1945** — Directs *The Lost Weekend*, the first major U.S. film on alcoholism; wins Palme d’Or and Oscars for Best Picture, Director, and Screenplay.
- **1950** — Releases *Sunset Boulevard*; wins Oscar for Best Original Screenplay (final collaboration with Brackett).
- **1955–1959** — Directs Marilyn Monroe in *The Seven Year Itch* (1955) and *Some Like It Hot* (1959); latter released without Production Code seal.
- **1960** — Releases *The Apartment*; wins Oscars for Best Picture, Director, and Original Screenplay.
- **1970** — Directs final major film, *The Private Life of Sherlock Holmes* (heavily cut by studio).
- **1986–1995** — Receives AFI Life Achievement Award, Kennedy Center Honors, National Medal of Arts, and BAFTA Fellowship.
- **March 27, 2002** — Dies of pneumonia in Los Angeles; epitaph reads: "I'm a writer but then nobody's perfect."

## Body

### Early Life and European Career
Born in Sucha (then Austria-Hungary), Wilder moved to Vienna in 1916 and became a journalist, forgoing university. A 1926 interview with bandleader Paul Whiteman led him to Berlin, where he worked as a taxi dancer before securing a tabloid job. From 1929 to 1933, he wrote 12 German films. He collaborated with future directors Fred Zinnemann and Robert Siodmak on *People on Sunday* (1930), a seminal *Strassenfilm* (street film) of the *Neue Sachlichkeit* (New Objectivity) movement that rejected Expressionism and prefigured Italian neorealism. He also scripted the 1931 adaptation of Erich Kästner’s *Emil and the Detectives*. After Hitler’s appointment as Chancellor in 1933, Wilder—recognizing the danger for a Jew—fled to Paris, where he made his directorial debut with *Mauvaise Graine* (1934).

### Hollywood Arrival and Partnership with Charles Brackett
Wilder arrived in Hollywood in 1934, spending time in Mexico to resolve visa issues—an experience later fictionalized in *Hold Back the Dawn* (1941). He became a U.S. citizen in 1939. His breakthrough came with *Ninotchka* (1939), a romantic comedy for Greta Garbo directed by fellow émigré Ernst Lubitsch, earning Wilder his first Oscar nomination. He formed a prolific, if volatile, writing partnership with Charles Brackett (1938–1950). Brackett described their process as Wilder initially rejecting ideas only to adopt them days later as his own. During this period, Wilder earned nominations for *Hold Back the Dawn* and *Ball of Fire* (both 1941) and made his Hollywood directorial debut with *The Major and the Minor* (1942). Tragically, his mother, stepfather, and grandmother were murdered in the Holocaust (at Plaszów, Belzec, and the Nowy Targ ghetto, respectively), a loss confirmed by archival research only in 2011.

### Defining Film Noir and Challenging Censorship (1944–1945)
Wilder’s third directorial effort, *Double Indemnity* (1944), adapted from a James M. Cain novel deemed "unfilmable" under the Hays Code due to its central adultery and murder-for-insurance plot. Co-written with Raymond Chandler, the film established noir visual tropes—"venetian blind" lighting, voice-over narration—and became a landmark in loosening Hollywood censorship. It garnered seven Oscar nominations. In 1945, Wilder directed *Death Mills* (*Die Todesmühlen*), a documentary for the U.S. Psychological Warfare Department exposing Nazi atrocities to German audiences. That same year, *The Lost Weekend* (from Charles R. Jackson’s novel) became the first major American film to seriously examine alcoholism. It won the Palme d’Or at Cannes and four Oscars, including Best Picture, Director, and Adapted Screenplay for Wilder, and Best Actor for Ray Milland. It remains one of only four films to win both Best Picture and the Palme d’Or.

### The 1950s: Cynicism, Comedy, and Marilyn Monroe
Wilder opened the decade with *Sunset Boulevard* (1950), a cynical noir about a faded silent star (Gloria Swanson) and a kept screenwriter (William Holden). It won Best Original Screenplay for Wilder and Brackett—their final collaboration. *Ace in the Hole* (1951), a media satire starring Kirk Douglas, flopped commercially but later gained critical stature; it also sparked a lawsuit (*Wilder v. Desny*) over story ownership, resulting in a $14,350 settlement. Wilder then adapted three Broadway plays: the POW drama *Stalag 17* (1953, Best Actor for Holden), the romantic comedy *Sabrina* (1954, with Audrey Hepburn and Humphrey Bogart), and *The Seven Year Itch* (1955), featuring Marilyn Monroe’s iconic subway-grate scene. In 1957, three Wilder films released: the Lindbergh biopic *The Spirit of St. Louis*; *Love in the Afternoon* (first script with I. A. L. Diamond, his partner for the rest of his career); and the courtroom thriller *Witness for the Prosecution*. He reunited with Monroe for *Some Like It Hot* (1959), a Prohibition farce featuring cross-dressing musicians (Jack Lemmon, Tony Curtis). The Production Code Administration denied it a seal due to sexual content, but the film became a massive hit, famous for its closing line: "Well, nobody's perfect."

### The 1960s: Peak Acclaim and Later Work
*The Apartment* (1960), starring Lemmon and Shirley MacLaine as an insurance clerk and an elevator operator entangled in corporate infidelity, won five Oscars (Picture, Director, Original Screenplay) and cemented Wilder’s reputation. He followed with the Cold War farce *One, Two, Three* (1961, James Cagney), the comedy *Irma la Douce* (1963, Lemmon/MacLaine), and the sex comedy *Kiss Me, Stupid* (1964), which drew sharp criticism for perceived vulgarity. *The Fortune Cookie* (1966) paired Lemmon with Walter Matthau for the first time (Wilder’s final Oscar nomination). *The Private Life of Sherlock Holmes* (1970), intended as a roadshow epic, was heavily cut by the studio to Wilder’s dismay.

### Final Films and Retirement
Through his company Phalanx Productions, Wilder directed *Avanti!* (1972) and *The Front Page* (1974). His last two films, *Fedora* (1978) and *Buddy Buddy* (1981), were poorly received initially, though *Fedora* has been re-evaluated favorably. Wilder hoped to adapt *Schindler’s Ark* as a memorial to his family but praised Steven Spielberg’s *Schindler’s List* instead. In retirement, he amassed a renowned modern art collection and created his own collaborative artworks with Bruce Houston, exhibited in 1993 as *Billy Wilder's Marché aux Puces*. He died in 2002; *Le Monde* headlined: "Billy Wilder is dead. Nobody is perfect."

### Directorial Style and Actor Direction
Wilder prioritized writing over visual flamboyance, avoiding the showy cinematography of Hitchcock or Welles to keep focus on story. He believed scripts should be tailored to actors' personalities rather than forcing performances beyond their limits. He coaxed legends Gloria Swanson and Erich von Stroheim out of retirement for *Sunset Boulevard* and cast against type: Fred MacMurray (known for wholesome roles) as a schemer in *Double Indemnity* and *The Apartment*; Humphrey Bogart shedding his tough-guy image for *Sabrina*; James Cagney in high-octane comedy for *One, Two, Three*. He directed 14 different actors to Oscar nominations, mentoring Jack Lemmon (seven films together) and pairing him with Walter Matthau. Roger Ebert noted Wilder "took the characters seriously... and got a lot of the laughs by playing scenes straight."

### Politics
Wilder opposed the House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC) and co-founded the Committee for the First Amendment to support "hostile" witnesses. At a Screen Directors Guild meeting, only Wilder and John Huston voted against a loyalty oath; Huston later called it "one of the bravest things that Billy, as a naturalized German, had ever done." Wilder avoided the blacklist but dismissed the Hollywood Ten as largely talentless. He enjoyed satirizing political rigidity, as in *Ball of Fire* where a burlesque queen compares her sore throat to the *Daily Worker*: "just as red and just as sore."

## Terms
- **Neue Sachlichkeit (New Objectivity)**: A 1920s German artistic movement rejecting Expressionist distortion in favor of unsentimental, realistic depiction; Wilder’s *People on Sunday* is a key cinematic example.
- **Strassenfilm (Street Film)**: A genre of late Weimar cinema focusing on everyday urban life and non-professional actors; a precursor to Italian neorealism and the French New Wave.
- **Hays Code / Production Code**: Hollywood’s self-censorship guidelines (enforced 1934–1968) prohibiting depictions of adultery, crime without punishment, and sexual perversion; Wilder’s *Double Indemnity* and *The Lost Weekend* tested its limits.
- **Film Noir**: A style of crime drama (1940s–50s) marked by cynicism, fatalism, and high-contrast lighting; Wilder directed two definitive entries: *Double Indemnity* and *Sunset Boulevard*.
- **Production Code Seal**: Official certification required for U.S. theatrical distribution; *Some Like It Hot* (1959) was released without one due to cross-dressing and sexual humor, signaling the Code’s decline.
- **Phalanx Productions**: Wilder’s independent film company, formed in the 1960s, through which he produced *The Fortune Cookie*, *The Private Life of Sherlock Holmes*, *Avanti!*, and *The Front Page*.
- **Committee for the First Amendment**: A 1947 group of ~500 Hollywood figures organized by Wilder and others to defend colleagues subpoenaed by HUAC who refused to cooperate.
- **Palme d’Or**: The top prize at the Cannes Film Festival; *The Lost Weekend* (1945) won it, making Wilder one of only four directors whose film won both the Palme and the Best Picture Oscar.
- **AFI Life Achievement Award**: The American Film Institute’s highest honor, awarded to Wilder in 1986.
- **National Film Registry**: The Library of Congress list of films deemed "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant"; seven Wilder films are preserved there.

## Debates and open questions
- **Schindler’s Ark adaptation**: Wilder actively pursued filming Thomas Keneally’s novel as a memorial to his murdered family but never secured the project; the extent of his serious development versus symbolic intent remains unclear.
- **Authorship in the Wilder-Brackett partnership**: Brackett’s diaries suggest a lopsided dynamic where Wilder claimed credit for jointly developed ideas; scholars debate the precise division of labor across their 12 films.
- **Wilder v. Desny (1951) precedent**: The California Supreme Court ruling on implied contract for story ideas (stemming from *Ace in the Hole*) is a landmark in entertainment law, but its long-term impact on Hollywood submission practices is rarely detailed in general histories.
- **Late-career critical reception**: *Fedora* (1978) and *Buddy Buddy* (1981) were dismissed on release; *Fedora*’s recent critical rehabilitation raises questions about whether Wilder’s late style was misunderstood or genuinely diminished.
- **Holocaust victim locations**: For decades, Wilder’s family was assumed killed at Auschwitz; 2011 archival research by Andreas Hutter revealed separate murders at Plaszów, Belzec, and Nowy Targ—highlighting gaps in Holocaust documentation even for prominent survivors.
