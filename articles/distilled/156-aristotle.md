# Aristotle

## Overview

Aristotle (384–322 BC) was an ancient Greek philosopher and polymath whose writings spanned natural sciences, philosophy, linguistics, economics, politics, psychology, and the arts. As founder of the Peripatetic school in Athens, he established the Aristotelian tradition that set the groundwork for modern science. His works contain the earliest known systematic study of logic, profoundly shaped medieval scholarship across Islamic, Jewish, and Christian traditions, and remained dominant in physics until the Enlightenment. He has been called the first scientist and the father of logic, biology, political science, and numerous other fields.

- **12th–13th c.** — Latin translations revived Aristotle in Western Europe; Aquinas called him "The Philosopher"

## Body

### Life

Aristotle was born in 384 BC in Stagira, about 55 km east of modern Thessaloniki. His father Nicomachus belonged to the medical guild of Asclepiadae, likely sparking Aristotle's early interest in biology. Both parents died when he was young; Proxenus of Atarneus became his guardian. At seventeen or eighteen, he joined Plato's Academy, remaining nearly twenty years. After Plato's death, he left—possibly disappointed with the Academy's direction under Speusippus, or due to anti-Macedonian sentiment. He went to Assos with Xenocrates, where he married Pythias, and conducted botanical and marine biological research with Theophrastus.

In 343/42 BC, Philip II invited Aristotle to tutor his son Alexander. Aristotle taught at Mieza, encouraging Alexander toward eastern conquest with an ethnocentric attitude toward Persia. After Alexander became regent, Aristotle returned to Athens in 335 BC. As a metic (resident alien), he could not own property, so he rented the Lyceum, establishing the Peripatetic school (named after the peripatos, or colonnade). He lectured for twelve years, building a large library. Near the end of Alexander's life, the two became estranged over governance and philosophical differences. After Alexander's death, Aristotle was denounced for impiety and fled to Chalcis, where he died of natural causes in 322 BC.

### Logic

Aristotle is credited with the earliest systematic study of logic, dominant in the West until 19th-century mathematical logic. His logical works were compiled into six books called the Organon: Categories, On Interpretation, Prior Analytics, Posterior Analytics, Topics, and On Sophistical Refutations. The order moves from simple terms to propositions to complex forms—syllogisms, demonstration, and dialectics. What is today called Aristotelian logic with its syllogisms (methods of logical argument), Aristotle called "analytics"; he reserved "logic" for dialectics. His Posterior Analytics concerns demonstrative knowledge—what would today be called epistemology—holding that knowledge is what is necessarily the case, along with the study of causes.

### Metaphysics

Aristotle called his metaphysics "first philosophy" or theology: "the study of being qua being"—that which is eternal, unchanging, and immaterial. He examined substance (ousia) and essence (to ti ên einai, "the what it was to be"), concluding that a particular substance combines matter and form—a theory called hylomorphism. Unlike Plato, who held that universals exist separately as forms that particulars imitate, Aristotle maintained that universals exist only when "instantiated" in particular substances and are multiply located. Concerning change, he distinguished coming-to-be from growth, locomotion, and alteration, introducing potentiality (dynamis) and actuality (entelecheia): matter has potentiality to become a form, and actuality is the fulfilment of that potential, which is also the final cause or end (telos).

### Natural philosophy

Aristotle's natural philosophy covered physics, biology, and other sciences. He related the four Empedoclean elements (earth, water, air, fire) to the qualities hot, cold, wet, and dry, adding a heavenly aether for the celestial spheres. He described "violent" motion (requiring an external agent) and "natural" motion (objects moving toward their natural resting places), implying that heavier objects fall faster—later corrected by Archimedes, Philoponus, and Galileo, though Carlo Rovelli argues Aristotle's physics is correct for objects in a fluid medium. Aristotle distinguished four causes: material (what something is made of), formal (its arrangement), efficient (the agent producing it), and final (its purpose). He made geological observations, proposed that earthquakes result from trapped gas, and correctly described the hydrologic cycle and desalination.

Aristotle was the first to study biology systematically, observing zoology on Lesbos for two years. He described about 500 animal species, grouping vertebrates as "animals with blood" and invertebrates as "animals without blood." He accurately described the four-chambered stomachs of ruminants and the ovoviviparous development of the hound shark. He did not do experiments in the modern sense but systematically gathered data and inferred causal explanations, correctly predicting that brood size decreases with body mass and lifespan increases with gestation period. He was critical of Empedocles's "survival of the fittest" theory, nowhere suggesting that species share ancestors or change into one another. In psychology, he posited three kinds of soul—vegetative, sensitive, and rational—placing the rational soul in the heart. He argued dreams result from lasting impressions of waking experiences, not divine foretelling.

### Practical philosophy

Aristotle was a virtue ethicist: ethics aims at becoming good, not knowing for its own sake. Virtue relates to proper function (ergon); humans must have a function specific to them—activity of the soul in accordance with reason. The aim is eudaimonia (happiness or well-being), achieved through habituated good character leading to practical wisdom (phronesis). In Politics, he considered the city a natural community, prior to the family and individual, and famously stated "man is by nature a political animal." The city's aim is not merely avoiding injustice but enabling citizens to live good lives and perform noble actions. He developed a theory of a mixed state combining oligarchic and democratic elements. In economics, he offered an early account of money's origin and considered retail trade and interest unnatural, as they use goods or money as means rather than ends. His Rhetoric proposed three appeals—ethos (character), pathos (emotion), logos (reason)—and three genres: epideictic, forensic, and deliberative. His Poetics held that all arts are acts of mimesis (imitation), with tragedy effecting catharsis of pity and fear through plot-structure, character, style, thought, spectacle, and lyric poetry.

### Legacy

Aristotle's immediate influence came through the Peripatetic school; Theophrastus wrote the pioneering History of Plants. Herophilus corrected Aristotle by placing intelligence in the brain. In late antiquity, Neoplatonists treated Aristotle's works as introductory to Plato, with Porphyry's Isagoge influencing later philosophy. In the medieval Islamic world, Aristotle was revered as the "First Teacher"; Al-Kindi, Al-Farabi, Avicenna, and Averroes harmonized his logic with Islamic theology. Maimonides based his Guide for the Perplexed on Aristotelianism, calling Aristotle the "chief of the philosophers." In Western Europe, Aristotle was nearly unknown from c. 600 to c. 1100 except through Boethius's Latin translation of the Organon; 12th–13th century translations revived interest, and Aquinas's Summa Theologica, working from Moerbeke's translations, stimulated a lasting revival. In the early modern period, Harvey and Galileo reacted against Aristotelian theories. Boole extended Aristotelian logic with algebraic logic in 1854. Darwin regarded Aristotle as the most important contributor to biology. Modern zoologists have confirmed several of his surprising claims, and Niko Tinbergen's four questions are based on Aristotle's four causes.

## Terms

- **Peripatetic school**: Aristotle's school at the Lyceum, named after the peripatos (colonnade) where he taught
- **Organon**: The six-book compilation of Aristotle's logical works, assembled c. 40 BC by Andronicus of Rhodes
- **Syllogism**: A method of logical argument; Aristotle called this study "analytics"
- **Hylomorphism**: The theory that a particular substance is a combination of matter and form
- **Potentiality/actuality** (dynamis/entelecheia): Matter's capacity to become something versus the fulfilment of that capacity
- **Four causes**: Material, formal, efficient, and final explanations for why something exists or changes
- **Eudaimonia**: Happiness or well-being, the aim of human action achieved through virtuous activity
- **Mimesis**: Imitation; the fundamental property of all arts according to the Poetics
- **Metic**: A resident alien in Athens who could not own property
- **Phronesis**: Practical wisdom, developed through habituated virtue

## Debates and open questions

Scholars debate whether Aristotle intended his physics to state quantitative laws; Henri Carteron held the "extreme view" that Aristotle's concept of force was basically qualitative, while others reject this. Carlo Rovelli argues Aristotle's physics of motion is correct within its domain—objects in Earth's gravitational field immersed in a fluid—while Galileo and Philoponus are said to have shown by experiment that heavier objects do not fall faster. Philosophers of science have concluded Aristotle was not interested in taxonomy, but zoologists think otherwise. A widespread ancient speculation that Aristotle played a role in Alexander's death rests only on an unlikely claim made six years after the death.
