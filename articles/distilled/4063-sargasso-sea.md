# Sargasso Sea

## Overview
The Sargasso Sea is a unique region of the North Atlantic Ocean defined not by land but by a clockwise-circulating system of four major ocean currents. It is ecologically significant as a critical breeding ground for eels and a habitat for specialized marine life, but it is also threatened by pollution, particularly plastic waste.

## Timeline
- **c. 5th century BC** — Carthaginian Himilco the Navigator possibly describes a seaweed-covered, windless part of the Atlantic.
- **1492** — Christopher Columbus writes about the seaweed and calm winds of the Sargasso Sea.
- **1609** — The English ship *Sea Venture* is blown onto the shores of Bermuda, near the sea's western fringe.
- **1920–1922** — The Dana expeditions, led by Johannes Schmidt, determine the European eel's breeding sites are in the Sargasso Sea.
- **1932** — William Beebe and Otis Barton conduct a pioneering deep-sea dive and radio broadcast from the area.
- **Late 1950s** — John Swallow develops the Swallow float for oceanographic research in the region.
- **1980s** — The cyanobacterium *Prochlorococcus* is discovered in the Sargasso Sea.
- **Early 2000s** — The Global Ocean Sampling Expedition reveals unexpectedly high microbial diversity.
- **2014** — The Sargasso Sea Commission is established by several governments and organizations.

## Body

### Boundaries and Characteristics
The Sargasso Sea is bounded by four currents: the Gulf Stream (west), North Atlantic Current (north), Canary Current (east), and North Atlantic Equatorial Current (south). These form the North Atlantic Gyre. The sea lies approximately between 20°–35° N and 40°–70° W, measuring about 1,100 km wide by 3,200 km long. Its borders are not fixed and can shift with the currents. The water is noted for its deep blue color, exceptional clarity (visibility up to 60 m), and floating mats of *Sargassum* seaweed.

### Ecology and Life
The sea is named for the genus *Sargassum*, a type of macroalgae (seaweed) that floats on the surface. This seaweed provides critical habitat. The Sargasso Sea is the breeding ground for several catadromous eel species (which live in freshwater but spawn at sea), including the European and American eels. Larvae hatch there and migrate to coastal waters; mature eels later return to spawn. Young loggerhead sea turtles also use the *Sargassum* as protective cover. The sargassum fish is a frogfish adapted to camouflage within the weed. Microbial life is diverse, and the *Sargassum* itself is a major oxygen producer, estimated to generate 2.2 billion litres of O₂ per hour.

### Threats and Conservation
The Sargasso Sea faces threats from industrial fishing, plastic pollution, oil drilling, and deep-sea mining. Surface currents concentrate non-biodegradable plastic, forming part of the North Atlantic garbage patch. Plastic debris can absorb toxins, posing a poisoning risk to marine life. While bacteria that consume plastic have been found, their ultimate ecological impact is unknown. In response, the Sargasso Sea Commission was established in 2014 to promote protection and management.

## Terms
- **Catadromous**: A life cycle pattern where fish live in freshwater but migrate to the sea to spawn (e.g., eels).
- **Macroalgae**: Large, multicellular algae, commonly called seaweed.
- **Metagenomics**: The study of genetic material recovered directly from environmental samples to assess microbial diversity.
- **North Atlantic Gyre**: A large system of clockwise-circulating ocean currents in the North Atlantic.
- **Prochlorococcus**: A very small, photosynthetic cyanobacterium discovered in the Sargasso Sea.
- **Sargassum**: A genus of brown macroalgae that floats in large masses on the ocean surface.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The claim that ancient mariners like Himilco the Navigator reached the Sargasso Sea is debated; modern scholars consider it unlikely.
- The exact ecological role of plastic-consuming bacteria in the Sargasso Sea is unknown—whether they remediate pollution or spread toxins is an open question.
