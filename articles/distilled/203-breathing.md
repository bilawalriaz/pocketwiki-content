# Breathing

## Overview
Breathing is the rhythmic process of inhaling oxygen-rich air into the lungs and exhaling carbon dioxide-laden air to support cellular respiration in aerobic organisms. In mammals, this relies on muscular expansion of the thoracic cavity—primarily via the diaphragm—to draw air through a branching airway tree to the alveoli, where gases diffuse across a thin blood-gas barrier into pulmonary capillaries. Breathing is regulated automatically by brainstem centers responding to arterial CO₂, O₂, and pH, but can be voluntarily overridden for speech, breath-holding, or emotional regulation. Ambient pressure changes at altitude or depth alter gas partial pressures and density, requiring physiological compensation or specialized equipment to avoid disorders such as altitude sickness or decompression illness.

## Body

### Mechanics of Ventilation
The lungs possess no intrinsic muscle; they expand only when the thoracic cavity volume increases. In mammals, quiet inhalation is driven mainly by **diaphragm** contraction (flattening the dome) and secondarily by **external intercostal muscles** lifting the rib cage (pump-handle and bucket-handle motions). Forceful inhalation recruits accessory muscles (scalenes, sternocleidomastoids) to further elevate the clavicles and upper ribs. Quiet exhalation is passive: inspiratory muscles relax, and elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall returns the system to **functional residual capacity (FRC)**—the volume remaining at rest (~2.5–3.0 L in adults). During heavy breathing (**hyperpnea**), exhalation becomes active via abdominal muscle contraction, pushing the diaphragm upward to lower end-expiratory volume. Even at maximal exhalation, **residual volume** remains. Diaphragmatic breathing causes visible abdominal motion; clavicular breathing signals labored effort (e.g., severe asthma, COPD exacerbation).

### Airway Architecture and Conditioning
Air ideally enters via the **nasal cavities**, where the septum and turbinates (conchae) create turbulence over a vast mucosal surface. This warms, humidifies, and filters inspired air, recovering some heat and moisture during exhalation. Below the larynx, the **tracheobronchial tree** branches ~23 generations in humans. Proximal airways (trachea, bronchi) conduct air; distal **respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts, and alveoli** perform gas exchange. The conducting airways constitute **anatomical dead space** (~150 ml in adults)—volume that does not participate in diffusion. This architecture ensures alveolar gas composition remains stable: each tidal breath mixes only a small fresh volume with the large FRC, preventing wild swings in O₂/CO₂ and allowing chemoreceptors to sense gradual changes.

### Gas Exchange and Transport
**External respiration** refreshes alveolar air so O₂ and CO₂ can diffuse down partial-pressure gradients across the **blood-gas barrier** (alveolar epithelium, basement membrane, capillary endothelium). Pulmonary capillary blood equilibrates with alveolar gas; the circulatory system then transports gases between lungs and tissues. Inhaled air: 78% N₂, 20.95% O₂, trace gases. Exhaled air: ~4–5% CO₂ (100× inhaled), O₂ reduced by ~4–5% absolute, saturated with water vapor (6.3 kPa at 37 °C), plus trace volatiles (isoprene, acetone, methanol, ammonia, CO, H₂) — some of which serve as disease biomarkers. Special gas mixtures (heliox, trimix, high-O₂) are used in technical diving and medicine; space suits use pure O₂ at ~20% sea-level pressure.

### Neural and Chemical Control
Breathing rhythm and depth are generated by **respiratory centers in the medulla and pons**. **Central chemoreceptors** (medulla) respond primarily to CSF/blood pH (driven by CO₂); **peripheral chemoreceptors** (carotid and aortic bodies) respond mainly to arterial PO₂. Integrated output travels via the **phrenic nerves** (diaphragm) and spinal nerves (intercostals/accessory muscles). At sea level, arterial PCO₂ is tightly defended (~5.3 kPa / 40 mmHg) at the expense of PO₂ variability; only when PO₂ falls severely does O₂ drive ventilation. Voluntary cortical override enables speech, singing, swimming, and breath-holding; conscious techniques (e.g., slow diaphragmatic breathing) can induce relaxation. Reflexes include the **diving reflex** (apnea, bradycardia, peripheral vasoconstriction during submersion), coughing, sneezing, and yawning.

### Effects of Altitude
Atmospheric pressure falls exponentially with altitude (halving ~every 5,500 m), while O₂ fraction stays constant (~21%) up to 80 km. Thus PO₂ drops proportionally: 21 kPa at sea level → 7.1 kPa on Everest summit (33.7 kPa total). Water vapor pressure in airways (6.3 kPa at 37 °C) further reduces tracheal PO₂ (to 5.8 kPa on Everest). The pressure gradient driving inflow is also reduced, but low air viscosity and slightly deeper breathing compensate. The homeostatic system automatically increases **respiratory minute volume**. Crucially, at ~2,500 m (75% sea-level pressure), the control priority switches from CO₂ to O₂ homeostasis. Abrupt switch → hyperventilation → hypocapnia → respiratory alkalosis (contributor to acute mountain sickness). Incomplete switch → dangerous hypoxia.

### Effects of Depth
Water pressure rises ~1 atm (100 kPa / 1 bar) per 10 m. Divers breathe gas at ambient pressure via a **regulator** that reduces cylinder pressure to surrounding pressure. Regulator performance (low work of breathing, smooth flow, Venturi-assisted inhalation) is critical. Compressed gases introduce risks: **pulmonary barotrauma** (lung overexpansion on ascent), **decompression sickness** (inert gas bubbles forming in tissues on rapid ascent), **nitrogen narcosis** (impaired judgment at depth), and **oxygen toxicity** (CNS seizures at high PO₂). Special mixtures (e.g., helium-based) mitigate narcosis and density-related work of breathing.

### Respiratory Disorders and Diagnostics
Abnormal patterns: **Kussmaul breathing** (deep, rapid; metabolic acidosis), **Biot’s respiration** (irregular clusters; brainstem injury), **Cheyne–Stokes respiration** (crescendo-decrescendo with apneas; heart failure, stroke). **Dyspnea** (breathlessness), **stridor** (upper airway obstruction), **apnea** (cessation), **obstructive sleep apnea**, **mouth breathing**, **snoring**. **Hypopnea** (shallow breathing); **hyperpnea** (appropriate deep/fast breathing, e.g., exercise); **hypoventilation** / **hyperventilation** (inappropriate shallow/deep breathing altering blood gases — terms often used interchangeably). **Breath tests** diagnose dietary intolerances; **rhinomanometry** measures nasal airflow acoustically.

### Society, Culture, and Voluntary Modulation
"Spirit" derives from Latin *spiritus* (breath); many traditions equate breath with life force (Hebrew *ruach*, Sanskrit *prana*, Polynesian *mana*, Greek *psyche*). **Tai chi** combines aerobic movement with diaphragmatic training for posture and *qi*. **Meditation** and **yoga** (e.g., *pranayama*, *anapanasati* — mindfulness of breath) use breath regulation for mental states. **Buteyko method** targets asthma via reduced ventilation. **Circular breathing** (wind players) and **breath support** (singers) enable sustained phrases. Cultural idioms: "catch my breath," "took my breath away," "inspiration" (literally "breathing in"), "expire." Mood-breathing link: slow diaphragmatic breathing is widely claimed to promote relaxation, inner peace (Buddhism), holistic health, or stress relief (business coaching). During exercise, deep breathing enhances O₂ uptake and generates **intra-abdominal pressure** (via lowered diaphragm) to stabilize the lumbar spine, aiding heavy lifting.

## Terms
- ****Alveoli**** — Blind-ended air sacs at the terminus of the bronchial tree where gas exchange occurs.
- ****Anatomical dead space**** — Volume of conducting airways (~150 ml in adults) that does not participate in gas exchange.
- ****Blood-gas barrier**** — Thin membrane (alveolar epithelium, basement membrane, capillary endothelium) across which O₂ and CO₂ diffuse.
- ****Central chemoreceptors**** — Sensors in the medulla responsive to CSF/blood pH (primarily driven by CO₂).
- ****Diaphragm**** — Dome-shaped primary inspiratory muscle; contraction flattens it, increasing thoracic volume.
- ****Functional residual capacity (FRC)**** — Lung volume at rest after passive exhalation (~2.5–3.0 L in adults); buffers alveolar gas composition.
- ****Hypercapnia / Hypocapnia**** — Abnormally high / low arterial CO₂ partial pressure.
- ****Hyperventilation**** — Ventilation excessive for metabolic needs, lowering arterial CO₂ and raising pH (respiratory alkalosis).
- ****Partial pressure (PO₂, PCO₂)**** — Pressure exerted by a single gas in a mixture; drives diffusion gradients.
- ****Phrenic nerves**** — Motor nerves (C3–C5) carrying respiratory center output to the diaphragm.

## Debates and open questions
- **Terminology overlap**: The distinction between *hyperpnea* (appropriate increased ventilation) and *hyperventilation* (inappropriate, causing hypocapnia) — and similarly *hypopnea* vs. *hypoventilation* — is not consistently observed in clinical practice, leading to interchangeable usage.
- **Breathing-mood causality**: While correlations between breathing patterns and mood states are documented, the extent to which voluntary adoption of a pattern *causes* a specific mood (vs. merely correlating) remains interpreted differently across disciplines (Buddhist, holistic, corporate wellness).
- **High-altitude acclimatization**: The precise mechanisms and individual variability in the switch from CO₂- to O₂-driven ventilatory control at ~2,500 m, and its role in acute mountain sickness vs. high-altitude cerebral/pulmonary edema, are active research areas.
- **Breath biomarker validity**: Hundreds of volatile organic compounds appear in exhaled breath; establishing specific, clinically reliable disease signatures (beyond research settings) is ongoing.
- **Optimal breathing techniques for exercise**: While deep breathing is recommended for core stabilization and O₂ uptake during heavy lifting, the ideal pattern across exercise modalities and populations is not definitively settled.
