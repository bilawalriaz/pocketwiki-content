# Allergy

## Overview

An allergy is an exaggerated immune response in which the body mistakenly identifies an ordinarily harmless allergen—such as pollen, certain foods, metals, insect stings, medications, or latex—as a threat. This response produces allergic diseases including hay fever, allergic conjunctivitis, allergic asthma, atopic dermatitis, food allergies, and anaphylaxis. The mechanism involves immunoglobulin E (IgE) antibodies binding to an allergen and then to receptors on mast cells or basophils, triggering release of inflammatory chemicals like histamine. Allergies are common and increasing in the developed world; the word "allergy" was first used by Clemens von Pirquet in 1906.

## Timeline

- **1906** — Clemens von Pirquet introduces the concept of "allergy" after observing quicker, more severe reactions to second injections of horse serum or smallpox vaccine
- **1963** — Philip Gell and Robin Coombs devise a classification scheme describing four types of hypersensitivity reactions
- **1966–67** — IgE is discovered simultaneously by Ishizaka's team in Denver and by Johansson and Bennich in Uppsala, Sweden
- **1969** — Their joint paper on IgE is published in April
- **1974** — Pharmacia Diagnostics AB invents and markets the radioallergosorbent test (RAST)
- **1989** — Pharmacia replaces RAST with the ImmunoCAP Specific IgE blood test using fluorescence-labeled technology
- **2008** — ACAAI and AAAAI issue the Joint Task Force Report declaring the term RAST obsolete
- **2015/2017** — US and UK pediatric guidelines revised to encourage introducing peanut products to high-risk infants at 4–6 months
- **2025** — Analysis finds a 43% drop in peanut allergy frequency following guideline revisions, with ~40,000 cases prevented

## Body

### Signs and symptoms

Many allergens are airborne particles, causing symptoms in areas exposed to air: eyes, nose, and lungs. Allergic rhinitis (hay fever) causes sneezing, itching, and red eyes; inhaled allergens can increase mucus production, causing shortness of breath, coughing, and wheezing. Food allergies cause abdominal pain, bloating, vomiting, diarrhea, itchy skin, and hives, but rarely respiratory reactions. Insect stings, food, antibiotics, and certain medicines may produce anaphylaxis, affecting multiple organ systems including digestive, respiratory, and circulatory systems; this can include skin reactions, bronchoconstriction, swelling, low blood pressure, coma, and death.

Skin contact with substances like latex causes contact dermatitis or eczema. Insect stings can produce a large local reaction—skin redness greater than 10 cm lasting one to two days. When an allergen enters the epidermis or dermis, it triggers mast cell activation, increasing blood vessel permeability and causing fluid leakage and swelling. A secondary response causes more widespread swelling about 8 hours after contact. Ingested allergens entering the bloodstream cause urticaria (hives). This skin reactivity allows allergists to test for allergies by injecting small allergen amounts into the skin.

Airway reactions include allergic rhinitis—characterized by sneezing, nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, and itching—triggered by pollen, house dust mites, animal dander, and mold spores. Lower airway reactions may contribute to allergic asthma with bronchoconstriction, wheezing, and shortness of breath. Severe airway swelling constitutes a medical emergency.

The gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) distinguishes pathogens from harmless food proteins. CD103+ dendritic cells present antigens to mesenteric lymph nodes, promoting Foxp3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) that maintain immune tolerance via cytokines like interleukin-10 and TGF-β. Disruption of these pathways may cause food allergy. The intestinal microbiome influences allergy development; reduced microbial diversity may impair oral tolerance. Sensitization can also occur through damaged skin. Early introduction of allergenic foods like peanuts and eggs during infancy has been associated with reduced food allergy risk.

### Cause

Risk factors fall into host and environmental categories. Host factors include heredity (most significant), sex, race, and age. Environmental candidates include altered exposure to infectious diseases in early childhood, environmental pollution, allergen levels, and dietary changes.

Dust mite allergy results from sensitization to house dust mite droppings; the mite's gut contains potent digestive enzymes (notably peptidase 1) that persist in feces and induce wheezing. Dust mite-proof encasements prevent chronic contact.

Ninety percent of food allergic responses are caused by cow's milk, soy, eggs, wheat, peanuts, tree nuts, fish, and shellfish. The most common food allergy in the US is crustacea sensitivity. Children can outgrow peanut allergies; egg allergies affect 1–2% of children but are outgrown by about two-thirds by age 5. Milk-protein allergies (distinct from lactose intolerance, which is an enzyme deficiency) are most common in children; ~60% are IgE-mediated. Tree nut allergies may involve one or many nuts; seeds like sesame and poppy contain allergenic proteins.

Latex allergy prevalence is believed under 1% in the general population but 7–10% among healthcare workers due to airborne exposure. The most prevalent response is allergic contact dermatitis, a delayed hypersensitivity reaction lasting 48–96 hours. Latex cross-reacts with banana, avocado, kiwifruit, and chestnut because latex proteins are structurally homologous with some plant proteins.

About 10% of people report penicillin allergy, but 90% of those turn out not to be allergic; serious allergies occur in ~0.03%. Urushiol-induced contact dermatitis from poison ivy and related plants involves urushiol acting as a hapten, chemically reacting with membrane proteins; ~25% of the population will have a strong response, and 80–90% of adults develop a rash if exposed to 0.0050 mg of purified urushiol.

Allergic diseases are strongly familial: identical twins share allergic diseases ~70% of the time, non-identical twins ~40%. The likelihood of developing allergies is inherited, but the specific allergen is not. IgE levels are highest in childhood and fall between ages 10 and 30. A 2023 review identified genetic loci clustering into four functional categories: inflammatory response regulation (IL-4, IL-13), epithelial barrier integrity (FLG), immune cell signaling (STAT6), and sensitization susceptibility (ORMDL3).

The hygiene hypothesis proposes that insufficient stimulation of the TH1 arm of the immune system leads to an overactive TH2 arm, causing allergic disease. It was developed to explain why hay fever and eczema were less common in children from larger families. Epidemiological data support it: immunological diseases are less common in the developing world, and antibiotic use in the first year of life links to asthma. Chronic stress can aggravate allergies via TH2-predominant responses. Allergic diseases are more common in industrialized and urban populations; historically, urban trees planted were predominantly male, causing high pollen counts ("botanical sexism"). Parasitic worms secrete chemicals suppressing the immune system, suggesting co-evolution of humans and parasites may mean the immune system functions correctly only in their presence.

### Pathophysiology

In the acute response, a type I hypersensitivity reaction against an allergen presented by an antigen-presenting cell causes TH2 lymphocytes to produce interleukin-4 (IL-4), stimulating B cells to produce IgE. Secreted IgE binds to FcεRI receptors on mast cells and basophils, sensitizing them. Later exposure cross-links IgE and Fc receptors, activating degranulation—release of histamine and other mediators causing vasodilation, mucous secretion, nerve stimulation, and smooth muscle contraction. Symptoms range from localized (asthma in the respiratory system, eczema in the dermis) to system-wide anaphylaxis.

Late-phase responses occur 2–24 hours after the acute response, due to migration of neutrophils, lymphocytes, eosinophils, and macrophages to the initial site. Allergic contact dermatitis is actually a type IV hypersensitivity reaction involving CD8+ T cells and activated macrophages, not type I.

### Diagnosis

Diagnosis relies on medical history; skin prick tests and blood tests have similar diagnostic value and are equally cost-effective. Skin prick testing involves introducing tiny allergen amounts into the skin; a positive response is a wheal ≥3 mm larger than the negative control. Patch testing identifies contact dermatitis via delayed type IV hypersensitivity, with patches left 48 hours and readings at 72–96 hours. Blood testing measures allergen-specific IgE and is unaffected by antihistamines; quantitative results help rank how substances affect symptoms. Laboratory methods include ELISA, radioallergosorbent test, fluorescent enzyme immunoassay, and chemiluminescence immunoassay. Challenge testing introduces suspected allergens under allergist supervision; elimination/challenge tests are used for foods and medicines. Unreliable tests include applied kinesiology, cytotoxicity testing, and skin titration. Differential diagnosis must consider other causes like vasomotor rhinitis.

### Prevention and management

Giving peanut products early in childhood may decrease allergy risk; breastfeeding during at least the first few months may decrease risk of allergic dermatitis. Following 2015/2017 guideline revisions encouraging peanut introduction at 4–6 months, a 2025 analysis found a 43% drop in peanut allergy frequency. Fish oil supplementation during pregnancy is associated with lower risk of food sensitivities; probiotics may help prevent atopic dermatitis.

Management involves avoiding triggers and taking medications: antihistamines, glucocorticoids, epinephrine, mast cell stabilizers, and antileukotriene agents. Anaphylaxis requires epinephrine injection, often via autoinjector. Allergen immunotherapy—exposing people to increasing allergen amounts—is effective for environmental allergies, insect bites, and asthma, but its benefit for food allergies is unclear. Sublingual immunotherapy has lesser evidence support. Enzyme potentiated desensitization (EPD) is experimental and not generally accepted; homeopathic treatments show no effectiveness. Saline nasal irrigation and butterbur have relatively strong evidence compared to other alternative treatments.

### Epidemiology and history

Allergic diseases have increased in the Western world over the past 2–3 decades; increases began in the 1960s and 1970s, though some suggest a steady rise since the 1920s. In the developed world, ~20% are affected by allergic rhinitis, food allergy affects 10% of adults and 8% of children, ~20% have or have had atopic dermatitis, 1–18% have asthma, and anaphylaxis occurs in 0.05–2%. Genetic factors cannot explain the rapid increase, pointing to environmental changes.

Three members of the Roman Julio-Claudian dynasty (Augustus, Claudius, Britannicus) are suspected to have had family atopy. All forms of hypersensitivity were previously classified as allergies until Gell and Coombs's 1963 classification restricted "true allergy" to type I hypersensitivities. The RAST test, invented in 1974, was replaced by ImmunoCAP in 1989; the term RAST is now considered obsolete.

### Medical specialty

Allergology is the medical specialty studying, diagnosing, and treating allergies. In the US, allergists/immunologists complete at least nine years of training: medical school, three years in internal medicine or pediatrics, board exams, and at least two additional fellowship years, followed by ABAI certification. In the UK, allergy is a subspecialty of general medicine or pediatrics; a 2003 Royal College of Physicians report and a 2007 House of Lords subcommittee both concluded allergy services were inadequate to deal with an "allergy epidemic."

## Terms

- **Allergen**: An ordinarily harmless substance that triggers an allergic immune response
- **IgE (immunoglobulin E)**: Antibody class central to allergic reactions; binds allergens and mast cell/basophil receptors
- **Mast cells/basophils**: Immune cells that release histamine and other inflammatory mediators when activated
- **Anaphylaxis**: Severe, potentially fatal systemic allergic reaction affecting multiple organ systems
- **TH2 cells**: T lymphocyte subset driving allergic responses via cytokines like IL-4
- **Hygiene hypothesis**: Theory that reduced microbial exposure leads to overactive TH2 responses and allergy
- **Hapten**: Small molecule (like urushiol) that becomes immunogenic only after binding to proteins
- **Type I hypersensitivity**: Immediate allergic reaction mediated by IgE; "true allergy"
- **Type IV hypersensitivity**: Delayed reaction mediated by T cells, as in allergic contact dermatitis
- **Allergen immunotherapy**: Treatment exposing patients to increasing allergen doses to change immune response

## Debates and open questions

The hygiene hypothesis remains debated: some studies in China and Ethiopia show increased allergy in people infected with intestinal worms, conflicting with the theory. Research on gut flora establishment and allergy is conflicting. Helminthic therapy clinical trials are testing whether parasitic worms can treat allergies; it may be that "parasite" is inappropriate and a previously unsuspected symbiosis is at work. Immunotherapy's benefit for food allergies is unclear and not recommended. The 2025 peanut guideline analysis noted pediatric experts caution that real-world implementation may be inconsistent.
