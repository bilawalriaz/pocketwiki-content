# New religious movement

## Overview

A new religious movement (NRM) is a religious or spiritual group with modern origins that exists on the margins of its society's dominant religious culture. These movements are either novel in origin or distinct from pre-existing denominations within wider religions. NRMs number in the tens of thousands worldwide, ranging from small groups to movements with over a million members. They emerged as a distinct field of academic study in the 1970s, with scholars contextualizing their rise as responses to modernity's processes of secularization, globalization, and individualization. The term itself remains contested, with debates over whether "new" refers to chronological recency or social perception of difference from established religions.

## Body

### Historical Development

The earliest recognized NRMs include the Latter Day Saint movement (1830), Tenrikyo (1838), and Bábism (1844), from which the Baháʼí Faith emerged in 1863. The late 19th century saw the rise of New Thought movements and the first Parliament of the World's Religions (1893), which provided Asian religious teachers their first wide American audience. The 20th century witnessed significant expansion, particularly after World War II, with Japanese new religions flourishing following the Shinto Directive of 1945 that separated state from Shinto religion.

The 1950s and 1960s marked a period of rapid growth coinciding with counterculture movements, producing groups like Scientology (1954), the Unification Church (1954), ISKCON (1966), and the Church of Satan (1966). The Beatles' visit to Maharishi Mahesh Yogi in 1967 brought Transcendental Meditation to public attention. This visibility sparked what scholars term the "cult boom" and "cult controversy," characterized by public anxiety over conversion, allegations of brainwashing, family conflicts, deprogramming, and litigation.

### Beliefs and Practices

NRMs exhibit enormous diversity in beliefs and practices, making generalizations problematic. Some venerate unique scriptures, while others reinterpret existing texts, often claiming to revive forgotten truths. These scriptures frequently incorporate modern scientific knowledge and may claim to unify science and religion. Some NRMs, particularly occult groups, feature prescribed systems of courses and grades for member progression.

Practices vary widely: some promote celibacy as a lifelong commitment or spiritual development stage, while others embrace family life. Violent incidents, though rare, have occurred primarily under charismatic leadership, including the Peoples Temple massacre (1978), the Order of the Solar Temple suicides (1994), and Heaven's Gate suicides (1997).

### Leadership and Membership

NRMs are typically founded by charismatic leaders, whose deaths create critical succession moments. Founders sometimes establish formal succession plans, as Mary Baker Eddy did for Christian Science, or appoint successors, as A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada did with 11 Western Gurus. However, succession often leads to fragmentation or doctrinal shifts.

Membership demographics show NRMs typically consist of first-generation believers with younger average ages than mainstream congregations. In Western countries, they appeal primarily to middle and upper-middle-class white youth, while in the Third World they attract poorer, oppressed populations. Conversion motivations include searches for community and spiritual fulfillment, though the "brainwashing" explanation lacks empirical support.

### Academic Study and Definitions

The academic study of NRMs, known as new religious studies (NRS), draws from anthropology, history, psychology, sociology, religious studies, and theology. Eileen Barker identified five information sources: group self-reports, ex-member accounts, information-collecting organizations, media coverage, and academic research. The field is unified by topic rather than methodology, with sociology initially playing a dominant role.

Defining NRMs remains contentious. J. Gordon Melton defines them by how dominant religious and secular forces treat them—as "unacceptably different." George Chryssides favors a simpler definition: organizations founded within the past 150 years that cannot be easily classified within major religious traditions. The question of when an NRM ceases to be "new" remains unresolved, as today's NRMs become tomorrow's established religions.

### Opposition

NRMs have faced consistent opposition throughout history, including anti-Mormonism, persecution of Jehovah's Witnesses and Baháʼís, and state violence against groups like Falun Gong in China. The Christian countercult movement, emerging in the 1930s, opposes most NRMs due to theological differences and is closely associated with evangelical Christianity. Walter Ralston Martin's "The Kingdom of the Cults" (1965) systematically examined numerous NRMs from a Christian perspective.

The secular anti-cult movement of the 1970s-1980s charged NRMs with psychological abuse and promoted deprogramming, often based on the discredited "brainwashing" concept. Scholars have criticized these groups for uncritically accepting ex-member testimonies and perpetuating moral panics. Eileen Barker notes that the vast majority of groups labeled as cults have not engaged in criminal activities.

## Terms

- **New Religious Movement (NRM)**: A religious or spiritual group with modern origins that exists on the margins of dominant religious culture.
- **Cult controversy**: The period of public anxiety and legal disputes surrounding NRMs in the 1970s-1980s, involving allegations of brainwashing and deprogramming.
- **Brainwashing**: The discredited theory that NRMs use coercive persuasion techniques to force conversion; academic research has found no evidence supporting this claim.
- **Deprogramming**: The practice of forcibly removing individuals from NRMs and attempting to convince them to reject their beliefs; often involved kidnapping.
- **Cybersectarianism**: The use of the Internet by NRMs for information dissemination, recruitment, and conducting online meetings and rituals.
- **Charismatic leadership**: The typical founding and leadership model of NRMs, where a single individual's personal authority drives the movement.
- **Countercultural NRM**: Movements that position themselves in opposition to mainstream society, often embracing alternative values and practices.
- **Denominationalisation**: The process by which NRMs become more structured and similar to established religious institutions over time.

## Debates and Open Questions

Scholars debate the fundamental definition of "new religious movement," with no consensus on whether "new" refers to chronological recency or social perception of difference from established religions. The question of when an NRM ceases to be "new" remains unresolved—as movements like Scientology and ISKCON age, they increasingly appear "old" to scholars.

The "brainwashing" thesis continues to generate controversy despite academic research demonstrating that coercive persuasion techniques do not exist in NRMs. The tension between religious freedom and public safety in cases of alleged abuse remains contentious, particularly regarding state persecution of groups like Falun Gong.

Academic research priorities also generate debate, as scholars tend to focus on controversial NRMs while neglecting less visible movements. Additionally, the interdisciplinary nature of NRS creates methodological tensions, as approaches developed for studying mainstream religions may not adequately address NRM phenomena.
