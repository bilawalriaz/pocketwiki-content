# Republic of Ireland

## Overview
The Republic of Ireland is a sovereign state in Northwestern Europe, covering about five-sixths of the island of Ireland. It is a parliamentary republic with a developed economy, known for its Celtic Tiger growth period, a policy of military neutrality, and a significant cultural influence stemming from its Gaelic heritage and large diaspora. Its modern history is defined by the struggle for independence from the United Kingdom, the subsequent civil war, and a more recent period of economic transformation and peace-building in Northern Ireland.

## Timeline
- **1 Jan 1801** — Ireland becomes part of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland via the Act of Union.
- **24 Apr 1916** — The Easter Rising, an armed insurrection against British rule, begins in Dublin.
- **6 Dec 1922** — The Irish Free State is created as a self-governing Dominion under the Anglo-Irish Treaty.
- **Jun 1922 – May 1923** — The Irish Civil War is fought between pro- and anti-treaty forces.
- **29 Dec 1937** — A new constitution comes into force, naming the state "Éire" or "Ireland".
- **18 Apr 1949** — The Republic of Ireland Act 1948 comes into force, officially declaring the state a republic.
- **1 Jan 1973** — Ireland joins the European Communities (EC), the predecessor of the EU.
- **1995–2007** — The "Celtic Tiger" period of rapid economic growth.
- **1998** — The Good Friday Agreement is approved, establishing power-sharing in Northern Ireland.
- **2008–2014** — The Great Recession and Irish banking crisis lead to an EU-IMF bailout.

## Body

### Name and Statehood
The names "Ireland" and "Éire" derive from the Old Irish *Ériu*. The state created in 1922 was the "Irish Free State". The 1937 Constitution declared the name of the state to be "Éire, or, in the English language, Ireland". The Republic of Ireland Act 1948 declared the state's *description* to be the "Republic of Ireland" to avoid conflict with the Constitution. The state is also informally called "the Republic" or "the South".

### Path to Independence and Civil War
From 1801, Ireland was part of the UK. A Home Rule movement gained prominence in the late 19th century. The 1916 Easter Rising, though a military failure, shifted public opinion. In 1919, elected Sinn Féin MPs established an independent parliament, Dáil Éireann, and declared an Irish Republic. After a War of Independence, the Anglo-Irish Treaty (1921) created the Irish Free State as a Dominion, but partitioned the island, allowing Northern Ireland to opt out. The treaty's ratification led to the Irish Civil War (1922–1923) between pro-treaty forces (who saw it as a step to freedom) and anti-treaty forces (who rejected the oath to the British king and the state's Dominion status).

### Constitution and International Relations
The 1937 Constitution replaced the Free State's, naming the state "Ireland" and defining its territory as the whole island, though its jurisdiction was limited to the 26 counties. Ireland remained neutral during World War II ("The Emergency"). It became a republic in 1949 and joined the UN in 1955. A key policy shift came with joining the EC in 1973, which accelerated economic reform. The state maintained military neutrality, not joining NATO, but has contributed to UN peacekeeping since 1960. The 1998 Good Friday Agreement, which resolved the territorial claim to Northern Ireland in the Constitution, established cross-border cooperation.

### Economy and the Celtic Tiger
Ireland is an open economy heavily reliant on foreign direct investment (FDI), particularly from US multinationals in tech and pharmaceuticals. A low corporation tax rate (12.5%) was central to its transformation. The "Celtic Tiger" period (1995–2007) saw massive growth fueled by borrowing and a property bubble. The 2008 global financial crisis caused a severe recession and banking collapse, requiring an €85 billion EU-IMF bailout. The economy recovered after 2014, but the dominance of multinational activity distorts traditional economic metrics like GDP.

### Demographics and Culture
The population was 5.15 million in 2022. Irish (Gaeilge) is the first official language, but English dominates daily life; Irish is spoken as a community language mainly in Gaeltacht regions. Christianity is predominant, though the Catholic Church's influence has declined sharply. Irish culture is globally recognized through its literature (e.g., James Joyce, W.B. Yeats), music, and sports like Gaelic football and hurling. The Irish diaspora has been a major cultural and economic force.

## Terms
- **Dáil Éireann**: The lower house of the Irish parliament (Oireachtas).
- **Seanad Éireann**: The upper house of the Irish parliament.
- **Taoiseach**: The prime minister and head of government.
- **Tánaiste**: The deputy prime minister.
- **Oireachtas**: The bicameral national parliament of Ireland.
- **Gaeltacht**: An area where the Irish language is spoken as the principal community language.
- **Celtic Tiger**: The period of rapid economic growth in Ireland from the mid-1990s to the late 2000s.
- **Good Friday Agreement**: The 1998 peace deal that largely ended the Troubles in Northern Ireland.
- **Garda Síochána**: The national police service of Ireland.
- **Common Law**: A legal system based on judicial precedent, used in Ireland.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Economic Distortion**: The role of multinational tax strategies in inflating GDP figures, leading to the creation of alternative metrics like modified GNI.
- **Neutrality**: The extent and future of Ireland's military neutrality, especially given its participation in NATO's Partnership for Peace and PESCO.
- **Language Revival**: The effectiveness of state policies in reviving the Irish language beyond Gaeltacht areas and official use.
- **Church-State Relations**: The ongoing secularization of Irish society and the future role of religious patronage in the education system.
