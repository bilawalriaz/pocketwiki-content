# Reproduction

## Overview

Reproduction is the biological process by which new individual organisms—offspring—are produced from their parent or parents. It exists in two fundamental forms: asexual, where an organism creates a genetically similar or identical copy of itself without another organism, and sexual, where genetic material from two parents is combined. The evolution of sexual reproduction is a major puzzle for biologists because it carries a "two-fold cost": only 50% of organisms reproduce, and each passes on only 50% of its genes. Yet sexual reproduction remains widespread, likely because the genetic variation it produces helps populations survive environmental change and disease.

## Body

### Asexual reproduction

Asexual reproduction creates genetically similar or identical copies without genetic contribution from another organism. Bacteria divide via binary fission; viruses take control of host cells to produce more viruses; hydras and yeasts reproduce by budding. Most plants can reproduce asexually, and the ant species *Mycocepurus smithii* is thought to reproduce entirely by asexual means. Some asexual species, like hydra, yeast, and jellyfish, may also reproduce sexually. Other asexual methods include parthenogenesis (embryo or seed development without fertilization, occurring naturally in some lower plants, invertebrates like water fleas and aphids, and rarely vertebrates), fragmentation, and spore formation involving only mitosis.

### Sexual reproduction

Sexual reproduction combines genetic material from two organisms through meiosis, a specialized cell division producing haploid gametes (cells with half the chromosomes of somatic cells). In anisogamous species, the two sexes are male (producing sperm or microspores) and female (producing ova or megaspores). In isogamous species, gametes look alike and cannot be classified as male or female—for example, the green alga *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii* has "plus" and "minus" gametes. Some organisms, like many fungi and the ciliate *Paramecium aurelia*, have more than two "sexes," called mating types.

Sexually reproducing organisms have different sets of genes for each trait (alleles), inheriting one from each parent. It is believed that "the masking of deleterious alleles favors the evolution of a dominant diploid phase in organisms that alternate between haploid and diploid phases." Bryophytes reproduce sexually, but the larger, commonly seen organisms are haploid; the diploid stage is small and short-lived. Since the haploid stage does not benefit from heterosis (the advantage of diploidy), this suggests sexual reproduction offers advantages beyond heterosis, such as genetic recombination allowing expression of a wider range of traits.

**Allogamy** is fertilization through cross-pollination, where a flower's ovum is fertilized by pollen from a different plant, transferred by pollen vectors or abiotic carriers like wind. **Autogamy** (self-fertilization) occurs in hermaphroditic organisms where both gametes come from the same individual, distinguished from geitonogamy (pollen transfer to a different flower on the same plant).

**Mitosis and meiosis** are the two types of cell division. Mitosis occurs in somatic cells, producing twice the original number of cells with the same chromosome count. Meiosis occurs in gametes, producing four times the original number of cells, each with half the chromosomes: a diploid cell duplicates, then undergoes two divisions (tetraploid to diploid to haploid) in two phases, meiosis I and II.

**Gametogenesis** in animals occurs in gonads: sperm via spermatogenesis, eggs via oogenesis. During mammalian gametogenesis, genes for DNA repair proteins show enhanced expression. Male germ cells use homologous recombinational repair and non-homologous end joining to repair DNA damage during meiosis. Oocytes in the primordial follicle are in a non-growing prophase-arrested state but can perform highly efficient homologous recombinational repair of double-strand breaks, maintaining genome integrity and protecting offspring health.

### Same-sex reproduction

Research is investigating same-sex procreation producing offspring with equal genetic contributions from two females or two males. Approaches include female sperm and male eggs. In 2004, Japanese scientists combined two mouse eggs to produce daughter mice. In 2010, American scientists produced viable mouse offspring from two fathers using genetically manipulated stem cells. In 2018, Chinese scientists created 29 female mice from two mothers but could not produce viable offspring from two fathers. Researchers noted little chance of applying these techniques to humans soon. In 2023, Japanese scientists created mouse pups from two fathers that grew to adulthood.

### Reproductive strategies

Species employ different strategies. K-selection involves few offspring with heavy investment in each (e.g., humans, northern gannets). R-selection involves many offspring with little individual investment, where most die young but enough survive (e.g., rabbits producing 10–30 offspring per year, fruit flies up to 900). Some organisms, like honey bees and fruit flies, retain sperm in a process called sperm storage to extend fertility. Polycyclic animals reproduce intermittently throughout life. Semelparous organisms reproduce once and often die shortly after (annual plants, some salmon, spider, bamboo, century plant)—associated with r-strategists. Iteroparous organisms produce offspring in successive cycles (perennial plants)—associated with K-strategists.

### Asexual vs. sexual reproduction

Asexual reproducers grow exponentially but, relying on mutation for variation, share similar vulnerabilities. Sexual reproducers yield fewer offspring but have greater genetic variation, making them less susceptible to disease. Many organisms (aphids, slime molds, sea anemones, some starfish, many plants) can do both: asexual reproduction exploits favorable conditions, while sexual reproduction is triggered by depleted resources or hostile climate. Sexual reproduction mixes the gene pool, allows selective adaptation, enables effective DNA repair during meiosis, and produces durable life stages (seeds, spores, eggs, pupae, cysts) that endure unfavorable conditions.

### Life without reproduction

Abiogenesis is the study of how reproducing organisms arose from non-reproducing elements. Scientists have produced simple viruses from non-living materials, but viruses are often regarded as not alive—just RNA or DNA in a protein capsule with no metabolism. A synthetic genome has been transferred into an existing bacterium, replacing native DNA and artificially producing a new *M. mycoides* organism. Debate exists over whether this cell is truly synthetic, since the genome was nearly a 1:1 copy of a natural one and the recipient was a natural bacterium. The Craig Venter Institute calls it a "synthetic bacterial cell" but clarifies it is "creating new life out of already existing life using synthetic DNA." Venter plans to patent the cells as "human inventions." Researchers suggest building synthetic life teaches us about life by construction, and that "true synthetic biochemical life" is relatively close with current technology.

### Lottery principle

George C. Williams used a lottery analogy to explain sexual reproduction's prevalence despite its energy costs. Asexual reproduction is like buying many tickets with the same number—little genetic variety limits the chance of "winning" (producing surviving offspring). Sexual reproduction buys fewer tickets with greater variety, increasing success chances. The principle is less accepted today because evidence shows asexual reproduction is more prevalent in unstable environments, the opposite of what it predicts.

## Terms

- **Gamete**: Specialized reproductive cell with half the chromosomes of somatic cells, created by meiosis
- **Meiosis**: Specialized cell division producing four haploid cells from one diploid cell
- **Mitosis**: Cell division in somatic cells producing two cells with the same chromosome count
- **Parthenogenesis**: Embryo or seed development without fertilization
- **Anisogamy**: Species with two distinct gamete types (male and female)
- **Isogamy**: Species with gametes similar or identical in form
- **Allogamy**: Cross-fertilization via pollen from a different plant
- **Autogamy**: Self-fertilization within the same individual or flower
- **Heterosis**: The advantage of diploidy, existing only in the diploid life generation
- **K-selection / r-selection**: Strategies of few offspring with high investment versus many offspring with low investment

## Debates and open questions

The evolution of sexual reproduction remains a major puzzle due to its two-fold cost. The lottery principle, once a leading explanation, is now questioned because asexual reproduction appears more prevalent in unstable environments, contradicting its predictions. Whether the synthetic *M. mycoides* cell can be considered truly synthetic is debated, since its genome was nearly identical to a natural one and its recipient cell was naturally occurring. The possibility of same-sex procreation in humans is considered unlikely in the near future by researchers.
