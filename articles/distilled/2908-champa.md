# Champa

## Overview
Champa was a collection of independent Austronesian-speaking polities that controlled the coast of present-day central and southern Vietnam from approximately the 2nd century CE until 1832. As a major maritime trading power connecting the Indian Ocean and East Asia, Champa developed a unique Hindu-Buddhist-Islamic culture, leaving a significant architectural and cultural legacy before its gradual absorption by the Vietnamese state.

## Timeline
- **c. 192 CE** — Khu Liên rebels against Chinese rule, establishing the predecessor state Lâm Ấp (Linyi).
- **4th century CE** — Wars with Funan lead to the adoption of Hinduism (Shaivism) and Sanskrit as a scholarly language.
- **380–413 CE** — Reign of King Bhadravarman, who establishes the Bhadresvara cult at Mỹ Sơn.
- **875 CE** — King Indravarman II founds the northern capital of Indrapura and establishes Mahayana Buddhism as the state religion.
- **982 CE** — A Vietnamese invasion led by Lê Hoàn destroys the northern capital of Indrapura.
- **1145–1220 CE** — Period of frequent warfare and Khmer invasions, including a Cham sack of Angkor in 1177.
- **1283–1285 CE** — Mongol Yuan invasion forces Cham kings into guerrilla resistance.
- **1471 CE** — Vietnamese Emperor Lê Thánh Tông invades, sacking the capital Vijaya and decimating northern Champa.
- **1653 CE** — The Vietnamese annex the principality of Kauthara.
- **1832 CE** — Emperor Minh Mạng annexes the last Cham principality, Panduranga, ending the kingdom.

## Body

### Origins and Formation
Champa evolved from the seafaring Austronesian Sa Huỳnh culture. The first Cham polity, known to the Chinese as Lâm Ấp (Linyi), was founded in 192 CE following a rebellion against Chinese rule. In the 4th century, wars with the neighboring Khmer kingdom of Funan introduced Indian cultural influences, including Hinduism (specifically Shaivism) and Sanskrit, which became central to Cham elite culture. The earliest acknowledged king in inscriptions is Bhadravarman (r. 380–413), who established the worship of a god-king linga at the temple complex of Mỹ Sơn.

### Golden Age and Maritime Power
Champa reached its apogee in the 9th and 10th centuries. A new dynasty moved the capital north to Indrapura in 875, making Mahayana Buddhism the state religion and initiating a "Golden Age" of art and culture. The Cham were skilled sailors who dominated maritime trade in the South China Sea, exporting goods like eaglewood and engaging in both commerce and piracy. Their wealth was concentrated in urbanized port-cities like Vijaya and Indrapura, which were connected by trade networks stretching from China to the Indian Ocean.

### Decline and Vietnamese Conquest
Champa's decline began under sustained pressure from Đại Việt (northern Vietnam). A pivotal invasion in 982 destroyed the northern capital. Although Champa experienced a resurgence under King Che Bong Nga in the late 14th century, a devastating Vietnamese invasion in 1471 sacked the capital Vijaya, killed or captured over 120,000 people, and effectively ended Champa as a major power. The kingdom was reduced to the southern principalities of Kauthara and Panduranga.

### Final Annexation and Legacy
The last principality, Panduranga, survived until 1832 when Emperor Minh Mạng annexed it, implementing harsh assimilation policies that suppressed Cham language and culture. The modern Chams are the major remnants of the kingdom. Their cultural legacy includes significant Hindu temple ruins like Mỹ Sơn and Po Nagar (both World Heritage Sites), a unique script, and a syncretic religious landscape. Today, Chams are divided between the Hindu Balamon group and the Muslim Bani group, who practice an indigenized form of Islam.

## Terms
- **Austronesian:** A large language family to which the Cham languages belong, originating from seafaring peoples.
- **Sa Huỳnh:** An archaeological culture in central Vietnam (c. 1000 BC – AD 200), considered the predecessor of Champa.
- **Lâm Ấp (Linyi):** The Chinese name for the early Cham polity that preceded the classical Champa kingdom.
- **Viṣaya:** An internal administrative district within the Cham kingdom.
- **Mandala:** A model of political organization in pre-modern Southeast Asia, describing a network of semi-autonomous centers under a dominant ruler, rather than a centralized state.
- **Balamon Chams:** The Cham ethnic group that adheres to an indigenized form of Hinduism.
- **Bani Chams:** The Cham ethnic group that adheres to an indigenized form of Islam, distinct from mainstream Sunni Islam.
- **Linga:** A stone pillar representing the Hindu god Shiva, a central object of worship in Cham Shaivism.
- **Eaglewood (Aloeswood):** A highly valuable aromatic wood, the most important export product of Champa.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Political Structure:** Scholars debate whether Champa was a unified kingdom or a loose federation of independent states. Revisionist historians argue it was a "multi-centric" confederation for most of its history, with periods of stronger central control under a "king of kings."
- **Etymology:** The origin of the name "Champa" is disputed. One theory derives it from the Sanskrit *campaka* (a flowering tree), possibly via contact with the Indian city of Champapuri. Another view, proposed by Michael Vickery, challenges this Indic origin, noting the Cham people's own name for themselves is *Čaṃ*.
- **Historiography:** The historical record is uneven across regions and periods, leading to debates about whether shifts in the richness of the record for a particular city (e.g., Indrapura in the 10th century) indicate a moving capital or simply the survival of more inscriptions from that area.
