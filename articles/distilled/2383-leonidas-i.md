# Leonidas I

## Overview
Leonidas I (c. 540–480 BC) was the king of Sparta who led a small allied Greek force in a legendary last stand against the invading Persian army at the Battle of Thermopylae. His death in this battle cemented his legacy as a symbol of heroic sacrifice and military valor, and the stand at Thermopylae became a pivotal moment in the successful Greek defense against the Second Persian invasion.

## Timeline
- **c. 540 BC** — Leonidas is born in Sparta.
- **c. 489 BC** — Leonidas ascends to the Agiad throne, succeeding his half-brother Cleomenes I.
- **492/491 BC** — Sparta vehemently rejects Persian demands for submission.
- **481 BC** — Leonidas is chosen to lead the confederated Greek forces against the Persian invasion.
- **August 480 BC** — Leonidas leads 300 Spartans and allied troops to defend the pass of Thermopylae.
- **11 August 480 BC** — Leonidas and his rearguard are killed at Thermopylae after being outflanked.

## Body

### Royal Lineage and Accession
Leonidas was the son of King Anaxandridas II and a member of the Agiad dynasty, which claimed descent from Heracles. His path to the throne was complex; his father's first wife was initially barren, leading to the birth of his half-brother Cleomenes from a second wife. Leonidas was the second son of the first wife. He married Cleomenes's daughter, Gorgo, and became heir after Cleomenes was deposed and exiled. Leonidas ascended to the throne around 489 BC.

### Selection for Command
In 481 BC, Leonidas was chosen to lead the Greek coalition against the Persian invasion led by Xerxes I. This selection was a tribute to his personal capability as a military leader, not just Sparta's reputation. This is evidenced by the fact that after his death, the coalition later preferred Athenian leadership over that of Leonidas's Spartan successors.

### The Battle of Thermopylae
In August 480 BC, Leonidas marched to the narrow pass of Thermopylae with a small force of 300 Spartans, 900 helots (Spartan serfs), and other allies, eventually forming a defensive army of about 7,000 Greeks. They faced a vastly larger Persian army, which Herodotus claimed numbered over two million, though modern estimates range from 70,000 to 300,000. The Greeks repulsed Persian attacks for two days. On the third day (11 August), a traitor named Ephialtes revealed a mountain path that allowed the Persians to outflank the position. Leonidas then dismissed most of the allied troops to preserve them for future battles. He remained with his 300 Spartans, 900 helots, 400 Thebans, and 700 Thespians, who refused to leave. The rearguard was attacked from both sides and annihilated, except for the Thebans who surrendered. Leonidas was killed, and his body was mutilated by Xerxes's order.

### Legacy and Commemoration
Leonidas entered myth as a hero. A cult honoring him, the Leonideia, was celebrated annually in Sparta until the 2nd century AD. His legacy endures in modern culture through statues at Thermopylae and Sparta, and he is a central figure in numerous works of fiction, including the film *300*.

## Terms
- **Agiad dynasty:** One of the two royal houses of Sparta, from which Leonidas descended.
- **Hoplite:** A heavily armed foot soldier of ancient Greece.
- **Helot:** A member of a class of serfs in ancient Sparta, bound to the land and serving the Spartans.
- **Perioikoi:** Free non-citizen inhabitants of Sparta who were artisans and traders.
- **Ephors:** Five annually elected administrators who held significant power in the Spartan constitution.
- **Agoge:** The rigorous education and training regimen for Spartan male citizens.
- **Homoios:** A full Spartan citizen, literally meaning "peer" or "equal."
- **Oracle at Delphi:** A major religious sanctuary where a priestess delivered prophecies from the god Apollo.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Reason for the small Spartan force:** Herodotus stated the 300 Spartans were an advance guard, with the main army delayed by the religious festival of the Carneia and the Olympic Games. Many modern commentators are dissatisfied with this explanation and suggest internal dissent or other political reasons.
- **Size of the Persian army:** Herodotus's figure of over two million men is considered a gross exaggeration by modern scholars, who propose estimates ranging from 70,000 to 300,000.
- **Leonidas's motivation for the last stand:** Herodotus offered theories that Leonidas dismissed the allies to save them for future battles or to secure glory for the Spartans, but the precise reasoning remains a subject of interpretation.
