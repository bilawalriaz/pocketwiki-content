# Umayyad Caliphate

## Overview
The Umayyad Caliphate (661–750) was the second caliphate in Islamic history, succeeding the Rashidun Caliphate. Ruled by the Umayyad dynasty from its capital in Damascus, it presided over a vast, multiethnic empire that expanded from Spain to Central Asia. It is significant for establishing hereditary rule, standardizing Islamic administration and coinage, and initiating the Arabization of the state, though its favoritism toward Arab elites and treatment of non-Arab converts contributed to its eventual overthrow by the Abbasids.

## Timeline
- **661** — Mu'awiya I becomes caliph after the First Fitna, establishing the Umayyad dynasty and moving the capital to Damascus.
- **680** — Battle of Karbala; Husayn ibn Ali is killed, deepening the Sunni-Shia split.
- **684** — Battle of Marj Rahit; Marwan I defeats rivals, founding the Marwanid line and restoring Umayyad rule.
- **692** — End of the Second Fitna; Caliph Abd al-Malik reunifies the caliphate after defeating Ibn al-Zubayr.
- **696-697** — Caliph Abd al-Malik introduces purely Arabic, aniconic coinage (dinar and dirham).
- **705-715** — Caliph al-Walid I's reign sees the empire's greatest territorial expansion, including into Hispania and Transoxiana.
- **711** — Tariq ibn Ziyad invades the Visigothic Kingdom of Hispania.
- **717-718** — Failed Umayyad siege of Constantinople under Caliph Sulayman.
- **732** — Frankish victory at the Battle of Tours halts major Umayyad expansion into Western Europe.
- **747** — Abbasid Revolution begins in Khurasan under Abu Muslim.
- **750** — Battle of the Zab; Abbasid forces defeat the Umayyads, ending the dynasty in the east.

## Body

### Origins and Rise to Power
The Umayyads were a prominent clan of the Quraysh tribe in Mecca. After initially opposing Muhammad, they converted to Islam and gained political influence. The third Rashidun caliph, Uthman ibn Affan, was an Umayyad who favored his kinsmen, provoking resentment that led to his assassination in 656. The ensuing civil war, the First Fitna, ended with Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan, the long-time governor of Syria, emerging as caliph in 661. He established a hereditary dynasty with Damascus as its capital, leveraging Syria's stable tribal base and administrative experience from the former Byzantine Empire.

### Consolidation and the Second Fitna
After Mu'awiya's death, his son Yazid I faced immediate challenges. The killing of Ali's son Husayn at Karbala (680) created lasting opposition. A major revolt by Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr in Mecca led to the Second Fitna. Umayyad authority nearly collapsed after 683, but Marwan ibn al-Hakam rallied loyalist Syrian tribes, defeating rivals at the Battle of Marj Rahit (684) and founding the Marwanid line. His son, Abd al-Malik, eventually reunified the caliphate by defeating Ibn al-Zubayr in 692.

### Administrative and Cultural Centralization
To consolidate power after civil war, Abd al-Malik and his successors implemented centralizing reforms. Arabic replaced Greek and Persian as the administrative language. A new, purely Arabic coinage was minted. The professional Syrian army became the empire's dominant military force, garrisoned across provinces like Iraq to suppress revolts. Architectural projects, such as the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem and the Great Mosque of Damascus, served as symbols of Islamic and Umayyad authority.

### Expansion and Social Structure
The Umayyads continued the early Muslim conquests, reaching their greatest extent under al-Walid I (r. 705–715), with victories in North Africa, Hispania, and Transoxiana. Society was hierarchical, with Muslim Arabs at the top. Non-Arab Muslim converts (*mawali*) were often treated as second-class citizens and subjected to the *jizya* poll tax, a major source of discontent. Non-Muslims (*dhimmis*) were allowed to practice their faith in exchange for paying the *jizya*. This social tension, alongside political and military setbacks, fueled opposition.

### Decline and Fall
The later Umayyad period was marked by internal strife (the Third Fitna), military defeats (e.g., at Tours in 732), and the costly Berber Revolt in North Africa. The Abbasid Revolution, which began in Khurasan in 747, exploited widespread discontent, especially among non-Arab Muslims. The Abbasids defeated the last Umayyad caliph, Marwan II, at the Battle of the Zab in 750 and massacred most of the Umayyad family. One survivor, Abd al-Rahman I, fled to al-Andalus and established an independent Umayyad Emirate in Córdoba.

## Terms
*   **Caliphate (Khilafa):** The political-religious state succeeding Muhammad, led by a caliph (successor).
*   **First Fitna:** The first major civil war in the Muslim community (656–661), following the assassination of Caliph Uthman.
*   **Second Fitna:** A period of widespread civil war and rebellion (680–692) challenging Umayyad authority.
*   **Mawali (singular: mawla):** Non-Arab clients or converts to Islam who were often attached to Arab tribes.
*   **Dhimmi:** A protected non-Muslim subject (e.g., Christian, Jew) in an Islamic state, granted religious autonomy in exchange for paying the *jizya* tax.
*   **Jizya:** A poll tax levied on non-Muslim adult males in exchange for protection and exemption from military service.
*   **Zakat:** An obligatory alms tax paid by Muslims, considered a religious duty.
*   **Kharaj:** A land tax, initially on non-Muslims, later applied to all cultivators regardless of religion.
*   **Jund (plural: Ajnad):** A military-administrative province in the Umayyad system, based around a fortified garrison city.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Nature of Rule:** Historians debate whether the Umayyads transformed the caliphate into a secular kingship (*mulk*) or maintained its religious character. They used the title *khalifat Allah* (deputy of God) rather than the traditional *khalifat rasul Allah* (successor of the Messenger of God).
*   **Sources and Bias:** Much of the historical record was written by scholars in the Abbasid period, who often portrayed the Umayyads negatively. This makes objective assessment challenging.
*   **Causes of Collapse:** While social inequality and the *mawali* question were key factors, the relative importance of tribal feuds (Qays vs. Yaman), military overextension, and ideological opposition (from Shia and Kharijite groups) in causing the Abbasid Revolution is debated.
