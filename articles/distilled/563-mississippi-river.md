# Mississippi River

## Overview
The Mississippi River is the primary river of the largest drainage basin in the United States and the second-longest river in the country (2,340 mi / 3,766 km from Lake Itasca). Its watershed drains 32 U.S. states and two Canadian provinces, covering nearly 40% of the continental U.S. landmass. The river has served as a vital transportation artery, a geopolitical boundary (defining borders for New Spain, New France, and the early U.S.), and a catalyst for westward expansion during the 19th century. Extensive 20th-century engineering—levees, locks, dams, and the Old River Control Structure—has stabilized its course for navigation and flood control, but has also reduced sediment transport by over 60% and created environmental challenges, including a Gulf of Mexico dead zone driven by agricultural runoff.

## Body

### Name and Significance
The name "Mississippi" derives from the French *Misi zipi*, rendering the Anishinaabe (Ojibwe) *Misi-ziibi* ("Great River"). The Ojibwe named specific reaches differently: *Omashkoozo-ziibi* (Elk River) at the outlet of Lake Itasca, *Gichi-ziibi* (Big River) below Leech Lake, and *Misi-ziibi* only after the Crow Wing River confluence. The Cheyenne called it *Máʼxe-éʼometaaʼe* (Big Greasy River). In the 18th century, the Treaty of Paris (1763) established the river as the primary border between British and Spanish empires; the 1783 treaty guaranteed its free navigation. After the Louisiana Purchase (1803), it became the symbolic dividing line between the eastern and western United States, commemorated by the Gateway Arch in St. Louis and used by the FCC as the dividing line for broadcast call signs (W east, K west).

### Divisions and Hydrology
The river is divided into three sections:
*   **Upper Mississippi** (Headwaters to Missouri River confluence at St. Louis): 493 mi of headwaters to St. Anthony Falls, then a 664-mi navigable channel formed by 29 locks/dams (built 1930s) maintaining a 9-ft depth. Flow is moderated by 43 dams total (14 above Minneapolis for power/recreation). Major tributaries: Minnesota, St. Croix, Chippewa, Wisconsin, Rock, Illinois, Des Moines. Topography shifts from entrenched bedrock bluffs (north) to multi-thread floodplain (south).
*   **Middle Mississippi** (Missouri confluence to Ohio confluence at Cairo, IL): 190 mi, relatively free-flowing, falling 220 ft (1.2 ft/mi). Few major tributaries (Meramec, Kaskaskia).
*   **Lower Mississippi** (Ohio confluence to Gulf): ~1,000 mi. At Cairo, the Ohio contributes ~281,500 cu ft/s vs. the Mississippi's ~208,200 cu ft/s, making the Ohio the volumetric main stem. Major tributaries: White, Arkansas, Big Black, Yazoo. At the Old River Control Structure (LA), 30% of combined Mississippi/Red River flow is diverted down the Atchafalaya River (a distributary) to the Gulf, shortening the route past Baton Rouge and New Orleans.

The watershed is the world's fourth-largest (1,245,000 mi²), reaching Mount Elbert (14,440 ft) in the Rockies. Average discharge is 200,000–700,000 cu ft/s (14th globally, 8% of Amazon). Retention time from Itasca to Gulf is ~90 days (avg. 26 mi/day). Pre-1900 sediment load was ~440 million short tons/yr; post-engineering (dams, cutoffs, soil conservation) it dropped to ~160 million short tons/yr. A salt wedge from the Gulf intrudes up to 64 mi upstream during droughts (2022), requiring underwater sills (1988, 1999, 2012, 2022) to protect drinking water. Freshwater plumes remain detectable via satellite (MODIS) around Florida into the Gulf Stream.

### Course Changes and Geology
The Mississippi Embayment formed as North America passed over the Bermuda hotspot (~95 Ma), uplifting the Appalachians, then subsiding 2.6 km as the plate moved away, creating the Reelfoot Rift (~80 Ma) flooded by the Gulf. The Laurentide Ice Sheet shaped the modern basin: its retreat deposited rich sediment, and glacial meltwater carved oversized valleys (Minnesota, James, Milk Rivers) later abandoned as drainage shifted to Hudson Bay. The Illinoian Stage (300–132 ka) blocked the river near Rock Island, IL, forcing it west to its current channel (followed today by the Hennepin Canal and Illinois River).

The lower river undergoes **avulsion (delta switching)** roughly every 1,000 years: silt clogs the channel, raising the bed until the river breaks to a steeper path. Abandoned channels become bayous. Over 5,000 years, this advanced the coastline 15–50 mi. The active **Birdfoot (Balize) Delta** dates from ~1000 AD. Historic avulsions include the 1876 shift near Reverie, TN (leaving a Tennessee enclave in Arkansas) and the 1881 capture of the lower Kaskaskia River, isolating Kaskaskia, IL (former state capital) as a Missouri-accessible enclave.

The **New Madrid Seismic Zone** (failed rift/aulacogen) produced four M~8 quakes in 1811–12, felt across the Midwest, creating Reelfoot Lake. Future course changes threaten capture by the Atchafalaya (steeper gradient) or Lake Pontchartrain (shorter path), prevented only by the Old River Control Structure, Morganza Spillway, and Bonnet Carré Spillway.

### Navigation and Flood Control
The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (est. 1802) maintains navigation. Early work (1829) targeted the Des Moines and Rock Island Rapids. The 4.5-ft channel project (1878) used wing dams and dredging; the 6-ft project (1907) was abandoned for the 9-ft project (Rivers and Harbors Act 1930), resulting in 23 new locks/dams in the 1930s. Lock and Dam No. 19 (Keokuk, 1913) was the first below St. Anthony Falls, built for hydroelectric power. Chain of Rocks Lock (1953) bypasses St. Louis rock ledges.

The **1927 Great Flood** (145 breaches, 27,000 mi² inundated to 30 ft) disproved the "levees-only" theory (that velocity would scour a deeper channel). Post-1927, the Corps adopted spillways, floodways, and intentional meander cutoffs. Key structures: Birds Point–New Madrid Floodway (MO), Old River Control Structure & Morganza Spillway (LA, divert to Atchafalaya), Bonnet Carré Spillway (LA, divert to Lake Pontchartrain). The Old River Control Structure (1963) fixes the Atchafalaya diversion at 30%; it nearly failed in 1973, prompting $300M in auxiliary works (1986). Lower Mississippi navigation depth is 45 ft (container ships to New Orleans); a 50-ft study (New Panamax) is underway.

### History: Native Americans to Civil War
The basin was an independent center of plant domestication (sunflower, goosefoot, marsh elder, squash by 4th millennium BC). The Woodland period (~1000 BC) brought settled life, pottery, weaving. The Hopewell interaction sphere (200–500 AD) linked Gulf to Great Lakes via waterways. Mississippian culture (~800 AD) developed intensive maize agriculture (Three Sisters), enabling stratified chiefdoms and urban centers like **Cahokia** (peak 8,000–40,000, larger than contemporary London), occupied 600–1400 AD. By European contact, Cahokia had dispersed.

European exploration: Pineda (1519), de Soto (1541, *Río del Espíritu Santo*), Jolliet/Marquette (1673, guided by Sioux *Ne Tongo*), La Salle (1682, claimed for France). New Orleans founded 1718. First levees (1727) used enslaved African labor. Post-Seven Years' War (1763), river became British/Spanish border. U.S. secured control via Louisiana Purchase (1803), Adams–Onís Treaty (1819), and repulse of British at New Orleans (1815).

**Steamboat era (1830–1870)**: *New Orleans* (1811) first full Lower Mississippi run; Upper Mississippi difficult until 1840s (two trips/year). Corps study (1851) produced seminal "Report upon the Physics and Hydraulics" (1861). Anchor Line (1859–98) ran luxury St. Louis–New Orleans packets. Railroads eclipsed steamboats by early 1900s.

**Civil War**: River was central to the Anaconda Plan. Union captured Island No. 10, Memphis (1862), New Orleans (1862 upriver), and Vicksburg/Port Hudson (1863), splitting the Confederacy. Vicksburg fell July 4, 1863, a pivotal Union victory.

### 20th–21st Centuries and Ecology
"Big Freeze" 1918–19 blocked coal traffic. 1927 Flood reshaped policy. 1930: Fred Newton first swam length (176 days). 1962–63: 3.5M gal soybean oil spill (St. Paul to Lake Pepin) spurred pollution control. 1976: *MV George Prince* ferry disaster (78 dead). 1988 drought exposed 19th-c. wrecks at West Memphis, AR. 1993 Flood hit Upper Mississippi. 1997: Two reaches designated American Heritage Rivers. 2015: "America's Rivershed Initiative" gave basin D+ (aging infrastructure, environmental problems). 2002: Martin Strel swam length (68 days).

**Ecology**: Basin hosts ~375 fish species (highest in temperate N. Hemisphere except Yangtze), including relicts (paddlefish, sturgeon, gar, bowfin). Upper Mississippi alone: ~120 species (walleye, bass, catfish, shovelnose sturgeon). 40% of US migratory birds (60% of N. American species, 326 total) use the corridor/flyway. Reptiles: alligators, turtles, amphibians, crayfish. **Invasive species**: Asian carp (silver carp jumping, outcompeting natives; approaching Great Lakes), zebra mussels, Eurasian watermilfoil (MN designates infested waters). Gulf dead zone driven by agricultural nutrient runoff.

### Recreation and Parks
Water skiing invented at Lake Pepin (MN/WI) by Ralph Samuelson (1922; jump 1925; 80 mph behind Curtiss flying boat). Seven National Park Service sites: Mississippi National River and Recreation Area (dedicated to the river), Effigy Mounds NM, Gateway Arch NP, Vicksburg NMP, Natchez NHP, New Orleans Jazz NHP, Jean Lafitte NHP&P.

## Terms
- ****Avulsion (delta switching)**** — Natural process where a river abandons its channel for a steeper, more direct route to the sea, typically every ~1,000 years on the lower Mississippi.
- ****Old River Control Structure**** — Engineering complex (completed 1963) in Louisiana that regulates flow distribution, diverting 30% of Mississippi/Red River flow down the Atchafalaya River to prevent the Mississippi from switching its main channel.
- ****Mississippi Embayment**** — A physiographic feature formed by subsidence of the crust (2.6 km) after the North American plate moved off the Bermuda hotspot (~80 Ma), creating a trough filled with sediment.
- ****Levees-only policy**** — Pre-1927 Corps strategy of closing side channels to increase main-channel velocity, theorized to scour a deeper bed; disproven by the 1927 Great Flood.
- ****Birdfoot (Balize) Delta**** — The currently active delta lobe of the Mississippi, shaped like a bird's foot, named for La Balize (first French settlement at the mouth); active since ~1000 AD.
- ****Salt wedge**** — Dense Gulf saltwater intruding along the river bottom near the mouth; during droughts it migrates upstream (64 mi in 2022), threatening freshwater intakes.
- ****Mississippian culture**** — Maize-based agricultural society (~800 AD onward) that built complex chiefdoms and urban centers (e.g., Cahokia) across the basin.
- ****Anaconda Plan**** — Union Civil War strategy to blockade Southern coasts and control the Mississippi River, splitting the Confederacy.
- ****Hydrological transport models**** — Computer simulations applied by the Corps since the 1970s to analyze flood flow and water quality.
- ****New Panamax**** — Ship class too large for original Panama Canal locks; requires 50-ft channel depth, motivating a feasibility study for the Lower Mississippi.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Atchafalaya capture**: Whether the Old River Control Structure can indefinitely prevent the Mississippi from switching its main channel to the steeper Atchafalaya course during a catastrophic flood ("The big one lies out there somewhere"—Fred Smith, Corps geologist). The 1973 near-failure and 2011 stress test remain key reference points.
*   **Lake Pontchartrain diversion**: Whether the Bonnet Carré Spillway and low natural levee (12–20 ft) can prevent a secondary avulsion into Lake Pontchartrain, which would shorten the route to the Gulf but with lesser consequences than Atchafalaya capture.
*   **Sediment deficit vs. coastal restoration**: Engineering (dams, cutoffs, revetments) has cut sediment load by ~64% (440 → 160 Mt/yr), starving Louisiana wetlands. Debates center on large-scale sediment diversions (reconnecting river to marshes) vs. navigation/flood-protection priorities.
*   **Nutrient pollution and Gulf dead zone**: Agricultural runoff (nitrogen/phosphorus) drives seasonal hypoxia. Basin-wide mitigation (voluntary state strategies, EPA task force) has not met reduction targets; the 2015 "D+" grade highlights aging infrastructure and unresolved environmental problems.
*   **Invasive species containment**: Asian carp (silver, bighead) threaten Great Lakes via Chicago Area Waterway System; Upper St. Anthony Falls Lock closed in 2015 as a barrier, but spread continues elsewhere. Zebra mussels and Eurasian watermilfoil already established in upper reaches.
*   **Climate change and flood frequency**: Urban sprawl and changing precipitation patterns may increase flood risk and frequency, challenging 20th-century infrastructure designed for historical hydrology.
