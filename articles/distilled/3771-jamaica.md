# Jamaica

## Overview
Jamaica is an island country in the Caribbean, the third-largest island in the Greater Antilles. It is a parliamentary constitutional monarchy and a member of the Commonwealth of Nations. The nation's history is marked by indigenous settlement, Spanish colonization, British conquest, a plantation economy reliant on enslaved Africans, and eventual independence in 1962. Its global cultural influence, particularly in music and sports, belies its small size.

## Timeline
- **c. 800 CE** — The Taíno people, likely from South America, settle the island.
- **1494** — Christopher Columbus claims Jamaica for Spain.
- **1655** — England conquers the island from Spain, renaming it Jamaica.
- **1660** — The Maroon community switches allegiance to support the English.
- **1739-40** — Treaties end the First Maroon War, granting autonomy to some Maroon communities.
- **1831** — The Baptist War, a major slave rebellion led by Samuel Sharpe, breaks out.
- **1838** — Full emancipation of all slaves in the British Empire is declared.
- **1865** — The Morant Bay rebellion leads to Jamaica becoming a Crown Colony.
- **1938** — Labour unrest culminates in a strike that turns into a riot, prompting political reform.
- **1944** — A new constitution establishes universal adult suffrage.
- **1962** — Jamaica gains independence from the United Kingdom.
- **2025** — The Jamaica Labour Party wins a third consecutive general election under Andrew Holness.

## Body

### Prehistory and Indigenous Peoples
There is no evidence of human presence until about 500 CE. The "Redware people" arrived circa 600 CE, followed by the agrarian Taíno circa 800 CE. At their height, the Taíno may have numbered 60,000 in around 200 villages. They were still present when the English took control in 1655, with some later merging with African Maroon communities.

### Colonial Rule (1509–1838)
Spain established its first settlement in 1509. The indigenous Taíno population declined sharply due to disease and enslavement, leading the Spanish to import enslaved Africans. After England conquered the island in 1655, the plantation economy boomed, making Jamaica a leading sugar exporter. This economy was entirely dependent on enslaved African labor. Maroon communities, formed by escaped slaves, maintained their freedom in the interior, fighting two major wars against the British. Full emancipation was declared in 1838.

### Post-Emancipation and Independence
Following emancipation, many freedmen resisted plantation work, leading the British to import indentured laborers from India (from 1845) and China (from 1854). Political unrest, exemplified by the 1865 Morant Bay rebellion, led to reforms and Jamaica becoming a Crown Colony. The 20th century saw the rise of nationalist movements, the founding of the Rastafari religion, and labour unrest in the 1930s. A new constitution with universal suffrage was granted in 1944. After a brief period in the Federation of the West Indies, Jamaica achieved full independence on 6 August 1962.

### Post-Independence Politics and Economy
The first decade of independence saw strong economic growth. Political rivalry between the Jamaica Labour Party (JLP) and the People's National Party (PNP) intensified, with periods of significant political violence. The economy faced challenges in the 1970s and 1980s, including debt and the decline of the bauxite industry. Since the 1990s, governments have pursued economic liberalization. Tourism has become the largest foreign exchange earner, averaging 4.3 million visitors annually. The JLP, led by Andrew Holness, has won the last three general elections (2016, 2020, 2025).

### Geography and Environment
Jamaica is a mountainous island with a tropical climate. Its highest point is Blue Mountain Peak at 2,256 m. The island has diverse ecosystems, including moist and dry forests, and significant biodiversity with many endemic species. It is vulnerable to hurricanes, with Hurricane Melissa in 2025 being the strongest landfalling storm in its history. Coral reef ecosystems are vital for tourism but face threats from pollution and development.

### Demographics and Culture
Most Jamaicans are of Sub-Saharan African ancestry, with significant minorities of European, East Asian, Indian, and mixed-race heritage. English is the official language, but Jamaican Patois, an English-based creole, is widely spoken. Christianity is the largest religion, with Rastafari, which originated in Jamaica in the 1930s, having a notable global influence. Jamaica is the birthplace of reggae, ska, dub, and dancehall music, and is internationally prominent in sports, especially sprinting and cricket.

## Terms
- **Maroon**: Communities of escaped African slaves and their descendants who established free settlements in Jamaica's interior.
- **Indentured labourer**: A person working under contract for a set period to pay off a debt or passage, used by the British after emancipation to replace slave labor.
- **Crown Colony**: A British colony governed directly by the Crown via an appointed governor, as Jamaica became in 1866.
- **Universal adult suffrage**: The right of all adult citizens to vote, established in Jamaica in 1944.
- **Jamaican Patois**: An English-based creole language spoken by the majority of the population.
- **Rastafari**: A religion founded in Jamaica in the 1930s that combines Christianity with Afrocentric theology, revering Haile Selassie.
- **Bauxite**: The primary ore of aluminium, a major mineral export for Jamaica.
- **Commonwealth realm**: An independent country that recognizes the British monarch as its head of state.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Monarchy vs. Republic**: Both major political parties have committed to transitioning Jamaica to a republic, but the process and timeline remain under discussion.
- **Legacy of Independence**: A 2011 survey indicated that about 60% of Jamaicans believed the country might have been better off remaining a British colony, citing social and fiscal mismanagement as problems.
- **Judicial Final Appeal**: There is an ongoing proposal to replace the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London with the Caribbean Court of Justice as the final court of appeal, as part of a push toward decolonization.
