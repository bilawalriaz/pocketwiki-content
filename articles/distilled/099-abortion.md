# Abortion

## Overview

Abortion is the termination of a pregnancy by removal or expulsion of an embryo or fetus. The unmodified word generally refers to induced abortion (deliberate actions to end a pregnancy), while spontaneous abortion, or "miscarriage," occurs without intervention in roughly 30–40% of all pregnancies. When done legally in industrialized societies, induced abortion is one of the safest procedures in medicine; unsafe abortions cause between 22,000 and 44,000 deaths and 6.9 million hospital admissions annually, responsible for 5–13% of maternal deaths, especially in low-income countries. Around 73 million abortions are performed yearly worldwide, with about 45% done unsafely, and the topic remains a major focus of moral, religious, ethical, and legal debate.

## Body

### Types

An induced abortion is a medical procedure to end a pregnancy, characterized as either therapeutic (performed for medical reasons such as saving the woman's life, preventing harm to physical or mental health, or reducing fetuses in multiple pregnancy) or elective (performed at the woman's request for non-medical reasons). About one in five pregnancies worldwide ends with induced abortion, and most result from unintended pregnancies.

Miscarriage, or spontaneous abortion, is the unintentional expulsion of an embryo or fetus before the 24th week of gestation. Studies show 40–60% of embryos do not progress to birth, and 15–30% of known pregnancies end in clinically apparent miscarriage, with 80% occurring in the first trimester. The most common cause is chromosomal abnormalities (at least 50% of early losses); other causes include vascular disease, diabetes, hormonal problems, infection, and uterine abnormalities. Advancing maternal age and prior miscarriages are the leading risk factors.

### Methods

Medical abortions use abortifacient pharmaceuticals. The most common early first-trimester regimens use mifepristone combined with misoprostol (up to 70 days' gestation), methotrexate with a prostaglandin analog (up to 7 weeks), or a prostaglandin analog alone. Mifepristone–misoprostol combinations work faster and are more effective than methotrexate combinations, and are 98% effective up to 9 weeks, decreasing to 94% at 9–10 weeks. If medical abortion fails, surgical abortion is required. Early medical abortions account for the majority of abortions before 9 weeks in Britain, France, Switzerland, the US, and Nordic countries.

Surgical methods include suction-aspiration or vacuum aspiration (most common up to 15 weeks), dilation and curettage (D&C), and dilation and evacuation (D&E, used after 12–16 weeks). Intact dilation and extraction (D&X) is a variant used after 18–20 weeks. Hysterotomy (similar to caesarean section) and gravid hysterectomy (removal of the whole uterus) are associated with much higher maternal morbidity and mortality. First-trimester procedures can use local anesthesia; second-trimester methods may require deep sedation or general anesthesia.

Labor induction abortion, sometimes called "induced miscarriage," may be performed from 13 weeks to the third trimester, particularly in places lacking D&E skills. It is very uncommon in the US but accounts for over 80% of second-trimester abortions in Sweden and nearby countries. After 18 weeks, it may be complicated by brief fetal survival, which can be legally characterized as live birth.

Historically, herbs such as tansy, pennyroyal, black cohosh, and the now-extinct silphium were used as abortifacients; such use can cause serious or lethal side effects and is not recommended by physicians. Other methods include abdominal trauma and, in Southeast Asia, forceful abdominal massage. Unsafe self-induced methods include misoprostol misuse and inserting implements like knitting needles or clothes hangers into the uterus.

### Safety

The health risks of abortion depend principally on how and under what conditions it is performed. Legal abortions in the developed world are among the safest procedures in medicine. In the US, the risk of maternal mortality is 14 times lower after induced abortion than after childbirth; the CDC estimated 17.2 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births versus 0.43 per 100,000 abortion procedures. In the UK, guidelines state that "abortion is generally safer than continuing a pregnancy to term." Abortion-related mortality increases with gestational age but remains lower than childbirth.

There is little difference in safety and efficacy between medical abortion (mifepristone–misoprostol) and surgical abortion (vacuum aspiration) in early first trimester. Vacuum aspiration is the safest surgical method; rare complications include uterine perforation, pelvic infection, and retained products of conception. Preventive antibiotics are typically given before surgical procedures. Having had a prior surgical uterine evacuation correlates with a small increase in preterm birth risk in future pregnancies, though the cause is undetermined.

Current evidence finds no relationship between most induced abortions and mental health problems beyond those expected for any unwanted pregnancy. The American Psychological Association concluded that a first-trimester first abortion is not a threat to mental health. "Post-abortion syndrome" is not recognized by medical or psychological professionals. A 2020 long-term US study found about 99% of women felt they made the right decision five years after abortion, with relief as the primary emotion; social stigma predicted negative emotions and regret.

Some purported risks lack scientific support. Major medical bodies (WHO, National Cancer Institute, American Cancer Society, Royal College of OBGYN, American Congress of OBGYN) have concluded abortion does not cause breast cancer. Historically, illegal abortions were not automatically unsafe; historian Linda Gordon states illegal abortions in the US had "an impressive safety record."

Unsafe abortions—performed by unskilled persons or in non-conforming environments—are a major cause of injury and death, with approximately 20 million performed annually, 97% in developing countries. Deaths range from 37,000 to 70,000 in the past decade, accounting for around 13% of maternal deaths. Countries with restrictive abortion laws have higher rates of unsafe abortion but similar overall rates. The 1996 South African legalization led to a more than 90% drop in abortion-related deaths; similar reductions followed liberalization in Romania and Nepal. Providing modern family planning could reduce unsafe abortions by up to 75%.

### Incidence

Abortion rate measures abortions per 1,000 women of reproductive age; abortion percentage measures abortions per 100 known pregnancies. Worldwide, 41.6 million abortions occurred in 2003 and 43.8 million in 2008, with a rate of 28 per 1,000 women (24 in developed, 29 in developing countries). The incidence is similar in restrictive and liberal countries, but restrictive laws increase the percentage performed unsafely. In 2008, legal abortion rates ranged from 7 per 1,000 women (Germany, Switzerland) to 30 per 1,000 (Estonia). About half of US women having abortions were using contraception at conception, often inconsistently.

### Motivation

Personal reasons include inability to afford a child, domestic violence, lack of support, feeling too young, and wishing to complete education or advance a career, as well as rape or incest. Societal pressures include sex or race preference, stigmatization of single motherhood or disability, insufficient economic support, and population control (such as China's one-child policy), sometimes resulting in compulsory or sex-selective abortion. Maternal health concerns motivated about a third of abortions in three of 27 countries analyzed in the 1990s. Cancer during pregnancy (0.02–1% of pregnancies) may lead to abortion, particularly for cervical cancer, where treatment "cannot co-exist with preservation of fetal life." Congenital disorders revealed by prenatal screening also motivate some abortions.

### History and religion

Abortion has been practiced since ancient times across civilizations including China, India, Egypt, and Rome. The Hippocratic Oath's prohibition is debated, as Hippocratic Corpus texts describe abortive techniques. Abortion was not always illegal or controversial until the 19th century, when anti-abortion laws were enacted in the US and UK. Soviet Russia legalized abortion in 1920, followed by Iceland (1935), Nazi Germany (1935), Sweden (1938), and Japan (1948).

In the Catholic Church, the Didache taught abortion was sinful as early as ~100 CE, but the Church did not vigorously oppose it until the 19th century. Pope Sixtus V first labeled all abortion as homicide in 1588, reversed in 1591. The Catechism considers abortion from conception as homicide. Protestant denominations supporting abortion rights with limits include the United Methodist, Episcopal, Evangelical Lutheran, and Presbyterian (USA) churches. In Judaism, the fetus is not considered a human life with a soul until safely outside the woman; abortion is permitted and necessary when the mother's life is in danger. In Islam, abortion is traditionally permitted until the soul enters the fetus (variously at conception, 40 days, 120 days, or quickening). Hindu views are diverse, generally regarding abortion as morally wrong but permissible to save the mother's life or in severe fetal abnormalities.

### Society and culture

The abortion debate centers on moral permissibility and legal justification. Groups favoring restrictions describe themselves as "pro-life"; those against restrictions as "pro-choice." Modern abortion laws are diverse, with requirements including waiting periods, parental consent for minors, or spousal consent. Some countries ban abortion almost entirely, with exceptions for rape, incest, fetal impairment, or maternal health. In Nicaragua, where abortion is banned entirely, rises in maternal death have been recorded.

Sex-selective abortion, most commonly of female fetuses, is partially responsible for birth-rate disparities in Taiwan, South Korea, India, and China. The WHO and UNICEF found that restricting abortion to reduce sex-selective abortion has unintended negative consequences, as women may seek unsafe extralegal abortions.

Anti-abortion violence has included murder, assault, arson, and bombing. Over 8,000 incidents have been recorded in the US and Canada since 1977, including over 200 bombings/arsons. At least four US physicians have been murdered, including David Gunn (1993), John Britton (1994), Barnett Slepian (1998), and George Tiller (2009). The American Freedom of Access to Clinic Entrances Act bars threats or violence to interfere with abortion access.

## Terms

- **Induced abortion**: Deliberate medical procedure to end a pregnancy.
- **Spontaneous abortion (miscarriage)**: Unintentional expulsion of an embryo or fetus before 24 weeks' gestation.
- **Mifepristone (RU-486)**: Antiprogestogen drug used with a prostaglandin analog for medical abortion.
- **Vacuum aspiration**: Surgical abortion method using suction; safest in the first trimester.
- **Dilation and evacuation (D&E)**: Surgical abortion after 12–16 weeks using suction and forceps.
- **Unsafe abortion**: Procedure by unskilled persons or in non-conforming environments.
- **Therapeutic abortion**: Abortion performed for medical reasons, such as saving the woman's life.
- **Elective abortion**: Abortion performed at the woman's request for non-medical reasons.
- **Quickening**: The point at which fetal movement is felt; historically used to mark permissible abortion.
- **Sex-selective abortion**: Termination of a fetus based on its sex, most commonly female.

## Debates and open questions

The central debate concerns whether an embryo or fetus is a person with a right to life (equating abortion with murder) versus whether abortion is a woman's reproductive right. Public health researchers frame legal access as a public health measure given its association with reduced maternal mortality. Scholars debate whether the Hippocratic Oath forbade abortion in Ancient Greece. The mental-health outcome of a woman's second or greater abortion is less certain than the first. Studies linking prior surgical uterine evacuation to preterm birth did not control for unrelated factors, leaving causation undetermined. Research gaps remain on whether self-administered medical abortion is as safe as provider-administered, and on methods to support women taking medication at home.
