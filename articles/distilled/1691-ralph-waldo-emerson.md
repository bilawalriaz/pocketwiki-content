# Ralph Waldo Emerson

## Overview
Ralph Waldo Emerson (1803–1882) was the central figure of American Transcendentalism, a philosophical and literary movement asserting that divine truth is accessible through intuition and nature rather than institutional religion or sensory experience alone. Through essays like *Nature* (1836) and *Self-Reliance* (1841), and the Phi Beta Kappa address *The American Scholar* (1837), he argued for intellectual independence from Europe and the "infinitude of the private man." His pantheistic view—that "the universe is composed of Nature and the Soul"—influenced Walt Whitman, Henry David Thoreau, Friedrich Nietzsche, and William James. A prolific lecturer (roughly 1,500 talks), he evolved from a Unitarian minister into a public intellectual who championed abolitionism, though his early writings reveal explicit racial hierarchies that complicated his antislavery advocacy.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Emerson was born into a long line of New England ministers. His father’s death in 1811 left the family in genteel poverty, mitigated by his aunt Mary Moody Emerson, whose fierce intellect and correspondence shaped his early development. At Harvard (1817–1821), he was an undistinguished student but began the journaling practice that became his primary literary laboratory. Poor health drove him to St. Augustine, Florida (1826–27), where conversations with Napoleon’s nephew Achille Murat expanded his philosophical horizons, and a slave auction witnessed outside a Bible Society meeting seared the contradiction of American Christianity into his consciousness.

### Ministry, Crisis, and European Turning Point
Emerson entered Harvard Divinity School and was ordained at Boston’s Second Church in 1829. The 1831 death of his young wife Ellen from tuberculosis shattered his conventional faith. He resigned in 1832, declaring the Communion rite "not suitable to me," and sailed for Europe. In Paris, the Jardin des Plantes’ taxonomic order sparked a visionary insight into nature’s interconnectedness, redirecting him from theology toward a naturalistic metaphysics. In England, Thomas Carlyle became a lifelong correspondent and intellectual model, reinforcing Emerson’s sense of the heroic individual.

### Transcendentalism and the Lecturing Career
Back in Concord, Emerson anonymously published *Nature* (1836), defining Transcendentalism: nature as a symbolic language revealing the "Over-Soul," the universal spirit uniting all particulars. The Transcendental Club formed days later, eventually launching *The Dial* (1840–44) under Margaret Fuller. *The American Scholar* (1837) declared cultural independence from Europe; the *Divinity School Address* (1838) scandalized Unitarianism by rejecting miracles and Christ’s unique divinity. Emerson then built a career as an independent lecturer—managing his own tours, earning up to $2,000 a season, and delivering roughly 1,500 lectures across the northern U.S. His essays grew from these lectures; *Essays: First Series* (1841) and *Second Series* (1844) contain the core doctrines of self-reliance, compensation, circularity, and the poet as liberating seer.

### Eastern Thought and Nondualism
Through Victor Cousin’s French translations, Emerson encountered Vedanta via the *Bhagavad Gita* and Henry Thomas Colebrooke’s *Essays on the Vedas* (1845). He adopted a nondualist framework: "the act of seeing and the thing seen, the seer and the spectacle, the subject and the object, are one." In *The Over-Soul*, he writes that "within man is the soul of the whole... the eternal ONE," and that life’s purpose is "spiritual transformation and direct experience of divine power, here and now on earth." This Eastern inflection distinguishes his pantheism from mere nature worship.

### Abolitionism and the Problem of Race
Emerson opposed slavery from youth but did not become a public abolitionist until 1844. The 1837 murder of Elijah Lovejoy prompted his first antislavery address; the 1856 caning of Charles Sumner galvanized regular speeches. He welcomed John Brown to Concord, voted for Lincoln (1860), and demanded immediate emancipation during the Civil War, meeting Lincoln in 1862. Yet his private journals (1822) reveal explicit racism: he ranked Africans near elephants in "sagacity," posited a hierarchy of reasoning faculties, and viewed Anglo-Saxons as a superior "race" destined to absorb others. Scholars argue these assumptions delayed and qualified his activism; only in later work did he envision a hybridized American "race" emerging from conflict and mixture.

### Civil War, Late Works, and Decline
*The Conduct of Life* (1860) framed war as national rebirth. Emerson lectured at the Smithsonian (1862), met Lincoln, and eulogized Thoreau (1862) and Nathaniel Hawthorne (1864). Elected to the American Academy of Arts and Sciences (1864) and American Philosophical Society (1867), he traveled the transcontinental railroad in 1871, meeting Brigham Young and a young John Muir in Yosemite. A 1872 house fire ended his lecturing career. Aphasia and memory loss silenced him by 1879; he died in 1882.

## Terms
- ****Transcendentalism**** — Mid-19th-century American movement holding that ultimate reality is spiritual, known through intuition rather than empiricism or institutional dogma; emphasizes the correspondence between nature and the human mind.
- ****Over-Soul**** — Emerson’s term for the universal, immanent spirit in which all individual souls participate; the "eternal ONE" linking subject and object, seer and seen.
- ****Self-Reliance**** — The ethical imperative to trust one’s own intuition and conscience above social convention, tradition, or external authority.
- ****The American Scholar**** — Emerson’s 1837 Phi Beta Kappa address urging American writers to create an original literature free from European models; called an "intellectual Declaration of Independence."
- ****Divinity School Address**** — 1838 Harvard graduation speech rejecting biblical miracles and Christ’s unique divinity, causing a 30-year breach with the university.
- ****The Dial**** — Transcendentalist quarterly (1840–44), edited by Margaret Fuller then Emerson; published early work by Thoreau, Ellery Channing, and others.
- ****Vedanta**** — Hindu philosophical tradition (nondualism) teaching that Atman (self) and Brahman (ultimate reality) are identical; shaped Emerson’s *Over-Soul* doctrine.
- ****Philosophers Camp**** — 1858 Adirondack wilderness excursion with Louis Agassiz, James Russell Lowell, and other intellectuals; symbolized the union of science, art, and literature.
- ****Fugitive Slave Act (1850)**** — Federal law compelling citizens to assist in capturing escaped slaves; Emerson denounced it as a statute "no man can obey... without loss of self-respect."
- ****Aphasia**** — Neurological language impairment; Emerson suffered progressive aphasia and memory loss in his final decade, ending his public career.

## Debates and open questions
- **Race vs. antislavery**: How do Emerson’s early biological racism (hierarchies of reason, Anglo-Saxon superiority) reconcile with his later moral abolitionism? Scholars debate whether his racial views *inhibited* earlier activism or evolved *through* it.
- **Journal as masterwork**: Some critics (e.g., Lawrence Rosenwald) argue the 16-volume journal, not the polished essays, is Emerson’s supreme literary achievement—raising questions about genre and authorial intention.
- **Pantheism vs. theism**: Emerson rejected a personal God separate from the world ("pantheist or pandeist"), yet invoked "Christ within us." The tension between impersonal Over-Soul and Christian language remains unresolved in his theology.
- **Political quietism vs. engagement**: Emerson insisted reform comes through "moral agreement" not "militant action," yet supported John Brown and framed the Civil War as necessary "revolution." The consistency of his political philosophy is contested.
- **Influence on Nietzsche**: Nietzsche called Emerson "the most gifted of the Americans" and annotated his essays heavily; the precise channels of influence (e.g., *Übermensch* vs. *Self-Reliance*) are still traced by comparatists.
