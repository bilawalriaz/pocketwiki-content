# Art

## Overview

Art is a diverse range of cultural activity centered around works utilizing creative or imaginative talents, expected to evoke a worthwhile experience through emotional power, conceptual ideas, technical proficiency, or beauty. There is no generally agreed definition of what constitutes art, and its interpretation has varied greatly throughout history and across cultures. In the Western tradition, the three classical branches of visual art are painting, sculpture, and architecture, with theatre, dance, literature, music, film, and interactive media included in a broader definition of "the arts." Until the 17th century, art referred to any skill or mastery and was not differentiated from crafts or sciences; after that, the fine arts were separated from decorative or applied arts. The nature of art and related concepts are explored in aesthetics, a branch of philosophy, with artworks studied in art criticism and the history of art.

## Body

### Early history and ancient civilizations

Artistic works have existed for almost as long as humankind, from early prehistoric art to contemporary art, though some theorists think the typical concept of "artistic works" does not fit well outside modern Western societies. A shell engraved by *Homo erectus* dates to between 430,000 and 540,000 years old, and a set of eight 130,000-year-old white-tailed eagle talons bear cut marks indicating manipulation by Neanderthals, possibly as jewelry. The oldest piece of art found in Europe is the Riesenhirschknochen der Einhornhöhle, dating back 51,000 years and made by Neanderthals. Sculptures, cave paintings, and petroglyphs from the Upper Paleolithic date to roughly 40,000 years ago, but their precise meaning is often disputed. The first undisputed sculptures, like the Venus of Hohle Fels, come from the Swabian Jura UNESCO World Heritage Site, with artifacts dating between 43,000 and 35,000 BC.

Many great traditions have foundations in the art of ancient civilizations: Egypt, Mesopotamia, Persia, India, China, Greece, Rome, Inca, Maya, and Olmec. Greek art saw a veneration of the human physical form and the development of skills to show musculature and anatomically correct proportions. Byzantine and Medieval art focused on biblical subjects with idealized, flat forms and gold backgrounds. Renaissance art emphasized realistic depiction of the material world, reflected in the corporeality of the human body and the development of systematic graphical perspective.

In the east, Islamic art's rejection of iconography led to emphasis on geometric patterns, calligraphy, and architecture. India and Tibet emphasized painted sculptures and dance. China saw jade carving, bronzework, pottery (including the Terracotta Army), poetry, calligraphy, music, painting, and drama, with styles named after ruling dynasties—Tang paintings are monochromatic and sparse, while Ming paintings are busy and colorful. Japan also named styles after imperial dynasties, and woodblock printing became important after the 17th century.

### Philosophical foundations

Philosophers like Plato, Aristotle, Socrates, and Immanuel Kant questioned the meaning of art. Socrates says poetry is inspired by the muses and is not rational, speaking approvingly of divine madness in the *Phaedrus*, yet in the *Republic* wants to outlaw Homer's great poetic art. The dialogue *Ion* suggests Homer's *Iliad* functioned in ancient Greece as divinely inspired literary art providing moral guidance. Aristotle considered epic poetry, tragedy, comedy, Dithyrambic poetry, and music to be mimetic or imitative art, each varying by medium, object, and manner—music imitates with rhythm and harmony, dance with rhythm alone, and poetry with language. Aristotle believed imitation is natural to mankind and constitutes one of mankind's advantages over animals.

The more recent sense of art as an abbreviation for creative art or fine art emerged in the early 17th century. Fine art refers to a skill used to express creativity, engage aesthetic sensibilities, or draw the audience toward consideration of finer works. Art may refer to a study of a creative skill, a process of using it, a product of it, or the audience's experience with it. For some scholars, such as Kant, the sciences represent the domain of knowledge and the arts the domain of the freedom of artistic expression. Often, if the skill is used in a common or practical way, people consider it a craft instead of art; if used commercially, it may be considered commercial art. Some argue the difference between fine and applied art has more to do with value judgments than clear definitional difference.

The nature of art has been described by philosopher Richard Wollheim as "one of the most elusive of the traditional problems of human culture." Leo Tolstoy identified art as a use of indirect means to communicate from one person to another. Benedetto Croce and R. G. Collingwood advanced the idealist view that art expresses emotions and essentially exists in the mind of the creator. The theory of art as form has roots in Kant and was developed by Roger Fry and Clive Bell. George Dickie offered an institutional theory defining a work of art as any artifact upon which qualified persons acting on behalf of "the art world" have conferred "the status of candidate for appreciation." Larry Shiner described fine art as "not an essence or a fate but something we have made... a European invention barely two hundred years old."

### Modern and contemporary developments

The western Age of Enlightenment in the 18th century saw artistic depictions of physical certainties of the clockwork universe and politically revolutionary visions. This led to Romantic rejections in favor of pictures of the emotional side and individuality of humans, exemplified in the novels of Goethe. The late 19th century saw movements such as academic art, Symbolism, impressionism, and fauvism. The history of 20th-century art is a narrative of endless possibilities and the search for new standards, each being torn down in succession by the next—Impressionism, Expressionism, Fauvism, Cubism, Dadaism, Surrealism. Increasing global interaction saw other cultures influence Western art: Japanese woodblock prints influenced impressionism, and African sculptures were taken up by Picasso and Matisse. Modernism, the idealistic search for truth, gave way in the latter half of the 20th century to a realization of its unattainability. Theodor W. Adorno said in 1970, "It is now taken for granted that nothing which concerns art can be taken for granted any more." Relativism was accepted as unavoidable, leading to contemporary art and postmodern criticism, where cultures are seen as changing forms appreciated with skepticism and irony.

Martin Heidegger described the essence of art in terms of being and truth, arguing that art is not only a way of expressing truth in a culture but the means of creating it. Works of art are not merely representations but actually produce a community's shared understanding. Historically, art has been spread through trade, as with the Silk Road, where Hellenistic, Iranian, Indian, and Chinese influences mixed—Greco Buddhist art is a vivid example.

### Forms, genres, and skill

The creative arts are often divided into categories such as media, genre, styles, and form. Art form covers the methods adopted by the artist and the physical composition of the artwork, including color, contour, dimension, medium, melody, space, texture, and value. There are three schools of philosophy regarding art, focusing on form, content, and context. Extreme Formalism holds that all aesthetic properties of art are formal, a view philosophers almost universally reject. Extreme Intentionalism holds that authorial intent plays a decisive role in meaning, also typically rejected because art may have multiple ambiguous meanings. Post-structuralism studies art's significance in cultural context, including religious and philosophical convictions, sociopolitical structures, or climate and geography.

Art can connote trained ability or mastery of a medium. Traditionally, skill of execution was viewed as inseparable from art; for Leonardo da Vinci, art was a manifestation of skill. A common contemporary criticism of some modern art objects to the apparent lack of skill required. In conceptual art, Marcel Duchamp's *Fountain* used found objects ("ready-made") with no traditionally recognized skills. Tracey Emin's *My Bed* and Damien Hirst's *The Physical Impossibility of Death in the Mind of Someone Living* follow this example—Hirst came up with the conceptual design but left creation to employed artisans.

### Purpose and public access

Art has had many different functions, grouped into non-motivated and motivated purposes (Lévi-Strauss). Non-motivated functions are integral to being human: basic instinct for harmony, balance, and rhythm; experience of the mysterious; expression of the imagination; and ritualistic and symbolic functions. Motivated purposes are intentional: communication, entertainment, political change (the avant-garde), social inquiry or subversion, raising awareness for causes, psychological healing (art therapy), propaganda or commercialism, and as a fitness indicator—the human brain's artistic ability being the equivalent of the peacock's tail for attracting mates.

Since ancient times, much fine art has represented a display of wealth or power. However, cheap media like pottery were available across large parts of society. In 1661, Basel opened the Kunstmuseum Basel, the first public museum of art. The opening of the Musée du Louvre in 1793 as a public museum marked an important stage in public access. Most modern public museums and art education programs trace back to this impulse. In the late 1960s and 1970s, artists attempted to create art that could not be bought and sold, leading to performance art, video art, and conceptual art. However, the art market later learned to sell limited edition DVDs and objects left over from conceptual pieces, and the marker of status became understanding the work rather than owning it.

### Controversies and theory

Art has long been controversial. Iconoclasm is the destruction of disliked art; aniconism is a general dislike of figurative images, crucial in Islamic art. The *Last Judgment* by Michelangelo was controversial for breaches of decorum through nudity. With Romanticism, the artist's vision became the usual determinant of content, increasing controversies. Édouard Manet's *Le Déjeuner sur l'Herbe* (1863) was scandalous because a nude woman is seated next to fully dressed men in contemporary clothing. Andres Serrano's *Piss Christ* (1989), a photograph of a crucifix submerged in urine, led to comments in the US Senate about public funding of the arts.

Before Modernism, Western aesthetics was concerned with balancing realism and the ideal. Richard Wollheim distinguishes three approaches to assessing aesthetic value: the Realist (absolute value independent of human view), the Objectivist (absolute but dependent on general human experience), and the Relativist (depends on human experience). Clement Greenberg's 1960 article "Modernist Painting" defines modern art as "the use of characteristic methods of a discipline to criticize the discipline itself," originally applied to Abstract Expressionism. Duchamp proposed that art is any activity of any kind, and the classification of only certain activities as art is a social construction. Larry Shiner finds evidence that Ancient Greek society did not possess the term art but *techne*, which included painting, sculpting, and music but also cooking, medicine, horsemanship, geometry, carpentry, prophecy, and farming.

The New Criticism school debated the intentional fallacy—whether the artist's intentions should be associated with criticism of the final product. In 1946, Wimsatt and Beardsley argued against the relevance of authorial intention, holding that the words on the page were all that mattered. They also discounted the reader's emotional reaction in "The Affective Fallacy," later repudiated by reader-response theorists. The "linguistic turn" debate discussed whether the conceptual encounter with art dominates over the perceptual encounter. Nelson Goodman held that the conceptual encounter predominated, but was challenged based on research by Nobel prize winner Roger Sperry, who maintained that other forms of psychological representations were equally defensible. Sperry's view prevailed by the end of the 20th century.

### Classification disputes and law

Classificatory disputes about whether to classify something as art have included cubist paintings, Duchamp's *Fountain*, conceptual art, and video games. Philosopher David Novitz argued that disagreement about the definition of art is rarely the heart of the problem; rather, disputes are more often about societal values. Arthur Danto suggested that "the status of an artifact as work of art results from the ideas a culture applies to it, rather than its inherent physical or perceptible qualities." Anti-art is a label for art that intentionally challenges established parameters, associated with Dadaism and Marcel Duchamp. Architecture is often included as a visual art, though practical considerations of use are essential.

The word art is also used to apply judgments of value, as in "that meal was a work of art." Making judgments of value requires a basis for criticism. "Good" art is not always aesthetically appealing to a majority of viewers—Francisco Goya's painting of the Spanish shootings of 3 May 1808 is a graphic depiction of a firing squad, yet demonstrates keen artistic ability and produces fitting social and political outrage. It has been argued by Emily L. Spratt that generative AI necessitates a re-evaluation of aesthetic theory and a reconsideration of the limits of human creativity.

Essential legal issues include art forgeries, plagiarism, replicas, and works strongly based on other works. Intellectual property law grants artists exclusive rights to reproduce, distribute, and display their creations. The UN, UNESCO, and Blue Shield International try to ensure protection of works of art in armed conflicts or disasters. Karl von Habsburg explained that "Cultural goods are part of the identity of the people who live in a certain place. If you destroy their culture, you also destroy their identity." UNESCO protects living human treasure through the Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage.

## Terms

- **Aesthetics**: The branch of philosophy exploring the nature of art and related concepts such as creativity and interpretation.
- **Mimesis**: Imitation or representation of reality, with deep roots in Aristotle's philosophy.
- **Fine art**: A skill used to express creativity or engage aesthetic sensibilities, distinguished from crafts or applied arts after the 17th century.
- **Iconoclasm**: The destruction of art disliked for various reasons, including religious ones.
- **Aniconism**: A general dislike of figurative images, often religious ones, a crucial factor in Islamic art.
- **Avant-garde**: Art movements using visual images to bring about political change, including Dadaism, Surrealism, Russian constructivism, and Abstract Expressionism.
- **Intentional fallacy**: The New Critical argument that an author's intention is irrelevant to analyzing a literary work.
- **Anti-art**: Art that intentionally challenges established parameters and values of art, associated with Dadaism and Marcel Duchamp.
- **Techne**: The Ancient Greek term for skilled activity, which included painting and music but also cooking, medicine, and farming, without distinguishing art from craft.
- **Ready-made**: Found objects used as art, as in Duchamp's *Fountain*, exercising no traditionally recognized set of skills.

## Debates and open questions

There is no generally agreed definition of what constitutes art, and its interpretation has varied greatly throughout history and across cultures. Some theorists think the typical concept of "artistic works" does not fit well outside modern Western societies. The precise meaning of prehistoric art is often disputed because so little is known about the cultures that produced them. Philosophers almost universally reject both Extreme Formalism and Extreme Intentionalism, but there is little consensus on terminology for informal properties of art. The linguistic turn debate asked whether the conceptual encounter with art dominates over the perceptual encounter, with Sperry's view eventually prevailing. Classificatory disputes about art are more often disputes about societal values than about theory proper, and the debate continues as to what mode of aesthetic satisfaction, if any, is required to define art.
