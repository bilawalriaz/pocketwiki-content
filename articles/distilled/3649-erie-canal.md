# Erie Canal

## Overview
The Erie Canal is a historic waterway in upstate New York that connected the Hudson River to Lake Erie. Completed in 1825, it was the first navigable waterway linking the Atlantic Ocean to the upper Great Lakes, drastically reducing transportation costs and accelerating U.S. westward expansion and the economic rise of New York City.

## Timeline
- **1808** — First formal survey of a canal route is conducted.
- **1817** — New York State Legislature authorizes construction; work begins at Rome, NY.
- **1825** — The entire canal opens on October 26; tolls cover the construction debt in the first year.
- **1834** — The First Enlargement begins, widening and deepening the canal.
- **1837** — The Mohawk and Hudson Railroad opens, beginning rail competition.
- **1882** — Voters approve an amendment to end tolls on the canal, effective 1883.
- **1905** — Construction of the New York State Barge Canal begins.
- **1918** — The Barge Canal is completed, replacing much of the original route.
- **1951** — Commercial shipping on the canal peaks at 5.2 million short tons.
- **1992** — The Barge Canal is renamed the New York State Canal System.
- **2000** — Congress designates the Erie Canalway National Heritage Corridor.

## Body

### Conception and Construction
Before railroads, water transport was the most cost-effective way to ship bulk goods. The Appalachian Mountains were a major obstacle to westward transport, and a canal through New York's Mohawk Valley offered the only break in the range north of Alabama. Early proposals were made in the 1780s, but a formal survey wasn't conducted until 1808. Governor DeWitt Clinton championed the project, which opponents ridiculed as "Clinton's Folly." The legislature authorized $7 million for construction in 1817.

The original canal was 363 miles (584 km) long, 40 feet (12 m) wide, and 4 feet (1.2 m) deep. Built primarily by Irish laborers and German stonemasons using human and animal power, it was a landmark civil engineering achievement. Key challenges included crossing the Montezuma Marshes, building aqueducts over rivers like the Genesee, and cutting through the Niagara Escarpment at Lockport. The canal was completed in 1825 at a cost of $7.143 million.

### Economic and Social Impact
The canal's immediate success transformed the region. It drastically lowered shipping costs—for example, the price to ship a barrel of flour from Rochester to Albany fell from $3 to 75¢. This made New York City the nation's premier port and fueled the growth of canal cities like Buffalo, Rochester, and Syracuse. Tolls repaid the construction debt within a year.

The canal facilitated massive migration westward and became a vital link in the Underground Railroad. It also spurred a national canal-building boom and inspired a new public esteem for practical engineering. Commercial traffic peaked in 1855 with 33,000 shipments.

### Evolution and Decline
The canal was enlarged in the 1830s–1860s to handle increased traffic. It remained competitive with railroads for freight until the early 20th century. In 1905, construction began on the New York State Barge Canal, a major expansion completed in 1918 that replaced over half the original route and canalized rivers. Commercial traffic peaked in 1951 but declined sharply due to competition from the Interstate Highway System and the St. Lawrence Seaway. The last regular commercial hauler ended service in 1994.

Today, the canal is part of the New York State Canal System and is used primarily for recreation and tourism. It was designated a National Heritage Corridor in 2000.

## Terms
- **Towpath:** A path alongside a canal used by horses or mules to tow boats.
- **Lock:** A device for raising and lowering boats between stretches of water of different levels, consisting of a chamber with gates.
- **Packet boat:** A boat designed for carrying passengers and mail on inland waterways.
- **Barge Canal:** The early 20th-century expansion and realignment of the Erie Canal, designed for larger, mechanically propelled vessels.
- **Hydraulic cement:** A cement that hardens underwater, used to seal leaks in the canal.
- **Mohawk Valley:** The only natural break in the Appalachian Mountains north of Alabama, providing the canal's critical route.
- **Niagara Escarpment:** A steep limestone ridge that the canal had to ascend using a series of locks at Lockport.
- **National Heritage Corridor:** A designation by Congress to protect and promote areas of historic and cultural significance.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The death toll from "swamp fever" (malaria) during construction of the Montezuma Marsh section is debated; recent research suggests the commonly cited figure of over 1,000 workers is likely exaggerated.
- The 1858 debate over closing the canal on Sundays centered on whether the canal, as an "artificial river," should be treated like natural waterways (which were not closed) or like public roads.
