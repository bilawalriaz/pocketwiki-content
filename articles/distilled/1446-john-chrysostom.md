# John Chrysostom

## Overview
John Chrysostom (c. 347 – 14 September 407) was a Church Father and Archbishop of Constantinople renowned for his eloquent preaching—earning the epithet "golden-mouthed"—and his advocacy for the poor. Born in Antioch, he studied rhetoric under the pagan sophist Libanius before turning to theology under Diodorus of Tarsus. After a period of extreme asceticism damaged his health, he served as a priest in Antioch (386–397), where his homilies converted pagans and calmed civic unrest. Appointed Archbishop of Constantinople in 397, he reformed clergy morals, founded hospitals, and denounced imperial extravagance, provoking conflict with Empress Aelia Eudoxia and Patriarch Theophilus of Alexandria. Deposed at the Synod of the Oak (403), briefly recalled, then exiled again, he died en route to a remote outpost. Venerated as a saint immediately, he is honored as one of the Three Holy Hierarchs in Eastern Orthodoxy and a Great Greek Father in Catholicism. His surviving corpus—roughly 600 sermons, 246 letters, and the *Divine Liturgy of Saint John Chrysostom*—remains the standard Eucharistic rite of the Byzantine tradition. His eight *Adversus Judaeos* homilies and treatise against homosexuality have sparked enduring scholarly debate regarding anti-Jewish rhetoric and sexual ethics in late antiquity.

## Timeline
- **c. 347** — Born in Antioch, Roman Syria; father dies shortly after.
- **368 or 373** — Baptised; tonsured as a reader.
- **c. 371–375** — Studies rhetoric under Libanius; later theology under Diodorus of Tarsus.
- **c. 375–377** — Lives as a hermit practicing extreme asceticism; health permanently damaged.

- **404** — Exiled again, first to Cucusus in Cappadocia.
- **405** — Appeals to Pope Innocent I and Western bishops; supports monks enforcing anti-pagan laws in Phoenicia.
- **14 Sept 407** — Dies at Comana Pontica during forced march to Pitiunt.
- **28 Jan 438** — Relics translated to Constantinople under Patriarch Proclus; enshrined in Church of the Holy Apostles.

- **27 Nov 2004** — Some relics returned to Orthodox Church by Pope John Paul II.
- **1908** — Named patron saint of preachers by Pope Pius X.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
John was born in Antioch around 347 to a high-ranking military officer and his mother Anthusa (described variously as pagan or devout Christian). After his father’s early death, Anthusa’s connections secured him an education under the celebrated sophist **Libanius**, from whom he mastered Greek rhetoric and literature. Though poised for a legal career, John turned to Christianity, studying theology at the **School of Antioch** under **Diodorus of Tarsus**, which emphasized literal-historical exegesis over Alexandrian allegory. Around 375 he embraced severe asceticism as a hermit—standing continually, scarcely sleeping, memorizing Scripture—permanently damaging his stomach and kidneys and forcing his return to Antioch.

### Ministry in Antioch (381–397)
Ordained deacon in 381 by Meletius of Antioch, John navigated the city’s schismatic factions (Meletian, Eustathian, Paulinian) and was ordained priest by Evagrius in 386. For twelve years he preached at the Golden Church, gaining immense popularity through direct, practical expositions of Scripture applied to daily life. His homilies emphasized **almsgiving** as inseparable from prayer and fasting, famously shaming the wealthy: “What good is it if the Eucharistic table is overloaded with golden chalices when your brother is dying of hunger?” During Lent 387, after citizens mutilated imperial statues, his twenty-plus homilies (*On the Statues*) calmed the populace, converted many pagans, and persuaded Emperor Theodosius I to spare the city. In this period he also delivered eight *Adversus Judaeos* homilies (386–387) targeting Christians participating in Jewish festivals, employing the *psogos* (blame/censure) rhetorical form to vilify the synagogue and warn against “Judaizing.”

### Archbishop of Constantinople (397–404)
Nominated without his knowledge by the eunuch **Eutropius**, John was consecrated in autumn 397. He refused lavish entertainments, reformed the clergy (ordering itinerant preachers back to their parishes without pay), and founded hospitals. His austerity and denunciations of feminine luxury antagonized **Aelia Eudoxia**, wife of Emperor Arcadius, while his protection of the **Tall Brothers**—Egyptian monks condemned by **Theophilus of Alexandria** for Origenist sympathies—provoked the patriarch’s enmity. In 403 the **Synod of the Oak**, convened by Theophilus and Eudoxia, charged John with Origenism and deposed him. Popular riots and an earthquake forced Arcadius to recall him within days. Peace collapsed when a silver statue of Eudoxia was erected near the cathedral; John denounced the dedication as pagan, alluding to Herodias demanding John the Baptist’s head. He was exiled again, this time to the Caucasus (Abkhazia). Riots ensued, burning the Constantinian Hagia Sophia, necessitating the Theodosian Hagia Sophia’s construction.

### Exile, Appeals, and Death (14 September 407)
From Cucusus (404–407), John maintained influence through letters. He appealed to **Pope Innocent I**, Bishop Venerius of Milan, and Bishop Chromatius of Aquileia; Innocent protested and sent a delegation led by Gaudentius of Brescia, which never reached Constantinople. Around 405 John supported monks destroying pagan temples in Phoenicia. Further exiled to Pitiunt (modern Georgia), he died at Comana Pontica on 14 September 407, his last words: “Glory be to God for all things.”

### Veneration and Relics
Venerated immediately, John’s adherents (Johannites) remained in schism for decades. Patriarch **Proclus** (434–446) preached reconciliation homilies, enabling the translation of relics to Constantinople on 28 January 438 under Theodosius II. Crusaders looted most relics to Rome in 1204; some were returned by Pope John Paul II in 2004 (now in the Church of St. George, Istanbul). The skull’s location is disputed: Vatopedi Monastery (Mount Athos), the Cathedral of Christ the Saviour (Moscow), and two Italian churches claim it. The right hand is at Philotheou Monastery (Mount Athos).

### Writings and Theological Method
Approximately 600 sermons, 246 letters, and a commentary on Acts survive. His exegetical homilies—67 on Genesis, 90 on Matthew, 88 on John, 55 on Acts—reflect the **Antiochene school’s** literal-historical method, though he employs allegory where useful. Style is direct, personal, and rhetorically polished (stenographer-recorded). Key treatises: *Against Those Who Oppose the Monastic Life* (pre-386), *On the Priesthood* (390–391), *Instructions to Catechumens*, *On the Incomprehensibility of the Divine Nature*, and seventeen letters to the deaconess Olympias.

### Liturgy
John revised the prayers and rubrics of the **Divine Liturgy** (Eucharistic celebration). The *Divine Liturgy of Saint John Chrysostom* remains the standard Byzantine Rite liturgy for Eastern Orthodox and Eastern Catholic churches, though scholars debate his exact authorship versus later attribution.

### *Adversus Judaeos* and Anti-Jewish Rhetoric
Delivered in Antioch (386–387), the eight homilies *Kata Ioudaiōn* (*Adversus Judaeos*) targeted Christians attending Jewish festivals (Shabbat, Rosh Hashanah), circumcising, and pilgrimaging to Jewish sites. John used the *psogos* convention—uncompromising vilification of opponents—to argue synagogues were dens of demons and that Jewish ritual was obsolete. Scholars debate whether the target was “Judaizing Christians” or Jews generally. Patristic scholars (e.g., Robert Wilken) caution against the anachronistic label “anti-Semite,” noting the *psogos* genre’s literary conventions, while acknowledging the homilies advanced **supersessionism** and collective Jewish responsibility for Christ’s death, influencing later anti-Jewish sentiment.

### Homily on Homosexuality (Romans 1:26)
In his fourth homily on Romans 1:26, John condemns same-sex acts as “mad lust,” “insult to nature,” and worse than murder because the passive male “lives under” enduring shame. He attributes the sin to “luxury” and argues the passive partner renounces manhood, deserving to be “driven out and stoned.” Scholar Michael Carden credits Chrysostom with shifting Christian interpretation of Sodom from inhospitality to sexual transgression; Kruger and Nortjé-Meyer counter that the Epistle of Jude already made this link. Theologian Stephen Morris urges nuance: Chrysostom’s preaching is situational and occasionally self-contradictory.

### Legacy and Influence
- **Eastern Christianity**: Central figure; one of the Three Holy Hierarchs (with Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus); “Great Ecumenical Teacher.”
- **Western Christianity**: Less central but cited 18 times in the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* (especially on the Lord’s Prayer); named patron of preachers (1908).
- **Protestant reception**: Praised by Richard Salter Storrs and John Henry Newman for eloquence and pastoral warmth.
- **Culture**: Inspired musical settings by Rachmaninoff, Tchaikovsky, Stetsenko, Pärt, Grechaninov; referenced in Joyce’s *Ulysses* (“Chrysostomos” = “golden-mouthed”).
- **Medieval legend**: The *Penance of Saint John Chrysostom* (fornication with a princess, bestial penance, miraculous infant absolution) became a popular artistic subject (Dürer, Cranach, Beham); Luther satirized it in 1537 to critique hagiography.

## Terms
- ****Antiochene school**** — Exegetical tradition centered at the School of Antioch emphasizing literal, historical, and grammatical interpretation of Scripture, contrasted with Alexandrian allegory.
- ****Divine Liturgy**** — The Eucharistic service of the Byzantine Rite; John’s revision remains the standard liturgy for Eastern Orthodox and Eastern Catholic churches.
- ****psogos** (ψόγος)** — Greek rhetorical form of “blame” or “censure,” employing deliberate vilification of opponents; conventional in late-antique polemic.
- ****Judaizing Christians**** — Christians who adopted Jewish practices (Sabbath, festivals, circumcision, pilgrimage) targeted by John’s *Adversus Judaeos* homilies.
- ****Three Holy Hierarchs**** — Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nazianzus, and John Chrysostom; jointly commemorated 30 January in Eastern Orthodoxy as supreme teachers.
- ****Tall Brothers**** — Four Egyptian monks (Ammonius, Dioscorus, Eusebius, Euthymius) condemned by Theophilus of Alexandria for Origenist views; sheltered by John.
- ****Synod of the Oak**** — 403 council near Constantinople convened by Theophilus and Eudoxia; deposed John on charges including Origenism.
- ****Origenism**** — Theological system derived from Origen (c. 185–254), later condemned for teachings on pre-existence of souls, subordination of the Son, and universal salvation.
- ****supersessionism**** — Theological view that the Christian Church has replaced Israel as God’s chosen people, rendering the Mosaic covenant obsolete.
- ****tonsure**** — Rite of cutting hair marking entry into clerical state; John was tonsured a **reader** (minor order) before diaconate.

## Debates and open questions
- **Authorship of the Divine Liturgy**: Experts dispute whether John composed the core anaphora or whether the liturgy bearing his name reflects later Byzantine development.
- **Target of *Adversus Judaeos***: Scholars disagree whether the homilies primarily attack “Judaizing Christians” or Jews as a group, and whether the *psogos* genre mitigates or amplifies their historical harm.
- **Interpretation of Sodom**: Whether Chrysostom originated the homosexual reading of Genesis 19 or inherited it from Jude and earlier tradition remains contested.
- **Consistency of sexual ethics**: Stephen Morris argues Chrysostom’s situational preaching resists systematization; his homily on Romans 1:26 may not represent a fixed “attitude.”
- **Relic authenticity**: Multiple sites claim the skull (Vatopedi, Moscow, Florence, Pisa); scientific verification is absent.
- **Mother’s religion**: Sources differ on whether Anthusa was pagan or Christian; the text preserves both traditions without resolution.
