# Crime

## Overview
Crime is an act defined by law as a public wrong, punishable by the state through procedures that restrict liberty. While acts like murder and theft are prohibited globally, the precise definition of a criminal offence varies by jurisdiction and legal tradition (civil, common, Islamic, or socialist law). A crime typically requires both a guilty act (*actus reus*) and a guilty mind (*mens rea*), though strict liability offences require only the act. Criminology studies crime as a social construct, legal category, and psychological phenomenon, analyzing its causes, prevention, and the state's response through punishment, rehabilitation, and incapacitation.

## Timeline
- **c. 2100–2050 BC** — Code of Ur-Nammu, the earliest known criminal code, enacted in Mesopotamia.
- **c. 1750 BC** — Code of Hammurabi establishes retaliatory justice (*lex talionis*), influencing subsequent civilizations.
- **12th century** — Henry II of England establishes traveling judges, founding the common law system based on precedent.
- **13th century** — Thomas Aquinas articulates natural law theory, arguing valid law must conform to rational nature and divine order.
- **1760s** — William Blackstone codifies common law; formulates Blackstone's ratio (better ten guilty escape than one innocent suffer).
- **18th century** — Imprisonment develops as a primary long-term penalty, replacing corporal punishment in developed nations.
- **19th century** — Urbanization and industrialization spur government intervention; criminology emerges as a distinct field; Cesare Lombroso popularizes anthropological criminology (biological determinism).
- **Early 20th century** — Eugenics movement links crime to genetic factors; animal prosecutions dwindle (last recorded 1910s–1920s).
- **Post-WWII** — Crime increasingly viewed as societal issue; criminal law used to protect public from antisocial behavior; rise of social democracy influences policy.
- **1980s** — Forensic science popularized; DNA profiling established as investigative tool.
- **2013** — Study estimates policing elasticity: -0.67 for murder, -0.56 for robbery (increased policing reduces crime).

## Body

### Definition and Theoretical Perspectives
Crime lacks a single universal definition. **Legalism** defines crime strictly as acts banned by authority and subject to criminal procedure; in common law systems, courts can create *common law offences* (e.g., using *malum in se*—acts inherently wrong) without legislation. **Sociology** treats crime as a social construct: acts violating social norms and causing harm, shaped by *legal moralism* (majority morality dictates law) or *paternalism* (including self-harm). **Psychology** focuses on the perpetrator's mental state and environmental relationship. Historically, crime was conflated with sin (divine transgression), a view superseded by modern criminal theories.

### Criminal Law and Procedure
Modern criminal law derives from civil, common, Islamic, or socialist traditions. **Criminalization**—the state's power to define acts as criminal—carries human rights implications regarding autonomy. **Criminal procedure** adjudicates guilt, balancing two errors: *error of impunity* (guilty go free) and convicting the innocent (Blackstone's ratio). **Liability** requires the actor to understand the process and the authority to have legitimate power. **International criminal law** addresses genocide, crimes against humanity, and war crimes via treaties and custom, prosecuted by tribunals rather than standard courts.

### Elements of a Crime: *Actus Reus* and *Mens Rea*
Classification as a crime usually requires *actus reus* (the physical act) accompanied by *mens rea* (criminal intent). **Strict liability crimes** are an exception: the prosecution must prove the act but not the intent for specific elements. Not all legal violations are crimes; breaches of private law (torts, contracts) are enforced via civil procedure, not state punishment.

### Typology of Crimes
*   **Violent crime:** Aggression against persons (homicide, assault, robbery); distinct from justified violence (self-defense, war).
*   **Property crime:** Burglary, theft, vandalism.
*   **Financial crime:** Counterfeiting, tax evasion, bribery; expanded with modern economics (17th century onward); includes occupational crime using computer systems.
*   **White-collar crime:** Financially motivated, nonviolent offences by professionals (fraud, insider trading, money laundering).
*   **Blue-collar crime:** Crimes by lower social classes for immediate gain (theft, assault, drug distribution).
*   **Corporate crime:** Financially motivated acts by legal/natural persons for company goals (price fixing, safety violations).
*   **Public order crime:** Violations of social norms (gambling, prostitution, vagrancy); linked to *broken windows theory* (disorder invites serious crime); includes *victimless crimes* increasingly decriminalized in the West.
*   **Political crime:** Challenges to the state (treason, sedition, terrorism); criminality often determined by political alignment; state repression of citizens may qualify.
*   **Inchoate crime:** Anticipatory acts (attempt, conspiracy) facilitating future crimes; renunciation of intent often absolves liability.
*   **Child criminal exploitation (CCE):** Coercion of minors (<18) into criminal activity via power imbalance; victims often misidentified as perpetrators.

### Participants: Criminals and Victims
A **criminal** is a natural or legal person committing a crime; no identifiable mental/physical trait distinguishes them from non-criminals. Historically, animals were prosecuted (until ~1920s). **Victims** suffer harm from criminal violations; victimization correlates with PTSD and reduced quality of life. **Victimology** studies victim roles and effects. *Repeat victimization* arises from close relationships with offenders (domestic violence) or target viability (displaying wealth). Victims and offenders share demographic traits; victims are more likely to have criminal histories. Victims often seek compensation over deterrence but lack economies of scale to administer justice.

### Crime Statistics and Public Perception
Statistics are collected by national/international agencies; methods vary. **Under-reporting** creates a *dark figure of crime*; victimization surveys narrow the gap for severe crimes. **Clearance rate** measures charges/convictions per crime. **Fear of crime** often diverges from actual probability. Media exposure (news, fiction) drives alarmism: selection bias over-represents violent crime; temporal clustering creates "crime wave" perceptions. Public morality shifts can decriminalize previously condemned acts.

### Causes and Correlates
Determinants include rational **cost–benefit analysis**, opportunity, and crimes of passion. Negative economic factors (unemployment, inequality) increase incentives; severe punishments may deter. **Social integration** is key: marginalized or forcibly integrated groups offend more; community involvement (e.g., religious) reduces crime; associating with criminals increases offending. **No genetic cause** exists; genes may influence traits but not compel acts. **Sex disparity:** men commit significantly more and severer crimes across cultures. **Recidivism** follows a long-tail distribution: few chronic offenders account for disproportionate crime; early onset predicts longer criminal careers.

### Criminal Justice Theories
**Natural-law theory** (Aquinas, Blackstone) argues law's validity derives from conformity to divine/reasonable natural order; coercion is moral only if law is just. **Legal positivism** (Austin, Hart) separates law from morality: validity stems from sovereign enactment and internal logic, not moral content. **Dworkin** counters with a right to equal respect, requiring legitimacy and legislative justice. Enforcing prevailing morality as law's primary function risks making moral critique of law impossible (valid law = moral law by definition).

### Corrections and Punishment
**Retributive justice** imposes suffering as deserved accountability and for its own sake. **Deterrence** uses punishment's threat to discourage crime. Developed nations favor fines and imprisonment over physical penalties. Historically, homicide was often resolved via compensation (*wergild*); where rule of law fails, extralegal punishment (lynching) occurs.

### Crime Prevention
**Law enforcement** (police) prevents crime via deterrence and sanction; *community policing* integrates officers into public life. A 2013 study found policing elasticity of -0.67 (murder) and -0.56 (robbery). **Incapacitation** removes offenders' capacity to reoffend (imprisonment). **Rehabilitation** addresses root causes (poverty, values, mental health) via counseling, education, and social support to reduce recidivism.

### Criminology
The interdisciplinary study of crime, covering norms, deviance, violence, motivations, and prevention. Subfields include penology, victimology, forensic science, and criminal psychology/sociology/psychiatry. Major theories: *social control* (bonds prevent crime), *subcultural* (group norms), *strain* (blocked goals), *differential association* (learned behavior), and *labeling theory* (stigma creates deviance).

### History
**Early:** Prehistoric societies used custom/religion/tribal rule. Earliest codes: Ur-Nammu (c. 2100 BC), Hammurabi (retaliatory justice). Romans systematized law (initially private compensation for assault); Imperial China maintained unbroken criminal justice for 2,000+ years. Crime equated with sin (Abrahamic/Islamic influence).
**Post-classical:** Feudal/local justice in Europe/East Asia; Russian/Italian feudal systems persisted to 19th century. English common law formalized under Henry II (12th C); earliest crime data recorded.
**Modern:** 17th C (Hobbes) frames crime as societal issue. 18th C: imprisonment replaces corporal punishment. 19th C: urbanization/industrialization spawn criminology. Late 19th C: Lombroso's biological determinism (social Darwinism). Early 20th C: eugenics. Post-WWII: modernism shifts focus to societal protection, aligning with social democracy. Mid-20th C: mass media sensationalizes crime (e.g., Dahmer). 1980s: DNA profiling revolutionizes forensics.

## Terms
- ****Actus reus**** — The "guilty act"—the physical, external element of a crime that the prosecution must prove occurred.
- ****Mens rea**** — The "guilty mind"—the mental state or intention to commit a criminal act, required for most offences.
- ****Strict liability crime**** — An offence where conviction requires proof of *actus reus* only, without needing to establish *mens rea* for one or more elements.
- ****Malum in se**** — Acts considered inherently wrong or evil by nature (e.g., murder), as opposed to *malum prohibitum* (wrong only because prohibited).
- ****Common law offence**** — A crime defined by judicial precedent rather than statute, recognized in England and Commonwealth countries.
- ****Dark figure of crime**** — The gap between actual crime incidence and official statistics caused by under-reporting and non-detection.
- ****Clearance rate**** — The proportion of recorded crimes resulting in a criminal charge or conviction.
- ****Broken windows theory**** — The hypothesis that visible signs of disorder (public order crimes) encourage further, more serious crime.
- ****Victimology**** — The study of crime victims, including their relationship to offenders, the impact of crime, and their role in the justice system.
- ****Recidivism**** — The tendency of a convicted criminal to reoffend; a small fraction of offenders account for a disproportionate share of crimes.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Definition of crime:** No consensus exists across law, sociology, and psychology; the category remains a philosophical issue without an agreed answer.
*   **Natural law vs. legal positivism:** Whether a law's validity depends on its moral content (Aquinas/Dworkin) or solely on its enactment by a sovereign (Austin/Hart).
*   **Moral enforcement:** Whether the law should enforce prevailing morality (legal moralism) risks making moral criticism of the law logically impossible.
*   **Causes of crime:** Tension between rational choice (cost-benefit), structural factors (inequality, integration), and biological traits (sex disparity, genetic influences on behavior) with no single deterministic cause identified.
*   **Punishment justification:** Conflict between retributive justice (deserved suffering), deterrence (crime reduction), and rehabilitation (addressing root causes).
*   **Political crime relativity:** Criminality of political acts (treason, terrorism) depends on state alignment; one nation's dissident is another's freedom fighter.
*   **Victim-offender overlap:** Demographic and behavioral similarities between victims and offenders challenge the binary distinction; repeat victimization mechanisms are complex.
*   **Media distortion:** News selection bias creates "crime waves" and inflates fear of crime independent of actual rates, complicating democratic policy-making.
*   **Corporate/white-collar accountability:** Difficulty detecting and prosecuting complex financial crimes camouflaged by occupational anonymity and computer systems.
*   **International enforcement:** Lack of a standard global legal system for genocide and war crimes; reliance on ad hoc tribunals and state consensus.
