# Great Bengal famine of 1770

## Overview
The Great Bengal famine of 1770 was a catastrophic event that struck Bengal and Bihar between 1769 and 1770, affecting approximately 30 million people. It occurred during a period of dual governance, after the East India Company had gained revenue collection rights but before it fully controlled civil administration. The famine, caused by crop failure and exacerbated by Company policies, led to massive depopulation and accelerated the end of dual governance.

## Timeline
- **1765** — East India Company granted diwani (revenue collection rights) in Bengal.
- **Autumn 1768** — Crop failure begins.
- **Summer 1769** — Further crop failure and smallpox epidemic.
- **Late 1769** — Rice prices double; Company purchases large rice stocks for its army.
- **January 1770** — Company official Cartier proposes a 7% tax remission, then reverses his stance.
- **Spring 1770** — Spring harvest is scanty; Company increases taxes by 10%.
- **May 1770** — Famine explodes into mass starvation and death.
- **October 1769 - April 1770** — Limited relief efforts, including rice distribution centers, are implemented.
- **Summer 1770** — Monsoon rains return but bring disease to the enfeebled population.
- **Post-1770** — Depopulation, piracy increases, and deserted villages become common.
- **Post-famine** — The famine hastens the end of dual governance; the Company becomes sole administrator.

## Body

### Background and Causes
The famine occurred in Bengal, then under the East India Company's control following military victories at Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764). The immediate triggers were crop failures in autumn 1768 and summer 1769, compounded by a smallpox epidemic. However, structural factors worsened the crisis. The Company, lacking trained administrators, farmed out tax collection, creating uncertainty. It also purchased large rice quantities for its army, while Company officials and their Indian agents (Gomasthas) created local grain monopolies. In Bihar, the passage of armies through drought-stricken areas further strained resources.

### Company Response and Mitigation
The East India Company's response was inadequate. Despite early warnings in September 1769, no comprehensive relief plan was devised. The Company did open some rice distribution centers in Murshidabad, Calcutta, and Burdwan, feeding about 3,000 people daily at a cost of 75 rupees. However, overall relief was minimal—about 4,000 pounds of rice over six months. Crucially, the Company did not reduce taxes; it even increased them by 10% in 1770 to meet revenue targets. Export-import embargoes failed, as the province lacked money to pay for scarce goods, causing trade to cease.

### Death Toll and Demographic Impact
Contemporary estimates suggested catastrophic loss of life. The Court of Directors initially estimated about one-third of the population (approx. 10 million) perished. Later reports claimed 500 deaths daily in Murshidabad alone, with cannibalism reported. Modern scholarship, however, considers these figures inflated. Historian Rajat Dutta suggests a revised toll of about 1.2 million deaths (4-5% of the population), noting the lack of reliable demographic data outside Calcutta in 1770. The famine devastated traditional livelihoods, particularly among spinners and weavers who had no food reserves. Depopulation was uneven, severe in north Bengal and Bihar, moderate in central Bengal, and slight in the east.

### Aftermath and Legacy
The 1770 monsoon brought marginal relief but also disease. The following year, most land lacked tillers. The famine's economic and cultural impact was long-lasting. It hastened the end of dual governance, with the East India Company becoming Bengal's sole administrator. A century later, the famine inspired Bankim Chandra Chatterjee's influential novel *Anandamath*.

## Terms
- **Diwani**: The right to collect revenue, granted to the East India Company by the Mughal emperor in 1765.
- **Nizamat**: Control of civil administration, which remained with the Mughal governor (Nawab) until after the famine.
- **Gomasthas**: Indian agents or factors employed by the East India Company's private servants.
- **Maund**: A unit of weight used in the Indian subcontinent, approximately 37 kg.
- **Seer**: A unit of weight/volume, approximately 0.93 liters.
- **Naib Nazim**: A deputy governor or viceroy in the Mughal administrative system.
- **Dual governance**: The period when the East India Company controlled revenue collection (diwani) while the Nawab retained civil administration (nizamat).

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary scholarly debate concerns the famine's death toll. Contemporary British estimates of 7-10 million deaths (25-33% of the population) are widely cited but contested. Modern historians like Rajat Dutta and Tim Dyson argue these figures are "inflated" and "barely credible," suggesting a toll closer to 1.2 million. This revisionism is based on the lack of reliable demographic data in 1770 and the mobility of the agrarian population. Another open question is the extent to which the East India Company's policies—such as tax collection methods, grain purchases for the army, and official monopolies—directly caused or merely exacerbated a natural disaster.
