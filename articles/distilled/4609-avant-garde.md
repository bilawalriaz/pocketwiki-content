# Avant-garde

## Overview
The avant-garde refers to innovative, experimental art and artists that challenge established aesthetic, social, and political norms. Originating as a military term for an advance guard, it was adopted in 19th-century France to describe progressive political reformers and later applied to artistic movements that use innovation to provoke societal change. The concept remains central to discussions of modernism, postmodernism, and the relationship between art and commerce.

## Timeline
- **1824** — Henri de Saint-Simon uses "vanguard" politically, urging artists to lead social reform.
- **Late 19th C.** — Avant-garde emerges as a term for art using aesthetic innovation for political/social change.
- **1915–1920s** — Dada movement exemplifies avant-garde socio-cultural disruption.
- **1939** — Clement Greenberg's essay "Avant-Garde and Kitsch" contrasts avant-garde with mass culture.
- **1939** — Walter Benjamin's essay "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" analyzes art's "aura."
- **1947** — Adorno and Horkheimer's *Dialectic of Enlightenment* critiques the culture industry.
- **1957–1972** — Situationist International continues avant-garde socio-cultural critique.
- **1960s–1970s** — American language poets represent postmodern avant-garde.
- **1960s** — Avant-garde jazz flourishes with artists like Ornette Coleman and John Coltrane.
- **1962** — Renato Poggioli publishes *The Theory of the Avant-Garde*.
- **1967** — Guy Debord's *The Society of the Spectacle* critiques capitalist co-optation.
- **1974** — Peter Bürger's *Theory of the Avant-Garde* analyzes institutional co-optation.

## Body

### Definition and Origins
The term "avant-garde" (French for "advance guard") is a military metaphor for a unit scouting ahead of an army. In 19th-century French politics, it identified left-wing reformists. By the mid-19th century, it described art that used aesthetic innovation for political and social change. Since the 20th century, it identifies a stratum of the intelligentsia—novelists, artists, architects—whose experimental work challenges the cultural values of bourgeois society.

### Theoretical Analysis
Theorists have analyzed the avant-garde's social function. Renato Poggioli (1962) surveyed its historical and philosophical aspects, noting shared values with bohemianism. Peter Bürger (1974) argued that the art establishment co-opts socially critical works, neutralizing their political content. Benjamin H. D. Buchloh (2000) advocates a dialectical approach to the political stances of avant-garde artists.

### Society and Co-optation
Sociologically, avant-garde artists produce works that oppose mainstream society's conformist values. Clement Greenberg (1939) stated the avant-garde opposes both high culture and the "dumbing down" of mass culture, which he termed "kitsch." Walter Benjamin (1939) and Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer (1947) argued that the capitalist culture industry voids art's "aura" through mechanical reproduction, making profitability the measure of value. Guy Debord (1967) and Paul Mann (1991) later argued that the avant-garde has been economically integrated into the very establishment it opposed, raising doubts about its continued relevance. Scholars have also noted troubling associations between some avant-garde figures (e.g., Ezra Pound, F.T. Marinetti) and authoritarian politics.

### Arrière-garde
The avant-garde is often defined in contrast to the "arrière-garde" (rearguard). Art historians Natalie Adamson and Toby Norris argue it is not merely reactionary but can refer to artists engaging with the avant-garde's legacy while aware of its anachronism. Critic Charles Altieri posits the two are interdependent.

### Examples Across Media
**Music:** Avant-garde music radically departs from tradition. Key 20th-century figures include Arnold Schoenberg, John Cage, and Karlheinz Stockhausen. Composer Larry Sitsky distinguishes avant-gardists from modernists, stating the latter's work was not conceived to "goad an audience." The 1960s saw free jazz pioneers like Ornette Coleman, while 1970s rock used "avant-garde" to mean aggressively experimental.
**Theatre:** Avant-garde traditions are pronounced in theatre and performance art, with movements like Fluxus, Happenings, and Neo-Dada.
**Photography:** Avant-garde photography experiments with the medium's procedures and perception. Early 20th-century Bauhaus figures like László Moholy-Nagy promoted cameraless techniques (photograms). Constructivists like Aleksandr Rodchenko used dramatic angles to create a "new vision." In interwar Japan, the "New Photography" movement was influenced by Surrealism.

## Terms
- **Avant-garde:** Experimental art and artists that challenge established norms, using innovation to provoke change.
- **Arrière-garde:** A rearguard force; in art, artists engaging with the avant-garde's legacy while aware of its anachronism.
- **Kitsch:** Mass-produced, commercial art considered to be in poor taste but appreciated ironically; contrasted with avant-garde art.
- **Culture Industry:** The commercial production of culture (publishing, film, music) by capitalist systems, which critics argue standardizes and commodifies art.
- **Aura:** The unique, authentic quality of an original artwork, which Walter Benjamin argued is diminished by mechanical reproduction.
- **Intelligentsia:** A class of people in society who are involved in intellectual work and are often politically progressive.
- **Bourgeoisie:** The middle class, whose social and cultural values the avant-garde often opposed.
- **Modernism:** A broad movement (late 19th–mid 20th C.) characterized by experimentation and a break with tradition, which the avant-garde is a part of but sometimes distinguished from.
- **Postmodernism:** A movement following modernism, often characterized by skepticism toward grand narratives and the blending of high and low culture.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Co-optation and Relevance:** Has the avant-garde been neutralized by its integration into the capitalist art market and culture industry, losing its critical edge?
- **Political Associations:** How should the legacy of avant-garde figures who allied with authoritarian or fascist movements be understood and assessed?
- **Definition vs. Modernism:** Is "avant-garde" a distinct category from "modernism," defined by its explicit political and social aims rather than just formal innovation?
- **Postmodern Status:** Has the concept of the avant-garde become redundant in a postmodern era where the boundaries between high art and mass culture have blurred?
