# Khazars

## Overview
The Khazars were a semi-nomadic Turkic people who established a major commercial empire in the late 6th century CE, dominating the Volga-Don steppes, Crimea, and the North Caucasus for about three centuries (c. 650–965). As a powerful buffer state between the Byzantine Empire and the Islamic Caliphates, Khazaria became a key crossroads on the Silk Road, facilitating trade between Europe and Asia. The Khazar elite's conversion to Judaism in the 8th century makes their state a unique and debated case in medieval history, with lasting scholarly interest in their origins, culture, and legacy.

## Timeline
- **c. 552–630** — Khazar tribes emerge from the breakup of the Western Turkic Khaganate.
- **c. 650–670** — A properly constituted Khazar Khaganate forms, becoming a major power.
- **722–737** — The Arab-Khazar wars culminate with a major Abbasid campaign into Khazar territory.
- **c. 740–920** — The Khazar royalty and nobility convert to Rabbinic Judaism.
- **830s** — Construction of the fortress Sarkel and minting of autonomous Khazar coinage.
- **862 onwards** — Varangian Rus' begin probing south along Khazar-controlled waterways.
- **913** — A major Varangian raid through Khazar territory leads to conflict.
- **965** — Sviatoslav I of Kiev destroys the Khazar capital of Atil, ending Khazar imperial power.
- **986** — Khazar Jews are present at the religious disputation in Kiev for Vladimir the Great.
- **1016** — A joint Rus'-Byzantine attack defeats the last known Khazar ruler, Georgius Tzul.

## Body

### Origins and State Formation
The Khazars were a confederation of Turkic-speaking peoples, not a single ethnic union. Their precise origins are uncertain due to the lack of surviving records in the Khazar language. The ruling elite likely hailed from the Ashina tribe of the Western Turkic Khaganate. The Khazar state took shape from the ruins of this empire after its disintegration under pressure from the Tang dynasty (c. 630–650). By c. 670, a fully formed Khazar Khaghanate emerged, subjugating the Onoghur-Bulghar union and establishing a "Pax Khazarica" that secured trade routes.

### Government and Society
Khazaria was a diarchy, with a sacral *khagan* (king) and a *khagan-bek* (bek) who managed military and administrative affairs. The ruling elite, possibly nine clans, were ethnically and linguistically distinct from the subject peoples, which included Oghuric Turkic tribes and Alano-As. The state controlled 25 to 30 different nations and maintained a standing army. The capital, Atil on the Volga, was divided into a western sector for the Khazar elite and an eastern sector for merchants, slaves, and adherents of various faiths.

### Economy and Trade
The Khazar economy was a self-sufficient "Saltovo" model combining pastoralism, agriculture, fishing, and craft production. Its primary wealth came from taxing international trade as a crossroads between Europe and the Muslim world. The Khazar slave trade was a major supplier of slaves to the Abbasid Caliphate. The state's revenue also came from tribute and customs duties, adjudicated by a commercial tribunal in Atil with judges for each religious community.

### Foreign Relations and Decline
Khazaria served as a crucial buffer between Byzantium and the Caliphates, forming dynastic and military alliances with the Byzantine Empire. It fought a series of wars with the Umayyad and Abbasid Caliphates in the 7th and 8th centuries. From the 9th century, Khazar power was undermined by the rise of the Rus', Pecheneg incursions, and a shift in Islamic trade routes that reduced its revenue. The final blow came in the 960s when Sviatoslav I of Kiev sacked Atil, leading to the state's disintegration by the mid-11th century.

### Religion: Tengrism and Conversion to Judaism
The Khazars' native religion was likely Tengrism, a Turkic shamanistic faith centered on the sky god Tengri. In the 8th century, the Khazar royalty and nobility converted to Rabbinic Judaism. The exact date and scope of the conversion are debated, but it is seen as a strategic move to maintain independence from both Christian Byzantium and the Muslim Caliphates. While the elite converted, the multiethnic population remained religiously diverse, including pagans, Christians, Muslims, and Jews.

## Terms
*   **Khagan (Qağan):** The sacral, supreme king of the Khazar state, whose role was primarily ceremonial and ritualistic.
*   **Khagan-Bek (Bek):** The deputy ruler and military commander of the Khazar state, who managed daily administration.
*   **Tengrism:** The traditional Turkic shamanistic religion centered on the worship of the sky god Tengri.
*   **Pax Khazarica:** The period of Khazar dominance (c. 7th–10th centuries) that ensured safe passage for merchants across the Eurasian steppes.
*   **Saltovo Economy:** A self-sufficient economic model combining pastoralism, agriculture, and craft production, named after the Saltovo-Mayaki archaeological culture.
*   **Diarchy:** A system of dual leadership, here referring to the joint rule of the khagan and the bek.
*   **Oghuric:** A branch of Turkic languages spoken by subject tribes within Khazaria, such as the Bulgars.
*   **Tudun:** An administrative official governing a settlement or district within the Khazar state.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Origins and Language:** The precise ethnic composition and language of the Khazar elite remain uncertain due to a lack of indigenous records.
*   **Jewish Conversion:** Scholars debate the exact date (c. 740–920 CE), the depth of conversion among the general population, and whether it was a strategic political move or a genuine mass religious shift.
*   **The "Khazar-Ashkenazi" Hypothesis:** A highly contested theory suggests that Ashkenazi Jews are primarily descended from Khazar converts. Most genetic and historical studies do not support this, viewing any Khazar contribution as minor. The theory is sometimes associated with antisemitic polemics.
*   **Legacy and Descendants:** Claims of Khazar ancestry are made by various groups (e.g., some Crimean Karaites, Kumyks, and Cossacks), but these connections are generally viewed with skepticism by specialists.
