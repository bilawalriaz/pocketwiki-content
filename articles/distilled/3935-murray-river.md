# Murray River

## Overview
The Murray River is Australia's longest river, stretching 2,508 km (1,558 mi) and forming the core of the Murray–Darling basin, the nation's most important irrigated agricultural region. Its flow is highly variable, and its health has been significantly impacted by human regulation, making it a focal point for environmental and water management debates.

- **c. 600,000 years ago** — Lake Bungunnia drains, marking a shift to more arid conditions.

- **1824** — Hamilton Hume and William Hovell cross the river at present-day Albury.
- **1830** — Captain Charles Sturt navigates the river and names it the Murray.
- **1853** — First paddle steamers operate on the river.
- **1915** — The River Murray Agreement is signed by NSW, Victoria, and South Australia.
- **1922–1937** — Thirteen locks and weirs are completed on the river.
- **1935–1940** — A series of barrages are built near the Murray Mouth.
- **1956** — A major flood inundates many towns in South Australia.
- **1979** — Lake Dartmouth reservoir is completed.
- **2000–2007** — Severe droughts stress the river system and its forests.

## Body
### Geography and Flow
The Murray rises in the Australian Alps and flows northwest, forming the border between New South Wales and Victoria before entering South Australia. It turns south at Morgan to reach Lake Alexandrina and empties into the Southern Ocean via the Murray Mouth. The river carries a small volume of water compared to global rivers of similar size, with great annual variability. It has dried up completely three times since official records began. The border between NSW and Victoria is defined as the top of the southern (Victorian) bank, placing the entire river within NSW until it enters South Australia.

### Ancient and Geological History
Between 2.5 and 0.5 million years ago, the river terminated in Lake Bungunnia, a vast freshwater lake covering 33,000 km². Its draining approximately 600,000 years ago signaled the onset of widespread arid conditions. About 25,000 years ago, displacement along the Cadell Fault raised the eastern edge of the floodplain, diverting the river and creating the conditions for the famous Barmah river red gum forests. This event also created the Barmah Choke, a narrow section that restricts water flow.

### Ecology and Environment
The river supports unique native species, including the Murray cod, Murray short-necked turtle, and river red gum forests. Its health has declined since European settlement due to flow regulation, drought, and introduced pest species like European carp, which degrade aquatic plants and increase turbidity. The 1956 flood was the most significant in recorded history. Since the early 2000s, continuous dredging at the Murray Mouth has been required to prevent it from silting up and to maintain the Coorong lagoon system.

### Human History and Transport
Roonka Flat near Blanchetown shows Aboriginal occupation since at least 7000 BC. The river features prominently in Aboriginal mythology, with stories of the ancestor Ngurunderi chasing the Murray cod Pondi, whose path carved the river. European exploration began in the 1820s. In the 19th century, the river supported a major paddle-steamer trade, which peaked around 1874 before declining due to railways. Today, traffic is primarily recreational.

### Water Storage and Regulation
To support irrigation and navigation, the river was extensively regulated. The 1915 River Murray Agreement led to the construction of locks, weirs, and large reservoirs like Lake Hume (1936) and Lake Dartmouth (1979). Barrages were built near the mouth (1935-1940) to prevent seawater intrusion. These structures inverted the river's natural seasonal flow, boosting agriculture but disrupting ecosystems and contributing to dryland salinity. As of 2010, the system received only 58% of its natural flow.

## Terms
- **Murray–Darling basin:** The catchment area of the Murray and its tributaries, covering about one-seventh of Australia.
- **Barmah Choke:** A narrow section of the Murray River that restricts water flow.
- **Cadell Fault:** A geological fault whose displacement about 25,000 years ago altered the river's course.
- **Lake Bungunnia:** A vast ancient freshwater lake that existed where the Murray now flows.
- **Locks and weirs:** Structures built to raise water levels for navigation and irrigation.
- **Barrages:** Barriers built near the river mouth to prevent seawater from entering during low flow.
- **Dryland salinity:** The rising of salt in soil and water due to land clearing and irrigation.
- **Turbidity:** The cloudiness of water caused by suspended particles.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The long-term salinity of the river is a major concern, as it supplies 40% of Adelaide's water.
- There is ongoing disagreement between states and groups over water allocation and environmental management.
- The proposed Wellington Weir in South Australia has been controversial.
- The effectiveness of dredging at the Murray Mouth versus restoring natural flow is a point of discussion.
