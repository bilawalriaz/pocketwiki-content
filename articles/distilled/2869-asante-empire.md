# Asante Empire

## Overview
The Asante Empire (1701–1902) was a powerful Akan state in West Africa, centered in modern-day Ghana. It was renowned for its military strength, sophisticated bureaucracy, and control of the gold trade. Its history is marked by internal consolidation, regional expansion, and a series of wars with the British Empire, culminating in its incorporation into the Gold Coast colony.

## Timeline
- **c. 1695–1717** — Reign of Osei Tutu I, who established the Asante Kingdom and the Golden Stool as a unifying symbol.
- **1701** — Asante defeats Denkyira at the Battle of Feyiase, gaining access to coastal trade.
- **1717** — Death of Osei Tutu I in war with Akyem; internal crisis follows.
- **1720s** — Reign of Opoku Ware I; major expansion into Bono, Gyaman, and northern regions.
- **1824** — First Anglo-Ashanti War begins; Asante defeats British forces and kills General Sir Charles MacCarthy.
- **1874** — Third Anglo-Ashanti War; British forces sack the capital, Kumasi.
- **1896** — Fourth Anglo-Ashanti War; Asantehene Prempeh I is deposed and exiled by the British.
- **1900** — War of the Golden Stool (Fifth Anglo-Ashanti War) led by Queen Yaa Asantewaa.
- **1902** — Asante Empire formally annexed by Britain as part of the Gold Coast colony.
- **1935** — British restore the Asante Confederacy as a traditional state under colonial oversight.
- **1957** — Ghana gains independence; the Asante Kingdom becomes a sub-national traditional state within the new nation.

## Body

### Origins and Foundation
The Asante people trace their origins to the Akan heartland, particularly the regions of Amansie and Adanse. By the 15th century, migrations from these areas led to the founding of towns like Kumasi. In the late 17th century, the Oyoko clan, under leaders like Oti Akenten, consolidated power in the Kumasi area. The Asante Kingdom was formally established by Osei Tutu I and his spiritual adviser Okomfo Anokye. They used the Golden Stool—a sacred symbol believed to contain the *sunsum* (spirit) of the Asante people—to unify the various states. In 1701, they defeated the dominant regional power, Denkyira, securing independence and access to European trade on the coast.

### Expansion and Regional Dominance
After 1701, the Asante Empire expanded aggressively under rulers like Opoku Ware I. It conquered neighboring states such as Bono, Gyaman, and Wassa, and extended its influence northward to the Dagomba kingdom. The empire's economy was based on gold mining, agriculture (including kola nuts), and trade. It established a sophisticated bureaucracy in Kumasi with a Foreign Office to manage relations with European powers. The Asante military, organized into a disciplined army with medical units, was crucial for this expansion and for maintaining control over a diverse, tributary-based empire.

### Relations with the British and the Anglo-Ashanti Wars
From the early 19th century, the Asante Empire clashed with the British, who were allied with coastal states like the Fante. Five Anglo-Ashanti Wars were fought between 1824 and 1900, primarily over control of the coastal territories and trade. The Asante won early victories, including the killing of British General Sir Charles MacCarthy in 1824. However, British technological superiority eventually prevailed. In 1874, British forces sacked Kumasi. The final blow came in 1896 when the British deposed and exiled the Asantehene, Prempeh I. A last major resistance, the War of the Golden Stool (1900), led by Queen Yaa Asantewaa, was also defeated.

### Government and Society
The Asante state was a hierarchical confederacy headed by the Asantehene (king), who was advised by the Asanteman council of paramount chiefs. Power was checked by a system of councils and the role of the Queen Mother (*Ohenemaa*), who was influential in the selection of rulers. The empire was divided into metropolitan and provincial states, each with its own chief. Society was matrilineal, with descent and inheritance traced through the mother's line. The legal system, based on the "Seventy-Seven Laws of Komfo Anokye," was theocratic, viewing crimes as sins against ancestors and the gods.

### Economy and Military
The Asante economy was driven by gold, agricultural exports, and trade. Infrastructure included a network of roads patrolled by wardens. The military was a formidable force, with a potential strength of 80,000 to 200,000 men. It was organized into advance guards, main bodies, and flanking units, and was adept at forest warfare. While firearms were primary weapons, indigenous organization and leadership were key to its effectiveness. The army supported the empire's expansion for over a century and resisted European colonization.

## Terms
- **Asantehene**: The king and supreme ruler of the Asante Empire.
- **Golden Stool (Sika dwa)**: A sacred symbol of Asante unity and political authority, believed to house the spirit (*sunsum*) of the nation.
- **Abusua**: The matrilineal clan system, which determined identity, inheritance, and political office.
- **Amanhene**: Paramount chiefs who ruled the various states within the Asante confederacy.
- **Ohenemaa**: The Queen Mother, a powerful political figure who played a key role in the nomination and selection of chiefs.
- **Obirempon**: A title for wealthy and influential entrepreneurs or "big men" who contributed to the state's economy and held political power.
- **Adinkra**: Symbols used in Asante culture for decoration, communication, and to convey philosophical concepts.
- **Sunsum**: The spirit or soul, as in the spirit of the Asante people believed to reside in the Golden Stool.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Scholars debate the precise nature of the pre-Asante "Akani" or "Arcania" polities—whether they were a centralized kingdom, a confederation, or a trading society.
- Historians like Larry Yarak and Ivor Wilks disagree on the relative power of the Asante bureaucracy versus the Asantehene himself, though both agree it was a highly developed system.
- The extent to which the Asante economy depended on the Atlantic slave trade versus domestic production and other trade is debated. Some historians argue it was not primarily based on slave-raiding for export.
