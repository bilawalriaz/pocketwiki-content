# Cook Strait

## Overview
Cook Strait is the narrow, turbulent body of water separating New Zealand's North and South Islands, connecting the Tasman Sea and South Pacific Ocean. It is a critical transport corridor, a historically dangerous waterway, and a site of significant geological, ecological, and engineering interest due to its powerful tidal flows and strategic location.

## Timeline
- **c. 1642** — Dutch explorer Abel Tasman sights the strait but mistakes it for a bay.
- **1770** — James Cook becomes the first European to sail through, confirming it is a strait.
- **1820s** — Māori leader Te Rauparaha leads a migration to and conquest of the Cook Strait region.
- **1840s** — Permanent European settlements begin at Wellington, Nelson, and Whanganui.
- **1851** — The barque *Maria* wrecks, killing 26.
- **1866** — First telegraph cable laid across the strait.
- **1909** — SS *Penguin* wrecks, killing 75.
- **1964** — HVDC Inter-Island power cables installed.
- **1968** — Ferry *TEV Wahine* capsizes, killing 53.
- **1980** — Oceanographer Ron Heath publishes analysis of currents using swimmer tracks.
- **2013** — Two large earthquakes (6.5 and 6.6) strike the strait.
- **2025** — Transpower announces a $1.4 billion plan to replace the 1991 HVDC cables by 2031. |

## Body

### History and Exploration
In Māori legend, the navigator Kupe discovered the strait. Dutch explorer Abel Tasman first saw it in 1642, naming it Zeehaen's Bight, believing it was a closed bay. In 1769, James Cook established it was a navigable strait, and it was later named for him. European settlement in the 19th century was driven by whaling, with bases established in the Marlborough Sounds and Kāpiti area. The strait became the central waterway for the new colony, with towns like Wellington and Nelson clustering around it. During World War II, coastal fortifications were built to defend the strait.

### Geography and Oceanography
Cook Strait is 22 km wide at its narrowest point. It was formed about 7,000 years ago when rising sea levels separated the North and South Islands. The strait is dominated by exceptionally strong tidal flows. This is because the main lunar tide circulates anti-clockwise around New Zealand, making the tidal elevations at each end of the strait almost exactly out of phase—high water on one side meets low water on the other. This difference drives currents up to 2.5 m/s (5 knots). The complex submarine topography, including canyons and basins, creates violent eddies and turbulence comparable to the Straits of Gibraltar.

### Transport and Shipwrecks
Regular ferry services operate between Picton and Wellington, a 93 km journey taking about 3.5 hours. The strait's exposure to the "roaring forties" makes it one of the world's most dangerous and unpredictable waterways, frequently disrupting sailings. This danger is evidenced by numerous shipwrecks with significant loss of life, including the *Maria* (1851), *City of Dunedin* (1865), *SS Penguin* (1909), and *TEV Wahine* (1968). The first ferry carrying rail vehicles began in 1962, and the first fast ferry in 1994. Proposals for a bridge or tunnel have been made, but a tunnel is estimated to cost NZ$50 billion.

### Infrastructure and Energy
The strait is crossed by critical infrastructure. The first telegraph cable was laid in 1866. The HVDC Inter-Island link, consisting of submarine power cables, was installed in 1964 and replaced in 1991, with a capacity of 1200 MW. A planned replacement and upgrade is scheduled for completion by 2031. The strait's powerful tides have been identified as a potential source of tidal energy, with experimental turbine projects proposed, though none have been built as of 2016.

### Marine Life and Swimming
Cook Strait is an important habitat for cetaceans, including dolphins, killer whales, and whales that attract winter whaling historically and modern surveys. A colony of fur seals lives on the Wellington coast. The strait is also a challenging open-water swim. According to tradition, the first swimmer was Hine Poupou. In modern times, Barrie Devenport made the first crossing in 1962. By 2010, 74 single crossings had been made. Swimmers' tracks have been used to study the strait's complex currents.

## Terms
- **Strait:** A narrow passage of water connecting two larger bodies of water.
- **Tidal flow:** The horizontal movement of water caused by the rise and fall of the tide.
- **Out of phase:** When the high tide at one end of the strait coincides with the low tide at the other.
- **HVDC:** High-Voltage Direct Current, a system for transmitting electricity efficiently over long distances, like under the sea.
- **Cetacean:** A marine mammal of the order including whales, dolphins, and porpoises.
- **Bathymetry:** The measurement of depth of water in oceans, seas, or lakes.
- **Virtual amphidrome:** A point in the ocean around which tidal lines rotate, but which is not a point of zero tidal range.

## Debates and Open questions
- The precise estimate of the "residual current" (net current after averaging out tides) in Cook Strait remains a topic of ongoing research, with computer simulations and large datasets being used to refine it.
- The feasibility and cost-effectiveness of harnessing Cook Strait's tidal energy for power generation are still under investigation, as proposed projects have not yet been realized.
