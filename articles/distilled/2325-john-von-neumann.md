# John von Neumann

## Overview
John von Neumann (1903–1957) was a Hungarian-American mathematician and polymath whose work spanned pure mathematics, quantum mechanics, economics, and computer science. He established the mathematical foundations for quantum mechanics, pioneered game theory, and was a key figure in the development of the modern computer and the hydrogen bomb. His extraordinary intellectual range and contributions to both theoretical and applied fields make him one of the most influential scientists of the 20th century.

## Timeline
- **1903** — Born in Budapest, Hungary.
- **1926** — Earns a chemical engineering degree from ETH Zurich and a PhD in mathematics from the University of Budapest.
- **1928** — Proves the minimax theorem, founding game theory.
- **1930** — Independently discovers key consequences of Gödel's incompleteness theorems.
- **1932** — Publishes *Mathematical Foundations of Quantum Mechanics*.
- **1933** — Accepts a professorship at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton.
- **1936-1940** — Publishes foundational papers on von Neumann algebras.
- **1944** — Co-authors *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior*.
- **1945** — Writes the *First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC*, describing stored-program computer architecture.
- **1946** — Co-develops the Fuchs–von Neumann patent for a thermonuclear weapon.
- **1950** — Leads the first numerical weather forecasts using the ENIAC computer.
- **1955** — Becomes a commissioner of the U.S. Atomic Energy Commission.
- **1957** — Dies of cancer in Washington, D.C.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born into a wealthy Jewish family in Budapest, von Neumann was a child prodigy who mastered calculus by age eight and published major mathematical papers by nineteen. He earned simultaneous degrees in chemical engineering and mathematics, then studied under David Hilbert in Göttingen. He became the youngest *Privatdozent* at the University of Berlin before moving to Princeton University in 1929 and joining the newly formed Institute for Advanced Study in 1933.

### Mathematics
Von Neumann's mathematical work was vast. He provided an axiomatization of set theory that resolved paradoxes like Russell's. In functional analysis, he was the first to axiomatically define an abstract Hilbert space, providing the mathematical language for quantum mechanics. He founded the study of operator algebras (von Neumann algebras) and made foundational contributions to ergodic theory, measure theory, and lattice theory, where he created the field of continuous geometry.

### Physics
Von Neumann created the rigorous mathematical framework for quantum mechanics, reducing its physics to the mathematics of Hilbert spaces and linear operators. He introduced the density matrix formalism and the von Neumann entropy. He also made significant contributions to fluid dynamics, including the Taylor–von Neumann–Sedov blast wave solution and the co-discovery of the ZND detonation model.

### Economics and Game Theory
He founded game theory as a mathematical discipline, proving the minimax theorem for zero-sum games. With Oskar Morgenstern, he published *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior*, which argued for using functional analysis over differential calculus in economics. He also developed an influential model of an expanding economy, introducing fixed-point theorems and linear inequalities into economic theory.

### Computer Science
Von Neumann was a founding figure in computing. He designed the IAS machine and described the "von Neumann architecture" for stored-program computers in the *First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC*. He invented the merge sort algorithm, contributed to the Monte Carlo method, and pioneered scientific computing. His work on cellular automata and the universal constructor analyzed self-replication before the discovery of DNA's structure.

### Defense Work and Later Life
During World War II, von Neumann was a key consultant for the Manhattan Project, developing the mathematical models for the implosive lenses used in the Fat Man bomb. He later chaired committees instrumental in the development of the U.S. intercontinental ballistic missile (ICBM) program. In 1955, he became a commissioner of the Atomic Energy Commission. He died in 1957 from cancer, possibly linked to radiation exposure.

## Terms
- **Hilbert Space:** An abstract vector space with an inner product, allowing for the generalization of Euclidean geometry to infinite dimensions. It is the mathematical foundation of quantum mechanics.
- **Von Neumann Algebra:** A *-algebra of bounded operators on a Hilbert space that is closed in the weak operator topology and contains the identity operator. A key structure in operator theory and quantum physics.
- **Minimax Theorem:** A theorem in game theory stating that in zero-sum games with perfect information, there exists a pair of optimal strategies for each player that minimizes their maximum possible loss.
- **Stored-Program Computer:** A computer architecture where both data and program instructions are stored in the same memory, a concept described in von Neumann's *First Draft* report.
- **Cellular Automaton:** A discrete model studied in computer science and mathematics, consisting of a grid of cells, each in one of a finite number of states, evolving through discrete time steps according to fixed rules.
- **Von Neumann Entropy:** A measure of the uncertainty or mixedness of a quantum state, given by the formula S(ρ) = -Tr(ρ ln ρ), where ρ is the density matrix.
- **Density Matrix:** A matrix used in quantum mechanics to represent the state of a system, allowing for the description of both pure and mixed states.
- **Ergodic Theory:** A branch of mathematics that studies the long-term average behavior of dynamical systems with an invariant measure.
- **Artificial Viscosity:** A numerical technique used in computational fluid dynamics to smooth shock wave discontinuities, co-developed by von Neumann and Robert D. Richtmyer.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Von Neumann's Proof Against Hidden Variables:** His 1932 proof that quantum mechanics cannot be based on hidden variables was later shown by Grete Hermann (1935) and John S. Bell (1966) to contain a conceptual error. However, some scholars argue the proof remains valid against a specific, important class of hidden variable theories.
- **Quantum Logic:** Von Neumann and Garrett Birkhoff proposed that quantum mechanics requires a non-distributive logic. Von Neumann himself was never satisfied with this work, considering it an unsolved problem for future mathematicians.
- **The Measurement Problem:** Von Neumann's analysis suggested that the collapse of the wave function could be attributed to the consciousness of the observer, an interpretation that never gained majority acceptance among physicists.
