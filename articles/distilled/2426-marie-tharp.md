# Marie Tharp

## Overview
Marie Tharp (1920–2006) was an American geologist and oceanographic cartographer whose work in the 1950s and 1960s was foundational to the acceptance of plate tectonics. Collaborating with geologist Bruce Heezen, she produced the first comprehensive scientific maps of the Atlantic Ocean floor, revealing a detailed topography that included a central rift valley along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. This discovery provided critical evidence for the theories of continental drift and seafloor spreading, fundamentally changing our understanding of Earth's geology.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Marie Tharp was born in 1920 in Ypsilanti, Michigan. Her father, a soil surveyor, introduced her to mapmaking through fieldwork. The family's frequent moves meant she attended over 17 schools before graduating high school in Ohio. She initially studied English and music at Ohio University, graduating in 1943. During World War II, with men away at war, she entered the previously male-dominated field of geology, earning a master's degree in petroleum geology from the University of Michigan in 1944. Her early career at an oil company was limited to desk work, as women were barred from fieldwork.

### Career at Lamont and Collaboration with Heezen
In 1948, Tharp moved to New York and was hired at Columbia University's Lamont Geological Observatory, becoming one of its first female scientists. She began working with geologist Bruce Heezen, who collected bathymetric (ocean depth) data from the research ship *Vema*. For 18 years, Tharp, barred from shipboard work, plotted this data to create maps of the ocean floor. This collaboration represented the first systematic attempt to map the entire ocean floor.

### Discovery of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and Rift Valley
Using echo sounder data, Tharp identified a continuous, V-shaped structure running along the axis of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, which she hypothesized was a rift valley formed by the seafloor being pulled apart. Heezen initially dismissed this idea as it supported the then-controversial theory of continental drift. However, when Tharp overlaid her bathymetric profile with a map of earthquake epicenters plotted by another researcher, the earthquakes aligned perfectly with her proposed rift valley. This correlation convinced Heezen and provided powerful evidence for seafloor spreading and plate tectonics.

### Mapping and Legacy
Due to Cold War restrictions, Tharp and Heezen initially published their maps in a realistic, physiographic style to avoid military classification. Tharp's name was omitted from major papers published between 1959 and 1963. She continued mapping, demonstrating the rift valley extended into the South Atlantic and finding similar structures in other oceans, suggesting a global rift system. The definitive world ocean floor map was published in 1977. After Heezen's death, Tharp continued at Columbia until 1983 and later received major awards, including the 1978 National Geographic Society’s Hubbard Medal.

## Terms
- **Bathymetry**: The measurement of water depth in oceans, seas, or lakes; used to map the topography of the ocean floor.
- **Mid-Atlantic Ridge**: A divergent tectonic plate boundary located on the floor of the Atlantic Ocean, forming a continuous submarine mountain range.
- **Rift Valley**: A lowland region that forms where Earth's tectonic plates are pulling apart, as identified by Tharp along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge.
- **Plate Tectonics**: The scientific theory that Earth's outer shell is divided into several plates that glide over the mantle, explaining continental drift and seafloor spreading.
- **Continental Drift**: The hypothesis that Earth's continents have moved over geological time relative to each other, a precursor to plate tectonics.
- **Echo Sounder**: A device used to measure water depth by transmitting sound pulses and timing their return from the seafloor.
- **Physiographic Map**: A map that depicts the physical features and landscape of an area, such as Tharp's realistic-style ocean floor maps.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary debate Tharp faced was the scientific resistance to continental drift. Her initial hypothesis of a rift valley was rejected by her collaborator, Bruce Heezen, because it supported this controversial theory. The acceptance of her discovery required corroboration from independent data (earthquake epicenters). A secondary issue was the systematic exclusion of her name from early major publications, reflecting the gender biases of the era. The source notes her name did not appear on key papers between 1959 and 1963.
