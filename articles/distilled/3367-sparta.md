# Sparta

## Overview
Sparta was a powerful ancient Greek city-state (polis) in Laconia, known as Lacedaemon. It became the dominant land military power in Greece during the 7th century BC, maintaining this status until its defeat at the Battle of Leuctra in 371 BC. Its unique, militarized social system and constitution, attributed to the lawgiver Lycurgus, made it a subject of fascination in its own time and throughout later Western history.

## Timeline
- **c. 8th–7th century BC** — Civil strife leads to the "Lycurgan Reforms," establishing Sparta's unique social and political system.
- **6th century BC** — Sparta founds the Peloponnesian League, achieving hegemony over most of the Peloponnese.
- **480 BC** — King Leonidas leads a legendary last stand at the Battle of Thermopylae against the Persians.
- **479 BC** — Sparta leads the Greek alliance to victory at the Battle of Plataea, ending the Persian invasion.
- **464 BC** — A major earthquake devastates Sparta, contributing to the outbreak of the First Peloponnesian War.
- **431–404 BC** — Sparta leads the Peloponnesian League against Athens, emerging victorious.
- **Early 4th century BC** — Spartan hegemony peaks; armies under Agesilaus II campaign in Anatolia.
- **371 BC** — Theban general Epaminondas decisively defeats Sparta at the Battle of Leuctra, ending its hegemony.
- **331 BC** — Spartan King Agis III is defeated and killed by Macedon at the Battle of Megalopolis.
- **192 BC** — Sparta is forced into the Achaean League, losing its political independence.
- **396 AD** — Sparta is sacked by the Visigoths under Alaric I.
- **1834 AD** — Modern Sparta is re-founded by decree of King Otto of Greece.

## Body

### Names and Geography
The ancient Greeks used three terms: "Sparta" for the capital in the Eurotas valley; "Lacedaemon" for the broader region and state's formal name; and "Laconice" for the surrounding territory. The earliest attested form is the Mycenaean Greek "Lakedaimonian." Located in Laconia, southeastern Peloponnese, its capital was naturally fortified by the Taygetus and Parnon mountains. Though landlocked, it controlled the vassal port of Gytheio.

### Mythology and Prehistory
Spartan origin myths connect its founders to Heracles. The legendary king Lacedaemon, son of Zeus, named the country after himself and the city after his wife, Sparta. Archaeological evidence shows settlement from the Middle Neolithic period. Later tradition holds that Dorian tribes conquered the area in the late Bronze Age, though the historical Dorian state is difficult to distinguish archaeologically from earlier periods.

### Archaic and Classical History
After internal strife, Sparta implemented the Lycurgan Reforms, creating a rigid, militarized society. It expanded through conquering Messenia in the Second Messenian War, securing the helot labor essential to its economy. Sparta led Greek forces in the Greco-Persian Wars, gaining prestige at Thermopylae and Plataea. It later led the Peloponnesian League to victory over Athens in the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC). Spartan hegemony was shattered by the Theban victory at Leuctra in 371 BC, which freed Messenia, depriving Sparta of its helot labor force and causing its terminal decline. It was absorbed by the Achaean League in 192 BC and later became a tourist attraction under Roman rule.

### Social Structure and Constitution
Spartan society was a rigid oligarchy ruled by two hereditary kings, a council of elders (Gerousia), and five annually elected ephors. The population was stratified into Spartiates (full citizen-soldiers), perioikoi (free non-citizens handling trade and crafts), and helots (state-owned serfs, primarily from Messenia). The agoge, a harsh state education and military training system, was mandatory for Spartiate boys from age seven. Citizenship required completing the agoge and membership in a communal mess (syssitia). Spartiates were barred from trade and manual labor, performed by helots and perioikoi.

### Economy and Daily Life
The economy was based on agriculture, worked by helots on equal plots of land (kleroi) assigned to each Spartiate. Helots retained half their produce and were treated with extreme brutality, including ritualized killing by the secret police (Krypteia). Spartan women enjoyed unusual freedoms, including formal education, physical training, and property control. Marriage was delayed until a woman's late teens or early twenties. The system was designed to produce elite warriors, with men living communally until age 30 and remaining in the military reserve until sixty.

## Terms
- **Polis**: An ancient Greek city-state, the fundamental political and social unit.
- **Helots**: State-owned serfs, primarily from Messenia, who formed the majority of Sparta's population and worked the land.
- **Perioikoi**: Free non-citizen inhabitants who engaged in trade and craft production.
- **Agoge**: The rigorous state-sponsored education and military training system for Spartiate boys.
- **Syssitia**: Mandatory communal dining clubs for Spartiate men, essential for citizenship.
- **Kleros**: A plot of land assigned to a Spartiate citizen, worked by helots.
- **Gerousia**: The Spartan council of elders, consisting of 28 men over 60 and the two kings.
- **Ephors**: Five annually elected officials who held significant executive and judicial power.
- **Laconic**: A style of very terse, direct speech, characteristic of the Spartans.
- **Laconophilia**: Love or admiration for Sparta and its culture.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **The "Spartan Mirage"**: All surviving accounts were written by non-Spartans, who often idealized it. Scholars debate how much of the traditional image reflects reality versus literary invention.
- **Lycurgus and the Reforms**: The existence and dating of the lawgiver Lycurgus are semi-mythical. The precise nature and timing of the reforms attributed to him are debated.
- **Helot Treatment and Revolts**: The scale and nature of helot revolts, and the exact methods of Spartan control (like the Krypteia), are subjects of scholarly discussion, based on often hostile sources.
- **Spartan Decline**: The relative importance of factors like the declining citizen population, the loss of Messenia, and military defeats in causing Sparta's long-term decline is analyzed by historians.
