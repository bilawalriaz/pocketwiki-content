# Cheese

## Overview
Cheese is a dairy product created by coagulating the milk protein casein, separating solid curds from liquid whey, and pressing the curds into a solid form. Originating before recorded history—likely discovered accidentally when milk was stored in animal stomachs containing rennet—cheesemaking evolved into a sophisticated art in ancient Rome and a major industry in modern Europe and America. With over a thousand varieties distinguished by milk source, bacteria, mold, aging, and processing, cheese is valued for its portability, long shelf life, and high density of protein, fat, calcium, and phosphorus. Modern production relies on industrial standardization using pure microbial cultures and recombinant rennet, though raw-milk and artisanal traditions persist alongside debates over health impacts, food safety, and cultural dietary restrictions.

## Body

### Origins and Early History
Cheesemaking predates writing; its birthplace is debated among Europe, Central Asia, and the Middle East. The process was likely discovered accidentally when milk stored in animal stomachs (containing rennet) separated into curds and whey. The earliest physical evidence comes from 5500 BCE Poland (strainers with milk-fat residues) and 5200 BCE Croatia. Egyptian tomb murals (c. 2000 BCE) and preserved cheese on 1615 BCE Chinese mummies confirm early spread. In ancient Greece, mythology credited Aristaeus; Homer’s *Odyssey* (8th c. BCE) depicts the Cyclops making cheese. By the Roman Empire, cheesemaking was a mature art: Columella (65 CE) described a technical process using rennet, pressing, salting, and aging, while Pliny the Elder (77 CE) cataloged diverse regional varieties, noting cheeses weighing up to a thousand pounds.

### Post-Roman to Modern Industry
After Rome, distinct European cheeses emerged: Cheddar (~1500), Parmesan (1597), Gouda (1697), Camembert (1791). Cheese remained rare in East Asia, the pre-Columbian Americas, and sub-Mediterranean Africa, centered instead in Europe, the Middle East, Tibet, and India (paneer). Industrialization began in Switzerland (1815) but scaled in the US after Jesse Williams (1851) used neighboring farms’ milk in an assembly line. The 1860s brought mass-produced rennet; by 1900, pure microbial cultures replaced environmental bacteria, standardizing output. Factory production surpassed traditional methods during World War II and dominates today. In 2022, global output reached 22.6 million tonnes (US 28%), with a carbon footprint of 6–12 kg CO₂eq per kg.

### Production: Curdling
Cheesemaking separates milk into curds (solids) and whey (liquid). Milk is acidified—usually by starter bacteria (*Lactococcus*, *Lactobacillus*, *Streptococcus*) converting lactose to lactic acid—then coagulated with rennet. Rennet (containing chymosin) creates a strong, rubbery gel at lower acidity than acid alone, preserving flavor-making bacteria and yielding drier, harder cheeses. Traditionally sourced from unweaned calf stomachs, most rennet today is recombinant (fermentation-produced chymosin). Vegetarian rennet comes from *Mucor miehei* fungus or *Cynara* thistles. Swiss cheeses use *Propionibacterium freudenreichii* to produce CO₂ bubbles ("eyes").

### Production: Curd Processing and Ripening
After coagulation, the moist gel is cut into cubes to expel whey. Hard cheeses are heated (35–55 °C) to expel more whey, requiring thermophilic bacteria. Salt preserves, draws moisture, and firms texture via protein interaction. Texture is further shaped by stretching (Mozzarella), cheddaring (piling/milling curd), or washing (Edam, Gouda—lowers acidity). Curds are pressed into molds; higher pressure yields harder cheese. Ripening (affinage) lasts days to years: microbes and enzymes break down casein and milkfat into amino acids, amines, and fatty acids, developing flavor and texture. Specific cultures create bloomy rinds (Brie, Camembert), blue veins (Roquefort, Stilton, Gorgonzola), or washed rinds (Limburger).

### Types and Classification
No single classification is universal. The traditional method groups by moisture content, then fat content and ripening. The International Dairy Federation recognizes ~500 varieties; other counts exceed 1,000. Britain claims ~700 local cheeses; France and Italy ~400 each. Criteria include aging, texture, method, fat, milk animal, and region.

### Cooking and Eating
Cold cheese has hard fat and stiff protein, muting flavor; warming to room temperature improves both. At 26–32 °C fats liquefy ("sweat"). Rennet-curdled cheeses melt when heat breaks protein bonds: soft cheeses ~55 °C, hard (Parmesan) ~82 °C. Acid-set cheeses (halloumi, paneer) do not melt; they firm as water evaporates. Acids or starches (e.g., wine in fondue) promote smooth melting. Browning adds distinct flavor. Cheeseboards traditionally contrast 4–6 types (hard, soft, blue, goat) with accompaniments; British custom serves cheese after dessert with port, French before dessert with red wine.

### Nutrition and Health
Cheese is a rich source (≥20% DV) of calcium, protein, phosphorus, sodium, and saturated fat. Composition varies widely: low-fat cottage cheese (2% fat, 11% protein) vs. cream cheese (34% fat, 6% protein). Major health bodies (AHA, NHS, Mayo Clinic) advise minimizing intake or choosing low-fat versions to lower LDL cholesterol and cardiovascular risk. Raw-milk cheeses carry pathogen risks (listeriosis, brucellosis, salmonellosis, tuberculosis); US law since 1944 mandates ≥60-day aging for raw-milk cheese. Pregnant women are warned against soft-ripened and blue-veined cheeses due to listeria. Pasteurization is increasingly standard even where not required.

### Cultural Attitudes
Cheese is marginal in traditional East/Southeast Asian cuisines (paneer, yak-milk chhurpi in Nepal, Datshi in Bhutan, Rushan/Rubing in Yunnan), though Chinese sales doubled 1996–2003. Islamic and Jewish dietary laws require rennet from halal/kosher-slaughtered animals. Animal rennet makes cheese non-vegetarian; vegetarian cheese uses microbial rennet. Vegans avoid dairy cheese entirely. Pungent cheeses (Limburger, Stilton, Roquefort) are acquired tastes; their odor molecules resemble decay, triggering innate aversion. A folk belief links cheese to nightmares (referenced in Dickens’ *A Christmas Carol*); studies variously suggest cheese may cause vivid dreams, disrupt sleep via saturated fat, or reduce dreaming.

## Terms
- ****Casein**** — The primary milk protein that coagulates to form cheese curds.
- ****Rennet**** — Enzyme preparation (containing chymosin) that coagulates casein; traditionally from calf stomachs, now often recombinant or microbial.
- ****Curd**** — The solid protein/fat mass separated from whey during cheesemaking.
- ****Whey**** — The liquid byproduct remaining after curds are removed.
- ****Starter culture**** — Bacteria (e.g., *Lactococcus*, *Lactobacillus*) added to acidify milk and develop flavor.
- ****Affineur / Affinage**** — A specialist who ages cheese / the controlled aging process that develops flavor and texture.
- ****Thermophilic bacteria**** — Heat-loving starter cultures (*Lactobacilli*, *Streptococci*) used for cheeses cooked at high temperatures.
- ****Cheddaring**** — Process of repeatedly piling and milling cut curd to expel moisture and develop texture (Cheddar-style cheeses).
- ****Raw-milk cheese**** — Cheese made from unpasteurized milk; regulated (e.g., US 60-day aging rule) due to pathogen risk.
- ****Acid-set cheese**** — Cheese coagulated by acid (vinegar, lemon juice, or bacterial acid) without rennet; does not melt (e.g., paneer, halloumi).

## Debates and open questions
- **Geographic origin**: Whether cheesemaking began in Europe, Central Asia, or the Middle East remains inconclusive; evidence points to independent invention in multiple regions.
- **Health impact**: Authorities recommend limiting cheese for cardiovascular risk, yet cheese provides dense nutrients; the net effect of dairy fat vs. matrix (calcium, fermentation products) is debated.
- **Raw-milk safety vs. tradition**: Regulators mandate pasteurization or long aging to prevent disease; artisans argue raw milk preserves microbial diversity and terroir, with some jurisdictions granting exceptions (e.g., Australian allowances for Gruyère, Roquefort).
- **Classification**: No universal taxonomy exists; moisture, fat, method, milk source, and geography compete as primary criteria.
- **Sleep effects**: Folklore and Dickens link cheese to nightmares; limited studies variously report vivid dreams, sleep disruption from saturated fat, or reduced dreaming—no consensus.
