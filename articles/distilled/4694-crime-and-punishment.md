# Crime and Punishment

## Overview
*Crime and Punishment* is a novel by Fyodor Dostoevsky, first published in 1866. It is a profound psychological exploration of guilt, morality, and redemption, following a impoverished student who commits murder based on a radical theory, only to be consumed by the psychological consequences. It is considered one of the greatest works of world literature and a cornerstone of Dostoevsky's mature period.

## Timeline
- **1864** — Dostoevsky's brother Mikhail dies, leaving him in debt.
- **September 1865** — Dostoevsky proposes a "story" about a young man to publisher Mikhail Katkov.
- **November 1865** — The project evolves from a story into a novel; Dostoevsky decides to use third-person narrative.
- **January 1866** — The first part of *Crime and Punishment* is published in *The Russian Messenger*.
- **December 1866** — The final installment is published, completing the novel.

## Body

### Background and Composition
Dostoevsky wrote *Crime and Punishment* under severe financial pressure, owing money and supporting his late brother's family. He secured an advance from the prestigious journal *The Russian Messenger* by proposing a story about a young man influenced by dangerous "unfinished ideas." The work evolved from a first-person novella into a third-person novel as Dostoevsky's protagonist developed beyond his initial conception. He completed it with the help of stenographer Anna Snitkina, whom he later married.

### Plot Summary
The novel follows Rodion Raskolnikov, a destitute former law student in Saint Petersburg. He formulates a theory that "extraordinary" men have the right to transgress moral law for a higher good. To test this and escape poverty, he murders a pawnbroker and her sister. Instead of liberation, he is wracked by paranoia, guilt, and illness. He is pursued psychologically by the detective Porfiry Petrovich and drawn to the self-sacrificing prostitute Sonya Marmeladova, who reads him the story of Lazarus. After a series of confrontations—including a false accusation against Sonya by the lawyer Luzhin and the suicide of the depraved Svidrigailov—Raskolnikov confesses. He is sentenced to eight years in Siberia, where Sonya follows him. His moral regeneration begins only after he humbly accepts her love and his guilt.

### Major Characters
*   **Raskolnikov:** The protagonist, whose intellectual pride and utilitarian theory lead him to murder and subsequent psychological torment.
*   **Sonya Marmeladova:** A prostitute who supports her family; she represents Christian compassion and becomes Raskolnikov's moral compass and path to redemption.
*   **Porfiry Petrovich:** The investigating magistrate who uses psychological pressure, not evidence, to provoke Raskolnikov's confession.
*   **Svidrigailov:** A wealthy, amoral figure who serves as a dark double to Raskolnikov, ultimately committing suicide.
*   **Dunya:** Raskolnikov's sister, whose planned sacrifice marriage motivates his crime; she later marries his friend Razumikhin.

### Themes and Style
The novel is a critique of nihilism, rationalism, and utilitarianism—the radical ideas Dostoevsky saw as dangerous in contemporary Russia. Raskolnikov's crime is the ultimate consequence of "ideological intoxication." The squalid, oppressive environment of Saint Petersburg is not just a setting but a reflection of Raskolnikov's tormented mind. Dostoevsky employs a innovative third-person narrative that fuses closely with characters' consciousness, using stream of consciousness and shifting perspectives to create a "dialogical" interior monologue, particularly within Raskolnikov.

### Structure and Reception
The novel is structured in six parts and an epilogue, with a symmetrical "flattened X" design: the first half shows the death of Raskolnikov's rational pride, and the second his rebirth through humility. The epilogue, depicting his spiritual awakening in Siberia, has been debated as either a necessary Christian resurrection tale or an anticlimactic addition. Initially a literary sensation, the novel was criticized by radicals who denied its portrayal of their ideas but was later celebrated by symbolists, existentialists, and psychoanalysts for its spiritual and psychological depth.

## Terms
*   **Nihilism:** A philosophical position rejecting all religious and moral principles, often in the belief that life is meaningless. Dostoevsky critiques its extreme form in Russian radical thought.
*   **Utilitarianism:** An ethical theory holding that the best action is the one that maximizes overall happiness. Raskolnikov uses a perverted version to justify murder for a greater good.
*   **Rational Egoism:** A Russian radical doctrine (associated with Chernyshevsky) that human behavior is governed by rational self-interest, which Dostoevsky saw as negating compassion.
*   **Stream of Consciousness:** A narrative mode depicting the continuous flow of a character's thoughts and feelings, used by Dostoevsky to convey Raskolnikov's mental state.
*   **Dialogical:** A concept from critic Mikhail Bakhtin describing Dostoevsky's art as a interplay of independent, conflicting voices and ideas, even within a single character's mind.
*   **Transgression:** The act of violating a law, moral code, or boundary. The Russian title (*Prestupléniye*) literally means "a stepping across."
*   **Hagia Sophia:** "Holy Wisdom," a feminine divine principle in Orthodox Christianity, symbolically embodied by Sonya.
*   **Mat Syra Zemlya:** "Moist Mother Earth," a figure from Russian folklore representing a nurturing, regenerative force, also associated with Sonya.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **The Epilogue's Necessity:** Critics debate whether the final pages in Siberia, showing Raskolnikov's spiritual rebirth, are a vital Christian conclusion or a weak, superfluous addition to the psychological tragedy.
*   **Raskolnikov's Motives:** Scholars analyze whether his crime stems primarily from ideological theory, psychological illness, social environment, or a combination of all three.
*   **Dostoevsky's Craftsmanship:** Early critics saw his narrative style as messy and untidy, but later analysis (e.g., by Bakhtin) reveals a highly sophisticated, intentional "dialogical" architecture.
*   **Political Interpretation:** Radicals like Pisarev initially rejected the novel's portrayal of their ideas, while Soviet critics often emphasized environmental determinism, downplaying its religious and philosophical dimensions.
