# Lake District

## Overview
The Lake District is a mountainous region and national park in Cumbria, North West England, renowned for its glacial lakes, rugged fells, and deep cultural associations with literature and art. Its landscape, shaped by glaciation and complex geology, supports unique wildlife and a tourism-based economy. Designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2017, it is England's largest national park and a major destination for domestic and international visitors.

## Body

### National Park and Governance
The Lake District National Park, established in 1951, covers 2,362 km² (912 sq mi) and is the largest in England and Wales. It was extended in 2016 and designated a World Heritage Site in 2017 after three previous failed attempts. The park is governed by the Lake District National Park Authority, which aims to protect the landscape from unwelcome commercial change. It received 17.73 million tourist visitors in 2024.

### Physical Geography and Geology
The region is a circular upland massif dissected by radial valleys carved by repeated glaciations over the last 2 million years. Its geology is complex, broadly divided into three bands: the northwestern Skiddaw Group (Ordovician sedimentary rocks), the central Borrowdale Volcanic Group (Ordovician lavas and tuffs), and the southeastern Windermere Supergroup (Silurian mudstones). A granite batholith underlies the area. The highest peak is Scafell Pike (978 m). The climate is the wettest in England, with average annual precipitation over 2,000 mm, and significant local variation.

### Human Geography and Economy
Major settlements within the park include Keswick, Windermere, and Ambleside, whose economies are tourism-dependent. The area is crisscrossed by a network of roads, railways, and extensive public footpaths and bridleways. Land ownership is mixed, with the National Trust owning about 25%. Historically, sheep farming (notably the Herdwick breed) and mining (slate, copper, graphite) were primary industries. Tourism, which began expanding with the railways, is now the dominant economic force, though it brings challenges like traffic congestion and soil erosion.

### Wildlife and Conservation
The varied habitats support diverse wildlife, including the native red squirrel (with England's largest population), rare fish species like the vendace, and reintroduced birds such as ospreys and red kites. Conservation efforts include fisheries byelaws to protect native fish from invasive species and rangers to manage tourism impacts. The wet climate fosters a distinctive montane lichen flora.

### Literature and the Arts
The landscape has inspired artists for centuries. It is intimately linked with the 18th- and 19th-century Lake Poets (Wordsworth, Coleridge, Southey) and later writers like Beatrix Potter and Arthur Ransome. Visual artists such as Alfred Heaton Cooper and modern art projects at Grizedale Arts have also drawn inspiration from the region. The area's cultural tourism includes museums, festivals, and literary trails.

## Terms
- **Fell**: A mountain or hill, from Old Norse *fjallr*.
- **Tarn**: A small mountain lake, typically in a glacial cirque, from Old Norse *tjörn*.
- **Heaf/Heft**: The "home territory" of a flock of sheep, which they learn to stay within without fencing.
- **Glacial Cirque**: A bowl-shaped hollow on a mountainside, carved by a glacier, often containing a tarn.
- **Batholith**: A large mass of intrusive igneous rock that forms from cooled magma deep in the Earth's crust.
- **UNESCO World Heritage Site**: A place listed for its cultural, historical, or scientific significance, protected by international treaty.
- **Right to Roam**: Legal access to open land (mountains, moors) for recreation, applicable to about 50% of the park.
- **Yan Tan Tethera**: A traditional system of sheep counting using a base-five numeral system.

The successful re-establishment of certain wildlife populations, like red kites and ospreys, represents a continuing conservation effort and debate about species reintroduction.
