# Ship

## Overview
A ship is a large watercraft designed for sea or ocean travel, carrying cargo, passengers, or supporting specialized tasks like warfare, fishing, or scientific research. Ships are distinguished from boats by size, seaworthiness, and crew requirements, and have been central to exploration, trade, warfare, migration, and science throughout history. Ship transport remains the backbone of global commerce, responsible for the majority of world trade by volume.

## Body

### Nomenclature and Classification
Ships are generally larger than boats and designed for extended sea voyages, though no universal legal distinction exists. A common rule holds that a ship can carry a boat, but not vice versa. Legally, a ship may be defined as a vessel carrying goods by sea. The term "ship" historically referred specifically to a full-rigged vessel with three or more square-rigged masts, though it also broadly denotes any large sea-going craft. Ships often bear individual names and may belong to classes named after their lead vessel. Maritime traditions sometimes refer to ships using feminine pronouns, though modern registries increasingly use neutral terms.

### Historical Development
For most of history, ship transport was cheaper, safer, and faster than land routes. Ancient civilizations developed sophisticated maritime technologies: Egyptians built seagoing sailboats like the Khufu ship; Phoenicians pioneered true seafaring with plank-built vessels capable of long-distance trade. Austronesian peoples expanded across the Pacific using catamarans, outriggers, and crab-claw sails. In medieval times, Chinese innovations included the compass and sternpost rudder, while the jong and later junk represented advanced Asian shipbuilding. European shipbuilding evolved through clinker and carvel techniques, culminating in the full-rigged ship that enabled the Age of Discovery.

### Modern Era and Industrialization
The 19th century saw steam power revolutionize shipping. Early steamships operated on shorter routes, but improvements in engine efficiency—particularly compound and triple-expansion engines—made long-distance steam commerce viable. The transition from wood to iron and steel hulls allowed larger vessels and supported naval artillery. Containerization from the 1960s onward dramatically altered commercial shipping, enabling massive economies of scale and dedicated port infrastructure. Cruise tourism also emerged as a major sector.

### Ship Types and Functions
Merchant ships are categorized into cargo vessels (bulk carriers, tankers, container ships), passenger ships (ocean liners, ferries, cruise ships), fishing vessels, and special-purpose craft (research, offshore support, icebreakers). Naval vessels include aircraft carriers, destroyers, frigates, submarines, and auxiliary ships. Inland waterways support specialized vessels like Great Lakes freighters and riverboats, adapted to shallower drafts and narrower channels.

### Design and Engineering
Ships operate on principles of buoyancy and stability, requiring hull designs that displace enough water to support their weight while maintaining structural integrity. Propulsion systems range from human-powered oars to wind-assisted sails, steam engines, diesel motors, gas turbines, and nuclear reactors. Steering relies on rudders, often supplemented by bow thrusters or azimuth drives. Modern designs emphasize fuel efficiency, environmental compliance, and modular construction.

### Lifecycle and Economics
Ship construction occurs in specialized shipyards, with build times ranging from months for mass-produced vessels to over a decade for complex warships. Costs vary widely: small cargo ships cost tens of millions of dollars, while aircraft carriers exceed $3 billion. Most ships have lifespans of 20–30 years before scrapping, though well-maintained steel vessels can last over a century. Maintenance includes regular dry-docking for hull inspection, repainting, and component replacement.

### Environmental Impact
Seagoing vessels are responsible for an estimated 14 percent of emissions of nitrogen from fossil fuels and 16 percent of the emissions of sulfur from petroleum uses into the atmosphere. Ballast water discharge spreads invasive species, exemplified by the comb jelly invasion of the Black Sea. Oil spills, though less frequent since regulatory reforms like double-hull requirements, remain environmentally devastating. Shipbreaking poses severe occupational and environmental hazards, particularly in developing nations lacking safety regulations.

## Terms
- **Deadweight tonnage**: Total weight a ship can safely carry, including cargo, fuel, crew, and supplies.
- **Block coefficient**: Ratio of a ship's hull volume to the volume of a rectangular box enclosing it; indicates hull fullness.
- **Carvel construction**: Method where hull planks are butted edge-to-edge and fastened to internal frames.
- **Clinker construction**: Method where hull planks overlap each other, traditionally used in Northern European shipbuilding.
- **Full-rigged ship**: Vessel with three or more masts, all square-rigged.
- **Hull speed**: Theoretical maximum speed of a displacement hull, calculated as 1.34 times the square root of waterline length in feet.
- **Plimsell mark**: Load line indicating maximum safe draft depth, mandated by international safety regulations.
- **Containerization**: Standardized cargo handling system using intermodal containers, revolutionizing port efficiency and costs.
- **Ballast water**: Water taken into ship holds to improve stability, often discharged at port and capable of transporting invasive species.
- **Scrapping**: Process of dismantling old ships for material recycling, often conducted in developing-country shipyards.

## Debates and Open Questions
The environmental impact of shipping remains highly contested, with debates over the accuracy of emission estimates and effectiveness of mitigation strategies. The role of alternative fuels—LNG, ammonia, hydrogen—in achieving carbon neutrality is actively discussed but lacks consensus on technical feasibility and economic viability. Shipbreaking practices continue to generate controversy between industry efficiency and worker/environmental protection advocates. The future of ocean liners versus air travel for transoceanic passenger transport reflects broader tensions between tradition and technological change.

## Fleet Statistics
In 2016, there were more than 49,000 merchant ships, totaling almost 1.8 billion deadweight tons. By 2024, ships had a global cargo capacity of 2.4 billion tons, with the three largest classes being ships carrying dry bulk (43%), oil tankers (28%) and container ships (14%).
