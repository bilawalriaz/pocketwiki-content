# Iguazu Falls

## Overview
Iguazu Falls is the world's largest waterfall system, located on the border between Argentina and Brazil. It is a major tourist destination and a UNESCO World Heritage Site, renowned for its immense scale, unique geological formation, and the dramatic natural beauty of the surrounding subtropical rainforest.

## Timeline
- **c. 1541** — Spanish Conquistador Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca becomes the first European to record the falls.
- **1876** — Engineer André Rebouças begins a campaign to preserve the falls in Brazil.
- **1984** — Argentina's Iguazú National Park is designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **1986** — Brazil's Iguaçu National Park is designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **2013** — The falls are inscribed as a single UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Body

### Geology and Geography
The falls are formed by the Iguazu River and consist of a two-step waterfall system created by three layers of basalt rock. The steps are 35 and 40 meters high. The river's edge is 2.7 km long and is divided by numerous islands into 150 to 300 separate waterfalls, ranging from 60 to 82 meters in height. The most significant feature is the Devil's Throat, a U-shaped, 80-90 meter wide and 70-80 meter deep chasm into which about half the river's flow plunges. The falls have a reversed "J" shape, with the Argentina-Brazil border running through the Devil's Throat. Brazil holds over 95% of the river basin but only about 20% of the falls, while Argentina contains nearly 80% of the falls.

### Access and Tourism
The falls are accessible from three main cities: Foz do Iguaçu (Brazil), Puerto Iguazú (Argentina), and Ciudad del Este (Paraguay). Each country has its own national park. On the Brazilian side, a walkway extends to the base of Devil's Throat, and helicopter tours are available. Argentina prohibits helicopter tours due to environmental impact. The Argentine side features an ecological train and a 1 km trail (Paseo Garganta del Diablo) that leads directly over the Devil's Throat. Inflatable boat services also take visitors close to the falls. Both parks have entrance fees and internal bus systems.

### Comparison with Other Falls
Iguazu is often compared to Niagara Falls (USA/Canada) and Victoria Falls (Zambia/Zimbabwe). It is wider than Niagara but shorter in height. While Victoria Falls has a larger single curtain of water, Iguazu is split into many distinct falls. In terms of average annual flow, Iguazu ranks sixth globally, with an average of 1,746 m³/s. Its maximum recorded flow was 45,700 m³/s on 9 June 2014. For comparison, Niagara's average flow is 2,400 m³/s and Victoria's is 1,088 m³/s.

### Climate
The region has a humid subtropical climate with high rainfall and temperatures year-round. The period of greatest water flow is typically December to February. A severe drought in the summer of 2006 unusually reduced the river's flow to 300 m³/s for several months.

## Terms
- **Basalt:** A dark, fine-grained volcanic rock that forms the geological layers over which the river flows.
- **Cataract:** A large or powerful waterfall.
- **Humid subtropical climate (Cfa):** A climate zone characterized by hot, humid summers and mild winters, with precipitation year-round.
- **Headwater erosion:** The process by which a river wears away the rock at the source or head of a waterfall, causing it to retreat upstream.
- **Vesicular basalt:** A type of basalt containing small cavities (vesicles) formed by gas bubbles, noted for its high resistance to erosion.
- **UNESCO World Heritage Site:** A location designated by the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization as having special cultural or physical significance.
