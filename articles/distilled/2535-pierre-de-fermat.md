# Pierre de Fermat

## Overview
Pierre de Fermat (1601–1665) was a French lawyer and amateur mathematician whose work, communicated mostly in letters, laid foundational stones for modern number theory, analytic geometry, probability, and calculus. Though he proved few of his claims formally, his insights were so profound that he is considered one of the two leading mathematicians of the first half of the 17th century, alongside René Descartes.

## Timeline
- **17 August 1601** — Born in Beaumont-de-Lomagne, France.
- **1623–1626** — Studies law at the University of Orléans, earning a bachelor's degree.
- **1629** — Begins serious mathematical research in Bordeaux; restores Apollonius's *De Locis Planis*.
- **1630** — Purchases the office of councilor at the Parlement de Toulouse.
- **1631** — Marries Louise de Long; becomes "Pierre de Fermat."
- **1636** — Circulates manuscript on analytic geometry and his method of adequality.
- **1654** — Corresponds with Blaise Pascal, laying foundations for probability theory.
- **12 January 1665** — Dies in Castres, France.
- **1679** — Posthumous publication of his mathematical works, including *Ad Locos Planos et Solidos Isagoge*.
- **1994** — Andrew Wiles proves Fermat's Last Theorem, a conjecture Fermat left in a margin.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Fermat was born into a wealthy family in Gascony. After studying law at Orléans, he began his mathematical research in Bordeaux, influenced by the algebraic methods of François Viète. In 1630, he secured a lifelong position as a magistrate at the Parlement de Toulouse, making mathematics a serious hobby rather than a profession. Fluent in six languages, he was also a respected Latinist and Hellenist.

### Mathematical Contributions
Fermat's work in analytic geometry, circulated in 1636, predated the publication of Descartes' *La géométrie*. He developed a technique called **adequality** for finding maxima, minima, and tangents to curves, which was functionally equivalent to the differential calculus later developed by Newton and Leibniz. He was the first to evaluate the integral of general power functions, a result that aided the later formulation of the fundamental theorem of calculus.

In number theory, Fermat's work was foundational. He studied equations like Pell's equation, discovered **Fermat's little theorem**, invented a factorization method, and popularized proof by infinite descent. His most famous claim, **Fermat's Last Theorem**, was found in a margin of his copy of Diophantus' *Arithmetica*, stating that no three positive integers satisfy the equation \(a^n + b^n = c^n\) for any integer \(n > 2\). He claimed to have a proof, but none was found.

### Probability and Physics
In 1654, Fermat corresponded with Blaise Pascal on the "problem of points," helping to establish the theory of probability. He is credited with the first rigorous probability calculation. In physics, he generalized earlier principles of light reflection into **Fermat's principle**, stating that light travels between two points along the path of shortest time, a key precursor to the principle of least action.

### Legacy and Assessment
Fermat communicated his work in letters, often without proof, leading to later disputes and doubts about some of his claims. Isaac Newton acknowledged that his own early calculus ideas came from "Fermat's way of drawing tangents." Mathematician André Weil stated that Fermat's methods for curves of genus 1 form the foundation of modern theory and that Fermat essentially created the modern theory of numbers. However, Fermat did make some mistakes, such as incorrectly claiming all Fermat numbers are prime.

## Terms
- **Adequality**: Fermat's technique for finding maxima, minima, and tangents, a precursor to differential calculus.
- **Fermat's Last Theorem**: The conjecture that \(a^n + b^n = c^n\) has no positive integer solutions for \(n > 2\), proven in 1994.
- **Fermat's little theorem**: A theorem in number theory stating that if \(p\) is prime, then \(a^p - a\) is divisible by \(p\).
- **Fermat's principle**: The principle that light travels between two points along the path of shortest time.
- **Proof by infinite descent**: A method of proof by contradiction used to show that a statement is false for all positive integers.
- **Parlement de Toulouse**: One of the High Courts of Judicature in France, where Fermat served as a magistrate.
- **Genus 1 curves**: Algebraic curves (like elliptic curves) whose modern theory is founded on Fermat's methods.

## Debates and open questions
- **Fermat's proof of his Last Theorem**: Did Fermat actually possess a valid proof? Most scholars doubt it, given the complexity of the eventual proof by Andrew Wiles.
- **Priority disputes**: Fermat's secrecy and lack of published proofs led to disputes with contemporaries like Descartes over discoveries in analytic geometry.
- **Accuracy of his claims**: Some of Fermat's number-theoretic claims were later shown to be incorrect, such as his assertion that all Fermat numbers are prime.
