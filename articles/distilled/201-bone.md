# Bone

## Overview
Bone is a rigid organ forming the vertebrate skeleton, composed of mineralized connective tissue (osseous tissue) that provides structural support, protects organs, enables movement via muscle attachment, and facilitates hearing through the auditory ossicles. Beyond mechanical roles, bone acts as a metabolic reservoir for minerals (calcium, phosphorus) and fat, produces blood cells via hematopoiesis in marrow, and functions as an endocrine organ regulating glucose and phosphate homeostasis. In humans, the skeleton develops from ~300 bones at birth to 206 in adulthood through fusion, with continuous remodeling throughout life balancing formation by osteoblasts and resorption by osteoclasts.

## Timeline
- **Fetal development** — Intramembranous ossification forms flat skull bones; endochondral ossification begins in long bones via primary ossification centers.
- **Birth** — ~300 bones present; secondary ossification centers appear in epiphyses postnatally.
- **Childhood/Adolescence** — Epiphyseal plates allow longitudinal growth; peak bone mass accrual influenced by mechanical loading and nutrition.
- **18–25 years** — Skeletal maturity: epiphyseal closure fuses diaphyses and epiphyses; peak bone mass achieved.
- **Adulthood** — Continuous remodeling replaces ~10% of skeletal mass annually; bone mass plateaus then declines with age.
- **Postmenopause / Old age** — Accelerated bone loss increases osteoporosis risk; oxidative DNA damage in bone cells implicated in age-related decline.

## Body

### Gross Anatomy and Classification
The human skeleton comprises five bone shapes. **Long bones** (e.g., femur, phalanges) feature a diaphysis (shaft) and epiphyses (ends), consisting mainly of cortical bone with a medullary cavity. **Short bones** (carpals, tarsals) are cube-shaped with thin cortices and spongy interiors for stability. **Flat bones** (skull, sternum) sandwich spongy bone between two compact layers. **Irregular bones** (vertebrae, pelvis) have complex shapes from multiple ossification centers or sinuses. **Sesamoid bones** (patella, pisiform) embed in tendons to increase mechanical leverage. Anatomical terminology uses Latin/Greek roots (e.g., *foramen* for hole, *condyle* for rounded articulation); joints with fibrous immobility are **sutures**.

### Mechanical, Synthetic, and Metabolic Functions
Mechanically, bone resists compression well (~170 MPa) but resists tension and shear poorly; collagen provides elasticity. The **ossicles** (malleus, incus, stapes) transduce sound. Synthetically, red marrow performs **hematopoiesis**, producing ~2.5 billion red cells/platelets and 50–100 billion granulocytes daily; aged cells are also destroyed here. Metabolically, bone stores calcium, phosphorus, growth factors (e.g., BMPs, TGF-β), and fat (marrow adipose tissue). It buffers blood pH, sequesters heavy metals, and acts endocrine: osteocalcin enhances insulin secretion/sensitivity and reduces fat, while FGF-23 reduces renal phosphate reabsorption. Calcium balance is maintained by coupled osteoclast resorption (releases Ca²⁺) and osteoblast formation (fixes Ca²⁺).

### Tissue Microstructure: Cortical and Cancellous Bone
Bone matrix is ~30% organic (90–95% Type I collagen/ossein) and ~70% inorganic (hydroxyapatite, Ca₁₀(PO₄)₆(OH)₂), giving synergistic tensile and compressive strength. **Cortical (compact) bone** forms the dense outer cortex (80% of mass), organized into **osteons** (Haversian systems): concentric lamellae around a central canal containing vessels/nerves, connected by Volkmann’s canals. **Cancellous (spongy/trabecular) bone** (20% mass, ~10x surface area) is a porous network of **trabeculae** aligned to stress lines, found at epiphyses and vertebral bodies. It is highly vascular, housing red marrow for hematopoiesis. Marrow transitions from hematopoietic (red) in neonates to fatty (yellow/MAT) in adults, persisting in femur, ribs, vertebrae, and pelvis. Bone receives ~10% of cardiac output; marrow oxygen tension is low (~6.6%).

### Cellular Histology and Remodeling
Four main cell types drive bone dynamics: **Osteoblasts** (mononucleate, from osteoprogenitors) secrete osteoid (Type I collagen) and mineralize it via alkaline phosphatase; trapped osteoblasts become **osteocytes** (in lacunae, connected by canaliculi/gap junctions for mechanosensing). **Osteoclasts** (multinucleate, monocyte lineage) resorb bone in Howship’s lacunae using acid phosphatase. **Bone lining cells** (flattened osteoblasts) cover quiescent surfaces. **Remodeling** (resorption → formation) renews ~10% of adult skeleton yearly, regulated by systemic hormones (PTH, calcitonin, vitamin D, sex steroids) and local cytokines (RANK/RANKL/OPG pathway). Mechanical loading thickens bone at stress points (**Wolff’s law**), possibly via piezoelectric potentials.

### Development: Ossification and Growth
**Intramembranous ossification** forms flat bones directly from mesenchyme (ossification center → calcification → trabeculae → periosteum). **Endochondral ossification** replaces a cartilage model: primary centers form diaphyses prenatally; secondary centers form epiphyses postnatally. The **epiphyseal plate** (growth plate) zones (reserve → proliferation → hypertrophy → calcification → bone deposition) drive longitudinal growth until closure (18–25 yrs). **Woven bone** (disorganized collagen, rapid, weak) forms first in fetus and fracture callus; replaced by **lamellar bone** (parallel collagen sheets/osteons, strong, slow ~1–2 µm/day). Peak bone mass (2nd–3rd decade) is modifiable by weight-bearing exercise (up to 20–40% variance), reducing later osteoporosis risk.

### Clinical Significance
**Fractures** result from high force, repetitive stress, or pathology (osteoporosis, Paget’s, cancer); classified by location/shape (e.g., Salter-Harris in children). Management: immobilization, fixation, rehabilitation. **Tumors**: benign (osteoma, enchondroma) vs. malignant. **Primary bone cancers** rare; **metastases** common (breast, lung, prostate, thyroid, kidney), lytic (destructive) or sclerotic (formative). Marrow cancers (leukemia, myeloma) affect bone. Paraneoplastic PTHrP causes hypercalcemia/resorption. **Osteoporosis**: BMD ≥2.5 SD below young adult mean (DEXA); postmenopausal estrogen loss accelerates resorption; oxidative DNA damage in bone cells drives age-related loss. Treatment: lifestyle, Ca²⁺/Vit D, bisphosphonates, SERMs, hormone therapy. **Diabetes** (T1/T2) inhibits osteoblasts, lowering BMD/BMC, raising fracture/osteoarthritis risk. **Other**: osteomyelitis (infection), osteomalacia (vit D deficiency), osteogenesis imperfecta, fluorosis.

### Comparative and Cultural Aspects
Bird bones are lightweight, hollow, and thin for flight; bats show convergent density reduction. Marine mammals/reptiles vary cortical proportion (pachyostosis in shallow divers, osteoporosis in pelagic). **Osteophagy** (bone eating) in herbivores replenishes phosphate. **Osteology** (study of bones/teeth) informs anthropology, archaeology, forensics (sex, age, ancestry, trauma). Culturally: bone tools, carving, glue, gelatin, broth, bone char (filtration/pigment), oracle bones (Shang China), wishbone divination, cranial deformation, foot binding.

## Terms
- ****Osteon (Haversian system)**** — The functional unit of cortical bone: concentric lamellae of matrix surrounding a central canal containing blood vessels and nerves.
- ****Trabecula**** — The rod- or plate-like structural unit of cancellous bone, forming a porous network aligned to mechanical stress.
- ****Osteoblast**** — Mononucleate bone-forming cell that secretes osteoid (Type I collagen) and mediates mineralization; derived from osteoprogenitors.
- ****Osteocyte**** — Mature bone cell derived from trapped osteoblasts; resides in lacunae, extends processes through canaliculi for mechanosensing and signaling.
- ****Osteoclast**** — Large, multinucleate cell of monocyte lineage that resorbs bone mineral and matrix in Howship’s lacunae; key for calcium homeostasis.
- ****Endochondral ossification**** — Bone formation via a cartilage intermediate; responsible for long bones and most of the skeleton.
- ****Intramembranous ossification**** — Direct bone formation from mesenchymal connective tissue without a cartilage model; forms flat skull bones, mandible, clavicles.
- ****Epiphyseal plate (growth plate)**** — Cartilaginous zone separating diaphysis and epiphysis; site of longitudinal bone growth via chondrocyte proliferation/hypertrophy.
- ****Wolff’s law**** — Principle that bone adapts its architecture (mass, geometry) to the mechanical loads it habitually experiences.
- ****Osteocalcin**** — Hormone secreted by osteoblasts; enhances insulin secretion/sensitivity, increases β-cell proliferation, and reduces fat deposition.

## Debates and open questions
- **Mechanotransduction mechanism**: Whether bone’s piezoelectric properties or fluid shear stress in canaliculi primarily drives osteocyte signaling under load remains unresolved.
- **Marrow adipose tissue (MAT) function**: Whether MAT is a passive fat store or an active endocrine/paracrine regulator of hematopoiesis and bone remodeling is debated.
- **Osteocalcin’s systemic role**: The extent and physiological relevance of osteocalcin’s endocrine effects on glucose metabolism and male fertility in humans (vs. mice) require further validation.
- **Diabetes-bone fragility link**: The precise molecular pathways by which T1/T2 diabetes suppress osteoblast activity and degrade bone quality (AGEs, sclerostin, Wnt signaling) are incompletely defined.
- **Evolution of bone density in aquatic taxa**: The selective pressures driving rapid shifts between pachyostosis (ballast) and osteoporotic-like spongy bone (buoyancy) in secondarily aquatic vertebrates are not fully mapped.
