# Lake Erie

## Overview
Lake Erie is the fourth-largest of the five Great Lakes by surface area and the eleventh-largest globally. It is the southernmost, shallowest, and warmest of the Great Lakes, with the shortest water residence time. Its environmental health, marked by issues like pollution, invasive species, and algal blooms, has been a major concern for decades, making it a focal point for international conservation efforts.

## Body

### Geography and Geology
Lake Erie is located on the U.S.–Canada border, with Ontario to the north and Michigan, Ohio, Pennsylvania, and New York to the west, south, and east. Its surface area is 9,910 square miles (25,667 km²), with a maximum depth of 210 feet (64 m). The lake is divided into shallow western, central, and eastern basins. It was carved by glacial erosion over the last 2 million years, with its current form less than 4,000 years old. The lake drains primarily via the Niagara River into Lake Ontario and is fed by the Detroit River and other tributaries.

### Climate and Weather
As the shallowest Great Lake, Erie is the first to freeze in winter and the warmest in summer. It produces significant lake-effect snow when cold air passes over its relatively warm waters, making cities like Buffalo among the snowiest in the U.S. The lake creates microclimates that support agriculture, including major fruit, vegetable, and grape-growing regions on its shores. Strong winds can cause seiches—rapid, temporary changes in water level—and dangerous waves.

### Ecosystems and Environmental Issues
Lake Erie's ecosystem has been heavily impacted by human activity. It was declared nearly "dead" in the 1960s due to industrial pollution and sewage. Cleanup efforts, including the Clean Water Act of 1972, led to significant recovery. However, new challenges have emerged, including invasive species like zebra mussels and gobies, and recurring eutrophication—nutrient overloading from agricultural runoff that causes toxic cyanobacterial (blue-green algae) blooms and low-oxygen "dead zones." The lake's water snake, once threatened, has rebounded.

### Economy and Human Use
The lake supports one of the world's largest freshwater commercial fisheries, historically dominated by walleye and yellow perch. Commercial fishing has declined on the U.S. side due to regulations and pollution, while the Canadian fishery remains intensively managed. Sport fishing is a major industry. The lake is a vital shipping lane, with traffic passing through the Welland Canal. Tourism includes shipwreck diving, island visits, and water sports. The Great Lakes Compact, signed in 2008, aims to prevent water diversion and set conservation standards.

## Terms
- **Eutrophication:** The process where a body of water becomes overly enriched with nutrients (like phosphorus and nitrogen), leading to excessive plant growth and oxygen depletion.
- **Seiche:** A standing wave in an enclosed or partially enclosed body of water, causing a rapid rise and fall of water level at one end of the lake.
- **Lake-effect snow:** Heavy snowfall that occurs when cold, dry air moves over the warmer waters of a lake, picking up moisture and depositing it as snow on downwind shores.
- **Invasive species:** Non-native organisms introduced to an ecosystem that cause harm to the environment, economy, or human health.
- **Dead zone:** An area in a body of water with very low oxygen levels (hypoxia), which cannot support most marine life.
- **Thermocline:** A distinct temperature gradient within a body of water, separating the warmer surface water from the colder deep water.
- **Great Lakes Compact:** An international agreement among U.S. states and Canadian provinces to manage and protect the water resources of the Great Lakes Basin.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The primary causes of recurring algal blooms and dead zones are debated, with factors including agricultural runoff, invasive zebra mussels, and municipal sewage all cited.
- The long-term impact of climate change on Lake Erie's water levels, temperature, and ice cover is uncertain, with reports conflicting on whether it is causing the lake to shrink.
- There is ongoing conflict between commercial and recreational fishing interests over management priorities and quota allocations.
- The effectiveness and monitoring of voluntary conservation practices by farmers to reduce phosphorus runoff are questioned, as blooms have returned despite past improvements.
