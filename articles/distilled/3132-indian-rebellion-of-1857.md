# Indian Rebellion of 1857

## Overview
The Indian Rebellion of 1857 was a major uprising against the rule of the British East India Company, which functioned as a sovereign power on behalf of the British Crown. It began as a military mutiny but expanded into a widespread civilian revolt, posing a serious threat to British power in India. The rebellion was a watershed event that led to the dissolution of the East India Company and the direct administration of India by the British government, marking the beginning of the British Raj.

## Timeline
- **10 May 1857** — Mutiny begins at Meerut; sepoys march to Delhi.
- **11 May 1857** — Rebels capture Delhi; Mughal Emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar is proclaimed leader.
- **27 June 1857** — Massacre of British civilians at Cawnpore (Kanpur).
- **July–Sept 1857** — Siege of Delhi by British forces.
- **14 Sept 1857** — British storm Delhi; Bahadur Shah Zafar flees.
- **Nov 1857** — British relieve Lucknow after a prolonged siege.
- **18 March 1858** — British recapture Lucknow.
- **17 June 1858** — Rani Lakshmi Bai of Jhansi dies at the Battle of Gwalior.
- **20 June 1858** — Last major rebel defeat at Gwalior.
- **1 Nov 1858** — British Crown assumes direct control; Queen Victoria issues proclamation of amnesty.
- **8 July 1859** — Hostilities formally declared ended.

## Body

### Causes and Outbreak
The rebellion resulted from accumulated resentments against the East India Company's rule. Key factors included invasive social reforms, harsh land taxes, and the annexation of states like Oudh (Awadh) in 1856 under the Doctrine of Lapse, which denied succession to adopted heirs. The Bengal Army, which recruited heavily from high-caste Hindu communities, was particularly discontented. The immediate trigger was the introduction of the Pattern 1853 Enfield rifle, whose cartridges were rumored to be greased with cow and pig fat, offending Hindu and Muslim religious sensibilities. The mutiny began at Meerut on 10 May 1857 when sepoys refused the cartridges and freed imprisoned comrades, then marched to Delhi.

### The Revolt and Key Events
Rebels quickly captured Delhi and proclaimed the aged Mughal Emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar their leader, though he was largely coerced. The uprising spread across the upper Gangetic plain and central India, with significant civilian participation from dispossessed nobles, taluqdars (landlords), and peasants. Major sieges occurred at Delhi, Cawnpore, and Lucknow. The British response was initially slow but eventually organized. Delhi was recaptured in September 1857 after a brutal siege. The massacre of British women and children at Cawnpore hardened British attitudes and led to severe reprisals. The rebellion was suppressed region by region, with the last major defeat of rebel forces at Gwalior in June 1858.

### Support and Opposition
The rebellion was not a unified national movement. While many sepoys and civilians revolted, others fought for the British. The Sikhs and Pathans of the Punjab, fearing a return to Mughal rule, provided crucial support to the British. Many princely states, such as Hyderabad and Kashmir, remained loyal. The rebels lacked centralized command and a coherent political program beyond restoring Mughal authority. The varied and often conflicting loyalties of different groups were a major cause of the rebellion's failure.

### Consequences and Reorganization
The rebellion led to the end of the East India Company's rule. The Government of India Act 1858 transferred power to the British Crown, establishing the British Raj. Queen Victoria's proclamation promised rights to Indians, though these were often not granted. The British reorganized the army, reducing the proportion of high-caste Bengali sepoys and increasing recruitment from "martial races" like Sikhs and Gurkhas. The ratio of British to Indian soldiers was increased. The administration was reformed to be more conservative, preserving traditional hierarchies to prevent future unrest.

## Terms
- **Sepoy**: An Indian soldier recruited into the service of the British East India Company.
- **Doctrine of Lapse**: A policy by which the British annexed any princely state where the ruler died without a natural male heir.
- **Taluqdar**: A landowner or feudal lord, particularly in Oudh, who held large estates.
- **Bengal Army**: The largest of the three presidency armies of the East India Company, which saw the most mutinies.
- **Martial Races**: A British colonial theory that certain groups (e.g., Sikhs, Gurkhas) were inherently more suited to military service.
- **Irregular Units**: Military regiments with fewer British officers and more responsibility given to Indian officers, seen as more reliable after 1857.
- **Government of India Act 1858**: The law that transferred governance of India from the East India Company to the British Crown.

## Debates and open questions
The rebellion's nature is contested. In India, it is often called the "First War of Independence," while in the UK it is commonly termed the "Indian Mutiny." Historians debate whether it was a unified war of national liberation or a series of disconnected mutinies and local revolts. Arguments against the "war of independence" label include the rebellion's limited geographic scope, the fact that 80% of the Company's forces were Indian, and the lack of a unified political program. Proponents note the widespread popular participation and the rebels' goal of driving out the British from "India." Modern historiography, influenced by scholars like Eric Stokes, emphasizes local economic grievances and the role of prosperous magnates over a general nationalist cause.
