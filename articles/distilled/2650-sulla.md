# Sulla

## Overview
Lucius Cornelius Sulla Felix (138–78 BC) was a Roman general and statesman who became the first man to march on Rome with his army and win a civil war. His career marked a critical turning point in the late Roman Republic, as he demonstrated that military force could be used to seize political power, setting a dangerous precedent for future leaders like Pompey and Julius Caesar.

## Timeline
- **c. 138 BC** — Born into an impoverished patrician family.
- **107–106 BC** — Serves as quaestor under Gaius Marius in the Jugurthine War; secures the capture of Jugurtha.
- **102–101 BC** — Fights against the Cimbri under Quintus Lutatius Catulus.
- **96–93 BC** — Governs Cilicia; restores Ariobarzanes to Cappadocia and meets a Parthian embassy.
- **90–89 BC** — Serves as a senior officer in the Social War, earning the Grass Crown.
- **88 BC** — Elected consul; marches on Rome to reverse the transfer of his Mithridatic command to Marius.
- **87–85 BC** — Fights the First Mithridatic War in Greece and Asia Minor.
- **86 BC** — Sacks Athens and wins the battles of Chaeronea and Orchomenos against Mithridates.
- **83–82 BC** — Returns to Italy and defeats the Marian faction in a civil war, culminating in the Battle of the Colline Gate.
- **82–81 BC** — Appointed dictator; institutes proscriptions and sweeping constitutional reforms.
- **80 BC** — Serves as consul for a second time, then resigns the dictatorship and retires.
- **78 BC** — Dies at his villa near Puteoli.

## Body

### Early Career and Rise to Prominence
Born into a poor branch of the patrician *gens Cornelia*, Sulla's early life was marked by financial hardship and a reputation for associating with actors and musicians. He gained military experience and crucial political connections serving under Gaius Marius in the Jugurthine War, where his diplomatic initiative led to the capture of the Numidian king Jugurtha. He later fought against Germanic tribes in the Cimbrian War and Italian allies in the Social War, where his victories, including the capture of Pompeii, earned him the Grass Crown, Rome's highest military honor.

### First March on Rome and the Mithridatic War
Elected consul for 88 BC, Sulla was initially assigned command against Mithridates VI of Pontus. However, the tribune Publius Sulpicius Rufus, in a deal with Marius, transferred the command to Marius. In response, Sulla took the unprecedented step of marching his army on Rome, forcing his opponents to flee. After securing his command, he left for the East. During his absence, Marius and his ally Lucius Cornelius Cinna recaptured Rome, purged Sulla's supporters, and declared him a public enemy. In the East, Sulla defeated Mithridates' forces in Greece and Asia, culminating in the sack of Athens and decisive victories at Chaeronea and Orchomenos in 86 BC. He then negotiated a peace treaty with Mithridates to free his army for the coming civil war in Italy.

### Civil War and Dictatorship
Sulla returned to Italy in 83 BC and, after a two-year civil war, defeated the Marian-Samnite forces at the Battle of the Colline Gate in 82 BC. He then initiated a reign of terror through proscriptions, publishing lists of enemies who could be legally killed and whose property was confiscated. Reviving the long-dormant office of dictator, Sulla enacted constitutional reforms aimed at strengthening the Senate and weakening the plebeian tribunate. He expanded the criminal courts, increased the number of magistrates, and barred tribunes from holding further office. After enacting these laws, he resigned his dictatorship in 80 BC, served a final consulship, and retired to private life.

### Legacy and Death
Sulla died in 78 BC, leaving behind a complex legacy. His reforms were designed to restore the traditional Republican system dominated by the Senate, but his methods—using military force to seize power and proscribing political opponents—shattered Republican norms. His career provided a direct precedent for later strongmen like Pompey and Caesar, who followed his example in using armies to achieve political aims. Many of his laws were repealed shortly after his death, but the damage to the Republic's stability was lasting.

## Terms
*   **Proscriptions:** Officially published lists of enemies of the state who could be legally killed, with their property confiscated and rewards given to informers.
*   **Dictator:** A temporary emergency magistrate in the Roman Republic granted absolute power. Sulla revived this office with an unlimited term to enact his reforms.
*   **Grass Crown:** The highest Roman military decoration, awarded for saving the life of a fellow soldier or an entire army.
*   **Plebeian Tribune:** A magistrate intended to protect the common people's interests, with the power to veto actions of other magistrates. Sulla severely restricted their powers.
*   **Social War (91–88 BC):** A war fought between Rome and its Italian allies (*socii*) over the issue of granting Roman citizenship.
*   **First Mithridatic War (88–85 BC):** A conflict between Rome and King Mithridates VI of Pontus over control of Asia Minor.
*   **Maiestas:** A Roman law of treason, which Sulla expanded to control provincial governors.
*   **Centuriate Assembly:** A popular assembly organized by wealth and military centuries, which elected senior magistrates and passed laws.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Sulla's Motivations:** Scholars debate whether Sulla's reforms were a genuine attempt to restore the traditional Republic or a personal power grab disguised with archaic rhetoric. His resignation from the dictatorship is a key point in this debate.
*   **The "Sullan Myth":** Historians question whether the uniformly hostile view of Sulla as a tyrant emerged immediately after his death or was a later construction, possibly shaped by Caesarian propaganda after the civil wars.
*   **Effectiveness of Reforms:** His constitutional changes, particularly the weakening of the tribunate and the expansion of the Senate, are debated as to whether they were workable long-term or inherently unstable, contributing to the Republic's final collapse.
