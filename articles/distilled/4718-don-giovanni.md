# Don Giovanni

## Overview
*Don Giovanni* (K. 527) is a two-act opera by Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart with a libretto by Lorenzo Da Ponte. Premiered in 1787, it is a *dramma giocoso* (a genre blending comedy and serious drama) that tells the story of the legendary libertine Don Juan. It is considered one of the greatest operas ever written, celebrated for its masterful fusion of comedy, melodrama, and supernatural elements.

## Timeline
- **1630** — Tirso de Molina's play *El burlador de Sevilla*, the source for the Don Juan legend, is published.
- **Early 1787** — Giovanni Bertati's opera *Don Giovanni Tenorio* premieres in Venice.
- **Jan-Feb 1787** — Mozart's successful trip to Prague, leading to the commission for *Don Giovanni*.
- **28 Oct 1787** — Mozart completes the score.
- **29 Oct 1787** — World premiere at the National Theatre (Estates Theatre) in Prague.
- **7 May 1788** — Vienna premiere, supervised by Mozart, with new arias and revisions.
- **Early 20th C** — The final ensemble, omitted for over a century, begins to be regularly restored.

## Body

### Composition and Premiere
The opera was commissioned following Mozart's triumphant visit to Prague in early 1787. The subject was likely chosen because the Don Juan sub-genre of opera originated there. Lorenzo Da Ponte's libretto is based on Giovanni Bertati's for *Don Giovanni Tenorio*, which premiered in Venice earlier that year. Da Ponte copied Bertati's structure by opening with the Commendatore's murder and setting the action in a generic "city in Spain." The premiere was rapturously received, with contemporary reports stating Prague had "never heard the like."

### Music and Scoring
The score is for double woodwinds, brass, timpani, strings, and basso continuo. Mozart specified special effects, including three separate onstage ensembles playing different dance music simultaneously in the Act 1 ballroom scene. In Act 2, Don Giovanni plays the mandolin, and the Commendatore's interventions are accompanied by a wind chorale.

### Vienna Revision and Performance Traditions
For the 1788 Vienna premiere, Mozart wrote new arias for Don Ottavio ("Dalla sua pace") and Donna Elvira ("In quali eccessi... Mi tradì quell'alma ingrata"), and a duet for Leporello and Zerlina. He also cut sections of the Finale to make it more incisive. Historically, the final ensemble was omitted from performances for over a century, beginning with the original run, but is now almost invariably performed. Modern productions typically use both the original and Viennese arias for Don Ottavio and Donna Elvira, but rarely include the Leporello/Zerlina duet from the Vienna version.

### Synopsis
The opera follows the libertine Don Giovanni. In Act 1, he kills the Commendatore while fleeing from an attempted seduction of the Commendatore's daughter, Donna Anna. He later encounters former conquest Donna Elvira, attempts to seduce the peasant Zerlina, and hosts a ball where his crimes are exposed by the masked Anna, Elvira, and her fiancé Don Ottavio. In Act 2, Giovanni continues his deceptions, including a scene where he beats Masetto. The climax occurs when the statue of the Commendatore appears at a dinner, invites Giovanni to dine, and drags him to Hell after he refuses to repent. The surviving characters then announce their future plans, and the opera concludes with a moralizing ensemble.

## Terms
- **Dramma giocoso**: A genre of opera blending comic and serious elements.
- **Libretto**: The text of an opera.
- **Basso continuo**: A continuous bass line, typically played by a keyboard instrument and a cello, used in recitatives.
- **Recitative**: A style of delivery in operas and oratorios in which a singer is allowed to adopt the rhythms of ordinary speech.
- **Aria**: A self-contained piece for one voice, with or without orchestral accompaniment, typically part of a larger work.
- **Finale**: The concluding section of an act or an entire opera, often a complex ensemble.
- **Ensemble**: A piece of music for several singers or instrumentalists.
- **Serenade**: A piece of music sung or played in the open air, typically at night.
- **Catalogue Aria**: Leporello's famous aria ("Madamina, il catalogo è questo") listing Don Giovanni's conquests.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The choice of genre is debated; Mozart entered it as an *opera buffa* (comic opera), but its blend of comedy, melodrama, and supernatural horror makes it a unique *dramma giocoso*.
- The extent of Bertati's influence on Da Ponte's libretto is a point of scholarly discussion.
- Performance tradition regarding the final ensemble and the choice of arias for certain roles (like Don Ottavio) has varied significantly over time.
