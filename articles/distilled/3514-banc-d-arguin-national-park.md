# Banc d'Arguin National Park

## Overview
Banc d'Arguin National Park is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and major nature reserve on Mauritania's Atlantic coast. It protects one of the world's most important areas for migratory and breeding waterbirds, as well as rich fisheries and unique geological formations. The park's significance stems from its vast, relatively undisturbed intertidal mudflats and seagrass beds, which support immense bird populations and diverse marine life.

## Body

### Conservation and Human Use
The park was established in 1976 to protect natural resources, fisheries, and geological sites. Its extensive intertidal flats contain some of the world's most pristine seagrass beds, with minimal human impact. Only a small indigenous community of about 500 Imraguen tribespeople is permitted to fish within the park using traditional, non-motorized methods. A major conservation challenge is illegal fishing for sharks and rays, driven by international market demand.

### Avifauna (Birds)
The park is a globally critical site for birds, designated an Important Bird Area (IBA). Its mudflats host over two million wintering waders from the Palaearctic region, including species like the dunlin (up to one million) and bar-tailed godwit (up to 500,000). It also supports the largest waterbird colonies in West Africa, with 25,000–40,000 breeding pairs of species such as the white pelican and various terns. The mild climate and lack of disturbance make it essential for these migratory populations.

### Marine Life and Flora
The surrounding waters are among the richest fishing grounds in western Africa. They support endangered marine mammals year-round, including the Mediterranean monk seal and Atlantic humpback dolphin. Other cetaceans like orcas, pilot whales, and various baleen whales are also recorded. The rare false shark ray is only known from this region. Coastal vegetation is sparse, consisting mainly of tamarisk bushes, dwarf acacias, and shrubs on dunes.

### Geology and Climate
The park's geology features a flat-topped carbonate platform with extensive shoals. The bank edge forms a sharp step, deepening from 10–20 meters to 30–50 meters. The central and southern outer shelf contains the Arguin and Timiris Mud Wedges, which have grown rapidly over the past 9,000 years. The climate is humid and temperate due to oceanic trade winds, with very low rainfall (less than 30 mm annually in Nouadhibou) and moderate temperatures.

### Colonial History
The territory's rich fishery and strategic location made it highly contested by European powers. Portugal established a fort at Arguin in 1455, initially for gold trade but quickly shifting to the slave trade. Control passed to Spain (1580), the Dutch (1638), and then the French (1678). Local Moorish leaders skillfully played these rivals against each other. French sovereignty was formally recognized in 1815.

## Terms
- **Intertidal flats:** Coastal areas that are exposed to the air at low tide and underwater at high tide.
- **Piscivorous:** Fish-eating.
- **Palaearctic:** A biogeographic realm covering Europe, Asia north of the Himalayas, and northern Africa.
- **Afrotropical:** A biogeographic realm covering sub-Saharan Africa.
- **Gum arabic:** A natural gum harvested from acacia trees, used in food, inks, and other products.
- **Harmattan:** A dry, dusty wind from the Sahara that blows towards the Gulf of Guinea.
- **Holocene:** The current geological epoch, beginning about 11,700 years ago.
- **Homoclinal ramp:** A gently sloping, uniform continental shelf profile.

## Debates and Open questions
The source notes that the presence of highly coastal whale species like the North Atlantic right whale and the now-extinct Atlantic grey whale in the area is only "possibly" historical, indicating uncertainty. The primary ongoing debate concerns the conservation challenge of illegal shark and ray fishing, which threatens the park's ecosystem despite regulations.
