# Robert Walpole

## Overview
Robert Walpole (1676–1745) was a British Whig statesman widely regarded as the first *de facto* Prime Minister of Great Britain, serving from 1721 to 1742. His record-breaking tenure of over 20 years established the template for the modern premiership, emphasizing parliamentary management, political stability, and a policy of peace and prosperity that defined the "Whig Supremacy."

## Timeline
- **26 August 1676** — Born in Houghton, Norfolk.
- **1701** — Elected to Parliament for Castle Rising.
- **1712** — Imprisoned in the Tower of London for corruption charges.
- **1714** — Accession of George I; Walpole rises in the Whig government.
- **1717** — Resigns from Cabinet, beginning the "Whig Split."
- **1720** — South Sea Bubble financial crisis erupts.
- **3 April 1721** — Appointed First Lord of the Treasury, Chancellor of the Exchequer, and Leader of the House of Commons.
- **1727** — George II succeeds George I; Walpole retains office on Queen Caroline's advice.
- **1730** — Lord Townshend retires, leaving Walpole as the dominant minister.
- **1733** — Defeated in Parliament over his proposed Excise Bill.
- **1739** — Britain enters the War of Jenkins' Ear against Spain.
- **February 1742** — Resigns after a vote of no confidence; created Earl of Orford.
- **18 March 1745** — Dies in London.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Born to a Whig gentry family, Walpole was educated at Eton and King's College, Cambridge, before inheriting the family estate. He entered Parliament in 1701 and quickly rose through government posts, including Secretary at War. His career was interrupted in 1712 when he was impeached and imprisoned for corruption related to forage contracts, an event that cemented his reputation as a political martyr among Whigs.

### Rise to Power
Walpole's ascent was enabled by the Hanoverian succession in 1714, which favored the Whigs. He held senior positions like Chancellor of the Exchequer but resigned in 1717 during a Whig split. He returned to prominence after the 1720 South Sea Bubble crisis, where his skill in managing the scandal and protecting key ministers earned him the nickname "The Screen." Following the deaths and resignations of his rivals, he was appointed First Lord of the Treasury in 1721, the date often marking the start of his premiership.

### Premiership and Policy
Walpole's dominance, sometimes called the "Robinocracy," was built on expert parliamentary management and a policy of moderation. He prioritized peace, low taxes, and national prosperity, successfully reducing the land tax and the national debt. His power relied on personal influence with the King and control of patronage, not on a formal office. He faced persistent opposition from figures like Lord Bolingbroke and William Pulteney, who attacked his government in publications like *The Craftsman*.

### Decline and Fall
Walpole's long peace policy collapsed with the War of Jenkins' Ear (1739), which he initially opposed but was forced to accept. Military failures and growing opposition, including from a faction led by Frederick, Prince of Wales, weakened his position. After losing a key parliamentary vote in 1742, he resigned, was created Earl of Orford, and moved to the House of Lords. He remained an influential advisor, the "Minister behind the Curtain," until his death.

### Legacy
Walpole is credited with establishing stable Whig political supremacy and securing the Hanoverian succession. He pioneered the role of Prime Minister as a bridge between Crown and Parliament, though his power stemmed from personal favor rather than institutional authority. His strategy of peace and economic management contributed to national prosperity. His legacy is complex, viewed by contemporaries and historians as both a corrupt manipulator and a prudent, effective statesman who preserved the constitutional settlement of 1688.

## Terms
- **Prime Minister**: The informal title for the leading government minister; Walpole was the first to hold this role *de facto*, though he rejected the term.
- **Whig**: One of the two major political parties of the 18th century, generally supporting constitutional monarchy, parliamentary supremacy, and the Hanoverian succession.
- **South Sea Bubble**: A catastrophic financial crisis in 1720 caused by the collapse of the South Sea Company's stock, which implicated many government ministers.
- **Sinking Fund**: A financial mechanism Walpole used to reduce the national debt by setting aside government revenue for repayment.
- **Jacobitism**: A political movement supporting the restoration of the deposed Stuart royal line to the British throne.
- **Pocket Borough**: A parliamentary constituency controlled by a single patron, often a wealthy landowner, who could effectively choose its MP.
- **Excise Tax**: A tax on goods produced and sold within a country; Walpole's attempt to expand this tax led to major political defeat.
- **Whig Supremacy**: The period of near-continuous Whig political dominance in Britain from 1714 to the 1760s.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Nature of his power**: Scholars debate whether Walpole's authority was based on parliamentary support or primarily on his personal influence with the monarch.
- **"Robinocracy"**: The exact dates and nature of his dominant political system are matters of scholarly debate.
- **Corruption vs. pragmatism**: Historians disagree on whether Walpole institutionalized political corruption or simply used the patronage tools available to any 18th-century minister to maintain stable government.
- **Historical ranking**: While some rank him among Britain's greatest prime ministers for his stability and longevity, others criticize his later years for military failure and waning political skill.
