# Robert Falcon Scott

## Overview
Robert Falcon Scott (1868–1912) was a British Royal Navy officer who led two major expeditions to Antarctica during the Heroic Age of Antarctic Exploration. His first, the *Discovery* Expedition (1901–1904), established a new southern record (82°17′S) and discovered the Antarctic Plateau. His second, the *Terra Nova* Expedition (1910–1913), aimed to secure the South Pole for the British Empire. Scott’s five-man party reached the Pole on 17 January 1912, only to find Roald Amundsen’s Norwegian team had beaten them by five weeks. On the return journey, a planned rendezvous with supporting dog teams failed to materialize, and Scott and his four companions perished from cold, starvation, and exhaustion just 12.5 miles from a supply depot. Their bodies, discovered eight months later, held the first Antarctic fossils (*Glossopteris*), proving the continent was once forested and connected to others. Scott was initially celebrated as a tragic national hero, but his reputation suffered severe revisionist criticism in the late 20th century regarding his competence and planning. Early 21st-century scholarship has partially rehabilitated him, citing the extreme, anomalous weather of March 1912 and the rediscovery of his written orders for the dog teams, which contradicted claims he had neglected to issue them.

## Body

### Early Life and Naval Career
Scott was born into a family with naval traditions but faced financial ruin after his father sold the family brewery and invested poorly. He entered the Royal Navy at 13, progressing steadily to lieutenant by 1889 and excelling in torpedo training. A mysterious eight-month gap in his records (1889–1890) while serving on HMS *Amphion* has fueled speculation about a scandal, though biographer David Crane disputes the length and significance. Family financial collapse in 1894 and the deaths of his father (1897) and brother Archie (1898) left Scott as sole provider, making promotion a pressing necessity. In June 1899, a chance meeting with Sir Clements Markham, President of the Royal Geographical Society (RGS), alerted him to the planned *Discovery* Expedition. According to biographer David Crane, Scott volunteered primarily for the command opportunity rather than polar passion.

### The *Discovery* Expedition (1901–1904)
Jointly organized by the RGS and Royal Society, the expedition suffered from inexperience: few had polar experience, dogs died of disease, and scurvy appeared. Scott, Ernest Shackleton, and Edward Wilson marched to 82°17′S, a new southern record, but Shackleton collapsed on the return and was sent home early, sparking later tension. In the second season, Scott’s western journey discovered the Polar Plateau, a major geographical achievement. Scientific results were significant in biology and geology, though meteorological data were later criticized as amateurish. Scott’s insistence on Royal Navy discipline caused friction with the merchant navy contingent, notably second-in-command Albert Armitage. Despite later rivalry with Shackleton, public civilities were maintained.

### Interwar Years: Fame, Dispute, and Marriage
Returning in 1904, Scott became a celebrity, promoted to Captain, and wrote *The Voyage of the Discovery*. He resumed naval duties, commanding battleships and moving in high social circles. A dispute erupted when Shackleton announced plans to use *Discovery*’s old McMurdo Sound base for his *Nimrod* Expedition. Scott claimed "prior rights" to the Ross Sea sector, supported by Wilson. Shackleton eventually agreed to work east of 170°W but broke the promise when forced to land at Cape Royds. Modern historians debate the ethics: Ranulph Fiennes calls it a technical breach by Shackleton; Beau Riffenburgh argues Scott’s demand was ethically excessive compared to Fridtjof Nansen’s generosity. In 1908, Scott married sculptor Kathleen Bruce; their son Peter later founded the WWF.

### The *Terra Nova* Expedition (1910–1913): Preparation and First Season
Scott aimed to reach the Pole first, stating the main objective was "to secure for the British Empire the honour of this achievement." He adopted a mixed transport strategy: motor sledges (caterpillar-tracked), ponies, dogs, and man-hauling, consulting Fridtjof Nansen in Norway. Dog handler Cecil Meares purchased poor-quality Manchurian ponies in Siberia. *Terra Nova* nearly sank in a storm and was trapped in pack ice for 20 days, causing a late arrival. Depot-laying was hampered by weak ponies; the critical One Ton Depot was placed 35 miles north of its planned 80°S location. Lawrence Oates urged killing ponies to advance the depot; Scott refused. Amundsen’s presence at Bay of Whales, 69 miles closer to the Pole with superior dog teams, was confirmed.

### Journey to the Pole
The march began 1 November 1911 with a large support caravan. On 20 October, Scott gave Meares written orders to take dog teams south in early February to meet the returning party around 1 March at 82°–82°30′S. Support teams turned back progressively; on 4 January 1912 at 87°34′S, Scott chose the five-man polar party (himself, Wilson, Bowers, Oates, E. Evans). They reached the Pole on 17 January, finding Amundsen’s tent and letter. Scott’s diary records profound despair: "Great God! This is an awful place."

### The Last March
The 862-mile return began 19 January. Progress was initially good, but Edgar Evans deteriorated rapidly after falls, dying 17 February at the Beardmore Glacier foot. The remaining four faced worsening weather, fuel shortages at depots (due to evaporation/leakage), hunger, and exhaustion. At Cape Evans, the relief ship arrived in February. Surgeon-Lieutenant Atkinson, ordered to go south with dogs, instead unloaded the ship. When he finally departed, he encountered the scurvy-stricken Teddy Evans and sent the short-sighted Apsley Cherry-Garrard only to One Ton Depot (visible from base), not to the 82°S rendezvous, effectively cancelling Scott’s orders. Scott reached the 82°S meeting point on 27 February, three days early, noting the "horrid element of doubt." By 10 March, temperatures plummeted below −40 °C. Oates, severely frostbitten, walked to his death on 16–17 March. The final three (Scott, Wilson, Bowers) were trapped by a blizzard 12.5 miles from One Ton Depot. Scott’s last diary entry is dated 29 March: "For God's sake look after our people."

### Discovery and Scientific Legacy
The search party found the tent on 12 November 1912. Bodies were left in situ under a snow cairn topped by a cross made from Tryggve Gran’s skis. Beside them lay 35 lbs of *Glossopteris* fossils—the first Antarctic fossils—proving the continent was once temperate and connected to other landmasses (Gondwana). A memorial cross inscribed with Tennyson’s "To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield" was erected on Observation Hill in January 1913.

### Reputation: Hero, Bungler, Revision
Immediate reaction canonized Scott as a national hero of duty and sacrifice; a memorial fund raised £75,000 (equivalent ~£7.4M today). Over 30 British monuments followed, including the Scott Polar Research Institute. Post-WWII, the film *Scott of the Antarctic* (1948) sustained the myth. The revisionist turn began with Reginald Pound (1966) and David Thomson (1977), peaking with Roland Huntford’s *Scott and Amundsen* (1979), which portrayed Scott as a "heroic bungler" whose flawed planning and leadership doomed the party. This became orthodoxy, elevating Shackleton’s reputation in parallel. The 21st century brought a "revision of the revisionist view": Susan Solomon (2001) attributed the disaster to unprecedented Barrier weather (−40 °C in March); David Crane (2005) restored Scott’s humanity; Karen May (2012) proved Huntford’s claim that Scott issued only a casual oral order for the dogs was false—Scott had issued detailed written orders on 20 October 1911. The New York Times Book Review cautioned that Crane does not fully exonerate Scott from responsibility for the demise.

## Terms
- **Antarctic Plateau (Polar Plateau)**: The high, flat ice sheet covering East Antarctica, on which the South Pole sits; discovered by Scott’s western journey (1903).
- **Man-hauling**: Pulling sledges by human power without animal or mechanical assistance; the primary transport method on the Polar Plateau for Scott’s party.
- **One Ton Depot**: The largest supply cache on the Ross Ice Shelf, intended for 80°S but laid at 79°29′S due to pony weakness; the final, missed rendezvous point.
- **Glossopteris**: An extinct genus of seed ferns (trees); fossils found with Scott’s bodies proved Antarctica was once forested and part of Gondwana.
- **Barrier (Ross Ice Shelf)**: The massive floating ice shelf fronting the Ross Sea; the "Barrier" surface was the final, fatal leg of the return journey.
- **Heroic Age of Antarctic Exploration**: Period (c. 1897–1922) of major expeditions by Scott, Shackleton, Amundsen, Mawson, and others, characterized by limited technology and high risk.
- **Clements Markham**: RGS Secretary (later President) who "collected" promising naval officers for polar work; Scott’s patron and champion.
- **Cecil Meares**: Dog handler on *Terra Nova*; sent to Siberia for dogs/ponies; recipient of Scott’s written orders (20 Oct 1911) to meet the polar party with dog teams.
- **Lawrence Oates**: Cavalry officer in charge of ponies; advised killing ponies to advance depot; sacrificed himself ("I am just going outside and may be some time") on 16–17 March 1912.
- **Apsley Cherry-Garrard**: Youngest member of *Terra Nova*; author of *The Worst Journey in the World*; sent by Atkinson to One Ton Depot but not to 82°S, failing to meet Scott.

## Debates and open questions
- **Competence vs. Misfortune**: Was the disaster caused by Scott’s planning errors (mixed transport, pony selection, depot placement, refusal to kill ponies, man-hauling insistence) or by the extraordinary weather anomaly of March 1912 (Solomon) and the failure of the dog-team relief (May)?
- **Dog-Team Orders**: Huntford (1979) claimed Scott issued only a vague oral order to Teddy Evans during the march. May (2012) confirmed written orders to Meares on 20 October 1911. Why did Atkinson not execute them? (Prioritizing ship unloading, then Teddy Evans’ rescue, then sending non-navigator Cherry-Garrard only to One Ton Depot.)
- **Shackleton Dispute**: Was Scott’s claim of "prior rights" to the Ross Sea sector ethically justified or an overreach? Riffenburgh argues Nansen’s open generosity was the superior model.
- **Scurvy and Nutrition**: The *Discovery* Expedition suffered scurvy; *Terra Nova* used seal meat but fuel shortages prevented melting snow/cooking, leading to dehydration and effective starvation.
- **Motor Sledges**: Scott bet on experimental caterpillar-tracked motors; both failed early. Was this a reasonable innovation or a diversion of resources from proven dog teams?
- **Reputation Historiography**: How much of Scott’s posthumous reputation reflects changing cultural values (Edwardian duty vs. post-WWI disillusionment vs. modern scientific reassessment) rather than historical facts?
