# History of astronomy

## Overview
Astronomy is one of the oldest natural sciences, originating in prehistoric attempts to understand the universe for timekeeping, navigation, and religious purposes. Its development from mythological interpretation to a mathematical science fundamentally reshaped humanity's place in the cosmos, culminating in the Copernican Revolution and modern astrophysics.

## Timeline
- **c. 35,000 BC** — Possible lunar phase markings on bone sticks (Marshack theory, debated).
- **c. 8,000 BC** — Warren Field calendar created in Scotland, the oldest known calendar.
- **c. 1600 BC** — Nebra sky disc created in Germany, an early lunisolar calendar device.
- **c. 1200 BC** — Earliest significant Babylonian star catalogues.
- **c. 476–550 AD** — Aryabhata proposes Earth's rotation and a computational planetary model in India.
- **1543** — Nicolaus Copernicus publishes *De revolutionibus*, proposing a heliocentric model.
- **1608** — Invention of the telescope.
- **1609** — Johannes Kepler publishes his first two laws of planetary motion.
- **1610** — Galileo Galilei observes Jupiter's moons and phases of Venus.
- **1687** — Isaac Newton publishes *Principia*, deriving Kepler's laws from universal gravitation.
- **1781** — William Herschel discovers Uranus, the first new planet found in modern times.
- **1920s** — The "Great Debate" and Edwin Hubble's work confirm galaxies exist beyond the Milky Way.

## Body

### Prehistory and Ancient Civilizations
Early astronomical interest is evidenced by artifacts like the 32,500-year-old ivory tusk (possibly a star chart) and the Nebra sky disc (c. 1600 BC), which depicted celestial phenomena to regulate a lunisolar calendar. Structures like Stonehenge and the Goseck circle (c. 5000 BC) likely had astronomical alignments. In Mesopotamia, Babylonian astronomers (c. 1200 BC onward) developed the first mathematical models to predict celestial events, using a sexagesimal (base-60) system. Their records, like the *Enūma Anu Enlil*, formed the basis for later Greek and Islamic astronomy. In India, the *Vedanga Jyotisha* (c. 1350 BC) described rules for tracking the Sun and Moon. Chinese astronomers kept detailed records from the 6th century BC, including the first recorded supernova (185 AD). The Maya in Mesoamerica developed complex calendars based on cycles of Venus and other planets.

### Greek and Hellenistic Astronomy
The Greeks transformed astronomy into a mathematical science. Eudoxus and Callippus (4th century BC) developed geometric models of nested spheres. Aristarchus of Samos first proposed a heliocentric model (3rd century BC). Hipparchus (2nd century BC) created the first star catalog and discovered the precession of the equinoxes. The Antikythera mechanism (c. 150–100 BC) was an early astronomical computer. This tradition culminated with Ptolemy (2nd century AD), whose geocentric *Almagest* became the authoritative text for over a millennium.

### The Copernican Revolution
The modern era began with Copernicus's heliocentric model (1543). Tycho Brahe's accurate observations disproved the immutability of the heavens and provided data for Kepler, who discovered that planets move in elliptical orbits (Kepler's laws, 1609). Galileo's telescopic observations (1610), including Jupiter's moons and Venus's phases, provided evidence for heliocentrism. Newton's *Principia* (1687) unified celestial and terrestrial mechanics with his laws of motion and universal gravitation, explaining *why* the planets move as Kepler described.

### Modern Astronomy
The 19th century saw the rise of astrophysics through spectroscopy and photography. The first stellar parallax was measured in 1838, proving the vast distances to stars. The 20th century brought transformative discoveries: the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram (1913) classified stars by luminosity and temperature; Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin (1925) showed stars are primarily hydrogen and helium; and Edwin Hubble proved the Milky Way is one of many galaxies and that the universe is expanding, supporting the Big Bang model. The field expanded into new wavelengths (radio, X-ray, etc.) and subfields like cosmology.

## Terms
- **Heliocentric model**: A model of the Solar System with the Sun at the center.
- **Geocentric model**: A model of the Solar System with the Earth at the center.
- **Parallax**: The apparent shift in position of a nearby star against distant background objects, used to measure distance.
- **Spectroscopy**: The study of the interaction between matter and electromagnetic radiation, used to determine the composition, temperature, and motion of celestial objects.
- **Hertzsprung–Russell diagram**: A scatter plot of stars showing the relationship between their absolute magnitudes (luminosities) and spectral types (temperatures).
- **Lunisolar calendar**: A calendar that combines lunar and solar cycles, often requiring intercalary months to stay synchronized with the seasons.
- **Sexagesimal**: A numeral system with a base of 60, used by the Babylonians for astronomical calculations.
- **Proper motion**: The apparent motion of a star across the sky relative to more distant stars.

## Debates and open questions
- The interpretation of prehistoric artifacts as astronomical (e.g., Marshack's bone sticks, Lascaux cave drawings) is debated.
- The precise function and cultural significance of megalithic structures like Stonehenge remain subjects of scholarly discussion.
- The "Great Debate" of the 1920s about the nature of spiral nebulae and the size of the universe was resolved by Hubble's observations, but questions about the universe's ultimate fate and composition continue.
