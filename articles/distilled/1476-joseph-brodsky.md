# Joseph Brodsky

## Overview
Iosif Aleksandrovich Brodsky (1940–1996) was a Russian-American poet and essayist whose expulsion from the Soviet Union in 1972 transformed him into a global literary figure. Born in Leningrad to a Jewish family, he endured the Siege of Leningrad, state persecution for "social parasitism," and internal exile before being forced onto a plane to Vienna. With the aid of W. H. Auden, he settled in the United States, where he taught at major universities, won the 1987 Nobel Prize in Literature, and served as U.S. Poet Laureate (1991–92). Brodsky viewed poetry not as art or entertainment but as humanity’s "anthropological, genetic goal"—the supreme linguistic operation distinguishing humans from other species. His work, written in Russian and English, blends classical metaphysics, exile, and the moral weight of language, earning him recognition as a canonized classic of Russian literature and a major Christian poet of the 20th century.

## Body

### Early Life and Soviet Persecution
Brodsky was born into a marginalized Jewish family in Leningrad; his father was a Navy photographer, his mother an interpreter. The Siege of Leningrad (1941–44) nearly killed them and left Brodsky with chronic health problems. He left school at fifteen, worked as a milling machine operator, a morgue attendant at Kresty Prison, and on geological expeditions, while teaching himself Polish and English to translate Czesław Miłosz and John Donne. By 1958 he was known in literary circles for poems like "The Jewish Cemetery near Leningrad." In 1960 Anna Akhmatova became his mentor. A love triangle involving Marina Basmanova and fellow poet Dmitri Bobyshev precipitated Bobyshev’s denunciation of Brodsky. In 1963 a Leningrad newspaper labeled his poetry "pornographic and anti-Soviet"; he was interrogated, twice confined to psychiatric institutions, and tried for social parasitism in 1964. The judge asked who had enrolled him in the ranks of poets; Brodsky replied, "Who enrolled me in the ranks of the human race?" Sentenced to five years hard labor, he served 18 months in Norenskaya (Arkhangelsk region), a period he later called among the best of his life. International protests from figures including Evgeny Evtushenko, Dmitri Shostakovich, and Jean-Paul Sartre secured his early release in 1965.

### Exile and American Career
After release, Brodsky continued writing but was denied travel permission. In 1971 he twice refused invitations to emigrate to Israel. In 1972, psychiatrist Andrei Snezhnevsky—architect of the "sluggish schizophrenia" diagnosis used to imprison dissidents—declared Brodsky "not a valuable person" without examining him. Within days, authorities seized his papers and put him on a plane to Vienna. He never returned to Russia. In Vienna, Carl Proffer and W. H. Auden facilitated his move to the United States. Brodsky taught at the University of Michigan, Queens College, Smith, Columbia, Cambridge, and Mount Holyoke (as Andrew Mellon Professor). He became a U.S. citizen in 1977, won a MacArthur "genius" grant (1981), and was elected to the American Academy of Arts and Letters (1979). His essay collection *Less Than One* won the National Book Critics Circle Award (1986); the Nobel Prize followed in 1987. As Poet Laureate (1991), he proposed distributing free poetry anthologies in hotels, airports, hospitals, and supermarkets, arguing that poetry is "language's highest degree of maturity" and that neglecting it dooms society to "inferior modes of articulation."

### Personal Life and Relationships
Brodsky’s son Andrei was born in 1967 to Marina Basmanova; the child took her surname to avoid political persecution. Basmanova was barred from marrying Brodsky or joining him in exile. After the USSR’s collapse in 1991, Andrei was allowed to visit; they maintained a relationship until Brodsky’s death. In 1990 Brodsky married Maria Sozzani (Russian-Italian background); their daughter Anna was born in 1993. Brodsky suffered a heart attack in 1979, underwent open-heart surgery and two bypasses, and remained in frail health. He died in his Brooklyn Heights apartment in 1996 and was buried in Venice’s San Michele cemetery, near Ezra Pound and Igor Stravinsky.

### Work: Themes, Forms, and Influences
Brodsky’s major poetry collections include *A Part of Speech* (1977) and *To Urania* (1988); his essays are gathered in *Less Than One* (1986) and *On Grief and Reason* (1995). He also wrote the play *Marbles* (1989) and the prose meditation *Watermark* (1992) on Venice. W. H. Auden described him as a traditionalist lyric poet fascinated by "encounters with nature, reflections upon the human condition, death, and the meaning of existence." Brodsky mixed the physical and metaphysical, drawing on Roman poetry, English metaphysical poets (Donne to Auden), Mexican and Caribbean literature, and classical philosophy. A recurring theme is the poet’s duty to society: literature develops language and culture, and the Western literary tradition helped humanity survive Nazism, Communism, and two World Wars. He insisted poetry is not entertainment but an "anthropological, genetic goal"—talking back to language itself as "beauty, sensuality, wisdom, irony."

Key influences: Anna Akhmatova (mentor), Nadezhda Mandelstam, Osip Mandelstam, W. H. Auden, John Donne, Robert Frost, Czesław Miłosz, and contemporaries Derek Walcott, Tomas Venclova, Octavio Paz, Robert Lowell. Translators Anthony Hecht, Howard Moss, Derek Walcott, and Richard Wilbur shaped his English volumes; his own translations were criticized for "turgidness, lacking a native sense of musicality" (Henri Cole). A controversial early-1990s poem *On the Independence of Ukraine*, recited but not published, was later exploited by Russian state media after the 2014 Crimea annexation; his estate has sought its removal from websites.

## Terms
- **Samizdat**: Underground self-publishing in the Soviet Union; banned literature copied and circulated secretly.
- **Social parasitism (тунеядство)**: Soviet criminal charge applied to able-bodied adults deemed insufficiently productive; used to target dissidents and nonconformists.
- **Slugish schizophrenia (вялотекущая шизофрения)**: Pseudo-medical diagnosis devised by Andrei Snezhnevsky to institutionalize political dissenters indefinitely without trial.
- **Acmeism**: Early-20th-century Russian poetic movement (Gumilyov, Akhmatova, Mandelstam) emphasizing clarity, craft, and concrete imagery; Brodsky is seen as its late heir.
- **Poet Laureate (U.S.)**: Annual Library of Congress appointment to raise national appreciation of poetry; Brodsky served 1991–92.
- **Nobel Prize in Literature (1987)**: Awarded "for an all-embracing authorship, imbued with clarity of thought and poetic intensity."
- **MacArthur Fellowship**: "Genius grant" awarded 1981 for exceptional creativity.
- **San Michele Cemetery**: Historic cemetery island in Venice; burial site of Brodsky, Ezra Pound, Igor Stravinsky.
- **Ardis Publishers**: Michigan-based press co-founded by Carl Proffer; primary Russian-language publisher for Brodsky and other émigré writers.
- **Less Than One**: 1986 essay collection (criticism, memoir, literary portraits) that won the National Book Critics Circle Award.

## Debates and open questions
- **Christian poet designation**: Scholars and peers (Daniel Murphy, Irene Steckler, Viktor Krivulin) call Brodsky a Christian poet; Brodsky himself said in 1972, "I consider myself a Christian. Not a good one but I try to be." The tension between his Jewish ancestry, self-identification as Christian, and secular literary reception remains debated.
- **Role of Dmitri Bobyshev in denunciation**: Bobyshev is "widely held responsible" for denouncing Brodsky after a romantic rivalry over Marina Basmanova, but the exact mechanism of the denunciation is not definitively documented in the source.
- **Quality of self-translation**: Critics (notably Henri Cole) argue Brodsky’s own English translations are "turgid" and lack musicality, while his collaborations with poet-translators (Hecht, Walcott, Wilbur) are superior. The extent to which this affects his standing in English-language canon is unresolved.
- **Political use of *On the Independence of Ukraine***: The poem, recited in the early 1990s but unpublished, was declared "Poem of the Year" by Russian state media after the 2014 Crimea annexation. Brodsky’s estate has prosecuted websites publishing it. The poem’s actual content, Brodsky’s intent, and the ethics of its posthumous weaponization are open questions.
- **Canonization vs. resentment**: Andrey Ranchin calls Brodsky the only modern Russian poet already a "canonized classic," yet Ludmila Shtern describes intense resentment in émigré circles, where his influence over contracts, posts, and grants made him a "fortune maker" and led to personal animosity. The gap between institutional canonization and community reception is a live scholarly topic.
