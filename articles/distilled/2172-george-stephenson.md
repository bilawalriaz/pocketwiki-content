# George Stephenson

## Overview
George Stephenson (1781–1848) was an English engineer renowned as the "Father of Railways." His pioneering work on early public railways, particularly the Stockton and Darlington Railway and the Liverpool and Manchester Railway, established the technical and commercial viability of steam locomotion. His chosen rail gauge became the basis for the world's standard gauge, making him a foundational figure of the Industrial Revolution.

## Body

### Early Life and Colliery Work
Born to illiterate parents, Stephenson worked in coal mines from a young age, becoming an expert in steam-driven machinery. He educated himself in reading, writing, and arithmetic at night school. His practical skill led to his promotion to enginewright at Killingworth Collieries in 1811, where he became responsible for all colliery engines.

### The Safety Lamp Controversy
In 1815, Stephenson independently developed a miner's safety lamp, the "Geordie Lamp," to prevent firedamp explosions. This coincided with Humphry Davy's work. A local committee exonerated Stephenson of plagiarism, and a later House of Commons committee found he had equal claim to the invention. The dispute gave him a lifelong distrust of theoretical scientific experts.

### Locomotive Development and Early Railways
Stephenson designed his first successful flanged-wheel adhesion locomotive, *Blücher*, in 1814. He solved early problems of locomotive weight damaging rails by distributing weight across multiple wheels. He built the Hetton Railway (1822), the first to use no animal power. His company, Robert Stephenson and Company, built *Locomotion No. 1* for the Stockton and Darlington Railway (1825), the first public railway to use steam locomotives for passengers. The gauge he chose for this line, 4 ft 8+1⁄2 in, later became the standard gauge.

### The Liverpool and Manchester Railway
Stephenson's greatest achievement was the Liverpool and Manchester Railway (1830), the first inter-city line to use locomotives. He engineered a challenging route, famously crossing the Chat Moss bog. The opening was marred by the death of MP William Huskisson, but the railway was a resounding success. His locomotive *Rocket*, designed with his son Robert, won the Rainhill Trials competition to supply the line.

### Later Career and Legacy
Stephenson became the most sought-after railway engineer in Britain, working on lines like the North Midland and Manchester and Leeds. He was conservative in his route planning, often favoring longer, level lines over direct, steep ones. He became the first president of the Institution of Mechanical Engineers in 1847. His work established the railway as a key component of the Industrial Revolution, and his standard gauge was adopted worldwide.

## Terms
- **Flanged-wheel adhesion locomotive**: A steam engine that gains traction through contact between its flanged wheels and the rail, rather than using gears or other mechanisms.
- **Firedamp**: A flammable gas (primarily methane) found in coal mines, which can form explosive mixtures with air.
- **Gauge**: The distance between the inner faces of the two rails of a railway track.
- **Rainhill Trials**: An 1829 competition to select a locomotive for the Liverpool and Manchester Railway, which *Rocket* won.
- **Skew bridge**: A bridge that crosses a railway or road at an angle other than 90 degrees, requiring specialized construction.
- **Standard gauge**: The 4 ft 8+1⁄2 in (1,435 mm) gauge adopted by most of the world's railways, based on Stephenson's gauge.
- **Wrought-iron**: A form of iron with a very low carbon content, malleable and tough, used for early railway rails as it was less brittle than cast iron.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The priority and originality of the safety lamp invention remains a historical debate between the claims of George Stephenson and Humphry Davy.
- Stephenson's conservative engineering philosophy, which favored longer, more expensive routes to avoid steep gradients, is contrasted with the more direct approaches of successors like Joseph Locke.
