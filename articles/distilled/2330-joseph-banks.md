# Joseph Banks

## Overview
Sir Joseph Banks (1743–1820) was a preeminent English naturalist, botanist, and scientific administrator whose career shaped 18th-century exploration, botany, and British colonial policy. As President of the Royal Society for over 41 years and a key advisor to King George III, he used his influence to promote global botanical collection, scientific voyages, and the British colonization of Australia, making him a central figure in the Enlightenment-era expansion of natural knowledge and empire.

## Timeline
- **1743** — Born in London; inherits Revesby Abbey estate in 1764.
- **1766** — Elected to the Royal Society; leads first scientific expedition to Newfoundland and Labrador.
- **1768–1771** — Serves as naturalist on Captain James Cook's first voyage on HMS *Endeavour*.
- **1771** — Returns to England from the *Endeavour* voyage with fame and vast collections.
- **1772** — Leads a scientific expedition to Iceland and the Hebrides.
- **1773** — Appointed informal director of the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew by King George III.
- **1778** — Becomes President of the Royal Society.
- **1781** — Made a baronet.
- **1795** — Invested as Knight of the Order of the Bath (KB).
- **1820** — Dies in London; buried at St Leonard's Church, Heston.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born into wealth, Banks was educated at Harrow, Eton, and Oxford, where he focused on natural history. He left Oxford without a degree in 1764, inheriting his father's estate. In London, he immersed himself in scientific circles at the Chelsea Physic Garden and British Museum, meeting Swedish botanist Daniel Solander and corresponding with Carl Linnaeus. This network fueled his ambition to join voyages of discovery.

### The *Endeavour* Voyage and Scientific Fame
Banks's reputation was cemented by his participation in Cook's first voyage (1768–1771). He funded and led a team of scientists and artists, including Solander and Sydney Parkinson. The expedition collected thousands of specimens from Brazil, Tahiti, New Zealand, and Australia, where they made the first major European collection of Australian flora. Banks documented new species, including the first recorded observation of a kangaroo. The voyage made him an immediate celebrity and provided the foundation for his later work.

### Patron of Science and Kew Gardens
Returning to England, Banks became the informal director of the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew in 1773. He dispatched plant hunters worldwide, transforming Kew into the world's leading botanical garden. As President of the Royal Society from 1778, he was a central node in the international scientific community, supporting voyages like those of George Vancouver and William Bligh, and fostering correspondence with figures like Alexander von Humboldt. He also advised the British government on scientific matters, including the official account of the Macartney Embassy to China.

### Advocacy for Australian Colonization
Banks was the most influential proponent of British settlement in New South Wales. He advised the government on all Australian matters for two decades, corresponding with its early governors. He advocated for Botany Bay as a convict settlement and later backed William Bligh's governorship, a move that contributed to the Rum Rebellion. His support for colonization was intertwined with his scientific interest in the continent's unique flora and fauna.

## Terms
*   **Linnean:** Referring to the system of biological classification developed by Carl Linnaeus, which Banks used to describe new species.
*   **Florilegium:** A collection of botanical illustrations; Banks's *Florilegium*, based on *Endeavour* specimens, was published posthumously in 35 volumes (1980–1990).
*   **President of the Royal Society:** The elected head of the UK's national academy of sciences, a position Banks held from 1778 to 1820.
*   **Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew:** A world-famous botanical research and education institution in London, which Banks helped establish as a global center for plant collection and study.
*   **Rum Rebellion:** The 1808 overthrow of Governor William Bligh in New South Wales, an event Banks supported and which embarrassed him after the rebels were acquitted.
*   **New South Wales:** The British colony established in 1788 in eastern Australia, which Banks strongly advocated for and advised on.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   The extent of Banks's direct responsibility for the harsh treatment of Aboriginal Australians, including his collection of the severed head of Pemulwuy, is a subject of historical scrutiny.
*   The "invisible ships" myth, which suggests Aboriginal Australians did not initially see the *Endeavour*, may have originated from Banks's own account of the approach to Botany Bay.
*   The dispersal of his papers after his death was contentious; the British Museum declined to purchase them, leading to their sale and scattering across institutions in Australia and the UK.
