# John Gielgud

## Overview
Sir John Gielgud (1904–2000) was an English actor and director whose eight-decade career defined classical British theatre. With Laurence Olivier, Ralph Richardson, and Michael Redgrave, he formed a quartet that dominated the mid‑20th‑century stage. A member of the Terry theatrical dynasty, Gielgud was celebrated for his mellifluous voice—once called a “silver trumpet muffled in silk”—and his mastery of Shakespearean verse, particularly as Hamlet, Richard II, and Prospero. He pioneered permanent West End companies in the 1930s, later embraced film in his sixties (winning an Oscar for *Arthur*, 1981), and achieved the rare EGOT (Emmy, Grammy, Oscar, Tony). His 1953 arrest for a homosexual offence briefly threatened his career, but public and professional support allowed him to continue. In his later decades he found new stage roles in works by Alan Bennett, David Storey, and Harold Pinter, cementing a legacy that bridged the classical tradition and modern drama.

## Body

### Background and early training
Gielgud was born into a family with deep theatrical roots: his mother, Kate Terry‑Gielgud, belonged to the Terry dynasty (Ellen, Fred, Marion Terry, Mabel Terry‑Lewis, Edith and Edward Gordon Craig). His father, Frank Gielgud, was a stockbroker of Lithuanian‑Polish descent whose family estates were lost after the 1830–31 uprising against Russian rule. After Westminster School—where he saw Sarah Bernhardt and music‑hall stars—Gielgud persuaded his reluctant parents to let him study drama. He trained at Constance Benson’s school (1921) and then at RADA (1923) under Kenneth Barnes, Helen Haye, and Claude Rains. Early professional experience came through his cousin Phyllis Neilson‑Terry (1922) and Nigel Playfair, who cast him in the British premiere of the Čapek brothers’ *The Insect Play* (1923). Repertory seasons at the Oxford Playhouse (1924–25) gave him wide classical and modern roles, notably Trofimov in *The Cherry Orchard*, his first Chekhov.

### Old Vic and the making of a Shakespearean
Harcourt Williams invited Gielgud to the Old Vic (1929–31), a subsidised house offering classics to working‑class audiences at low prices. There he played Romeo, Richard II, Hamlet (complete text, a radical innovation), Mark Antony, Benedick, and King Lear. Critics hailed his Richard II as “work of genuine distinction” and his Hamlet as “the high water‑mark of English Shakespearean acting of our time” (James Agate). The Hamlet transfer to the Queen’s Theatre drew West End audiences and cemented his status. He also formed a lifelong, if initially wary, friendship with Ralph Richardson, whom Williams saw as a potential successor.

### West End stardom and the Queen’s Theatre company
Returning to the West End, Gielgud scored a commercial hit with *The Good Companions* (1931, 331 performances) and began directing (Oxford University *Romeo and Juliet*, 1932). He starred in the modern‑language history *Richard of Bordeaux* (1932), a three‑year sell‑out, and directed a celebrated *Hamlet* (1934) and *Romeo and Juliet* (1935) with Ashcroft and Olivier—where he and Olivier swapped Mercutio/Romeo mid‑run. After a critical failure (*He Was Born Gay*, 1937), he invested his American *Hamlet* earnings (£5,000) to form a resident company at the Queen’s Theatre (1937–38), presenting Richard II, *The School for Scandal*, *Three Sisters*, and *The Merchant of Venice* with a roster including Redgrave, Ashcroft, and Devine. The venture earned superlative reviews but little profit.

### War, post‑war, and the Old Vic triumvirate
During World War II Gielgud entertained troops, directed *The Beggar’s Opera* (1940), and reopened the Old Vic with Granville‑Barker and Guthrie, earning praise for a “virile Renaissance” Prospero. He declined Richardson’s 1944 proposal for a three‑man directorate (Gielgud, Olivier, Richardson) at the Old Vic, predicting conflict. His 1944–45 Haymarket season included a Hamlet many called his finest. Post‑war, he toured *Hamlet* and *Blithe Spirit* for ENSA (1945–46), played Raskolnikoff in *Crime and Punishment* (1946–47), and directed hits like *The Heiress* (1949, 644 performances) and *The Lady’s Not for Burning* (1949), the latter launching Richard Burton and Claire Bloom.

### 1950s: film breakthrough, personal crisis, and *The Ages of Man*
At Stratford, Peter Brook’s *Measure for Measure* (1950) revealed a new naturalism in Gielgud’s Angelo. His first Hollywood film, *Julius Caesar* (1953), cast him as Cassius alongside Brando and Mason; he enjoyed California’s relaxed attitude toward homosexuality. Knighted in 1953, he was arrested that October for “cottaging” (cruising in a public lavatory). The Home Secretary, David Maxwell Fyfe, pursued homosexual prosecutions vigorously. Gielgud feared ruin, but a standing ovation at his next performance (*A Day by the Sea*) signalled public loyalty. He suffered a nervous breakdown months later but never spoke publicly of the incident. With few new plays suiting him—British theatre turned to avant‑garde works like Osborne’s *The Entertainer* (1957)—he sustained his profile through the one‑man anthology *The Ages of Man* (1956–67), performing Shakespeare speeches in evening dress on a bare stage. It won a Special Tony (1959) and a Grammy for its 1979 recording. He also directed Peter Shaffer’s debut, *Five Finger Exercise* (1958), and made his television debut (1959).

### 1960s: film embrace and modern stage roles
An Oscar nomination for King Louis VII in *Becket* (1964) confirmed film’s value. Gielgud directed Burton’s Broadway *Hamlet* (1964) and told his agent to accept reasonable film offers. He appeared in *The Loved One* (1965), *Chimes at Midnight* (1966), and character roles in *The Shoes of the Fisherman* (1968) and *Oh! What a Lovely War* (1969). In 1962 he met Martin Hensler, a Hungarian‑born interior designer; they became a long‑term couple, moving to Wotton House, Buckinghamshire. The breakthrough modern stage role came in 1968 as the Headmaster in Alan Bennett’s *Forty Years On*, earning raves for a “delicious comic creation.” He also directed Chekhov’s *Ivanov*, Ustinov’s *Half Way Up the Tree*, and Mozart’s *Don Giovanni*.

### 1970s: career revival with Storey, Pinter, and film character work
Gielgud and Richardson reunited in David Storey’s *Home* (1970), a minimalist two‑hander in a mental‑home garden, hailed as “two of the greatest performances of two careers” (Clive Barnes). He directed Coward and Maugham revivals (1973–74) and ended his directing career with *The Gay Lord Quex* (1975). In Pinter’s *No Man’s Land* (1975), directed by Peter Hall at the National, he played Spooner to Richardson’s Hirst—a critical and box‑office success touring to Broadway and television. Julian Mitchell’s *Half‑Life* (1977) brought further acclaim. Film work included *Galileo* (1974), *Murder on the Orient Express* (1974), the “embarrassing” *Caligula* (1979), and Alain Resnais’s *Providence* (1977), for which he won a New York Film Critics Circle award as a dying, drunken author. Television roles ranged from Lord Henry Wotton (*The Picture of Dorian Gray*, 1976) to Chorus in *Romeo and Juliet* (1978).

### Later years: film proliferation, final stage bows, and legacy
In the 1980s Gielgud made over twenty films, often cameos “lending distinction” (*The Elephant Man*, *Chariots of Fire*, *Gandhi*, *The Shooting Party*, *Plenty*). His greatest screen success was Hobson in *Arthur* (1981), winning the Oscar for Best Supporting Actor; he avoided ceremonies, disliking “mutual congratulation baloney.” Television highlights included Edward Ryder in *Brideshead Revisited* (1982) and Aaron Jastrow in *War and Remembrance* (Emmy nomination). His last West End play was *The Best of Friends* (1988), where he momentarily forgot lines upon seeing a 1938 review of his own *Three Sisters* performance. He played Prospero for the final time in *Prospero’s Books* (1990), and his last feature film was Pope Pius V in *Elizabeth* (1998). He continued BBC radio work until 1994 (over fifty productions). For his 90th birthday he performed Lear with a star cast (Branagh, Dench, Atkins, Thompson). Partner Martin Hensler died in 1999; Gielgud declined physically and died at home on 21 May 2000, aged 96.

### Honours, character, and reputation
State honours: Knight Bachelor (1953), Légion d’Honneur (1960), Companion of Honour (1977), Order of Merit (1996). President of RADA (1977–89); first honorary fellow (1989). The Globe Theatre was renamed the Gielgud Theatre (1994). Gielgud was indifferent to religion and politics (once asking Clement Attlee “Whereabouts are you living now?”). Critics and peers placed him in the “great trinity of theatrical knights” with Olivier and Richardson. Michael Coveney wrote that Gielgud was “the greatest speaker of Shakespearean verse this century” and a pioneer of permanent West End companies without whom “there would be no National Theatre or Royal Shakespeare Company.” In his own words, the theatre was “more than an occupation or a profession; for me it has been a life.”

## Terms
- ****Old Vic**** — A London theatre (south of the Thames) run by Lilian Baylis, offering classic plays and operas at low prices; the principal training ground for Shakespearean actors in the mid‑20th century.
- ****RADA**** — Royal Academy of Dramatic Art; the UK’s leading drama school where Gielgud trained in 1923.
- ****Cottaging**** — British slang for seeking or engaging in homosexual sex in public lavatories; illegal until the 1960s.
- ****The Ages of Man**** — Gielgud’s one‑man anthology of Shakespeare speeches and sonnets (compiled by George Rylands), performed worldwide 1956–67 in evening dress on a bare stage.
- ****EGOT**** — The distinction of winning an Emmy, Grammy, Oscar, and Tony Award; Gielgud achieved this rare feat.
- ****Terry dynasty**** — A British theatrical family including Ellen, Fred, and Marion Terry, Mabel Terry‑Lewis, and Edith and Edward Gordon Craig; Gielgud’s maternal relatives.
- ****Queen’s Theatre company**** — Gielgud’s self‑financed resident company (1937–38) at the Queen’s Theatre, presenting classic repertory with a permanent ensemble.
- ****ENSA**** — Entertainments National Service Association; organised entertainment for British armed forces during World War II.
- ****Order of Merit**** — A British honour limited to 24 living recipients, awarded for exceptional distinction in the arts, learning, literature, or science; Gielgud received it in 1996.
- ****Prospero’s Books**** — Peter Greenaway’s 1990 film adaptation of *The Tempest*, featuring Gielgud’s final leading screen role as Prospero.

## Debates and open questions
- **Impact of the 1953 arrest on artistic choices**: Scholars debate whether the scandal accelerated Gielgud’s turn to the safer, solo *Ages of Man* and delayed his engagement with the new “angry young men” drama of the late 1950s.
- **Reluctance toward film before the 1960s**: Gielgud later called his early dismissal of cinema “stupid”; historians weigh how much this cost him artistically versus preserving his stage primacy.
- **Legacy of the Queen’s Theatre company**: Though short‑lived and unprofitable, it is cited as a model for the later National Theatre and Royal Shakespeare Company; the extent of its direct influence remains a topic of theatre‑historical discussion.
- **Private vs. public advocacy for gay rights**: Gielgud donated privately to campaign groups but refused public endorsement; biographers differ on whether this reflects caution, generational reticence, or a belief that his art spoke sufficiently.
