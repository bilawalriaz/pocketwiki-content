# Animation

## Overview

Animation is a filmmaking technique in which pictures are created or manipulated and played in sequence to create the illusion of moving images. It is a recognized artistic medium, particularly in entertainment, and is contrasted with live action, though hybrid films combining both are common. Animation matters because it spans art, technology, and industry—from hand-drawn cels to computer-generated imagery (CGI)—and is used in entertainment, education, propaganda, and commerce. The illusion of motion relies on rapid image succession, traditionally explained by persistence of vision, phi phenomenon, or beta movement, though exact neurological causes remain uncertain.

## Timeline

- **1833** — Joseph Plateau's phenakistiscope introduces stroboscopic animation.
- **1892–1900** — Émile Reynaud screens hand-painted animations at the Musée Grévin, Paris.
- **1908** — Émile Cohl's *Fantasmagorie*, regarded as the oldest complete hand-drawn animation on film.
- **1917** — Quirino Cristiani makes first feature-length animated film *El Apóstol* (now lost).
- **1926** — Lotte Reiniger releases *Die Abenteuer des Prinzen Achmed*, oldest extant animated feature.
- **1928** — *Steamboat Willie* popularizes synchronized sound, launching Disney's dominance.
- **1937** — Disney's *Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs*, first major animated feature.
- **1941** — *Princess Iron Fan* becomes Asia's first animated feature.
- **1960** — Hanna-Barbera's *The Flintstones*, first prime-time animated series.
- **1980s–1990s** — CAPS system enables fully digital feature (*The Rescuers Down Under*); *Toy Story* (1995) makes 3D CGI dominant.
- **1991** — *Beauty and the Beast* first animated film nominated for Best Picture.

## Body

### Etymology and early history

The word *animation* derives from Latin *animātiō*, meaning "bestowing of life"; its earlier English meaning was "liveliness." Long before cinema, puppets, automatons, shadow puppets, and magic lantern projections captivated audiences. In 1833, Joseph Plateau's phenakistiscope introduced stroboscopic animation—sequential pictures on a spinning disc viewed through apertures—later applied in the zoetrope (1866), flip book (1868), praxinoscope (1877), and film. Émile Reynaud's Théâtre Optique screenings (1892–1900) likely inspired cinematographic pioneers.

### Silent era and golden age

Cinematography emerged in the 1890s, but animation took years to reach cinemas. *The Haunted Hotel* (1907) popularized stop motion and reportedly inspired Émile Cohl's *Fantasmagorie* (1908). Ladislas Starevich created puppet animations from 1910; Winsor McCay made influential hand-drawn films like *Little Nemo* (1911) and *Gertie the Dinosaur* (1914). In the 1910s, John Randolph Bray and Earl Hurd patented cel animation, dominating the industry for decades. Felix the Cat (1919) became the first fully realized anthropomorphic animal character. *Steamboat Willie* (1928) popularized synchronized sound, and Mickey Mouse's success marked the start of the American golden age (lasting to the 1960s), with studios introducing iconic characters like Donald Duck, Betty Boop, Popeye, Bugs Bunny, and Tom and Jerry.

### Global production and features before CGI

Europe contributed significantly: Starevich's *The Cameraman's Revenge* (1912), Alexandre Alexeïeff's pinscreen animation *A Night on Bald Mountain* (1933), and Lotte Reiniger's silhouette feature *Die Abenteuer des Prinzen Achmed* (1926). China's *Princess Iron Fan* (1941) was Asia's first animated feature. Cristiani's *El Apóstol* (1917) was the first feature-length film but is now lost. Disney's *Snow White* (1937) was a landmark; later features like *Pinocchio* and *Fantasia* (both 1940) failed partly due to WWII cutting off foreign markets. Disney remained the only regular American feature producer until Ralph Bakshi and later Sullivan-Bluth Studios (starting with *An American Tail*, 1986). The Soviet Soyuzmultfilm studio produced 1,582 titles by 2018.

### Television and the computer switch

From the 1950s, animation became popular on television, especially Saturday-morning cartoons for children. Hanna-Barbera produced hits like *The Flintstones* (1960–1966), *Scooby-Doo* (since 1969), and *The Smurfs* (1981–1989). American TV's demand for quantity led to cheaper limited animation and formulaic scripts, until a "renaissance" with *The Simpsons* (1987) and *SpongeBob SquarePants* (since 1999). Japanese anime became internationally successful from the 1960s, and European producers often co-produced with Japanese studios. Computer animation developed since the 1940s; *The Rescuers Down Under* was the first fully digital feature (using Pixar's CAPS system). *Toy Story* (1995) made 3D CGI dominant, and most cel studios switched to computer animation around the 1990s as it proved cheaper and more profitable.

### Production process

Animated films share labor intensity and high costs with live action, but the marginal cost of an extra shot is higher in animation, as every take must be manually rendered. Disney began storyboarding in the 1930s to ensure scenes advance the plot. Consistency across large teams (typically 500–600 people, 50–70 animators) is achieved through visual development artists who set an overall look and palette; character designers draw model sheets. Traditionally, storyboard artists wrote films, but Hanna-Barbera pioneered the written screenplay method for television in the 1960s, which became dominant despite tension between writers and artists. Disney adopted this slowly: *One Hundred and One Dalmatians* (1961) was a short-lived experiment, followed by *The Great Mouse Detective* (1986). Actors record vocal tracks separately, allowing more stars per film.

### Techniques

**Traditional animation** (cel or hand-drawn) photographs drawings on transparent acetate cels over painted backgrounds; it became obsolete by the early 21st century, replaced by digital scanning and coloring, sometimes called "tradigital." **Full animation** uses detailed, smooth movement (12–24 drawings per second); **limited animation** uses fewer drawings for economy or stylization. **Rotoscoping** (patented by Max Fleischer, 1917) traces live-action footage. **Live-action blending** combines drawn characters with live actors, as in *Who Framed Roger Rabbit* (1988). **Stop motion** physically manipulates objects, with subtypes including puppet animation, claymation, cutout, silhouette, model animation (e.g., Ray Harryhausen's work), go motion (with motion blur, invented for *The Empire Strikes Back*), and pixilation (using live humans). **Computer animation** includes 2D (bitmap/vector graphics) and 3D (polygon meshes with rigging and keyframes); related techniques include cel shading, machinima, motion capture, and physically based animation. **Mechanical animation** includes animatronics (Disney's Audio-Animatronics) and the magic lantern. Other methods include drawn-on-film, paint-on-glass, pinscreen, sand animation, and flip books.

## Terms

- **Cel animation**: Traditional hand-drawn animation on transparent acetate sheets.
- **CGI**: Computer-generated imagery; animation created digitally.
- **Stop motion**: Animating by photographing manipulated real objects frame by frame.
- **Stroboscopic effect**: Illusion of motion from rapid succession of slightly different images.
- **Storyboard**: Sequence of drawings showing each scene, used to plan animation.
- **Limited animation**: Economic technique using fewer drawings per second.
- **Rotoscoping**: Tracing live-action movement frame by frame.
- **Rigging**: Adding a digital skeletal structure to a 3D mesh to control movement.
- **Animatronics**: Mechatronic machines that appear animate, used in theme parks.
- **Pixilation**: Stop-motion technique using live humans as characters.

## Debates and open questions

The exact neurological causes of the illusion of motion remain uncertain; it has traditionally been attributed to persistence of vision, phi phenomenon, or beta movement, but this is still debated. The 1954 British adaptation of *Animal Farm* is speculated to have been funded by the CIA for Cold War propaganda purposes, though this is not confirmed. The term "cartoon" was shortened to "toon" in the late 1980s, popularized by *Who Framed Roger Rabbit* (1988), reflecting evolving terminology.
