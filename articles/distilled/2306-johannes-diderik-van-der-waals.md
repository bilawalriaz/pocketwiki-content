# Johannes Diderik van der Waals

## Overview
Johannes Diderik van der Waals (1837–1923) was a Dutch theoretical physicist whose work on the equation of state for gases and liquids fundamentally shaped modern molecular science. He received the 1910 Nobel Prize in Physics for this work, which provided the first theoretical model explaining the continuity between gas and liquid phases and affirmed the real existence of molecules at a time when their reality was debated.

- **8 March 1923** — Died in Amsterdam.

## Body

### Early Career and Education
Van der Waals began his career as a primary school teacher, lacking the classical language education required for university entrance. He later qualified as a teacher for the new HBS secondary schools, which allowed him to resume university studies part-time. A change in Dutch law finally granted him dispensation from the classical language requirement, enabling him to pursue doctoral studies.

### The 1873 Thesis and the Equation of State
Van der Waals's doctoral thesis, *On the Continuity of the Gaseous and Liquid State*, was a landmark work. At the time, the molecular structure of fluids was not widely accepted, and liquid and vapor were often considered chemically distinct. Van der Waals attributed the non-ideal behavior of real gases to intermolecular interactions. He derived an equation of state by assuming molecules have a finite volume and attract each other. This model showed that gas and liquid phases merge continuously, affirming the reality of molecules and allowing estimates of their size and attractive strength. His work revolutionized the study of equations of state and was praised by James Clerk Maxwell.

### Later Scientific Work and Influence
In 1880, Van der Waals published the law of corresponding states, which expressed his equation in a universal form applicable to all substances using critical-point parameters. This law was a guide for later experiments leading to the liquefaction of hydrogen and helium. He also developed a thermodynamic theory of capillarity (1893) and a theory of binary solutions (1890). His work directly influenced 20th-century molecular physics by establishing that molecular properties (size, shape, attraction) are the basis for formulating the thermodynamic behavior of fluids. Heike Kamerlingh Onnes, who first liquefied helium in 1908, was significantly influenced by Van der Waals's pioneering work.

### Personal Life
Van der Waals married Anna Magdalena Smit in 1865; they had three daughters and one son, Johannes Diderik van der Waals Jr., who also became a theoretical physicist. His wife died of tuberculosis in 1881, and her death deeply affected him, leading to a decade-long pause in his publications. He never remarried.

## Terms
- **Equation of state:** A mathematical relationship between the state variables (pressure, volume, temperature) of a substance.
- **Intermolecular forces:** Forces of attraction or repulsion between molecules.
- **Critical temperature:** The temperature above which a gas cannot be liquefied by pressure alone.
- **Law of corresponding states:** A principle stating that all gases, when compared at their critical points, behave similarly.
- **Capillarity:** The ability of a liquid to flow in narrow spaces without the assistance of, or even in opposition to, external forces like gravity.
- **Binary solution:** A mixture composed of two components.

## Debates and Open questions
Van der Waals's work was developed during a period when the existence of molecules was actively disputed. A strong philosophical current, led by figures like Ernst Mach and Wilhelm Ostwald, denied the reality of molecules, considering the molecular hypothesis unnecessary and unproven. Van der Waals's thesis and subsequent work were a direct affirmation of molecular reality, helping to win over opponents to the molecular theory.
