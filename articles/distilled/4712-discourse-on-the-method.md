# Discourse on the Method

## Overview
*Discourse on the Method* is a foundational 1637 philosophical treatise by René Descartes that outlines his method for achieving certain knowledge. It is pivotal for modern philosophy and science, introducing the famous principle "I think, therefore I am" and establishing the epistemological system known as Cartesianism through a process of radical doubt.

## Timeline
- **1637** — *Discourse on the Method* is published in Leiden, Netherlands, in French.
- **1641** — A similar argument to "I think, therefore I am" appears in *Meditations on First Philosophy*.
- **1644** — The Latin version "Cogito, ergo sum" is found in *Principles of Philosophy*.
- **1656** — A Latin translation of the *Discourse* is published in Amsterdam.

## Body

### Purpose and Context
Descartes wrote the *Discourse* to introduce three scientific works (*La Dioptrique*, *Les Météores*, and *La Géométrie*) and to present his philosophical method. He chose French over Latin to reach a wider audience. The work addresses the problem of skepticism by proposing a new method to find an incontrovertible foundation for knowledge.

### The Method and Its Rules
Descartes describes his method using a building metaphor: he resolves to demolish old foundations and rebuild knowledge from scratch. He proposes four precepts: 1) accept nothing as true unless clearly and distinctly known; 2) divide problems into parts; 3) proceed from simple to complex; and 4) make complete enumerations to ensure nothing is omitted.

### Morals and Practical Maxims
While applying his method of doubt, Descartes adopts temporary "maxims" to function in society. These include obeying laws and customs, being resolute in action, and focusing on changing his own desires rather than the world, as only his thoughts are absolutely in his power.

### The Foundation of Knowledge: God and the Soul
Applying his method to itself, Descartes finds three indubitable truths: his own existence as a thinking thing ("I think, therefore I am"), the existence of reason, and the existence of a non-deceiving God who guarantees the reliability of reason. He provides proofs for God's existence, including an ontological argument.

### Physics and the Soul
Descartes sketches his mechanical laws of nature, using a hypothetical "new world" to explain them freely. He endorses William Harvey's discovery of blood circulation but disagrees on its cause, attributing it to heat rather than the heart's pumping. He concludes that the body's motions are independent of the mind, distinguishing the rational soul (âme) from the physical body.

### Call for Scientific Advancement
Descartes alludes to the trial of Galileo as a reason for initially withholding his work. He argues that experimental investigation is indispensable for advancing knowledge of nature and encourages readers to test and critique his scientific theories.

## Terms
- **Cartesianism**: The epistemological system based on Descartes' philosophy.
- **Method of Doubt**: The process of doubting all preconceived notions to find an indubitable foundation for knowledge.
- **Cogito, ergo sum**: Latin for "I think, therefore I am," the foundational truth that the act of doubting proves one's existence as a thinking entity.
- **Ontological Proof**: An argument for God's existence based on the concept of a perfect being.
- **Mechanical Laws**: Descartes' view of nature as governed by physical laws of motion and matter.
- **Âme**: The French term Descartes uses for mind, spirit, or soul, identified with the faculty for rational thinking.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The source text notes that the precise wording "I think, therefore I am" appears in the *Discourse*, while similar arguments without that exact phrasing are in other works.
- Descartes' explanation for blood circulation (heat vs. muscular contraction) contradicts William Harvey's findings, which he otherwise endorses.
- His hypothetical "new world" is framed as a theoretical model, though it reflects his project to understand the actual universe through quantitative laws.
