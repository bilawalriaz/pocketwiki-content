# Hua Mulan

## Overview
Hua Mulan is a legendary Chinese folk heroine from the Northern and Southern dynasties era (4th–6th century AD). Her story, centered on filial piety and gender disguise, has evolved from a folk ballad into a global cultural icon through countless adaptations, most famously by Disney.

## Timeline
- **c. 386–535 AD** — The *Ballad of Mulan* is believed to have been composed during the Northern Wei dynasty.
- **557–589 AD** — The ballad is included in the anthology *Musical Records of Old and New* (now lost).
- **11th/12th C.** — The earliest extant text appears in Guo Maoqian's *Music Bureau Collection*.
- **c. 1593** — Playwright Xu Wei dramatizes the tale as *The Female Mulan*.
- **c. 1675** — Chu Renhuo incorporates Mulan into the novel *Romance of Sui and Tang*.
- **1917** — Mei Lanfang stars in the play *Mulan Joins the Army*.
- **1939** — The film *Hua Mu Lan* is released during the Second Sino-Japanese War.
- **1975** — Maxine Hong Kingston's *The Woman Warrior* popularizes the story in the West.
- **1998** — Disney releases its animated film *Mulan*.
- **2020** — Disney releases a live-action *Mulan* film.

## Body

### Origins and First Mentions
The first known written record is the *Ballad of Mulan*, a folk song from the Northern Wei dynasty (386–535 AD). Its historical setting is usually the Northern Wei's campaigns against the nomadic Rouran. The ballad was first transcribed in the 6th-century anthology *Musical Records of Old and New*. The earliest surviving text comes from the 11th- or 12th-century *Music Bureau Collection*.

### The Ballad's Narrative
The poem tells of Mulan, who sighs at her loom when her father is named in imperial conscription notices. As the eldest child with no adult male relatives, she buys a horse and gear, disguises herself as a man, and takes his place. After a ten-year campaign, she is honored by the emperor (referred to as both "Son of Heaven" and "Khagan") but declines a high-ranking ministerial position (*shangshulang*), asking only for a swift horse to return home. Her family rejoices at her return. She changes back into women's clothing, astonishing her comrades who had not realized she was a woman during their twelve years of service.

### Historical Context and Setting
The ballad's references point to the Northern Wei dynasty, founded by the Xianbei people. The Xianbei rulers used both Chinese and nomadic titles, and their society retained traditions where women were skilled horseback riders. The conflict with the Rouran, described as "barbarians," matches historical records of Northern Wei campaigns to protect its frontier garrisons in modern Inner Mongolia.

### Name and Historicity
The heroine's family name varies across versions: it is unknown in the ballad, given as Zhu or Wei in later texts, and popularized as Hua ("flower") by Xu Wei. Whether Mulan was a historical person is unclear, as her name does not appear in official dynastic biographies. Some evidence suggests she may have been Xianbei rather than ethnic Han Chinese.

### Later Literary Adaptations
The 17th-century novel *Romance of Sui and Tang* by Chu Renhuo adds significant plot elements. In this version, Mulan serves under the Western Turkic Khaganate and befriends a warrior princess, Xianniang. The novel gives Mulan a tragic end: she commits suicide to avoid becoming the Khan's concubine after discovering her father has died and her mother has remarried. This tragic conclusion is unique to this adaptation and is not found in earlier legends.

### Modern Adaptations
The story has inspired numerous stage, film, and television adaptations worldwide. Key milestones include the 1939 Chinese film made during wartime, Maxine Hong Kingston's influential 1975 book *The Woman Warrior*, and Disney's 1998 animated film and its 2020 live-action remake. The character has also appeared in video games and comics.

## Terms
- **Ballad of Mulan**: The earliest known written version of the legend, a folk song from the Northern Wei dynasty.
- **Northern Wei**: A dynasty (386–535 AD) in northern China founded by the Xianbei people.
- **Xianbei**: A proto-Mongolic nomadic people who ruled the Northern Wei dynasty.
- **Rouran**: A nomadic people who were frequent adversaries of the Northern Wei.
- **Khagan**: A title for the leader of nomadic kingdoms, used alongside the Chinese "Son of Heaven" by Northern Wei emperors.
- **Shangshulang**: A high-ranking ministerial position in the imperial Chinese government.
- **Filial piety**: A core Confucian virtue of respect and duty to one's parents and family.
- **Sinification**: The process of adopting Chinese culture and customs, as the Xianbei rulers did.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Historicity**: It is unclear whether Mulan was a real historical figure or a purely legendary character, as her name is absent from official historical records of the Northern Wei.
- **Ethnicity**: Given the Northern Wei's Xianbei rulers and the ballad's context, scholars debate whether Mulan was of Xianbei or Han Chinese origin.
- **Tragic Ending**: The suicide of Mulan in the *Romance of Sui and Tang* is considered a later interpolation by author Chu Renhuo, with some commentators suggesting it may carry an anti-Qing political message.
