# Philippine–American War

## Overview
The Philippine–American War (1899–1902) was a conflict between the United States and the First Philippine Republic. It began after the U.S. annexed the Philippines from Spain via the Treaty of Paris, rejecting the independence declared by Filipino nationalists. The war resulted in massive Filipino civilian casualties and established U.S. colonial rule, which lasted until 1946.

## Timeline
- **June 12, 1898** — Philippine Declaration of Independence issued.
- **Dec. 10, 1898** — Treaty of Paris signed; Spain cedes Philippines to the U.S. for $20 million. |
- **Jan. 23, 1899** — First Philippine Republic (Malolos Republic) formally established.
- **Feb. 4, 1899** — First shots fired in Manila; Battle of Manila begins.
- **June 2, 1899** — Philippine government formally declares war on the U.S.
- **Nov. 13, 1899** — Aguinaldo decrees official shift to guerrilla warfare.
- **Mar. 23, 1901** — U.S. forces capture President Emilio Aguinaldo.
- **July 4, 1902** — U.S. declares the war officially over.
- **June 15, 1913** — Final defeat of Moro forces at Battle of Bud Bagsak ends the Moro Rebellion.
- **July 4, 1946** — U.S. grants Philippine independence via the Treaty of Manila.

## Body

### Origins and Outbreak
Following the Spanish–American War, the Treaty of Paris (1898) transferred sovereignty of the Philippines to the United States, ignoring the newly declared First Philippine Republic. Tensions escalated through early 1899 due to mutual suspicion, conflicting proclamations (including U.S. President McKinley's "Benevolent Assimilation"), and a series of confrontations. The war began on February 4, 1899, when Private William Grayson fired on Filipino soldiers in Manila. U.S. General Elwell Otis rejected Filipino attempts to cease hostilities, declaring the fighting must "go on to the grim end."

### Conventional and Guerrilla Phases
Initially, Filipino forces fought conventionally but were outmatched by superior U.S. firepower and training. After U.S. President McKinley's 1900 re-election and battlefield losses, President Aguinaldo authorized a shift to guerrilla tactics in September 1899, officially decreed in November. This prolonged the conflict, inflicting significant U.S. casualties. The U.S. responded with counterinsurgency tactics, including "zones of protection" (concentration camps) and scorched-earth campaigns, particularly in Batangas under General J. Franklin Bell.

### Atrocities and Casualties
Both sides committed atrocities. U.S. forces engaged in reprisals, village burnings, and the use of torture (e.g., the "water cure"). Notable incidents include General Jacob H. Smith's order to kill everyone over ten in Samar after the Balangiga massacre. Filipino forces also tortured and killed prisoners. The war caused catastrophic civilian deaths, primarily from famine and disease. The U.S. State Department cites over 20,000 Filipino combatant deaths and up to 200,000 civilian deaths, with some estimates reaching one million. A major cholera epidemic at the war's end killed 150,000–200,000.

### End of the War and Aftermath
The capture of Aguinaldo in 1901 crippled the First Philippine Republic, though fighting continued under leaders like Miguel Malvar until 1902. The U.S. established a civil government in 1901 with William Howard Taft as governor. The official end came on July 4, 1902. However, resistance continued in other forms, notably the Moro Rebellion in the south (until 1913) and the Pulahan religious movement. The U.S. implemented policies that disestablished the Catholic Church, introduced English as the primary language, and established a public school system with American "Thomasite" teachers.

### Path to Independence
U.S. colonial policy was framed as tutelage for eventual independence. The Philippine Organic Act (1902) created a legislature. The Jones Act (1916) promised independence and established an elected senate. The Tydings–McDuffie Act (1934) created the Commonwealth of the Philippines and set a ten-year path to independence, which was delayed by World War II and Japanese occupation. Full sovereignty was achieved on July 4, 1946.

## Terms
- **First Philippine Republic (Malolos Republic):** The revolutionary government established by Filipino nationalists in January 1899, led by President Emilio Aguinaldo.
- **Treaty of Paris (1898):** The agreement that ended the Spanish–American War, in which Spain ceded the Philippines to the United States for $20 million.
- **Benevolent Assimilation:** President McKinley's 1898 proclamation justifying U.S. control of the Philippines as a civilizing mission.
- **Guerrilla Warfare:** Irregular, hit-and-run military tactics adopted by Filipino forces after late 1899 to counter U.S. conventional superiority.
- **Zones of Protection (Reconcentrados):** Areas where U.S. forces forcibly relocated Filipino civilians to separate them from guerrillas, often resulting in high death rates from disease and malnutrition.
- **Moro Rebellion:** The prolonged conflict (1899–1913) between U.S. forces and Muslim groups in the southern Philippines who resisted American rule.
- **Thomasites:** The first wave of American public school teachers who arrived in the Philippines in 1901 to establish the U.S. education system.
- **Philippine Organic Act (1902):** The U.S. law that established a civilian government in the Philippines and created the Philippine Assembly.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Casualty Figures:** The total number of Filipino civilian deaths is highly debated, with modern estimates ranging from 200,000 to over one million. Historians dispute the validity of higher figures.
- **Nature of the Conflict:** Scholars debate whether the post-1902 resistance (e.g., Moro Rebellion, Pulahan) constitutes part of the Philippine–American War or separate conflicts.
- **U.S. Imperialism:** The war sparked intense debate in the U.S. between imperialists, who saw it as a civilizing mission, and anti-imperialists (like Mark Twain and Andrew Carnegie), who argued it betrayed American democratic principles.
- **Filipino Leadership:** Historians analyze the internal weaknesses of Aguinaldo's government, including political ineptitude, military blunders, and failure to maintain unified support, as key factors in its defeat.
