# Mosaic

## Overview
A mosaic is an image or pattern made from small pieces of colored stone, glass, or ceramic (tesserae) set in plaster or mortar. It is a durable art form used for decoration on floors, walls, and ceilings, with a history spanning from ancient Mesopotamia to modern street art. Mosaics are significant for their role in religious and secular architecture across numerous cultures, reflecting artistic exchange and theological ideas.

## Timeline
- **3rd millennium BC** — Earliest known mosaics found at a temple in Abra, Mesopotamia.
- **c. 1500 BC** — First glazed tiles appear at Susa and Chogha Zanbil.
- **4th century BC** — Pebble mosaics found at Macedonian palace-city of Aegae.
- **425–430** — Mosaics decorate the Mausoleum of Galla Placidia in Ravenna.
- **542–570** — The Madaba Map mosaic floor is created in Jordan.
- **688–692** — The Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem is decorated with glass mosaics.
- **706–715** — The Umayyad Mosque in Damascus is built with extensive mosaics.
- **1176–1186** — The Monreale Cathedral mosaics are executed in Sicily.
- **1320** — The Chora Church mosaics are created in Constantinople.
- **16th–18th c.** — Mosaics are used to decorate the interior of St. Peter's Basilica, Rome.

## Body

### Origins and Classical Techniques
The earliest mosaics used natural pebbles and shells. The art form developed significantly in the Greek and Roman worlds, where it was used for elaborate floor pavements. Two main techniques emerged: *opus vermiculatum*, using tiny tesserae for fine detail, and *opus tessellatum*, using larger pieces for broader areas. Roman mosaics, found from Britain to North Africa, often depicted mythological scenes, hunting, and daily life, as seen in the Villa Romana del Casale in Sicily.

### Christian and Byzantine Mosaics
From the 4th century, Christian basilicas adopted wall and ceiling mosaics. Early examples in Rome and Ravenna used classical styles for religious narratives. Mosaic art flourished in the Byzantine Empire (6th–15th centuries), becoming central to church decoration with golden backgrounds and hieratic figures. Key sites include the Hagia Sophia in Constantinople and the Chora Church. The tradition influenced Norman Sicily, Venice, and Kievan Rus', as seen in St. Mark's Basilica and Saint Sophia Cathedral in Kyiv.

### Islamic and Jewish Mosaics
Early Islamic art used mosaics without human figures, focusing on floral and geometric patterns. The Dome of the Rock and the Umayyad Mosque in Damascus are prime examples. The technique later evolved into decorative tilework like *zellij*. Under Byzantine influence, Jewish communities in the Levant decorated synagogues with floor mosaics, often featuring zodiac wheels and biblical scenes, as at Beit Alfa and Sepphoris.

### Decline and Revival
Mosaic art declined in Western Europe after the early Middle Ages, replaced by fresco painting. It experienced a revival during the Renaissance and Baroque periods, notably in St. Peter's Basilica. In the modern era, mosaics have been used in architecture, public art, and as a popular craft, employing diverse materials from glass to found objects.

## Terms
- **Tesserae**: Small, individual pieces of stone, glass, or ceramic used to create a mosaic.
- **Opus vermiculatum**: A technique using tiny tesserae to create fine detail and follow the contours of a design.
- **Opus tessellatum**: A technique using larger tesserae laid in horizontal or vertical rows.
- **Iconoclasm**: A historical movement opposing the use of religious images, which led to the destruction of many Byzantine mosaics.
- **Zellij**: A style of Islamic mosaic tilework using geometrically shaped pieces.
- **Pantokrator**: A common Byzantine mosaic subject depicting Christ as "ruler of all."
- **Theotokos**: A title for the Virgin Mary, meaning "God-bearer," frequently depicted in mosaics.
- **Deesis**: A Byzantine mosaic composition showing Christ flanked by the Virgin Mary and John the Baptist.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that the exact dating and attribution of some early mosaics, like those at the Bizere Monastery, are debated between Orthodox and Catholic origins. The function and symbolism of certain elements, such as the zodiac wheels in synagogues, are interpreted as either a fusion with pagan culture or a representation of Jewish calendar months. The reasons for the decline of figural mosaics in the Islamic world after the 8th century are also a subject of historical discussion.
