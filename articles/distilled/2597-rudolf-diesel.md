# Rudolf Diesel

## Overview
Rudolf Diesel (1858–1913) was a German engineer who invented the diesel engine, a high-efficiency internal combustion engine that revolutionized power generation and transportation. His work fundamentally changed engine design by achieving far greater fuel efficiency than steam engines, though his mysterious disappearance at sea remains unsolved.

## Timeline
- **18 March 1858** — Born in Paris, France, to Bavarian immigrant parents.
- **1870** — Family deported to England during Franco-Prussian War; later sent to Augsburg, Germany.
- **1875** — Enrolled at Royal Bavarian Polytechnic of Munich on a merit scholarship.
- **1880** — Graduated with highest honours; began working with Carl von Linde in Paris.
- **1892** — Received German patent DRP 67207 for his engine theory.
- **1893** — Published foundational treatise *Theory and Construction of a Rational Heat-engine*.
- **1897** — First successful diesel engine (Motor 250/400) officially tested.
- **29 September 1913** — Disappeared from the steamer SS Dresden; presumed dead.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Diesel was born in Paris and faced early financial hardship, working in his father's leather goods workshop. After the Franco-Prussian War, he was sent to Augsburg, Germany, to improve his German and attend technical school. He demonstrated early academic excellence and, at age 14, declared his intent to become an engineer. He studied at the Industrial School of Augsburg and later at the Royal Bavarian Polytechnic of Munich, where his professor Carl von Linde became a key mentor.

### Development of the Diesel Engine
Diesel's career began in refrigeration engineering with Linde. He then shifted focus to improving engine efficiency, aiming to approach the theoretical maximum of the Carnot cycle. He understood that steam engines wasted up to 90% of fuel energy. His key innovation was an engine where air was compressed internally to high pressure and temperature, igniting injected fuel without a spark plug. This design was smaller, lighter, and more fuel-efficient than steam engines. After initial theoretical errors, he refined his design and, with support from industrialist Heinrich von Buz and the Krupp firm, developed the first successful diesel engine in 1897.

### Fuel and Later Work
Diesel tested various fuels, including coal distillates, petroleum products, and even vegetable oils. In 1900, a diesel engine successfully ran on peanut oil without modification. He obtained patents internationally, including in the United States. His engine's high compression ratio gave it superior thermal efficiency but required heavier construction, limiting its early use in aviation.

### Disappearance and Death
On 29 September 1913, Diesel vanished from the steamer SS Dresden en route to a meeting in London. His cabin was empty, and personal items were found. Ten days later, a decomposed body was recovered with his belongings. Theories about his death include suicide due to financial distress, murder for refusing exclusive rights to the German military, or a staged disappearance to aid the British. The case remains unsolved.

### Legacy and Recognition
After his death, the diesel engine was further developed and became crucial for ships, submarines, locomotives, trucks, and stationary power. Diesel received numerous honors, including election to the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (1912) and induction into the National Inventors Hall of Fame (1976). The asteroid 10093 Diesel is named in his honour.

## Terms
- **Diesel engine**: An internal combustion engine that ignites fuel by compressing air to high temperatures, eliminating the need for a spark plug.
- **Carnot cycle**: A theoretical model for the most efficient possible heat engine, setting a maximum limit for thermal efficiency.
- **Thermal efficiency**: The ratio of useful work output to heat energy input in an engine.
- **Compression ratio**: The ratio of the maximum to minimum volume in an engine cylinder; diesel engines use higher ratios than gasoline engines.
- **Internal combustion engine**: An engine where fuel combustion occurs inside the engine cylinder, as opposed to an external source like a boiler.

## Debates and Open Questions
The primary unresolved question is the cause of Rudolf Diesel's disappearance and presumed death. Theories range from suicide, supported by his financial notes and a cross drawn in his diary, to murder or a staged defection to Britain. The lack of definitive evidence means his fate remains a historical mystery.
