# Paris Commune

## Overview
The Paris Commune was a radical socialist and revolutionary government that ruled Paris from March 18 to May 28, 1871. It emerged from the chaos of the Franco-Prussian War and the collapse of the Second French Empire, representing a brief but influential experiment in workers' self-governance. Its brutal suppression during the "Bloody Week" and its legacy as a model for later socialist and communist movements make it a pivotal event in modern history.

## Timeline
- **2 September 1870** — France defeated at Battle of Sedan; Napoleon III captured; Second Empire collapses.
- **28 January 1871** — French government signs armistice with Prussia; Paris not occupied, but National Guard remains armed.
- **18 February 1871** — Adolphe Thiers elected chief executive of the new Third Republic.
- **18 March 1871** — Thiers' attempt to seize Parisian cannons fails; National Guard seizes control of the city.
- **26 March 1871** — Elections held for the Paris Commune council.
- **28 March 1871** — Paris Commune officially proclaimed.
- **2 April 1871** — Commune's failed march on Versailles; first summary executions of prisoners.
- **5 April 1871** — Commune passes the Decree on Hostages.
- **21 May 1871** — French Army enters Paris, beginning the "Bloody Week" (*Semaine sanglante*).
- **28 May 1871** — Last Communard barricades fall; Commune suppressed.

## Body

### Prelude and Establishment
The Commune resulted from deep social divisions and the trauma of the Franco-Prussian War. Paris, with a large working-class population and a history of radicalism, was defended during the siege by the politicized National Guard. After the armistice, the conservative national government under Adolphe Thiers, based in Versailles, attempted to seize the Guard's cannons on March 18. The operation failed when soldiers fraternized with the crowd, and two French generals were killed. Thiers and the government evacuated Paris, allowing the National Guard's Central Committee to take power. Elections on March 26 produced a council dominated by radical republicans, socialists, and anarchists, which proclaimed the Commune on March 28.

### Governance and Policies
The Commune governed for two months, implementing progressive, anti-religious, and social-democratic policies. Key decrees included the remission of rent, abolition of child labor and night work in bakeries, the right of workers to take over deserted enterprises, and the separation of church and state. It closed Catholic churches and schools. The Commune was an eclectic mix of ideologies, including feminism, communism, and anarchism. Women played active roles, forming the Women's Union for the Defence of Paris, though they could not vote or hold office. The Commune maintained the Bank of France's reserves to avoid currency collapse, a decision later criticized by Marxists.

### War and Suppression
The conflict with the Versailles government escalated into open warfare. The Commune's attempted march on Versailles on April 3 failed, leading to reprisal executions. The Versailles army, reorganized under Marshal MacMahon, besieged Paris. Internal divisions weakened the Commune, which established a Committee of Public Safety. On May 21, the army entered Paris through an undefended gate. The ensuing "Bloody Week" saw fierce street fighting, during which Communards burned public buildings, including the Tuileries Palace and the Hôtel de Ville. The army executed thousands of captured Communards. The Commune retaliated by executing hostages, including Archbishop Georges Darboy. By May 28, all resistance was crushed.

### Aftermath and Legacy
The suppression was devastating. Historians debate the death toll, with estimates ranging from 6,000 to over 20,000 Communards killed. Over 43,000 were imprisoned, and thousands were exiled, many to New Caledonia. A general amnesty was granted in 1880. The Commune profoundly influenced socialist thought. Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels analyzed it as the first "dictatorship of the proletariat," a model of workers' government. Vladimir Lenin and the Bolsheviks studied its lessons, viewing it as a precursor to the Russian Revolution. The Commune inspired later uprisings and remains a potent symbol of revolutionary struggle and working-class aspiration.

## Terms
*   **National Guard:** A militia composed largely of working-class Parisians, which became the armed force of the Commune.
*   **Third Republic:** The republican government of France established in September 1870 after the fall of the Second Empire.
*   **Semaine sanglante ("Bloody Week"):** The period from May 21-28, 1871, when the French Army violently suppressed the Paris Commune.
*   **Dictatorship of the proletariat:** A Marxist concept describing a transitional state where the working class holds political power, which Marx and Engels applied to the Commune.
*   **Decree on Hostages:** A Commune law allowing the execution of hostages in retaliation for the killing of Communard prisoners.
*   **Blanquists:** Followers of Louis Auguste Blanqui, a professional revolutionary advocating for a disciplined, vanguard-led uprising.
*   **First International:** The International Workingmen's Association, a socialist organization that influenced many Communards.
*   **Pétroleuses:** A term for alleged female arsonists during the Bloody Week; their existence and role are debated by historians.
*   **New Caledonia:** A French penal colony in the Pacific where many Communards were deported after the suppression.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Casualty Figures:** The number of Communards killed remains disputed. Estimates range from the 6,000-7,000 proposed by historian Robert Tombs based on cemetery records, to over 10,000 or 15,000 as argued by others like Michele Audin, with older claims of 20,000 now considered exaggerated.
*   **Thiers' Strategy:** Historians debate whether Adolphe Thiers deliberately provoked the Commune to have a pretext for crushing Parisian radicalism and consolidating the Third Republic.
*   **Marxist Interpretation:** While Marx and Engels praised the Commune as a prototype for proletarian government, they also criticized its strategic errors, such as its failure to seize the Bank of France and its "excessive magnanimity" towards enemies. Lenin later echoed these critiques.
*   **Role of Women:** The extent of women's participation, particularly the myth of the *pétroleuses* (female arsonists), is contested. While women were active in support roles and fighting, evidence for widespread organized arson by women is considered weak.
