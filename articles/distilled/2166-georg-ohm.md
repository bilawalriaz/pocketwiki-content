# Georg Ohm

## Overview
Georg Simon Ohm (1789–1854) was a German mathematician and physicist whose experimental work established the fundamental relationship between voltage, current, and resistance in an electrical circuit, now known as Ohm's law. His work, initially met with indifference, became a cornerstone of electrical theory and earned him lasting recognition, including the SI unit of resistance being named the ohm.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Ohm was born to a self-educated locksmith and a tailor's daughter. His father provided him and his brother Martin (a future mathematician) with rigorous home instruction in mathematics, physics, chemistry, and philosophy. After his mother's death, Ohm attended the Erlangen Gymnasium but received little scientific training there, relying on his father's teachings. He later studied mathematics privately in Switzerland and Neuchâtel before returning to the University of Erlangen.

### Teaching Career and Scientific Work
Ohm's academic career was marked by a series of teaching positions at various schools. His breakthrough came when he secured a post at the Jesuit Gymnasium of Cologne in 1817, where a well-equipped physics laboratory allowed him to conduct experiments. Using equipment of his own creation, he investigated the relationship between electrical potential difference and current. His findings were published in his seminal 1827 work, *Die galvanische Kette, mathematisch bearbeitet*. This book presented his complete theory of electricity, including the law stating that the electromotive force between the ends of a circuit part is the product of the current and the resistance of that part. The work was initially "coldly received" but later became highly influential.

### Later Career and Recognition
Despite his groundbreaking work, Ohm's college did not appreciate his contributions, leading him to resign. He later worked at the Polytechnic School of Nuremberg and eventually became a professor of experimental physics in Munich. His work was eventually recognized internationally: he received the Copley Medal in 1841 and became a foreign member of the Royal Society in 1842. He also published on molecular physics and optics, though he discontinued one project after learning a Swedish scientist had anticipated his discovery.

## Terms
*   **Ohm's Law:** The principle that the current through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage across the two points. The constant of proportionality is the resistance.
*   **Electromotive Force (EMF):** The electrical potential produced by an electrochemical cell or other source; the "voltage" that drives current through a circuit.
*   **Galvanic Circuit:** An electrical circuit powered by a galvanic cell (like Volta's battery), which converts chemical energy into electrical energy.
*   **Contiguous Action:** Ohm's theoretical framework for electricity, which posited that electrical effects are communicated between adjacent particles, opposing the concept of "action at a distance."
*   **Copley Medal:** A prestigious scientific award given by the Royal Society of London, which recognized Ohm's work in 1841.
*   **Ohm (Ω):** The SI unit of electrical resistance, named in his honor.

## Debates and Open Questions
Ohm's work was initially met with significant resistance and indifference from the scientific community. His theory of "contiguous action" was presented in opposition to the prevailing concept of "action at a distance." Furthermore, his acoustic law, which states that a musical sound is perceived as a set of pure harmonic tones, is noted in the source as being "well known to be not quite true."
