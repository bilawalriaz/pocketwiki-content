# Le Corbusier

## Overview
Charles-Édouard Jeanneret (1887–1965), known as Le Corbusier, was a French-Swiss architect, urban planner, painter, and writer who became a primary pioneer of modern architecture. Rejecting historical ornament in favor of industrial materials like reinforced concrete, he developed a rigorous theoretical framework—including the Five Points of Architecture, the Modulor proportioning system, and the "Ville Radieuse" urban model—to standardize housing and reorganize cities for efficiency and hygiene. His built works, from the Villa Savoye to the Unité d'Habitation in Marseille and the Capitol Complex in Chandigarh, India, established the vocabulary of architectural modernism. A founding member of CIAM (Congrès Internationaux d'Architecture Moderne), his influence shaped post-war reconstruction globally. However, his legacy is deeply contested: his urban plans are criticized for authoritarian social engineering and the destruction of existing urban fabric, while his political engagements in the 1920s–40s included contributions to fascist journals, antisemitic writings, and collaboration with the Vichy regime's eugenics foundation.

## Body

### Early Formation and the "Voyage d'Orient"
Born into a watchmaking family in the Jura Mountains, Le Corbusier absorbed the Masonic lodge's geometric symbolism (right angle, compass) as "time-honored ideas." Lacking formal architectural training, he studied applied arts under Charles L'Eplattenier, who directed him toward architecture and painting from nature. Crucial formative trips followed: working for **Auguste Perret** (1908–10) taught him reinforced concrete construction; working for **Peter Behrens** (1910–11) exposed him to industrial design and future Bauhaus leaders. His 1911 "Voyage d'Orient" through the Balkans to Greece and Italy provided a visual library—especially the Parthenon and the **Florence Charterhouse** (Certosa di Galluzzo)—that fueled his lifelong theories on proportion, the "architectural promenade," and collective living organized around a cloister.

### Theoretical Breakthrough: Dom-Ino, Purism, and the Five Points
During WWI, Le Corbusier systematized reinforced concrete into the **Dom-Ino House** (1914–15): a structural skeleton of columns and slabs freeing the plan and façade from load-bearing walls. In Paris (1917), he partnered with cousin **Pierre Jeanneret** and, with painter **Amédée Ozenfant**, launched **Purism** (rejecting Cubism as "romantic") and the journal *L'Esprit Nouveau*. Here he codified the **Five Points of Architecture** (1927):
1.  **Pilotis**: Building raised on columns, freeing ground level.
2.  **Roof Terrace**: Flat roof as garden/terrace, replacing consumed ground.
3.  **Free Plan**: Interior walls non-structural, infinitely configurable.
4.  **Ribbon Window**: Façade freed for horizontal bands of light.
5.  **Free Façade**: Exterior skin independent of structure.
These points were realized in the **Villa Savoye** (1928–31), the movement's canonical demonstration.

### Urban Vision: From Plan Voisin to Ville Radieuse
Le Corbusier viewed the chaotic 19th-century city as a pathological problem requiring surgical modernist intervention. The **Ville Contemporaine** (1922) proposed a tabula rasa of cruciform towers in parks for 3 million. The **Plan Voisin** (1925) shockingly applied this to central Paris, demanding demolition of the Marais for a business district of 60-story towers—a provocation that framed the debate on slum clearance. The **Ville Radieuse** (1935) refined the model: zoning by function (not class), housing allocated by family size, high-density towers freeing 95% of ground for nature, and a transportation hierarchy separating pedestrians from high-speed traffic. Though never fully built, these diagrams, codified in the **Athens Charter** (1943), became the blueprint for post-war CIAM-influenced planning worldwide (e.g., Brasília, British "Cities in the Sky," US urban renewal).

### The Unité d'Habitation and "Brutalism"
After a decade of unrealized commissions, the **Unité d'Habitation, Marseille** (1947–52) realized the Ville Radieuse at building scale. A 337-unit "vertical village" on massive pilotis, it internalized the street: every third floor hosted shops, a school, and services; the roof held a track, pool, and theatre. Apartments were two-story duplexes ("bottles in a rack") with double-aspect light, standardized by the **Modulor**—his proportioning system based on the golden section and a 2.26m ceiling height. The raw, board-marked concrete (*béton brut*), necessitated by steel shortages, birthed **Brutalism**. Four more Unités followed (Nantes-Rezé, Berlin, Briey, Firminy).

### Chandigarh: The Complete Capital
Invited by **Nehru** (1950) to design Punjab's new capital, Le Corbusier (with Fry, Drew, and Pierre Jeanneret) executed his only full city plan. The **Capitol Complex**—High Court (parasol roof, brise-soleil), Secretariat (250m ramped block), and Assembly (conical tower, tapestry)—arranged around a vast esplanade with the **Open Hand** monument (symbol of "give and receive"). He enforced *béton brut* against contractors' urge to smooth it, creating a monumental, climate-responsive government architecture that remains his largest built ensemble.

### Sacred Architecture: Ronchamp and La Tourette
An atheist seeking "ineffable space," Le Corbusier produced two masterpieces of spiritual modernism. **Notre-Dame-du-Haut, Ronchamp** (1950–55) sits on a hilltop: a sculptural concrete shell with thick, tapered walls pierced by deep, colored glazing slots; the roof floats on columns, draining via a gargoyle into a fountain. **Convent of La Tourette** (1953–60), for Dominican friars, is a raw concrete fortress on a slope: U-shaped dormitories enclosing a courtyard, with a stark "Box of Miracles" chapel lit by a single roof square and "light cannons" projecting colored beams into the crypt. Both use the Modulor and the architectural promenade to choreograph ritual movement.

### Furniture, Color, and the Modulor
Le Corbusier treated furniture as "equipment for living." With **Charlotte Perriand** and Pierre Jeanneret (1928 onward), he designed tubular steel classics: **LC4 Chaise Longue**, **LC3 Grand Confort**, **LC1 Sling Chair**—intended for mass production but initially expensive. His **Polychromie Architecturale** (1931, expanded 1959) organized 63 colors into keyboards for spatial effects. The **Modulor** (1948, 1955) aimed to reconcile metric and imperial measures via a 2.26m "man with arm raised," subdivided by the golden ratio at navel, knees, throat—a "mathematization of the body" claimed as universal harmony.

## Terms
- ****Pilotis**** — Reinforced concrete columns (stilts) raising the building mass above ground, freeing the ground plane for circulation/nature and enabling the Free Plan and Free Façade.
- ****Free Plan (Plan Libre)**** — Interior layout unconstrained by load-bearing walls; partitions placed freely because structure is carried by an independent column grid.
- ****Free Façade (Façade Libre)**** — Exterior envelope non-structural, allowing ribbon windows or glass curtains independent of the structural grid.
- ****Ribbon Window (Fenêtre en longueur)**** — Horizontal window band running the façade length, made possible by the Free Façade, distributing light evenly.
- ****Roof Terrace (Toit-jardin)**** — Flat, accessible roof replacing the pitched roof; recovers the building's footprint as garden, promenade, or recreation.
- ****Dom-Ino House**** — 1914–15 prototype: three concrete slabs on six columns with a stair; a "juxtaposable system" for mass housing where residents infill walls.
- ****Ville Radieuse (Radiant City)**** — 1935 urban model: strict functional zoning, high-density cruciform towers on pilotis in continuous parkland, housing by family size, segregated traffic hierarchies.
- ****Plan Voisin**** — 1925 proposal to raze central Paris (Right Bank) for a grid of 60-story towers; a polemical manifesto for tabula rasa modernism.
- ****Modulor**** — Anthropometric proportioning system (1948) based on a 2.26m human figure, the golden section, and Fibonacci series; used to dimension all building elements.
- ****Unité d'Habitation**** — "Housing Unit of Standard Size": high-rise slab on pilotis containing duplex apartments, internal "streets" with services, and roof amenities; prototype for Brutalist social housing.
- ****Béton Brut**** — "Raw concrete": unfinished, board-marked concrete showing formwork texture; aestheticized in Unité d'Habitation and Chandigarh, defining Brutalism.
- ****Architectural Promenade**** — Choreographed spatial sequence experienced by moving through a building (ramps, stairs, turning views), blending interior/exterior, derived from Arab architecture and the Acropolis.
- ****Open Hand (La Main Ouverte)**** — Recurring sculptural motif: open palm symbolizing peace, reconciliation, "open to give, open to receive"; monumental version in Chandigarh.
- ****CIAM**** — Congrès Internationaux d'Architecture Moderne (1928–1959); international modernist congress founded by Le Corbusier; produced Athens Charter, disseminated functionalist urbanism.
- ****Purism**** — Post-Cubist movement (1918) co-founded with Ozenfant; advocated clear, geometric, machine-age forms in painting and architecture, rejecting decorative fragmentation.

## Debates and open questions
- **Political complicity**: Le Corbusier wrote for fascist journals (*Plans, Prélude, L'Homme Réel*) in the 1920s, expressed antisemitic views in letters (1940), and sought work from the Vichy regime (including a post at Alexis Carrel's eugenics foundation). Scholars debate whether this reflects opportunism, ideological affinity, or a technocratic belief that any state could implement his rational plans.
- **Urban planning legacy**: Critics (Jane Jacobs, Lewis Mumford, Witold Rybczynski) argue the Ville Radieuse's functional zoning, superblocks, and tower-in-park typology produced social isolation, dead streets, and the destruction of historic fabric—realized in failed US public housing and Brasília. Defenders note CIAM's adaptation by others and Le Corbusier's own later emphasis on "urban landscape."
- **Standardization vs. humanity**: The Modulor and Unité's "bottle rack" logic are read either as humanist universalism (Vitruvian continuity) or as the "mathematization of the body" imposing abstract norms on diverse cultures (e.g., Chandigarh's climate/caste realities).
- **Authorship of furniture**: The iconic LC series emerged from the studio of Le Corbusier, Pierre Jeanneret, and **Charlotte Perriand**; Perriand's role was long minimized, raising questions about collaborative credit in modernist mythmaking.
- **Preservation vs. adaptation**: Many works (Villa Savoye, Unités, Chandigarh) suffer from concrete degradation, leaking roofs, and obsolescence. UNESCO listing (2016) intensifies debate on how to conserve "machines for living" that were designed for industrial replacement, not permanence.
