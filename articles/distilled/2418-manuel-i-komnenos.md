# Manuel I Komnenos

## Overview
Manuel I Komnenos (r. 1143–1180) was a Byzantine emperor whose reign marked the last major resurgence of imperial power and cultural revival in the 12th century. He pursued an ambitious foreign policy to restore Byzantine hegemony in the Mediterranean, achieving significant successes in the Balkans and the Crusader states but suffering a pivotal defeat against the Seljuk Turks that ended hopes of recovering Anatolia.

## Timeline
- **1118** — Born in Constantinople, son of Emperor John II Komnenos.
- **1143** — Succeeds his father as emperor after being chosen on his deathbed.
- **1146** — Leads a punitive expedition against the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum.
- **1147–49** — Manages the passage of the Second Crusade through Byzantine territory.
- **1155–56** — Leads an unsuccessful invasion of Norman southern Italy.
- **1159** — Triumphs in Antioch, securing its submission as a Byzantine vassal.
- **1167** — Decisive victory over Hungary at Sirmium, expanding into the Balkans.
- **1169** — Joint invasion of Fatimid Egypt with the Kingdom of Jerusalem fails.
- **1176** — Suffers a major defeat at the Battle of Myriokephalon against the Seljuks.
- **1180** — Dies in Constantinople, succeeded by his young son Alexios II.

## Body

### Foreign Policy and Military Campaigns
Manuel inherited a strengthened empire and used its resources for energetic campaigns. He managed the Second Crusade (1147) adroitly, though relations with the German and French armies were tense. He formed alliances with the West, including a pact with Pope Adrian IV and King Conrad III of Germany against the Normans of Sicily. His 1155 invasion of Apulia initially succeeded but ended in defeat at Brindisi (1156), demonstrating the limits of Byzantine power in Italy. In the East, he established a protectorate over the Crusader states, with the Prince of Antioch and the King of Jerusalem becoming his vassals. He participated in a failed joint invasion of Egypt (1169) with King Amalric I of Jerusalem. His most significant eastern campaign ended in defeat at Myriokephalon (1176) against the Seljuk Sultan Kilij Arslan II, which halted Byzantine attempts to recover the Anatolian interior.

### Balkan and Hungarian Relations
Manuel achieved his greatest territorial successes in the Balkans. After conflicts with Serbia and Hungary, he won a decisive victory at Sirmium (1167), forcing Hungary to cede Syrmia, Bosnia, and Dalmatia. He pursued a diplomatic annexation of Hungary by making the Hungarian heir, Béla, his ward and intended successor. Although Béla later became King Béla III of Hungary, he honored his oath to Manuel and did not reclaim the lost territories during Manuel's lifetime. Manuel also engaged in diplomacy with the Rus' principalities to counter Hungarian influence.

### Internal Affairs and Economy
Manuel's reign saw a flourishing economy, with Constantinople as Europe's largest city (population estimated between 500,000 and 1,000,000). Wealth was generated through trade, especially the *kommerkion* (customs duty) and commerce with Italian merchants and the Crusader states. However, his lavish spending on the army, diplomacy, and building projects strained the treasury. He raised taxes and spent heavily, which later historians like Choniates criticized. The empire experienced a cultural revival, though Manuel's western-influenced tastes, such as jousting, were novel to Byzantines.

### Religious Affairs
Manuel involved himself in theological controversies, asserting imperial authority over the church. He intervened in disputes about the Eucharist (1156–57) and the interpretation of Christ's words "My Father is greater than I" (1166), using synods to enforce his views. He also attempted to negotiate church union with the Papacy to secure western alliances, but these efforts failed due to irreconcilable demands from both sides.

## Terms
*   **Porphyrogenitus**: "Born in the purple," a title for children born to a reigning emperor in the Purple Chamber of the Great Palace.
*   **Sebastokrator**: A high-ranking court title created by the Komnenian dynasty, ranking just below emperor.
*   **Kommerkion**: A major customs duty levied on all imports and exports through Constantinople, a key source of imperial revenue.
*   **Hyperpyron**: The standard gold coin of the later Byzantine Empire.
*   **Outremer**: The Crusader states established in the Levant after the First Crusade.
*   **Sultanate of Rum**: The Seljuk Turkish state in central Anatolia, a major rival of Byzantium.
*   **Megas Doux**: The commander-in-chief of the Byzantine navy.
*   **Epistemonarkhes**: A title Manuel used to assert his role as the disciplinarian or overseer of the church.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Assessment of Achievements**: Historians debate whether Manuel's military and diplomatic successes were his own doing or merely the result of the Komnenian dynasty's prior restoration. Some argue his ambitious policies were unsustainable and contributed to the empire's rapid decline after his death.
*   **Impact of Myriokephalon**: While often seen as a catastrophic defeat comparable to Manzikert (1071), some sources suggest the Byzantine army was not destroyed and remained capable, as shown by subsequent victories. The battle's long-term strategic consequences are debated.
*   **Cause of Post-Manuel Decline**: Scholars examine whether the empire's swift collapse after 1180 was due to flaws in Manuel's policies (e.g., financial exhaustion, overextension) or solely to the weak succession of his son Alexios II and the ensuing political instability.
