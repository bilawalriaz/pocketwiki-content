# Video game

## Overview
A video game is an electronic game requiring interaction with a user interface to generate visual feedback on a display device. Emerging from 1950s mainframe experiments and commercialized in the early 1970s, the medium survived a 1983 market crash to become a dominant global entertainment industry, generating an estimated US$159 billion in 2020. Games are defined by their platform (arcade, console, PC, mobile, cloud, VR), genre (based on gameplay interaction), and mode (single-player, multiplayer). Beyond entertainment, they serve educational, artistic, and therapeutic purposes, while sparking ongoing debates regarding violence, addiction, labor practices ("crunch"), and preservation.

## Timeline
- **1947** — Patent filed for "cathode-ray tube amusement device" (Goldsmith Jr. & Mann), the earliest known interactive electronic game.
- **1958** — *Tennis for Two* created by William Higinbotham on an oscilloscope.
- **1962** — *Spacewar!* developed by MIT students on a DEC PDP-1; influential precursor to commercial games.
- **1971** — *Computer Space* released by Nolan Bushnell and Ted Dabney; first commercial arcade video game.
- **1972** — *Pong* released by Atari; Magnavox Odyssey released as first home console (based on Ralph Baer's "Brown Box").
- **1973** — Term "video game" emerges in print (BusinessWeek, November; Cashbox letter, July).
- **1978–1983** — "Golden age" of arcade video games; rapid industry growth followed by market saturation.
- **1983** — North American video game market crashes (revenues drop ~$3B to $100M by 1985) due to loss of publishing control and oversaturation.
- **1985** — Nintendo Entertainment System launches in North America, revitalizing the industry and establishing licensing/quality control standards.
- **2000s** — Industry centralizes around high-budget "AAA" games; rise of digital distribution enables independent ("indie") game development.
- **2011** — *Brown v. Entertainment Merchants Association*: U.S. Supreme Court rules video games are protected speech/art.
- **2020** — Global market revenues estimated at US$159 billion (Mobile: 48%, Console: 28%, Personal computer: 23%). |
- **2020–2021** — COVID-19 pandemic boosts visibility of video games as a social distancing pastime.

## Body

### Origins and Early Prototypes
The conceptual lineage begins with a 1947 patent for a "cathode-ray tube amusement device" using analog circuitry to simulate missile trajectories against paper targets. Throughout the 1950s and 1960s, academic and research institutions produced distinct prototypes: the Nimrod computer (1951) used lights for the game of Nim; *OXO* (1952) displayed tic-tac-toe on the EDSAC; *Tennis for Two* (1958) rendered a side-view court on an oscilloscope; and *Spacewar!* (1962) featured two-player spaceship combat on a DEC PDP-1 vector display. These disparate display technologies (lights, graphical displays, oscilloscopes, vector monitors) established the technical vocabulary for the medium.

### Commercial Birth and the 1983 Crash
Ralph Baer devised a system to play table tennis on a television screen in 1966, leading to the "Brown Box" prototype licensed to Magnavox and released as the Odyssey in 1972. Separately, Nolan Bushnell and Ted Dabney, inspired by *Spacewar!*, created *Computer Space* (1971), the first arcade game, then founded Atari and released the hit *Pong* (1972). The success of *Pong* (arcade and home versions) and the Odyssey launched the commercial industry. Rapid expansion during the "golden age" of arcades (late 1970s–early 1980s) led to a flood of clones and loss of publishing control, causing the 1983 North American market crash. Revenues plummeted from ~$3 billion to $100 million by 1985, wiping out many US companies.

### Japanese Dominance and Modern Structure
Nintendo revitalized the market in 1985 with the Nintendo Entertainment System, implementing strict licensing and quality control to prevent unlicensed development—practices still standard for console manufacturers. The industry matured around a publisher-developer dichotomy, Japanese companies (Nintendo, Sega, Sony) dominated hardware, and by the 2000s, development centralized on high-budget, low-risk "AAA" titles. The advent of digital distribution (Steam, App Stores) and mobile platforms (iOS/Android) in the late 2000s lowered barriers to entry, enabling a boom in independent ("indie") development focused on experimental gameplay and art styles.

### Platforms and Hardware
A video game requires a **platform**—a specific hardware/software combination—to process input and render video output.
*   **PC Games:** Run on personal computers; offer openness, modding, emulation, and hardware variability.
*   **Home Consoles:** Dedicated devices (PlayStation, Xbox, Nintendo) connecting to TVs; fixed hardware targets simplify development; exclusivity drives competition.
*   **Handheld Consoles:** Portable, self-contained units (screen, controls, speakers); waned in 2010s due to smartphone competition.
*   **Arcade Games:** Specialized, coin-operated cabinets (often single-game); declined as dedicated venues but persist in entertainment centers.
*   **Mobile Games:** On smartphones/tablets; leverage accelerometers, GPS, cameras for augmented reality; largest revenue sector (48% in 2020).
*   **Cloud Gaming:** Game computed on remote servers (e.g., Xbox Cloud Gaming); requires minimal local hardware but high-speed internet.
*   **Virtual Reality (VR):** Head-mounted displays with stereoscopic screens and motion tracking; requires tethered or standalone processing unit.
*   **Backward Compatibility/Emulation:** Modern platforms play older games via hardware support (backward compatibility) or software simulation (emulation); legal for emulators, but ROM distribution often violates copyright.

### Game Media and Distribution
Early systems were dedicated hardware with built-in logic. Modern platforms are programmable, reading games from **physical media** (ROM cartridges, magnetic tape, floppy disks, optical discs/CD-ROM/DVD, flash cards) or **digital distribution** (downloads, cloud streaming). Games are extended via **expansion packs** (physical) or **downloadable content (DLC)** (digital), monetizing post-launch. **User-generated content** and **mods** (modifications) extend PC games, sometimes with official developer support.

### Input and Output Devices
**Input** translates human action to game logic. Standard **game controllers** (gamepads, joysticks) feature face buttons, shoulder triggers, analog sticks, and d-pads. Keyboards/mice dominate PC. Specialized peripherals include racing wheels, light guns, dance pads. Cameras, microphones, and motion sensors enable controller-free or enhanced VR interaction. **Output** targets external displays (CRT, LCD, projectors, monitors, headsets). Graphics range from fixed LED/LCD, text, 2D, 3D, to augmented reality. **Audio** includes sound effects and dynamic soundtracks. **Haptic feedback** (controller vibration) provides tactile sensation.

### Classification: Genre, Mode, and Type
**Genre** categorizes by gameplay interaction (primary mechanic), not narrative setting (e.g., shooter, RPG, roguelike, battle royale). Hierarchies exist (top-level: "action"; sub-genre: "FPS"). **Mode** defines player count: single-player, multiplayer (local, LAN, online), massively multiplayer online (MMO), or zero-player (simulation observation). **Types** distinguish purpose:
*   **Core/Hardcore:** Deep mechanics, long mastery curves (AAA focus).
*   **Casual:** Accessible, simple rules, short sessions (match-3, puzzle); **hyper-casual** uses minimal rules for infinite replay (e.g., *Flappy Bird*).
*   **Educational/Edutainment:** Teach via rote or problem-solving (*Oregon Trail*, *Minecraft* in classrooms).
*   **Serious Games:** Primary purpose non-entertainment (fitness: *Wii Fit*; simulation: *Flight Simulator*; advergames; newsgames).
*   **Art Games:** Prioritize emotional expression/societal critique over win conditions (*Passage*, *Flower*, *That Dragon, Cancer*); often indie.

### Content Rating Systems
National/regional boards assign age-based ratings based on violence, sexual content, drugs, gambling.
*   **ESRB (US):** E, E10+, T, M, AO (voluntary; upheld by *Brown v. EMA* 2011 as protected speech).
*   **PEGI (Europe):** 3+, 8+, 12+, 16+, 18+.
*   **ACB (Australia):** G, PG, M, MA15+, R18+, X (legally enforceable; Refused Classification = banned).
*   **CERO (Japan):** A, B, C, D, Z.
*   **USK (Germany):** 0, 6, 12, 16, 18 (historically banned Nazi imagery; relaxed 2018 for "social adequacy").
*   **IARC:** Cross-regional streamlining tool.
*   **China:** Strict government censorship; foreign games require modification.

### Development Process
Development is cross-disciplinary: programmers, artists, designers, audio, writers, managed by producers. Early games were solo efforts; modern AAA teams range 5–50+ (e.g., *Assassin's Creed II*: 450 staff reported in May 2009). **Game engines** (Unity, Unreal) handle core logic/rendering; **middleware** adds physics, video playback, networking. **Quality assurance** testing precedes launch; patches fix post-release bugs. Rising team sizes and costs increase "crunch" (mandatory overtime) and risk of rushed releases. **Indie development** (small teams, no publisher control) flourished post-2000s via digital distribution and accessible tools.

### Game Studies: Ludology vs. Narratology
Two academic frameworks analyze games as art:
*   **Narratology** (Janet Murray): Views games as "Cyberdrama"—interactive storytelling where the player inhabits another world (Holodeck model).
*   **Ludology** (Espen Aarseth): Argues games are defined by **rules, interface, and play**; narrative elements (plot, character) are incidental to gameplay mechanics. Aarseth famously dismissed Lara Croft's body as irrelevant to player agency.
*   **Emergent Narrative/Gameplay:** Complex systems (AI, physics) generate unscripted stories/situations (e.g., traffic jams in racing games from simple "avoid obstacle" rules).

### Intellectual Property
Games are protected by **copyright** (code, art, music, writing) under the Berne Convention. **Gameplay mechanics** are generally **not copyrightable** (idea–expression distinction), allowing genre evolution (e.g., *Doom* → FPS; *GTA III* → open-world clones). **Patents** cover hardware/technical processes; **trademarks** protect titles/logos. **Cloning** (near-exact replication with asset swaps) plagued early arcades and modern mobile markets (especially where IP enforcement is weak, e.g., China). The industry struggles to distinguish iterative refinement from infringing clones.

### Industry Economics and Roles
The 2020 market was ~$159B (Mobile 48%, Console 28%, Personal computer 23%). Key roles:
*   **Developers:** Create the game.
*   **Publishers:** Fund, market, manage distribution, influence creative direction.
*   **Distributors/Retailers:** Physical (stores, trade-in) and digital (Steam, App Store) storefronts.
*   **Hardware Manufacturers:** Console makers (licensing fees, exclusivity deals); PC component vendors (GPUs).
*   **Journalism/Influencers:** Pre-release hype, reviews, streaming impact sales.
*   **Esports:** Pro leagues, massive viewership (esp. Southeast Asia).
*   **Trade Groups (ESA):** Lobbying, conventions (E3).
*   **Gamers:** Consumer base; demographics shifted mid-2010s toward women/older players, driving mobile/casual growth.

### Societal Effects: Culture, Art, Benefits
**Culture:** Global subculture; "gamer" identity; social networks via multiplayer; esports acceptance; COVID-19 pandemic during 2020–2021 gave further visibility to video games as a pastime to enjoy with friends and family online as a means of social distancing. **Art:** Debate settled legally in 2011 (*Brown v. EMA*). Games inspire transmedia (films, shows, comics); early adaptations poorly received, recent successes (*Detective Pikachu*, *Sonic*). **Game music** (chiptunes to orchestral) fuels concerts (Video Games Live). **Machinima** uses game engines for real-time 3D filmmaking; engines (Unreal) now used in virtual production (*The Mandalorian*). **Benefits:** Educational potential ("learning by doing," creative thinking). Cognitive gains: action games improve hand-eye coordination, peripheral attention, object tracking. Mental health: Oxford (2020) and Saskatchewan studies link play to wellbeing, stress reduction, social connection. Millennials report gaming as primary stress coping strategy.

### Controversies
*   **Violence:** Claims linking games to real-world aggression (e.g., Columbine 1999); medical bodies (WHO: "gaming disorder"; APA: insufficient evidence for violence link) debate causality; industry uses **compulsion loops** (dopamine-driven retention) which critics argue fosters addiction.
*   **Regulation:** 1993 Congressional hearings (*Mortal Kombat*) created ESRB. Legal challenges (Jack Thompson vs. *GTA*, *Manhunt*).
*   **Content:** "No Russian" level (*Call of Duty: MW2*, 2009) allowed shooting civilians.
*   **Harassment/Discrimination:** Gamergate (2014) exposed misogyny; ongoing issues with gender/racial/LGBTQ+ representation in games and industry workforce.
*   **Monetization/Gambling:** **Loot boxes** (randomized rewards) scrutinized as gambling mechanics.
*   **Labor:** "Crunch time" (extended mandatory overtime pre-launch) standard but criticized.

### Collecting and Preservation
**Collecting:** Retro gaming drives market; sealed rare games exceed $100,000 (2020). **Preservation:** Media degradation (bit rot), hardware obsolescence, and defunct publishers threaten history. Archivists operate within copyright exemptions. **Museums:** National Videogame Museum (Frisco, TX), Computer Games Museum (Berlin), Museum of Soviet Arcade Machines (Moscow), The Strong (Rochester, NY), Smithsonian (permanent display: *Pac-Man*, *Dragon's Lair*, *Pong*), MoMA (20 games + console in Architecture/Design collection since 2012).

## Terms
- ****Platform**** — The specific hardware/software combination (console, PC, mobile) required to run a video game.
- ****AAA (Triple-A)**** — High-budget, high-profile games produced by large studios/publishers, typically low-risk with large marketing.
- ****Indie Game**** — Games developed by small teams without publisher control, often experimental in mechanics or art.
- ****Game Engine**** — Core software framework (e.g., Unreal, Unity) handling rendering, physics, logic, enabling cross-platform development.
- ****DLC (Downloadable Content)**** — Post-launch game additions (levels, cosmetics, story) delivered digitally, often monetized.
- ****Mod (Modification)**** — Player-created alterations to game code/assets, ranging from fixes to total conversions; mostly PC.
- ****Haptic Feedback**** — Tactile sensations (vibration, force) delivered via controllers to simulate physical interaction.
- ****Compulsion Loop**** — Core design cycle: action → reward → dopamine → motivation to repeat; basis for retention and addiction concerns.
- ****Loot Box**** — Monetized virtual container awarding random items; controversial for resembling gambling.
- ****Crunch**** — Mandatory extended overtime (weeks/months) before a game's release to meet deadlines.
- ****Emergent Gameplay**** — Complex, unscripted behaviors arising from simple interacting systems (AI, physics), not explicit design.
- ****Machinima**** — Filmmaking technique using real-time 3D game engines to render animated narratives.
- ****Backward Compatibility**** — Ability of a newer platform to natively run software from previous generations (vs. emulation).
- ****ESRB / PEGI / CERO**** — Major content rating boards for North America, Europe, and Japan respectively, assigning age suitability.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Definition of "Video Game":** No industry-standard definition existed by 2021 (highlighted in *Epic v. Apple*). Judge Rogers set a floor: "interactivity affecting visual display" + "graphically rendered/animated," but acknowledged the category is "highly eclectic." The term "video game" emerged around 1973. Walking simulators (adventure games that allow for exploration but lack any objectives) like *Gone Home*, and empathy games (video games that tend to focus on emotion) like *That Dragon, Cancer* challenge "win condition" requirements.
*   **Violence and Addiction Causality:** Conflicting medical consensus: WHO recognizes "gaming disorder"; APA finds insufficient evidence for violence/addiction links. Core mechanic of compulsion loops remains a design ethics flashpoint.
*   **Gameplay Copyrightability:** Mechanics are generally uncopyrightable (idea-expression), enabling genre iteration but also enabling predatory cloning. Legal line between "refinement" and "clone" is blurred, especially in jurisdictions with weak IP enforcement.
*   **Art Status vs. Commercial Product:** Tension between games as commercial entertainment and artistic expression (interactivity vs. authorial intent). Resolved legally in US (2011), but curatorial debates persist (e.g., MoMA/Smithsonian exhibits).
*   **Preservation vs. Copyright:** Legal barriers to archiving server-dependent games (MMOs, live-service), proprietary hardware, and defunct publisher IP. Current exemptions are narrow and temporary.
*   **Labor Ethics:** "Crunch" normalized as industry standard; unionization efforts growing but face structural resistance.
*   **Monetization Ethics:** Loot boxes, battle passes, and "games as a service" models blur line between design and gambling regulation; governments (Belgium, Netherlands) have banned specific mechanics.
