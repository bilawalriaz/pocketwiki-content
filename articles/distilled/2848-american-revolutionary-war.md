# American Revolutionary War

## Overview
The American Revolutionary War (1775–1783) was the armed conflict through which the Thirteen Colonies won independence from Great Britain, establishing the United States. It began as a colonial revolt over taxation and governance, evolved into a global war involving European powers, and resulted in a new nation founded on republican principles, though its legacy was complicated by issues of slavery and Native American displacement.

## Timeline
- **April 19, 1775** — Battles of Lexington and Concord; first military engagements of the war.
- **June 14, 1775** — Second Continental Congress establishes the Continental Army and appoints George Washington as commander-in-chief.
- **July 4, 1776** — Second Continental Congress adopts the Declaration of Independence.
- **October 17, 1777** — British General Burgoyne surrenders at Saratoga, a pivotal victory that secures French alliance.
- **February 6, 1778** — France signs treaties of alliance and commerce with the United States.
- **Winter 1777–78** — Continental Army endures harsh winter at Valley Forge; Baron von Steuben trains troops.
- **October 19, 1781** — British General Cornwallis surrenders at Yorktown, the last major battle.
- **September 3, 1783** — Treaty of Paris is signed, formally ending the war and recognizing U.S. independence.

## Body

### Prelude to War
Tensions escalated after the Seven Years' War (1763) as Britain sought to tax the colonies to pay for defense and control westward expansion. Measures like the Stamp Act (1765) and Townshend Acts provoked colonial resistance, summarized by the slogan "no taxation without representation." The Boston Massacre (1770) and Boston Tea Party (1773) further inflamed relations. Britain's punitive Intolerable Acts (1774) led to the formation of the First Continental Congress and, ultimately, the armed clashes at Lexington and Concord in April 1775.

### The War for Independence
The conflict was a protracted war of attrition. Early Patriot victories, like the siege of Boston (1776), were followed by severe defeats in New York. Washington's strategic retreats and surprise attacks at Trenton and Princeton (1776–77) preserved the Continental Army. The American victory at Saratoga (1777) was the war's turning point, convincing France to enter the war as an American ally in 1778. The British shifted their strategy to the South, achieving initial successes but suffering a decisive defeat at Yorktown (1781), where a Franco-American force trapped Cornwallis. The Treaty of Paris (1783) confirmed British recognition of American independence and ceded territory east of the Mississippi River.

### Global Conflict and Foreign Alliances
The war became a global struggle after 1778. France provided crucial money, troops, and naval power, culminating in the joint victory at Yorktown. Spain (1779) and the Dutch Republic (1780) also fought against Britain, though Spain did not formally ally with the U.S. These alliances stretched British resources and forced them to divert military focus to the Caribbean and Europe. The British also employed approximately 30,000 German soldiers (often called "Hessians"), a move that bolstered Patriot support for independence.

### Civil War and Society
The Revolution was also a civil war between Patriots (pro-independence) and Loyalists (pro-British), with an estimated 20% of the population in each camp and the rest neutral. This division was especially violent in the South. Women participated as camp followers, nurses, and occasionally in combat, though their legal status remained largely unchanged. African Americans fought on both sides; Lord Dunmore's 1775 proclamation offered freedom to enslaved people who joined the British, while about 5,000 served in the Continental Army after 1778. Native American tribes were divided; most allied with Britain to resist American expansion, leading to devastating campaigns like the Sullivan Expedition (1779) against the Iroquois.

## Terms
- **Continental Army**: The unified military force of the Thirteen Colonies, established by the Second Continental Congress in 1775 and commanded by George Washington.
- **Loyalist**: A colonist who remained loyal to the British Crown during the American Revolution.
- **Patriot**: A colonist who supported independence from Great Britain.
- **Hessian**: A German soldier hired by the British to fight in the American Revolutionary War; the term broadly refers to all German troops in British service.
- **Treaty of Paris (1783)**: The agreement that ended the American Revolutionary War, in which Great Britain recognized the independence of the United States.
- **Fabian strategy**: A military strategy (employed by Washington) of avoiding pitched battles and frontal assaults against a superior enemy, instead wearing them down through skirmishes and strategic retreats.
- **Privateer**: A privately owned armed ship authorized by a government to attack enemy shipping during wartime.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Historians debate whether Patriot victory was inevitable or a "miracle," given Britain's military advantages. Some argue British strategic errors, like Howe's failure to pursue Washington decisively in 1776, were decisive.
- The extent of popular support for the Revolution is contested; estimates of the population split between Patriots, Loyalists, and neutrals vary (e.g., 40% Patriot, 40% neutral, 20% Loyalist).
- The war's legacy regarding liberty is debated: while it established a republic, it failed to end slavery or significantly improve the status of women and Native Americans, whose lands were ceded without their consent in the Treaty of Paris.
