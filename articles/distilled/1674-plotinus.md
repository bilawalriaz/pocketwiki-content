# Plotinus

## Overview
Plotinus (c. 204/5–270 AD) was a Greek philosopher from Roman Egypt, regarded by modern scholars as the founder of Neoplatonism. A student of the self-taught Platonist Ammonius Saccas, he synthesized Plato’s doctrines with Aristotelian, Stoic, and Pythagorean elements into a systematic metaphysics centered on three fundamental principles: the One (the transcendent source), the Intellect (Nous), and the Soul. His philosophy posits that all reality emanates necessarily from the One in descending degrees of perfection, rather than being created by an act of will. The ultimate human goal is *henosis* (mystical union with the One), achieved by turning consciousness inward away from the material world. His collected treatises, the *Enneads*, edited by his student Porphyry, shaped the development of Christian, Islamic, and Jewish theology, Renaissance humanism, and modern idealism.

## Timeline
- **c. 204/5 AD** — Born in Lyco, Roman Egypt (likely Asyut or Deltaic Lycopolis).
- **c. 232 AD** — Begins philosophy studies at age 28 in Alexandria; becomes student of Ammonius Saccas for 11 years.
- **242–243 AD** — Joins Emperor Gordian III’s Persian expedition to study Eastern philosophy; campaign fails, Gordian dies; Plotinus escapes to Antioch.
- **c. 244 AD** — Arrives in Rome at age 40 under Emperor Philip the Arab; establishes a school attracting senators, doctors, and women.
- **c. 253–270 AD** — Writes the treatises later compiled as the *Enneads*.
- **263–268 AD** — Attempts to persuade Emperor Gallienus to found a "City of Philosophers" in Campania based on Plato’s *Laws*; imperial subsidy denied.
- **270 AD** — Dies in Campania at age 66 during reign of Claudius II; final words urge raising "the divine in yourselves to the divine in the all."
- **c. 301 AD** — Porphyry edits and arranges the *Enneads* into six groups of nine treatises; writes *Life of Plotinus*.

## Body

### Biography and Intellectual Formation
Plotinus was born in Lyco, Egypt, likely to a Hellenized family; his native language and education were Greek. At twenty-eight, he traveled to Alexandria, the intellectual center of the Roman East, but found no satisfactory teacher until he encountered Ammonius Saccas, a self-taught Platonist. Recognizing Ammonius as "the man I was looking for," Plotinus studied with him for eleven years. During this period, he also engaged deeply with Aristotle, the pre-Socratics Empedocles and Heraclitus, Middle Platonists (Alexander of Aphrodisias, Numenius of Apamea), Stoics, and Neopythagoreans. In 242–243, seeking Persian and Indian wisdom, he joined Emperor Gordian III’s ill-fated Persian campaign. After the emperor’s death, he barely escaped to Antioch, arriving in Rome around 244. There he taught for twenty-six years, attracting a diverse circle including the senator Castricius Firmus, the physician Eustochius, the Arab-descended Zethos (who bequeathed him an estate), and the future editor Porphyry. He gained the respect of Emperor Gallienus and Empress Salonina but failed to secure funding for a utopian "City of Philosophers." He died in 270 on Zethos’s Campanian estate; Porphyry records a snake slipping away at the moment of death.

### The Metaphysical Hierarchy: The One, Intellect, and Soul
Plotinus’s system rests on three *hypostases* (fundamental realities). **The One** (τὸ Ἕν) is absolutely simple, transcendent, and beyond being, non-being, thought, and self-awareness. It is identified with the Good and Beauty. Because it is perfect and unchanging, it cannot "create" through action; instead, the multiple necessarily **emanates** (ἀπορροή) from the simple, like light from the sun or reflection from a mirror, without diminishing the source. The first emanation is **Nous** (Intellect/Divine Mind), the realm of Platonic Forms and the "first will toward Good." Nous contemplates the One and thereby generates its own content (the Forms). From Nous emanates the **World Soul**, which Plotinus divides into a higher part (contemplating Nous) and a lower part (governing nature and generating individual souls). Matter (ὕλη) is the final, least perfected emanation—utterly formless, non-being, and the principle of evil as privation. This hierarchy is not temporal but a constant, timeless ontological dependence: lower levels exist *because* higher levels exist.

### Emanation, the Cosmos, and the Status of Matter
Emanation offers an alternative to *creatio ex nihilo*. The One remains transcendent and unaffected; the cosmos unfolds as a continuous procession (πρόοδος) of decreasing unity and perfection. Plotinus insists on the **divine nature of material creation** because it ultimately derives from the One via Nous and Soul. Matter itself is not independently real but a substrate wholly dependent on Form; it is "non-existent" in the strict sense, an illusion of separation. The World Soul administers the cosmos providentially; individual human souls descend into bodies but retain their higher, intellective nature. Plotinus argues against causal astrology (Ennead 2.3), asserting that while stars are ensouled and their movements indicate cosmic order, they do not *cause* human fortune—such determinism would imply irrationality in a perfect universe and undermine moral responsibility.

### Ethics, Happiness, and Henosis
For Plotinus, the **true human** is not the soul-body composite but the incorporeal, contemplative intellect. Authentic happiness (*eudaimonia*) is therefore independent of physical circumstance, sickness, or even torture; it consists solely in the activity of the highest part of the soul directed toward the Forms and the One. The happy person (the "Proficient") lives in "interiority," focused inward. The summit of ethics is **henosis** (ἕνωσις), mystical union with the One. This is achieved by a reversal of the ontological procession: stripping away all multiplicity, discursive thought (Nous), and duality (dyad) through a meditation-like "uncontemplation" until the subject dissolves into the Monad. Porphyry reports Plotinus attained this union four times. Henosis is not an intellectual exercise but an experiential identification where "seer and seen are one." Matter is rejected as an impediment; the body is a "poor image" (mimesis) of true being.

### The *Enneads*: Composition and Transmission
Plotinus wrote treatises from c. 253 until shortly before his death. Due to poor eyesight and atrocious handwriting, he did not revise or organize them; they served as lecture notes. Porphyry edited the fifty-four treatises into six "Enneads" (groups of nine), polishing syntax and spelling, and wrote the biography *On the Life of Plotinus*. The standard modern editions are the Henry-Schwyzer Greek text (1951–1973) and translations by A.H. Armstrong (Loeb, 1966–1988) and Lloyd P. Gerson et al. (Cambridge, 2018).

### Relation to Plato and the Gnostics
Plotinus viewed himself not as an innovator but as Plato’s faithful interpreter. E.R. Dodds (1929) and the Tübingen School later argued that Neoplatonism is the culmination of Plato’s "unwritten doctrines," not a distortion. Against the **Gnostics**, Plotinus wrote *Ennead 2.9* (*Against the Gnostics*). He criticized specific sects for: (1) misusing Platonic terminology (e.g., "demiurge," "aeon") in a sectarian, irrational way; (2) despising the material world and its maker, which Plotinus held is the best possible imitation of the intelligible; (3) teaching salvation without moral struggle. A.H. Armstrong saw Plotinus as vitriolic; Lloyd P. Gerson reads the treatise as a corrective to friends attracted to Gnostic doctrine, emphasizing respect for the sensible cosmos as an emanation of the Good.

### Historical Influence
**Ancient World:** Emperor Julian the Apostate and Hypatia of Alexandria were Neoplatonists. **Christianity:** Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite and St. Augustine (mediated by Neoplatonists) integrated Plotinian metaphysics; Bertrand Russell called Plotinus historically decisive for medieval theology. The East/West schism on divine energies (Palamas vs. Aquinas) reflects differing receptions of Plotinian vs. Aristotelian frameworks. **Islam:** The Abbasid Mutazilites, Ismaili Shia, and Persian philosophers (al-Nasafi, al-Sijistani, al-Kirmani, Nasir Khusraw) absorbed Neoplatonism via Arabic translations (the "Theology of Aristotle"). **Judaism:** Solomon ibn Gabirol (Avicebron) and Moses Maimonides adopted apophatic theology and the privative theory of evil. **Renaissance:** Marsilio Ficino’s Florentine Academy (patron Cosimo de’ Medici) and Pico della Mirandola revived Plotinian Platonism to reconcile it with Christianity. **England:** The 17th-century Cambridge Platonists and poets from Coleridge to Yeats and Kathleen Raine drew on Plotinus. **India:** Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan and Ananda Coomaraswamy compared Plotinus to Advaita Vedanta (non-dualism), noting affinities with the Upanishadic *neti neti* (negation) method and yoga; J.F. Staal and others have systematically compared the two traditions.

## Terms
- ****Neoplatonism**** — Modern term (19th c.) for the philosophical system founded by Plotinus, developing Plato’s metaphysics into a hierarchical emanationism.
- ****Hypostasis**** — A fundamental, subsistent level of reality; Plotinus posits three: One, Intellect (Nous), Soul.
- ****The One (τὸ Ἕν)**** — The supreme, absolutely simple, transcendent principle beyond being and thought; source of all; identified with the Good.
- ****Nous (Νοῦς)**** — Divine Intellect; first emanation from the One; realm of Platonic Forms; thinks the One and thereby constitutes itself.
- ****World Soul (Ψυχή)**** — Third hypostasis; emanates from Nous; governs cosmos; subdivided into higher (contemplative) and lower (natural) aspects.
- ****Emanation (ἀπορροή)**** — Necessary, timeless outflow of multiplicity from the One’s superabundance, without diminishing the source (analogy: light from sun).
- ****Henosis (ἕνωσις)**** — Mystical "union" with the One; reversal of emanation via stripping away multiplicity and discursive thought.
- ****Matter (ὕλη)**** — Ultimate substrate of the sensible world; furthest emanation; formless, non-being, principle of evil as privation.
- ****Eudaimonia**** — True happiness; for Plotinus, the activity of the true human (intellect) contemplating the Forms/One, independent of body.
- ****Demiurge**** — In Plato’s *Timaeus*, the craftsman-god; Plotinus identifies this metaphorically with Nous, not a separate creator.

## Debates and open questions
- **Birthplace and ethnicity:** Sources differ on Lyco (Asyut vs. Deltaic Lycopolis); scholars debate whether Plotinus was ethnically Greek, Hellenized Egyptian, or Roman (Gerson: "almost certainly Greek"; Armstrong: Greek language/education certain).
- **Relation to Christianity:** Whether Plotinus knowingly opposed Christian doctrine (e.g., *creatio ex nihilo*) or developed an independent pagan metaphysics; the *Enneads* never mention Christianity explicitly.
- **Interpretation of the Gnostic treatise:** Armstrong (vitriolic heresiology) vs. Gerson (philosophical correction of friends); identity of the targeted Gnostic sect (anti-daemon, anti-polytheist, "salvation without struggle").
- **Continuity with Plato:** Whether Neoplatonism is a faithful development of Plato’s "unwritten doctrines" (Tübingen School) or a novel synthesis incorporating Aristotelian, Stoic, and Oriental elements.
- **Nature of the One:** Whether the One is a personal deity, an impersonal principle, or beyond the distinction; Plotinus denies it sentience, self-awareness, or action (*ergon*).
- **Indian parallels:** Extent of direct influence vs. structural affinity between Plotinus’s *henosis* / negation method and Advaita Vedanta / Upanishadic *neti neti*; Coomaraswamy and Radhakrishnan argue for deep methodological kinship.
