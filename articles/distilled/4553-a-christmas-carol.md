# A Christmas Carol

## Overview
*A Christmas Carol* is a novella by Charles Dickens, first published in 1843, that tells the story of Ebenezer Scrooge, a miser who is transformed into a generous man after being visited by ghosts. It is a foundational text of modern Christmas tradition, capturing the Victorian revival of the holiday and using a conversion narrative to critique social indifference to poverty.

## Timeline
- **1842** — Dickens visits the Field Lane Ragged School, inspiring the story's social themes.
- **October 1843** — Dickens begins writing *A Christmas Carol*.
- **19 December 1843** — First edition published by Chapman & Hall.
- **Christmas Eve 1843** — First edition sells out.
- **January 1844** — An unauthorised, condensed version is published; Dickens sues the publishers.
- **1849** — Dickens begins public readings of the story.
- **1870** — Dickens gives his final public reading of the story before his death.

## Body

### Background and Creation
Dickens wrote the novella during a period of Victorian Christmas revival, influenced by his own childhood poverty and contemporary social reports on child labour. He initially planned a political pamphlet but decided a Christmas story would reach a wider audience. The book was written quickly in six weeks and published with illustrations by John Leech. Dickens funded the production himself, but high costs reduced his profits.

### Plot Summary
The story is divided into five "staves." On Christmas Eve, the miser Ebenezer Scrooge is visited by the ghost of his deceased partner, Jacob Marley, who is condemned to wander Earth chained by his greed. Marley warns Scrooge he will be visited by three spirits to offer him a chance at redemption. The Ghost of Christmas Past shows Scrooge his lonely childhood and lost love. The Ghost of Christmas Present reveals the current joy of others, including the poor but loving Cratchit family and their ill son, Tiny Tim. The Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come shows Scrooge a future where his death is unmourned and Tiny Tim has died. Horrified, Scrooge awakens on Christmas morning a changed man, embracing generosity and kindness.

### Themes and Interpretation
The central theme is the transformation of Scrooge from selfishness to sympathy. Scholars debate whether the story is a Christian allegory of redemption or a secular parable. Dickens uses the tale to critique social attitudes toward poverty, personified by the allegorical figures of Ignorance and Want. The story advocates for practical benevolence and social reconciliation, linking the celebration of Christmas to charity and community.

### Publication, Reception, and Legacy
The first edition was a commercial success, selling out by Christmas Eve and going through thirteen editions by 1844. Contemporary reviews were largely positive, praising its moral message and humour. The story was adapted for the stage almost immediately and has since been adapted for film, television, and other media more than any other Dickens work. Its phrases "Merry Christmas" and "Bah! Humbug!" entered popular language, and the name "Scrooge" became synonymous with miser. Dickens's public readings of the story were highly successful and helped cement its place in popular culture.

## Terms
*   **Novella:** A work of fiction longer than a short story but shorter than a novel.
*   **Stave:** A chapter heading used by Dickens, evoking a musical carol.
*   **Miser:** A person who hoards wealth and spends as little money as possible.
*   **Ragged School:** A free school for destitute children in 19th-century Britain.
*   **Allegory:** A story, poem, or picture that can be interpreted to reveal a hidden meaning, typically a moral or political one.
*   **Zeitgeist:** The defining spirit or mood of a particular period of history.
*   **Polemical:** A strong verbal or written attack on someone or something.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Secular vs. Christian Allegory:** Scholars debate whether the story is primarily a secular tale of social reform or a Christian allegory about redemption.
*   **Economic Critique:** Some critics, both contemporary and modern, have questioned the story's economic logic, asking who provides the surplus that enables Scrooge's charity.
*   **Adaptation vs. Original:** The story has been adapted so frequently that the "remembered version" in popular culture often differs from Dickens's original text, with scenes added or omitted.
