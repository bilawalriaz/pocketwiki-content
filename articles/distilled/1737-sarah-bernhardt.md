# Sarah Bernhardt

## Sarah Bernhardt

## Overview

Sarah Bernhardt (1844–1923) was a French stage actress who became one of the most famous performers in the world during the late 19th and early 20th centuries. She starred in popular French plays, played both female and male roles (including Hamlet), toured globally, and was an early pioneer in both sound recordings and motion pictures. She was also a painter and sculptor, and her fame helped launch the Art Nouveau poster career of Alphonse Mucha.

## Body

### Early life and training

Bernhardt was born in Paris's Latin Quarter to Judith Bernard, a Dutch Jewish courtesan. Her father's identity was long unknown; a reconstituted birth certificate (1914) named an Édouard Bernhardt of Le Havre. Her mother travelled frequently and placed Bernhardt in boarding schools. At age 10, with sponsorship from Charles de Morny (half-brother of Napoleon III), she entered a convent school near Versailles. She received her first communion as a Roman Catholic in 1856 but never forgot her Jewish heritage. In 1857, after her father's death, a family council—including Morny—decided she should become an actress. Morny arranged her first theatre visit (a Racine play) and her audition at the Paris Conservatory, where she won a place by reciting a La Fontaine fable.

### Rise to fame

Bernhardt studied at the Conservatory from 1860 to 1862, changing her surname from "Bernard" to "Bernhardt." She debuted at the Comédie-Française on 31 August 1862 in *Iphigénie*; after a subsequent incident in which she slapped a senior actress, she was asked to leave. After a brief period at the Gymnase and a scandalous recital of Victor Hugo poems before Napoleon III, she travelled to Belgium, where she had an affair with Prince Henri de Ligne, resulting in the birth of her son Maurice in December 1864. She joined the Odéon theatre in early 1866, where her sincerity and "golden voice" won praise. Her breakthrough came in *Kean* (1868), followed by *Le Passant* (1868). During the Franco-Prussian War (1870–71), she converted the Odéon into a hospital, personally nursing wounded soldiers. Her 1872 performance as the Queen in Hugo's *Ruy Blas* made her a star; Hugo knelt and kissed her hand.

### Comédie-Française and international fame

Bernhardt returned to the Comédie-Française in 1872, excelling in *Phèdre* (1873) and *Hernani* (1877). She maintained a theatrical lifestyle, keeping a satin-lined coffin in her bedroom. In 1879, the company toured London, where her *Phèdre* was a triumph despite the audience's limited French. She resigned in 1880 after a dispute with the director, then toured America with great financial success, performing *La Dame aux Camélias* (renamed "Camille") 65 times. During the tour she made a side trip to Menlo Park, where she met Thomas Edison, who made a brief recording of her reciting from *Phèdre*. She returned to Paris in 1881 to a cold reception but restored her standing by reciting the Marseillaise at a patriotic spectacle. She married Jacques Damala in 1882; the marriage collapsed within months due to his morphine addiction and infidelity. She starred in Sardou's *Fédora* (1882) and *Theodora* (1884), the latter a financial success that paid off most of her debts.

### World tours and theatre management

Bernhardt toured the world repeatedly to replenish her finances, visiting South America (1886–87), Europe and Russia (1888), and an extensive 1891–92 tour including Australia and New Zealand. In 1887, she starred in Sardou's *La Tosca*, which inspired Puccini's opera. She bought the Théâtre de la Renaissance in 1893, directing and starring in nine plays over five years. In 1894, she commissioned the unknown Czech artist Alphonse Mucha to create a poster for *Gismonda*; his Art Nouveau style made him famous. In 1899, she leased the larger Théâtre des Nations, renamed it the Théâtre Sarah Bernhardt, and opened with *La Tosca*. She played Hamlet in 1899 and the Duc de Reichstadt in Rostand's *L'Aiglon* (1900), a huge success popular with visitors to the Paris Exposition.

### Later career and final years

Bernhardt made four "farewell" tours of America (1905–06, 1910, 1913–14, and a final tour during World War I). In 1906, she injured her right knee leaping in *La Tosca* in Rio de Janeiro; the injury never fully healed. She received the Légion d'honneur in 1914. In February 1915, gangrene forced the amputation of her right leg. She continued performing, often seated or propped, and entertained troops at Verdun and the Argonne. She died of uremia on 26 March 1923.

### Motion pictures, art, and writing

Bernhardt was among the first actresses to appear in films. Her 1900 *Le Duel d'Hamlet* was an early sound film (with a phonograph recording). Her 1912 film *The Loves of Queen Elizabeth*, produced by Adolph Zukor, was a financial success that helped found Paramount Pictures. She was also a serious sculptor, exhibiting at the Paris Salon and earning an honourable mention in 1876; about 50 works are documented. In her final years, she wrote *L'Art du Théâtre* (published 1923), a textbook on acting emphasising voice control, breathing, and expressing emotion through gesture.

## Terms

- **Comédie-Française**: The most prestigious state theatre in Paris, where Bernhardt debuted and later returned.
- **Pensionnaire**: A salaried actor at the Comédie-Française, below the highest rank of societaire.
- **Odéon**: A Paris theatre second in prestige only to the Comédie-Française, known for modern productions.
- **La Dame aux Camélias**: Play by Alexandre Dumas fils; Bernhardt's most famous role, performed over a thousand times.
- **Art Nouveau**: Decorative style popularised in part by Alphonse Mucha's posters of Bernhardt.
- **Légion d'honneur**: French order of merit; Bernhardt was made a Chevalier in 1914.
- **Uremia**: Kidney failure; the cause of Bernhardt's death.

## Debates and open questions

- **Father's identity**: Unknown for certain; the original birth certificate was destroyed in the 1871 Paris Commune fire. A 2009 biography by Hélène Tierchant suggests her father may have been a young man named De Morel, whose family were notable shipowners and merchants in Le Havre.
- **Date of birth**: Uncertain; she celebrated 23 October, but the reconstituted certificate gives 25 October, and other sources say 22 October.
- **Paternity of her son Maurice**: Attributed to Prince Henri de Ligne, but Bernhardt never confirmed it; some accounts say the Prince denied responsibility, while others claim he later offered to recognise Maurice.
- **Relationship with Louise Abbéma**: Rumoured to be lovers; a 1990 donation of a painting to the Comédie-Française included a letter describing it as painted "on the anniversary of their love affair."
- **Vegetarianism**: Described as a strict vegetarian (later termed vegan), but a 1923 biography notes she consumed fish and cheese in later years.
- **Critical reception**: Admirers (Hugo, Wilde, Freud) praised her "golden voice" and hypnotic presence; detractors (Shaw, Chekhov, Turgenev) called her acting artificial and egotistical.
