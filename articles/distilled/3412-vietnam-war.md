# Vietnam War

## Overview
The Vietnam War (c. 1955–1975) was a prolonged armed conflict in Southeast Asia, primarily between communist North Vietnam (Democratic Republic of Vietnam) and anti-communist South Vietnam (Republic of Vietnam). It was a proxy war of the Cold War, with the Soviet Union and China backing the North, and the United States supporting the South. The war was a defining postcolonial struggle and a civil war that ended with the communist victory and reunification of Vietnam, profoundly impacting global politics and leaving a legacy of devastation.

## Timeline
- **1954** — Geneva Conference temporarily divides Vietnam at the 17th parallel.
- **1955** — Ngô Đình Diem declares the Republic of Vietnam (South Vietnam).
- **1959** — North Vietnam approves a "people's war" to reunify Vietnam.
- **1960** — National Liberation Front (Viet Cong) formed in the South.
- **1964** — Gulf of Tonkin incident leads to U.S. congressional resolution for military escalation.
- **1965** — First U.S. combat troops land at Da Nang; Operation Rolling Thunder bombing begins.
- **1968** — Tet Offensive by North Vietnamese/Viet Cong forces shocks U.S. public.
- **1969** — U.S. President Nixon begins "Vietnamization" and troop withdrawals.
- **1970** — U.S. and South Vietnamese forces invade Cambodia.
- **1973** — Paris Peace Accords signed; last U.S. combat troops leave Vietnam.
- **1975** — North Vietnamese Spring Offensive captures Saigon; South Vietnam surrenders.

## Body

### Origins and Early Conflict
The conflict's roots lie in Vietnamese resistance to French colonial rule, culminating in the First Indochina War (1946–1954). The 1954 Geneva Accords ended French involvement but temporarily divided Vietnam, with Ho Chi Minh's communist Viet Minh controlling the North and the U.S.-backed Ngô Đình Diem leading the South. The promised reunification elections never occurred. In the South, Diem's authoritarian rule and discrimination against Buddhists fueled internal dissent. Meanwhile, North Vietnam supported the communist Viet Cong (VC) insurgency in the South, which intensified from 1957. By 1959, North Vietnam had approved a "people's war" and began using the Ho Chi Minh Trail through Laos to supply southern forces.

### U.S. Escalation and the Ground War
Following the disputed Gulf of Tonkin incident in 1964, the U.S. Congress granted President Lyndon B. Johnson broad authority to escalate military involvement. The first U.S. combat troops arrived in 1965, and the conflict transformed into a major American war. U.S. strategy, led by General William Westmoreland, focused on attrition through "search and destroy" operations and massive firepower, including the sustained bombing campaign Operation Rolling Thunder against the North. By 1969, U.S. troop levels peaked at over 540,000. The war was characterized by a clash between U.S. conventional firepower and the guerrilla tactics of the VC and North Vietnamese Army (PAVN).

### Turning Points and U.S. Withdrawal
The 1968 Tet Offensive, a massive surprise attack by communist forces on over 100 cities, was a tactical defeat for the North but a strategic victory. It shattered U.S. public confidence in official claims of progress and turned opinion against the war. President Johnson declined to run for re-election. His successor, Richard Nixon, pursued "Vietnamization"—building up South Vietnamese forces while withdrawing U.S. troops. The war expanded into Cambodia and Laos, sparking massive anti-war protests in the U.S. The 1973 Paris Peace Accords ended direct U.S. involvement but left North Vietnamese forces in place in the South.

### Final Offensive and Aftermath
Fighting continued after the U.S. withdrawal. In 1975, a swift North Vietnamese offensive, the Spring Offensive, overwhelmed the South Vietnamese army. Saigon fell on April 30, 1975, leading to the reunification of Vietnam under communist rule. The war caused immense human and environmental costs. Estimates of Vietnamese military and civilian deaths range from 970,000 to 3 million. The U.S. suffered 58,220 deaths. The conflict also destabilized neighboring Cambodia and Laos, leading to the Khmer Rouge genocide and the Indochina refugee crisis, which saw millions flee, with an estimated 200,000–250,000 dying at sea. The U.S. experienced a "Vietnam syndrome," a lasting reluctance to commit to overseas military interventions.

## Terms
- **Viet Cong (VC):** Communist guerrilla force based in South Vietnam, supported by North Vietnam.
- **PAVN (People's Army of Vietnam):** The regular army of North Vietnam.
- **Ho Chi Minh Trail:** Network of supply routes through Laos and Cambodia used by North Vietnam to support forces in the South.
- **Vietnamization:** U.S. policy under President Nixon to transfer combat responsibility to South Vietnamese forces while withdrawing American troops.
- **Tet Offensive:** Major 1968 surprise attack by North Vietnamese and VC forces during the Vietnamese New Year (Tet) holiday.
- **Agent Orange:** Toxic herbicide used by the U.S. military to defoliate jungles, causing long-term health and environmental damage.
- **Domino Theory:** U.S. foreign policy rationale that if one country fell to communism, neighboring countries would follow.
- **Paris Peace Accords:** 1973 agreement that ended direct U.S. military involvement in the war.
- **Re-education camps:** Prisons established by the unified Vietnamese government to detain former South Vietnamese officials and soldiers.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Was the war a civil war or a foreign invasion?** The conflict is debated as both an internal Vietnamese struggle and a proxy war driven by Cold War geopolitics.
- **Could the U.S. have achieved military victory?** Scholars debate whether U.S. military strategy was flawed or if the political objectives were ultimately unattainable.
- **What was the impact of the anti-war movement?** Historians disagree on the extent to which domestic opposition in the U.S. influenced the decision to withdraw and the war's outcome.
- **How responsible was the U.S. for the post-war refugee crisis and regional instability?** The long-term consequences, including the Cambodian genocide, are analyzed in relation to U.S. policies and bombing campaigns.
