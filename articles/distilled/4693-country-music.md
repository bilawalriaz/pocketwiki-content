# Country music

## Overview
Country music is a genre originating in the United States, known for its ballads, dance tunes, and instrumentation including guitars, banjos, fiddles, and harmonicas. It evolved from a blend of British folk, American folk, blues, and other traditions, and has remained a major part of American popular culture, experiencing a recent resurgence in the 2020s.

## Timeline
- **1922** — First commercial instrumental country recordings ("Arkansas Traveler" and "Turkey in the Straw") by Henry Gilliland & A.C. Robertson.
- **1923** — First commercial vocal country song recording, "Little Log Cabin in the Lane" by Fiddlin' John Carson.
- **1925** — Grand Ole Opry radio show begins airing in Nashville, Tennessee.
- **1927** — Historic Bristol recording sessions, featuring the Carter Family and Jimmie Rodgers.
- **1930s-40s** — "Hillbilly music" term is replaced; "country and western" becomes standard.
- **1944** — Billboard replaces the term "hillbilly" with "folk songs and blues."
- **1949** — Billboard switches to "country and western" for its chart terminology.
- **1953** — First all-country radio station established in Lubbock, Texas.
- **1958** — Country Music Association is founded.
- **1976** — *Wanted! The Outlaws* album becomes the first country album certified platinum.
- **1990** — Billboard changes its country chart methodology to rely solely on radio airplay.
- **2019** — Lil Nas X's "Old Town Road" spends 19 weeks at #1 on the Billboard Hot 100.

## Body

### Origins and Early Development
Country music developed in the Southern United States from a blend of traditions, including British folk music brought by immigrants, Appalachian music, cowboy and vaquero Western music, African-American folk songs and spirituals, and influences from Mexican, Irish, Gospel, and Hawaiian music. The U.S. Congress has recognized Bristol, Tennessee, as the "Birthplace of Country Music" due to the 1927 Bristol recording sessions. The genre gained a national audience through radio shows like the Grand Ole Opry (1925) and early recordings by artists such as Jimmie Rodgers and the Carter Family.

### Generational Evolution
The genre evolved through distinct generations. The first generation (1920s) saw the rise of radio and early recording stars. The second generation (1930s-40s) was marked by the Great Depression, the popularity of "barn dance" radio shows, and the development of subgenres like western swing and honky tonk. The third generation (1950s-60s) incorporated rock and roll elements, leading to rockabilly, and saw the rise of the polished "Nashville sound." The fourth generation (1970s-80s) featured the outlaw country movement and the emergence of country pop. The fifth generation (1990s-2000s) was dominated by superstars like Garth Brooks and Shania Twain, who achieved massive crossover success. The sixth generation (2010s-present) is characterized by the rise of alt-country, country rap, and continued fusion with pop and other genres.

### Regional and International Styles
While rooted in the American South and Southwest, country music has developed distinct regional styles. The Bakersfield sound emerged in California, while Red Dirt, Texas country, and Tejano music developed in the Southwest. Internationally, the genre has been adopted and adapted in many countries. Australia has a strong tradition of "bush music" and artists like Slim Dusty and Keith Urban. Canada has produced global stars like Shania Twain. In the UK, acts like Ward Thomas and the Shires have achieved chart success, and the "Country and Irish" genre blends American country with Irish folk. Country music also has growing scenes in Africa, Asia, and Latin America.

### Culture and Politics
Country music has often been associated with conservative, patriotic, and working-class values, particularly from the 1960s onward. This was cemented by artists like Merle Haggard and reinforced after events like the September 11 attacks. However, the genre's history is multicultural, drawing from African and European traditions, though it is today largely associated with white Americans. This has led to ongoing debates about race and inclusion, highlighted by controversies like the removal of Lil Nas X's "Old Town Road" from country charts and the experiences of Black artists like Mickey Guyton. Recent years have seen a broadening of themes, with some artists exploring topics like feminism and social justice.

## Terms
- **Honky Tonk:** A subgenre of country music with roots in western swing and blues, characterized by a raw sound and lyrics often about barroom life.
- **Bluegrass:** A style of country music featuring acoustic string bands, with roots in Appalachian folk music, popularized by Bill Monroe.
- **Rockabilly:** A fusion of country music and rock and roll that emerged in the 1950s.
- **Nashville Sound:** A polished, pop-oriented production style that dominated country music in the late 1950s and early 1960s.
- **Outlaw Country:** A subgenre that emerged in the 1970s, rejecting the polished Nashville sound in favor of a more traditional, rough-edged style.
- **Bro-Country:** A commercial country pop style from the early 2010s, often thematically focused on partying, trucks, and girls.
- **Americana:** A broad genre encompassing roots music forms like country, folk, blues, and rock, often associated with a more traditional sound.
- **Country Rap:** A fusion genre blending country music themes and instrumentation with hip-hop beats and rapping.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Race and Inclusion:** The genre's multicultural origins contrast with its modern association with white Americans, leading to debates about racial bias in the industry and on country radio.
- **Genre Boundaries:** The increasing fusion of country with pop, rock, and hip-hop sparks ongoing debate about what constitutes "authentic" country music.
- **Political Messaging:** While often associated with conservatism, the genre has a history of diverse political expression, and recent songs have sparked controversy over their perceived political messages.
