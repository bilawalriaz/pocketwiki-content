# Plant

## Overview

Plants are eukaryotic organisms that constitute the kingdom Plantae. They are predominantly photosynthetic, obtaining energy from sunlight using the green pigment chlorophyll in their chloroplasts to produce sugars from carbon dioxide and water, though some have evolved to be parasitic. By the definition used in this article, plants form the clade Viridiplantae (green plants), consisting of green algae and embryophytes (land plants). There are about 380,000 known species of plants, of which the majority, some 260,000, produce seeds. Green plants provide a substantial proportion of the world's molecular oxygen, and the sugars they create supply the energy for most of Earth's ecosystems. The scientific study of plants is known as botany.

## Body

### Definition and taxonomic history

All living things were traditionally placed into one of two groups, plants and animals. This classification dates from Aristotle (384–322 BC), who distinguished levels of beings based on whether they had a "sensitive soul" or, like plants, only a "vegetative soul". Theophrastus, Aristotle's student, continued his work in plant taxonomy. Much later, Carl Linnaeus (1707–1778) created the basis of the modern system of scientific classification but retained the animal and plant kingdoms, naming the plant kingdom the Vegetabilia. Historically, the plant kingdom encompassed all living things that were not animals, including algae and fungi; current definitions exclude fungi and some algae. A genome-based definition includes the Viridiplantae, along with red algae and glaucophytes, in the clade Archaeplastida.

### Evolution and diversity

There are about 382,000 accepted species of plants, of which some 283,000 produce seeds; about 85–90% of all plant species are flowering plants. Plants range from single-celled organisms such as desmids (from 10 micrometres across) and picozoa (less than 3 μm across) to the largest trees, such as the conifer *Sequoia sempervirens* (up to 120 metres tall) and the angiosperm *Eucalyptus regnans* (up to 100 metres tall). The naming of plants is governed by the International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants and the International Code of Nomenclature for Cultivated Plants.

The ancestors of land plants evolved in water. An algal scum formed on land 1,200 million years ago, but the first land plants appeared only in the Ordovician, around 450 million years ago. Fossils of organisms with a flattened thallus in Precambrian rocks suggest multicellular freshwater eukaryotes existed over 1000 million years ago. Early plant anatomy is preserved in the Rhynie chert, where plants were petrified in silica-rich volcanic hot springs. By the end of the Devonian, roots, leaves, and secondary wood were present. The Carboniferous saw forests dominated by clubmosses and horsetails, and the appearance of early gymnosperms. The Permo-Triassic extinction event radically changed community structures, possibly enabling the evolution of flowering plants in the Triassic, with rapid adaptive radiation in the Cretaceous. Conifers diversified from the Late Triassic onwards and became dominant in the Jurassic.

### Physiology

Plant cells have distinctive features other eukaryotic cells lack: the large water-filled central vacuole, chloroplasts, and the strong flexible cell wall outside the cell membrane. Chloroplasts derive from a symbiosis of a non-photosynthetic cell and photosynthetic cyanobacteria. The cell wall, made mostly of cellulose, allows cells to swell with water without bursting. Plant cells differentiate into tissues such as vascular tissue with specialised xylem and phloem, and organs including roots, stems, leaves, and flowers.

Plants photosynthesise, manufacturing sugars using light energy captured by chlorophyll. This releases oxygen into the atmosphere. Plants that have secondarily adopted a parasitic lifestyle may lose the genes involved in photosynthesis and chlorophyll production. Growth is determined by the interaction of a plant's genome with its physical and biotic environment; factors include temperature, water, light, carbon dioxide, soil nutrients, crowding, grazing, symbiotic bacteria and fungi, and attacks by insects or diseases. Some plants have antifreeze proteins, heat-shock proteins, and sugars that enable them to tolerate frost and dehydration.

Plants reproduce sexually, involving gametes, or asexually. Sexual reproduction involves alternation of generations: the diploid sporophyte gives rise to the haploid gametophyte. In seed plants, the sporophyte forms most of the visible plant. Flowering plants reproduce sexually using flowers; stamens create pollen, which fertilises the egg cell within carpels or ovaries, which develop into fruits containing seeds. Asexual reproduction occurs via structures such as runners, tubers, bulbs, and gemmae (small clumps of cells that detach and grow). Plants use pattern-recognition receptors to recognise pathogens; the first such receptors were identified in rice and *Arabidopsis thaliana*. Plants have some of the largest genomes of all organisms: wheat encodes ≈94,000 genes, while the smallest published genome is the bladderwort (*Utricularia gibba*) at 82 Mb and the largest is Norway spruce (*Picea abies*) at 19.6 Gb.

### Ecology

Plants are distributed almost worldwide, inhabiting many biomes; only hardy Antarctic flora (algae, mosses, liverworts, lichens, and two flowering plants) have adapted to that continent. Photosynthesis by land plants and algae is the ultimate source of energy in nearly all ecosystems. Photosynthesis, first by cyanobacteria and later by photosynthetic eukaryotes, changed the early Earth's anoxic atmosphere, which is now 21% oxygen. Plants form about 80% of the world's biomass at about 450 gigatonnes of carbon.

Numerous animals have coevolved with plants. Flowering plants have evolved pollination syndromes—suites of flower traits favouring reproduction—with insect and bird pollinators visiting flowers for pollen or nectar. Many animals disperse seeds adapted for gut passage or attachment to fur. Myrmecophytes are plants that coevolved with ants, providing them a home and food in exchange for defence. Most plant species have mycorrhizal fungi associated with their roots in mutualistic symbiosis: fungi help plants gain water and minerals, while plants give fungi carbohydrates. Many legumes have *Rhizobium* nitrogen-fixing bacteria in root nodules. Some 1% of plant species are parasitic, ranging from semi-parasitic mistletoe to fully-parasitic broomrape. Epiphytes grow on other plants without parasitising them; hemiepiphytes like the strangler fig eventually overpower and kill their host. Some 630 species of plants are carnivorous, trapping small animals to obtain mineral nutrients, especially nitrogen and phosphorus. Plants compete for shared resources including sunlight, water, and nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium.

### Importance to humans

Human cultivation of plants is the core of agriculture, which has played a key role in the history of world civilisations. About 7,000 species of plant have been used for food, though most of today's food derives from only 30 species. Major staples include cereals such as rice and wheat, starchy roots and tubers such as cassava and potato, and legumes such as peas and beans. Coffee, tea, and chocolate are major crops. Medicinal plants are a primary source of organic compounds; modern medicines derived from plants include aspirin, taxol, morphine, quinine, reserpine, colchicine, digitalis, and vincristine. Dioscorides' *De materia medica*, describing some 600 medicinal plants, was written between 50 and 70 CE and remained in use until around 1600 CE.

Nonfood products from plants include essential oils, natural dyes, pigments, waxes, resins, tannins, alkaloids, amber, cork, soaps, perfumes, rubber, lubricants, plastics, and inks. Renewable fuels include firewood, peat, and other biofuels; fossil fuels derive from the remains of aquatic organisms including phytoplankton. Wood is used for buildings, boats, and furniture, and is pulped to make paper. Cloth is made from cotton, flax, ramie, or rayon derived from plant cellulose. Thousands of plant species are cultivated for beauty, shade, and other purposes, supporting a multibillion-dollar tourism industry. In science, breeding pea plants allowed Gregor Mendel to derive the basic laws of inheritance, and examination of chromosomes in maize allowed Barbara McClintock to demonstrate their connection to inherited traits. *Arabidopsis thaliana* is used as a model organism. Tree rings provide dating in archaeology and a record of past climates. Plants appear in mythology, religion, and literature, including the world tree motif found in Indo-European, Siberian, and Native American religions. Negative effects include weeds, invasive species, windblown pollen causing hay fever, and toxins harmful to humans and livestock.

## Terms

- **Chloroplast**: Organelle containing chlorophyll, derived from a symbiosis of a non-photosynthetic cell and photosynthetic cyanobacteria.
- **Viridiplantae**: The clade of green plants, consisting of green algae and embryophytes (land plants).
- **Embryophytes**: Land plants, including hornworts, liverworts, mosses, lycophytes, ferns, conifers, and flowering plants.
- **Sporophyte**: The diploid generation (2 sets of chromosomes) in alternation of generations.
- **Gametophyte**: The haploid generation (1 set of chromosomes) in alternation of generations.
- **Mycorrhiza**: Mutualistic symbiosis between fungi and plant roots, where fungi help plants gain water and minerals in exchange for carbohydrates.
- **Epiphyte**: A plant that grows on another plant, usually a tree, without parasitising it.
- **Alternation of generations**: Complex lifecycle involving two generations, the diploid sporophyte and haploid gametophyte.
- **Xylem and phloem**: Specialised vascular tissues for transporting water and synthesised molecules.
- **Economic botany/ethnobotany**: The study of plant uses by people.

## Debates and open questions

The placing of algal groups in the 2019 phylogeny is supported by phylogenies based on genomes from the Mesostigmatophyceae and Chlorokybophyceae. Both "chlorophyte algae" and "streptophyte algae" are treated as paraphyletic in this analysis, as land plants arose from within those groups. The classification of Bryophyta is supported both by Puttick et al. 2018 and by phylogenies involving hornwort genomes. The rapid adaptive radiation of flowering plants in the Cretaceous remains what Charles Darwin called "an abominable mystery."
