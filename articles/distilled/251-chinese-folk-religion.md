# Chinese folk religion

## Overview

Chinese folk religion is the indigenous, syncretic system of beliefs and practices of the Han Chinese, encompassing veneration of *shen* (spirits/gods), ancestors, and local deities. It lacks a single founder, canonical scripture, or centralized institution, functioning instead as a "diffused religion" embedded in family, kinship, and community life. By the Song dynasty (960–1279), it had blended with Buddhist, Confucian, and Taoist teachings into a popular system persisting today. The Chinese state historically regulated cults, suppressed "feudal superstition" in the early 20th century, and devastated practice during the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976). Since 1978, a massive revival has occurred, with the government now recognizing folk religion as cultural heritage and allowing temples to register as "folk belief activity sites." Surveys estimate 55–81% of China’s population (roughly 578 million to 1 billion people) participate in these practices.

## Timeline

- **Prehistory** — Ancestor veneration established as core practice; ancestors seen as intermediaries between heaven and earth.
- **Han dynasty (202 BCE – 220 CE)** — Formation of *shè* (community altars); term *Shendao* ("Way of the Gods") used to distinguish indigenous religion from Buddhism.
- **3rd–6th century** — Indian Buddhist influences penetrate folk religion; cult of Ganesha attested (531 CE); processional rituals adopted.
- **Song dynasty (960–1279)** — Folk practices fully syncretized with Buddhism, Confucianism, and Taoism into a stable popular religious system.
- **1368 (Ming)** — Hongwu Emperor systematizes indigenous cults using the term *Shendao*.
- **1898** — Kang Youwei persuades Emperor to confiscate non-state temples for schools (shortly reversed).
- **1911** — Xinhai Revolution; most temples destroyed or repurposed as schools.
- **1928** — Nationalist government issues "Standards for retaining or abolishing gods and shrines," permitting only culture heroes.
- **1949** — Communist victory; Land Reform seizes temple land; anti-superstition campaigns destroy temples in 1950s.
- **1966–1976** — Cultural Revolution: systematic eradication of folk religion in mainland China; preserved in Taiwan under Chiang Kai-shek.
- **1978** — Reform era begins; rapid revival with millions of temples rebuilt; women lead fundraising.
- **1990s–2000s** — Peak temple reconstruction; local governments support heritage preservation (e.g., Mazuism, Yellow Emperor worship).
- **2010s–present** — Xi Jinping administration encourages temple registration as "folk belief activity sites"; state views folk religion as traditional culture.

## Body

### Diversity and Unity
Chinese folk religion is highly localized, varying by village and province, yet unified by a common theological core: *Tian* (Heaven, transcendent moral source), *qi* (vital energy/matter), *jingzu* (ancestor veneration), and *bao ying* (moral reciprocity). It is "diffused" into secular institutions—family, lineage, village—rather than organized as a separate "church." Participation requires no conversion or creedal belief, only "belonging" to a community (kinship, village, or deity association) and performing its rituals. Sociologists describe this as "immanent transcendence": the sacred is accessed through concrete human relationships and communal morality.

### Terminology and Classification
Historically, Chinese lacked a word for "religion" (*zongjiao*), a term adopted c. 1900 initially for Christianity. The state recognizes five religions (Buddhism, Taoism, Catholicism, Protestantism, Islam) but classifies folk religion as "cultural heritage" due to its ambiguous boundaries. Scholars debate nomenclature: *minjian zongjiao* (folk religion) technically refers to organized sects; *minjian xinyang* (folk beliefs) was a Japanese colonial term in Taiwan. Proposed formal names include "Chinese native religion" (*minsu zongjiao*), "Chinese ethnic religion" (*minzu zongjiao*), or "Shenism" (*shenjiao*). The term *Shendao* (Way of the Gods) appears in the *I Ching*; the Hongwu Emperor (14th c.) used it to systematize state cults. Jan Jakob Maria de Groot coined "Chinese Universism" for the metaphysical unity of heaven, earth, and man.

### History: Imperial China to Modern Revival
**Imperial Era:** By the Han, worship centered on *shè* (earth god altars). From the 3rd century, Indian Buddhism introduced processions and deities like Ganesha. Late imperial religion centered on territorial cults (community protector gods) administered by local notables (*toushou*), with women excluded from certain rites.
**Suppression (19th–Mid-20th C.):** The Taiping and Boxer Rebellions destroyed temples. Reformers (1898, 1904) confiscated temple property for schools. The Nationalist 1928 decree abolished all cults except those to culture heroes (Yellow Emperor, Guan Yu, Mazu, Confucius, etc.). Communists continued suppression: Land Reform (1950s) seized land; the Cultural Revolution (1966–76) was the "most serious and last systematic effort to destroy" the religion.
**Revival (1978–Present):** Post-Mao, millions of temples were rebuilt. The 1980s policy of *wu wei* (benign neglect) toward rural life allowed resurgence. Women frequently led reconstruction. By the 2010s, the state shifted to active support, registering temples as "folk belief activity sites" and promoting Mazuism, Yellow Emperor worship, and the Sanyi teaching as cultural heritage.

### Core Theology and Cosmology
**Tian, Qi, and Li:** *Tian* is both physical heaven and moral authority (Mandate of Heaven). *Qi* is the "psychophysical stuff" constituting all things—matter, life, thought, gods. *Li* (pattern/order), developed by Neo-Confucians (Zhu Xi), is the principle through which *qi* structures itself via *yin-yang* polarity. Taoism identifies *Tao* with both *Tian* and *li*.
**Yin, Yang, Shen, Gui:** *Yin* (dense, sinking, contracting) and *Yang* (light, rising, expanding) are modes of *qi*. *Shen* (spirits/gods) are *yang* forces of growth/extension; *Gui* (ghosts) are *yin* forces of contraction/chaos. *Shen* include nature spirits, deified humans (Guan Yu), household gods, and ancestors. Neo-Confucians rationalized them as *li*’s operations (Zhu Xi) or *qi*’s traces (Cheng Yi).
**Hun, Po, and Ancestors:** The human soul splits into *hun* (yang, rational, heavenly, perpetual) and *po* (yin, animal, earthly, dissipates at death). Ancestral worship (*jingzu*) feeds the *hun*; neglected ancestors become *gui*. Culture heroes (lineage founders, inventors) become *xian* (immortals). Humans are the "Three Powers" (*Sancai*) medium completing creation between Heaven and Earth.
**Bao Ying, Ming Yun, Yuan Fen, Wu:** *Bao ying* (moral reciprocity) ensures actions reap cosmic rewards/punishments. *Ming yun* (personal destiny) is fixed by *Tian* but flexible via individual choice in *bao ying*. *Yuan fen* (fateful coincidence) attributes chance to deeper rhythm, reducing guilt/pride. *Wu* (awareness) of these mechanisms creates responsibility to align with cosmic order.
**Ling and Xianling:** *Ling* (holy/numinous) is the medium of *yin-yang*, the inchoate order of creation. Entities crossing categories (albinos, suicide victims) are numinous. *Xianling* (divine efficacy) is a god’s manifested power (*ling qi*), proven by miracles (cures, omens). Temples accumulate *ling qi* over time. The relationship is transactional: sincere devotion (*cheng xin*) elicits divine efficacy, repaid by vows (*huan yuan*).

### Sociological Typology
**Deity Worship (North):** *Shenshe* (god societies) worship tutelary gods of place/nature (Thunder God, Dragon King) or national ancestors (Yellow Emperor). Villages often named after temples (e.g., *Leishenmiao*). Festivals (*miaohui*) involve processions, operas, and pilgrimages to "summon blessings, drive away harm." Prevalent in North China where lineage is weak.
**Lineage Religion (South):** *Zongzu xiehui* (kinship associations) worship deified ancestors in ancestral halls (*zongci*). Strength of lineage bonds correlates with temple grandeur. Prevails in South China where corporate lineage resources are shared equally.
**Ritual Specialists:**
*   *Wuism/Shamanism:* *Wu* (shamans) control spirits to repair nature’s dysfunction; historically political (Ministry of Rites). Revived since 1980s. *Nuo* is an exorcistic system in central-south China.
*   *Confucian Liturgies:* Elders (*lisheng*) lead "orthoprax" rites in temples, alternating with Taoist rites. Confucian churches (e.g., Yidan xuetang) and a national Church of Confucius (2015) exist.
*   *Taoist Orders:* *Zhengyi* priests (*sanju daoshi*) perform rituals (offering, exorcism) for communities; incorporate local gods. *Fashi* (ritual masters/"Red Taoism") operate outside official Taoism, "filling" (*kataphatic*) vs. Taoist "emptying" (*kenotic*).
**Organized Sects ("Salvationist/Redemptive Societies"):** Egalitarian, charismatic-founder groups (Yiguandao, Xiantiandao, De teaching, Tiandi teachings, Weixinism) with millenarian eschatology, healing, cultivation, and philanthropy. Banned in Republican and Communist eras; some now cooperate with mainland academics. *Sanyi* teaching (16th c., Fujian) is legally recognized. Martial sects (e.g., Meihuaism) combine secret doctrine (*wenchang*) with public body cultivation (*wuchang*).

### Texts
No single canon. Heritage includes Confucian classics (Four Books/Five Classics), Taoist canon (*Daodejing*, *Daozang*), Mohist/legalist texts, and Han-dynasty cosmologies (*Huainanzi*, Dong Zhongshu’s *Interactions Between Heaven and Mankind*). Vernacular *baojuan* (precious scrolls) record sectarian mythology. Recent finds (Guodian texts, 1990s; *Huangdi sijing*, 1970s) blur Confucian/Taoist lines. Mythology: *Classic of Mountains and Seas*, *Investiture of the Gods*, *Journey to the West*. Texts were recited/heard for efficacy; writing never fully replaced oral power.

### Places of Worship
*   *Miao* (shrine/sacred enclosure): general term for deity temples.
*   *Ci / Zongci* (ancestral shrine): lineage worship.
*   *Gong* (palace): highest gods (Queen of Heaven).
*   *Guàn / Daoguan*: Taoist monasteries (state-registered).
*   *Tang* (hall): folk sect worship.
*   *Jing*: god’s territory marked by processions.
*   *Fenxiang* (incense division): hierarchical temple networks; new temples receive ashes from senior temple (*zumiao*), creating pilgrimage routes.
*   *Miaohui* (temple gatherings): collective festivals (New Year, god’s birthday) with rituals, operas, processions. North: week-long, massive; South: smaller, village-based.

### Demographics and Economy
**Mainland/Taiwan:** Yang & Hu (2012): 55.5% adults (578M) practice folk religion (20% communal/ancestor, rest individual). Dean: 680M (51%). CFPS (2012): 81% (1B) worship gods/ancestors; 12.6% in state religions; 6.3% atheist; 2.2% (30M) in folk sects. Taiwan: 42.7% self-identified folk believers; 88% engage in ancestor/god worship.
**Economy:** Temples function as "embedded capitalism" (Mayfair Yang): circulating wealth into "sacred capital" (temple construction, festivals), fostering kinship ethics of generosity. In Pearl River Delta, economic development correlated with folk revival, contradicting Weberian disenchantment.
**Overseas:** Maintained in Southeast Asia with "re-territorialized" deities linked to dialect associations (Hainanese, Fujianese). Mazu (Queen of Heaven/Sea) is paramount. Sects (Deism, Yiguandao) spread successfully.

## Terms

- ****Shen (神)**** — Spirits, gods; forces of growth/extension (*yang*); includes nature spirits, deified humans, ancestors.
- ****Qi (氣)**** — Vital breath/energy/matter; the "psychophysical stuff" constituting all things (inanimate, living, thought, gods).
- ****Tian (天)**** — Heaven; physical sky and transcendent moral source; grants/withdraws Mandate of Heaven based on virtue.
- ****Bao ying (報應)**** — Moral reciprocity/retribution; cosmic mechanism rewarding good and punishing evil according to action's moral quality.
- ****Ming yun (命運)**** — Personal destiny; *ming* (given life/status from *Tian*) + *yun* (circumstance/choice); fixed yet flexible via *bao ying*.
- ****Yuan fen (緣分)**** — Fateful coincidence; karmic affinity linking chance events to deeper rhythm, reducing guilt/pride, preserving harmony.
- ****Ling (靈)**** — Holy, numinous; the medium of *yin-yang* polarity; inchoate order of creation; manifests as *xianling* (divine efficacy).
- ****Xian (仙)**** — Immortals/transcendents; exalted beings (culture heroes, cultivated humans) whose *qi* nexus persists after death via worship.
- ****Fenxiang (分香)**** — "Incense division"; ritual founding of a new temple using ashes from a senior temple, creating hierarchical networks/pilgrimage routes.
- ****Miaohui (廟會)**** — Temple gatherings/festivals; collective rituals (processions, operas, offerings) to greet gods on birthdays/holidays.

## Debates and Open Questions

*   **Definition and Boundaries:** Scholars dispute whether "Chinese folk religion" is a unified "national religion" (like Hinduism/Shinto) comparable to *Hindu Dharma*, or an analytical category covering distinct "folk beliefs" (*minjian xinyang*) and organized "folk religion" (*minjian zongjiao*). The state’s refusal to classify it as a "religion" (*zongjiao*) complicates legal status.
*   **Shamanism’s Role:** The extent of shamanism in ancient China is disputed (Paul R. Goldin), though its continuity in folk religion and Taoism is acknowledged. Confucianism’s derivation from Shang shamanic discourse (Andreea Chirita) vs. its marginalization of "dysfunctional" shamanism is debated.
*   **Ritual Master Identity:** *Fashi* (ritual masters/"Red Taoism") operate identically to *Zhengyi* priests but are denied the title "Taoist" by the state Taoist Church and Celestial Master lineages. The distinction between "kataphatic" (filling) folk ritual and "kenotic" (emptying) official Taoism (Kristofer Schipper) remains a taxonomic tension.
*   **Sinicization vs. Autonomy:** The "Taoisation" of minority religions (Tujia, Yi, Yao, Zhuang) is debated as cultural re-orientation vs. elimination. Recent state support for non-Taoist indigenous priesthoods (e.g., Yi *bimo*) suggests a shift from assimilation to preservation.
*   **Demographic Accuracy:** Surveys vary wildly (55% to 81% participation) due to definitions of "belief" vs. "practice," overlap with Buddhism/Taoism, and the "diffused" nature of the religion (no membership rolls).
*   **Sect Legalization:** "Salvationist" sects (Yiguandao, Tiandi, Weixinism) remain largely illegal/underground in mainland China despite some academic cooperation. The criteria for state recognition vs. suppression (*xiejiao* designation) are opaque and politically contingent.
