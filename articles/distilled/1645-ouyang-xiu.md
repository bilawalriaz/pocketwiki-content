# Ouyang Xiu

## Overview
Ouyang Xiu (1007–1072 CE) was a polymath of the Northern Song dynasty—historian, calligrapher, epigrapher, poet, essayist, and statesman—widely regarded as the central figure of the Eight Masters of the Tang and Song. He revived the Classical Prose Movement, championing the unadorned "ancient prose" of Han Yu against the prevailing ornate *pianwen* style, and institutionalized it in the imperial examinations, shaping the literary trajectory of later masters such as Su Shi. As a historian, he led the compilation of the official *New Book of Tang* (completed 1060) and privately authored the *Historical Records of the Five Dynasties*, the only work in the Twenty-Four Histories written by a single individual. Politically, he was a principal architect of the Qingli Reforms (1043–1044), a ten-point platform addressing examination standards, bureaucratic favoritism, and official salaries; its collapse led to his repeated demotions. In his final decade, he became the most prominent conservative opponent of Wang Anshi’s New Policies, retiring in 1071 after refusing to implement reforms he deemed harmful.

## Body

### Early Life and Intellectual Formation
Ouyang Xiu was born in Sichuan to a judge of modest lineage from Luling, Jiangxi. Orphaned at three, he was educated by his literate mother, whose practice of teaching him by writing characters in sand with a reed stalk became the celebrated *chengyu* **畫荻教子** (*huadi jiaozi*, "a mother's admirable dedication to the education of her children"). Too poor for formal tutoring, he was largely self-taught, finding his stylistic lodestar in the Tang essayist **Han Yu**, the original advocate of the Classical Prose Movement. He passed the *jinshi* degree on his third try in 1030 at age 22.

### Official Career and the Qingli Reforms
His first post was a judgeship in Luoyang, where he gathered a circle devoted to Han Yu’s "ancient prose." In 1034 he became a text collator at the Imperial Academy in Kaifeng, aligning with Prefect **Fan Zhongyan**. When Fan was demoted for criticizing the chief councillor, Ouyang defended him and was likewise exiled. The Liao and Xi Xia threats of 1040 recalled Fan to power; Ouyang declined a secretarial post, preferring a catalogue role at the Imperial Library. In 1043, at the reform movement’s zenith, Ouyang and Fan launched the **Qingli Reforms**—a ten-point platform including meritocratic examinations, anti-nepotism measures, and salary increases. A "Minor Reform" was briefly enacted in 1043, but conservative opposition forced the emperor to rescind it; Fan fell, and Ouyang was sent to the provinces. He returned to court in 1049 but served a mandatory mourning leave (1052–1054) for his mother.

### Historian and Epigrapher
Upon returning, Ouyang headed the Hanlin Academy commission compiling the **New Book of Tang**, finished in 1060. Privately, he wrote the **Historical Records of the Five Dynasties**, the sole single-author volume in the Twenty-Four Histories. Critics have charged him with excessive didacticism, yet he pioneered **epigraphy**—the study of inscriptions on stone and bronze—as a rigorous historiographic method, integrating it with calligraphy and connoisseurship into a Confucian-Daoist practice of self-cultivation recorded in his *Record of the Eastern Study*. His 1044 essay *On Factions* argued that like-minded political coalitions (*pengdang*) were constructive, not subversive.

### Prose Mastery and the Classical Prose Movement
Ouyang made "ancient prose" (*guwen*) a public cause, founding a Luoyang society to promote it and earning a place among the Eight Masters of the Tang and Song. His prose ranges from literary criticism and political memorials to botanical studies. The masterpiece **Zuiwengting Ji** (*An Account of the Old Toper’s Pavilion*), named for his art name **Zuiweng** ("Old Drunkard"), describes his pastoral life near Chuzhou. Early commentators read it as *fengyue* (aesthetic enjoyment of nature); Qing critics discerned beneath the playfulness a sincere Confucian joy in the people’s welfare. A famous anecdote contrasts his concise narrative—"A galloping horse killed a dog in its path"—with his juniors’ wordier versions, illustrating his ideal of direct, unadorned expression.

### Poetry: *Shi* and *Ci*
Ouyang wrote both **shi** (regulated verse) and **ci** (lyric verse set to tunes). His *shi* stripped away late-Tang ornamentation, returning to early-Tang austerity. He is best known for *ci*, especially the ten-poem series *West Lake Is Good* (to the tune *Picking Mulberries*), which helped legitimize *ci* as a vehicle for serious poetry. Mature poems (1050s) introduced unprecedented themes: friendship, family, food, antiques, and politics. He employed prose-derived techniques—self-caricature, exaggeration, and elaborate rhyme-game structures—exemplifying the literary playfulness characteristic of Northern Song poetry.

### Final Years: Opposition to the New Policies
With Emperor Shenzong’s accession (1067), Ouyang—now Assistant Chief Councillor, Hanlin Academician, Vice Commissioner of Military Affairs, and Vice Minister of Revenues—faced a politically motivated investigation alleging impropriety with his daughter-in-law. Though baseless, the inquiry tarnished him. Denied retirement, he was dispatched as a magistrate to Shandong and Anhui. There he openly refused to enforce Wang Anshi’s **Green Sprouts Law** (low-interest loans to farmers), the flagship of the New Policies. He had once patronized Wang’s early career but came to view the reforms as fiscally reckless and socially disruptive. He was finally allowed to retire in 1071 and died the following year.

### Legacy
Even Wang Anshi eulogized him as the greatest literary figure of the age. Ming scholar Li Dongyang held him as the paragon of the scholar-official uniting public service and literary art, praising his "tranquility and propriety." Ouyang’s *guwen* standard dominated the examinations for centuries, and his historical methodology—especially epigraphy—remains foundational.

## Terms
- ****Classical Prose Movement (古文運動)**** — A literary reform begun by Han Yu and Liu Zongyuan in the Tang, revived by Ouyang Xiu in the Song, advocating clear, direct "ancient prose" (*guwen*) over the parallel, ornamental *pianwen* style.
- ****Eight Masters of the Tang and Song (唐宋八大家)**** — Canonical group of eight prose masters—Han Yu, Liu Zongyuan, Ouyang Xiu, Su Xun, Su Shi, Su Zhe, Wang Anshi, Zeng Gong—who defined the *guwen* tradition.
- ****Qingli Reforms (慶曆新政)**** — A 1043–1044 ten-point reform program led by Fan Zhongyan and Ouyang Xiu targeting examination content, bureaucratic nepotism, and official salaries; partially implemented as the "Minor Reform" before being rescinded.
- ****New Book of Tang (新唐書)**** — Official dynastic history of the Tang, compiled under Ouyang Xiu’s leadership and completed in 1060; one of the Twenty-Four Histories.
- ****Historical Records of the Five Dynasties (五代史記)**** — Private history of the Five Dynasties period (907–960) written solely by Ouyang Xiu; the only single-author work in the Twenty-Four Histories.
- ****Epigraphy (金石學)**** — The study of inscriptions on metal and stone; Ouyang Xiu established it as a critical historiographic tool for verifying textual records.
- ****Ci (詞)**** — Lyric poetry written to fixed musical tunes; Ouyang Xiu elevated it from popular entertainment to a serious literary form.
- ****Shi (詩)**** — Classical Chinese poetry in regulated or ancient styles; Ouyang’s *shi* favored early-Tang simplicity over late-Tang ornament.
- ****Zuiwengting Ji (醉翁亭記)**** — Ouyang’s most famous prose essay, "An Account of the Old Toper’s Pavilion," blending landscape description with Confucian joy in the people’s welfare.
- ****Green Sprouts Law (青苗法)**** — Wang Anshi’s flagship New Policy providing low-interest state loans to farmers; Ouyang Xiu refused to implement it as a provincial magistrate.

## Debates and open questions
- **Historical methodology**: Scholars debate whether Ouyang’s didactic, moral-judgment approach in the *New Book of Tang* and *Five Dynasties* history compromises its factual reliability, or whether his pioneering epigraphy offsets this bias.
- **Political consistency**: His early patronage of Wang Anshi versus his later vehement opposition raises questions about whether his stance shifted on principle (fiscal conservatism, anti-centralization) or reflected factional dynamics.
- **Zuiwengting Ji interpretation**: The essay’s tone oscillates between Daoist reclusion (*fengyue*) and Confucian public engagement; the balance remains contested from Qing commentators to modern scholars.
- **Authorship of *Five Dynasties* history**: The work was discovered posthumously; its relationship to the official *New History of the Five Dynasties* (which Ouyang also supervised) complicates textual transmission studies.
- **Charges of 1067**: The sexual-misconduct investigation is universally regarded as a political frame-up, but its exact orchestrators and the extent of Emperor Shenzong’s complicity are unclear.
