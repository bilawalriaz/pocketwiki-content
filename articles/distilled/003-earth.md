# Earth

## Overview

Earth is the third planet from the Sun and the only astronomical object known to harbor life, made possible by its status as an ocean world—the only one in the Solar System sustaining liquid surface water. Its dynamic atmosphere, magnetic field, and plate tectonics create conditions for a global climate system and a biosphere that has altered the planet itself over billions of years. Humans emerged 300,000 years ago and now depend on Earth's biosphere, but humanity's current impact on climate and biosphere is unsustainable, threatening livelihoods and causing widespread extinctions.

## Body

### Etymology

The Modern English word *Earth* developed from Old English *eorðe*, with cognates in every Germanic language (reconstructed as *erþō*). It translated Latin *terra* and Greek *gē*: ground, soil, dry land, the human world, and the globe itself. Late Norse mythology included Jörð ('Earth'), a giantess often given as Thor's mother. Historically lowercase, "the earth" gained capital "E" during Early Modern English, especially alongside other heavenly bodies. The name *Terra* appears in scientific writing and science fiction; *Tellus* appears in poetry. Adjectives include *terrestrial*, *terran*, *terrene*, *tellurian*, and *telluric*.

### Natural history

Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago from gas and dust in the early Solar System via nebular theory: a solar nebula collapsed gravitationally, spun into a circumstellar disk, and planetesimals accreted over an estimated 70–100 million years. The leading hypothesis for the Moon's origin is the giant-impact hypothesis: a Mars-sized object named Theia (about 10% of Earth's mass) collided with Earth, and some of its mass merged with Earth. Between approximately 4.0 and 3.8 Ga, the Late Heavy Bombardment caused significant surface changes.

Earth's atmosphere and oceans formed by volcanic activity and outgassing; water vapor condensed into oceans, augmented by water from asteroids, protoplanets, and comets. Greenhouse gases kept oceans from freezing when the young Sun had only 70% of its current luminosity. The first solid crust was mafic (rich in magnesium and iron); the first continental crust was more felsic (silica-rich), formed by partial melting. Two models explain continental crust growth: steady growth (supported by radiometric dating) versus rapid early growth in the Archean (supported by hafnium and neodymium isotopes); these can be reconciled by large-scale crustal recycling. Plate tectonics, driven by heat loss from Earth's interior, formed supercontinents: Rodinia broke apart ~750 Ma, Pannotia formed 600–540 Ma, and Pangaea broke apart 180 Ma. The most recent ice-age pattern began ~40 Ma, intensifying ~3 Ma, with glaciation cycles every ~21,000, 41,000, and 100,000 years.

Chemical reactions produced first self-replicating molecules ~4 billion years ago; the last common ancestor arose a half-billion years later. Photosynthesis allowed direct harvesting of solar energy; resulting oxygen formed the protective ozone layer. Eukaryotes (complex cells) developed from incorporation of smaller cells within larger ones. The Neoproterozoic "Snowball Earth" hypothesis suggests much of Earth may have been ice-covered, preceding the Cambrian explosion (535 Ma), when multicellular life greatly increased in complexity. An African ape species gained upright posture several million years ago, facilitating tool use and larger brains, leading to humans.

Earth's long-term future ties to the Sun: solar luminosity will increase 10% in 1.1 billion years and 40% in 3.5 billion years. CO2 levels may drop lethally low for plants in 100–900 million years; mean temperature may reach 100 °C in 1.5 billion years, with oceans evaporating within 1.6–3 billion years. The Sun will become a red giant in ~5 billion years, expanding to ~1 AU; Earth's fate is unclear—it may move to a 1.7 AU orbit or be vaporized, though a 2026 study suggests survival.

### Composition and structure

Earth is the most massive rocky Solar System object (81 times the Moon's mass) and the densest planetary-mass object. Its mass is ~5.97×10²⁴ kg, composed mostly of iron (32.1%), oxygen (30.1%), silicon (15.1%), and magnesium (13.9%). The core is primarily iron (88.8%) with nickel (5.8%) and sulfur (4.5%). The interior divides into layers: a silicate crust (6 km under oceans, 30–50 km under continents), a viscous mantle, a liquid outer core, and a solid inner core (radius about one-fifth of Earth's). The lithosphere (crust plus rigid upper mantle) rides on the asthenosphere, a low-viscosity layer. Internal heat comes from primordial heat and radioactive decay (potassium-40, uranium-238, thorium-232); the core may reach 6,000 °C and 360 GPa. Mean heat loss is 87 mW/m².

The lithosphere divides into tectonic plates moving at convergent, divergent, or transform boundaries, producing earthquakes, volcanoes, and mountain-building. Oceanic crust is subducted and recycled; most ocean floor is less than 100 Ma old, while the oldest continental crust dates to 4,030 Ma. The seven major plates are Pacific, North American, Eurasian, African, Antarctic, Indo-Australian, and South American. Oceanic crust is basaltic; continental crust contains granite and sediments. Earth's surface (510 million km²) is 70.8% ocean and 29.2% land. The pedosphere (soil layer) supports arable land (10.7% of land surface). Elevation ranges from −418 m (Dead Sea) to 8,848 m (Mount Everest). Earth's rotation makes it an ellipsoid, bulging 43 km at the equator; the geoid provides a reference mean sea level.

### Gravity and magnetic field

Earth's magnetic field is generated by a dynamo process in the core, converting convection kinetic energy into electrical and magnetic energy. The field is approximately a dipole near the geographic poles, with surface strength 3.05×10⁻⁵ T at the equator. The magnetic poles drift and periodically reverse; the most recent reversal was ~700,000 years ago. The magnetosphere deflects solar wind; the day-side is compressed to ~10 Earth radii, and the night-side extends into a long tail. The Van Allen radiation belts contain high-energy particles; magnetic storms can cause auroras. Surface gravity is ~9.8 m/s². Earth's Hill sphere (gravitational influence) is ~1.5 million km in radius.

### Moon and orbital space

The Moon is about one-quarter Earth's diameter, the largest moon relative to its planet in the Solar System. Earth and Moon orbit a common barycenter every 27.32 days (sidereal); the synodic month is 29.53 days. The Moon's apparent size nearly matches the Sun's (the Sun is ~400 times larger but ~400 times more distant), enabling total and annular eclipses. Tidal interaction causes lunar tidal locking (same face always toward Earth), lunar tides, the Moon receding ~38 mm/year, and Earth's day lengthening ~23 μs/year. During the Ediacaran (~620 Ma), a year had 400±7 days of 21.9±0.4 hours. The Moon may stabilize Earth's axial tilt, though this is disputed. Earth also has quasi-satellites (at least seven, including 469219 Kamoʻoalewa), a trojan asteroid (2010 TK7), and as of September 2021, 4,550 operational satellites plus over 16,000 pieces of tracked debris.

### Orbit and rotation

Earth's mean solar day is 86,400 seconds; its stellar day is 23h 56m 4.0989s. Earth orbits the Sun at ~150 million km (the astronomical unit, AU), completing one revolution every 365.2564 days (sidereal year) at an average speed of 29.7827 km/s. Earth's axial tilt is ~23.439281°, causing seasons: summer occurs when the Tropic of Cancer (Northern) or Capricorn (Southern) faces the Sun. The tilt undergoes nutation (18.6-year period) and precession (25,800-year cycle). The orbit is slightly elliptical: perihelion ~3 January, aphelion ~4 July, causing ~6.8% more solar energy at perihelion. Earth orbits the Milky Way's center at ~28,000 light-years distance.

### Atmosphere and climate

The atmosphere is 78.084% nitrogen, 20.946% oxygen, 0.934% argon, with trace CO2 and variable water vapor (0.01–4%, averaging ~1%). Three-quarters of its mass lies within the first 11 km (the troposphere). The greenhouse effect—water vapor, CO2, methane, nitrous oxide, and ozone capturing thermal energy—raises average surface temperature from −18 °C (without it) to +15 °C. The ozone layer in the stratosphere blocks ultraviolet radiation. The Kármán line (100 km) defines the atmosphere-space boundary. Earth receives 1361 W/m² of solar irradiance; mean sea-level air temperature decreases ~0.4 °C per degree of latitude. Climate zones include tropical, subtropical, temperate, and polar; the Köppen classification has five broad groups. The water cycle—evaporation, cloud formation, precipitation—supports land life and drives erosion. Clouds cover about two-thirds of Earth's surface.

### Hydrosphere

The hydrosphere includes the global ocean, atmospheric water, lakes, rivers, and groundwater. Ocean mass is ~1.35×10¹⁸ metric tons, covering 361.8 million km² with mean depth 3,682 m. About 97.5% of water is saline; 68.7% of fresh water is ice in caps and glaciers. Glaciers form from accumulated snow and flow under their own gravity, eroding U-shaped valleys. Average ocean salinity is ~35 g/kg. The oceans act as a large heat reservoir; shifts like El Niño–Southern Oscillation cause significant weather changes. Earth's stable liquid surface water is unique among Solar System planets.

### Biosphere

Life developed in early bodies of water roughly 4 billion years ago. Earth provides liquid water for organic molecules to assemble and sufficient energy for metabolism. Life has shaped Earth's atmosphere and surface, causing the Great Oxidation Event. Biodiversity and biomass peak in shallow waters and equatorial forests; polar regions, high altitudes, and arid areas are relatively barren. Extreme weather caused an average of 11,800 human deaths per year from 1980 to 2000. Human activities cause pollution, acid rain, deforestation, species extinction, and greenhouse gas emissions driving global warming.

### Human geography

Humans originated in Eastern Africa 300,000 years ago; agriculture began in the 10th millennium BCE. Population grew exponentially since the 19th century to eight billion in the 2020s, projected to peak ~10 billion in the late 21st century. Ninety percent inhabit the Northern Hemisphere; most live in urban areas by the 21st century. Earth's land is divided among 205 sovereign states, most forming the United Nations. Non-renewable resources include fossil fuels (coal, petroleum, natural gas) and mineral ores. In 2019, 39 million km² was forest, 40 million km² used for grazing, and 11 million km² cultivated as cropland. Global temperatures in 2020 were 1.2 °C warmer than preindustrial baseline. Of nine planetary boundaries, five have been crossed: biosphere integrity, climate change, chemical pollution, destruction of wild habitats, and the nitrogen cycle.

### In culture

Earth is personified as a mother goddess in many cultures; creation myths involve supernatural deities. The Gaia hypothesis (mid-20th century) views Earth's environments and life as a single self-regulating organism. Ancient Greeks (Pythagoras, Parmenides) proposed a spherical Earth; geocentrism persisted until the 16th century. Lord Kelvin estimated Earth's age at 20–400 million years in 1864; radioactivity and radioactive dating later proved billions of years. Space images like *Earthrise* and *The Blue Marble* altered human perception, emphasizing Earth's beauty and fragility—the "overview effect"—and spurred global environmental action.

## Terms

- **Asthenosphere**: Low-viscosity upper-mantle layer on which tectonic plates ride
- **Biosphere**: The global sum of all ecosystems; the zone of life on Earth
- **Eukaryotes**: Complex cells formed by incorporation of smaller cells within larger ones
- **Ga/Gya**: Giga-annum/billion years ago
- **Geoid**: Idealized Earth shape from a smooth, undisturbed global ocean, used as reference for topography
- **Greenhouse effect**: Trace atmospheric gases capturing thermal energy, raising surface temperature
- **Hill sphere**: Radius (~1.5 million km for Earth) within which Earth's gravity dominates over the Sun's
- **Lithosphere**: Earth's rigid outer layer (crust plus upper mantle), divided into tectonic plates
- **Magnetosphere**: Region of space where Earth's magnetic field deflects solar wind
- **Tidal locking**: When a body's rotation period equals its orbital period, keeping one face toward its partner

## Debates and open questions

- Continental crust growth: steady growth versus rapid early growth in the Archean; reconcilable via large-scale crustal recycling
- Moon's stabilizing effect on Earth's axial tilt is disputed
- Earth's fate during the Sun's red-giant phase is unclear (vaporization versus survival; a 2026 study suggests survival)
- "Snowball Earth" hypothesis: whether much of Earth was ice-covered during the Neoproterozoic
- Inner core rotation rate: proposed 0.1–0.5° per year advance, though higher and lower rates have also been proposed
