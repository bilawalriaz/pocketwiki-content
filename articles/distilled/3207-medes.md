# Medes

## Overview
The Medes were an ancient Iranian people who established one of the first major Iranian states in the 7th century BC. They played a pivotal role in the fall of the Neo-Assyrian Empire and formed a powerful kingdom that, for a time, was one of the great powers of the ancient Near East. Their history is reconstructed almost entirely from foreign sources, as they left no written records, leading to scholarly debate about the true extent of their political power.

## Timeline
- **c. 11th century BC** — Iranian tribes, including the Medes, occupy northwestern Iran.
- **9th century BC** — Medes first appear in contemporary Assyrian texts.
- **8th century BC** — Iranian tribes become the majority in the future Median territory.
- **819/818 BC** — First major Assyrian campaign into Median territory under Shamshi-Adad V.
- **c. 700–675 BC** — Reign of Deioces, the traditional founder of the Median kingdom (per Herodotus).
- **675–653 BC** — Reign of Phraortes.
- **652–624 BC** — Period of Scythian rule over Media.
- **624–585 BC** — Reign of Cyaxares, who unites the Medes and destroys the Assyrian Empire.
- **585–550 BC** — Reign of Astyages, the last Median king.
- **550 BC** — Cyrus the Great of Persia defeats Astyages, ending the Median kingdom.

## Body

### Origins and Early History
The Medes were an Iron Age Iranian people who settled in the mountainous region of northwestern Iran by the 11th century BC. Their political consolidation is believed to have occurred in the 8th century BC. Before Median dominance, the region was politically fragmented among various peoples and smaller states like Mannaea and Assyria. The Medes first appear in historical records in 9th-century BC Assyrian texts, which describe them as numerous petty chieftains (*bēl āli* or "city lords") rather than a unified kingdom.

### Assyrian Intervention and the Rise of a Kingdom
From the 9th to the late 7th centuries BC, the western parts of Media fell under the domination of the Neo-Assyrian Empire, which sought control for horses and strategic access. Early Assyrian campaigns were for plunder, but under Tiglath-Pileser III (744–727 BC) and Sargon II (721–705 BC), Assyria established provinces and permanent fortresses in the Zagros. This direct control was limited and relied on deals with local chiefs. By the late 8th century BC, tribal confederations began to emerge. The Assyrian grip weakened in the 7th century BC, allowing a paramount chief, likely Cyaxares, to unite the Medes and other peoples.

### The Median Empire and Fall
After allying with Babylon to destroy the Assyrian Empire (616–609 BC), the unified Median state became a major power, with territory stretching from northeastern Iran to Anatolia. However, the precise extent of this empire is debated. The kingdom ended when Cyrus the Great, a Persian vassal, revolted. In 550 BC, Cyrus defeated the last Median king, Astyages, whose own nobles betrayed him. Media then became a prized province within the Persian Achaemenid Empire, where Medes held a privileged status next to the Persians.

### Society and Culture
Median society was tribal and patriarchal, with polygamy permitted. Herodotus lists six tribes, including the sacred Magi priestly caste. The common population likely lived in villages, while elites resided in fortified settlements. Archaeological sites like Tepe Nush-i Jan and Godin Tepe show urban centers with religious and administrative buildings, indicating a mixed economy of agriculture, pastoralism (especially horse breeding), and crafts. Their architecture influenced later Achaemenid styles.

### Religion and Language
Median religion is poorly understood but included a priestly caste (the Magi) and likely involved fire worship, as seen at Tepe Nush-i Jan. Personal names suggest the use of the word *arta-* ("truth") and the god Ahura Mazda, indicating a form of pre-Zoroastrianism or early Zoroastrianism. The Median language was an Old Iranian tongue, but no original texts survive. It is known only from place names, personal names, and loanwords in Old Persian, suggesting it was closely related to Old Persian.

### Art and Artifacts
Median art is difficult to define, as few artifacts can be conclusively attributed to them. Archaeological finds show strong influence from Assyrian, Babylonian, and Elamite styles. Objects like cylinder seals and bronze vessels often display a hybrid style, sometimes described as "halfway Assyrian, halfway Achaemenid." Herodotus describes a magnificent palace at Ecbatana with seven concentric, colorfully painted walls, but this is unconfirmed archaeologically.

## Terms
*   **Bēl āli**: An Assyrian term meaning "city lord" or "chieftain," used to describe the petty rulers of the fragmented Median tribes before unification.
*   **Magi**: A priestly caste among the Medes (and later Persians) who served as religious advisers, interpreters of dreams, and custodians of sacred rites.
*   **Transhumance**: A seasonal movement of people and livestock between fixed summer and winter pastures, a likely practice in the mountainous Median homeland.
*   **Old Iranian**: The branch of the Indo-Iranian language family to which Median and Old Persian belonged.
*   **Neo-Assyrian Empire**: The final and most powerful phase of the Assyrian Empire (911–609 BC), which dominated the Near East and interacted extensively with the Medes.
*   **Achaemenid Empire**: The first Persian Empire (c. 550–330 BC), founded by Cyrus the Great after his conquest of Media.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Extent of the Median State**: A major scholarly debate exists over whether the Medes formed a powerful, centralized empire as described by Herodotus or remained a loose confederation of tribes. Some specialists suggest a powerful Median kingdom may never have existed.
*   **Kurdish Connection**: The proposed link between the Medes and the ancestors of the modern Kurds is contested. While some historians and Kurdish traditions assert a connection, linguistic evidence is inconclusive, and many scholars argue there is no demonstrable genetic affinity between the Median language and Kurdish.
*   **Religion**: Scholars debate whether the religion of the Magi was a form of pre-Zoroastrianism, early Zoroastrianism, or a distinct Mazdaism with common Iranian traditions.
