# Terence

## Overview

Publius Terentius Afer (c. 195/185 – c. 159 BC), known in English as Terence, was a Roman playwright of the Republican period and author of six comedies adapted from Greek originals by Menander or Apollodorus of Carystus. All six plays survive complete, making him one of the best-preserved Latin comic dramatists. His plays became standard school texts within a generation and remained central to European education until the 19th century, exercising a formative influence on later authors including Shakespeare and Molière. His traditional biography—born in Carthage, brought to Rome as a slave, freed and educated—is largely derived from ancient scholars writing long after his death and is often considered speculative.

## Timeline

- **c. 195/185 BC** — Terence born (date uncertain; Suetonius implies 184 BC, variant reading suggests 194 BC)
- **166 BC** — *Andria* produced at the Ludi Megalenses
- **165 BC** — First (abortive) production of *Hecyra* at the Ludi Megalenses
- **163 BC** — *Heauton Timorumenos* produced at the Ludi Megalenses
- **161 BC** — *Eunuchus* (Ludi Megalenses) and *Phormio* (Ludi Romani) produced
- **160 BC** — *Adelphoe* and second abortive *Hecyra* at funeral games of Aemilius Paullus; third, successful *Hecyra* at Ludi Romani

- **4th–5th c. AD** — Codex Bembinus, earliest surviving manuscript, copied
- **1470** — First printed edition of Terence appears in Strasbourg
- **1476** — First certain post-antique performance (*Andria*, Florence)

## Body

### Life and career

Terence's plays survive with *didascaliae*—production notices recording dates, occasions, and personnel of early performances, plus the Greek original's author. All six plays were originally produced by the acting company of Lucius Ambivius Turpio, with music by a *tibicen* (flute-player) named Flaccus. The traditional biography derives from the *Vita Terenti*, attributed by Aelius Donatus to Suetonius, but scholars doubt its reliability: contemporaries would likely not have considered a dramatist important enough for biography, and the narrative is often seen as conjecture based on play texts and didascaliae.

In the 2nd century BC, plays were staged at four annual Roman festivals (Ludi Romani, Ludi Plebeii, Ludi Apollinares, Ludi Megalenses), plus votive games, triumphs, and aristocratic funerals. Because the Roman calendar ran about two and a half months ahead of the Sun in the 160s, plays scheduled for April's Megalensia actually premiered in late January. There was no permanent theatre until the Theatre of Pompey (55 BC); performances used temporary wooden stages accommodating fewer than 2,000 people. Admission was free to all, with Senate seats reserved after 194 BC; audiences included women, children, slaves, and the urban poor. Whether actors wore masks is debated: an 1877 dissertation argued they did not, but most modern authorities consider masked performance "highly likely" or even "inconceivable" otherwise; Donatus states masks were worn in the original *Eunuchus* and *Adelphoe*.

### The prologues

Unlike Greek New Comedy and Plautus, Terence abandoned the expository prologue, using it instead to reply to criticism of his work. He refers to a "spiteful" older poet, identified by Donatus as Luscius Lanuvinus (no names appear in the prologues). Terence's charge is that Luscius "by translating them well and writing them badly, has made good Greek plays into Latin ones that aren't good." Nothing of Luscius survives except two lines, and Volcacius Sedigitus rated him ninth-best Latin comic poet (Terence sixth).

### Suetonian biography

According to Suetonius, Terence was born in Carthage, came to Rome as a slave in the household of Senator P. Terentius Lucanus, who educated and freed him; Terence took his *nomen* from his patron. He supposedly became a member of the "Scipionic Circle." A famous anecdote has Terence, shabbily dressed, reading his first play *Andria* to the older poet Caecilius, who invited him to dinner; its historicity is doubted (similar stories attach to Accius and Pacuvius, and Jerome's dating implies Caecilius died two years before *Andria*'s production). The *Eunuchus* earned 8,000 *nummi*—the highest price ever paid for a comedy at Rome—and was acted twice in one day; the sum may represent 8,000 sesterces or, if *nummus* means denarius, 32,000 sesterces.

Around age 25 (or 35, per some manuscripts), Terence travelled to Greece or Asia and never returned. Sources disagree on motive, destination, and whether he died of disease in Greece or by shipwreck on return. One source claims he died returning "with 108 stories dramatised by Menander"—possibly 108 new Terentian plays, though this is improbable given his slow output, and some editors delete the number as a copying error. Suetonius describes Terence as "moderate height, slender, and of dark complexion," likely inferences from his supposed African origin and verse style. He was survived by a daughter who married a Roman knight; sources conflict on whether he left 20 acres of gardens on the Appian Way or died poor.

### Name and ethnicity

His *cognomen* Afer ("the [North] African") may indicate Libyan origin, but such names did not necessarily denote origin (e.g., Domitius Afer was not African). Claims of Berber descent rest on a Latin distinction between *Afri* (Berbers) and *Poeni* (Carthaginians) that lexicographic evidence does not support for Terence's lifetime. If born a slave in Carthage, his mother might have been an Italian war captive; Carney argues he came from the Italiote Greek population enslaved by Hannibal, explaining his Greek and Latin proficiency.

### The plays

Like Plautus, Terence adapted late Attic comedy, but his style emphasized simple, conversational Latin over visual humor. Five plays concern young men in love (in *Hecyra*, one already-married man); all feature two women, one a citizen and one a prostitute. In four plays (not *Hecyra* nor *Adelphoe*), a recognition (*anagnorisis*) proves a girl is a long-lost citizen's daughter, enabling marriage. The six plays: *Andria* (166 BC), *Heauton Timorumenos* (163 BC), *Eunuchus* (161 BC), *Phormio* (161 BC), *Hecyra* (165 BC, successfully performed 160 BC), and *Adelphoe* (160 BC). Plots typically involve wily slaves, mistaken identities, threatened disinheritance, and last-minute recognitions resolving romantic entanglements.

### Ancient commentary and manuscripts

Donatus' commentary on Terence survives only in a medieval redaction, likely reconstructed from marginal notes; citations not in the extant version appear in Priscian and scholia. A second commentary by Eugraphius (otherwise unknown) also survives. The commentary is prefaced by Suetonius' *Vita*, an essay *De fabula* by Evanthius (identified via a quotation by Rufinus of Antioch), and a shorter *De comoedia* attributed to Donatus.

Manuscripts divide into two groups: the Codex Bembinus (4th–5th century AD, rustic capitals, one of the earliest surviving Latin manuscripts) and approximately 650 later "Calliopian" manuscripts (9th century onward, minuscule), named for subscriptions indicating correction by a certain Calliopius. These subdivide into classes γ and δ, deriving from lost archetypes Γ and Δ, both from a single lost archetype Σ; the Bembinus generally has a better text than Σ, which shows changes perhaps designed for school use.

### Cultural legacy

Terence became a literary "classic" by the end of the 2nd century BC. Cicero recalls school assignments from the *Andria*; by the late 4th century AD, Terence was one of four canonical school authors (with Cicero, Sallust, and Vergil), canonised by Arusianus Messius. Jerome, Augustine, and Sidonius Apollinaris all attest to school reading of his plays. Terence maintained a continuous presence in medieval literacy; his maxims (*sententiae*) circulated widely, often quoted as proverbs without attribution. Augustine quoted Terence 38 times but criticised the *Eunuchus*' rape scene as "vileness" unnecessary for learning vocabulary.

In the 10th century, Hrotsvit of Gandersheim wrote six Christian plays modeled on Terence's six, inverting his plots: her heroines triumph by resisting advances rather than being pursued, and happy endings are figurative marriages to Christ. Her influence was minimal until her rediscovery in 1501. Dante placed Terence in Limbo among virtuous pagans and showed the *Eunuchus* character Thais in hell's circle of flatterers. Renaissance humanists prized him: Boccaccio copied all six plays by hand; Erasmus called him "pure, concise, and near to everyday conversation"; Luther wrote "I love Terence," finding his comedies useful for teaching language and society. Shakespeare certainly studied Terence at grammar school; echoes appear in *The Taming of the Shrew*, *Love's Labour's Lost*, *Othello*, and *The Merry Wives of Windsor*. John Adams excerpted ~140 passages from Terence for his grandsons, though he worried about the plays' morals; his son John Quincy and grandson Charles Francis debated the relative moral weight of slaves versus citizens in the plays. Thornton Wilder based *The Woman of Andros* on *Andria*. Due to his cognomen Afer, Terence has been claimed as the first poet of the African diaspora by writers including Juan Latino, Alexandre Dumas, Langston Hughes, and Maya Angelou, though Thomas Jefferson claimed he was "of the race of whites." Questions about whether Terence received help writing—or was not the true author—were debated in antiquity (Cicero and Quintilian mention the gossip) and later (accepted by Montaigne, rejected by Diderot).

## Terms

- **Didascaliae**: Production notices recording dates, occasions, and personnel of early performances.
- **Anagnorisis**: A recognition scene proving a character's true identity, enabling marriage.
- **Tibicen**: A flute-player who provided musical accompaniment.
- **Ludi**: Public games/festivals at which plays were staged.
- **Cognomen**: A third name indicating family branch or personal characteristic; "Afer" means "the African."
- **Sententiae**: Maxims or edifying quotations, a rich source from Terence for school exercises.
- **Scipionic Circle**: A supposed group of aristocratic intellectuals around Scipio, to which Terence allegedly belonged.
- **Codex Bembinus**: The earliest surviving Terence manuscript (4th–5th century AD).
- **Calliopian manuscripts**: The ~650 later manuscripts, named for subscriptions attributing correction to a Calliopius.

## Debates and open questions

- **Biography reliability**: The Suetonian *Vita* is widely considered speculative, built from inferences from the plays and didascaliae rather than reliable records.
- **Date of birth**: Uncertain—184 BC (from Suetonius' age at death) or 194 BC (variant reading, supported by Fenestella's claim Terence was older than Scipio and Laelius).
- **Masks**: Ancient sources conflict; modern scholars mostly believe Roman actors wore masks, but the question is not settled.
- **Didascalic numbering**: The didascaliae number *Eunuchus* second and *Heauton Timorumenos* third, contradicting production dates; explanations include an unattested earlier production or numbering by composition order.
- **Death**: Sources disagree on whether Terence died of disease in Greece or by shipwreck; the voyage itself may be a speculative explanation for his small output.
- **Authorship**: Ancient and later gossip ascribed the plays to Scipio; Montaigne accepted this, Diderot rejected it.
- **Ethnicity**: Whether Afer indicates Berber, Libyan, or other origin is debated; the Berber/Carthaginian distinction may not apply in his lifetime.
