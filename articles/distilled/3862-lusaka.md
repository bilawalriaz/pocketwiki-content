# Lusaka

## Overview
Lusaka is the capital and largest city of Zambia, serving as the nation's political, economic, and transportation hub. Its development from a colonial railway stop to a major African metropolis reflects Zambia's history of colonialism, federation, independence, and economic fluctuation. The city is one of the fastest-developing in southern Africa, but faces significant challenges with informal settlements and infrastructure.

## Timeline
- **6th century AD** — Earliest evidence of settlement in the Lusaka area.
- **11th century** — First known village settlement, near modern Olympia suburb.
- **17th-18th century** — Lenje and Soli peoples arrive in the region.
- **1905** — Modern Lusaka founded as a water stop on the BSAC railway, named after Chief Lusaaka.
- **1929** — British colonial administration decides to move Northern Rhodesia's capital from Livingstone to Lusaka.
- **1935** — Lusaka formally becomes the capital of Northern Rhodesia.
- **1953** — Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland formed; Lusaka loses some status to Salisbury (Harare).
- **1964** — Zambia gains independence; Lusaka is named the national capital.
- **1969** — Lusaka hosts the Fifth Summit Conference, producing the Lusaka Manifesto.
- **2014** — National Heroes Stadium opens.
- **2022** — City population reaches approximately 2,204,059.

## Body

### Colonial Founding and Early Growth
The earliest settlement evidence dates to the 6th century AD, with the first known village from the 11th century. The Lenje and Soli peoples inhabited the area from the 17th or 18th century. The modern city was founded in 1905 by the British South African Company (BSAC) as a water stop on a railway linking the Copperbelt to Cape Town, named after a local Lenje chief. White Afrikaner farmers settled nearby, making Lusaka a regional trading centre. In 1929, the British colonial administration chose Lusaka as the new capital of Northern Rhodesia due to its central location and railway access, moving it from Livingstone. Town planner Stanley Adshead designed the city, which was built over subsequent decades with racial segregation built into its plans.

### Federation and Independence
Lusaka lost some status when Salisbury became the capital of the Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland in 1953, causing economic decline. It regained its prominence as the capital of newly independent Zambia in 1964. A large-scale building programme followed, including government buildings, the University of Zambia, and a new airport. The city became a center for anti-colonial resistance in southern Africa, hosting the 1969 conference that produced the Lusaka Manifesto, a pledge of solidarity for majority rule.

### Post-Independence Challenges and Growth
Zambia's economy suffered from the 1970s due to falling copper prices and rising oil prices, leading to a decline in formal employment in Lusaka. Despite this, the city's population grew rapidly due to migration from the Copperbelt and natural increase, reaching around 4% annually in the 1990s. A lack of formal housing led to the proliferation of unplanned shanty towns. The economy grew in the 2000s, prompting urban development plans, but inequality and underinvestment in housing persist, with 70% of residents living in unplanned settlements as of 2015.

### Geography and Cityscape
Lusaka sits on a high-altitude plateau at about 1,280 metres. Its geology features limestone and schist, leading to underground karsts and a lack of major rivers. The central business district (CBD) is centered on Cairo Road. The city is divided into a wealthy eastern sector (e.g., Woodlands, Ibex Hill) and western and southern fringes dominated by informal settlements. Major shopping malls are located along Great East Road.

### Demographics and Languages
The city is ethnically diverse due to nationwide migration and the government's "One Zambia, One Nation" policy. English is the official language, used in government and education. The local hybrid language, Town Nyanja, is based on Nyanja but incorporates vocabulary from English, Bemba, and Nsenga.

### Economy and Transport
Lusaka is Zambia's economic hub, dominated by the service sector, finance, and trade. It is a major transport crossroads, served by Kenneth Kaunda International Airport and the Zambia Railways main line. The city is at the junction of three major highways: the T2 (Great North Road), Great East Road (T4), and Mongu Road (M9). Public transport is primarily provided by privately operated minibuses.

### Healthcare and Education
The city hosts two of Zambia's five national tertiary hospitals: the University Teaching Hospital (UTH), the country's principal medical training center, and Chainama Hills, the only psychiatric hospital. The University of Zambia, established in 1965, is the nation's oldest and largest institution of higher learning.

## Terms
- **BSAC (British South African Company):** A British colonial company chartered to colonize and develop land in southern Africa, which controlled the region before direct British rule.
- **Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland:** A federation of three southern African territories (1953-1963) under British rule, with Salisbury as its capital.
- **Karst:** A landscape formed from the dissolution of soluble rocks like limestone, characterized by underground drainage systems and sinkholes.
- **Leptosols/Phaeozems:** Types of soil; Leptosols are shallow, and Phaeozems are dark, fertile soils, both common in Lusaka's geology.
- **Parastatal:** A government-owned commercial enterprise or corporation.
- **Shanty town:** An informal settlement with makeshift housing, often lacking basic services and legal tenure.
- **Town Nyanja:** A hybrid language spoken in Lusaka, based on Nyanja with influences from English, Bemba, and Nsenga.
- **Trans-African Highway (TAH):** Part of a network of roads planned by the United Nations Economic Commission for Africa to link the continent.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The medical rationale for moving the capital from Livingstone to Lusaka in 1929 was not explicitly published at the time, leaving the official reason partially obscured.
- The economic benefits and political implications of the Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland (1953-1963) remain a point of historical analysis, as it was popular with white settlers but strongly opposed by the African population.
- The effectiveness of Zambia's long-term goal of providing universal health care is debated, as free primary health provision is notionally achieved but often limited by capacity constraints.
