# Lever

## Overview
A lever is a simple machine consisting of a rigid beam pivoted at a fixed point called a fulcrum, used to amplify an input force to produce a greater output force, thereby providing mechanical advantage. It is one of the six classical simple machines identified by Renaissance scientists and operates on the principle that forces applied at different distances from the fulcrum produce different torques. Levers are fundamental to understanding mechanical systems and have been used since prehistoric times, with evidence of their application in ancient civilizations for tasks ranging from lifting heavy stones to operating looms.

## Timeline

- **c. 4400 BC** — Foot pedal used for earliest horizontal frame loom in ancient Egypt
- **c. 3000 BC** — Shadouf, a lever-based crane-like device, invented in Mesopotamia

## Body

### Etymology
The word "lever" entered English around 1300 from Old French *levier*, derived from the Latin *levare* meaning "to raise," which itself comes from the adjective *levis* meaning "light." The ultimate origin is the Proto-Indo-European stem *legwh-*, meaning "light," "easy," or "nimble."

### Historical Development
Prehistoric use of levers likely began with the digging stick, possibly invented by prehistoric women. The earliest known cultural evidence of lever application dates to ancient Egypt around 5000 BC, where it was used in balance scales. By 4400 BC, foot pedals were employed in horizontal frame looms. Around 3000 BC, the shadouf—a crane-like device using a lever mechanism—was developed in Mesopotamia. Ancient Egyptians used levers to move and uplift massive obelisks weighing over 100 tons, as evidenced by recesses and handling bosses in large stone blocks. The earliest surviving writings on levers come from the third century BC, traditionally attributed to Archimedes, who famously stated that given a lever long enough and a fulcrum, he could move the world.

### Force and Mechanical Advantage
A lever is a beam connected to ground by a hinge (fulcrum). In an ideal lever with no friction or bending, power input equals power output, and the ratio of output to input force equals the ratio of distances from the fulcrum to the points of force application. This relationship, known as the law of the lever, is expressed as mechanical advantage (MA) = F₂/F₁ = a/b, where a and b are the perpendicular distances from the fulcrum to the input and output forces respectively. The mechanical advantage can also be determined through the balance of moments or torque about the fulcrum.

### Types of Levers
Levers are classified into three types based on the relative positions of fulcrum, effort, and load:
- **Class I**: Fulcrum between effort and resistance (e.g., seesaw, crowbar, scissors). Mechanical advantage can be greater than, less than, or equal to 1.
- **Class II**: Resistance between effort and fulcrum (e.g., wheelbarrow, nutcracker, bottle opener). Mechanical advantage is always greater than 1; called a force multiplier.
- **Class III**: Effort between resistance and fulcrum (e.g., hoe, tweezers, human mandible). Mechanical advantage is always less than 1; called a speed multiplier.
The mnemonic "fre 123" helps remember these classifications.

### Compound Levers
A compound lever consists of multiple levers acting in series, where the resistance from one lever becomes the effort for the next. Examples include scales, nail clippers, and piano keys. In human anatomy, the malleus, incus, and stapes bones in the middle ear function as compound levers, transferring sound waves from the eardrum to the cochlea's oval window.

### Virtual Work and the Law of the Lever
The lever can be analyzed using the principle of virtual work, where the generalized force associated with the rotation angle θ is zero at equilibrium. This yields the equation aF_A = bF_B, confirming that mechanical advantage equals the ratio of distances from the fulcrum. The velocity-based approach shows that points farther from the pivot move faster, requiring less force to maintain equal power, thus explaining why longer input arms amplify force.

## Terms
- **Fulcrum**: The fixed pivot point on which a lever rotates
- **Mechanical Advantage (MA)**: The ratio of output force to input force in a machine
- **Torque**: Rotational force, calculated as force multiplied by the perpendicular distance from the pivot point
- **Effort**: The input force applied to operate a lever
- **Load/Resistance**: The output force exerted by the lever, typically the weight being lifted
- **Law of the Lever**: The principle stating that for equilibrium, the product of force and distance on each side of the fulcrum must be equal
- **Compound Lever**: A system of multiple levers operating in series
- **Principle of Virtual Work**: A method for analyzing equilibrium by considering infinitesimal displacements

## Debates and Open Questions
The attribution of the earliest lever writings to Archimedes is described as "by common belief," suggesting scholarly debate about whether he actually authored these works. Additionally, Autumn Stanley's argument that the digging stick represents the first lever positions prehistoric women as inventors of lever technology, which may be a subject of archaeological interpretation rather than established consensus.
