# International Date Line

## Overview
The International Date Line (IDL) is an imaginary line in the Pacific Ocean, roughly following the 180° meridian, that serves as the boundary between one calendar day and the next. It is a cartographic convention, not defined by international law, allowing countries to shift it for political or economic convenience. Crossing it eastbound subtracts a day, while crossing westbound adds a day.

## Timeline
- **1522** — Magellan–Elcano expedition confirms the one-day offset from circumnavigation.
- **1521** — Philippines claimed by Spain, placed on the eastern (American) side of the IDL.
- **1814** — British forces align Pitcairn Islands with American dates (east of IDL).
- **1844** — Philippines shifts to the western (Asian) side of the IDL.
- **1867** — Alaska transferred from Russia to the U.S., moving from the western to the eastern side of the IDL.
- **1892** — Samoa shifts from Asian to American dates.
- **1899** — Cook Islands and Niue cross to the eastern side of the IDL.
- **1917** — Anglo-French Conference establishes the de jure nautical date line for ships.
- **1993** — Kwajalein Atoll returns to the western side of the IDL.
- **1994** — Kiribati shifts its eastern islands west of the IDL, creating UTC+14:00.
- **2011** — Samoa shifts back to the western side of the IDL; Tokelau follows.

## Body

### Geography and Function
The IDL is a cartographic construct based on the 180° meridian, halfway from the Greenwich prime meridian. It deviates to accommodate political boundaries, keeping all of Russia west of it and most of the U.S. east of it. Key deviations wrap around the Aleutian Islands, the Chukchi Peninsula, and Kiribati. The line creates time zones from UTC−12:00 (e.g., Baker Island) to UTC+14:00 (e.g., Kiribati's Line Islands). For two hours daily (10:00–12:00 UTC), three calendar dates exist on Earth simultaneously.

### De Facto vs. De Jure Lines
The IDL on maps is a *de facto* line based on national laws for land and territorial waters. The *de jure* nautical date line, established by the 1917 Anglo-French Conference, is a pole-to-pole dashed line along 180°, interrupted by territorial waters. In practice, ships use international time zones for radio communication but may choose their own internal time.

### History of Alterations
The IDL's path has been altered multiple times for economic and political reasons. The Philippines moved from the American to the Asian date in 1844 to align with Asian trade. Alaska moved to the American date in 1867 with the U.S. purchase. Samoa switched to the American date in 1892, then back to the Asian date in 2011 to facilitate trade with Australia and New Zealand. Kiribati's 1994 shift created the UTC+14:00 zone, making its eastern islands the first to experience each new day and the year 2000.

### Religious and Cultural Implications
Most Christian churches recognize the IDL, celebrating holidays like Christmas on the local date. However, it creates challenges for churches observing a fixed weekly Sabbath, such as Seventh-day Adventists in Tonga and Samoa, who debate which side of the line marks the "true" Sabbath day. Islamic and Jewish communities generally follow the local date for religious observances, though Jewish law has complex discussions about the IDL's practical application for travelers.

## Terms
- **International Date Line (IDL):** The imaginary line in the Pacific Ocean where the calendar date changes.
- **De facto line:** The IDL as drawn on maps, based on national laws and cartographic convention.
- **De jure nautical date line:** The legally defined date line for ships on the high seas, based on international agreement.
- **UTC (Coordinated Universal Time):** The primary time standard by which the world regulates clocks and time.
- **Meridian:** A line of longitude connecting points of equal longitude.
- **180° meridian:** The line of longitude halfway around the world from the Greenwich prime meridian.
- **Time zone:** A region of the globe that observes a uniform standard time.
- **Julian calendar:** The solar calendar introduced by Julius Caesar, later replaced by the Gregorian calendar.
- **Gregorian calendar:** The internationally adopted civil calendar.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The precise course of the IDL in international waters is arbitrary and not fixed by treaty, leading to cartographic inconsistencies.
- Religious communities near the IDL, particularly Seventh-day Adventists, debate which day constitutes the true Sabbath due to the line's artificial imposition on the natural day-night cycle.
- The 1917 nautical date line convention remains legally valid, but modern maps often do not depict it, instead following national *de facto* boundaries.
