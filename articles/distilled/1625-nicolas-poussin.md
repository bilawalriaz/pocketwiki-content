# Nicolas Poussin

## Overview
Nicolas Poussin (1594–1665) was the leading painter of the classical French Baroque style, though he spent most of his career in Rome. Rejecting the emotionalism and coloristic brilliance of mainstream Baroque artists like Caravaggio and Pietro da Cortona, Poussin championed *disegno* (nobility of design), clarity, logic, and stoic restraint. His history paintings—drawn from the Bible, classical mythology, and ancient literature—were composed with intellectual rigor for a small circle of learned Italian and French collectors. A brief, unhappy tenure as First Painter to Louis XIII (1640–42) confirmed his preference for private patronage over court service. In his later decades, landscape became the dominant protagonist in his work, culminating in the poetic *Seasons* series. Poussin’s authoritative classicism shaped the French Academy under Charles Le Brun, fueled the *Poussiniste* vs. *Rubeniste* debate, and provided a structural model for artists from Jacques-Louis David and Ingres to Cézanne and the Cubists.

## Timeline
- **June 1594** — Born near Les Andelys, Normandy; receives education including Latin.
- **c. 1612** — Runs away to Paris; studies briefly under Ferdinand Elle and Georges Lallemand; discovers royal collections (Raphael, Giulio Romano) via Alexandre Courtois.
- **1617–18** — First attempt to reach Rome; turns back at Florence after an accident.
- **1622** — Receives first major commission: six paintings for the Jesuits honoring St. Francis Xavier’s canonization (lost). Produces "Marino drawings" (Ovid’s *Metamorphoses*) for poet Giambattista Marino.
- **Spring 1624** — Arrives in Rome; lodges with Simon Vouet; studies Raphael, Carracci, Domenichino; meets patrons Cardinal Francesco Barberini and Cassiano dal Pozzo.
- **1627** — Paints *The Death of Germanicus* for Barberini, establishing his reputation through erudite antiquarianism and stoic clarity.
- **1628** — Completes *Martyrdom of St. Erasmus* altarpiece for St. Peter’s (Vatican Pinacoteca); official displeasure and loss of San Luigi dei Francesi competition lead him to abandon public commissions.
- **1630** — Marries Anne-Marie Dughet; buys house on Via Paolina (1632); enters most productive period painting for private collectors (e.g., *Seven Sacraments* first series, *Massacre of the Innocents*, *Bacchanals*).
- **Dec 1640 – Autumn 1642** — Returns to Paris as First Painter to the King; overwhelmed by workload (Louvre Galerie, tapestries, Richelieu commissions) and court intrigues; flees back to Rome permanently.
- **1644–48** — Paints second *Seven Sacraments* series for Paul Fréart de Chantelou; *Landscape with Diogenes*.
- **1650s** — Health declines; hand tremor appears. Paints "townscapes" (*Death of Saphire*) and major late landscapes (*Landscape with Pyramus and Thisbe*, *The Seasons* 1660–64).
- **1664** — Wife Anne-Marie dies.
- **19 Nov 1665** — Dies in Rome; buried in San Lorenzo in Lucina.

## Body

### Early Formation and the Move to Rome
Poussin’s Norman upbringing included Latin, providing direct access to classical texts. In Paris (c. 1612), exclusion from the guild forced him into minor studios (Elle, Lallemand), but the decisive education came from the royal collections, where engravings of Raphael and Giulio Romano ignited his classicizing vocation. Two failed attempts to reach Rome (1617–18, 1622) bracketed his first professional commissions in Paris, notably the lost Jesuit cycle and the *Marino drawings* for the court poet Giambattista Marino. Marino’s household became Poussin’s conduit to Rome; when Marino returned in 1623, Poussin followed in spring 1624.

### First Roman Period (1624–1640): From Crisis to Classicism
Rome offered the antique, Raphael, and a network of French artists (Duquesnoy, Stella, Vouet) and patrons. Early years were desperate: Marino died (1625), Barberini and Cassiano dal Pozzo left for Spain (1624–26), and Poussin survived syphilis only through the care of the Dughet family (he married Anne-Marie in 1630). The Barberini’s return brought two pivotal commissions. *The Death of Germanicus* (1627), sourced from Tacitus and the Meleager sarcophagus, codified Poussin’s "stoic" mode: frieze-like composition, archaeological fidelity, emotional restraint. The *Martyrdom of St. Erasmus* (1628), executed from Pietro da Cortona’s design for St. Peter’s, was his only overtly Baroque work (plunging diagonal, high drama); its cool reception and his loss of the San Luigi dei Francesi fresco competition drove him to renounce public altarpieces. He reoriented toward *quadri da stanza*—cabinet pictures for private devotees—gaining control over subject, pace, and style. Patrons now included Cassiano dal Pozzo (first *Seven Sacraments*), Cardinal Rospigliosi (*Shepherds of Arcadia* second version), Cardinal Omodei (*Triumphs of Flora*), Vincenzo Giustiniani (*Massacre of the Innocents*), and Cardinal Richelieu (*Bacchanals*, *Birth of Venus*). By 1632, settled on Via Paolina, Poussin entered his mature phase: cooler palette, drier touch, figures staged in measured, box-like spaces parallel to the picture plane.

### The Paris Interlude (1640–42) and Permanent Return
Superintendent Sublet de Noyers recruited Poussin as First Painter to Louis XIII, offering the Tuileries residence and honors. The reality was a bureaucratic deluge: Louvre Galerie vaults, tapestry cartoons (Gobelins), book frontispieces, Richelieu’s allegorical *Time Defending Truth*, and Jesuit altarpieces—all while working slowly and alone. Criticism from Simon Vouet’s faction and court intrigues compounded the misery. When the court departed for Languedoc (autumn 1642), Poussin seized the pretext to flee Rome for good, selling his Tuileries furnishings in 1643.

### Final Roman Decades (1642–1665): Landscape as History
Post-1642, the Barberini were exiled, Pope Innocent X favored Spanish culture, and Poussin leaned on Cassiano dal Pozzo, Camillo Massimi, and Paris-based patrons (Chantelou, Fouquet, Pointel). The second *Seven Sacraments* (1644–48) for Chantelou traded the first series’ freshness for austerity and compositional mastery. Landscape, formerly a backdrop, became the protagonist. Poussin composed "ideal landscapes" where vertical trees and columns balance horizontal water and architecture, guiding the eye to diminutive figures enacting tragic narratives from myth, Bible, or literature (e.g., *Landscape with Pyramus and Thisbe*, 1651). Skies modulated mood: serene "Poussin skies" for faith (*St. John on Patmos*), tempestuous for tragedy. The 1650–55 "townscapes" (*Death of Saphire*) substituted classical architecture for nature to stage simultaneous moral contrasts. Declining health and a worsening hand tremor (visible in late drawings) did not halt ambition: *Orion Blinded*, *Landscape with Hercules and Cacus*, *The Seasons* (1660–64), and the unfinished *Apollo and Daphne* (1665) synthesize Christian and pagan themes—Spring as Eden, Summer as Ruth—while framing vitality against sterility and death.

### Subjects: Narrative, Allegory, and the *Affetti*
Every painting narrates. Poussin conflated sequential episodes into single frames, using *affetti* (facial expressions) to differentiate reactions. He avoided contemporary subjects. **Religion** dominated (church and domestic demand); Old Testament vagueness allowed invention (*Moses Saved*, three versions). *Massacre of the Innocents* concentrated horror into one brutal group; *Judgement of Solomon* (1649) reads entirely through varied *affetti*. The two *Seven Sacraments* series (1637–42, 1644–48) illustrated moral theology via Christ’s life. **Mythology/Classical Literature** fueled early Bacchanals, *Death of Germanicus* (Tacitus), *Rape of the Sabine Women* (two versions), and Venus/Adonis (Ovid). Later mythologies grew somber, embedding mortality symbols (*Apollo and Daphne*: life left, death right). **Poetry/Allegory** drew from Tasso’s *Jerusalem Delivered* (*Renaud and Armide*, *Tancred and Hermiene*) and Cardinal Rospigliosi’s moral theater. *A Dance to the Music of Time* (c. 1639–40) encodes a Christian cycle: Poverty → Work → Riches → Luxury → Poverty, overseen by Apollo’s chariot (Time). *Et in Arcadia ego* (c. 1630, late 1630s) juxtaposes ideal shepherds with a tomb inscription: death in paradise.

### Style and Method: *Disegno* over *Colore*
Poussin rejected Baroque *colore* and Berninian theatricality for Renaissance *disegno*—intellectual clarity via line, structure, and antique precedent. He studied Titian’s Bacchanals, Domenichino, Guido Reni, and the Antique directly. By the 1630s, his palette cooled, touch dried, space became stage-like (*Triumph of David*, *Rape of the Sabines*). Unusually, he made no detailed preparatory figure drawings and used no assistants. Composition studies (pen/ink wash) and landscape sketches from nature sufficed. For complex groupings, he arranged miniature wax figures in a box-stage (open one side) to test lighting and disposition. Pierre Rosenberg notes his drawing lacked virtuosity but possessed "uncompromising rigour: never an irrelevant mark."

### Legacy: The Canon of Classicism
Poussin’s style became French academic orthodoxy via Charles Le Brun (student, later Academy head). The *Poussiniste* (drawing) vs. *Rubeniste* (color) debate structured 17th-century theory. During the Revolution, David adopted Poussin’s severity for Republican virtue (*Brutus*, *Death of Marat*). Benjamin West modeled *Death of General Wolfe* on *Death of Germanicus*. In the 19th century, Ingres (Director, French Academy Rome) called Poussin the sole painter to capture Italy’s nature ("Poussinesque"); Delacroix hailed him as painting’s greatest innovator, breaking Mannerist craft for intellectual art. Cézanne revered Poussin’s structural reordering of nature ("Every time I leave a Poussin, I know better who I am"); Denis dubbed Cézanne "the Poussin of Impressionism." Seurat admired his formal geometry. In the 20th century, Picasso (1963 series on *Rape of the Sabines*), Braque (analytic Cubism), Derain, Balthus, and Lüpertz acknowledged his structural authority. Major collections: Louvre, National Gallery London, Dulwich, Hermitage, Prado, Musée Condé, National Gallery of Scotland.

## Terms
- **Disegno** (Italian: "design/drawing"): The Renaissance principle privileging linear structure, intellectual composition, and drawing as the foundation of art; Poussin’s core aesthetic against *colore*.
- **Colore** (Italian: "color"): The Venetian/Baroque emphasis on color, brushwork, and atmospheric effect over linear design; championed by Rubens and the *Rubenistes*.
- **Affetti** (Italian: "affects/emotions"): The codified facial expressions and gestures used in history painting to convey distinct psychological states; Poussin’s primary narrative device.
- **Quadri da stanza** (Italian: "room pictures"): Small-to-medium cabinet paintings for private collectors’ study rooms, distinct from public altarpieces; Poussin’s preferred format after 1628.
- **Seven Sacraments**: Two series (1637–42, 1644–48) depicting the seven Catholic rites (Baptism, Confirmation, Eucharist, Penance, Orders, Marriage, Extreme Unction) through Gospel episodes; landmarks of Poussin’s didactic classicism.
- **Et in Arcadia ego** (Latin: "Even in Arcadia I [Death] exist"): Allegorical subject showing shepherds discovering a tomb in pastoral paradise; Poussin painted two versions meditating on mortality.
- **Poussiniste / Rubeniste**: Members of the late-17th-century French Academy debate: *Poussinistes* (Le Brun) upheld drawing/design; *Rubenistes* (de Piles) upheld color.
- **Ideal Landscape / Classical Landscape**: Poussin’s mature mode—composed, balanced nature (vertical/horizontal rhythms, "Poussin skies") as a stage for historical/mythological narrative, not topographical record.
- **Wax-figure stage**: Poussin’s unique compositional tool: a box open on one side holding posed wax figurines, used to study lighting and grouping for paintings.
- **First Painter to the King (Premier Peintre du Roi)**: Prestigious court title granted 1640; entailed overwhelming decorative duties (Louvre, tapestries, Richelieu commissions) that caused Poussin’s flight back to Rome.

## Debates and open questions
- **Martyrdom of St. Erasmus reception**: Why did this altarpiece—painted from Cortona’s design—draw official displeasure and kill Poussin’s papal career? Sources cite "unknown reasons."
- **San Luigi dei Francesi competition**: The lost fresco competition that cemented his turn from public commissions lacks documentation; its exact subject and jury verdict are unrecorded.
- **Syphilis and health timeline**: Biographers (Bellori, Félibien) note illness c. 1624–29 and a late hand tremor (post-1650), but the precise impact on technique (e.g., unfinished *Apollo and Daphne*) is inferred from drawings, not medical records.
- **Marino drawings dating**: The Windsor Castle Ovid series is "among the earliest identifiable works," but exact execution dates (Paris vs. early Rome) remain debated.
- **Poussin vs. Caravaggio**: Poussin’s reported hatred ("born to destroy painting") reflects a stylistic polemic; scholars debate whether he ever saw Caravaggio’s works directly or knew them only through copies/reputation.
- **Workshop practice**: The claim he "used no assistants" relies on absence of evidence; some conservators argue for limited studio help in late large landscapes.
- **Cézanne’s "Poussin of Impressionism"**: Maurice Denis’s 1907 formulation is a critical metaphor, not a documented influence; the exact transmission of Poussin’s structural lessons to Cézanne is interpretive.
