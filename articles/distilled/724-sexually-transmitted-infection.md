# Sexually transmitted infection

## Overview
A sexually transmitted infection (STI) is an infectious disease spread primarily through sexual contact, including vaginal, anal, oral, or manual activity. STIs encompass bacterial, viral, parasitic, and fungal pathogens, many of which can be asymptomatic yet still transmissible. They pose significant global health burdens, contributing to infertility, chronic illness, and mortality. The term "STI" is preferred over older terms like "venereal disease" because it includes subclinical cases.

## Timeline
- **c. 1550 BCE** — Earliest recorded documentation in the Ebers Papyrus
- **8th–7th century BCE** — References in the Hebrew Bible/Old Testament
- **1494–98** — First major syphilis outbreak in Europe during the Italian War
- **1497** — Mercury first used as a treatment for syphilis
- **1924** — Brussels Agreement on free STI treatment for merchant seamen
- **1960s–70s** — Public perception that STIs were no longer a serious threat due to antibiotics
- **1980s** — Emergence of HIV/AIDS as an incurable STI
- **1999** — WHO recommends the term "sexually transmitted infection"

## Body

### Types and Causes
STIs are caused by various microorganisms: bacteria (e.g., *Chlamydia trachomatis*, *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*, *Treponema pallidum*), viruses (e.g., HIV, HPV, HSV), parasites (*Trichomonas vaginalis*), fungi (*Trichophyton mentagrophytes*), and others. Transmission occurs mainly via mucous membrane contact with infected bodily fluids. Some STIs, like HIV, can also be transmitted from mother to child during pregnancy or breastfeeding.

### Signs and Symptoms
Many STIs are initially asymptomatic, increasing transmission risk. Common symptoms include genital discharge, ulcers, pelvic pain, and infertility if untreated. In prepubescent children, STIs may indicate sexual abuse.

### Pathophysiology
Transmission is facilitated through mucous membranes in the genital tract, rectum, mouth, and eyes. Even minor contact with infectious fluids can lead to infection. Asymptomatic carriage is common, particularly in women, who may develop pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) without obvious signs.

### Diagnosis
Diagnostic tests vary by pathogen and include nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs), serology, and microscopy. Testing serves multiple purposes: diagnosing symptoms, screening asymptomatic individuals, ensuring safe partnerships, and preventing maternal-fetal transmission. A window period exists post-exposure where tests may yield false negatives.

### Prevention
Prevention strategies include vaccination (HBV, HPV), mutual monogamy, reduced partner count, abstinence, and consistent condom use. Behavioral counseling and regular screening are advised for high-risk groups. Proper condom application is crucial; improper use increases failure rates. Female condoms and diaphragms offer partial protection but not against HIV.

### Management
Effective treatments exist for most bacterial STIs using antibiotics. Partner notification and treatment (patient-delivered partner therapy) reduce reinfection. For incurable viral STIs like HIV and herpes, antiretroviral therapy and antiviral medications manage symptoms and lower transmission risk. Post-exposure prophylaxis is used after rape.

### Epidemiology
In 2015, over 1.1 billion people globally had STIs excluding HIV/AIDS. In the U.S., 19 million new cases occurred annually around 2010. Syphilis cases rose significantly since 2008, especially among men who have sex with men. HPV affects 290 million women worldwide. HIV/AIDS remains a leading cause of death in Sub-Saharan Africa.

### History
Historical records of STIs date back millennia. Medieval evidence suggests treponemal infections existed before the 1494 syphilis outbreak in Europe. The Renaissance saw widespread stigma and mercury-based treatments. The 19th century introduced institutional responses like the Contagious Diseases Acts. The 20th century brought effective antibiotics and later, awareness of incurable diseases like HIV/AIDS.

## Terms
- **STI (Sexually Transmitted Infection)**: An infection spread through sexual contact, often asymptomatic.
- **VD (Venereal Disease)**: An outdated term for STIs, now largely replaced by "STI."
- **PID (Pelvic Inflammatory Disease)**: A complication of untreated chlamydia or gonorrhea causing pelvic pain and infertility.
- **NAAT (Nucleic Acid Amplification Test)**: A highly sensitive diagnostic test for detecting STI pathogens.
- **ARV (Antiretroviral Therapy)**: Medications that suppress HIV replication, improving quality of life and reducing transmission.
- **HPV (Human Papillomavirus)**: A common viral STI linked to cervical and other cancers; preventable via vaccine.
- **HSV (Herpes Simplex Virus)**: Causes genital herpes; manageable but incurable.
- **Contact Tracing**: Identifying and testing partners of infected individuals to control spread.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Origins of Syphilis**: Whether syphilis emerged in Europe before or after the Columbian Exchange remains debated.
- **Effectiveness of School-Based Interventions**: Limited evidence supports school-based programs in improving adolescent sexual health outcomes.
- **Microbicide Efficacy**: Nonoxynol-9 was found ineffective and potentially harmful; dapivirine shows promise but requires further study.
- **Asymptomatic Carriage**: The true prevalence and impact of subclinical STIs remain understudied, especially in low-resource settings.
