# Eisaku Satō

## Overview
Eisaku Satō was a Japanese politician who served as Prime Minister from 1964 to 1972, the longest uninterrupted tenure in Japanese history. He is best remembered for overseeing Japan's rapid economic growth, securing the return of Okinawa from U.S. administration, and winning the 1974 Nobel Peace Prize for his nuclear non-proliferation efforts, a decision later clouded by revelations of secret agreements with the United States.

## Timeline
- **27 March 1901** — Born in Tabuse, Yamaguchi Prefecture.
- **1924** — Graduates from Tokyo Imperial University; joins Ministry of Railways.
- **1949** — Enters the National Diet as a member of the Liberal Party.
- **1964** — Succeeds Hayato Ikeda as Prime Minister and LDP president.
- **22 June 1965** — Signs the Treaty on Basic Relations with South Korea, normalizing ties.
- **11 December 1967** — Introduces the "Three Non-Nuclear Principles."
- **1969** — Signs a secret agreement with the U.S. regarding nuclear weapons in Okinawa.
- **1970** — Allows the U.S.–Japan Security Treaty to automatically renew.
- **1971** — Faces "Nixon Shocks" from U.S. policy changes on China and trade.
- **15 May 1972** — Okinawa is formally returned to Japanese sovereignty.
- **11 December 1974** — Awarded the Nobel Peace Prize.
- **3 June 1975** — Dies in Tokyo at age 74.

## Body

### Early Life and Political Rise
Satō was born into a prominent political family in Yamaguchi Prefecture; his elder brother, Nobusuke Kishi, also became Prime Minister. After graduating from Tokyo Imperial University in 1924, he became a bureaucrat in the Ministry of Railways. He entered the National Diet in 1949 and served in several cabinet positions under Prime Minister Shigeru Yoshida, including as Chief Cabinet Secretary. He later held senior roles under his brother Kishi and Hayato Ikeda, including Finance Minister and Minister of International Trade and Industry.

### Premiership and Domestic Policy
Satō became Prime Minister in 1964 after Ikeda's resignation. He presided over a period of strong economic growth, supported by business and finance interests. His administration faced major domestic challenges, including the 1968–1969 university crisis, where student protests against the Vietnam War and the U.S. Security Treaty were forcibly cleared by riot police. He allowed the Security Treaty to automatically renew in 1970, cementing the U.S. alliance but angering activists.

### Foreign Policy and Okinawa
Satō's foreign policy balanced close ties with the United States against regional relations. He normalized relations with South Korea in 1965 and maintained a pro-Taiwan stance, opposing the entry of the People's Republic of China into the UN. His signature achievement was negotiating the 1969 agreement with President Nixon to return Okinawa, which was completed in 1972. However, this deal controversially allowed U.S. military bases to remain.

### Nuclear Policy and Nobel Prize
Initially, Satō had argued for Japan to develop nuclear weapons to counter China. Under U.S. pressure, he instead introduced the "Three Non-Nuclear Principles" in 1967 and shepherded Japan's entry into the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty. For this, he was awarded the 1974 Nobel Peace Prize. His reputation was later tarnished by declassified documents showing he had secretly agreed with the U.S. to allow nuclear weapons in Okinawa and had discussed their potential use against China.

### Resignation and Later Life
Satō's popularity collapsed in 1971 due to the "Nixon Shocks"—the U.S. president's sudden announcement of a visit to China and new trade tariffs, which were seen as humiliating for Japan. He resigned in 1972. His chosen successor, Takeo Fukuda, lost the LDP leadership election to Kakuei Tanaka. Satō remained an influential elder statesman until his death from a stroke in 1975.

## Terms
- **Three Non-Nuclear Principles**: A policy introduced by Satō in 1967 pledging that Japan would not produce, possess, or allow the introduction of nuclear weapons.
- **Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT)**: An international treaty to limit the spread of nuclear weapons, which Japan signed in 1970 under Satō.
- **U.S.–Japan Security Treaty**: A 1960 treaty allowing U.S. military bases in Japan, which Satō allowed to automatically renew in 1970.
- **Nixon Shocks**: The 1971 U.S. policy announcements on China and trade that severely damaged Satō's government and the Japanese economy.
- **Okinawa Reversion**: The 1972 return of the Okinawa islands from U.S. administration to Japanese sovereignty, a key goal of Satō's premiership.
- **Keynesian economic policies**: Government economic strategies involving spending to stimulate growth, which Satō supported.
- **Yoshida school**: A group of postwar Japanese politicians and bureaucrats, including Satō, who were mentored by Prime Minister Shigeru Yoshida and emphasized economic growth and a close U.S. alliance.

## Debates and Open questions
The central debate concerns the contradiction between Satō's public anti-nuclear stance and his secret agreements with the U.S. Scholars question the extent to which he was a genuine advocate for non-proliferation versus a pragmatist making hidden concessions. His Nobel Prize remains controversial in light of these revelations. Another point of analysis is whether his foreign policy, particularly his staunch anti-China position, ultimately left Japan diplomatically isolated and unprepared for the "Nixon Shocks."
