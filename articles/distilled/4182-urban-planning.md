# Urban planning

## Overview
Urban planning is the interdisciplinary process of designing and managing land use and the built environment in settlements. It guides orderly development to address public welfare, including efficiency, sanitation, environmental protection, and social and economic activity. The field has evolved from a top-down, physical layout focus to incorporating social well-being, sustainability, and community participation.

## Timeline
- **c. 3000 BCE** — Evidence of urban planning in Mesopotamian, Indus Valley, Minoan, and Egyptian civilizations.
- **8th century BCE** — Greek city-states adopt orthogonal (grid-like) plans.
- **498–408 BC** — Hippodamus of Miletus, considered the father of European urban planning, develops the Hippodamian plan.
- **15th century** — Theoretical treatises on architecture and urban planning begin to appear.
- **19th century** — Baron Georges-Eugène Haussmann redesigns Paris under Napoleon III.
- **1899** — The Town and Country Planning Association is founded.
- **1909** — The first academic course in urban planning is offered at the University of Liverpool.
- **1933** — Le Corbusier presents his "Radiant City" concept.
- **1961** — Jane Jacobs publishes *The Death and Life of Great American Cities*, challenging modernist planning.
- **2018** — The UN predicts 2.5 billion more people will occupy urban areas by 2050.

## Body

### History and Evolution
Urban planning has ancient origins, with grid-pattern streets found in third-millennium BCE civilizations. The ideas of the ancient Greeks and Romans spread with their empires but declined with Rome's fall. Planning re-emerged during the Renaissance and Enlightenment, with rulers redesigning capitals. The 19th-century industrial city's problems led to a paradigm shift around 1900, with theorists developing models to provide healthier environments. The 20th century saw planning recognized as a separate profession, initially dominated by modernist ideas like Le Corbusier's towers. By the 1960s, critics like Jane Jacobs argued for considering resident experiences, shifting focus toward livability and diversity.

### 21st-Century Practices and Theories
Contemporary planning addresses externalities like congestion and climate change. It incorporates new concepts like "blue zones" and "innovation districts" to improve quality of life. Eight principal procedural theories of planning exist, including the rational-comprehensive, incremental, and communicative approaches. Conceptual theories include Ebenezer Howard's Garden Cities and the Burgess Concentric Model. Participatory planning, which involves the entire community, emerged in the U.S. during the 1960s and 70s.

### Technical Aspects and Tools
Technical planning involves scientific processes for land use, transportation, and infrastructure. Planners use techniques like population prediction, zoning, and Geographic Information Systems (GIS) to model and project impacts. Regulations include building codes, zoning, and planning permissions. Modern tools increasingly use incentive-based frameworks, urban digital twins (UDTs) with AI and IoT for simulation, and crowdsourced data for informed decision-making.

### Urban Planners and Criticisms
An urban planner is a professional who optimizes land use and infrastructure, analyzing economic, environmental, and social trends. Their importance grows with challenges like population growth and climate change. Criticisms include arguments from neoclassical economics that planning is unnecessary, and that consensus-based models merely reinforce existing power structures. Debates focus on inclusivity in decision-making, often using Sherry Arnstein's "ladder of citizen participation" as a framework. Participatory planning has also been criticized for contributing to housing crises.

## Terms
- **Urban planning**: The process of developing and designing plans for land use and the built environment in settlements.
- **Orthogonal plan**: A city layout based on a grid of streets at right angles, also known as the Hippodamian plan.
- **Modernism (in planning)**: A mid-20th-century approach emphasizing uniformity, towers, and separation of functions.
- **Participatory planning**: An approach that involves the entire community in the planning process.
- **Geographic Information System (GIS)**: A technical tool used to create models of existing planning and project future societal, economic, and environmental impacts.
- **Zoning**: A regulatory method that governs how cities are constructed and used by designating land for specific purposes.
- **Urban digital twin (UDT)**: A digital simulation of an urban area that uses AI and IoT to predict development scenarios.
- **Externalities**: Negative impacts caused by an activity, such as congestion from larger highway systems.

## Debates and Open questions
- **Necessity of planning**: Neoclassical economics argues planning is unnecessary or harmful, believing market efficiency allows for effective land use.
- **Power and consensus**: The consensus-building model is criticized for being based on, rather than challenging, existing community power structures. Agonism has been proposed as an alternative framework.
- **Inclusivity**: A major debate concerns who is included or excluded in decision-making, with top-down approaches often failing to include residents. The "ladder of citizen participation" is used to assess inclusivity.
- **Housing crisis**: Participatory urban planning has been criticized for contributing to housing crises in parts of the world.
