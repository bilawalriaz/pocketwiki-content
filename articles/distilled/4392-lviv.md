# Lviv

## Overview
Lviv is the fifth-largest city in Ukraine and the largest in its western region, serving as a major cultural, academic, and economic center. Its history is a complex tapestry of shifting sovereignty, having been a capital in the Kingdom of Galicia–Volhynia, a royal city in the Polish Crown, a provincial capital in the Habsburg Empire, and a key city in interwar Poland before becoming part of the Soviet Union and, since 1991, independent Ukraine. This layered past is reflected in its UNESCO-listed historic center, which showcases a fusion of Central European, Italian, and German architectural traditions.

## Timeline
- **c. 1250** — Founded by King Daniel of Galicia, named for his son Lev.
- **1349** — Annexed by the Crown of the Kingdom of Poland after a war of succession.
- **1356** — Granted Magdeburg (town) rights by King Casimir III the Great.
- **1772** — Becomes the capital of the Habsburg Kingdom of Galicia and Lodomeria after the First Partition of Poland.
- **1918** — Site of the Polish-Ukrainian War following the collapse of Austria-Hungary; briefly the capital of the West Ukrainian People's Republic.
- **1939** — Annexed by the Soviet Union following the German-Soviet invasion of Poland.
- **1941-1944** — Occupied by Nazi Germany; site of the Lwów Ghetto and Janowska concentration camp.
- **1945** — Transferred to the Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic by the Allied powers.
- **1991** — Remains part of Ukraine after the dissolution of the Soviet Union.
- **1998** — Historic city center inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **2022** — Becomes a de facto western capital and safe haven during the Russian invasion of Ukraine.

## Body

### Founding and Medieval Period
The area was settled by the 5th century, but the city of Lviv was founded around 1250 by King Daniel of Galicia. It was invaded by the Mongols in 1261 and later rebuilt by his son, King Lev, who made it his capital. In 1349, during wars of succession, the city was captured by Poland's King Casimir III the Great. He granted it town rights in 1356, initiating a period of growth and Polonization. From 1434, it was the capital of the Ruthenian Voivodeship within the Polish Crown, becoming a major trading hub and one of Poland's largest royal cities.

### Habsburg and Interwar Rule
After the First Partition of Poland in 1772, Lviv (German: Lemberg) became the capital of the Austrian Kingdom of Galicia and Lodomeria. The 19th century saw rapid population growth and the development of its Polish, Ukrainian, and Jewish cultural institutions. Following World War I, the city was contested in the Polish-Ukrainian War (1918-1919) and became part of the Second Polish Republic. It was a vibrant academic and cultural center, home to the renowned Lwów School of Mathematics and a thriving Jewish community.

### World War II and Soviet Era
In 1939, the city was annexed by the Soviet Union, and in 1941, it was occupied by Nazi Germany. The Jewish population, which had swelled to over 200,000, was systematically murdered in the Holocaust, with only a few hundred survivors. After Soviet re-occupation in 1944, a population exchange expelled most of the city's Polish inhabitants. Under Soviet rule, the city was heavily industrialized and Russified, though it remained a center of Ukrainian dissent. In 1989, it hosted a massive pro-independence rally.

### Independent Ukraine and Recent History
Since Ukrainian independence in 1991, Lviv has been a stronghold of Ukrainian national identity and a key player in events like the Orange Revolution (2004) and Euromaidan (2014). Its economy has shifted towards services, tourism, and information technology. Following the 2022 Russian invasion, the city became a crucial logistical hub and refuge for displaced Ukrainians, though it has also been targeted by missile strikes.

## Terms
- **Magdeburg Rights**: A set of town privileges granting self-governance, first adopted in the German city of Magdeburg and widely used in medieval Central and Eastern Europe.
- **Voivodeship**: A historical administrative region or province in the Polish Crown and later in the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth.
- **Galicia**: A historical region in Central Europe, divided today between southeastern Poland and western Ukraine.
- **Ruthenian Voivodeship**: An administrative division of the Polish Crown, established in 1434, with its capital in Lviv.
- **Lwów School of Mathematics**: An influential group of Polish mathematicians who gathered in Lviv in the 1930s, making major contributions to functional analysis and topology.
- **Janowska concentration camp**: A Nazi German concentration camp established in Lviv in 1941, where tens of thousands were murdered.
- **Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church**: An Eastern Catholic church in full communion with Rome, using Byzantine liturgy. It was forcibly dissolved by Soviet authorities in 1946.
- **Banderstadt**: A pejorative Soviet-era nickname for Lviv, referencing Ukrainian nationalist leader Stepan Bandera, later reclaimed by some residents.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Ethnic and Political Identity**: The city's identity has been a point of contention, with Polish, Ukrainian, and Jewish communities all claiming it as a cultural heartland. This is reflected in debates over historical monuments and street names.
- **Historical Memory**: The legacy of figures like Stepan Bandera and Roman Shukhevych, honored in Lviv, is highly controversial due to their roles in WWII-era collaborations and violence.
- **Cultural Heritage**: The impact of the Russian invasion on the city's UNESCO-listed historic center remains a significant concern, with ongoing efforts to protect it from damage.
