# Linguistics

## Overview
Linguistics is the scientific study of language, analyzing its structure, meaning, use, and evolution across multiple subfields. It investigates how sounds (phonetics/phonology), words (morphology), sentences (syntax), and meaning (semantics/pragmatics) form systems governed by subconscious rules. The field bridges theoretical inquiry into universal cognitive structures and practical applications in education, technology, and forensics. It shifted from historical comparison (philology) to synchronic structural analysis in the 20th century, establishing descriptive—rather than prescriptive—as its core methodology.

## Timeline
- **6th century BC** — Pāṇini composes *Aṣṭādhyāyī*, a formal description of Sanskrit morphology (3,959 rules).
- **c. 280 BC** — Alexandrian school founded; Dionysius Thrax writes *Art of Grammar*, first use of "grammar" in modern sense.
- **1716** — Term "philology" first attested in English.
- **1786** — William Jones proposes comparative method, identifying Indo-European language family.
- **1822** — Jacob Grimm publishes *Deutsche Grammatik*, foundational for comparative linguistics.
- **1847** — Term "linguistics" first attested in English.
- **1916** — Ferdinand de Saussure’s *Course in General Linguistics* published posthumously, establishing synchronic structuralism.
- **Early 20th c.** — Leonard Bloomfield, Louis Hjelmslev, and Zellig Harris advance structural and discourse analysis.
- **1960s** — Sound recording enables dialect archives; rise of linguistic typology and language universals research.
- **Late 20th c.** — Digital corpora and language technology expand computational and applied linguistics.

## Body

### Core Structural Subdisciplines
Linguistics analyzes language as a system of form-meaning pairings (Saussurean signs) across hierarchical levels. **Phonetics** studies the physical production, acoustics, and perception of speech sounds (or manual gestures in sign languages). **Phonology** abstracts these into cognitive categories (phonemes) that distinguish meaning and govern combinatorial rules. **Morphology** examines word-internal structure via **morphemes** (minimal meaningful units), distinguishing free roots (e.g., *catch*) from bound affixes (e.g., *-ing*), and tracks inflection (tense, number) and derivation. **Syntax** governs how words and morphemes combine into phrases and sentences, addressing word order, constituency, agreement, and cross-linguistic variation. **Semantics** analyzes conventional, coded meaning (lexical and compositional); **formal semantics** uses logic to model denotations, while **cognitive semantics** links meaning to general cognition (e.g., prototype theory). **Pragmatics** studies meaning in context—speech acts, implicature, and interaction—where interpretation depends on speaker intent, shared knowledge, and situational factors.

### Grammar and Discourse
**Grammar** is the integrated rule system spanning phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, and pragmatics. Modern frameworks include structural, functional, and generative approaches. **Discourse** is language as social practice; **discourse analysis** examines how ideologies are embedded in texts and how context shapes linguistic form down to phonological levels. A **register** is a discourse variety tied to a specific purpose (e.g., medical discourse), marked by specialized vocabulary and stylistic choices. **Stylistics** interprets textual style (rhetoric, irony, dialect) across genres and media. The **lexicon** is the mental catalogue of words and bound morphemes; **lexicography** maps it into dictionaries. Vocabulary size correlates with comprehension (≈98% coverage needed for reading), but generative capacity resides in grammar, not lexicon size.

### Historical and Comparative Linguistics
**Historical linguistics** studies language change over time (diachronic) or across contemporary variants (synchronic). Emerging from 18th-century philology, it established **comparative linguistics** via the **comparative method** (reconstructing proto-languages from daughter languages) and **internal reconstruction** (inferring earlier stages from alternating forms within one language). The **uniformitarian principle** (Whitney) assumes past changes obey same principles as present. Early work focused on well-documented Indo-European and Uralic families; later expanded to Austronesian and Native American families. **Macrofamily** hypotheses (e.g., Nostratic) remain controversial due to methodological limits at 8,000–10,000-year depths. Modern research examines sound change, grammaticalization, syntactic shift, and sociolinguistic drivers across all structural levels.

### Methodology: Description vs. Prescription
Modern linguistics is **descriptive**: it documents and explains linguistic systems without value judgments, analogous to zoology. **Prescription** promotes specific usages (often a prestige dialect/acrolect) to standardize communication or exert social influence; extreme forms appear in censorship. Prescription remains standard in language pedagogy (ELT). **Data priority**: spoken/signed data are considered more fundamental than writing because speech is universal, precedes writing historically, is acquired earlier, and captures features (phonological rules, errors) absent in writing. However, written corpora are more tractable for computational processing; **graphemics** studies writing systems as a linguistic subfield.

### Analytical Perspectives
Pre-20th century analysis was **diachronic** (historical comparison). Saussure introduced **synchronic** analysis (system state at one time). **Syntagmatic** analysis examines linear sequencing rules (e.g., article → noun). **Paradigmatic** analysis examines substitution sets (paradigms) where items of same category alternate in a slot. Research spans monolingual/multilingual, child/adult, acquisition/learning, abstract/cognitive, written/oral, and fieldwork/experimental modalities.

### Interdisciplinary and Applied Areas
**Sociolinguistics** studies social shaping of language variation (dialects, registers, idiolects) synchronically. **Developmental linguistics** investigates L1 acquisition in children and L2 acquisition in adults. **Neurolinguistics** maps brain structures (e.g., cerebellum’s predictive role) to language processing using aphasiology, imaging, and modelling. **Applied linguistics** engineers solutions for real-world problems—language education, translation, language planning, natural language processing—drawing on sociology and anthropology, not just linguistic theory. **Forensic linguistics** applies linguistic analysis to legal evidence (authorship, asylum interviews). **Clinical linguistics** informs speech-language pathology. **Computational linguistics** models language statistically and rule-wise for machine translation, speech recognition, and text analysis. **Evolutionary linguistics** applies sociobiological and cultural evolution theory to language emergence and dispersal. **Language documentation** creates enduring records (audio, video, grammars, dictionaries) of endangered languages, rooted in Boasian field anthropology.

## Terms
- ****Morpheme**** — Smallest meaningful unit in a language; includes free roots and bound affixes.
- ****Phoneme**** — Abstract cognitive sound category that distinguishes meaning in a specific language.
- ****Syntax**** — Rules governing how words/morphemes combine into phrases and sentences.
- ****Semantics**** — Study of conventional, coded meaning (lexical and compositional).
- ****Pragmatics**** — Study of meaning in context, dependent on speaker intent, shared knowledge, and situation.
- ****Synchronic**** — Analyzing a language system at a single point in time.
- ****Diachronic**** — Analyzing language change across historical time periods.
- ****Comparative method**** — Reconstructing proto-languages by systematic comparison of daughter languages.
- ****Register**** — A language variety defined by specialized vocabulary and style for a specific domain/purpose.
- ****Descriptive linguistics**** — Objective analysis of how language is actually used, without prescriptive judgments.

## Debates and open questions
- **Macrofamily validity**: Whether deep-time groupings (Nostratic, Eurasiatic) can be proven given comparative method limits at 8,000–10,000 years.
- **Nature of linguistic capacity**: Whether generative syntax reflects an innate universal grammar (generative view) or emerges from general cognition and usage (functional/cognitive views).
- **Modularity of mind**: Degree to which language processing is encapsulated vs. integrated with other cognitive systems (neurolinguistics/psycholinguistics).
- **Applied linguistics scope**: Whether the field merely "applies" theoretical findings or independently engineers solutions drawing on multiple disciplines.
- **Asylum interview reliability**: Validity and ethics of linguistic analysis for nationality determination in refugee adjudication.
- **Writing vs. speech primacy**: Balancing the theoretical primacy of speech with the practical necessity of written corpora for large-scale computational work.
