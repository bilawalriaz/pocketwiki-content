# Literary criticism

## Overview
Literary criticism is the study, evaluation, and interpretation of literature, often influenced by literary theory—the philosophical analysis of literature's goals and methods. While closely related, the two are distinct, with criticism typically applying theory to specific works. The field has evolved from ancient rhetorical analysis to a diverse, interdisciplinary practice central to humanities education.

## Timeline
- **4th century BC** — Aristotle writes the *Poetics*, introducing concepts like mimesis and catharsis.
- **9th century** — Arabic literary criticism flourishes with works by Al-Jahiz and Abdullah ibn al-Mu'tazz.
- **1498** — Recovery of Aristotle's *Poetics* in Latin sparks Renaissance criticism.
- **1570** — Lodovico Castelvetro publishes influential commentaries on Aristotle.
- **1654** — Emanuele Tesauro's *Il Cannocchiale aristotelico* develops Baroque aesthetic theory.
- **1700s–1800s** — Enlightenment criticism grows with rising literacy and commercial publishing.
- **Early 19th century** — Romantic criticism introduces new aesthetic ideas, valuing the sublime and fragmentation.
- **Early 20th century** — Russian Formalism and New Criticism dominate, emphasizing close reading of texts.
- **1957** — Northrop Frye publishes *Anatomy of Criticism*, influencing modern conservative thought.
- **Late 1960s–1990s** — "Theory" rises in Anglo-American departments, influenced by structuralism and post-structuralism.
- **Present** — Diverse approaches coexist, including ecocriticism, Darwinian studies, and postcritique.

## Body

### Definition and Relationship to Theory
Literary criticism is the practical evaluation of specific literary works, while literary theory provides the abstract, philosophical framework for that evaluation. The distinction is debated; some sources, like *The Johns Hopkins Guide to Literary Theory and Criticism*, treat the terms as synonymous. Criticism is often published in academic journals or popular periodicals like *The New York Times Book Review*.

### Historical Development
Criticism began in the classical period with Aristotle's *Poetics*. Medieval criticism focused on religious texts, influencing secular study. Renaissance critics revived classical ideas, championing literature's cultural centrality. Enlightenment criticism expanded with a growing literate public, though commercialization sometimes conflicted with idealistic goals. Romanticism shifted focus to the sublime and emotional power of literature.

### Twentieth-Century Schools and the Rise of Theory
Early 20th-century movements like Russian Formalism and New Criticism prioritized close reading and textual analysis over authorial intent or reader response. Mid-century, Northrop Frye's work noted critics' ideological biases. From the late 1960s, explicitly philosophical "theory" (e.g., structuralism, post-structuralism) rose in universities, though it peaked in the 1990s. Today, theoretical and conventional methods coexist.

### Current State and Interdisciplinary Approaches
Modern criticism is diverse. Beyond traditional analysis, critics engage with cultural studies (reading popular texts), ecocriticism (linking literature and natural science), Darwinian literary studies (examining evolutionary influences), and postcritique (developing new reading methods). Many also work in film or media studies. The field intersects with the history of the book, which studies text production, circulation, and reception.

## Terms
- **Literary Criticism**: The study, evaluation, and interpretation of literature.
- **Literary Theory**: The philosophical analysis of literature's goals and methods.
- **Mimesis**: Aristotle's concept of literature as an imitation of reality.
- **Catharsis**: Aristotle's idea of the purging of emotions through art.
- **Hermeneutics**: The theory and methodology of interpretation, especially of texts.
- **Close Reading**: Detailed analysis of a text's specific words and structures.
- **New Criticism**: An early 20th-century school emphasizing close reading and textual autonomy.
- **Structuralism**: A theoretical approach analyzing underlying structures in texts and culture.
- **Post-structuralism**: A movement critiquing structuralism, emphasizing instability of meaning.
- **Ecocriticism**: The study of the relationship between literature and the physical environment.

## Debates and Open Questions
- Whether literary criticism should be considered a separate field from literary theory is a matter of controversy.
- The influence of a critic's personal ideology or experiences on their judgment of literature is debated (e.g., E. Michael Jones's argument about Stanley Fish).
- The goals and methods of literary criticism, which characterized the "rise" of theory, have seen declining disagreement in recent years.
