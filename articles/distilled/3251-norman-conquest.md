# Norman Conquest

## Overview
The Norman Conquest of England was the 11th-century invasion and occupation of England by an army led by the Duke of Normandy, William the Conqueror. It was a pivotal event that replaced the Anglo-Saxon ruling class with a Norman-French elite, fundamentally altering England's language, culture, and political structures, and linking it more closely to continental Europe.

## Timeline
- **911** — Treaty of Saint-Clair-sur-Epte grants Normandy to Viking leader Rollo.
- **1042** — Edward the Confessor, with Norman ties, becomes King of England.
- **Jan 1066** — Edward the Confessor dies; Harold Godwinson is elected king.
- **20 Sep 1066** — Norwegian King Harald Hardrada defeats English earls at the Battle of Fulford.
- **25 Sep 1066** — Harold Godwinson defeats and kills Hardrada at the Battle of Stamford Bridge.
- **28 Sep 1066** — William's Norman army lands at Pevensey, Sussex.
- **14 Oct 1066** — Battle of Hastings: William defeats Harold, who is killed.
- **25 Dec 1066** — William is crowned King of England at Westminster Abbey.
- **1069-70** — "Harrying of the North": William's forces devastate Northumbria to crush rebellion.
- **1086** — The Domesday Book, a great survey of English landholdings, is completed.

## Body

### Origins and Succession Crisis
The Normans descended from Vikings settled in Normandy in 911, who adopted French language and culture. A link to England was established when Edward the Confessor, son of an English king and a Norman duchess, took the throne in 1042. Edward's death in 1066 without an heir sparked a succession crisis. Harold Godwinson was crowned, but William of Normandy claimed Edward had promised him the throne, and Harold had sworn an oath to support this claim. Another claimant, Norwegian King Harald Hardrada, also invaded.

### The Conquest of 1066
In September 1066, Hardrada invaded northern England, defeating local earls at Fulford. Harold Godwinson rushed north and surprised the Norwegians, defeating them at Stamford Bridge on 25 September. Three days later, William landed in the south. Harold marched his exhausted army back south to meet him. On 14 October, at the Battle of Hastings, William's force of cavalry, infantry, and archers defeated Harold's army, which was composed mainly of foot soldiers. Harold was killed, and William became king.

### English Resistance and Norman Control
William faced years of rebellions to secure his throne. He responded by confiscating lands from the English elite and granting them to his Norman followers, building castles to control strategic points, and brutally suppressing uprisings, most notably in the "Harrying of the North" (1069-70). By 1072, he had largely secured the kingdom. To administer his new realm, he commissioned the Domesday Book (1086), a comprehensive survey of English landholdings for taxation and control.

### Consequences for Society and Culture
The conquest led to the near-total replacement of the English aristocracy with a Norman-French elite. By 1086, only about 5% of land in southern England remained in English hands. Norman French became the language of the court, government, and upper classes, while Old English evolved into Middle English. The Normans adopted and refined the sophisticated Anglo-Saxon systems of shire administration, taxation, and coinage, but changed the personnel and language of government. The formal abolition of slavery, which was underway before 1066, accelerated after the conquest. Intermarriage between Normans and English was initially uncommon but became widespread within a century.

## Terms
*   **Feudal Tenure:** The system where all land was held directly from the king in return for military service and loyalty.
*   **Fyrd:** The Anglo-Saxon militia, primarily composed of foot soldiers.
*   **Housecarls:** The professional, well-armed personal troops of a nobleman.
*   **Motte-and-Bailey:** A type of early Norman castle with a wooden tower (motte) on an artificial mound, enclosed by a courtyard (bailey).
*   **Domesday Book:** A manuscript record of the "Great Survey" of much of England completed in 1086 for William the Conqueror.
*   **Witenagemot:** The council of Anglo-Saxon nobles and bishops that advised the king and elected Harold Godwinson.
*   **Shire Reeve (Sheriff):** The royal official responsible for administering a shire (county).
*   **Geld:** A land tax used by Anglo-Saxon and later Norman kings.
*   **Varangian Guard:** An elite unit of the Byzantine army, largely composed of Scandinavian and later Anglo-Saxon mercenaries.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Impact and Radicalism:** Historians debate whether the conquest was a radical transformation or an acceleration of existing trends. Richard Southern argued it was a uniquely radical change, while others like H. G. Richardson saw less transformation.
*   **The "Norman Yoke":** A 17th-century theory that Anglo-Saxon society was freer and more equal than the post-conquest society. This is seen as more reflective of later political thought than historical fact.
*   **Papal Support:** Whether William obtained Pope Alexander II's blessing for the invasion before it happened is disputed; contemporary claims are treated with caution.
*   **Harold's Death:** The exact manner of Harold Godwinson's death at Hastings is unknown, with sources giving conflicting accounts (arrow to the eye, killed by William, etc.).
