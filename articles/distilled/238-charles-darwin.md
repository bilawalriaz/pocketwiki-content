# Charles Darwin

## Overview
Charles Darwin (1809–1882) was an English naturalist whose theory of evolution by natural selection became the unifying foundation of the life sciences. His five-year voyage on HMS *Beagle* (1831–1836) provided the geological and biogeographical evidence—particularly the distribution of mockingbirds, tortoises, and fossils in South America and the Galápagos—that led him to conclude species were not fixed but descended with modification from common ancestors. He conceived natural selection in 1838 after reading Thomas Malthus on population pressure, realizing that favorable variations are preserved in the "struggle for existence" while unfavorable ones are lost. After two decades of research, including an eight-year study of barnacles that cemented his biological reputation, he was prompted to publish by Alfred Russel Wallace’s independent discovery of the same mechanism. Their theories were jointly presented to the Linnean Society in 1858; *On the Origin of Species* (1859) sold out immediately and convinced the scientific community of evolution’s reality, though natural selection remained a minority view until the modern evolutionary synthesis (1930s–1950s) integrated Mendelian genetics. Darwin extended his theory to human descent, sexual selection, and plant physiology, demonstrating that complex adaptations arise through natural law rather than design.

## Body

### Early Influences and Education
Darwin was born into a wealthy, intellectual family; his grandfathers Erasmus Darwin and Josiah Wedgwood were abolitionists, and Erasmus had speculated on common descent in *Zoonomia* (1794). After neglecting medical studies at Edinburgh (1825–1827)—where he learned taxidermy from the freed slave John Edmonstone and joined the radical Plinian Society—he was sent to Cambridge to train as an Anglican parson. There, botany professor John Stevens Henslow became his mentor, introducing him to natural theology (Paley) and the inductive science of Herschel and Humboldt. A geology field trip with Adam Sedgwick in Wales (August 1831) prepared him for the *Beagle* voyage.

### The *Beagle* Voyage and Geological Foundations
The five-year survey voyage (1831–1836) was the crucible of Darwin’s thinking. Captain FitzRoy gave him Lyell’s *Principles of Geology*, which argued for uniformitarianism—slow, continuous geological change over immense time. Darwin applied this lens at St. Jago (seashells in volcanic cliffs), Patagonia (fossil megafauna like *Megatherium* and *Glyptodon* beside modern shells, indicating recent extinction without catastrophe), and the Andes (marine shells at high altitude, earthquake uplift). He theorized that as continents rose, oceanic islands sank, with coral reefs growing upward to form atolls—a hypothesis published in *Coral Reefs* (1842). Biogeographical puzzles multiplied: distinct but related species of rheas replaced each other southward; Galápagos mockingbirds and tortoises varied by island; the Falkland Islands fox hinted at trans-oceanic dispersal. In Tierra del Fuego, the stark contrast between "civilized" Fuegians returned from England and "miserable, degraded savages" led him to reject an unbridgeable gap between humans and animals. Meeting John Herschel at the Cape (1836) reinforced the search for a natural law governing species replacement.

### Inception of Natural Selection (1837–1838)
Back in London, experts catalogued his collections. Richard Owen identified the fossil mammals as giant extinct relatives of living South American forms (geographical continuity). John Gould revealed the Galápagos "blackbirds, gros-beaks, and finches" were twelve species of finches, and the mockingbirds were separate species per island. By March 1837, Darwin’s Red Notebook speculated "one species does change into another." In July, his "B" notebook sketched a branching genealogical tree, discarding Lamarck’s ladder of progress. The mechanism arrived 28 September 1838: reading Malthus’s *Essay on Population*, he saw that geometric population growth against finite resources created a "struggle for existence" analogous to Augustin de Candolle’s "warring of the species." Favorable variations would be preserved, unfavorable ones destroyed—"a force like a hundred thousand wedges" fitting structures into nature’s economy. He recognized the analogy to artificial selection (selective breeding), later terming his mechanism "natural selection."

### The Long Gestation: Barnacles, Illness, and the "Big Book" (1839–1858)
Darwin married Emma Wedgwood (1839), moved to Down House, Kent (1842), and pursued a dual track: public geology and zoology (the *Beagle* zoology volumes, *Coral Reefs*, *Volcanic Islands*, *South America*), and private transmutation research. Chronic illness—stomach pains, vomiting, palpitations, boils—plagued him from 1838 onward, often triggered by stress. A £1,000 Treasury grant funded the *Zoology of the Voyage of H.M.S. Beagle*. From 1846–1854 he monographed barnacles (*Cirripedia*), using his theory to find homologies (modified limbs for feeding, minute parasitic males showing an intermediate stage in sex evolution). This work won the Royal Medal (1853) and established his credibility as a biologist. In 1844 he expanded his sketch to a 230-page "Essay" to be published if he died. The anonymous *Vestiges of the Natural History of Creation* (1844) stirred public debate but Darwin dismissed its science. By 1854, barnacles finished, he realized divergence of character was explained by descendants adapting to "diversified places in the economy of nature."

### Wallace, Joint Publication, and *Origin* (1858–1859)
Lyell, seeing Wallace’s 1855 Sarawak paper, urged Darwin to publish for priority. Darwin began a "short paper" in May 1856, which ballooned into *Natural Selection*. On 18 June 1858, Wallace’s manuscript from Borneo arrived, describing natural selection almost identically. "Forestalled," Darwin deferred to Lyell and Hooker, who arranged a joint reading at the Linnean Society on 1 July 1858 (Darwin absent, his infant son dying of scarlet fever). The event drew little notice. Darwin then compressed his big book into an "abstract"—*On the Origin of Species*—published 22 November 1859. It presented "one long argument": variation, struggle for existence, natural selection, inheritance, and the resulting descent with modification. He avoided explicit human evolution but wrote, "Light will be thrown on the origin of man." The final paragraph’s "endless forms most beautiful" used "evolved" (the only occurrence in the first five editions).

### Reception and the "Eclipse of Darwinism"
*Origin* sold out instantly. Reviews ranged from Huxley’s "How extremely stupid not to have thought of that!" to Owen’s condescending dismissal and Samuel Haughton’s claim that "all that was new was false, and what was true was old." The 1860 Oxford debate (Wilberforce vs. Huxley) became iconic. Asa Gray promoted theistic evolution; Lyell and Henslow remained skeptical. By the late 1860s most scientists accepted evolution, but few accepted natural selection as the *primary* mechanism—alternatives like Lamarckism, orthogenesis, and saltationism flourished during the "eclipse of Darwinism" (c. 1880–1930s). Darwin responded with *Fertilisation of Orchids* (1862, predicting the *Angraecum* moth), *Variation Under Domestication* (1868, including the failed pangenesis hypothesis of heredity), *Descent of Man* (1871, sexual selection, human continuity), *Expression of Emotions* (1872, photographic evidence), and a series of innovative plant physiology books (*Insectivorous Plants*, *Power of Movement in Plants*). He died in 1882, honored by burial in Westminster Abbey beside Newton and Herschel.

### Human Evolution, Sexual Selection, and Botany
Darwin applied natural selection to humans in *Descent of Man* (1871), arguing for continuity of physical and mental traits, and introducing sexual selection to explain "impractical" features (peacock’s tail) and human racial differences—while insisting all humans are one species. *Expression of Emotions* (1872) used photographs to show evolutionary continuity in psychology. His botanical work—orchid coevolution, climbing plants, carnivorous plants, cross- vs. self-fertilization—transformed plant science, demonstrating natural selection’s power in intricate ecological relationships.

### Religious and Social Views
Darwin moved from orthodox Anglicanism (quoting the Bible on the *Beagle*) to agnostic theism. Natural selection removed the need for design; the problem of evil (ichneumon wasps paralyzing caterpillars) made an omnipotent benevolent deity implausible, yet he could not accept the universe as "brute force." In 1879 he wrote, "an agnostic would be the most correct description." He opposed slavery passionately (reinforced by Edmonstone and *Beagle* encounters), rejected polygenism (separate species for races), and saw European colonization as often extinguishing native civilizations. On eugenics (named by Galton in 1883), Darwin acknowledged inheritance of mental traits but warned that withholding aid to the weak would destroy sympathy, "the noblest part of our nature," preferring publicizing inheritance’s importance over state policy.

## Terms
- **Uniformitarianism**: Geological doctrine (Lyell) that present slow processes (erosion, uplift) operating over immense time explain Earth’s features; opposed to catastrophism.
- **Transmutation of species**: Pre-Darwinian term for the transformation of one species into another over time; Darwin later preferred "descent with modification."
- **Natural selection**: Darwin’s mechanism: heritable variation + struggle for existence (Malthusian pressure) → differential survival/reproduction → adaptation and speciation. Analogy: artificial selection by breeders.
- **Struggle for existence**: Metaphor from Malthus/de Candolle: populations grow geometrically, resources arithmetically, so individuals compete for limited necessities.
- **Homology**: Structural similarity due to common ancestry (e.g., barnacle feeding limbs modified from walking legs; fossil *Glyptodon* armor homologous to armadillo).
- **Sexual selection**: Selection driven by mating success (male competition, female choice) rather than survival; explains traits like peacock plumage or human racial differences.
- **Pangenesis**: Darwin’s provisional hypothesis (1868) that "gemmules" from all body parts collect in reproductive organs, blending inheritance; later disproven by Mendelian genetics.
- **Modern evolutionary synthesis**: 1930s–1950s integration of Mendelian genetics, population genetics, and natural selection, establishing selection as the primary mechanism.
- **Eclipse of Darwinism**: Period (c. 1880–1930s) when evolution was accepted but natural selection was a minority view; alternatives (Lamarckism, orthogenesis) dominated.
- **Artificial selection**: Human-directed breeding of domestic plants/animals for desired traits; Darwin’s primary analogy for natural selection.

## Debates and open questions
- **Mechanism of heredity**: Darwin lacked a working theory of inheritance; his pangenesis (1868) failed. The synthesis awaited Mendel’s rediscovery (1900) and population genetics (Fisher, Haldane, Wright).
- **Level of selection**: Darwin focused on individual advantage; group selection and kin selection were later debates.
- **Human origins**: *Origin* avoided the topic; *Descent of Man* (1871) argued for African ancestry (speculative then) and sexual selection for racial differences—still debated.
- **Rate and mode**: Gradualism vs. punctuated equilibrium (Eldredge/Gould, 1972) revisits Darwin’s insistence on "natura non facit saltum."
- **Origin of life**: Darwin’s "warm little pond" speculation (1871 letter to Hooker) remains an open research area.
- **Social Darwinism vs. Darwin’s views**: Post-1880s ideologies (laissez-faire, eugenics, imperialism) invoked his name; Darwin himself cautioned against deriving social policy from natural struggle.
