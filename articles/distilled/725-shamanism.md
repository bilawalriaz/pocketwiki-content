# Shamanism

## Overview  
Shamanism is a spiritual practice in which a practitioner (shaman) enters altered states of consciousness to interact with the spirit world, typically to heal, divine, or otherwise aid others. Originating among Indigenous peoples of Siberia, Mongolia, and northern Europe, it has drawn scholarly attention across disciplines, though the term itself is contested as a Western construct that may misrepresent diverse Indigenous traditions.

## Body  

### Terminology and Definitions  
The word *shamanism* derives from the Russian *шаман*, itself from the Tungusic *samān*, possibly from the Evenki or Manchu languages. Some link it to the Tungusic root *sā-* (“to know”), though linguist Juha Janhunen finds this phonologically irregular. Mircea Eliade proposed the Sanskrit *śramaṇa* as an alternative origin. Europeans adopted the term in the 17th century, sometimes conflating it with *shaitan* (“devil”). Definitions vary: some define shamans broadly as spirit-contactors in trance; others narrowly restrict the term to Siberian Indigenous religions. Anthropologist Manvir Singh proposes a three-part definition: entering non-ordinary states, engaging unseen realities, and providing services like healing or divination.

### Beliefs  
Core beliefs include the existence of spirits that influence human life, the shaman’s ability to communicate with them, and the spiritual causation of disease. Shamans may retrieve lost souls, banish malevolent entities, and journey to otherworlds for guidance. They often use trance-inducing techniques, animal spirit guides, and herbal knowledge. In the Peruvian Amazon, *icaros* (medicine songs) are taught by spirits. Soul and spirit concepts explain phenomena like hunting success and illness. Some cultures believe shamans can both heal and harm.

### Practice  
Shamans enter altered states via drumming, dancing, entheogens, or ritual to traverse the *axis mundi* and access the spirit world. Training often involves a calling through dreams or illness, followed by years of apprenticeship. The “wounded healer” archetype reflects initiatory crises where future shamans face near-death experiences. Tools include drums, amulets, and totemic objects. Shamans serve multiple roles: healer, storyteller, diviner, and *psychopomp* (soul guide). Specialized types exist, such as Nanai psychopomps or Oroqen assistants (*jardalanin*).

### Roles and Functions  
Shamans mediate between communities and spirits, performing healing, sacrifices, and soul retrieval. They may specialize by spirit type or realm. Among the Inuit, *angakkuq* fetch animal souls; among the Tucano, shamans manage ecological resources through myth-symbolic systems. Gender dynamics vary: in Korea, female shamans gained economic independence during the Chosun Dynasty. In modern contexts, ayahuasca tourism in Peru has created new economic niches for shamans.

### Ecological and Economic Aspects  
Shamans often regulate hunting and fishing through taboos and rituals, preventing resource depletion. Inuit shamans receive payments believed to feed helping spirits. Economic roles extend beyond subsistence—shamanism can empower marginalized groups, particularly women, by offering income and social agency.

### Academic Study  
Cognitive approaches include Michael Winkelman’s “neurotheological theory,” which sees shamanism as evolutionarily adaptive due to trance-induced cognitive integration. Manvir Singh’s “by-product model” frames shamanism as a cultural technology exploiting psychological biases. Ecological and systems theory approaches emphasize non-linear causality and Indigenous-science collaboration. Historical origins trace to the Paleolithic, with debated theories like Michael Witzel’s migration hypothesis. Semiotic and hermeneutic approaches analyze shamanic symbolism and ritual as complex communication systems. Medical anthropology highlights illness as social disruption, with shamans restoring communal harmony.

### Decline and Revitalization  
Traditional shamanism has declined due to colonialism, urbanization, and cultural suppression. However, revitalization movements among the Sakha, Tuvans, and others seek to preserve or reconstruct practices. Native American communities reject the label “shamanism,” warning against fraudulent practitioners.

## Terms  
- **Shaman**: A practitioner who enters altered states to interact with spirits.  
- **Axis mundi**: The world axis connecting physical and spiritual realms.  
- **Psychopomp**: A guide of souls, often to the afterlife.  
- **Entheogen**: A psychoactive substance used in spiritual contexts.  
- **Trance**: An altered state of consciousness, often induced ritually.  
- **Icaros**: Healing songs taught by spirits in Amazonian shamanism.  
- **Wounded healer**: An archetype describing a shaman’s initiatory illness.  
- **Neurotheological theory**: A cognitive model linking shamanism to brain integration.  
- **By-product model**: A theory viewing shamanism as exploiting psychological biases.  
- **Ethnohermeneutics**: The interpretation of cultural symbols and rituals.

## Debates and Open Questions  
Scholars debate whether shamanism constitutes a unified global phenomenon or a collection of unrelated practices mislabeled by outsiders. Critics like Alice Kehoe argue the term reinforces colonial and racist stereotypes. The etymology of “shaman” remains disputed, with competing links to Tungusic and Sanskrit roots. The antiquity of shamanism is debated—some claim Paleolithic origins, others caution against projecting modern categories onto ancient remains. The validity of cognitive theories (neurotheological vs. by-product) remains contested. Additionally, the authenticity of modern revivals and the ethics of Western appropriation continue to generate scholarly and ethical debate.
