# South America

## Overview

South America lies entirely in the Western Hemisphere and mostly in the Southern Hemisphere, bordered by the Pacific Ocean to the west, the Atlantic to the north and east, and the Drake Passage to the south. It ranks fourth in area (17,840,000 sq km) and fifth in population (over 434 million as of 2021), with Brazil holding nearly half the continent's people and generating half its GDP. Its history spans human arrival via the Bering Land Bridge at least 15,000 years ago, pre-Columbian civilizations like the Inca, European colonization from the 1530s, independence wars in the early 1800s, and a modern era of democratization and economic diversification.

## Timeline

- **c. 15,000 years ago** — First humans cross Bering Land Bridge into the Americas, eventually reaching South America
- **c. 3500 BC** — Caral–Supe civilization emerges on central Peruvian coast; llama/alpaca domestication begins in Andes
- **1438–1533** — Inca Empire dominates the Andes from capital Cusco
- **1494** — Treaty of Tordesillas divides non-European lands between Portugal and Spain
- **1530s** — Spanish and Portuguese conquistadors begin exploiting South America
- **1807–14** — Peninsular War destabilizes colonies, sparking independence movements
- **1822** — Dom Pedro I proclaims independent Kingdom of Brazil; Bolívar and San Martín meet at Guayaquil
- **1825** — Cisplatine Province declares independence from Brazil, leading to creation of Uruguay
- **1864–70** — Paraguayan War devastates Paraguay (60% population loss, 90% of men)
- **1879–83** — War of the Pacific: Chile defeats Bolivia and Peru, leaving Bolivia landlocked
- **1960s–70s** — Military dictatorships overthrow governments in Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Uruguay, Paraguay
- **1980s–90s** — Wave of democratization; Argentina defaults on debt in early 21st century

## Body

### Prehistory and Pre-Columbian civilizations

South America's first inhabitants crossed the Bering Land Bridge at least 15,000 years ago, migrating south through the Isthmus of Panama. The Monte Verde II site in Chile dates to around 14,500 years ago. The Fishtail projectile point style spread from about 13,000 years ago and vanished around 11,000 years ago, coinciding with the extinction of the continent's megafauna. Maize appeared in northern South America by roughly 6,000 years ago, and by 2000 BC agrarian communities with irrigation and widespread fishing were settled throughout the Andes.

The earliest known civilization was Caral–Supe on the central Peruvian coast (c. 3500 BC), a pre-ceramic culture with monumental architecture, contemporary with Ancient Egypt's pyramids. Chavín civilization (900–300 BC) followed. The Moche (100 BC–700 AD), Paracas and Nazca (400 BC–800 AD) flourished in Peru, while Tiahuanaco (100 BC–1200 AD) in Bolivia ran a large commercial network. The Muisca in Colombia formed a confederation of clans. The Inca Empire (1438–1533), known as Tawantin suyu, dominated the Andes, connecting nine to fourteen million people via a 25,000 km road system with precise stonework and terrace farming. The Mapuche in Chile resisted European settlers for over 300 years in the Arauco War.

### European colonization and slavery

In 1494, Portugal and Spain signed the Treaty of Tordesillas, dividing non-European lands along a meridian 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands; Spain received most of South America, Portugal the east (Brazil). From the 1530s, conquistadors exploited the land and people. European diseases (smallpox, influenza, measles, typhus) caused large-scale depopulation, as did forced labor systems like haciendas and the mit'a. Enslaved Africans, who had immunities, replaced them; nearly 40% of all African slaves trafficked to the Americas went to Brazil, with an estimated 4.9 million arriving between 1501 and 1866. Spanish colonists mainly enslaved indigenous Americans, unlike other European colonies. The Portuguese Crown abolished indigenous enslavement in Brazil in 1750. After independence, Chile abolished slavery first (1823), Brazil last (1888).

### Independence and nation-building

The Peninsular War (1807–14) transformed colonial politics. Napoleon invaded Portugal (the Braganza court fled to Brazil) and captured Spain's King Ferdinand VII. Spanish colonies formed local Juntas, beginning the Spanish American wars of independence between patriots and royalists. Simón Bolívar and José de San Martín led liberation; they met at the Guayaquil Conference in 1822 but disagreed on governance. Bolívar's forces won at the Battle of Ayacucho in 1824. Brazil's Dom Pedro I proclaimed independence in 1822, accepted by Portugal in 1825. New nations fragmented: the Republic of Entre Ríos (1820) was reincorporated, the Cisplatine Province became Uruguay (1828) after British intervention, and the Riograndense Republic was reincorporated into Brazil in 1845.

### Wars and conflicts

The Paraguayan War (1864–70) was the longest and bloodiest inter-state war in Latin American history, triggered by Paraguayan dictator Francisco Solano López invading Brazil. The Triple Alliance (Brazil, Argentina, Uruguay) defeated Paraguay, which lost 40% of its territory and 60% of its population. The War of the Pacific (1879–83) saw Chile defeat Bolivia and Peru, annexing territories and leaving Bolivia landlocked. Brazil was the only South American country in World War I and World War II. The Falklands War (1982) ended in Argentine defeat. The last international war on South American soil was the 1995 Cenepa War between Ecuador and Peru.

### Dictatorships and contemporary politics

In the 1960s–70s, military dictatorships overthrew governments in Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Uruguay, and Paraguay, detaining tens of thousands of political prisoners under US Cold War "National Security" doctrine. Since the 1980s, democratization has spread. A "pink tide" of leftist governments began with Hugo Chávez's election in Venezuela (1998). All South American countries are presidential republics except Suriname (parliamentary); it is the only inhabited continent without monarchies. Regional integration includes the Andean Community, Mercosur, and Unasur.

### Geography and climate

The Andes dominate western South America; the east has highlands and vast lowlands with the Amazon, Orinoco, and Paraná rivers. The continent holds world superlatives: Angel Falls (highest uninterrupted waterfall), the Amazon River (largest by volume), the Atacama Desert (driest non-polar place), and the Amazon rainforest (largest). Most climate zones are present; the Amazon basin averages 27°C with high rainfall, while Tierra del Fuego has a cold "Siberian type" climate. Sea currents like the Humboldt and Falkland currents influence climate. South America is highly biodiverse, though 83% of its large mammals went extinct around 11,000 years ago.

### Economy

Since 1930, the continent has diversified economically. Brazil, Argentina, Colombia, and Chile are the most industrialized, accounting for over 80% of the region's economy. Brazil is the world's largest producer of sugarcane, soy, coffee, and orange, and the largest exporter of chicken meat. Mining is crucial for Chile, Peru, and Bolivia: Chile produces about a third of world copper; Brazil is the second-largest iron ore producer; Colombia is the world's largest emerald producer. Brazil is the 10th-largest oil producer (2.8 million barrels/day in 2019). Energy is mostly hydroelectric; Brazil and Argentina have nuclear power plants. Tourism is growing, with Brazil hosting the 2016 Olympics.

### Demographics and culture

The population (over 428 million) forms a "hollow continent" concentrated on the margins. Spanish and Portuguese each have about 200 million speakers. Indigenous languages include Quechua, Aymara, and Guaraní (recognized as national languages alongside Spanish). About 90% of South Americans are Christian (82% Roman Catholic). Genetic admixture is high: in Argentina, European influence is 65–80%; in Peru, Amerindian ancestry averages 63.7%. Mestizos are the largest ethnic group in Peru, Paraguay, Venezuela, Colombia, and Ecuador. Culture blends indigenous, Iberian, African, and immigrant influences, with famous music genres (samba, tango, cumbia), the Latin American Boom in literature, and football as the most popular sport (Brazil has won the FIFA World Cup a record five times).

## Terms

- **Mestizo**: Person of mixed European and Amerindian ancestry
- **Mit'a**: Forced labor system in Spanish colonial mining
- **Caudillo**: Military strongman or political boss
- **Uti possidetis iuris**: Principle that new nations inherit colonial borders
- **Dreadnought**: New class of heavily armed battleship that triggered a naval arms race
- **Pink tide**: Wave of leftist and center-left governments elected in the region from 1998
- **Terere**: Paraguayan version of mate, served cold
- **Megafauna**: Large animals (over 44 kg) that mostly went extinct ~11,000 years ago
- **Intertropical Convergence Zone**: Belt of low pressure near the equator where trade winds meet

## Debates and open questions

The Treaty of Tordesillas line was not strictly enforced due to inaccurate longitude measurements, enabling Portuguese expansion of Brazil. The dating of Chavín civilization (900 BC) is given "according to some estimates and archaeological finds," suggesting scholarly debate. Whether Panama belongs to North or South America is contested—the Darién watershed or Panama Canal are both cited as possible borders. The sovereignty of the Falkland Islands is disputed by Argentina.
