# Tropical cyclone

## Overview
A tropical cyclone is a warm-cored, non-frontal low-pressure system with a closed circulation and deep convection, forming over warm tropical oceans and deriving energy from water vapor condensation. Depending on location and strength, it is called a hurricane (Atlantic/Northeast Pacific), typhoon (Northwest Pacific), or simply cyclone (Indian/South Pacific). These systems affect millions annually, causing catastrophic wind, flood, and storm-surge damage, and are intensifying under climate change.

## Body

### Definition and Terminology
A tropical cyclone is a warm-cored, non-frontal synoptic-scale low-pressure system with a well-defined center surrounded by deep convection and a closed surface wind circulation. Formation is confirmed when mean surface winds exceed 35 kn (65 km/h). The term varies geographically: hurricane (Atlantic/Northeast Pacific), typhoon (Northwest Pacific), severe cyclonic storm (North Indian Ocean), and tropical cyclone (Indian/South Pacific). "Tropical" refers to warm-sea origin; "cyclone" describes counterclockwise (Northern Hemisphere) or clockwise (Southern Hemisphere) wind rotation driven by the Coriolis effect.

### Formation
Tropical cyclones develop over warm ocean waters (>26–27°C) via the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), where heated air rises in thunderstorm clusters. Required conditions include high sea surface temperatures, low vertical wind shear, atmospheric instability, mid-tropospheric humidity, sufficient Coriolis force, and a pre-existing disturbance. Climate oscillations like ENSO and the Madden-Julian Oscillation modulate timing and frequency. Globally, ~86 tropical storms form annually, with 47 reaching hurricane strength and 20 becoming intense (Category 3+).

### Intensity
Intensity depends on wind speed and pressure. The record holder is Typhoon Tip (1979, 870 hPa, 165 kn winds). Rapid intensification occurs when winds increase ≥30 kn in 24 hours, requiring sea surface temperatures near 30°C, deep warm water, low wind shear, and upper-level anticyclones. Warm ocean layers (high Tropical Cyclone Heat Potential) fuel intensification, while upwelling cools surface waters and weakens storms. Smaller cyclones intensify faster; the Fujiwhara effect can weaken smaller systems interacting with larger ones.

### Structure
Mature cyclones feature a central eye (30–65 km diameter) of sinking air and calm weather, surrounded by the eyewall—the zone of strongest winds, heaviest rain, and tallest clouds. Outer rainbands spiral outward. Eyewall replacement cycles occur in intense storms, where outer rings replace inner eyewalls, temporarily weakening the storm. Size ranges from 100–2,000 km; Typhoon Tip spanned 2,170 km, while Tropical Storm Marco (2008) was only 37 km wide.

### Movement
Storm tracks result from environmental steering (background winds) and beta drift (poleward-westward drift due to latitude-varying Coriolis force). Cyclones typically move westward along the subtropical ridge, then recurve poleward via interaction with mid-latitude westerlies. The Fujiwhara effect causes two nearby cyclones to orbit each other. Translation speeds have slowed since 1949, possibly due to climate change.

### Classification and Naming
Classification varies by basin: hurricanes (Atlantic/Eastern Pacific), typhoons (Western Pacific), severe cyclonic storms (North Indian Ocean). Naming began with Clement Wragge in 1887 and is now managed by twelve regional meteorological services using predetermined lists. Names of catastrophic storms are retired. Systems receive identification codes (e.g., two-digit number + suffix letter).

### Climate Interactions
Tropical cyclones peak globally in September, with each basin having distinct seasons. ENSO significantly influences tracks: El Niño reduces Atlantic activity and shifts Pacific impacts westward; La Niña enhances Atlantic activity and increases Philippines/China risk. Climate change increases rainfall (high confidence), intensifies Category 4–5 storms (+13% with 2°C warming), and extends poleward ranges. Rapid intensification trends are rising in the Atlantic. However, overall frequency may decrease, with regional variations.

### Effects
Storm surge causes ~90% of cyclone deaths; Cyclone Mahina (1899) produced a 13 m surge. Inland flooding, tornadoes, and wind damage follow. Notable events include the 1970 Bangladesh cyclone (300,000 deaths), Hurricane Katrina ($125 billion damage), and Cyclone Idai (1,302 deaths). Cyclones also redistribute heat globally, influence ocean currents, and reshape coastlines—Cyclone Bebe expanded Tuvalu, while Hurricane Walaka destroyed East Island.

### Observation and Forecasting
Satellites, reconnaissance aircraft, and Doppler radar track cyclones. The Dvorak technique uses satellite imagery to estimate intensity via T-numbers. Modern forecasting relies on computer models and GPS dropsondes. Track forecasts have improved significantly, but intensity prediction remains challenging due to complex internal dynamics.

## Terms
- **Coriolis effect**: Apparent deflection of moving air due to Earth’s rotation; enables cyclonic spin.
- **Eyewall**: Ring of thunderstorms around the eye with maximum winds and rainfall.
- **Saffir-Simpson scale**: Hurricane intensity scale (Category 1–5) based on wind speed.
- **Tropical Cyclone Heat Potential (TCHP)**: Ocean heat content fueling cyclone intensification.
- **Beta drift**: Slow poleward-westward motion caused by latitude-varying Coriolis force.
- **Fujiwhara effect**: Orbital interaction between two nearby tropical cyclones.
- **Upwelling**: Wind-driven mixing of cold deep water during cyclone passage, weakening the storm.
- **Rapid intensification**: Wind speed increase ≥30 kn in 24 hours.
- **Recurvature**: Poleward and eastward track shift near western ocean basin edges.
- **Dvorak technique**: Satellite-based method assigning T-numbers to estimate cyclone intensity.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Frequency trends**: Climate models project overall frequency decreases, but observations show mixed regional signals (increases in North Atlantic/central Pacific, decreases in South Indian/Western North Pacific).
- **Translation speed slowdown**: Observed since 1949, but attribution to climate change remains uncertain.
- **Intensity attribution**: While Category 4–5 increases are linked to warming, quantifying human influence on individual storms (e.g., Hurricane Ida) involves probabilistic assessments.
- **Paleostorm records**: Proxy data suggest centuries-long activity cycles, but linking these to modern anthropogenic trends is debated.
- **Extraterrestrial cyclogenesis**: Theoretical models suggest tropical cyclones could form on tidally locked exoplanets, but observational evidence is lacking.
