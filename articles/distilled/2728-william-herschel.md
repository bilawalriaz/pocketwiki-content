# William Herschel

## Overview
Frederick William Herschel (1738–1822) was a German-born British astronomer and composer whose pioneering work fundamentally expanded humanity's view of the cosmos. He is most famous for discovering the planet Uranus in 1781, the first new planet found since antiquity, and for his systematic surveys that revealed thousands of nebulae and double stars. His innovations in telescope construction and his discovery of infrared radiation established him as a foundational figure in observational astronomy.

## Timeline
- **15 November 1738** — Born in the Electorate of Hanover, Germany.
- **1757** — Emigrated to England from Hanover.
- **1766** — First recorded astronomical observation.
- **1774** — Constructed his first large telescope.
- **13 March 1781** — Discovered the planet Uranus.
- **1782** — Published first catalogue of double stars; appointed "The King's Astronomer."
- **1786** — Published first catalogue of nebulae.
- **1789** — Discovered the moons Mimas and Enceladus of Saturn using his 40-foot telescope.
- **1800** — Discovered infrared radiation.
- **1802** — Published catalogue of 500 new nebulae; proposed the term "asteroid."
- **1816** — Made a Knight of the Royal Guelphic Order.
- **25 August 1822** — Died in Slough, England.

## Body

### Early Life and Musical Career
Herschel was born in Hanover, one of ten children of a military band oboist. Following his father into the Hanover Military Band, he fled to England in 1757 during the Seven Years' War. He established himself as a successful musician, playing multiple instruments and composing symphonies, concertos, and church music. He became organist and director of concerts in Bath, where his sister Caroline joined him in 1772.

### Transition to Astronomy and Telescope Building
Herschel's interest in astronomy began around 1766. He taught himself telescope construction, spending up to 16 hours a day grinding and polishing mirrors from speculum metal. He developed the "Herschelian" reflecting telescope, which eliminated the small diagonal mirror to improve light-gathering. He built over 400 mirrors and sold at least 60 complete telescopes. His most famous instrument was the 40-foot telescope, funded by King George III, which was the largest scientific instrument of its time.

### Discovery of Uranus and Double Stars
On 13 March 1781, while surveying double stars, Herschel noticed an object with a disk in Gemini. After verification, it was confirmed as a new planet beyond Saturn, initially named "Georgian star" but later universally called Uranus. This discovery brought him instant fame and royal patronage. His systematic search for double stars led to catalogues of over 800 systems. In 1802, he announced the groundbreaking hypothesis that many double stars were "binary sidereal systems" orbiting each other under mutual gravitational attraction.

### Deep Sky Surveys and Nebulae
From 1782 to 1802, Herschel conducted systematic surveys for "deep-sky" objects, discovering over 2,400 nebulae and clusters. He published three major catalogues, classifying objects by brightness and structure. His work revealed that many objects listed as nebulae in the Messier catalogue were actually star clusters. His discoveries, supplemented by his sister Caroline and son John, formed the basis for the New General Catalogue (NGC), still used today.

### Collaboration with Caroline Herschel
Caroline Herschel was William's essential collaborator. She polished telescope mirrors, copied catalogues, and became his recorder, shouting observations from the telescope for her to write down. She made independent discoveries, including eight comets and the nebula NGC 205 (M110). In 1787, she became the first woman in England to receive a government salary as an astronomer. After William's death, she continued his work, organizing nebulae into zones that later formed the NGC.

### Scientific Contributions and Speculations
Herschel pioneered astronomical spectrophotometry. In 1800, while testing filters for sunlight, he discovered a temperature rise beyond the red end of the visible spectrum, identifying infrared radiation. He studied the Sun, correlating sunspot activity with wheat prices to suggest a link between solar variation and climate. He also proposed the first observational model of the Milky Way as a disk (incorrectly placing the Sun at its center) and speculated about life on other planets, including the Moon and even the Sun's interior.

## Terms
*   **Speculum metal:** A copper-tin alloy used to make mirrors for reflecting telescopes before silvered glass.
*   **Herschelian telescope:** A reflecting telescope design that tilts the primary mirror to view the image directly, eliminating the small diagonal mirror.
*   **Binary star system:** A pair of stars that orbit a common center of mass under mutual gravitational attraction.
*   **Nebula:** A generic term for any diffuse astronomical object, including galaxies, before the extragalactic nature of many was confirmed.
*   **Asteroid:** A term introduced by Herschel in 1802 to describe the star-like appearance of small celestial bodies like minor planets.
*   **Infrared radiation:** Electromagnetic radiation with wavelengths longer than those of visible light, discovered by Herschel in 1800.
*   **Solar apex:** The direction toward which the Sun is moving relative to nearby stars.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Sunspot-climate correlation:** Herschel's hypothesis linking low sunspot numbers to high wheat prices was ridiculed by contemporaries. The significance of this correlation remains debated, though some studies find support over specific historical periods.
*   **Life on other worlds:** Herschel's confident assertions of life on the Moon and other planets were in line with contemporary scientific thought but were speculative and not based on empirical evidence.
*   **Galactic model:** His model of the Milky Way as a disk with the Sun at the center was incorrect, later replaced by 20th-century work showing a galactic center far from our solar system.
