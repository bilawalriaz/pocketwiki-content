# Hormone

## Overview
A hormone is a signaling molecule produced in multicellular organisms (animals, plants, fungi) that travels from its site of synthesis to distant target tissues to regulate physiology and behavior. Hormones are defined by function rather than structure; they encompass diverse chemical classes including peptides, steroids, amino acid derivatives, eicosanoids, and gases. In vertebrates, specialized endocrine glands secrete hormones into the bloodstream, where water-soluble types bind surface receptors via second messengers and lipid-soluble types enter cells to regulate gene transcription. Secretion is governed by negative feedback loops maintaining homeostasis. The field originated in the 19th century with Berthold’s demonstration of a testicular factor (testosterone), Oliver and Schäfer’s isolation of adrenaline (the first hormone discovered), and Bayliss and Starling’s identification of secretin and coining of the term "hormone."

## Timeline
- **1849** — Arnold Adolph Berthold demonstrates that testes secrete a chemical factor (later identified as testosterone) responsible for male secondary sexual characteristics and behavior in roosters.
- **1880** — Charles and Francis Darwin propose a "transmissible substance" (later auxin) communicates light direction from plant tip to stem.
- **1894** — George Oliver and Edward Albert Schäfer discover adrenaline in adrenal extracts, the first hormone identified.
- **1902** — William Bayliss and Ernest Starling identify secretin, proving humoral (blood-borne) control of pancreatic secretion.
- **1905** — Ernest Starling coins the term "hormone" from Greek *hormôn* ("setting in motion").
- **1920s** — Frits Warmolt Went and Nikolai Cholodny independently prove asymmetric auxin accumulation causes phototropic bending.
- **1933** — Kögl, Haagen-Smit, and Erxleben isolate and name auxin, the first plant hormone.

## Body

### Chemical Classes and Distribution
Hormones are functionally defined signaling molecules found in multicellular organisms including plants, animals, fungi, brown algae, and red algae. They are structurally diverse: **eicosanoids** (prostaglandins, thromboxanes), **steroids** (oestrogen, brassinosteroid), **amino acid derivatives** (epinephrine, auxin), **proteins/peptides** (insulin, CLE peptides), and **gases** (ethylene, nitric oxide). Unicellular organisms produce similar signaling molecules, but consensus is lacking on whether these qualify as hormones. Vertebrates utilize endocrine glands; invertebrates possess unusual hormones like the sesquiterpenoid juvenile hormone; plants produce hormones (abscisic acid, auxin, cytokinin, ethylene, gibberellin) without specialized glands, with production sites shifting by age and environment.

### Mechanisms of Action
Hormones bind specific receptor proteins on target cells. **Water-soluble hormones** (peptides, amines) bind cell-surface receptors—often G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs)—triggering second-messenger cascades (e.g., cyclic AMP) that alter cytoplasmic proteins, ion channels, or gene transcription. **Lipid-soluble hormones** (steroids, thyroid hormones) diffuse through the plasma membrane to bind intracellular nuclear receptors; the hormone-receptor complex regulates DNA transcription. Exceptions exist: brassinosteroids are lipid-soluble but bind cell-surface receptors. Hormones may also act via non-genomic pathways that synergize with genomic effects.

### Transport and Activation
Water-soluble hormones travel freely in blood. Lipid-soluble hormones bind carrier glycoproteins (e.g., thyroxine-binding globulin) to form ligand-protein complexes, extending half-life and creating a reservoir that buffers concentration fluctuations. Some hormones (insulin, growth hormone) are secreted fully active; others are released as inactive **prohormones** requiring controlled activation steps. The endocrine system secretes directly into fenestrated capillaries; the exocrine system uses ducts; **paracrine** hormones diffuse locally through interstitial spaces.

### Regulation of Secretion
Secretion rates are controlled by homeostatic negative feedback triggered by overproduction of a hormone's *effect*, not merely high hormone concentration. Stimuli include: other hormones (tropic hormones like TSH), plasma ion/nutrient levels, neural input, and environmental changes (light, temperature). **Tropic hormones** stimulate other endocrine glands (e.g., TSH → thyroid). Cells may store inactive pre-/prohormones for rapid activation. **Eicosanoids** act as local hormones with rapid degradation limiting spread. **Receptor antagonists** (competing ligands) inhibit hormone action by blocking receptor binding.

### Hormone-Behavior Interactions
Hormone concentrations influence the probability of behaviors but do not determine them outright; external stimuli remain relevant. A bidirectional feedback loop exists: hormones affect behavior, and behavior/environment affect hormone levels. This loop stabilizes episodic secretion (behaviors triggered by hormones prevent continuous release). Three criteria infer a hormone-behavior link: (1) behavior frequency matches hormonal source frequency; (2) behavior is absent if the hormonal source is absent; (3) restoring the hormonal source restores the behavior.

### Comparison with Neurotransmitters
Hormones act over larger spatial/temporal scales (seconds to hours, body-wide via circulation) versus neurotransmitters (milliseconds, micrometer distances along fixed nerve tracts). Neural signaling is all-or-nothing (digital); hormonal signaling is continuously variable (analog, concentration-dependent). **Neurohormones** bridge both: produced by neuroendocrine cells receiving neural input, they are released into blood like classic hormones but originate from electrical neural signals.

### Therapeutic Use
Hormones and analogs are widely used medically: estrogens/progestogens (contraception, HRT), thyroxine/levothyroxine (hypothyroidism), steroids (autoimmune, respiratory diseases), insulin (diabetes), adrenaline equivalents (otolaryngology), vitamin D/steroid creams (dermatology). **Pharmacologic (supraphysiological) doses** exceed natural levels; effects may differ qualitatively and carry adverse effects (e.g., glucocorticoids suppress inflammation at high doses).

## Terms
- **Hormone** — A signaling molecule produced in one tissue, transported (usually via blood) to distant target cells to regulate physiology/behavior.
- **Endocrine gland** — Specialized organ secreting hormones directly into the bloodstream (e.g., thyroid, testes).
- **Prohormone** — Biologically inactive precursor requiring enzymatic conversion to become an active hormone.
- **Tropic hormone** — Hormone that stimulates another endocrine gland to secrete its hormones (e.g., TSH).
- **G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR)** — Seven-transmembrane cell-surface receptor class mediating many peptide/eicosanoid hormone signals via second messengers.
- **Second messenger** — Intracellular molecule (e.g., cyclic AMP) generated after hormone-receptor binding to amplify and relay the signal.
- **Negative feedback** — Homeostatic loop where a hormone's effect inhibits its own further secretion.
- **Paracrine signaling** — Hormone diffusion through interstitial fluid to nearby target cells, not via bloodstream.
- **Neurohormone** — Hormone released by neuroendocrine cells in response to neural electrical signals; enters bloodstream.
- **Pharmacologic dose** — Hormone amount far exceeding natural physiological levels, used therapeutically.

## Debates and open questions
- Whether signaling molecules in unicellular organisms meet the definition of hormones remains unresolved.
- The hierarchical model of hormonal signaling (linear steps) is acknowledged as an oversimplification; real systems involve multiple target cell types and tissue-specific responses to the same hormone.
- Brassinosteroids challenge the lipid-soluble/hydrophilic receptor paradigm: they are lipid-soluble polyhydroxysteroids yet bind cell-surface receptors.
- The precise mechanisms by which hormone concentration probabilities translate into specific behavioral outcomes, and the relative weighting of hormonal vs. environmental stimuli, are active research areas.
