# Chernobyl disaster

## Overview
The Chernobyl disaster was a catastrophic nuclear accident on 26 April 1986 at the No. 4 reactor of the Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant in the Ukrainian SSR, Soviet Union. It is the worst nuclear disaster in history, one of only two rated Level 7 on the International Nuclear Event Scale. The event released massive radioactive contamination across Europe, prompted large-scale evacuations, and had profound long-term health, environmental, and political consequences, contributing to the Soviet Union's dissolution.

## Timeline
- **25 April 1986** — A safety test on Reactor No. 4 begins with a planned power reduction.
- **26 April 1986, 01:23** — The test commences; a power surge causes explosions and a graphite fire.
- **26 April 1986, 05:00** — Fires on adjacent roofs are extinguished; the core fire continues.
- **27 April 1986** — Pripyat (population ~49,000) is evacuated.
- **28 April 1986** — Elevated radiation is detected in Sweden; the USSR publicly acknowledges the accident.
- **May 1986** — A massive "liquidator" workforce begins cleanup; a concrete sarcophagus is built.
- **December 1986** — The sarcophagus is completed, sealing the destroyed reactor.
- **15 December 2000** — The last operational reactor (No. 3) at Chernobyl is shut down.
- **2016–2018** — The New Safe Confinement (NSC) is constructed and slid over the old sarcophagus.
- **February 2025** — A drone strike damages the NSC, compromising its confinement function.

## Body

### Accident Sequence and Causes
The disaster occurred during a safety test to see if the reactor's steam turbine could power coolant pumps during a brief outage. The test was delayed, leading an inexperienced night shift to conduct it. An unexpected power drop caused operators to withdraw too many control rods, leaving the reactor unstable. When the test began and the emergency shutdown (scram) was initiated, a fatal design flaw in the RBMK reactor's control rods caused a power surge instead of a shutdown. This led to a steam explosion that destroyed the reactor, followed by a second explosion that breached containment and started a graphite fire, spreading radioactive contamination.

### Emergency Response and Immediate Aftermath
The initial response was hampered by a lack of information and delayed official acknowledgment. Firefighters, unaware of the extreme radiation, battled roof fires. Pripyat was not evacuated until 36 hours after the explosion. A government commission, led by Valery Legasov, ordered the evacuation of a 10 km zone, later expanded to 30 km. Over 500,000 "liquidators" were involved in the cleanup, which included dropping materials on the reactor from helicopters and constructing a concrete sarcophagus. The immediate human toll included 2 direct deaths from the explosions, 28 deaths from acute radiation syndrome (ARS) within three months, and 134 confirmed ARS cases.

### Long-Term Consequences and Remediation
The disaster contaminated approximately 100,000 square kilometers of land. The most documented long-term health effect is a sharp increase in thyroid cancer among those who were children or adolescents at the time, with about 6,000 cases reported by 2005. Estimates of eventual cancer deaths vary widely, from a UN estimate of up to 4,000 to other studies suggesting tens of thousands. The economic cost is estimated between $235 billion and $700 billion. The original sarcophagus was replaced by the New Safe Confinement (NSC) to enable future debris removal, with cleanup scheduled for completion by 2065. The 2,600 km² Chernobyl Exclusion Zone remains largely uninhabited, though it has become a wildlife refuge and tourist destination.

### Global Impact and Legacy
The accident profoundly impacted global nuclear policy, leading to a decline in new reactor construction and strengthening international safety standards and treaties, such as the Convention on Early Notification of a Nuclear Accident. It exposed severe flaws in Soviet safety culture and transparency, acting as a catalyst for glasnost. The disaster also galvanized anti-nuclear movements in several countries, notably Germany and Italy. It remains a potent cultural symbol, inspiring numerous works of art, film, and literature.

## Terms
- **RBMK-1000**: A Soviet-designed graphite-moderated, water-cooled reactor with a known positive void coefficient, a key design flaw.
- **Positive Void Coefficient**: A characteristic where increased steam bubbles in the coolant lead to increased reactivity, creating a dangerous positive feedback loop.
- **Scram (AZ-5)**: An emergency shutdown achieved by rapidly inserting all control rods into the core.
- **Control Rod**: Neutron-absorbing rods used to control fission. In the RBMK design, they had graphite tips that initially increased reactivity when inserted.
- **Acute Radiation Syndrome (ARS)**: Health effects present within 24 hours of high-dose ionizing radiation exposure.
- **Corium**: A lava-like mixture of molten nuclear fuel, cladding, concrete, and other materials formed during a meltdown.
- **Sarcophagus**: The original concrete and steel structure built in 1986 to seal the destroyed reactor.
- **New Safe Confinement (NSC)**: A massive arch-shaped structure completed in 2016, designed to enclose the old sarcophagus for 100 years to allow dismantling.
- **Liquidators**: The estimated 500,000–800,000 personnel who dealt with the disaster's consequences.
- **Exclusion Zone**: The officially designated area around Chernobyl from which people were evacuated and where settlement is forbidden.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Cause of the Second Explosion**: While a steam explosion is accepted, alternative hypotheses include a hydrogen explosion or a localized nuclear fizzle.
- **Long-Term Health Effects**: The precise number of attributable cancer deaths is highly debated, with estimates ranging from a few thousand to tens of thousands.
- **Fate of the Corium**: The long-term stability of the "Elephant's Foot" and other corium is uncertain, with concerns about potential radioactive release over centuries.
- **Criticality Risk**: Observations of increasing neutron density suggest ongoing low-level fission reactions. The potential for a self-sustaining reaction remains a concern for future cleanup.
