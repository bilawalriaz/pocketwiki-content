# Muqaddimah

## Overview
The *Muqaddimah* is a 1377 book by the historian Ibn Khaldun, originally intended as the introduction to his larger work of world history, the *Kitab al-ʿIbar*. It is now regarded as an independent masterpiece and a foundational text for the social sciences, including sociology, historiography, and economics. Its core innovation is a "new science" (*ʿilm al-ʿumrān*) that uses a historical, empirical, and rational method to study the evolution of human society and civilization.

## Timeline
- **1377** — Ibn Khaldun completes the first version of the *Muqaddimah* at Qalʿat ibn Salama.
- **15th c.** — The work is cited by some Maghrebi and Egyptian scholars, but receives mixed reception.
- **1657-1716** — Ottoman historians value its theories but do not apply them to their own society.
- **1749** — The first five chapters are translated into Ottoman Turkish by Mehmed Pirizade.
- **1857** — The *Muqaddimah* is first printed as a standalone volume in Cairo.
- **1858** — Étienne-Marc Quatremère publishes the first Arabic edition in Paris.
- **1863** — William McGuckin de Slane publishes the first French translation.
- **1958** — Franz Rosenthal publishes the first English translation.

## Body

### Critique of Historiography and Scientific Method
Ibn Khaldun begins by criticizing common errors in historical writing, such as partisanship, over-reliance on sources, and the failure to understand social laws. He argues that historians often exaggerate figures, especially for armies and wealth, due to a desire for sensationalism. To counter this, he introduces a "new science" for studying *ʿumrān* (human society and civilization), emphasizing empirical observation, rational analysis, and a critical assessment of sources based on their historical context.

### Sociology and the Cycle of Civilization
The central concept is *ʿasabiyyah* (group feeling or social cohesion), which Ibn Khaldun identifies as the glue that binds tribes and enables the formation of states. He posits a cyclical theory of history: a dynasty rises on the periphery using strong *ʿasabiyyah*, establishes a sedentary civilization, but then becomes lax and luxurious. This decline in *ʿasabiyyah* allows a new, more cohesive group to overthrow it, restarting the cycle. He contrasts nomadic (*badāwa*) and sedentary (*ḥaḍāra*) societies, arguing that complex urban life, crafts, and sciences require prolonged dynastic stability.

### Economics and Political Theory
Ibn Khaldun links *ʿasabiyyah* to economic growth, stating that greater social cohesion enables a more complex division of labor and increased wealth. He describes a theory of prices based on supply and demand and introduces a labor theory of value. Politically, he argues that the best state is a caliphate based on divine law (*sharia*), followed by a just secular state, with tyranny being the worst. He famously describes government as "an institution which prevents injustice other than such as it commits itself."

### Natural Sciences and Theology
Ibn Khaldun presents a hierarchical view of creation, from minerals to plants to animals to humans, suggesting a form of development where each stage is "prepared" for the next. He notes that humans evolved from a stage "from the world of the monkeys," though scholars debate whether this anticipates modern evolution or reflects the medieval "great chain of being." Theologically, he follows the Ash'ari school of Sunni Islam, critiques alchemy, and discusses the science of hadith and Islamic jurisprudence (*fiqh*), acknowledging the inevitability of change in human customs and laws.

### Assessment of Civilizations
Ibn Khaldun applies his framework to various societies. He attributes the historical influence of Berbers in North Africa to their strong nomadic *ʿasabiyyah* and notes the Maghreb's lack of deep sedentary culture before Islam. He uses climatic determinism to describe Sub-Saharan African societies, often in derogatory terms, while accurately documenting states like the Ghana Empire and the logistics of the East African slave trade. He also observes that early Islamic scholarship was dominated by non-Arab (*ʿAjam*) populations due to their sedentary urban traditions.

## Terms
- **ʿAsabiyyah**: Social cohesion, group feeling, or solidarity that binds a community and enables political power.
- **ʿUmrān**: Human society and civilization; the subject of Ibn Khaldun's "new science."
- **Badāwa**: Nomadic or Bedouin lifestyle, associated with strong *ʿasabiyyah* but limited urban development.
- **Ḥaḍāra**: Sedentary, urbanized life, which fosters crafts, sciences, and complex economies.
- **ʿAjam**: A term for non-Arab populations, particularly Persians, who contributed significantly to Islamic sciences.
- **Sharia**: Islamic divine law, which Ibn Khaldun considers the foundation for the ideal state.
- **Fiqh**: Islamic jurisprudence; the human understanding and application of divine law.
- **Laffer Curve**: A modern economic concept anticipating Ibn Khaldun's observation that excessive taxation reduces revenue by discouraging production.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Evolutionary Theory**: Scholars debate whether Ibn Khaldun's description of a progression from minerals to humans anticipates modern biological evolution or is a description of the static medieval "great chain of being."
- **Climatic Determinism**: His theories on the influence of climate on human traits, especially regarding Sub-Saharan Africans, are criticized as reflecting ancient Greek models and containing racist elements.
- **Historical Accuracy**: Modern historians challenge his demographic claims, such as the "Ajam" monopoly on early Islamic scholarship, arguing he overlooked the Arab diaspora in eastern cities.
- **Almoravid Conquest**: His account of the Almoravid conquest causing the fall of the Ghana Empire is contested by archaeologists, who find little evidence for a military invasion in 1076.
