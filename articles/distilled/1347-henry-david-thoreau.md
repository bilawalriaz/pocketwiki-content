# Henry David Thoreau

## Overview
Henry David Thoreau (1817–1862) was an American naturalist, essayist, poet, and philosopher, best known as a leading Transcendentalist. His two most influential works—*Walden* (1854), a reflection on simple living in nature, and "Civil Disobedience" (1849), an argument for resisting unjust government—have shaped environmentalism, civil rights, and anarchist thought. His detailed journals and observations of natural history anticipated modern ecology and environmental history.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Born into a modest New England family, Thoreau was named after his deceased uncle. His father was a pencil maker of French Protestant descent; his maternal grandfather, Asa Dunbar, led Harvard’s 1766 "Butter Rebellion." Thoreau studied at Harvard (1833–1837), where he refused to pay for his master’s diploma, declaring, “Let every sheep keep its own skin.” After graduation, he taught briefly and co-founded the Concord Academy with his brother John, introducing progressive ideas like nature walks. John died in 1842 from tetanus, deeply affecting Thoreau.

### Transcendentalism and Intellectual Circles
Returning to Concord, Thoreau met Ralph Waldo Emerson, who became a mentor and patron. Through Emerson, he joined a circle including Margaret Fuller, Bronson Alcott, and Nathaniel Hawthorne. Transcendentalism, a loosely organized philosophical movement, emphasized intuition over doctrine and viewed nature as a reflection of spiritual truth. Thoreau embraced these ideals but later diverged, focusing more on empirical observation and individual action.

### *Walden* and Simple Living
In 1845, Thoreau built a cabin near Walden Pond at Emerson’s invitation, living there for two years, two months, and two days. He sought to “live deliberately” and strip life to its essentials. During this time, he completed *A Week on the Concord and Merrimack Rivers* (1849), a tribute to his brother John. *Walden* (1854), drawn from his journal entries, became his most enduring work, blending memoir, philosophy, and natural observation.

### Civil Disobedience and Political Activism
Thoreau’s refusal to pay poll taxes in 1846—protesting slavery and the Mexican–American War—led to a night in jail. This experience inspired his essay "Resistance to Civil Government," advocating nonviolent resistance to unjust laws. He supported abolition, participated in the Underground Railroad, and defended John Brown, calling him an “angel of light.” His political writings influenced Gandhi, Martin Luther King Jr., and Leo Tolstoy.

### Natural History and Ecology
From 1851 onward, Thoreau devoted himself to studying natural history, keeping a two-million-word journal over 24 years. He documented seasonal changes, bird migrations, and forest succession, notably observing how squirrels aid tree regeneration. His ecological insights, published posthumously in essays like “The Succession of Trees” and “Wild Apples,” positioned him as a pioneer in environmental science.

### Later Years and Death
Thoreau traveled extensively in New England and Canada, producing works such as *The Maine Woods* and *Cape Cod*. He suffered from tuberculosis throughout his life and died in 1862 at 44. His final words were “Now comes good sailing,” followed by “moose” and “Indian.” He was buried in Sleepy Hollow Cemetery in Concord.

## Terms
- **Transcendentalism**: A 19th-century American philosophical movement emphasizing individual intuition, nature, and spiritual transcendence of the material world.
- **Civil Disobedience**: The act of peacefully refusing to obey unjust laws as a form of protest, as advocated by Thoreau.
- **Conté Process**: A method of making pencils using clay as a binder with graphite, revived by Thoreau.
- **Plumbago**: A historical term for graphite, used in electrotyping and pencil-making.
- **Bioregionalism**: A philosophy that emphasizes harmony between human communities and their local ecosystems, aligned with Thoreau’s views.
- **Ecology**: The study of interactions between organisms and their environment; Thoreau’s work prefigured this field.
- **Ecocriticism**: A literary approach analyzing the relationship between literature and the physical environment.
- **Passivity vs. Resistance**: Thoreau distinguished between passive nonresistance and active moral resistance to injustice.
- **Walden Pond**: A lake in Concord, Massachusetts, where Thoreau lived and wrote *Walden*.
- **Underground Railroad**: A network of secret routes and safe houses used by abolitionists to help enslaved people escape to freedom.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Anarchism**: While sometimes labeled an anarchist, Thoreau preferred the term “proto-anarchist,” advocating for a “better government” rather than no government.
- **Sexuality**: Thoreau never married and had no known romantic relationships. Scholars debate whether he was heterosexual, homosexual, or asexual, with some citing homoerotic undertones in his writings.
- **Autism**: Some modern readers speculate that Thoreau exhibited traits consistent with Asperger’s Syndrome, though no formal diagnosis exists.
- **Hypocrisy**: Critics accuse Thoreau of inconsistency—for example, relying on his family’s pencil business while advocating simplicity—but defenders argue his critique was systemic, not personal.
- **Legacy**: Thoreau’s influence spans movements from environmentalism to civil rights, yet his reception during his lifetime was mixed, with many contemporaries dismissing him as impractical or self-righteous.
