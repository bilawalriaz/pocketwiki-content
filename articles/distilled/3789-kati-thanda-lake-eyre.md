# Kati Thanda–Lake Eyre

## Overview
Kati Thanda–Lake Eyre is Australia's largest ephemeral (temporary) endorheic (internally draining) lake, located in the remote Far North of South Australia. It is the lowest natural point on the Australian continent and acts as the terminal basin for a vast river system. The lake is most often a dry salt pan, but rare, massive floods transform it into a vital ecosystem for waterbirds and a site of scientific and recreational interest.

## Timeline
- **c. 1840** — First European sighting by Edward John Eyre.
- **1860s** — Explorer Peter Egerton-Warburton describes the lake as dry and sterile.
- **1964** — Donald Campbell sets a land speed record of 403.10 mph on the salt flats.
- **1974** — The lake experiences its largest recorded historical filling, reaching a depth of 6 m.
- **1985** — The Kati Thanda–Lake Eyre National Park is gazetted, restricting visitor activities.
- **2012** — The lake is officially dual-named Kati Thanda–Lake Eyre; native title is recognized for the Arabana and Dieri peoples.
- **2019** — Floodwaters from Queensland arrive in just two months, following Cyclone Trevor.
- **2025** — A new management plan adds further restrictions; major floodwaters are predicted to arrive.

## Body

### Geography and Formation
The lake is the depocentre (lowest point of sediment deposition) of the massive Lake Eyre Basin, which covers about 15% of the Australian landmass. It was formed by aeolian (wind-driven) processes after tectonic uplift to the south at the end of the Pleistocene epoch. The lakebed contains Australia's lowest point, approximately 15 m below sea level. It is divided into two sections, Lake Eyre North and Lake Eyre South, connected by the Goyder Channel. The basin is endorheic, meaning water flows in but does not drain to the sea, escaping only via evaporation.

### Flooding and Salinity
The lake is usually dry, with water collecting in over 200 smaller sub-lakes. Major floods occur when monsoon rains in Queensland's Channel Country send water down rivers like the Warburton. A 1.5 m flood happens roughly every three years, while a complete fill is rare—occurring only three times between 1860 and 2025. When full, the lake's salinity is similar to seawater. As it evaporates, it becomes hypersaline, with a salt crust up to 50 cm thick. At saturation, the water turns pink due to the alga *Dunaliella salina*.

### Ecology and Significance
When flooded, the lake is one of Australia's most important sites for waterbirds. Species like pelicans and banded stilts travel vast distances to breed and feed on the fish and invertebrates brought by floodwaters. Scientists are still unsure how birds detect the filling from hundreds of kilometers away. The lake is listed as a wetland of national importance and an Important Bird Area. Historically, proposals like the Bradfield Scheme aimed to flood it with seawater to increase regional rainfall, but these have been deemed impractical due to high evaporation and salt buildup.

### Human Use and Protection
The lake is within the traditional lands of the Arabana and Dieri peoples. It was a site for 1960s land speed record attempts. Protected areas include the Elliot Price Conservation Park (1967) and the much larger Kati Thanda–Lake Eyre National Park (1985). The 1985 gazetting restricted activities like swimming, boating, and off-track driving. A 2025 management plan added further restrictions to protect the fragile salt crust and ecosystem, and for visitor safety in the remote area with no mobile coverage. The Lake Eyre Yacht Club, based in Marree, sails on the rare floods.

## Terms
*   **Ephemeral:** A lake that is temporary, existing only for a short time after filling.
*   **Endorheic:** A closed drainage basin that retains water and allows no outflow to other external bodies of water, such as rivers or oceans.
*   **Depocentre:** The area within a basin where the greatest amount of sediment accumulates.
*   **Aeolian:** Pertaining to wind; in geology, landforms created by wind action.
*   **Hypersaline:** Having a very high concentration of salt, greater than that of seawater.
*   **Native title:** The recognition in Australian law that Indigenous Australians have rights and interests to land and waters according to their traditional laws and customs.
*   **Gazetted:** Officially declared and listed in a government gazette (official public journal).
*   **Pluvial lake:** A lake that is fed primarily by precipitation.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Practicality of seawater diversion schemes:** Proposals to flood the lake with seawater to increase rainfall have been debated since 1883 but are generally considered impractical due to high evaporation rates and the inevitable blocking of channels by salt deposits.
*   **Impact of new management restrictions:** The 2025 management plan's additional restrictions on visitor activities have been criticized by some, like the commodore of the Lake Eyre Yacht Club, while being supported by authorities concerned with environmental damage and visitor safety.
*   **Bird navigation:** Scientists remain unable to determine how waterbirds detect the filling of the remote lake from hundreds of kilometers away to arrive in such large numbers.
