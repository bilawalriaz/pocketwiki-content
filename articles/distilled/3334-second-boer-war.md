# Second Boer War

## Overview
The Second Boer War (1899–1902) was a conflict between the British Empire and the two independent Boer republics—the South African Republic (Transvaal) and the Orange Free State—primarily over control of the gold-rich Witwatersrand region. It evolved from a conventional war into a brutal guerrilla campaign, marked by British scorched-earth tactics and the use of concentration camps, which caused massive civilian casualties and had a lasting impact on South African politics and British imperial confidence.

## Timeline
- **1884** — Discovery of gold on the Witwatersrand, triggering a rush of British "Uitlanders" into the Transvaal.
- **1895** — The failed Jameson Raid, an attempted coup by British-backed Uitlanders, heightens tensions.
- **May 1899** — The Bloemfontein Conference between British High Commissioner Milner and Boer President Kruger fails.
- **October 1899** — Boer republics issue an ultimatum; Britain declines, and war begins on 11 October.
- **Dec 1899** — "Black Week": British suffer defeats at Stormberg, Magersfontein, and Colenso.
- **Feb 1900** — British relieve the sieges of Kimberley and Ladysmith; capture of General Cronjé at Paardeberg.
- **March 1900** — British capture Bloemfontein, capital of the Orange Free State.
- **June 1900** — British capture Pretoria, capital of the Transvaal.
- **Sept 1900** — British formally annex both republics; Boer leaders begin a guerrilla war.
- **1900–1902** — British implement scorched-earth policy and build a system of blockhouses to counter guerrillas.
- **1900–1902** — Over 100,000 Boer and 20,000 Black civilians interned in concentration camps; tens of thousands die.
- **May 1902** — Treaty of Vereeniging signed, ending the war and placing the republics under British rule.

## Body

### Origins and Outbreak
The war stemmed from long-standing conflict between the British and Boers, exacerbated by the 1884 Witwatersrand gold discovery. The influx of British Uitlanders into the Transvaal, who were denied political rights, provided a pretext for British intervention. Failed diplomacy, the legacy of the 1895 Jameson Raid, and a Boer military buildup (including modern German rifles and artillery) set the stage. Britain, seeking to secure its imperial influence and protect Uitlander interests, prepared for war despite some government reluctance.

### The Conventional War (1899–1900)
The war began with a successful Boer offensive, besieging British garrisons at Ladysmith, Mafeking, and Kimberley. Initial British relief attempts failed disastrously during "Black Week" (December 1899). The British government then sent a massive expeditionary force under Lords Roberts and Kitchener. This force relieved the sieges, captured the Boer capitals (Bloemfontein and Pretoria), and formally annexed the republics by September 1900, leading Britain to believe the war was over.

### Guerrilla War and British Counterinsurgency
Boer leaders, refusing to surrender, launched a highly effective guerrilla campaign using fast-moving commandos to attack supply lines. In response, the British under Kitchener implemented a brutal counterinsurgency strategy. This included a "scorched earth" policy of destroying farms and crops, and the construction of over 8,000 blockhouses connected by barbed wire to restrict Boer movement. The most devastating tactic was the mass internment of Boer and Black civilians in concentration camps, where overcrowding, poor sanitation, and disease led to catastrophic death tolls.

### Conclusion and Aftermath
By 1902, British containment tactics and the suffering in the camps pressured Boer leaders to negotiate. The Treaty of Vereeniging (May 1902) ended the war, with the Boer republics becoming British colonies. Britain promised £3 million for reconstruction and eventual self-government, granted in 1906–07. In 1910, these colonies merged with Natal and the Cape Colony to form the Union of South Africa, a self-governing dominion within the British Empire. The war's high cost, unexpected duration, and civilian suffering damaged British prestige and sparked debates about imperial overreach.

## Terms
- **Uitlanders**: "Foreigners," primarily British migrants who flocked to the Transvaal after the 1884 gold rush.
- **Commando**: A Boer citizen militia unit, with members providing their own horse and rifle.
- **Scorched Earth**: A British military strategy of destroying anything that could sustain guerrillas, including farms, crops, and livestock.
- **Blockhouse**: A small, fortified guard post built by the British to protect railways and roads and restrict Boer movement.
- **Concentration Camp**: Camps established by the British to intern Boer and Black civilians displaced by the scorched-earth policy, where disease and starvation caused massive fatalities.
- **Bittereinders**: "Irreconcilable" Boer fighters who refused to surrender and wished to continue the war.
- **Hensoppers**: "Hands-uppers," Boers who surrendered and took an oath of neutrality, often despised by fellow Boers.
- **Treaty of Vereeniging**: The peace agreement signed on 31 May 1902 that ended the war.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Justification for War**: Scholars debate whether the war was primarily a conflict over Uitlander rights and gold, or a deliberate act of British imperial expansion.
- **Concentration Camps**: The morality and necessity of the British internment policy remain highly controversial. While the British saw it as a legitimate counterinsurgency measure, it is widely condemned for the high civilian death toll.
- **Military Legacy**: The war is studied as an early example of modern guerrilla warfare and counterinsurgency, with its tactics influencing later conflicts. The effectiveness and ethics of British methods are still analyzed.
- **Impact on Empire**: The war's surprising length and cost are seen by some historians as a turning point that began to erode the myth of British invincibility and fueled anti-imperial sentiment.
