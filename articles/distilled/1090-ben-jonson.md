# Ben Jonson

## Overview

Ben Jonson (c. 11 June 1572 – 18 August 1637) was an English playwright, poet, and actor whose classical learning and satirical wit made him the dominant comic dramatist of the Jacobean and Caroline eras. Regarded as the second most important English dramatist after Shakespeare during James I's reign, Jonson popularized the comedy of humours and authored enduring works such as *Every Man in His Humours*, *Volpone*, *The Alchemist*, and *Bartholomew Fair*. His influence extended beyond drama into poetry, masques, and literary criticism, shaping the careers of the "Tribe of Ben" and the broader trajectory of English literature.

## Body

### Early Life and Education

Jonson was born into a Protestant family whose fortunes were ruined under Queen Mary I. His father lost his property, was imprisoned, and, as a Protestant, suffered forfeiture under Queen Mary. Becoming a clergyman upon his release, he died a month before Jonson's birth. Raised by his mother and stepfather, a bricklayer, Jonson received an education at Westminster School under the tutelage of William Camden, whose classical scholarship profoundly influenced his literary development. On leaving Westminster School in 1589, Jonson attended St John's College, Cambridge, but returned after a month due to his unwilled apprenticeship to his bricklayer stepfather. After having been an apprentice bricklayer, Jonson went to the Netherlands and volunteered to soldier with the English regiments of Sir Francis Vere in Flanders.

### Career and Controversies

Returning to England, Jonson worked as both actor and playwright. His early career was marked by controversy: *The Isle of Dogs* (1597), co-written with Thomas Nashe, was suppressed for its perceived seditious content. In 1598, he killed fellow actor Gabriel Spenser in a duel and was imprisoned. During his incarceration, he converted to Catholicism, possibly through the influence of fellow-prisoner Father Thomas Wright, a Jesuit priest. Despite these setbacks, *Every Man in His Humours* (1598) launched his reputation, followed by a series of satirical comedies that defined his style.

### Royal Patronage and Masques

With James I's accession in 1603, Jonson adapted to the new court's demand for masques and entertainments. Collaborating with designer Inigo Jones, he produced nearly two dozen masques, including *The Masque of Blackness* (1605) and *Oberon, the Faery Prince* (1611). These elaborate theatrical productions, blending speech, dance, and spectacle, solidified his status as a court favorite. In 1616, he received a yearly pension of 100 marks (about £60), leading some to identify him as England's first Poet Laureate, although the royal family never formally granted him the title. This sign of royal favour may have encouraged him to publish the first volume of the folio-collected edition of his works that year.

### Religious Conversion and Return

Jonson's conversion to Catholicism occurred in October 1598, while he was on remand in Newgate Gaol charged with manslaughter. He remained a Catholic for twelve years, facing recusancy charges in 1606. Following the assassination of French King Henry IV in 1610, he returned to the Church of England in a dramatic ceremony, drinking the full chalice of communion wine to demonstrate his renunciation of the Catholic rite. Though he rejoined Protestantism, his interest in Catholic belief and practice remained with him until his death.

### Later Years and Decline

Jonson's productivity declined in the 1620s, though he continued writing plays like *The Staple of News* (1626) and *The New Inn* (1629). The death of James I and the accession of King Charles I in 1625 marked a turning point; Jonson felt neglected by the new court and quarreled with Jones, harming his career as a writer of court masques. Despite financial support from Charles—who increased Jonson's annual pension to £100 and included a tierce of wine and beer—his later works were poorly received. He suffered strokes in the 1620s, leaving him partially paralyzed, and died in relative poverty in 1637.

### Literary Style and Influence

Jonson's dramatic works emphasized classical structure, satirical precision, and vivid character types. His comedies of humours explored the idea that individual personality traits could dominate behavior. His poetry, including epigrams and country house poems like "To Penshurst," displayed classical learning and formal discipline. The "Tribe of Ben"—poets like Robert Herrick and Richard Lovelace—adopted his stylistic approach, cementing his influence on 17th-century English poetry.

### Relationship with Shakespeare

Jonson's relationship with Shakespeare was complex, characterized by both rivalry and mutual respect. While Drummond's accounts record Jonson's criticisms of Shakespeare's lack of classical learning, Jonson's elegy for the First Folio praised Shakespeare's natural genius, calling him "not of an age, but for all time." Their professional collaboration included Shakespeare's company producing several of Jonson's plays, and Jonson's contributions to the First Folio helped establish Shakespeare's posthumous reputation.

## Terms

- **Comedy of Humours**: A dramatic genre based on the theory that human behavior is dominated by one overriding character trait or "humour."
- **Masque**: An elaborate form of English Renaissance drama combining speech, dance, music, and scenic effects, typically performed at court.
- **Benefit of Clergy**: A legal privilege allowing clergy (and later literate offenders) to receive reduced punishment by reciting a Bible verse.
- **Recusancy**: The refusal to attend Anglican church services, particularly by Catholics, which was illegal in England.
- **Country House Poem**: A genre of poetry celebrating the idealized rural estate, often written to honor aristocratic patrons.
- **Epigram**: A brief, witty poem or statement, often satirical in tone.
- **City Comedy**: A subgenre of English comedy focusing on urban life, commerce, and social satire.
- **Poet Laureate**: A poet appointed by the monarch to compose verse for state occasions (though Jonson never received formal appointment).

## Debates and Open Questions

Several aspects of Jonson's life and work remain subjects of scholarly debate. The exact circumstances of his birth date are uncertain, with sources suggesting either 6 or 11 June 1572. His wife's identity remains obscure, with "Ann Lewis" proposed but unconfirmed. The location of his burial—whether upright or flat—reflects conflicting accounts of his final circumstances. His skull's whereabouts became a mystery after multiple 19th-century grave disturbances, with conflicting claims about its possession. Scholars continue to debate the extent of his collaboration with Shakespeare and the authenticity of various attributed works. The interpretation of his religious conversions—whether motivated by conviction or expedience—remains contested. Additionally, his precise influence on the development of English neoclassicism and his relative standing compared to Shakespeare continue to generate critical discussion.
