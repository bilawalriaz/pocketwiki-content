# World Heritage Site

## Overview
World Heritage Sites are landmarks and areas legally protected under an international treaty administered by UNESCO for their cultural, historical, or scientific significance. They represent humanity's shared legacy, and their designation aims to ensure their conservation for future generations, recognizing them as irreplaceable sources of life and inspiration.

## Timeline
- **1954** — Egypt decides to build the Aswan High Dam, threatening cultural sites in the Nile valley.
- **1959** — Egypt and Sudan request UNESCO assistance to protect monuments.
- **1960** — UNESCO launches the International Campaign to Save the Monuments of Nubia.
- **1965** — A White House conference proposes a "World Heritage Trust."
- **1972** — The Convention Concerning the Protection of the World Cultural and Natural Heritage is adopted by UNESCO.
- **1975** — The World Heritage Convention enters into force.
- **2005** — The selection criteria are unified into a single set of ten.
- **2007** — The Arabian Oryx Sanctuary (Oman) is the first site delisted.
- **2009** — The Dresden Elbe Valley (Germany) is delisted.
- **2021** — Liverpool Maritime Mercantile City (UK) is delisted.

## Body

### Origin and Convention
The World Heritage program originated from the successful 1960-1980 UNESCO campaign to rescue Nubian monuments from the flooding caused by the Aswan High Dam. This success led to further safeguarding efforts and the drafting of a convention. The United States and the International Union for Conservation of Nature also proposed ideas for protecting global heritage. After seven years of development, the Convention Concerning the Protection of the World Cultural and Natural Heritage was adopted in 1972. As of November 2024, it has been ratified by 196 states, making it one of the most widely recognized international agreements.

### Objectives and Process
UNESCO's mission is to encourage conservation commitment, provide emergency and technical assistance, and build public awareness. Listing a site grants it international recognition, legal protection, and potential access to the World Heritage Fund. The nomination process requires a country to first identify sites on a Tentative List. A selected site is then evaluated by the International Council on Monuments and Sites and the World Conservation Union before the World Heritage Committee makes a final decision. Sites must meet at least one of ten criteria demonstrating "outstanding universal value."

### Criteria and Modifications
The ten selection criteria cover both cultural and natural heritage. Cultural criteria include representing human creative genius, bearing testimony to a civilization, or being associated with significant events or traditions. Natural criteria include containing superlative natural phenomena, representing major stages of Earth's history, or containing important natural habitats for biological diversity. Countries may request minor or significant boundary changes, name changes, or modifications to a site's criteria, with significant changes following a process similar to a new nomination.

### Endangerment and De-listing
A site can be placed on the List of World Heritage in Danger if threatened by factors like armed conflict, natural disasters, pollution, or uncontrolled development. The Committee reviews these sites annually and may request measures, remove them from the danger list, or consider de-listing if integrity is lost. Only three sites have ever been delisted: the Arabian Oryx Sanctuary (2007), the Dresden Elbe Valley (2009), and Liverpool Maritime Mercantile City (2021). A global assessment found that 63% of Natural World Heritage Sites have been damaged by human pressures over the last two decades.

### Criticism
The program has faced criticism for perceived under-representation of sites outside Europe, the high cost of nomination bids disadvantaging poorer countries, and the adverse impact of mass tourism. Lobbying by governments, such as Australia's efforts regarding the Great Barrier Reef, has been reported. Some critics argue that listing can lead to a homogenization of sites to attract tourists and that it may ignore contemporary local usage, sometimes causing conflict and damage. UNESCO has also been criticized for alleged geographic and racial bias in inscriptions.

## Terms
- **World Heritage Site**: A landmark or area with legal protection under an international treaty administered by UNESCO for its cultural, historical, or scientific significance.
- **UNESCO**: The United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization, the UN agency responsible for the World Heritage program.
- **World Heritage Committee**: The body of 21 elected "states parties" that administers the World Heritage List and makes final decisions on nominations and site status.
- **Convention Concerning the Protection of the World Cultural and Natural Heritage**: The 1972 international agreement that established the World Heritage program.
- **Tentative List**: A document a country must prepare, listing its significant cultural and natural sites before it can nominate any for World Heritage status.
- **Outstanding Universal Value**: The fundamental requirement for a site to be inscribed, meaning it has cultural and/or natural significance so exceptional as to transcend national boundaries.
- **List of World Heritage in Danger**: A list of sites facing specific threats, intended to raise awareness and encourage protective measures.
- **States Parties**: Countries that have ratified the World Heritage Convention.

## Debates and Open questions
- **Geographic Bias**: There is criticism that the list under-represents heritage sites outside of Europe, East Asia, and North America, potentially reflecting racial or colorist biases in inscription.
- **Tourism Impact**: The economic benefits of listing can lead to mass tourism that threatens the very integrity of a site, creating a conflict between conservation and revenue.
- **Local Community Relations**: Recognition can sometimes ignore contemporary local usage of sites, leading to conflicts and even intentional destruction, as seen in cases like the Tadrart Acacus rock art in Libya.
- **Political Lobbying**: Governments may lobby to protect a site's economic interests, as seen with Australia's campaigns regarding the Great Barrier Reef, potentially overriding scientific assessments of danger.
