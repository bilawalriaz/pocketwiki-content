# Edward III

## Overview
Edward III (1312–1377) was King of England for fifty years, one of the longest reigns in English history. He is primarily noted for his military successes in the Hundred Years' War, which transformed England into a major European power, and for presiding over significant developments in English government and Parliament. His reign also saw the devastating impact of the Black Death.

## Body

### Early Life and Accession
Edward was the son of the unpopular King Edward II. His father's reliance on favourites and military failures created widespread noble discontent. In 1325, Edward was sent to France to perform homage for Aquitaine, accompanying his mother, Isabella. Isabella conspired with Roger Mortimer to depose Edward II, and with an invasion force, they succeeded. Edward was crowned in 1327 at age fourteen, but real power was held by Mortimer. In 1330, at age seventeen, Edward led a coup at Nottingham Castle, captured Mortimer, and began his personal rule.

### Military Campaigns and the Hundred Years' War
Edward's early reign focused on war in Scotland, where he won victories like the Battle of Halidon Hill (1333). However, his main focus became France. In 1337, Philip VI of France confiscated Aquitaine. Edward responded by claiming the French throne himself, initiating the Hundred Years' War. After early setbacks, English forces won major victories at Crécy (1346) and Poitiers (1356), where the French king was captured. These successes led to the Treaty of Brétigny (1360), which granted England vast territories in full sovereignty. The war resumed in 1369, and by 1375, England had lost most of its conquests, retaining only Calais, Bordeaux, and Bayonne.

### Government, Parliament, and Legislation
Edward's reign saw crucial developments in English governance. To finance war, he relied on Parliament for taxation, which strengthened the institution, particularly the House of Commons. The Commons gained influence through granting taxes and presenting petitions. Key legislation included the Statute of Labourers (1351), which attempted to fix wages after the Black Death, and the Treason Act (1351). The Justices of the Peace were also given expanded powers to try cases. Edward created new peerages, including the first dukedoms, to build a loyal nobility and founded the Order of the Garter (c. 1348) to foster chivalric camaraderie.

### Later Reign and Death
Edward's later years were marked by declining health, military failure, and domestic strife. He became increasingly reliant on his sons and ministers like William Wykeham. His mistress, Alice Perrers, gained significant influence and wealth. The "Good Parliament" of 1376 impeached royal ministers and banished Perrers, highlighting political discontent. Edward died in 1377, succeeded by his ten-year-old grandson, Richard II, as his eldest son, the Black Prince, had predeceased him.

## Terms
*   **Chevauchée:** A large-scale mounted raid through enemy territory, designed to destroy resources and demoralize the enemy, a common English tactic in the Hundred Years' War.
*   **Statute of Labourers (1351):** A law passed to combat labour shortages and wage inflation after the Black Death by fixing wages at pre-plague levels and restricting peasant mobility.
*   **Order of the Garter:** A chivalric order founded by Edward III around 1348, the oldest and most senior order of knighthood in Britain.
*   **Good Parliament (1376):** A Parliament notable for the first use of impeachment against royal officials and for asserting the Commons' political power.
*   **Treason Act 1351:** A statute that formally defined the crime of treason for the first time in English law.
*   **Justices of the Peace:** Local officials given expanded powers during Edward's reign to investigate crimes and try cases, forming a key part of local administration.
*   **Babylonian Captivity:** The period from 1309 to 1376 when the papacy was under the control of the French crown, based in Avignon, leading to tensions with England.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Nature of the French Claim:** Historians debate whether Edward III's claim to the French throne was a genuine dynastic ambition or primarily a political and military tool to pressure the French government.
*   **Responsibility for the Wars of the Roses:** While 19th-century historians blamed Edward for creating the ducal families (Lancaster and York) whose rivalry led to the 15th-century civil wars, modern scholarship largely rejects this, viewing it as a common policy of the age.
*   **Historical Reputation:** Edward's legacy has shifted. He was revered for centuries as a model warrior-king, but 19th-century Whig historians like William Stubbs criticized him as an irresponsible adventurer. Modern historians generally credit him with significant administrative and military achievements.
