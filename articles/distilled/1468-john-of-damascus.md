# John of Damascus

## Overview
John of Damascus (c. 675/676 – 4 December 749) was a Syrian Christian monk, priest, and polymath who stands as the last great figure of the Patristic era and a pivotal Doctor of the Church. Born into a prominent family serving the Umayyad Caliphate in Damascus, he received a Hellenic education before retiring to the Mar Saba monastery near Jerusalem. He is best known for his systematic theology, particularly *The Fountain of Knowledge*, and his decisive defence of icon veneration against Byzantine Iconoclasm. His treatises on holy images, written in accessible language, mobilized popular resistance and shaped the outcome of the Second Council of Nicaea (787). He also produced the first extensive Christian refutation of Islam, categorizing it as the "Heresy of the Ishmaelites," and developed the concept of *perichoresis* to explain the interpenetration of Christ’s natures and the Trinitarian relations.

## Timeline
- **c. 675/676** — Born in Damascus as Yūḥana ibn Manṣūr ibn Sarjūn to a prominent Syrian Christian family serving the Umayyad administration.
- **c. 635** — Grandfather Mansur ibn Sarjun negotiates capitulation of Damascus to Muslim forces under Khalid ibn al-Walid.
- **c. 705** — Father Sarjun ibn Mansur leaves Umayyad fiscal administration (possibly c. 705–706); John may have left Damascus for Mar Saba monastery around this time.
- **717–718** — Siege of Constantinople; John is already a monk at Mar Saba before the iconoclastic controversy erupts.
- **726** — Emperor Leo III issues first edict against veneration of icons; John begins writing apologetic treatises.
- **735** — Ordained priest at Mar Saba (traditional date).
- **754** — Condemned by anathema at the iconoclastic Council of Hieria.
- **4 Dec 749** — Traditional date of death at Mar Saba.
- **787** — Rehabilitated by the Second Council of Nicaea; writings play key role in restoring icon veneration.
- **1890** — Declared a Doctor of the Church by Pope Leo XIII; feast inserted into General Roman Calendar (27 March).
- **1969** — Feast day moved to 4 December in Roman Calendar, aligning with Byzantine, Lutheran, and Anglican calendars.

## Body

### Family Background and Early Life
John was born into a distinguished Syrian Christian family in Damascus. His grandfather, Mansur ibn Sarjun, held high Byzantine office under Emperors Heraclius and Maurice and negotiated the city’s surrender to Muslim forces in 635, securing favorable terms. The family retained their positions under the Umayyad Caliphate: Mansur’s son, Sarjun ibn Mansur (John’s father), served as a senior fiscal official, possibly under Caliph Abd al-Malik. The family’s ethnic designation is debated; scholars variously describe them as Arab Christians (Kalb or Taghlib tribes) or non-Arab Semitic Syrians who adopted Arabic names after the conquest. John received a thorough Hellenic education, reportedly from a Sicilian monk named Cosmas (kidnapped by Arabs), who taught him arithmetic, geometry, and the Latin Christian tradition. He likely grew up bilingual and shows familiarity with the Quran in his later writings.

### Monastic Career and the Iconoclastic Controversy
John entered the Mar Saba monastery near Jerusalem, possibly around 706 when Caliph al-Walid I increased Islamicization of the administration, though sources only confirm his father’s departure at this time. He was ordained a priest in 735. In 726, Emperor Leo III initiated Iconoclasm, banning the veneration of icons. John responded with three *Apologetic Treatises against those Decrying the Holy Images*. He adopted a simplified style to reach the laity, arguing that the Incarnation of Christ justified depicting the invisible God. His writings stirred popular resistance. A later hagiographical tradition claims Leo III forged letters implicating John in a plot against Damascus, leading the Caliph to order John’s right hand cut off; the hand was miraculously restored after prayer before an icon of the Theotokos (the "Three-handed" or *Tricherousa* icon). In 754, the iconoclastic Council of Hieria anathematized him; the Second Council of Nicaea (787) reversed this condemnation, relying heavily on his theology.

### The Fountain of Knowledge and Systematic Theology
John’s magnum opus, *The Fountain of Knowledge* (*Pēgē gnōseōs*), synthesized Christian doctrine into a structured textbook that directed medieval Latin thought and became the principal text of Greek Orthodox theology. It comprises three parts:
1.  **Philosophical Chapters (Dialectic):** Based on Porphyry’s *Isagoge* (introduction to Aristotelian logic), it equips the reader with logical tools for theology.
2.  **Concerning Heresy:** Adapted from Epiphanius of Salamis’s *Panarion*, it catalogues 80 heresies up to the 4th century. John added 20 heresies (81–100) covering post-Epiphanius disputes like Nestorianism and Monophysitism. The final entry (101), the "Heresy of the Ishmaelites," is the first extensive Christian refutation of Islam.
3.  **An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith:** The first systematic theology in Eastern Christianity, summarizing the Early Church Fathers and influencing later Western Scholasticism.

### Views on Islam
In *Concerning Heresy* (ch. 101), John identifies Islam as a Christian heresy ("Heresy of the Ishmaelites"). He claims Muslims were formerly worshipers of Aphrodite who followed Muhammad for his "seeming show of piety." He asserts Muhammad encountered an Arian monk and read the Bible, resulting in a distorted theology. John criticizes specific Quranic claims: that Mary is the sister of Aaron (Surah 19:28) and that Jesus was not crucified but raised alive to heaven (Surah 4:157–158). He recounts dialogues with Muslims, challenging the lack of witnesses to Muhammad’s revelation (contrasting Sinai) and the absence of biblical prophecy foretelling Muhammad. He mocks the claim of nocturnal revelation ("You're spinning me dreams") and accuses Muslims of idol worship regarding the Black Stone in Mecca (claiming it was a statue head of Aphrodite). He argues that if Jesus is the Word and Spirit of God, he must be God, since word and spirit are inseparable from their source. He also criticizes Islamic permission of polygamy and Muhammad’s marriage to Zaynab bint Jahsh.

### Hymnography and Liturgical Legacy
John perfected the *canon*, a structured hymn form for Byzantine Rite liturgies, and compiled the *Octoechos* (the book of eight tones). His hymns remain in use globally in Eastern Orthodoxy and in Western Lutheranism (notably at Easter). He is credited with the *Trisagion* hymn tradition and sermons on the Assumption (Dormition), earning the Catholic title "Doctor of the Assumption."

### Historical Sources and the *Barlaam and Josaphat* Attribution
The primary biography is a 10th-century Arabic hagiography by an unknown author, translated into Greek by John of Jerusalem (Patriarch) and later expanded by the monk Michael in 1084. It is considered hagiographical—prone to exaggeration and legend (e.g., the severed hand miracle)—but contains valuable historical elements. The 10th-century novel *Barlaam and Josaphat*, once attributed to John, is now assigned to the Georgian monk Euthymius of Athos (d. 1028); most scholars reject John’s authorship.

## Terms
- ****Chrysorroas**** — Epithet meaning "streaming with gold" (golden speaker), given to John for his eloquence.
- ****Iconoclasm**** — 8th–9th century Byzantine movement opposing the veneration of religious images (icons), initiated by Emperor Leo III in 726.
- ****Iconodulism**** — The veneration (honoring) of icons; the position defended by John against Iconoclasm.
- ****Perichoresis**** — Technical term employed by John describing the mutual interpenetration of the divine and human natures in Christ and the coinherence of the Trinitarian persons (hypostases).
- ****Theotokos**** — Title for Mary meaning "God-bearer" (Mother of God); central to John’s defence of icons and the miracle of his restored hand.
- ****Panarion**** — "Medicine chest" (bread basket); Epiphanius of Salamis’s 4th-century catalogue of 80 heresies, used by John as a structural model.
- ****Isagoge**** — Porphyry’s 3rd-century introduction to Aristotelian logic; basis for John’s *Philosophical Chapters*.
- ****Heresy of the Ishmaelites**** — John’s designation for Islam (ch. 101 of *Concerning Heresy*), framing it as a Christian heresy rather than a separate religion.
- ****Canon (hymnography)**** — Structured hymn form used in Byzantine liturgy, perfected by John; consists of nine odes based on biblical canticles.
- ****Octoechos**** — Liturgical book of eight modes (tones) governing Byzantine chant; compiled by John.
- ****Patristic period**** — Era of the Church Fathers (c. 100–c. 750); John is considered its last major Greek representative.
- ****Doctor of the Church**** — Title given by the Catholic Church to saints whose writings significantly advanced theology; conferred on John by Leo XIII (1890).

## Debates and open questions
*   **Historicity of the severed hand miracle:** The story of John’s hand being cut off by the Caliph and miraculously restored appears only in later hagiography (10th century+) and is absent from his own writings; scholars regard it as legendary.
*   **John’s civil service:** Whether John himself held high office in the Umayyad administration (as claimed by John of Jerusalem) is debated. No trace of him exists in Umayyad archives, unlike his father and grandfather. Scholars (e.g., Hoyland) deny it; others (Sahas, Meyendorff) suggest a lower-level tax role.
*   **Sources for the 20 added heresies:** The origins of John’s heresies 81–100 are uncertain. Lequien (18th c.) speculated dependence on Sophronius, Leontius, Theodoret, and Timothy, but modern scholarship (e.g., Chase 1958) finds "no foundation" for these specific claims.
*   **Ethnic identity:** Whether the Mansur family were Arab Christians (Kalb/Taghlib) or Arabized Syrians remains unresolved; sources support both "Arab Syrian" and "Syrian origin" readings.
*   **Dating of monastic entry:** The traditional date (c. 706) links to al-Walid’s Islamicization, but Muslim sources only mention Sarjun’s departure, not John’s.
