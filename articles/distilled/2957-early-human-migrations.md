# Early human migrations

## Overview
The global spread of humans is a multi-stage process beginning with early hominins leaving Africa over 2 million years ago. The successful, lasting colonization of the world by anatomically modern humans (*Homo sapiens*) occurred in waves, primarily from Africa, with the most significant dispersal happening between 70,000 and 50,000 years ago. This migration involved interbreeding with archaic human populations like Neanderthals and Denisovans, shaping the genetic makeup of contemporary humans.

## Timeline
- **c. 2.12 million years ago** — Earliest evidence of hominins outside Africa (Shangchen, China).
- **c. 1.8 million years ago** — *Homo erectus* migrates out of Africa to Eurasia.
- **c. 300,000 years ago** — *Homo sapiens* emerges in Africa.
- **c. 210,000–177,000 years ago** — Early, failed dispersals of *H. sapiens* to Eurasia (e.g., Misliya Cave, Apidima Cave).
- **c. 70,000–50,000 years ago** — Major "recent dispersal" of *H. sapiens* from East Africa along southern coastal routes.
- **c. 65,000–50,000 years ago** — *H. sapiens* reaches Australia and New Guinea.
- **c. 45,000–40,000 years ago** — *H. sapiens* spreads across Europe, replacing Neanderthals.
- **c. 20,000 years ago** — Paleo-Indians cross Beringia to populate the Americas.
- **c. 4,000 years ago** — Paleo-Eskimo expansion reaches Arctic Canada and Greenland.
- **c. 3000–1200 BCE** — Austronesian expansion from Taiwan colonizes Island Southeast Asia and Remote Oceania.
- **c. 1200–1200 CE** — Polynesian expansion reaches Hawaii, Rapa Nui, and New Zealand.

## Body

### Early Human Dispersals (Pre-*H. sapiens*)
The earliest migrations were by archaic humans. *Homo erectus* left Africa around 1.8 million years ago, spreading across the Old World. Later, *Homo heidelbergensis* (c. 600,000 years ago) gave rise to Neanderthals in Europe/West Asia and Denisovans in East/Central Asia. These groups expanded into northern latitudes, with Neanderthals possibly reaching the Arctic boundary by c. 32,000 years ago.

### Early *Homo sapiens* Dispersals and Failures
*Homo sapiens* emerged in Africa around 300,000 years ago. Early expansions into Eurasia, evidenced by fossils in Israel (c. 194,000–177,000 years ago) and possibly Greece (c. 210,000 years ago), appear to have been failed attempts, with populations likely replaced by local Neanderthals.

### The Major Coastal Migration
The successful, lasting global spread began with a "recent dispersal" from East Africa between 70,000 and 50,000 years ago. A small group (possibly <1,000 individuals) crossed the Red Sea at Bab-el-Mandeb, spreading rapidly along the southern coast of Asia. This wave reached Australia by 65,000–50,000 years ago, requiring seafaring to cross the Wallace Line. Along the way, these migrants interbred with local archaic populations, contributing less than 10% to the genomes of non-African modern humans.

### Colonization of Eurasia and the Americas
From South Asia, populations spread further. Modern humans reached Europe around 40,000 years ago, rapidly replacing Neanderthals after a period of overlap and interbreeding. In East Asia, the "Basal-East Asian" (ESEA) lineage expanded from Mainland Southeast Asia around 50,000 years ago. During the Last Glacial Maximum (c. 20,000 years ago), populations in North Asia crossed the Beringia land bridge to populate the Americas.

### Holocene Migrations and Later Expansions
After the Last Glacial Maximum, the Holocene (beginning 12,000 years ago) saw migrations that shaped modern populations. In Europe, ancestry is a mix of Western Hunter-Gatherers, Early European Farmers from the Near East, and Ancient North Eurasians. In Africa, the Bantu expansion (beginning c. 3,000 years ago) spread agriculture and languages across the continent. The Austronesian expansion, beginning from Taiwan c. 3000 BCE, was the last major Neolithic migration, using advanced sailing technology to colonize the Indo-Pacific, reaching Polynesia by 1200 CE.

## Terms
- **Chronospecies**: A species that evolves continuously over time without a clear split, making the designation of start and end points a matter of convention (e.g., *Homo erectus*).
- **Haplogroup**: A genetic population group of people who share a common ancestor on the patrilineal or matrilineal line, identified by mutations in DNA (e.g., mitochondrial haplogroup L3).
- **Archaic humans**: Non-modern human species like Neanderthals and Denisovans, from whom modern humans sometimes interbred.
- **Beringia**: A land bridge that connected Siberia and Alaska during periods of low sea level, allowing human migration to the Americas.
- **Refugia**: Areas where a population can survive through periods of unfavorable conditions (e.g., during ice ages).
- **Austronesian expansion**: The last major Neolithic migration, originating from Taiwan, where seafaring peoples colonized the Indo-Pacific islands.
- **ESEA lineage**: The "East- and Southeast Asian lineage," a basal population ancestral to modern East Asians, Southeast Asians, and Polynesians, originating in Mainland Southeast Asia around 50,000 years ago.

## Debates and open questions
- **Routes of the major dispersal**: Evidence supports both a northern route via the Sinai and a southern coastal route via the Red Sea for the successful *H. sapiens* exit from Africa.
- **Cause of early dispersal failures**: The reasons why early *H. sapiens* expansions into Eurasia (e.g., 210,000 years ago) did not persist are debated, with replacement by Neanderthals being a leading hypothesis.
- **Impact of the Toba eruption**: The extent to which the Toba super-eruption (c. 74,000 years ago) caused a human population bottleneck is contested.
- **First peopling of the Americas**: The exact timing, routes (ice-free corridor vs. coastal migration), and number of migrations into the Americas remain subjects of active research.
- **Late survival of archaic humans in Africa**: The possibility of late survival and hybridization with *H. sapiens* in West Africa until as late as 12,000 years ago is argued based on archaeological evidence.
