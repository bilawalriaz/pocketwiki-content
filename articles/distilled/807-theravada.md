# Theravada

## Overview

Theravāda, meaning "Doctrine of the Elders," is Buddhism's oldest surviving school, preserving the Buddha's teachings in the Pāli Canon for over two millennia. As the second-largest Buddhist branch (36% of Buddhists as of 2010), it is the official religion of Sri Lanka, Myanmar, and Cambodia, and dominant in Thailand and Laos. Known for its conservative approach to doctrine and monastic discipline, Theravāda rejects Mahāyāna sutras and emphasizes individual liberation through one's own effort, making it central to understanding early Buddhist thought and practice.

## Timeline

- **3rd century BCE** — Theravāda descends from Vibhajjavāda division of Sthāvira nikāya; Moggaliputta-Tissa compiles Kathavatthu
- **3rd century BCE** — Ashoka's patronage spreads school throughout India and to Sri Lanka via missionary monks
- **1st century BCE** — Pāli Canon committed to writing in Sri Lanka
- **5th century CE** — Term "Theravāda" first used at Mahāvihāra in Anuradhapura
- **1153–1186** — Parākramabāhu I reforms Sri Lankan sangha, unifies under Mahāvihāra sect
- **1044–1077** — Burmese king Anawrahta converts to Theravāda
- **1851–1910** — Thai kings Mongkut and Chulalongkorn centralize sangha, create Dhammayuttika Nikaya
- **1950** — "Theravāda" becomes official designation at World Fellowship of Buddhists
- **1954–1956** — Sixth Buddhist council redacts Pāli Canon in Myanmar

## Body

### Historical Development

Theravāda traces its origins to the Sthāvira nikāya, one of two major orders arising after the first schism in the early Buddhist community. The tradition developed significantly in India from the 3rd century BCE, particularly through the writing down of the Pāli Canon and growth of commentarial literature. From India and Sri Lanka, it spread to Southeast Asia, becoming dominant through royal patronage. The 19th and 20th centuries saw Buddhist modernism, the Vipassana movement, and westward expansion.

### Scriptures and Authority

The Pāli Canon (Tipiṭaka) is Theravāda's highest authority, consisting of three parts: the Vinaya Pitaka (monastic discipline), Sutta Pitaka (discourses), and Abhidhamma Pitaka (philosophical analysis). Committed to writing in the 1st century BCE, it survives in the Sri Lankan Mahāvihāra recension. Theravāda rejects Mahāyāna sutras as inauthentic, maintaining conservative doctrinal positions. Key non-canonical texts include Buddhaghosa's 5th-century commentaries and the Visuddhimagga, a comprehensive guide to the path.

### Core Doctrine

Theravāda doctrine centers on the Four Noble Truths, karma based on intention, rebirth within saṃsāra, and nibbana as the final release. It emphasizes the three marks of existence (impermanence, suffering, not-self), the Noble Eightfold Path, and the Middle Way between extreme asceticism and sensual indulgence. The tradition teaches four stages of awakening culminating in arahantship, where an individual achieves liberation through personal effort.

### Practice and Path

Theravāda practice follows the threefold training: sīla (moral conduct), samādhi (concentration), and paññā (wisdom). Meditation divides into samatha (calming) and vipassanā (insight), with the Visuddhimagga outlining seven stages of purification. Monastic life is highly valued, with temporary ordination common among laymen. The Thai Forest Tradition and Burmese Vipassana movement represent major modern developments in meditation practice.

### Social and Modern Developments

Modern Theravāda has engaged with Western thought through Buddhist modernism, emphasizing rationalism and scientific compatibility. Figures like Anagarika Dhammapala and King Mongkut promoted reform. Contemporary developments include socially engaged Buddhism, lay meditation movements, and global expansion. Women's ordination remains controversial, with full bhikkhuni ordination emerging in the late 20th century despite traditional restrictions.

## Terms

- **Pāli Canon (Tipiṭaka)**: Theravāda's sacred scriptures, written in Pāli, consisting of Vinaya, Sutta, and Abhidhamma pitakas
- **Nibbana**: The final goal of Theravāda practice; complete cessation of suffering and rebirth
- **Arahant**: One who has achieved the highest stage of awakening, free from all defilements
- **Abhidhamma**: Systematic philosophical analysis of mind and matter, presenting doctrine in formalized language
- **Sīla**: Moral conduct, particularly the five precepts observed by lay practitioners
- **Vipassanā**: Insight meditation aimed at developing understanding of impermanence, suffering, and not-self
- **Samatha**: Calming meditation focusing on a single object to develop concentration
- **Karma**: Intentional action determining future rebirth; central to Theravāda ethics
- **Saṃsāra**: The cycle of birth, death, and rebirth driven by karma
- **Bhikkhu/Bhikkhuni**: Male and female monastics following the Buddhist monastic code

## Debates and Open Questions

Several significant debates persist within Theravāda. The authenticity of the Abhidhamma Pitaka is questioned by some modern scholars, with traditional belief holding it was taught by the Buddha while critical scholarship dates it to the 3rd century BCE. Women's ordination remains contentious, with full bhikkhuni ordination emerging in 1996 despite traditional prohibitions and ongoing disputes about validity. The nature of nibbana—whether it is a real existent or conceptual construct—is debated among scholars. The existence of an intermediate state (antarabhāva) between rebirths is contested, with traditional Theravāda rejecting it while some modern scholars find supporting canonical passages. Additionally, the relationship between traditional forest monasticism and modern state-controlled sanghas creates ongoing tension in countries like Thailand.
