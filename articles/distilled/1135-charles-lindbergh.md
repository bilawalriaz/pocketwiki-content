# Charles Lindbergh

## Overview
Charles Augustus Lindbergh (1902–1974) was an American aviator whose 1927 solo nonstop flight from New York to Paris made him an international celebrity and catalyzed the global aviation industry. Beyond aviation, he was a military officer, author, inventor, and later an environmentalist and controversial political figure whose isolationist views and associations during the 1930s sparked enduring debate.

## Timeline
- **1927, May 20–21** — First solo nonstop transatlantic flight, New York to Paris
- **1927** — Receives Medal of Honor and Distinguished Flying Call; Time’s first "Man of the Year"
- **1932, Mar 1** — Son Charles Jr. kidnapped and murdered ("Crime of the Century")
- **1936–1939** — Lives in Europe; makes speeches opposing U.S. entry into WWII
- **1941, Apr 28** — Resigns from Army Air Corps after FDR rebukes his views
- **1944** — Flies 50 combat missions in the Pacific as civilian consultant
- **1954** — Promoted to brigadier general, U.S. Air Force Reserve
- **1974, Aug 26** — Dies of lymphoma in Maui, Hawaii

## Body

### Early Aviation Career
Born in Detroit and raised in Minnesota and Washington, D.C., Lindbergh dropped out of the University of Wisconsin to pursue flying. He trained at Nebraska Aircraft Corporation, then barnstormed across the U.S. as a wing walker and parachutist. In 1923, he bought a surplus Curtiss JN-4 "Jenny" and made his first solo flight in Americus, Georgia. By 1924, he entered military flight training with the U.S. Army Air Service, graduating first in his class despite a serious mid-air collision that forced him to bail out.

### Air Mail Pilot
In 1925, Lindbergh joined the Robertson Aircraft Corporation as chief pilot for Contract Air Mail Route #2 (CAM-2), flying mail between St. Louis and Chicago. His experience navigating hazardous conditions and bailing out twice due to weather and mechanical failures honed his skills and prepared him for the transatlantic challenge.

### New York–Paris Flight
Inspired by René Fonck’s failed 1926 attempt, Lindbergh secured funding from St. Louis businessmen and partnered with Ryan Airlines to build the Spirit of St. Louis. On May 20–21, 1927, he completed the 3,600-mile journey solo in 33.5 hours, using dead reckoning navigation and enduring icing, fog, and sleep deprivation. The flight won him the $25,000 Orteig Prize and global acclaim.

### Global Fame
Lindbergh became an instant icon. He toured Europe, receiving honors including the French Legion of Honor and British Air Force Cross. Time named him its first "Man of the Year." His autobiography *WE* sold over 650,000 copies, and his cross-country tour promoted aviation, triggering the "Lindbergh Boom"—a surge in pilot licenses, aircraft sales, and air mail usage.

### Scientific Activities
With surgeon Alexis Carrel, Lindbergh developed a perfusion pump (the "Model T") in the 1930s, pioneering research into organ preservation and artificial hearts. Though later deemed impractical, it laid groundwork for modern heart-lung machines. He also advocated for Robert Goddard’s rocket research and designed a navigation watch with Longines.

### Personal Life and Kidnapping
Married to Anne Morrow Lindbergh in 1929, he had six children. In 1932, their firstborn, Charles Jr., was kidnapped and murdered, leading to the federal "Lindbergh Law" criminalizing interstate kidnapping. The trauma drove the family to Europe for safety until 1939.

### Pre-War Politics and Controversy
Lindbergh’s admiration for German aviation led him to make several trips to Nazi Germany, where he received the Order of the German Eagle in 1938. His isolationist speeches, especially a 1941 radio address blaming “British, Jewish, and Roosevelt Administration” influences for pushing America toward war, branded him a Nazi sympathizer. President Roosevelt publicly condemned him, prompting Lindbergh’s resignation from the Army Air Corps.

### World War II Service
Despite political controversy, Lindbergh served as a civilian consultant during WWII, flying 50 combat missions in the Pacific. He improved P-38 fighter fuel efficiency and was credited with shooting down a Japanese aircraft. His military commission was restored in 1954.

### Later Life and Legacy
Post-war, Lindbergh shifted focus to environmentalism and conservation, advocating for simplicity and protecting endangered species and indigenous peoples. He helped establish Haleakalā and Voyageurs National Parks and supported the Tasaday people in the Philippines. He died in 1974 and was buried in Maui.

## Terms
- **Spirit of St. Louis**: Custom-built monoplane used for the 1927 transatlantic flight.
- **Orteig Prize**: $25,000 award for first nonstop flight between New York and Paris.
- **Dead Reckoning**: Navigation method using direction, speed, and time without external references.
- **Lindbergh Boom**: Surge in aviation interest and investment following his 1927 flight.
- **Lindbergh Law**: Federal statute making kidnapping across state lines a capital offense.
- **America First Committee**: Isolationist group advocating U.S. neutrality before WWII.
- **Perfusion Pump**: Device developed by Lindbergh and Carrel to oxygenate organs outside the body.
- **Order of the German Eagle**: Nazi honor awarded to foreign dignitaries; Lindbergh received it in 1938.
- **Tasaday**: Indigenous Philippine tribe Lindbergh sought to protect in the 1970s.
- **Lindbergh Hour Angle Watch**: Navigation aid co-designed with Longines, still in production.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Nazi Sympathizer?**: Lindbergh never endorsed Nazism publicly but accepted German honors and made statements aligning with isolationist and antisemitic tropes. Historians debate whether he was a sympathizer or simply naive and politically misguided.
- **Kidnapping Case Guilt**: Richard Hauptmann was convicted and executed for the 1932 kidnapping, but many question the evidence, citing lack of fingerprints, questionable witness testimony, and possible frame-up.
- **Political Impact**: Lindbergh’s pre-war influence on U.S. isolationism remains contentious—some argue he delayed necessary intervention, while others see him as a principled voice against entanglement.
- **Scientific Legacy**: The Lindbergh-Carrel perfusion pump was groundbreaking in concept but ultimately impractical. Its historical significance lies more in inspiration than direct application.
- **Personal Integrity**: Revelations of seven secret children with three mistresses after his death have complicated his legacy, raising questions about hypocrisy versus private indiscretion.
