# Immanuel Kant

## Overview
Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) was a German philosopher from Königsberg, East Prussia, whose systematic works established him as a central figure of the Enlightenment and modern Western philosophy. His "critical project" sought to secure the possibility of knowledge and morality against skepticism (notably David Hume’s) and determinism by arguing that the human mind actively structures experience. In the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781/1787), he defended **transcendental idealism**: space and time are a priori "forms of intuition" (subjective conditions of sensibility), not properties of things in themselves, meaning we know only appearances (*phenomena*), not *noumena* (things in themselves). This "Copernican Revolution" in philosophy posits that objects conform to the mind’s cognitive structure. In ethics, Kant grounded morality in reason alone, formulating the **categorical imperative**—a universal, unconditional moral law requiring that one act only on maxims that can be universalized and that humanity be treated as an end, never merely as a means. He argued for a republican political order and a federation of states to secure perpetual peace, and viewed true religion as grounded in moral practice. His influence spans German Idealism, modern ethics, political theory, aesthetics, and the philosophy of mind.

## Body

### Early Life and Pre-Critical Period
Kant was born into a Pietist Lutheran family of modest means. His early education was strict and disciplinary, emphasizing Latin and religion over science. At the University of Königsberg (1740), Martin Knutzen introduced him to Newtonian physics and dissuaded him from Leibnizian rationalism (pre-established harmony) and traditional idealism. After his father’s death (1746), Kant worked as a private tutor for nearly a decade while continuing research. During this "pre-critical" phase, he contributed to natural science: the *Universal Natural History* (1755) proposed the **nebular hypothesis** (solar system formation from a gas cloud), correctly identified the Milky Way as a disk of stars, and suggested distant nebulae were other galaxies. He also anticipated the Coriolis force (1756) and offered naturalistic explanations for earthquakes (1756). In the early 1760s, he published logical and metaphysical works (*The False Subtlety of the Four Syllogistic Figures*, *The Only Possible Argument for the Existence of God*) and the aesthetic *Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime* (1764). His 1770 Inaugural Dissertation (*On the Form and Principles of the Sensible and Intelligible World*) introduced the crucial distinction between sensibility (receptivity) and understanding (spontaneity), identifying the error of **subreption** (confusing the two) as the bane of metaphysics.

### The Critical Turn: *Critique of Pure Reason*
Awakened from "dogmatic slumber" by David Hume’s skepticism regarding causality and the self, Kant sought to ground synthetic a priori knowledge—judgments that are necessary/universal yet amplify knowledge (e.g., mathematics, "every event has a cause"). He distinguished **analytic judgments** (predicate contained in subject, a priori) from **synthetic judgments** (predicate adds to subject, usually a posteriori). The central problem: *How are synthetic a priori judgments possible?*

Kant’s solution is **transcendental idealism**. In the *Transcendental Aesthetic*, he argues space and time are not empirical concepts nor things in themselves, but pure **forms of intuition** (space = outer sense, time = inner sense). They are "transcendentally ideal" (mind-dependent conditions of experience) but "empirically real" (valid for all objects of possible experience). Consequently, we know only **appearances** (*phenomena*), not **things in themselves** (*noumena*).

In the *Transcendental Analytic*, Kant deduces the **categories** (12 pure concepts of understanding, e.g., causality, substance) as necessary conditions for unifying the manifold of intuition into objective experience. The **Transcendental Deduction** argues that the "I think" (transcendental unity of apperception) requires synthesis according to categories. The *Analytic of Principles* (schematism, analogies of experience) shows how categories apply to temporal/spatial intuition: substance persists, events follow causal laws, coexisting substances interact (community).

The *Transcendental Dialectic* critiques reason’s attempt to know the unconditioned (soul, world-whole, God). Reason inevitably generates **antinomies** (contradictory but equally plausible proofs: world finite/infinite, matter divisible/indivisible, freedom/determinism, necessary being exists/does not). Kant resolves these via transcendental idealism: the antinomies arise from applying categories beyond possible experience. He refutes traditional proofs for God (ontological, cosmological, physico-theological) but assigns the "ideas of reason" a **regulative** function: guiding inquiry toward systematic unity.

### Moral Philosophy: Autonomy and the Categorical Imperative
Kant’s ethics (developed in *Groundwork* 1785, *Critique of Practical Reason* 1788, *Metaphysics of Morals* 1797) centers on the **good will**—the only unconditionally good thing—which acts from **duty** according to the **moral law** legislated by reason. This law is the **categorical imperative** (CI), distinct from hypothetical imperatives (conditional on desires). The CI binds all rational agents *qua* rational.

Kant offers three main formulations claimed to be equivalent:
1.  **Formula of Universal Law**: Act only on maxims you can will as universal law.
2.  **Formula of Humanity**: Treat humanity (rational agency) always as an end, never merely as a means.
3.  **Formula of Autonomy/Realm of Ends**: Act as a legislating member of a kingdom of ends.

A **maxim** is a subjective principle of volition. It fails the CI test if universalizing it yields a **contradiction in conception** (the maxim self-destructs, e.g., false promising destroys the institution of promising) or a **contradiction in the will** (no rational being could will the resulting world, e.g., universal non-beneficence).

Kant distinguishes the **transcendental idea of freedom** (a theoretical problem regarding causality) from the **practical concept of freedom** (independence from sensuous determination). He argues we must presuppose freedom for morality to be possible, resolving the Third Antinomy by placing the noumenal self outside the deterministic phenomenal chain. In *The Doctrine of Virtue* (1797), he elaborates a teleological ethics: duties are "ends that are duties"—one’s own perfection (natural and moral) and others' happiness.

### Political Philosophy and Perpetual Peace
Kant’s political theory (*Doctrine of Right*, *Perpetual Peace* 1795) defines the state as a **Rechtsstaat** (union under law) based on the universal principle of right: an action is right if its maxim can coexist with everyone’s freedom under a universal law. He favored **republican** (representative) constitutions over direct democracy (which he deemed despotic) and advocated a voluntary **federation of free states** (*foedus pacificum*) to secure perpetual peace. He opposed standing armies, wars of conquest, and colonialism, arguing that nature’s "mechanical course" drives history toward a lawful cosmopolitan condition (providence/fate). In *Perpetual Peace*, he explicitly rejected European colonialism and racial hierarchy as morally unjust.

### Religion, Aesthetics, and Anthropology
In *Religion within the Bounds of Bare Reason* (1793), Kant interprets Christianity as a symbolic representation of moral religion: "true religion" is morality legislated by reason. He criticized ritual, superstition, and hierarchical church authority as "counterfeit service." This led to a royal censorship order (1794) forbidding him to publish on religion; he responded in *The Conflict of the Faculties* (1798).

The *Critique of the Power of Judgment* (1790) analyzes **judgments of taste**. Beauty is not an object’s property but the consciousness of pleasure from the "free play" of imagination and understanding. It is **disinterested** (no concept/interest), yet claims **universal validity** via *sensus communis* (common sense). The **sublime** (mathematical: imagination fails at boundlessness, reason asserts infinity; dynamical: nature’s might threatens, reason resists) reveals our supersensible vocation and supports morality. Kant also developed an incongruity theory of humor.

Kant lectured on **anthropology** for 23 years (*Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View*, 1798), pioneering a dimensional approach to temperament (energy/balance vs. emotionality) influencing later psychology. However, his writings contain explicit **scientific racism** (hierarchical racial categories, derogatory remarks on non-Europeans, anti-Semitism, opposition to miscegenation) and **sexism** (denying women full moral autonomy and civic equality, assigning them "beautiful virtue" based on feeling). Scholars debate the centrality of these views to his system; Pauline Kleingeld argues he rejected racial hierarchy in the 1790s as incompatible with universal morality, though he never explicitly repudiated earlier claims.

### Influence and Legacy
Kant’s "Copernican Revolution" established the **human subject as the center of inquiry**, the **limits of knowledge**, the **conditions of possibility**, the **active constitution of experience**, **moral autonomy**, and the **ends-principle**. Though German Idealism (Fichte, Schelling, Hegel) revised his system, and neo-Kantianism revived it (1865+), his framework underpins phenomenology, existentialism, critical theory, and analytic philosophy. He influenced the democratic peace theory (Alexander Wendt), Rawls and Habermas (political liberalism), Piaget and Jung (psychology), and Einstein (early view on synthetic a priori geometry). In the 2020s, his theory of mind informs cognitive science and formal logic.

## Terms
- ****Transcendental Idealism**** — The doctrine that space and time are a priori forms of human sensibility (conditions of possible experience), not properties of things in themselves; we know appearances (*phenomena*), not *noumena*.
- ****Synthetic a Priori**** — A judgment that is necessary/universal (a priori) yet expands knowledge (synthetic), e.g., "7+5=12" or "every event has a cause." The central target of Kant’s epistemology.
- ****Categories**** — The 12 pure concepts of the understanding (e.g., unity, plurality, causality, substance) that structure the manifold of intuition into objective experience.
- ****Transcendental Deduction**** — Kant’s argument that the categories apply necessarily to objects of experience because the unity of self-consciousness ("I think") requires synthesis according to these concepts.
- ****Noumena / Phenomena**** — *Noumena*: things as they are in themselves, unknowable to us. *Phenomena*: things as they appear, structured by our forms of intuition and categories.
- ****Categorical Imperative (CI)**** — The supreme, unconditional moral law of reason: act only on maxims universalizable as law; treat humanity as an end in itself; legislate for a realm of ends.
- ****Maxim**** — A subjective principle of volition (the agent’s policy), distinct from the objective practical law. Tested by the CI for contradictions in conception or will.
- ****Autonomy**** — The property of the will whereby it is a law to itself (self-legislation), independent of sensuous impulses; the basis of human dignity and morality.
- ****Regulative Ideas**** — Concepts of reason (Soul, World, God) that cannot be objects of knowledge but guide inquiry toward systematic unity.
- ****Sensus Communis**** — "Common sense": a subjective principle claiming universal agreement for judgments of taste (beauty), grounding their subjective universality.

## Debates and Open Questions
*   **Interpretation of Transcendental Idealism**: "Two-world" (appearances and things-in-themselves are distinct ontological realms) vs. "Two-aspect/One-world" (same objects considered from different epistemic standpoints). Henry Allison defends the latter; the debate shapes whether Kant is a phenomenalist or a metaphysical idealist.
*   **Success of the Transcendental Deduction**: Whether Kant successfully proves the categories apply to *all* objects of experience, or merely to the structure of judgment. The "gap" between the subjective unity of apperception and objective validity remains contested.
*   **Unity of the Categorical Imperative Formulations**: Kant claims the three formulas are equivalent; many scholars (e.g., Allen Wood, Christine Korsgaard) argue they yield different duties or are logically distinct.
*   **Role of the "Fact of Reason"**: In the 2nd *Critique*, Kant claims moral consciousness of the CI is a "fact of reason" needing no deduction. Critics debate whether this avoids the need for a metaphysical proof of freedom.
*   **Racism and Sexism vs. Universalism**: Whether Kant’s explicit racial hierarchy and gender subordination (pre-1790s) are contingent prejudices or structural consequences of his anthropology/teleology. Kleingeld argues his late cosmopolitanism (1795) rejects hierarchy as incompatible with the CI; Mills and Bernasconi view him as a founder of "scientific racism."
*   **Thing-in-Itself**: Whether the concept is coherent (Schopenhauer, Hegel, early critics argued it is a contradiction: causally affecting us yet outside causality) or a necessary limiting concept.
*   **Religion**: Whether *Religion within the Bounds of Bare Reason* reduces Christianity to ethics (secularization) or elevates morality to the status of religion (theological affirmation).
