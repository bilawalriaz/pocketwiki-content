# Alexander Island

## Overview
Alexander Island is the largest island in Antarctica, located west of the Antarctic Peninsula. It is significant for its unique geological history, its role in early Antarctic exploration, and its status as a major, ice-covered landmass whose insular nature was only confirmed in the 20th century.

## Body
### Discovery and Territorial Status
Alexander Island was discovered in 1821 by Fabian Gottlieb von Bellingshausen and named for Tsar Alexander I. It was mistakenly believed to be part of the Antarctic mainland until December 1940, when a two-person sledge party led by Finn Ronne and Carl Eklund proved it was an island. The island has been claimed by the United Kingdom (1908), Chile (1940), and Argentina (1942), but under the Antarctic Treaty, no claim is officially recognized. It hosts the British Fossil Bluff meteorological centre and refuelling base.

### Geography and Glaciology
The island's surface is predominantly ice-covered, with exposed nunataks (rocky peaks protruding through ice) forming north-south trending mountain ranges like the Douglas Range. Glaciers flow west into the Bach and Wilkins Ice Shelves and east into the George VI Ice Shelf, which connects Alexander Island to Palmer Land. A notable feature is Hodgson Lake, a former subglacial lake sealed beneath perennial ice. The lake was covered by an ice sheet at least 470 m thick during the Last Glacial Maximum, which began thinning about 13,500 years ago and left the lake ice-covered before 11,000 years ago.

### Geology
The island's geology is primarily shaped by the subduction of proto-Pacific oceanic crust along the Antarctic Peninsula margin from the latest Triassic to Late Tertiary times. The western portion features the LeMay Group, an accretionary prism complex of deformed sedimentary and igneous rocks. The eastern coast is dominated by the 4-km thick Fossil Bluff Group, a sedimentary sequence containing fossils like ammonites and belemnites. After subduction ceased, alkali basalts erupted, with ages ranging from 7.6 million years ago to less than 1 million years ago. The LeMay Range Fault separates these major geological units.

## Terms
- **Nunataks**: Isolated peaks of rock that project above the surface of an ice sheet or glacier.
- **Accretionary prism**: A mass of sedimentary material scraped off a subducting oceanic plate and added to the edge of the overriding continental plate.
- **Subduction**: A geological process where one tectonic plate moves under another and sinks into the mantle.
- **Arkose**: A type of sandstone containing at least 25% feldspar.
- **Turbidite**: A geologic deposit of a type of turbidity current, which is essentially an underwater landslide of sediment.
- **Alkali basalt**: A fine-grained volcanic rock rich in sodium and potassium.
- **Perennial ice**: Ice that persists year-round, not melting completely in summer.
- **Subglacial lake**: A lake that has formed under a glacier or ice sheet.
