# Dante Alighieri

## Overview
Dante Alighieri (c. May 1265 – September 14, 1321) was an Italian poet, writer, and philosopher whose *Divine Comedy* is considered the greatest literary work in the Italian language and a cornerstone of medieval literature. Writing in the Tuscan vernacular rather than Latin, he established the foundation for the modern standardized Italian language and pioneered the *terza rima* interlocking rhyme scheme. His political career in Florence ended in permanent exile in 1302 amid factional strife between White and Black Guelphs; this displacement catalyzed his most ambitious philosophical and poetic works, including the *Comedy*, *Monarchia*, and *Convivio*. He died in Ravenna, where he remains buried despite centuries of Florentine requests for his remains.

## Body

### Early Life and Family
Dante was born in Florence around May 1265, deduced from the *Inferno*’s opening line placing the pilgrim at age 35 in the year 1300 (half the biblical lifespan of 70). His father, Alighiero di Bellincione, was a moneylender of low public standing; his mother, Bella degli Abati, died before he was ten. The family claimed descent from ancient Romans via great-great-grandfather Cacciaguida (born c. 1100) and aligned with the Guelph faction supporting the papacy against the imperial Ghibellines. Though Ghibellines expelled Guelphs after the 1260 Battle of Montaperti, Dante’s family avoided reprisals due to Alighiero’s obscurity. At nine, Dante met Beatrice Portinari, daughter of Folco, initiating a lifelong spiritual obsession. At twelve, he was betrothed to Gemma di Manetto Donati of the powerful Donati family; they married c. 1285 and had three children (Pietro, Jacopo, Antonia) before his exile. Dante fought at the 1289 Battle of Campaldino as a *feditore* (first-attack cavalry), a victory that reformed the Florentine constitution requiring guild membership for office; he joined the Physicians’ and Apothecaries’ Guild c. 1295.

### Education, Poetry, and the *Dolce Stil Nuovo*
Dante likely studied at home or church schools, mastering Tuscan poetry, Provençal troubadour verse (notably Arnaut Daniel), and Latin classics (Cicero, Ovid, Virgil). He revered Bolognese poet Guido Guinizelli as his "father" in *Purgatorio* XXVI. Around age 18, he joined Guido Cavalcanti, Lapo Gianni, Cino da Pistoia, and scholar Brunetto Latini as leaders of the *dolce stil nuovo* ("sweet new style"), a term Dante coined for poetry exploring love’s spiritual and philosophical dimensions. His love for Beatrice—exchanged only as street greetings after age 18—became the engine of his verse; she appears semi-divine, offering spiritual instruction. Her death in 1290 drove him to philosophy (*Convivio* records reading Boethius’s *Consolation of Philosophy* and Cicero’s *On Friendship*). He studied at the Dominican Santa Maria Novella, engaging in disputes between Franciscans (following Bonaventure) and Dominicans (following Aquinas). Roughly fifty lyric poems (*Rime*) survive, many later embedded in *Vita Nuova* and *Convivio*.

### Florentine Politics and the Guelph Schism
After the Guelph victory at Campaldino, the faction split into White Guelphs (led by Vieri dei Cerchi, Dante’s party), who sought independence from papal interference, and Black Guelphs (led by Corso Donati), who supported Pope Boniface VIII. Whites initially expelled Blacks, but Boniface planned military occupation via Charles of Valois (brother of Philip IV of France). In 1301, after Florence insulted papal ambassadors, Dante was sent in a delegation to Rome to dissuade the Pope. Boniface dismissed the others and detained Dante; simultaneously, Charles entered Florence with Black Guelphs, destroying property and installing a Black government under podestà Cante dei Gabrielli da Gubbio.

### Exile and Philosophical Maturity
In March 1302, Dante was condemned *in absentia* to two years’ exile and a heavy fine for alleged corruption during his 1300 priorate. Refusing to pay (assets seized, innocence claimed), he received a perpetual exile sentence—death by burning if he returned. He wandered: Verona (guest of Bartolomeo I della Scala), Sarzana, possibly Lucca (with Gentucca, thanked in *Purgatorio* XXIV). Claims of Paris or Oxford visits (from Boccaccio decades later) lack evidence; a 1311 letter places him "beneath the springs of Arno, near Tuscany." He joined White Guelph attempts to retake Florence but abandoned them due to treachery and infighting, vowing to be "a party of one." In 1310, he pinned hopes on Emperor Henry VII (5,000 troops), writing to him and Italian princes demanding destruction of Black Guelphs and composing *Monarchia* advocating universal monarchy under Henry. Henry’s 1312 attack on Florence failed to restore Dante; Henry died in 1313, ending imperial hope. Dante settled in Verona under Cangrande I della Scala (later placed in *Paradiso* XVII), corresponding with Dominican theologian Nicholas Brunacci (student of Aquinas and Albert the Great). In 1315, Florence offered amnesty requiring public penance and fine; Dante refused. When Uguccione della Faggiuola later threatened Florence, the sentence was commuted to house arrest if Dante swore never to enter; he refused, and the death sentence was extended to his sons.

### Composition of the *Divine Comedy*
The *Comedy* was conceived during exile, likely after Dante accepted his political ruin. It surpasses earlier works in ambition and theological depth. Beatrice returns as guide with expanded significance beyond *Vita Nuova*. Francesco da Barberino’s *Documenti d’Amore* (c. 1314–15) notes Dante following the *Aeneid* in a poem called "Comedy" set in the underworld. *Inferno* was circulating by 1317 (marginal quotes in Bolognese records); *Paradiso* was likely finished before death but possibly published posthumously. The three canticles—*Inferno*, *Purgatorio*, *Paradiso*—trace a pilgrimage from moral confusion to the vision of God, guided first by Virgil (reason/classical virtue) then Beatrice (revelation/grace). Dante wrote in a literary "Italian" based on Tuscan with Latin and other dialect elements, deliberately reaching laymen, clergy, and poets across Italy. He invented *terza rima* (ABA BCB CDC...), an interlocking three-line rhyme scheme. The title *Commedia* reflects the classical sense: a journey from disorder to an ordered, divinely willed good ending. Boccaccio later added *Divina*.

### Death, Burial, and Posthumous Fate
Dante died in Ravenna on September 14, 1321, aged ~56, of quartan malaria contracted returning from a diplomatic mission to Venice. He was attended by his children and buried at San Pier Maggiore (later Basilica di San Francesco). Bernardo Bembo erected a tomb in 1483. In 1329, Cardinal Bertrand du Pouget (nephew of Pope John XXII) condemned *Monarchia* as heretical and sought to burn Dante’s bones; allies Ostasio I da Polenta and Pino della Tosa prevented it. Florence repeatedly requested the remains; Ravenna custodians refused, once hiding bones in a false monastery wall. Florence built an empty tomb in Santa Croce in 1829 inscribed *Onorate l’altissimo poeta* ("Honor the most exalted poet," *Inferno* IV). In 1945, the fascist government considered moving the remains to the Valtellina Redoubt as a symbol of "Italianness" but did not act. A displayed death mask (since 1911) is likely a 1483 carving, not a true mask.

### Legacy and Reception
Boccaccio wrote the first biography (*Vita di Dante*, post-1348), supplemented by Giovanni Villani’s *Nuova Cronica*. Sixteenth-century Protestants (John Bale, John Foxe) claimed Dante as proto-Protestant for opposing the pope. The 19th-century "Dante revival" (part of Romantic medievalism) cemented his global stature: Carlyle called him "world-deep… spokesman of the Middle Ages"; Longfellow, Cary, and Hunt translated him. Italy named its first dreadnought *Dante Alighieri* (1913). Pope Benedict XV’s 1921 encyclical *In praeclara summorum* hailed him as Catholic genius and pride of humanity. Pope Paul VI’s 1965 *Altissimi cantus* and a golden cross (blessed by Pope Francis in 2020) honored the 700th birth anniversary. A 2007 facial reconstruction altered traditional iconography. Florence formally apologized in 2008; a symbolic 2021 virtual retrial cleared his name. The 750th birth anniversary (2015) was celebrated at Italy’s Senate with Pope Francis’s apostolic letter *Cando lucis aeternae*.

### Works
**Major works (chronological):**
- *Il Fiore* and *Detto d’Amore* (c. 1283–87): early sonnet sequences adapting *Roman de la Rose*; attribution debated.
- *La Vita Nuova* (c. 1294): *prosimetrum* (verse-prose mix) narrating love for Beatrice; pioneering vernacular commentary.
- *De vulgari eloquentia* (1302–05): Latin treatise defending vernacular literature; inspired by Raimon Vidal de Bezaudun’s *Razos de trobar*.
- *Convivio* (c. 1304–07): unfinished commentary on three long poems; vernacular philosophical encyclopedia.
- *Monarchia* (c. 1313): Latin political treatise arguing universal monarchy (under Henry VII) necessary for temporal peace, subordinate to Church for eternal peace; burned as heretical in 1329.
- *Divine Comedy* (c. 1308–1320): 100 cantos in *terza rima*; journey through Hell, Purgatory, Paradise.
- *Eclogues* (1320): two Latin pastoral poems addressed to Giovanni del Virgilio.
- *Quaestio de aqua et terra* (1320): theological treatise on Earth’s land-water configuration.
- *Le Rime*: posthumous collection of miscellaneous lyrics.

**Collections:** Key holdings at Dante’s House Museum (Florence, opened 1965, refurbished 2020), Princeton University Library (160 vols., two 15th-c. *Comedy* editions), University College London Special Collections (~3,000 vols., bequeathed by Henry Clark Barlow 1876), and Beinecke Library at Yale (c. 1385–1400 *Comedy* manuscript).

## Terms
- **Terza rima**: Interlocking three-line rhyme scheme (ABA BCB CDC...) invented by Dante for the *Comedy*.
- **Dolce stil nuovo** ("sweet new style"): Poetic movement coined by Dante emphasizing love’s spiritual/philosophical dimension; practitioners included Cavalcanti, Gianni, Cino da Pistoia.
- **Guelphs / Ghibellines**: Rival factions in medieval Italian cities; Guelphs supported the papacy, Ghibellines the Holy Roman Emperor.
- **White Guelphs / Black Guelphs**: Post-1289 split; Whites (Cerchi) wanted Florentine autonomy from Rome; Blacks (Donati) supported papal authority.
- **Prior**: Highest executive office in the Florentine republic; Dante served a two-month term in 1300.
- **Podestà**: Chief magistrate appointed from outside the city to administer justice; Cante dei Gabrielli da Gubbio installed by Blacks in 1301.
- **Prosimetrum**: Mixed verse-and-prose form used in *Vita Nuova* and *Convivio*.
- **Monarchia**: Dante’s Latin treatise arguing a universal temporal monarchy is necessary for world peace, guided spiritually by the Church.
- **De vulgari eloquentia**: Treatise "On Eloquence in the Vernacular" analyzing Romance languages and defending literary use of Tuscan.
- **Quartan malaria**: Fever recurring every fourth day (72-hour cycle); cause of Dante’s death.

## Debates and open questions
- **Birth date**: Inferred from *Inferno* I and *Paradiso* XXII (Gemini, May 11–June 11 Julian 1265); not documented.
- **Education**: No records survive; home, church school, or university (Bologna?) are conjectured.
- **Paris/Oxford visits**: Claimed by Boccaccio (c. 1350s) but unsupported by contemporary evidence; likely legendary embellishments from Dante’s erudition.
- **Composition and publication of the *Comedy***: Uncertain start date (post-1302?); *Inferno* circulating by 1317; whether canticles released singly or together unknown; *Paradiso* possibly posthumous.
- **Authorship of *Il Fiore* / *Detto d’Amore***: Attributed to Dante in some manuscripts; style suggests early novice work; scholarly consensus divided.
- **Location of remains**: Bones hidden in false wall (17th–18th c.?); 1865 Ravenna exhumation confirmed skeleton; Florence’s Santa Croce tomb remains empty.
- **Political sincerity in *Monarchia***: Debate whether Dante genuinely expected Henry VII to fulfill the role or used imperial theory to critique papal overreach.
