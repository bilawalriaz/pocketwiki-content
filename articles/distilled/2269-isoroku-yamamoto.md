# Isoroku Yamamoto

## Overview
Isoroku Yamamoto was a prominent Imperial Japanese Navy admiral who commanded the Combined Fleet during World War II. He is best known for planning the attack on Pearl Harbor and for his early advocacy of naval aviation, but he also expressed deep reservations about a protracted war with the United States. His death in 1943, resulting from a U.S. intelligence operation, was a significant blow to Japanese military morale.

## Timeline
- **April 4, 1884** — Born as Isoroku Takano in Nagaoka, Japan.
- **1904** — Graduated from the Imperial Japanese Naval Academy.
- **1905** — Wounded and lost two fingers at the Battle of Tsushima.
- **1919–1921** — Studied at Harvard University and served as a naval attaché in Washington, D.C.
- **1939** — Appointed commander-in-chief of the Combined Fleet.
- **December 7, 1941** — Oversaw the attack on Pearl Harbor.
- **June 4–7, 1942** — Commanded Japanese forces at the Battle of Midway, resulting in a major defeat.
- **April 18, 1943** — Killed when his aircraft was shot down by U.S. fighters over Bougainville.

## Body

### Early Life and Career
Yamamoto was born into a samurai family and adopted into the Yamamoto family in 1916. He graduated from the Imperial Naval Academy in 1904 and served in the Russo-Japanese War, where he was severely wounded at the Battle of Tsushima. His subsequent studies at Harvard University and postings as a naval attaché in Washington, D.C., gave him a deep understanding of American industrial capacity and naval power. These experiences convinced him that Japan could not win a prolonged war against the United States.

### Strategic Views and Rise to Command
Yamamoto became a leading advocate for naval aviation, believing aircraft carriers would be decisive in future conflicts. He opposed Japan's alliance with Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy and the invasion of China, positions that made him a target of assassination threats from pro-war militarists. In 1939, he was appointed commander-in-chief of the Combined Fleet, partly to protect him from assassins. Despite political opposition, his popularity within the fleet and ties to the imperial family secured his position. He planned a surprise attack on Pearl Harbor to cripple the U.S. Pacific Fleet, believing Japan's only hope was a quick, decisive victory.

### World War II Command
The attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, was a tactical success, sinking or damaging eight U.S. battleships and destroying 188 aircraft. However, it failed to destroy the American aircraft carriers and galvanized U.S. resolve for war. Yamamoto had predicted Japan would "run wild" for six months to a year but had no confidence for the long term. This proved accurate; after initial conquests, Japan's advance was halted at the Battle of Midway in June 1942, where four Japanese carriers were sunk. Yamamoto then committed forces to the attritional Guadalcanal campaign, which further depleted Japan's irreplaceable trained aviators.

### Death and Legacy
In April 1943, U.S. code breakers intercepted Yamamoto's flight itinerary for an inspection tour. On April 18, U.S. Army Air Forces P-38 Lightning fighters intercepted and shot down his aircraft over Bougainville, killing him. His death was a major morale blow to Japan. Yamamoto is remembered as a brilliant but reluctant strategist who foresaw the futility of a long war with the United States but was bound to execute the policy of Japan's military leadership.

## Terms
- **Combined Fleet**: The main operational fleet of the Imperial Japanese Navy during World War II.
- **Naval aviation**: The use of aircraft, particularly from aircraft carriers, for naval warfare.
- **Tripartite Pact**: The 1940 alliance between Japan, Nazi Germany, and Fascist Italy.
- **Kantai Kessen**: The traditional Japanese naval doctrine of seeking a single, decisive battleship engagement.
- **Kidō Butai**: The Japanese carrier strike force, notably used in the Pearl Harbor attack.
- **JN-25**: The Japanese naval code broken by U.S. intelligence, crucial to the victory at Midway.
- **Operation Vengeance**: The U.S. military operation to intercept and shoot down Admiral Yamamoto's aircraft.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Pearl Harbor Decision**: Historians debate whether Yamamoto's plan violated the principle of concentration of force and was overly complex, or if its failure was due to unforeseen American intelligence and tactical responses.
- **Midway Planning**: The reasons for the failure of Japanese reconnaissance before Midway, and whether Yamamoto's divided dispositions doomed the operation, are subjects of ongoing analysis.
- **Yamamoto's True Beliefs**: There is discussion about whether Yamamoto truly believed a surprise attack could lead to a negotiated peace, or if he was merely executing a strategy he knew would ultimately fail.
