# Jeongjo of Joseon

## Overview
Jeongjo (r. 1776–1800) was the 22nd monarch of Joseon, widely regarded as one of Korea's greatest leaders. His reign represented a cultural and political renaissance, marked by reforms to strengthen royal authority, promote scholarship, and balance factional politics. Despite his achievements, his death left unresolved issues that contributed to the later era of "power politics."

- **1775** — Appointed regent by King Yeongjo, but without military power.
- **10 March 1776** — Coronation at Gyeonghuigung following Yeongjo's death.
- **1776** — Survives a coup attempt by members of the Noron faction.
- **1782** — Begins selecting officers by competitive examination for the royal guard.
- **1785** — Establishes the Jangyongyeong royal guard unit.
- **1791** — Enacts the Shinhae Tonggong free trade law.
- **18 August 1800** — Dies suddenly at age 47 under uncertain circumstances.
- **7 December 1899** — Posthumously honored as Jeongjo Sanghwangjae.

## Body

### Early Life and Succession
Jeongjo was the son of Crown Prince Sado, who was executed by King Yeongjo in 1762. To secure his succession, King Yeongjo made him the adoptive son of the deceased Crown Prince Hyojang in 1764. This move was contested by the Noron faction, who argued Jeongjo was ineligible as the "son of a prisoner." He became regent in 1775 but was denied military authority.

### Reign and Political Struggles
Upon ascending the throne, Jeongjo immediately declared his identity as the son of Crown Prince Sado, signaling his intent to clear his father's name. He continued the Tangpyeong policy of political reconciliation but prioritized recruiting talented scholars regardless of faction. He faced constant threats, including a 1776 coup attempt by Noron members, which he personally helped suppress. His reliance on the controversial politician Hong Guk-yeong, while initially strengthening royal power, later left a legacy of influence by close retainers.

### Cultural Renaissance and Reforms
Jeongjo fostered a cultural golden age. He established the Kyujanggak royal library to improve governance and recruit gifted officials, and opened government positions to those previously barred by social status. He supported Silhak (Practical Learning) scholars and promoted Neo-Confucian philosophy. Key reforms included the 1791 Shinhae Tonggong law, which abolished exclusive merchant rights and allowed free trade. He also commissioned the construction of Hwaseong Fortress to guard his father's tomb, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

### Death and Legacy
In his final years, Jeongjo arranged the marriage of his heir, Sunjo, to a daughter of the Andong Kim clan. He died suddenly in 1800 at age 47. This marriage decision inadvertently empowered the Andong Kim family, setting the stage for the subsequent era of Sedo politics, where royal authority was dominated by powerful in-law families.

## Terms
- **Tangpyeong**: A political policy of "magnificent harmony" aimed at reconciling and balancing rival political factions.
- **Noron, Soron, Namin**: Major political factions in Joseon; Jeongjo often favored Soron and Namin to check the powerful Noron.
- **Silhak**: "Practical Learning," a scholarly movement emphasizing empirical study and reform.
- **Kyujanggak**: A royal library and academic institute established by Jeongjo to promote scholarship and recruit talent.
- **Jangyongyeong**: A royal guard unit established by Jeongjo, selected by competitive examination to replace the older Naekeunwe.
- **Sedo politics**: "Power politics" or "in-law politics," an era after Jeongjo where royal authority was dominated by powerful noble families through marriage ties.
- **Shinhae Tonggong**: A free trade law enacted in 1791 that abolished exclusive merchant rights and allowed open market trade.
- **Munchebanjong**: A literary reform movement led by Jeongjo to critique and correct new styles of Korean writing composition.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The exact cause of Jeongjo's sudden death in 1800 remains uncertain and is the subject of speculation.
- The long-term impact of his appointment of Hong Guk-yeong is debated, as it both strengthened royal power and set a precedent for influence by close retainers.
- Scholars debate whether his final marriage arrangement for his heir was a strategic error that directly enabled the subsequent Sedo politics.
