# Palaeography

## Overview
Palaeography is the study of historical writing systems, encompassing the deciphering, dating, and analysis of ancient handwriting and scripts. It is a foundational auxiliary science for historians, enabling the authentication and interpretation of manuscripts and documents by examining their physical and stylistic characteristics.

## Timeline
- **c. 1000–600 BC** — Aramaic script emerges as an international trade language in the Ancient Middle East.
- **c. 250 BC** — King Ashoka's edicts are inscribed in Brahmi and Kharosthi scripts in India.
- **1681** — Jean Mabillon publishes *De re diplomatica*, founding palaeography and diplomatics.
- **1708** — Bernard de Montfaucon coins the term "palaeography" in *Palaeographia Graeca*.
- **8th–9th c.** — Carolingian minuscule develops under Charlemagne, becoming the standard European script.
- **12th c.** — Carolingian minuscule evolves into Gothic minuscule (blackletter).
- **Early 15th c.** — Italian humanists, like Poggio Bracciolini, revive Carolingian forms to create the humanist script.
- **1465** — Printers begin using the humanist script as the basis for Roman typefaces.

## Body

### Definition and Founding
Palaeography is the study of historical writing systems, focusing on the forms, processes, and relationships of scripts as seen in manuscripts and documents. Its primary concern is the analysis of handwriting and printed media, with deciphering and dating as key applications. The discipline was founded by Jean Mabillon with his 1681 work *De re diplomatica*, and the term was coined by Bernard de Montfaucon in 1708.

### Application and Dating
Palaeography is essential for historians to decipher, date, and authenticate texts. It requires knowledge of letterforms, abbreviations, and writing materials. However, it is considered a "last resort for dating," with scholars cautioning that precise dating is difficult. A "rule of thumb" suggests avoiding dating a hand more precisely than a range of at least seventy or eighty years, as methodologies often rely on circular reasoning.

### Aramaic Palaeography
The Aramaic script, a modified Phoenician alphabet, became the international trade language of the Ancient Middle East. It introduced the *matres lectionis* system to indicate vowels and evolved into several subgroups, including square Hebrew and Nabataean. Imperial Aramaic, standardized under Darius I (522–486 BC), was the official language of the Achaemenid Empire.

### Greek Palaeography
Greek handwriting evolved from angular epigraphic capitals to rounded uncials and cursives, influenced by writing materials like papyrus. The Ptolemaic period (3rd–1st centuries BC) saw distinct book-hand and cursive styles. The Roman period brought greater variety and the "biblical hand," a square uncial script. The Byzantine period developed the minuscule script in the 8th century, which became the standard for Greek manuscripts.

### Latin Palaeography
Latin writing is divided into majuscule (capital-based) and minuscule (lowercase-based) periods. Majuscule scripts include square capitals, rustic capitals, and uncials. The minuscule period began with early cursive, which evolved into regional "national hands" like Merovingian and Visigothic after the Roman Empire's fall. The Carolingian minuscule, developed in the 8th century, unified writing across Europe and was later revived by Renaissance humanists.

### Indian Palaeography
Indian writing systems include Brahmi, the ancestral script of most South Asian scripts, and Kharosthi, derived from Aramaic. The Edicts of Ashoka (c. 250 BC) are the earliest major inscriptions. Scripts evolved through periods like the Gupta Empire, with regional variations leading to modern scripts like Nāgarī, Tamil, and Kannada. Many inscriptions remain undeciphered due to a decline in palaeographic expertise.

### Rise of Modern Writing
The Renaissance humanist script, based on Carolingian minuscule, was developed by figures like Poggio Bracciolini in the early 15th century. It was adopted by printers, forming the basis for Roman typefaces. The Italian cursive hand evolved into italic type. Gothic scripts persisted in some regions, but the humanist model became dominant in Western Europe.

## Terms
- **Palaeography**: The study of historical handwriting and scripts.
- **Uncial**: A rounded majuscule script used for books from the 4th to 8th centuries.
- **Minuscule**: A script with letters of varying height, based on four lines.
- **Cursive**: A rapid, flowing style of handwriting used for documents.
- **Carolingian minuscule**: A clear, standardized script developed under Charlemagne in the 8th century.
- **Gothic minuscule**: A bold, angular script that evolved from Carolingian minuscule in the 12th century.
- **Humanist script**: A Renaissance revival of Carolingian minuscule, the basis for modern Roman type.
- **Matres lectionis**: Letters used to indicate vowels in consonantal scripts like Aramaic.
- **Ductus**: The shape, order, and direction of strokes used to form letters.

## Debates and Open questions
- The precise origins of the Carolingian minuscule are disputed, with proposed locations including Rome, Tours, and Corbie.
- The reliability of palaeography for precise dating is debated, with scholars noting its reliance on circular reasoning and insufficient data.
- The development of Indian scripts like Brahmi is contested, with some scholars arguing for a discontinuous invention rather than gradual evolution from earlier glyphs.
