# The Beach Boys

## Overview

The Beach Boys are an American rock band formed in Hawthorne, California, in 1961 by brothers Brian, Dennis, and Carl Wilson, their cousin Mike Love, and friend Al Jardine. They became one of the most popular and influential acts of the rock era, known for vocal harmonies and lyrics reflecting a Southern California youth culture of surfing, cars, and romance—the "California sound." Under Brian Wilson's leadership, they incorporated classical and jazz elements and unconventional recording techniques, helping legitimize popular music as an art form and influencing psychedelia, power pop, progressive rock, punk, alternative, and lo-fi music. They have sold over 100 million records worldwide and, as of 2026, have had 37 songs reach the top 40 of the U.S. Billboard Hot 100, the most by an American band, with four topping the chart. In 2004, the group was ranked number 12 on Rolling Stone's list of the greatest artists of all time, the highest ranking of any American band.

## Body

### Formation and early years (1958–1962)

Brian Wilson received a reel-to-reel tape recorder for his 16th birthday in 1958 and taught himself overdubbing. He and Carl listened to Johnny Otis's KFOX radio show and R&B records, which inspired Brian to write songs. Dennis, the only avid surfer, suggested the group write about surfing. The band recorded "Surfin'" in 1961 and was renamed the Beach Boys by Candix Records. Murry Wilson managed the group and landed their first paying gig on New Year's Eve 1961. Jardine left in early 1962 and was replaced by David Marks.

### Capitol era and commercial peak (1962–1966)

The Beach Boys signed with Capitol in 1962. Their early albums—*Surfin' Safari*, *Surfin' U.S.A.*, *Surfer Girl*, and *Little Deuce Coupe*—established the surf and hot rod sound, though their vocal approach differed from instrumental surf bands like Dick Dale's. The British Invasion diminished Capitol's support, and "I Get Around" (1964) proved they could compete, becoming their first U.S. number 1. Brian withdrew from touring in January 1965 after a panic attack; Glen Campbell temporarily replaced him, then Bruce Johnston became a full member. *The Beach Boys Today!* (1965) marked a shift toward album-oriented, autobiographical songwriting.

Brian collaborated with Tony Asher on *Pet Sounds* (1966), which was widely influential but sold only about 500,000 units in the U.S. In Britain, publicist Derek Taylor's "Brian Wilson is a genius" campaign helped the album reach number 2. "Good Vibrations" (1966) was the most expensive single ever recorded, became their third U.S. number 1, and is widely acclaimed as one of the greatest rock recordings. The *Smile* project with lyricist Van Dyke Parks was shelved in 1967 after months of postponement, becoming the most legendary unreleased album in pop music history.

### Decline and internal fractures (1967–1978)

*Smiley Smile* (1967) was their worst-selling album to that date, and the group was virtually blacklisted by the music press. *Wild Honey* (1967) and *Friends* (1968) failed commercially. Brian's songwriting output declined; he admitted himself to a psychiatric hospital in 1968. The band's catalog was sold to Irving Almo Music in 1969 for $700,000, generating over $100 million in publishing royalties that neither Murry nor the band members ever received. The Manson Family murders in 1969 tainted the band by association, as they had recorded a Manson song.

Signing with Reprise in 1970, the band released *Sunflower* (1970) and *Surf's Up* (1971), which improved their critical standing. Blondie Chaplin and Ricky Fataar joined in 1972. After Murry's death in 1973, Brian retreated into drug abuse and overeating. The *Endless Summer* compilation (1974) unexpectedly became their second U.S. number 1 album, reviving their popularity. Brian returned to produce *15 Big Ones* (1976) and *The Beach Boys Love You* (1977), but the band split into factions—Dennis and Carl versus Mike and Jardine—with Brian neutral. They signed an $8 million CBS deal in 1977, and Love gained control of Brian's vote.

### Later years and legacy (1978–present)

Dennis drowned in 1983. "Kokomo" (1988) was their first U.S. number 1 in 22 years. After Carl's death in 1998, Jardine left and Love secured exclusive legal rights to tour as the Beach Boys. The full reunion album *That's Why God Made the Radio* (2012) debuted at number 3. Brian died in 2025; Johnston departed in 2026, leaving Love as the only official member to tour with the band. The band's intellectual property was sold to Irving Azoff's Iconic Artists Group in 2021.

In 2003, Rolling Stone ranked *Pet Sounds* at number two on the magazine's list of the greatest albums of all time, a position it retained on the list's later remakes and revisions in 2012, 2020, and 2023. In 2004, *Pet Sounds* was preserved in the National Recording Registry by the Library of Congress for being "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant." Their recordings of "In My Room", "Good Vibrations", "California Girls" and the entire *Pet Sounds* album have been inducted into the Grammy Hall of Fame. In 2017, a study of AllMusic's catalog indicated the Beach Boys as the sixth most frequently cited artist influence in its database.

### Musical style and innovations

The Beach Boys combined 1950s rock and roll, black R&B, and pre-rock pop vocal groups. Their signature elements included nasal vocal quality, falsetto harmony, and sudden group chiming on key lines. Brian handled most stages of the recording process, employing the Wrecking Crew studio musicians heavily in 1966–1967; the band themselves played most instruments on their other 1960s recordings. *Pet Sounds* was the first rock album to feature an Electro-Theremin and the first to depart entirely from the small-ensemble electric rock format. "Good Vibrations" was unprecedented in production complexity. The band's "California sound" helped supplant New York as the center of popular music and later influenced punk, alternative, and indie rock, with *Smiley Smile* cited as an origin of lo-fi bedroom pop.

## Terms

- **California sound**: The group's musical style reflecting Southern California youth culture of surfing, cars, and romance
- **Wrecking Crew**: Professional studio musicians who played backing tracks on select Beach Boys recordings, especially 1966–1967
- **Electro-Theremin**: An easier-to-play version of the theremin, first used in popular music on *Pet Sounds*
- **Wall of Sound**: Phil Spector's dense production technique; many of his musicians played on *Surfer Girl*
- **Brother Records**: The band's independent label launched in 1967
- **Transcendental Meditation**: Meditation technique taught by Maharishi Mahesh Yogi, influencing *Friends* (1968)
- **Chamber pop**: A term for artists influenced by the lush orchestrations of Brian Wilson, Lee Hazlewood, and Burt Bacharach
- **Yacht rock**: A West Coast style that "scavenged" the Beach Boys' aesthetic, per critic Dan O'Sullivan

## Debates and open questions

The use of studio musicians was a longstanding controversy, stemming from a misinterpreted statement in David Leaf's 1978 biography. Unedited session tapes and AFM sheets leaked in the 1990s revealed the Beach Boys played most instruments on their recordings until *The Beach Boys Today!* (1965), with 1966–1967 as the only years Wilson used the Wrecking Crew almost exclusively. The extent of Brian's sole creative control was also disputed; bandmates resented the "Brian as genius" narrative, though Dennis stated flatly: "Brian Wilson is the Beach Boys." Love's 1992 lawsuit over songwriting credits resulted in 35 songs being amended to credit him, and the parties disputed what Love was properly owed in royalties.
