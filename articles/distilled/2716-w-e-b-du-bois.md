# W. E. B. Du Bois

## Overview
W. E. B. Du Bois (1868–1963) was a pioneering American sociologist, historian, and civil rights activist whose work laid the intellectual foundation for the modern civil rights movement. He was the first African American to earn a doctorate from Harvard and a founder of the NAACP. Du Bois championed full civil rights and Pan-Africanism, arguing that the "color line" would be the defining problem of the 20th century. His scholarship and activism challenged racist ideologies and shaped global conversations on race, colonialism, and equality.

## Timeline
- **1868** — Born in Great Barrington, Massachusetts.
- **1895** — Becomes the first African American to earn a Ph.D. from Harvard University.
- **1899** — Publishes *The Philadelphia Negro*, the first case study of a Black community in the U.S.
- **1903** — Publishes *The Souls of Black Folk*, introducing the concept of "double consciousness."
- **1905** — Co-founds the Niagara Movement, demanding full civil rights.
- **1909** — Co-founds the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP).
- **1910** — Becomes editor of the NAACP's journal, *The Crisis*.
- **1935** — Publishes *Black Reconstruction in America*, challenging racist interpretations of the Reconstruction era.
- **1945** — Attends the founding conference of the United Nations.
- **1951** — Indicted for peace activism; passport confiscated by the U.S. government.
- **1961** — Joins the Communist Party and moves to Ghana.
- **1963** — Dies in Accra, Ghana, at age 95.

## Body

### Early Life and Education
Du Bois grew up in a tolerant community in Massachusetts, where his intellectual gifts were encouraged. He attended Fisk University, experiencing Southern racism for the first time, before earning two bachelor's degrees and a Ph.D. from Harvard. His graduate studies in Germany exposed him to leading social scientists like Max Weber. His early academic work, including the sociological study *The Philadelphia Negro* (1899), used empirical data to combat stereotypes and laid the groundwork for modern sociology.

### Activism and the NAACP
Du Bois rose to prominence opposing the "Atlanta Compromise," a plan by Booker T. Washington that accepted segregation in exchange for basic education and economic opportunity. Du Bois instead demanded full civil rights and political representation, believing they would be achieved by a "talented tenth" of educated African American leaders. He co-founded the Niagara Movement (1905) and the NAACP (1909). As editor of *The Crisis* (1910–1933), he used the magazine to campaign against lynching, segregation, and racial injustice, reaching a circulation of 100,000.

### Scholarship and Pan-Africanism
Du Bois was a prolific author whose work targeted racism. His seminal collection of essays, *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903), introduced the concept of "double consciousness"—the experience of being both American and Black. He popularized the term "color line" to describe racial injustice. His 1935 magnum opus, *Black Reconstruction in America*, argued that Black people were central to the Reconstruction era and that its failure was due to white supremacy, not Black incompetence. A lifelong Pan-Africanist, he organized several Pan-African Congresses to fight for the independence of African colonies.

### Later Life and Legacy
In his later years, Du Bois became increasingly focused on global peace and anti-colonialism. His socialist sympathies and peace activism led to government persecution; he was indicted in 1951 and had his passport confiscated for eight years. In 1961, he joined the Communist Party and moved to Ghana to work on the *Encyclopedia Africana*. He became a Ghanaian citizen and died there in 1963. His work profoundly influenced the civil rights movement and continues to shape scholarship on race, history, and sociology.

## Terms
- **Double Consciousness**: A concept Du Bois coined to describe the internal conflict experienced by subordinated groups in an oppressive society, specifically the sense of always looking at oneself through the eyes of others.
- **Color Line**: A phrase popularized by Du Bois to represent the global system of racial segregation and discrimination.
- **Talented Tenth**: A term Du Bois used for the one in ten Black men who, through higher education, would become leaders to uplift the race.
- **Atlanta Compromise**: An unwritten agreement (1895) where Southern Black people would accept segregation and disenfranchisement in exchange for basic education and economic opportunities.
- **Niagara Movement**: A civil rights organization founded in 1905 by Du Bois and others to demand full political, civil, and social rights for African Americans.
- **Pan-Africanism**: A worldwide movement that aims to encourage and strengthen bonds of solidarity between all indigenous and diaspora people of African descent.
- **The Crisis**: The official magazine of the NAACP, edited by Du Bois from 1910 to 1933, which became a leading platform for civil rights advocacy.
- **Racial Uplift**: The ideology that African Americans should better themselves through education, moral conduct, and economic progress to counter racist stereotypes.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Du Bois vs. Washington**: Scholars debate the depth of the personal animosity between Du Bois and Booker T. Washington. Some, like Thomas Sowell, argue the conflict was more pronounced among their followers than between the two men themselves.
- **Stance on Segregation**: Du Bois's 1934 reversal, where he stated "separate but equal" was an acceptable goal, caused a major rift with the NAACP and remains a point of scholarly discussion regarding his evolving ideology.
- **Relationship with Communism**: Du Bois's late-life embrace of the Communist Party is debated. Biographer David Levering Lewis suggests he endorsed it not for its own sake, but because "the enemies of his enemies were his friends," viewing it as a potential counter to capitalist-driven racism.
- **Views on Japan**: Du Bois's ambivalent views on Imperial Japan, which he initially saw as a counter to Western imperialism, are critically examined, especially his initial downplaying of its colonial brutality in Manchuria.
