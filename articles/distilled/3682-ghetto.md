# Ghetto

## Overview
A ghetto is a part of a city where a particular racial, religious, or minority group lives closely together and apart from others, often due to political, social, or economic pressure. The term originated with the Venetian Ghetto in 1516, where Jews were legally segregated. It later became a key concept for understanding racial segregation and poverty in the United States, particularly for African-American communities, and was infamously used by the Nazis for the confinement and extermination of Jews during the Holocaust.

## Timeline
- **1516** — The Venetian Ghetto is established, legally confining Jews to a specific quarter in Venice.
- **1933–1945** — Nazi Germany establishes over 1,000 ghettos in occupied Europe to confine Jewish populations as part of the Final Solution.
- **1914–1970** — The Great Migration sees over a million African Americans move from the rural South to Northern and Western U.S. cities.
- **1936** — The FHA Underwriting Manual includes racial provisions that encourage residential segregation.
- **1968** — The Fair Housing Act is passed, outlawing discrimination in housing sales and rentals.
- **2010–2021** — The Danish government officially uses the term "ghetto" to designate vulnerable social housing areas.
- **2021** — Denmark removes the term "ghetto" from legal texts, replacing it with "parallel societies."

## Body

### Etymology and Early Use
The word "ghetto" originates from the Venetian Ghetto, the Jewish quarter in Venice where Jews were confined following a 1516 decree. The etymology is debated; common hypotheses include derivation from an Italian word for "foundry" or a Germanic word for "street," though linguists reject other theories like a link to the Hebrew word for "letter of divorce." By 1855, the term had extended to mean any densely populated urban area occupied predominantly by a particular social or ethnic group under pressure.

### Ghettos in Europe
Historically, Jewish ghettos in Christian Europe were segregated quarters resulting from religious discrimination. Their character varied from relatively affluent to impoverished. During World War II, the Nazis created a new type of ghetto as a transitional stage for the Final Solution, intended for confinement before extermination. These were fundamentally different from historical ghettos, which were long-term communities. The term is also applied to Roma ghettos in the EU and was used officially in Denmark (2010–2021) to describe vulnerable social housing areas, a practice criticized as stigmatizing and imprecise.

### Ghettos in the United States
The development of U.S. ghettos is tied to immigration waves and internal migration. Early ethnic enclaves included Irish, German, Italian, and Jewish neighborhoods. The formation of Black or African-American ghettos accelerated during the Great Migration (1914–1970), when African Americans moved to Northern and Western cities but faced discrimination and were often restricted to poor housing. Post-World War II "white flight" to suburbs, aided by discriminatory practices like redlining (denying services based on race), further concentrated poverty in inner-city Black neighborhoods. The Fair Housing Act of 1968 led to a "black middle class exodus," leaving behind a more isolated and impoverished "underclass."

### Theories on Black Ghetto Development
Scholars debate the causes of Black ghettos. Race-based theories argue they are maintained by systemic racism and white hegemony. Class-based theories, the more dominant view, emphasize broader economic structures like deindustrialization and the intergenerational transmission of poverty. An alternative theory by Thomas Sowell attributes modern ghetto culture to historical "redneck" culture from the American South. Contemporary ghettos are characterized by racial concentration, poverty, crime, and dependence on state services, which some argue undermines community autonomy.

## Terms
- **Ghetto**: A segregated urban area where a particular minority group lives, often under social or economic pressure.
- **Redlining**: The discriminatory practice of denying services (like mortgages) to residents of specific, often racially determined, areas.
- **White Flight**: The large-scale migration of white people from racially mixed urban regions to more racially homogeneous suburban regions.
- **Great Migration**: The movement of over a million African Americans from the rural South to cities in the North and West between 1914 and 1970.
- **Final Solution**: Nazi Germany's plan for the genocide of Jews during World War II, for which ghettos served as a transitional tool.
- **Banlieue**: In France, a suburb of a large city; the term often now refers to low-income housing projects with immigrant populations.
- **Parallel Societies**: The term adopted by Denmark in 2021 to replace "ghetto" for describing vulnerable social housing areas.

## Debates and Open Questions
The etymology of the word "ghetto" remains debated among linguists, with no single theory universally accepted. Scholars also debate the primary cause of Black ghettos in the U.S., with race-based and class-based theories offering competing explanations. The use of the term "ghetto" itself is controversial; its official use in Denmark was criticized as stigmatizing and inaccurate for multi-ethnic areas. Furthermore, the concept of the "ghetto" and "underclass" has been critiqued for over-reliance on case studies from cities like Chicago, which may limit understanding of socially disadvantaged neighborhoods elsewhere.
