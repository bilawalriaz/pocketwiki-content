# Ancestral Puebloans

## Overview
The Ancestral Puebloans were an ancient Native American culture of the Four Corners region (Utah, Arizona, New Mexico, Colorado), known for their complex societies, distinctive architecture, and eventual migration from their homeland. They are the forerunners of contemporary Pueblo peoples, and their cultural legacy is preserved in several UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

## Timeline
- **c. 12th century BCE** — Emergence of the culture during the Early Basketmaker II Era (Pecos Classification).
- **700–1130 CE** — Rapid population growth and cultural spread during the Pueblo I and II Eras.
- **c. 900–1150 CE** — Cultural "Golden Age" with large communities, long-distance trade, and architectural innovation.
- **1000–1125 CE** — Construction of major Chacoan great houses and the extensive road system.
- **c. 1130 CE** — Onset of the "Great Drought," a 300-year period of aridity.
- **12th–13th centuries** — Migration from established homes in the Colorado Plateau homeland.
- **13th century** — Trend of building dense, defensible cliff dwellings (e.g., Mesa Verde).
- **1350 CE** — End of the Pueblo III Era; major abandonment of the Four Corners region.

## Body

### Origins and Geography
The Ancestral Puebloans developed from the Oshara tradition and occupied the Colorado Plateau, centered in the Four Corners region. Their homeland featured high-elevation mesas, steep-walled canyons, and sandstone overhangs, which they utilized for shelter and defense. They were one of four major prehistoric traditions in the American Southwest (Oasisamerica), alongside the Mogollon, Hohokam, and Patayan.

### Cultural Characteristics and Architecture
The culture is renowned for its stone-and-earth dwellings, evolving from early pit-houses to grand pueblos and cliff-side communities. They built multistory apartment complexes and structures of stone and adobe, often in defensive positions. Chaco Canyon was a major population center, where developers quarried sandstone and hauled timber from up to 70 miles away to build 15 major "great houses"—the largest buildings in North America until the late 19th century. These complexes featured planned plazas, ceremonial kivas (congregational spaces), and evidence of archaeoastronomy (e.g., the Sun Dagger petroglyph). The Chaco Road system, over 180 miles of wide, engineered roads, connected these centers, possibly for trade or ceremonial purposes.

### Social Organization and Daily Life
Society was complex, with evidence of matrilineal succession (tracing lineage through the female line) found at Pueblo Bonito. They excelled in pottery, with distinct regional styles (e.g., black-on-white designs in the north), and rock art, including petroglyphs and pictographs. Trade networks brought exotic goods like macaws, turquoise, and seashells into Chaco Canyon.

### Migration and Decline
Beginning in the 12th century, the Ancestral Puebloans left their established homes. The primary cause is debated but likely involved a combination of factors: the Great Drought (beginning c. 1130), environmental degradation, and pressure from migrating Numic-speaking peoples (e.g., Utes, Shoshones) and Athabaskan-speaking Diné (ancestors of Navajo and Apache). The archaeological record shows deliberate dismantling of ceremonial structures and abandonment of settlements. Modern Pueblo oral traditions state the people migrated to areas with better rainfall and merged into contemporary Pueblo communities, rather than "vanishing."

### Warfare and Conflict
Environmental stress may have led to social changes and conflict. Some communities relocated to defensible mesa tops, and archaeological evidence (e.g., at Cowboy Wash and Sacred Ridge) shows signs of violence, dismemberment, and possible cannibalism, though this interpretation is debated. The influx of new peoples may have exacerbated tensions.

## Terms
- **Pueblo**: Spanish for "village," referring to both the people and their communal dwelling style.
- **Kiva**: A circular, subterranean room used for ceremonies and congregational gatherings.
- **Great House**: A massive, multi-story complex in Chaco Canyon, serving as elite residential and ceremonial centers.
- **Pecos Classification**: A chronological system for Southwestern archaeology, dividing the Ancestral Puebloan period into eras (e.g., Basketmaker, Pueblo I-IV).
- **Archaeoastronomy**: The study of how ancient peoples understood and incorporated celestial phenomena into their architecture and culture.
- **Matrilineal Succession**: A social system where descent and inheritance are traced through the female line.
- **Numic-speaking peoples**: A group of Native American languages including Ute, Shoshone, and Paiute, whose speakers migrated into the region.
- **Oasisamerica**: A cultural region of the North American Southwest, analogous to Mesoamerica to the south.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Terminology**: The use of the term "Anasazi" (a Navajo exonym meaning "enemy ancestors") is controversial and rejected by many contemporary Pueblo peoples, who prefer "Ancestral Puebloans."
- **Cause of Migration**: Scholarly consensus points to a combination of climate change (the Great Drought) and pressure from migrating peoples, but the precise interplay of factors is debated.
- **Interpretation of Violence**: Evidence of warfare and cannibalism is hotly debated, with alternative explanations including extreme social stress, ritual practice, or invasion by nomadic raiders.
- **Cultural Continuity**: While archaeologists define distinct cultural units, these are modern constructs. The ancient people likely had more diverse social and political identities than the material record suggests, and clear-cut territorial boundaries did not exist.
