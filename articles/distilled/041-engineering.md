# Engineering

## Overview

Engineering is the systematic application of natural science and mathematics to design and improve systems, devices, or processes that solve problems under constraints. It is motivated by satisfying human needs—producing bridges, engines, smartphones, pacemakers, the internet, spacecraft, and washing machines—while balancing competing demands such as safety, performance, aesthetics, cost, laws, and time, all within fundamental limits like the laws of physics. The traditional disciplines are civil, mechanical, electrical, and chemical, but modern practice spans many specialized subfields and follows the engineering design process. As a human endeavor, engineering has existed since ancient times, evolving from simple machines and monumental structures into a scientific profession during the Industrial Revolution, and now underpins nearly all modern infrastructure, technology, and energy systems.

## Body

### Definition and origins

The American Engineers' Council for Professional Development (predecessor of ABET) defines engineering as "the creative application of scientific principles" to design or develop structures, machines, apparatus, or manufacturing processes, or to construct or operate them with full cognizance of their design, forecasting their behavior under specific operating conditions—all with respect to intended function, economics of operation, and safety to life and property. The term derives from the Latin *ingenium* (c. 1250), meaning "innate quality, especially mental power, hence a clever invention." The word "engineer" dates to the 14th century, when an *engine'er* built or operated siege engines (military machines like catapults); this obsolete usage survives in terms like military engineering corps. Later, as civilian bridge and building design matured, "civil engineering" distinguished non-military from military engineering.

### Ancient era

Ancient engineering achievements include the Egyptian pyramids, Mesopotamian ziggurats, the Acropolis and Parthenon, Roman aqueducts, Via Appia, and Colosseum, Teotihuacán, and the Brihadeeswarar Temple. The Hanging Gardens of Babylon and Pharos of Alexandria, now lost, were among the Seven Wonders. The six classic simple machines were known in the ancient Near East: the wedge and inclined plane since prehistoric times; the wheel in Mesopotamia (5th millennium BC); the lever around 5,000 years ago in the Near East, used in balance scales and the shadoof; pulleys in Mesopotamia and Egypt (early 2nd millennium BC); and the screw in Mesopotamia during the Neo-Assyrian period (911–609 BC). The Egyptian pyramids used three of these—inclined plane, wedge, and lever. Imhotep, an official of Pharaoh Djosèr, likely designed the Step Pyramid (c. 2630–2611 BC). Water-powered machines (water wheel, watermill) appeared in the Persian Empire by the early 4th century BC; Kush developed the animal-powered Sakia and hafirs (reservoirs) in the 4th century BC. Ancient Greece produced the Antikythera mechanism (an early analog computer) and Archimedes' inventions, requiring sophisticated gearing knowledge later used in the Industrial Revolution. Chinese, Greek, Roman, and Hunnic armies employed artillery, triremes, ballistas, catapults, and trebuchets.

### Middle Ages and Industrial Revolution

The earliest practical wind-powered machines appeared in the Muslim world by the 9th century AD; the earliest steam-powered machine, a steam jack, was described in 1551 by Taqi al-Din in Ottoman Egypt. The cotton gin (India, 6th century) and spinning wheel (Islamic world, early 11th century) preceded the spinning jenny of the Industrial Revolution. The earliest programmable machines—a music sequencer by the Banu Musa brothers (9th century) and Al-Jazari's programmable automata (1206)—also emerged in the Muslim world. Before modern engineering, mathematics was used by artisans like millwrights and clockmakers; universities had little practical technological significance. The Renaissance mining treatise *De re metallica* (1556) was the standard chemistry reference for 180 years.

Classical (Newtonian) mechanics formed the scientific basis of modern engineering. John Smeaton, the first self-proclaimed civil engineer, conducted seven years of water wheel experiments, introduced iron axles and gears, improved the Newcomen steam engine, and pioneered hydraulic lime (mortar that sets under water) and dovetailed granite blocks in the Eddystone Lighthouse—work leading to Portland cement. The steam engine developed through Torricelli's barometer (1643), von Guericke's Magdeburg hemispheres (1656), Papin's piston experiments (published 1707), and Savery's 1698 steam pump, culminating in Newcomen's 1712 commercial piston engine. Steam-powered blast cylinders increased iron production, enabling the shift from charcoal to coke; Henry Cort's puddling process (1784) and Neilson's hot blast (1828) further lowered costs. High-pressure steam engines made steamboats and locomotives practical, while Bessemer and open-hearth processes enabled heavy engineering. Notable engineers included Isambard Kingdom Brunel (railroads, steamships), Nikola Tesla, Alexander Graham Bell, George Stephenson, and Nicolaus Otto. The demand for metal machinery led to machine tools—John Wilkinson's boring machine being the first—and precision techniques enabling interchangeable parts and large-scale factory production by the late 19th century.

### Development of new fields

The 1850 U.S. Census first listed "engineer" with 2,000 practitioners; fewer than 50 engineering graduates existed before 1865. Gibbs earned the first U.S. engineering PhD in 1863. Electrical engineering grew from experiments by Volta, Faraday, and Ohm, plus the telegraph (1816) and electric motor (1872); Maxwell's and Hertz's theoretical work gave rise to electronics, accelerated by the vacuum tube and transistor. Chemical engineering emerged in the late 19th century to design industrial chemical plants. Materials science, deriving from ceramics and metallurgy, was advanced by the Space Race's need for alloys, silica, and carbon materials. Aeronautical engineering traces to aviation pioneers around 1900 (with Sir George Cayley's work dated to the late 18th century); military aircraft development in World War I accelerated the field.

### Practice, methodology, and social context

Engineers apply mathematics and physical sciences in the engineering design process, weighing multiple solutions against constraints (physics, resources, cost, safety, marketability, productivity, serviceability) to derive specifications. Genrich Altshuller, analyzing patents, suggested compromises mark "low-level" designs while the best designs eliminate core contradictions. Engineers predict performance via prototypes, scale models, simulations, and destructive/nondestructive/stress tests. They include a factor of safety, embodying Cicero's Creed—*salus populi suprema lex esto* ("the health of the people shall be the supreme law"). Forensic engineering studies failed products to prevent recurrence; failures range from machine breakdowns to large-scale disasters from miscalculation, miscommunication, fatigue, or faulty software. Computers are central: CAD software creates 3D models and schematics; CAE tools (finite element method, analytic element method) analyze designs without physical prototypes; CAM generates CNC instructions; and product lifecycle management (PLM) software coordinates the entire process.

Engineering projects are typically funded by companies, investors, or governments, though pro bono and open-design engineering exist. Engineering impacts environment, society, economies, and public safety, and can be controversial (nuclear weapons, the Three Gorges Dam, SUVs, oil extraction). Many engineering societies have codes of ethics; the National Society of Professional Engineers emphasizes honesty, integrity, and dedication to public health, safety, and welfare. In Canada, engineers wear the Iron Ring as a reminder of these obligations. The profession faces an "image crisis," notably in the UK, where fewer engineers are trained than retiring.

### Relationships with other disciplines

Science studies the world as it is; engineering creates what has never been. Both rely on observation, mathematics, and classification, but engineering research often deals with problems too complex for exact solutions, employing semi-empirical methods like parameter variation. Engineers must also consider safety, efficiency, economy, reliability, constructability, environment, ethics, and legal liability. Medicine and engineering share the study of the human body as a biological machine—the heart as a pump, the skeleton as levers, the brain producing electrical signals—leading to biomedical engineering, bionics, artificial intelligence, and robotics. Engineering connects to art through architecture, industrial design, and figures like Leonardo da Vinci. Business engineering and engineering management address the relationship between engineering, IT, and administration, with specialized degrees and management consulting for large-scale transformations. The term "engineering" has been borrowed in political science (social and political engineering) and finance (financial engineering).

## Terms

- **Simple machine**: One of six basic mechanical devices (wedge, inclined plane, wheel, lever, pulley, screw) known since antiquity.
- **Hydraulic lime**: A form of mortar that sets under water, pioneered by Smeaton.
- **Puddling process**: Henry Cort's 1784 method for producing large-scale wrought iron.
- **Forensic engineering**: The study of failed products to identify causes and prevent recurrence.
- **Computer-aided design (CAD)**: Software for creating 3D models, 2D drawings, and schematics.
- **Finite element method**: A numerical analysis technique for calculating stresses and other properties in complex components.
- **Factor of safety**: A design margin included to reduce the risk of unexpected failure.
- **Product lifecycle management (PLM)**: Software-assisted management of a product from design through testing and manufacturing.
- **Bionics**: The study of synthetic implants pertaining to natural systems.
- **Engineering design process**: The methodology of applying mathematics and science to find novel or improved solutions under constraints.

## Debates and open questions

- **Engineering vs. science**: Walter Vincenti argues engineering research differs from scientific research—it deals with well-understood physics but problems too complex for exact solutions, and employs semi-empirical methods foreign to pure science. The source quotes Fung et al.: "Scientists try to understand nature. Engineers try to make things that do not exist in nature."
- **Design philosophy**: Altshuller's patent analysis suggests low-level designs rely on compromises, while higher-level designs eliminate core contradictions—a debated view of optimal engineering.
- **Workforce crisis**: The engineering profession, especially in the UK, faces an "image crisis" with fewer engineers trained than retiring, raising economic, political, and ethical concerns.
