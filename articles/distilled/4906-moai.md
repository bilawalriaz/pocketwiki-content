# Moai

## Overview
Moai are monolithic human figures carved by the Rapa Nui people of Easter Island between 1250 and 1500 CE. They represent deified ancestors and embody the sacred power (*mana*) of chiefs, serving as symbols of political and religious authority. The production and transport of over 900 statues is a remarkable feat of engineering and social organization, central to understanding Rapa Nui culture.

## Timeline
- **c. 800–1200 CE** — Forests on Easter Island are gradually logged during settlement.
- **1250–1500 CE** — Main period of moai carving and erection on stone platforms (*ahu*).
- **1722** — European contact begins; moai are still standing.
- **Late 18th–early 19th centuries** — All erected moai are toppled, possibly due to European contact or tribal wars.
- **1862** — Devastating slave raids decimate the Rapa Nui population.
- **1955–1978** — Archaeologist William Mulloy leads extensive investigation and restoration projects.
- **1979** — Archaeologists discover moai eye sockets were designed to hold coral eyes.
- **1995** — Rapa Nui National Park and moai become a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **2019** — Study suggests moai quarrying may have been linked to improving soil fertility.
- **2022** — Wildfire damages moai at Rano Raraku quarry.

## Body

### Description and Symbolism
Moai are monolithic statues carved primarily from volcanic tuff. They feature oversized heads (accounting for three-eighths of their size), elongated noses, and no visible legs. Their arms rest at their sides, with hands on hips. The statues represent the living faces (*aringa ora*) of deified ancestors. They were erected on *ahu* (stone platforms) around the island's perimeter, almost always facing inland to watch over communities. The larger the statue, the greater the *mana* (sacred power) of the chief who commissioned it. Some later moai wore *pukao*—red scoria topknots symbolizing a chief's hair, where *mana* was preserved.

### Craftsmanship and Quarrying
Most moai were carved from tuff at the Rano Raraku quarry, where 394 statues in various states of completion remain. The process involved outlining the figure in the rock wall and chipping away the excess. The quarry was likely subdivided into clan territories. Carvers were either a specialized professional class or members of each clan. The average moai is about 4 meters tall and weighs 12.5 tonnes. The tallest erected moai, Paro, was nearly 10 meters high and weighed 82 tonnes.

### Transportation Theories
The method of moving moai across the island is debated. Early theories proposed sledges or rollers, but these required substantial timber, which was scarce. The leading modern theory, supported by archaeologists Terry Hunt and Carl Lipo, suggests statues were "walked" upright using ropes to rock them side-to-side. Experiments have shown this method is feasible. The statues' wide, curved bases and the pattern of breakage along prehistoric roads support this hypothesis. Different methods may have been used depending on distance and resources.

### Toppling and Decline
All erected moai were toppled between the late 18th and mid-19th centuries. Causes may include European contact, internecine tribal wars, or earthquakes. The Rapa Nui population was devastated by 1862 slave raids and subsequent assimilation under missionary influence. The moai-building era had already ended, possibly with the rise of the *tangata manu* (birdman) cult, which shifted focus from ancestor worship to a competitive ritual.

### Preservation and Modern Era
Archaeologist William Mulloy led major restoration efforts in the 20th century. The moai are protected as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Ten or more have been removed to museums worldwide, including the British Museum's *Hoa Hakananai'a*. Threats include vandalism, accidents, and wildfires, such as the 2022 fire at Rano Raraku.

## Terms
- **Moai**: Monolithic human figures carved by the Rapa Nui, representing deified ancestors.
- **Ahu**: Stone platforms upon which moai were erected.
- **Mana**: A magical spiritual essence or power believed to reside in properly crafted objects and people.
- **Tuff**: A soft rock formed from solidified volcanic ash, the primary material for moai.
- **Pukao**: Red scoria cylinders placed on some moai heads, representing a chief's topknot.
- **Tangata manu**: The "birdman" cult that replaced the moai-building era, involving a competitive ritual.
- **Rapa Nui**: The indigenous Polynesian people of Easter Island and their language.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Transport Method**: The exact technique for moving moai remains debated, with "walking" via ropes being the leading theory, but other methods like sledges on rollers are also considered.
- **Cause of Toppling**: Whether moai were primarily toppled due to internal social conflict, European influence, or natural events is not definitively known.
- **End of Construction**: The precise reasons for the abrupt abandonment of the Rano Raraku quarry and the cessation of moai production are still explored, with the rise of the birdman cult being a key factor.
