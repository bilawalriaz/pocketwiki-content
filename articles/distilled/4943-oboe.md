# Oboe

## Overview
The oboe is a soprano woodwind instrument with a distinctive, penetrating tone produced by a double reed. It is a core member of the modern symphony orchestra, where it is famously used to sound the tuning note (concert A). Its versatile sound is featured in classical, film, and some folk and jazz music.

## Timeline
- **c. 13th century** — The Arabic zurna reappears in Europe, influencing the development of the oboe.
- **Mid-17th century** — The regular oboe (hautbois) first appears in France, derived from the shawm.
- **c. 1770** — The spelling "oboe" is adopted into English from the Italian *oboè*.
- **18th century** — The Baroque oboe is standardized with three keys and a boxwood body.
- **Classical period** — The oboe's bore is narrowed, more keys are added, and its upper register is expanded.
- **Late 19th century** — The Wiener (Viennese) oboe model is developed by Josef Hajek.
- **19th century** — The Triébert family in Paris develops the complex key system leading to the modern conservatoire oboe.
- **20th century** — Minor improvements to the modern oboe's bore and keywork continue; historical copies are made.

## Body

### Sound and Construction
The oboe's sound is produced by blowing air through a double reed, causing it to vibrate. Its conical bore (unlike the cylindrical bore of flutes and clarinets) gives it a rich, penetrating timbre that allows it to be heard clearly in large ensembles. The standard soprano oboe is pitched in C, is not a transposing instrument, and has a range from B♭3 to G6. Orchestras tune to a concert A played by the first oboe because its pitch is secure and its sound is ideal for cutting through the ensemble. The reed is critical to the instrument's sound and intonation; professional oboists typically make their own reeds to control tone color and responsiveness. The reed's performance is sensitive to variations in cane, construction, and environmental factors like temperature and humidity.

### History and Development
The oboe's ancestors include ancient reed instruments and the medieval shawm. The modern oboe emerged in mid-17th century France as the *hautbois*, likely developed by makers like the Philidor and Hotteterre families. It spread quickly across Europe. The Baroque oboe had three keys and a boxwood body. During the Classical period, the bore was narrowed and more keys were added, expanding its upper register. The 19th century saw the development of two main modern systems: the Viennese oboe, which retains a wider bore and historical characteristics, and the French conservatoire system, which uses a complex key mechanism based on ideas from the Boehm flute. The modern standard oboe is typically made of grenadilla (African blackwood) and has a full conservatoire key system with about 45 pieces of keywork.

### Family and Use
The oboe family includes several related instruments. The most common is the cor anglais (English horn), an alto member pitched in F. Other members include the oboe d'amore (in A), the bass oboe (one octave lower), and the rare heckelphone. Folk versions exist across Europe. While primarily a classical instrument, the oboe has been used in jazz (notably by Bob Cooper and Yusef Lateef), rock and pop (e.g., by Peter Gabriel and Andy Mackay of Roxy Music), and is a staple of film music for poignant scenes, such as in Ennio Morricone's "Gabriel's Oboe."

## Terms
- **Double reed**: Two thin blades of cane tied together that vibrate to produce sound, used by the oboe and bassoon.
- **Conical bore**: A tube that tapers from a wider to a narrower end, characteristic of the oboe, contributing to its rich tone.
- **Transposing instrument**: An instrument whose written notes sound at a different pitch than concert pitch; the oboe is *not* transposing.
- **Tessitura**: The comfortable, most frequently used range of an instrument or voice.
- **Conservatoire system**: The standard modern key system for the oboe, developed in 19th-century France.
- **Grenadilla**: African blackwood, the most common material for professional oboes.
- **Embouchure**: The way a player applies their mouth and lips to the reed or mouthpiece.
- **Overblowing**: Increasing air pressure to make the instrument sound a higher harmonic note.

## Debates and open questions
The exact date, location, and inventors of the hautbois (the oboe's direct predecessor) are obscure and debated. The relative merits and tonal differences between the French conservatoire system and the Viennese oboe system remain a point of discussion among players and scholars.
