# Ballroom dance

## Overview
Ballroom dance is a set of European partner dances enjoyed socially and competitively worldwide, primarily for performance and entertainment. Its modern form emerged in the early 20th century from a shift toward independent couple dancing, popular music, and the codification of steps for widespread teaching. The practice is now governed by two principal schools—the International and American—each with distinct competitive categories, and is recognized by the International Olympic Committee.

## Timeline
- **1588** — Jehan Tabourot publishes *Orchésographie*, recording early ballroom dances like the basse danse and pavane.
- **1650** — The Minuet is introduced to Paris and dominates ballrooms until the late 18th century.
- **c. 1812** — The modern waltz takes root in England, initially facing opposition for its closed hold.
- **1840s** — New dances like the polka and mazurka appear in ballrooms.
- **Early 20th C.** — Modern ballroom dance develops from a move to independent couple dancing and new popular music.
- **1910–1930** — Numerous dance crazes emerge, leading to efforts to codify dances for public teaching.
- **1930s** — Fred Astaire and Ginger Rogers' films popularize social and choreographed dance.
- **1950s** — The Cha Cha is developed by Enrique Jorrín as a slower alternative to Mambo.
- **Present** — Competitive ballroom (Dancesport) is recognized by the International Olympic Committee.

## Body

### Definitions and History
The term derives from the Latin *ballare* ("to dance"). Historically, ballroom dancing was social dancing for the privileged classes. Early authoritative records date to the late 16th century. The Minuet dominated from 1650 until the late 18th century. The 19th century saw the waltz's rise and the introduction of the polka. Modern ballroom dance crystallized in the early 20th century due to a move away from sequence dances, the influence of popular music like jazz, and a concerted effort by professionals (e.g., the Castles, Josephine Bradley) to analyze and standardize dances for wider teaching. This was facilitated by organizations like the Imperial Society of Teachers of Dancing and the Arthur Murray studios.

### Two Principal Schools
Modern competitive ballroom is divided into two main schools. The **International School**, regulated by the World Dance Council (WDC) and World DanceSport Federation (WDSF), is prevalent globally except the U.S. It has two categories: **Standard** (Waltz, Tango, Viennese Waltz, Slow Foxtrot, Quickstep) and **Latin** (Samba, Cha Cha, Rumba, Paso Doble, Jive). The **American School**, common in the U.S. and regulated by USA Dance, has analogous categories: **Smooth** (American Waltz, Tango, Foxtrot, Viennese Waltz) and **Rhythm** (Cha Cha, Rumba, East Coast Swing, Bolero, Mambo). Dances with the same name (e.g., Foxtrot) can differ significantly in technique and figures between schools.

### Competitive Dancing (Dancesport)
Competitions range from world championships to local events, divided into professional and amateur divisions. Judging criteria include poise, hold, musicality, timing, and footwork. Competitors progress through syllabus levels (Newcomer, Bronze, Silver, Gold) with restricted moves, and open levels (Novice, Pre-Champ, Champ) with no restrictions. The Blackpool Dance Festival is the most prestigious international event. The International Olympic Committee recognizes the WDSF as the sole representative for dancesport in the Games.

### Dance Styles and Characteristics
**Standard/Smooth** dances are typically danced counter-clockwise around the floor. In competition, Standard attire is formal (gowns, tailcoats), while Smooth allows more creative outfits. **Latin/Rhythm** dances are often performed in place to contemporary Latin music, with costumes emphasizing leg and body action. Key dances include the Waltz (originating in 17th-century Austria), Tango (from late 19th-century Buenos Aires), Foxtrot (an American dance from 1914), and Samba (the national dance of Brazil). The categorization of dances as "ballroom" is fluid, sometimes including nightclub dances like Lindy Hop or salsa.

## Terms
- **Dancesport**: The competitive form of ballroom dance.
- **World Dance Council (WDC)**: A major international regulatory body for competitive ballroom.
- **World DanceSport Federation (WDSF)**: The IOC-recognized governing body for dancesport.
- **International Style**: The school of ballroom dance prevalent outside the U.S., with Standard and Latin categories.
- **American Style**: The school of ballroom dance common in the U.S., with Smooth and Rhythm categories.
- **Syllabus Levels**: Competition divisions (Newcomer to Gold) where dancers are restricted to a set list of figures.
- **Open Levels**: Advanced competition divisions (Novice to Champ) with no restrictions on dance figures.
- **Line of Dance**: The counter-clockwise direction couples travel around the floor in Standard/Smooth dances.
- **Skating System**: The method used in finals to determine placements based on ordinals from judges.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that judging in performance-oriented sports like dancesport is "inevitably subjective," leading to controversy and complaints over placements. The definition of what constitutes a "ballroom dance" is also fluid and debated, with new dances being added or removed from the competitive repertoire over time. The categorization of dances varies by region (e.g., Soviet Programme, Australian New Vogue), reflecting local adaptations rather than universal standards.
