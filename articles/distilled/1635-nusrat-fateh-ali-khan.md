# Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan

## Overview
Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan (1948–1997) was a Pakistani vocalist and composer universally regarded as the greatest exponent of **qawwali**, a form of Sufi devotional music originating in South Asia. Born into a 600-year family lineage of qawwals, he transformed the genre from an "arcane religious tradition" into a global phenomenon through his extraordinary vocal range, improvisational mastery of **sargam** (solfege), and ability to sustain high-intensity performances for up to ten hours. His collaborations with Western artists like Peter Gabriel and Michael Brook, alongside his work on film soundtracks from Bollywood to Hollywood, established him as a primary architect of the "world music" category. He remains a profound influence on South Asian pop, Bollywood, and international artists ranging from Jeff Buckley to A. R. Rahman.

## Body

### Early Life and Hereditary Tradition
Khan was born in Lyallpur (now Faisalabad) into the **Delhi Gharana** (also known as Qawwal Bachchon ka Gharana), a musical lineage founded by Amir Khusrau in the 13th century. His father, Fateh Ali Khan, was a musicologist and qawwal who initially opposed his son following the family vocation due to its low social status, preferring he become a doctor or engineer. Khan’s aptitude forced his father’s acquiescence. He joined the family party as a **tabla** (paired hand drums) player in his youth. A pivotal moment came when classical vocalist Ustad Munawar Ali Khan praised Nusrat’s tabla accompaniment during a 1960s visit, cementing his father’s support. Following his father’s 1964 death, Nusrat assumed leadership of the party in 1965, sharing duties with his uncle Mubarak Ali Khan until the latter’s death in 1971.

### Musical Style and Innovations
Khan sang primarily in Urdu and Punjabi, with occasional Persian, Braj Bhasha, and Hindi. He was a master of **Hindustani classical music**, integrating its rigorous structures—specifically **khayal** (a classical vocal form emphasizing improvisation) and **sargam** (solfege improvisation using note names)—into the qawwali framework. This synthesis allowed for unprecedented melodic complexity and rhythmic virtuosity. His 1971 composition "Ni Main Jana Jogi De Naal" (lyrics by 17th-century Sufi poet Bulleh Shah) became an early signature hit. He also composed **manqabats** (songs praising Sufi saints), such as "Aastan Hai Yeh Kis Shah-e-Zeshan Ka" for Abdul Qadir Jilani. His live performances were legendary for their duration and intensity, often exceeding ten hours, driven by a state of spiritual ecstasy (*wajd*).

### International Breakthrough and the "World Music" Era (1980s)
Khan’s global ascent began with his 1985 performance at the **WOMAD (World of Music, Arts and Dance)** festival in London. Subsequent concerts in Paris (recorded by Radio France for the Ocora label) and Japan (1987 Asian Traditional Performing Art Festival) solidified his reputation. In 1988, Peter Gabriel recruited him for Martin Scorsese’s *The Last Temptation of Christ* soundtrack, initiating a long partnership. Gabriel’s **Real World Records** signed Khan in 1989, providing a platform for both traditional releases and experimental "fusion" projects. That same year, Khan recorded fusion tracks in Birmingham with composer Andrew Kristy and producer Johnny Haynes, leading to a UK tour and a televised *Big World Café* performance with violinist Nigel Kennedy. Notably, during his 1989 UK stay, he performed Sikh devotional music at a Gurdwara in Slough, upholding a centuries-old tradition of Muslim qawwals singing at Sikh temples.

### Experimental Collaborations and Film Work (1990s)
The 1990s saw Khan balance traditional qawwali albums (five on Real World) with genre-defying experiments. *Mustt Mustt* (1990), produced by Michael Brook, pioneered a downtempo, atmospheric fusion that reached electronic and rock audiences. *Night Song* (1996) continued this vein. He collaborated with Pearl Jam’s Eddie Vedder on two tracks for *Dead Man Walking* (1995) and recorded unfinished vocals for Jonathan Elias’s *The Prayer Cycle* (completed posthumously with Alanis Morissette). In Bollywood, he composed for *Aur Pyaar Ho Gaya*, *Kartoos*, and *Kachche Dhaage* shortly before his death; his songs were sung by legends Asha Bhosle and Lata Mangeshkar. His duet "Gurus of Peace" with A. R. Rahman (on Rahman’s *Vande Mataram*, 1997) was among his final recordings. Rahman later dedicated "Tere Bina" (*Guru*, 2007) to Khan.

### Plagiarism, Tolerance, and Bollywood Impact
Khan’s melodies were extensively plagiarized by Bollywood music directors, most famously Viju Shah’s "Tu Cheez Badi Hai Mast Mast" (*Mohra*, 1994), lifted from Khan’s "Dam Mast Qalandar." Khan responded with reported tolerance, jokingly awarding "Best Copy" prizes to offenders like Anu Malik. However, he expressed hurt when Malik adapted the devotional chant "Allah Hoo" into the romantic "I Love You" (*Auzaar*), stating religious songs deserved respect. Despite this, Khan’s influence on Bollywood was foundational, inspiring composers like A. R. Rahman and lyricist Javed Akhtar (with whom he collaborated on the album *Sangam*, 1996).

### Teaching, Gender, and Legacy
Breaking with the conservative norms of the Delhi Gharana—which traditionally discouraged female professional singers—Khan openly taught women, including noted Pakistani singer Humaira Arshad. His daughter, Nida, confirmed he taught her directly and supported her professional aspirations. His musical legacy is carried forward primarily by his nephews, **Rahat Fateh Ali Khan** (a global star in his own right) and **Rizwan-Muazzam**. Posthumous tributes include Gaudi’s *Dub Qawwali* (2007), the Brooklyn Qawwali Party jazz ensemble, Google Doodles (2015), and citations by artists from Jeff Buckley ("He's my Elvis") to Zayn Malik.

### Awards and Recognition
Khan received Pakistan’s **Pride of Performance** (1987), the **UNESCO Music Prize** (1995), **Grand Prix des Amériques** (Montreal, 1996), and the **Fukuoka Asian Culture Prize** (1996). In Japan, he was dubbed "Budai" (Singing Buddha). He was nominated for two Grammys in 1997 (*Intoxicated Spirit*, *Night Song*). He held the Guinness World Record for "Most Qawwali Recordings" (over 125 albums). *Rolling Stone* ranked him #91 on its 2023 "200 Greatest Singers" list; *Time* named him one of 12 Asian Heroes (2006); NPR listed him among 50 Great Voices (2010).

## Terms
- ****Qawwali**** — A form of Sufi Islamic devotional music originating in South Asia, characterized by poetic lyrics, rhythmic handclapping, and call-and-response vocals aimed at inducing spiritual ecstasy (*wajd*).
- ****Gharana**** — A system of hereditary musical lineage in Hindustani classical music, defined by a distinct style, repertoire, and pedagogical tradition passed down through generations (e.g., Delhi Gharana).
- ****Sargam**** — The Indian equivalent of solfege (Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni); in performance, rapid improvisation using these note names as lyrics to demonstrate virtuosity.
- ****Khayal**** — A major Hindustani classical vocal form emphasizing improvisation and exploration of a raga (melodic framework) within a rhythmic cycle (tala).
- ****Tabla**** — A pair of hand drums (dayan and bayan) serving as the primary percussion instrument in Hindustani music and qawwali.
- ****Manqabat**** — A qawwali sub-genre praising Ali ibn Abi Talib or Sufi saints (e.g., Abdul Qadir Jilani), distinct from *hamd* (praise of God) and *naat* (praise of the Prophet).
- ****WOMAD**** — World of Music, Arts and Dance; an international arts festival founded by Peter Gabriel (1982) that became a primary platform for "world music" artists.
- ****Real World Records**** — A record label founded by Peter Gabriel (1987) specializing in world and roots music; Khan’s primary Western label from 1989.
- ****Fusion (in Khan's context)**** — The blending of traditional qawwali vocals and melodies with Western electronic production, rock instrumentation, or ambient textures (e.g., *Mustt Mustt*, *Night Song*).
- ****Bollywood**** — The Hindi-language film industry based in Mumbai; a major consumer of Khan’s compositions (both licensed and plagiarized) and a vector for his popular influence.

## Debates and open questions
*   **Artistic Integrity vs. Accessibility:** Scholars and fans debate whether Khan’s experimental fusion albums (*Mustt Mustt*, *Night Song*) diluted the spiritual rigor of traditional qawwali or successfully translated its essence for global audiences without compromise.
*   **Plagiarism Ethics:** The rampant unauthorized adaptation of Khan’s compositions by Bollywood directors (e.g., Viju Shah, Anu Malik) raises questions about intellectual property in the Indian film industry and the power dynamics between a Sufi maestro and commercial composers.
*   **Genre Classification:** Khan’s work straddles "traditional folk," "world music," "classical," and "film music." His Grammy nominations in both *Best Traditional Folk Album* and *Best World Music Album* categories (1997) reflect the industry’s difficulty in categorizing a genre he effectively redefined.
*   **Succession and Authenticity:** While nephews Rahat Fateh Ali Khan and Rizwan-Muazzam are recognized heirs, the extent to which any successor can replicate Khan’s unique synthesis of classical mastery, spiritual authority, and improvisational daring remains an open critical question.
