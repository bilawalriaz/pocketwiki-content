# Campbell's Soup Cans

## Overview
Andy Warhol's *Campbell's Soup Cans* (1961–62) is a series of 32 paintings that became a defining symbol of Pop art. By elevating a ubiquitous commercial product to the status of high art, the series challenged traditional distinctions between fine art and mass culture, sparking debates about originality, reproduction, and the role of art in a consumer society.

## Timeline
- **1961** — Warhol begins producing canvases based on commercial imagery like comic strips.
- **Late 1961** — Warhol starts working on the *Campbell's Soup Cans* series at his New York studio.
- **June 1962** — Warhol completes the definitive set of 32 hand-painted canvases.
- **July 9, 1962** — The series is first exhibited at the Ferus Gallery in Los Angeles.
- **August 4, 1962** — The Ferus exhibition closes; Warhol begins silkscreen portraits of Marilyn Monroe.
- **October 1962** — Warhol shows related works in the landmark *The New Realists* exhibition in New York.
- **1965** — Warhol produces a set of 20 larger, multi-colored *Colored Campbell's Soup Cans*.
- **1968-1969** — Warhol issues two portfolios of ten screen prints each, *Campbell's Soup I* and *II*.
- **1996** — The Museum of Modern Art acquires the original 32 canvases.

## Body

### Background and Production
After establishing himself as a commercial illustrator, Warhol sought a unique direction within the emerging Pop art movement, distinct from peers like Roy Lichtenstein. He turned to everyday commercial imagery. In late 1961, he began the *Campbell's Soup Cans* at his home studio. The series consists of 32 hand-painted canvases, each 20 by 16 inches, depicting a different soup variety. Contrary to later confusion, these original works were not silkscreens but were traced and painted using commercial-grade acrylic paint and stamped details. Warhol used a rubber stamp for the gold fleur-de-lis, marking these as among his last largely hand-painted works.

### Exhibition and Initial Reception
The series was first shown at the Ferus Gallery in Los Angeles in July 1962. The 32 canvases were displayed in a single row on narrow ledges, like grocery items on a shelf. This presentation initially perplexed visitors and drew mockery from a rival gallery and critics. Sales were minimal. However, gallery director Irving Blum recognized their significance and persuaded the initial buyers to relinquish their purchases, allowing him to acquire the complete set for $1,000. Blum's insistence on keeping the series intact was crucial to its legacy. The exhibition helped establish Pop art as a national movement and propelled Warhol to fame.

### Interpretation and Analysis
Warhol's work embraced ordinary consumer culture, rejecting the emotional gesture of Abstract Expressionism. His detached, mechanical style—using stencils, stamps, and later silkscreens—emphasized repetition and sameness, making the concept of mass production the subject itself. This approach unsettled critics, who saw the stark, industrial images as cold and impersonal compared to traditional still life. For some, the work was a Marxist critique of American capitalism or a commentary on dehumanization in mass culture. Warhol maintained an apolitical stance, stating he wanted to show the monotony of everyday life. The series is now regarded as a canonical symbol of Pop art and a pivotal moment in 20th-century art.

### Variations and Market
Following the original series, Warhol created numerous variations, including larger grid paintings, the 1965 *Colored Campbell's Soup Cans* set, and two portfolios of screen prints in 1968 and 1969. The market value of these works has grown dramatically. In 1970, a torn-label painting set a record for a living American artist. By the 2000s, individual canvases were selling for millions, with *Big Campbell's Soup Can with Can Opener (Vegetable)* (1962) achieving over $27.5 million in 2017. The series' fame has also led to unauthorized reproductions and thefts.

### Legacy and Cultural Impact
The *Campbell's Soup Cans* are widely considered one of the most influential bodies of art of the 20th century. They introduced a mass audience to fine art and reshaped aesthetic criteria. Campbell's, initially wary, eventually embraced the association, commissioning Warhol for projects and licensing his imagery. The series has permeated popular culture, inspiring derivative works by artists like Richard Pettibone and Jeff Koons, appearing in films and television, and being referenced in fashion and commercial collaborations.

## Terms
- **Pop art**: An art movement that emerged in the mid-1950s in Britain and the late 1950s in the United States, using imagery from popular and mass culture.
- **Silkscreen**: A printmaking technique where ink is pushed through a mesh screen onto a surface, allowing for rapid, repeated production of images.
- **Abstract Expressionism**: A post-WWII art movement characterized by spontaneous, automatic, or subconscious creation, emphasizing the artist's emotional expression.
- **Readymade**: A term coined by Marcel Duchamp for ordinary manufactured objects that the artist designates as art.
- **Fair use**: A legal doctrine that permits limited use of copyrighted material without permission for purposes such as criticism, commentary, and parody.
- **Appropriation art**: The practice of using pre-existing objects or images with little or no transformation applied to them.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Inspiration**: The exact inspiration for the series is debated. Stories include advice from Muriel Latow, Warhol's personal nostalgia for the soup from his childhood, and possible influences from artists like Yves Klein or Frank Stella.
- **Interpretation**: Scholars debate whether the work is a celebration of consumer culture, a critique of it, or a neutral, deadpan observation. Some see it as a Marxist commentary, while Warhol himself claimed an apolitical stance.
- **Authenticity and Confusion**: The original 32 canvases are often confused with later screen print sets. The creation of unauthorized "Sunday B Morning" prints and the dissolution of the Andy Warhol Art Authentication Board in 2012 have contributed to ongoing issues of authentication.
