# Genealogy

## Overview
Genealogy is the study of families, their history, and the tracing of lineages using records, interviews, and genetic analysis. It helps individuals and communities establish identity, preserve heritage, and understand historical and social contexts. The field has evolved from a practice for nobility to a widespread pursuit aided by technology and DNA testing.

## Timeline
- **c. 12th century CE** — Panji genealogical records begin in India's Mithila region.
- **16th century** — Parish registers for vital records start in England and Germany.
- **1752** — England and its American colonies adopt the Gregorian calendar.
- **1820s** — U.S. antiquarians like John Farmer begin producing genealogical tracts.
- **1845** — New England Historic Genealogical Society (NEHGS) is founded.
- **1894** — Genealogical Society of Utah (later FamilySearch) is founded.
- **Late 1970s** — Alex Haley's *Roots* TV series boosts public interest.
- **Early 2000s** — Internet use for genealogy becomes increasingly popular.
- **2006** — FamilySearch begins digitally scanning microfilm from its vault.
- **2012** — The 1940 U.S. Census is released and indexed by genealogical groups.
- **2019** — The Board for Certification of Genealogists updates standards to include genetic genealogy.
- **2022** — FamilySearch and Ancestry partner to use AI for indexing records, starting with the 1950 U.S. Census.

## Body

### Definition and Scope
Genealogy traces lineages and demonstrates kinship, often displayed in charts or narratives. It is distinct from, but often used synonymously with, family history, a broader biographical study of a family and its community. Research is conducted for personal curiosity, medical history, legal needs, scholarly purposes, or to establish identity and social status.

### Motivations and Uses
Individuals research genealogy for personal heritage, especially if family history is unknown due to adoption or separation. It serves community and religious obligations, such as in Māori culture (whakapapa) or for the LDS Church's doctrine of baptism for the dead. Historically, royal families used genealogy to establish right to rule. In modern times, it helps establish citizenship (e.g., in Ireland and Greece) and is used in legal/forensic contexts to locate heirs or identify crime victims. Genealogy has also been used for discrimination, such as the U.S. "one-drop rule" and Nazi Germany's racial laws.

### Historical Development
Pre-modern genealogy focused on rulers and nobles, often claiming divine descent. Court genealogists preserved or invented lineages, as in Japan and Polynesia. In Western societies, it overlapped with heraldry. Many claimed noble ancestries are now regarded as fabrications. The practice became widespread among commoners in modern times, boosted by media like *Roots* and the internet. Traditional practices persist in some regions, like India's *pothi* records maintained by priests at pilgrimage sites, though they are declining.

### Research Process and Methods
Genealogical research is a complex process using historical records and sometimes genetic analysis. It begins with collecting family documents and working backward in time. Key methods include documentary research, evaluating source quality, and using genetic analysis of mtDNA (maternal line), Y-DNA (paternal line), and autosomal DNA (all lines). Collaboration is common, with data shared via GEDCOM files and social networking sites. Volunteer efforts, like indexing records, are prominent. Specialized software helps collect, store, and display data.

### Records and Documentation
Genealogists use a wide array of records, including vital records, census data, church records, and military records. Governments began keeping records of commoners with parish registers in the 16th century. In Asia, genealogy books have been used for centuries. The LDS Church's FamilySearch operates the world's largest genealogical library and a vast digital collection. Indexing, the transcription of records to digital databases, is a major volunteer and professional activity, now aided by AI. Records are often lost to fire, war, or neglect (e.g., the 1890 U.S. Census), making preservation efforts critical.

### Types of Information and Reliability
Core information includes place names, occupations, family names, given names, and dates. Family names can be confusing due to patronymics, name changes, and immigration. Given names are subject to nicknames and naming traditions. Place names can be ambiguous due to spelling variants and changing borders. Dates must be treated with caution due to memory errors, calendar changes (e.g., Julian to Gregorian), and intentional alteration. Occupational data helps distinguish individuals and understand social context. The reliability of sources depends on the informant's knowledge and motivation, the passage of time, and copying errors. Sources are categorized as original or derivative, and primary or secondary.

### Standards and Ethics
Professional genealogists follow research standards, such as the Genealogical Proof Standard, which requires thorough documentation, source citation, analysis of all evidence, and logical reasoning. Ethical guidelines mandate respecting copyright, protecting the privacy of living individuals, not fabricating information, and being sensitive to uncomfortable findings. Standards for genetic genealogy were formally adopted in 2019.

## Terms
- **Genealogy**: The study of families, family history, and the tracing of their lineages.
- **Family History**: A broader biographical study of a genealogically proven family and its community.
- **Pedigree Chart**: A diagram showing the direct ancestors of an individual.
- **GEDCOM**: A standardized format (GEnealogical Data COMmunication) for sharing genealogical data between software programs.
- **mtDNA**: Mitochondrial DNA, passed from a mother to all her children, used to trace the direct maternal line.
- **Y-DNA**: DNA present only in males, passed from father to son, used to trace the direct paternal line.
- **Autosomal DNA (atDNA)**: DNA from the 22 non-sex chromosomes, inherited from both parents, used to find relatives from any branch.
- **Primary Source**: A record created at the time of the event, like a death certificate.
- **Secondary Source**: A record created days or years after an event.
- **Genealogical Proof Standard**: A set of standards for conducting and documenting genealogical research to ensure accurate conclusions.

## Debates and Open Questions
- The reliability of many historical sources, especially census data and compiled genealogies, is a constant concern due to informant error, bias, and copying mistakes.
- The distinction between "genealogy" and "family history" is debated, with some organizations defining them differently while others use the terms interchangeably.
- The ethical use of DNA information and the privacy of living individuals in genetic genealogy are ongoing areas of standard-setting and discussion.
- The impact of urbanization, migration, and technology on traditional genealogical practices, such as those in India, is a noted concern for preservation.
