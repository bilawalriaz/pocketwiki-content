# Das Kapital

## Overview
*Capital: A Critique of Political Economy* is Karl Marx's foundational analysis of the capitalist mode of production. It argues that capitalism is a historically specific system driven by the exploitation of labor, where unpaid work (surplus value) is the source of profit. The work reveals the economic laws, internal contradictions, and crisis tendencies of capitalism, aiming to provide a scientific basis for the workers' movement and the critique of political economy.

## Timeline
- **1840s** — Marx begins intensive economic studies, producing early manuscripts like the *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844*.
- **1857–58** — Marx writes the *Grundrisse*, a major preparatory manuscript for *Capital*.
- **1859** — *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* is published, a preliminary work covering a fraction of his planned critique.
- **1867** — Volume I of *Capital* is published in German.
- **1872–75** — The French edition of Volume I, revised by Marx, is published; he considered it the definitive version.
- **1883** — Marx dies, leaving manuscripts for Volumes II and III incomplete.
- **1885** — Friedrich Engels edits and publishes Volume II from Marx's manuscripts.
- **1887** — The first English translation of Volume I is published.
- **1894** — Engels edits and publishes Volume III from Marx's manuscripts.
- **1905–10** — Karl Kautsky edits and publishes *Theories of Surplus Value*, often considered Volume IV.
- **2024** — A new English translation of Volume I, based on the 1872 German edition, is published.

## Body

### Background and Influences
Marx synthesized and critiqued three major intellectual traditions: classical political economy (e.g., Adam Smith, David Ricardo), German idealist philosophy (especially Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel), and French utopian socialism (e.g., Charles Fourier, Pierre-Joseph Proudhon). From Hegel, he adopted a dialectical method to understand processes of motion and contradiction, which he "turned right side up" in a materialist framework. His aim was to transform utopian socialism into a "scientific socialism" by interrogating political economy with philosophical tools.

### Aims and Method
Marx's goal was to "lay bare the economic law of motion of modern society," uncovering the specific historical laws of capitalism rather than universal economic laws. His method involved a "descending path" of inquiry to uncover essence from appearances, followed by an "ascending path" of presentation, starting from abstract concepts (like the commodity) to explain the concrete totality. He used abstraction to isolate capitalism's fundamental dynamics and a dialectical method to view economic phenomena as an integrated totality in constant motion, driven by internal contradictions.

### The Writing Process
The writing was a decades-long process, interrupted by political events and personal hardship. The *Grundrisse* (1857–58) was a key milestone. Marx's perfectionism and side projects delayed completion. Friedrich Engels provided crucial financial and intellectual support. Marx's initial 1857 plan for six books was revised; the published volumes correspond only to the first book of that plan.

### Volume I: The Process of Production of Capital
Volume I analyzes the immediate production process. Marx begins with the **commodity**, which has a **use-value** and an **exchange-value**. He argues that the substance of value is **abstract labour**, measured by **socially necessary labour time**. This leads to the concept of **commodity fetishism**, where social relations between producers appear as relations between things.

Money arises as a universal equivalent, and capital is defined as the circuit M-C-M' (money-commodity-more money), where the increment is **surplus value**. This surplus originates from the unique commodity **labour-power**, whose use-value (capacity to create value) exceeds its exchange-value (wage). The capitalist extracts surplus value by extending the working day (**absolute surplus value**) or by increasing productivity to shorten necessary labour time (**relative surplus value**). This process involves the cooperation of workers, the division of labour in manufacture, and finally, machinery and large-scale industry, which revolutionizes production but also degrades the worker.

The **general law of capitalist accumulation** states that accumulation tends to create a **reserve army of labour** (unemployed population) and leads to the relative impoverishment of the working class. Marx concludes Volume I with the history of **primitive accumulation**, the violent historical process (e.g., enclosure of common lands) that divorced producers from the means of production, creating the conditions for capitalism.

### Volume II: The Process of Circulation of Capital
Volume II examines the circulation of capital, focusing on how value and surplus value are realized. It analyzes the three circuits of industrial capital (money, productive, and commodity capital) and the **turnover of capital**, distinguishing between **fixed capital** (machinery) and **circulating capital** (raw materials, wages). Using reproduction schemas, Marx models the equilibrium conditions between Department I (means of production) and Department II (means of consumption) for both simple and expanded reproduction, highlighting the potential for disproportionality and crises.

### Volume III: The Process of Capitalist Production as a Whole
Volume III integrates production and circulation to explain capitalism's concrete forms. **Surplus value** appears to the capitalist as **profit**, calculated on total capital. Competition leads to the formation of an **average rate of profit** and **prices of production**, which deviate from labour values. Marx posits a **tendency of the rate of profit to fall** as the **organic composition of capital** (c/v) rises, though counteracting tendencies exist.

The volume analyzes the division of profit into **interest** (paid to money-capitalists) and **profit of enterprise**, the role of **commercial capital** and **fictitious capital** (e.g., stocks), and **ground rent** paid to landowners. It concludes by critiquing the **trinity formula** (Capital–Profit, Land–Rent, Labour–Wages), which mystifies the origin of all revenue in surplus value extracted from labour.

## Terms
- **Commodity:** An object produced for exchange, possessing both use-value and exchange-value.
- **Use-value:** The utility of a commodity, satisfying a human want or need.
- **Exchange-value:** The quantitative proportion in which a commodity exchanges for others.
- **Value:** The substance of exchange-value, representing congealed abstract human labour.
- **Socially necessary labour time:** The average time required to produce a commodity under normal conditions of production with average skill and intensity.
- **Abstract labour:** Labour as a general social mediation, unique to capitalism, which creates value.
- **Surplus value:** The difference between the value created by a worker and the value of their labour-power (wage); the source of profit.
- **Labour-power:** The worker's capacity to labour, sold as a commodity.
- **Organic composition of capital:** The ratio of constant capital (c) to variable capital (v), reflecting the level of productivity.
- **Commodity fetishism:** The process by which social relations between producers appear as objective relations between things.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **The Labour Theory of Value:** Critics argue it fails to explain market prices and is superseded by subjective utility theories. Defenders interpret it as a critical social theory of value as a form of mediation.
- **The Transformation Problem:** The logical consistency of Marx's method for transforming labour values into market prices of production is heavily debated.
- **The Tendency of the Rate of Profit to Fall:** Its theoretical derivation and empirical validity are contested. Critics like Nobuo Okishio argue innovations can raise the rate of profit.
- **Crisis Theory:** Marx did not provide a single, coherent theory of crisis; the relative importance of underconsumption, disproportionality, and the falling rate of profit is debated.
- **Historical Predictions:** The resilience of capitalism and rising living standards in the 20th century challenged predictions of immiseration and inevitable collapse.
- **Feminist Critique:** Critics like Silvia Federici argue Marx's analysis ignores the crucial role of women's unpaid domestic labour in reproducing labour-power.
- **Ecological Critique:** Early critics saw a "Promethean" bias, but later ecosocialists highlight Marx's concept of the "metabolic rift" as an ecological critique.
- **Political Legacy:** The association with 20th-century communist states leads to debates about whether they represented a failure of Marx's ideas or a distortion of them.
