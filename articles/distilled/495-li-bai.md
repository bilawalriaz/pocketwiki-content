# Li Bai

## Overview
Li Bai (c. 701–762), courtesy name Taibai, was a preeminent poet of the Tang dynasty, a period celebrated as the "Golden Age of Chinese Poetry." Alongside Du Fu, he defined the era's literary heights, blending technical mastery of regulated verse (*lüshi*) and old-style poetry (*gushi*) with Daoist-inspired fantasy, hyperbolic imagery, and a celebrated devotion to wine. His roughly 1,000 extant poems—collected in the *Heyue yingling ji* (753), compiled by Yin Fan, and later anthologized in the *Three Hundred Tang Poems*—chronicle a life of aristocratic wanderings, brief imperial favor, and catastrophic upheaval during the An Lushan Rebellion (755–763). Li Bai’s work reflects the dynasty’s early prosperity and its violent fragmentation, shifting from exuberant nature lyrics to poems of exile. His influence permeates East Asian literature and, via Ezra Pound’s *Cathay* (1915) and Gustav Mahler’s *Das Lied von der Erde* (1908), shaped Western modernism. The expression "Three Wonders" denotes Li Bai's poetry, Pei Min's swordplay, and Zhang Xu's calligraphy.

## Timeline
- **c. 701** — Born likely in Suyab (modern Kyrgyzstan) or Mianyang, Sichuan; family of merchant background, claiming descent from Western Liang nobility.
- **705** — Family relocates secretly to Jiangyou, Sichuan; childhood spent in Qinglian studying classics, swordplay, and Daoist texts.
- **725** — Leaves Sichuan, begins wandering the Yangzi region; meets celebrities, disperses wealth.
- **727** — Marries first wife (Xu family) in Anlu, Hubei; resides near Mount Bi for ~10 years.
- **742** — Daoist priest Wu Yun praises Li to Emperor Xuanzong; summoned to Chang'an, appointed to Hanlin Academy.
- **744** — Meets Du Fu; forms close friendship (only two in-person meetings). Marries second wife, poet Zong.
- **745** — Dismissed from court after eunuch Gao Lishi and consort Yang Guifei take offense; becomes Daoist, wanders Shandong.
- **755–756** — An Lushan Rebellion erupts; Li joins staff of Prince Yong (Xuanzong’s son).
- **757** — Prince Yong defeated; Li imprisoned at Jiujiang, sentenced to death. Guo Ziyi intervenes; sentence commuted to exile in Yelang (Guizhou).
- **759** — Pardoned en route to exile at Wushan; returns eastward, visits Baidicheng.
- **762** — Stays with relative Li Yangbing in Dangtu, Anhui; falls ill, dies (exact date/cause unknown).
- **764** — Emperor Daizong posthumously appoints Li court counsellor; edict arrives after death.

## Body

### Names and Identity
Li Bai’s given name (白) carries two Standard Chinese readings: literary *bó* (Wade–Giles *Po*) and colloquial *bái* (Pinyin *Bai*), yielding romanizations Li Po, Li Bo, and Li Bai. The reconstructed Tang pronunciation is *Bhæk*. His courtesy name **Taibai** (太白, "Great White") refers to the planet Venus; the *New Book of Tang* claims his mother dreamt of Venus during pregnancy. He also bore the art name **Qinglian Jushi** (青莲居士, "Householder of Azure Lotus") and epithets reflecting his legend: **Shixian** (诗仙, "Immortal of Poetry"), **Jiuixian** (酒仙, "Wine Immortal"), **Zhexianren** (谪仙人, "Banished Transcendent"), and **Shixia** (诗侠, "Poet-Knight-Errant"). In Japan he is known as **Ri Haku**.

### Background and Early Life
The *Old* and *New Books of Tang*, plus a preface by relative Li Yangbing, are the primary biographical sources. Li’s ancestry traces to Li Gao, founder of Western Liang, linking him to the Tang imperial house (Longxi Li lineage). During the Sui dynasty, his branch was exiled westward as commoners, settling as merchants in Silk Road cities Suiye (Suyab) and possibly Tiaozhi (near Ghazni, Afghanistan). Around 705, his father Li Ke moved the family illegally to Sichuan. Li read Confucian classics (*Shijing*, *Shujing*) and esoteric Daoist/astrological works, mastering poetry before age ten. He cultivated swordsmanship, claiming at fifteen to have "challenged quite a few great men" and killed several men in *youxia* (knight-errant) chivalry before twenty. Governor Su Ting deemed him a genius in 720, yet Li never sat the civil service exam.

### Imperial Sojourn and Dismissal
After wandering the Yangzi and Yellow River basins (725–742), Li met Daoist Wu Yun, whose praise secured a 742 summons from Emperor Xuanzong (Minghuang). The emperor, fascinated, hosted a banquet where he personally seasoned Li’s soup. Li served as a translator (knowing a non-Chinese language) and Hanlin academician, composing poems for the court, often while drunk. He wrote lyrics for the emperor’s consort Yang Guifei. A likely apocryphal anecdote recounts the powerful eunuch Gao Lishi being ordered to remove Li’s muddy boots; Gao retaliated by turning Yang Guifei against Li’s poems. Xuanzong dismissed Li with lavish gifts in 744–745. Li formally became a Daoist and wandered for a decade.

### Friendship with Du Fu
In autumn 744, Li met Du Fu (712–770); they shared lodging, travel, hunting, wine, and poetry, forging a profound bond. They met once more in 745. Though they never met again, they exchanged poems—Du Fu wrote a dozen to or about Li; one surviving poem from Li addresses Du Fu. Their pairing became the archetypal "Li-Du" duality in Chinese criticism, representing contrasting geniuses: Li the transcendent romantic, Du the Confucian realist. Unlike his younger friend Du Fu, Li did not live to see the end of the chaos.

### Rebellion, Exile, and Pardon
The An Lushan Rebellion (12/755) shattered the dynasty. Xuanzong fled to Sichuan; Crown Prince declared himself Emperor Suzong. Li joined Prince Yong (a lower-ranked son) as staff adviser in 756. When Yong’s forces were crushed by his brother (the new emperor) in 757, Li fled, was captured at Jiujiang, and sentenced to death. General Guo Ziyi—whom Li had saved from court-martial in 735—offered his own rank for Li’s life. The sentence was commuted to exile in remote Yelang (Guizhou). Li traveled leisurely, writing en route; at Wushan in 759, an imperial pardon overtook him. He returned via Baidicheng (immortalized in "Departing from Baidi in the Morning"), settling around Nanjing, Xuancheng, and Li Yang.

### Final Years and Death
In 762, Li joined relative Li Yangbing, magistrate of Dangtu, Anhui. Both Xuanzong and Suzong died; Daizong ascended. Li volunteered for General Li Guangbi’s staff against residual rebels but fell critically ill at 61. Daizong appointed him court counsellor in January 764, but the edict arrived over a year after Li’s death in 1762. Cause and exact date are lost; later accounts cite chronic thoracic suppuration or the legendary drowning while drunkenly embracing the moon’s reflection.

### Calligraphy and Textual Transmission
One authenticated calligraphy scroll survives: "Going Up to the Sun Terrace" (38.1 × 28.5 cm), held in Beijing’s Palace Museum, with later inscriptions by Song Emperor Huizong and Qing Emperor Qianlong. Li’s manuscripts were entrusted to Li Yangbing. Even Li Bai and Du Fu, the two most famous and most comprehensively edited Tang poets, were affected by the destruction of the imperial Tang libraries and the loss of many private collections in the periods of turmoil (An Lushan Rebellion and Huang Chao Rebellion). Song scholars edited his work; the most comprehensive textual studies appear in the Qing *Complete Tang Poems*. Variant texts and lost poems remain a philological challenge.

### Themes and Poetics
**Poetic Tradition:** Li saw himself as heir to the past. Burton Watson calls his work "backward-looking," a "revival and fulfillment" rather than innovation. ~1/6 of his poems are *yuefu* (reworked folk ballads); his *Gu Feng* ("In the Old Manner") collection of fifty-nine poems is, in part, tribute to the poetry of the Han and Wei dynasties. He alludes to Qu Yuan, Tao Yuanming, and Du Fu, and writes *huaigu* (meditations on past ruins).

**Wine and the Moon:** John C.H. Wu notes "no-one has written more poems about wine." Li belonged to Du Fu’s "Eight Immortals of the Wine Cup." James J.Y. Liu translates *zui* not as "drunk" but "rapt with wine"—mentally carried from normal preoccupations. Wine facilitates transcendence and communion with nature/moonlight.

**Fantastic Imagery:** Watson links Li’s fantasy and "childlike wonder" to Daoist *xian* (immortal) lore: mountain ascents modulate into visionary journeys among deities and "jade maidens." This connects to *Chuci* and early *fu* traditions, manifesting in hyperbole and playful personification of mountains and celestial bodies.

**Nostalgia:** Liu cites "Quiet Night Thought" (20 words) as the quintessential homesickness poem: moonlight and frost evoke the vast distances and cultural chasm between capital and frontier.

**Persona:** Li adopts diverse voices, including women’s personas (*Zi Ye* "Lady Midnight" style, Han folk-ballad style).

**Technical Virtuosity:** Critics agree Li introduced no major formal innovations but brightened old forms with unmatched skill. He excelled in *gushi* (old style)—e.g., "Shu Dao Nan" ("Hard Roads in Shu"), where irregular line lengths (4–11 characters) mimic Sichuan’s jagged terrain. He was deemed the Tang’s greatest *jueju* (cut-verse) master by Ming poet Li Panlong. His *lüshi* (regulated verse) mastery was near-total; Watson notes a celebrated violation of parallelism rules in "Seeing a Friend Off," excused by Chinese critics as the privilege of genius.

## Terms
- ****Taibai** (太白)** — Li Bai’s courtesy name; literally "Great White," the Tang name for the planet Venus.
- ****Qinglian Jushi** (青莲居士)** — Li’s art name: "Householder of Azure Lotus," referencing his childhood home Qinglian.
- ****Youxia** (游侠)** — Knight-errant; a chivalric warrior-errant tradition Li practiced in youth, involving swordsmanship and aiding the oppressed.
- ****Hanlin Academy** (翰林院)** — Imperial scholarly institute providing literary expertise and poetry for the emperor; Li served here c. 742–744.
- ****An Lushan Rebellion** (安史之乱)** — Cataclysmic revolt (755–763) by general An Lushan (continued by Shi Siming) that devastated northern China and ended the Tang "golden age."
- ****Yuefu** (乐府)** — "Music Bureau" poems: reworked lyrics from traditional folk ballads; ~1/6 of Li’s corpus uses this form.
- ****Gushi** (古诗)** — "Old style" poetry: less regulated form allowing freedom in line length, rhyme, and structure; Li’s specialty.
- ****Jueju** (绝句)** — "Cut-verse": a quatrain form (5- or 7-character lines) with strict tonal rules; Li deemed its greatest Tang master.
- ****Lüshi** (律诗)** — "Regulated verse": 8-line form with strict tonal patterns and mandatory parallelism in middle couplets; the era’s most demanding form.
- ****Zui** (醉)** — In poetic context, not mere intoxication but being "rapt with wine"—mentally transported from ordinary concerns (per James J.Y. Liu).

## Debates and open questions
- **Birthplace:** Suyab (Kyrgyzstan) vs. Mianyang, Sichuan; sources conflict, complicated by the family’s unauthorized move from a border region.
- **Ethnic/regional identity:** Whether Li’s Central Asian upbringing and merchant background influenced his cosmopolitan outlook and non-Chinese language skills.
- **Court dismissal:** The "muddy boots" anecdote (Gao Lishi, Yang Guifei) is likely apocryphal; the precise political or personal reasons for Xuanzong’s expulsion of Li remain unclear.
- **Death:** Cause and exact date unknown (1762). Competing accounts: chronic illness (Pi Rixiu, 9th c.) vs. legendary drowning while embracing the moon’s reflection.
- **Textual authenticity:** Many poems attributed to Li are spurious; variant readings abound due to library destructions. The *Complete Tang Poems* (Qing) is the standard edition, but earlier Song editions sometimes preserve superior readings.
- **Li-Du debate:** Centuries of criticism frame Li and Du Fu as opposing poles (romantic/transcendent vs. realist/Confucian); modern scholars (e.g., Paula Varsano) argue this binary obscures their shared depths and mutual influence.
