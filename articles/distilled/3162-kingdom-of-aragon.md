# Kingdom of Aragon

## Overview
The Kingdom of Aragon was a medieval Iberian kingdom that became the core of the Crown of Aragon, a composite monarchy ruling territories across the Mediterranean. Its union with Castile in 1479 formed the nucleus of modern Spain. The kingdom's history illustrates the complex process of Iberian state formation, dynastic union, and the eventual centralization of power.

## Timeline
- **c. 922** — The first dynasty of Aragonese counts ends without a male heir.
- **1035** — Kingdom of Navarre is divided; Ramiro receives Aragon, becoming its first de facto ruler.
- **1096** — Capital moves from Jaca to Huesca.
- **1118** — Capital moves to Zaragoza after its conquest.
- **1134** — Aragon and Navarre permanently separate after the death of Alfonso the Battler.
- **1150** — Dynastic union of Aragon and the County of Barcelona creates the Crown of Aragon.
- **1410** — House of Barcelona extinct; Ferdinand of Antequera elected king in 1412.
- **1479** — Crowns of Aragon and Castile united under Ferdinand and Isabella.
- **1707-1715** — Nueva Planta decrees end Aragon's political independence.
- **1833** — Kingdom of Aragon dissolved as an administrative unit.
- **1982** — Aragon becomes an autonomous community of Spain.

## Body

### Origins and Independence
Aragon began as a Carolingian county centered on Jaca, a vassal of the Kingdom of Navarre. Upon the death of Sancho III of Navarre in 1035, his illegitimate son Ramiro received the County of Aragon. Ramiro achieved independence from Navarre, and his son Sancho Ramírez was the first to style himself "King of the Aragonese." The kingdom expanded southward from Al-Andalus, moving its capital from Jaca to Huesca (1096) and then to Zaragoza (1118). By 1285, the southernmost areas of modern Aragon were conquered.

### The Crown of Aragon
In 1150, the Kingdom of Aragon and the County of Barcelona were united through the marriage of Queen Petronilla and Count Ramon Berenguer IV. This created the Crown of Aragon, a composite monarchy where the King of Aragon also ruled Catalonia and later acquired other Mediterranean territories like Valencia, Majorca, Sicily, and Naples. The king held various titles (e.g., Count of Barcelona, Duke of Athens) corresponding to different territories. In the 14th century, the king's power was significantly restricted by the Union of Aragon, a political institution.

### Union with Castile and Aftermath
The House of Barcelona's rule ended in 1410. In 1412, Ferdinand of Antequera, a Castilian prince, was elected king, facing Catalan opposition. His successor, John II, secured the dynastic union of the crowns by having his heir, Ferdinand, marry Isabella of Castile. Upon John II's death in 1479, the union was complete, forming the basis of modern Spain. Aragonese territories retained autonomous institutions like the Corts until the Nueva Planta decrees (1707-1715) by Philip V abolished them after the War of the Spanish Succession, centralizing power. The Kingdom of Aragon persisted as an administrative unit until 1833.

## Terms
- **Crown of Aragon**: A composite monarchy formed in 1150, uniting the Kingdom of Aragon with the County of Barcelona and later other territories under a single monarch.
- **Corts**: The parliamentary and administrative institutions of the Crown of Aragon, which retained autonomy until the 18th century.
- **Nueva Planta decrees**: Laws issued by Philip V between 1707 and 1715 that abolished the separate political institutions of Aragon and other territories, centralizing power in Spain.
- **Al-Andalus**: The term for the territories of the Iberian Peninsula under Muslim rule during the Middle Ages.
- **Union of Aragon**: A 14th-century political institution that greatly restricted the power of the Aragonese monarch.

## Debates and open questions
The etymology of the name "Aragon" is debated; it may derive from the Basque words for "good upper valley" or from the earlier Roman province of Hispania Tarraconensis.
