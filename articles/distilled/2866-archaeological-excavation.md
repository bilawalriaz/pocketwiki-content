# Archaeological excavation

## Overview
Archaeological excavation is the systematic exposure, processing, and recording of buried remains to understand past human activity. It is a destructive science, as the site is permanently altered, making meticulous recording essential for preserving the information it contains. Modern excavation methods focus on understanding the chronological sequence of a site's formation to reconstruct its history.

## Timeline
- **6th century BC** — Nabonidus, king of Babylon, conducts one of the first known excavations of an ancient temple floor.
- **Early Roman periods** — Julius Caesar's men loot bronze artifacts.
- **Medieval period** — Europeans begin digging up partially emerged pots and weapons from farmlands.
- **Early to mid-19th century** — Meticulous, methodical archaeological excavation begins to replace antiquarian digging.
- **1967** — One of the earliest uses of earth-moving machinery occurs at Durrington Walls.
- **1970s** — The single context recording system is developed by the Museum of London.

## Body

### History and Motivation
Early digging, from Babylonian kings to medieval Europeans, was often for loot or curiosity. Antiquarians in North America and Europe excavated burial mounds, frequently destroying artifacts and their context. Modern, methodical excavation emerged in the 19th century, emphasizing careful recording and preservation. Today, excavations are primarily either **research excavations** (academic, leisurely) or **development-led excavations** (professional, time-pressured, funded by developers). Development-led work often uses trial methods like **test pits** or **watching briefs** (observing non-archaeological trenches) to assess a site's potential.

### Core Concepts: Stratigraphy and Phasing
Archaeological material accumulates in layers, creating an **archaeological sequence** or record. **Stratigraphy** is the study of these layers, based on the **Law of Superposition** (lower layers are older). Each discrete layer or cut is a **context**. The physical location and relationship of contexts are vital for interpretation. Archaeologists group contexts into larger units like **sub-groups** (e.g., a burial) and **phases**. A **phase** represents a near-contemporaneous horizon, like an occupation surface. An **intrusion** is an object or feature from a later period found within an earlier phase.

### Methods and Recording
The primary method is **stratigraphic excavation**, which removes deposits in reverse order of creation to build a chronological **Harris matrix** (a two-dimensional sequence chart). This involves carefully cleaning surfaces to isolate context edges before removal. **Strip, map and sample** is a UK method for development sites, involving machine stripping, plotting features, and partial excavation. **Mechanical excavation** uses machinery to quickly remove overburden but can be controversial for its potential to damage deposits or reduce recording precision.

Because excavation is unrepeatable, recording is critical. The **single context recording system** assigns a unique number to each context, documenting it on sheets, plans, and sections. **Digital recording** uses tools like GPS, 3D scanners, and databases to create precise, shareable records.

### Finds Processing
Artifacts are primarily recovered by hand during excavation. To maximize recovery of small items like seeds or bone fragments, techniques like **sieving** (screening) and **flotation** (separating finds by floating them on water) are used. **Spot dating** by specialists during excavation provides immediate chronological feedback, helping to confirm or adjust the working hypothesis of the site's phasing and strategy.

## Terms
- **Archaeological context**: The physical location of an artifact or feature, including its relationship to other finds, crucial for determining age and function.
- **Ecofacts**: Organic remains like animal bones, pollen, or charcoal that provide evidence of human activity.
- **Feature**: A non-portable modification to a site, such as a post mold, hearth, or burial.
- **Harris matrix**: A diagrammatic representation of a site's stratigraphic sequence, showing the chronological order of contexts.
- **Law of Superposition**: The principle that in undisturbed layers, the lowest stratum is the oldest.
- **Phase**: A grouping of contexts representing a near-contemporaneous period of activity or an occupation horizon.
- **Single context recording**: A system where each excavated layer or feature is given a unique number and recorded individually.
- **Stratigraphic excavation**: The method of removing archaeological deposits in the reverse order they were created to preserve the chronological sequence.

## Debates and Open questions
- The use of mechanical excavation is controversial, as it can result in less discrimination in recording the archaeological sequence.
- New terminology like "strip, map and sample" has been criticized within the profession as jargon that may cover up for falling standards of practice.
- Processual archaeologist Lewis Binford highlighted that the archaeological record may not fully reflect primary historical activities, as secondary "boredom reducer" tasks leave more visible traces.
