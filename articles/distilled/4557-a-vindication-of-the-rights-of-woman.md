# A Vindication of the Rights of Woman

## Overview
*A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792) is a foundational essay by Mary Wollstonecraft arguing women deserve rational education and equal moral standing with men. Written amid French Revolution debates, it challenges 18th-century theories denying women full education, asserting their essential role as mothers and companions. A pioneering feminist philosophy text, its classification as "feminist" is debated due to historical context and Wollstonecraft's nuanced gender role views.

## Body

### Historical Context and Motivation
The essay emerged from the British "Revolution controversy" pamphlet war. Wollstonecraft had entered the debate with *Rights of Men* (1790), arguing rights must be based on reason, not tradition. She was galvanized to write *Rights of Woman* after reading Talleyrand's 1791 report recommending women receive only domestic education. She dedicated the book to him, urging reconsideration. The work extends her arguments from specific men's rights to the abstract category of "woman," engaging philosophers like Locke and Rousseau on natural rights.

### Rational Education
Wollstonecraft's central argument is that women must be educated rationally to become virtuous companions and effective mothers. She attacks theorists like Rousseau and conduct-book writers like James Fordyce, who believed women incapable of abstract thought and educated only for male pleasure. She contends women's apparent frivolity stems from a "false system of education" valuing beauty over intellect. She proposes a national system of free, co-educational day schools, arguing men and women, as "the cement of society," should be "educated after the same model."

### Feminism and Equality
Often called the "ur-document of modern liberal feminism," though the term "feminist" did not exist then. Wollstonecraft does not argue for identical roles but for equality in moral responsibility and education, based on belief that men and women are equal before God and subject to the same moral law. This exposed the sexual double standard. However, she acknowledges "the superiority of masculine strength and valour" and calls on men, not women, to initiate social change, as women's lack of education prevents them from altering their situation.

### Critique of Sensibility
Wollstonecraft scathingly criticizes "false and excessive sensibility," the cult of heightened emotion. She argues women who are "the prey of their senses" cannot think rationally and endanger civilization. For her, reason and feeling should inform each other, but passion must be governed. This leads her to downplay female sexuality, advising passion should "subside into friendship" in marriage. Scholars debate whether this reflects fear that female desire would lead to male domination, or was a necessary rhetorical sacrifice linking female independence to sexual vice.

### Republicanism and Class
Scholar Claudia Johnson calls the work a "republican manifesto" seeking to restore a civic ethos with distinct but equal gender roles. Wollstonecraft's republicanism is moral, focusing on individual virtue and the family as the school of patriotism. The text has a bourgeois perspective, addressing the middle class and criticizing the wealthy for "false-refinement" and corruption. While attacking the rich, she does not champion the poor, viewing their struggles as protection from idle vice. Her national education plan retains class distinctions after age nine.

### Rhetoric and Style
Wollstonecraft blends rational philosophical treatise language with passionate sensibility rhetoric, using personal address and emotional exclamations, especially against Rousseau. She employs analogies comparing women to slaves, tyrants, and soldiers to illustrate their constrained social position. This hybrid style demonstrates her argument that rationality and sensibility can coexist.

## Terms
- **Proto-feminist**: A thinker whose ideas anticipate later feminist movements, though the term "feminist" did not exist in their time.
- **Sensibility**: An 18th-century cultural and literary movement emphasizing refined emotion, sympathy, and nervous sensitivity, often associated with women.
- **Conduct book**: A popular genre of advice literature, often for women, prescribing social and moral behavior.
- **Natural rights**: Rights believed to be inherent and universal, granted by God or nature, not by government or tradition.
- **Companionate marriage**: An ideal of marriage based on love, friendship, and intellectual companionship, rather than economic or social arrangement.
- **Republicanism**: A political ideology advocating for a state without a monarchy, emphasizing civic virtue and the common good.
- **Double standard**: A set of principles applied more strictly to one group (often women) than to another (often men), particularly regarding sexual morality.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Feminist classification**: Scholars debate whether Wollstonecraft is a true feminist or a proto-feminist, given the anachronism of the label and her complex views on gender roles and sexuality.
- **Political rights**: It is unclear if Wollstonecraft advocated for women's full voting rights or other political rights, making her political stance difficult to categorize.
- **View on sexuality**: Scholars like Cora Kaplan argue Wollstonecraft's work expresses a "violent antagonism to the sexual," while others see it as a strategic move to protect women from domination in a patriarchal society.
- **Legacy and reception**: The modern assumption that the work was initially poorly received is a misconception; it was favorably reviewed until Wollstonecraft's private life was posthumously exposed, leading to a century of repute.
