# Cyrus McCormick

## Overview
Cyrus Hall McCormick was an American inventor and businessman who patented and commercially developed the mechanical reaper, a machine that dramatically increased agricultural productivity. His work, building on earlier efforts by his father and others, helped industrialize farming and fueled urban migration. He founded the McCormick Harvesting Machine Company, which later became part of International Harvester.

## Timeline
- **Feb 15, 1809** — Born in Raphine, Virginia.
- **1831** — First public demonstration of his mechanical reaper design.
- **Jun 21, 1834** — Granted first patent for the reaper.
- **1840s** — Began selling reapers in increasing numbers; licensed designs for mass production.
- **1847** — Moved to Chicago with his brother Leander to establish a factory.
- **1851** — Displayed reaper at London's Crystal Palace Exhibition, winning a gold medal.
- **1855** — Lost patent infringement lawsuit against competitor John Henry Manny.
- **1864** — Unsuccessfully ran for Congress as a Democrat with a peace platform.
- **1871** — Chicago factory destroyed in the Great Chicago Fire; rebuilt by 1873.
- **May 13, 1884** — Died in Chicago.

## Body
**Invention and Early Development**
McCormick claimed sole credit for the mechanical reaper, but he was one of several engineers producing working models in the 1830s. His work built on over two decades of development by his father, Robert McCormick Jr., with the aid of Jo Anderson, an enslaved man held by the family. Cyrus McCormick's first reaper demonstration was in 1831, and he received his first patent in 1834. Early sales were poor due to the machine's unreliability, but sales grew after 1842 as he improved the design and began licensing production.

**Business Expansion and Legal Battles**
In 1847, McCormick moved his operations to Chicago for its superior transportation links. He developed innovative marketing and sales techniques, including a nationwide network of trained salesmen who demonstrated and repaired machines in the field. His business success was challenged by patent disputes with competitors like Obed Hussey and John Henry Manny. McCormick lost key court cases, including an 1855 infringement suit against Manny, and his patent was not renewed in 1861, while Hussey's was.

**Personal Life, Activism, and Legacy**
McCormick married Nancy "Nettie" Fowler in 1858; they had seven children. A devout Presbyterian and lifelong Democrat, he advocated for reconciliation before the Civil War and later proposed international arbitration for peace. He was a major benefactor of educational and religious institutions, including the McCormick Theological Seminary. His reaper reduced farm labor and increased productivity, contributing to the industrialization of agriculture. His company eventually merged into International Harvester in 1902.

## Terms
- **Mechanical reaper:** A horse-drawn machine that cuts and gathers grain, replacing manual labor.
- **Patent:** A government grant giving an inventor exclusive rights to an invention for a set period.
- **Mass production:** Manufacturing goods in large quantities using standardized processes.
- **Calvinist traits:** Values like self-denial, thrift, and efficiency associated with the Protestant theology of John Calvin.
- **International Harvester Company:** A major agricultural equipment manufacturer formed in 1902 from the merger of McCormick Harvesting Machine Company and others.

## Debates and Open Questions
The source notes that McCormick "always claimed credit as the single inventor" of the reaper, but he was "one of several designing engineers" in the 1830s. His work also built on his father's 28 years of development and the aid of Jo Anderson. This challenges the narrative of a lone inventor. Additionally, his brother Leander later tried to "emphasize the contributions of others in the family to the reaper invention, especially their father," creating a family dispute over credit.
