# Howard Hughes

## Overview

Howard Hughes Jr. (1905–1976) was an American aviator, filmmaker, aerospace engineer, and business magnate whose innovations in aviation and aerospace defined mid-20th-century technology. He rose to prominence as a Hollywood producer in the late 1920s, later founding Hughes Aircraft Company and setting numerous aviation records. His later life was marked by increasing reclusiveness driven by obsessive-compulsive disorder, chronic pain, and sensory impairments.

## Body

### Early Life and Education

Born into immense wealth as the son of inventor Howard R. Hughes Sr., whose two-cone roller bit revolutionized oil drilling, Hughes inherited 75% of the family fortune at age 19 following his parents' deaths. His early fascination with science and technology led him to build Houston's first wireless radio transmitter at age 11 and construct a motorized bicycle using parts from his father's steam engine. Despite being an indifferent student, he showed particular aptitude in mathematics and mechanics, taking flying lessons at 14 and briefly attending Caltech before withdrawing from Rice University after his father's death.

### Film Career

Hughes launched his film career in 1926 with *Swell Hogan*, which he financed and produced but ultimately destroyed after deeming it a failure. His subsequent productions—including *Everybody's Acting* (1926), *Two Arabian Knights* (1927), *The Racket* (1928), and *Hell's Angels* (1930)—achieved both critical acclaim and controversy. *Scarface* (1932) faced censorship battles over its violent content. In 1948, Hughes gained control of RKO, a struggling major Hollywood studio, by acquiring the 929,000 shares owned by Floyd Odlum's Atlas Corporation, for $8,825,000. His tenure was marked by political purges, reduced production, and eventual sale to General Tire and Rubber Company in 1955 for a reported personal profit of $6.5 million.

### Aviation and Aerospace Innovation

Hughes founded the Hughes Aircraft Company, a division of Hughes Tool Company, in a rented corner of a Lockheed Aircraft Corporation hangar in Burbank, California, in 1932, initially to build the revolutionary H-1 Racer, which featured retractable landing gear and flush-mounted rivets to reduce drag. On September 13, 1935, he set the landplane airspeed record of 352 mph (566 km/h) over his test course near Santa Ana, California. A year and a half later, on January 19, 1937, flying the same H-1 Racer fitted with longer wings, Hughes set a new transcontinental airspeed record by flying non-stop from Los Angeles to Newark in seven hours, 28 minutes, and 25 seconds. His 1938 round-the-world flight in a Lockheed 14 Super Electra covered the globe in 91 hours, earning him the Collier Trophy. The H-4 Hercules, nicknamed the "Spruce Goose," flew only once on November 2, 1947, for a distance of one mile at 70 feet altitude. Despite its limited flight history, it remains the aircraft with the longest wingspan ever built (319 feet 11 inches).

### Military Aircraft Development

Hughes developed several military aircraft, including the D-2 (later XF-11), a twin-boom reconnaissance plane that suffered a catastrophic crash on July 7, 1946, near Hughes Airfield in Culver City, California. He sustained severe injuries including crushed ribs, a collapsed lung, and third-degree burns. His recovery was complicated by chronic pain and addiction to codeine. The XF-11 program was plagued by design disputes with the USAAF, particularly over the use of Duramold construction materials, and was ultimately canceled after the war ended.

### Business Empire and Las Vegas

Beginning in the 1960s, Hughes expanded his empire into Las Vegas real estate, acquiring numerous hotels and casinos including the Desert Inn, Sands, Frontier, and Castaways. His purchase of the Desert Inn in 1967 marked the beginning of a four-year period during which he became Nevada's largest employer and transformed Las Vegas from a gambling frontier town into a cosmopolitan entertainment destination. His obsessive-compulsive behaviors intensified during this period, leading to extreme reclusiveness and elaborate demands on his staff.

### Personal Life and Decline

Hughes's personal relationships were marked by numerous high-profile romances with Hollywood stars including Katharine Hepburn, Ava Gardner, and Jane Russell. He married Jean Peters in 1957, but the marriage deteriorated due to his worsening mental health. His later years were characterized by severe obsessive-compulsive behaviors, chronic pain from multiple aircraft crashes, increasing deafness, and complete withdrawal from public life. In the last ten years of his life, 1966 to 1976, Hughes lived in hotels in many cities – including Beverly Hills, Boston, Las Vegas, Nassau, Freeport and Vancouver. He lived almost exclusively in the penthouse of the Xanadu Beach Resort & Marina for the last four years of his life.

### Death and Legacy

Hughes died on April 5, 1976, at 1:27 p.m. on board an aircraft, Learjet 24B N855W, owned by Robert Graf and piloted by Roger Sutton and Jeff Abrams. He was en route from his penthouse at the Acapulco Princess Hotel (now the Princess Mundo Imperial) in Mexico to the Methodist Hospital in Houston. An autopsy revealed kidney failure as the immediate cause of death, though investigators noted evidence of prolonged malnutrition, bedsores, and five broken hypodermic needles embedded in his arms. His death triggered a protracted legal battle over his estate, including a disputed "Mormon Will" that was ultimately declared a forgery. His legacy includes the Howard Hughes Medical Institute, one of the world's largest private biomedical research organizations, and Hughes Holdings Inc., which continues to develop major real estate projects.

## Terms

**Allodynia**: Pain from stimuli that don't normally cause pain, possibly contributing to Hughes's refusal to wear clothing.

**Congressional Gold Medal**: The highest civilian honor awarded by Congress, which Hughes received in 1939 for aviation achievements.

**Duramold**: A type of molded plywood construction used in Hughes's D-2 aircraft design.

**Harmon Trophy**: Award given annually for outstanding achievement in aviation, which Hughes won in 1936 and 1938.

**H-1 Racer**: Hughes's custom-built monoplane that set multiple speed records in 1935 and 1937.

**H-4 Hercules ("Spruce Goose")**: The world's largest flying boat, built primarily of birch wood due to wartime material restrictions.

**Project Azorian**: CIA operation in 1974 to recover Soviet submarine K-129 from the ocean floor, using the Glomar Explorer vessel.

**RKO Pictures**: One of Hollywood's Big Five studios, acquired by Hughes in 1948.

**Trans World Airlines (TWA)**: Major American airline in which Hughes acquired a controlling interest in 1944.

**Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD)**: Mental health condition characterized by intrusive thoughts and repetitive behaviors, which significantly affected Hughes's later life.

## Debates and Open Questions

Several aspects of Hughes's life remain subjects of scholarly debate. The authenticity of his 1972 "Mormon Will" continues to generate controversy, with some historians questioning whether Hughes actually signed the document found at the LDS Church office. The circumstances surrounding his death are also disputed, with forensic analysis suggesting possible medical negligence or even deliberate harm through unnecessary injections. His relationship with organized crime figures in Las Vegas and his role in the 1960s political corruption scandals involving Richard Nixon remain areas of ongoing historical investigation. Additionally, the extent to which his obsessive-compulsive behaviors were exacerbated by his physical injuries versus being independent psychological conditions continues to be debated among medical historians.
