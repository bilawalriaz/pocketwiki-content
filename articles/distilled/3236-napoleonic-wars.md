# Napoleonic Wars

## Overview
The Napoleonic Wars (1803–1815) were a series of global conflicts fought between France and various European coalitions, originating from the political upheaval of the French Revolution. They resulted in French domination over Continental Europe and profoundly reshaped the continent's political map, military practices, and the rise of nationalism. The wars are a defining event of the early 19th century, ending with Napoleon's final defeat and a new European order established by the Congress of Vienna.

## Timeline
- **18 May 1803** — Britain declares war on France, ending the Peace of Amiens.
- **21 Oct 1805** — British victory at the Battle of Trafalgar secures naval supremacy.
- **2 Dec 1805** — Napoleon defeats the Russo-Austrian army at the Battle of Austerlitz.
- **14 Oct 1806** — French victory at Jena-Auerstedt destroys the Prussian army.
- **7 Jul 1807** — Treaties of Tilsit end the War of the Fourth Coalition.
- **1808–1814** — The Peninsular War begins as a major drain on French resources.
- **5–6 Jul 1809** — Napoleon defeats Austria at the Battle of Wagram.
- **24 Jun 1812** — Napoleon invades Russia with the Grande Armée.
- **7 Sep 1812** — Indecisive Battle of Borodino precedes the French capture of Moscow.
- **16–19 Oct 1813** — Coalition victory at the Battle of Leipzig ("Battle of the Nations").
- **30 Mar 1814** — Allied forces capture Paris; Napoleon abdicates in April.
- **18 Jun 1815** — Napoleon is defeated at the Battle of Waterloo, ending the wars.

## Body

### Origins and Early Conflicts
The wars grew out of the French Revolutionary Wars (1792–1802) and the political instability of the French Revolution. After a brief peace, Britain declared war in 1803, angered by Napoleon's reordering of Europe and threats to British trade and colonies. The Third Coalition (Britain, Austria, Russia) was defeated in 1805, culminating in Napoleon's masterpiece victory at Austerlitz, which dissolved the Holy Roman Empire. The Fourth Coalition (Prussia, Russia, Britain) was crushed in 1806–07, leaving Britain as France's sole major enemy, secure behind its naval supremacy won at Trafalgar.

### The Continental System and the Peninsular War
To weaken Britain economically, Napoleon implemented the Continental System, closing European ports to British trade. This policy led to the invasion of Portugal in 1807 and the subsequent French takeover of Spain in 1808, installing Napoleon's brother as king. This provoked a popular uprising and the Peninsular War (1808–1814), a protracted guerrilla conflict that became a major drain on French manpower and resources, described by Napoleon as the "Spanish ulcer." Britain, Spain, and Portugal fought France in this theatre for six years.

### Coalition Wars and the Invasion of Russia
Austria re-entered the war in 1809 (Fifth Coalition) but was defeated at Wagram. The Continental System's strain on Russia led Napoleon to invade in 1812 with a Grande Armée of about 450,000 men. After the bloody, indecisive Battle of Borodino, Napoleon captured a burning Moscow. The subsequent catastrophic retreat, due to scorched-earth tactics, disease, and the Russian winter, destroyed his army. This disaster encouraged Prussia, Austria, and others to join Russia and Britain in the Sixth Coalition.

### Final Defeats and Aftermath
The Sixth Coalition defeated Napoleon at Leipzig in 1813 and invaded France, capturing Paris in March 1814. Napoleon abdicated and was exiled to Elba. He escaped in 1815 for the "Hundred Days," but was finally defeated at Waterloo by a Seventh Coalition army. He was exiled to Saint Helena, where he died in 1821. The Congress of Vienna redrew Europe's map, aiming for a balance of power and a century of relative peace.

### Consequences and Legacy
The wars introduced mass conscription and total war, vastly increasing army sizes. Politically, they spread the Napoleonic Code and ideals of the French Revolution, while also fueling nationalism that would later unify Germany and Italy. Britain emerged as the world's dominant naval and economic power. The conflicts directly triggered independence movements in Spanish America and weakened the Spanish and Portuguese empires.

## Terms
- **Continental System:** Napoleon's economic blockade, established by the Berlin Decree (1806), designed to cripple Britain by closing all European ports to its trade.
- **Grande Armée:** The name given to the French Imperial Army at its peak size, especially during the 1812 invasion of Russia.
- **Levée en masse:** A mass conscription decree of the French Revolution that mobilized the entire nation for war, creating large citizen armies.
- **Napoleonic Code:** The French civil code introduced by Napoleon in 1804, which standardized law and was adopted in many territories he controlled.
- **Peninsular War:** The 1808–1814 conflict in the Iberian Peninsula where Britain, Spain, and Portugal fought against French occupation.
- **Scorched-earth tactics:** A military strategy of destroying anything useful to the enemy while retreating, famously used by Russia in 1812.
- **Total war:** A conflict in which a nation's entire resources and population are mobilized for the war effort, blurring the line between combatant and civilian.

## Debates and Open Questions
- **Start Date:** There is no consensus on when the Napoleonic Wars began. Possible dates include Napoleon's coup in 1799, Britain's declaration of war in 1803, or Napoleon's coronation as Emperor in 1804.
- **Napoleon's Intentions:** Historians debate whether Napoleon sought a stable European peace after 1807 or was driven by an insatiable ambition for conquest. Some argue he could have secured a compromise peace, while others contend his aggressive posture made conflict inevitable.
- **Role of Ideology:** Scholars debate the primary drivers of the wars' scale and brutality, citing factors like ideological clashes between revolution and absolutism, the rise of nationalism, or broader intellectual shifts in the culture of war during the Enlightenment.
