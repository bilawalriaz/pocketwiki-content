# Emotion

## Overview

Emotions are physical and mental states brought on by neurophysiological and neuropsychological changes, associated with subjective experiences such as thoughts, feelings, and behavioral responses. There is no scientific consensus on a definition, and emotions are often intertwined with mood, temperament, personality, and disposition. Research on emotion has increased over the past two decades across psychology, medicine, history, sociology, computer science, and philosophy, with theorizing about evolutionary origins dating back to Charles Darwin. From a mechanistic perspective, emotions can be defined as "a positive or negative experience that is associated with a particular pattern of physiological activity," involving multiple components including subjective experience, cognitive processes, expressive behavior, and psychophysiological changes.

## Body

### Etymology and Definitions

The word *emotion* comes from Latin, meaning "a movement of something from the inside towards the outside," and was coined in 1543 by French humanist Étienne Dolet (1509–1546). In the early 1800s, especially under the influence of Thomas Brown, the word became a fully fledged theoretical term referring to mental experience. Some cross-cultural studies indicate that the categorization of "emotion" and classification of basic emotions such as "anger" and "sadness" are not universal, though others argue there are universal bases. In psychiatry and psychology, an inability to express or perceive emotion is sometimes referred to as alexithymia.

Emotions can be occurrences (e.g., panic) or dispositions (e.g., hostility), and short-lived (e.g., anger) or long-lived (e.g., grief). Psychotherapist Michael C. Graham describes all emotions as existing on a continuum of intensity—fear might range from mild concern to terror. Joseph LeDoux has defined emotions as the result of a cognitive and conscious process which occurs in response to a body system response to a trigger.

### Components and Differentiation

According to Scherer's Component Process Model (CPM), there are five crucial elements of emotion: cognitive appraisal (evaluation of events and objects), bodily symptoms (physiological component), action tendencies (motivational component for motor responses), expression (facial and vocal), and feelings (subjective experience). The CPM provides a sequence of events that describes the coordination involved during an emotional episode.

Emotion can be differentiated from similar constructs: *feelings* are subjective representations of emotions private to the individual; *moods* are enduring affective states considered less intense than emotions and appear to lack a contextual stimulus. A distinction can also be made between emotional episodes and emotional dispositions, where someone may be generally disposed to experience certain emotions (e.g., an irritable person).

### Classification Theories

Paul Ekman has supported the view that emotions are discrete, measurable, and physiologically distinct, examining six basic emotions: anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, and surprise. Recent cross-cultural studies led by Daniel Cordaro and Dacher Keltner extended the list to include amusement, awe, contentment, desire, embarrassment, pain, relief, and sympathy. Robert Plutchik developed the "wheel of emotions," suggesting eight primary emotions grouped on a positive or negative basis: joy versus sadness; anger versus fear; trust versus disgust; and surprise versus anticipation. Jaak Panksepp carved out seven biologically inherited primary affective systems: SEEKING, FEAR, RAGE, LUST, CARE, PANIC/GRIEF, and PLAY.

Psychologists have also used factor analysis to map emotions onto underlying dimensions, typically valence (how negative or positive the experience feels) and arousal (how energized or enervated the experience feels). Using statistical methods, Cowen and Keltner identified 27 varieties of emotional experience, including admiration, adoration, aesthetic appreciation, amusement, anger, anxiety, awe, awkwardness, boredom, calmness, confusion, craving, disgust, empathic pain, entrancement, excitement, fear, horror, interest, joy, nostalgia, relief, romance, sadness, satisfaction, sexual desire, and surprise.

### Historical Theories

In Hinduism, Bharata Muni enunciated the nine rasas (emotions) in the *Nātyasāstra*, written between 200 BC and 200 AD, including romance/love, laughter/mirth, fury/anger, compassion/mercy, disgust/aversion, horror/terror, pride/heroism, and surprise/wonder. In Stoic theories, normal emotions are described as irrational impulses from incorrect appraisals, while "good emotions" come from correct appraisals. Aristotle believed emotions were an essential component of virtue. In Chinese antiquity, excessive emotion was believed to cause damage to qi, which damages the vital organs. In the early 11th century, Ibn Sina theorized about the influence of emotions on health and behaviors.

### Evolutionary and Somatic Theories

Darwin's 1872 book argued that emotional expressions have evolutionary origins and can serve adaptive and communicative functions, concluding that expressions are universal across human cultures and that homologous forms occur in other animals. Contemporary evolutionary psychology posits that both basic and social emotions evolved to motivate behaviors adaptive in the ancestral environment. Damasio's study of a subject with ventromedial frontal lobe damage demonstrated how loss of physiological capacity for emotion resulted in lost capacity to make decisions despite robust rational faculties.

Somatic theories claim bodily responses, rather than cognitive interpretations, are essential to emotions. The James–Lange theory (1884) proposed that the perception of an "exciting fact" directly led to a physiological response, which then produces an emotional experience—"we feel sad because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble." The Cannon–Bard theory argued physiological responses were too slow to account for rapid subjective awareness, proposing that an emotion-evoking event triggers simultaneously both a physiological response and conscious experience. Schachter's two-factor theory suggested emotions result from a two-stage process: general physiological arousal followed by cognitive appraisal of that arousal.

### Neurobiological and Emergent Explanations

The neurobiological explanation holds that emotion is a pleasant or unpleasant mental state organized in the limbic system of the mammalian brain, involving neurochemicals such as dopamine, noradrenaline, and serotonin. The amygdala plays a key role in coordinating behavioral responses to threat stimuli. Pioneering work by Paul Broca (1878), James Papez (1937), and Paul D. MacLean (1952) suggested emotion is related to the limbic system, including the hypothalamus, cingulate cortex, and hippocampi.

Bud Craig (2003) distinguished "classical" emotions (love, anger, fear) evoked by environmental stimuli from "homeostatic emotions"—attention-demanding feelings evoked by body states such as pain, hunger, and fatigue. Derek Denton calls these "primordial emotions," defined as "the subjective element of the instincts." Joseph LeDoux differentiates between the human's defense system and emotions such as fear, noting the amygdala may release hormones due to a trigger, "then we elaborate it through cognitive and conscious processes." Lisa Feldman Barrett argues emotions are socially constructed: "They are not triggered; you create them."

### Disciplinary Approaches

Many disciplines study emotions. In sociology, emotions are conceptualized in terms of multidimensional characteristics including cultural labels, physiological changes, expressive movements, and appraisals. Jonathan Turner identified four primary emotions founded on human neurology: assertive-anger, aversion-fear, satisfaction-happiness, and disappointment-sadness. Randall Collins formulated interaction ritual theory, proposing we experience different levels of emotional energy during face-to-face interactions. Émile Durkheim wrote about collective effervescence experienced during totemic rituals.

The history of emotions has become increasingly popular, with scholars arguing it is an essential category of analysis. Research in historical trauma suggests some traumatic emotions can be passed from parents to offspring to second and even third generation, presented as examples of transgenerational trauma. In computer science, affective computing deals with the design of systems that can recognize, interpret, and process human emotions, originating with Rosalind Picard's 1995 paper.

### Effects on Memory

Emotion affects the way autobiographical memories are encoded and retrieved. Emotional memories are reactivated more, remembered better, and have more attention devoted to them. Through remembering past achievements and failures, autobiographical memories affect how we perceive and feel about ourselves.

## Terms

- **Alexithymia**: An inability to express or perceive emotion
- **Affective computing**: A branch of computer science dealing with systems that recognize, interpret, and process human emotions
- **Core affect**: A two-dimensional component of emotion capturing hedonic tone (valence) and felt energy (arousal)
- **James–Lange theory**: The theory that physiological responses precede and cause emotional experience
- **Limbic system**: A group of brain structures including the hypothalamus, cingulate cortex, and hippocampi, associated with emotion
- **Primordial emotions**: "The subjective element of the instincts" including thirst, hunger for air, hunger for food, and pain
- **Rasas**: Nine emotions enunciated by Bharata Muni in the *Nātyasāstra*
- **Valence**: How negative or positive an experience feels
- **Arousal**: How energized or enervated an experience feels
- **Emotional labor**: Arlie Russell Hochschild's concept regarding the role of emotions in organizations

## Debates and open questions

There is no scientific consensus on a definition of emotion. Scholars debate whether the categorization of "emotion" and classification of basic emotions such as "anger" and "sadness" are universal or culture-specific. The inclusion of cognitive appraisal as one of the elements of emotion is slightly controversial, since some theorists assume emotion and cognition are separate but interacting systems. The James–Lange theory's issue is that of causation (bodily states causing emotions and being a priori), not bodily influences on emotional experience. The situated perspective on emotion states that conceptual thought is not an inherent part of emotion, contrasting with cognitivist and neo-Jamesian theories that see emotion as a purely internal process.
