# Literature review

A literature review is a critical overview and synthesis of previously published research on a particular topic. It maps the current state of thinking on that topic, traces how knowledge in the field has evolved, identifies what is generally accepted, and points out which questions or areas remain insufficiently explored. The term can refer to a full scholarly paper or to a section within a book, thesis, or article. In a research paper the review usually sits before the methodology and results, because its job is to situate the current study inside the existing body of knowledge and to give the reader context for what follows.

A good literature review is shaped by a proper research question, a theoretical framework, or a chosen research method. Producing one is a common requirement in graduate and postgraduate work, appearing in theses, dissertations, journal articles, and the research proposals that precede them.

## Narrative versus systematic

Since the concept of a systematic review was formalized in the 1970s, reviews have been split into two broad families.

**Narrative reviews** are the conventional default when a source says "literature review" without further specification. They come in three main forms: *evaluative*, *exploratory*, and *instrumental*. They treat the literature as a body of work to be interpreted, organized, and argued about rather than aggregated.

**Systematic reviews** answer a specific research question by identifying, appraising, selecting, and synthesizing all high-quality evidence relevant to that question. A **meta-analysis** is a systematic review that also uses statistical methods to combine the data from the selected studies, producing a more reliable pooled result. Because the word "meta-analysis" describes this quantitative extension, it is not usually called a "literature review" in the narrative sense.

## Other review approaches

Several further approaches sit alongside the narrative/systematic split. Torraco (2016) describes an **integrative literature review**, whose purpose is to generate new knowledge on a topic by reviewing, critiquing, and synthesizing the literature under investigation. George et al. (2023) group review approaches by their purpose, object, subject, community, and practices, and identify six types:

- **Exploratory or scoping reviews** prioritize breadth over depth.
- **Systematic or integrative reviews** pull together empirical studies on a topic.
- **Meta-narrative reviews** are qualitative and use literature to compare different research or practice communities.
- **Problematizing or critical reviews** propose new perspectives on a concept by associating it with other literature.
- **Meta-analyses and meta-regressions** integrate quantitative studies and identify moderators.
- **Mixed research syntheses** combine several of the above approaches within a single paper.

## Process and product

Shields and Rangarajan (2013) distinguish the ongoing **process** of reviewing the literature from the finished **product**, the written review itself. The process is rarely a single bounded step: it informs many parts of an empirical research project, from framing the question to interpreting the findings, and calls on different kinds of thinking. Both Shields and Rangarajan (2013) and Granello (2001) link the activities of doing a literature review to Bloom's revised taxonomy of the cognitive domain, covering remembering, understanding, applying, analyzing, evaluating, and creating.

## Artificial intelligence in literature reviews

AI is reshaping how literature reviews are produced. Generative pre-trained transformers such as ChatGPT are now used by both students and academics to assist with reviews, and since 2023 a growing number of tools powered by large language models have been developed to help automate or generate them. The central problem with using ChatGPT in academic reviews is its tendency to "hallucinate," producing plausible but fabricated citations or claims. Efforts to mitigate this include integrating plugins; Rad et al. (2023), for example, used ScholarAI for reviews in cardiothoracic surgery.
