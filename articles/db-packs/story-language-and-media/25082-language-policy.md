# Language policy

Language policy is a body of ideas, laws, regulations, rules and practices intended to achieve planned language change in a society, group, or system. The field studies what governments decide about language and how those decisions play out in practice.

The field sits between sociolinguistics and applied linguistics. Joshua Fishman and Ofelia García treat it as part of sociolinguistics, while Bernard Spolsky, Robert B. Kaplan, and Joseph Lo Bianco place it under applied linguistics. Related fields include language ideology (the beliefs people hold about language), language revitalization (efforts to rescue endangered languages), and language education. The field is also known as language planning or language policy and planning.

Spolsky (2004) breaks the field into three components: language practices, the habitual patterns by which speakers choose among the varieties in their repertoire; language beliefs or ideology, the assumptions speakers hold about language and its proper use; and language management, deliberate efforts to modify practice through planning or intervention.

The traditional focus is language regulation: what governments do through legislation, court decisions, or policy to determine how languages are used, cultivate skills needed for national priorities, and establish the rights of groups to maintain their languages. Implementation varies by state and is often shaped by contingent historical circumstances. States also differ in how explicit their policies are. France's Toubon law and Quebec's Charter of the French Language are explicit examples, setting out which language must be used in signage, contracts, and public life.

Language policy can create inequality. Tollefson argues that planning language is a way of institutionalizing language as a basis for distinguishing social groups, deciding who gains access to political power and economic resources. Policy is one mechanism by which dominant groups establish hegemony in language use.

States, local authorities, and pressure groups can promote bilingual signage, agitate for translations, or push one language over another. Although nations historically used policy to elevate one official language at the expense of others, many now design policy to protect regional and ethnic languages whose viability is threatened. Granting language rights to minorities can build citizens' trust in central government, so linguistic diversity is treated less as a threat to cohesion than as a long-term asset.

More than half of the roughly 6000 languages spoken today are estimated to be in danger of disappearing during the 21st century. Survival depends on the size of the native-speaker population, use in formal communication, and the geographic and socio-economic weight of the speakers. National language policies can either mitigate or worsen these pressures.

The comparative study of policy has its own reference infrastructure. Sociolinguist Jacques Leclerc built the French-language site L'aménagement linguistique dans le monde, hosted by Université Laval under the CIRAL, which by2008 documented linguistic portraits and policies for 354 states or autonomous territories across 194 recognised countries.
