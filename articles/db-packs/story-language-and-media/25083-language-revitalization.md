# Language revitalization

Language revitalization is the attempt to halt or reverse the decline of a threatened language or to revive an extinct one. Some distinguish *revival* (resurrecting a language with no native speakers) from *revitalization* (rescuing a declining one). The work is typically done by linguists, community groups, or governments.

Why it matters. In 2012 the UN estimated that over half of the world's languages had fewer than 10,000 speakers and a quarter had fewer than 1,000, with most projected to vanish within a century without intervention. Between first European contact and around 2000, Indigenous languages in Canada reportedly fell from about 450 to 60 and in Australia from 250 to 145. Communities often treat a language as core to cultural identity, ancestry, and connection to land, and elevating it to national status, such as Quechua in Peru, is one political response.

How languages become endangered. Colonialism is the most common cause, through forced assimilation, boarding schools that punished native speech, and the promotion of a colonial lingua franca. In 20th-century Mexico, schools taught almost exclusively Spanish; US and Canadian boarding schools operated under what one scholar called "a mission of linguicide." Creole languages, often born in plantation colonies, face similar suppression and added pressure from globalization, which privileges dominant languages. Physical danger, economic exploitation, genocide, and assimilation all reduce the speaker base.

UNESCO's six-point endangerment scale, from healthiest to most at risk, runs: *safe* (used by all generations across many settings), *definitively endangered* (mainly older speakers, not fully transmitted), *severely endangered* (only a few adult speakers, no longer a child's native language), *critically endangered* (only grandparents and older), *extinct* (no one remembers it). UNESCO also assesses nine factors per language: intergenerational transmission, absolute number of speakers, proportion of speakers in the population, use across domains, response to new media, materials for literacy, official status and policy, community members' own attitudes, and quality of documentation.

Two influential frameworks guide the work.

Joshua Fishman's eight-stage "reversing language shift" model argues that effort must concentrate on the earliest stages first and consolidate before advancing: (1) adults learn as apprentices when only isolated elderly speakers remain, (2) build a socially integrated group of active speakers, (3) encourage informal daily use across ages in neighborhoods and families, (4) add literacy without depending on the state, (5) move into state schooling, (6) the workplace, (7) local government and mass media, and finally (8) higher education and national government. Campaigning for television or official use is wasteful when families are not yet speaking the language at home.

Ghil'ad Zuckermann's "Revival Linguistics" (proposed 2011) treats reclamation as the extreme case of second-language learning and seeks universal constraints across revival efforts, noting that basic vocabulary and verb conjugations are easier to resurrect than sounds or word order. A live disagreement runs through the field: traditionalists warn that heavy borrowing from the majority language can produce a new creole-like variety, such as the proposed "Neo-Hawaiian" or "Urban Irish," while compromisers argue that rigid purism and insistence on historic forms can itself hasten extinction.

David Crystal, in *Language Death*, lists conditions that raise the odds of success: increased prestige, wealth, and legitimate power for speakers; strong presence in schools; a written form; and access to electronic technology. Sarah Thomason highlights that the two most cited success stories, modern Hebrew and Māori in New Zealand, both relied on full immersion of children, Hebrew in early kibbutzim and Māori in *kōhanga reo* ("language nests").

Only one case meets the strictest test. Modern Hebrew is the only documented example of a language with no native speakers being revived into the everyday first language of millions, becoming Israel's national language. Sanskrit has symbolic revivalists and "Sanskrit villages," but experts consider census reports a matter of prestige rather than real first-language transmission: figures swung from 6,106 self-reported speakers in 1981 to 49,736 in 1991, 14,135 in 2001, and 24,821 in 2011.

Other notable efforts. Māori in New Zealand recovered after the 1970s through *kōhanga reo*, the Māori Language Commission (1987), Māori-language schooling, and Māori-language media. Basque, suppressed under Franco, revived after 1968 through a unified Standard Basque, schooling, Basque-language media, and literacy campaigns. Irish remains dominated by English outside Gaeltacht areas, but urban immersion schools called Gaelscoileanna have built a young, educated network of urban speakers. Hawaiian immersion schools, Navajo and Hawaiian courses on Duolingo added in 2018, and language-nest programs reflect broader strategies. Manx lost its last native speaker in 1974 and was declared extinct by UNESCO in 2009, yet is now taught in schools and has roughly 1,800 second-language speakers. Livonian died in 2013 with its last native speaker, Grizelda Kristiņa, leaving20 to 40 people who speak it at B1 level or above as a second language. Ainu in Japan was recognized by law on 19 April 2019; a 2006 Hokkaido survey found only 4.6% of Ainu could speak it even a little, and efforts include radio programs since 2001, the National Ainu Museum opened 12 July 2020, and an AI conversation partner called AI Pirika. Old Prussian, extinct for centuries, is being reconstructed from Luther's catechisms and place names, and several dozen people, including a few natively bilingual children, use it across Lithuania, Kaliningrad, and Poland.

Studies of Indigenous Australian communities, including the Barngarla in South Australia, link language reclamation to improved mental health, stronger cultural identity, and reduced intergenerational trauma stemming from policies like the Stolen Generations.

Two prominent critics push back. John McWhorter argues that most revival programs will not be very effective and that language loss is a side effect of formerly isolated peoples coming together. Kenan Malik calls public policies to preserve languages like Manx or Cornish irrational, arguing that a language people do not participate in "is not a language anymore," and that convergence can aid communication and the economy.
