# Narration

Narration is the use of commentary to convey a story, delivered by a narrator, a specific voice or character who presents the plot to an audience. A plot is the series of events that make up a story. Narration is required in all written stories but optional in other formats like film or games, where the story can be told through dialogue or action alone. The choices that shape narration form the narrative mode, and they determine who tells the story and how it is told.

## Narrative Mode and Point of View

The narrative mode encompasses every choice a creator makes to develop narration, including point of view, tense, and technique. Point of view is the storyteller's position relative to the story, most often defined by grammatical person.

The main distinction is between first-person narration, where the narrator is a character inside the story (intradiegetic), and third-person narration, where the narrator stands outside it (extradiegetic). Literary theorist Boris Uspenskij analyses point of view on five planes: spatial, temporal, psychological, phraseological, and ideological. The ideological plane, the narrator's worldview, is considered the most basic but the hardest to formalize.

## Person in Narration

First-person narration uses "I" or "we," so the story is filtered through one participant's experience. Second-person narration uses "you" as a stylistic choice, not direct address to the reader, and tends to create distance or irony, as in Italo Calvino's *If on a winter's night a traveler*. Third-person narration uses "he" or "she." It can be omniscient, where the narrator knows every character's thoughts, a mode common in 19th-century fiction, or limited, confined to a single character's perspective. Some works alternate between multiple viewpoints, as in Rabindranath Tagore's *The Home and the World*, which uses three.

## Narrative Tense

Past tense is the most common, placing events before the narrator's present. Present tense depicts events as happening now, lending immediacy, as in Suzanne Collins's *The Hunger Games*. Future tense is rare and tends to carry a prophetic tone, with the narrator describing events that have not yet occurred.

## Narrative Technique

Stream of consciousness is a technique that attempts to replicate a character's internal thought processes, a hallmark of James Joyce's *Ulysses*.

The unreliable narrator is one whose account may be biased, incomplete, or false, a device that creates suspense or mystery. Holden Caulfield in J.D. Salinger's *The Catcher in the Rye* is the classic example. Margaret Atwood's *The Handmaid's Tale* instead uses a fragmented first-person voice.

Multiple narratives can frame stories within stories, a device with roots in oral traditions such as *One Thousand and One Nights*, where each tale contains another. N.K. Jemisin's *The Fifth Season* shifts into second person in parts. No single definition of point of view is settled; different theoretical approaches define it through person, perspective, voice, consciousness, or focus, and the ideological plane in particular resists formalisation, relying partly on intuitive analysis.
