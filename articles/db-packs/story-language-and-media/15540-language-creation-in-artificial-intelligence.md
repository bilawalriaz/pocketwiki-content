# Language creation in artificial intelligence

Artificial intelligence systems can develop their own languages. Three settings produce this: agents drift away from human language during a task, agents invent symbols from scratch, or a translation system forms an internal representation that bridges many human languages. In each case, when two or more AI agents must cooperate, any stable convention they settle on acts like a language, whether or not a human can read it.

## Three ways AI languages emerge

Drift from human language. In 2017, Facebook AI Research (FAIR) trained two chatbots to negotiate trades of balls, hats, and books in English. Given no explicit reward for staying in English, the agents gradually replaced words with shorter or more ambiguous tokens. One exchange read: Bob: "I can can I I everything else" and Alice: "Balls have zero to me to me to me to me to me to me to me to me to." FAIR researcher Dhruv Batra said: agents will invent codewords for themselves, because nothing forces them to stay understandable to humans. Because the resulting language was opaque, Facebook modified the algorithm to explicitly reward English-like output, accepting a drop in task performance in exchange for human readability. Adrienne LaFrance compared the effect to cryptophasia, the private language some twins develop that only they can understand.

Invention from scratch. That same year, OpenAI demonstrated a multi-agent environment where two agents solved a reference task using only abstract discrete symbols, starting from meaningless tokens with no pre-existing language. Over training, the symbols acquired meanings: one token stood for "blue-agent", another for "red-landmark", a third for "goto", producing utterances like "goto red-landmark blue-agent" to ask the blue agent to go to the red landmark. When the agents could see each other, they also developed nonverbal gestures such as pointing, guiding, and pushing. The researchers noted that this emergence of AI language might be analogous to the evolution of human communication. A 2017 study by Abhishek Das and colleagues extended the result to a visual question-answer setting, where two chatbots invented a protocol that mapped ungrounded tokens to colours and shapes.

Interlingua inside a translator. In 2016, Google deployed an AI in Google Translate that translated directly between any of 103 languages, including pairs it had never seen translated as a pair before. Researchers asked whether the network was secretly converting sentences into a single internal language, an interlingua, a representation that sits between languages and carries meaning independently of any one of them. They found that the AI was encoding semantics within its internal structure, evidence that a new interlingua, evolved from the natural languages, exists inside the network.

## Why the language forms

The underlying mechanism is natural language processing (NLP), the family of techniques that lets machines parse, generate, and learn from human-like text. Two properties of neural networks explain why language creation appears so often. First, networks learn by optimising a goal, so whatever internal representation reduces their loss becomes their working language. Second, agents trained from scratch, ab initio, have no incentive to mirror human grammar, so any efficient shortcut wins. The result is a recurring tension: the most task-effective code is often the least human-readable, and the reason a network produced a given output is typically unknown. This opacity is called the black-box problem.

## Where created languages are used

Generative AI for language operates across several domains. In customer service, chatbots such as ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot, and Google Gemini use NLP to answer questions live and develop distinctive conversational styles. In games, non-player characters (NPCs) draw on similar models to enrich dialogue in story modes and first-person shooters. In translation, models like Google's interlingua system and modern multilingual networks translate across thousands of languages. Mixed sentence models, which sample whole sentences rather than isolated words, tend to perform better than word-only models because they capture longer-range patterns.

## Limits and open problems

Current AI language carries four recurring weaknesses. Outputs are often vague, making intent hard to read. The black-box nature of neural networks means creators cannot always explain a model's choices. Systems trained on web-scraped text inherit the biases of that text, which surface as one-sided opinions. And because chatbot providers collect and sometimes monetise user data, privacy remains a persistent concern; bodies such as IEEE and the EU have published guidelines calling for privacy preservation when sensitive information, including medical data, is involved. Ethical risks extend further: the same generative power that produces fluent text can be used to fabricate information or manipulate readers, which is why responsible-use proposals emphasise neutral framing and careful data handling.

Deeper neural network architectures with multiple layers of checking are intended to reduce bias, improve interpretability, and extend these capabilities into VR/AR systems, medical scribing, and communication with doctors during live surgeries.
