# Postcolonial literature

Postcolonial literature is writing by authors from formerly colonised countries that examines the effects of colonial rule and the process of decolonisation. It addresses the political and cultural independence of formerly subjugated peoples and recurring themes such as racism, national identity, language, and the persistence of colonial influence. A distinct branch of literary theory, called postcolonial theory, analyses how literature both perpetuates and resists what Edward Said termed "cultural imperialism," meaning the spread of European assumptions, values, and stereotypes as if they were universal.

## Origins and terminology

European colonisation of the Americas began in the 15th century and continued into the 19th; colonisation of Africa and Asia peaked in the 19th century. By 1900, most non-European regions were under European rule. After the Second World War, anti-colonial movements brought decolonisation across Africa, Asia, and the Americas. The "post-" prefix remains contested: "post-colonial" (hyphenated) suggests a clear historical break after formal colonial rule, while "postcolonial" (no hyphen) emphasises the continuing influence of colonialism across periods and regions.

Before "postcolonial literature" gained currency, scholars used "commonwealth literature" for writing in English from former British colonies. Post-structuralist critics rejected this label for separating non-British writing from British literature and for offering too narrow a view of colonial legacies. Pramod Nayar defines postcolonial literature as writing that "negotiates with, contests, and subverts Euro-American ideologies and representations."

## Critical approaches

Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978) examined how European writers such as Balzac, Baudelaire, and Conrad shaped and were shaped by fantasies of European racial superiority, founding colonial discourse analysis: the study of how language and representation sustain colonial power. Homi K. Bhabha (born 1949) introduced influential concepts including hybridity (cultural mixing that produces new identities), third-space (a location between cultures where new meanings emerge), mimicry (the colonised subject's partial imitation of the coloniser), and ambivalence (the simultaneous attraction and resistance in colonial relations). Western canonical works, including Shakespeare's *The Tempest*, Brontë's *Jane Eyre*, and Conrad's *Heart of Darkness*, have been targets of this analysis. Later critics focus on texts that "write back" to the colonial centre, responding to and reframing metropolitan literature from the perspective of the colonised.

## Nationalism and imagined communities

Anti-colonial movements used language and literature to build a shared national identity. Benedict Anderson's concept of the "imagined community" describes the nation as a socially constructed community whose members feel connection despite never meeting most of their fellow members, with the printing press enabling this identification across distance. Postcolonial literature both consolidated national identity and critiqued the European pedigree of nationalism. Salman Rushdie's novels show how the supposedly homogeneous nation was built by silencing marginalised voices.

Negritude, developed by francophone African intellectuals in 1930s France, asserted a common Black African identity in opposition to French colonialism. Its founders, Aimé Césaire, Léopold Sédar Senghor, and Léon Damas, inspired later movements including Pan-Africanism. Frantz Fanon (1925–1961), a Martinique-born psychiatrist and revolutionary, argued instead for a national literature aimed at liberation, analysing the psychopathology of colonisation. Marcus Garvey advanced Pan-Africanism as a global mass movement through the Universal Negro Improvement Association, inspiring the Nation of Islam and Rastafari. Paul Gilroy later argued against treating Black literature as either racially unified or nationally bounded, describing it instead as diasporic and transnational, shaped by transatlantic slavery.

The "anti-conquest narrative" recasts colonised peoples as victims rather than enemies, but risks absolving colonisers by implying native inhabitants were "doomed." Mary Louise Pratt uses the term differently: to describe how European travel writing secures the innocence of imperialists by framing survival stories as adventure rather than domination.

## Postcolonial feminism

Postcolonial feminism emerged in response to the Eurocentric focus of mainstream feminism, analysing how racism and the lasting effects of colonialism shape non-Western women's lives. Audre Lorde's essay "The Master's Tools Will Never Dismantle the Master's House" argues that Western feminism cannot dismantle patriarchy using the same tools that produced it. Pivotal texts include Chimamanda Adichie's *Half of a Yellow Sun* and Nawal El Saadawi's *The Fall of the Iman*, alongside voices such as Maryse Condé, Gayatri Spivak, and Trinh T. Minh-ha.

## Regional traditions

In the Pacific, Witi Ihimaera (New Zealand's first published Māori novelist), Albert Wendt, and Sia Figiel draw on oral traditions to explore colonial and postcolonial identities. In Australia, Indigenous writing began with David Unaipon's *Legendary Tales of the Australian Aborigines*, continued through Oodgeroo Noonuccal's *We Are Going* (1964) and Sally Morgan's *My Place* (1987), and includes Bruce Pascoe's *Dark Emu* (2014), which sold over 100,000 copies by 2019.

African postcolonial literature includes Chinua Achebe's *Things Fall Apart*, which defended using English while criticising Conrad's racism, and Wole Soyinka, the first African Nobel laureate in Literature (1986), whose plays and political activism opposed successive Nigerian governments. West Indian literature, shaped by BBC Radio's *Caribbean Voices* and local magazines, often blends standard English with creole, termed "nation language" by poet Kamau Brathwaite. Derek Walcott won the Nobel Prize in 1992; V. S. Naipaul also won the Nobel. Jean Rhys's *Wide Sargasso Sea* (1966), a prequel to Brontë's *Jane Eyre*, remains influential.

In the Middle East, Naguib Mahfouz drew on witnessing the 1919 Egyptian Revolution; Edward Said's *Orientalism* reshaped the field. In India and Pakistan, the debate over Indian Writing in English versus vernacular literature remains active, with Rushdie, Amit Chaudhuri, and Amitav Ghosh holding divergent positions. Italian postcolonial literature, emerging from the 1990s, addresses Italy's colonial history in Eritrea, Somalia, and Ethiopia through authors such as Ribka Sibhatu and Igiaba Scego, challenging the erasure of colonial memory from national identity.

South Africa presents a particular complication: with 11 official languages and deep cultural fragmentation inherited from colonial borders, the idea of a unified "South African literature" is contested, with scholars debating whether African-language literatures, Afrikaans literature, and English-language writing can be treated as a single national tradition.
