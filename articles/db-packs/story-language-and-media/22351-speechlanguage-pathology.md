# Speech–language pathology

Speech–language pathology is the healthcare discipline that evaluates, treats, and prevents communication and swallowing disorders across the lifespan. A practitioner is called a speech–language pathologist (SLP), or speech and language therapist (SLT) in the UK. It is an allied health profession regulated by bodies including the American Speech–Language–Hearing Association (ASHA) in the US, the Royal College of Speech and Language Therapists (RCSLT) in the UK, and Speech Pathology Australia.

## Scope

The field addresses far more than articulation drills or stuttering therapy. SLPs work across speech production, language comprehension and expression, voice, cognition-communication, literacy, social communication (pragmatics), and swallowing. Each domain breaks into measurable components:

- Speech: phonation (sound production by the vocal folds), articulation (shaping sound into consonants and vowels), fluency (smooth flow), resonance, and voice quality.
- Language: phonology (sound patterns), morphology (word formation), syntax (sentence structure), semantics (meaning), and pragmatics (social use), in oral, written, graphic, and manual forms.
- Cognition: attention, memory, problem-solving, and executive function when they interfere with communication.
- Swallowing: oral, pharyngeal, and esophageal phases, including infant feeding. Esophageal evaluation is performed only to refer to physicians.
- Voice: hoarseness (dysphonia), low volume (hypophonia), rough or breathy quality. Voice therapy has strong support for Parkinson's-related voice decline.
- Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC): tools and strategies for people whose speech or language is too impaired for verbal communication.

A common misconception restricts the profession to articulation or stuttering. In practice it covers semantic word-finding problems, grammatical impairments, reading and writing difficulties rooted in phonics or comprehension, social communication breakdowns, cognitive-communication deficits after brain injury, dysphagia at any age, and voice disorders that impair social or professional function. Early language intervention reduces compensatory error patterns later in life, a finding especially important for cleft palate, where outcomes improve when surgical repair happens early.

## Causes

Communication and swallowing problems arise from stroke, brain injury, hearing loss, developmental delay, cleft palate, cerebral palsy, emotional issues, and progressive neurological disease. Causes are not confined to childhood; Parkinson's, multiple sclerosis, motor neuron disease, dementia, head and neck cancers, and traumatic brain injury generate large adult caseloads.

## Assessment

Two frames coexist. Impairment-level analysis pinpoints which cognitive or linguistic component is broken, often using a cognitive neuropsychological approach. Functional analysis asks how communication can be supported in daily life, regardless of which component is impaired. Tools based on older anatomical models of language have been shown unreliable, though they remain in use where medical teams request a diagnostic "type" and severity rating. School-based services are tied to state funding criteria, so qualification is stricter and sometimes slower than private clinics, which treat on a fee basis.

## Education and training

In the US, the standard credential is a master's degree from an ASHA-accredited program, followed by a national board exam and a Clinical Fellowship Year under supervision before full state licensure. Practitioners may obtain ASHA's Certificate of Clinical Competence (CCC) and pursue a clinical doctorate, PhD, or EdD. In the UK, SLTs are educated through RCSLT-accredited undergraduate or postgraduate programs.

## Settings and collaboration

SLPs work in hospitals, private practices, skilled nursing and long-term acute care facilities, hospice, home healthcare, and across public and private schools, colleges, and universities. Following ASHA's 2005 telepractice approval, video-delivered services are now routine and reach remote areas such as New Zealand's South Island. SLPs collaborate with audiologists, physicians, dentists, nurses, occupational therapists, rehabilitation psychologists, dietitians, educators, behaviour consultants, and families.

## Client groups

Infants and children include premature infants, those with birth injuries, feeding and swallowing difficulties, cleft palate, Down syndrome, DiGeorge syndrome, ADHD, autism spectrum disorder, developmental delay, hearing loss, craniofacial anomalies, language delay, specific language impairment, articulation disorders, childhood apraxia of speech, and paediatric traumatic brain injury.

Lifespan conditions include dyslexia, auditory processing disorder, cerebral palsy, traumatic brain injury, stuttering, dysarthria, anomia, dysgraphia, cognitive communication disorders, laryngectomy, tracheostomy, and various ENT, respiratory, and oncology needs.

Adult caseloads centre on aphasia, dysphagia, motor neuron disease, Alzheimer's, Huntington's, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's, traumatic brain injury, stroke, mental health issues, and progressive neurological conditions. Transgender-specific voice training, including feminization and masculinization, also falls within the scope.

## Research

The discipline uses experimental, empirical, hypothesis-testing methods alongside qualitative research that examines lived experience of communication disability and therapy interaction.

## Regional service realities

Children in the UK are entitled to NHS assessment, often by referral but also on direct parental request. A 2019 Children's Commissioner report found per-head spending on children's speech-therapy services ranging from £291.65 in some areas to £30.94 or less in others, leaving 193,971 English primary pupils on the special educational needs register for speech and language support in 2018.
