# Mass media

## Overview

Mass media refers to the technologies and institutions that reach large audiences through mass communication. It spans broadcast media (radio and television), digital media (the Internet and mobile phones), print media (books, magazines, newspapers), social media, streaming, advertising, and events. It carries news, advocacy, entertainment, and public service announcements, and overlaps with marketing, journalism, public relations, propaganda, and political communication. Because ownership concentrates in media conglomerates, scholars from anthropology, economics, history, law, philosophy, psychology, and sociology have studied its social effects and the risk of media capture.

## Defining Characteristics

Sociologist John Thompson identified five traits of mass communication: reliance on technical and institutional methods of production and distribution; the "commodification of symbolic forms"; separate contexts of production and reception; reach to recipients "far removed" in time and space; and "one-to-many" communication. "Mass" means products are available in principle to a large audience, not that a specific number receive them.

A widely used classification lists seven mass media in order of emergence: print (late 15th century), recordings (late 19th century), cinema (c. 1900), radio (c. 1910), television (c. 1950), the Internet (c. 1990), and mobile phones (c. 2000). Radio and television are called broadcast media; the Internet and mobile phones are digital media. Some scholars add video games as a distinct form.

## Historical Development

The first dated print book, the *Diamond Sutra*, appeared in China in 868 AD. Movable clay type followed in 1041, but European popular prints from about 1400 are probably the first printed mass medium, since low literacy and high paper costs delayed earlier mass use. Johannes Gutenberg's movable-type press, used for the Latin Bible in 1453, enabled mass book production, though books stayed too costly to qualify as a mass medium for at least a century.

Newspapers developed from c. 1612, with the first English example in 1620, but mass readership only emerged in the early 1800s, when London's high-circulation papers used the rotary steam press and railroads. Higher circulation reduced feedback, turning newspapers into a one-way medium.

The phrase "the media" began in the 1920s, and "mass media" originally meant only print. After World War II, radio, television, and video broadened the category. Duplication and electronic broadcasting drove 20th-century growth under linear replication economics: rising volumes lowered unit costs and lifted margins. Mobile phones, introduced in Japan in 1979, became mass media in 1998 when downloadable ringing tones launched in Finland. The Internet later became the most widely used mass medium, delivering information through websites and search engines.

Between 1985 and 2018, roughly 76,720 media industry deals were announced, totaling about US$5,634 billion. Three merger waves occurred in 2000, 2007, and 2015, with 2007 the most active year at about 3,808 deals. The United States dominated: 41 of the top 50 deals involved a US acquirer, and the largest was AOL Inc.'s acquisition of Time Warner for US$164,746.86 million.

## Influence and Sociological Effects

In 1997, J. R. Finnegan Jr. and K. Viswanath identified three effects. The Knowledge Gap holds that media widens knowledge differences based on content appeal, channel accessibility, and community conflict. Agenda Setting shows that media groups shape how audiences think about issues through editorial choices, with framing used by groups competing to define issues symbolically. Cultivation of Perceptions describes how prolonged exposure gradually shapes perceptions; television can act as a "homogenising agent," though effects depend on socioeconomic factors.

Three competing theories describe media's power. Limited-effects theory argues people select media based on existing beliefs, so influence is minimal. Class-dominant theory holds that media reflects a minority elite that controls it. Culturalist theory combines both, claiming audiences create their own meanings from messages. In 2012, one source reported that 90 percent of US mass media was owned by six companies: GE, News-Corp, Disney, Viacom, Time Warner, and CBS.

## Discrimination and Ethics

Through framing and agenda-setting, mass media shapes how groups are perceived; coverage may be limited or skewed, reinforcing stereotypes. Stephen Balkaran argues that historical portrayals of African Americans in crime, drug, and gang contexts have produced distorted perceptions and contributed to their treatment as second-class citizens. Negative portrayals of men have similarly shaped harmful public views.

Critics note that mass outlets often skip local stories to serve broad demographics. The label "mass" implies passive, undifferentiated audiences, tied to critiques of "mass culture" as bland and homogeneous, though interactive digital media have weakened the older read-only paradigm. Theorist Lance Bennett argues mass media no longer exists in its original form, since fragmented audiences now consume different news from different sources, and social media blurs mass and interpersonal communication.

## Key Terms

- **Mass communication**: One-to-many communication in which symbolic forms are mass-produced and distributed to large audiences.
- **Framing**: Leading the organization of public discourse about an issue.
- **Agenda setting**: The influence of media groups' selective choices on how the public thinks about issues.
- **Cultivation**: The degree to which prolonged media exposure shapes audience perceptions over time.
- **Persistence of vision**: The effect by which the eye retains a visual image briefly after its source disappears, enabling the illusion of motion in film.
