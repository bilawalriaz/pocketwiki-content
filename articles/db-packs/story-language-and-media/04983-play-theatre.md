# Play (theatre)

A play is a form of theatre built from scripted dialogue between speakers, written by a playwright for performance before a live audience. Unlike a novel, which is read in private, a play exists to be enacted in a shared space, and most of its meaning comes through spoken words, action, and staging rather than narration.

The earliest plays, in ancient Greece, already split into the two genres that still anchor the form: comedy, which provokes laughter, and tragedy, which confronts suffering. Comedy later diversified into farce (exaggerated, physical humour) and satire (humour aimed at social or political targets). Tragedy centres on a protagonist whose own flaw drives the disaster. Historical drama became a recognisable genre largely through Shakespeare, who turned English and Roman history into a sustained body of plays.

Musical theatre weaves songs, dance, and dialogue into a single narrative. Its modern shape took form in the Victorian era; by the 1920s the thirty-two-bar song had become a standard building block. The Great Depression then drew many Broadway talent to Hollywood, weakening the musical's theatrical core, and from the 1960s onward Broadway relied more on adaptations of films and novels than on original work, a trend that continued into the 1990s. In 1767 the first indigenous American musical, *The Disappointment*, premiered in Philadelphia.

The twentieth century also produced theatrical philosophies that treated the play as more than entertainment. Antonin Artaud's Theatre of Cruelty, developed in the 1940s, replaced language with bodily expression meant to make the audience feel the violence beneath ordinary life. The Theatre of the Absurd, associated with Beckett, Sartre, Ionesco, Adamov, and Genet, posed metaphysical questions by stripping away rational dialogue: characters say things that contradict what they do, forcing the audience to sit with the gap rather than resolve it.

A play's written text is the script, called a libretto for a musical. The script opens with a dramatis personae, a list naming the characters with short descriptions, and is then divided into acts (large units, like chapters) and scenes (subdivisions set in a single location, whose numbering restarts with each new act). A one-act play is called a one-acter; a short one is a playlet; a non-musical play is sometimes called a straight play. Stage directions are the italicised instructions in brackets that tell actors when to enter or exit, how to deliver a line (e.g., [Aside]), or what sound to hear offstage (e.g., [Thunder]).

Two long-running tensions remain unresolved. Restoration comedy of the seventeenth century explored sex and social manners so frankly that later critics have read it as out of step with the era's ethics, and the genre faded within a century. Some dramatists, Shaw among them, cared little whether their plays were staged or simply read, keeping alive the question of whether a play is primarily a literary text or a performed event.
