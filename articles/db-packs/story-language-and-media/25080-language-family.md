# Language family

A language family is a group of languages descended from a single common ancestor, called the **proto-language** of that family. The term is borrowed from biology: linguists draw family trees (also called *dendrograms* or *phylogenies*) analogous to phylogenetic trees in evolutionary biology, and the relationship between two languages in the same family is called *genetic* or *genealogical*. Some linguists prefer "genealogical" because "genetic" here has nothing to do with DNA.

Divergence usually happens through **geographical separation**: regional dialects of one language drift apart through regular sound changes, and after enough time speakers at the ends of the chain can no longer understand each other. The Romance family is the textbook case. Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese, Romanian, Catalan, and Romansh are all daughters of Vulgar Latin. Romance is itself a subfamily of Indo-European, which also contains Germanic, Slavic, Indic, Iranian, Celtic, and many other branches, all believed to descend from Proto-Indo-European, a language never written down but reconstructed by linguists. Indo-European has the most living speakers of any family.

A family normally has at least two members, though a **language isolate** is sometimes treated as a one-member family (Basque is the classic European example). There is no upper size limit: Austronesian holds over 1,000 languages. Estimates of how many families exist range from about 140 (Ethnologue) to 423 (Glottolog, counting 184 isolates). The five biggest by speakers, Indo-European, Sino-Tibetan, Afroasiatic, Niger-Congo, and Austronesian, account for about five-sixths of humanity.

## How genetic relationships are established

When a family's history is written down, the case is simple. Latin is attested, Ancient Norse is attested, and the daughter languages between them are attested at intermediate stages, so the Romance and North Germanic families are directly documented.

For unattested ancestors like Proto-Indo-European, linguists use the **comparative method**, a procedure formalised in the 19th century by August Schleicher. The researcher lines up words with similar sound and meaning across candidate languages (*cognates*, words inherited from the same ancestral word). Chance resemblance is ruled out by collecting large sets of matching pairs; borrowing from neighbours is ruled out by excluding contact-induced patterns. What remains is inheritance, which proves the languages share an ancestor. Sound correspondences are the strongest evidence because regular sound change leaves predictable, systematic traces that borrowing rarely reproduces so neatly.

## Structure: branches and shared innovations

A language family is **monophyletic**: every member descends from the common ancestor, and the family contains all known descendants of that ancestor (the biological analogy is a *clade*). Large families split into branches and subfamilies. Germanic is a branch of Indo-European, whose own common ancestor Proto-Germanic was a descendant of Proto-Indo-European. Subfamilies are recognised through **shared innovations**, features inherited from a more recent ancestor that were absent in the family's overall proto-language. The closer two branches sit on the tree, the more closely their languages are related.

Families can be subdivided further into *groups* and *complexes*, though linguists disagree on where to draw the lines. A top-level family is sometimes called a *phylum* or *stock*; the label *macrofamily* or *superfamily* is reserved for proposed deep groupings that mainstream historical linguistics considers unproven.

## What does not make a family

Borrowing is not ancestry. **Language contact** can make two unrelated languages look similar, which is why linguists treat shared features suspiciously. Mongolic, Tungusic, and Turkic share many traits, but most scholars now attribute those similarities to centuries of contact rather than common descent. A **sprachbund** (or *areal* group) is a region where unrelated languages converge on shared features because of contact; the Indian subcontinent is a common example.

Contact can also produce languages with no single ancestor: **pidgins** arise when two groups need a quick trade language, and **creoles** arise when a pidgin becomes a community's first language. **Mixed languages** combine elements of two parents without descending linearly from either. Sign languages form their own families (the Francosign family, with around 70 members, is the largest) and are not listed in most spoken-language classifications.

Many language families take the form of **dialect continua**, where neighbouring varieties shade into one another and only the ends of the chain are mutually unintelligible; Arabic is the textbook continuum. The Japonic family is classified as anywhere from one language (treating Ryukyuan as Japanese dialects) to roughly twenty.

One minority theory, **monogenesis**, holds that all non-creole, non-pidgin, non-sign languages descend from a single ancestor too old to reconstruct; mainstream linguistics treats this as unprovable, since contact and developmental universals offer alternative explanations for deep commonalities.

Even the oldest demonstrable family, **Afroasiatic**, is far younger than language itself. Intense contact and irregular internal change eventually erase the inherited features that let the comparative method work, so beyond a certain depth genetic relationships become unrecoverable.

## Tree model and its critics

The family tree represents languages as splitting cleanly from common ancestors, with no contact between branches after the split. The **wave model** is the main alternative: it uses *isoglosses* (boundaries between linguistic features) to group varieties that can overlap, capturing the fact that neighbouring languages keep influencing each other. *Historical glottometry* is a quantitative version of the wave approach. A third view borrows from microbial evolution, treating linguistic history more like lateral gene transfer than animal speciation, because distantly related languages exchange features across the tree.

Most well-attested languages can still be placed unambiguously in one family or another, even when that family's deeper connections remain unknown.
