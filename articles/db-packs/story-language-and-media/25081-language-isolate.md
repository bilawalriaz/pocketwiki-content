# Language isolate

A language isolate is a language with no demonstrable genealogical relationship to any other known language. Because a language family is defined as a set of languages descended from a single common ancestor, an isolate effectively forms a family of one.

The label means only that no genetic link has been *demonstrated*, not that none could exist. Most established families, including isolates themselves, are assumed to descend from a common origin too distant to reconstruct through ordinary comparative methods. Whether a language qualifies as an isolate therefore depends on how much comparative work has been done and how convincingly relationships can be ruled out.

A sharper distinction separates isolates from *unclassified languages*. The latter cannot be placed because the data is too thin, whereas an isolate must have enough material that historical-comparative work has actually failed to find relatives. Many extinct and poorly documented languages fall into the unclassified category. Some languages, including Hattic, Gutian, and Kassite, are called isolates by a minority of linguists and unclassified by others, showing that the boundary is genuinely fuzzy.

A language once labelled an isolate can lose that status. Japanese is now treated as a small family, Japonic, including the Ryukyuan languages, and Georgian heads the Kartvelian family. The Australian language Yanyuwa was moved into Pama–Nyungan. Conversely, Ket in central Siberia belongs to the Yeniseian family whose other members are extinct, so Ket is sometimes counted as an isolate in the present-day sense even though it has recorded relatives.

Languages can also be classified as isolates because all their close relatives have died out. Sumerian and Elamite are safely treated as isolates because they are well documented: if living relatives existed, they would be recognisable. Languages that went extinct without being recorded are usually called unclassified rather than isolates. Sign languages form a separate case. Some, such as Nicaraguan Sign Language and the Balinese village sign language Kata Kolok, appear to have arisen independently of any other language through linguistic polygenesis rather than descent from a shared ancestor.

Well-known isolates illustrate the global distribution: Basque in Europe, Burushaski in Pakistan and India, Ainu in Japan, Sandawe and Hadza in Africa, Haida and Zuni in North America, Kanoê and Trumai in South America, Tiwi in Australia, and Sumerian and Elamite among extinct languages. The "Papuasphere" around New Guinea holds dozens more, a number likely to shrink as documentation improves.
