# World literature

World literature is the totality of national literatures together with the movement of literary works outward, beyond their country of origin, into wider reading publics. Once the term largely meant masterpieces of Western Europe; today it covers translated works from dozens of countries and is studied as an international field.

The defining feature is circulation, not canon size. David Damrosch puts it plainly: a work enters world literature first by being read as literature, then by circulating beyond its linguistic and cultural point of origin. Works that survive the journey, and even gain meaning through translation, count as world literature.

## A nineteenth-century invention

The concept is modern. Johann Wolfgang Goethe used it in the 1820s after reading Chinese novels, Persian poetry, and Serbian verse, and after watching his own plays travel into French translation. In January 1827 he told Johann Peter Eckermann that "the epoch of world literature is at hand" and that national literature was becoming "a rather unmeaning term." Eckermann published their conversations in 1835, fixing the phrase in European thought.

Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels gave the idea an economic edge in the *Communist Manifesto* (1848). Just as material production was drawing products of distant climates into a single market, "the intellectual creations of individual nations become common property," and from many national literatures "there arises a world literature." The manifesto itself was published in four languages and distributed across Europe, becoming a working example of the cosmopolitan literary trade it described.

Others pushed the origin further back. In 1886 the Irish scholar H. M. Posnett argued that world literature first arose in ancient empires such as Rome, long before modern national literatures existed. Today the field covers both classical texts and contemporary works written for a global audience.

## How the field expanded

After the Second World War, comparative and world literature were revived in American universities, typically taught around Greek, Roman, and modern Western European canons. The end of the Cold War, economic globalization, and new immigration flows in the late 1980s and early 1990s pushed anthologies open. The *Norton Anthology of World Masterpieces*, purely Western and North American in its 1956 first edition, relaunched as the *Norton Anthology of World Literature* and by 1995 included non-Western selections. Major surveys from Longman, Bedford, and Norton now feature several hundred authors from dozens of countries.

## Competing methods

The expansion provoked theoretical argument. Damrosch, in *What Is World Literature?* (2003), keeps the focus on circulation and reception, studying individual works closely even after they cross languages. Franco Moretti, in "Conjectures on World Literature" (2000), argues the scale is now too large for close reading and proposes "distant reading," borrowing evolutionary theory and Immanuel Wallerstein's world-systems analysis to detect large patterns across publication records and national literary histories. Emily Apter's *The Translation Zone* (2006) extended that conversation.

A related approach comes from Pascale Casanova's *La République mondiale des lettres* (1999), which borrows Pierre Bourdieu's sociology of cultural production to describe a global literary space in which peripheral writers must circulate through metropolitan centers to gain recognition. Critics have pushed back. Jing Tsu shows how Macanese poets such as Yiling revel in peripheral status rather than seeking metropolitan consecration. Han Au Chua reads Yu Dafu's fiction as a case where cosmopolitan borrowings from Russian, English, and fin-de-siècle European writing complicate, rather than confirm, the assumption that translation equalizes peripheral writers. Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak warns that studying literature only in translation can erase linguistic richness and the political force a text carries at home.

Institutional infrastructure has followed the debate. World literature series now publish in China and Estonia; the Institute for World Literature held sessions at Peking University in 2011, Istanbul Bilgi University in 2012, and Harvard in 2013.
