# First language

A **first language** (also called L1, native language, native tongue, or mother tongue) is the language a person is exposed to from birth or within the **critical period**, the early childhood window in which language acquisition happens most easily. The term is widely used but hard to pin down, because birth order, identity, and proficiency do not always line up.

## Why the definition is contested

A speaker's earliest language need not be the one they end up knowing best. Order of acquisition is not the same as order of proficiency: a French-speaking couple who raise a child in France and then move to an English-speaking country produce a child who learns French first but becomes most proficient in English. The first language can also fade. Immigrant children, and people whose home language differs from the surrounding community, can partially or fully lose their first language through **language attrition**.

"Mother tongue" has a specific origin. According to Ivan Illich, Catholic monks first used it for the local language they spoke from the pulpit instead of Latin, and colonial administrations later spread the phrase. In countries such as Kenya, India, Belarus, Ukraine, and several in East and Central Asia, "mother tongue" or "native language" in everyday use means the language of one's ethnic group, even if the person never actually spoke it; Singapore applies the same rule officially. Statistics Canada instead defines mother tongue as "the first language learned at home in childhood and still understood by the individual at the time of the census."

## How "native" is defined

Scholars sort the competing definitions into six common criteria:

- **Origin**: the language learned first, where the first lasting verbal contacts were made.
- **Internal identification**: the language a speaker personally identifies with.
- **External identification**: the language others identify the speaker with.
- **Competence**: the language known best.
- **Function**: the language used most.
- **Nationhood**: the dominant language of the region.

These criteria pull apart in practice. A child raised in a minority-language home inside a majority-language society satisfies "origin" in one language and "function" in another, which is exactly why the term is considered imprecise.

## What makes a native speaker

A native speaker is generally someone born and immersed in a language while young, in a family whose adults shared that experience. They acquire the language through natural exposure rather than classroom study, which gives strong intuition for grammatical rules even when those rules cannot be articulated. No test can reliably identify a native speaker, and researchers still debate whether "native speaker" describes a real category of people or a prototype that real speakers approximate to varying degrees.

The Asian EFL Journal's article "The Native Speaker: An Achievable Model?" summarises six principles broadly accepted by language experts: the language was acquired in early childhood and is still used; the speaker has intuitive knowledge of it and produces fluent, spontaneous discourse; the speaker is communicatively competent across social contexts; the speaker identifies with, or is identified by, a language community; and the speaker does not have a foreign accent.

## Bilingualism and multilingualism

Children raised with more than one language have more than one first language and can be bilingual or multilingual. A **second language** is any language learned after the first. The strictest sense of bilingualism is balanced bilingualism, roughly equal fluency in two languages. Peal and Lambert, the first to study only balanced bilinguals, found that such children outperform monolinguals on tasks requiring mental flexibility, show greater awareness that language is conventional rather than natural, and choose word associations by meaning rather than by sound.

## Cultural weight and timing

A first language shapes personal, social, and cultural identity, and it carries the patterns of speaking and interacting that succeed in a community. Immersion research gives a practical benchmark: non-native speakers can reach functional fluency in a new language after roughly two years of immersion, but it typically takes five to seven years to match native-speaking peers in working language tasks. 21 February is International Mother Language Day, designated by UNESCO in 1999 to mark the value of mother languages worldwide.
