# Publishing

Publishing is the activity of putting written or recorded content into a public audience's hands. It spans books, newspapers, journals, magazines, websites, audio, and multimedia, in both physical and digital form. The basic arrangement is a creator hands a work to a publisher, the publisher produces and distributes copies, and revenue flows back to both sides through sales, subscriptions, or advertising. Legal publication requires the copyright holder's consent under frameworks such as the Berne Convention.

## Technical History

For most of history, the limit on publishing was the speed of copying. Movable type, in which individual letters are cast separately and reused, broke that limit. Bi Sheng developed earthenware movable type in China around 1045, and Ch'oe Yun-ŭi produced the first metal movable type in Korea between 1234 and 1250. Johannes Gutenberg's press in Europe around 1450 made mass book production economically viable. The first modern newspaper appeared in Germany in 1609; magazines followed in 1663. The first U.S. printing press opened in Massachusetts in 1638, and New York City became the country's publishing center by the mid-19th century. The World Wide Web, established in 1989, removed the need for physical production entirely. Print-on-demand, which prints a single copy after an order is placed, cut inventory costs and let small publishers stock every title at no warehousing cost.

## How Publishers Are Organized

Mainstream publishers produce large print runs, cover production and marketing costs, and distribute through established retailers. Authors sign over rights in return for a royalty (a percentage of each sale) and sometimes an advance, an upfront payment later recouped from royalties. The U.S. trade book market is dominated by the "Big Five": Penguin Random House, Hachette, HarperCollins, Simon & Schuster, and Macmillan, which as of 2022 held roughly 80% of that market. Penguin Random House itself held more than 25% global market share after the 2013 merger of Penguin (Pearson) and Random House (Bertelsmann).

Small presses, often called independent or indie, run the same model with smaller budgets, more flexible contracts, and rarely any advance. Hybrid publishers split costs and risk with the author, a model scrutinized because some vanity presses rebrand as hybrids. Vanity presses accept any manuscript and charge the author to print it, with weak quality control and minimal distribution. Self-publishing leaves the author in full control: rights, editing, design, marketing, and freelance hires all rest with the writer.

Other formats follow similar economics with different audiences. Newspaper publishing runs on advertising and subscriptions. Journal publishing relies on peer review, where independent experts vet a paper before publication. Textbook publishing increasingly bundles print with online learning platforms. Tie-in publishing extends media franchises such as Star Wars or Harry Potter into spin-off books, games, and merchandise.

## Recent Shifts

Accessible publishing formats a work in XML and outputs it as large print, dyslexia-friendly fonts, Braille, DAISY, or audiobook, so readers with disabilities can use the same title. Green publishing combines print-on-demand with just-in-time manufacturing to avoid overproduction. Online publishing now competes with print in nearly every category, and small authors use niche social-media marketing to reach readers without a traditional distributor. A 2016 survey of 34 U.S. publishers found the workforce was overwhelmingly straight, able-bodied, white, and female, with the least diversity in senior editorial roles; a 2020 follow-up by the same group found no statistically significant change.

## The Antitrust Question

In November 2020, ViacomCBS agreed to sell Simon & Schuster, the third-largest U.S. book publisher, to Penguin Random House, which would have created the world's largest publisher. On November 2, 2021, the U.S. Department of Justice sued to block the deal on antitrust grounds, arguing it would harm author compensation by reducing competition for manuscripts. On October 31, 2022, the D.C. District Court ruled for the government and imposed a permanent injunction. The case is the clearest recent example of consolidation pressure meeting legal resistance in publishing.
