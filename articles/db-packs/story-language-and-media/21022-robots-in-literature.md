# Robots in literature

The literary idea of the artificial human has three layers: ancient myths frame animated beings as instruments of divine will, nineteenth-century tales treat human attempts to create them as a presumptuous blasphemy with inevitable consequences, and twentieth-century robot stories split between warnings about runaway technology and ethical questions raised by sentient machines.

## Before the word "robot"

The earliest examples were presented as results of divine intervention rather than human invention. The Book of Ezekiel (Chapter 37) describes dry bones coming to life. The Iliad (Book xviii) shows Hephaestus forging self-navigating three-legged tables. Pygmalion's prayers animate the statue Galatea.

Later human-made examples shift the moral frame. The brooms in the sorcerer's-apprentice legend come from a tale by Lucian of Samosata in the 1st century AD. The Jewish golem is shaped from clay like Adam. Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein* belongs to the same family: tales that indict human folly at presuming to take on the role of creator.

Mechanical humans in fiction begin as puppets without autonomy or self-awareness. The life-sized singing puppet Olimpia appears in E. T. A. Hoffmann's short story "The Sandman" (1816). *The Steam Man of the Prairies* by Edward S. Ellis (1868) introduces a bipedal anthropomorphic mechanism. In both cases the device is human-controlled.

The first character to satisfy a modern humanoid-robot definition, a machine shaped like a person and capable of acting on its own, is **Tik-Tok**, the clockwork man in L. Frank Baum's children's novel *Ozma of Oz* (1907). Three clockwork movements power his thinking, movement, and speech, none of which he can wind up himself. This is roughly fifteen years before the word "robot" is coined.

## The word and its first use

The modern sense of a robot begins with Karel Čapek's play *R.U.R. (Rossum's Universal Robots)*, written in 1920, first performed in Czechoslovakia in 1921 and New York City in 1922, and published in English in 1923. Čapek's Robots are manufactured from organic materials to labor for humans, revolt, and overthrow their creators, yet the play closes optimistically: their artificial biology lets a male and female Robot fall in love, preserving the spirit of humanity.

Čapek later wrote to the *Oxford English Dictionary* crediting his brother, painter and writer Josef Čapek, as the true inventor of the word. In a 1933 article for the Czech newspaper *Lidové noviny* he explains he had originally wanted "laboři" from the Latin *labor* but found it too bookish; Josef suggested "robots". The word derives from the Czech *robota*, meaning drudgery, and means work in Slovak. In Čapek's play it is always capitalized.

## Robots as a science-fiction genre

The robot became a stock subject of science fiction, and the defining contributor is Isaac Asimov, much of whose work centres on robots and their interaction with humans. He is best known for his **Three Laws of Robotics**: (1) a robot may not harm a human being, or through inaction allow a human being to come to harm; (2) a robot must obey orders from humans except where such orders conflict with the First Law; (3) a robot must protect its own existence except where this conflicts with the First or Second Law. Asimov uses these laws to define robot behaviour and to drive the moral puzzles of his stories.
