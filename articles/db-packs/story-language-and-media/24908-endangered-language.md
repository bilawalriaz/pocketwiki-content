# Endangered language

An endangered language is one whose speakers are dying out or shifting to other languages, putting it on a path toward extinction. A language becomes extinct when no fluent speakers remain, though recordings and written material can preserve it in static form. The present pace of loss is unusually fast. Linguists estimate that 50% to 90% of the roughly 6,000–7,000 languages spoken today will be severely endangered or extinct by 2100. The 20 largest languages, each with over 50 million speakers, account for half the world's population, while most languages are spoken by fewer than 10,000 people. More than half of all endangered languages are concentrated in eight countries: India, Brazil, Mexico, Australia, Indonesia, Nigeria, Papua New Guinea, and Cameroon.

## How endangerment progresses

Language loss unfolds in stages. A language is potentially endangered when external pressure is strong but communities still pass it to children. It becomes endangered when only a few speakers remain and children largely stop learning it. From there, a language is seriously endangered (unlikely to survive another generation), then moribund, and finally extinct. UNESCO formalises this on a scale from "vulnerable" (not spoken by children outside the home) through "definitely endangered" and "severely endangered" to "critically endangered" (youngest speakers are grandparents or older, often semi-speakers, meaning people with only partial command of the language). Its Atlas of the World's Languages in Danger lists 2,473 endangered languages across these categories.

Speaker count alone does not determine vitality. UNESCO's nine factors include intergenerational transmission, speaker proportions, domains of use, materials and education, government policy, community attitudes, and documentation quality. A language with tens of thousands of speakers in Indonesia can be endangered if children shift to Indonesian, while a language with only 500 speakers can be considered alive if all children in the community grow up speaking it.

## Why languages shift

The Cambridge Handbook of Endangered Languages groups the causes into four types. First, physical threats to speaker populations: war, genocide, natural disaster, famine, and disease. Examples include Tasmanian languages destroyed by colonial violence, Miskito and Mayan languages damaged by Central American civil war, and Andaman Islands languages devastated by the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami.

Second, marginalisation, the most common cause. Cultural, political, or economic pressure pushes speakers toward a more prestigious language tied to jobs and social mobility. Cases include Welsh, Scottish Gaelic, Irish, Sardinian, Ryukyuan languages, Ainu, and Chamorro. Third, political repression, where nation-states restrict minority languages in schools, media, and public life, or forcibly resettle communities. Breton, Occitan, Alsatian, Kurdish, many Native American languages, and Australian languages have faced this. Fourth, structural pressures: urbanisation, which forces rural speakers to adopt a city's lingua franca, and intermarriage, which pushes couples toward the dominant language. These factors often act together, and poverty or disaster hits minority communities hardest, accelerating dispersal and decline.

## What is lost

Endangered languages carry knowledge that does not survive translation: songs, myths, poetry, local remedies, ecological and geological expertise, and social patterns embedded in speech. When the language goes, identity and social cohesion can erode, a pattern sometimes called anomie (a breakdown of social norms), and political status tied to language can disappear. Indigenous language revitalisation correlates with better health outcomes in those communities.

During the shift itself, the dying language typically simplifies, shedding grammatical or phonological features absent from the dominant language it is converging toward. Sign languages face the same risks: small communities like Alipur Village Sign Language in India, Adamorobe Sign Language in Ghana, Ban Khor Sign Language in Thailand, and Plains Indian Sign Language in North America can collapse when contact with a larger sign language or dispersal of the deaf community changes the environment.

## The debate and the response

A small minority of linguists, including Peter Ladefoged and Salikoko Mufwene, view language death as a natural outcome of communities choosing to adopt new languages. The majority treat it as an ethical problem, since most communities would keep their languages given a real choice, and a scientific one, because losing most of linguistic diversity would leave future linguists with a narrow picture of what human language can be. Some scholars compare linguistic diversity to biological diversity and frame language loss as cultural extinction.

Three practical responses exist. Documentation records grammar, vocabulary, and oral traditions in archives for future study. Revitalisation tries to expand the active speaker base through education and community programmes. Maintenance protects a still-viable language from external pressure. UNESCO promotes minority languages in education and culture, and a "post-vernacular maintenance" preserves selected words and concepts of a lost language as cultural memory when full revival is no longer realistic.
