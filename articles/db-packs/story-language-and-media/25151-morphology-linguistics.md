# Morphology (linguistics)

Morphology is the branch of linguistics that studies how words are built and how they relate to one another inside a language. Most approaches analyse word structure in terms of **morphemes**, the smallest units carrying meaning or grammatical function. A morpheme may be a **root** that stands alone as a word (English *catch*) or an **affix** that only appears attached to one (*‑ing*, producing *catching*). Morphology also covers how words are inflected to mark grammatical categories such as number, tense, and aspect, and how speakers coin new forms, a property called **productivity**.

Morphology sits between two neighbouring fields. It operates at a larger scale than **phonology**, which classifies the speech sounds that distinguish one morpheme from another, and at a smaller scale than **syntax**, which builds phrases and sentences from words. A separate field, morphological typology, sorts languages by the kinds of morphological features they exhibit.

## Core building blocks

Because "word" has no precise cross-linguistic definition, morphologists work with two related terms. A **lexeme** is the abstract lexical item (think of *eat*); a **word-form** is an inflected realisation of it (*eat, eats, eaten, ate*). The lexeme *eat* excludes *eater*, which is a separate lexeme because it denotes a different entity.

Morphemes split into two functional kinds. **Free morphemes** (roots) can occur alone; **bound morphemes** (affixes) cannot, and include prefixes and suffixes.

## Inflection and word formation

Morphological rules divide into two types. **Inflection** produces different word-forms of the same lexeme without changing its grammatical category: *dog* → *dogs* signals plural; *go* → *goes* agrees with a third-person singular subject. **Word formation** yields new lexemes, and the output can shift category: *depend* (verb) → *dependent* (adjective) → *independently* (adverb) through the prefix *in-* and suffixes *-ent* and *-ly*; *dish* + *wash* + *-er* → *dishwasher* (noun). Compounding (*dishwasher*) and derivation (*dependent*) are the two main kinds of word formation.

The boundary is fuzzy, but a diagnostic exists for English. Inflectional distinctions feed syntax: choosing *dog* versus *dogs* selects a verb form (*chase* versus *chases*), so the plural *-s* is inflectional. Choosing *dependent* versus *independent*, or *dog* versus *dog catcher*, changes nothing about surrounding syntax, so those are word-formation outputs. The study of this interface is **morphosyntax**, which covers agreement and government alongside inflection. A **paradigm** is the complete set of word-forms associated with a lexeme, such as a verb conjugation or a noun declension, organised by inflectional categories that syntax actually cares about, including person, number, gender, case, tense, aspect, and mood.

## Allomorphy and phonology

Meaning rarely maps one-to-one onto a single form. English pluralises *dog/dogs, cat/cats, dish/dishes, ox/oxen, goose/geese*, and leaves *sheep/sheep* unchanged. Different surface shapes of what is functionally the same morpheme are **allomorphs**. Some allomorphy is phonological: appending *-s* to *dish* would yield the illegal *[dɪʃs]*, so English inserts a vowel and produces *[dɪʃɪz]*; the *-s* of *dogs* and *cats* is pronounced differently because the final sound of the root is voiced versus voiceless. Morphological rules therefore interact with phonological rules rather than operating blindly.

## Models of morphology

Three principal approaches try to capture these facts.

**Morpheme-based morphology** treats word-forms as concatenations of morphemes strung together like beads on a string ("item-and-arrangement"). *Independently* = *in- + depend + -ent + -ly*. This view fits **agglutinative** languages such as Turkish, where each morpheme carries one clear piece of meaning.

**Lexeme-based morphology** uses an "item-and-process" view: rules apply to a stem to produce a new word-form or derived stem. Inflectional rules output word-forms; derivational rules output new stems.

**Word-based morphology** takes a word-and-paradigm view, stating generalisations across the cells of a paradigm rather than rules that build forms piece by piece. This handles **fusional** (inflectional) languages such as Latin, Greek, Russian, and Pashto, where a single ending fuses person, number, tense, and other categories. **Isolating** languages such as Chinese rely on word order rather than inflection, and **polysynthetic** languages pack much of a sentence into a single complex word. Many real languages mix types.

## A worked example: Pingelapese

Pingelapese shows morphology layering meaning onto roots. The suffix *-kin* attaches to verbs to mean "with" or "at": *ius* ("to use") becomes *ius-kin* ("to use with"). The verbal prefix *sa-* negates: *pwung* ("to be correct") becomes *sa-pwung* ("to be incorrect"). Directional suffixes such as *-da* ("up") and *-di* ("down") attach to motion verbs (*alu* → *aluh-da*, *aluh-di*) and, with figurative readings, to non-motion verbs.

## History and lexical morphology

Morphological analysis is ancient. The Indian grammarian Pāṇini formulated rules of Sanskrit morphology in the *Aṣṭādhyāyī* using a constituency grammar. The term "morphology" itself entered linguistics in 1859 when August Schleicher borrowed it from the natural sciences.

**Lexical morphology** focuses on the lexicon, the inventory of lexemes, and so centres on word formation: derivation and compounding. A persistent complication is the **clitic**, a hybrid unit that behaves grammatically like an independent word but prosodically clings to its neighbour, blurring the line between word and affix across many of the world's languages.
