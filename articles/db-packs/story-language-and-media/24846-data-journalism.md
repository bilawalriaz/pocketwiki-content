# Data journalism

Data journalism, also called data-driven journalism (DDJ), is news reporting built by filtering and analysing large datasets to create or strengthen a story. It blends journalism with data visualization, computer science, and statistics, reflecting how numerical information now drives much public communication in the digital era.

## How practitioners define the workflow

Several definitions describe the same underlying process at different levels of detail. Mirko Lorenz frames it as digging into data (scraping, cleansing, structuring), filtering it, visualising it, and making a story. Paul Bradshaw describes finding data (often with database or scripting tools such as MySQL or Python), interrogating it (which requires statistical literacy and familiarity with domain jargon), then visualising and combining it with open-source tools. Henk van Ess argues data journalism can use any data that must be processed with tools before a story becomes possible, even without visualisation. Veglis and Bratsas define it as extracting useful information from data, writing from it, and embedding interactive visuals so readers grasp significance or find data relevant to themselves. Antonopoulos and Karyotakis add that the practice can reveal hidden issues not previously treated as priorities in news coverage.

## Roots in computer-assisted reporting

Data journalism extends computer-assisted reporting (CAR), a label used mainly in the United States since the 1960s, and Philip Meyer's 1972 concept of "precision journalism," which urged reporters to apply social-science methods. Data-driven journalism widened that idea by relying on freely available open data and open-source tools, fed by open-access publishing, public records requests, and leaks.

A 1952 CBS attempt to use a mainframe to predict a presidential election is one of the earliest examples of computing in journalism. Philip Meyer, working at the Detroit Free Press, used a mainframe in 1967 to analyse riots spreading through the city, then collaborated with Donald Barlett and James Steele on conviction-sentencing patterns. In 1989, Bill Dedman of The Atlanta Journal-Constitution won a Pulitzer Prize for "The Color of Money," a series that used CAR techniques to analyse racial discrimination by mortgage lenders in middle-income Black neighbourhoods. The National Institute for Computer Assisted Reporting (NICAR), formed at the Missouri School of Journalism with Investigative Reporters and Editors (IRE), held the first CAR conference in 1990; NICAR conferences remain the largest annual gathering of data journalists.

The term "data journalism" is sometimes attributed to political commentator Ben Wattenberg, who layered statistics with narrative in the mid-1960s, though the origin is disputed. The Guardian launched its Datablog in March 2009, the first recorded data desk at a major news organisation, and the term entered wider use after WikiLeaks released the Afghan War documents in July 2010. Data teams now operate at Reuters, ProPublica, La Nación in Argentina, The Guardian, and Berliner Morgenpost. Investigative data journalism combines the practice with investigative reporting, sometimes using big-data analytics on large textual or financial datasets. The annual Data Journalism Awards recognise the field, and Pulitzer Prizes in 2017 (Public Service) and 2018 (International Reporting) went to data-driven work.

## The core process

Bradshaw's 2011 "Inverted Pyramid of Data Journalism" organises the workflow into six phases.

| Phase | What happens |
|---|---|
| Find | Locate data on the web via open portals (data.gov, data.gov.uk, World Bank Data API), Freedom of Information requests, web scraping, OCR on PDFs, or crowdsourcing |
| Clean | Filter, restructure, and reformat data for analysis using tools such as OpenRefine, Data Wrangler, or Google Spreadsheets |
| Visualise | Display patterns as static or animated charts, maps, or graphs using Tableau Public, Many Eyes, RGraph, or HTML5 canvas libraries |
| Publish | Embed visuals and data into articles, dossiers, or microsites |
| Distribute | Make data accessible across web, tablet, and mobile, ideally linking the underlying dataset so others can investigate |
| Measure | Track how stories and datasets are used; the lightweight open-source tracker PixelPing, built by ProPublica and DocumentCloud, measures usage without collecting personal data |

Because datasets often have omissions, errors, or restrictive formats such as PDFs, journalists must scrutinise data quality, and refusals to release data can themselves become stories.

## Forms of data stories

Several taxonomies classify the outputs. Simon Rogers groups projects into five types: just the facts, data-based news stories, local data telling stories, analysis and background, and deep-dive investigations. Veglis and Bratsas offer a hierarchy distinguishing articles with just numbers, tables, or visualisations (interactive or not), with interactive visualisations further split into transmissional, consultational, and conversational.

## Landmark example

The 2010 WikiLeaks Afghan War Diary release, roughly 91,000 secret military reports covering 2004 to 2010, is a defining case. The Guardian produced an interactive map of about 16,000 IED (improvised explosive device) attacks; The New York Times let readers roll over underlined text to reveal military terms; Der Spiegel built hybrid graph-and-map visuals of insurgent bombing deaths. For the 2010 Iraq War Logs, The Guardian used Google Fusion Tables to map every recorded death, a method it repeated for the 2011 England riots.
