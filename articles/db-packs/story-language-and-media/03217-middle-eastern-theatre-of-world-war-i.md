# Middle Eastern theatre of World War I

The Middle Eastern theatre of World War I (1914–1918) was a sprawling conflict fought between the Ottoman Empire, allied with Germany, and the Allied Powers across the Caucasus, Mesopotamia, Palestine, and the Dardanelles. Its outcome dissolved the Ottoman Empire and redrew the political map of the modern Middle East.

## Why the Ottomans Entered

In August 1914 the Ottoman Empire signed a secret alliance with Germany, joining the Central Powers. The Ottomans hoped to recover provinces lost to Russia in 1877–78, including Kars and Batum, and to threaten British control of Egypt and the Suez Canal. Germany wanted to cut Russia off from Caspian oil and stir unrest in British India. The Allies aimed to protect Persian and Caucasian oil, safeguard the route to India, and, in Russia's case, eventually seize Constantinople and the Straits.

When Ottoman warships shelled Russian Black Sea ports on 29 October 1914, the Allies declared war.

## The Caucasus Front

The Caucasus became the first major battleground. A disastrous Ottoman offensive at Sarikamish (December 1914 – January 1915) collapsed from cold and supply failure. Russian forces then advanced, taking Erzurum in February 1916 and Trabzon in April 1916.

The Russian advance ended with the 1917 Russian Revolution. The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (March 1918) withdrew Russia from the war and returned Kars and Batum to the Ottomans. The vacuum produced a new conflict with the First Republic of Armenia, settled by the Treaty of Batum in June 1918.

## Mesopotamia and Palestine

In Mesopotamia (modern Iraq), British Indian forces advanced up the Tigris and Euphrates, capturing Basra in November 1914. The campaign then stalled: a British force besieged at Kut surrendered in December 1916, the worst British defeat of the war. Reinforced, the British took Baghdad in March 1917 and pushed north.

The Palestine campaign opened with the failed Allied naval attack on the Dardanelles and the Gallipoli landings of April 1915, both ending in Allied withdrawal. After a long stalemate at Gaza, General Edmund Allenby's forces captured Jerusalem in December 1917. The decisive Battle of Megiddo in September 1918 shattered Ottoman resistance, leading to the fall of Damascus.

## Local Forces and Asymmetrical Warfare

Conventional armies alone did not determine the outcome. The Arab Revolt, launched in 1916 in the Hejaz under Sharif Hussein and advised by T. E. Lawrence, used guerrilla raids to sabotage Ottoman rail and supply lines. Armenian volunteer units fought alongside Russia and later formed the Armenian Corps. Assyrians, after suffering their own massacres, allied with the British and Russians. The Ottomans relied on Kurdish tribal cavalry, including the Hamidiye; some proved loyal, others revolted.

These irregular forces diverted large numbers of Ottoman troops and made the theatre a mix of modern and asymmetrical warfare.

## The Armenian Genocide

On 24 April 1915 the Ottoman government invoked the Tehcir Law, ordering the forced deportation of Armenians from war zones. The deportations, combined with mass killings, constituted the Armenian Genocide, a central atrocity of the theatre that also destroyed a potential internal ally.

## Forces and Cost

The Ottoman Army had been reformed but suffered from limited industry, poor infrastructure, and the exemption of non-Muslims from conscription, which shrank its manpower pool. It depended on German equipment and advisers. Allied forces included British, French, and Russian regulars, plus colonial troops from India, Australia, and elsewhere.

Allied military losses reached roughly one million. Ottoman military deaths are estimated between 975,000 and 1,680,701, with disease, enabled by collapsed medical services, killing more than combat. Including civilians, total Ottoman losses may have reached twenty-five percent of the population.

## End of the Fighting

The Megiddo collapse opened the way for the Armistice of Mudros, signed on 30 October 1918, which ended Ottoman participation in the war. The empire's defeat set in motion its partition under the later Treaty of Sèvres (1920), itself replaced by the Treaty of Lausanne in 1923, which fixed the borders of modern Turkey and confirmed the end of Ottoman rule across the Arab world.
