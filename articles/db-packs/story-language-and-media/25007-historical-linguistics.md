# Historical linguistics

Historical linguistics, also called diachronic linguistics, is the scientific study of how languages change over time. It seeks to describe the nature and causes of linguistic change, reconstruct ancestral languages, classify languages into families, and trace cultural and social influences on language development. The discipline rests on the uniformitarian principle: the processes observable in present-day language change are assumed to have operated in the past unless evidence shows otherwise.

Modern historical linguistics emerged in the late 18th century from philology, the study of ancient texts. Its first major project was linguistic reconstruction, using the comparative method and internal reconstruction to establish language families and unrecorded proto-languages. Work began with the Indo-European languages, then expanded to the Uralic, Austronesian, and many Native American families. Some scholars have proposed super-families, such as Nostratic linking Indo-European and Uralic, but these proposals have not won wide acceptance because chance resemblances multiply with time-depth. A practical limit of roughly 10,000 years is usually assumed for the comparative method, and dating of proto-languages remains approximate.

Ferdinand de Saussure's distinction between synchronic analysis (a language at one point in time) and diachronic analysis (a language through time) organises the discipline. Modern linguistics treats synchronic study as primary, defining diachronic linguistics as the study of successive synchronic stages. In practice, purely synchronic description is impossible before the invention of the gramophone, because writing always lags behind speech, and written records are hard to date precisely; dating often relies on inscriptions or carbon dating. Sociolinguists have also shown that synchronic states are not uniform: differences between older and younger speakers represent change in progress.

A synchronic-diachronic contrast illustrates why both views matter. English *sing, sang, sung* looks irregular synchronically, since speakers memorise the forms. Diachronically, these are remnants of Indo-European ablaut, a once-regular system of internal vowel changes, and historical linguistics generally avoids the label "irregular verb".

Comparative linguistics compares languages to establish historical relatedness, either by genetic descent from a common ancestor or by convergence through borrowing. From such comparisons, linguists construct language families and reconstruct proto-languages.

Etymology traces individual words: when they entered a language, from what source, and how form and meaning shifted. Words may be borrowed as loanwords, built from existing elements, or formed by phono-semantic matching, a hybrid of sound and meaning resemblance. By applying comparative methods, etymologists can sometimes push word roots back to ancestral languages such as Proto-Indo-European, and much modern work now targets families like Uralic and Austronesian, which lack early written records.

Dialectology studies regional language varieties, including how two local dialects diverge from a common ancestor and how immigrant groups create new varieties in settlement areas. Phonology, the study of sound systems, supplies much of the evidence for sound change, a core mechanism of divergence. Where phonetics concerns the physical production and perception of speech sounds, phonology treats sounds as functional units within a language: in English, aspirated and unaspirated *p* are allophones of one phoneme, while in Thai and Quechua the same difference distinguishes words, making them separate phonemes. Phonological principles also apply to sign languages, since they describe abstract patterning independent of modality.

Morphology (word-formation patterns) and syntax (sentence structure) are compared across related languages and searched for universal grammar rules that hold across languages and time periods.

In evolutionary terms, historical linguistics studies the Lamarckian, cultural evolution of language: traits acquired by speakers in one generation can be passed to the next, unlike biological traits. Languages are described as conservative or innovative based on how rapidly they change relative to comparable varieties.
