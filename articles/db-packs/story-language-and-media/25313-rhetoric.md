# Rhetoric

Rhetoric is the art of persuasion through language and symbolic action. It is one of the three arts of discourse in the classical *trivium* (grammar, logic, and rhetoric itself), and he defined rhetoric as "the faculty of observing in any given case the available means of persuasion," calling it a combination of logic and the ethical branch of politics. The word comes from the Greek *rhētorikós*, meaning "oratorical."

## The Three Appeals

Every persuasive act rests on three audience appeals, identified by Aristotle:

- **Logos** — appeal to reason, evidence, and logical argument
- **Pathos** — appeal to emotion and feeling
- **Ethos** — appeal to the speaker's character, credibility, and authority

The discipline is neutral about truth: rhetoric names the techniques by which any of those appeals is made, whether the cause is good or bad. This is why Plato attacked the Sophists for using rhetoric as flattery, calling it an image of politics rather than a genuine art.

## Three Genres by Occasion

Aristotle divided rhetoric into three genres by audience and time frame:

- **Deliberative** — addressed to an assembly about future policy- **Forensic** — addressed to a jury about past events (guilt or innocence)
- **Epideictic** — addressed to observers in the present, praising or blaming

## The Five Canons

The five canons, codified in Rome especially by Cicero, describe how a persuasive speech is produced:

1. **Inventio** (invention) — finding and developing the argument
2. **Dispositio** (arrangement) — organizing the argument for effect
3. **Elocutio** (style) — choosing words and figures of speech
4. **Memoria** (memory) — learning the speech
5. **Pronuntiatio** (delivery) — gestures, tone, and pace

Memory was added later to the original four. Cicero added that a good orator needed *sapientia* (wisdom) as well as *eloquentia* (eloquence), uniting moral integrity with stylistic skill.

## The Enthymeme and Contingent Reasoning

Aristotle singled out the *enthymeme* — a *syllogism* (a logical proof from general premises to a conclusion) shortened to fit probable rather than certain premises — as rhetoric's core logical unit. Because public deliberation concerns matters that admit multiple legitimate opinions, the enthymeme fits the *contingent* (uncertain, debatable) nature of civic argument. Aristotle restricted rhetoric to this domain of the probable, where reasonable people can disagree.

## Historical Currents

Rhetoric's scope has expanded and contracted. The Sophists taught these skills in 5th-century BCE Greece, where democratic assemblies made public speech the primary tool of political influence. Aristotle systematized the appeals and genres. The Romans (Cicero, Quintilian) built the educational framework, and rhetoric became central to Western and Islamic education, training orators, lawyers, and statesmen until the late 19th century.

In the medieval *trivium*, rhetoric sat alongside grammar and logic. Renaissance writers like Erasmus revived classical models. In the 16th century, Petrus Ramus split the five canons, assigning invention and arrangement to dialectic and leaving only style, memory, and delivery to rhetoric. The 20th century saw Kenneth Burke redefine rhetoric as "identification," the use of symbolic action to induce cooperation, extending persuasion beyond overt argument to subtler forms of shared meaning. Modern comparative rhetoric, developed by scholars including George Kennedy and LuMing Mao, studies persuasive traditions across cultures, such as the Indian *Nyāya Sūtras* and Chinese Confucian rhetoric, on their own terms rather than through Western categories.

Every act of public communication, including a courtroom defense, a legislative speech, a scientific paper, and an advertisement, draws on these techniques. Science itself persuades: researchers convince audiences that findings are reliable by demonstrating sound method and sufficient evidence, which is why rhetoric now examines how language constructs meaning, shapes culture, and creates knowledge through persuasion and dialogue.
