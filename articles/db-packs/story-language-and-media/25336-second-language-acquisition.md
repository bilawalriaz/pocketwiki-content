# Second-language acquisition

Second-language acquisition (SLA) is the process by which people learn any language beyond their first, including third, fourth, and later languages. SLA research investigates what learners do, distinct from language teaching, though teaching can shape acquisition. The field sits within applied linguistics but draws on cognitive science, education, psychology, and sociology. Two papers mark its modern start: Pit Corder's 1967 essay *The Significance of Learners' Errors* and Larry Selinker's 1972 article *Interlanguage*.

SLA differs from first-language acquisition in two ways. Children's brains are still developing; adults have mature minds anchored by at least one native language (the L1) that shapes how they think and speak. Adult pronunciation tends to remain non-native, an observation usually attributed to the critical period hypothesis. When progress stalls, the plateau is called *fossilization*. Learning a second language also subtly changes the first: French speakers learning English produced the /t/ sound differently from monolinguals; English speakers shifted their /p t k/ and certain vowels after starting Korean. Vivian Cook's *multi-competence* framework treats a person's languages not as separate systems but as connected systems in one mind. A 2025 study found that adult learners can attune to a new language's prosody after brief exposure, but concurrent exposure to unfamiliar orthography, the writing system, hampers this, suggesting learning conditions also shape prosodic difficulty.

The central concept is *interlanguage*: the linguistic system that develops in a learner's mind. Interlanguage is neither a defective copy of the target language nor simple transfer from the L1, but a system with its own rules, analyzable across syntax (sentence structure), phonology (sound system), lexicon (vocabulary), and pragmatics (use in context). Interlanguage obeys typological universals, principles found across the world's languages, suggesting those universals constrain its development.

Research in the 1970s found a consistent sequence in which English learners acquire inflectional morphology (grammatical endings such as tense or plural markers), largely independent of L1, age, or classroom instruction: plural *-s*, progressive *-ing*, copula *be*, auxiliary *be*, articles, irregular past tense, third-person *-s*, possessive *'s*. Researchers therefore speak of *sequences* rather than a rigid order. Universality and individuality coexist: articles, tense, and progressive aspect are especially hard for speakers of languages such as Japanese and Korean that do not mark them overtly, while third-person *-s* is less affected by L1 background.

Two theoretical ideas explain these sequences. *Learnability theory* appeals to principles such as the uniqueness principle (a preference for one-to-one form-meaning mappings) and the subset principle (starting with the narrowest grammar compatible with available data). Both help explain how learners judge grammaticality without negative evidence (corrections showing something is wrong) and how overgeneralization produces errors. Pienemann's *teachability hypothesis* holds that instruction is only effective when matched to a learner's current developmental stage; teaching what is not yet ready to be acquired tends to fail.

Acquisition is not a tidy ladder. As learners move between stages, their interlanguage shows considerable variability. Some variation is *free variation*, swapping forms interchangeably, but most is *systemic*, dependent on context. Forms may shift with linguistic context (pronoun versus noun subject), social context (formal with superiors, informal with friends), or psycholinguistic context (planned versus spontaneous speech).

*Language transfer* is the influence of already-known languages on the one being learned, from any prior language, not only the L1, and across grammar, pronunciation, vocabulary, discourse, and reading. Some researchers prefer *cross-linguistic influence* as a theory-neutral term.

Three research traditions dominate the field. **Cognitive approaches** treat SLA as a special case of general learning. The dominant *computational model* has three stages: features of input are retained in short-term memory as *intake*, some intake becomes L2 knowledge in long-term memory, and that knowledge produces output. Researchers distinguish implicit versus explicit knowledge and declarative (facts) versus procedural (skills) knowledge; some argue for a dual-mode system storing some knowledge as rules and some as fixed items. **Sociocultural approaches** reject language as purely psychological. Rod Ellis identifies three social layers: sociolinguistic setting (the language's status in society), specific social factors (age, gender, class, ethnic identity), and situational factors (the immediate interaction). Schumann's *acculturation model* links achievement to social and psychological distance from the target-language community; Gardner's *socio-educational model* ties achievement to integrative motivation; Norton's theory relates acquisition to power and identity. Vygotsky's *sociocultural theory* introduces the *zone of proximal development* (ZPD), the gap between what a learner can do alone and with a more competent partner, arguing that mediated social interaction drives internalization. **Linguistic approaches** consider language knowledge unique. Generative approaches invoke Chomsky's *universal grammar*, an innate mental blueprint. Cited evidence includes L2 users' unsourced intuitions about ungrammaticality and the *poverty of the stimulus*, the idea that children receive too little data to fully learn a grammar from input alone, though L2 learners also get negative evidence through corrections.

Individual differences in aptitude, age, motivation, personality, and strategy use affect outcomes. *Second-language attrition*, the loss of a previously acquired language through disuse, follows stages; the *regression hypothesis* holds that attrition reverses acquisition, productive skills lost before receptive ones. Teaching isolated grammar and vocabulary does not produce communicative ability. Krashen's *input hypothesis* claims acquisition requires comprehensible input, language the learner can mostly understand. Classroom research increasingly favors *task-based language teaching*, in which meaning-focused tasks also draw attention to form, and tracks development through *complexity, accuracy, and fluency (CAF)*. Corrective feedback during peer interaction, including prompts and recasts (repetitions that adjust a learner's utterance toward correct form), improves both accuracy and fluency.

No single theory is accepted as complete, and researchers now combine perspectives for a multilayered account.
