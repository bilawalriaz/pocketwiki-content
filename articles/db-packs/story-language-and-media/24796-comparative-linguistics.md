# Comparative linguistics

Comparative linguistics is a branch of historical linguistics that compares languages to establish that they descend from a common ancestor, or proto-language. From such comparisons linguists build language families, reconstruct the ancestral proto-language, and describe the changes that produced the attested daughter languages. To keep reconstructed forms visually distinct from documented ones, an asterisk is prefixed to any form not found in surviving texts (for example, \*pəter).

## What the comparative method actually does

The core technique is the comparative method: take two or more suspected relatives and align their phonological systems, morphology, syntax, and core vocabulary through cognates (words inherited from the same ancestral form, not borrowed). The guiding assumption is regularity: systematic differences between related languages should be explainable by consistent sound changes. Where enough systematic correspondences are found, an unattested proto-language can be reconstructed. Although that proto-language is hypothetical, reconstructions can be predictive.

The most famous demonstration is Ferdinand de Saussure's proposal that the Indo-European consonant system once contained laryngeals, a class of consonants not surviving in any Indo-European language known in his time. When Hittite was deciphered, it preserved exactly those consonants in the environments Saussure had predicted, confirming the hypothesis.

The method has limits. It works best on closely related languages; attempting to relate two already-reconstructed proto-languages has not generally won acceptance, and sub-family classifications often conflict between scholars. For distant relationships linguists turn to statistical tools.

## Methods beyond the comparative method

- **Internal reconstruction** uses a single language, comparing word variants whose alternations betray an earlier stage. It is more resistant to interference but can only recover changes that left morphophonological traces (form alternations tied to grammatical context).
- **Lexicostatistics**, associated with Morris Swadesh, replaces long word lists with a short list of basic vocabulary (Swadesh used 100, earlier 200, items) and derives distance measures between language pairs. It can judge relatedness but not reconstruct a proto-language.
- **Glottochronology** grew out of lexicostatistics in the 1950s and tried to date language splits with a constant-rate formula based on retention of that core vocabulary. Later variants allowed variable rates, but none proved reliable, and the approach is now rarely used.
- **Mass comparison**, developed by Joseph Greenberg, scans vocabularies for lexical similarity without establishing regular sound changes. Greenberg proposed it as a first-pass grouping tool, but the absence of regular correspondences has led most historical linguists to reject it.
- **Computerised phylogenetic methods**, in use since the mid-1990s, apply tree- and network-based statistical testing. Character-based methods resemble the comparative method; distance-based methods resemble lexicostatistics. They can estimate relatedness and rough split dates but cannot, on their own, reconstruct proto-language features. Without phonological correspondences, two words cannot be shown to be true cognates rather than look-alikes.

## Related fields that also compare languages

- **Linguistic typology** classifies languages by structural features (word order, vowel inventory) to uncover universals. Typological similarity does not imply common descent, though typological plausibility can be used to choose between competing reconstructions.
- **Contact linguistics** studies the results of language contact, especially loanwords, which are historical by nature and matter to comparative work because failing to recognise a loan can distort cognate sets.
- **Contrastive linguistics** compares present-day languages to aid second-language learning and is not historical.

## Pseudoscientific comparisons

Comparative linguistics proper insists on regular correspondences and testable hypotheses. Unsound comparisons recur across centuries: Cheikh Anta Diop in the 1960s linked Ancient Egyptian to Wolof on thin evidence; the 1930s Sun Language Theory elevated Turkish as the original tongue; British Israelists derive "British" from Hebrew *brit* "covenant" plus *ish* "man"; Marija Gimbutas tied Basque to Pictish and Etruscan to salvage an "Old European" past. Hugo Grotius, paid by Sweden, "proved" in 1625 that Mohawk descended from Scandinavian; Johannes Goropius Becanus derived Hebrew from Dutch in 1580. Joseph Scaliger's reaction to Becanus, "never did I read greater nonsense," prompted Leibniz to coin *goropism* for any far-fetched etymology.

Specialists reject these efforts for two reasons. The similarity criterion is subjective and so cannot be verified or falsified, and because every language draws from a limited inventory of speech sounds and a large vocabulary, accidental resemblances between unrelated words are statistically easy to produce.
