# Investigative journalism

Investigative journalism is a form of journalism in which a reporter or small team devotes weeks, months, or years to uncovering a single hidden problem. Targets include serious crimes, corruption, abuse of power, corporate wrongdoing, and social or racial injustice. Because the subjects usually want the matter kept secret, the work is slow, expensive, and risky. Practitioners also call it *watchdog reporting* or *accountability reporting*.

Steve Weinberg of the University of Missouri defined it as "Reporting, through one's own initiative and work product, matters of importance to readers, viewers, or listeners." British theorist Hugo de Burgh added that it differs from police, legal, or audit work because it is not legally founded, has no limit on its targets, and depends on publicity rather than legal authority.

## How an investigation is built

An investigator typically assembles a case from many sources, because no single source proves a hidden pattern on its own. Common tools include:

- **Document analysis:** lawsuits, tax records, government and regulatory reports, corporate financial filings.
- **Public records databases** and **open-source intelligence (OSINT)** tools, which are free resources anyone can use.
- **Subscription databases** such as LexisNexis.
- **Freedom of Information Act requests** to government agencies.
- **On-the-record interviews** combined, when necessary, with anonymous sources such as whistleblowers. The photocopier, available from the 1960s, made it easier for whistleblowers to pass documents.

A single story usually weaves several of these threads. Tax records might show a suspicious payment, a leaked memo explains what it paid for, and an interview confirms intent. None of the three alone would be enough.

## A short history

Investigative work in the American press runs back to colonial newspapers such as *Publick Occurrences* and Benjamin Franklin's *New England Courant*, both suppressed by British authorities. The tradition sharpened in the early 1900s through "muckrakers," reporters who exposed monopolies and unsafe products. American textbooks credit the muckraking standards promoted by *McClure's Magazine* around 1902 as "integral to the character of modern investigative journalism."

Legal and political conditions shaped the field later. The Freedom of Information Act and *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan* gave reporters stronger tools and protection against public-figure libel claims. At the same time, the growth of media conglomerates from the 1980s cut investigative budgets, and a 2002 study concluded that investigative journalism "has all but disappeared from the nation's commercial airwaves."

Two structural responses filled the gap. Worker-owned and nonprofit outlets such as *ProPublica* fund in-depth work through donations rather than advertising. International collaborations also grew: the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists (ICIJ), launched in 1997 by the Center for Public Integrity, links reporters in more than 65 countries. The ICIJ's coordinated leaks, including the Panama Papers, Paradise Papers, and Pandora Papers, exposed offshore finance and the holdings of politicians and corporations worldwide. In Berlin, the Investigative Commons center opened in 2021 and houses Forensic Architecture, Bellingcat, and the European Center for Constitutional and Human Rights.

## Cases that show the range

| Year | Reporter / Outlet | Target | Outcome |
|------|-------------------|--------|---------|
| 1887 | Nellie Bly (undercover as a patient) | New York Women's Lunatic Asylum | Grand jury investigation; increased funding for the Department of Public Charities and Corrections |
| 1892 | Ida B. Wells-Barnett (black-owned press) | Lynching as a campaign of racial oppression | White mob destroyed her press; her reporting shaped anti-lynching awareness |
| 1904 | Ida Tarbell | Standard Oil monopoly practices | Helped break up the company |
| 1905 | Upton Sinclair | Unsanitary U.S. meatpacking | Led to creation of the Food and Drug Administration |
| 1972–74 | Woodward & Bernstein (*Washington Post*) | Nixon campaign abuses | Prompted Nixon's 1974 resignation (Watergate) |
| 1988 | Bill Dedman (*Atlanta Journal-Constitution*) | Racial discrimination in mortgage lending | 1989 Pulitzer; early model of computer-assisted database journalism |
| 1990s | Brian Deer (*Sunday Times* of London) | MMR–autism study fraud | Showed the *Lancet* paper was fraudulent; *Lancet* retracted it in 2010 |
| 1996 | John M. Crewdson (*Chicago Tribune*) | Lack of defibrillators on U.S. airliners | Argued 3 lives saved per year would justify the cost; American Airlines later reported 80 lives saved after installation |
| 2002 | *Boston Globe* Spotlight team | Sexual abuse in the Archdiocese of Boston | Pulitzer Prize |
| 2020 | Hopewell Chin'ono (Zimbabwe) | US$60 million siphoned to a company linked to President Mnangagwa | Health Minister Obadiah Moyo dismissed and arrested; Chin'ono himself was arrested in an apparent attempt to silence him |

The pattern across these cases is consistent: a reporter or small team uses documents, records, and protected sources to build a case that official channels missed or suppressed, then publishes so the public, courts, or legislators can act.

## Costs, risks, and limits

Because each investigation can take a year or more and may require travel, legal review, and secure communications, the total cost often exceeds what a single advertiser-funded newsroom can absorb, which is why nonprofit and cross-border networks now do much of the heavy lifting. The personal cost can be severe: whistleblowers and journalists face legal threats, physical danger, and retaliation. Chin'ono's arrest, and the destruction of Wells-Barnett's press in 1892, show that exposing abuse of power is itself an act that draws pressure from those in power.
