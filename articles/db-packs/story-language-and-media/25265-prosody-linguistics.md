# Prosody (linguistics)

Prosody is the layer of speech that rides on top of individual vowels and consonants: the rhythm, pitch movement, loudness, and timing that spread across groups of segments rather than living inside any one of them. Linguists call these spreading features **suprasegmentals**. Because prosody is not captured by grammar, punctuation, or vocabulary choice, it is the channel through which speakers signal whether an utterance is a statement, question, or command, where the focus falls, whether irony or sarcasm is intended, and what emotional stance the speaker takes.

## The four building blocks

Speech can be described using two parallel sets of measurements. **Auditory** measures are the listener's subjective impressions; **acoustic** measures are physical properties of the sound wave. The two do not map onto each other linearly.

| Auditory impression | Acoustic correlate | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Pitch | Fundamental frequency | Hertz |
| Length | Duration | Milliseconds or seconds |
| Loudness | Sound pressure level | Decibels |
| Timbre / voice quality | Spectral shape | Distribution of energy across frequencies |

Additional variables include voice quality and pausing. These variables can be tracked as continuous contours across an utterance or as boundary events marking the edges of prosodic units.

A speaker's habitual pitch range is a personal voice trait, not prosody in the linguistic sense. Prosody is the deliberate, contrastive use of these variables to convey meaning, and it has been documented in every language studied.

## Intonation

Intonation is the pitch melody of an utterance. In English it does three jobs: divides speech into chunks, highlights particular words or syllables, and chooses pitch movements such as rises, falls, or fall-rise contours. Consider:

- "That's a cat?" — pitch rises on *cat*, marking a question.
- "Yup. That's a cat." — pitch falls on *cat*, marking confirmation.
- "A *cat*? I thought it was a mountain lion!" — a fall-rise conveys incredulity.

Speakers also shift their overall pitch range upward or downward, a change of *key* that can carry meaning, often associated with excitement or arousal.

## Stress

Stress makes one syllable stand out from its neighbours. It combines several cues: pitch prominence, increased duration, increased loudness, and in English a more peripheral (less centralized) vowel quality. The cues are unequal: pitch is strongest, length intermediate, loudness weakest at drawing the listener's ear. When pitch prominence alone carries the work, linguists often rename the result *accent* rather than stress. The role of stress varies sharply across languages, from highly significant in English word recognition to nearly absent in others.

## Rhythm, tempo, and pausing

Languages are loosely classified as stress-timed, syllable-timed, or mora-timed based on how regularly their stressed syllables, syllables, or morae recur. This **isochrony** hypothesis has not held up under measurement.

Pauses are prosodic events in their own right. They divide speech into chunks (aiding perception of phrases and constituents), mark grammatical boundaries, and reset the pitch baseline at the start of a new intonation unit. Some pauses are filled with hesitation sounds like *um* or *er*, or with expressive noises like sighs. The chunk "Know what I mean?" can collapse into something that sounds like "No-wada-MEEN?" through rapid elision across word boundaries.

## What prosody does

**Grammar.** Pauses, tempo changes, and pitch reset help listeners parse continuous speech and resolve ambiguity. "They invited Bob and Bill and Al got rejected" is ambiguous on paper; spoken aloud, an inserted pause after either *Bob* or *Bill* selects one of the two parses. The same effect has been demonstrated in Bulgarian.

**Focus.** Stress and intonation together create the **accentual function**. "I never said she stole my money" has seven distinct meanings, one for each word that can be highlighted.

**Discourse and pragmatics.** Prosody marks turn-taking cues, signals whether information is new or given, and conveys attitudes such as approval, uncertainty, or dissatisfaction.

**Emotion.** Darwin noted that even monkeys express strong feelings in different pitches, and argued that emotional prosody therefore predates language. Studies of actors reading neutral text show that suprasegmental cues are exceptionally good at conveying surprise (around 96% recognition) and segmental cues at conveying anger or happiness (above 90%). In ordinary conversation, listeners often identify emotion at only around 50%. Sarcasm is typically marked by a lowered mean fundamental frequency relative to sincere speech.

Infants benefit from exaggerated, high-pitched, strongly stressed **infant-directed speech**, which seems to help them acquire phonemes, segment words, and recognize phrasal boundaries, though it is not strictly necessary for language acquisition.

## When prosody breaks down

**Aprosodia** is an acquired or developmental impairment in producing or comprehending prosody, sometimes seen in autism. Three types are recognized:

- **Lexical prosody** — stress on specific syllables within a word, as in *CONvert* (noun) versus *conVERT* (verb), or the noun/verb stress shift in *INcrease* / *inCREASE*.
- **Phrasal prosody** — stress within phrases, as in *a hot DOG* (a warm canine) versus *a HOT dog* (a frankfurter).
- **Clausal prosody** — contrastive emphasis across a clause, as in *the HORSES were racing from the barn* versus *the horses were racing from the BARN*.

The right hemisphere dominates the perception and production of prosody. Producing it depends on right-hemisphere motor areas in the face, mouth, and throat (Brodmann areas 44 and 45); understanding it depends on the right-hemisphere perisylvian region, especially area 22. Damage to these right-hemisphere sites produces motor or sensory aprosodia, leaving patients sounding monotonous or unable to read emotion in others' voices.
