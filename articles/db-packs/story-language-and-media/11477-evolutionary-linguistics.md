# Evolutionary linguistics

Evolutionary linguistics, also called Darwinian linguistics, treats language as a product of nature and studies how the human biological capacity for language arose. Practitioners situate linguistics inside sociobiology and evolutionary psychology, alongside evolutionary anthropology, cognitive linguistics, and biolinguistics. The field is contrasted with humanistic approaches, especially structural linguistics.

A core difficulty is empirical: no archaeological traces of early human language survive, so researchers rely on computational biological modelling and clinical work with artificial languages. Although biology shapes the brain that processes language, no clear link has been established between biology and any specific linguistic structure or universal. Evidence for the field's basic claims remains thin, and some have been labelled pseudoscience.

## Two main camps

Evolutionary linguistics divides into functionalism and formalism, terms distinct from their humanistic counterparts.

Functionalism treats language as an adaptation to the human mind, often equated with Cognitive Linguistics. It holds that language is not a separate faculty but coincides with general capacities such as perception, attention, motor skills, and visual processing. The brain binds action schemes to form–meaning pairs called constructions, and construction grammar studies how these units are selected and adapted. The camp draws on Richard Dawkins's memetics, which treats linguistic units as replicators. William Croft calls these units linguemes in his theory of Utterance Selection, and current usage-based linguistics frames language as a complex adaptive system while keeping the Neo-Darwinian concepts of replication and replicator populations.

Formalism holds that linguistic structures are crystallised. August Schleicher, inspired by 19th-century crystallography, compared language types to plants, animals, and crystals. The frozen-drop image reappeared in tagmemics, and modern biolinguistics likens the X-bar tree to ferromagnetic droplets and snowflakes. Noam Chomsky proposed that syntactic structures follow from a random mutation in the human genome, an argument he extended to ethics. Steven Pinker countered in 1990 that they are instead the outcome of evolutionary adaptations.

## Historical arc

Schleicher made the language-as-organism comparison literal in 1863, proposing linguistics as a testing ground for natural selection; a review appeared in the first issue of *Nature* in 1870. Darwin endorsed the view in *The Descent of Man* (1871), claiming languages change through natural selection as words "struggle for life" and that language evolved from animal mating calls. These ideas fed into social Darwinism and later National Socialist politics and were largely banished from human sciences after World War II. The Paris Linguistic Society banned the topic of language evolution in 1866, and Ferdinand de Saussure's *Course in General Linguistics* (1916) replaced evolutionary linguistics with structuralism.

In the United States, behaviourist American structuralism gave way to Chomsky's generative framework at MIT. By the early 21st century, interdisciplinary funding favoured biological humanities, and French structuralism had declined. Generative grammar came under pressure in applied linguistics, where usage-based linguistics, derived from memetics, gained ground.

## Evidence and criticism

A hoped-for breakthrough came with the FOXP2 gene, but there is little support for calling it "the grammar gene" or linking it to the recent emergence of syntactic speech. Its relevant mutations may have occurred around 1.8 million years ago, in the time of *Homo habilis* and *Homo ergaster*, and the gene also affects vocalisation in birds, mice, and bats. The language-instinct hypothesis is disputed, and memetics is sometimes dismissed as pseudoscience.

Saussure mocked 19th-century evolutionary linguistics as treating language "a fourth natural kingdom" full of absurd reasoning. Esa Itkonen argues the Darwinian revival is hopeless because linguistic change involves intelligent problem-solving absent in biological evolution, and because innovation and acceptance share the same source, the speech community, whereas mutation and selection in biology have different sources. Mark Aronoff counters that the Schleicher era was historical linguistics' golden age and welcomes a return of Darwinian approaches.
