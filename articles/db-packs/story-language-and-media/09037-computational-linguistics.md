# Computational linguistics

Computational linguistics is the interdisciplinary field that builds computational models of natural language and applies them to linguistic questions. It draws on linguistics, computer science, artificial intelligence, mathematics, logic, philosophy, cognitive science, and psychology, and is closely related to mathematical linguistics.

The field began in the 1950s in the United States as part of artificial intelligence, with efforts to automatically translate Russian scientific journals into English. Researchers assumed that because computers handle arithmetic far faster and more accurately than humans, the lexicon, morphology, syntax, and semantics of language could be learned through explicit rules. Rule-based machine translation failed. David Hays then coined the term "computational linguistics" in the 1970s to separate the field from AI, and co-founded the Association for Computational Linguistics (ACL) and the International Committee on Computational Linguistics (ICCL). Machine translation grew into the broader field of natural language processing.

## Annotated corpora

Meticulous study of language required large annotated text collections. The Penn Treebank, one of the most widely used, contained over 4.5 million words of American English from IBM computer manuals and transcribed telephone conversations. Each word carried a part-of-speech tag and each sentence a syntactic bracketing, a tree structure marking grammatical roles. Japanese sentence corpora later revealed a log-normal distribution of sentence length, where most sentences are short and a long tail extends toward very long ones.

## Computational semantics

Computational semantics builds models of how humans produce and interpret meaning, then tests them against data from human participants. It mirrors linguistics' internal structure: lexical semantics, the study of word meaning, and frame semantics, the study of meaning tied to structured situations, each have active research communities.

A central methodology is distributional semantics, which infers word meaning from patterns of co-occurrence in text. It underpins modern word embeddings and analyses of large language models, and traces to the work of Zellig Harris. Traditional topics include meaning representations, semantic underspecification, anaphora resolution (tracking what pronouns and other references point to), presupposition projection, and quantifier scope resolution. Methods draw on formal semantics or statistical semantics. Since 1999 the ACL has hosted SIGSEM, a special interest group on computational semantics.

## Modeling language acquisition

Children acquire language largely from positive evidence, hearing examples of correct forms with no explicit correction of incorrect ones. This posed a problem for computational models in the late 1980s, before deep learning. Research later showed that languages can be learned from simple input delivered incrementally as a child's memory and attention grow, explaining the long acquisition period in infants.

Robots have been used to test linguistic theories. One affordance-based model, where an affordance is a possible action an object offers, linked actions, perceptions, and effects to spoken words, and acquired functioning word-to-meaning mappings without grammatical structure. Researchers have also combined the Price equation, a formula from evolutionary biology, with Pólya urn dynamics, a probability process, to model linguistic evolution and reconstruct the history of modern languages.

## Chomsky and computational models

Chomsky's theories shaped the field, especially in explaining how infants learn complex grammatical structures such as those described by Chomsky normal form. Researchers have built models that attempt to show how a child learns a grammar in that form, combining structural analysis with large corpora like the Penn Treebank to uncover acquisition patterns.

## Software

Common tools include spaCy, WordNet, a large lexical database of English, NooJ, Foma, Grammatical Framework, and GloVe. Adjacent fields include natural language processing, grammar induction, corpus linguistics, lexicography, machine translation, translation memory, and computational lexicology. The journal *Computational Linguistics* is a primary publication venue.
