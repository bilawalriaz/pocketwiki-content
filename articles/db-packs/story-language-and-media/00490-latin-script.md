# Latin script

The Latin script (also called Roman script) is the alphabet descended from a Greek alphabet used at Cumae in southern Italy, modified first by the Etruscans and then by the ancient Romans. It is the world's most widely adopted writing system and the basis for the International Phonetic Alphabet and the ISO basic Latin alphabet — the 26 uppercase and 26 lowercase letters (A–Z, a–z) standardized in ISO/IEC 646 and inherited from English. Around 2.6 billion people, roughly 36 percent of the world population in 2020, use it.

## How it spread

Three successive waves drove the script's geographic dominance. First, the Roman Empire carried it across the western Mediterranean while the eastern Mediterranean kept Greek. Second, during the Middle Ages, Western Christianity pulled northern European peoples into Latin literacy: Celtic (replacing Ogham), Germanic (replacing Runic), Baltic, and the Uralic languages Hungarian, Finnish, and Estonian. West and South Slavic speakers came with Roman Catholicism; East Slavic speakers took Cyrillic with Orthodox Christianity. Serbian uses both, with Cyrillic official. Third, after 1500, colonization by Spanish, Portuguese, English, French, German, and Dutch powers brought the script to the Americas, Oceania, much of Africa and Asia, and the Pacific. Portuguese missionaries devised a Latin alphabet for Vietnamese, replacing Chinese characters, and French administration completed that shift in the 19th century. In Austronesia, Arabic and Brahmic scripts gave way to Latin in the Philippines, Malaysia, and Indonesia.

## Letter evolution

The classical Latin alphabet used **I** for both vowel and consonant and **V** the same way, an ambiguity that became a problem when Germanic and Romance languages needed to distinguish them. **W** originated as a doubled **V** (VV) in 7th-century Old English to spell the /w/ sound, replacing the Runic letter wynn (Ƿ) by the 11th century. In the 16th century a rounded minuscule **u** produced capital **U** for the vowel while a pointed **v** was kept for the consonant; a word-final swash form **j** split off from **I** for the consonant, entered English in the 17th century, and was only universally ordered as a distinct letter in the 19th century.

## 20th- and 21st-century script reforms

State-driven transitions reshaped the script's map repeatedly:

- 1928: Türkiye adopted Latin for Turkish under Atatürk, replacing modified Arabic.
- 1930s: Soviet Turkic peoples (Azeri, Yakuts, Tatars, Bashkirs, Kazakh, Kyrgyz, and others) and Moldovans adopted the Unified Turkic Alphabet; all were reversed to Cyrillic in the 1940s, except Latvians, Lithuanians, Estonians, Germans, Poles, Finns, Georgians, and Armenians.
- 1930s–40s: Most Kurds replaced Arabic script with two Latin alphabets that remain widely used.
- 1957/1982: China reformed Zhuang from Sawndip (a Chinese-character-based script) first to a Latin/Cyrillic/IPA mix, then standardized to Latin alone.
- 1991: After the Soviet collapse, Azerbaijan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, and Moldova adopted Latin alphabets; Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, and Transnistria kept Cyrillic. Ethiopian groups including Kafa, Oromo, Sidama, Somali, and Wolaitta switched from Geʽez to Latin; Hadiyya and Kambaata still debate the move.
- 1999: Tatarstan passed a law for Latin co-official status; Russia's federal government overruled it in 2000.
- 2015: Kazakhstan announced a Latin alphabet for Kazakh to replace Cyrillic by 2025.
- 2019: Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami announced a unified Latin-based writing system for Inuit languages, modeled on Greenlandic.
- 2021: Ukraine approved a Latin switch for Crimean Tatar by 2025, but the 2014 Russian annexation of Crimea imposed Cyrillic there, leaving Crimean Tatars outside Crimea in Latin.

## Encoding standards

Computing and telecommunications required a non-proprietary encoding. ISO/IEC 646, finalized in the 1960s, encapsulated the Latin alphabet on US ASCII's 26 × 2 letters. ISO/IEC 10646 (Unicode Latin) kept that core and added extensions for other languages. DIN 91379 specifies a Unicode subset covering all EU and EFTA official languages, including Greek and Cyrillic, plus German minority languages, with transliteration rules built on ISO standards; it is being pushed toward a European CEN standard.

## Adapting Latin to new languages

Languages extend the 26-letter base in four ways. **Diacritics** modify a letter with a small mark such as the German umlauts ä, ö, ü or Romanian ă, â, î, ș, ț. **Ligatures** fuse two or more letters into one glyph: Æ/æ from AE, Œ/œ from OE, the ampersand & from Latin *et*, and ẞ/ß from ſs. **Multigraphs** combine letters for one sound: English *ch*, *sh*, *th*; Dutch *ij* (capitalized as IJ or Ĳ); German *sch* and Breton *c'h* as trigraphs. **New letter forms** add shapes such as Runic wynn, thorn, and eth in Old English, the African reference alphabet's Ɛ, Ɔ, Ŋ, Ɓ, Ɗ, Ƙ, the Turkish/Azerbaijani/Kazakh dotted İ/i and dotless I/ı, and Azerbaijani Ə.

Collation, the ordering of letters in a dictionary, is not uniform across Latin-script languages. Swedish treats å, ä, ö as separate letters at the end of the alphabet; German sorts ä, ö, ü with their base letters; Spanish places ñ between n and o but sorts accented vowels with their unaccented forms. Capitalization rules also differ: Old English rarely used capitals, while 18th-century English capitalized all nouns, a convention modern German still follows.

## Romanization

Non-Latin languages are routinely transliterated into Latin for international use, a process called romanization. It mattered most in early computing, when 7-bit ASCII was the only widely supported encoding; Unicode reduced that pressure, but keyboards in countries like China and Japan still frequently restrict input to romanized forms, so the habit persists.
