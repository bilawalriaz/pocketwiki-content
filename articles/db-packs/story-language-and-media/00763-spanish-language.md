# Spanish language

Spanish (also called *castellano*) is a Romance language descended from the colloquial Latin that Roman soldiers, settlers, and administrators brought to the Iberian Peninsula after 210 BC. Over more than two millennia it absorbed vocabulary from pre-Roman Iberian languages, medieval Arabic, and indigenous American tongues, while losing most of Latin's case system. Today it is the world's second-most spoken native language (about 519 million native speakers, 636 million total), the most widely spoken Romance language, and the fourth-most spoken overall after English, Mandarin, and Hindustani. Mexico has the largest national population of native speakers.

## Origins and spread

The oldest surviving texts with recognisable Spanish features come from northern Iberia in the 9th century. According to the philologist Ramón Menéndez Pidal, the language crystallised from the local Vulgar Latin spoken around Burgos and moved south to Toledo, where a written standard took shape in the 13th century. A cluster of sound shifts between the 15th and 16th centuries, known as the *reajuste de las sibilantes*, reshaped the sibilant system and produced the modern contrast between the ⟨j⟩ [x] sound and the ⟨z⟩ or soft ⟨c⟩ [θ] sound.

Colonial expansion from the 16th century carried the language to the Americas, the Spanish East Indies, and Equatorial Guinea. The 1492 *Gramática de la lengua castellana* by Elio Antonio de Nebrija was the first published grammar of a modern European language. In 1713 Madrid founded the Royal Spanish Academy (RAE) to standardise usage, and in 1991 Spain created the Instituto Cervantes, which now runs 88 centres in 45 countries. The 1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, which ended the Mexican–American War, left a large Spanish-speaking population inside the United States, where they became a minoritised community.

## Names

In Spain, *español* and *castellano* are both used; the 1978 constitution names *castellano* as the country's official language, partly to distinguish it from the co-official regional languages such as Catalan, Basque, and Galician. The RAE prefers *español* and treats the two as synonymous. *Castellano* comes from *Castilla* ("castle land"), while *español* derives from an Occitan word that itself traces to the Vulgar Latin *hispaniolus* ("of Hispania").

## Grammar and sounds

Spanish is a fusional language, so one word can carry several grammatical features at once. It is also a pro-drop (null-subject) language, meaning speakers routinely omit the subject pronoun because the verb's ending already identifies the person. Basic word order is subject–verb–object.

A typical verb has about fifty conjugated forms, built from three tenses (past, present, future), two past aspects (perfective, imperfective), four moods (indicative, subjunctive, conditional, imperative), three persons, and two numbers. Three non-finite forms (the infinitive, gerund, and past participle) round out the system. Address forms split into formal *usted* and informal *tú* (or *vos* in parts of Latin America).

The vowel system is small: five pure vowels. The consonant inventory has 17 to 19 phonemes depending on the dialect. Several sound changes separate modern Spanish from Latin. Lenition weakened intervocalic consonants, turning *vīta* into *vida*. Palatalisation of doubled consonants turned *annum* into *año*. Initial /f/ was lost in many words, leaving pairs like Latin *farina* and Spanish *harina*. A widespread merger, *yeísmo*, folds the old /ʎ/ and /ʝ/ sounds into a single one in most modern dialects.

## Dialects

Two phonological features mark the main regional splits. *Distinción*, found in central and northern Spain, keeps the sounds written ⟨z⟩ and soft ⟨c⟩ as [θ] (so *caza* "hunt" and *casa* "house" sound different). *Seseo*, dominant in southern Spain and across the Spanish-speaking Americas, merges those sounds with /s/. *Voseo*, the use of *vos* in place of *tú*, is common in much of Latin America and follows regional verb-conjugation patterns. In Hispanic America, *ustedes* does double duty as both the formal and informal second-person plural, while in Spain *vosotros* serves the informal plural. Mexican Spanish is the most widely spoken national variety. Across the Atlantic, written Spanish and written Portuguese remain largely mutually intelligible even when the spoken forms diverge.

## Writing system

Spanish uses the Latin alphabet with 27 letters, the extra one being ⟨ñ⟩. The digraphs ⟨ch⟩, ⟨ll⟩, and ⟨rr⟩ were once alphabetised as single letters; the RAE reclassified them as letter pairs in 2010. Stress is regular: it falls on the penultimate syllable unless an acute accent marks otherwise. Spanish also opens every question with an inverted mark (¿) and every exclamation with (¡), and closes them with the standard (?), (!).

## Vocabulary influences

Roughly 75% of Spanish vocabulary descends from Latin. About 8% of words come from Arabic, absorbed during the centuries of Al-Andalus, including everyday items such as *alfombra* (carpet) and *azúcar* (sugar). Contact with indigenous American languages, especially Nahuatl and Quechua, added words for unfamiliar plants, animals, and tools during the colonial period. French contributed loanwords in the 18th century, and English has been a growing source of borrowings since the 20th, particularly in technology and computing.

## Ladino

Judaeo-Spanish, or Ladino, is the variety that Sephardi Jews carried with them after their 15th-century expulsion from Spain. It preserves medieval features lost in standard Spanish and has absorbed vocabulary from Hebrew, French, Greek, and Turkish. Closely related Haketia developed in northern Morocco. Ladino is classified as endangered, though a modest revival is underway in music and cultural circles.

## Official status and digital reach

Spanish is the official language of twenty countries and one territory in the Americas, of Spain, and of Equatorial Guinea, and is one of the six official languages of the United Nations. It also holds official status in the European Union, the Organization of American States, and other international bodies. On the internet, it ranks as the third-most used language by user count (after English and Chinese) and the second-most used by number of websites (after English).
