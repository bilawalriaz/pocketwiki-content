# Novel

A novel is a long work of narrative fiction written in literary prose. Four traits carry most of the meaning: it is **made up** (fictional), it is **prose** rather than verse, it reads as an **intimate** experience for a single reader, and it is **long** enough to develop a substantial world. Length is the loosest of these. E. M. Forster proposed a 50,000-word minimum, while György Lukács argued length matters because only a long canvas can hold the totality of a life. Anything shorter is usually a novella or short story; anything older and in verse is an epic, not a novel. The boundary with non-fiction has always been porous, since ancient histories mixed in invented speeches, and modern works like *Ông cố vấn* are explicitly factual yet read like novels.

The genre has roughly two millennia of roots, and its identity shifted each time a new technology or social context rewrote what readers expected.

## Antiquity and the Middle Ages

The earliest surviving Western examples come from Greek and Roman prose: Chariton's *Callirhoe* (mid-1st century AD), Petronius's *Satyricon*, and Apuleius's *The Golden Ass* (2nd century). Parallel traditions grew elsewhere. Classical Sanskrit produced *Vasavadatta* and *Kadambari* (5th–8th centuries), and the Chinese *You Xian Ku* in the 7th century counts as an early example, with longer vernacular Chinese novels arriving during the Ming and Qing dynasties once print spread.

In medieval Europe, the dominant long-form fiction was the **chivalric romance**, a verse or prose tale of courtly love and heroic quest. Prose romances from the 13th century, like the *Romance of Flamenca*, sat closer to serious history and helped train readers to treat long prose fiction as plausible rather than mythical. A separate, shorter tradition, the **novella**, flourished in story collections such as Boccaccio's *Decameron* (1354) and Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales* (1386–1400), often satirical or moral. A **chapbook** is a small, cheap printed booklet of the kind that would later spread popular tales.

## Printing and the Early Modern Novel (1500–1700)

Johannes Gutenberg's printing press, around 1439, made long prose cheap to produce and circulate. Chapbooks spread popular tales, while the Spanish *Amadis de Gaula* became the model romance. In the 16th century, history and fiction were not yet separate categories. William Caxton marketed *Le Morte d'Arthur* (1485) as a true history even though it was largely invented.

Two strands matter most for the novel's later shape. **Pastoral and heroic romances**, such as Honoré d'Urfé's *L'Astrée* (1610), kept medieval themes alive in elegant, refined prose. **Satirical romances**, from *Lazarillo de Tormes* (1554) to Miguel de Cervantes's *Don Quixote* (1605), used a witty protagonist to mock social norms. *Don Quixote* is the landmark because it knowingly interrogates the romance form it inherits, and is often treated as the first modern novel. The romance label also let authors slip past censors by claiming fiction while hinting at real targets.

## The 18th Century: The Novel Becomes a Form

The 18th century is where critics, especially Ian Watt in *The Rise of the Novel* (1957), locate the novel as a distinct genre. Three shifts made this possible.

- **Realism as a contract with the reader.** Novels began to imitate the surface of ordinary life rather than the elevated world of romance.
- **The intimate voice.** The **epistolary novel**, stories told through letters, reached its first peak with Aphra Behn's *Love-Letters Between a Nobleman and His Sister* (1684–87) and taught writers how to render a single consciousness in prose.
- **The sentimental and the experimental.** Samuel Richardson's *Pamela* (1740) made moral feeling the engine of plot, Voltaire's *Candide* (1759) carried philosophical argument into narrative, and Laurence Sterne's *Tristram Shandy* (1759–67) bent the form into self-conscious play.

Novels were printed in small, portable formats, often issued anonymously, and circulated through lending libraries, all of which widened the middle-class readership.

## The 19th Century: Novels Take Over Literature

Industrialization, urban growth, and mass education made the novel the dominant literary form of the Victorian age. **Romanticism** revived the romance in a darker key, spawning **Gothic fiction** (Horace Walpole's *The Castle of Otranto*, 1764) and the **historical novel** (Walter Scott's *Waverley*, 1814), which braided research with imagined characters. Realist and psychological novelists such as Charles Dickens, George Eliot, Gustave Flaubert, and Fyodor Dostoevsky turned the form into a vehicle for social critique and interior life. **Science fiction** appeared with H. G. Wells and Jules Verne, channeling anxieties about machines and empire. Across these variants, the novel became a way for nations to imagine themselves.

## The 20th and 21st Centuries: Form Breaking Open

**Modernism** changed how a novel could sound. James Joyce's *Ulysses* (1922) used **stream of consciousness**, a technique that mimics the continuous flow of a character's thoughts, alongside work by Marcel Proust, Virginia Woolf, and William Faulkner. **Postmodernism** then fragmented structure and questioned authorship, in writers such as Thomas Pynchon and John Barth. Genre fiction matured commercially. J. R. R. Tolkien's *The Lord of the Rings* (1954–55) reshaped fantasy, while war, totalitarianism, and identity politics reshaped what serious novels were expected to address.

Digital media loosened the format further. **Ebooks**, web serials, audiobooks, and **graphic novels** (long comic-strip-format works) expanded how fiction is published and read, and self-publishing platforms lowered the barrier to authorship. Web fiction generates large revenues in China and South Korea, on platforms such as Joara, though print still dominates in many markets. Scholars continue to debate whether the novel began in classical antiquity, in medieval romance, or in the 18th-century realist tradition, and whether digital forms count as a continuation of the genre or a break from it.
