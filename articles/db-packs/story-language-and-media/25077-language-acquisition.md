# Language acquisition

Language acquisition is the process by which humans gain the capacity to perceive, comprehend, and produce language. It usually refers to first-language acquisition, how infants learn their native tongue, spoken or signed, and is distinguished from second-language acquisition, the later learning of additional languages. The capacity to acquire language is a defining human trait.

## What makes human language special

Human language has productivity: a finite vocabulary and a finite set of rules can generate an effectively infinite number of novel sentences. Hockett named this design feature, which animal communication systems lack. The infinity comes from recursion, the ability to nest structures inside themselves, supported by three recursive operations: relativization, complementation, and coordination. Animals trained on sign language, most famously the chimpanzee Nim Chimpsky, acquired individual signs but never recombined them into novel messages, showing the syntax gap.

## Guiding principles and early stages

Two principles guide first-language acquisition. Speech perception precedes speech production. The child's system builds up step by step, beginning with discrimination of phonemes, the smallest contrastive sound units of a language. Deaf babies babble manually around eleven months, compared to about six months for hearing babies, and prelinguistic sensitivity to the native language is present at birth. Newborns discriminate their native language from an unfamiliar one within days and respond differently to native versus non-native vowels, evidence of fetal auditory learning. They also encode the edges of multisyllabic sequences better than internal components, an early form of syntactic processing.

## The nature–nurture debate

The central question is how children acquire complex grammar so rapidly from limited, ambiguous input.

Nativists argue that input alone is insufficient and that innate, biologically specified constraints, a universal grammar that narrows the hypothesis space, guide the child. They note that children rarely receive explicit grammatical correction and often ignore it when offered, the poverty of the stimulus argument. Chomsky's review of B. F. Skinner's behaviorist account called it "largely mythology."

Empiricists and emergentists counter that general cognitive and social learning mechanisms suffice. MacWhinney's competition model treats acquisition as emerging from biological predispositions interacting with environmental input. Tomasello's usage-based theory holds that children learn constructions through exposure to language in social contexts. Statistical learning researchers show that infants around eight months use distributional cues to segment words from fluent speech, and connectionist models can simulate word and grammar learning from these cues. Social interactionists, following Vygotsky, emphasize guided interaction with adults, including the zone of proximal development, the set of linguistic tasks a child can perform with help but not alone.

## Sensitive period

From birth to about six months, infants discriminate phonetic contrasts across all human languages. After this, perception narrows to the phonemes of the ambient language. Penfield placed the close of the sensitive period near age nine; by around twelve, fluent native-like acquisition becomes much harder. Deaf children who acquire a first language late show weaker grasp of complex grammar. Severe privation after the sensitive period, as in the case of Genie, isolated until age thirteen, leaves large vocabulary but no grammatical mastery.

## Representation in the brain

Language processing concentrates in two left-hemisphere regions: Broca's area in the frontal cortex, involved in production, and Wernicke's area in the temporal cortex, involved in comprehension. Damage to either produces aphasia. Acquisition coincides with rapid brain growth and synaptic pruning, and the emergence of dedicated language areas reflects genetically guided wiring combined with experience-driven validation of synapses during extended human postnatal maturation.

## Vocabulary and meaning

Children learn on average ten to fifteen new word meanings per day, but only one can be traced to direct instruction; the rest are inferred from context. Children use heuristics such as the whole-object assumption, holding that a new label refers to the whole object rather than a part, and mutual exclusivity, treating a novel word as a category name rather than a thematically related item. A vocabulary below fifty words by twenty-four months classifies a child as a late-talker with poorer later language outcomes.

## Language diversity

Acquisition differs across languages. Otomanguean languages have complex laryngealized vowels whose acquisition requires mastering the timing of laryngeal gestures within the vowel. Ergative Mayan languages like K'iche' mark the subject of transitive verbs differently from the subject of intransitive verbs, and Mam extends ergative marking to both, which children acquire proficiently by age two regardless of how often the extended marking appears in adult speech. With roughly half the world's languages expected to disappear this century, documenting child language across typologically diverse languages is now treated as urgent for both science and language revitalization.

## Bilingual, sign, and clinical contexts

Bilingual first-language acquisition follows the same course as monolingual acquisition when both languages receive sufficient input. Sign language acquisition mirrors spoken language acquisition, with manual babbling in deaf infants and parallel milestones. Prelingual deafness affects two to three per thousand U.S. children; with appropriate signed-language input, deaf children's linguistic abilities parallel those of hearing peers. Cochlear implants stimulate the auditory nerve directly but yield variable outcomes, and a solid first language, signed or spoken, is the strongest predictor of success with a second spoken language.
