# Sign language

Sign languages are natural languages expressed through the visual-manual modality: manual articulation combined with non-manual markers such as facial expression, head, and body movements. They are not gestures, mime, or spoken languages translated into signs. Wherever deaf people have formed communities, local sign languages have developed, complete with their own lexicon and grammar.

About 150 sign languages appear in the 2021 edition of Ethnologue; the SIGN-HUB Atlas of Sign Language Structures lists over 200. Indo-Pakistani Sign Language is the most widely used worldwide. Sign languages are usually not mutually intelligible: British Sign Language (BSL) and American Sign Language (ASL) are entirely distinct, even though the UK and US share English. A single country can also have multiple sign languages, or several spoken languages may share one sign language in regional variants.

## How signs are built

Linguists describe signs using five parameters: handshape, orientation, location on or near the body, movement, and non-manual expression. These function analogously to phonemes (minimal sound contrasts that signal meaning differences) in spoken languages and were once called cheremes. Like spoken languages, sign languages exhibit duality of patterning (meaningless units combining into meaningful ones) and recursion, the property that grammatical rules can reapply to their own output, allowing subordinate clauses within subordinate clauses.

Signs are largely conventional and arbitrary rather than iconic, meaning visually resembling their referent. Iconicity is still more systematic in sign than in spoken language because the visual medium can mimic shape, movement, and spatial relations. Classifiers are handshapes representing a class of objects (vehicles, long thin things, flat surfaces) that the signer combines with movement and location to depict situations such as a thin thing falling onto a flat surface. Some signs are metaphorical, layered mappings from a concrete image to an abstract meaning, as with ASL LEARN, which mimes placing a book onto the head.

## Grammar uses space and simultaneity

Spoken language is largely linear; sign language is visual and can convey several pieces of information at once through space, facial expression, and body posture. Signers establish locations in signing space to represent discourse participants and referents, then point to or move signs between those locations to show who does what to whom. Motor constraints keep most sentences sequentially ordered; simultaneity shows up mainly inside individual signs, at the level of morphology (the internal grammatical structure of a single sign).

Non-manual markers carry grammar. Raised eyebrows with a forward head tilt mark yes/no questions in both ASL and BSL. Facial expression distinguishes lexical pairs: ASL "late" versus "not yet," which adds a tongue-to-lip contact and head shake. Adverbial content can also be facial: in ASL a slightly open mouth with the tongue visible means "carelessly," while in BSL a similar expression means "boring." Turn-taking is regulated by gaze; the addressee must watch, and the signer controls who speaks next by making or withholding eye contact.

## Origin and spread

Sign languages have arisen naturally for centuries. European contact with Indigenous peoples of the Gulf Coast (Texas and northern Mexico) in the 1500s, documented by Cabeza de Vaca in 1527 and Coronado in 1541, found a fully formed sign language already in use. In 1620 Juan Pablo Bonet published the first modern treatise of sign language phonetics in Madrid. In 1755 Charles-Michel de l'Épée founded the first free deaf school in Paris; his former student Laurent Clerc went with Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet to found the American School for the Deaf in Hartford, Connecticut, in 1817. Edward Miner Gallaudet founded what became Gallaudet University in Washington, D.C., still the only liberal arts university for deaf people in the world.

Sign languages borrow from spoken languages through fingerspelling, a handshape alphabet used mainly for proper names and new concepts, but they remain independent: ASL shares more syntactic similarity with spoken Japanese than with English. French Sign Language gave rise to ASL and many others; BANZSL (British, Australian, and New Zealand Sign Language) is a related family. Village sign languages arise over generations in insular communities with high deafness, including the extinct Martha's Vineyard Sign Language, Adamorobe Sign Language in Ghana, and Al-Sayyid Bedouin Sign Language in Israel.

## Acquisition and endangerment

Children exposed to a sign language from birth acquire it on the same timeline as hearing children acquire spoken language. McGill University research found that native ASL signers performed better on sentence-copying tasks than those who acquired ASL later, evidence for a critical period in language learning. Sign languages without writing systems, and those spoken by small communities, are vulnerable: Hawaiʻi Sign Language is nearly extinct, and even nationally recognised languages like New Zealand Sign Language are losing users.

International Sign, formerly known as Gestuno, is used at events such as the Deaflympics and World Federation of the Deaf meetings. Research treats it as more complex than a typical pidgin (a simplified contact language between speakers of different languages), approaching a full sign language. Hearing figures such as de l'Épée or Gallaudet are often misidentified as inventors of sign languages; the languages themselves were developed by deaf people, with hearing educators contributing to manual alphabets and pedagogy rather than to the languages.
