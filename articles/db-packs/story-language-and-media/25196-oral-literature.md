# Oral literature

Oral literature, also called *orature* or *folk literature*, is literature spoken or sung rather than written, though much of it has later been transcribed. A common working description is literature characterized by oral transmission and the absence of any fixed form, encompassing the stories, legends, and histories a community passes down by voice.

## Why "orature" exists

The Ugandan scholar Pio Zirimu introduced *orature* to avoid the oxymoron in "oral literature," since *literature* derives from *writing*. *Oral literature* remains the more common label in academic and popular use. Zirimu defined orature as "the use of utterance as an aesthetic means of expression," and *The Encyclopaedia of African Literature* adds that orature "comes to life only in a living community. Where community life fades away, orality loses its function and dies."

The distinction matters because written literature can survive on a page, whereas oral literature needs live performance. As Mbube Nwi-Akeeri has argued, oral literature in places such as Africa cannot be fully captured by words alone: gesture, dance, and the storyteller-audience interaction are part of the work, making oral literature a performance as much as a narrative.

## What counts as oral literature

Pre-literate societies, which by definition have no writing, often possess rich oral traditions in genres including folk epics, folk narratives (fairy tales and fables), folk drama, proverbs, and folksongs. Even when folklorists and paremiographers (scholars of proverbs) record and publish these, the result is still called "oral literature."

Literate societies retain oral literature too. Bedtime stories, urban legends, jokes, and oral poetry such as slam poetry (televised on Russell Simmons' *Def Poetry*) all qualify; performance poetry is a related genre that consciously avoids the written form.

"Persistent orality" names traditions that keep thriving through spoken or sung performance inside literate societies. Bhojpuri folk song traditions of the Indian diaspora in Mauritius and Trinidad illustrate this, surviving through weddings, fêtes, carnivals, recordings, radio, film, and YouTube rather than print. Adaptation often produces linguistic and musical creolisation, as in Chutney music, which blends Bhojpuri with English lyrics and Caribbean rhythms. Scholars describe the resulting fragments, melodies, and evocative words as "soft texts," pieces that keep cultural resonance even as original forms change.

## How the field developed

Verbal transmission of knowledge and culture is as old as language itself, preceding both painting and writing. As a modern scholarly concept, oral literature was shaped by:

- **Hector Munro Chadwick and Nora Kershaw Chadwick**, whose comparative work *The Growth of Literature* (1932–40) circulated the concept widely.
- **Albert B. Lord**, whose *The Singer of Tales* (1960) examined fluidity in ancient and later texts and the "oral-formulaic" principles bards (traditional performing poets) use while composing-in-performance, especially among contemporary South Slavic bards.
- **Ruth Finnegan** (*Oral Literature in Africa*, 1970 and 1977), alongside scholars such as Görög-Karady (1976) and Bauman (1986), the *World Oral Literature Project*, and the journal *Cahiers de Littérature Orale*. From the 1970s onward, "oral literature" became standard across literary scholarship and anthropology.

## Deaf culture

Although deaf people communicate manually rather than orally, their storytelling, jokes, and poetry, passed from person to person with no written medium, are conventionally grouped with oral literature.
