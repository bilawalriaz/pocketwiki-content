# Creole language

A creole is a stable language that develops when a simplified contact tongue, usually a pidgin, becomes the native language of a community and expands into a full system with regular grammar and a large vocabulary. A pidgin has no native speakers and unstable grammar; a creole has both. The shift from pidgin to native language, called nativization, can occur in a single generation.

About one hundred creoles have appeared since 1500, most based on English, French, Portuguese, or Spanish, the result of colonial expansion and the Atlantic slave trade that forced speakers of mutually unintelligible languages into contact. Non-European bases also exist, including Arabic, Chinese, and Malay, with creoles such as Nubi and Sango derived solely from non-European languages. Most arose in coastal regions across the equatorial belt: the Caribbean, the Guyanas, West Africa, the Indian Ocean islands, Southeast Asia, and the Pacific.

Vocabulary usually comes from the socially dominant parent language, while grammar is often original, with features that differ substantially from any single source. The terms substrate (the less dominant contributing language) and superstrate (the dominant one) come from language-replacement theory. Their application to creoles is disputed, since multiple superstrata are often involved, as in Papiamento, and the original substrates are usually unattested.

For centuries creoles were stigmatised as degenerate dialects, and many went extinct. Decolonisation and academic shifts in the late twentieth century changed this: creoles entered education, media, and official use in places like Haiti and the Seychelles, and linguists now treat them as full languages. The word creole descends from Portuguese crioulo and Spanish criollo, colonial terms for people born in the colonies, from Latin creare, to produce.

Theories of creole genesis split into four families. European-input theories include the monogenetic theory, which traces all Atlantic creoles to a relexified West African Pidgin Portuguese and is now widely rejected for lack of evidence, and the European dialect origin hypothesis, which treats French creoles as descendants of a seventeenth-century koiné French spoken in Paris and the Atlantic harbours. Substrate theories attribute shared creole features to transfer from African substrate languages through relexification, though critics call this the cafeteria principle, the unfalsifiable practice of assigning features to whichever substrate seems convenient. Gradualist models see creoles as the end point of ordinary linguistic change when a pidgin stabilises over generations. Universalist approaches, especially Derek Bickerton's language bioprogram, argue that children exposed to unstructured pidgin input use innate grammatical capacities to build a creole, which would explain apparent similarities across creoles. No single theory has won general acceptance, and the parallel debate continues over whether creoles are structurally exceptional or merely sociohistorical products of displacement and slavery.

John McWhorter has proposed a creole prototype defined by three absences: little inflection for case, gender, or agreement; little functional tone; and little semantically opaque word formation, such as words whose meaning cannot be read from their parts, like understand. He calls Haitian Creole, which shows all three, the most creole of creoles. Critics counter that non-creole languages such as Manding, Soninke, Magoua French, and Riau Indonesian also lack these features, while Berbice Dutch Creole and Papiamentu show inflection and tone respectively. Opponents including Michel DeGraff, Salikoko Mufwene, and Henri Wittmann argue that creole is a sociohistorical concept rather than a linguistic one, and that no agreed yardstick for creoleness has emerged.

A practical consequence is that standardisation, official status, and educational use have grown most where creole-speaking populations achieved political autonomy, as in Haiti and the Seychelles.
