# Narrative

A narrative is an account of connected events, presented through words, images, or both, that turns a sequence of things happening into a story someone can follow. Unlike a bare description, a narrative needs agents who act, temporal progression, and a teller (explicit in writing, often invisible in film or music). The word comes from Latin *narrare* ("to tell"), rooted in *gnarus* ("knowing, skilled"), and entered English from French in the 15th century. Narratives cover both nonfiction (biography, journalism, history) and fiction (myths, novels, films), the latter departing from fact through imagined or supernatural elements.

What separates narrative from a list of events is structure. Six elements recur across literary theory, often called the elements of fiction: character (agents whose choices drive events, from named protagonists to ensemble casts; archetypes like hero and villain give audiences a foothold; consistent characterisation governs empathy); conflict (tension demanding resolution, classified as character against character, nature, society, fate, or self; internal struggle usually shadows external); narrative mode (framing choices of perspective, scope, language, and medium); plot (causal sequence assembled through *emplotment* so one thing leads to another, producing suspense as conflict is confronted and resolved); setting (time, place, and social context, which can act as an agent in its own right); theme (abstract underlying idea, open to interpretation and often diverging from authorial intent). A common scaffold, Tzvetan Todorov's, reads plot as a cycle: equilibrium is disrupted, then restored. Anatoly Propp's analysis of Russian folk tales found 31 recurring functional components, suggesting stories draw on a finite toolkit of moves.

Storytelling predates writing. The oldest archaeological evidence comes from Lothal in the Indus Valley (c. 2600–1900 BCE): one large vessel shows birds with fish in their beaks perched in a tree above a fox-like animal, paralleling "The Fox and the Crow" from the later *Panchatantra*; a miniature jar depicts a thirsty crow dropping stones into a narrow-mouthed vessel so a deer can drink. Written narratives then emerge in Egyptian, Greek, Chinese, and Indian civilisations, fixing mythologies and histories that oral tradition had carried for generations.

Janet Bacon's 1921 categorisation sorts traditional stories by intent and source. Myth draws on reason and claims sacred truth, explaining natural or ritual phenomena; its modern analogue is science. Legend draws on memory, claims historical truth rooted in real people and places (Agamemnon, King Arthur, Davy Crockett); its analogue is history. Folktale draws on imagination, makes no factual claim, and exists for entertainment; its analogue is romance. Georges Dumézil's trifunctionalism adds a structural layer: Indo-European myths (Norse, Greek, Roman, Indian) repeatedly map society onto three functions: sovereignty (kings, cosmic order; Odin, Tyr), heroism (warriors, physical force; Thor, Valhalla), and fertility or prosperity (commoners, crops, pleasure; Freyr). The same three-tier shape, Dumézil argued, prefigures the Christian Trinity.

Humans impose story structure on experience, which is both a strength and a hazard. Owen Flanagan observes that humans in all cultures cast their identity in some narrative form, calling them "inveterate storytellers." Identity, memory, and meaning-making lean on narrative. In psychology, the breakdown of personal narrative is linked to psychosis, and its restoration through narrative therapy to recovery. Illness narratives follow three archetypal patterns: restitution (temporary detour ending in cure), chaos (permanent decline, as in advanced Alzheimer's), and quest (suffering reframed as transformation, as in some cancer survivorship stories). Self-narrative language even correlates with Big Five personality traits: extraversion tracks social words, neuroticism tracks negative-emotion words.

The cognitive grip of story has a downside. The *narrative fallacy*, the tendency to prefer a coherent story over messy data, produces unwarranted causal claims and can obscure statistical reality. In historiography, Lawrence Stone (1979) defined historical narrative as chronological, single-stranded, descriptive, people-focused, and particular. Mark Bevir argued that narratives explain action through actors' beliefs and desires, an alternative to natural-scientific explanation, while social-science historians criticise narrative as anecdotal. Where statistical cases are too few, Peter Abell's Bayesian narratives model action as directed graphs of states, actors, and actions, computing likelihood ratios for causal links from subjective evidence. In the social sciences, Gubrium and Holstein distinguished "big stories" (institutional discourses) from "little stories" (everyday accounts), a constructionist framework for analysing how identity is narrated. In oral and Indigenous cultures, open-ended narratives (Western Apache warning tales, Métis humorous stories, Navajo dead-animal metaphors) train holistic thinking and communal belonging rather than delivering fixed morals.

In music, no explicit narrator exists, so scholars disagree about whether it is narrative at all. Adorno argued music "narrates without narrative"; Abbate located narrating voices in disruptive gestures; Nattiez held that any plot exists only in the listener's imagination, prompted by titles and framing. Film lacks a textual narrator but compensates with a "visual narrative instance" (José Landa): cinematography, editing, sound design (diegetic, belonging to the story world, versus non-diegetic, external to it), and *mise-en-scène* (onscreen arrangement of subjects, setting, and visual elements) together do the work a written narrator does in prose.

Storytelling transmits history, values, and identity across generations, which is why *storytelling rights* have become a distinct ethical concern. Amy Shuman frames these as "the important and precarious relationship between narrative and event," raising questions of consent, empathy, accurate representation, and whose interests a retelling serves. Narratives can give marginalised groups agency and advocacy, but the same machinery can produce misunderstanding and exploitation. In military thinking, the "battle of the narratives" treats the cognitive dimension (the reasons and outcomes a conflict is remembered by) as a terrain to be shaped alongside the physical one. Broader applications include narrative environments in architecture and exhibition design, narrative photography and poetry, narrative-based learning, and *metanarratives* (master cultural schemas such as the rags-to-riches plot that order knowledge and shape identity across societies).

Scholars disagree on what specifically separates narrative from non-narrative discourse, whether structural models like Todorov's and Propp's are universal or Western-biased, whether music is intrinsically narrative, and whether narrative is the fundamental nature of self or one rhetorical tool among many. The choice of model determines what counts as explanation, what patterns get noticed, and whose stories are treated as evidence.
