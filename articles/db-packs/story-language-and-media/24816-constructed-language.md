# Constructed language

A constructed language, or *conlang*, is a language for human communication deliberately devised by an individual or group rather than emerging naturally. The discipline that studies them is **interlinguistics**.

## The central idea

Most languages accumulate gradually from social interaction. A conlang inverts this: someone designs phonology, grammar, orthography, and vocabulary intentionally, often from a blank slate, sometimes drawing on existing tongues. The defining question is authorship and purpose, not surface form.

## Why people build them

Conlangs serve overlapping goals. **Auxiliary languages (auxlangs)** ease communication between speakers of different native languages. **Engineered languages (engelangs)** test ideas in logic, philosophy, or linguistics, and include logical languages (loglangs), philosophical languages, and experimental languages. **Artistic languages (artlangs)** serve fiction, aesthetics, humour, secret speech, or mysticism. **Controlled natural languages** strip ambiguity from technical and machine-readable text. Categories overlap: a logical language made for beauty also counts as an artlang.

## A priori versus a posteriori

The most useful split is by source material.

An **a priori** conlang invents its features rather than borrowing them. Examples include John Quijada's *Ithkuil* (2004), which packs a small phoneme set with high information density; Sonja Lang's *Toki Pona* (2001), with a deliberately tiny lexicon; and most fictional languages with no real-world ancestry, such as Tolkien's *Quenya* and *Sindarin* (first published 1937) and Marc Okrand's *Klingon* for *Star Trek* (1985).

An **a posteriori** conlang borrows from existing languages. Most auxlangs are a posteriori because they have to be learnable. *Volapük* (Johann Martin Schleyer, 1879) collapsed within a decade after a schism. *Esperanto* (L. L. Zamenhof, 1887) became the most successful and spawned descendants still spoken today. *Interlingua* (International Auxiliary Language Association, 1951) is the most recent auxlang to reach a significant speaker base. Many artlangs borrow wholesale from a language family to anchor alternate histories, such as Andrew Smith's *Brithenig* from Latin and Welsh (1996).

The boundary is fuzzy: many conlangs are a priori in grammar but a posteriori in vocabulary.

## A short history

Grammatical speculation about how language works is ancient. Plato's *Cratylus* asks whether words are naturally linked to what they name. Around the same period, Pāṇini's Sanskrit grammar mixed description with construction. The medieval *Lingua Ignota*, recorded in the 12th century by Hildegard of Bingen, appears to be the first wholly artificial language, a mystical glossolalia; the 16th-century *Balaibalan* extended this tradition in the Middle East.

The 17th and 18th centuries brought *a priori* philosophical languages designed to classify knowledge, from Francis Lodwick (1647) and John Wilkins (1668) to Leibniz's 1678 *lingua generalis*, aimed at a lexicon from which one could calculate true propositions, work that fed into binary calculus. Many such projects were *pasigraphies*, written-only systems without fixed pronunciation. After the *Encyclopédie* failed to fix knowledge in a hierarchy, philosophical languages drifted to the margins.

The 19th and 20th centuries shifted attention to *a posteriori* auxlangs. Couturat and Leau catalogued 38 projects in 1903. Esperanto set the template. Modern *a priori* projects with small followings, such as *Loglan* (1955) and its descendant *Lojban*, still pursue syntactic unambiguity.

## How conlangs are made and how they change

Modern designers, called **conlangers**, usually work as hobbyists, worldbuilders, or contractors for fiction. A typical workflow fixes phonology, then grammar and syntax, then vocabulary, which requires at least a working grasp of linguistics. Online communities grew around the Conlang Mailing List (1991) and later the Zompist Bulletin Board (2001), where members share drafts and run translation challenges.

Once a conlang has a community, it drifts. Modern Hebrew, revived from earlier stages of Hebrew but heavily shaped by Yiddish and other languages of its revivalists, has kept evolving since Israel's founding in 1948; linguist Ghil'ad Zuckermann calls the result a Semito-European hybrid. Esperanto has diverged enough from Zamenhof's 1887 blueprint that the 1903 *Fundamenta Krestomatio* now needs footnotes.

## Speaker numbers and ownership

Speaker populations remain small. The 2011 Hungarian census recorded 8,397 Esperanto speakers; the 2010 Russian census counted about 992 (the 120th most common language there) and 9 Ido speakers. Ethnologue estimates 200–2,000 native Esperanto speakers.

Whether a conlang can be owned is unresolved. In 2015 CBS and Paramount sued the *Star Trek* fan film *Axanar*, claiming intellectual property over the Klingon language. The Language Creation Society filed an amicus brief arguing that a language is a system of communication, not an expression of an idea, and therefore not copyrightable under section 102(b) of the 1976 Copyright Act. Designer Marc Okrand publicly doubted Paramount's claim; conlanger David Peterson argued anyone should be free to publish in languages he created. Few disputes have been tested in court, so legal consensus remains unsettled. The ISO 639-2 code *art* covers constructed languages generally, and several prominent conlangs have their own ISO 639 codes (*eo* and *epo* for Esperanto, *jbo* for Lojban, *ia* and *ina* for Interlingua, *tlh* for Klingon, *tok* for Toki Pona).
