# Latin literature

Latin literature is the body of writing produced in the Latin language, beginning formally in 240 BC and remaining the dominant language of European scholarship into the early modern era. It began as an adaptation of Greek literary forms, matured into a distinct Roman voice, absorbed Christianity, and then became the shared language of Western learning for more than a thousand years.

The starting point was a performance in 240 BC, when a Greek prisoner of war named Livius Andronicus staged the first Latin play. That event marks the birth of formal Latin literature because Roman culture had previously been oral or practical, not literary in the Greek sense. Within decades, Roman writers were producing their own tragedies, comedies, and epics by reshaping Greek models. The earliest surviving comedies are those of Plautus and Terence, who adapted Greek "New Comedy." The first major prose work was Cato the Elder's *On Agriculture* (160 BC), and by the late 2nd century BC, Gaius Lucilius had invented Roman satire, a genre that mocked public figures and social customs.

Roman writers absorbed Greek forms rather than inventing from nothing. Greek dactylic hexameter, the six-foot meter of Homer, was adopted by Quintus Ennius for his *Annals* and later used by Virgil for the *Aeneid*. Roman drama came in two varieties: the *fabula palliata* (comedy adapted from a Greek original, set in Greece) and the *fabula praetexta* (tragedy on Roman historical or legendary subjects, introduced by Gnaeus Naevius). The distinction between "imported Greek subject matter" and "native Roman subject matter" runs through the entire tradition.

The conventional high point is the "Golden Age" from 81 BC to AD 17, usually split into the Ciceronian and Augustan phases. Cicero perfected Latin prose through orations, philosophical treatises, and letters, and his style became the model for clear, periodic Latin. Caesar and Sallust showed that history could be written as literature, with Sallust favoring a terse, archaic style. Catullus introduced learned lyric poetry, and Lucretius expounded Epicurean philosophy (the school teaching that pleasure, understood as absence of pain, is the highest good) in verse. The Augustan Age (27 BC–AD 14), under the patronage of Emperor Augustus, produced the canonical Latin poets. Virgil's *Aeneid* gave Rome a national epic linking its founding to Troy. Horace perfected lyric and satirical verse. Ovid's *Metamorphoses* became the principal source of classical mythology for the next millennium. Livy wrote a monumental prose history of Rome. Because Latin is an inflected language, with word endings rather than fixed word order carrying grammatical function, writers could rearrange sentences freely for rhythmic and rhetorical effect, a flexibility Cicero exploited throughout his prose.

After Augustus died in AD 14, writers turned toward stylistic innovation and darker subject matter. The Stoic philosopher Seneca (a Stoic teaches that virtue and acceptance of fate are sufficient for a good life) wrote tragedies and moral essays that influenced Elizabethan drama. Lucan's *Pharsalia* and Statius's *Thebaid* continued the epic tradition, while Petronius's *Satyricon* (c. AD 60) is often called the first picaresque novel, a narrative built around the adventures of a roguish lower-class protagonist. Martial perfected the short, witty epigram (a brief, pointed poem). Juvenal wrote savage satirical invective. Historians Tacitus and Suetonius offered critical accounts of the early empire. Apuleius's *The Golden Ass* is the only Latin novel to survive complete. The traditional dating of the Golden Age has been questioned by recent scholarship, which challenges the assumption that works from outside the 81 BC–AD 17 window are inherently inferior. A separate ongoing question concerns Virgil's *Aeneid*: he left it unfinished at his death in 19 BC, and scholars debate how much editing others did.

In the 3rd to 5th centuries AD, Christian writers including Augustine, Jerome, and Prudentius created a Christian Latin literature that layered biblical interpretation and theology onto the classical tradition. Latin survived the fall of Rome as the language of the Church and of medieval scholarship, reaching a peak during the Carolingian Renaissance and again in the Scholastic period, when virtually all philosophy, theology, and science were written in it. The Renaissance revived classical Latin as a model ("Neo-Latin"), and it remained the *lingua franca* of European learning through the 17th century, used by Bacon, Descartes, Spinoza, and Newton.
