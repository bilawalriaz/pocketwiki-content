# Chiral media

A medium is chiral when its structure cannot be superimposed on its mirror image, the way a left hand cannot be rotated or translated to match a right hand. The word comes from the Greek for "hand." When electromagnetic waves pass through such a medium, left-handed and right-handed circularly polarized waves (waves whose electric fields trace opposite-handed helices along the direction of travel) behave differently. This difference is what makes chiral media useful for controlling light.

## Intrinsic and extrinsic chirality

**Intrinsic chirality** belongs to the object itself. A molecule with no plane of mirror symmetry, or a liquid full of such molecules, is intrinsically chiral. The helicoidal arrangement of molecules in a cholesteric liquid crystal is another example. J. C. Bose produced artificial intrinsically chiral structures in 1898.

**Extrinsic chirality** arises from the arrangement, not the components. An achiral (mirror-symmetric) metasurface (a thin engineered layer of sub-wavelength structures) becomes chiral when light hits it at an oblique angle, because the combination of beam direction and surface orientation has no mirror image. C. W. Bunn predicted in 1945 that this geometry would rotate polarization, and the effect was later measured in liquid crystals and metamaterials.

In two dimensions, a flat shape such as the letter "S" or a planar spiral has planar chirality. A 2D-chiral material transmits left- and right-handed circularly polarized waves differently depending on which face the light enters, because the sense of twist reverses when you flip the viewing direction. The same effect appears extrinsically when light strikes an achiral planar structure at an oblique angle.

## Optical effects in chiral media

**Circular birefringence** (different real parts of the refractive index for the two circular polarizations) causes left- and right-handed circularly polarized waves to travel at different speeds. They accumulate a phase difference that rotates the polarization of linearly polarized light. If strong enough, this yields a negative refractive index for one handedness, meaning the wave bends the opposite way at an interface.

**Circular dichroism** (different imaginary parts of the refractive index) means the two polarizations are absorbed unequally. Birefringence and dichroism together constitute optical activity. In natural materials such as sugar solution, optical activity is faint. In chiral metamaterials, engineered composites whose sub-wavelength building blocks are themselves chiral, it can be orders of magnitude stronger.

**Circular conversion dichroism** appears in 2D-chiral structures. The same circularly polarized wave experiences different transmission when entering from the front versus the back, because the efficiency of converting left-handed to right-handed polarization (or vice versa) depends on the propagation direction. A chiral mirror exploits this: it reflects one handedness without flipping it while absorbing the other.

**Linear conversion dichroism** is the analogous effect for linearly polarized waves in anisotropic 3D-chiral structures: the efficiency of converting x-polarized light to y-polarized light differs for forward versus backward travel.

## 3D versus 2D chirality

In 3D-chiral media, the optical response is the same whether light travels forward or backward, just as a helix looks the same from either end. In 2D-chiral media, the response reverses, because flipping the viewing direction reverses the apparent sense of twist. This difference governs whether a device acts as a polarization rotator (3D) or a directional filter (2D). Specular optical activity, the same rotation and differential absorption seen in reflected rather than transmitted light, follows the same rule and is weak in natural materials but large in achiral metasurfaces with extrinsic 3D chirality.

## Handedness conventions for waves

Whether a circularly polarized wave is "left" or "right" depends on convention. Physicists and astronomers view the wave as it travels toward them; engineers view it from behind the source. The two conventions give opposite labels, so any description of chiral effects must specify which is in use.

## Why metamaterials matter

Natural chiral media, from amino acids to quartz, produce weak effects because their chiral building blocks are far smaller than the wavelength of light. Metamaterials solve this by making the building blocks themselves comparable to the wavelength, boosting optical activity by orders of magnitude. At optical frequencies, extrinsic 3D chirality in metasurfaces has yielded nonlinear optical activity (an effect whose strength scales with light intensity) about 30 million times stronger than in lithium iodate, the natural benchmark. At microwave frequencies, an intrinsically 3D-chiral structure exceeded the same benchmark by roughly twelve orders of magnitude.
