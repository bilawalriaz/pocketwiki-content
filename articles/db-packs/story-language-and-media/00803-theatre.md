# Theatre

Theatre is live performance in which actors, singers, and dancers use speech, gesture, music, and movement to present real or imagined events to an audience in a shared space, almost always a stage. Stagecraft (lighting, scenery, sound, costumes) shapes that encounter. Modern Western theatre inherits its vocabulary, genres, and structures from ancient Greece, and the surviving record of Western drama begins there.

## Greek foundations

Western theatre began in Athens, tied to religious festivals honouring Dionysus. Citizens competed in the City Dionysia, where playwrights staged a *tetralogy*: three tragedies followed by one satyr play, a short chorus-driven piece on the same mythological material. Only fragments of 6th-century BCE drama survive; the first complete works are the 5th-century BCE tragedians Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides. Tragedy handled myth; comedy passed through Old, Middle, and New phases. Greek actors were all male, wore masks, and performed in semi-circular open-air auditoria holding up to 20,000 people. Tragic performances at the City Dionysia may have begun around 534 BCE, but the official competition record starts only in 501 BCE. Aeschylus's *The Persians* (472 BCE) is the one surviving Greek tragedy that dramatises a recent historical event rather than myth: the Persian response to their defeat at Salamis in 480 BCE. Aristotle's *Poetics* (c. 335 BCE) is the earliest surviving dramatic theory and remains the most influential attempt to explain what tragedy is. The *theatron*, literally "place for viewing," gives the art its name.

## Roman and Asian expansion

Rome absorbed Greek drama during its Mediterranean expansion between 270 and 240 BCE. The first Roman playwright, Livius Andronicus, began in 240 BCE; Gnaeus Naevius followed five years later. None of their plays survive. What does survive is Roman comedy by Plautus and Terence, both in the form called *fabula palliata* (named for the Greek *pallium* cloak worn by the characters), which adapted Greek originals, dropped the chorus, and added musical accompaniment. Roman tragedy is largely lost; the surviving examples are Seneca's *fabula crepidata* and the anonymous *Octavia* (*fabula praetexta*, on a Roman subject). Unlike Greek practice, Roman stages admitted female performers, some of whom became famous and formed guilds such as the Sociae Mimae.

Sanskrit theatre emerged between the 2nd century BCE and the 1st century CE and flourished under royal patronage until the 10th century CE, developing after Greek and Roman theatre and before theatre in most of the rest of Asia. Its foundational treatise, the *Nātyaśāstra* attributed to Bharata Muni, is hard to date (scholars place it anywhere from 200 BCE to 200 CE) and codifies acting styles under the term *abhinaya*, split into realistic (*lokadharmi*) and conventional (*natyadharmi*) modes. Major dramatists include Kālidāsa and Bhavabhuti. East Asian traditions developed largely independently: Chinese shadow puppetry emerged in the Tang dynasty, with northern (Pekingese) and southern (Cantonese) styles distinguished by puppet construction and rod placement; Japanese Nō, Kabuki, and Kyōgen appeared in the 17th century; Indonesian *wayang* puppetry drew on Hindu epics. In the Islamic world, medieval theatre included the *ta'ziyeh* Shia passion plays and puppet shows.

## Early modern and modern developments

Between the 15th and 19th centuries, Western theatre diversified into commedia dell'arte, melodrama, and eventually naturalistic prose. The English Puritan Interregnum closed theatres from 1642 to 1660, and the Restoration of Charles II in 1660 licensed two competing companies (the Duke's and the King's), with Thomas Killigrew designing the first West End theatre. The Restoration also introduced French influence, the proscenium stage, and women on the English stage. The 19th century carried Romanticism, Realism, and Naturalism, and the 20th century added Stanislavski's psychological realism, Brecht's political "epic theatre," and postmodern directors such as Peter Brook and Robert Wilson. Wagner's ideal of the *Gesamtkunstwerk*, the "total work of art" unifying drama, music, and design, belongs to opera but shaped how 20th-century directors conceived theatrical unity.

## Forms, organisation, and recurring terms

Theatre covers drama, musical theatre, comedy, tragedy, and improvisation. A production is a collaboration among playwright, director, actors, and a technical crew, with the *sutradhara* ("holder of strings") playing the stage-manager role in Sanskrit tradition. Western professional production is shaped by repertory companies, touring ensembles, and resident houses, with labour standards set by unions such as Actors' Equity Association and IATSE. Edwardian musical comedy of the 1890s and the 1920s–30s Princess Theatre shows and comedies fed into *Oklahoma!* (1943), which integrated song, dance, and story so tightly that it is usually credited with redirecting the form of the musical.

Aristotle credits Dionysian festivals with the origin of Greek tragedy, but the evidence is thin and the precise moment of emergence remains obscure. The *Nātyaśāstra* cannot be securely dated, and scholars dispute how far Roman *fabula palliata* and *crepidata* diverged from their Greek models.
