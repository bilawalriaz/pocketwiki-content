# Writing system

A writing system is a conventional set of symbols (a script) plus rules for combining them, used to represent a particular spoken or signed language. The symbols, called graphemes, are the minimal meaningful units from which texts are built, much as phonemes are in spoken language. Every writing system presupposes some analysis of language structure, and every written language originally emerged to record an existing spoken one.

Writing is far younger than language itself and was invented independently only a handful of times. It generally grew from proto-writing, where a small set of ideographs was used in ways that could not fully encode language. Examples include Jiahu symbols on Neolithic tortoise shells, Vinča symbols in Neolithic Europe, and the Inca knotted-cord quipu. The decisive step came through the rebus principle: a symbol for a concrete word was borrowed to stand for a different word that sounded similar, allowing abstract terms, grammatical particles, and names to be written down.

Independent inventions of full writing include cuneiform in Mesopotamia (Sumerian, with Akkadian texts around 2350 BC), Egyptian hieroglyphs (proto-writing between 3400 and 3100 BC, coherent texts from about 2600 BC), Chinese characters in the Yellow River valley around 1200 BC, and Mesoamerican systems including Olmec and Maya. The Indus script of roughly 2600–2000 BC remains undeciphered, and its status as true writing is disputed.

Writing systems are classified by what linguistic unit their graphemes represent. The broadest split is between phonographic systems, where graphemes stand for units of sound, and morphographic systems, where graphemes stand for units of meaning such as morphemes. Recent scholarship prefers "morphographic" over the older "logographic," since units are usually morphemes rather than whole words. Most real systems are mixed.

Three phonographic categories exist. Syllabaries assign a symbol to each syllable or mora (a rhythmic unit roughly the size of a syllable); Japanese hiragana and katakana each have around 100 mora symbols. Alphabets assign letters to phonemes and divide into three subtypes: pure alphabets with letters for consonants and vowels, abjads with letters only for consonants (historically earliest, mostly for Semitic languages whose morphology makes vowel marking often redundant), and abugidas, where each consonant carries an inherent vowel and other vowels are shown by predictable modifications such as attached marks or rotations. Most South and Southeast Asian scripts, including the Brahmic family, are abugidas. A small additional category, featural systems, encodes sub-phonetic features such as voicing or place of articulation; Hangul for Korean is the clearest example, a deliberately designed system sometimes called a grammatogeny.

Alphabets descend from Proto-Sinaitic, used for a Semitic language in the Sinai Peninsula around 1800 BC. Descendants include the Phoenician alphabet (about 1050 BC) and the Greek alphabet (about 800 BC), whose Latin descendant is now the most widely used script.

Graphemes appear as visual shapes called glyphs, and a single grapheme can have many variants called allographs: the lowercase ⟨a⟩, for instance, can be double-storey, single-storey, cursive, or printed. Allograph choice depends on medium, handwriting style, and neighbouring letters. The community conventions governing such choices form orthography. All writing is linear in the broad sense that spatial arrangement indicates reading order, though diacritics make some scripts more complex. Horizontal scripts read left-to-right (Latin, Greek) or right-to-left (Arabic, Hebrew); vertical scripts can read top-to-bottom or bottom-to-top. Early alphabetic writing was often boustrophedonic, reversing at each line, before Phoenician stabilised right-to-left around 800 BC.

Orthographies vary in how cleanly graphemes map to sounds. Shallow orthographies have predictable one-to-one correspondences; deep orthographies allow polyphony (one grapheme for several sounds) and polygraphy (several spellings for one sound). English is deep: the vowel of "fiancé," "raid," "bay," "steak," and "vein" is one phoneme in five spellings. Deep spellings persist because they mark etymological connections invisible in pronunciation, distinguishing homophones like "sign" and "signal," or preserving family resemblance between "child" and "children."

Braille, which encodes the same structure through raised tactile patterns, shows that writing is fundamentally spatial rather than visual.
