# Communication

Communication is the transmission of information from a sender to a receiver through a channel, though scholars dispute whether intent, success, or shared meaning is required. A standard model has a source encode a message, send it through a channel, and have a receiver decode it. The main academic field is communication studies, and the subject spans human, animal, plant, fungal, bacterial, and computer communication. The word derives from the Latin *communicare*, meaning "to share."

## Definitions and models

Definitions diverge on three points: whether intent is required, whether failed transmissions count, and whether meaning is transmitted or co-created. Claude Shannon and Warren Weaver treated communication as a technical transmission, with source, encoder, channel, decoder, and receiver, plus noise that distorts the signal. Transactional views reject the transmission-only picture, holding that meaning is produced during the exchange. Dean Barnlund's 1970 model captures this: communication is "the production of meaning, rather than the production of messages."

Models fall into three types. Linear models show one-way flow; Harold Lasswell's asks "Who? Says what? In which channel? To whom? With what effect?" Interaction models add a feedback loop; Wilbur Schramm introduced encoding, decoding, and overlapping fields of experience, the shared knowledge needed to understand each other. Transactional models allow simultaneous sending and responding, as with gestures during face-to-face talk.

## Human communication

**Verbal communication** uses language, a conventional system of symbols governed by grammar. It includes speech, writing, and sign language. Natural languages like English developed unplanned; artificial ones like Esperanto and programming languages are designed. Verbal communication exchanges information, expresses feeling, maintains social bonds, coordinates action, and supports thought.

**Non-verbal communication** uses facial expressions, gestures, posture, personal space, touch, vocal qualities like pitch, and timing. It often occurs unintentionally and accompanies speech, and some researchers argue it carries the majority of social meaning.

**Interpersonal** communication is between distinct people, synchronously (in real time) or asynchronously (taking turns). **Intrapersonal** communication is with oneself, external (talking aloud, journaling) or internal (thinking, daydreaming); it regulates attention, internalizes norms, and solves problems. **Channels** are the technical means messages travel through; face-to-face combines auditory and visual channels. **Communicative competence** is the ability to communicate effectively (achieving goals) and appropriately (meeting social standards), drawing on empathy, flexibility, and skill.

## Non-human communication

Biologists often require an observable receiver response to count communicative behavior. Animal communication uses visual, auditory, tactile, chemical, and sometimes gustatory signals for courtship, defense, and territory. Vervet monkeys, prairie dogs, and red squirrels give distinct alarm calls for different predators, evidence of referential signaling. Whether such systems are innate or learned is debated.

Plants, fungi, and bacteria communicate mainly chemically because they lack nervous systems. Plants release volatile organic compounds to warn neighbors of herbivores and exchange signals through shared fungal networks in roots, called the Wood-Wide Web. Fungi use pheromones; bacteria use quorum sensing, releasing signaling molecules that coordinate group behaviors like bioluminescence and biofilm formation.

## Computer communication

Devices exchange data over analog or digital channels, characterized by bandwidth (capacity) and latency (delay). Networks range from small local setups to the global internet. Devices share a communication protocol that sets message format and timing. Links run one direction (simplex), alternate directions (half-duplex), or carry traffic both ways at once (full-duplex). Cryptography secures connections. Human-computer interfaces evolved from text-based command lines to graphical interfaces, often using skeuomorphic designs that mimic physical objects.

## History

Language emerged tens of thousands of years ago from calls, gestures, and expressions. Stable records drove the rise of writing: pictographic symbols around 9000 BCE, Sumerian cuneiform around 3500 BCE, and alphabetic systems that cut the number of required symbols. Papyrus around 2500 BCE improved portability, and mid-15th-century printing with movable type expanded circulation and literacy. Telegraphs and telephones removed the need to transport documents, radio and television enabled mass broadcasting, satellites made it global, and the late-20th-century internet added interactive digital networks.

## Open questions

Whether communication requires intent or success, whether it transmits or creates meaning, whether animal signals are truly referential or recursive, how to weigh innate versus learned signaling, and how to periodize communication history all remain unsettled.
