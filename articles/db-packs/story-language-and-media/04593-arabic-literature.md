# Arabic literature

Arabic literature is the body of writing in the Arabic language, stretching from 6th-century oral tribal poetry to contemporary global fiction. Its development has been shaped above all by the Qur'an, which standardised the language and gave it a foundational text, and by the cultural exchanges of the Islamic Golden Age. The scholar Jan Just Witkam has suggested that Arabic may now be the largest literary corpus in the world.

## Pre-Islamic roots

Before Islam, literature was almost entirely oral. Poets functioned as tribal spokesmen, satirists, and memory keepers, reciting at markets such as Souq Okaz. The most famous pre-Islamic works are the *Mu'allaqat*, a small canon of odes preserving ideals of honour, lineage, and warfare. The term for this body of work, *al-shi'r al-Jāhilī* ("poetry of the Jahiliyyah"), names the baseline against which later Arabic poetry defines itself.

## The Qur'an and early Islamic literature

The Qur'an, revealed in the 7th century, is the single most influential text in the language. It fixed a register of rhymed prose (*saj'*) and gave rise to the doctrine of *i'jaz*, the claim that its style is divinely inimitable. Because of the Qur'an, Arabic became a unifying literary language across an expanding empire. Under the Rashidun caliphate, literary centres emerged in Mecca, Medina, Damascus, Kufa, and Basra, and poetry served the spread of Islam. Under the Umayyads, literature became more politically factionalised, and poetry markets like Mirbad flourished in the new garrison cities.

## The Abbasid Golden Age

The Abbasid period, centred on Baghdad, was the peak of classical Arabic literature. The House of Wisdom hosted polymaths such as Al-Jahiz and translated Greek, Persian, and Indian works into Arabic. This era produced the story cycle *One Thousand and One Nights*, the *maqama* (rhymed-prose narratives featuring a wandering trickster), encyclopaedic treatises on topics from cooking to geography, and the formalisation of Arabic grammar, lexicography, and literary criticism. Religious scholarship around the Qur'an and the life of Muhammad drove much of this technical work, because fixing the sacred text required an exact description of the language.

## Andalusi literature

From the 8th to the 15th centuries, Islamic Iberia developed its own tradition. Andalusi poets produced the *muwashshah*, a strophic poem mixing classical Arabic with Romance registers, and shaped literary taste in both the Maghreb and the Mashriq.

## The Nahda and modernity

The 19th-century *Nahda* ("Renaissance") revived Arabic letters through neoclassicism, the translation of Western works, and a new press. It produced the earliest modern Arabic novels, including Muhammad Husayn Haykal's *Zaynab* (1913), and the first Arabic-language theatre. The novel became a vehicle for social critique on identity, colonialism, and political upheaval, exemplified later by writers such as Tayeb Salih and recognised internationally when Naguib Mahfouz won the 1988 Nobel Prize in Literature.

## Modern poetry

After World War II, the Iraqi poets Badr Shakir al-Sayyab and Nazik Al-Malaika led the free verse movement (*shi'r hurr*), abandoning classical metre and rhyme. The form evolved into prose poetry and further modernist experiment, and it remains the dominant form of serious Arabic poetry.

## Women in the tradition

Women's literary voice was historically narrow, beginning with the elegist al-Khansa in the pre-Islamic period. The Nahda opened more space, with figures such as Zaynab Fawwaz active in the new literary salons. The 20th century brought wider recognition: Fadwa Touqan wrote from under colonial rule, and contemporary writers including Hanan al-Shaykh, Ahdaf Soueif, and Nawal el-Saadawi use fiction and memoir to address gender, politics, and diaspora identity. Critical reception of women's poetry has often been contested, with ongoing debate over its adherence to classical principles and its place in the canon.

## Two open debates

The genre of the Qur'an itself is disputed. Some scholars treat it as a form of homily, others as a perfected version of pre-Islamic *saj'*, and Muslim tradition regards it as a miracle of style (*i'jaz*) that stands outside ordinary literary categories. The origins of the Arabic novel are similarly contested, with several 19th-century works each claimed as the "first," reflecting both European influence and the revival of older narrative forms such as the *maqama* and the *Nights*.
