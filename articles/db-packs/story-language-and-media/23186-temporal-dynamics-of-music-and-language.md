# Temporal dynamics of music and language

Music and language share rhythmic and melodic structure built from a finite set of basic elements (tones or words) combined in ordered ways. The brain recruits overlapping regions for both, while assigning some areas more to one than the other.

## Shared and specialised regions

Listening to a melody or a sentence activates much the same network: the primary motor cortex, supplementary motor area, Broca's area, anterior insula, primary auditory cortex, thalamus, basal ganglia, and cerebellum. Brown, Martinez and Parsons documented this overlap.

Specialisation persists alongside it. Broca's area handles language production and grammar; damage causes poor grammar and slow speech. The inferior frontal gyrus, a fold of the frontal lobe, supports timing and verb comprehension. Wernicke's area, on the posterior superior temporal gyrus, decodes vocabulary and written language. The primary auditory cortex in the temporal lobe determines pitch and volume, and severe damage can eliminate hearing. The frontal cortex and cerebellum together manage melody, harmony, and tempo, showing strong activity on fMRI and PET scans when patients tap or reproduce tones.

Children with specific language impairments also struggle to match tones and keep tempo with a metronome, the same shared machinery producing deficits on both sides. TMS work by Walsh, Stewart and Frith localised the divergence: stimulation of Broca's area disrupted melody production most, while stimulation of the left frontal lobe disrupted language output most. Music and language creation overlap, but not identically.

## Real-time imaging and perturbation

Three tools dominate the field. fMRI tracks haemoglobin's magnetic properties; oxygen-rich blood displaces oxygen-poor blood where neurons fire, and the magnetic disturbance maps activity in near real time. PET scans inject a short-lived radioactive tracer bound to glucose; active regions absorb more, and the decay signal localises the activity, with about one second of lag. MEG uses an array of superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs), housed in a magnetically shielded room, to record the tiny magnetic fields produced by neural currents.

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) does not image but interrupts. A rapidly changing magnetic field induces weak currents that depolarise or hyperpolarise neurons, letting researchers test which regions are causally necessary for a task.

## Early development

Basic auditory discrimination is present before birth. A French study monitored fetal heartbeats after 28 weeks of gestation and found responses to changes in pitch, tempo, and loudness, but not to syllable patterns. Pitch, tempo, and loudness detection appear innate; speech-pattern processing develops after birth. A separate study found that musical stimulation during speech therapy accelerated phonological gains and comprehension in children with speech difficulties, suggesting the auditory machinery bootstraps language learning once it engages with structured sound.

## Clinical applications

Music engages dopamine release, neural synchrony, and clear signalling, a combination linked to neuroplasticity, the brain's capacity to reorganise and adapt. Three conditions illustrate the range.

Parkinson's disease reduces dopamine, producing shuffling gait, postural instability, and difficulty changing direction. Rhythmic and musical auditory cues increase walking speed and stride length while reducing cadence.

Huntington's disease disrupts movement, cognition, and psychiatric function. Music and dance therapy improve cognitive and motor engagement, helping patients sustain long-term participation in rehabilitation.

In dementia, higher auditory functions like melodic contour perception decline, yet basic pitch, timbre, and rhythm awareness persist, as do music-evoked emotions and memories. Singing practice enhances verbal working memory, remote episodic memory, and executive function.

These clinical gains depend on the same preserved basic auditory processing that the fetal studies identified, the earliest layer of a temporal system the brain builds on throughout life.
