# Language education

Language education is the deliberate teaching of a second or foreign language, distinct from the way children acquire a first language through immersion. The field combines applied linguistics (using linguistic theory in practical contexts such as teaching and policy), psychology, and education, and aims for communicative competencies, proficiencies, cross-cultural experiences, and multiple literacies. Its main driver is economic: globalization has made multilingual ability a workforce requirement in trade, tourism, diplomacy, technology, media, translation, and science, which is why countries such as Korea, Japan, and China now require at least one foreign language in primary and secondary school.

The roots of modern language education lie in Latin instruction. Latin dominated European education, commerce, religion, and government for centuries, but by the late 16th century it was being displaced by French, Italian, and English. In the 17th century John Amos Comenius tried to restore Latin's status and became one of the first theorists to write systematically about how languages are learned. He argued that acquisition must be tied to sensation and experience, that teaching should be oral, and that classrooms should use pictures and real objects; his illustrated children's book *Orbis sensualium pictus* was the first of its kind. As Latin lost everyday use, its study was reframed as mental training, and grammar schools from the 16th to 18th centuries taught Classical Latin grammar, with advanced students adding rhetoric. Modern languages entered European schools in the 18th century through the grammar-translation method: memorizing rules, translating abstract sentences, and minimal oral work.

The 19th and 20th centuries brought rapid innovation. Key applied linguists such as Henry Sweet, Otto Jespersen, and Harold Palmer tried to ground teaching in linguistic and psychological theory, and two rival traditions emerged. The empiricist camp, including Jespersen, Palmer, and Leonard Bloomfield, treated learning as habit formation through mimicry, memorization, and pattern drills, culminating in the audio-lingual method developed for the U.S. Army during World War II. The rationalist camp, including Francois Gouin, M. D. Berlitz, and Emile B. De Sauzé, held that language reflects an innate universal grammar unique to humans. Their approach, later reinforced by Noam Chomsky, generated methods that ask students to produce original sentences, ranging from grammar-translation and Gouin's series method to the direct methods of Berlitz and De Sauzé. Despite these advances, 20th-century programs largely failed: very few U.S. university foreign-language majors reach minimum professional proficiency, and even the reading knowledge required for a PhD is comparable to that of second-year students. Old methods are rarely abandoned; the Berlitz direct method is still in use.

In the 21st century language education has become a core school subject in many countries, called World Languages in the United States and LOTE (Languages Other Than English) in Australia. French, Spanish, and German are the most studied in English-speaking systems, and English as a Second Language is widely offered.

Classroom teaching is organized into three nested levels. An approach is a theory of language and learning, with three main types: the structural view (language as a grammatical system), the functional view (language as a tool for achieving goals such as requesting), and the interactive view (language as social negotiation), the dominant lens since the 1980s. A method is a practical plan for presenting material, such as grammar-translation and audio-lingual (structural), the oral or situational approach (functional), or communicative language teaching, immersion, the Silent Way, suggestopedia, the natural approach, and total physical response (interactive). A technique is a specific classroom move, like the sandwich technique, where the teacher inserts a mother-tongue equivalent between repetitions of a foreign phrase (L2 → L1 → L2), or back-chaining, where a polysyllabic word is built from the last syllable to the first. Humanistic language teaching, associated with Earl Stevick and Gertrude Moskowitz, adds attention to participation and student-centeredness across all three levels. Teaching targets listening, speaking, reading, and writing; since the 1970s and 1980s these have been integrated rather than taught in a fixed sequence, and pair and group work is emphasized because speaking is harder to teach and assess. Code-switching, alternating between languages in a single conversation, is also used as a teaching strategy.

Self-study uses audio recordings, books, and software. Software can record learners, analyze pronunciation, and adapt exercises. The internet has added language-exchange websites that pair speakers of different languages, virtual worlds such as Second Life, translation and text-to-speech tools, learning management systems like Moodle, and free government resources such as BBC Online, Book2, and the Foreign Service Institute.

Learning strategies are conscious choices about how to study. Focused listening helps learners catch the small pronunciation differences that change meaning; reading for grammar and discourse style complements comprehension; conversation practice through classes, meet-ups, exchanges, or travel builds speaking skill; and vocabulary can be built through translation, rote memorization, guessing from context, or spaced repetition (reviewing material at increasing intervals) using apps such as Duolingo and Anki. Esperanto, the constructed language created in 1887 by L. L. Zamenhof, has been proposed as a stepping-stone language, with some studies suggesting that a year of Esperanto followed by another language produces higher long-term proficiency than studying the target language alone, though critics note weak methodology in the underlying research.

Language learning varies by life stage. Infants recognize phonological elements of surrounding languages at birth, recognize concrete words like food and body parts by six months, and produce grammatically adult-like sentences by two years. Compulsory schooling offers the first formal access for most learners. Adult learners bring stronger self-supervision alongside greater time pressure, and elderly learners face the steepest challenge because of memory decline, though language study can slow cognitive degeneration. Language study holidays, combining lessons, cultural excursions, homestays, and travel, are especially popular in Europe, with Malta and the UK the leading destinations.

Minority language education is promoted for three reasons: a multilingual workforce, intellectual and cultural benefits, and inclusion in the global information society, and access to minority-language schooling is recognized as a human right under instruments such as the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages. Bilingual education is used in many countries, including the United States, to support both minority and majority languages, though suitable teaching materials are often scarce.

Despite heavy spending on language teaching worldwide, the share of students who actually use the target language outside the classroom remains unclear, a gap that matters because nine out of ten U.S. employers rely on employees with languages other than English, and roughly a third of those employers report a high level of dependence.
