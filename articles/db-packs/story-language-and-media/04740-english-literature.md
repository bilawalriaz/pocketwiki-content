# English literature

English literature is the body of written work in the English language, with roughly 1,400 years of continuous development shaped by the language's own evolution, by political events such as invasions and conquests, and by the global spread of English through the British Empire and the United States. Literary forms changed dramatically across the centuries, yet the language and the canon of major works evolved through a recognisable chain of influences.

The most useful distinction is the language itself. The earliest form, **Old English** (c. 450–1066), was the language of Anglo-Saxon England. It looks and sounds like a foreign tongue, dominated by **alliterative verse**, poetry whose structure rests on repeated initial sounds rather than rhyme. Almost all authors are anonymous, but Cædmon, Bede, King Alfred, and Cynewulf are known. The single greatest work is *Beowulf*, a national epic set in Scandinavia, whose surviving manuscript dates to around 1000. The poem's exact date and authorship are debated, with composition placed between the 8th and early 11th centuries.

The Norman Conquest of 1066 ended this period. French became the language of the court, and **Middle English** gradually emerged as the everyday speech, the **vernacular**. Middle English literature (1066–1485) blended religious writing with new forms such as chivalric romance and drama that grew out of church ritual into mystery, miracle, and morality plays. Geoffrey Chaucer's *The Canterbury Tales* (c. 1387–1400) showed that English could carry sophisticated poetry and helped legitimise the vernacular for serious literature. Other major works include William Langland's *Piers Plowman* and the anonymous *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight*. The first complete English Bible translation, Wycliffe's Bible, appeared in this era. The period closed with the **Chancery Standard**, a written English developed in government offices, and William Caxton's introduction of the printing press in 1476, both of which stabilised the language.

The Tudor and Stuart periods (1485–1714) form the English Renaissance. Classical learning and the Reformation produced Thomas More's *Utopia* (1516) and the sonnet forms introduced by Thomas Wyatt and Henry Howard. The Elizabethan age was the high point of English Renaissance drama, dominated by William Shakespeare, with Christopher Marlowe and Ben Jonson as major contemporaries. The 17th century brought John Donne and the **Metaphysical poets**, the King James Bible of 1611, and John Milton's epic *Paradise Lost* (1667).

The Georgian and Romantic eras (1714–1837) saw the novel rise to dominance. The early 18th century, the **Augustan** age, prized satire, order, and classical reference, with Daniel Defoe (*Robinson Crusoe*, 1719) and Jonathan Swift shaping the new form. The mid-century Age of Sensibility added sentiment and inward feeling. **Romanticism** then reacted against the Industrial Revolution by foregrounding emotion, nature, and the individual; its major poets were Wordsworth, Coleridge, Byron, Shelley, and Keats, whose *Lyrical Ballads* (1798) is the founding manifesto. The novel matured through Walter Scott's historical romances and Jane Austen's social realism. In the same period, American literature began to diverge, with early works by Benjamin Franklin and Thomas Paine.

The Victorian era (1837–1901) made the novel the leading genre, often published in serial instalments and engaged with social problems. Charles Dickens, the Brontë sisters, George Eliot, William Makepeace Thackeray, and Thomas Hardy were the central novelists, while Thomas Carlyle and John Ruskin developed sage writing, a mode of social criticism. Poetry was led by Alfred, Lord Tennyson and Robert Browning, whose **dramatic monologue**, a poem spoken by a character to an imagined audience, became his signature form. New commercial genres appeared: detective fiction (Wilkie Collins), science fiction (H. G. Wells), and fantasy (George MacDonald). American literature matured with Mark Twain, Henry James, and Emily Dickinson.

The 20th century opened with **Modernism**, a break with Victorian confidence driven by new philosophy and science. Joseph Conrad, W. B. Yeats, and T. S. Eliot were early figures. The key technical innovation was **stream of consciousness**, a narrative method that mimics the flow of thoughts and feelings through a character's mind, used in Joyce's *Ulysses* (1922) and Virginia Woolf's novels. Between the wars, Aldous Huxley and George Orwell wrote influential dystopias. After 1945, drama saw the "Angry Young Men" and the Theatre of the Absurd (Samuel Beckett), while the novel remained central through writers such as Graham Greene and Iris Murdoch. **Postmodernism** followed, marked by fragmentation, irony, and metafiction, though scholars disagree about when the shift occurred. In the same decades, literature from the former British Empire, including writers from Nigeria, India, and the Caribbean, became a major strand of the English tradition, joined in the 1990s by global phenomena such as J. K. Rowling's *Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone* (1997).

A small set of open questions runs through the field. The date and authorship of *Beowulf* remain uncertain. The origin of the English novel cannot be fixed to a single year, with candidates ranging from Restoration works such as Aphra Behn's *Oroonoko* (1688) to 18th-century fiction. And the boundary between modernism and postmodernism is itself contested.
