# Comparative literature

Comparative literature is the academic study of literature across linguistic, national, geographic, and disciplinary boundaries. Comparatists read works from different countries and traditions side by side, ask how literary forms travel between cultures, and place texts in conversation with history, politics, philosophy, art, and science. Defined broadly, the field studies "literature without borders". Its scholars share two commitments: examining literature beyond national boundaries, and reading foreign texts in their original languages. Many also want to connect literary experience with cultural phenomena such as historical change and social movements.

Because of this breadth, comparatists typically know several languages and draw on sociology, history, anthropology, translation studies, critical theory, cultural studies, and religious studies. University programs are often designed by scholars from several departments. This eclecticism has drawn criticism that the field is poorly defined and risks dilettantism, though placement data shows graduates are hired at rates comparable to English literature graduates. The terms "comparative literature" and "world literature" are often used interchangeably, with "comparative literature" more common in the United States.

## Historical foundations

Antecedents appear in Goethe's vision of *Weltliteratur* (world literature) and in the work of Alexander Veselovsky, whom Viktor Zhirmunsky called the most remarkable representative of comparative literary study in nineteenth-century Russian and European scholarship. Later foundational figures include Hugo Meltzl de Lomnitz, founding editor of *Acta Comparationis Litterarum Universarum* (1877), and H. M. Posnett, author of *Comparative Literature* (1886). Late nineteenth-century scholars such as Fyodor Buslaev sought the *Zeitgeist*, or "spirit of the times", in each nation's literary output. Much of this work would now be judged Eurocentric, though the scholars usually aimed at understanding other cultures rather than asserting superiority.

## The French School

From the early twentieth century until World War II, the French School dominated. Scholars like Paul Van Tieghem examined works forensically for "origins" and "influences" between nations, a method called *rapport des faits*, tracing how a literary idea or motif travelled across borders over time.

## The German School

After World War II, Peter Szondi shaped the German version of the field at the Free University Berlin. He developed *Allgemeine und Vergleichende Literaturwissenschaft* ("General and Comparative Literary Studies"), focusing on drama, hermetic lyric poetry, and hermeneutics, the theory of interpretation. Though oriented toward the West, he was influenced by East European structuralists from the Russian and Prague schools. By 2003, 31 German universities offered a diploma in comparative literature, though the field has since been squeezed by traditional philologies, the detailed study of historical languages and their texts, on one side and more vocational programs on the other.

## The American School

Reacting against the French School, postwar American scholars returned the discipline to literary criticism, de-emphasising detective work on influences. Aligned with the internationalist visions of Goethe and Posnett, the American School looked for universal human truths expressed through literary archetypes across cultures. Erich Auerbach's *Mimesis: The Representation of Reality in Western Literature* exemplifies the earlier Western focus on English, German, French, Italian, and Spanish literatures. The field today routinely covers Chinese, Arabic, and most other major world literatures, and is claimed by some as the forerunner of the 1970s–80s Cultural Studies boom.

## Current developments

A movement is pushing comparative literature away from its nation-based roots toward a cross-cultural approach that ignores borders. Key works include David Damrosch's *What is World Literature?*, Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak's *Death of a Discipline*, and Pascale Casanova's *The World Republic of Letters*. The field's earlier grounding in nation-based thinking makes this shift an open question, though globalization studies may favour it. Joseph Hankinson has stressed comparison's "affiliative" potential.

While the discipline faces institutional constriction in parts of the West, it is thriving in Asia, Latin America, the Caribbean, and the Mediterranean. Post-colonial figures now central to transnational studies include J. M. Coetzee, Wole Soyinka, Derek Walcott, and V. S. Naipaul. Linda Hutcheon's work on opera and her *A Theory of Adaptation* (2012) extends comparative method into literature's relation to other arts.
