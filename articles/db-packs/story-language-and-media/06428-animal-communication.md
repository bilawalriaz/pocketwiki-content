# Animal communication

Animal communication is the transfer of information between a sender and a receiver that changes the receiver's current or future behavior. Information may be sent on purpose, as in a courtship display, or by accident, as when a predator leaves scent on prey via kairomones (chemicals that benefit the receiver rather than the sender). When the transfer alters a receiver's behavior, the information is called a signal. Signaling theory holds that signals persist when both sender and receiver gain a net benefit, and that signal production and response coevolve.

## Channels

Animals use every available physical channel, often combined.

- Visual. Body postures, gestures, facial expressions, and color changes. Parent herring gulls display a red bill spot; chicks tap it to trigger regurgitation. Octopuses and cuttlefish change skin color using chromatophores, and a courting male cuttlefish can show a male pattern toward the female and a female pattern toward rival males at the same time. Bioluminescence in fireflies, glowworms, and deep-sea anglerfish carries signals in the dark, from mate attraction to luring prey.
- Auditory. Vervet monkeys give distinct alarm calls for leopards, eagles, and snakes, and listeners respond differently to each. Prairie dog calls encode predator type, size, shape, color, and speed, with sentries whistling an alarm whose length tracks the danger. Whales and dolphins show dialects acquired through social learning. Non-vocal sound includes stridulation in crickets, swim-bladder vibrations in bony fish, and tail rattling in snakes. Burrowing mammals such as marmots whistle to signal threat.
- Olfactory. The oldest channel. Minnows detect predator chemicals and hide; damaged Atlantic salmon release an alarm cue to nearby conspecifics (members of the same species). Scent marking and rubbing by bears and wolves convey identity, territory, and breeding condition. Ocean acidification disrupts these chemical cues, with downstream effects on behavior.
- Touch. Used in fighting, mating, grooming, and recruitment. Social grooming removes parasites, reinforces bonds, and lets the groomer sample the partner's scent. Ants recruit foragers by antennal tapping and leading. The honeybee waggle dance encodes the direction and distance of food. Huddling transfers heat along with tactile and olfactory information.
- Electric. Mostly aquatic. Weakly electric fish generate a field with an electric organ and read it with electroreceptors; waveform and frequency encode species, sex, and identity. Sharks and rays eavesdrop on these fields. The platypus senses electric fields, though whether this serves communication is unclear.
- Seismic. Vibrations through soil, water, webs, or plant stems, produced by drumming and sensed by the sacculus of the inner ear. Short range and short persistence reduce predator detection. Used by frogs, kangaroo rats, mole rats, bees, and nematode worms.
- Thermal. Pitvipers, some boas, and pythons sense infrared at 5–30 µm through facial pit organs, striking vulnerable body parts even when blinded. Common vampire bats have infrared sensors in their nose-leaf, locating blood-rich regions on prey within 10–15 cm.

## Functions

Signals serve contests (aggression, submission), mating (courtship displays, copulation calls, symmetrical pair-bond rituals such as the great crested grebe's reed ceremony), territorial defense, food recruitment (waggle dance, pheromone trails, raven food calls), and alarm (vocal calls, crushed-ant alarm pheromones). Meta-communication changes the meaning of the next signal: a dog's play face reinterprets a following bite as play, not attack.

## Autocommunication, deception, and predator–prey signaling

In autocommunication the sender is also the receiver. Bats and toothed whales echolocate; weakly electric fish use active electrolocation, and bats memorize echolocation patterns to recognize spaces in the dark. Predators and prey also signal each other. Warning coloration in wasps deters predators, and harmless hoverflies mimic them. Rattlesnake rattles advertise venom. The anglerfish dangles a bioluminescent lure; the ant-mimicking spider Myrmarachne waves its forelegs like antennae. Pursuit-deterrent signals benefit both sides: stotting by antelopes such as Thomson's gazelle advertises that the predator's surprise is lost, so the predator saves energy by not chasing.

## How communication evolves

Ethologists such as Konrad Lorenz and Desmond Morris showed that displays originate when an existing movement, like grass-finch beak-wiping for preening, gets co-opted into a courtship or alarm context. Early group-selectionist explanations gave way to a gene-centered view: signals spread when they help the genes of sender and receiver. John Krebs and Richard Dawkins argued that alarm calls and courtship signals can evolve under individual selection, opening the door to dishonest signals such as mimicry and the anglerfish lure. Amotz Zahavi's handicap principle holds that a costly trait, like the peacock's tail, is an honest signal of condition because only high-quality males can bear it. Runaway selection couples an arbitrary female preference with an exaggerated male trait in a positive feedback loop until survival costs halt the escalation.

## Identity, names, and the gap from human language

Bottlenose dolphins broadcast identity through signature whistles whose shape, independent of the caller's voice or location, encodes who is calling; mothers inflect these whistles in their calves' presence, reaching higher or wider frequencies, a parallel to human child-directed speech. Marmosets and African elephants also use name-like calls. Animal communication generally lacks the features that define human language: double articulation (meaningful words built from meaningless phonemes), displacement (reference to things distant in time or space), and open-ended creativity. Vervet alarm calls and prairie dog calls show referential specificity, and Campbell's mona monkey shows a hint of affixation, but the gulf remains wide.

## Interpreting animals and signaling back

Reading animals is error-prone because the same gesture can mean different things in different contexts, and anthropomorphism is misleading: an ape's smile often signals aggression. Humans can also send signals animals read. Dogs follow human pointing and gaze to locate hidden food, though pointing denotes a direction rather than an object. Vervet monkeys refine alarm-call use with experience, and the banner-tailed kangaroo rat signals alertness to snakes by foot-drumming. The meaning of a signal depends on context, prior experience, and the sensory system of the receiver.
