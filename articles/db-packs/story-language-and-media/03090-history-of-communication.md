# History of communication

Human communication is the layered set of techniques humans have used to move information across time and distance, from spoken words to global digital networks. Each major advance, from durable inscriptions to instant worldwide messaging, has altered who can record ideas, who can read them, and how societies coordinate. The arc moves from ephemeral voice to permanent record, then to mass reproduction, and finally to instantaneous electronic exchange.

## From speech to symbols

Spoken language is the oldest medium but is bound to a single moment and a small physical radius. To overcome this, humans began marking their surroundings. The earliest known examples are the cave paintings of Chauvet Cave, dating to about 30,000 BCE. By 10,000–12,000 BCE, people were carving petroglyphs into rock, and around 9000 BCE they were drawing pictograms. The key advantage of pictograms over petroglyphs was sequence: a pictogram could be ordered to tell a story across a surface. Pictograms in turn evolved into ideograms, which could stand for abstract concepts like "day" or "sadness" rather than only physical objects. Each step widened what could be recorded and how flexibly it could be combined.

## The invention of writing

True writing systems emerged in the late 4th millennium BCE, contemporary with the Bronze Age. The earliest were logographic, meaning each sign encoded a whole word or morpheme (the smallest meaningful unit of a word). Sumerian cuneiform and Egyptian hieroglyphs are the oldest examples. Cuneiform began as pictographs impressed into clay and by 2800 BCE had absorbed enough phonetic signs (symbols for individual sounds) to serve as a general-purpose script. Chinese script may have developed independently around the 16th century BCE.

The next breakthrough shrank the symbol set. Around 2000 BCE in Ancient Egypt, the first alphabets appeared. Early Semitic alphabets, called abjads, mapped symbols to single consonant sounds rather than whole words. A few dozen signs could represent any utterance. The Greek alphabet adapted the West-Semitic system by the 8th century BCE, adding vowels and giving rise to nearly all modern Western alphabets.

## Printing and mass reproduction

Writing made ideas durable, but each copy still had to be made by hand. In 105 CE, Cai Lun in China standardised paper, a cheap portable surface. Mass reproduction began with wooden block movable type in China by 1305. The decisive European step was Gutenberg's 1440 press with metal movable type, which drove down the cost of books and widened literacy. Later refinements included wood-pulp paper in 1844 and the photocopier in 1938. The recurring pattern is clear: cheaper reproduction expands the audience for a given text.

## Telecommunication and the digital age

Long-distance signalling began with non-electrical aids such as smoke signals and drums, which carry only a few prearranged meanings. The electrical era opened in the 1830s. Samuel Morse's telegraph of 1837 transmitted coded messages along wires, collapsing distance for written information. Alexander Graham Bell exhibited the first electric telephone in 1876, adding real-time voice. The twentieth century multiplied both speed and audience: radio broadcasting in 1920, television in 1925, and the first geosynchronous communications satellite in 1963.

Digital tools then reshaped both the storage and the routing of information. ENIAC, the first programmable electronic computer, ran in 1946. The first email was sent in 1965, and in 1969 the first hosts of ARPANET were connected. ARPANET was the packet-switching network (a network that breaks messages into small packets and routes them independently) that formed the technical foundation of the internet. Tim Berners-Lee built the World Wide Web prototype in 1989 and released it to the public in 1993, after which search engines, social media, and smartphones absorbed communication into a single global, always-on network. The defining shift is that information is no longer merely reproduced; it is addressed, routed, and retrieved on demand.

## Key milestones at a glance

- c. 100,000 BCE: origin of human speech.
- c. 30,000 BCE: first symbols, including the Chauvet cave paintings.
- c. 9000 BCE: pictograms introduce sequence and narrative.
- Late 4th millennium BCE: cuneiform and hieroglyphs, the first true writing.
- c. 2000 BCE: abjads, the first alphabets, appear in Egypt.
- 105 CE: Cai Lun standardises paper.
- 1440: Gutenberg's metal movable-type press in Europe.
- 1837: Morse's telegraph.
- 1876: Bell's electric telephone.
- 1969: first ARPANET hosts connected, the seed of the modern internet.

The arc of communication history is a sequence of compressions: of the human voice into symbols, of many symbols into a small alphabet, of hand copying into mechanical reproduction, and of point-to-point signalling into routed digital networks. Each compression made information cheaper to store, easier to copy, or faster to reach, and each time the social order reorganised around the new reach.
