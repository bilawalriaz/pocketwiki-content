# Linguistics

Linguistics is the scientific study of language, treating it as a system of unconscious rules that link sound, form, and meaning. It is descriptive rather than prescriptive: a linguist records how people actually use language, as a zoologist documents animal behaviour, without judging usages as correct or incorrect.

## Levels of linguistic structure

Language is conventionally broken into five interlocking levels, each with its own subdiscipline.

**Phonetics** studies the physical production, acoustics, and perception of speech sounds, including manual gestures in sign languages. **Phonology** abstracts these sounds into cognitive categories called phonemes, the minimal sound contrasts that distinguish meaning, and states the rules governing how phonemes combine.

**Morphology** examines word-internal structure. A morpheme is the smallest meaningful unit; it may be a free root such as *catch* or a bound affix such as *-ing*. Morphology distinguishes inflection, the grammatical changes of tense or number, from derivation, the building of new words, as from *nation* to *national*.

**Syntax** governs how words and morphemes combine into phrases and sentences, addressing word order, constituency, and agreement across languages. **Semantics** analyses conventional, coded meaning at the word level and at the level of how meanings combine; formal approaches use logic to model denotations, while cognitive approaches link meaning to general cognition, as in prototype theory. **Pragmatics** studies meaning in context, including speech acts, implicature, and the role of shared knowledge and speaker intent in interpretation.

Grammar is the integrated rule system spanning all five levels, studied through structural, functional, and generative frameworks. Discourse analysis extends the picture to language as social practice, examining how ideology and context shape linguistic form. The lexicon is the mental catalogue of words and bound morphemes that lexicography turns into dictionaries. Reading comprehension typically requires roughly 98% vocabulary coverage, but the capacity to build and understand novel sentences resides in grammar, not in lexicon size.

## Historical and comparative study

Historical linguistics emerged from 18th-century philology, which compared texts to trace word histories. William Jones proposed in 1786 that Sanskrit, Greek, and Latin descend from a common ancestor, founding the comparative method. This method reconstructs unattested proto-languages by systematically comparing daughter languages, while internal reconstruction infers earlier stages from alternating forms within one language. The uniformitarian principle, associated with William Dwight Whitney, assumes that past changes obey the same principles as observable present-day changes.

Early work concentrated on the Indo-European and Uralic families; later scholarship extended to Austronesian and Native American families. Macrofamily hypotheses such as Nostratic attempt to group these further back in time, but the comparative method becomes unreliable at roughly 8,000 to 10,000 years, so such deep groupings remain controversial. Modern historical linguistics examines sound change, grammaticalization, syntactic change, and sociolinguistic drivers across every structural level.

## Methodology: description and data

The descriptive commitment shapes what counts as data. Spoken and signed language is treated as more fundamental than writing because it is universal, historically prior, acquired earlier by children, and captures features such as phonological rules and speech errors that writing omits. Written corpora are more tractable for computational analysis, which is why graphemics, the study of writing systems, exists as its own subfield. Prescription, the promotion of specific usages such as a prestige dialect, remains standard in language pedagogy and standardization more broadly, but it is not what descriptive linguistics does.

## Analytical perspectives

Before the 20th century, linguistics was largely diachronic, comparing languages across time. Ferdinand de Saussure's posthumous *Course in General Linguistics* (1916) established synchronic analysis, the study of a language's system at a single moment. Syntagmatic analysis examines linear sequencing rules, such as the fact that in English an article typically precedes a noun. Paradigmatic analysis examines substitution sets, the items of the same category that can occupy a given slot.

## Applied and interdisciplinary areas

Sociolinguistics studies how social factors shape variation in dialect, register, and idiolect. Developmental linguistics investigates first-language acquisition in children and second-language acquisition in adults. Neurolinguistics maps brain structures to language processing using aphasia studies, imaging, and computational modelling. Applied linguistics engineers solutions for language teaching, translation, language planning, and natural language processing, drawing on sociology and anthropology alongside linguistic theory. Forensic linguistics applies linguistic analysis to legal evidence, including authorship disputes and asylum interviews, a use whose validity and ethics remain contested. Clinical linguistics informs speech-language pathology, and computational linguistics builds statistical and rule-based models for machine translation, speech recognition, and text analysis. Evolutionary linguistics applies sociobiological and cultural-evolution theory to the emergence and spread of language, while language documentation creates lasting audio, video, and textual records of endangered languages, rooted in Boasian field anthropology.

Open questions include whether deep-time macrofamilies can be validated beyond the comparative method's reach, whether generative syntax reflects an innate universal grammar or emerges from general cognition and use, how encapsulated language processing is from other cognitive systems, how far applied linguistics extends beyond applying theory, and how to balance the theoretical primacy of speech with the practical indispensability of written corpora.
