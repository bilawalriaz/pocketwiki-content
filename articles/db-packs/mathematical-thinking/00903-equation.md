# Equation

An equation is a mathematical statement that two expressions are equal, written with the equals sign "=" between a left-hand side and a right-hand side. It encodes a relationship between quantities so that unknown values can be determined from known ones, which is why equations are central to every branch of mathematics and its scientific applications. The equals sign was introduced by Robert Recorde in 1557.

An equation is solved by substituting values for its variables, the unknown quantities represented by letters, until both sides match. A single equation behaves in one of two ways. An identity is true for every value of its variables and states a general rule. A conditional equation is true only for specific values, and those values are its solutions. Solving relies on a balance principle: performing the same reversible operation on both sides, such as adding the same number, subtracting, multiplying or dividing by a non-zero constant, or applying a valid algebraic identity, preserves the equality. Applying a function to both sides, for example squaring, can introduce extraneous solutions that emerge from the transformation but do not satisfy the original equation, so candidates must be checked against it.

Polynomial equations, the most studied class, are built from sums of variable powers. They are classified by degree, the highest exponent on a single variable: degree 1 is linear, degree 2 is quadratic, degree 3 is cubic. Equations with one variable are univariate; those with several are multivariate. The Abel–Ruffini theorem, proved by Niels Henrik Abel and Paolo Ruffini in the nineteenth century, shows that the general polynomial of degree five or higher cannot always be solved using radicals, meaning closed-form expressions built from roots. Systems of linear equations, collections of linear relations in several unknowns solved together, underpin linear algebra and appear throughout science and engineering.

Equations also describe geometry. Analytic geometry, built on the Cartesian coordinates introduced by René Descartes in the seventeenth century, translates shape into algebra: a plane in three-dimensional space is ax + by + cz + d = 0, a circle centred at the origin is x² + y² = r². Parametric equations express each coordinate as a function of a parameter, a known quantity often named with an early-alphabet letter such as t; the unit circle is x = cos t, y = sin t. This turns geometric questions about curves and surfaces into algebraic ones.

Number theory focuses on a narrower target. A Diophantine equation, named after Diophantus of Alexandria, asks for integer solutions and typically has fewer equations than unknowns, so solutions form lattice points on curves or surfaces. Related to these is the distinction between algebraic numbers, real or complex values that are roots of some non-zero polynomial with rational coefficients, and transcendental numbers, which are not roots of any such polynomial; π is transcendental. Proving that a specific constant beyond π and e is transcendental is notoriously hard.

A different kind of equation governs change. A differential equation relates a function to its derivatives, capturing rates of change in physics, chemistry, biology and economics. Ordinary differential equations (ODEs) involve a function of one independent variable, while partial differential equations (PDEs) involve functions of several variables and their partial derivatives, and describe heat flow, sound and fluid motion. Solutions can be exact closed-form expressions or, for most realistic PDEs, numerical approximations. Related families include integral equations, difference equations for discrete processes, and stochastic differential equations, which include random terms.

Two frontier results illustrate the limits of the subject. Hilbert's tenth problem, resolved by Yuri Matiyasevich in 1970, showed that there is no general algorithm to decide whether an arbitrary Diophantine equation has an integer solution. For polynomials, Galois theory fully characterises solvability by radicals, but finding roots of high-degree polynomials efficiently remains computationally demanding.
