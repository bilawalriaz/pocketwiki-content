# Line (geometry)

A line is an infinitely long, one-dimensional figure with no thickness and no curvature. It is the idealisation of a physical straightedge, a taut string, or a light ray, and it is the basic building block of Euclidean geometry. Euclid's *Elements* called a line a "breadthless length" that "lies evenly with its points," but that description relied on physical intuition rather than logic. Modern mathematics treats a line as either a primitive notion whose behaviour is fixed by axioms, or as the set of points that satisfy a linear equation.

## Core properties

A Euclidean line has three defining properties. Any two distinct points determine exactly one line. Two distinct lines meet in at most one point. In two dimensions, lines that do not meet are called parallel. In three or more dimensions, non-intersecting lines are either parallel (lying in the same plane) or skew (not coplanar). All Euclidean lines are congruent to one another, and any line can be slid, rotated, or translated to coincide with any other.

## Coordinate equations

In a Cartesian plane, every line is the set of points $(x, y)$ satisfying a linear equation $ax + by = c$, where $a$ and $b$ are not both zero. The slope-intercept form $y = mx + b$ describes a non-vertical line by its slope $m$ and y-intercept $b$; vertical lines correspond to $b = 0$ in the general form. A line through two points $P_0(x_0, y_0)$ and $P_1(x_1, y_1)$ can be written as $(y - y_0)(x_1 - x_0) = (y_1 - y_0)(x - x_0)$.

In three dimensions, a line is the intersection of two non-parallel planes, so it is described by two simultaneous linear equations rather than one.

The Hesse normal form $x \cos \varphi + y \sin \varphi - p = 0$ encodes a line by the length $p$ of the perpendicular from the origin and the angle $\varphi$ that perpendicular makes with the $x$-axis. In polar coordinates, a line that misses the origin is $r = \frac{p}{\cos(\theta - \varphi)}$, while a line through the origin at angle $\alpha$ to the $x$-axis satisfies $\theta = \alpha$ or $\theta = \alpha + \pi$.

## Parametric and vector forms

An alternative writes coordinates as functions of a parameter $t$: $x = x_0 + at$, $y = y_0 + bt$, $z = z_0 + ct$, where $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ is a known point on the line and $(a, b, c)$ is a direction vector. The vector form $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a} + \lambda(\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a})$ describes the line through points with position vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$. Restricting $\lambda \geq 0$ or $\lambda \leq 0$ gives a ray starting at $\mathbf{a}$, and restricting $0 \leq \lambda \leq 1$ gives the line segment between the two points.

## Collinearity

Three or more points are collinear when they lie on a single line. In coordinates, points $X$, $Y$, $Z$ are collinear if the determinant of $\begin{bmatrix}1 & x_1 & x_2 \\ 1 & y_1 & y_2 \\ 1 & z_1 & z_2\end{bmatrix}$ is zero, or equivalently, if the slope between every pair of points is the same. This determinant test generalises to higher dimensions through the rank of an analogous matrix.

## Relations with curves and triangles

A line can meet a conic in several ways: as a tangent touching at one point, a secant crossing at two points, an exterior line that misses the conic, or a directrix, which is the line used in the standard focus-directrix definition of a conic. More generally, a line may be an $i$-secant, meeting an algebraic curve in $i$ points, or an asymptote that the curve approaches without touching.

Triangles and quadrilaterals have named lines associated with them, including the Euler line, Simson lines, central lines, the Newton line joining the midpoints of a quadrilateral's diagonals, and the Pascal and Pappus lines that arise from hexagons inscribed in conics.

## Generalisations

The concept of a line shifts with the geometry. In differential geometry, a "line" may be a geodesic, the shortest path between two points on a curved surface. In projective geometry, a line is modelled as a 2-dimensional vector space. In elliptic geometry, lines appear as great circles on a sphere with opposite points identified, or as planes through the origin. These constructions preserve the two-point uniqueness property even though their visual appearance differs from a Euclidean line.

Modern axiomatic systems, most notably David Hilbert's, formalised these behaviours with rigorous axioms in the early twentieth century, after non-Euclidean, projective, and affine geometries had already begun generalising the concept in the late nineteenth century. Whether a line is best treated as a primitive notion or defined analytically depends on the framework.
