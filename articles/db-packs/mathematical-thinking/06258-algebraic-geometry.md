# Algebraic geometry

Algebraic geometry studies geometric shapes by encoding them as the solution sets of polynomial equations. A circle, a parabola, a sphere, an elliptic curve, and a far more complicated surface such as the Togliatti surface of degree five are all examples of **algebraic varieties**. The discipline uses tools from **commutative algebra** (the algebra of polynomial rings and their ideals) to translate geometric questions into algebraic ones, and back.

## From equations to shapes

A point of the plane lies on an algebraic curve when its coordinates satisfy a polynomial equation. The unit sphere in three-dimensional space is the set of all points (x, y, z) with x² + y² + z² − 1 = 0. Intersecting that sphere with the plane x + y + z = 0 gives a slanted circle, defined by two simultaneous equations. Any **algebraic set** is the vanishing set V(S) of some collection S of polynomials: the points where every polynomial in S equals zero.

Standard examples include lines, circles, parabolas, ellipses, hyperbolas, cubic curves such as elliptic curves, and quartic curves such as lemniscates and Cassini ovals. The same machinery handles high-degree surfaces and higher-dimensional objects defined by many equations in many variables.

## The algebra–geometry correspondence

Two operations link subsets of affine space and collections of polynomials. For a subset U, the set I(U) of all polynomials vanishing on U is an **ideal** of the polynomial ring (closed under addition and multiplication by any polynomial). For a set of polynomials S, V(S) is its vanishing set. Two foundational results govern this link:

- **Hilbert's basis theorem** says every ideal in a polynomial ring is generated by finitely many polynomials, so algebraic sets are described by finite data.
- **Hilbert's Nullstellensatz** says that, over an algebraically closed field, the polynomials vanishing on V(S) are exactly the **radical** of the ideal generated by S. With enough algebraic closure, geometry and algebra recover each other.

The closed sets of the **Zariski topology** on affine space are precisely the algebraic sets, a topology that mirrors the polynomial ring's structure. An algebraic set is **irreducible** when it cannot be written as the union of two smaller algebraic sets; irreducible algebraic sets are called **varieties**. Every algebraic set splits uniquely into finitely many irreducible components.

The regular (polynomial) functions on a variety V form its **coordinate ring** k[V], the quotient of the ambient polynomial ring by I(V). A **morphism** between affine varieties is given by polynomial coordinate functions, and morphisms correspond to ring homomorphisms between coordinate rings running in the opposite direction. This equivalence of categories seeds modern scheme theory.

## Projective space and points at infinity

A variety's birational type and topology often depend on its behaviour at infinity. To capture this, affine space is embedded in **projective space**, obtained by adding points where parallel lines meet. In the projective plane, the parabola y = x² has a smooth point at infinity whose tangent is the line at infinity, while the cubic y = x³ has a **cusp** there, a difference the Riemann–Roch theorem forces. Because projective space records such differences and sharpens classical results such as Bézout's theorem on the number of intersection points, it is the natural setting for most of the theory.

## Subfields

Twentieth-century algebraic geometry split into branches. **Real algebraic geometry** studies real solutions; order matters, so x² + y² − a = 0 is a circle for a > 0 and empty for a < 0. Researchers also study **semi-algebraic sets**, defined by polynomial equalities and inequalities. **Diophantine** and **arithmetic geometry** study varieties over the rationals, number fields, finite fields, function fields, and p-adic fields. **Singularity theory** focuses on the singular points of varieties. **Computational algebraic geometry** designs algorithms and software to manipulate explicitly given varieties.

## The modern abstract viewpoint

The twentieth century regrounded the subject in increasingly abstract algebra, culminating in Alexander Grothendieck's **scheme theory** in the early 1960s. Schemes extend the idea of a point: in the classical setting, a point of an affine variety corresponds (via the Nullstellensatz) to a **maximal ideal** of the coordinate ring, while a point of the corresponding affine scheme corresponds to any **prime ideal**, so a single scheme point can represent a whole subvariety. This uses **sheaf theory** in parallel with differential and analytic geometry and unifies classical algebraic geometry with algebraic number theory. **Wiles' proof of Fermat's Last Theorem** is the most celebrated demonstration of the approach's power. Further extensions include algebraic spaces, algebraic stacks (Artin and Deligne–Mumford), and **derived algebraic geometry**, which replaces commutative rings with differential-graded or simplicial analogues.

## Computational methods

Two algorithmic pillars dominate. **Gröbner bases**, introduced by Bruno Buchberger in 1965, are well-chosen generators of a polynomial ideal that let one decide whether a variety is empty, compute its dimension and degree, and remove unwanted components. Worst-case complexity is doubly exponential, but Lazard's 1979 algorithm and the Faugère F4 and F5 algorithms often make computations with varieties of degree above 100 routine. **Cylindrical algebraic decomposition**, introduced by George E. Collins in 1973, implements the Tarski–Seidenberg quantifier-elimination theorem for the reals and can in principle solve every problem of real algebraic geometry expressible in first-order logic, though its doubly exponential complexity limits it in practice to about four variables. A complementary **numerical algebraic geometry** uses homotopy continuation with floating-point arithmetic.

## A short history

Geometric work on equations goes back to the Hellenistic Greeks: Menaechmus around 350 BC studied the Delian problem by intersecting conics, and Archimedes and Apollonius developed coordinate-like methods for conic sections in the third century BC. Medieval mathematicians including Omar Khayyam solved cubics algebraically and interpreted them geometrically, though Jeffrey Oaks argues the systematic study of curves via equations began with Descartes. In the seventeenth century, Vieta, Descartes, and Fermat introduced coordinate geometry, and Pascal and Desargues developed projective geometry. During the nineteenth century, work on Abelian integrals led Bernhard Riemann to **Riemann surfaces**, while algebraisation produced Hilbert's basis theorem, the Nullstellensatz, and Macaulay's multivariate resultant. In the 1930s, B. L. van der Waerden, Oscar Zariski, and André Weil rebuilt the foundations on commutative algebra; in the 1950s and 1960s, Jean-Pierre Serre and Grothendieck introduced sheaf and scheme theory, stabilising the field by the 1970s. **Elliptic curves**, the prototypical abelian varieties, were central to Wiles' proof of Fermat's Last Theorem and to elliptic-curve cryptography.

Today the ideas of algebraic geometry appear in statistics, control theory, robotics, error-correcting codes, phylogenetics, geometric modelling, string theory, and integer programming.
