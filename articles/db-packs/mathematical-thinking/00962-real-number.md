# Real number

A real number is a value that measures a continuous one-dimensional quantity such as length, duration, or temperature, and can be written as an infinite decimal expansion. The set of all real numbers, written ℝ, contains every rational number (integers and fractions) and every irrational number (algebraic numbers like √2, transcendental numbers like π and e). What makes ℝ special is a property called Dedekind completeness: every non-empty subset of ℝ that has an upper bound has a least upper bound, called a supremum.

Dedekind completeness, together with the rules of arithmetic and a total order, is what distinguishes ℝ from the rationals ℚ. The set {x ∈ ℚ : x² < 2} is bounded above in ℚ but has no least rational upper bound, since any candidate is beaten by a smaller one. In ℝ the same set has √2 as its supremum. Dedekind completeness implies the Archimedean property (for any real x there is an integer n with n > x), the existence of a square root for every positive real, and the fact that every odd-degree polynomial with real coefficients has a real root. These consequences are why ℝ is called a real closed field, and they are the reason calculus can be built on ℝ without gaps.

Arithmetic in ℝ is the familiar arithmetic of school. Addition and multiplication are commutative, associative, and distributive, with identities 0 and 1, additive inverse −a for every a, and multiplicative inverse a⁻¹ for every nonzero a. A total order < satisfies trichotomy (for any two reals a and b, exactly one of a < b, a = b, b < a holds) and transitivity, and it is compatible with the operations: a < b implies a + c < b + c, and 0 < a together with 0 < b implies 0 < ab. Subtraction, division, and absolute value are derived from these primitives. The natural numbers ℕ, integers ℤ, and rationals ℚ all embed into ℝ as ordered subfields, so every familiar rational is a real number.

Decimal notation captures this structure. A non-negative real corresponds to a decimal expansion bₖ…b₀.a₁a₂…, interpreted as the sum of the integer part and a series of fractional terms. Formally, the number is the least upper bound of the successive truncations, and the Archimedean property guarantees the integer part exists while Dedekind completeness guarantees the supremum does. Every real has at least one decimal expansion, and excluding those that end in infinitely many 9s makes the correspondence one-to-one. The same construction works in any base B ≥ 2.

ℝ is also complete as a metric space. A Cauchy sequence is one whose terms eventually come arbitrarily close: for every ε > 0 there is an N past which any two terms differ by less than ε. A sequence converges to a limit x if its terms eventually come within ε of x. In ℝ the two notions coincide: a sequence converges if and only if it is Cauchy. This equivalence fails in ℚ: the decimal approximations of √2 form a Cauchy sequence of rationals, but √2 is not in ℚ, so the sequence has no rational limit. Topological completeness is what allows calculus to prove a limit exists, for instance for the exponential series eˣ = ∑ xⁿ/n!, by showing the partial sums are Cauchy, without computing the limit.

The Dedekind-complete ordered field is unique up to isomorphism, which is why "the real numbers" refers to a single structure rather than many. Two standard constructions from ℚ yield it: Dedekind cuts (partitions of ℚ into a lower set with no greatest element and an upper set) and equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences of rationals.

The size of ℝ is uncountable. Cantor's 1874 diagonal argument shows that any list of reals leaves some real out, and the cardinality of ℝ, called the continuum and written 𝔠 or 2^ℵ₀, is strictly greater than ℵ₀, the cardinality of ℕ. The Continuum Hypothesis (CH) asserts that no cardinality lies strictly between ℵ₀ and 𝔠. Paul Cohen proved in 1963 that CH is independent of ZFC: it can be neither proved nor disproved from the standard axioms of set theory.

ℝ also supports the Lebesgue measure, the unique translation-invariant measure on ℝ normalised so that the unit interval has measure 1. Under the Axiom of Choice, non-measurable sets exist, the standard example being a Vitali set. As a vector space over ℚ, ℝ has a basis called a Hamel basis, whose existence requires the Axiom of Choice but which has no explicit description, and Choice likewise gives a well-ordering of ℝ, though not the familiar one.

In computation, a real is computable if an algorithm can produce its digits to any requested precision. Only countably many reals are computable, so almost every real is uncomputable, and even for computable reals, testing whether two are equal is undecidable. Practical computing uses finite-precision floating-point approximations, typically about 16 decimal digits, which violate the field axioms (for example, associativity of addition), and the study of the resulting errors is numerical analysis.

Greek mathematicians discovered around 500 BC that √2 is irrational, and Eudoxus (c. 390–340 BC) defined equality of irrational proportions in a way that anticipated Dedekind cuts. Indian, Chinese, and Arabic mathematicians of the Middle Ages accepted zero, negative numbers, and fractions. Simon Stevin established modern decimal notation in the 16th century, and Descartes coined "real" in the 17th century to distinguish these numbers from the new "imaginary" roots of polynomials. Lambert and Legendre proved π irrational in 1761–1794, Hermite proved e transcendental in 1873, and Lindemann proved π transcendental in 1882. The rigorous definition of ℝ was supplied in 1872 independently by Dedekind (cuts) and Cantor (Cauchy sequences), and in 1874 Cantor proved ℝ is uncountable.

ℝ admits several extensions. The complex numbers ℂ are algebraically closed but cannot be ordered. The affinely extended reals [−∞, +∞] are compact and totally ordered but no longer form a field. The real projective line ℝ ∪ {∞} is compact and allows division by zero but is again not a field. The hyperreal and surreal numbers are non-Archimedean ordered fields containing both infinitesimals and infinite numbers; the hyperreals in particular underpin nonstandard analysis, in which the derivative is defined using actual infinitesimal ratios. In physics, position, mass, charge, and the constants of nature are modelled as reals, and the manifolds and Hilbert spaces of classical mechanics, quantum mechanics, general relativity, and the Standard Model are built on ℝ, though whether spacetime is fundamentally continuous or discrete at the Planck scale remains an open question in quantum gravity.
