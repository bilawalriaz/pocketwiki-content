# Mathematical constant

A mathematical constant is a number whose value is fixed by an unambiguous definition, usually given a special symbol (often a Greek or Latin letter) or a name so it can be reused across problems. Constants such as π and e appear in geometry, number theory, statistics, and calculus alike. Some arise from a fundamental principle, like the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter. Others are notable mainly for historical reasons. Every named mathematical constant is a definable number, and most are also computable; Chaitin's constant is a significant exception.

## Basic constants

**Pythagoras' constant √2** is the unique positive number that, when multiplied by itself, gives 2. Geometrically it is the diagonal of a unit square and the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs of length 1. It is irrational, possibly the first number proved so, and algebraic. Its value begins 1.41421 35623 73095... Before electronic calculators, the rational approximation 99/70 ≈ 1.41429 was common, accurate to better than 1/10,000. Its continued fraction is the repeating pattern [1; 2, 2, 2, ...].

**Archimedes' constant π** is the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter, hence also the area of a unit circle. It appears in the Gaussian integral, the complex roots of unity, and Cauchy distributions in probability, and surfaces throughout physics. The ground-state wave function of the hydrogen atom, for example, contains 1/√(π a₀³), where a₀ is the Bohr radius. π is irrational and transcendental, beginning 3.14159 26535 89793... Unusually accurate rational approximations are 22/7 and 355/113.

**Euler's number e** can be defined as the limit of (1 + 1/n)ⁿ as n → ∞, and it underlies the exponential function x ↦ eˣ. Jacob Bernoulli discovered it through compound interest: continuous compounding at rate R turns $1 into eᴿ dollars. In probability, if a slot machine wins once in n plays and is played n times, the probability of never winning tends to 1/e. The same constant solves the hat-check (derangement) problem, where the probability that no guest receives his own hat also approaches 1/e. e is irrational and transcendental, beginning 2.71828 18284 59045...

**The imaginary unit i** extends the real numbers to the complex plane. Its defining property is i² = −1, which no real number satisfies. As with any nonzero real number, −1 has two square roots: i and −i. In electrical and control engineering, j is often used instead, because i is already taken for electric current.

**The golden ratio φ** = (1 + √5)/2 ≈ 1.61803 39887... appears in pentagonal geometry (a regular pentagon's diagonal is φ times its side) and gives an explicit formula for the Fibonacci numbers. Kepler showed φ is the limit of the ratio of consecutive Fibonacci numbers. Its continued fraction converges the slowest of any irrational number, making it a worst case in Lagrange's approximation theorem and an extremal case of Hurwitz's inequality; this is thought to explain why angles near φ appear in phyllotaxis, the spiral arrangement of leaves.

## Constants in advanced mathematics

The **Euler–Mascheroni constant γ** is the limiting difference between the harmonic series and the natural logarithm: γ = lim(n→∞)(−ln n + Σ(k=1 to n) 1/k) ≈ 0.57721 56649... It appears in Mertens' third theorem, the growth rate of the divisor function, the gamma function, and the zeta function. Despite its ubiquity, it is unknown whether γ is rational, irrational, algebraic, or transcendental.

**Apéry's constant ζ(3)** = Σ(1/n³) is the Riemann zeta function evaluated at 3, approximately 1.20205 69031... Euler solved the Basel problem by giving ζ(2) = π²/6 in closed form, but no analogous closed form is known for ζ(3), and none is conjectured to exist. Roger Apéry proved in 1979 that ζ(3) is irrational, though whether it is algebraic or transcendental remains open. It enters physics in the higher-order corrections to the electron's gyromagnetic ratio.

**Catalan's constant G** = Σ(−1)ⁿ/(2n+1)² ≈ 0.91596 55941... is the Dirichlet beta function at s = 2. Named after Charles Eugène Catalan, it appears in combinatorics, number theory, and the mass distribution of spiral galaxies. Its irrationality and transcendence are strongly suspected but unproven.

The **Feigenbaum constants α ≈ 2.5029 and δ ≈ 4.6692** govern period-doubling bifurcations in the logistic map and other iterative processes. Mitchell Feigenbaum showed they are universal across such maps, much as π governs circles and e governs exponential growth. Neither is known to be irrational, let alone transcendental, though proofs of their universality exist.

## Curiosities and notation

Liouville's constant, the sum of 10^(−n!) over n ≥ 1, was the first number proved transcendental. Champernowne's constant 0.1234567891011... is a simple normal number in base 10. Chaitin's constant Ω, the probability that a randomly chosen Turing machine halts, is transcendental and normal but not computable.

Constants are typically written by giving their decimal expansion, but irrationals have no finite or repeating expansion, and expansions are not unique: 0.999... and 1 are the same number. Modern computers have pushed π, e, and √2 beyond 10¹⁴ digits, while Ludolph van Ceulen spent much of the 1500s computing only the first 35 digits of π. Some constants, such as Graham's number, are so large they need Knuth's up-arrow notation to write down. The convention of symbolizing constants with letters was standardised by René Descartes in the 17th century and Leonhard Euler in the 18th, using lowercase Latin a, b, c and Greek α, β, γ for general constants; more important ones earn richer symbols like ℵ₀, β*, or B₂. The names "googol" (10¹⁰⁰) and "googolplex" (10^googol) were coined by Edward Kasner's 9-year-old nephew.
