# Applied mathematics

Applied mathematics is the use of mathematical methods to solve practical problems in fields such as physics, engineering, medicine, biology, finance, business, computer science, and industry. The term also names the profession in which mathematicians build and study mathematical models.

Practical problems have repeatedly driven the creation of new mathematics, which later became topics of pure mathematics; pure ideas then find new applications. This two-way exchange is the defining feature of the field.

## Historical roots

Applied mathematics grew up alongside physics. Its classical core was applied analysis, especially differential equations, approximation theory (asymptotic, variational, and numerical methods), and applied probability. Before the mid-19th century the line between mathematician and physicist was blurred, and American universities sometimes taught classical mechanics in applied mathematics departments. That overlap is why fluid mechanics still appears in applied math curricula.

## Distinguishing the term

Mathematicians separate three overlapping ideas. Applied mathematics means developing mathematical methods for practical use. Applicable mathematics means any area, even pure subjects such as number theory, that solves real problems; cryptography is the standard example. Applications of mathematics means a non-mathematician using existing tools, such as a biologist applying a population model. Some mathematicians, including Poincaré and Arnold, reject the label "applied mathematics" and describe only applications. The rise of high-performance computing has also created computational mathematics, computational science, and computational engineering, usually treated as interdisciplinary rather than as subfields.

No consensus exists on which branches count as applied mathematics, partly because universities organize departments differently.

## Associated mathematical sciences

Applied mathematics overlaps heavily with several neighbouring fields.

Engineering. Every branch of engineering uses mathematics, and several engineering-adjacent areas have become mathematical research fields. Continuum mechanics underlies civil, mechanical, and aerospace engineering, and has generated deep open problems in partial differential equations, of which the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness question is the best known. Control theory, the mathematical foundation of control engineering, plays an enabling role in electrical, mechanical, and aerospace engineering.

Statistics. Statistics has the largest overlap with applied mathematics. Statistical theory relies on probability and decision theory, and uses algebra and combinatorics for the design of experiments. Many institutions house statisticians and applied mathematicians in a single department of mathematical sciences.

Computer science. Computing relies on logic, algebra, discrete mathematics, graph theory, and combinatorics. Numerical analysis sits at the intersection of applied math and computer science, providing the algorithms that make scientific computing possible.

Other fields. Operations research and management science draw on optimization and probability, often taught in engineering or business faculties. Actuarial science applies probability, statistics, and economic theory to risk in insurance and finance. Mathematical economics uses statistics, probability, mathematical programming, operations research, and game theory to formalize and analyze economic theories; it is related to but distinct from financial mathematics.

## Academic structure and growth

Institutions vary widely. Some have a single mathematics department; others split pure and applied, and statistics is often a separate department at research universities. Many applied mathematics programs consist of cross-listed courses and jointly appointed faculty. The Brown University Division of Applied Mathematics is the oldest applied math program in the United States, while Cambridge's Department of Applied Mathematics and Theoretical Physics, home of the Lucasian Chair once held by Newton, Dirac, and Hawking, is a notable European example. Since World War II, growth in economics, biology, and computing has driven the creation of new areas, including game theory, social choice theory, mathematical finance, and data science.

## Professional societies

The Society for Industrial and Applied Mathematics (SIAM) is the main international organization, with about 14,000 individual members as of 2024. The American Mathematical Society maintains a separate Applied Mathematics Group.
