# Logic

Logic is the study of correct reasoning. It is generally divided into formal logic, which analyzes deductively valid inferences and logical truths through abstract symbols, and informal logic, which evaluates arguments, fallacies, and critical thinking in natural language. Logic underpins philosophy, mathematics, computer science, and linguistics by examining whether premises support conclusions. Systems of logic are frameworks for assessing arguments; Aristotelian logic dominated the West for over two millennia before modern formal logic emerged in the late 19th century.

## Historical Development

Aristotle developed term logic in antiquity (*Organon*, *Prior Analytics*), while Stoic logic, Nyaya, and Mohism arose independently. In the Islamic world, Ibn Sina founded Avicennian logic, displacing Aristotelian logic as dominant, and Fakhr al-Din al-Razi formulated early inductive logic. By mid–late 19th century, foundations-of-mathematics work (Frege's *Begriffsschrift*, Boole's Boolean algebra, Peirce's logic of relatives) spurred modern symbolic logic. In the early 20th century, the logicist program (Frege, Whitehead, Russell) aimed to reduce mathematics to logic, but Russell's paradox crippled Frege's *Grundgesetze*, and Gödel's incompleteness theorems defeated Hilbert's program.

## Formal and Informal Logic

Formal logic studies logical form independent of content using abstract symbols. It targets deductively valid arguments, where true premises guarantee a true conclusion, following rules of inference such as modus ponens ("p; if p then q; therefore q"). An alternative definition treats logic as the study of logical truths: propositions true under all interpretations of their non-logical terms, such as "either it is raining, or it is not." These definitions differ but connect: if p entails q, "if p then q" is logically true.

Informal logic arose because natural language is often ambiguous, vague, and context-dependent. Its definition is disputed, variously treated as the study of natural-language arguments, argumentation standards, non-deductive arguments, or informal fallacies.

## Premises, Truth, and Structure

Premises and conclusions must be truth-bearers, meaning they can be true or false. Contemporary philosophy treats them as propositions or sentences rather than thoughts (psychologism is not widely accepted). Propositions have internal structure: complex propositions combine simpler ones via connectives such as ∧ (and) and → (if...then), while simple ones consist of singular terms and predicates, as in "Mars is red," where "red" is a predicate applied to the term "Mars." Truth tables show how connectives determine the truth of complex expressions from their inputs; for example, p ∧ q is true only when both p and q are true. Other connectives include ¬ (not), ∨ (or), and ↑ (Sheffer stroke).

## Deductive and Ampliative Reasoning

Deductive validity depends only on form, holds *a priori*, and holds by necessity, three features identified by Tarski. Validity does not require actually true premises. Because deduction is truth-preserving, some consider it uninformative, though a counter-argument distinguishes surface information (explicit) from depth information (total), showing deduction makes implicit depth information explicit, as in mathematical proofs.

Ampliative arguments yield new information but make conclusions merely probable. They are defeasible and non-monotonic, meaning conclusions may be retracted when new information appears. Induction generalizes from observations ("all ravens I've seen are black" implies "all ravens are black"), while abduction reasons to the best explanation (breadcrumbs at dawn suggest a midnight snack, not a burglar). Terminology is inconsistent: some authors use "induction" for all non-deductive arguments; others add conductive arguments or restrict induction to statistical grounds.

## Fallacies

A fallacy is flawed reasoning, even if the conclusion happens to be true. Mill restricts fallacies to arguments that appear correct, explaining their psychological allure, though this restriction is controversial. Formal fallacies stem from argument form, such as denying the antecedent, while informal fallacies stem from content or context and are categorized as ambiguity, presumption, or relevance.

## Formal Systems and Systems of Logic

A formal system comprises a formal language (alphabet and syntactic rules producing well-formed formulas), axioms (statements accepted without proof), and a proof system such as natural deduction or a sequent calculus. Some add semantics mapping expressions to denotations, often truth values. A system is sound if its proofs never derive non-entailed conclusions, and complete if it derives every entailed conclusion.

Aristotelian logic classifies syllogisms, three-proposition arguments sharing major, minor, and middle terms, as valid or invalid. Classical logic comprises propositional logic (full propositions as units) and first-order logic, which adds singular terms, predicates, and quantifiers (∃, ∀). It rests on excluded middle, double negation elimination, explosion, and bivalence. Extended logics retain these and add modal operators (◊ possible, □ necessary) or higher-order quantification over predicates.

Deviant logics reject classical intuitions: intuitionistic logic drops excluded middle and double negation elimination, grounding truth in verification; multi-valued and fuzzy logics reject bivalence; paraconsistent logics avoid explosion, often motivated by dialetheism, the view that some contradictions are true (Priest).

## Current Research and Debates

Research areas include metalogic (soundness, completeness, decidability), mathematical logic (model theory, set theory, computability), computational logic (theorem provers, Prolog, Boolean circuits), and formal semantics of natural language (Montague, Partee). Bayesian approaches treat reasoning as updating credences (degrees of belief as subjective probabilities).

Open questions include the definition of informal logic, what counts as a logic, whether deduction is uninformative, whether fallacies must appear correct, and whether logic is knowable *a priori*. Putnam and Maddy argue that logical truths depend on the empirical world, citing quantum mechanics as allegedly refuting distributivity and supporting quantum logic.
