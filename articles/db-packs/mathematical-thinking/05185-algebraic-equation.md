# Algebraic equation

An algebraic equation is an equation of the form P = 0, where P is a polynomial, usually with rational coefficients. A polynomial is a sum of terms, each a coefficient multiplied by a variable raised to a whole-number power, such as 3x² or −5x. For example, x⁵ − 3x + 1 = 0 is an algebraic equation in one variable with integer coefficients, while y⁴ + (xy)/2 − x³/3 + xy² + y² + 1/7 = 0 is one in several variables with rational coefficients. Equations containing sine, exponentials, or 1/x are not polynomial, because those functions are not polynomials, though eᵀx² + (1/T)xy + sin(T)z − 2 = 0 becomes a polynomial equation in x, y, and z if T is treated as a parameter.

The highest exponent of the variable in the polynomial is its degree. Linear, quadratic, cubic, and quartic equations have degrees 1, 2, 3, and 4. Solving an equation means finding all values of the variable that make the polynomial equal to zero, called roots or solutions. A degree-n polynomial has at most n roots, and solving it is equivalent to factoring it into aₙ(x − z₁)(x − z₂)⋯(x − zₙ) = 0, whose roots are z₁ through zₙ. A polynomial equation with rational coefficients can always be rewritten with integer coefficients by clearing denominators. Multiplying y⁴ + (xy)/2 = x³/3 − xy² + y² − 1/7 by 42 gives 42y⁴ + 21xy − 14x³ + 42xy² − 42y² + 6 = 0, an equivalent equation with the same solution set.

## Real and complex solutions

Not every polynomial equation has a real solution. For example, x² + 1 = 0 has no real root because no real number squared equals −1. The fundamental theorem of algebra states that every polynomial equation of degree one or more with complex coefficients has a complex solution, and complex roots of a real polynomial occur in conjugate pairs, giving x² + 1 = 0 the roots i and −i. Any monic polynomial of odd degree with real coefficients, such as x³ + 2x − 1, has at least one real root: its polynomial function is continuous, tends to −∞ as x → −∞ and to +∞ as x → +∞, and so by the intermediate value theorem must cross zero.

## Solving by radicals, and the degree-5 barrier

For centuries mathematicians sought solutions written as radical expressions, meaning finite combinations of the coefficients using addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and nth roots. The quadratic formula was described in words by Brahmagupta in 628 AD and derived again by al-Khwarizmi in the 9th century. It gives the discriminant Δ = b² − 4ac, which determines whether ax² + bx + c = 0 has two distinct real roots (Δ > 0), one repeated real root (Δ = 0), or two complex conjugate roots (Δ < 0). Cardano published radical solutions for degree 3, due to del Ferro and Tartaglia, and degree 4, due to Ferrari, in 1545, and the change of variable x = y − aₙ₋₁/(naₙ), developed by Euler for cubics, is a standard preliminary step that removes the second-highest term.

In 1824 Abel proved that no general formula using only arithmetic operations and roots can solve every polynomial equation of degree 5 or higher, and Galois theory gives a precise criterion for which particular equations of any degree are solvable by radicals. Some higher-degree equations, such as those associated with the cyclotomic polynomials of degree 5 and 17, do admit radical solutions, and Charles Hermite showed that degree-5 equations can be solved using elliptic functions. For equations with no closed-form solution, numerical methods such as Newton's method give approximate roots.

## Related areas of study

The study of polynomial equations underlies several branches of mathematics. Algebraic number theory examines univariate equations with rational coefficients. Field theory extends these ideas to settings where every element of an enlarged field satisfies some polynomial equation over the base field. Transcendental number theory studies the real numbers that are not roots of any such equation. A Diophantine equation is a polynomial equation, usually in several variables with integer coefficients, for which only integer solutions are sought. Algebraic geometry studies the full solution sets of multivariate polynomial equations in algebraically closed fields. Two equations are equivalent when they share a solution set, so P = Q and P − Q = 0 are the same problem in different form.
