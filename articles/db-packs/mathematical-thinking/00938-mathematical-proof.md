# Mathematical proof

A mathematical proof is a deductive argument that shows a statement follows logically from axioms and from theorems already established. Unlike empirical or inductive reasoning, which only suggest that something is plausible, a valid proof gives certainty: once every step is accepted, the conclusion cannot be denied without rejecting the rules of logic.

The modern idea of proof grew out of ancient Greek geometry. Around 600–500 BCE, Thales and Hippocrates of Chios produced some of the first known geometric proofs. By about 300 BCE, Euclid formalised the approach in the *Elements*: start from a small list of axioms, taken as self-evidently true, and from undefined basic terms such as point and line, then derive every further result by deduction. The *Elements* covered geometry, including the Pythagorean theorem, and number theory, including the irrationality of √2 and the infinitude of the primes. In the medieval Islamic world, Al-Hashimi extended the method by treating numbers abstractly rather than as lengths on a line, and around 1000 CE Al-Karaji introduced mathematical induction in *Al-Fakhri*, applying it to arithmetic progressions and binomial-type coefficients.

A proof in practice is a rigorous natural-language argument written to convince a reader. Standards of rigour have shifted over time and still differ between communities, so proof is not a single fixed object. In proof theory, however, a proof is modelled as a formal data structure: a finite sequence of formulas in a precise language, each one a logical consequence of earlier ones or of the axioms. The word itself comes from the Latin *probare*, "to test," reflecting that earlier arguments were closer to plausibility checks using pictures and analogies.

The simplest form is direct proof: combine axioms, definitions, and earlier theorems, and chain deductions until the desired conclusion appears. Several other patterns cover situations where a direct chain is awkward. Proof by mathematical induction proves a base case and then proves that if the statement holds for n it also holds for n+1, which establishes it for every natural number. Proof by contraposition proves "if p then q" by instead showing "if not q then not p," which is logically equivalent. Proof by contradiction, or *reductio ad absurdum*, assumes the statement to be proved is false and derives a logical impossibility, as in the classical argument that √2 is irrational. Proof by construction exhibits a concrete example to show something exists. Proof by exhaustion splits the claim into finitely many cases and checks each one; the four color theorem was settled this way in 1976 by checking 1,936 configurations with a computer. Nonconstructive proofs establish existence without producing the object, sometimes using probability to show an object must exist with certainty, and combinatorial proofs count the same set in two different ways to show two expressions are equal.

These techniques have limits. A statement is undecidable from a given set of axioms when neither it nor its negation can be proved. The parallel postulate is undecidable from Euclid's other axioms, and accepting or rejecting it yields Euclidean geometry or the non-Euclidean geometries. In 1931, Gödel's first incompleteness theorem showed that any sufficiently rich axiomatic system, including ZFC set theory, necessarily contains undecidable statements, so no fixed axiom system can settle every question expressible in its language.

The status of proof continues to be discussed. Philosophers still debate whether proofs are analytic, purely logical, or synthetic, involving real insight, a question raised by Kant and later contested by Quine. The 1976 four color proof raised a separate concern: if a result depends on a long computer search, programming or runtime errors could invalidate it, and such computer-assisted proofs remain controversial. Whether probabilistic evidence such as a primality test can ever count as a genuine proof is also disputed; some philosophers argue yes, most mathematicians say no, and experimental and heuristic methods are increasingly used alongside formal argument. By convention, a finished proof is marked Q.E.D., from the Latin *quod erat demonstrandum*, "that which was to be demonstrated."
