# Mathematics

Mathematics is the study of abstract objects such as numbers, geometric shapes, sets, functions, and probabilities, advanced through logical reasoning and proof rather than experiment. Results are established as theorems, and the field underpins science, engineering, medicine, finance, computer science, and the social sciences.

Mathematical objects are either abstractions from nature or entities stipulated by axioms, which are statements accepted as true without proof. A proof applies deductive rules to axioms, postulates, and earlier theorems. Because results follow by deduction rather than measurement, the relation between mathematics and reality has been debated since antiquity. Some branches, such as game theory, grow alongside their uses (applied mathematics); others find applications only later.

## Major areas

The 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification lists sixty-three first-level areas. The long-standing branches are:

- **Number theory** studies natural numbers, integers, and rationals. Founded by Euclid and Diophantus, modernized by Fermat and Euler, and matured with Legendre and Gauss. Fermat's Last Theorem, stated in 1637, needed scheme theory, category theory, and homological algebra for Andrew Wiles's 1994 proof. Goldbach's 1742 conjecture, that every even integer greater than 2 is a sum of two primes, is still open.
- **Geometry** began as surveying and architecture, gained Greek proof (Euclid's *Elements*, c. 300 BC), and split into synthetic and analytic geometry with Descartes' coordinates. Nineteenth-century non-Euclidean geometries, which reject the parallel postulate, helped trigger the foundational crisis.
- **Algebra** became autonomous when Viète introduced variables between 1540 and 1603. In the 19th century, variables came to represent non-numerical objects such as matrices, modular integers, and transformations, producing abstract algebra and category theory.
- **Calculus and analysis** were developed independently by Newton and Leibniz in the 17th century; Euler added functions in the 18th. Analysis now covers real and complex analysis, multivariable calculus, functional analysis, differential equations, and numerical analysis.
- **Discrete mathematics** studies countable structures where calculus does not directly apply. It includes combinatorics, graph theory, coding theory, and computational complexity. The four color theorem and optimal sphere packing have been solved; the P versus NP problem remains open.
- **Logic and set theory** grew from Cantor's diagonal argument, which established different sizes of infinity, and Russell's paradox, which exposed contradictions in naive set theory. Together with non-Euclidean geometry these provoked the foundational crisis, resolved by Hilbert's axiomatic set theory (formalism, c. 1910). Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that any consistent formal system containing arithmetic has true but unprovable statements. Brouwer's intuitionism rejected the law of excluded middle.
- **Computational mathematics** covers numerical analysis, computer algebra, automated proof assistants, algorithmics, and public key cryptography. The Feit–Thompson theorem has been formally verified by software.

## History

The word derives from Greek *máthēma*, meaning "something learned." Babylonians developed place-value sexagesimal numerals, still used for angles and time. Greeks introduced proof: Euclid's *Elements* is the most influential mathematics textbook ever written, Archimedes developed the method of exhaustion, and Apollonius, Hipparchus, and Diophantus advanced conics, trigonometry, and pre-modern algebra. India contributed the Hindu–Arabic numeral system, the sine and cosine, and early infinite series. The Islamic Golden Age of the 9th and 10th centuries added algebra (Al-Khwarizmi; the word itself comes from *al-jabr*), spherical trigonometry, and the decimal point. Early modern Europe produced Viète, Napier, Descartes, Newton, Leibniz, and Euler's 18th-century unification. Gauss dominated the 19th century and Gödel transformed foundations in the 20th.

## Relationship with other fields

Mathematics models phenomena, and predictive success depends on the model. Mercury's perihelion precession required general relativity, not Newton's gravity. Wigner called mathematics' "unreasonable effectiveness" unexplained: conic sections became Kepler's planetary ellipses, non-Euclidean geometry and manifolds describe spacetime, and the Dirac equation predicted the positron and the Ω⁻ baryon before their discovery. Statistics applies probability to data, differential equations model populations and chemical reactions, and the social sciences rely on statistics and idealized agents such as *Homo economicus*, which critics fault for ignoring limited information and altruism.

## Philosophy and practice

Plato held that mathematical abstractions exist independently of space and time, a position called Platonism that most working mathematicians tacitly accept. No consensus defines mathematics or settles whether it is a science or an art. Long or computer-assisted proofs, such as the 255-page Feit–Thompson proof, raise the question of whether such proofs carry certainty or only probability. The Fields Medal (1936, every four years, up to four recipients) is the field's top honor. Hilbert's 1900 list of 23 problems inspired the seven Millennium Prize Problems of 2000, each worth one million dollars; only the Poincaré conjecture, solved by Perelman, has been settled.
