# Discrete mathematics

Discrete mathematics is the branch of mathematics that studies **discrete** structures — those built from countable, separable pieces such as integers, graphs, and logical statements — as opposed to **continuous** structures like the real numbers studied in calculus. A set is discrete when it is either finite or can be put in one-to-one correspondence with the natural numbers; this property is called *countable*. There is no exact boundary for the field, but its modern prominence comes from one fact: digital computers operate in discrete steps and store data in discrete bits, so nearly every computational idea — algorithms, data, logic, networks — has a discrete shape.

## Core subfields

**Theoretical computer science** rests on discrete mathematics and draws heavily on graph theory and mathematical logic. It breaks into algorithms and data structures, computability (what can in principle be computed, tied to logic), complexity (the time, space, and other resources a computation needs), and automata and formal language theory. Petri nets and process algebras model running computer systems; discrete methods analyse VLSI circuits; computational geometry applies algorithms to geometric problems; image analysis applies them to pictures.

**Logic** studies valid reasoning — inference, consistency, soundness, and completeness. Most formulas and proofs are discrete: a proof is a finite tree (or directed acyclic graph) in which each inference step fuses its premise branches into one conclusion. Truth values are usually a two-element set {true, false}, though fuzzy logic uses continuous values, and infinitary logic studies infinite proof trees. The study of mathematical proof directly led to automated theorem proving and formal verification of safety-critical software.

**Set theory and combinatorics** sit at the heart of the field. Discrete mathematics focuses on the countable parts of set theory, beginning with Georg Cantor's distinction between kinds of infinite sets, motivated by trigonometric series. Combinatorics studies how discrete structures combine and arrange: enumerative combinatorics counts them (the *twelvefold way* unifies permutations, combinations, and partitions), analytic combinatorics derives asymptotic formulas using complex analysis, design theory studies subsets with prescribed intersection properties, order theory studies partially ordered sets, and partition theory — once part of number theory — studies integer partitions and connects to q-series and orthogonal polynomials.

**Graph theory** is sometimes grouped with combinatorics and sometimes treated as its own subject. A *graph* models relations and processes among objects and appears in physical, biological, and social systems; communication networks; data organisation; computation flow; and geometry and topology, including knot theory.

**Number theory and algebraic structures** are also discrete in flavour. Number theory studies integers, with applications to cryptography through modular arithmetic, Diophantine equations, congruences, primes, and primality testing; its analytic branch uses continuous techniques. Important discrete algebraic structures include Boolean algebra (the basis of logic gates and programming), relational algebra (databases), finite groups, rings, and fields (coding theory), and semigroups and monoids (formal languages).

**Information theory and coding theory** quantify information and design efficient, reliable transmission and storage. Both also touch continuous topics such as analog signals and encryption, but their core ideas are discrete.

## Discrete analogues of continuous mathematics

Many continuous concepts have discrete counterparts, and these are often what a computer can actually compute. In *discrete calculus*, a function on an integer interval is a sequence, defined explicitly, by formula, or *implicitly* by a *recurrence relation* (also called a difference equation) that links each term to its neighbours, replacing differentiation with finite differences and approximating differential equations. *Time scale calculus* unifies difference and differential equations for mixed discrete-and-continuous data; *hybrid dynamical systems* are a related model. *Discrete geometry* studies combinatorial properties of geometric collections, with plane tiling as a long-standing example. *Algebraic geometry* extends curves to finite fields via the spectra of polynomial rings, with Zariski tangent spaces bringing calculus-style tools into a finite setting. *Discrete modelling* fits discrete formulas, often recurrence relations, to data; *discretization* converts continuous models into discrete ones, as in numerical analysis.

## A short history

The four colour theorem was first stated in 1852. Hilbert's 1900 list of open problems included the consistency of arithmetic and Hilbert's tenth on Diophantine equations. Gödel's 1931 second incompleteness theorem showed arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency. In the Second World War, codebreaking at Bletchley Park — where Alan Turing helped build the first programmable digital electronic computer and wrote *On Computable Numbers* — drove cryptography and theoretical computer science forward. In 1970 Yuri Matiyasevich proved Hilbert's tenth problem unsolvable; in 1976 Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken proved the four colour theorem with substantial computer assistance. Discrete mathematics entered university curricula in the 1980s, shaped by ACM and MAA efforts into a course that builds mathematical maturity in first-year students and now serves as a prerequisite for some mathematics majors. High-school texts use it as preparation, much like pre-calculus. The Cold War sustained cryptography, public-key cryptography was developed, and telecommunications drove progress in graph theory and information theory. The **P = NP problem**, asking whether every problem whose solution can be verified quickly can also be solved quickly, remains a famous open question and carries a one-million-dollar Clay Mathematics Institute prize.
