# Domain (mathematical analysis)

In mathematical analysis, a **domain** is a non-empty, connected, open set in a topological space. The most common case is a non-empty connected open subset of the real coordinate space ℝⁿ or the complex coordinate space ℂⁿ, and such sets are routinely chosen as the domain of a function.

**Connectedness** is the subtlest ingredient. An open set is connected when it cannot be written as the union of two disjoint non-empty open sets. In ℝ a connected set is an interval; in ℝ² it is a region with no splits; in ℝ³ a solid with no gaps. Open means every point has a ball around it that stays inside the set, so a domain has none of its own boundary points; any boundary lies outside.

Examples: the whole real line, the entire complex plane, the open unit disk, and the open upper half-plane are all domains. The closed unit disk is not, because it contains its boundary. A union of two disjoint open intervals, such as (0,1) ∪ (2,3), is open but not connected, so it is not a domain.

## Conventions: domain versus region

Authors do not all use these words the same way. The most common modern convention defines a **domain** as a connected open set and a **region** as a domain together with some or all of its limit points. Under that convention, a *closed region* or *closed domain* is the union of a domain with all of its limit points, so the closed unit disk is a closed domain.

Other conventions exist. Carlo Miranda, in his monographs on elliptic partial differential equations, uses "region" for an open connected set and reserves "domain" for a more restrictive object: an internally connected perfect set, each of whose points is an accumulation point of interior points. Under his convention, if *A* is a region then its closure cl *A* is a domain. Some authors, including Townsend, treat "region" and "domain" as synonyms; some, like Churchill, do not even require a region to be connected or open. To remove the ambiguity, many writers fall back on the phrase "non-empty connected open subset".

## Varieties of domains

- **Bounded domain.** A domain contained in some ball, for instance the open unit disk. A bounded region is defined the same way.
- **Exterior (or external) domain.** A domain whose complement is bounded, for example the outside of a closed ball. Smoothness conditions on its boundary are sometimes added.
- **Complex domain.** Any connected open subset of the complex plane ℂ, used as the domain of definition of a holomorphic function. In several complex variables, the definition extends to connected open subsets of ℂⁿ.
- **Boundary regularity classes.** For integral theorems such as Green's theorem and Stokes' theorem, for Sobolev-space properties, and for defining measures and trace spaces on the boundary, one requires the boundary to be well-behaved. Standard choices are continuous, Lipschitz, C¹, and higher orders of smoothness.

In one, two, and three Euclidean dimensions, domains of finite extent are curves, surfaces, and solids, and their sizes are measured as length, area, and volume.

## Origin of the term

The precise definition of a domain as a non-empty open connected set is due to Constantin Carathéodory, in his 1918 book *Vorlesungen über reelle Funktionen*. He wrote: "An open point set is called connected if it cannot be represented as the sum of two open point sets. An open connected point set is called a *Gebiet*." Hans Hahn, in 1921, credited Carathéodory with this definition and noted that the German word *Gebiet* had previously been used as a loose synonym for "open set".

The rough idea is older. In the nineteenth and early twentieth century, "domain" and "region" were often used informally and interchangeably. Forsyth's 1893 treatise on functions of a complex variable spoke of a "region" and a "part of the *z*-plane" without formal definition; the first edition of Whittaker's *Modern Analysis* (1902) did the same. By the second edition (Whittaker and Watson, 1915), an "open region" was defined as the interior of a simple closed curve, and a "closed region" or "domain" as that interior together with its boundary curve. The French tradition, in Goursat's 1905 treatise, defined *région* or *aire* as a connected portion of the plane.

Because the terms have never been fixed across authors, modern texts often prefer the phrase "non-empty connected open subset" and let "domain" serve as a convenient shorthand.
