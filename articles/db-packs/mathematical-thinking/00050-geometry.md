# Geometry

Geometry is a branch of mathematics concerned with the properties of space: distance, shape, size, and the relative position of figures. A practitioner is called a geometer. Originally developed to model the physical world, geometry now applies across nearly all sciences, art, architecture, and graphics. Until the 19th century it was almost exclusively Euclidean. Discoveries such as non-Euclidean geometries and Gauss's Theorema Egregium then enlarged its scope, splitting the field into many subfields and shifting "space" from the physical world's three dimensions to any mathematical structure on which geometry can be defined.

## Ancient Origins

The earliest recorded geometry comes from Mesopotamia and Egypt in the 2nd millennium BC, where empirical rules for lengths, angles, areas, and volumes served surveying, construction, and astronomy. Key sources are the Rhind Papyrus, Moscow Papyrus, and the Babylonian tablet Plimpton 322.

In the 7th century BC, Thales of Miletus applied geometry to pyramid heights and ship distances, and is credited with the first use of deductive reasoning. Pythagoras's school produced the first proof of the Pythagorean theorem. Eudoxus developed the method of exhaustion, calculating areas and volumes of curvilinear figures by successive approximation, and a theory of ratios that avoided incommensurable magnitudes (lengths with no common unit, like the side and diagonal of a square). Around 300 BC, Euclid's *Elements* introduced rigor through the axiomatic method: arranging definitions, axioms, theorems, and proofs into a single logical framework. Archimedes used exhaustion to compute the area under a parabola, approximated pi, studied his spiral, and derived volumes of surfaces of revolution.

## India and the Islamic World

The *Sulba Sutras* contain the earliest known verbal statement of the Pythagorean theorem and lists of Pythagorean triples (integer triples satisfying a² + b² = c², such as 3, 4, 5). The Bakhshali manuscript uses a decimal place-value system with a dot for zero. Brahmagupta's *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* (628) states a theorem on diagonals of a cyclic quadrilateral (one inscribable in a circle), an area formula generalizing Heron's, and a description of rational triangles.

In medieval Islam, Al-Mahani reduced problems like duplicating the cube to algebra. Omar Khayyam solved cubic equations geometrically. Theorems of Ibn al-Haytham, Khayyam, and Nasir al-Din al-Tusi on quadrilaterals extended inquiry into the parallel postulate, the Euclidean assumption that through a point not on a line there is exactly one parallel line. By the 19th century this inquiry led to hyperbolic geometry.

## Early Modern and 19th-Century Transformations

Descartes and Fermat created analytic geometry, representing geometric objects with coordinates and equations, a precursor to calculus. Desargues systematically studied projective geometry, which examines properties of shapes preserved under projection, including those used in artistic perspective.

Three 19th-century developments transformed the field. Lobachevsky, Bolyai, and Gauss discovered non-Euclidean geometries: consistent systems built without the parallel postulate. Klein's Erlangen programme (1872) made symmetry, expressed through transformation groups (sets of operations like rotations or reflections, composed under a group structure), the defining principle of each geometry. Gauss's Theorema Egregium showed Gaussian curvature is intrinsic to a surface, independent of how it sits in Euclidean space, enabling the study of curved spaces as stand-alone objects. Riemann introduced Riemann surfaces, and Poincaré founded algebraic topology. Non-Euclidean geometry now underlies general relativity.

## Main Concepts

Points are elements of an axiomatically defined set called space. Lines, planes, curves, surfaces, and solids are generalized by manifolds, spaces where every point has a local neighborhood matching Euclidean space. Angles, formed by two rays sharing an endpoint, underpin trigonometry. Length, area, and volume describe extent in one, two, and three dimensions; length generalizes to metrics (Euclidean, hyperbolic, Lorentz). Congruence and similarity describe relations between figures. Dimension extends beyond three to higher, infinite (Hilbert spaces), and non-integer (fractal) values.

## Contemporary Geometry and Applications

Euclidean geometry remains standard in curricula. Differential geometry, using calculus and linear algebra, is central to general relativity's description of curved spacetime. Non-Euclidean geometry arises by replacing the parallel postulate (hyperbolic, elliptic) or admitting isotropic quadratic forms. Topology studies properties preserved under continuous deformation: connectedness, compactness. Algebraic geometry studies algebraic sets, defined by systems of polynomial equations. Hilbert's Nullstellensatz linked it to commutative algebra, and Grothendieck's scheme theory enabled Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem. Complex geometry builds on Riemann surfaces and includes Calabi–Yau manifolds, used in string theory.

In applications, perspective inspired projective geometry; tessellations and hyperbolic geometry appear in M. C. Escher's work. Geometric morphometrics analyzes biological shape; fractal geometry models vascular and bronchial structures. Coordinates also let plane curves be expressed as functions, a foundation for calculus.

## Key Terms

- **Axiomatic method**: deducing theorems from self-evident postulates.
- **Method of exhaustion**: successive approximation for curvilinear areas and volumes.
- **Non-Euclidean geometry**: geometries developed without the parallel postulate.
- **Theorema Egregium**: Gaussian curvature is intrinsic to a surface.
- **Manifold**: a space locally resembling Euclidean space at every point.
- **Erlangen programme**: geometry defined by its symmetry group.
- **Scheme theory**: Grothendieck's framework enabling topological methods in algebraic geometry.
- **Geodesic**: a generalization of a straight line to curved spaces.
