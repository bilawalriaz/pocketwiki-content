# Measurement

Measurement is the quantification of an attribute by comparing it against a standard reference quantity. It underpins trade, science, technology, and quantitative research. The scientific discipline devoted to it is metrology.

A measurement has four components. Its **type** is the methodological character of the comparison: a ratio scale lets you say one object is twice as long as another, while an ordinal scale only ranks them. Its **magnitude** is the numerical value read from an instrument. Its **unit** is a weighting factor that relates the magnitude to a standard. Its **uncertainty** captures the random and systematic errors, expressing how much confidence the reading deserves. Two qualities describe an instrument: **accuracy**, how close a reading is to the true value, and **precision**, how repeatable it is. A ruler can be precise yet inaccurate if mis-marked; a sensitive scale can be accurate on average but imprecise on any single reading.

## Units and the SI

A **unit** is a standardised quantity used to express other quantities of the same kind. For centuries, regional systems proliferated. In the 18th century, efforts to unify them began, accelerated by the 1875 Metre Convention, which created the General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM). The result, formalised in 1960, is the **International System of Units (SI)**, a decimal metric system that reduces every physical measurement to combinations of seven **base units**: the kilogram (mass), metre (length), second (time), ampere (electric current), kelvin (temperature), mole (amount of substance), and candela (luminous intensity). **Derived units** are built mathematically from these, so power becomes the watt, equal to m²·kg·s⁻³, and density becomes kg·m⁻³. Prefixes scale every unit by powers of ten, so one metre equals 100 centimetres without changing the underlying quantity.

Since 1960 the SI has been defined without reference to physical artefacts. In 1983 the metre was redefined in terms of the speed of light, and in 2019 the kilogram was redefined in terms of the Planck constant. These **artefact-free definitions** fix units to invariant constants, so a kilogram defined in one laboratory matches a kilogram defined anywhere else, and no platinum cylinder can drift through corrosion or wear. Standards are overseen internationally by the CGPM and nationally by bodies such as NIST (United States), NPL (United Kingdom), and the National Measurement Institute (Australia). Laws regulating measurement originally existed to prevent commercial fraud.

Older foot–pound–second systems survive in the United States and a handful of Caribbean nations, and the United Kingdom retains miles for road distances, miles per hour for speed limits, imperial pints for draught beer, and stones and pounds for body weight. Many partially metricated Commonwealth countries still sell land in acres and fuel in gallons.

## Significant Figures and Practical Error

**Significant figures** record the exactness of a number. Every non-zero digit counts, as do any zeros between them; the number 12000 therefore has two significant figures. Trailing zeros after a decimal point add precision: 1.0 implies a value between 0.95 and 1.05, while the bare integer 1 only implies a value between 0.5 and 1.5.

A dropped-ball experiment makes the error sources concrete. Gravity is conventionally given as 9.8 m/s², precise to two significant digits, and it varies with altitude. Computing the fall time requires a square root that must be rounded. Experimental errors add further noise: timing inaccuracy, error in the measured drop height, air resistance, slight differences in human posture, and simple carelessness. Scientific practice accepts that all measurements are approximations, so the discipline is to minimise error and report uncertainty honestly.

## Theoretical Foundations

Several frameworks compete to define what measurement is. The **classical definition**, used in the physical sciences, holds that measurement determines the ratio of a quantity to a standard unit, a tradition traced to John Wallis, Isaac Newton, and ultimately Euclid. **Representational theory** reframes measurement as the assignment of numbers to entities so that the relations among the numbers reflect relations among the entities; the strong form, **additive conjoint measurement**, requires these structural correspondences to be empirically verified, while weaker Stevens-type forms only require a consistent rule of mapping. The **uncertainty view** treats every measurement as a statistical estimate, a range of plausible values that narrows as more observations are gathered, blurring the line between measurement and estimation.

In quantum mechanics, measuring a property such as position, momentum, or energy samples from a probability distribution, alters the system in a process described as wavefunction collapse, yet repeated measurements yield reproducible statistics. The unambiguous meaning of this process is an unresolved fundamental problem. Biology has no comparable general theory of measurement; subfields for genetic and species diversity exist, but the broader theoretical context, particularly evolution, is still being worked out.
