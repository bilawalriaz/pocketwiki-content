# Arithmetic

Arithmetic is the foundational branch of mathematics concerned with numbers and the operations performed on them. In its core sense it covers addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division; in a wider sense it extends to exponentiation, root extraction, and logarithms. It underpins algebra, calculus, statistics, the sciences, and everyday tasks such as computing change. The word derives from Greek *arithmos* ("number") and *arithmetike tekhne* ("the art of counting"). A narrow definition restricts arithmetic to natural numbers; the more common view includes integers, rationals, reals, and sometimes complex numbers. Arithmetic is closely tied to number theory—once called "higher arithmetic"—which focuses on the divisibility, factorization, and primality of integers.

## Numbers and numeral systems

Natural numbers {1, 2, 3, …} exclude 0; whole numbers include it. Integers (ℤ) add negatives. Cardinals answer "how many?"; ordinals "what position?" Rationals (ℚ) are ratios of two integers with finite or repeating decimal expansions; irrationals such as √2 and π have infinite non-repeating expansions. Together they form the reals (ℝ); wider classes include complex numbers and quaternions.

Early civilizations used non-positional systems: unary tallies, Egyptian hieroglyphs, Roman numerals. Positional systems assign digits a value by position and a radix (base). Babylonians invented the first around 1800 BCE using radix 60; the Hindu–Arabic decimal system (radix 10) underlies modern writing, so 532 = 5·10² + 3·10¹ + 2·10⁰. Computers use binary (radix 2), where each digit is one bit.

## Operations

For addition, 0 is the identity and −n the additive inverse; for multiplication, 1 is the identity and the reciprocal the multiplicative inverse. Subtraction and division undo addition and multiplication. Addition and multiplication are commutative (order-independent) and associative (grouping-independent); exponentiation is neither, lacks general identity or inverse elements, and its inverse is the logarithm: log₁₀ 1000 = 3.

Integer arithmetic uses carries, long multiplication and long division, and exponentiation by squaring (3⁶⁵ in roughly 7 multiplications instead of 64). It is not closed under division: 7 ÷ 2 = 3.5, handled by rounding or remainders. Rational arithmetic operates on numerators and denominators over a common denominator and is closed under division. Real arithmetic must truncate or round irrationals: π truncated to four places is 3.141, rounded 3.142. Modular arithmetic wraps on finite sets: 9 + 4 ≡ 1 (mod 12).

Measurements carry uncertainty, shown as significant digits or bounds such as 1.62 ± 0.005 m. Absolute uncertainties add under addition and subtraction; relative ones add under multiplication and division. Interval arithmetic computes on ranges ([1, 2] + [3, 4] = [4, 6]); computers use floating-point arithmetic under IEEE 754, where rounding can violate associativity. Scientific notation writes numbers as significand × 10^exponent, as in 8.276 × 10⁶.

## Key results in number theory

The fundamental theorem of arithmetic states that every integer factors uniquely into primes. Euclid's theorem shows infinitely many primes exist. Fermat's Last Theorem states that aⁿ + bⁿ = cⁿ has no positive integer solutions for n > 2.

## Axiomatic foundations

The Dedekind–Peano axioms formalize the natural numbers using 0, "natural number," and a successor function: 0 is natural; every natural has a successor; distinct naturals have distinct successors; 0 is not a successor; and induction: if a set contains 0 and is closed under successor, it contains all naturals. Set theory builds 0 as ∅, each next number as n ∪ {n}, integers as ordered pairs of naturals, rationals as pairs of integers, and reals via Dedekind cuts (partitions of ℚ).

## History

The Lebombo bone (c. 43,000 years old) and Ishango bone (c. 22,000–30,000) are possibly the oldest arithmetic artifacts, though this is disputed. By c. 3000 BCE Egyptians and Sumerians used numeral systems; c. 1800 BCE Babylonians introduced positionality. In the 7th–6th centuries BCE, Thales and Pythagoras began abstract study with proofs and discovered irrationality; Diophantus advanced number theory in the 3rd century CE. Brahmagupta wrote rules for zero and negatives c. 628 CE; Aryabhata detailed decimal operations around the turn of the 6th century. During the Islamic Golden Age, Al-Khwarizmi refined the decimal system, which Fibonacci spread westward in the 12th–13th centuries. Cardano conceived complex numbers in the 16th century; Napier discovered logarithms and Pascal and Leibniz built mechanical calculators in the 17th. Euler and Gauss founded modern number theory in the 18th–19th centuries, with Cantor and Dedekind–Peano formalizing foundations. Electronics transformed computation in the 20th century.

## In other fields

Elementary education builds number sense through concrete tools (beads, number lines, abacuses) before introducing negatives, fractions, reals, and algorithms. Psychology studies how humans and animals learn numbers, noting innate pre-verbal abilities; *numeracy* correlates with academic and economic outcomes. Philosophers debate ontology: Platonism (mind-independent objects), intuitionism (mental constructions), logicism (reducible to logic), formalism (symbol-manipulation rules). Arithmetic is traditionally held *a priori*; naturalists like Quine ground it empirically. Beyond pure mathematics, it supports business, engineering, and cryptography, and underlies algebra, calculus, geometry, statistics, physics, computer science, and economics.

## Open questions

The boundaries of "arithmetic" remain contested. Whether the Lebombo and Ishango bones are truly arithmetic artifacts, and whether number sense predates language, are unresolved. The deepest debates concern the nature of numbers and how arithmetic knowledge is possible.
