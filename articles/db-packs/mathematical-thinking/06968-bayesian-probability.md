# Bayesian probability

Bayesian probability is an interpretation of probability in which a number between 0 and 1 represents a reasonable expectation, a state of knowledge, or a personal belief, rather than a long-run frequency or physical propensity. The interpretation extends propositional logic so that one can reason about propositions whose truth is uncertain. A hypothesis can be assigned any probability in [0, 1], whereas under frequentist inference a hypothesis is typically treated as either true or false without receiving a probability at all.

The Bayesian approach to evidence has three steps. First, the analyst specifies a prior probability distribution for the unknown quantities, encoding whatever information or belief is available before seeing the data. Second, new evidence arrives and is incorporated using Bayes' theorem, producing a posterior distribution. Third, that posterior becomes the next prior when further data appear, so learning is sequential. The central relationship is

P(H | E) = P(E | H) · P(H) / P(E),

where H is a hypothesis, E is observed evidence, P(H) is the prior, P(E | H) is the likelihood of the evidence under H, and P(H | E) is the posterior. The same machinery handles parameter estimation by treating the unknown parameter as a random quantity with a distribution. Probability is evidential in this sense: the prior encodes initial evidence and is updated as new evidence arrives.

Two main schools interpret what the prior means. For objectivists, probability is an extension of logic, so the prior quantifies the reasonable expectation that any agent with the same knowledge would share, justified by Cox's axioms. For subjectivists, the prior encodes personal belief, and rationality is enforced by coherence constraints such as the Dutch book argument, decision theory, and de Finetti's theorem. The mathematics is identical; the schools differ chiefly in how they choose and defend the prior. When genuine prior information is lacking, Bayesians use objective or default priors built by methods such as maximum entropy, transformation group analysis, or reference analysis.

The history runs from Thomas Bayes (1702–1761), who proved a special case of the theorem for Bernoulli trials (repeated independent yes/no experiments) with beta priors in "An Essay Towards Solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances," to Pierre-Simon Laplace (1749–1827), who formulated the general version and applied it to celestial mechanics, medical statistics, reliability, and jurisprudence. Early Bayesian work with uniform priors was called "inverse probability" because it reasons backward from observations to parameters or from effects to causes. It was largely displaced by frequentist methods after the 1920s. The modern revival began with Harold Jeffreys's *Theory of Probability* (1939), continued with Abraham Wald (1950) and Leonard J. Savage (1954), and accelerated in the 1980s when Markov chain Monte Carlo methods made the computations tractable for complex models. The adjective "Bayesian" dates to the 1950s.

Several arguments support Bayesian updating. Cox showed that it follows from a small set of axioms including two functional equations and a differentiability assumption, though Halpern later gave a counterexample using finite Boolean algebras, so the differentiability assumption does not hold universally. The Dutch book argument shows that incoherent beliefs leave a gambler vulnerable to guaranteed losses, but as Ian Hacking pointed out, avoiding Dutch books does not by itself force Bayesian updating; non-Bayesian rules such as Jeffrey's rule can also be coherent. Wald's decision-theoretic result strengthens the case: every admissible statistical procedure is either Bayesian or a limit of Bayesian procedures, and every Bayesian procedure is admissible. Personal probabilities can in principle be measured experimentally, as Frank P. Ramsey, Bruno de Finetti, and later psychologists have done, so the subjective interpretation is empirically testable rather than purely introspective. Modern work on these experiments uses the randomization, blinding, and Boolean-decision procedures of the Peirce–Jastrow experiment.

Because individuals act on different probability judgments, their probabilities are personal yet amenable to objective study. Personal probabilities are problematic for science when decision-makers lack the knowledge or time to specify an informed distribution, which is why Bayesian statisticians have developed the default-prior methods noted above. The quest for a universal method of constructing such priors continues to attract statistical theorists.
