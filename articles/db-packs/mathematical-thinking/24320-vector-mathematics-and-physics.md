# Vector (mathematics and physics)

A vector is an object with magnitude and direction that can be added to other vectors and scaled by ordinary numbers, called scalars. The same word also names any element of a vector space, the algebraic structure that generalizes arrows, tuples, and many other objects.

## Geometric and physical vectors

Geometric vectors arose in geometry and mechanics to represent quantities with both size and direction: displacements, velocities, and forces. A vector is drawn as an arrow from an initial point A to a terminal point B; its length is the magnitude and its orientation is the direction. The Latin *vector* means "carrier," because the arrow carries A to B, and 18th-century astronomers investigating planetary motion first used the term this way.

Two vectors are added tip-to-tail, and a vector can be stretched or shrunk by multiplying it with a scalar. These operations obey commutativity, associativity, and distributivity, the same algebraic laws that govern real numbers.

In physics, a vector quantity is a physical quantity that carries a unit of measurement alongside its magnitude and direction. A position in space, for instance, is given by three Cartesian coordinates in metres. Velocity, acceleration, and force are further examples. Physical vectors can carry extra structure beyond the plain geometric arrow:

- A bound vector attaches an ordinary vector to a specific point of application. A force on a plane is described by two components in newtons together with the two-coordinate position, in metres, where the force acts, four numbers in total.
- A sliding vector attaches to a line of action rather than a single point; it may be translated along that line without changing its physical effect.
- A free vector has no specified location. A displacement vector from one point to another is the prototype.

When the underlying geometry is not flat Euclidean space, the metric changes but the vector formalism survives. An event in spacetime is a position four-vector with three spatial coordinates and a timelike component expressed in metres through the speed of light; here the Minkowski metric replaces the Euclidean one. Vector quantities are also extended to vector fields, where each point of a region is assigned a vector, with wind velocity over Earth's surface as a standard example, and to objects that transform like vectors under coordinate changes, including pseudovectors and tensors.

## Vectors as elements of a vector space

The geometric notion generalizes into a vector space: a set whose elements can be added pairwise and scaled by numbers from some field, with the addition and scaling required to satisfy axioms such as commutativity and distributivity. A vector space whose elements are geometric arrows is a Euclidean vector space; one whose elements are finite ordered lists (tuples) of real numbers, added and scaled component by component, is a coordinate vector space, and tuples are the most familiar computational form. The scalars need not be real numbers; real and complex vector spaces are the most studied, but vector spaces also underlie polynomial rings, algebras including field extensions and Lie algebras, and function spaces. In advanced contexts the term "vector" is usually reserved for geometric vectors, tuples, and the elements of unspecified vector spaces; elements of specific algebraic structures are given their own names.

A vector space has a dimension, the number of independent directions it contains. Finite-dimensional spaces arise in geometry; infinite-dimensional spaces appear in analysis, with many function spaces as examples. Two vector spaces over the same field with the same dimension are isomorphic, which is why a coordinate space of n real numbers can stand in for any n-dimensional geometric space. Many vector spaces are given extra structure, such as a norm (normed and Banach spaces) or an inner product (Hilbert spaces), to support analysis and geometry.

## Vectors in calculus

Vector calculus extends differentiation and integration to vector-valued functions. Its central operators are the gradient of a scalar field and the divergence and curl of a vector field, and its integral tools include line, surface, and volume integrals over vector quantities, which together underpin calculations of work, flux, and fluid flow in physics and engineering.
