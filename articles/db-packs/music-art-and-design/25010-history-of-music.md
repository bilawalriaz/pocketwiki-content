# History of music

Music is a cultural universal, present in every known society, though its definitions vary widely. Its origins remain contested, and most scholarly debate treats it as inseparable from the question of language. Three positions recur: that music arose as a kind of proto-language before speech; that music is a spandrel, a byproduct of evolution that arose alongside language rather than serving an adaptive function; or that both descended from a shared precursor some call "speech-music." None commands consensus.

The earliest firm evidence dates to the Upper Paleolithic, roughly 38,000 BC, in the form of bone flutes. Eight flutes from caves in Germany's Swabian Jura, including Geissenklösterle, Hohle Fels, and Vogelherd, are the oldest widely accepted instruments. A purported Neanderthal flute from Divje Babe in Slovenia, made from a cave bear femur, is far more contested; if genuine, it would push musical culture into the Middle Paleolithic. The scarcity of older instruments likely reflects the perishability of reeds, skins, gourds, and bark.

With writing came ancient music in literate civilizations of China, Egypt, Greece, India, Mesopotamia, Persia, Rome, and the Middle East. Despite their diversity, these cultures shared monophony (a single melodic line without harmony), improvisation, and a tight bond between text and sound. The oldest surviving written music is the Hurrian songs from Ugarit, Syria, of which the Hymn to Nikkal, around 1400 BC, is the most complete. The Seikilos epitaph, from the 2nd century CE, is the earliest entirely complete notated composition. Trade along the Silk Road carried instruments and ideas across Eurasia: Central Asian music shaped Tang dynasty China, which in turn influenced Japanese gagaku and Korean court music.

Religion repeatedly shaped musical traditions. The Hindu Vedas underpinned Indian classical music; Confucian classics defined Chinese ritual music; Islamic culture, spreading in the 7th century, produced a Golden Age of music theory across Persia and the Arab world. In the West, Christian church chant inaugurates the Western classical tradition.

In medieval Europe (roughly 6th to 15th centuries), Gregorian chant is the earliest surviving repertory. The 9th century saw church efforts to unify chant, the first polyphony in the form of organum, and the reinvention of notation. Schools at Notre Dame, with Léonin and Pérotin, produced the first music for more than two voices around 1200, while troubadours cultivated secular song. The 14th-century ars nova style, led by Guillaume de Machaut, set refined secular forms.

The Renaissance, beginning in northern France and the Low Countries around 1400, shifted toward clear singable melody and balanced polyphony. Franco-Flemish composers such as Dufay and Josquin des Prez established Europe's first international style, spread by printing and by musicians migrating to Italian courts. By 1600, music moved from modal to tonal harmony, and opera was born in Florence through attempts to recreate Greek drama.

The Baroque era (1600–1750) consolidated major forms: fugue, sonata, and concerto, with Bach, Handel, Vivaldi, and Monteverdi as towering figures. The Classical period (roughly 1750–1820) brought homophonic texture, an obvious melody with simple accompaniment, and the dominance of instrumental genres such as the symphony and string quartet, with Haydn and Mozart defining the sound. Romantic music, which followed, prized emotion, expanded the orchestra, and wove music into national identity through composers such as Chopin, Wagner, Brahms, and Tchaikovsky.

The twentieth century transformed music's reach. Radio and recording changed how listeners encountered it; electronic amplification and synthesizers enabled new timbres. In classical music, Arnold Schoenberg and Igor Stravinsky defined competing paths, with figures such as Bartók, Shostakovich, Messiaen, and Cage pushing further. Meanwhile, popular music, rooted in late-nineteenth-century American Tin Pan Alley, became a dominant global idiom built on verse-chorus structures and broad melodic appeal.
