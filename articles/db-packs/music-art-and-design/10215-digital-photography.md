# Digital photography

Digital photography records images with an electronic sensor instead of photographic film. A lens projects the scene onto an array of photodetectors (light-sensitive elements); each element measures the intensity of light falling on a small area called a pixel, and an analog-to-digital converter turns each measurement into a number stored as a computer file. Because the output is already digital, the image can be reviewed, edited, copied, and transmitted without chemical development, and the cost of one more picture is essentially zero.

## How the image is captured

Most colour cameras use a monochrome sensor that records only light intensity, overlaid with a colour filter mosaic in the Bayer pattern (red, green, or blue filters over individual pixels). Camera software called demosaicing combines neighbouring filtered pixels into a full red-green-blue colour value. A few sensors, such as the Foveon X3, stack three photodetectors at each pixel location; both approaches produce a colour image from intensity-only silicon. Some special-purpose cameras record only grayscale, and a few scientific cameras record more than three colour bands for multispectral imaging.

Two silicon technologies dominate. The charge-coupled device (CCD), invented by Willard Boyle and George Smith at Bell Labs in 1969, shifts photo-generated charge from pixel to pixel toward a single output amplifier. The complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) active-pixel sensor places an amplifier at each pixel, allowing the chip to be read like computer memory and integrated with other circuits on the same die. CCDs once led on image quality; CMOS now dominates because it is cheaper to manufacture, uses less power, and can include processing and analog-to-digital conversion on the same chip.

## A short history

The pipeline that made digital photography practical took about fifty years. In 1951 the first digital image signals were saved to magnetic tape. Russell Kirsch produced the first stored digital image in 1957. The CCD was invented in 1969, and NASA's Mariner 4 returned the first close-up Mars images in 1965, digitised onboard and stored on tape for slow transmission to Earth. Michael Tompsett used a CCD to make the first published colour digital photograph in 1972. Steven Sasson at Eastman Kodak built the first self-contained digital camera in 1975, an 8-pound prototype that recorded 0.01-megapixel black-and-white images to a cassette in about 23 seconds. The JPEG compression standard, published in 1992 and based on the discrete cosine transform proposed by Nasir Ahmed in 1972, made image files small enough to share and is credited with popularising digital photography. The first widely available consumer digital camera was the Dycam Model 1 in 1990; Sharp and Samsung released the first camera phones in 2000; mirrorless interchangeable-lens cameras overtook DSLRs in the 2010s by dropping the mechanical mirror and using an electronic viewfinder.

## Image quality and how it is measured

Pixel count is the total number of samples in the array, n = w × h for w horizontal and h vertical pixels; a 1600 × 1200 image is 1.92 megapixels. Manufacturers market this number because it is easy to compare, but it is not the most important figure. Doubling megapixels without enlarging the sensor splits the same light across smaller pixels, which usually raises noise (random brightness variations). A larger sensor with the same pixel count generally produces cleaner images than a smaller one, which is why DSLRs outperform compact cameras of equal resolution.

Linear resolution, the ability to record fine detail, scales with the number of pixels across a scene rather than the total count, so the meaningful comparison is the square root of total pixel count. Going from 4 megapixels (2560 × 1600) to 6.5 megapixels (3200 × 2048) increases total pixels by 1.6× but linear resolution by only about 1.25×.

Dynamic range is the ratio between the brightest and darkest tones a system can record while preserving detail. Both digital and film systems are limited: highlights above the range clip to white, shadows below it clip to black. High-dynamic-range imaging extends the range by combining several exposures in software.

Storage is overwhelmingly flash-memory cards, now almost all Secure Digital (SD), with CompactFlash and XQD seen mainly in high-end bodies. Reformatting a card in the camera refreshes its file system and reduces the chance of corrupted images.

## How digital differs from film

Digital photography replaces the chemical chain of film with a numerical one, and the change shows up in cost, workflow, and reliability. Film and development costs disappear because a memory card holds hundreds of images and can be reused indefinitely. The workflow becomes immediate because the captured image is reviewed on the rear screen within seconds, exposure can be retried on the spot, and the file is already in a form computers can edit. Image processing is deterministic: the same numerical steps on the same data produce the same result, so an edit can be repeated, audited, or undone, in contrast to the variability of darkroom chemistry. Digital capture is also faster in bulk: the Nikon D5 records over 100 RAW frames before its buffer fills, compared with 36 frames of a loaded Nikon F5 film body, and a RAW file transfers from a card in seconds while a film negative takes minutes to scan well.

The trade-offs are real. Digital cameras need electricity, so battery failure in the field is a hard stop, whereas many classic film cameras such as the Leica M2 need none. Because the image is a sample on a regular grid, fine repetitive patterns such as textile weaves or distant window blinds can produce coloured moiré and false detail (aliasing); film grain, by contrast, is randomly distributed and aliases less visibly. Sensor chips much larger than about one square centimetre become uneconomical because defect rates rise, which is why full-frame and medium-format digital sensors remain expensive.

## Industry and social consequences

The market shift was sudden. By 2003 digital cameras were outselling film cameras. Kodak announced in January 2004 that it would stop selling film cameras in the developed world; Nikon followed in January 2006 and Konica Minolta exited the camera business; Kodak filed for bankruptcy in 2012. Dedicated digital camera sales peaked in March 2012 at roughly 11 million units per month, fell to about 3 million by 2014, and have hovered near that level since, displaced by smartphones whose cameras improve with every product cycle. Because files can be copied losslessly and edited freely, the cultural assumption that a photograph is reliable evidence has weakened, and long-term preservation now requires active migration of files to current formats as hardware and decoding software become obsolete.
