# Design

Design is the intentional creation of a concept, plan, or proposal for an object, process, or system to satisfy specific goals and constraints. It is a fundamental human cognitive activity, not limited to professionals, that transforms existing situations into preferred ones through research, creativity, and problem-solving.

## How designers actually work

Two dominant paradigms describe how designs are produced. The **rational model** is plan-driven and discrete, treating design as a sequence of stages (analysis, specification, problem-solving, testing) that optimises a solution against known constraints. It is criticised for not matching how designers actually work and for assuming goals are fixed.

The **action-centric model** is improvised and iterative, treating problem definition and solution as things that co-evolve. Designers alternate between *framing* a problem and *making moves* (tentative decisions) in a reflective cycle. Donald Schön's *reflection-in-action* and the sensemaking–coevolution–implementation framework both fit this view. The design cycle is circular: think of an idea, express it, share and perceive it, then begin a new cycle with critical rethinking.

## Brief history

Formal design education in the West began in the 19th century, with institutions such as Norway's (1818) and the UK's Government School of Design (1837). The German Bauhaus, founded in 1919, tied studio-based learning to industrial reality and shaped modern design education.

Design history separated from art history as an academic discipline in the 1970s, and design entered general education curricula. In 1969 Herbert A. Simon published *The Sciences of the Artificial*, arguing that everyone designs and helping legitimise design as a general cognitive activity. By the 1980s Simon, and independently Gerhard Pahl and Wolfgang Beitz in engineering, had developed the rational model.

## Approaches in practice

Different philosophies emphasise different values. *Conscious design* is intentional and systems-aware, prioritising long-term impact on human well-being and ecological health. *User-centered design* focuses on the needs and limitations of the end user. *Participatory design* (or co-design) involves all stakeholders in the creative process. *Critical design* uses artifacts to critique cultural values rather than to solve commercial problems. *Ecological design* prioritises environmental impact across a product's lifecycle. *Service design* organises the experience around a product and its associated service.

## Open debates

Three questions remain unresolved. First, historians disagree on where design history begins, with some starting at pre-industrial artifacts and others at the Industrial Revolution and mass production. Second, the rational and action-centric models give incompatible accounts of how designers work, and no synthesis is settled. Third, it is unclear whether design is a universal human cognitive skill or a specialised professional one, a tension Simon's "everyone designs" claim opened but did not close.
