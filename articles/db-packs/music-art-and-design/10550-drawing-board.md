# Drawing board

A drawing board, also called a drawing table, drafting table, or architect's table, is a desk with an adjustable, inclined working surface designed for drawing, drafting, reading oversized documents, or sketching on large sheets of paper. The defining features are a flat board (often with a small lip along one edge to stop paper sliding) and a mechanism for tilting and raising that surface. In a modern steel-framed drafting table, a foot-pedal clutch and a lead-shot counterweight let the user set the angle and height safely, while a parallel-motion drafting machine replaces the older T-square and set square for accurate horizontal and angled lines.

## From studio furniture to workshop tool

During the 18th and 19th centuries the drawing table was a multipurpose desk in studies and private libraries, often paired with a pedestal desk. Draftsmanship was a general skill of educated men, and the table was usually built from fine woods and brass with only simple notches, ratchets, levers, or cogs to tilt the top. Set horizontal at 29 inches, it looked like an ordinary writing table, the only clue being the lip on the desktop; raised to full height, it served as a standing desk.

As the Industrial Revolution made draftsmanship a specialised trade, the table left the library and became utilitarian: steel frames replaced oak, and the form shifted from elegant furniture to workshop equipment. Despite computer-aided drafting (CAD) since the late 20th century, paper-and-pencil work on a drafting table persists among many older architects and some structural designers.

## Fixing paper to the surface

Historically, a sheet was dampened and its edges glued to the board so that, on drying, the paper shrank taut and smooth; the finished drawing was then cut free. Later, draftspeople used drawing pins or C-clamps. Modern practice favours self-adhesive drafting tape, including individual adhesive dots dispensed from a roll. Some boards are magnetised, holding paper with long steel strips, and boards used for overlay drafting or animation carry registration pins or peg bars to align successive layers.

## Construction of a modern drafting table

A contemporary drafting table uses a steel frame carrying a thick compressed-fibreboard top laminated with Formica on all faces and screwed in place so it can be removed for transport. A thin vinyl board cover gives a smooth drafting surface and protects the laminate from compass points; it must be cleaned often because graphite smears fresh work. A strip of aluminium or steel along the bottom edge holds pencils, with deeper trays available for the same purpose.

Height and tilt are controlled through a mechanical linkage in the frame. A single foot pedal engages a clutch that locks the surface where the user wants it. Inside the linkage hangs a counterweight of lead shot: if the pedal is released, the counterbalance prevents the heavy top from snapping upright and striking the user, so the clutch and its counterweight must be kept in good order. A few heavy tables add electric motors for powered adjustment, trading portability for push-button control.

## Tools and the drafting machine

Traditional instruments rested directly on the board: T-squares for parallel lines, set squares for perpendicular and oblique lines, protractors for angles, and compasses, French curves, and stencils for arcs, curves, and symbols. A drafting machine is a mechanical arm fixed to the board that carries pre-calibrated scales and a built-in protractor; its parallel rules stay parallel to the top edge of the board as they slide up and down, replacing the T-square and allowing accurate measurement and angled lines without separate hand tools.

## Back to the drawing board

The idiom "back to the drawing board" is used when a plan has failed badly and must be redesigned from scratch. The phrase first appeared in the caption to a Peter Arno cartoon in *The New Yorker* on 1 March 1941.
