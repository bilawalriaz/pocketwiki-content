# Compass (drawing tool)

A compass (also called a pair of compasses) is a hinged two-leg instrument that draws circles and arcs and, with two points instead of a point and pencil, measures distances on a map. It is one of the oldest tools in geometry, drafting, and navigation. Most professional drawing is now done in computer-aided design (CAD), so the physical tool survives mainly as a teaching aid in plane geometry and as a working tool in trades like carpentry.

## How it works

The two legs meet at an adjustable hinge. One leg ends in a sharp spike that anchors the tool at the centre of the circle; the other leg carries the drawing point, usually a short length of pencil lead, though an ink nib or technical pen can be substituted. Opening or closing the hinge sets the radius, and a screw through the hinge locks that angle so the legs do not drift.

To draw a circle, the user presses the spike into the paper, sets the drawing point down, and sweeps the legs through a full turn. The radius is the distance from spike tip to drawing tip, so changing that distance changes the circle's size. People who cannot rotate the tool smoothly often anchor the spike and rotate the paper; the result is the same.

A short knurled handle above the hinge, gripped between thumb and forefinger, gives control without blocking the legs. A quality metal compass adds a serrated wheel between the legs for fine radius adjustment and a spring around the hinge that snaps the legs open after each closing. This form is sold as a spring-bow compass.

## The chisel point

A metal compass sharpens its lead to a chisel edge rather than a point. The flat side rides against the paper so the line keeps a constant thickness around the whole circle; a conical tip would gouge or thicken as the leg swings. A separate nut on the drawing leg clamps the lead or pen in place and lets the user replace it.

## Variants

A beam compass uses a wooden or brass beam with sliding cursors; the radius is the distance from a fixed point on the beam to a movable pencil socket, so the same idea scales to circles a metre or more across.

Scribe-compasses have crimped metal branches, a pencil sleeve on one side and a fine point on the other, and a wing nut that locks the radius and clamps the pencil in one motion. They are standard in carpentry. Loose-leg wing dividers are forged-steel tools with two sharp points, used for stepping off repeated measurements rather than drawing inked lines. Reduction compasses, also called proportional dividers, are a pivoted cross whose two ends open in a fixed ratio, so a pattern can be copied at a smaller or larger scale while preserving angles. Ellipse compasses use a linked four-bar mechanism so the pencil point traces an ellipse instead of a circle.

## Compass and straightedge in geometry

In classical plane geometry a compass is paired with an unmarked straightedge for ruler-and-compasses constructions. Textbook drawings are made with a real compass, but the compass in a proof is an ideal device: it can draw a perfect circle of any chosen radius centred on any chosen point, and nothing else. In its strictest form the ideal tool is a collapsing compass, which vanishes after drawing one circle and cannot simply be lifted to draw another of the same radius elsewhere. In the second proposition of Book I of the *Elements*, Euclid showed that a collapsing compass can transfer a distance from one point to another, so the weaker abstract tool can do everything a real, reusable compass can.

## Distances on a map

Fitted with two spikes and no pencil, the same hinged tool is a divider (or dividing compass). Set so the spike-to-spike gap represents a known ground distance, then stepped between two points on a map, it counts how many of those units fit into the route, and that count is the map distance.

## History and current use

Compasses and other manual drafting tools were once sold in sets with interchangeable parts. By the mid-twentieth century, circle templates and, later, CAD programs had taken over professional drafting, so the physical compass is now used mainly in teaching geometry and technical drawing, and in trades where a scribe-compass or divider remains the most direct tool for the job.

## Symbol

Because the compass draws a true circle from a fixed centre, it is a long-standing emblem of precision. It appears in the Freemasons' Square and Compasses, in software icons for design tools, on the former national emblem of East Germany, and in literary imagery: the English poet John Donne used the compass as a conceit in *A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning* (1611) to picture two souls fixed at a common centre while the outer end traces a wider arc.
