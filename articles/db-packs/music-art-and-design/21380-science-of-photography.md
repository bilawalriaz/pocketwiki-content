# Science of photography

Photography rests on physics (light behaviour, optics, electronics) and chemistry (film and print processes), and every camera, digital or analog, traces back to a single trick: the camera obscura. Light entering a small hole in a dark box projects an inverted, two-dimensional image of the scene onto the opposite wall. A tiny pinhole makes the image sharp but dim, so exposures are long; adding a lens brightens it (shorter exposures, handheld cameras) and lets the designer correct aberrations.

Photographic lenses combine multiple elements to cancel optical defects. Spherical aberration happens because rays hitting a lens near its edge refract more strongly than central rays, blurring the image. Chromatic aberration arises because a lens's refractive index depends on wavelength, so blue light bends more than red and colours fringe. Curved focal surface is the dependence of best focus on distance from the lens axis. All three are reduced by combining elements (the three-element Cooke triplet is the minimal example) or by using aspheric elements with non-spherical surfaces. Small aspheric lenses are now moulded cheaply enough for consumer cameras; Fresnel lenses appear rarely because of weight. Every lens design is a compromise among cost, size, speed, and correction, and zoom lenses sacrifice some peak performance compared to prime lenses.

Depth of field and focus are linked. When the lens focuses on a distant object, nearer objects across a range of distances stay approximately sharp; this in-focus range is the depth of field, and it grows as the aperture closes (higher f-number). The blur outside this range can be used artistically (its subjective quality is called bokeh) or eliminated almost entirely by focusing at or beyond the hyperfocal distance, which is how fixed-focus cameras keep everything from about half the hyperfocal distance to infinity acceptably sharp. Focus itself means rays from one object point converge at one spot on the sensor regardless of where they passed through the lens; modern SLRs autofocus by using a mirror-box sensor to measure image contrast, then an application-specific integrated circuit moves lens elements to maximise that contrast, with extra algorithms for motion prediction. Diffraction, the wave behaviour of light through any aperture, sets a separate resolution floor: the diffraction-limited spot size is roughly the f-number times the wavelength (about 0.0005 mm), so image detail scales with sensor size divided by f-number. A 35 mm camera at f/11 resolves roughly 6,000 elements across the 36 mm width. Aberrations dominate at wide apertures, diffraction at narrow ones, and a diffraction-limited lens is one whose only remaining flaw at a given aperture is diffraction.

Chemical processes capture light on film. The gelatin silver process is the dominant black-and-white chemistry and the basis of modern analog colour. Older methods each addressed a specific limitation: the daguerreotype (first publicly available process, dominant in the 1840s–1850s), the collodion wet-plate process (coated, sensitised, exposed, and developed within about fifteen minutes, demanding a portable darkroom; dry collodion worked but needed minutes-long exposures, restricting it to landscapes), cyanotypes (cyan-blue prints using ferric ammonium citrate and potassium ferricyanide, used as blueprints into the twentieth century), platinum prints, and gum bichromate (a 19th-century dichromate process capable of painterly or natural-colour layered prints). A chromogenic print, also called a C-print, layers three gelatin emulsions of silver halide with subtractive-colour dye couplers; when developed, the three layers combine into a full-colour image. Film grain is the visible structure of silver halide crystals in the emulsion; larger crystals mean faster, more sensitive film and a coarser image, smaller crystals mean finer grain but slower exposure, summarised by the ISO rating (generally multiples of 10 or 100).

Digital sensors replace the emulsion with semiconductors. An image sensor converts the variable attenuation of light waves into small electrical signals. In a photodiode, an intrinsic layer with very few charge carriers blocks current; an incoming photon with enough energy can raise an electron from the full band to the empty band, freeing the electron and its "hole" to carry a measurable current. The fraction of incident photons that produce such carrier pairs is the quantum efficiency, set largely by the semiconductor material. Aliasing is the distortion that appears whenever an image is sampled too coarsely for its resolution; many digital cameras and scanners install anti-aliasing filters that deliberately blur the image before sampling.

The law of reciprocity ties exposure to aperture area, exposure time, and scene luminance: Exposure ∝ Aperture Area × Exposure Time × Scene Luminance. Each doubling or halving is called a stop. Cutting light by one stop can be done by closing the aperture one stop, halving the shutter time, or halving scene lighting, and the same trade holds in reverse. Film speed (ISO) sets the light required for correct exposure, and large-format cameras can swap individual sheets or polaroid backs of different speeds while digital cameras simply change the simulated exposure index. A worked example: starting at 1/60 s at f/16, opening to f/4 brightens the image by four stops; to compensate, shutter speed must rise by four stops to 1/1000, though closing the aperture in the first place would have hit the diffraction limit. Reciprocity itself only describes total exposure, and films, papers, and sensors exhibit reciprocity failure at very long exposures in dim light or very short exposures in bright light.

Motion blur appears whenever camera or subject moves during exposure. Panning follows a fast subject so the subject stays sharp while the background streaks, conveying speed; light trails from cars on roads at night or from star-trail photographs use the same long-exposure principle. To avoid blur from camera shake, a common rule sets the minimum shutter speed in seconds at the reciprocal of the 35 mm-equivalent focal length in millimetres, so a 50 mm lens needs at least 1/50 s and a 300 mm lens at least 1/300 s, which conflicts with low-light needs. Tripods help beyond about 1/15 s, with cable releases, infrared remotes, self-timers, and mirror lock-up all reducing the small vibrations that would otherwise soften the image; freezing subject motion simply demands a shutter speed matched to the subject's speed.
