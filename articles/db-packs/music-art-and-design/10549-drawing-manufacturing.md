# Drawing (manufacturing)

Drawing is a manufacturing process that uses tensile force to stretch a workpiece, making it longer and thinner as it is pulled through a shaped opening called a die. Material is pulled, not pushed: in extrusion the stock is forced out under compression, while in drawing the workpiece is anchored at one end and pulled in tension through the die, which constrains its cross-section.

Drawing is normally a cold-working operation at room temperature, starting from hot-rolled bar, rod, tube, or coiled wire. Successful drawing depends on plastic flow and stretch, so the material must be ductile, meaning it can deform permanently without breaking. Steels, copper alloys, and aluminium alloys are the most common metals drawn; the same principle is also used for glass and plastic. Drawing can be performed at higher temperatures to hot-work large wires, rods, or hollow tubes in order to reduce the required force.

## Sheet metal drawing

Sheet metal drawing forms a curved three-dimensional shape from a flat blank. The die forces the sheet to flow over a curved axis, reshaping the surface. The operator balances two risks: if material flows too freely, the part wrinkles; if the blank is restrained too much, the wall thins and tears. Hold-down pressure and lubrication are adjusted until the sheet stretches smoothly into shape. Shallow drawing has a depth less than the smallest hole dimension; deep drawing occurs when the drawn part is longer than its diameter, and is usually combined with other forming steps such as piercing, ironing, necking, rolling, and beading.

## Bar, tube, and wire drawing

Bar, tube, and wire drawing share one principle: the stock is pulled through a die to reduce its cross-section and increase length. The starting end is pointed so it can enter the die, then gripped and pulled through. The die is mounted on a draw bench, which applies tension along the bar.

The pulling force cannot exceed the tensile strength of the section being pulled, or the workpiece will snap. This limit is most visible in thin-wire drawing, where each incremental reduction of diameter raises the required force. The reduction in area per pass is therefore held between 20% and 50%, since larger reductions would exceed the material's strength and ductility. To reach a final small size, the workpiece passes through a series of progressively smaller dies, with intermediate annealing, a heat treatment that softens the material, restores ductility, and relieves internal stress, between passes. Bars and rods are drawn straight on straight-pull draw benches, using chain drives for pieces up to 30 m and hydraulic cylinders for shorter pieces. Cold-drawn cross-sections gain better dimensional accuracy, a smoother surface finish, and higher strength from work hardening, so cheaper base stock can substitute for more expensive alloys.

Tube drawing applies the same principle to a hollow section. A mandrel or a floating plug may sit inside the tube to control wall thickness while the outside diameter is reduced. Wire drawing uses a long sequence of dies of decreasing size to produce flexible wire. Die materials must resist abrasive wear; tungsten carbide and diamond are the most common.

## The cold drawing sequence for steel

A typical cold drawing operation for steel bar or wire follows these steps:

1. Lubrication: the surface is coated with phosphate or oil to reduce friction and wear.
2. Push pointing: the leading end is swaged or extruded down so it can pass through the die opening.
3. Drawing: the pointed end is gripped and pulled through the die, which reduces the cross-section, sets the profile, and lengthens the stock.
4. Multi-pass drawing: for complex shapes, the piece is drawn repeatedly through smaller dies, with annealing between passes to restore ductility and remove internal stress.
5. Annealing: heat treatment applied before, between, or after passes to soften the material, modify its microstructure, and relieve stress.

The output is called "cold drawn" or "cold finished": it has a bright surface, tighter dimensional tolerances, and better mechanical properties than the hot-rolled starting stock.

## Drawing in glass and plastics

The same principle applies to glass: glassblowing and the production of glass optical fibre both rely on pulling softened glass to reduce its diameter.

In plastics, the process is also called cold drawing and is used to make synthetic fibres. The polymer is first spun into filaments by extruding a melt through a spinneret, a plate with many small pores. The filaments emerge partly aligned but still amorphous, so they are stretched further on a draw twister machine. Stretching aligns the polymer chains, raising crystallinity (the fraction of the material organised into ordered crystalline regions), tensile strength, and stiffness. Nylon is typically stretched to four times its spun length, and the resulting crystals are held together by hydrogen bonds between the amide hydrogen of one chain and the carbonyl oxygen of another. Polyethylene terephthalate (PET) sheet is drawn in two perpendicular directions to make BoPET (biaxially-oriented PET), which has much higher mechanical strength than undrawn sheet. The polymer version of drawing was discovered in 1930 by Julian W. Hill, who found that an early polyester could be cold-drawn into strong fibres.
