# Circuit design

Circuit design is the engineering process of turning a desired electrical behaviour into a working electronic circuit, ranging from individual transistors inside an integrated chip to large multi-board systems. A single person can sketch a simple circuit on paper in one go, but more complex designs are handled by teams working from a structured process with computer simulation. In integrated circuit (IC) design automation, "circuit design" is the specific step that produces the schematics (a symbols-and-wires representation of the circuit) sitting between the logic design and the physical layout of the chip.

## The stages

Traditional circuit design follows a recurring sequence, though stages are revisited whenever new information forces a change.

1. **Specification.** A document written with the customer describing what the finished circuit must do, not how. It lists electrical requirements (input and output signals, available power supplies, allowed power consumption) and physical requirements (size, weight, moisture resistance, temperature range, thermal output, vibration and acceleration tolerance). The specification is not fixed: as the design matures, the designer tightens parameters, adds acceptance tests, and resubmits changes that conflict with the original brief for the customer's approval. A fuzzy or shifting specification causes "design creep," in which the finished circuit has too much functionality, or the wrong functionality, because initial expectations were unrealistic and communication with the client broke down later.
2. **Design.** The specification is first converted into a block diagram, where each block represents a function the circuit must perform without yet specifying the components inside. This "black box" decomposition lets a complex task be split among team members or tackled in order. Each block is then expanded into more detailed sub-circuits, often requiring research or mathematical modelling to check feasibility; if a block cannot meet its targets, neighbouring blocks are adjusted instead. Only at the end are real components chosen, with their physical layout and electrical connections drawn as artwork for a printed circuit board (PCB) or IC. This final stage is slow because the choice of components is vast; a calculated value that no supplier actually stocks leaves the problem unsolved, so designers rely on "catalog engineering," picking standard available parts. The general flow covers architecture scope, materials selection, schematic capture, PCB layout with power and signal integrity, and test and validation.
3. **Verification and testing.** Verification proves on paper and in simulation that every stage of the design will meet the specification; it is largely mathematical and often forces significant rework when flaws appear. Testing is the physical counterpart: at least one prototype is built, often on a breadboard (a solderless board for plugging components in and rewiring quickly), and exercised against the test procedures in the specification. Prototyping is iterative, and skipping it risks circuits that overheat, catch fire, or injure users, because small errors only become visible in a real build.
4. **Production and documentation.** A construction method is chosen, parts lists and layout drawings are issued to draughtspeople and mechanical engineers, several prototypes are type-tested for compliance, and the final manufacturing drawings are signed off. Every commercial design ships with documentation: at minimum the specification, the test procedures, and a compliance statement such as the EU's CE Declaration naming the directives met and the person responsible.

## Costs

Design cost rises with complexity: more components and novelty mean more skilled engineering hours, and small details (such as the effect of changing a transistor's size) can absorb disproportionate effort. In flexible electronics, replacing the usual polyimide substrate with PEN or PET can cut costs by a factor of 5 to 10. Design cost per product is almost always far higher than the production cost of each unit, because production cost and behaviour depend directly on the design itself. Most PCBs are made by subtractive methods (etching copper away from a coated board), but additive 3D printing of circuits is emerging as a cheaper alternative that also removes etching waste.

## Software

Designers use electronic design automation (EDA) tools to capture schematics, simulate behaviour, and lay out boards. Common packages include Altium Designer, KiCad, OrCAD, EasyEDA, gEDA, pcb-rnd, NI Multisim, and the SPICE family of circuit simulators.

## Why the process loops

The defining feature of circuit design is iteration. A specification tightens once feasibility is known; a block's constraints shift when a neighbouring block cannot be built; a simulation failure forces a redesign before any hardware exists; a prototype test invalidates an assumption made months earlier. The cost of catching an error rises sharply at each later stage, so designers keep options open as long as practical, because removing an unused element is cheap while retrofitting a missing one late in the project is not.
