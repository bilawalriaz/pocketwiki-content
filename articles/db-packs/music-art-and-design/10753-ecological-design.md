# Ecological design

Ecological design, also called ecodesign, is an approach to designing products, buildings, landscapes, and services that minimises environmental harm by accounting for the full lifecycle of whatever is being made. Sim Van der Ryn and Stuart Cowan, who popularised the term in 1996, defined it as "any form of design that minimizes environmentally destructive impacts by integrating itself with living processes." The relevant unit of analysis is the entire trajectory from raw material extraction through manufacturing, transport, use, and disposal, not the finished object in isolation.

## The lifecycle perspective

A lifecycle lens matters because environmental damage is often displaced rather than eliminated. A designer who switches a product's material to cut factory emissions may make disposal more polluting instead. Life Cycle Analysis (LCA) is the standard tool for tracing these effects. It inventories the resources consumed and the emissions, noise, and waste released at each stage, then ranks stages by impact so the most damaging ones can be targeted. LCA and the related economic metric Life Cycle Cost together help designers see that reducing impact in one phase can raise it in another.

The ideal outcome is a cradle-to-cradle loop in which every output of one process becomes input for another, leaving no waste. In a cradle-to-cradle design, materials circulate as nutrients, the way they do in biological ecosystems. Mimicking natural cycles in this way is the conceptual link between ecodesign and a circular economy, an economy that keeps materials in use rather than discarding them. In practice, ecodesign is proactive: environmental review is built into product development, with representatives from design, production, marketing, purchasing, and project management working together so changes in one stage do not silently create problems in another.

## The roots of the field

Concern about design's environmental role predates the term. Designer Victor Papanek argued in 1972 that designers share responsibility for pollution and waste because they choose materials and processes. Inventor R. Buckminster Fuller promoted "ephemeralization," doing more with less through efficiency, a principle that later became central to ecological design. Ian McHarg's 1969 book *Design with Nature* introduced layered site analysis, mapping hydrology, soils, vegetation, and history as separate overlays, and this method became the foundation of Geographic Information Systems (GIS), the computerised mapping tools now standard in landscape design. Bill Mollison and David Holmgren coined "permaculture" in 1978 for designing regenerative human ecosystems.

The 1992 Earth Summit in Rio concluded that prevailing patterns of energy production and consumption are unsustainable, and identified six priority areas: quality of life, efficient resource use, protecting the global commons, managing settlements, handling chemicals and waste, and sustainable economic growth. These priorities continue to frame ecodesign problems. Indigenous knowledge systems are also recognised in the source literature as a long-standing source of design wisdom for living with ecological limits.

## What gets designed and how

Ecodesign has spread across disciplines. In product design it draws on EcoMaterials, locally sourced or certified inputs such as wood certified by the Forest Stewardship Council (FSC), and reuses reclaimed components to avoid new manufacturing. In architecture it covers off-grid homes (buildings disconnected from utility grids), biophilic interiors that connect occupants to nature, and active and passive systems. Active systems generate energy on-site from solar, wind, biomass, geothermal, or hydropower; passive systems instead work with the building itself, using orientation, thermal mass (heavy materials like concrete that store and slowly release heat), green roofs, and cross-ventilation to regulate temperature and light without mechanical input.

Urban ecological design is a related but distinct strand. It is interdisciplinary, combining urban planning, landscape architecture, ecology, and justice studies, and treats cities as ecosystems to be designed for resilience, biodiversity, and resource efficiency in the face of climate change and urban growth. A complementary approach called undesign uses self-inhibition, removal, or restoration rather than addition. Digital detox applications, which limit screen time and reduce phone distractions, are cited in the source as a contemporary example, though their primary intent is shaping user behaviour rather than energy saving.

## Justice dimensions

Ecodesign has social consequences that designers cannot ignore. Green or environmental gentrification occurs when well-intended greening raises property values and displaces existing residents, a critique developed by Gould and Lewis. Hostile architecture, such as benches fitted with bars to prevent lying down, restricts who benefits from green space. Internationally, polluting facilities are sited disproportionately in low-income communities, and a "race to the bottom" pushes heavy industry toward countries with weaker regulations. The 2021 EcoSWaD framework was created to evaluate waste sites on five dimensions: location suitability, operational sustainability, environmental sustainability, socioeconomic sustainability, and site capacity, since most existing sites were never assessed this way.

## Research directions

Current ecodesign research clusters around three questions: what barriers slow adoption of environmental design practices, what tools and methods make adoption tractable, and how ecodesign interacts with adjacent fields such as industrial ecology, which studies material and energy flows through industrial systems the way ecology studies them through natural ones. Review studies of this literature trace a shift from product-level fixes toward system-level design for sustainable transitions.
