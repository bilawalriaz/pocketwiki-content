# Art

Art is human activity that uses creative or imaginative skill to produce works meant to evoke an experience through emotional power, ideas, technical mastery, or beauty. No single definition has ever been agreed upon, and what counts as art has shifted dramatically across cultures and centuries.

## A surprisingly recent category

In the West, until the 17th century, *art* referred to any skill or mastery, with no separation between what we now call fine art and craft or science. The Ancient Greek term *techne* covered painting, sculpture, and music, but also cooking, medicine, geometry, carpentry, and farming. The modern sense of fine art as a separate, higher-status domain emerged in the early 17th century. Larry Shiner described it as "not an essence or a fate but something we have made... a European invention barely two hundred years old."

In the Western tradition, the three classical branches of visual art are painting, sculpture, and architecture; theatre, dance, literature, music, and film are usually included in a broader "the arts." The split between *fine arts* and *decorative* or *applied arts* hardened through the 17th and 18th centuries, and by the 19th century the Romantic movement had elevated the individual artist's vision into the main measure of a work's content.

## Deep roots, even if the label is new

Image-making and skilled making are ancient. A shell engraved by *Homo erectus* dates to roughly 430,000–540,000 years ago. Neanderthals cut markings into white-tailed eagle talons about 130,000 years ago, possibly for jewelry, and made the Riesenhirschknochen der Einhornhöhle, the oldest artwork found in Europe, around 51,000 years ago. The first undisputed sculptures, such as the Venus of Hohle Fels, appear between 43,000 and 35,000 BC in the Swabian Jura region. The meaning of prehistoric art is often unresolved because so little is known about the cultures that produced it.

The traditions of ancient Egypt, Mesopotamia, Persia, India, China, Greece, Rome, and the Americas each set enduring patterns: Greek art pursued anatomically correct human figures; Byzantine and Medieval art flattened forms under gold backgrounds for biblical subjects; Renaissance art developed linear perspective; Islamic art, rejecting figurative imagery, emphasized geometry, calligraphy, and architecture; Chinese and Japanese traditions often named styles after ruling dynasties, and Japanese woodblock printing later reshaped European art.

## Three ways to think about meaning

Philosophers have long disagreed about what makes something a work of art. Aristotle treated poetry, music, and dance as *mimetic*, imitations of nature and action, a tradition still influential. The branch of philosophy that studies art and related concepts is *aesthetics*. Three broad positions organise most modern debate:

- **Form**: the work's internal structure, its lines, colours, rhythms, and composition, is where meaning lives. Roger Fry and Clive Bell developed this Kantian line. Extreme Formalism, which claims all aesthetic value is formal, is almost universally rejected.
- **Authorial intent**: the artist's intention is the key to meaning. Extreme Intentionalism is also widely rejected, because many works sustain multiple readings. In 1946 Wimsatt and Beardsley called the appeal to authorial intent the "intentional fallacy," arguing only the work itself should be judged.
- **Context**: meaning is shaped by cultural, religious, political, and institutional setting. Post-structuralism and George Dickie's *institutional theory* fall here. Dickie defined a work of art as any object on which the qualified "art world" has conferred the status of candidate for appreciation.

Arthur Danto argued that an object's status as art comes from the *ideas a culture applies to it*, not from its physical traits. Marcel Duchamp pushed this further, proposing that confining art to certain activities is itself a social construction. In 1917 his *Fountain*, an ordinary urinal signed under a pseudonym, became the landmark example of *anti-art*, art that exists to challenge the very idea of art. Tracey Emin's *My Bed* and Damien Hirst's shark in formaldehyde follow this logic; Hirst came up with the concept but left the making to employed artisans.

## Why art matters: functions and access

The anthropologist Claude Lévi-Strauss grouped art's purposes into two kinds. *Non-motivated* functions are built into being human: an instinct for harmony, balance, and rhythm; attraction to the mysterious; expression of the imagination; and ritual or symbolic use. *Motivated* purposes are deliberate: communication, entertainment, political change (the role of the *avant-garde*, art movements that aim to drive social change), social inquiry, art therapy, propaganda, commerce, and, evolutionarily, a costly display of fitness like the peacock's tail. From ancient times much fine art also signalled wealth or power, even as cheap media like pottery spread across societies.

A turning point in public access came in 1661, when Basel opened the Kunstmuseum Basel, the first public art museum. The Uffizi in Florence became a full gallery in 1765, and the Musée du Louvre opened as a public museum in 1793 during the French Revolution. In the late 1960s and 1970s, artists tried to make work that could not be bought and sold, giving rise to performance, video, and conceptual art. The market adapted: status came to depend on understanding the work, not on owning it.

## Controversy, law, and protection

Art has long been controversial. *Iconoclasm* is the deliberate destruction of disliked art; *aniconism* is a more general dislike of figurative images, especially religious ones, and shapes Islamic art. Édouard Manet's *Le Déjeuner sur l'Herbe* (1863) scandalised viewers by seating a nude woman beside fully dressed men in contemporary clothing. With Romanticism, the artist's vision became the usual determinant of content, sharpening such disputes. The 20th century then saw Impressionism, Cubism, Dadaism, and Surrealism succeed one another, each redefining what art could be. Modernism, the search for truth through art, gave way in the late 20th century to postmodern criticism, in which cultures are seen as changing forms appreciated with irony.

Legal and institutional frameworks now do work that philosophy once did alone. Intellectual-property law grants artists exclusive rights to reproduce, distribute, and display their creations. The UN, UNESCO, and Blue Shield International try to protect works in armed conflict and disaster, while UNESCO also safeguards living human traditions through its convention on intangible cultural heritage.
