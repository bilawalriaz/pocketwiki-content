# Music theory

## Core building blocks

**Pitch** is the perceived highness or lowness of a tone. Middle C vibrates at about 261.626 Hz, and the A above it is usually tuned to 440 Hz today, though France standardised435 Hz in 1859 and many cultures leave pitch flexible. The distance between two pitches is an *interval*; the *octave* (double or half the frequency) is so basic that all Cs in any octave belong to the same *pitch class*.

**Scales and modes** select notes from the octave. Western music divides the octave into twelve equal *semitones* and builds the major, harmonic minor, melodic minor, natural minor, pentatonic, and octatonic scales from patterns of whole and half steps. Many non-Western systems (Ottoman, Persian, Indian, Arabic) instead use quarter tones and "neutral" intervals of three or seven quarter tones. *Transposition* shifts a whole piece up or down while preserving interval relationships. The *circle of fifths* maps how the most-used Western keys relate, and a *key signature* at the start of a staff names the pitches of the chosen scale.

**Tuning systems** fix the exact size of intervals. *Equal temperament*, the international standard today, divides the octave into twelve equal semitones so fixed-pitch instruments like the piano can play in any key, at the cost of slightly flattening or sharpening the purer whole-number ratios of *just intonation*.

**Consonance and dissonance** label intervals as stable or unstable. Western common practice treats perfect fourths, fifths, octaves, thirds, and sixths as consonant, with everything else dissonant to some degree, but the line is culture- and era-dependent: the same major second can sound consonant in Debussy and dissonant in Bach. Indian classical theory still links keys to moods, times of day, and extra-musical meanings, and it does not use equal temperament.

**Rhythm and metre** organise sounds and silences in time. A *time signature* specifies how many beats fill a measure and which note value gets the beat. *Syncopation* stresses unexpected beats; *polyrhythm* layers different metres at once.

**Melody** is a sequence of pitches forming a tune, built from pitch, duration, rhythm, and tempo, and usually drawn from a scale or mode.

**Chords and harmony** stack three or more notes heard together. The most common are *triads* (three notes) classified as major, minor, augmented, or diminished, and built on a *root* note; adding more notes gives sevenths, extended chords, and added-tone chords. A *chord progression* is a sequence of chords, often labelled by *Roman numerals* showing each chord's role in the key. *Harmony* is the "vertical" dimension of music (simultaneous pitches), while *counterpoint* weaves independent melodic lines against each other.

**Timbre** is what makes a trumpet sound different from a flute at the same pitch: the blend of overtones and the attack-and-release envelope. It has no fully standardised vocabulary and is often described with words like *dolce* or symbols like *sul tasto* (bow over the fingerboard).

**Dynamics, articulation, texture, and form** round out the toolkit: dynamics (*piano* to *forte*) and articulation marks (staccato, legato, accent) shape performance; texture ranges from a *monophonic* single line to thick *polyphonic* layering; *form* organises a piece into sections, balancing repetition and contrast.

## A brief history

Mesopotamian clay tablets from before 1500 BCE, the oldest surviving evidence of musical thought anywhere, list intervals and tunings using a stable terminology for over a thousand years. Chinese theory, recorded in texts such as the *Lüshi chunqiu* (c. 238 BCE), builds scales around twelve pitches and the eight material classes of instruments (metal, stone, silk, bamboo, gourd, clay, leather, wood); Confucian and Daoist thinkers later debated music's moral and mystical roles. India's *Natya Shastra* (200 BCE–200 CE) systematically discusses scales (*Grāmas*), intervals (*Śrutis*), consonance and dissonance, and melodic types (*Jātis*).

Greek theorists split into two streams: technical manuals (Aristoxenus, Ptolemy, Cleonides) describing scales, notation, and rhythm, and philosophical treatises (Pythagoras, Philolaus, Archytas) arguing that music reveals universal numerical order. Pythagorean *ratios* of small whole numbers, observed when a vibrating string is divided, became the foundation of Western tuning. Medieval Arabic and Persian theorists (al-Kindi, al-Fārābi, Avicenna, Safi al-Din al-Urmawi) extended Greek tuning and developed modal systems; al-Kindi's oud fretting produced a 25-degree chromatic scale.

In medieval Europe, Boethius's *De institutione musica* (c. 500 CE) preserved Greek theory in Latin and dominated until Greek sources were retranslated in the 15th century. Guido d'Arezzo's 1028 letter introduced syllable-based *solmization* that evolved into today's do-re-mi, and Franco of Cologne's *Ars cantus mensurabilis* (c. 1280) established *mensural notation*, the proportional system of note-shapes whose descendants still read European rhythm. As Western influence spread in the 1800s, equal temperament and staff notation became an international standard, though oral traditions in Africa, India, and elsewhere kept their own tunings, modes, and theory.

## Theory as an academic discipline

In modern universities, music theory sits inside *musicology* (the broader study of music history and culture). Research methods include Schenkerian analysis (a reductive method tracing a tonal piece back to a deep *Ursatz*, or fundamental structure, of passing and neighbour notes around a tonic triad), transformational theory (David Lewin's approach modelling chord changes as operations in a mathematical group), musical set theory (classifying collections of pitches as numbered sets, with major chords written {0 4 7} and minor chords {0 3 7}), and empirical music psychology. Music and mathematics have been linked since the Pythagoreans: modern theorists use set theory and abstract algebra to analyse pitch, and the twelve pitch classes of an equal-tempered octave form an *abelian group* (a set where operation order does not affect the result). Arnold Schoenberg's twelve-tone technique, which orders all twelve chromatic notes into a *row* governing melody, harmony, and structure, evolved into serialism, extending serial control to duration, dynamics, and timbre. The Society for Music Theory, founded in 1977, helped establish theory as an independent academic discipline with its own journals, conferences, and doctoral programmes.
