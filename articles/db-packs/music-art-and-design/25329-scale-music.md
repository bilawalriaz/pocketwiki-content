# Scale (music)

A scale is a sequence of notes ordered by pitch from a starting note up or down to its octave, a note at double the frequency where the pattern repeats. The word comes from Latin *scala*, "ladder", because each scale is defined by its pattern of intervals between successive notes. Two scales with the same step-pattern are the same scale starting from different pitches; "C major" names both the pattern and the starting note.

Every scale has a tonic, the note chosen as the first degree and treated as the home pitch; Western pieces typically begin and end on it. The remaining notes are numbered by their steps from the tonic. In C major (C–D–E–F–G–A–B–C) the degrees are 1 through 7, with C again as 8. These labels are not inherent to the pattern: the same white-key notes, started on A, become the A minor scale. The seven traditional names of a heptatonic (seven-note) scale are tonic, supertonic, mediant, subdominant, dominant, submediant, and subtonic, or leading-tone when it sits a semitone below the tonic. A semitone, or half step, is the smallest standard interval in Western tuning.

Steps are measured by interval size. The two basic sizes in Western music are the whole step (two semitones, e.g. C to D) and the semitone (e.g. C to D♭). Steps need not be equal; microtonal music uses intervals smaller than a semitone. The major scale has the interval pattern whole–whole–half–whole–whole–whole–half (W–W–H–W–W–W–H); the natural minor rearranges the same seven notes. A scale containing semitones is hemitonic; one without them is anhemitonic, like the common pentatonic.

Most scales are octave-repeating, so the pattern recurs in every octave and can be drawn as a ring of pitch classes (notes grouped by octave position rather than exact pitch). The Bohlen–Pierce scale divides a tritave, a 3:1 frequency ratio, into13 equal steps instead of repeating at the octave.

Scales are classified by how many distinct pitch classes they contain per octave:

| Notes per octave | Name | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | ditonic | prehistoric music |
| 3 | tritonic | prehistoric music |
| 4 | tetratonic | rare |
| 5 | pentatonic | folk and Asian music (anhemitonic form) |
| 6 | hexatonic | Western folk music |
| 7 | heptatonic | standard modern Western scale |
| 8 | octatonic | jazz and modern classical |
| 9 | nonatonic | chromatic variant of the blues scale |
| 12 | chromatic, or dodecatonic | every semitone |

Further categories include hemitonic versus anhemitonic, atritonic versus tritonic (a tritone is an interval of three whole steps), and symmetry-based labels such as palindromic, chiral, or rotationally symmetric, the last exemplified by Messiaen's modes of limited transposition.

Even heard mostly one note at a time, a scale's notes form pairwise harmonic intervals. A scale of n notes yields n(n−1)/2 such pairs: 10 for 5 notes, 15 for 6, 21 for 7, 28 for 8. Theorists agree that the specific intervals present shape the scale's tonal character more than the note count.

Western music built up its scale vocabulary over time. Medieval and Renaissance practice used the white-note diatonic scale C–D–E–F–G–A–B with rare accidentals (notes outside the key signature), often to avoid the tritone. The common practice period standardized four seven-note scales, major, natural minor, harmonic minor, and melodic minor, in all transpositions (shiftings of every note by the same number of steps), and introduced modulation, the systematic shift from one scale to another. A typical move is from a tonic key to its dominant, the key a fifth above; C major modulates to G major, which adds F♯. The 19th and 20th centuries added the chromatic scale (12 notes), the whole-tone scale (6 notes), the pentatonic, and the octatonic or diminished scale (8 notes).

Western tuning divides the octave into 12 equal semitones under 12-tone equal temperament. Each semitone multiplies the frequency by the twelfth root of 2, about 1.059463. A scale selects a subset of these steps, typically seven.

Jazz and blues extend the system downward. Blue notes sit between standard pitches, are neither major nor minor, and are produced by bending the pitch on guitar, saxophone, trombone, or trumpet, since a piano cannot. Blues typically uses a pentatonic base, while jazz combines many modes and scales, often with chromatic motion.

Non-Western traditions rest on different foundations. Chinese folk music uses the pentatonic gong, shang, jue, chi, and yu (C, D, E, G, A). Indian rāgas are movable seven-note frameworks that often use śrutis, inflections smaller than a semitone. Turkish makams and Arabic maqamat use quarter-tone intervals. Gamelan music employs scales such as Pélog and Sléndro, which are neither equally tempered nor derived from the harmonic series (the natural overtones of a vibrating string or air column). Most scales in Indonesia and Indochina instead come from inharmonic resonance (vibration whose frequencies are not whole-number multiples of a fundamental) in metalophones and xylophones.
