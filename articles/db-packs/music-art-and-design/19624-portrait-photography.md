# Portrait photography

Portrait photography, or portraiture, is photography aimed at capturing the personality of a person or group through controlled lighting, backdrops, and poses. A portrait may be artistic or clinical, and is often commissioned for weddings, school events, yearbooks, or commercial use, displayed anywhere from a personal website to a business lobby.

## History

In the 19th and early 20th centuries, portraits rarely showed smiles, reflecting Victorian and Edwardian conventions. The mid-19th-century daguerreotype lowered cost and shortened sittings, making photographic portraits more popular than painted ones. Early works followed a painterly aesthetic and required long exposures, which is why "hidden mother" photography arose: mothers hid in the frame to calm and steady young children. Subjects typically sat against plain backdrops lit by an overhead window, supplemented with mirrors. As exposures shortened, photographers could work outside the studio.

## Lighting techniques

**Three-point lighting** is the standard studio setup, using a key light, fill light, and back light to reveal form and separate the subject from the background. The **key light**, placed 30–60° to the left, right, or above the subject's face, shapes features and controls shadow falloff through its distance from the camera. The **fill light**, placed opposite the key (for example, 30° camera-right if the key is 30° camera-left), softens or removes those shadows; a reflector often serves this role. The **back light**, placed behind the subject, separates them from the background and emphasises hair, and can create lens flare. A fourth light may illuminate the background.

**High-key lighting** produces a bright, mostly shadow-free image with a brighter background, typically using all three lights. **Low-key lighting** produces a dark image with strong shadows and a darker background, using just one light.

**Butterfly lighting** uses two lights: a key directly in front of and above the subject (higher than in three-point setup) and a fill (often a reflector) below the camera. It produces strong highlights on the forehead, nose bridge, and upper cheeks, with a small shadow beneath the nose. Hollywood portraitist George Hurrell made it famous, which is why it is also called Paramount lighting.

Modern portrait lighting usually relies on flash, diffused through umbrellas or soft boxes (fabric enclosures around a strobe). Hair and background lights are typically left undiffused. Snoots, barn doors, flags, and gobos shape light precisely, and colour gels create coloured backgrounds.

**Window light** predates artificial sources. A portrait needs only a window and a reflector: a white reflector evens contrast on the darker side of the face, and slow shutter speeds require a tripod. Early morning or late afternoon gives the most intense window light. Positioning the camera behind the subject creates a silhouette, beside the subject blends shadow and soft light, and facing the light produces high-key effects.

## Styles and approaches

Four approaches are commonly distinguished: **constructionist** (the photographer builds an idea around the subject, standard in studio, social, and advertising work), **environmental** (subject shown in their own setting, often doing something relevant), **candid** (unposed, as in street photography), and **creative** (image manipulation changes the final result).

## Lenses

Portrait lenses are typically fast moderate-telephoto primes. For head-and-shoulders portraits on 35 mm cameras, the classic focal length range is 75–135 mm, giving an 18–32° diagonal angle of view, roughly 1.5–2.5× a normal lens. This range produces flattering perspective flattening, with the difference between 85 mm and 135 mm being subtle. Medium- and large-format cameras need proportionally longer focal lengths, roughly 150–400 mm, for the same framing.

For full-length figure portraits, a 50 mm lens (47°) works on 35 mm format, and a 35 mm wide-angle lens (63.5°) captures groups up to 3 m wide. Shorter focal lengths force the photographer closer, producing unflattering distortion such as a larger nose and smaller ears. Longer focal lengths flatten perspective more but require the photographer to stand further away, making communication harder, which is why fashion photographers sometimes rely on loudspeakers.

Primes are preferred over zooms because they are lighter, cheaper, faster, and sharper, and zooms can introduce barrel or pincushion distortion. A 70–200 mm zoom covers the full head-and-shoulders range.

**Lens speed** matters because wide apertures create shallow depth of field, isolating the subject from a distracting background. Stopping down one or two stops, with focus on the eyes, keeps the whole face sharp while blurring the background.

For environmental portraits, where showing context matters, wider lenses with less background blur work better. Portrait lenses are often inexpensive because their focal lengths sit near the normal range: a 50 mm normal lens on a 1.6× cropped sensor yields an 80 mm equivalent, at the wide end of the classic portrait range.
