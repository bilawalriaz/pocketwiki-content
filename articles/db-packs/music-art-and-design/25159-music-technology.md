# Music technology

Music technology is the use of any device, mechanism, machine, or tool by a musician or composer to compose, notate, perform, record, analyze, or edit music. The subject spans three overlapping layers: mechanical devices that shape, store, or amplify sound physically; electric systems that convert vibration into electrical signals; and electronic or digital systems that generate or process audio using circuits and software. Each layer built on the previous one, and each new technology changed how music was made, distributed, and heard.

## From bone to staff

The earliest evidence of music technology is prehistoric. Paleolithic flutes, including the disputed Divje Babe flute carved from a cave bear femur, are over 40,000 years old. Ancient Egyptians built harps, lyres, double clarinets, and metal cymbals, while the Indus Valley civilization produced seven-holed flutes and early stringed instruments such as the Ravanahatha. The Greeks classified their instruments into strings, winds, and percussion, with the lyre, kithara, aulos, syrinx, and hydraulis (a water-pressurised pipe organ described by Vitruvius and Heron) among the most important.

Notation was itself a technology. During the Middle Ages, neumes were introduced as signs above chant texts to indicate melodic shape; by the ninth century these signs were firmly established. Guido d'Arezzo, around 1000–1050, is credited with completing the four-line staff, which gave singers a fixed reference for pitch. The printing press, invented around 1450, made sheet music cheap and abundant, allowing musical styles to spread faster and further than before.

## Pianos, valves, and industrial materials

The Baroque era produced the piano, invented before 1700 by Bartolomeo Cristofori of Padua. Its key innovation was a hammer that struck the string and immediately fell away, giving both loudness and dynamic control where the harpsichord offered only uniform volume. The Industrial Revolution supplied stronger materials: steel wire, cast-iron frames (patented in Boston in 1825 by Alpheus Babcock), felt hammer coverings (introduced 1826 by Jean-Henri Pape), and the double-escapement action of Sébastien Érard (1821), which made rapid repetition possible. The piano's range expanded from Mozart's five octaves to more than seven octaves by 1820.

The Romantic era also saw brass instruments transformed when valves, patented beginning in 1814, allowed horns, trumpets, and tubas to play every note of the chromatic scale rather than only the tones of the overtone series. Hector Berlioz used the new valved instruments in his *Overture to Les Francs-juges* in 1826. Adolphe Sax patented the saxophone in 1846, combining a single-reed mouthpiece with a brass body to bridge woodwind and brass families.

## Electricity and amplification

In the early twentieth century, electromagnetic pickups, amplifiers, and loudspeakers enabled instruments whose sound came entirely from electrical reproduction. The electric piano appeared in 1929, the electric guitar in 1931, the electro-mechanical Hammond organ (developed by Laurens Hammond and John M. Hanert) around 1934, and the electric bass in 1935, though the bass did not become commercially successful until the 1950s. None of these is audible on its own: a pickup converts string vibration into a weak signal that must pass through an amplifier and loudspeaker to reach the audience. The ability to alter that signal with effects such as reverb and distortion reshaped jazz, then rock, and eventually most popular music.

Sound recording had equally large effects. The gramophone (commercialised 1892) and radio broadcasting (commercial from around 1919–1920) vastly expanded listening. Multitrack recording let bands and producers overdub many layers of instruments and vocals, producing sounds impossible in a single live take, and enabled the acousmatic and *Musique concrète* schools of electronic composition.

## Electronics, computers, and synthesis

The transistor, invented in 1947, made portable electronic instruments practical. Synthesizers generate sound directly from electrical signals, without strings, pipes, or metal tines, and can imitate existing instruments or create new timbres. They entered pop music in the 1960s and reached the mass market in the early 1980s. Drum machines, sequencers, and digital audio workstations (software environments for recording, mixing, and editing) followed as microchips grew more powerful. Electronic and digital music technology now refers broadly to any computer, effects unit, or program used in performance, composition, recording, mixing, analysis, or editing.

Recurring innovations, including water-pressure organs in antiquity, valve-driven brass in the nineteenth century, the transistor in the twentieth, and software synthesis today, repeatedly extended what a single performer could control, and each changed both how music sounded and how it reached listeners.
