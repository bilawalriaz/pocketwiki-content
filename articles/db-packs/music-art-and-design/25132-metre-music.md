# Metre (music)

In music, *metre* is the regular pattern of strong and weak beats that listeners perceive in a piece, even when those accents are not literally played. It is distinct from rhythm: rhythm is the actual surface of sounds, while metre is the abstract grid the listener imposes on those sounds. The same clock ticks, "tick–tock–tick–tock", can be heard in groups of two (ONE-two-ONE-two) or three (ONE-two-three-ONE-two-three); the ticks are identical, only the heard metre differs.

Metre arises from two interacting levels of motion: a faster pulse and a slower grouping of those pulses into repeating units. Once a listener establishes this hierarchy, they will preserve it as long as minimal evidence remains, a tendency Joel Lester calls the persistence of metric organisation. Each bar (also called a *measure*) typically contains one such group, and a bar's beats are counted from one accent to the next, the musical equivalent of a poetic *foot*.

Western notated metre is indicated by a *time signature*, written as a fraction. The lower number names the note value that represents one beat (4 = quarter note, 8 = eighth note); the upper number names how many of those beats fill each bar. Two dimensions classify nearly all metres:

- **Number of beats per bar**: duple (2), triple (3), quadruple (4), or higher (5, 7, etc.).
- **How each beat subdivides**: *simple* (each beat splits into 2 equal parts) or *compound* (each beat splits into 3 equal parts).

| Number of beats per bar | Simple (beat = 2) | Compound (beat = 3) |
|---|---|---|
| 2 (duple) | 2/4, 2/2 | 6/8, 6/4 |
| 3 (triple) | 3/4, 3/8 | 9/8, 9/4 |
| 4 (quadruple) | 4/4, 4/2 | 12/8, 12/4 |

In simple metre the top number gives the beat count directly (3/4 has three quarter-note beats); in compound metre the top number gives subdivisions, so 6/8 has two beats, each a dotted quarter that divides into three eighth notes. The two can produce bars of the same length — 3/4 and 6/8 both contain six eighth notes — yet they feel different because the accents fall in different places. Leonard Bernstein exploits this slippage in "America" from *West Side Story*, where the melody slides between 3/4 and 6/8.

Metres above four beats (quintuple 5/4, septuple 7/8) are unusual in common-practice Western music. Classical theory holds that listeners parse such bars as additive groupings of duple and triple units: a bar of 5/4 is heard as 2+3 or 3+2, and a bar of 7/8 as 2+2+3, 3+2+2, or 2+3+2. Such *aksak* ("limping") metres are routine in Bulgarian, Macedonian, and Indian classical music, and the Macedonian dance metre 3+2+2+3+2 is a well-known example.

Three broader classes of rhythm sit beneath metre. *Metrical rhythm*, the dominant Western type, divides time as multiples or fractions of one fixed beat with regular recurring accents. *Measured rhythm* (also called *additive rhythm*) keeps a fixed beat unit but places accents irregularly. *Free rhythm* uses no fixed unit at all, so regular accents become impossible; plainchant and the Japanese *honkyoku* shakuhachi repertoire are common examples. The Italian term *senza misura*, "without metre", instructs a performer to play without a beat, using wall-clock seconds if a duration must be measured.

Metre in song is inherited from poetic metre: the *foot* (a pulse-group) becomes the bar, and the arrangement of feet into lines, couplets, and stanzas becomes the arrangement of bars into phrases and sections. Common hymn and ballad *metres* specify both syllable counts per line (8, 6, 8, 6 for *common metre*) and a rhyme scheme (ABAB), which is why one tune can carry many texts and why "Amazing Grace" can be sung to the melody of "The House of the Rising Sun".

Several extensions of basic metre appear frequently in modern music:

- **Hypermetre** is metre at a higher level, where bars themselves act as beats grouped into four-bar *hypermeasures*. Four-bar phrases are the prototype for country music, and Edward T. Cone coined the term in 1968.
- **Polymetre** runs two or more metres simultaneously that share the same beat. Four bars of 7/4 equal seven bars of 4/4, so the parts realign periodically; The Cars' "Touch And Go" places drums and bass in 5/4 against vocals and guitar in 4/4.
- **Polyrhythm** fixes the bar length while the beats differ, as in 4:3, where three beats of 3/4 are stretched to fill the same time as four beats of 4/4. The two metres meet after 12 beats, the lowest common denominator.
- **Changing metre** and **metric modulation**, switching the prevailing metre mid-piece, became common in 20th-century music; Stravinsky's *The Rite of Spring* is a landmark example.

Meshuggah's metal, Frank Zappa's "Toads of the Short Forest" (with simultaneous metres of 7/8, 3/4, 5/8, and more), and Balkan folk dance music all demonstrate that the metric framework need not be uniform across players or even within a single line.
