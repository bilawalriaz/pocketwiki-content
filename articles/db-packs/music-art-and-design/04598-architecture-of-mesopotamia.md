# Architecture of Mesopotamia

Mesopotamian architecture, built between the 10th millennium BC and the 6th century BC in the Tigris–Euphrates region by the Sumerians, Akkadians, Babylonians, and Assyrians, is one of the earliest fully urban built traditions. Its core idea is simple: a planned city organised around a temple, built almost entirely from mudbrick, and adapted to a hot riverine environment.

The defining material was sun-baked mudbrick, which is far less durable than fired brick. As a result, every few generations a new building was raised directly on the rubble of the old, and the city slowly rose. These accumulated mounds are called **tells** and are the most visible trace of Mesopotamian cities today. Plano-convex bricks (flat on one side, domed on the other) were a signature of the Early Dynastic Period. Babylonians later favoured brilliant colours, glazed tiles, and three-dimensional figures, while Assyrians lined their palace walls with carved upright stone slabs called **orthostats** and flanked their gates with **lamassu**, colossal figures with a human head, winged bull or lion body, whose function was *apotropaic* (to avert evil). Imported cedar and lapis lazuli signalled prestige.

The Sumerians were the first to conceive of a city as a planned construct, with Uruk in the 4th millennium BC serving as the template. A typical plan placed residential, commercial, and civic zones under a high temple complex at the core, while irrigated farmland encircled the walls. Movement within and beyond the city ran through a three-tiered network of roads and canals. Growth was a mix of deliberate planning (walls, main streets) and organic infill within those limits.

The standard dwelling was the inward-facing **courtyard house** (*bītu*), with rooms ranged around an open central space that drew in cool air and screened the interior from the street. Walls were mudbrick on a foundation of mud plaster and reeds, with wooden doors and roofs, and often multiple stories: shops or storage below, living quarters above. The plan enforced a sharp public–private boundary through a single, indirect entrance, so a visitor had to turn past a wall before reaching the court.

Palaces (*ekallu*, "big house") appeared in Early Dynastic I, c. 2900 BC, and grew as political power concentrated. They were not merely royal homes but large socio-economic institutions containing workshops, storehouses, shrines, and residential quarters. Iron Age Assyrian palaces at Nimrud, Khorsabad, and Nineveh are best known for their narrative stone reliefs and ceremonial courtyards.

Temples often predated the cities around them, evolving from small shrines into massive complexes understood as the meeting point of gods and humans and the symbolic **axis mundi**, the connection between heaven, earth, and underworld. Early temples used a *bent-axis* approach, forcing worshippers to turn ninety degrees before facing the cult statue; later temples used a *straight axis*. The most distinctive temple form was the **ziggurat**, a stepped pyramidal tower with a mudbrick core and a baked-brick facing, supporting a shrine at its summit. The Ziggurat of Ur, with three receding stages and monumental staircases, is the classic surviving example, though the reconstructed third stage reflects excavator Leonard Woolley's interpretation since only two stages are actually preserved.

Landscape mattered. Open space was deliberately reserved for orchards and gardens, and irrigation agriculture produced some of the earliest enclosed garden forms, including the quadrangular *sar*, a precursor to later Persian paradise gardens. A carved water basin from Girsu, c. 3000 BC, shows fountains and water features were already part of the designed landscape.

Two points remain genuinely uncertain. The exact symbolic purpose of ziggurats is debated; a popular hypothesis that they imitated mountains has limited textual or archaeological support. The daily life of poorer inhabitants, who likely built with perishable materials, is poorly documented because such remains rarely survive in the archaeological record.
