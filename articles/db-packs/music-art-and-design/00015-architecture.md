# Architecture

Architecture is the art and science of designing and constructing buildings and other physical structures, uniting civil engineering technique with visual intent. It operates as both a process (sketching, planning, designing) and a product (the finished built work). Surviving buildings are durable, so historical civilizations are often identified by their architecture, and architectural works commonly serve as cultural symbols.

## Theory

The philosophy of architecture, a branch of the philosophy of art, examines aesthetic value, meaning, and cultural role; thinkers from Plato to Foucault, Venturi, and Wittgenstein have engaged it. Vitruvius's foundational triad is **firmitas** (durability), **utilitas** (utility), and **venustas** (beauty). Alberti, in *De re aedificatoria*, located beauty in objective proportion modeled on the idealized human figure and the golden mean, treating it as inherent rather than applied. The concept of architectural "style" emerged only in the 16th century with Vasari; Serlio's treatise was the first practical handbook and the first to catalog the five **classical orders**. Pugin championed Gothic as the only "true Christian form," while Ruskin held that a building must be "adorned" to qualify as architecture. Le Corbusier distinguished mere construction from Architecture, which "touches my heart"; Mies van der Rohe located architecture's beginning in the careful placement of two bricks.

Sullivan's "form follows function" replaced Vitruvian utility with a broader notion of function that included aesthetic, psychological, and cultural dimensions. Aesthetics remain contested: studies show strong public preference for traditional and classical styles over modernist ones; Curl criticizes modernism as alienating and environmentally damaging, while Krier calls traditional styles' dominance in private architecture "an overwhelming democratic reality." Major strands include Rationalism, Empiricism, Structuralism, Poststructuralism, Deconstruction, and Phenomenology. Sustainability entered architectural theory in the late 20th century.

## History

Architecture began as rural, oral **vernacular** building refined by trial and error; early urban centers such as Çatalhöyük and Mohenjo-daro grew with expanding economies. Ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia used monumentality to express divine engagement and political power, with Egyptian state architects tied to the priestly class. Greek and Roman practice shifted toward civic ideals, codifying the classical orders, with Roman work incorporating Greek elements.

Asian architecture developed independently: Indian and Chinese forms influenced surrounding regions (Japanese did not). Buddhist architecture showed regional diversity, while Hindu temple architecture emerged around the 5th century CE, governed by Shastras and expressing macrocosm and microcosm. Early Asian texts include China's *Kao Gong Ji* (7th–5th centuries BC) and India's *Shilpa Shastras*. African architecture is highly diverse: fractal scaling, Great Zimbabwe's walls, wattle-and-daub, Sudano-Sahelian and Sobrado styles in West Africa, coral-stone Swahili Coast homes, and Ethiopia's 13th-century rock-hewn Lalibela churches.

Islamic architecture began in the 7th century, drawing on ancient Middle Eastern and Byzantine forms, and spread across the Middle East, Africa, Iberia, the Mediterranean, India, and the Balkans via Ottoman expansion. Medieval Europe developed guilds and master masons (*Magister lathomorum*), great abbeys and cathedrals, and pan-European Romanesque and Gothic styles. From c. 1400 the Renaissance revived Classical learning and humanism; named architects such as Brunelleschi, Alberti, Michelangelo, and Palladio emerged, though artist, architect, and engineer remained undivided. Later, science and new materials separated architecture from engineering; the "gentleman architect" served wealthy clients, Beaux-Arts training emphasized drawings over feasibility, and industrialization cheapened ornament.

Modernism arose from dissatisfaction with revivalist decoration: the Deutscher Werkbund (1907) and Bauhaus (Weimar, 1919) redefined architecture as a synthesis of art, craft, and technology. Post-WWI modernists rejected historical styles for pure functional forms, exposing steel and concrete; Wright developed **organic architecture** (Robie House, Fallingwater); Mies, Johnson, and Breuer pursued simplified geometry enabling high-rises; Fazlur Rahman Khan's tube structure permitted greater height. Mid-century Modernism became the **International Style**. Resistance produced Brutalism, then critiques of austerity produced phenomenology and postmodernism, crystallized in Venturi's contrast of the "decorated shed" against the modernist "duck."

Since the 1980s, complexity has split "design" from "project" architects. Sustainability is now mainstream (passive solar, green roofs, biodegradable materials, LEED), and New Urbanism and New Classical architecture promote smart growth against sprawl.

## Types

Residential architecture fits lifestyles within codes and zoning; commercial serves business, government, and religious institutions; industrial focuses on safe movement of labor and goods. Landscape architecture shapes outdoor areas; interior architecture addresses spaces within structural shells, including **adaptive reuse**; urban design integrates landscape, planning, and engineering at city scale. Green Building emphasizes renewable energy, sustainable materials, and water conservation, growing after the 1973 oil embargo. Naval architecture covers marine vessel design and certification, and "architecture" is used metaphorically in computing, business, cognition, and systems. Robert Reitherman coined **seismic architecture** in 1985 to describe architectural expression of earthquake resistance.

## Key Terms

- **Firmitas / utilitas / venustas**: Vitruvius's durability, utility, and beauty.
- **Vernacular architecture**: Non-academic building based on cultural tradition.
- **Classical orders**: Canonical Greek and Roman architectural styles.
- **Organic architecture**: Form defined by environment and purpose (Wright).
- **"Decorated shed" vs. "duck"**: Venturi's contrast of ornament versus unnecessary expression.

## Open Questions

Whether architecture is distinct from mere building remains philosophically contested. Aesthetic preferences divide architects and the public. Metaphoric architecture's status—as postmodern aspect or independent school—is debated. Some professionals criticize both Modernism and Postmodernism as neglecting everyday needs, arguing design should draw on behavioral, environmental, and social sciences.
