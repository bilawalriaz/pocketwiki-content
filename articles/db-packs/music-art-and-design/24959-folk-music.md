# Folk music

Folk music is music made by ordinary people, passed on by memory and performance rather than by sheet music or recordings, and rooted in the daily life, work, and identity of a community. The term itself is recent: English antiquarian William Thoms coined "folklore" in 1846, and the music sense was shaped by German Romantics, especially Johann Gottfried Herder, who used *Volk* ("the people") in the 18th century. Folk music resists a sharp musical definition, though common markers include oral transmission, anonymous or absorbed authorship, performance on traditional instruments, ties to a particular region or culture, and gradual change across generations through what scholars call the "folk process."

## Defining traits

Traditional folk music is typically learned and passed down by ear, because most people were illiterate before the 20th century. It is culturally particular, tied to a region, language, or ethnic group, which is why immigrant communities such as Greek Australians or Punjabi Canadians use it to mark identity. It accompanies the calendar and the life cycle: harvest songs, Christmas carols, wedding and funeral music, religious festivals. Most traditional songs have no copyright because their authors are unknown or forgotten, though since the 1940s almost every recorded folk song credits an arranger. Performers usually improvise chords and harmonies, because broadsheets rarely specified them. The most common tune structure is AABB (binary form, two repeated sections), and related tunes cluster into "tune families."

## Origins and functions

Before recorded music existed, singing and playing were the main leisure entertainment and the natural accompaniment to manual labour. Work songs set the pace for planting, reaping, threshing, weaving, and milling, reduced boredom, and coordinated group effort. In preliterate societies, music had to be learned by ear, which is why oral transmission is central to folk music worldwide. Different cultures draw the boundary between folk and art or court music differently: Indian traditions have always distinguished classical from folk, while in America and Germany the line between "folk," popular song, and national song has long been loose. Some scholars argue folk music began as art music reshaped by oral transmission.

## Subject matter and forms

Traditional folk songs carry narrative verse: epics, ballads of battles and tragedies, laments, and celebrations of victory such as the Biblical Song of Deborah. They preserve folk heroes like John Henry and Robin Hood. Work songs use call and response to coordinate labour, as in American jody calls (rhythmic marching chants) and sailors' sea shanties. Love poetry, often tragic, and nursery rhymes are widespread. Religious music and hymns are frequently of traditional, unknown origin, and Western musical notation was originally created to preserve Gregorian chant.

## The folk process

Because transmission is oral, no two singers perform a song identically. Variants proliferate, so there is rarely one authoritative version of a traditional ballad. Folklorist Cecil Sharp compared this to biological natural selection: over time, the most appealing variants are the ones singers remember and pass on. The Afghan song *Mullā Mohammed Jān*, which spread from Herat around 1970, acquired countless regional versions thanks to its repetitive, predictable structure.

## The 20th-century revival and contemporary folk

Starting in the mid-20th century, a new form of popular folk music emerged from traditional folk music, particularly in the English-speaking world. This second folk revival peaked in the 1960s and produced what is now called contemporary folk music: songs fixed in form, written by known songwriters like Bob Dylan and Donovan, often sharing stages with traditional performers. The Grammy Awards added a "Folk" category in 1959, renamed it "Best Ethnic or Traditional Recording" in 1970, and split it into traditional and contemporary categories in 1987. Fusion forms such as folk rock, folk metal, and anti-folk grew out of the same moment. A parallel Spanish-language movement, *Nueva Canción*, fused folk with political activism across Chile, Spain, and Latin America in the 1960s and 1970s.

## Scholarly preservation

From the early 19th century, scholars and amateur collectors raced to record vanishing traditions. Francis James Child gathered over 300 English and Scots ballads. Cecil Sharp, Sabine Baring-Gould, and the English Folk Dance and Song Society preserved rural English song. Krišjānis Barons collected 217,996 Latvian folk songs between 1894 and 1915. Audio recording, available from the late 19th century, transformed the field. In the United States, John Lomax, Alan Lomax, and Robert Winslow Gordon built vast field archives for the Library of Congress. Folk material also fed classical composition: Dvořák, Grieg, Bartók, Vaughan Williams, and Percy Grainger wove folk melodies into concert works.

## Regional variety

Folk music exists in every inhabited region, each with distinctive instruments and forms. Chinese folk music reaches back 7000 years and is largely pentatonic (built on a five-note scale), featuring ensembles like *sizhu* ("silk and bamboo") and the *sheng*, a mouth organ that is the ancestor of Western free-reed instruments such as the accordion. Sri Lankan folk music centres on a classical orchestra of drums, with the Kandyan dance built around the *gatabera* drum. Australian bush ballads, the most famous being "Waltzing Matilda," carry the rural and rebellious spirit of the 19th-century outback. Latin American folk music fuses Indigenous, Iberian, and African sources; the Andean *Nueva Canción* movement of the 1970s revived the genre across the region. North American folk includes Appalachian, Cajun, and African-American spirituals, which fed bluegrass, country, blues, gospel, and later rock and roll.

## Decline and revival

Industrialisation and mass media have eroded traditional folk music fastest where commercialisation is most advanced, though loss occurs everywhere. Traditional folk music survives best where it remains a badge of cultural or national identity, and UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage programme is the main international instrument for protecting regional traditions. Folk festivals from the National Folk Festival in the United States (1934), Newport (1959), and Cambridge (1965) to Australia's National Folk Festival sustain the tradition by gathering tens of thousands of performers and audiences each year.
