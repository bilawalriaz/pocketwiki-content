# Interaction design

Interaction design (IxD) is the practice of designing interactive digital products, environments, systems, and services. Where other design fields centre on form, IxD centres on behavior: how a product responds over time and how a user steers it. Because it deals in imagined possibilities rather than observed facts, IxD is treated as a design discipline rather than a science or engineering one.

The field borrows from psychology, human–computer interaction, information architecture, and user research. Unlike software engineering, which often optimises for technical stakeholders, IxD optimises for the user's experience within business and technical limits. Practitioners usually work as UX or UI designers. Because interaction problems have many possible users, many contexts, and many states, tools like Figma and Adobe XD exist to manage that complexity.

## How it began

The term was coined by Bill Moggridge and Bill Verplank in the mid-1980s, but it took about ten years to spread. Verplank framed it as user interface design adapted for industrial designers; Moggridge framed it as an improvement on his 1984 coinage "soft-face," describing industrial design applied to products containing software.

The first program officially named "Interaction Design" was a Master of Design at Carnegie Mellon in 1994, originally screen-focused before broadening to people, organizations, culture, and services. Earlier academic work included MIT's Visible Language Workshop (Muriel Cooper, 1975) and NYU's Interactive Telecommunications Program (1979). In 1990 Gillian Crampton Smith founded the Computer-Related Design MA at the Royal College of Art, renamed Design Interactions in 2005. In 2001 she helped found the Interaction Design Institute Ivrea (IDII) in Italy; after IDII closed in 2007, several of its people started the Copenhagen Institute of Interaction Design (CIID).

## Core methods

- **Goal-oriented design** (Alan Cooper) starts from user needs and goals, not the provider's. Cooper's *cognitive friction* names the frustration from interfaces that are inconsistent, mode-heavy, or hard to predict.
- **Usability** asks "can someone use this?" Jakob Nielsen frames it as a quality attribute; Shneiderman's Eight Golden Rules of Interface Design are standard heuristics for improving it.
- **Personas** are fictional archetypes that capture key behaviour patterns and goals, giving designers and stakeholders a shared character to design for.
- **Cognitive dimensions** offer a lightweight vocabulary — *consistency*, *error-proneness*, *hard mental operations*, *viscosity*, *premature commitment* — for critiquing an interface and steering redesigns through "design maneuvers."
- **Affective design** treats emotional response as a design material. Fonts, color, animation, and sound shape how a product feels, and studies show affective judgements shift perceived usability. Don Norman's emotional design model, Patrick Jordan's pleasure model, and McCarthy and Wright's Technology as Experience framework explain how.

## The five dimensions

Crampton Smith described four design languages IxD draws on; Kevin Silver added a fifth, *behavior*, which ties the others together by defining how the system responds and how the user reacts.

| Dimension | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Words | The language users read or speak to the system |
| Visual representations | Typography, icons, diagrams, and other graphics |
| Physical objects or space | The hardware or environment the user inhabits |
| Time | Media that change over time, such as sound, video, and animation |
| Behavior | How the system responds, and how the user reacts |

## Adjacent disciplines

Industrial design contributes knowledge of form, color, perception, and physical usability. Human factors and ergonomics contribute anthropometry, biomechanics, kinesiology, and physiology as they apply to built environments. Cognitive psychology contributes mental models, mapping, interface metaphors, and affordances — most laid out in Donald Norman's *The Design of Everyday Things*. Human–computer interaction contributes usability research methods, including cognitive dimensions and cognitive walkthroughs. Architecture becomes relevant as IxD expands into ubiquitous computing, urban informatics, and urban computing, where making and placing things in context matters. UI design overlaps heavily, but IxD centres on the parts of an interface that define and present its behavior over time, with the system adapting to the user rather than the reverse.
