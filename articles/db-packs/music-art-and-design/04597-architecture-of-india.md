# Architecture of India

Indian architecture spans over 4,000 years and shifts in form with each ruling power, religion, and foreign contact. A few structural choices repeat across styles, so recognizing them builds a working model of the whole tradition.

## Structural systems and materials

Two construction systems define the tradition. The **trabeate** system uses horizontal beams (lintels) carried by vertical posts and dominates early Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain buildings. The **arcuate** system uses arches and domes and arrives with Indo-Islamic architecture after 1206 CE. Most of the oldest surviving buildings are **rock-cut** caves rather than freestanding structures, because early wooden buildings did not survive India's climate.

## Indus Valley and Mauryan foundations (c. 2600–185 BCE)

The **Indus Valley Civilisation** (c. 2600–1900 BCE) built cities of fired brick with grid streets, drainage, and wells, but its buildings were utilitarian and left no religious monuments. After its decline, the **Maurya Empire** (c. 321–185 BCE) introduced monumental stone work, including the Pillars of Ashoka and the polished Pataliputra capital, probably influenced by Achaemenid Persian and Hellenistic models.

## Hindu temple architecture (4th century CE onward)

A Hindu temple centers on a small inner sanctum, the **garbhagriha**, which holds the main deity. A tower rises over it: the curvilinear **shikhara** in northern **Nagara** temples and the stepped, pyramidal **vimana** in southern **Dravidian** temples. A third style, **Vesara**, is a hybrid of the two found in the Deccan. Regional variations include the star-shaped, heavily carved Hoysala temples and the Khajuraho complex, both built between the 10th and 12th centuries. Dravidian temple compounds eventually added tall gateway towers called **gopurams**, which often dwarf the central sanctuary.

## Buddhist and Jain architecture (6th century BCE onward)

Buddhist architecture centers on three building types: **stupas** (reliquary mounds), **chaityas** (prayer halls, often rock-cut with vaulted ceilings), and **viharas** (monasteries). Famous rock-cut examples survive at Ajanta and Ellora. Jain temples borrow the Hindu plan but are usually identified by large open-air statues of **tirthankaras** (the 24 enlightened teachers) and dense ornamental carving, as at the Dilwara Temples. The **Māru-Gurjara** style, developed in Gujarat and Rajasthan, spread across India as a pan-regional Jain idiom.

## Indo-Islamic and Mughal architecture (1206–1857)

The **Delhi Sultanate** (1206–1526) introduced Indo-Islamic architecture, replacing trabeate construction with arches and domes. The **Mughal Empire** (1526–1857) refined the synthesis into a recognizable style: bulbous domes, slender minarets, and symmetrical four-part gardens called **charbagh**. The Taj Mahal is the most cited example. Mughal forms also shaped Rajput palaces and later Sikh gurdwaras, including the Golden Temple at Amritsar.

## Colonial and modern architecture (1757–present)

British rule introduced Neoclassical and Gothic Revival, which merged with Indian motifs into **Indo-Saracenic** buildings such as railway stations and government offices. After independence in 1947, India turned to international modernism. The Swiss-French architect Le Corbusier planned the new city of **Chandigarh**, and his concrete forms became a reference point for state architecture. Economic liberalization after 1991 brought private towers, glass facades, and the first generation of Indian skyscrapers.

## Open questions

The origins of the true arch in India remain debated: its use in Hindu temples is rare, but pre-Islamic examples at the Mahabodhi Temple and Bhitargaon suggest an indigenous tradition. Scholars also disagree on whether the **chaitya** arch form came from Lycian rock-cut tombs through Persia or evolved locally from wooden prototypes. The **Vesara** style is classified by some as a distinct hybrid and by others as a regional branch of Dravidian architecture.
