# Islamic architecture

Islamic architecture is the built environment of Muslim-majority societies from the 7th century onward, stretching from West Africa to East Asia. Despite regional variety, its buildings share recurring forms, techniques, and decorative logics, and most were built on terrain that already had powerful architectural traditions, especially Byzantine (Eastern Roman) and Sasanian (pre-Islamic Persian) ones.

The earliest form was a courtyard. In 622, the Prophet Muhammad built the first mosque in Medina as a simple open courtyard, and this plan became the *hypostyle* model: a roofed prayer hall supported by a grid of columns, often opening onto a courtyard (*ṣaḥn*). A niche called the *mihrab* marks the *qibla*, the wall facing Mecca, and a *minaret* rises beside the building to issue the call to prayer. The Umayyad Caliphate (661–750) added the horseshoe arch and extensive mosaic decoration, fusing Byzantine and Sasanian motifs at sites such as the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem and the Great Mosque of Damascus.

After the Abbasid Caliphate (750–1258) moved the political center to Baghdad and Samarra, new forms appeared: stucco ornament, the four-centred arch, and the spiral minaret, with the Ibn Tulun Mosque in Cairo (879) showing this Abbasid-influenced style in Egypt. From the 10th century onward, as the caliphate fragmented, regional traditions grew more distinct.

In the western Islamic world, *Moorish* architecture in al-Andalus and the Maghreb became known for horseshoe arches, ribbed domes, and intricate geometric tilework called *zellij*; the Great Mosque of Córdoba (785–786) is its starting point. In Iran and Central Asia, the Seljuks (11th–12th centuries) popularised the four-*iwan* mosque plan, a courtyard framed by four vaulted halls walled on three sides and open on the fourth, together with brick vaulting and glazed tilework later refined under the Timurids. In Egypt and Syria, the Mamluks (1250–1517) built dense, multi-functional urban complexes of carved stone topped by bulbous domes; the Fatimids had earlier founded al-Azhar Mosque in Cairo in 970. In West Africa, the Songhai and Hausa built with mud-brick and pyramidal minarets from the 14th to 16th centuries.

The two most influential later traditions were Ottoman and Mughal. From 1453 onward, the Ottomans, inspired by the Byzantine church of Hagia Sophia, perfected monumental central-dome mosques, brought to their highest expression by the architect Mimar Sinan. The Mughals (1526–1857) blended Persian, Timurid, and Indian elements in monuments such as the Taj Mahal. Domes reflect these regional choices: Ottoman domes are centralized and spherical, Persian domes are often double-shelled and bulbous, and Maghrebi domes are ribbed. *Muqarnas*, three-dimensional honeycomb vaulting used to transition between square rooms and round domes, evolved from earlier Umayyad diaphragm arches (flat spans built into a wider roof to stiffen it) into a defining decorative and structural device.

Decoration follows a religious constraint: because figural imagery was generally avoided in religious settings, ornament relied on geometric patterns, *arabesques* (stylized, rhythmic vegetal motifs), and calligraphy. A *mashrabiya*, a projecting wooden lattice screen, allowed residents to see out without being seen, shaping a domestic ethic in which mosques and markets formed public zones and inward-facing courtyard houses formed the private ones. In Iran, the *chahar bagh*, a four-part garden divided by walkways or water channels, symbolised paradise and became integral to palaces and tombs.

Whether "Islamic architecture" is one style or simply the architecture of Muslim-majority regions remains debated, given the diversity. The origins of the minaret and muqarnas are uncertain and may have arisen independently in several places. The relative weight of pre-Islamic Arabian building versus the dominant influence of Byzantine and Sasanian traditions absorbed through conquest is still reassessed, and the role of religious symbolism versus function or aesthetics in shaping form remains a topic of scholarly interpretation.
