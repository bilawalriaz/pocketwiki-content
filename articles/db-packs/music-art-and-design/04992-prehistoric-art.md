# Prehistoric art

Prehistoric art is the artistic production of cultures that existed before writing or sustained contact with literate societies. It is the primary material evidence for the cognitive and symbolic capacities of early humans, including abstract thought, ritual, and aesthetic sensibility. The label does not end at one global date: it closes for a region only when that region develops writing, so prehistoric art in the Americas continues until the 15th century CE, while in Europe it effectively ends with the Bronze and Iron Ages.

## When art began

The earliest undisputed figurative art appears around 40,000 years ago, in the Upper Paleolithic of Europe, with works such as the Venus of Hohle Fels (a small female figurine with exaggerated features) and the Lion-man (a hybrid human-feline sculpture). Around the same time, cave paintings appear at Pettakere in Indonesia, and this near-simultaneous emergence on two continents is one reason scholars suspect figurative art may have an older common origin, perhaps in Africa, though this remains unproven.

Earlier behaviour is contested. A 73,000-year-old crosshatched pattern on a silcrete flake from Blombos Cave in South Africa is the oldest known drawing by *Homo sapiens* and is widely accepted as symbolic, though a few scholars such as Richard Klein still treat the Blombos engravings with caution. A bone carving dated to about 51,000 years ago and cave paintings in Spain dated to 64,800 years ago have been attributed to Neanderthals; if accepted, they push symbolic art back before *Homo sapiens* dominated Europe. Even older finds, such as engraved shells associated with *Homo erectus* roughly 500,000 years ago, are known, but their status as deliberate art rather than incidental marks is debated.

## The Paleolithic toolkit

Paleolithic art divides into two practical categories. Cave painting and rock art are fixed to a place, surviving at sites such as Chauvet and Lascaux in France. Portable art covers small movable objects: Venus figurines (small female statuettes with emphasised hips, breasts, and abdomen, found across Paleolithic Eurasia), animal carvings, and decorated tools.

## After the ice: the Neolithic

The Neolithic brought agriculture, pottery, settled villages, and large-scale construction. The most conspicuous new art form is the megalith, a massive stone used in monuments such as the menhirs, dolmens, and passage tombs of Europe. Göbekli Tepe in Turkey, dating to the 10th–8th millennium BCE, contains the world's oldest known megaliths with carved reliefs. The 5th-millennium BCE Megalithic Temples of Malta are the oldest surviving free-standing stone buildings. Newgrange in Ireland, built around 3200 BCE, is a passage mound whose stones carry elaborate spiral carvings. Their purposes are not directly readable from the art itself; ritual and religious functions are inferred from scale, orientation, and labour cost.

The earliest petroglyphs, images made by incising, picking, or abrading a rock surface, date to the boundary between the Upper Paleolithic and the Mesolithic, roughly 12,000–10,000 years ago, and the practice spread through Africa, Asia, and the Americas in this period.

## Metals, regional styles, and the end of prehistory

Metalworking in the Bronze Age, beginning in Europe around the 3rd millennium BCE, introduced new media and specialised artisans producing decorated weapons, gold ornaments, and ceremonial objects. The European Iron Age is defined stylistically by the geometric Hallstatt phase and the more curvilinear La Tène phase, whose flowing vegetal forms influenced later Celtic art. In the Mediterranean and Asia, the rise of literate civilisations closes the prehistoric period for those regions.

## Africa, Oceania, and the Americas

In Africa, rock art extends from the Blombos drawing to a deep record in the Sahara, Zimbabwe, South Africa, and Namibia, with some Saharan paintings reaching back about 8,000 years. The Saharan sequence is conventionally divided into climatic phases such as the Bubalus Period (depicting now-vanished megafauna, indicating a wetter Sahara) and the later Camel Period (depicting domesticated camels, indicating aridity). In Oceania, Aboriginal Australian art, including the Gwion Gwion rock-painting style, is among the oldest continuous artistic traditions on Earth.

In the Americas, the Vero Beach bone dates to about 11,000 BCE. Mesoamerica produced Olmec monumental sculpture and sophisticated ceramics around 1000 BCE, while the Andes saw a long succession: Huaca Prieta textiles (c. 2500 BCE), then Chavín, Nazca, and Moche. The Inca, expanding from 1438 CE, absorbed and standardised artistic traditions across the Andes, particularly fine stonework.

## The standing debates

Three questions remain open. First, when does art begin: the engraved *Homo erectus* shells, the Blombos crosshatching, and Middle Paleolithic finds such as the Tan-Tan figurine are all claimed as earliest art, and the disagreement is about what counts as deliberate symbolic expression rather than incidental marking. Second, did Neanderthals make art: the Spanish cave paintings and the 51,000-year-old bone carving, if correctly attributed, imply symbolic cognition was not exclusive to *Homo sapiens*. Third, why was any of it made: whether geometric marks, figurines, and megaliths served ritual, communication, territorial signalling, or aesthetic play cannot be settled from the objects alone, since the makers left no written explanations. The art itself is unambiguous; its meaning is not.
