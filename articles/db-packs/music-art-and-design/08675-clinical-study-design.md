# Clinical study design

Clinical study design is the way researchers plan investigations in human medicine, covering clinical trials, other experiments, and observational studies in epidemiology. The goal of a clinical study is to assess the safety, efficacy, or mechanism of action of an investigational medicinal product, procedure, device, or drug. The treatment may be unapproved by a health authority such as the Food and Drug Administration, or already approved and in need of further study, typically of long-term effects or cost-effectiveness. Although the field shares principles with the general design of experiments, it adds two concerns uncommon elsewhere: patient confidentiality and medical ethics.

## The two main branches

Clinical studies split into two families. Treatment studies deliberately intervene to test an effect, while observational studies record what happens without intervening.

Treatment studies include the randomised controlled trial, in which participants are randomly assigned to intervention or control arms. A blind trial hides the assignment from patients, clinicians, or both; a non-blind trial does not. An adaptive clinical trial allows planned modifications to the trial as data accumulate. A platform trial evaluates several treatments against a common control. A nonrandomised trial, also called a quasi-experiment, lacks true randomisation. An interrupted time series design takes repeated measurements from the same population before and after a manipulated or naturally occurring event and is treated as a type of quasi-experiment. A single-arm study gives every participant the same intervention with no internal control.

Observational studies divide into descriptive and analytical. Descriptive designs include the case report (a single patient), the case series (a small group of similar patients), and the population study. Analytical designs ask comparative questions. A cohort study follows exposed and unexposed groups over time and can be prospective (moving forward from exposure) or retrospective (using existing records to look backward). A case-control study starts with people who already have the condition and matches them to similar people who do not, then looks back at exposures; a nested case-control study embeds this design inside an existing cohort. A cross-sectional study, including the community survey form, measures everyone at a single point in time. A time series study tracks a population repeatedly to spot patterns. An ecological study compares groups rather than individuals.

The split between randomised and nonrandomised work is the most consequential distinction. Randomisation is what lets a trial attribute a difference in outcomes to the treatment rather than to pre-existing differences between patients, so every design that omits it must justify that choice in its interpretation.

## Other important trial types

Three trial goals matter beyond the intervention/observation split. A superiority trial aims to show that one treatment is more effective than a reference, often a placebo or the current best option. A non-inferiority trial aims to show that a new treatment is not appreciably worse, which is useful when the new option is cheaper, safer, or more convenient than the established standard of care. An equivalence trial aims to show that two treatments are equally effective.

Two structural choices also shape interpretation. In a parallel-groups design, each patient receives one treatment. In a crossover study, each patient receives several treatments in different orders, serving as their own control. A longitudinal study measures subjects at two or more points in time, whereas a cross-sectional study measures them at only one; case-control, cohort, and randomised studies are not cross-sectional.

## Choosing a design and the biases that follow

Different designs are vulnerable to different biases. Recall bias is likely in cross-sectional or case-control studies, where subjects are asked to remember past exposures; a patient with breast cancer may remember hormone replacement therapy more readily than a healthy control, distorting the apparent link. The ecological fallacy arises when conclusions about individuals are drawn from group-level data, and such analyses tend to overestimate the degree of association between variables.

The term "retrospective study" is sometimes used as a synonym for case-control study, but this usage is misleading and is discouraged, because other designs, including retrospective cohort studies, also look backward.

Seasonal indications such as allergies, Seasonal Affective Disorder, and influenza force rapid enrolment and introduce weather and seasonal variation as confounders that the design must accommodate. Patient confidentiality and medical ethics constrain which designs are permissible in clinical research.
