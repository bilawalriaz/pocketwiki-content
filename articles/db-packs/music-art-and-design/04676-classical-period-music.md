# Classical period (music)

The Classical period in Western art music, roughly 1750 to 1820, replaced the dense, interwoven polyphony of the Baroque with a clearer aesthetic built on homophony, a texture where one melody sits above a chordal accompaniment. Listeners could now follow a single tune, supported but not obscured by harmonies underneath. This shift produced the foundational forms of later Western music, including the symphony and the string quartet, and the period's emphasis on balance, structure, and expressive clarity became the bedrock on which Romantic and most subsequent concert music was built.

## The central aesthetic

Classical composers favoured homophony, balanced phrases, and well-defined contrasts between musical keys. The style galant, a mid-18th-century taste for lightness and elegance over Baroque grandeur, fed directly into this. The most important new structural tool was sonata form, a three-part plan in which an exposition presents two contrasting themes, a development manipulates and recombines them under shifting harmonies, and a recapitulation restates the opening material in a unified home key. Sonata form became the organising principle of first movements in symphonies, string quartets, and solo sonatas, and its reconciliation of melodic argument with harmonic journey is the period's most durable technical invention.

Orchestras grew and standardised. A typical Classical ensemble paired a string section (violin, viola, cello, double bass) with pairs of woodwinds (flute, oboe, clarinet, bassoon), natural horns and trumpets, and timpani. The harpsichord's role in the basso continuo, a Baroque practice of an improvised bass line with chordal realisation, faded by about 1775. The fortepiano, an early piano whose hammers strike strings, took its place and allowed genuine dynamic gradation between loud and soft, something the plucked harpsichord could not produce. Chamber music, especially the string quartet (two violins, viola, cello), gained a prominence it has not lost.

## How the period unfolded

The transition out of the Baroque ran from about 1750 to 1775. Some composers passed through Empfindsamkeit, a "sensitive style" marked by sudden, unmotivated changes of mood, and through the related Sturm und Drang ("storm and stress") phase of the 1760s and 1770s, which favoured dramatic emotionalism. Classical masters, especially Haydn, absorbed these impulses but made their dramatic shifts logically motivated by the music's own structure.

Three figures dominate. Joseph Haydn, working from the late 1750s, has been called the "father of the symphony" and the "father of the string quartet"; his Opus 20 quartets (1772) still lean on Baroque counterpoint, while his Opus 33 set (1781) established the integrated four-movement Classical quartet model. Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart arrived in Vienna in 1781 and applied the matured style to opera and the virtuoso concerto, fusing Italian melodic brilliance with Germanic structural cohesion. By the 1780s public taste had consolidated around the Haydn–Mozart standard. Ludwig van Beethoven, emerging in the 1790s, kept Classical forms but stretched their scale and emotional range; his *Eroica* Symphony (composed 1803–04), *Fidelio* (1805), and the Fifth and Sixth Symphonies (both 1808) pushed the language toward the expressivity that defines Romanticism.

The trio of Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven is often called the First Viennese School. The label is loose: they shared a city and a stylistic foundation, knew each other, and influenced one another, but they did not operate as a collaborative group, and Vienna hosted many other important composers of the era.

## Legacy and limits

The period's chronological edges are conventional. Some scholars push the start back to 1730, others forward to 1770, and the end is usually drawn around 1820, with Beethoven's death in 1827 and Schubert's in 1828 often cited as the moment the style gives way to Romanticism. Schubert is a borderline case: he is sometimes grouped with the Viennese triumvirate and sometimes treated as the first Romantic because his harmonic language already points past Classical norms. Whether the "First Viennese School" names a true movement or simply three towering individuals who happened to work in the same city remains a small scholarly question.

What the Classical period left behind is concrete: a standard orchestra, the piano as the dominant keyboard instrument, sonata-form architecture that organised symphonic thought for the next century, and the string quartet as a privileged chamber genre. Its underlying conviction, that music's emotional weight could be carried by a singable melody supported, developed, and resolved within an audible structure, remained the working assumption of Western composition well into the twentieth century.
