# Dynamics (music)

In music, *dynamics* means how loud or soft a sound is, and how that changes from note to note. Dynamics are written on the score using Italian words and symbols, but those markings never give an exact volume. They are relative: a passage marked *piano* (soft) should simply be quieter than one marked *forte* (loud). Actual loudness depends on the instrument, the room, the musical phrase, and the performer's interpretation. Dynamics extend beyond loudness to include changes in timbre and occasionally in tempo rubato.

## How markings are interpreted

A *piano* marking on a tuba will usually sound louder than a *forte* marking on a guitar, because instruments differ in natural volume. A piccolo playing high in its register can cut through an ensemble even at a low decibel level. Within one phrase, the middle is normally played louder than the edges so the line has shape, even when the whole phrase is marked *piano*. In multi-part writing, the melody and bass are typically pushed forward, so a passage marked at one dynamic level still has internal contrasts. Because markings are relative, performers decide how much contrast to give.

## Static levels

The two basic markings are *p* (piano, "soft") and *f* (forte, "loud"). Halfway points add *mp* (mezzo piano, "moderately quiet") and *mf* (mezzo forte, "moderately loud"). On the quiet side, *pp* (pianissimo) and *ppp* (pianississimo) extend the range. On the loud side, *ff* (fortissimo) and *fff* (fortississimo) do the same. *più p* and *più f* mean "quieter" and "louder" than the current level. Special markings include *sfz* or *sf* (sforzando, "suddenly very loud" on one beat), *rfz* or *rf* (rinforzando, a sudden reinforcement within a phrase), and *n* or *ø* (niente, "nothing"), used to indicate silence as part of a larger dynamic gesture.

## Gradual and sudden changes

Three Italian words describe gradual change: *crescendo* (cresc., "growing"), *decrescendo* (decresc., "decreasing"), and *diminuendo* (dim., "diminishing"); *decrescendo* and *diminuendo* mean roughly the same thing. The same idea is shown by hairpins, a wedge opening to the right for a crescendo and closing to the right for a decrescendo, placed under the staff. Hairpins cover short spans up to a few bars, while *cresc.*, *decresc.*, and *dim.* span longer passages, sometimes extended across pages with dashes. *molto* ("much") intensifies a change; *poco* ("a little") softens it; *poco a poco* means "little by little". *morendo* ("dying") indicates a gradual reduction in both dynamics and tempo. Sudden changes use *subito* ("suddenly"), as in *sub. p* or *sub. f*.

Accented notes carry a *>* sign above or below the note to emphasise the attack. A sharper, shorter emphasis is *^* (marcato). *fz* (forzando/forzato) is a forceful accent, often strengthened as *sfz* (subito forzando). *fp* (fortepiano) means a loud note followed immediately by a soft one. *Messa di voce* is a singing ornament that swells and tapers on a single held pitch.

## Extreme markings

The standard range runs from *ppp* to *fff*, but composers have pushed further. These extremes can mean a very wide dynamic range, or small differences within an already wide one, and they appear most often in orchestral and piano works from the late 1800s onward. Orchestrally, they depend on mass: a true *ffff* is produced by stacking brass, percussion, and strings together. Holst writes *ffff* in "Mars" and "Uranus" of *The Planets*; Tchaikovsky marks a bassoon solo *pppppp* in his *Pathétique* Symphony; Verdi writes *ppppppp* in *Otello*; Mahler writes *fffff* for cellos and basses with a footnote instructing them to pluck so hard the strings hit the wood; Ligeti opens his Cello Concerto with *pppppppp* and ends *L'Escalier du Diable* with *ffffffff*; in *Le Grand Macabre* he writes *ffffffffff* with a hammer stroke.

## History

Ancient Greek writing mentions dynamic transitions, but dynamics receive little attention. Giovanni Gabrieli, in the Renaissance, was among the first to notate dynamics. Early Baroque music relied on *terraced dynamics*, alternating *piano* and *forte* sections through *raddoppio* ("doubling") and *ripieno* ("filling"), where a small group of players contrasted with a larger one, often in a 2:1 ratio. This contrast structured the concerto grosso and solo concerto. Later Baroque composers, including Vivaldi and Bach, used more varied dynamics; Bach wrote terms like *forte* and *pianissimo* in full, and Johann Joachim Quantz wrote in 1752 that "light and shade must be constantly introduced … by the incessant interchange of loud and soft." The harpsichord naturally grew louder as texture thickened, so more notes meant more sound without any change in marking.

In the Classical period Haydn and Mozart specified six levels (*pp* to *ff*). Beethoven added *ppp* and *fff*, and Brahms used many qualifiers; in his Trio for violin, horn and piano (Op. 40) he writes *ppp*, *molto piano*, and *quasi niente* to describe different qualities of quiet. By the Romantic era, *più p* and *più f* brought the standard scale to ten levels between *ppp* and *fff*. An effective Classical-era contrast appears in Smetana's overture to *The Bartered Bride*, where scurrying pianissimo second violins form a background to a fortissimo melodic statement.

## Dynamics in recording and notation software

Modern recording uses dynamic range compression, which narrows the difference between loud and soft at both micro and macro scales, so the same notation can produce a different listening experience than the composer intended. To separate the two ideas, scholars use *performed dynamics* for the loudness shaping the performer controls directly. Notation programs translate dynamics into MIDI key velocity on a 0–127 scale, and the mappings differ between programs, which is one reason the same marking can sound different depending on the software; a grand piano covers a far wider volume range than a recorder.
