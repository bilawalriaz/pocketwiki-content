# Design of experiments

Design of experiments (DOE) is the practice of planning which conditions to create, which to hold fixed, and which to measure, so the resulting data can show, with calculable confidence, how a system actually responds. It is used across the natural and social sciences, engineering, agriculture, manufacturing, medicine, marketing, and policy.

A working experiment has three kinds of variables. **Independent variables** (also called factors, inputs, or predictors) are the conditions the experimenter changes. **Dependent variables** (responses or outputs) are what gets measured and are expected to shift when the inputs do. **Control variables** are held constant so that outside factors cannot be mistaken for the effect of interest. The core statistical task is to separate signal from noise, where noise is every uncontrolled source of variation that could mimic or hide a real effect.

A few design ideas do most of the work. **Randomization** assigns units to treatments by chance, so unknown nuisance factors spread roughly evenly across groups and cannot consistently bias the comparison. **Replication** repeats measurements so that random error can be estimated and reduced; replicating the whole experiment also lets independent researchers check the result. **Blocking** groups similar units together (such as plots of similar soil, or batches from the same shift) so that a known source of between-unit variation is removed before the treatment effect is estimated. **Orthogonality** means the treatment contrasts are uncorrelated, so each one carries independent information and effects can be estimated cleanly. **Multifactorial designs** vary several factors at once, exposing not only each factor's main effect but also **interactions** between factors, which a one-factor-at-a-time approach misses. Analysis rests on **analysis of variance (ANOVA)**, which partitions the total observed variation into components attributable to each factor, to interactions, and to residual error.

Sequential and adaptive designs go further by letting each experiment's results shape the next, including the decision of when to stop collecting data. Abraham Wald pioneered **sequential analysis** in the 1940s, and the **multi-armed bandit** problem formalizes the choice between the best treatment seen so far and an uncertain alternative.

A weighing example attributed to Harold Hotelling, building on Frank Yates, shows why arrangement matters. Eight objects are weighed on a pan balance that returns a noisy measurement. The first design weighs each object alone, so the estimate of an object's weight has variance σ². The second design uses the same eight weighings but combines all eight objects in each weighing in a balanced pattern described by a Hadamard matrix, so each weight is estimated from a sum of all eight readings divided by eight. The variance of that estimate is σ²/8, eight times more precise, for the same number of weighings. This is a special case of **combinatorial design**, where the schedule of conditions is chosen from a structured set of patterns. Hadamard matrices, Plackett–Burman designs, fractional factorials, and orthogonal arrays all share this character: the choice of which runs to perform, not just how many, determines the information an experiment yields.

Validity also depends on the human side of the experiment. A true experiment randomly assigns participants to conditions, including an otherwise identical **control group** that receives no intervention, so that any average difference in outcome can be attributed to the intervention with a known probability of error. When random assignment is impossible, a **quasi-experiment** compares naturally occurring groups but can establish association, not causation. A **double-blind** design hides the assignment from both participants and the experimenter during data collection. A **triple-blind** design extends the same protection to the analyst, guarding against **p-hacking**, the practice of trying many analyses until one crosses the p < 0.05 threshold. Preregistration of the analysis plan, and complete documentation of the method, make both practices harder to carry out unnoticed.

The field's intellectual lineage runs through Charles S. Peirce, who in 1876 published the first English-language paper on optimal experimental design and in 1877–1878 argued for randomization-based inference, then carried out a blinded, repeated-measures weight-discrimination experiment. The modern methodology was systematized by Ronald Fisher in *The Arrangement of Field Experiments* (1926) and *The Design of Experiments* (1935), drawing on agricultural applications and including the "lady tasting tea" test of whether she could tell whether milk or tea was poured first. After Fisher, Raj Chandra Bose and K. Kishen (1940, Indian Statistical Institute) found efficient main-effect designs, and C. R. Rao introduced **orthogonal arrays**, which Genichi Taguchi later carried into industrial quality control during a 1950s visit to the Indian Statistical Institute. Gertrude Mary Cox and William Cochran's *Experimental Designs* (1950) became the standard reference. Modern theory rests on linear models and draws on linear algebra and combinatorics, with both frequentist and Bayesian frameworks in active use.

DOE is recognized as a core tool of **Quality by Design (QbD)** in pharmaceutical and industrial process development, where the aim is to build quality into a process by understanding how inputs drive outputs. Real-world constraints shape what can be done: ethics may forbid certain human experiments, toxicity testing on laboratory animals is used to set safe human exposure limits, and **institutional review boards** enforce informed consent and confidentiality.
