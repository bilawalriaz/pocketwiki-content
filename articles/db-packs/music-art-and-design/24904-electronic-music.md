# Electronic music

Electronic music is music made with circuitry-based instruments, audio technology, or general-purpose computers instead of (or alongside) traditional acoustic sound sources. It spans roughly130 years, from late-19th-century inventions to today's laptop productions, and now shapes most popular music.

## What counts as "electronic"

The source distinguishes two families. Pure electronic instruments generate sound entirely from electrical signals, with no acoustic waves first produced mechanically: examples are the theremin (played by waving hands near antennas), the electronic oscillator, and the synthesizer. Electromechanical instruments still create sound mechanically (strings, reeds, hammers) but use magnetic pickups, amplifiers, and loudspeakers to capture and project it: examples include the electric guitar, electric piano, Hammond organ, and the original Telharmonium. Electroacoustic composition uses microphones and tape or digital recording to capture real-world sounds and then manipulate them.

## How the technology took shape

The Telharmonium (around 1900) sent synthesized orchestra sounds over telephone lines. Vacuum-tube advances in the 1920s made instruments smaller and louder; the theremin, ondes Martenot, and trautonium were commercially available by the early 1930s. Practical tape recorders arrived by 1935, with commercial models by 1948.

Two parallel streams emerged. In Paris in 1948, Pierre Schaeffer pioneered musique concrète: editing together fragments of recorded real-world sounds. His October 1948 broadcast of *Étude aux chemins de fer* marked the genre's beginning. In Egypt in 1944, Halim El-Dabh used a wire recorder to capture a *zaar* ceremony, then processed it with reverberation and re-recording, producing *The Expression of Zaar*, believed to be the earliest tape-music composition. In Cologne from 1953, Werner Meyer-Eppler, Robert Beyer, and Herbert Eimert opened a studio for elektronische Musik: sounds synthesized entirely from oscillators and filters, the opposite philosophy to musique concrète's found sounds.

The 1950s and 1960s added algorithmic composition (rules a computer follows to generate music), pioneered by Lejaren Hiller's *Illiac Suite* (1956) and Max Mathews's MUSIC programs at Bell Labs (1957), and live electronics, where performers manipulate sound in real time through microphones, filters, and ring modulators.

The 1970s brought affordable instruments: the Mini-Moog (1970) put synthesis on stage, and Japanese companies built the drum machines and electronic organs that defined the coming decade.

## Pop music absorbs electronics

The Berlin Love Parade, starting in 1989, drew over a million people and signalled electronic music's arrival as mass culture. Three converging currents defined popular electronic music from the 1970s on.

Synth-pop in the late 1970s and 1980s (Kraftwerk, Yellow Magic Orchestra, Depeche Mode, Gary Numan) drove synthesizers to the top of the charts.

Jamaican dub, developed in 1960s Kingston by engineers like King Tubby and Lee "Scratch" Perry, used studio mixing boards as instruments, stripping reggae to bass and drums, layering echo and reverb, and feeding deejays' toasts over pre-recorded tracks. DJ Kool Herc brought these techniques to New York in the early 1970s, seeding hip-hop.

Dance music crystallized through Roland's TR-808 drum machine (1980) and TB-303 bass synthesizer (1981), which became the foundation of house and techno, and direct-drive turntables like the Technics SL-1200 (early 1970s), whose strong motor enabled scratching.

A key standardization was MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface), finalized in 1983: a protocol letting keyboards, computers, and drum machines exchange control signals.

## Where the technology lives today

Sampling (storing short recordings of real sounds and triggering them from a keyboard) became practical with the Fairlight CMI in 1979 and exploded with cheap digital memory. Digital audio workstations on laptops, software synthesizers, and live coding mean a single computer can replace rooms of hardware. Many sub-genres (EDM, electronica, indie electronic, chiptune, ambient) now coexist across clubs, festivals, and home recordings. Pop electronic music is most recognizable in its 4/4 dance form, distinct from earlier experimental forms that stayed in niche markets.

Across this history, each new tool, from tape to oscillator to sampler to laptop, has progressively substituted electrical signal for mechanical sound and recorded or generated waveform for performed gesture.
