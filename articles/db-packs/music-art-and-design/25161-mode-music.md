# Mode (music)

In Western music theory, a **mode** is a scale type paired with characteristic melodic and harmonic behaviour. It can also mean a "generalised tune" built on that scale, so the term sits between raw pitch collection and full melody. The same word has been used since antiquity for intervals, individual notes, rhythms, and transpositional systems; outside Western classical music, analogous concepts include Arabic *maqam*, Byzantine *echos*, Persian *dastgah*, Indian *raga*, and Javanese *pathet*.

## Ancient Greek origins

Greek theorists described three related ideas. A **harmonia** (pl. *harmoniai*) was originally a regional melodic style rather than a fixed scale, associated with peoples such as Dorians, Lydians, and Phrygians. Around 400 BC, theorists called the *harmonicists* reorganised these styles into seven **octave species**, each an octave-wide arrangement of intervals within two tetrachords (four-note groups). Three **genera** were recognised: diatonic (tones and semitones), chromatic (semitones plus a minor third), and enharmonic (a major third plus two quarter tones). The Greeks also used **tonoi**, transpositions of the system by semitone across an octave; Cleonides attributes thirteen tonoi to Aristoxenus, while Ptolemy's system has only seven, one per octave species. Greek scales and tonoi had no true hierarchy of tension and rest, though the *mese* ("middle note") acted as a loose centre. Plato and Aristotle wrote that different *harmoniai* shaped character: Dorian produced moderation, Phrygian excitement, Mixolydian grief, Lydian softness. This idea of musical *ethos*, that music shapes moral character, persisted across later modal theory.

## Medieval and Renaissance European systems

The **eight church modes** (or Gregorian modes) dominated European music from roughly the ninth century. They form four pairs that share a **final** note, named protus, deuterus, tritus, and tetrardus, each appearing in **authentic** form (range centred above the final) and **plagal** form (range centred below). Standard finals are D, E, F, G. Each pair has a **reciting tone** (the note around which chant melodies revolve), a fifth above the final in authentic modes and a third above in plagals. The only accidental used commonly in Gregorian chant is B♭, mainly in modes 5 and 6 and the upper tetrachord of mode 4. Authors from Guido of Arezzo to Juan de Espinosa Medrano assigned each mode an *affect* (emotional character), ranging from "happy" Lydian to "tearful" Hypolydian.

In 1547, the Swiss theorist Henricus Glareanus added four modes in his *Dodecachordon*, bringing the total to twelve: Aeolian on A and Ionian on C. Gioseffo Zarlino later renumbered by ordering finals up the hexachord (C–D–E–F–G–A), producing a long-running conflict between numbering systems. The resulting confusion means scholars today usually stick with the original Roman-numeral modes I–VIII.

## The modern diatonic modes

After the common-practice period collapsed strict major–minor tonality, composers revived the modes in a stricter form than Renaissance practice had used. Modern usage treats each mode as a rotation of the major scale, starting on a different scale degree. With **W** = whole tone and **H** = semitone, the seven diatonic modes are:

| Mode | Tonic on C major | Interval pattern | Distinguishing feature |
|------|------------------|------------------|----------------------|
| Ionian | C (I) | W–W–H–W–W–W–H | Major scale |
| Dorian | D (ii) | W–H–W–W–W–H–W | Minor with a major 6th |
| Phrygian | E (iii) | H–W–W–W–H–W–W | Minor with a minor 2nd |
| Lydian | F (IV) | W–W–W–H–W–W–H | Major with an augmented 4th |
| Mixolydian | G (V) | W–W–H–W–W–H–W | Major with a minor 7th |
| Aeolian | A (vi) | W–H–W–W–H–W–W | Natural minor scale |
| Locrian | B (vii°) | H–W–W–H–W–W–W | Diminished 5th (theoretical only) |

Reading down the column, each step to the next mode lowers one more interval against the tonic: Lydian's augmented 4th becomes a perfect 4th in Ionian, Ionian's major 7th becomes a minor 7th in Mixolydian, and so on until Locrian has a lowered 2nd, 3rd, 5th, 6th, and 7th. Because the tonic triad is diminished, Locrian is considered theoretical; its root-to-fifth interval is a tritone, enharmonically the same as Lydian's augmented 4th, so diminished triads cannot serve as cadential goals in traditional harmony.

Modes appear widely in folk traditions. Irish traditional music uses Ionian, Aeolian, Mixolydian, and Dorian extensively, with the keys G major / A Dorian / D Mixolydian / E Aeolian especially common. Flamenco draws on Phrygian, often with the 3rd and 7th raised. Composers from Debussy and Bartók to Holst, Kodály, and de Falla built whole works on modal foundations. The principle generalises: any scale whose interval pattern repeats only at the octave has exactly as many modes as it has notes, so the pentatonic scale yields five modes, the melodic-minor and harmonic-minor scales each yield seven, and equal-step scales such as the chromatic or whole-tone yield only one or two because rotation reproduces the same pattern.
