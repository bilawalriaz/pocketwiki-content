# Staff (music)

A staff is the framework Western musical notation uses to write down pitch. The standard version has five horizontal lines with four spaces between them; each line and each space names one pitch, with higher positions meaning higher notes. Noteheads sit "on a line" or "in a space," and notes outside the staff's range ride on short extra **ledger lines** added above or below.

Vertical position encodes pitch; horizontal position encodes when a note sounds. So a staff resembles a graph of pitch over time, read left to right, but with two differences: the number of semitones between adjacent lines and spaces depends on the key, and exact timing comes from the chosen note symbol plus the tempo rather than from horizontal spacing alone.

A **clef** at the left fixes one staff line to a specific reference pitch, and every other line and space follows by one diatonic step. The treble clef, also called the G clef, wraps its curl around the second line and names that line the G above middle C. The bass clef, by contrast, fixes the F below middle C to its second line. Once a clef is in place, the key signature and any accidentals raise or lower individual notes.

A **time signature** to the right of the clef sets how beats map onto note values, while vertical **bar lines** divide the staff into measures. Percussion staves are a special case with one to five (or more) lines, each naming an instrument rather than a pitch; guitar tablature uses up to six lines, each naming a string.

When two or more staves must be read together, a vertical line on the left joins them into a **system**, meaning play simultaneously. A **brace** is the heavy curl used for one instrument, such as a piano, organ, harp, or marimba; a lighter square **bracket** groups instruments that act as a unit, like an orchestral string section, and a second bracket can show paired parts such as first and second violins. SATB hymn settings compress four voices onto two staves, with soprano and alto sharing the top staff and tenor and bass sharing the bottom, a technique called divisi.

A **grand staff** is two staves joined by a brace for a single performer, usually a keyboard instrument or harp. The upper staff uses the treble clef and the lower uses the bass clef, so middle C sits exactly between them and can be written on the first ledger line below the upper staff or the first ledger line above the lower one. On piano and harp the upper staff is played with the right hand and the lower with the left; organ music with pedals adds a third staff for the feet.

The staff evolved over centuries. Early medieval chant used neumes, small marks above words that showed only whether a melody rose or fell and served as memory aids for singers who already knew the tune. During the 9th to 11th centuries diastematic neumes appeared, whose vertical height on the page corresponded to actual pitch, and Beneventan and Longobardian manuscripts from around 1000 show this. Guido d'Arezzo (c. 990–1050) is credited with the staff as it is now used; his four-line version is still the standard in Gregorian chant publications. Four-, five-, and six-line staves coexisted into the 1600s, but the five-line staff promoted by Ugolino da Forlì in 13th-century Italy became universal, which is why modern Western music reads so consistently across instruments and centuries.
