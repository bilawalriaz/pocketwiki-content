# Classical music

Classical music is the written tradition of Western art music, distinct from folk and popular music by its formality, complex form, and use of harmony, especially polyphony, in which multiple independent melodic lines sound at once. Because it has been primarily a written tradition since at least the ninth century, it developed notation that lets composers fix works in detail and let others reproduce them long after creation.

## Origins and the name

The Western classical tradition formally begins with music made for the early Christian Church, which sought to separate itself from pagan Greco-Roman practice. Surviving early medieval music is therefore chiefly religious, monophonic (a single unaccompanied melody line), and vocal. The earliest manuscripts date from the Carolingian Empire (800–887), and Western plainchant gradually unified into Gregorian chant. By the mid-12th century, France was the major European center: the Notre-Dame school first fully explored organized rhythm and polyphony, while secular music flourished among the troubadours and trouvères. This led to the French ars nova and Italian Trecento, then the highly complex ars subtilior.

The word "classical" comes from Latin classicus, the highest class of Roman citizen. In the Renaissance it meant "formal, orderly, of approved excellence." The term was first applied to a particular canon of performed music in 18th-century England, when London's public concert scene grew and organizations such as the Academy of Ancient Music promoted revered 16th- and 17th-century works. In the early 19th century, Mozart, Haydn, and Beethoven were grouped as the "First Viennese School," giving the term a narrower stylistic sense that overlaps with, but is not identical to, the broader meaning.

## Major eras

Western classical music divides into eras, each with characteristic styles, forms, and instruments:

| Era | Dates | Defining traits |
|---|---|---|
| Medieval | c. 500–1400 | Monophonic chant, then early polyphony; staff notation invented |
| Renaissance | c. 1400–1600 | Elaborate polyphony, madrigal and mass, music printing, first operas c. 1597 |
| Baroque | c. 1600–1750 | Major-minor tonality, basso continuo, opera, oratorio, fugue, growing orchestra |
| Classical | c. 1750–1820 | Balanced forms, symphony, string quartet, fortepiano, public concerts |
| Romantic | c. 1800–1910 | Programmatic music, virtuosity, expanded orchestra, nationalism |
| Modernism | from c. 1890 | Rejection of tonality for serialism, impressionism, or folk sources |
| Contemporary | from c. 1945 | Minimalism, Spectral, New Simplicity, New Complexity, Postmodern, global participation |

The Renaissance ran from roughly 1400 to 1600, with Franco-Flemish composers such as Josquin des Prez and Palestrina refining polyphonic vocal music, the invention of music printing, and the first operas around 1597 (Peri's *Dafne* and *Euridice*).

The Baroque standardized major-minor tonality and complex counterpoint, with Italy as the birthplace of opera, the soloist-centered concerto, the sonata form, and large vocal genres. Bach's *Well-Tempered Clavier* exemplified contrapuntal complexity.

The Classical era was the shorter, pivotal age of Mozart, Haydn, and early Beethoven, who set norms of balanced form, clear melody, and the fortepiano (an early version of the piano) as the leading keyboard instrument. The symphony, string quartet, and concerto were the representative vehicles.

Romantic music shifted toward programmatic music, where the art song, symphonic poem, and piano genres became central, and virtuosity, immensity, philosophy, and nationalism were celebrated. Orchestras grew from around 40 players in the Classical era to over 100 by the early 20th century. Wagner's operas are the era's most ambitious synthesis.

After about 1890, the unity of the common-practice style dissolved. Modernist composers like Debussy, Stravinsky, Schoenberg, Bartók, and Ravel abandoned tonality for serialism, folk sources, or rhythmic innovation. After World War II, for the first time audiences valued older music more than contemporary works, a preference reinforced by the spread of commercial recordings. Late-20th- and early-21st-century trends include Minimalism, Spectral music, Postmodern music, and Postminimalism, with growing global participation by composers from the Americas, Africa, and Asia.

## Performance and training

Classical performance requires sight-reading, ensemble skill, ear training, and knowledge of period style. A defining feature is the written score, which fixes rhythm, pitch, and coordination between parts and preserves music across centuries. From the Baroque through the Romantic era, performers were expected to improvise ornaments, figured-bass harmonies, and concerto cadenzas; this tradition has largely faded since the 20th century.

## Relationship to other traditions

Classical music has long borrowed from popular and folk sources, including jazz (Ravel, Gershwin's *Rhapsody in Blue* in 1924), student songs (Brahms), and the nationalist folk idioms of Dvořák, Smetana, Bartók, and Khachaturian. In the opposite direction, heavy-metal lead guitarists such as Ritchie Blackmore and Randy Rhoads have modeled their styles on Baroque and Classical-era technique, and works like Pachelbel's Canon have become standard pop-cultural material through advertising, film, and television. The 1990s "Mozart effect" claim, that listening to Mozart temporarily raises IQ by 8 to 9 points, was overstated by promoters; one of the original researchers argued the money was better spent on actual music education.
