# Sculpture

Sculpture is the three-dimensional branch of visual arts, creating form in height, width, and depth. It belongs to the plastic arts and has two historical roots: carving, which removes material from a block, and modeling, which shapes malleable material such as clay. Modernism expanded materials and processes beyond these roots, incorporating plastics, found objects, light, and motion.

Surviving ancient sculpture is dominated by durable media such as stone, metal, and ceramics. Perishable materials like wood rarely endure, and most ancient works were originally painted. The paint has usually been lost, so the bare stone or bronze that survives gives a false impression of how these objects looked. Sculpture has long served religious, political, and commemorative functions. Large works have typically required institutional funding, making monumental sculpture a marker of cultural sophistication and organized resources.

## Forms and Scale

Sculpture divides into **sculpture in the round**, which is free-standing and viewable from all sides, and **relief**, which remains attached to a background. Relief is classified by how far the figures project: low relief (bas-relief), high relief, and mid-relief. **Sunk relief**, where the design is cut into a surface rather than raised from it, is an Egyptian specialty. **Monumental sculpture** refers to large works, often attached to buildings or placed in public space.

Small forms include **figurines** (typically under 18 inches), plaquettes, coins, and hardstone carvings. Modern categories include **kinetic sculpture**, which incorporates movement, **land art** and **site-specific art**, which depend on their location, and **soft sculpture**, made from fabric, rubber, and similar materials.

## Materials and Techniques

Traditional materials are stone, metal (especially bronze through casting), wood, ceramics, and ivory. The core techniques are **carving** (subtractive) and **modeling** (additive). **Casting** uses molds, with lost-wax casting as the main method for bronze. Welding and forging shape and join metal. Polychromy, the application of paint, was common in antiquity and is now often reconstructed by conservation science. Modern sculptors also work in plastics, found objects, ice, sand, and light.

## Functions and Subjects

Religious devotion is a primary function, producing cult images, Buddhist figures, Hindu lingams, and Christian icons. Monumental sculpture signals power, so pharaohs, emperors, and rulers are frequent subjects. Portrait sculpture began in Egypt and Mesopotamia, flourished in Rome, and continued through the Renaissance. Totem poles and hill figures express cultural identity. Animals and mythical creatures are common subjects. Commemorative portraiture honors elites, while small figurines serve personal or decorative roles.

## Historical Development

The oldest known sculptures, the Löwenmensch and the Venus of Hohle Fels, date to roughly 35,000 to 40,000 years ago. Around 9,000 BCE, the Urfa Man became the earliest large stone figure. Egyptian sculpture emphasized idealization and used sunk relief extensively. Greek sculpture moved from Cycladic abstraction to Classical naturalism in the work of Phidias and Myron, and then to Hellenistic emotional intensity in works like the Laocoön and the Dying Gaul, after 323 BCE. After Rome conquered Greece in 146 BCE, Greek sculptors were brought to Rome, and Roman sculpture absorbed Greek styles while emphasizing portraiture and narrative relief.

Medieval sculpture was largely religious and reached a high point in Gothic cathedrals. The Renaissance revived classical forms, beginning with Ghiberti's 1403 Baptistry doors competition and continuing through Donatello and Michelangelo. Mannerism and Baroque introduced dynamism, peaking in the work of Bernini in the 1600s. Neoclassicism returned to idealism in sculptors like Canova. Modernism broke from representational conventions through Picasso's assemblages, Brâncuși's abstraction, and Calder's kinetics. Marcel Duchamp's 1917 *Fountain* introduced the readymade, a found object presented as art. Picasso's 1967 Chicago Picasso marked a major public modernist commission. Contemporary art embraces conceptual, environmental, and multimedia approaches, with the impact of African and Oceanic sculpture on European Modernism now widely acknowledged.

## Sculptors and Iconoclasm

Historically, sculptors were artisans, often anonymous. Phidias gained fame in Greece but remained a craftsman by social classification. Renaissance masters such as Michelangelo and Donatello achieved celebrity. Women were largely excluded from the field until the 20th century.

Aniconism in Judaism and Islam rejected figurative religious imagery. Christianity initially opposed religious images but later embraced them. Protestant iconoclasm destroyed many sculptures, and the Taliban's 2001 destruction of the Bamiyan Buddhas is a modern example. Because preservation favors stone and bronze, the surviving record skews our understanding of ancient practice, especially the original appearance of painted works and the lost traditions of prehistoric wood sculpture.
