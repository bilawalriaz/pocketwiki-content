# Music sequencer

A music sequencer is a device or piece of application software that records, edits, or plays back music by handling note and performance information. It does not store sound directly; it stores instructions about which notes play, when, for how long, and at what loudness or pitch, then sends those instructions to a synthesizer, sampler, or audio engine that produces the actual sound. Most modern sequencers also handle audio and automation data, which is why today's digital audio workstations (DAWs) are sequencers extended with multitrack recording.

## Data formats

Sequencers communicate using several standards. CV/Gate (control voltage plus a gate signal) is the analog format used by modular synthesizers. MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface), introduced in 1983, is the digital industry standard for note, controller, and timing data. Open Sound Control (OSC) is a newer network-based alternative. Audio sequencers additionally handle recorded sound, and DAWs add automation lanes for parameters such as volume or filter cutoff.

## Step and realtime modes

Sequencers split into two recording philosophies.

A step sequencer divides time into equal steps, usually sixteenth notes, and lets the user enter a note or rest at each step without precise timing. Drum machines, bass machines, and grooveboxes are built around this grid, making it ideal for rigid rhythmic patterns.

A realtime sequencer captures notes as they are played, like a tape recorder, then plays them back at a chosen tempo with quantization and pitch editing. It mirrors multitrack tape workflow and dominates software sequencers and DAWs, where punch in/out editing originated.

## Software sequencers and DAWs

A software sequencer is application software running on a general-purpose computer, usually controlled through a graphical interface such as a piano roll (a grid where horizontal position is time and vertical position is pitch), often paired with a physical MIDI controller keyboard.

A DAW adds multitrack audio recording and editing, virtual instruments implemented as plug-ins, and mixing automation. Loop-based software added time-stretching, allowing audio samples to be played like MIDI notes at chosen pitches and durations. Trackers, a class of software from 1987, sequence samples using a vertical text pattern and remain common for chiptune and demoscene music.

## Hardware and analog sequencers

Standalone hardware sequencers have largely given way to software. Workstation keyboards still ship with built-in sequencers, and drum machines and grooveboxes retain step sequencers.

Analog sequencers use analog electronics: a row of knobs or sliders sets the pitch for each step, and an internal clock advances through them. Because the knobs can be turned during performance, the sequence mutates live, producing the shifting repeated phrases associated with minimalist electronic styles.

## History

Mechanical sequencers predate electronics by centuries. The Banū Mūsā brothers described a hydropowered organ with exchangeable pinned cylinders and a steam-powered automatic flute player in their 9th-century Book of Ingenious Devices, the first programmable sequencer. In 1206, Al-Jazari built a programmable robot band whose peg-and-cam drummers were the first programmable drum machine. Barrel organs, music boxes, and player pianos using piano rolls spread from the 18th century onward, and punched paper rolls still controlled the RCA Mark II Sound Synthesizer in 1957.

During the 1940s–1960s, Raymond Scott built electro-mechanical sequencers including the relay-based Wall of Sound with 16 oscillators. Analog sequencer modules for modular synthesizers followed on the Buchla 100 and Moog Modular.

The first commercial digital sequencer products appeared in the early 1970s with EMS's Synthi Sequencer. Roland's MC-8 MicroComposer (1977) was an early polyphonic microprocessor-based CV/Gate sequencer with up to 5,200 notes in memory and eight-channel polyphony, enabling polyrhythmic sequences that shaped 1970s and 1980s electronic music.

In 1981, Ikutaro Kakehashi and Dave Smith began designing a universal digital interface; MIDI was unveiled in 1983. The first MIDI sequencer, the Roland MSQ-700, appeared that year, and personal computers began hosting sequencers. From the 1990s onward, DAWs integrated MIDI sequencing with multitrack audio, and loop-based workflows converged with traditional sequencing.
