# Chinese art

Chinese art is the visual art produced in or by Chinese artists across roughly eight thousand years. Its defining trait is unusual continuity: the same materials, motifs, and techniques are recognised, debated, and reworked across dynastic changes, foreign invasions, and political revolutions. The story is less about invention and more about long-term refinement of a shared vocabulary that includes painting, calligraphy, sculpture, ceramics, jade, lacquer, and architecture.

The earliest objects are pottery, especially painted wares from the Yangshao culture of the sixth millennium BC, whose geometric designs appear to have held spiritual meaning. Centuries later, the Liangzhu culture (c. 3300–2300 BC) developed a sophisticated jade tradition, producing ritual objects such as cylindrical cong tubes and flat bi discs. With the Bronze Age, beginning around 1500 BC under the Shang dynasty, the focus shifted to large ritual vessels cast with intricate zoomorphic designs, the best known being the symmetrical taotie mask, whose origin and meaning remain unclear. Over time these bronzes moved from religious objects toward aesthetic ones. The most famous early monumental work is the Terracotta Army, assembled for Qin Shi Huang's mausoleum between 210 and 209 BC, an extraordinary example of life-sized fired clay sculpture.

The single most important external influence was Buddhism, which reached China in the first century CE. Buddhist sculpture evolved from the symbolic, otherworldly style of the Northern dynasties into the rounded, lifelike naturalism of the Tang (581–907 CE), the high point of Chinese figurative sculpture. Roughly in the same era, the scholar-official class, known as literati, elevated two brush-and-ink practices to the highest art forms: calligraphy and ink wash painting (shui-mo). The goal is not photographic accuracy but capturing the spirit or rhythm (qi) of the subject, especially in landscape painting, called shanshui, literally "mountain-water." The Song dynasty (960–1279 CE) is regarded as the great age of Chinese landscape; the Yuan, produced under Mongol rule, saw literati painters retreat into revived ancient styles and establish the "Four Great Masters" lineage. A contrasting tradition, gong-bi, uses meticulous fine brushwork for figures and narrative subjects and was more associated with court and workshop artists.

Chinese ceramics developed in parallel. The Song is famous for restrained monochrome glazes, while the Ming dynasty (1368–1644) perfected blue-and-white porcelain. Jingdezhen has been the main porcelain production centre since at least the Yuan dynasty. Decorative arts, including jade carving, lacquerware, and cloisonné enamel, were executed with great skill throughout. Chinese architecture emphasises the horizontal axis and timber-frame construction, and its roof and bracket systems strongly influenced Japan, Korea, and Vietnam. Ming furniture, with elegant proportions and mortise-and-tenon joinery assembled without nails or glue, is especially prized.

Modern Chinese art is shaped by contact with the West and by twentieth-century politics. During the late Qing and early Republic (1840–1912), artists such as Xu Beihong and Liu Haisu introduced European academic techniques, and the Shanghai School (Haipai) combined traditional brushwork with vivid colours and commercial subjects. After 1949, Socialist Realism became the official style, and during the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976) traditional arts were actively suppressed. Since the 1980s, traditional forms have been rehabilitated, and contemporary artists such as Ai Weiwei and Zhang Xiaogang have gained international attention by using China's visual heritage to comment on rapid social change, raising debate over how much of this work reflects internal artistic concerns versus the international market.
