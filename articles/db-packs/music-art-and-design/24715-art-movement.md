# Art movement

An art movement is a tendency or style in art guided by a specific philosophy or goal, followed by a group of artists during a defined period, usually months, years, or decades. Because most names end in -ism (cubism, futurism), they are informally called "isms." In music the parallel is genres and styles. Art movements matter most in modern art, where each new movement was treated as a fresh avant-garde, a deliberate break with what came before.

## Why movements arose

From the Renaissance to the mid-19th century, Western art rested on perspective and the effort to reproduce visible reality, an approach called figurative art. By the late 1800s many artists felt this logic could not contain the changes in technology, science, and philosophy, so they sought a new style. The result was abstract art and, with it, the modern era in which movements are defined by shared philosophy and often by an art manifesto.

## The modern period and what came after

Modernism is the era in which art movements become the organising principle of art history. Modern art is usually said to have changed character around the middle of the 20th century, after which work is generally called contemporary art. Postmodernism runs as a parallel to late modernism rather than a clean break. Theorists disagree on whether postmodernism is a real era or a passing phase, and some place its end in the 21st century. A second strand of thought holds that movements have become less discernible since postmodernism, since artists mix past styles rather than declare a new one. Even so, critics, dealers, and collectors continue to welcome new philosophies as they appear.

## How a movement is recognised

A movement typically shows a common visual style linking its artists. The name and ideas may be set out by the artists themselves, often in a manifesto, or clarified later by critics and historians. The dates attached to movements are conventional: a heyday may fall within a few years while the influence stretches longer.

## A compressed chronology

Major movements cluster by phase, though artists worked across boundaries and styles bled into one another:

- 19th century: Neoclassicism, Romanticism, Realism, Impressionism, Post-Impressionism.
- 1900–1921: Fauvism, Expressionism, Cubism (Analytic then Synthetic), Futurism, Suprematism, De Stijl, Dada, Constructivism.
- 1920–1945: Art Deco, Bauhaus, Surrealism, Social Realism, International Style.
- 1940–1965: Abstract Expressionism, Action painting, Color Field, Art Informel, CoBrA.
- 1965–2000: Conceptual art, Minimalism, Pop art, Land art, Performance art, Installation art, Postmodernism, Neo-expressionism.
- 21st century: Digital art, Internet art, Postinternet, Metamodernism, New media art.

## Scope beyond the Western canon

Art movements are not a European invention. Asia has long-standing traditions (Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Indian, Southeast Asian) organised by dynasty, region, and religion (Buddhist, Hindu, Islamic, Taoist). Africa, the Americas, and Oceania have their own named traditions (Egyptian, Igbo, Yoruba, Benin, Maya, Inuit, Hawaiian, Australian Aboriginal) and are also classified by technique (painting, sculpture, architecture, photography, digital art) and by type (abstract, figurative, narrative, naturalist). The term covers a Renaissance school, a 19th-century national romantic style, a manifesto-driven 20th-century avant-garde, and a 21st-century digital collective, all sharing a recognisable tendency tied to a time, a place, or a shared idea.
