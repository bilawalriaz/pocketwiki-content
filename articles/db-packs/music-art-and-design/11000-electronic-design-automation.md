# Electronic design automation

Electronic design automation (EDA), also called electronic computer-aided design (ECAD), is a category of software tools for designing electronic systems such as integrated circuits (ICs) and printed circuit boards. The tools work together in a *design flow* that chip designers use to design and analyse entire semiconductor chips. Because a modern chip can contain billions of components, EDA tools are essential: no team could lay out, check, and manufacture such a design by hand.

The tools fit into four stages. *Design* converts a high-level behavioural description (often C/C++) into a register-transfer-level (RTL) specification, then into a gate-level *netlist* (a list of connected logic gates), and finally into a physical layout. *Simulation* checks behaviour at three granularities: behavioural (cycle-accurate architecture), logic (Boolean 0/1), and transistor (device-physics). *Analysis and verification* confirm that the design is correct and will run fast enough. This includes functional verification, formal verification (mathematically proving properties such as the absence of deadlock), equivalence checking between RTL and the synthesised netlist, static timing analysis (worst-case timing across all inputs), layout extraction (estimating wire parasitics), electromagnetic field solving (Maxwell's equations, slower but more accurate than extraction), physical verification (the layout is manufacturable), and yield analysis. *Manufacturing preparation* turns the finished layout into photomasks through mask data preparation, chip finishing (seal rings, filler cells), resolution enhancement techniques such as optical proximity correction or inverse lithography, and automatic test pattern generation. Analog circuits are still designed largely by hand, because matching, parasitics, and non-ideal device behaviour require judgement that automated tools handle poorly.

EDA output must satisfy a *foundry's* (semiconductor fabrication facility's) rules. Foundries supply *process design kits*: cell libraries with simulation models that plug into standard tools. The same RTL can therefore be retargeted to different fabrication processes without rewriting it.

EDA began in the 1950s at IBM, which documented its 700-series computers and ran the Automated Logic Diagram (ALD) system on IBM 704/705 mainframes. Engineers drafted logic schematics by hand, transcribed them onto templates, and punched them into cards. General Motors' DAC-1, built with IBM, showed that interactive, screen-based editing was practical for complex engineering data, an idea adopted by IC layout tools. Before EDA, integrated circuits were laid out by hand, though some shops used geometric software to drive Gerber photoplotters, most famously Calma, whose GDSII stream file format (1978) is still the de facto mask exchange standard. In the early 1970s placement and routing tools automated the first physical-design steps. The 1980 publication of *Introduction to VLSI Systems* by Carver Mead and Lynn Conway, still the standard textbook for chip design, popularised the idea of writing behaviour in a textual language and letting tools derive the physical design, an approach that remains the basis of digital IC design. Academic projects such as the Berkeley VLSI Tools Tarball (including the Espresso logic minimiser and the Magic layout editor) and the MOSIS consortium gave students cheap access to real silicon fabrication.

EDA became a commercial industry in 1981, when developers spun out of Hewlett-Packard, Tektronix, and Intel. Daisy Systems, Mentor Graphics, and Valid Logic Systems (collectively DMV) were the first wave. The U.S. Department of Defense began funding VHDL that year; Verilog followed in 1986 via Gateway Design Automation. Within a few years logic-synthesis back ends could compile HDL directly into gates. Consolidation by acquisition has continued since, driven by the trend toward putting entire electronic systems on a single chip: Siemens acquired Mentor Graphics in 2017 (renamed Siemens EDA in 2021), Synopsys acquired Magma and SpringSoft in 2012, and Synopsys announced an acquisition of Ansys in 2024. The leading vendors as of mid-2025 are Siemens, Cadence, Synopsys, Ansys, Zuken, and Altium.

Two newer trends are reshaping the field. Machine-learning optimisation engines are embedded at every stage from high-level synthesis to sign-off, improving power, performance, and area while shortening turnaround. DARPA's IDEA programme funded the OpenROAD project, which released a no-human-in-the-loop RTL-to-GDS flow that has successfully taped out (completed the final design step before fabrication) real designs.
