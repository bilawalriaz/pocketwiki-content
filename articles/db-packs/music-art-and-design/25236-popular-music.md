# Popular music

Popular music is music with broad appeal, distributed to large audiences through the music industry, especially recordings and broadcast. Unlike art music, the classical and similar traditions disseminated through written scores and concert performance, popular songs are designed to be learned by ear, easily sung, and performed by listeners with little or no training. The term originally described the 1880s Tin Pan Alley sheet-music trade in New York. "Popular music" and "pop music" are not synonyms: popular music is the wide category, while pop music is one specific genre inside it.

## Defining the category

Scholars define popular music by overlapping traits: mass appeal, accessibility without sheet music, and treatment as a commodity sold by a capitalist industry. Each definition has weaknesses. Sales figures miss repeated listens of the same song. Audience size and social-class labels map poorly onto styles. And understandings shift over time, because popular music reflects the values of the era in which it circulates.

Anahid Kassabian sorted popular music into four political framings: *populist* (jazz, Latin music, R&B as liberation), *folk* (country, reggae, gospel as music of the people), *counterculture* (punk, heavy metal, hip-hop as resistance), and *mass* (music used as a tool of oppression). Robert Christgau coined "semipopular music" in 1970 for records with populist appeal that nonetheless failed commercially. Pete Seeger offered a craft definition: "professional music which draws upon both folk music and fine arts music."

## How songs are built

Form in popular music is sectional, built from repeating blocks. The verse carries changing lyrics over a stable melody. The chorus, or refrain, repeats the same melody and a hook lyric, the part listeners remember. A bridge provides contrast and transition, usually appearing once. Many songs also use an introduction and a coda (outro), and rock- or blues-influenced pop often includes an improvised solo over the chord progression (the repeating pattern of chords backing the song).

The most common large-scale structures are *verse–chorus* (ABA), *twelve-bar blues*, and *thirty-two-bar form* (AABA, four eight-bar sections). These can combine; compound AABA forms, for instance, layer verse–chorus inside the thirty-two-bar frame.

## Industry and technology

Popular music has always been shaped by how it is sold and played. In the 19th century the key medium was inexpensive printed sheet music, distributed to a growing middle class who played songs at home on the piano. Tin Pan Alley publishers promoted songs aggressively, and player pianos let one pianist's performance be replayed on many instruments. By 1920 there were roughly 80 record companies in Britain and 200 in the US. Radio, which began broadcasting music in the early 1920s, brought professional performances to listeners who could never afford concert tickets. Sound film, television, and 1960s multitrack recording, which let engineers stack vocals and effects impossible to capture live, each reshaped what a song could sound like and how it reached people.

Through the 1970s the recording industry consolidated into five transnational majors (WEA, RCA, CBS, EMI, PolyGram). The 1980s brought digital mixing desks, synthesizers, and samplers. The 1990s put inexpensive recording software on a laptop, letting amateurs produce albums at home, though studio audio quality still outpaces home setups. From the 1960s through the mid-2000s, the album era, the LP and CD album was the dominant unit of consumption. Digital files and streaming then dissolved the album into individual songs, and in the 2010s consolidation extended across media, with record companies merged into film, television, and magazine conglomerates for cross-promotion.

## Documented changes since the 1960s

Several measurable shifts separate mid-century popular music from the streaming era. Average tempo has slowed from roughly 116 BPM (beats per minute) in the 1960s to about 100 BPM in the 2000s. Songs on 1960s radio averaged about three minutes, constrained by the physical length of a vinyl single; most Billboard Top 5 hits in 2018 ran 3:21 to 3:40. After the rise of streaming, which pays artists per play, average length fell again, to about 3:30 in 2018, twenty seconds shorter than in 2014. Major keys have declined: 85% of 1960s songs were in a major key, against roughly 40% today. Lyrics have grown sadder, more antisocial, and more self-focused, and mismatches between musical mood and lyrical mood (fast songs with sad content, slow songs in major keys) have become more common. Possible drivers include the use of popular music to promote social and political agendas, and the diversification of global audiences.

## Global circulation

Western popular music reaches distant rural areas through urban media, and those areas feed back into the center, producing hybrid styles. Urbanization, modernization, and exposure to foreign media drive this exchange, and multinational studios spread Western styles internationally. Music originating outside the West is often lumped together as "world music," a label critics argue flattens real differences.

In Africa, popular styles have grown from traditional entertainment rather than ceremonial music, though they keep strong ties to local roots. Political and protest songs have been central, from colonial-era marches adapted from Western hymn forms to anti-apartheid songs built on tribal styles. Hip-hop, remade from the North American template by artists who compare themselves to the griot, a West African oral storyteller and social commentator, now addresses racism, education, and civic empowerment, and has become one of the continent's dominant youth genres. In the United States, R&B and hip-hop displaced pop at the top of the charts after 2017, building on DJ Kool Herc's 1970s technique of crossfading two identical records. In Indonesia, dangdut fuses indopop and underground rock, and local punk scenes reinterpret global bands like Green Day as a movement rather than a decline. In China, students surveyed in 2015 reached Anglo-American pop through post-1970s opening, regional television, and streaming. Across the Arab world and Iran, local musicians absorb Western instrumentation while defending indigenous identity. Underground metal and rock scenes in Iran, composed largely of young, urban, educated listeners, articulate rebellion against social restrictions, with interviewees from 1990 to 2004 consistently preferring Western pop even where it was banned.
