# Street photography

Street photography is photography made for art or inquiry that captures unmediated chance encounters in public places, usually aiming for a decisive, fleeting moment through framing and timing. It overlaps with candid photography but is defined less by subject than by stance: reactive, curious, and disinterested, producing an unmanipulated mirror of public life rather than a premeditated argument. A street need not literally appear; the work can be rural or even object-based, provided the image projects a human character.

## Origins and enabling technology

Depictions of everyday public life appear across Western art from the Northern Renaissance through Realism and Impressionism. Photography joined this tradition once its tools allowed. In 1838 or 1839, Louis Daguerre made the first photograph of a figure in the street, a daguerreotype of the Boulevard du Temple in Paris taken from his studio window. The long exposure required a man having his boots brushed to stay still, so only his boots and legs registered while the moving crowd vanished. Charles Nègre first froze people in motion on Parisian streets in 1851. John Thomson, working with the journalist Adolphe Smith, made everyday street life a serious subject through *Street Life in London*, issued in twelve monthly installments from February 1877. Eugène Atget helped canonise the Parisian street from the 1890s to the 1920s, with Berenice Abbott later popularising his work in New York.

The genre's modern form depended on hardware. First sold in 1925, the Leica was the first commercially successful 35 mm camera: compact, with a bright viewfinder and quality changeable lenses from 1930, so a photographer could move through a crowd and fire at a fleeting instant. Without that portability, candid street photography as it is now understood could not exist.

## Mid-twentieth-century practice

In the United Kingdom, Paul Martin pioneered candid unposed photographs of Londoners and seaside visitors in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, the first recorded London photographer to do so with a disguised camera. The 1937 Mass-Observation project placed Humphrey Spender photographing the industrial town of Bolton, anonymised as "Yorktown", for a social survey of everyday British life.

In post-war France, the Humanist School, including Brassaï, Cartier-Bresson, Doisneau, Izis, and Ronis, worked on streets and in bistros in black and white and available light, finding what Pierre Mac Orlan called the *fantastique social de la rue* (the social fantastic of the street). Edward Steichen exported the genre internationally through MoMA exhibitions, culminating in *The Family of Man* (1955), which toured the world. Henri Cartier-Bresson's *Images à la Sauvette* (1952), titled in English *The Decisive Moment*, became the movement's defining text: the idea that form and content fuse into a single whole at the shutter's release.

In the United States, Walker Evans built a hidden 35 mm Contax, strapped to his chest and wired down his sleeve, to make candid subway portraits from 1938 to 1941; sensitivities about originality and subject privacy kept the work unpublished until *Many Are Called* appeared in 1966. Helen Levitt documented New York children's chalk drawings from 1938. Robert Frank's *The Americans* (1958), raw, often out of focus, and explicitly subjective, attacked the formalist orthodoxy of Cartier-Bresson and the wholesome pictorialism of *LIFE*, and has been called "perhaps the most influential photography book of the 20th century". John Szarkowski's 1967 MoMA exhibition *New Documents*, featuring Diane Arbus, Lee Friedlander, and Garry Winogrand, institutionalised this subjective turn. In the 1960s and 70s, Winogrand, Friedlander, and Joel Meyerowitz extended Frank's approach in New York. Returning from the US in 1965, Tony Ray-Jones brought this sensibility to British seaside crowds in high-contrast monochrome; Martin Parr later mined the same vein in hyper-saturated colour.

## Distinctions

The classical visual signature is a wider-angle lens, around 35 mm, in an urban setting, though the genre exceeds any formula. Documentary photography is premeditated, with a defined message and an intent to record particular events in service of journalism, education, or social change. Street photography, by contrast, is reactive and disinterested, with usually unaware subjects. A candid street photograph is unposed; a street portrait, taken after the photographer speaks to a stranger, becomes posed by virtue of that interaction, even though the encounter itself is unplanned.

## Law and ethics

Because the genre is nonconsensual, its legal status varies sharply. In the United States, photography is protected free speech under the First Amendment, and *Nussenzweig v. DiCorcia* established that taking, publishing, and selling street photographs, including street portraits, is legal without subject consent. The United Kingdom permits taking photographs in public without consent, with publication limits for news media under the Human Rights Act. The European Union balances Article 8 privacy against Article 10 expression through a proportionality test, so publication can be restricted even when taking the photo is lawful. Germany allows taking pictures without consent but restricts publication through a "right to one's own picture", with exceptions for non-focal bystanders, public figures, and art. Japan requires permission, or signalled intent without refusal, for both taking and publishing recognisable people, with image rights (*shōzōken*). South Korea's portrait rights block commercial use of a person's likeness, and Article 14 of the Act on Special Cases Concerning the Punishment of Sexual Crimes criminalises non-consensual photography of women's bodies in revealing clothing, with up to seven years' imprisonment or fines up to 50 million won.

Beyond the law, two ethical objections recur. On privacy, critics argue that assumption of risk in public cannot rest on conscious consent, and that a photograph accentuates and detaches details in a way the unaided eye does not. On autonomy, an unaware subject has no control over how, where, or whether their image appears, denying them the right to govern representations of themselves.
