# Art criticism

Art criticism is the discussion and evaluation of visual art, usually grounded in aesthetics and aimed at finding a rational basis for appreciation. Judgments depend on aesthetic theory, perception, and cultural context, so criticism is inherently subjective, and practitioners disagree on its scope. Art historian R. Siva Kumar notes that "the borders between art history and art criticism... are no more as firmly drawn as they once used to be." The main working distinction is between historical criticism, a branch of art history that interprets past work, and contemporary criticism, which evaluates work by living artists. Different movements have also spawned their own criteria: what counts as a good Impressionist canvas is not what counts as a good Minimalist object.

The standard method has two parts. A critic first describes the work in enough verbal detail that a reader can picture it, then evaluates it, either following or interspersed with the description. At one end, critics record immediate sensory impressions; at the other, they draw on technical knowledge, a chosen aesthetic theory, and the artist's sociocultural context to reconstruct intent. Because the subjective component is large, no method commands universal assent. Critics of the past are regularly ridiculed for dismissing artists later venerated, such as the early Impressionists, and several now-prestigious movement names, including Impressionism and Cubism, were originally coined as insults before being reclaimed as badges of honour. The 1820s French split between Étienne-Jean Delécluze's defenders of finished classical form and Stendhal's Romantics, who demanded feeling, shows how adversarial criticism can shape a movement.

The genre took its modern form in the 18th century. Jonathan Richardson, an English painter, coined "art criticism" in his 1719 *An Essay on the Whole Art of Criticism*, in which he ranked paintings across seven categories, including drawing, composition, invention, and colouring, each scored 0–18 and summed. The vocabulary spread as Europe's middle class bought art as a status symbol. In France, Jean-Baptiste Dubos (*Réflexions critiques sur la poésie et sur la peinture*, 1718) and Étienne La Font de Saint-Yenne (writing on the 1746 Salon) became the first writers with individual reputations as critics, while Denis Diderot's "Salon of 1765" was among the first sustained attempts to translate paintings into prose. In England, the Royal Academy's 1768 founding institutionalised exhibition reviewing, and the *Morning Chronicle* in the 1770s was the first newspaper to systematically review exhibition art.

By the 19th century, criticism was a recognised vocation. The English painter-essayist William Hazlitt championed art's capacity to enlarge sympathy. John Ruskin, in *Modern Painters* (1843), produced what E. H. Gombrich called "the most ambitious work of scientific art criticism ever attempted", grounded in minute observation of what the artist actually saw. Charles Baudelaire's *Salon of 1845* jolted readers by championing Eugène Delacroix and insisting that "criticism should be partial, impassioned, political". Ruskin's 1877 attack on James McNeill Whistler's *Nocturne in Black and Gold – The Falling Rocket* ("a coxcomb ask two hundred guineas for flinging a pot of paint in the public's face") provoked Whistler's failed libel suit, a Pyrrhic victory (a win that costs more than it gains) that still shapes debates about who controls artistic reputation.

The early 20th century saw criticism formalise around new movements. Roger Fry's 1910 London Post-Impressionist exhibition and his lecture arguing that art should pursue "the language of pure imagination" rather than scientific likeness helped redirect modern taste, prompting Virginia Woolf's quip that "in or about December 1910 human character changed." Clive Bell's 1914 *Art* coined "significant form", the distinctive arrangement of shapes and colours that Bell claimed arouses "aesthetic emotion", and became foundational for formalism, the view that a work's value lies in its formal qualities rather than its subject. Fry's claim that he was indifferent whether he painted "a Christ or a saucepan" summed up that doctrine. Herbert Read championed Paul Nash, Ben Nicholson, Henry Moore, and Barbara Hepworth, and edited the *Burlington Magazine*.

After 1945, criticism professionalised further, especially in New York. Clement Greenberg, a Trotskyist writing for the *Partisan Review* and *The Nation*, argued that Abstract Expressionism and Jackson Pollock in particular marked painting's "purer" reduction to its essential task. Harold Rosenberg reframed the same painters as "action painters", whose canvases recorded existential events rather than pictures. Critics built careers by championing protégés while casting rivals as "followers" or ignoring them; in 1958, when Mark Tobey became the first American painter since Whistler to win the Venice Biennale's top prize, New York's two leading art magazines declined to cover it. Barnett Newman, both artist and critic, wrote catalogue forewords and reviews to construct his own reception, admitting in 1955 that "my struggle against bourgeois society has involved the total rejection of it." Meyer Schapiro and Leo Steinberg lent scholarly weight to the movement, while Michael Fried, Rosalind Krauss, and Robert Hughes extended its critical debate. Feminist art criticism emerged in the 1970s from the broader feminist movement to examine both images of women in art and work made by women.

Critics now publish in newspapers, magazines, books, television, radio, blogs, and social media, curate exhibitions, and write catalogues for museums. The International Association of Art Critics, affiliated with UNESCO and organised into around 76 national sections, represents the profession internationally. Artists have always had an uneasy relationship with their critics, needing positive notices to reach buyers yet often understood only by later generations, a tension that remains the field's defining condition.
