# Beat (music)

A beat is the basic unit of time in music, the regular pulse that listeners tap to and performers count while playing. Pulse is the steady underlying ticking; meter is the grouping of pulses into patterns of strong and weak accents; tempo is the speed at which those pulses pass. The beat is pulse once it is felt and counted inside a metric framework. In practice the units a performer counts aloud are often the first multiple level above the true beat.

## Metric levels

The beat sits in the middle of a hierarchy of time levels. Levels faster than the beat (dividing it) are division levels, and slower levels (spanning several beats) are multiple levels. A hyperbeat is one unit of hypermeter, usually a whole measure, treating the measure itself as a single beat at a higher level.

## Division and meter

How many beats are grouped together, and how each beat is split, defines meter. Two beats per group is duple meter, three is triple. Splitting each beat in two gives simple meter (simple duple 2/4 or 4/4, simple triple 3/4); splitting each beat in three gives compound meter (compound duple 6/8, compound triple 9/8). Tuplets such as five equal parts of a quarter note are irregular divisions.

## Downbeat and upbeat

The downbeat is beat 1 of a bar, usually the strongest accent and the most likely point for a chord change. The upbeat is the last beat of the previous bar, which leans into and anticipates the downbeat. Conductors' hand motion mirrors this: a downbeat moves down, marking arrival; an upbeat moves up, preparing it. A note or short figure that falls before the first barline of a piece is called a pickup or anacrusis, a term borrowed from poetry meaning literally "pushing up."

## On-beat and off-beat

In 4/4 time counted "1 2 3 4", beats 1 and 3 are the strong on-beats, while beats 2 and 4 are the weaker off-beats. Stressing the off-beats instead of the on-beats is syncopation, a defining trait of African polyrhythm that carried into Western popular music. Genres such as rock'n'roll and ska are built on off-beat emphasis. The downbeat cannot be the off-beat, because it is the strongest beat in 4/4.

## Backbeat

A backbeat is a syncopated accent on the off-beats, beats 2 and 4 in 4/4, and a defining feature of R&B, rock, and most modern popular music. Earl Palmer claimed the honor for Fats Domino's 1949 "The Fat Man," though earlier examples exist, including Pete Johnson and Big Joe Turner's 1938 "Roll 'Em Pete" and a 1939 Harry James track. Outside the U.S., Luiz Gonzaga's 1949 "Mangaratiba" shows an early non-U.S. backbeat. The snare drum usually carries the backbeat today; hand claps and stacked cymbals can do the same job, with the hi-hat as a softer option in jazz.

A double backbeat plays one of the off-beats as two eighth notes rather than a quarter, used in songs like The Beatles' "Please Please Me" and Blondie's "Hanging on the Telephone." Early funk often delayed one of the backbeats by an eighth note to give the beat a "kick," and the delayed or late backbeat, where the snare lands slightly after beat 2 or 4 while tempo is held steady, became common in rock and soul from the late 1960s to the mid-1970s. Clyde Stubblefield's "Funky Drummer" break is a well-known example.

## Other beat-related concepts

A tatum is the smallest time division that most often lines up with note onsets in a piece, finer than the beat. An afterbeat stresses beats 2, 3, and 4 after the downbeat. Reggae's "one drop" goes further, silencing beat 1 entirely so the emphasis lands later in the bar. James Brown did the opposite, building his funk on the downbeat, "on the one," rather than the R&B backbeat. A cross-rhythm overlays a pattern of accents that conflicts with the prevailing meter rather than just displacing a single note.

## Beat perception

Beat perception is the human ability to extract a repeating time pattern from sound and synchronize movement to it, which underlies tapping and dancing. Infants across cultures already move rhythmically to music, but matching movement to the actual beat of an auditory stimulus develops between roughly two-and-a-half and four-and-a-half years of age.
