# Pitch (music)

Pitch is the perceptual quality that lets a listener order sounds as "higher" or "lower," the way melodies do. It is one of the four main auditory attributes of musical tones, alongside duration, loudness, and timbre. Pitch is closely tied to frequency, the rate at which a sound wave vibrates, but the two are not the same. Frequency is an objective, measurable property of the wave. Pitch is the subjective sensation a person has when hearing it, so it is measured indirectly by asking listeners to compare sounds. Higher pitch corresponds to faster vibration, lower pitch to slower vibration. Most languages describe this ordering with words for vertical height, even though nothing physical is moving up or down.

For most sounds, perceived pitch matches the repetition rate of the waveform, or the reciprocal of the time between similar peaks. This holds for speech and for musical notes. Pitch depends almost entirely on frequency and very little on amplitude, the wave's intensity. It does shift slightly with loudness: below about 1,000 Hz, louder tones sound slightly lower, and above about 2,000 Hz, slightly higher. The original measurements by Stevens and Snow reported noticeable shifts, but later work by Cohen found that, after averaging out matching errors, the remaining shifts were small, no more than about 2 percent of frequency, or roughly a semitone.

A complex tone combines many simultaneous vibrations. The slowest is the **fundamental frequency**; the others are **overtones**, collectively called **partials**. When overtones are integer multiples of the fundamental they are **harmonics**, and the spectrum is harmonic. The ear often perceives a pitch equal to the fundamental even when that frequency is physically weak or missing, because the brain infers it from the spacing of the harmonics. This is the **missing fundamental**: a tone built from 1000 Hz and 1200 Hz sine waves is commonly heard as having a strong pitch at 200 Hz, the greatest common divisor of the two frequencies. Some complex tones are ambiguous and can be heard as two or three pitches at once, including spectral pitches from the pure components and a combination tone from the repetition rate.

Not every sound has a clear pitch. Sounds with harmonic spectra tend to have **definite pitch**; sounds with non-harmonic, or inharmonic, spectra, such as a snare drum or a bass drum, have **indefinite pitch**. A snare drum still sounds higher than a bass drum because it contains higher frequencies, but neither maps cleanly onto a single named note.

The smallest pitch change a listener can reliably detect, the **just-noticeable difference (JND)**, depends on frequency. Below 500 Hz, the JND is about 3 Hz for a sine tone and about 1 Hz for a complex tone. Above 1,000 Hz, the JND is roughly 0.6 percent of frequency, about 10 cents, where a cent is one hundredth of an equal-tempered semitone. Across the full hearing range of about 16 Hz to 16,000 Hz, listeners distinguish roughly 1,400 pitch steps, compared with the 120 notes of the equal-tempered chromatic scale over the same range.

Two broad families of theories explain how the brain extracts pitch. **Place theories** say pitch comes from where the basilar membrane in the cochlea vibrates most strongly, a mapping called tonotopy, and this must operate at high frequencies, since auditory neurons can only phase-lock their firing to slow signals. **Temporal theories** say pitch comes from the timing of neural firing, particularly the phase-locking of action potentials to the waveform, possibly through an autocorrelation-like operation on nerve firing patterns. Neither view is complete. Place coding alone cannot explain accurate low-frequency pitch, and no neural delay long enough to perform a true autocorrelation has been found, though some models achieve the same effect through phase shifts between cochlear filters.

Pitch can be split into **chroma**, the note's identity within an octave, and **height**, the octave it sits in. The split matters because pitch perception is roughly logarithmic with respect to frequency: the perceived distance from 220 Hz to 440 Hz sounds the same as from 440 Hz to 880 Hz, even though the first step is 220 Hz and the second is 440 Hz. Music theorists and digital systems therefore represent pitch on a logarithmic scale, using the MIDI formula

p = 69 + 12 × log₂(f / 440 Hz),

which assigns A440 the number 69, gives every octave size 12, and makes distance match musical interval. In equal temperament, every semitone is the same size, the twelfth root of 2 (about 1.05946) in frequency, and the octave is exactly a factor of 2. Pianos in their extreme registers deviate slightly because their strings are inharmonically stiff, so tuners use **octave stretching** to make octaves sound in tune. The A above middle C is conventionally set at 440 Hz, a standard adopted in 1939, although period-instrument ensembles use lower references: Baroque pitch at A415, Classical pitch near A427–A430, and Romantic-era French pitch at A435. Transposing instruments such as the B♭ clarinet or trumpet sound at pitches different from their written parts, so musicians use **concert pitch** to name the actual sounding note.

Pitches are written using several systems. **Scientific pitch notation** labels notes by letter and octave number, with middle C as C4 and the 16 Hz low C as C0. **Helmholtz pitch notation** uses a different letter-case convention, such as a′ for the A above middle C. Frequencies can be quoted in hertz, as in 440 Hz. In equal temperament, pitch is insensitive to spelling, so G4 double-sharp and A4 refer to the same frequency. Some music uses microtones, pitches between adjacent semitones; in the MIDI numbering above, the pitch halfway between C (60) and C♯ (61) is 60.5.

A few **aural illusions** exploit how the brain interprets pitch. The **Shepard scale** is a sequence of tones whose pitches are masked so the listener hears them as rising or falling forever. The **tritone paradox** shows that two tones a tritone apart can each sound higher or lower than the other, depending on the listener. Discrete pitches are nearly universal in music, but **gliding pitches**, continuous slides between notes, appear in many cultures, almost always as decoration around an underlying discrete scale.
