# Art history

Art history is the academic study of visual objects and images: their makers, meanings, patrons, materials, and the cultures that produced and received them. It is distinct from **art criticism**, which judges individual works, and from **aesthetics**, the philosophy of beauty and taste.

## What art historians do

Historians situate artworks in the world that made them, examining the artist's intentions, patrons' agendas, contemporary iconography (symbolic images), and the broader social and political pressures of the time. They also analyze **form**: how the artist uses line, shape, color, texture, and composition on a flat surface or in three-dimensional space. The result can be representational art, which depicts recognisable subjects, or non-representational art, which does not. Many historians work alongside **conservators**, the specialists who physically preserve artworks; the field requires enough chemistry to keep objects in condition good enough to study.

## How the discipline formed

The earliest surviving art-historical writing is Pliny the Elder's *Natural History* (c. AD 77–79), which traces Greek artistic development from earlier writers such as the sculptor Xenokrates of Sicyon (c. 280 BC). In 6th-century China, scholar-official critics such as Xie He formulated the **Six Principles of Painting**, a canon for judging artists.

The discipline took its modern shape in Europe. Giorgio Vasari's *Lives of the Most Excellent Painters, Sculptors, and Architects* (mid-16th century) is often called the first true history of art; it recounted Italian artists' biographies and modeled art as a progressive story. A century later, Johann Joachim Winckelmann (1717–1768) shifted attention from the artist's personality to the trained viewer's response. His *Reflections on the Painting and Sculpture of the Greeks* (1755) and *History of Art in Antiquity* (1764) — the first book to carry "history of art" in its title — marked the beginnings of **art criticism** as a distinct practice and pushed art into mainstream philosophical discourse, influencing Kant's *Critique of Judgment* (1790) and Hegel's *Lectures on Aesthetics*. Karl Schnaase's 19th-century *Geschichte der bildenden Künste* helped establish art history as an autonomous, university-taught discipline.

## Modern methodologies

In the late 19th and 20th centuries, several schools defined competing methods:

- **Heinrich Wölfflin** (1864–1945) introduced a comparative, "scientific" analysis of formal style, compared paintings to distinguish stylistic periods, and pursued an "art history without names." He also studied art through ideas of nationhood, asking whether Italian and German styles were inherently different.
- The **Vienna School**, led by Alois Riegl and Franz Wickhoff, rehabilitated Late Antiquity and the Baroque. The Second Vienna School (Hans Sedlmayr and others) pushed formalism further, gaining a reputation for what critics called unrestrained formalism.
- The **Warburg circle** in Hamburg (Aby Warburg, Fritz Saxl, Erwin Panofsky, Gertrud Bing) developed **iconography**, the study of subject matter and symbols drawn from texts such as scripture and mythology, and the broader **iconology** of cultural symbolism. Forced from Hamburg in the 1930s, Saxl and Panofsky transplanted these methods to London (Warburg Institute) and Princeton (Institute for Advanced Study), shaping Anglophone art history.
- The **Frankfurt School** and Marxist critics applied historical materialism, the idea that art reflects the economic class structure of its era. Walter Benjamin's 1935 essay *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* argued that an artwork possesses an "aura," its unique presence in a specific time and place, and that this aura falls into decay as mechanical reproduction makes it harder to apprehend the original context.
- **Feminist art history** emerged in the 1970s. Linda Nochlin's 1971 essay "Why Have There Been No Great Women Artists?" argued that women's exclusion from the canon stemmed from cultural conditions denying them training, not from any lack of talent. Griselda Pollock, Mary Garrard, and Norma Broude extended the field, which often re-reads canonical works such as Picasso's *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon*.

## Scope today

Where the 19th-century discipline was Eurocentric and limited to Western fine and decorative arts (painting, drawing, sculpture, architecture), contemporary art history is global, covering European, Asian, African, and American Indigenous arts alike, and treats visual culture broadly, including photography, graphic arts, and digital media. Subfields range from Marxist and feminist art history to design history and visual culture studies. Historians specialize by period (Prehistoric, Ancient, Medieval, Renaissance, Modern, Contemporary), by movement (Romanesque, Gothic, Renaissance, Baroque, Rococo, Neoclassicism, Romanticism, Realism, Impressionism, Post-Impressionism, Cubism, Modernism, Postmodernism, and many more), by region, by religion, or by technique.

## Institutional life

Museums, galleries, and archives employ art historians as curators, registrars, and researchers. In the United States, the **College Art Association** runs an annual conference and publishes *Art Bulletin* and *Art Journal*; in the United Kingdom, the **Association of Art Historians** publishes *Art History*. Museum studies, including the history of collecting and display, has become a specialization in its own right.
