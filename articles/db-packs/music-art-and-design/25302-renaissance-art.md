# Renaissance art

Renaissance art refers to the painting, sculpture, and decorative arts produced in Europe roughly between 1350 and 1620. It began as a distinct style in Italy around 1400, then spread northward, running in parallel with developments in philosophy, literature, science, and technology. For art historians it marks Europe's transition from the medieval period to the Early Modern age.

The label "Renaissance" means rebirth, implying a clean break with what came before. Scholars no longer accept that. Across much of Europe, Early Renaissance work was produced alongside Late Medieval art. The change was real but gradual.

## What the artists inherited and what they added

Renaissance artists built on three foundations.

Classical antiquity. Greek and Roman sculpture, architecture, and texts were the noblest tradition and were consciously imitated. Brunelleschi and Donatello made the first archaeological study of Roman remains, and a classical style of architecture inspired a parallel classicism in painting and sculpture visible in the 1420s in Masaccio and Uccello.

Northern European technique. Oil paint, refined by Belgian and French painters including Jan van Eyck, Rogier van der Weyden, Robert Campin, and Hugo van der Goes, was adopted in Italy from about 1475 and permanently changed painting worldwide.

Scientific observation. Contemporary knowledge of optics, anatomy, and mathematics let artists render light, volume, and perspective more accurately than before. Alberti's *De pictura* (1435) formalised linear perspective; his *De re aedificatoria* (1452) did the same for architecture.

These conditions were concentrated in Florence, whose wealth came largely from the Medici Bank. Cosimo de' Medici set a new standard for patronage independent of church or monarchy. A similar cluster of talent formed in Venice around the Bellini family, Mantegna, Giorgione, Titian, and Tintoretto.

## Proto-Renaissance, c. 1280–1400

Late 13th- and early 14th-century Italian sculpture by Nicola Pisano and his son Giovanni, working at Pisa, Siena, and Pistoia, already showed classicising tendencies, probably picked up from ancient Roman sarcophagi. Their masterpieces are the pulpits of Pisa's Baptistery and Cathedral. In Florence, Giotto developed a naturalistic, three-dimensional figurative style that broke from the flatter Byzantine manner of his teacher Cimabue. His fresco cycle of the Life of Christ in the Arena Chapel, Padua, was admired by Vasari as having rescued art from the "crude, traditional, Byzantine style."

## Early Renaissance in Italy, 1400–1495

The first recognisably Renaissance artists emerged in Florence at the 1401 competition for the bronze doors of the Baptistery, where Brunelleschi, Donatello, and the winner Lorenzo Ghiberti were among the seven entrants. Donatello sculpted the humanist *David* and the first large equestrian bronze since antiquity (the Gattamelata monument). Masaccio, painting from 1425, carried Giotto's solidity of form and naturalism into the new era, especially in the Brancacci Chapel frescoes begun with Masolino, which deeply influenced Michelangelo. Piero della Francesca made systematic studies of light and linear perspective visible in his *History of the True Cross* fresco cycle at Arezzo. Antonello da Messina brought oil painting into Italy from Naples around 1450, influencing the Venetian school. The period closed with another group commission: Pope Sixtus IV's decoration of the Sistine Chapel by Botticelli, Perugino, Ghirlandaio, and Cosimo Rosselli.

## Early Netherlandish art, 1425–1525

Painting in the Low Countries developed partly independently of Italy, without any deliberate striving to revive antiquity. Working in oil on panel, painters such as Jan van Eyck, Hubert van Eyck, Hans Memling, and Hugo van der Goes kept a medieval sense of hierarchical proportion while achieving a meticulous realism of texture, light, and surface that suited the oil medium. The Netherlandish style reached Italy through works such as Hugo van der Goes' *Portinari Altarpiece*, which arrived in Florence in 1475 and influenced Ghirlandaio. Hieronymus Bosch, active toward the end of the period, populated manuscript border ornament with surreal, fanciful forms, as in the *Garden of Earthly Delights* triptych.

## High Renaissance in Italy, 1495–1520

The High Renaissance centres on three figures. Leonardo da Vinci spent a lifetime recording observations of nature, used oil paint to model light and atmosphere with unprecedented naturalism (*Mona Lisa*, 1503–1506), dissected cadavers to understand skeletal and muscular anatomy (*Saint Jerome in the Wilderness*, c. 1480), and set a benchmark for religious emotion in *The Last Supper* (1495–1498). Michelangelo, almost exclusively interested in the human body, produced the *Pietà* and *David* in his early twenties, then the Sistine Chapel ceiling (c. 1511) and, between 1534 and 1541, the *Last Judgement*, whose elongated figures mark the shift into Mannerism. Raphael, dying in 1520 at 37, completed the trio with lifelike portraits and serene Madonna compositions such as the *Sistine Madonna*; many historians take his death as the close of the High Renaissance. Venetian painters Giovanni Bellini, Giorgione, and the young Titian added colour, atmosphere, and drama on a grand scale.

## Northern Renaissance: Germany, France, Britain

Renaissance influence reached Germany in the late 15th century. Albrecht Dürer (1471–1528) travelled to Italy and was, as Gardner writes, "the first northern artist who fully understood the basic aims of the southern Renaissance," though he often worked in his native German style. Hans Holbein the Younger (c. 1497–1543) more successfully fused Italian ideas with northern realism. Other major figures included Matthias Grünewald, Albrecht Altdorfer, and Lucas Cranach the Elder; metal engraving flourished alongside the printing trade. France's Renaissance drew on Italian and Netherlandish currents, with Jean Fouquet, who visited Italy in 1437, credited as the inventor of the portrait miniature and painter of the earliest known formal self-portrait (1450). Britain was slow to develop a native style, relying on imported court artists such as Holbein, though the portrait miniature flourished early through Nicholas Hilliard.

## Techniques that defined the era

Linear perspective. First treated as a window into space by Giotto in the early 14th century; formalised by Brunelleschi and Alberti.

Foreshortening. Shortening lines to create an illusion of depth.

Sfumato. Coined by Leonardo; from Italian *sfumare*, "to evaporate." Blending tones with thin glazes so that outlines soften, giving a smoky depth.

Chiaroscuro. Strong light–dark contrast to model volume, fully exploited in the later Baroque.

Renaissance artists worked across religious commissions, mythological and history painting drawn from Ovid and the *Golden Legend* (1260), and an expanding range of secular portraiture, but the unifying thread was the willingness to use direct observation, classical precedent, and scientific method as the basis for making pictures that looked convincingly like the world they depicted.
