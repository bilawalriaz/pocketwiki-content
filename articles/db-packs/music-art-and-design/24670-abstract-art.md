# Abstract art

Abstract art is visual art that builds compositions from shape, form, color, and line with a degree of independence from recognizable visual references. Closely related terms, sometimes interchangeable and sometimes distinct, include non-figurative, non-objective, and non-representational art.

Abstraction is a continuum, not a single category. A painting that conspicuously alters color or form is partially abstract; total abstraction bears no trace of recognizable objects. Figurative and total abstraction are nearly mutually exclusive, but figurative and representational art often contain partial abstraction. Fauvism (altered color) and Cubism (altered form) embody partial abstraction; geometric abstraction and lyrical abstraction are often totally so.

## Origins

From the Renaissance to the mid-19th century, Western art was governed by perspective and the goal of reproducing an illusion of visible reality. By the late 1800s, artists wanted a new art equal to changes in technology, science, and philosophy, and patronage had shifted from the church to a private market able to support independent careers. Romanticism broadened imaginative license, Impressionism (Whistler's *Nocturne in Black and Gold*, 1874) emphasized sensation over objects, and Expressionism (Munch, Ensor) replaced subject matter with distorted, emotionally charged color. Cézanne's reduction of nature to cylinder, sphere, and cone, built up through modulated color in flat planes from a single viewpoint, became the basis of Cubism. In Eastern Europe, Theosophical mysticism (Blavatsky) and the teachings of Gurdjieff and Ouspensky influenced pioneer geometric artists including Hilma af Klint, Kandinsky, Mondrian, Malevich, and Kupka.

## The opening, 1900–1915

Fauvism (Matisse, Braque, Derain, Dufy, Metzinger, c. 1905 onward) developed an expressive color language that directly influenced Kandinsky. Cubism, derived from Cézanne and advanced mainly by Picasso and Braque, broke and reassembled form, opening the door to abstraction. Between 1909 and 1913 several artists produced experimental works aiming at a "pure" abstraction: Picabia's *Caoutchouc* (c. 1909); Kandinsky's *Untitled (First Abstract Watercolor)* (1913) and the *Improvisation* and *Impression* series; Kupka's *Amorpha, Fugue en deux couleurs* (1912), exhibited at the 1912 *Salon de la Section d'Or*, where Apollinaire named the new tendency Orphism, "the art of painting new structures out of elements that have not been borrowed from the visual sphere, but had been created entirely by the artist… it is a pure art"; Delaunay's *Simultaneous Windows* and *Formes Circulaires, Soleil n°2* (1912–13); Survage's *Colored Rhythm* (1913); and Mondrian's *Tableau No. 1* and *Composition No. 11* (1913). Malevich completed the first entirely abstract work, the Suprematist *Black Square*, in 1915.

## Russia, the Bauhaus, and the music parallel

Many Russian abstract artists became Constructivists, holding that art was no longer remote from life but life itself. Tatlin's slogan "Art into life!" expressed the belief that the artist should become a technician using modern production tools; Stepanova and Exter turned to theatre and graphic design. Malevich, Pevsner, and Gabo argued instead that art is essentially spiritual, creating the individual's place in the world rather than organizing material life. After the revolutionary period of 1917–1921 ended, only socialist realism was permitted in the USSR by the 1930s; Pevsner, Gabo, and Kandinsky emigrated.

Kandinsky, himself an amateur musician, argued that as visual art becomes more abstract it acquires characteristics of music: an art of abstract sound and divisions of time. Color and mark could "resonate in the soul," and Theosophy's spread of Indian and Chinese sacred texts fed interest in an "objectless" inner image. The universal shapes of geometry, the circle, square, and triangle, function in abstract art as fundamental systems underlying visible reality.

The Bauhaus, founded by Walter Gropius at Weimar in 1919 and moved to Dessau in 1925, taught the unity of architecture, painting, and craft, drawing on the Arts and Crafts movement and the Deutscher Werkbund. Teachers included Klee, Kandinsky, Itten, Josef Albers, Anni Albers, and Moholy-Nagy. The school was closed under Nazi pressure in 1932, and the 1937 *Entartete Kunst* exhibition of degenerate art triggered an exodus of European modernists to Paris, London, and especially America.

## Paris, London, and the move to New York

In 1930s Paris, Sophie Tauber-Arp and Jean Arp produced organic-geometric collaborations, and Katarzyna Kobro applied mathematical ideas to sculpture. *Cercle et Carré* (Torres-García, Seuphor) was criticized by Theo van Doesburg as too indefinite; he responded with the journal *Art Concret*, defining an abstract art in which line, color, and surface alone are concrete reality. *Abstraction-Création*, founded 1931, regrouped in London after 1935; Nicolete Gray organized the international *Abstract and Concrete* exhibition the following year with Mondrian, Miró, Hepworth, and Nicholson. Hepworth, Nicholson, and Gabo continued their constructivist work at St. Ives in Cornwall.

By the early 1940s the principal European modernists (Duchamp, Léger, Mondrian, Lipchitz, Masson, Ernst, Breton) had fled to New York, where Mondrian's *Composition No. 10* (1939–1942), with primary colors, a white ground, and a black grid, distilled a radical classical approach to the rectangle. American artists coalesced into the Abstract Expressionists and the New York School, with Graham and Hofmann as bridge figures between the European arrivals and a younger American generation. Rothko moved from surrealist imagery toward powerful color compositions; the expressionistic gesture and the act of painting itself became central for Pollock, Motherwell, and Franz Kline; de Kooning and Gorky moved from figurative work into abstraction by the end of the 1940s.

## After1945 and social readings

Postwar abstraction diversified into color field, lyrical abstraction, hard-edge painting, geometric abstraction, minimalism, monochrome painting, assemblage, op art, neo-Dada, shaped-canvas painting, and Minimalist sculpture (Judd, Stella), and persists as a live tradition rather than a single style.

Theodor W. Adorno proposed that modernist abstraction responds to and reflects the growing abstraction of social relations in industrial society. Frederic Jameson linked modernist abstraction to the abstract power of money, equating all things as exchange-values, so that the social content of abstract art is the abstract nature of social existence itself: legal formalities, bureaucratic impersonalization, and information/power in late modernity. Post-Jungians, by contrast, read the disintegration of conventional ideas of form and matter in quantum theory as underlying the divorce of the concrete and the abstract in modern art.

The deliberate, programmatic claim that a picture could be a composition of pure visual elements with no obligation to describe the world became possible only when Cubism had loosened representation enough for artists to step through the gap it opened.
