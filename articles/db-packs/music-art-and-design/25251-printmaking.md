# Printmaking

Printmaking creates artworks by transferring ink from a *matrix* (a prepared printing surface) onto paper, fabric, or other material. Each piece pulled from the matrix is called an **impression** and is an original work of art, not a copy. A group of impressions from one matrix forms an **edition**; since the late 19th century artists usually sign and number them, then destroy the matrix to limit the run.

## The Four Families

Techniques divide into four families based on which part of the matrix carries the ink.

| Family | Where the ink sits | Techniques |
|---|---|---|
| **Relief** | The raised original surface; carved-away areas stay clean | woodcut, wood engraving, linocut, metalcut |
| **Intaglio** | Grooves or cavities below the surface | engraving, etching, drypoint, mezzotint, aquatint |
| **Planographic** | A flat surface treated so image and non-image areas repel different inks | lithography, monotyping |
| **Stencil** | Ink pushed through cut-out areas of a screen or sheet | screen printing, risograph, pochoir |

Outside these sit viscosity printing and water-surface techniques like paper marbling.

## Relief: Woodcut

Woodcut is the oldest printmaking technique, probably first used to decorate cloth, then text and images on paper in China by the 5th century. European image woodcuts appeared around 1400, with Japan following later. The artist draws on a wood plank; a carver removes the non-printing areas with sharp tools; the raised surface is inked (with a *brayer* in the West, a brush in Japan); and paper is pressed on top using a *baren*, a spoon, or a press.

**Reduction printing** produces many colors from a single block. The artist prints one color, carves more material away, then prints the next color on top so earlier layers show through. The block is destroyed layer by layer, so the edition is capped at the end of the process.

## Intaglio: Engraving, Etching, Drypoint, Mezzotint, Aquatint

In intaglio the ink sits in recesses below the surface. The plate is fully inked, then wiped clean on top, leaving ink only in the grooves. Dampened paper is forced into those grooves under a high-pressure press.

**Engraving** developed in Germany in the 1430s from goldsmiths' metalwork. A hardened steel *burin* pushes lines directly into a copper plate, producing crisp, deliberate marks. Several hundred impressions can be pulled before the plate wears.

**Etching** replaces physical cutting with chemistry. A waxy *ground* coats the plate; the artist draws through it with a needle, exposing the metal; acid (nitric acid or ferric chloride) bites the exposed lines. Etching is believed to have been invented by Daniel Hopfer (c. 1470–1536), and it challenged engraving because drawing skills transfer directly while metalworking does not. Raised areas stay blank; crevices hold ink, the opposite of a woodcut.

**Drypoint** uses a sharp needle instead of a burin. The displaced metal leaves a *burr* along each line, giving drypoint a soft, velvety quality. The burr crushes quickly under the press, so editions are tiny, often ten to twenty impressions. Nineteenth-century *steelfacing* (electro-plating) hardens plates and extends runs.

**Mezzotint** (Italian *mezzo* "half" + *tinta* "tone") works from dark to light. A *rocker* roughens the whole copper plate; a *burnisher* smooths selected areas back toward white. Rough zones hold more ink and print dark; smooth zones print light. Invented by Ludwig von Siegen (1609–1680), mezzotint became England's preferred portrait-reproduction medium in the 18th century.

**Aquatint** turns intaglio into a tonal process. Acid-resistant rosin dusts onto the plate and fuses; acid bites exposed metal between rosin particles, creating a grain that holds tonal ink. Aquatint is almost always combined with etching for linework. The grain wears quickly, perhaps 100 strong impressions before contrast fades, a limitation that helped establish the limited-edition print in the 19th century.

## Planographic: Lithography

Lithography was invented by Alois Senefelder in 1798 and exploits the repulsion of oil and water. The artist draws on limestone with a greasy medium; acid fixes the drawing into the stone; gum arabic seals the bare surface. Water clings only to non-image areas and repels the oil-based ink, which adheres to the drawing. A press transfers the image to paper, capturing fine gradations in shading and small detail.

## Stencil: Screen Printing

Screen printing uses a mesh (silk, nylon, polyester, or stainless steel) stretched over a frame. A stencil blocks parts of the mesh; a squeegee forces ink through the open areas onto the surface below. No press is required, which is why printmakers value screen printing for its low-tech accessibility. It can print on paper, cloth, canvas, glass, metal, walls, and granite.

## One-Off Techniques: Monotype and Monoprint

**Monotype** is the painterly exception: a unique image made by drawing or painting on a smooth, non-absorbent surface (historically copper) and transferring it to paper under a press. Most ink transfers in the first pull, so a second "ghost print" is faint and considered inferior.

**Monoprint** uses a reusable matrix (woodblock, litho stone, copper plate) but alters ink, color, or wiping between pulls, so each impression is unique. A *variable edition* is a series of related monoprints from one matrix.

## Color and Registration

Color is added by separate plates, blocks, or screens (typically three to four, occasionally up to seven) printed in sequence from lightest to darkest, or by progressively cutting one block in reduction printing.

**Registration** aligns successive color layers. Most matrices carry small marks or holes to position the paper; improper registration can be a deliberate artistic choice.

## Digital Printmaking

Digital prints use inkjet printers instead of a press, on paper, cloth, or plastic canvas. *Giclée*, coined by Jack Duganne in 1991 for inkjet prints, originally described dye-based output and now usually refers to pigment-based prints on large-format machines. Pigment inks (finely ground, suspended particles) outlast dye inks (dissolved color), which penetrate fibers but fade faster on thin coatings. *Direct-to-garment printing* uses aqueous inkjet to print onto textiles.

## Safety and Preservation

Etchers and lithographers handle acids with rubber gloves, ventilation hoods, and respirators; aquatint adds rosin dust as a respiratory hazard. Alkaline acid-free paper can last over 1,000 years, while older acidic stock yellows and *foxes* (brown blotches from mold or chemical reaction). Color prints fade in light, so sensitive works are lit below 50 lux with UV-filtering sleeves, and archival matting is required because acidic boards attack even acid-free prints.
