# Key (music)

A key in Western tonal music names a piece's home pitch and its prevailing mode. Every melody that feels resolved ends by returning to a tonic pitch within a recognized scale. The key tells you which pitch serves as that anchor and whether the surrounding mode is major or minor.

## The two ingredients of a key

A key has two components. The first is the tonic pitch, named by a letter from A through G, optionally modified by ♯ (sharp) or ♭ (flat). Twelve pitch classes exist in standard Western music, so twelve possible tonics exist. The second is the mode, either Major or Minor. With twelve tonics and two modes, twenty-four distinct keys exist, though some can be spelled with different names that sound identical (C♯ Major and D♭ Major name the same set of pitches). When someone says "the key of C" without specifying, they usually mean C Major.

## Why twelve pitches, and why a scale

Standard Western music groups audible vibrations into twelve pitch classes within an octave, the distance when one frequency doubles. Seven are natural pitches: A, B, C, D, E, F, G. On a piano these are the white keys. The other five sit between certain white keys and appear as black keys, notated with ♯ or ♭.

Humans judge pitch logarithmically: the ratio between frequencies matters more than their absolute difference. Two vibrations where one has exactly twice the frequency of the other sound like the same note an octave apart. This octave equivalence tiles the audible range into a repeating pattern of twelve pitch classes.

A scale selects seven of those twelve pitches and arranges them ascending, beginning and ending on the tonic one octave apart. The scale that fits a key tells the performer which pitches are at home (diatonic) and which are visitors (chromatic).

## Major and minor scales

A major scale follows the step pattern tone–tone–semitone–tone–tone–tone–semitone. A minor scale lowers the third, sixth, and seventh pitches by one semitone, giving tone–semitone–tone–tone–semitone–tone–tone. This shift is why major keys tend to sound brighter or more stable while minor keys feel darker, a generalization, not a rule, but a useful one. The tonic anchors both versions.

Two scales that share the same set of pitches but start on different tonics are modes. C Major and A Minor form such a pair: C Major uses only white keys starting on C; A Minor uses only white keys starting on A. Same seven pitches, different tonic, different character.

## Diatonic, chromatic, and the tonic

The seven pitches of a key's scale are diatonic. The other five are chromatic. Music in a key uses diatonic pitches most of the time and chromatic pitches rarely. A piece does not forbid chromatic notes; it makes them feel like departures. The tonic itself acts as a point of stability, and compositions almost always conclude on the tonic, which is what gives the ending a sense of arrival.

## Key signatures on the page

Written music opens with a key signature, a row of ♯ or ♭ symbols at the start of the staff that tells the performer which pitches are diatonic throughout the piece. Sharps are always ordered F, C, G, D, A, E, B; flats reverse that order. A key signature lets the composer avoid writing an accidental on every note, but it does not identify the tonic, so a signature with no sharps or flats could mean either C Major or A Minor.

## Transposing and transposing instruments

Moving a whole piece up or down by the same number of semitones is transposition. The melody stays identical; only the starting pitch changes. Some instruments, including the clarinet, trumpet, and saxophone, are transposing instruments because their written music is offset from what they actually sound. A B♭ clarinet plays a written C as a B♭, so its player reads music in a different key from the one audiences hear. The specific transposing instrument is often chosen to minimize accidentals in the written key signature.

## Numbering chords by position

Chord patterns can be written as numbers instead of pitch names. In F Major, a triad built on F is 1, and one built on C (the fifth scale degree) is 5. In G Major the same patterns become 1 and 5 again, just on different pitches. The Nashville number system uses Arabic numerals (1, 4, 5); classical analysis often uses Roman numerals (I, IV, V), uppercase for major chords and lowercase for minor. This lets composers refer to common progressions such as the blues sequence (1, 4, 1, 5, 4, 1) or the doo-wop progression (1, 6, 4, 5) without tying them to any specific key.

## The Circle of Fifths

Keys relate to one another through a diagram called the Circle of Fifths. Moving clockwise, each key sharpens one pitch from the previous key; moving counterclockwise, each key flattens one. Adjacent keys share six of seven diatonic pitches. Keys directly opposite share only one or two. Because adjacent keys differ by just one pitch, modulating, shifting from one key to another within a piece, is easiest between neighbors or between a major key and its relative minor (the minor key built on that major scale's sixth degree, sharing all the same pitches).

Keys with the same tonic but different modes, such as D Major and D Minor, are parallel keys. They differ in three pitches and sit three positions apart on the circle.

## When a key does not apply

Not all music fits a key. Some Romantic-era works, including parts of Wagner's *Tristan und Isolde*, deliberately blur tonal centers. Twentieth-century atonal composers such as Schoenberg abandoned tonal hierarchy entirely, treating all twelve pitches as equally available.
