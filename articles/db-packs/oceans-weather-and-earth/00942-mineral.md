# Mineral

A mineral is a solid, naturally occurring substance with a well-defined chemical composition and an ordered atomic arrangement. Minerals are the building blocks of rocks; a rock is an aggregate of one or more minerals (or mineraloids, which are natural solids that lack a crystalline structure, such as opal). The International Mineralogical Association (IMA) defines and names mineral species and recognized 6,145 as of May 2025. The concept was first classified by Theophrastus around 315 BCE in *On Stones*, then refined by Georgius Agricola, Carl Linnaeus, and James Dwight Dana, whose 1837 *System of Mineralogy* became the foundation for modern classification.

## The IMA definition

A qualifying substance must be solid, naturally occurring, and possess a definite crystallographic structure; native mercury is the historical exception to the solid requirement. Chemical composition may vary within limits, producing two recurring patterns. A solid solution has two or more elements substituting freely in equivalent structural sites; mackinawite is (Fe,Ni)₉S₈, with iron and nickel interchangeable. A mineral group shares a structural template across a family of related species; the olivine group is (Mg,Fe)₂SiO₄, with magnesium and iron interchangeable. Hypothetical minerals known only from laboratory synthesis or theoretical prediction are excluded. Anthropogenic and biogenic substances are excluded unless geological processes produced them, a boundary that remains contested.

## Biogenic minerals

The IMA's exclusion of biominerals is the most actively debated edge of the definition. In 2005, Skinner proposed that any "element or compound, amorphous or crystalline, formed through biogeochemical processes" should count as a mineral. More than 60 such biominerals had been described and named before the IMA ever listed them, and they remain recognized in the Dana classification even though they sit outside the official catalogue. The IMA's Working Group on Environmental Mineralogy and Geochemistry studies mineral-forming microorganisms, but the formal line is still drawn at geological origin.

## Chemistry and why minerals form

Mineral chemistry is controlled by crustal chemistry. Eight elements, oxygen, silicon, aluminium, iron, magnesium, calcium, sodium, and potassium, make up more than 98 percent of the crust by mass, and mineral diversity reflects that elemental abundance. Substitution between ions of similar size and charge is routine; aluminium (Al³⁺) regularly replaces silicon (Si⁴⁺) in mineral structures. A coordination polyhedron describes the geometry of a cation surrounded by anions (almost always oxygen), and the silica tetrahedron [SiO₄]⁴⁻ is the single most important building block in the crust, since it is the foundation of all silicates. Temperature, pressure, and composition set which minerals are stable; changes in those conditions drive transitions through weathering, metasomatism, and the rock cycle.

## Silicates and other classes

Silicates dominate the crust at roughly 90 percent, because their tetrahedra link into chains, sheets, and frameworks. The subclasses are ordered by how much the tetrahedra polymerize: tectosilicates are three-dimensional frameworks (quartz, feldspars), phyllosilicates are sheets (micas, clays), inosilicates are single or double chains (pyroxenes, amphiboles), cyclosilicates are rings (tourmaline, beryl), sorosilicates are paired tetrahedra (epidote), and orthosilicates are isolated tetrahedra linked through other cations (olivine, garnet). The remaining 8 percent of the crust consists of non-silicates, including native elements, sulfides, oxides, halides, carbonates, sulfates, phosphates, and a small organic category. Two systems organize all of this: the Dana system, which assigns a four-part number reflecting composition and structure, and the Strunz system, which combines chemical and structural criteria.

## Physical properties used for identification

Because most minerals are identified in hand sample, a set of physical properties is diagnostic. Crystal structure belongs to one of 32 point groups grouped into 6 crystal families, and twinning (the systematic intergrowth of two crystals) and crystal habit (the characteristic aggregate shape) are often telling. Hardness is ranked on the Mohs scale, an ordinal 1-to-10 scale of scratch resistance, though hardness can vary with crystallographic direction, as in kyanite. Lustre is either metallic or non-metallic. Colour is often misleading because trace impurities (allochromatic) can tint a mineral that is otherwise colourless, whereas idiochromatic colour comes from essential structural elements; streak, the colour of the powdered mineral, is more reliable, especially for metallic species. Cleavage follows planes of structural weakness, parting follows defects such as twin boundaries, and fracture describes any break that ignores those planes. Specific gravity, controlled by the atomic masses of the constituent elements, generally rises with metallic lustre.

## Rocks, ores, gems, and industrial minerals

Rocks are defined by the relative proportions of their constituent minerals: granite by quartz, alkali feldspar, and plagioclase; limestone almost entirely by calcite. About 150 minerals are abundant enough to be considered rock-forming, with quartz, feldspars, micas, amphiboles, pyroxenes, olivines, and calcite the most important. Ores are minerals mined for a valuable element, such as cinnabar for mercury and sphalerite for zinc. Gems, roughly 20 species, add aesthetic value to the usual requirements of durability and rarity. Industrial minerals, muscovite mica being a familiar example, have commercial uses unrelated to gems, ores, or fuels.

## Polymorphs and ongoing debates

Two minerals with identical chemistry but different crystal structures are polymorphs of each other: quartz and stishovite are both SiO₂, but stishovite forms only at the high pressures of impact sites, and its dense silicon packing gives it distinctive physical properties. Several boundary questions remain open. The IMA still rejects amorphous substances, and it has not set a minimum crystal size, leaving the status of natural nanoparticles unresolved. Stability is also unsettled: the IMA requires only that composition and structure be well-determined, so meridianiite, a hydrated magnesium sulfate stable below 2 °C, is accepted. The 2011 acceptance of icosahedrite, a naturally occurring quasicrystal with an ordered but non-periodic atomic arrangement, extended "ordered atomic structure" beyond the traditional 32 point groups.
