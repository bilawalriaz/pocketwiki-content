# Fracture (geology)

A fracture is any separation in rock that divides it into two or more pieces. It forms when stress overcomes the rock's cohesion along its weakest plane, and may appear as a tight crack or open into a fissure. Fractures give otherwise impermeable rock pathways for fluids, making heavily fractured rocks good aquifers and hydrocarbon reservoirs.

## Brittle deformation and fracture modes

Rock near the surface responds to stress by brittle deformation: it breaks rather than flows. The two primary processes are tensile fracturing, which produces joints with no slip, and shear fracturing, where the rock slides along the break. Secondary processes like frictional sliding and cataclastic flow can follow on existing joints and faults. Fracture profiles tend to look like blades, ellipsoids, or circles.

Three modes describe how any crack can be loaded:
- **Mode I (opening)**: tensile stress pulls the crack faces apart, perpendicular to the crack plane. Most joints are Mode I.
- **Mode II (sliding)**: shear acts parallel to the crack plane and perpendicular to the crack front.
- **Mode III (tearing)**: shear acts parallel to both the crack plane and the crack front.

## How tensile fractures form

A rock contains many pre-existing microfractures. In **axial stretching**, a remote tensile stress lets these microcracks open; stresses at their tips intensify until the rock ruptures. This happens during rapid overburden erosion or on the crest of an anticline, where folding stretches the upper layers.

In **hydraulic fracturing**, pore fluid pressure rises above the least principal stress (from rapid sediment compaction, thermal expansion of fluids, or fluid injection). The crack then opens perpendicular to the least stress.

Compression can also cause tension. In a Brazilian disc test, a compressive load squeezes a disc so its sides bulge outward; the induced tension splits the disc parallel to the loading axis. Natural longitudinal splitting works the same way.

## Griffith's criterion and fracture mechanics

A. A. Griffith, working during World War I, framed crack growth as an energy balance: a crack advances when the elastic strain energy released by breaking bonds exceeds the surface energy of the new surfaces. For a crack of half-length *a* in a material with Young's modulus *E*, the critical tensile stress is σ_f = (2Eγ / πa)^(1/2). Longer cracks and lower surface energy both lower the rock's strength.

**Linear elastic fracture mechanics (LEFM)** generalises this by describing the stress field near the crack tip through the stress intensity factor *K*. As distance from the tip approaches zero, the geometry function becomes fixed. LEFM works well for Mode I but is less suited to Modes II and III in geology, where friction on the crack faces absorbs energy that an idealised model assumes drives growth.

## Shear fractures, faults, and Coulomb's criterion

A fault is a fracture across which the two faces have slipped. Faults start as shear failure but often rotate during propagation so they sit perpendicular to the least principal stress, so large faults do not behave like simple Mode II or III cracks. As the faces slide, tension at the tip spawns **wing cracks** (tensile fractures) that propagate in the direction of the maximum principal stress, letting the shear fracture extend by joining with them.

Charles Coulomb proposed that compressive shear failure occurs when σ_s = C + μ(σ_n − σ_f), where C is cohesion, μ is the coefficient of internal friction, σ_n is the normal stress, and σ_f is the pore fluid pressure. When pore fluid pressure approaches **lithostatic pressure** (the weight of overlying rock), the effective normal stress collapses and shear failure happens at very low applied stress.

A Mohr-Coulomb diagram shows that any existing crack oriented within roughly ±α/4 of the optimal angle will slip before a new fracture forms. The **asperities** that jut from each face touch only at small spots, shrinking the real contact area and the resulting friction.

## Crack propagation in three dimensions

Rock is polycrystalline, so cracks grow by joining microcracks in front of the tip, a region called the brittle process zone. The shear crack propagates because tensile wing cracks open perpendicular to the least principal stress, briefly stabilise, and link up. Microcracks spread in all directions and leave a weakened zone behind that is sensitive to later changes in pore pressure.

Deep in the crust, high temperature and pressure push rock into semi-brittle and plastic behaviour. There, cracks can blunt rather than propagate, and in the brittle-ductile transition zone **cataclastic flow** mixes brittle and plastic deformation.

## Joint geometries

Field geologists recognise several joint patterns:
- **Plumose structures**: feathery networks fanning out from a joint origin, with a smooth mirror zone near the start, mist and hackle zones further out, and barbs branching from the plume axis.
- **Orthogonal joints** meet at 90°; **conjugate joints** meet at smaller angles; **systematic joints** are sub-parallel with even spacing.
- **Columnar joints** form hexagonal columns from cooling and contraction in lava flows or shallow intrusions; **desiccation cracks** form hexagons when mud dries.
- **Sheeting joints** run parallel to the ground surface, produced by unloading.
- **Pinnate joints** form at 35°–45° to a fault's shear face; **release joints** open when local tension appears.
- **En echelon** arrays are tensile fractures stepping parallel within a fault zone.

## Subcritical crack growth

Fluids can extend fractures at stresses well below the Griffith limit. Water reacts with quartz at the crack tip, substituting OH for O in the lattice; because the OH bond is much weaker, the local strength drops and the fracture creeps forward.

## Engineering and modelling

In geotechnical work a fracture is a discontinuity that governs the strength and deformation of rock around tunnels, foundations, and slopes. In the energy sector, low-porosity brittle rocks like the Austin Chalk in South Texas produce hydrocarbons almost entirely from fractures; tectonic stress generated one of the world's most extensive fractured reservoirs, and horizontal wells were first used there to intersect as many fracture networks as possible. Natural fractures also act like Griffith cracks in unconventional reservoirs, supplying the permeability that makes tight rock commercially productive. The downside is drilling risk: a high-pressure fracture can cause a blowout by feeding fluid into the wellbore, while a low-pressure fracture can lose hydrostatic balance and trigger a kick from a higher formation.

Since the mid-1980s, computers have modelled these networks statistically as **discrete fracture networks** (DFN), assigning probability distributions to size, shape, and orientation so that engineers can predict where fractures connect and plan wells and excavations accordingly.
