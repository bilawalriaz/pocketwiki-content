# Engineering geology

Engineering geology is the application of geology to engineering practice so that the geological factors affecting the location, design, construction, operation, and maintenance of engineering works are recognized and addressed. Its central concern is earth–structure interaction: how the earth and earth processes affect human-made structures, and how those structures in turn affect the ground. The principal objective of the engineering geologist is protection of life and property from damage caused by geological conditions.

Engineering geology overlaps with geological engineering and geotechnical engineering. Where the content differs, the distinction usually lies in the training and experience of the practitioner rather than in the work performed. Engineering geologists provide geological and geotechnical recommendations, analysis, and design associated with human development and structures.

## History

Engineering geology became a recognized discipline only in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The first book titled *Engineering Geology* was published in 1880 by William Penning. In the early 20th century, Charles Peter Berkey, considered the first American engineering geologist, worked on water-supply projects for New York City and later on the Hoover Dam. In 1914, Ries and Watson wrote the first American engineering geology textbook.

In 1921, Reginald W. Brock started the first undergraduate and graduate programs in geological engineering at the University of British Columbia, observing that students with an engineering foundation made first-class practising geologists. In 1925, Karl Terzaghi, an Austrian-trained engineer and geologist, published the first soil mechanics text. Terzaghi treated soil mechanics as a sub-discipline of engineering geology and in 1929 co-published an *Engineering Geology* text with Redlich and Kampe.

The need for geologists on engineering works attracted worldwide attention in 1928 when the St. Francis Dam failed in California, killing 426 people. Subsequent failures reinforced the requirement for engineering geologists on large projects. In 1951, one of the earliest formal definitions of the engineering geologist was issued by the Geological Society of America.

## Practice and scope

A central role is interpreting landforms and earth processes to identify geological and human-made hazards that could seriously affect civil structures and human development. A geology background gives the engineer an understanding of how the earth works, which is essential for minimizing earth-related hazards. Most engineering geologists hold graduate degrees with specialized training in soil mechanics, rock mechanics, geotechnics, groundwater, hydrology, and civil design. Combined with geological training, this equips them to mitigate hazards at the interface of earth and structure.

Studies run across the planning, environmental impact analysis, design, and construction phases of public and private projects, and continue into post-construction and forensic phases. Typical projects include residential, commercial, and industrial developments; public works such as drainage, power plants, transmission lines, pipelines, tunnels, canals, dams, reservoirs, foundations, railroads, highways, bridges, and airports; mine and quarry developments; wetland and habitat restoration; hazardous waste remediation; coastal engineering and waterfront works; and offshore platforms, outfalls, and sub-sea pipelines.

## Geohazards and adverse geological conditions

Hazards evaluated and mitigated by engineering geologists include fault rupture on seismically active faults; earthquake effects such as ground shaking, liquefaction, lateral spreading, lurching, tsunami, and seiche; landslides, mudflows, rockfalls, debris flows, and avalanches; unstable slopes; erosion; slaking and heave of geological formations, including frost heaving; ground subsidence from groundwater withdrawal, sinkhole or cave collapse, decomposition of organic soils, and tectonic movement; volcanic hazards including eruptions, hot springs, pyroclastic flows, debris flows and avalanches, gas emissions, and volcanic earthquakes; rock that is non-rippable or marginally rippable, requiring heavy ripping or blasting; weak and collapsible soils and foundation bearing failures; and shallow groundwater and seepage.

Engineering geologists or geophysicists may also evaluate the excavatability, or rippability, of earth and rock to determine whether pre-blasting is needed and to assess vibration impacts during blasting.

## Soil and rock mechanics

Soil mechanics applies principles of engineering mechanics, including kinematics, dynamics, fluid mechanics, and mechanics of materials, to predict the mechanical behaviour of soils. Rock mechanics is the theoretical and applied science of the mechanical behaviour of rock and rock masses, concerned with their response to the force fields of their physical environment. Both disciplines involve the behaviour of porous media and together form the basis for solving many engineering geology problems.

## Methods and reporting

Methods include geological field mapping of structures, formations, soil units, and hazards; review of geologic literature, maps, geotechnical and environmental reports, engineering plans, aerial photographs, remote sensing data, GPS data, topographic maps, and satellite imagery; sampling and logging of earth and rock materials in drilled borings, test pits, trenches, fault trenches, and bulldozer pits; geophysical surveys such as seismic refraction, resistivity, ground-penetrating radar, magnetometer, electromagnetic, and high-resolution sub-bottom profiling; and deformation monitoring, the systematic measurement and tracking of changes in the shape or dimensions of an object under stress, performed manually or with automatic systems.

Fieldwork culminates in data analysis and a report. Common types are the engineering geologic report, the geotechnical report, the design brief, fault or seismic hazard reports, geophysical reports, and groundwater or hydrogeologic reports. A report describes objectives, methodology, references cited, tests performed, findings, and recommendations for development and detailed design. Engineering geologists also place geologic data on topographic maps, aerial photographs, geological maps, and GIS maps for use by designers and planners.
