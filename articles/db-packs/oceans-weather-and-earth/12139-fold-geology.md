# Fold (geology)

A geological fold is a stack of originally planar surfaces, typically sedimentary strata, that have been bent or curved during permanent deformation. Folds range from microscopic crinkles to mountain-sized structures, occur in soft sediments, metamorphic rocks, and some igneous rocks, and form under varied conditions of stress, pore pressure, and temperature gradient.

## Anatomy

The **hinge** is the zone of maximum curvature on a folded surface. The **limbs** are the straighter flanks on either side, meeting the next limb at the hinge zone. The **hinge line** connects points of maximum curvature and may be straight or curved. The **axial surface** joins the hinge lines of stacked folded layers; if planar, it is the **axial plane**, described by its strike and dip.

A fold generated by moving a straight line parallel to itself is **cylindrical**, and the fold axis often coincides with the hinge line. The **crest** is the highest point of the fold surface, the **trough** the lowest, and the **inflection point** on a limb is where concavity reverses.

## Shape, tightness, and symmetry

Folds may be chevron (planar limbs meeting at an angular hinge), cuspate or circular (curved limbs), or elliptical with unequal wavelength. **Tightness** is measured by the interlimb angle: gentle 180°–120°, open 120°–70°, close 70°–30°, tight 30°–0°. **Isoclinal** folds have interlimb angles of 10°–0° with essentially parallel limbs.

**Symmetrical** folds have limbs of roughly equal length. **Asymmetrical** folds have unequal limbs and an axial plane tilted relative to the original layering.

## Major types

- **Anticline**: linear, strata dip away from the axial centre, oldest beds in the core.
- **Syncline**: linear, strata dip toward the axial centre, youngest beds in the core.
- **Antiform / Synform**: same dip pattern as anticline/syncline but age relation unknown or inverted.
- **Monocline**: flat-lying strata interrupted by a single step dipping in one direction.
- **Recumbent**: axial plane at a low angle, overturning strata in one limb.
- **Dome**: nonlinear, strata dip outward in all directions, oldest in core.
- **Basin**: nonlinear, strata dip inward in all directions, youngest in core.

**Concentric** folds keep uniform layer thickness and form by active buckling. **Similar** folds thin in the limbs and thicken in the hinge and tend to form by shear flow where layers are not mechanically active.

## Causes

Layer-parallel shortening. The response depends on layer thickness and the contrast in mechanical properties between layers. Isolated thick competent layers in a weaker matrix produce rounded buckle folds. Regular alternations such as sandstone–shale sequences produce kink bands, box folds, and chevron folds.

Fault-related folding. Many folds grow directly from faults. **Fault-bend folds** form where the hanging wall deforms over a non-planar fault, producing rollover anticlines on listric normal faults and ramp anticlines on thrust ramps. **Fault-propagation folds** form at the tip of a propagating fault, often as monoclines. **Detachment folds** form above a planar detachment such as the Triassic evaporites beneath the Jura Mountains and tend to show box-fold geometry.

Shear, sediment, and intrusion. Shear zones carry asymmetric minor folds whose vergence matches shear sense; some have highly curved hinge lines and are called **sheath folds**. Soft, unlithified sediment produces **synsedimentary** folds by slumping (asymmetry records palaeoslope direction), rapid dewatering, or differential compaction. High-level igneous intrusions such as laccoliths dome and fold the overlying country rock.

## Mechanisms

Folding must accommodate deformation while preserving rock volume.

- **Flexural slip**: layer-parallel slip between beds, analogous to slip between the pages of a bent phone book. A fold produced this way from competent beds is a **flexure fold**.
- **Buckling**: simple bending of a planar layer; volume is conserved by layer-parallel shortening combined with thickening across the fold, producing the thinned-limb, thickened-hinge geometry of similar folds.
- **Mass displacement**: when slip and buckling are insufficient, material is removed from high-strain zones by pressure dissolution and redeposited in low-strain zones, generating folds with strong axial-planar cleavage, as in many migmatites.

## Mechanical controls

Fold geometry reflects the stress field at the time of deformation and the **rheology** of the layers. Rocks that deform easily develop many short-wavelength, high-amplitude folds; stiffer rocks develop long-wavelength, low-amplitude folds. Where rock flows as a fluid (deeply buried strata or rock salt), it undergoes **flow folding**, with the strata acting as passive markers of whatever shape surrounding rigid rocks impose.

## Economic importance

In the hinge zone of a fold, layers must accommodate large deformations, opening voids where water pressure drops. These voids trigger mineral deposition, and over millions of years this concentrates trace minerals into veins, which is why mining geologists target highly folded rock. In petroleum geology, a porous sandstone capped by low-permeability shale and folded into an anticline forms an **anticlinal trap**, with oil and gas accumulating at the crest.
