# Crust (geology)

In geology, the crust is the outermost solid shell of a planet, dwarf planet, or natural satellite. It is usually distinguished from the underlying mantle by its chemical makeup, though on icy satellites the distinction is by phase: a solid crust above a liquid mantle.

The crusts of Earth, Mercury, Venus, Mars, Io, the Moon, and other planetary bodies formed through igneous processes and were later reshaped by erosion, impact cratering, volcanism, and sedimentation. Most terrestrial planets have fairly uniform crusts. Earth is the exception, with two distinct kinds: continental crust and oceanic crust, which differ in composition, physical properties, and origin.

## Three categories of crust

Planetary geologists sort crust into three categories based on how and when it formed.

**Primary crust** is a planet's original skin. It crystallised from a global magma ocean as a young terrestrial planet cooled at the end of accretion. No confirmed primary crust survives on Earth. Erosion and plate-tectonic recycling have destroyed every rock older than about 4 billion years, so Earth's original crust is gone. Geologists look elsewhere: Mercury's highlands may be primary crust, and the bright anorthosite highlands of the Moon are primary crust, formed when plagioclase feldspar floated to the top of the lunar magma ocean. The lunar crust formed in a dry system while Earth had water, so the Moon is not a perfect analogue. The Martian meteorite ALH84001 may sample Mars's primary crust, but this is debated. Venus, like Earth, has been resurfaced and modified repeatedly and shows no surviving primary crust. Most primary crust was probably obliterated and re-formed many times during the Era of Heavy Bombardment.

**Secondary crust** forms by partial melting of silicate material in the mantle, so it is usually basaltic. It is the most common crust type in the Solar System. Most surfaces of Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars are secondary crust, as are the dark lunar maria. On Earth, secondary crust forms mainly at mid-ocean spreading centres, where mantle rises and melts without losing heat to the surroundings (an adiabatic rise).

**Tertiary crust** is more chemically evolved than primary or secondary. It forms either by partial melting of secondary crust followed by differentiation or dehydration, or by erosion and sedimentation of pre-existing crust. The only confirmed example is Earth's continental crust, and plate tectonics is thought to be essential for making it. Earth is the only known planet with plate tectonics, so other worlds probably lack tertiary crust.

## Earth's crust

Earth's crust is a thin shell, less than 1% of the planet's volume, sitting atop the lithosphere, a layer that includes the crust and the uppermost mantle. The lithosphere is broken into tectonic plates whose motion lets heat escape from Earth's interior to space.

## The Moon's crust

The Moon is thought to have formed when a Mars-sized protoplanet called Theia struck the young Earth and ejected debris that re-accreted. The outer Moon was briefly molten as a lunar magma ocean, from which plagioclase feldspar crystallised and floated upward. Those cumulate rocks became the crust. The upper crust averages about 88% plagioclase, near the 90% threshold that defines anorthosite; even the lower crust, which holds more pyroxene and olivine, is still about 78% plagioclase. The underlying mantle is denser and richer in olivine.

Crustal thickness on the Moon ranges from roughly 20 to 120 km, with the far side about 12 km thicker than the near side; average estimates cluster around 50–60 km. Most of this plagioclase-rich crust formed soon after the Moon itself, between about 4.5 and 4.3 billion years ago. Later additions make up no more than 10% of the crust, the largest being the mare basalts that erupted between about 3.9 and 3.2 billion years ago, with minor volcanism possibly continuing until around 1 billion years ago. The Moon shows no evidence of plate tectonics.

The Moon demonstrates that a rocky body far smaller than Earth, with a radius only about a quarter of Earth's, can still grow a thick crust almost immediately after formation. Igneous rocks younger than 3.9 billion years cover only a small fraction of the lunar surface, so most crust-building finished well before the period of intense meteorite impacts ended.
