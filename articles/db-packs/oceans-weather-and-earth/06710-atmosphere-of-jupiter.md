# Atmosphere of Jupiter

Jupiter's atmosphere is the largest planetary atmosphere in the Solar System. It is mostly molecular hydrogen (H₂) and helium in roughly solar proportions, with smaller amounts of methane, ammonia, hydrogen sulfide, and water. Nitrogen, sulfur, and the noble gases argon, krypton, and xenon sit at about three times solar abundance. The atmosphere has no sharp lower boundary: temperature and pressure stay well above the critical points of hydrogen and helium, so the gas grades smoothly into the planet's liquid interior. Hydrogen becomes a supercritical fluid above 33 K and 13 bar. Scientists use the 1 bar pressure level, about 90 km below the visible clouds, as a convenient "surface" of Jupiter, with the 10 bar level (around 340 K) often taken as the base of the troposphere.

## Vertical structure

Four layers stack from bottom to top: troposphere, stratosphere, thermosphere, exosphere; Jupiter lacks Earth's mesosphere. The troposphere cools with height to the tropopause, about 50 km above the 1 bar level, at roughly 0.1 bar and 110 K. The stratosphere then warms to about 200 K near 320 km, and the thermosphere climbs to roughly 1000 K at 1000 km. The exosphere fades into interplanetary space near 5,000 km.

The troposphere holds three cloud decks stacked by condensation temperature. Ammonia ice clouds sit at 0.6–0.9 bar. Below them, ammonium hydrosulfide or ammonium sulfide clouds form at 1–2 bar, and the densest layer, water clouds, lies at 3–7 bar. No methane clouds exist because temperatures are too high for methane to condense. Water dominates the dynamics because it is more abundant than the sulfur or nitrogen carriers and releases more heat on condensation. Hazes of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and hydrazine float in the stratosphere, where solar ultraviolet light breaks down methane.

The thermosphere hosts aurorae, X-ray emission, and the ionosphere, and was the first place outside Earth where the trihydrogen cation H₃⁺ was detected. H₃⁺ radiates strongly in the mid-infrared (2–5 μm) and is the thermosphere's main cooling mechanism. For over fifty years, models predicted thermospheric temperatures no higher than 400 K, while observations reached 800–1000 K. The "energy crisis" is now resolved: Joule heating from auroral currents, redistributed by ion drag, supplies the extra energy.

## Zones, belts, and jets

Jupiter's visible face is striped. Light zones and darker belts alternate in bands parallel to the equator, bounded by powerful east–west winds called jets. Zones are colder and correspond to upwelling ammonia-rich air that condenses into bright ice clouds at high altitude; belts are warmer descending air where the ammonia evaporates, exposing darker clouds below. The exact coloring agents are uncertain but may include sulfur, phosphorus, and carbon compounds.

Jets reach more than 100 m/s. Juno's gravity measurements show they extend thousands of kilometers into the interior, parallel to the rotation axis, consistent with the Taylor–Proudman theorem. Two competing explanations exist: a shallow model, where weather-layer turbulence and Rossby waves shape the jets, and a deep model, where the jets are the surface imprint of cylinders of flow wrapped around the planet's spin axis. Each captures some observations; neither captures them all. Galileo probe data showed winds continuing steadily down to at least 22 bar, and Juno microwave measurements reaching ~240 bar revealed large Ferrel-like cells where air rises in belts and sinks in zones at depth, the reverse of the upper atmosphere.

## Storms, vortices, and lightning

Anticyclones dominate, making up more than 90% of vortices larger than 2000 km across; cyclones tend to be smaller, darker, and confined to belts. Vortices live from days to centuries and never appear within 10° of the equator, where they are unstable.

The Great Red Spot is a giant anticyclone at 22° south, 24,000–40,000 km across, large enough to swallow two or three Earths, and has been observed for at least 350 years. It rotates counterclockwise every six Earth days, is confined by jets on its northern and southern edges, and is shrinking: it lost about 15% of its diameter along its major axis between 1996 and 2006, and at the current rate could become circular by 2040, though neighboring jets will likely distort it. A 2014 analysis of Cassini data suggests the red color forms when solar ultraviolet irradiation breaks down simple compounds in the upper atmosphere.

Oval BA formed in 2000 from the merger of three white ovals dating to the 1940s. It turned red in 2005–2006, peaked around 2008 with wind speeds of 618 km/h, and faded back to white by 2019.

Storms are short-lived convective plumes, typically 100 km tall, rising from the water-cloud level (5–7 bar) to 0.2–0.5 bar, and they always come with lightning. Jovian lightning is on average a few times more powerful than Earth's, and Juno observations place it above the water clouds. Every 15–17 years especially violent outbreaks appear at 23° N, where the strongest eastward jet (~150 m/s) sits, last observed in 2007.

The poles host striking cyclone patterns. Juno found eight cyclones in an octagon around a central cyclone at the north pole and five around the south pole; southern cyclones are larger (5,600–7,000 km radius) than northern ones (4,000–4,600 km). Their stability comes from a balance between beta-drift, which pulls cyclones toward the pole, and mutual repulsion through their vorticity fields.

## Composition and internal heat

The Galileo probe plunged into Jupiter on 7 December 1995, the only direct sampling of a giant planet's atmosphere, and measured composition, wind, temperature, and clouds down to 22 bar. It found the deep atmosphere enriched in carbon, nitrogen, sulfur, and possibly oxygen by a factor of 2–4 over solar, probably because Jupiter captured icy planetesimals during formation. Neon is severely depleted. The likely explanation is helium rain: deep inside, helium separates from metallic hydrogen and falls toward the core, dissolving neon on the way down.

Jupiter radiates about 1.67 times more heat than it receives from the Sun, a total emitted power of 335 PW from an internal flux of 5.44 W/m². Most of this is primordial heat from formation, with a possible contribution from ongoing helium rain. The deep convection that carries this heat outward also evens out temperatures from equator to pole, despite Jupiter's small 3° obliquity leaving the poles weakly lit.
