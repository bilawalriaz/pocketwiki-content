# River

A river is a natural channel of freshwater that flows downhill under gravity toward an ocean, lake, or another river. It is fed by precipitation (rainfall, snowmelt, glacier melt, and groundwater seepage) gathered in headwaters and recycled through the water cycle. As water descends, it reshapes land through erosion, supports freshwater ecosystems, and has anchored human settlement for drinking water, irrigation, transport, and power.

## Drainage basins and flow

Every river lies inside a drainage basin, the land area where all precipitation drains to a common outlet. Drainage divides, such as the Continental Divide in North America, separate adjacent basins. A few endorheic basins have no ocean outlet and lose water only by evaporation. Some rivers flow only seasonally; others run through karst systems, soluble rock formations cut by caves and fissures.

As a river descends, it erodes rock and soil and carries the loosened material, called alluvium. Coarse gravel and sand drop near the source, while fine silt travels much farther. In flat lowlands, periodic flooding spreads nutrient-rich sediment across broad floodplains. Sediment yield, the amount of material removed per unit area, is monitored as a measure of erosion and ecosystem health.

Rivers rarely stay straight. Obstacles deflect the current, which deposits sediment on the inside of a bend and cuts away the outside, producing the looping curves called meanders. Stream order, ranked by the Strahler system, classifies this complexity: a headwater stream with no tributaries is first order, two first-order streams merging produce a second-order stream, and so on. Higher order correlates with larger basin size and longer channels.

## River ecosystems

Life in a river depends on channel width, current speed, and shade from overhanging vegetation. The River Continuum Concept groups aquatic organisms into four feeding types: shredders that chew leaf litter, grazers and scrapers that eat algae, collectors that filter fine organic matter, and predators. The Flood Pulse Concept adds that seasonal flooding of the surrounding riparian zone, the vegetated strip beside the river, drives productivity because floodplain plants stabilize banks and trap nutrients. Larger rivers with greater discharge tend to support more fish species, a pattern called Fish Zonation.

Some fish depend on rivers for part of their life cycle. Anadromous species such as salmon hatch in freshwater, mature at sea, and return upriver to spawn. Catadromous species, including freshwater eels, spawn in the ocean and grow in rivers.

## Human use and engineering

Rivers have drawn settlement since the first river-valley civilizations emerged around 3500–5500 BCE in the Tigris–Euphrates, Nile, and Indus systems. Early engineers cut canals in Egypt by about 3000 BCE and built the Sadd el-Kafara dam on the Nile around 2500 BCE. Medieval Europe spread water-powered mills; a single watermill could do the work of thirty to sixty people, and by the 1300s England alone had over ten thousand. Industrialization added steam and large-scale hydropower, turning the Mississippi basin, about forty percent of the United States, into a major cargo artery. France's Canal du Midi linked river systems to create long-distance navigation routes.

Modern river engineering includes dams for hydroelectricity and reservoirs, levees for flood control, dikes for straightening channels, and channelization, the practice of cutting off meanders to shorten a river's path. The Mississippi once discharged roughly four hundred million tons of sediment per year; reservoirs, levee construction, and the replacement of natural banks with revetments, hard armoured surfaces, have cut that output by about sixty percent. The United States has at least seventy-five thousand dams higher than six feet, and globally reservoirs now cover around 501,000 square kilometres. Dam construction peaked in the 1970s, when two or three large dams were finished every day, and has since declined.

Urban rivers have suffered from impermeable paving, stormwater runoff, and historic sewage dumping, though treatment upgrades are reversing some damage. Paris's Seine, off-limits to swimmers for more than a century because of E. coli and other pollution, was cleaned up enough for open swimming in 2024.

## Politics and culture

Rivers ignore political borders. About twenty-three percent of international boundaries follow large rivers, and up to sixty percent of a nation's freshwater can originate outside its borders. Treaties such as those administered by the International Boundary and Water Commission on the Rio Grande, or the Aswan Dam agreements that secure Egypt's share of the Nile, manage these shared flows.

Rivers carry strong symbolic weight. Greek myth names the Styx and Lethe; the book of Genesis describes four rivers flowing from Eden; and flood stories recur in Gilgamesh, Norse, and Aboriginal traditions. Sacred rivers include the Ganges in Hinduism, the Nile in Egyptian religion, and the Ogun in Yoruba belief, and they remain sites of ritual bathing and cremation.

## Modern threats and rivers beyond Earth

Dams block fish migrations and trap sediment, starving downstream channels. Pollution from PFAS, fertilizers, and sewage creates oxygen-starved dead zones. Climate change shifts the timing of snowmelt, shrinks glaciers, and warms the water itself. Conservation responses include protecting wetlands, removing obsolete dams, and enforcing stricter pollutant limits.

River-like channels are not unique to this planet. Mars shows ancient valley networks and deltas dating to about 3.7 billion years ago, evidence that the planet once had enough precipitation to support a water cycle. Saturn's moon Titan has rivers and floodplains carved by liquid methane and ethane, giving geologists a biology-free laboratory for studying erosion.
