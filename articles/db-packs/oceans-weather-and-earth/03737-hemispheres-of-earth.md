# Hemispheres of Earth

A hemisphere is any division of the globe into two equal halves (half a sphere). Because a sphere has no natural front or middle, every hemisphere is a human convention for slicing the planet so regions, climates, and cultures can be compared. The two most common slices are North–South and East–West; other useful divisions exist.

## The Two Standard Geographical Hemispheres

The North–South split uses the Equator, the imaginary line of latitude at 0° circling the planet halfway between the poles. Everything above is the Northern Hemisphere; everything below is the Southern Hemisphere. This division tracks real planetary behaviour: the Equator receives the most direct sunlight year-round, so the two halves differ in seasons, day length, and prevailing winds.

The East–West split uses the Prime Meridian, the line of longitude at 0°, paired with the antimeridian at 180° on the opposite side of the globe. Everything east of the Prime Meridian (to the antimeridian) is the Eastern Hemisphere; everything west is the Western Hemisphere. Unlike the Equator, the Prime Meridian has no physical basis. It is a chosen line, fixed at Greenwich, England, by the International Meridian Conference in 1884. The idea of a spherical Earth dates to early civilisations around 3000 BC and became measurable when Eratosthenes calculated Earth's circumference in the 3rd century BC.

## Continents and Oceans Across the Halves

Continents and oceans are not distributed evenly. Africa and Europe sit mostly in the Northern Hemisphere; South America sits mostly in the Southern. The Pacific Ocean, the largest ocean, spans both the Eastern and Western Hemispheres because it is wide enough to cross the 180° line. This unevenness is why alternative schemes exist.

## Alternative Hemispheres

Geographers sometimes rotate the dividing plane to maximise one feature. The Land Hemisphere, centred near 47°N, 1°E (close to Nantes, France), encloses the largest possible area of land on Earth. Its mirror, the Water Hemisphere, centred near 47°S, 179°W in the South Pacific, encloses the largest possible area of water. The two are complementary: any point in one is mirrored by a water- or land-dominated point in the other.

Hemispheres can also be drawn along cultural or religious lines, such as the historic "Christian world" and "Islamic world." These cultural hemispheres do not coincide with the geographical ones.

## Limits of the Concept

The East–West split is a cartographic convention, not a natural fact. No single meridian is objectively correct, which is why the Prime Meridian's location was a diplomatic decision in 1884. Socioeconomic labels like the "Global North" and "Global South" describe wealth and politics rather than latitude, and they do not align with the geographical hemispheres.
