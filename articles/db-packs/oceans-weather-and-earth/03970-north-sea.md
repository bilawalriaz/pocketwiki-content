# North Sea

The North Sea is a shallow sea on the European continental shelf, bordered by the UK, Norway, Denmark, Germany, the Netherlands, Belgium, and France. Over 970 km long and 580 km wide, with a mean depth of 90 metres, it connects to the Atlantic through the English Channel and to the Norwegian Sea to the north. The seabed lies mostly in shallow shelf waters, except where the Norwegian trench cuts a deeper channel along the coast of Norway. Its shallowest large feature, the Dogger Bank, has long been one of the world's richest fishing grounds.

Atlantic inflow drives an anti-clockwise circulation, supplying relatively warm, salty water (salinity 34–35 g/L) that shifts the sea between average summer temperatures of 17°C and winter temperatures of 6°C. The combination of shallow water, productive fisheries, navigable routes to the Atlantic, and underlying energy reserves has made the North Sea one of the most economically and strategically exploited seas on Earth.

## A Repeatedly Drowned Landscape

The North Sea basin has existed for millions of years, but its outline has changed repeatedly with sea level. During glacial periods, much of the basin was dry land, with a region known as Doggerland connecting Britain to continental Europe. Around 8150–6000 BCE, the Storegga Slides off Norway triggered a major tsunami that flooded parts of this landscape. Later, the Romans expanded North Sea shipping after their 43 CE conquest of Britain, and from 793 the Vikings used the sea for raiding and trade for roughly 250 years. Throughout the medieval and early modern periods, control of North Sea trade routes was repeatedly contested: by the Hanseatic League from the 13th century, by the Dutch Republic, and by Britain, with conflicts like the Anglo-Dutch Wars (1652–1673) fought explicitly over that commerce. The sea remained a major naval theatre through both World Wars.

The coastlines on either side differ sharply and demand different defences. Norway's coast is carved by deep fjords cut by glaciers, while southern coasts from the Netherlands to Denmark are low-lying, marshy, and in places below sea level. Roughly 27% of the Netherlands lies below sea level, a vulnerability that has driven centuries of coastal engineering. Communities built artificial dwelling hills (terps) as early as 500 BC, and connected dike lines emerged around 1200 AD. The cost of failure is severe: a 1228 storm surge killed over 100,000 people, and the 1953 North Sea Flood killed more than 2,000, prompting major modern upgrades in dikes, surge barriers, and dune stabilisation.

## Living Resources and Industrial Pressure

The sea's shallow, well-mixed waters sustain more than 230 fish species, including the commercially dominant cod, herring, and mackerel, alongside seals, porpoises, and millions of seabirds. Heavy industrialisation, however, has degraded this ecosystem. The North Sea once hosted grey whales and walruses, both now locally extinct, and decades of trawling, pollution, and nutrient runoff have driven widespread eutrophication (excess nutrient loading that starves deeper water of oxygen) and habitat loss. International agreements, notably the OSPAR Convention and the MARPOL Accords, set joint rules for pollution control and habitat protection among the bordering states.

## Energy: From Oil to Wind

The modern economic history of the North Sea is dominated by hydrocarbons. The 1969 discovery of the Ekofisk oil field off Norway opened large-scale offshore production, later joined by giant fields such as Troll. Oil pumped from the North Sea is priced against Brent Crude, a benchmark that influences global energy markets. Alongside oil and gas, the seabed carries roughly 71,000 km of subsea cables and dense networks of pipelines, a critical infrastructure whose disruption (through anchors, fishing gear, or sabotage) would affect both energy supply and telecommunications.

Since the 1990s the sea has also become a global leader in offshore wind. The 2002 completion of Horns Rev 1, one of the world's first large-scale offshore wind farms, marked the start of a build-out that now includes very large arrays such as the London Array, with the planned Dogger Bank wind farm set among the largest anywhere. Ports like Rotterdam, on its southeastern edge, rank among the world's busiest, and fishing still contributes over 5% of international commercial catch, though overfishing has thinned many stocks.

The same shallow waters that made the North Sea easy to fish, drain, and drill also make it physically vulnerable. Storm surges still threaten densely populated lowlands, and the dense web of cables, pipelines, and wind installations is increasingly recognised as a security liability in a region where political tensions can spill into undersea infrastructure.
