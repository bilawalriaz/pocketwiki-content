# Atlantic Ocean

The Atlantic is the world's second-largest ocean, covering about 85.1 million km² (roughly 17% of Earth's surface, 24% of its water area). Including its marginal seas, it reaches 106.5 million km². It separates the Americas from Europe and Africa, has an average depth of 3,646 m, and reaches a maximum of 8,376 m in the Milwaukee Deep of the Puerto Rico Trench. Its two signature features, the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and the Gulf Stream system, explain its geology and its climate.

## How the ocean formed

The Atlantic opened when the supercontinent Pangaea broke apart. Around 200 million years ago, the Central Atlantic Magmatic Province (CAMP) erupted over roughly 4.5 million km², an event linked to the Triassic–Jurassic mass extinction, and tore a rift between what is now North America and northwest Africa. The South Atlantic opened in the Early Cretaceous, when South America split from Africa. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge (MAR), a submarine mountain range running from 87°N to about 54°S, marks the seam where the North American, South American, Eurasian, and African plates are still pulling apart. It rises 2–3 km above the surrounding seafloor, and its rift valley is the divergent boundary between those plates. Discoveries along this ridge in the 1950s gave geologists the evidence for seafloor spreading and plate tectonics.

Around 2.8 million years ago, the Central American Isthmus closed the Central American Seaway, separating Atlantic and Pacific circulation and changing salinity and currents on both sides.

## Water, salt, and circulation

Surface temperatures range from below −2°C near the poles to over 30°C near the equator. Surface salinity spans 33 to 37 parts per thousand, making the Atlantic the saltiest of the major oceans. Salinity peaks around 25° north and south, where evaporation dominates, and drops near the poles and major river mouths.

The Coriolis effect, the apparent deflection of moving water caused by Earth's rotation, drives North Atlantic surface water clockwise and South Atlantic surface water counter-clockwise, forming the North Atlantic and South Atlantic Gyres. Within the North Atlantic Gyre lies the Sargasso Sea, a 4,000-km-wide region in the western North Atlantic bounded by the Gulf Stream, the North Atlantic Drift, and the North Equatorial Current. It is the only sea defined not by land but by floating Sargassum seaweed, and it is the spawning ground for European and American eels, which migrate more than 5,000 km and 2,000 km respectively to reach it.

The Atlantic's deep circulation is what sets the climate of Europe apart. Warm, salty surface water flows north, cools, becomes dense, and sinks in the far North Atlantic, forming North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW). This sinking powers the Atlantic branch of the global meridional overturning circulation (MOC), a planet-scale conveyor that redistributes heat, salt, and carbon. The Gulf Stream carries warm water north-east from Cape Hatteras, and its continuation, the North Atlantic Current, releases that heat into the atmosphere. Without this transport, European winters would be far colder, and Europe would sit at the same latitude as cold eastern Canada.

## Climate and hazards

The ocean moderates nearby land: the Gulf Stream softens winters in southeastern North America, while the North Atlantic Drift keeps the British Isles and northwestern Europe mild and cloudy. Cold currents along eastern Canada and northwestern Africa produce heavy fog. The Icelandic Low drives frequent winter storms. Icebergs drift into shipping lanes near the Grand Banks of Newfoundland from February through July. Hurricanes form in the western North Atlantic each summer and autumn; the South Atlantic rarely sees tropical cyclones because of strong wind shear and a weak Intertropical Convergence Zone.

## Human history

The first clear "Atlantic" references come from the Greek poet Stesichorus in the mid-6th century BC and from Herodotus around 450 BC, who described "the sea beyond the pillars of Hercules" (the Strait of Gibraltar), named for the Titan Atlas.

Genetic evidence places a major African demographic expansion at 80,000–60,000 years ago, with dispersal out of Africa around 65,000 years ago. During the Last Glacial Maximum about 20,000 years ago, people abandoned the North Atlantic coasts of Europe and retreated to the Mediterranean, returning permanently only around 9,000–8,500 years ago. Norse settlers reached the Faroe Islands and Iceland in the 9th and 10th centuries, and Greenland before 1000 CE; the Greenland colony was lost by 1409 during the early Little Ice Age, with cold, conflict with Inuit, and economic failure as causes.

The ocean's modern human role began with Columbus's 1492 voyage under the Spanish flag, followed in 1497 by John Cabot's discovery of abundant cod off Newfoundland, and in 1500 by Pedro Álvares Cabral's Brazil landfall, made possible by the South Atlantic Gyre. Vasco da Gama reached India in 1498 via the Atlantic, proving the Atlantic and Indian Oceans connect. The trans-Atlantic slave trade moved an estimated 9.5 million enslaved Africans to the Americas between the 15th century and 1888. Trans-Atlantic commerce from 1500 to 1800 shifted wealth toward Atlantic-facing European states, with their urbanisation rising from about 8% in 1300 to 24.5% in 1850. By the late 17th century, Atlantic trade volume had overtaken Mediterranean trade.

## Resources and pressures

The Atlantic yields petroleum, fish, marine mammals, sand, gravel, placer deposits, polymetallic nodules, and stones. The Grand Banks, Georges Bank, and Icelandic waters are the most productive fisheries. The Northwest Atlantic cod fishery collapsed in the early 1990s, after overfishing was recognised in the 1960s but regulated only from the late 1970s. Today roughly 21% of stocks in the Northeast Atlantic and 31% in the Northwest Atlantic are overfished.

Endangered species include manatees, seals, sea lions, turtles, and whales. Drift-net fishing kills dolphins, albatrosses, and seabirds. River-borne fertiliser creates low-oxygen dead zones. A North Atlantic garbage patch spans hundreds of kilometres. The Atlantic meridional overturning circulation slowed by about 30% from 1957 to 2004, and weakened by roughly 12% over the two decades to 2024; warming North Atlantic surface water has also intensified hurricanes, and thermal expansion of Atlantic water contributes to sea-level rise. Open questions remain over the exact date of the central Atlantic's opening (estimates range from 200 to 170 Ma), the settlement of the Americas, and whether the MOC will continue to weaken.
