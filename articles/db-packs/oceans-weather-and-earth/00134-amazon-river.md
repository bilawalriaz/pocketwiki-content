# Amazon River

The Amazon is the largest river on Earth by the volume of water it carries into the ocean, delivering roughly 20% of all global riverine freshwater at an average discharge of about 215,000–230,000 cubic metres per second. Its drainage basin spans around 7 million square kilometres, covers about 40% of South America, and supports more than 10% of all known species. Whether it is also the longest river is disputed with the Nile, and the dispute cannot be settled by measurement alone because the answer depends on which headstream counts as the source, where the mouth is drawn, and how length is calculated.

## How the river came to flow the other way

For tens of millions of years the proto-Amazon drained westward into the Pacific while Africa and South America were joined in Gondwana. The continents split around 80 million years ago, and the Andes began rising about 15 million years ago as the Nazca Plate subducted beneath the South American Plate. The new mountain belt blocked the westward flow and trapped water inland, creating a vast swampy lake. Between roughly 11 and 10 million years ago the trapped water cut eastward through soft sandstone, and the Amazon began flowing toward the Atlantic. The rainforest spread across the basin as the river reached its present shape around 2.4 million years ago. The current is so large that no bridge spans the main channel.

## A continental plumbing system

The name "Amazon" applies only to the section below the Rio Negro confluence near Manaus; the upper Brazilian stretch is called the Solimões. Above that, the Apurímac joins the Mantaro to form the Ene, then the Tambo, then the Ucayali, which meets the Marañón to begin the Amazon proper. The most distant water source was long thought to be Nevado Mismi (5,597 m) in the Apurímac drainage; a 2014 study identified Cordillera Rumi Cruz in the Mantaro drainage instead, about 80 km farther upstream, a choice that directly changes the river's measured length.

The basin behaves as a network. The river surface rises more than 9 m between seasons, swelling the flooded area from about 110,000 km² in the dry season to 350,000 km² in the wet season. At the "Meeting of Waters" near Manaus, the dark Rio Negro and the sandy Solimões run side by side for over 6 km without mixing. The estuary is unusually wide and poorly defined: counting the Pará distributary and Marajó island frontage it stretches about 325 km, while the main channel is only about 15 km. The freshwater plume can cover up to 1.3 million km² of the tropical North Atlantic, measurably lowering salinity and pH far offshore. A tidal bore called the pororoca can reach 7.6 m high and travel 800 km inland, and tributaries like the Madeira flood on their own schedules, often peaking two months before the main stem. Ocean freighters reach Manaus 1,500 km inland, and smaller vessels push 3,600 km upriver to Iquitos.

## People before and after European contact

By 1541, when Europeans first descended the river, archaeologists estimate more than 3 million indigenous people lived in the basin. Pre-Columbian inhabitants altered the landscape deliberately, building terra preta de índio, dark, nutrient-rich soil created by controlled burning, charcoal, and continuous cultivation, and using it for large-scale agriculture; some evidence dates the practice to around 11,000 years ago. Major settlements sat on shell mounds, artificial earth platforms, and figurative mounds, and Marajó island alone may have supported 100,000 people with social stratification.

Francisco de Orellana's 1541–1542 descent named the river after women warriors his crew fought, whom he compared to the Amazons of Greek myth. The 19th-century rubber boom turned Iquitos from a hamlet of 200 in 1851 to a city of 20,000 by 1900, while typhus and malaria killed an estimated 40,000 native Amazonians. The Cabanagem revolt (1835–1840) cost 30–40% of Grão-Pará's roughly 100,000 people. The 1970s Trans-Amazonian Highway relocated tens of thousands of northeastern families onto rainforest land they could not farm, and most colonists abandoned their plots within a decade. Manaus now holds about 1.9 million people and is the largest city on the river, but the main stem itself remains undammed, though roughly 412 dams operate on tributaries, and ecologists warn that further damming could block spawning migrations and collapse the riverine ecosystem.

## A biological engine

The basin holds more than 5,600 fish species as of 2011, with about 50 new ones described each year, including the arapaima (up to 4.6 m), piranhas, parasitic candirú catfish, electric eels, and the goliath catfish (up to 3.6 m, 200 kg). The boto, or Amazon river dolphin, grows to 2.6 m, shifts from grey to pink to white with age, and hunts by echolocation. Bull sharks, able to tolerate a wide range of salinity, have been reported 4,000 km upriver at Iquitos, far from the sea. The basin also contains over 100 electric fish species and microbial communities dominated by Actinomycetota and several classes of Proteobacteria.

## Why the numbers keep changing

The length question turns on measurement choices: a 2008 Brazilian study claimed 6,992 km for the Amazon versus 6,853 km for the Nile, but was never peer-reviewed, while a 2009 peer-reviewed study found the Nile longer at 7,088 km versus the Amazon's 6,575 km. The mouth's width depends on whether the Pará distributary is counted, and the most distant source depends on which tributary headstream is followed. What stays constant is the underlying physical reality: a river of extraordinary discharge, a basin of extraordinary size, and an ecosystem whose scale has no equal on the planet.
