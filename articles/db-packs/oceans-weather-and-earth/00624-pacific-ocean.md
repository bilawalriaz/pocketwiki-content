# Pacific Ocean

The Pacific is Earth's largest and deepest ocean, holding about half of all oceanic water and covering more area than every landmass combined. Its mean depth is 4,280 metres, the Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench reaches 10,928 metres (the deepest point on Earth), and the Ring of Fire along its margins generates most of the world's earthquakes, volcanoes, and tsunamis.

## A younger ocean than the name suggests

The Pacific formed around 750 million years ago when the supercontinent Rodinia broke apart, surrounding itself in a global ocean called Panthalassa. When Pangea split about 200 million years later, the Pacific as we know it emerged. The oldest surviving Pacific floor is only about 180 million years old because surrounding subduction zones (where one plate slides beneath another and sinks into the mantle) have consumed everything older. The ocean still shrinks by roughly 2.5 centimetres per year as the Atlantic grows.

## Shape, size, and the internal boundary

The Pacific stretches about 15,600 kilometres north-to-south and 19,800 kilometres east-to-west near the equator, centred near 4°58′S 158°45′W in eastern Kiribati. It holds around 710 million cubic kilometres of water (50.1 percent of global oceanic volume). The equator divides it into North and South Pacific, each with its own large rotating current system (gyre), clockwise in the north and counter-clockwise in the south, driven by the Coriolis effect (the deflection of moving water to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere caused by Earth's rotation). The International Date Line further splits east from west, producing four quadrants.

The andesite line is the most important internal boundary: a petrologic border where the dark, basalt-like (mafic) rock of the deep Central Pacific Basin meets the lighter, silica-rich (felsic) rock of the continental margins. Inside the line lie deep trenches, seamounts (underwater mountains that do not reach the surface), and volcanic island chains such as the Hawaiian–Emperor and Louisville Ridge. Outside lies the explosive volcanism of the Ring of Fire.

## Water and weather

Surface temperatures range from about −1.4 °C near the poles to 30 °C at the equator. Salinity peaks at 37 parts per thousand in the southeast, falls to 34 at the equator (heavy rainfall dilutes the surface), and drops below 32 in the far north where evaporation is low. The North Equatorial Current near 15°N flows west, becoming the warm Kuroshio Current near the Philippines and splitting near 45°N into the Aleutian Current to the north and the southward California Current. In the south, the South Equatorial Current bends around New Guinea, feeds the Antarctic Circumpolar Current near 50°S, and re-enters the tropics along South America as the cold Humboldt Current.

The El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO) drives much of the Pacific's climate variability. A shift of more than 0.5 °C above or below average sea-surface temperature in the Niño 3.4 region, about 3,000 km southeast of Hawaiʻi, shifts tropical rainfall, storm tracks, and fisheries across half the planet. Tropical cyclones peak in September, with the northwestern and eastern Pacific being the two most active basins on Earth.

## Human settlement in waves

Modern humans reached the western Pacific 60,000–70,000 years ago via a southern coastal route out of Africa, crossing at least 80 km of open water to reach Sahul (the joined Australia–New Guinea landmass) on bamboo rafts. Seafaring is confirmed by the settlement of Buka around 32,000 years ago (a 180 km voyage) and Manus around 25,000 years ago (230 km).

The Neolithic Austronesian expansion began from Taiwan around 3000–1500 BCE, using outrigger boats, catamarans, lashed-lug construction (planks tied to a frame rather than sewn or nailed), and the crab-claw sail, a triangular rig on curved spars that allowed efficient travel against the wind. Austronesians settled the Philippines by about 2200 BCE, reached the Marianas, Palau, and Yap by 1500 BCE (the first humans in Remote Oceania), and later spread west to Madagascar by about 500 CE. The Lapita branch (1300–1200 BCE) pushed through the Bismarck Archipelago to Fiji, New Caledonia, Tonga, and Samoa. A second surge between 700 and 1200 CE settled the Cook Islands, Tahiti, the Marquesas, Hawaiʻi, Rapa Nui, and New Zealand. Whether Austronesians reached the Americas remains unresolved.

## European contact and the modern Pacific

Vasco Núñez de Balboa sighted the "South Sea" from Panama in 1513, and Ferdinand Magellan's expedition crossed it in 1520, naming it "Pacific" for its deceptive calm; Juan Sebastián Elcano completed the first circumnavigation in 1522. Spain operated the Manila galleon route between Manila and Acapulco from 1565 to 1815, treating the Pacific as a *mare clausum* (a legally "closed sea" under exclusive Spanish control). Dutch explorers Willem Janszoon (1606) and Abel Tasman (1642) charted Australia and New Zealand, and later Russian, French, and British expeditions completed European reconnaissance.

By 1900, European powers, the United States, Japan, and Chile had occupied nearly every Pacific island. The 1921 Washington Naval Treaty ended the Anglo-Japanese Alliance and withdrew the Royal Navy from the South Pacific, beginning a century of United States maritime dominance that the Second World War cemented. Decolonisation after 1945 produced the modern island states.

## Resources

Petroleum and natural gas come from the continental shelves off Australia and New Zealand. Pearls are harvested off Australia, Japan, Papua New Guinea, Nicaragua, Panama, and the Philippines, though volumes have fallen. Fisheries yield herring, salmon, sardines, snapper, swordfish, tuna, and shellfish, but overfishing has halved catches in places like the Sea of Okhotsk since the 1990s. Deep-sea mining now targets manganese nodules rich in magnesium, nickel, copper, zinc, and cobalt in the Clarion–Clipperton fracture zone between Mexico and Hawaiʻi, though the risk of irreversible damage to poorly understood abyssal ecosystems (the ocean floor at 3,000–6,000 m depth) remains unresolved.

## Environmental pressures

Microplastics concentrate heavily in the Northwestern Pacific near Japan and China, and northeast Pacific fragments increased roughly a hundredfold between 1972 and 2012. The Great Pacific Garbage Patch between California and Japan covers an area about three times the size of France and holds an estimated 80,000 metric tons, or 1.8 trillion pieces, of plastic. Agricultural runoff creates oxygen-starved dead zones, and spacecraft such as Mars 96, Fobos-Grunt, and UARS have been deliberately crashed in remote parts of the ocean. The United States detonated 67 nuclear weapons in the Marshall Islands between 1946 and 1958, a one-megaton bomb was lost in the 1965 Philippine Sea A-4 incident, and the 2011–2013 Fukushima leak released 20–40 trillion becquerels of tritium (a radioactive form of hydrogen), prompting a 2021 Japanese decision to discharge treated wastewater over 30 years.
