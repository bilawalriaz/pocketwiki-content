# Plate tectonics

Plate tectonics is the theory that Earth's rigid outer shell, the **lithosphere**, is broken into plates that move slowly over the weaker, hotter layer beneath, the **asthenosphere**. The lithosphere includes the crust and uppermost mantle and transfers heat by conduction. The asthenosphere is ductile and flows by convection, the heat-driven circulation in which hot material rises and cool material sinks. The boundary between the two is set by temperature, not chemistry: a given volume of mantle can shift between lithosphere and asthenosphere as it heats or cools. Oceanic lithosphere averages about 100 km thick, thinning to roughly 6 km at mid-ocean ridges and thickening past 100 km near subduction zones. Continental lithosphere is thicker, around 200 km on average, and more variable.

## Plate composition

Plates carry two kinds of crust. **Oceanic crust**, sometimes called *sima*, is dense and basaltic, rich in iron and magnesium, and forms at spreading centers. **Continental crust**, or *sial*, is lighter and granitic, rich in silicon and aluminium, built up through arc volcanism and the accretion of terranes (small crustal fragments welded onto a continent's edge). The density contrast keeps buoyant continents above sea level while dense ocean floor sits low, a balance called **isostasy**, the gravitational equilibrium between crustal columns of different density and thickness.

## Plate boundaries

Plates interact at three kinds of boundaries.

**Divergent (constructive)** boundaries form where plates pull apart. Rising asthenosphere melts, erupts as basalt, and creates new oceanic crust at mid-ocean ridges such as the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. On land, the same process splits continents, as the East African Rift is doing today.

**Convergent (destructive)** boundaries form where plates collide. When dense oceanic lithosphere meets lighter continental or younger oceanic lithosphere, the dense slab sinks into the mantle in **subduction**, producing deep-ocean trenches, earthquake-generating **Wadati–Benioff zones** (planes of seismicity tracing the descending slab at 40–60°), and volcanic arcs such as the Andes. When two oceanic plates meet, the older, colder one subducts, building island arcs like the Marianas. When two continents collide, neither can sink, so the crust crumples upward into ranges like the Himalayas, which rose from the closure of the Tethys Ocean as India drove into Asia.

**Transform (conservative)** boundaries form where plates slide horizontally past each other. Along faults like the San Andreas, the motion is strike-slip, with little vertical movement, conserving crust but releasing strain as earthquakes.

## Why the plates move

The energy source is heat escaping from Earth's interior. The dominant force is **slab pull**: a cold, dense subducting slab sinks under its own weight and drags the rest of its plate along. **Ridge push** is a secondary effect, the gravitational slide of new lithosphere away from the elevated topography of a mid-ocean ridge. Mantle convection contributes through **basal drag** (friction between flowing asthenosphere and the plate base) and **slab suction** (flow drawn toward sinking slabs). Plates attached to subducting slabs, such as the Pacific, move fastest, up to about 160 mm/year, while plates welded to continents, like North America and Africa, move at 10–40 mm/year. The relative importance of these forces, and whether lunar tidal drag adds a small westward push, remains debated.

## How the theory came together

Alfred Wegener assembled fossil and geological evidence for "continental drift" between 1912 and 1915 but could not supply a convincing mechanism, so most geologists rejected the idea. Arthur Holmes suggested in 1928 that mantle convection might drive the continents, but proof was lacking. The breakthrough came in the 1950s and 1960s. Marine surveys mapped a continuous mid-ocean ridge system with thin basaltic crust and symmetrical magnetic stripes. In 1962 Harry Hess proposed **seafloor spreading**, the idea that new crust forms at ridges and moves outward, and in 1963 Vine, Matthews, and Morley showed that the magnetic stripes recorded geomagnetic reversals captured in cooling rock. Tuzo Wilson added transform faults in 1965, and in 1967 W. Jason Morgan and Xavier Le Pichon independently described the geometry of rigid plates, completing the revolution. Since the 1990s, GPS satellites have measured plate motions directly.

## Supercontinents and the Wilson Cycle

The same process that opens oceans eventually closes them, a loop known as the **Wilson Cycle**. Columbia (or Nuna) existed around 2.0–1.8 billion years ago, Rodinia around 1 billion years ago, and Pangaea around 200 million years ago, which split into Laurasia in the north and Gondwana in the south. Pangaea's breakup opened the Atlantic and set India on its collision course with Asia.

## Earth's plates today

Seven or eight major plates carry the continents and oceans: African, Antarctic, Eurasian, Indo-Australian (sometimes split into Indian and Australian), North American, South American, and Pacific. Smaller plates include the Arabian, Caribbean, Cocos, Juan de Fuca, Nazca, Philippine Sea, Scotia, and Somali. Modern surveys also identify roughly 1,200 terranes, smaller crustal blocks that reveal how fragmented the major plates are at finer scales.

## Tectonics beyond Earth

Plate tectonics appears to be rare elsewhere. Venus shows no current plate motion, and its surface age of 500–750 million years hints at catastrophic global resurfacing; the absence of liquid water may prevent the shear-zone weakening, a loss of rock strength along narrow bands of deformation, needed for subduction. Mars has an ancient crustal dichotomy but no active plates; its magnetic stripes fail the reversal test, showing patterns that do not mirror symmetrically about a ridge, so they were not produced by seafloor spreading. Jupiter's moon Europa shows an ice shell that fractures and shifts in tectonic-like patterns. Whether exoplanets sustain plate tectonics depends on their mass, water content, and internal heat.
