# Earthquake preparedness

Earthquake preparedness is the set of measures taken before an earthquake to reduce death, injury, and damage. Preparations fall into two broad kinds: survival measures, such as stocking food and water and learning what to do during shaking, and mitigating measures that reduce the earthquake's destructive reach, such as anchoring bookcases, cabinets, and screens so they cannot topple onto people. Avoiding storage above beds and sofas reduces the same falling-object risk. Planning for a tsunami that may follow a coastal quake is also part of preparedness, because the wave often causes more damage than the shaking that triggered it.

## Building design and retrofitting

Seismic codes in earthquake-prone regions specify requirements for new construction, and older buildings that fall short of code can be modified through a process called seismic retrofit. The same techniques apply to elevated freeways and bridges. The goal is not a building that suffers zero damage; codes aim to prevent collapse and protect occupants, and limited structural damage is treated as an accepted trade-off. Modern codes and modification techniques are designed to prevent total destruction of buildings for earthquakes up to 8.5 on the Richter scale, though localised shaking intensity, how violently the ground moves at a specific site, often matters more for a given structure than the quake's overall magnitude.

## Levels of preparedness

Preparedness runs along a continuum from the individual through family, community, business, non-profit, and government. FEMA models this as a pyramid: citizens form the foundation, supporting local, state, and federal government above them, though some organisations blur the levels. Businesses are encouraged to maintain a Disaster Recovery Plan, and the US Small Business Administration publishes worksheets aimed at small-business owners.

Several groups need tailored planning. Children may be frightened, confused, and insecure, whether they directly experience the event, see it happen to a friend, or only encounter it on television. People with disabilities may need copies of prescriptions, charging equipment for motorised wheelchairs, and roughly a week's supply of medication. Preparedness also covers pets and the psychological wellbeing of both survivors and disaster workers. A multi-hazard approach, preparing communities for several kinds of disaster at once, has gained favour because it produces more resilient communities than single-hazard plans.

## Utilities, institutions, and household shut-offs

Long power outages can cause damage that exceeds the original earthquake. Emergency generators and other emergency power systems reduce this risk, and the US Department of Energy notes that homeowners, business owners, and local leaders may need to handle energy disruptions on their own. Major institutions such as hospitals, military bases, and schools typically maintain extensive backup power; the Department of Health and Human Services highlights hospital-specific needs including safe temperatures, electricity for life-support systems, and evacuation under extreme conditions.

Natural gas leaks pose an explosion risk after a quake. Ready.gov advises that every household member know how to shut off the gas and that property owners keep any special tool required for their connection. Electricity should be shut off circuit by circuit before the main, and the main water valve should be known to all responsible household members, with the understanding that a rusty valve may need replacement.

## Achieving preparedness

Levels of preparedness generally remain low, despite attempts to increase public awareness. Many promotion methods exist but are poorly documented and rarely tested for effectiveness. Hands-on training, drills, and face-to-face interaction have proved more successful at changing behaviour than passive campaigns, and digital methods, including educational videogames, have also been used.
