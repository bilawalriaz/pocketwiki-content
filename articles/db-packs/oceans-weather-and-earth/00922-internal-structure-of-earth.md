# Internal structure of Earth

Earth is layered from surface to centre into chemically and mechanically distinct shells that differ in composition, pressure, and flow behaviour. Because no one has sampled below a few kilometres, this structure is inferred from seismic waves (vibrations from earthquakes that bend and reflect at boundaries inside the planet), from gravity and magnetic field measurements, and from high-pressure laboratory experiments. These layers explain plate tectonics, the geomagnetic field, and the early separation of dense metal from lighter rock.

## Global properties and the two classification schemes

Earth's mass is about 6×10²⁴ kg and its average density 5.515 g/cm³, both derived from gravitational and satellite orbital measurements. Layers can be sorted in two ways. *Compositionally*, Earth is divided into the silicate crust, the iron–magnesium mantle, and the iron–nickel core. *Mechanically*, it is divided into the rigid lithosphere, the ductile asthenosphere, the mesosphere (the stiffer lower mantle), the liquid outer core, and the solid inner core. The two schemes overlap only partly, so they are kept separate.

## Crust and lithosphere

The crust ranges from 5 km thick beneath the oceans to 70 km beneath continents. Oceanic crust is dense and *mafic* (rich in magnesium and iron-bearing minerals), while continental crust is less dense and *felsic* (rich in light minerals such as those in granite). A traditional shorthand labels silica-rich continental-type rock as *sial* (aluminium silicates) and silica-poor oceanic-type rock as *sima* (magnesium silicates). The base of the crust is the Mohorovičić discontinuity, or *Moho*, where seismic wave speeds jump from basalt-like to peridotite-like values, signalling a density change from crust to mantle. The oldest crustal minerals are about 4.4 billion years old, fixing when a solid crust had formed.

## Mantle

The mantle reaches 2,890 km depth and makes up 83.7% of Earth's volume. It is split by a transition zone into an upper and lower mantle, with a thin D″ layer sitting directly on the core–mantle boundary. Although solid, mantle rock deforms and creeps over geological time because of extreme heat and pressure, and slow convection in the mantle drives the motion of tectonic plates. Heat comes from the radioactive decay of uranium, thorium, and potassium, and from residual heat left over from Earth's formation. Mantle viscosity rises sharply with depth, from roughly 10²¹ pascal-seconds in the upper mantle to 10²⁴ pascal-seconds deeper down.

## Core

The outer core is liquid and extends from 2,890 km to 5,150 km depth. It is mainly iron with some nickel and lighter elements. Its motion generates Earth's magnetic field: convection in the electrically conductive fluid, twisted by the Coriolis effect from Earth's rotation, acts as a self-sustaining *geodynamo*. The field would not exist without this fluid motion, and the inner core helps stabilise it but cannot itself hold permanent magnetisation because temperatures there exceed the Curie point, the temperature above which a material loses permanent magnetism.

Beneath the outer core lies the solid inner core, discovered in 1936 by Danish seismologist Inge Lehmann, who noticed that some P-waves arrived in a shadow zone, a region on the opposite side of Earth where compressional waves from an earthquake should not arrive but do, and inferred a solid inner region inside the liquid core. Its radius is about 1,220 km. Diamond-anvil experiments suggest it contains large iron crystals aligned roughly north–south, though whether it behaves as a true solid or a dense plasma remains debated. The core formed early through *planetary differentiation*, the sinking of dense molten metal as the young Earth heated and separated, and full core solidification is estimated to be roughly 91 billion years away.

## How the layers are detected

Seismic waves are the main probe. *P-waves* (compressional waves) travel through solids and liquids by squeezing and stretching material along their direction of travel, while *S-waves* (shear waves) shake material side to side and cannot pass through liquid. The S-wave shadow on the side of Earth opposite an earthquake reveals the liquid outer core, and the way P-waves refract and reflect at each boundary, governed by Snell's law, maps the depths of the crust, Moho, mantle transitions, and core.
