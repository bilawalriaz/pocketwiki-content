# Brahmaputra River

The Brahmaputra is a trans-boundary river that crosses China, India, and Bangladesh. It ranks as the world's 9th largest river by discharge (the volume of water passing a point per second) and 15th longest. It cuts through the Himalayas in deep gorges and supports roughly 130 million people in its delta through irrigation, transportation, and the fertile sediment its annual floods deposit. It is sacred to Hindus, and unusually for a river, carries a masculine name meaning "son of Brahma."

## Course and Names

The river takes a different name in each country. In Tibet it begins as the Yarlung Tsangpo, rising on the Angsi Glacier near Mount Kailash. It flows east for about 1,100 km, then plunges through the Himalayas in the Yarlung Tsangpo Grand Canyon, one of the deepest gorges on Earth. Entering India's Arunachal Pradesh, it is called the Siang or Dihang, then becomes the Brahmaputra in Assam. In Bangladesh it is known as the Jamuna, where it merges with the Ganges (Padma) and finally the Meghna before emptying into the Bay of Bengal. The 1884–1886 exploration established the link between the Yarlung Tsangpo and the river's lower course.

A key geological fact explains why the river cuts directly through the rising Himalayas rather than flowing around them. It is an **antecedent river**, meaning it existed before the mountains uplifted and kept its original course by downcutting as the land rose, so the canyon sits deeper than the surrounding peaks.

## Hydrology and Floods

The Brahmaputra discharges an average of about 22,000 m³/s, with flood peaks reaching 103,000 m³/s. Its flow has two high-water seasons: an early summer peak from Himalayan snowmelt, and a later, larger peak from summer monsoon rains. Because the basin sits in one of the world's wettest monsoon belts, the river is prone to catastrophic flooding. The 1998 flood inundated more than 70% of Bangladesh and affected 31 million people. Deforestation in the watershed has worsened the problem by increasing siltation (the build-up of fine sediment in the channel) and flash floods.

## Channel Movement and Engineering

The Brahmaputra is a classic **braided river**, splitting into many interwoven channels separated by temporary sand and gravel bars. A major **avulsion** (a sudden abandonment of one channel for a new one) between 1776 and 1850 shifted the main course 80 km west in Bangladesh. An even earlier avulsion, triggered by a c. 1762 earthquake of about magnitude 8.8, created the present Jamuna channel. Bank erosion is constant, often through **slab failure**, where large blocks of cohesive sediment collapse into the current.

Engineering has tried to tame the shifting river. In India, bridges include the Old Saraighat Bridge (1962, the first combined rail-road bridge) and the Bogibeel Bridge. In Bangladesh, the Bangabandhu Bridge (Jamuna Bridge) opened in 1998. China operationalised the Zangmu Dam on the upper course in 2015, with further dam and bridge construction continuing.

## Shared Waters and Disputes

The river's water is shared by three countries, and its management is a recurring source of geopolitical tension. Chinese dam-building, beginning with Zangmu, has raised downstream concerns in India and Bangladesh about reduced flow and sediment delivery. China maintains its projects have no significant effect; the downstream countries remain unconvinced, and the long-term impact on flood behaviour and delta fertility is still debated. The cause of the major historical course change in Bangladesh is similarly unresolved, with competing theories pointing to tectonic shifts, changes in the Teesta River's contribution, and gradual avulsion through bank erosion.

A **tidal bore** (a strong tide that pushes upriver against the current) can occur in the lower Meghna–Brahmaputra system, briefly reversing the flow near the coast. The river's combination of antecedence, massive discharge, and a flood-prone path through one of the most densely populated deltas on Earth makes its annual cycle of destruction and renewal a defining feature of the region.
