# Lake Superior

Lake Superior is the largest freshwater lake on Earth by surface area, straddling the Canada–U.S. border as the highest, westernmost, and northernmost of the five Great Lakes. It holds 31,700 square miles of water, drains into Lake Huron through the St. Marys River, and is so vast that its cold, nutrient-poor waters behave more like an inland sea than a typical lake.

## A lake that mixes itself twice a year

Like the other Great Lakes, Superior is **dimictic**: its waters turn over from top to bottom twice a year, once in spring and once in fall, when surface and deep temperatures equalise. This mixing keeps oxygen distributed through the entire water column, supporting life at depth. The lake sits at an average surface elevation of 600 feet above sea level, and outflow is controlled at the St. Marys River by the **Compensating Works**, a regulating structure that sets how much water leaves the lake. Nearby, the **Soo Locks** bypass rapids so ships can move between Superior and Huron despite the height difference.

## Cold, clear, and nutrient-starved

Superior is **oligotrophic**, meaning low in nutrients and high in dissolved oxygen, with cold, clear water. That combination supports more than 80 fish species, including native lake trout and lake sturgeon, but it also makes the lake biologically fragile. Introduced species such as sea lamprey and stocked salmon have damaged native populations. The same cold that starves the ecosystem of nutrients also slows decomposition, which is why bodies from the lake's many shipwrecks often stay submerged rather than refloating.

Cold air crossing the relatively warmer water picks up moisture and dumps it as **lake-effect snow** on downwind shores, while the same body of water cools nearby summers.

## A basin carved by rifting and ice

The lake basin sits inside the **Midcontinent Rift**, a roughly 1.1-billion-year-old (Mesoproterozoic) rift valley whose ancient Precambrian granites now form the Canadian Shield along the northern shore. The modern shoreline is much younger. The Wisconsin glaciation, ending about 10,000 years ago, gouged out the basin and left behind a predecessor called Lake Minong. As the ice retreated, people moved in: Plano hunters around 10,000 BC, then Shield Archaic peoples around 5000 BC, who worked regional copper and built trade networks.

## People, fur, and iron

Indigenous groups including the **Anishinaabe** (Ojibwe) became the dominant inhabitants of the region by the mid-18th century. French explorers arrived in the 17th century and named the lake *le lac supérieur* ("the upper lake"), referring to its position upstream of the other Great Lakes; the British anglicised this to "Superior" after taking control in the 1760s. The Ojibwe name *gichi-gami*, "great sea," captures the same intuition.

The 19th century brought industrial use. The Hudson's Bay Company and the North West Company competed in the fur trade from 1814 onward, and the steamboat *Independence* entered service on the lake in 1847, opening bulk transport. Superior became a key shipping lane for iron ore and grain, with a season that typically runs from late March to mid-January. The southern shore earned the nickname "Graveyard of the Great Lakes" because of its concentration of wrecks, the most famous being the SS *Edmund Fitzgerald*, which sank in 1975 with all 29 crew lost. J. Val Klump reached the lake's deepest point in 1985, a record low water level was set in September 2007, and monthly high-water records followed in 2019 and 2020.

## A lake under stress

Two long-running changes worry researchers. The lake's surface temperature has risen faster than the surrounding air, roughly 4.5°F between 1979 and 2007, and although some early models predicted Superior could be routinely ice-free by 2040, more recent data through 2021 do not support that trajectory. Nitrate concentrations have also been climbing for over a century, remaining well below harmful thresholds, but the cause of the slow rise is unresolved and may be tied to human alteration of the regional nitrogen cycle. The International Lake Superior Board of Control, established in 1914, continues to regulate water levels against these shifting conditions.
