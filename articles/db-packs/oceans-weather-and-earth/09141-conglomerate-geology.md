# Conglomerate (geology)

A conglomerate is a sedimentary rock built from rounded gravel-sized fragments called **clasts**, surrounded by a finer-grained **matrix** of sand, silt, or clay, then cemented by calcium carbonate, iron oxide, silica, or hardened clay. Conglomerates form when rounded gravels deposited by water or glaciers are buried and lithified over time. They occur in sedimentary sequences of all ages but make up less than 1 percent by weight of all sedimentary rocks.

The defining feature is **clast roundness**: rounded or subrounded gravels produce a conglomerate; angular gravels produce a **breccia**. Mixed populations are called breccio-conglomerate. A rock counts as a conglomerate when at least 30 percent of its clasts exceed 2 mm in diameter (granules, pebbles, cobbles, or boulders) and are rounded to subangular.

Two textural varieties reflect transport and deposition:

- **Orthoconglomerate**: clasts touch each other (clast-supported), typically cross-bedded, well-cemented by calcite, hematite, quartz, or clay, and deposited by flowing water.
- **Paraconglomerate**: clasts "float" in abundant matrix (matrix-supported), often unstratified, typical of glacial tills or debris flows.

## Classification

Conglomerates are sorted in three independent ways, often combined:

1. **By roundness**: conglomerate versus breccia.
2. **By clast composition**: *monomict* (one rock or mineral type) versus *polymict* (two or more types). A polymict conglomerate with metastable or unstable clasts is *petromict*. Conglomerates whose clasts match the surrounding matrix and formed within the same basin are *intraformational*; those with clasts foreign to the basin are *extraformational*. The two main intraformational types are shale-pebble conglomerates (rounded mud chips from river or lakeshore erosion) and flat-pebble or edgewise conglomerates (flat lime-mud clasts broken by storms, tsunamis, or tidal currents).
3. **By dominant clast size**: granule, pebble, or cobble conglomerate.

## Where conglomerates form

Each environment leaves a recognisable signature:

- **Deep-water marine (turbidites)**: very well sorted, well-rounded clasts with strong A-axis imbrication in the coarse basal layer of each bed.
- **Shallow marine**: *basal conglomerates* above an unconformity, marking the position of an advancing shoreline. They are diachronous, representing different ages at different places.
- **Fluvial**: well-rounded but poorly sorted clasts carried as bedload during high flow. Maximum clast size decreases downstream by attrition, so conglomerates dominate immature rivers and appear only as pebble lags at channel bases in mature ones. Imbrication is typically AB-plane type.
- **Alluvial fans and braidplains**: the thickest conglomerate deposits on Earth, mostly clast-supported with AB-plane imbrication. Matrix-supported debris-flow deposits on fans in rapidly eroding terrain, especially deserts, are **fanglomerates**, which likely preserve the largest gravel accumulations in the geologic record.
- **Glacial**: *tillites* are poorly sorted, matrix-supported conglomerates with a matrix of milled rock flour; water-laid glacial gravels form eskers.

## Examples and uses

Conglomerate shapes several landscapes. The jagged peaks of Montserrat near Barcelona and the Scottish cliffs from Arbroath to Stonehaven, where Dunnottar Castle sits on a promontory of conglomerate, are carved from rock strong enough for building stone, as in the Santa Maria de Montserrat Abbey. The Crestone Conglomerate in Colorado's San Luis Valley consists of red and green fanglomerates from ancient alluvial fans. Kata Tjuta in Australia's Northern Territory, the Buda Hills in Hungary, and the Miocene Nagelfluh of the Swiss Alps are all conglomerates. In Pennsylvania, the Pottsville conglomerate underlies the anthracite coal measures. On Mars, NASA's Curiosity rover examined slabs of conglomerate at the "Hottah" outcrop; the rounded pebbles, ranging from sand-sized to golf-ball-sized, were deposited by an ancient stream flowing at walking pace, ankle- to hip-deep.

If a conglomerate is later altered by heat and pressure, it becomes a **metaconglomerate**.
