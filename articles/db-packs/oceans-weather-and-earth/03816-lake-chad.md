# Lake Chad

Lake Chad is a large, shallow freshwater lake in west-central Africa, shared by Nigeria, Niger, Chad, and Cameroon. It sustains more than 30 million people with drinking water, irrigation, and fish from a fishery of 84 species, and it sits inside one of the continent's most important wetlands for migratory birds. Its defining story is loss: the lake was once one of the largest bodies of water on Earth, and it has since collapsed to a small fraction of that size, though its area has stabilised since the 1990s.

## A vanished giant

During the Quaternary period, the basin held a "Mega-Chad" covering about 400,000 km², large enough for its waters to reach the Atlantic via the Benue River. That water body supported early fishing settlements, agriculture, and migrations. By 500 BC, permanent villages and the Sao civilisation had taken hold along its shores, and from about 900 AD the Kanem Empire rose northeast of the lake, eventually followed by Kanem-Bornu, which dominated the region into the 19th century. The c. 1800 BC Gajiganna pottery culture near the lake and the c. 1200–1000 BC appearance of the earliest domesticated pearl millet in the region point to a long record of settled life around this water.

## The modern lake

Today Lake Chad is endorheic, meaning a closed drainage basin with no outlet to the sea, and unusually shallow for its size, with a deeper southern pool and a shallower northern pool separated by a low underwater ridge (a sill). About 95% of its inflow arrives through the Chari River system, so the lake's level tracks seasonal rains across a wide catchment area almost perfectly. Beneath the lakebed lies a massive Quaternary aquifer, an underground layer of water-bearing rock that recharges from rainfall and seepage and acts as a long-term groundwater buffer, sustaining the region even when surface water contracts.

## Collapse and stabilisation

Open water covered roughly 25,000 km² in 1960, near the end of a relatively wet phase. Major Sahel droughts during the 1970s and 1980s, combined with growing irrigation diversions, drove a catastrophic decline: by 1980 the open lake had fallen to about 2,000 km², and in 1994 it hit a low of roughly 1,756 km², a loss of more than 90% in a generation. Since the mid-1990s the average surface area has stabilised at about 1,876 km², far below any historical level but no longer shrinking. Whether climate variability or human water use caused more of the loss is still debated, along with how much future pressure the aquifer can absorb.

## Human and ecological cost

Shrinkage has reduced biodiversity, altered vegetation, and displaced the people who fish and farm the shore. Scarcity of water and grazing land has fed local conflict, deepened economic hardship, and worsened regional insecurity, with groups such as Boko Haram exploiting the destabilisation. Four states responded in 1964 by creating the Lake Chad Basin Commission to coordinate management, and a long-debated inter-basin transfer from the Congo River has been proposed to refill the lake, though its feasibility and ecological effects remain contested.
