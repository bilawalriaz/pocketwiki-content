# Geologic time scale

The geologic time scale organises Earth's 4.54 ± 0.05 billion year history into named intervals, from eons down to ages. It pairs two parallel hierarchies: chronostratigraphic units describe the rock layers themselves, while geochronologic units describe the corresponding spans of time. The current Quaternary period began 2.58 million years ago; the current Holocene epoch began 11,700 years ago.

## How the scale is built

Two disciplines combine to produce it. **Chronostratigraphy** arranges rock sequences into relative age order using a handful of principles: superposition (in undisturbed layers, older rocks lie below younger ones), original horizontality (sediments are initially laid flat), lateral continuity (layers extend sideways until they thin out or meet a barrier), cross-cutting relationships (a feature that cuts across a rock is younger than that rock), and faunal succession (each layer carries a distinctive fossil assemblage that succeeds others in a predictable order). **Geochronology** assigns numerical ages, primarily through radiometric dating, and refines those numbers when better measurements arrive.

Ages use the suffix "Ma" for megaannum (millions of years ago), "Ga" for gigaannum (billions), and "ka" for kiloannum (thousands). The base of the Jurassic is given as 201.4 ± 0.2 Ma.

## Defining the boundaries

Each formal chronostratigraphic boundary is anchored to a specific point in a specific rock outcrop, a Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point (GSSP), informally a "golden spike." The spike marks the base of a stage; everything above belongs to one time interval, everything below to another. Because the spike sits in real rocks, the rock definition stays fixed; only the numerical age attached to it is refined as dating improves. The base of the Cambrian was redated from 541 Ma to 538.8 Ma in early 2022 without any change to the boundary's rock definition.

In the Proterozoic, Archean and Hadean, where fossils are scarce or absent, boundaries are set by absolute age alone (Global Standard Stratigraphic Ages) rather than by biological events in rock.

## The hierarchy

| Geochronologic (time) | Chronostratigraphic (rocks) | Typical span |
|---|---|---|
| Eon | Eonothem | Hundreds of millions to ~2 billion years |
| Era | Erathem | Tens to hundreds of millions of years |
| Period | System | Millions to tens of millions of years |
| Epoch | Series | Hundreds of thousands to tens of millions of years |
| Age | Stage | Thousands to millions of years |

Four eons are formally defined: Hadean, Archean, Proterozoic, and Phanerozoic. The Phanerozoic contains ten eras, 22 periods (including the current Quaternary), and 37 formally defined epochs. The Carboniferous is the only period split into subperiods (Mississippian and Pennsylvanian). The subdivisions Early, Middle, and Late replace the chronostratigraphic terms Lower, Middle, and Upper when speaking of time.

## Why the Phanerozoic dominates the story

The Phanerozoic covers roughly the last 538.8 million years, only about 11.8% of Earth's history, while the preceding three eons span ~88.2%. The imbalance reflects fossil abundance: hard-bodied life diversified during the Cambrian onward, giving chronostratigraphy the biostratigraphic markers it needs to divide time finely. Earlier eons, lacking such markers, are divided mainly by absolute ages and global events such as the Great Oxidation Event (~2.4 Ga) and Snowball Earth glaciations (~720–635 Ma).

## Major mass extinctions as calibration points

Five mass extinctions punctuate the Phanerozoic and serve as natural boundaries: the Late Ordovician (~445 Ma, ~85% of marine invertebrate species lost), Late Devonian (Kellwasser ~372 Ma and Hangenberg ~359 Ma), end-Permian (~252 Ma, the largest, linked to Siberian Traps eruptions and eliminating trilobites and most therapsids), end-Triassic (~201 Ma, linked to CAMP eruptions), and end-Cretaceous (~66 Ma, the Chicxulub impact, eliminating non-avian dinosaurs and ammonoids).

## How the scale became authoritative

Early estimates assumed a Biblical ~6,000 year age until James Hutton's uniformitarianism (presented 1785, popularised by John Playfair and Charles Lyell) established that slow processes operating today explain the rock record, implying vast spans of time. Arthur Holmes published the first modern geological time scale in 1911, combining emerging radiometric dating with stratigraphy. The International Commission on Stratigraphy (ICS), a body of the International Union of Geological Sciences founded after the IUGS was established in 1961, now maintains the International Chronostratigraphic Chart, versioned by year and month, with at least one revision per year.

In 2019 the Anthropocene Working Group voted to propose a formal Anthropocene epoch beginning in the mid-20th century; the proposal, using a Crawford Lake (Ontario) site marked by 1952 plutonium fallout, was rejected as a formal epoch in early 2024, leaving the term as an informal descriptor of human impact.
