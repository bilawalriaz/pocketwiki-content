# Climate

## Definition and Measurement

Climate is the long-term weather pattern in a region, typically averaged over 30 years, but more rigorously defined as the mean and variability of meteorological variables over periods from months to millions of years. The World Meteorological Organization (WMO) sets 30 years as the standard averaging period: long enough to filter year-to-year variations such as El Niño–Southern Oscillation, yet short enough to reveal longer trends. A "climate normal" is the 30-year arithmetic average of a climate element, used as a reference point. The first reference period (1901–1930) was designated in 1934; 1961–1990 now serves as the baseline, with 1991–2020 to follow. The common summary: "Climate is what you expect, weather is what you get."

The climate system comprises the atmosphere, hydrosphere, cryosphere, lithosphere, and biosphere, and their interactions. Measured variables include air temperature, pressure, precipitation, wind, humidity, cloud amount, and solar radiation. Determinants that change only over millions of years include latitude, altitude, land-to-water proportion, and proximity to oceans and mountains, altered by plate tectonics. Dynamic determinants include the thermohaline circulation, which warms the northern Atlantic by 5 °C (9 °F) relative to other basins; regional ocean currents; vegetation, which affects absorption, water retention, and rainfall; and greenhouse gas levels, chiefly carbon dioxide and methane, which govern solar energy retention.

## Classification

Climate classifications sort the world's climates and often correlate with biomes, because climate strongly shapes life. The most widely used scheme is the Köppen classification, first developed in 1899. Modern methods divide into genetic methods, which focus on causes such as air mass frequency or synoptic disturbance locations, and empiric methods, which focus on effects such as plant hardiness or evapotranspiration. The Thornthwaite system, introduced in 1948, incorporates evapotranspiration with temperature and precipitation and is used in studying biological diversity and climate impacts. The Bergeron and Spatial Synoptic Classification systems focus on air mass origins. A common shortcoming is that these schemes produce distinct boundaries rather than the gradual transitions that dominate in nature.

## The Climate Record

Paleoclimatology studies ancient climates, explaining variations across Earth since its formation. Because direct observations were scarce before the 19th century, past climates are inferred from proxy variables: non-biotic evidence such as lake bed sediments and ice cores, and biotic evidence such as tree rings and coral. The modern record relies on thermometers, barometers, and anemometers, but instrument changes in frequency, error, environment, and exposure must be considered. Records skew toward population centres and affluent countries; since the 1960s, satellite observations have allowed global coverage, including the Arctic and oceans.

## Variability and Change

Climate variability describes variations in the mean state and other characteristics on all spatial and temporal scales beyond individual weather events, including random variability and periodic oscillations. Climate oscillations correlate with astronomical factors, including barycenter changes, solar variation, cosmic ray flux, cloud albedo feedback, and Milankovic cycles, and with ocean–atmosphere heat distribution modes. Natural oscillations may be masked by volcanic eruptions, impact events, proxy data irregularities, positive feedback processes, or anthropogenic emissions. "Climate change" now implies long-term, human-caused change, whereas in the 1960s it referred to what is now called climate variability.

Climate change reflects changes in variability or average state over decades to millions of years, driven by internal Earth processes, external forces such as variations in sunlight intensity, or human activities. Earth's Energy Imbalance (EEI), the balance between incoming and outgoing radiation, is a fundamental metric of global change status. In policy, "climate change" often refers only to modern changes including global warming, sometimes with a presumption of human causation, as in the UNFCCC, which reserves "climate variability" for non-human causes. Earth has undergone four major ice ages, with colder glacial periods separated by warmer interglacials. Snow and ice raise surface albedo, reflecting more solar energy and reinforcing lower temperatures; greenhouse gas increases, such as from volcanism, can produce interglacials. Suggested natural causes include continent positions, orbital variations, solar output, and volcanism, but these act far more slowly than the present rate of change driven by human greenhouse gas emissions.

## Climate Models

Climate models use quantitative methods to simulate interactions and radiative energy transfer between the atmosphere, oceans, land surface, and ice through physics equations. All models balance incoming short-wave (visible) radiation with outgoing long-wave (infrared) radiation; any imbalance changes average temperature. Resolution ranges from over 100 km down to 1 km, with high-resolution global models requiring significant computational resources. Global models can be downscaled dynamically or statistically to regional models, such as ICON or CHELSA, to analyze local impacts. Recent applications focus on increasing greenhouse gases, primarily carbon dioxide, and predict an upward trend in global mean surface temperature, with the most rapid increase projected for higher northern latitudes.
