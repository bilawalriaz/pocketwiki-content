# Bering Sea

The Bering Sea is the northernmost **marginal sea** of the Pacific Ocean, a sea partially enclosed by islands, archipelagos, or peninsulas, separating Eurasia from North America. Covering more than two million square kilometres, it is a highly productive ocean ecosystem that supplies major commercial fisheries, though its biology is already shifting under climate pressure.

## Geography

Alaska and the Alaska Peninsula form the eastern shore, the Russian Far East and the Kamchatka Peninsula the western shore, the Aleutian Islands the southern limit, and the narrow Bering Strait the northern connection to the Arctic. The International Hydrographic Organization sets the formal southern boundary as a line from Kabuch Point in Alaska, through the Aleutian and Komandorski Islands, to Cape Kamchatka. Major embayments include Bristol Bay, the Gulf of Anadyr, and Norton Sound. The sea floor holds sixteen submarine canyons, including Zhemchug Canyon, the world's largest.

## Why the Ecosystem Is So Productive

Two physical mechanisms combine to make the Bering Sea exceptionally rich.

First, the **shelf break**, the point where the shallow continental shelf drops into the deep North Aleutians Basin, drives **upwelling**, the rise of cold, nutrient-rich deep water toward the surface. This upwelling sustains a continuous "Greenbelt" of **phytoplankton** growth, the microscopic photosynthetic organisms that form the base of the marine food web.

Second, seasonal **sea ice**, frozen ocean water that forms and melts each year, controls the spring bloom. As the ice retreats, fresh meltwater stratifies the water column and traps phytoplankton in the sunlit surface layer, while the underside of the ice itself provides a surface for ice algae to grow before the open-water bloom begins. This annual ice-driven pulse of production distinguishes the Bering Sea from most temperate seas.

## Biodiversity

The high productivity supports a dense web of life. At least 419 fish species inhabit the sea, alongside numerous whale species: beluga, humpback, bowhead, gray, blue, sperm, fin, sei, and the critically rare North Pacific right whale. Other marine mammals include walrus, Steller sea lion, northern fur seal, orca, and polar bear. The region is critical habitat for more than 30 seabird species, with roughly 20 million breeding individuals. Two species recorded here, Steller's sea cow and the spectacled cormorant, were driven extinct by human overexploitation.

## Fisheries and Economy

The same productivity that supports wildlife fuels major commercial fisheries for king crab, snow crab, Bristol Bay salmon, pollock, and other groundfish. Annual U.S. commercial catches from the Bering Sea are worth roughly one billion dollars, and Russian catches about six hundred million dollars, with Alaskan landings supplying about half of the total U.S. fish and shellfish catch.

## Climate Change

Average seasonal **primary productivity**, the rate at which phytoplankton convert sunlight into organic matter, has fallen by an estimated 30 to 40 percent over the last fifty years, indicating a reduced capacity to support the food web above it. Yet between 1979 and 2012 the Bering Sea region gained a small amount of sea ice extent, in contrast to the substantial summer ice loss observed in the Arctic Ocean to the north. The overall trajectory of the sea's climate and ecosystem remains uncertain as Arctic conditions continue to change.

## Human History

The sea's geography has shaped human prehistory. During the last glacial period, lowered sea levels exposed the Bering land bridge, a land connection between Asia and North America, allowing the migration of humans and animals between the continents; this route is accepted by most, though not all, scientists as the first entry point for people into the Americas. In 1728 the Danish-born Russian navigator Vitus Bering became the first European to systematically explore the sea, and on 18 December 2018 a large meteor exploded above it at an altitude of 25.6 km, releasing 49 kilotons of energy.
