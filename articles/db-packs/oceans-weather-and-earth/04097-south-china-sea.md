# South China Sea

The South China Sea is a marginal sea of the Western Pacific, bounded by China, Taiwan, the Philippines, Malaysia, Brunei, Indonesia, and Vietnam. It carried roughly $3.4 trillion in maritime shipping in 2016 and holds major fisheries and hydrocarbon reserves, which is why almost every coastal state claims part of it.

## How the basin formed

About 45 million years ago the "Dangerous Ground" block rifted away from southern China, and around 30 million years ago seafloor spreading opened the V-shaped basin. One theory ties the opening to the extrusion of Indochina, a wedge of crust pushed sideways by India's collision with Asia. The sea averages 1,212 m deep and reaches over 5,500 m. Major rivers, including the Mekong, Pearl, and Red, deliver large sediment loads, building deltas that trap oil and gas.

## Names as claims

The dominant English name comes from 16th-century Portuguese sailors (*Mare da China*). The earliest recorded Chinese name, *Nanfang Hai*, appears in the *Yi Zhou Shu* between 1046 and 771 BCE. In Southeast Asia the sea was once called the Champa Sea. Modern names now encode modern claims: Vietnam calls it the East Sea (*Biển Đông*); the Philippines officially uses "West Philippine Sea" for the part inside its exclusive economic zone (EEZ); Indonesia in 2017 renamed its northern sector the North Natuna Sea. The name on a map is rarely neutral here.

## Islands, fish, and fuel

The sea holds more than 250 small islands, atolls, and reefs, almost all uninhabited, with the Spratly and Paracel groups dominating the disputes. In 2010 the sea produced about 14% of the world's commercial catch. Proven oil and gas reserves exist, but most sit near undisputed coastlines rather than inside the contested Spratly and Paracel zones, which weakens the "resource war" logic often invoked for the region.

## Overlapping claims

China and Taiwan assert rights to nearly the entire sea through a "nine-dash line" on their maps, a U-shaped boundary with no clear legal basis under modern ocean law. Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaysia, Brunei, and Indonesia base their claims on EEZs, zones extending up to 200 nautical miles from a coast where the coastal state controls resources, as defined by UNCLOS (the 1982 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea). China's sweeping line overlaps the EEZs of several neighbors, so the claims collide directly. The clash has produced real violence: China seized the Paracels from South Vietnam in 1974, and a 1988 naval engagement in the Spratlys killed more than 70 Vietnamese sailors. China has also built artificial islands and military outposts on reefs, hardening its physical presence.

## The 2016 ruling and its limits

In January 2013 the Philippines launched arbitration against China under UNCLOS. In July 2016 a tribunal at The Hague ruled largely in Manila's favor, holding that China had "no legal basis" for any historic rights inside the nine-dash line and criticizing its island-building. Beijing rejected the ruling outright, exposing a hard edge of international law: a binding decision from a legitimate court can still be unenforceable when a major power refuses to comply. ASEAN has tried to manage the fallout through a long-negotiated Code of Conduct with China, and a June 2020 summit statement reaffirmed UNCLOS as the foundation for maritime rights, but no comprehensive settlement is in sight.

## Why outside powers engage

The United States runs "freedom of navigation" operations, sailing warships through claimed waters to contest what it calls excessive maritime claims, a routine that irritates Beijing and reassures some Southeast Asian capitals. Other non-claimants, including Australia, Japan, and India, have grown more vocal. China prefers bilateral talks, where its size gives leverage, while most ASEAN members prefer multilateral frameworks.
