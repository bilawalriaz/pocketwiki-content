# Earthquake light

An earthquake light is a reported luminous phenomenon that eyewitnesses have associated with tectonic stress, seismic activity, or volcanic eruptions, appearing as flashes, glows, orbs, sheet-like illuminations, or streamers before, during, or after an earthquake. Earthquake lights are not an established geophysical phenomenon. Experts disagree on whether the reports describe one real effect, several unrelated effects, or misidentified natural and artificial light sources, and no confirmed geological mechanism has won consensus.

Reports of luminous phenomena linked to earthquakes span at least 2,400 years, from accounts of the 373 BCE destruction of Helice and Buris in Greece and Italian reports from 89 BCE, through 901's description of "strange lights in the sky" during the 869 Jōgan earthquake, to twenty-first-century smartphone and dashcam footage. The first photographic record came from Japanese dentist Tōru Kuribayashi during the 1965–1967 Matsushiro earthquake swarm, where he captured ten examples, eight in colour. Some seismologists, including Tsuneji Rikitake, later argued those photographs may have been forgeries; the Smithsonian nevertheless described the Matsushiro images as the point at which scientists acknowledged the phenomenon's validity. The first known video recording came from the 2007 Peru earthquake. Since then, lights have been filmed before or during many large events worldwide, including the 2008 Sichuan, 2009 L'Aquila, 2010 Chile, 2016 Ecuador, 2017 Mexico (Chiapas, M 8.2, lights seen more than 700 km from the epicentre), 2021 Guerrero, 2022 Düzce, and 2023 Al Haouz earthquakes.

Reported appearances and behaviour. Witnesses describe white or blue flashes, glowing orbs, and band-like, sheet-like, spherical, columnar, and fire-like forms, in colours including white, bluish-white, red, pink, orange, and yellow. Reports are more common in South America, China, France, Germany, Greece, and Italy. Viewable distance from the epicentre varies; 1930 North Izu reports placed lights up to 110 km (70 mi) away, and 1896 and 1933 Sanriku reports described bluish-white luminescence over seabed exposed by receding tsunami water. Matsushiro observations noted that the lights were more likely at certain times of day, lasted tens of seconds, brightened suddenly, then faded gradually, and were often bright enough to be mistaken for fire or daylight.

Proposed mechanisms. Most theories assume tectonic stress generates electric charge or polarisation in rock that then produces light. The leading candidates are piezoelectricity, the generation of electric charge by stress in certain crystals, electrokinetic effects from streaming potentials in water-filled porous rock, triboelectric or fracture-electrification mechanisms, frictional heating near shear zones, and rock-stress or positive hole charge-carrier models in which mobile charges migrate toward the surface, ionise air, and produce corona discharges or plasma. One study found that subvertical faults in continental rift settings have the highest reported incidence, suggesting fault geometry matters. None of these has been confirmed.

Alternative explanations. Many individual cases now have non-seismic explanations. After the 2021 Guerrero earthquake, UNAM seismologists attributed viral "blue flash" videos to ordinary thunderstorm lightning and to electrical arcing as power lines swayed or transformers blew during shaking. Arcing power lines and grid failures are a recurring confound, and it can be hard to disentangle them from genuine reports. A separate example: a 1999 report claiming volcanic activity, fireballs, and "magmatic" rocks near Çınarcık after the İzmit earthquake was classified by the Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program as a false volcanic report, with the Smithsonian suggesting the lights could instead have been earthquake lights.

Few reliable images exist, eyewitness accounts are uncertain, and photographs and videos cannot by themselves distinguish earthquake-related emissions from coincident weather, arcing grid equipment, or ordinary lightning.
