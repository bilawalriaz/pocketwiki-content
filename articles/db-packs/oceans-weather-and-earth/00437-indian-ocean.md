# Indian Ocean

The Indian Ocean covers about 20% of Earth's water surface, making it the third-largest ocean and the geologically youngest. Its defining trait is warmth. A deep layer of hot water known as the Tropical Warm Pool drives the Indian Ocean monsoon, a seasonal reversal of winds and currents that shapes the climate of the surrounding continents. The ocean is landlocked to the north, fed by enormous rivers, and warmer than any other ocean.

## How it formed and why it looks the way it does

The basin opened about 156 million years ago when Africa split from East Gondwana. As the seafloor spread, three mid-ocean ridges met at the Rodrigues Triple Point, and a mantle plume punched through the crust to form the Réunion hotspot, whose ancient eruptions left the Deccan Traps in India. The ocean floor still carries the marks of that birth: the Bengal and Indus submarine fans, the world's two largest, sit where Himalayan and Tibetan rivers have deposited sediment for tens of millions of years.

The continental shelf is unusually wide in the north. This is why the Arabian Sea is the saltiest part of the ocean (little river input) while the Bay of Bengal is the freshest (heavy runoff from the Ganges and Brahmaputra). Surface currents form two large gyres, and water flows westward from the Pacific into the Indian Ocean through the Indonesian Throughflow, a permanent low-latitude passage between the two basins.

## Life in the water

Productivity concentrates where winds pull deep water upward, in upwelling zones off Somalia, Oman, and western India, feeding major tuna fisheries. Coral reefs, mangroves, and seagrass beds line the coasts, and nine of Earth's 36 biodiversity hotspots, including Madagascar and Sundaland, sit on the ocean's margins. Their high endemism, the presence of species found nowhere else, comes from long isolation combined with a complex geological history of rifting islands from Gondwana.

These ecosystems are under severe stress. Phytoplankton has dropped by up to 20% over the past six decades, tuna catch rates have fallen 50–90%, and reefs are bleaching as the ocean warms. Models predict accelerated warming and more frequent marine heatwaves through the 21st century.

## A human highway for at least 75,000 years

Modern humans began dispersing along the northern coast roughly 75,000 years ago. By 2000 BCE, maritime trade linked the Indus Valley, Persian Gulf, and Egypt into a single network. In the 1st century CE, Roman and Tamil merchants rode monsoon winds between the Red Sea and southern India, a system documented in the Greek *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*. The Indian Ocean World functioned as an early global economy whose rhythm was set by seasonal winds.

From the 15th century onward, the pattern shifted. Chinese Admiral Zheng He's Ming treasure fleets (1405–1433) crossed the basin, and in 1497 Vasco da Gama rounded the Cape of Good Hope, opening a direct European sea route to India. The Suez Canal opened in 1869, linking the Indian Ocean to the Mediterranean and completing a continuous waterway from Europe to East Asia.

## Modern pressures

About 80% of seaborne oil trade transits narrow chokepoints, primarily the Strait of Hormuz and the Strait of Malacca, making the ocean's stability a global economic concern. In 2004 a massive earthquake off Sumatra generated a tsunami that killed around 236,000 people around the rim. In 2010 an Indian Ocean garbage patch of at least 5 million km² was discovered. In 2019 the International Court of Justice advised the United Kingdom to transfer the Chagos Archipelago to Mauritius, a sovereignty question that remains unresolved. Scientists still debate how the garbage patch will evolve, whether it shrinks over decades or feeds a growing global accumulation over millennia.
