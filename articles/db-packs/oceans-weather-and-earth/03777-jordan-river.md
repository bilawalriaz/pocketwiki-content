# Jordan River

The Jordan River is a 251-kilometre endorheic river (a drainage basin with no outlet to the sea) in the Levant. It flows north to south from northern Israel through the Sea of Galilee to the Dead Sea, marking borders between Jordan, Syria, Israel, and Palestine. The river holds central religious importance in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam.

## Course and Basin

The river begins at the merger of the Dan and Hasbani rivers in northern Israel. Its upper course drops through the Hula Valley into the Sea of Galilee, a freshwater lake below sea level. South of the lake, the lower course meanders through the Jordan Rift Valley, a depression that runs from Syria through the Dead Sea to the Red Sea. The Jordan ends at the Dead Sea, the lowest exposed point on Earth's surface at about 430 metres below sea level. Major tributaries in the lower course include the Yarmouk and Zarqa rivers. The river's path forms international borders and the boundary of the Israeli-occupied Golan Heights and West Bank.

## Flow Reduction

In the 20th century the Jordan became a critical freshwater source. Israel's National Water Carrier, operational since 1964, diverts water from the Sea of Galilee, and Jordan's East Ghor Canal diverts Yarmouk River water. Conflict over these diversions contributed to the Six-Day War. By 2010 diversions had reduced the river's annual flow into the Dead Sea from 1.3 billion cubic metres to 20–30 million, roughly 2% of its historic volume. The river's name was first recorded as "Yārdon" on an Egyptian papyrus around 1303–1213 BC, and explorers Costigan, Molyneux, Lynch, and MacGregor surveyed it in the 19th century.

## Ecology

The basin is a meeting point for fish from different biogeographic regions (areas defined by their characteristic species), including native species like the Jordan bream and Levantine scraper. Introduced common carp and Nile tilapia compete with native populations. Reduced flow, high salinity from agricultural runoff, and untreated sewage have left the ecosystem critically endangered. The most degraded section is the 100-kilometre stretch from the Yarmouk confluence to the Dead Sea, and in 2007 the World Wildlife Fund named the Jordan one of the world's 100 most endangered ecological sites.

## Religious Significance

In the Hebrew Bible the Jordan is the river the Israelites crossed into the Promised Land and the site of miracles by Elijah and Elisha. In the New Testament, John the Baptist baptized Jesus in the river, making it a central Christian pilgrimage site. Al-Maghtas on the Jordanian side is traditionally identified as the baptism site and became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2015. In Islam the river is part of a "blessed land" associated with prophets. The 1994 Israel–Jordan peace treaty regulates water sharing between the two countries.

A wadi is a valley or streambed that is dry except in the rainy season, a landscape feature common across the Jordan basin.
