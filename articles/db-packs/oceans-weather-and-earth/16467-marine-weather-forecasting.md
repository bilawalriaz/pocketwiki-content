# Marine weather forecasting

Marine weather forecasting predicts conditions over the open ocean for shipping, aviation, and coastal safety. Governments began organizing such forecasts after the 1859 wreck of the Royal Charter off Anglesey, which prompted Vice-Admiral Robert FitzRoy to start a warning service in 1861, and again after the 1912 sinking of the Titanic, which led to the 1914 International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea. Wind is the primary driver of marine weather: it generates waves and swells, and its circulation around the subtropical ridge sustains warm boundary currents like the Gulf Stream.

## How wind shapes the ocean

In the tropics, trade winds blow westward; at mid-latitudes, westerlies blow eastward. This pattern stresses the ocean surface with negative curl, producing equatorward Sverdrup transport, the net water flow driven by wind rotation. Conservation of potential vorticity, the tendency of a rotating fluid column to keep its spin, on the ridge's western edge concentrates that flow into a narrow, accelerating poleward current along the basin's western boundary, outweighing friction with the cold Labrador Current. Bends in the current occasionally pinch off into warm and cold eddies, a process called western intensification.

## Swell dispersion

Swells travel far from their storm source, sorting themselves by speed as they go. Longer periods arrive first because they move faster, so peak wave period falls as the swell disperses. In deep water, travel time follows t = 4πX / (gT), where X is distance, T is wave period, and g is gravitational acceleration. For a storm 10,000 km away, 15-second-period swells arrive about 10 days after the storm, followed 17 hours later by 14-second swells. Sea state in the storm has a broad frequency spectrum, while distant swell narrows to roughly 2% of the peak. Wave groups grow from about seven waves per group near the storm to 20 or more from very distant storms.

## Tropical cyclone avoidance

Mariners avoid tropical cyclones by splitting the storm into halves. In the Northern Hemisphere, the right side relative to storm motion is the dangerous semicircle, where the storm's translation speed adds to its rotational wind, producing the strongest winds and seas. The left side is the navigable semicircle, where translation and rotation partly cancel. Sailors are advised to avoid the forecast track entirely, called crossing the T, and to keep the wind on the starboard bow in the dangerous semicircle or on the starboard quarter in the navigable one. The 1-2-3 rule defines a danger area by expanding the forecast track by historical National Hurricane Center errors of 100-200-300 nautical miles at 24-48-72 hours, adjusted by forecast wind radii; current errors have fallen to roughly 50-100-150 nautical miles.

## Numerical prediction

Modern forecasts rely on numerical weather prediction feeding third-generation wave models, which solve the spectral wave transport equation to simulate generation, propagation, shoaling, refraction, energy transfer, and dissipation of waves. Earlier first- and second-generation models, developed from the 1960s through the 1980s, overestimated direct wind input and could not realistically depict swell or wind-driven waves inside rapidly changing tropical-cyclone wind fields.

## Observing platforms

Voluntary ship reports, moored weather buoys in use since 1951, drifting buoys since 1972, and weather satellites since 1960 feed the models. Buoy wind data has smaller error than ship wind data, and roughly 1,250 drifting buoys operate worldwide. Polar-orbiting satellites cover the whole Earth asynchronously; geostationary satellites hover over the equator. Beginning with Nimbus 3 in 1969, satellites began retrieving temperature profiles through the atmospheric column, producing significant forecast improvements. The international weather ship network, established during World War II and operated under ICAO, supported transatlantic aviation, search and rescue, and ocean research until the agreement ended in 1985.

## Responsible agencies

Three agencies issue marine forecasts for the Northern Hemisphere. The Japan Meteorological Agency operates marine observatories at Hakodate, Maizuru, Kobe, and Nagasaki, covering the Northwestern Pacific, Sea of Japan, and Sea of Okhotsk. The United Kingdom Met Office produces the BBC Radio 4 Shipping Forecast four times daily for waters around the British Isles, divided into named sea areas. The United States National Weather Service works through the Ocean Prediction Center, created in 1995, which issues forecasts up to five days ahead for ocean areas north of 31°N and west of 35°W in the Atlantic, and across the northeast Pacific north of 30°N and east of 160°E. The National Hurricane Center covers marine areas south of those parallels between 35°W and 140°W, while the Honolulu Forecast Office covers between 140°W and 160°E from 30°N to the equator. Forecasts are transmitted to ships at sea by Navtex, RTTY, and Radiofax, with text products such as High Seas, Offshore, and Coastal Waters forecasts now supplemented by gridded significant wave height fields first produced experimentally by the OPC in 2006.
