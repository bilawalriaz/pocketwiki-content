# Dike (geology)

A dike is a thin sheet of rock that fills a fracture in older rock. Dikes are either magmatic (solidified from magma) or sedimentary (sediment filling a crack). The geological term is unrelated to flood-control levees; it refers to the natural wall or ridge that often remains after erosion, since dike rock is typically more resistant than the surrounding rock it cuts through.

## Geometry and orientation

A dike is much thinner than it is long or tall, with roughly parallel walls and nearly constant thickness. Thicknesses range from a few millimetres to hundreds of metres, most commonly one to a few tens of metres, while lateral extent can reach tens of kilometres; dikes tens of metres thick commonly extend beyond 100 km. Most dikes are steeply dipping (nearly vertical), though later tectonic deformation can rotate them to horizontal.

A dike cuts *across* the bedding of layered rock; a sill runs *parallel* to bedding between layers.

## Magmatic dikes

A magmatic dike forms when magma rises into a fracture and solidifies. At shallow depths, magma can exploit an existing fissure opened by the bulging of rock above a filling magma chamber. Deeper down, the pressurized magma wedges rock apart by hydraulic fracture, propagating along the plane normal to the minimum principal stress (the direction of weakest compression). At greater depths the rock is hotter and less brittle, so the magma shoves rock aside along shear planes; at the greatest depths the rock deforms plastically and a diapir (a rising plug) forms instead of a dike.

The thickness-to-length ratio decreases with depth, because deep host rock is stiffer. Near the surface the ratio is roughly 0.01 to 0.001; at depth it is 0.001 to 0.0001. A surface dike 10 m thick extends about 3 km, while a similar dike at depth extends about 30 km. This tendency has been offered as an explanation for en echelon dike sets, where short overlapping segments form a longer line, the overlapping portions being thinner so the combined thickness matches a single segment.

A few large dikes, including the 120 m Medford dike in Maine and the 500 m Gardar dike in Greenland, show no evidence of fissure widening. They may form by stoping, in which the magma fractures and disintegrates the rock at its tip rather than prying it apart.

## Texture and internal structure

Most dikes are basaltic in composition, with a slightly coarser texture than surface basalt, producing a rock called diabase. Grain size increases from the walls toward the centre, where cooling was slowest. Shallow dikes commonly show chilled margins, 1–5 cm of glassy or fine-grained rock formed by rapid cooling against cold host rock, and columnar jointing perpendicular to the walls. The columns are usually 5- or 6-sided, ranging from a few centimetres to over 0.3 m across, with larger columns indicating slower cooling. Dike rock is usually dense and vesicle-poor, though vesicles may appear near the top, aligned parallel to walls and elongated in the flow direction. Phenocrysts on the margins also align with flow.

Where multiple magma injections follow the same fissure, a multiple dike forms if the compositions match and a composite dike if later injections differ; subsequent injections typically follow the centre of the earlier dike.

## Dike swarms and sheeted complexes

Dike swarms contain hundreds of dikes emplaced during a single event, are almost always diabase, and are associated with flood basalts of large igneous provinces. They record crustal extension, since the dikes form at right angles to the direction of maximum extension. The Mackenzie dike swarm in Canada's Northwest Territories is the world's largest. Swarms are characteristic of divergent plate boundaries: Jurassic swarms in New England and Paleogene swarms in western Scotland record the early opening of the Atlantic, and present-day swarms are forming along Iceland's spreading ridge. Hawaiian rift zones expose dike swarms 2.5–5 km wide with about 50–100 dikes per kilometre near the rift centre, rising to 500 per kilometre where dikes make up half the rock volume.

In sheeted dike complexes beneath mid-ocean ridges, nearly all the rock is dikes, each chilled on only one side, recording a continuous sequence in which each new dike split the previous one in half.

## Ring dikes, cone sheets, and clastic dikes

Ring dikes and cone sheets surround shallow magma chambers beneath calderas. Cone sheets form when injected magma lifts and fractures the overlying rock into concentric cones dipping into the chamber; when a caldera later empties explosively, its roof collapses into a ring fracture that rising magma fills to produce a ring dike.

A clastic (or sedimentary) dike is a vertical body of sedimentary rock that interrupts other layers. It forms when fluid pressure in coarse-grained layers beneath impermeable clay exceeds the lithostatic overburden and drives sediment upward, or when cracks in permafrost fill with sediment falling from above.
