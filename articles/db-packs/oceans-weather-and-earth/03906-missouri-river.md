# Missouri River

The Missouri is North America's longest river, running 2,341 miles (3,767 km) from the Rocky Mountains to the Mississippi and draining more than 500,000 square miles of semi-arid land across ten U.S. states and two Canadian provinces. At St. Louis it supplies 45% of the Mississippi's flow. Humans have depended on it for roughly 12,000 years, and its modern course is one of the most heavily engineered rivers on the continent.

## Geography and origins

The Missouri begins at the confluence of the Jefferson and Madison Rivers in Montana, then flows east and south through the Great Plains, passing large reservoirs such as Fort Peck Lake, Lake Sakakawea, and Lake Oahe. Its largest tributary by volume is the Yellowstone River. The basin has a continental climate, with most rain falling in summer.

The river's path is ancient. The Laramide Orogeny, a mountain-building episode 70 to 45 million years ago, raised the Rockies and set the slope that the modern drainage follows. During the Quaternary Ice Age, glaciers diverted the river southeastward, merging its headwaters into a single system. The Missouri earned the nickname "Big Muddy" because it carried an enormous sediment load, originally 175 to 320 million short tons of silt and sand per year. Dams now trap most of that material, and the current load is only 20 to 25 million tons annually.

## People, exploration, and conflict

Archaeological evidence shows human presence in the watershed for about 12,000 years. Major tribes along the river included the Mandan, Hidatsa, Arikara, and Sioux; the introduction of the horse transformed their nomadic way of life. French explorers Louis Jolliet and Jacques Marquette first saw the river in May 1673, and Étienne de Bourgmont led the first formal European expedition upstream in 1714, reaching at least the Platte River. The United States acquired the entire basin through the Louisiana Purchase in 1803, and the Lewis and Clark Expedition (1804–1806) produced the first complete maps and ecological descriptions of the region.

The river then became the main artery of westward expansion. Manuel Lisa's 1807 expedition opened the upper Missouri to the fur trade, which peaked in the 1830s and blazed trails later used by settlers. Between the 1830s and 1860s, more than 500,000 pioneers departed from Missouri river towns for Oregon, California, and other western destinations. As settlers pushed into the Great Plains, violence followed: Red Cloud's War and the Great Sioux War of 1876–77 ended with the tribes confined to reservations.

## Engineering and the Pick-Sloan Plan

Catastrophic floods in 1844, 1881, and 1926–27 pushed the U.S. government toward large-scale control. Fort Peck Dam, the first major dam on the main river, was completed in 1940. The 1944 Flood Control Act authorized the Pick-Sloan Plan, a federal program for comprehensive development of the basin through flood control, irrigation, and hydroelectric power. In the 1950s construction began on the plan's five mainstem dams: Garrison, Oahe, Big Bend, Fort Randall, and Gavins Point. In total, fifteen mainstem dams now form the largest reservoir system in the United States.

These dams deliver flood control, hydropower, and irrigation, but they also flooded vast tracts of Native American reservation land, including 150,000 acres of the Fort Berthold Reservation, which triggered long-running legal disputes over treaty rights. Engineers also straightened and stabilized the lower channel for navigation, shortening the river by nearly 200 miles. This process, called channelization, deepened the channel for barges but destroyed riparian habitat, the vegetated zone along the riverbanks that many species depend on.

## Navigation, ecology, and ongoing trade-offs

Steamboat traffic peaked in 1858 and was soon eclipsed by railroads. Today a barge channel runs from Sioux City to St. Louis, but traffic has declined because of drought, flood disruptions like those of 1993 and 2011, and competition from other transport modes. The lower channel is expensive to maintain, and its long-term navigation use is uncertain.

Heavy development has damaged the river's ecology by destroying floodplain habitat, reducing sediment flows, and adding pollutants. Restoration projects are now removing some levees and dikes, though these efforts risk re-releasing trapped sediments and worsening nutrient pollution downstream in the Gulf of Mexico. The river that once carried 300 million tons of silt a year now carries a tenth of that, a single measure of how thoroughly its natural character has been rewritten.
