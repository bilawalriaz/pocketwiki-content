# Geological history of Earth

Earth formed about 4.54 billion years ago by accreting from a disk of dust and gas around the young Sun. It began molten, cooled into a rocky crust, and acquired oceans and an atmosphere within its first few hundred million years. Since then, its surface has been continuously reshaped: continents drift, collide into supercontinents, and break apart, while life diversifies and climate swings between greenhouse warmth and ice ages.

Geologists read this 4.54-billion-year story from the rock record, using **stratigraphy** (the study of layered rocks) to assign relative ages, and **radiometric dating** of minerals to assign absolute ages. The result is the **geologic time scale**, which divides Earth's history into four eons—Hadean, Archean, Proterozoic, and Phanerozoic—based mainly on changes in the fossil and rock record.

## Precambrian: the first 90 percent

The Precambrian spans 4.6 billion to 539 million years ago and accounts for about 90 percent of geologic time, but it leaves the sparsest rock record.

**Hadean (4.6–4.0 billion years ago).** Earth was a magma ocean pummeled by impacts. The Moon formed soon after, when a large protoplanet struck Earth and ejected debris that reassembled in orbit. As the surface cooled, water vapor condensed to form the earliest oceans, and volcanic outgassing produced the primordial atmosphere. The oldest known mineral grains, **zircons**, date to about 4.4 billion years ago.

**Archean (4.0–2.5 billion years ago).** The crust cooled enough for the first stable continents and shallow seas. Whether modern **plate tectonics** (horizontal motion of rigid crustal plates) operated this early is debated; some evidence points to a "stagnant-lid" style, in which the crust formed a single static shell that lost heat through vertical melting and resurfacing rather than through horizontal plate motion. By 3.5 billion years ago Earth's magnetic field had established itself, shielding the atmosphere from the solar wind. Distinctive Archean rocks include **banded iron formations** (alternating iron- and silica-rich layers precipitated from ancient seawater) and **greenstone belts** (alternating volcanic and sedimentary metamorphic rocks representing early island arcs).

**Proterozoic (2.5 billion to 539 million years ago).** This eon has a much richer record, including the earliest-known supercontinent, **Rodinia**, which began breaking apart around 750 million years ago. Its fragments later recombined into **Pannotia** (about 600–540 million years ago) and eventually Pangaea (much later, in the Permian). The Proterozoic also brought the first major glaciations, culminating in the "Snowball Earth" glaciations of the Neoproterozoic, when ice may have reached the equator.

## Phanerozoic: the last 539 million years

The Phanerozoic contains nearly all the fossil record that geologists use to subdivide time. It is divided into three eras.

### Paleozoic (539–252 million years ago)

Pannotia was breaking apart as the Paleozoic opened. Six periods record the assembly of Pangaea: Cambrian, Ordovician, Silurian, Devonian, Carboniferous, and Permian. The late Ordovician saw a mass extinction when Gondwana drifted over the South Pole and triggered an ice age. By the Carboniferous, continents had collided to form the Hercynian, Alleghenian, and Ural mountain belts, and vast coal swamps flourished in tropical lowlands. By the Permian, essentially all of Earth's landmass had merged into the single supercontinent Pangaea, surrounded by the global ocean Panthalassa.

### Mesozoic (252–66 million years ago)

Pangaea rifted apart, first into a northern half (Laurasia) and a southern half (Gondwana), then into the continents we recognize today. The Atlantic Ocean opened as Europe and Africa separated from the Americas, and the Tethys Sea gradually narrowed. Shallow seas flooded continental interiors, depositing thick chalk beds—especially in the Cretaceous, which produced more chalk than any other period. The era ended with the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction 66 million years ago.

### Cenozoic (66 million years ago to today)

India's collision with Asia raised the Himalayas, and Africa's collision with Europe closed the Tethys, leaving the Mediterranean as its remnant. Around 40 million years ago, as Australia separated from Antarctica, the Antarctic Circumpolar Current began isolating the southern continent, cooling it permanently and initiating the present pattern of ice ages. The Pliocene brought the Isthmus of Panama, which redirected warm equatorial currents and contributed to Atlantic cooling. The current ice age began about 2.58 million years ago; polar ice has since advanced and retreated in cycles lasting 40,000 to 100,000 years. The last glacial period ended roughly 10,000 years ago, raising global sea level about 35 meters as the great ice sheets melted.
