# Southern Ocean

The Southern Ocean is the body of water that encircles Antarctica, generally defined as the seas south of 60° S latitude. It is the second-smallest of the five oceans, but disproportionately important: it drives a global ocean-circulation system, supports one of the most productive ecosystems on Earth, and is governed by an international framework built for peace and science.

## How the ocean is defined

The Southern Ocean's official name and northern boundary were debated for most of the 20th century. The International Hydrographic Organization (IHO) first mapped it in 1928, dropped it in 1953, and voted in 2000 to define it as the waters south of 60° S, a line that roughly follows the northern edge of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current. That 2000 definition is widely used, including by Australia, though the IHO never formally adopted it because member states could not agree on the name. The 60° S boundary is unusual: rather than running along a coastline, it cuts across open ocean and is set by currents, not land.

## Physical setting and circulation

Geologically, the Southern Ocean is the youngest of the oceans. About 30 million years ago, Antarctica and South America drifted apart, opening the Drake Passage. This gap let a current form that now flows eastward around the entire continent: the Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC), the longest ocean current in the world, carrying roughly 130 million cubic metres of water per second.

Two features make the ocean a climate engine:

- The Antarctic Convergence is a narrow zone where cold, northward-flowing surface water sinks beneath warmer subantarctic water. The sinking injects oxygen into the deep ocean and concentrates nutrients near the surface.
- Antarctic Bottom Water forms under sea ice, becoming cold, dense, and salty enough to sink to the ocean floor and spread across the global abyss. This sinking limb is a key part of thermohaline circulation, the global conveyor of ocean water driven by differences in temperature and salinity.

The ACC and these sinking water masses connect the Southern Ocean to every other ocean on Earth.

## Ecosystem

The cold, sunlit surface waters of the Southern Ocean are extraordinarily productive, fuelled by nutrients brought up at the Antarctic Convergence and by large-scale upwelling. The base of the food web is phytoplankton, microscopic plants that feed nearly everything else.

The keystone species is Antarctic krill (*Euphausia superba*), a small crustacean with a total biomass of about 500 million tonnes. Krill feeds whales, seals, penguins, fish, and seabirds. Emperor penguins breed on winter sea ice. Crabeater seals are the most abundant seals in the region.

Many fish belong to the suborder Notothenioidei and produce antifreeze proteins that stop their blood from freezing. One species, the crocodile icefish, has blood that lacks haemoglobin, the molecule that carries oxygen in most animals.

## Human use and threats

The Antarctic Treaty, signed in 1959, froze territorial claims, banned military activity, and reserved the continent for peaceful scientific research. Two later instruments extend this protection to the sea: the Southern Ocean Whale Sanctuary, which prohibits commercial whaling south of 40° S, and the Convention for the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources (CCAMLR), which manages fisheries and ecosystems.

The economy is small: a limited fishery, mostly for krill and Patagonian toothfish, and a network of research stations that double as the ocean's main ports.

The main threats are environmental. The ozone hole has increased ultraviolet radiation at the surface, reducing phytoplankton productivity. Illegal, unreported, and unregulated (IUU) fishing for toothfish is estimated at five to six times the legal catch, and long-line methods also kill large numbers of seabirds. Climate change poses a deeper risk: Antarctic Bottom Water formation is sensitive to warming and sea-ice loss, and a substantial slowing of this overturning circulation would alter weather and marine ecosystems worldwide.

## Open questions

The international community has yet to agree on a single, formal name and northern boundary for the Southern Ocean; until then, organisations choose their own definitions. It is also still unclear how much the ocean's overturning circulation will weaken as the climate warms, and what the consequences will be for the rest of the planet.

The deepest point surveyed is 7,434 m, measured in 2019 by the Five Deeps Expedition in the South Sandwich Trench, a candidate name for which is the Factorian Deep.
