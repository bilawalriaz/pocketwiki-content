# Sea

The sea is the single connected body of saltwater that covers 71% of Earth's surface and holds 97.2% of all known water. Because every ocean is joined, heat, salt, dissolved gases, nutrients, and pollutants can travel between basins on currents that take centuries to complete a circuit. This interconnection is what lets the sea regulate climate, support marine life, and constrain human activity at the same time.

## What counts as a "sea"

In casual use, "sea" means either the whole global ocean or a smaller, partly enclosed basin such as the Mediterranean or the Red Sea. The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) calls all of it "sea," including the Atlantic, Pacific, Indian, Southern, and Arctic divisions. Smaller seas are usually partly landlocked, but the Sargasso Sea is bounded only by currents, not by coastlines.

## Water itself

Seawater has a volume of about 1.335 billion cubic kilometres and an average salinity of 35‰ (parts per thousand), meaning 35 grams of dissolved solids per litre. Sodium chloride makes up roughly 85% of those solids. Salinity shifts with evaporation, which leaves salt behind, with precipitation and river inflow, which dilute it, and with ice formation, which rejects salt into the surrounding water.

Temperature spans more than 30 °C at sunny tropical surfaces down to about −2 °C beneath polar ice, while deep water sits in a narrow −2 °C to 5 °C range. The sea is slightly alkaline, with surface pH near 8.2. Dissolving carbon dioxide forms carbonic acid, which has pushed average pH below 8.1 in a process called ocean acidification.

## Movement

Surface currents are pushed by wind and deflected by the Coriolis effect (the curving of moving objects caused by Earth's rotation) into large circular systems called gyres that redistribute heat between the equator and the poles. Beneath them, the thermohaline circulation sinks cold, salty, dense water near the poles and slowly carries it through every ocean basin over roughly 1,000 years, regulating long-term climate and oxygen distribution.

Waves are generated by wind friction and can occasionally exceed 25 metres. Tsunamis, usually set off by underwater earthquakes, landslides, or impacts, move fast in deep water and only build height as they enter shallow coastal zones. Tides are a separate rhythm, driven by the gravitational pull of the Moon and Sun and producing roughly two cycles per day.

## The seabed

The sea floor is made of oceanic crust, dense basalt 5–10 km thick that rests on tectonic plates floating on the underlying mantle. At mid-ocean ridges, plates pull apart and magma rises to form new crust, while at subduction zones one plate dives beneath another, creating deep trenches and volcanic island arcs. The Mariana Trench is the deepest point at 10.994 km below the surface. Moving shoreward, the seabed steps down through continental shelves, continental slopes, and the flat abyssal plains of the deep ocean.

## Human dependence and damage

People have crossed the sea since prehistory, with milestones such as Austronesian expansion (c. 3000 BC), Viking voyages, and 15th-century European circumnavigation shaping trade and culture. The 1872–1876 HMS Challenger expedition is credited with founding oceanography. UNCLOS sets the rules, including the 200-nautical-mile Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) that gives coastal states rights to offshore resources. The sea underpins fishing, aquaculture, offshore wind and tidal energy, and a global trade network worth more than $4 trillion a year.

That dependence is straining the system. Plastic debris concentrates in gyres, forming features like the Great Pacific Garbage Patch, while oil spills, agricultural nutrient runoff that starves coastal water of oxygen, and heavy metals accumulate through the food web. Industrial overfishing has cut some fish stocks by around 80%, and climate change is simultaneously warming the surface, lowering pH, expanding low-oxygen dead zones, raising sea level by about 1.8 mm per year, and shrinking Arctic ice.

Two unresolved questions frame the coming decades. The exact site where life first arose in the sea is still debated, with candidate environments ranging from sunlit surface water to deep-sea hydrothermal vents. The long-term damage from microplastics, deep-sea mining, and continued acidification depends on how quickly greenhouse-gas emissions fall, making the sea's future state a direct measure of cumulative policy choices made on land.
