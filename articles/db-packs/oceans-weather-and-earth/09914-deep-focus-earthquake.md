# Deep-focus earthquake

A deep-focus earthquake is one whose hypocenter—the underground point where rupture starts—lies deeper than 300 km. Shallow quakes rupture brittle rock near the surface; at 300 km depth rock is hot and pressurised enough to deform plastically, like putty, rather than snap. That deep quakes happen at all is a standing puzzle in seismology, and they are now used as natural probes of the deep Earth.

They occur almost exclusively at convergent plate boundaries, where oceanic lithosphere dives beneath another slab along a dipping band of seismicity called the Wadati–Benioff zone. They are also called plutonic earthquakes. Because their seismic waves pass up through the upper mantle and variable crust only once, body waves lose less energy than those from shallow events, producing sharp peaks on seismograms while surface waves stay weak.

The first scientific hint came in 1922 from Herbert Hall Turner; in 1928 Kiyoo Wadati proved that quakes occur well below the lithosphere. The deepest event on record is a magnitude 4.2 quake beneath Vanuatu in 2004 at 735.8 km; the strongest is the magnitude 8.3 Okhotsk Sea earthquake of 2013 at 609 km, with an unconfirmed aftershock of the 2015 Ogasawara earthquake reported at 751 km.

## Why deep quakes are puzzling

Shallow quakes are explained by brittle fracture and frictional slip on a fault plane: stress builds, rock cracks, energy releases. At 300–700 km the pressure–temperature conditions should make rock flow plastically, so something else must let it fail suddenly. Four proposals share roughly equal standing, except the oldest, which is largely rejected.

- **Solid–solid phase transition (largely discredited).** The olivine-to-spinel transition near 410 km changes volume; if metastable olivine in a cold slab suddenly converted, the implosion could source a quake. Deep quakes lack the strong isotropic signature such an implosion would produce, so this idea is mostly set aside.
- **Dehydration embrittlement.** Water-bearing minerals release fluid as the slab sinks, raising pore pressure. That lowers the effective normal stress on a fault and lets it slip where dry rock would creep. Most dehydration reactions finish by 150–300 km, so this likely matters in the upper deep zone and probably not much beyond about 350 km.
- **Transformational (anticrack) faulting.** In a fine-grained shear zone of metastable olivine, a phase change to wadsleyite or spinel at roughly 320–410 km localises shear along planes of weakness, and slip runs like a shallow quake. The slab must be very cold and nearly dry for olivine to survive that deep.
- **Shear instability / thermal runaway.** Plastic deformation heats the rock; if heat cannot escape, the hot zone weakens further, strain concentrates, and a runaway feedback may rupture the slab, possibly with local partial melting. No clear natural or laboratory example has been documented.

## How deep quakes reveal slab stress

The moment tensor, drawn as a "beachball" diagram, records the source type: an isotropic implosion, a double-couple from slip on a planar fault, or a compensated linear vector dipole from uniform shortening. Deep quakes mix these, and the mix changes with depth. Below 400 km, down-dip compression dominates, meaning the slab is squeezed along its length as it sinks. Between about 250 and 300 km—a depth range where earthquake counts also dip—stress is closer to down-dip tension, with the slab being pulled apart.

## Where the deepest quakes happen

The deepest, most active belt runs from Papua New Guinea through Fiji to New Zealand, where the Pacific plate plunges beneath the Australian, Tonga, and Kermadec plates. Quakes of magnitude 4.0 or greater occur almost daily, with depths past 735 km. Other major deep zones are the western Pacific (Pacific plate under the Okhotsk and Philippine Sea plates, site of the 2013 Okhotsk event), the Philippines and Indonesia (events to 675 km), and the Andes, where the Nazca plate sinks beneath South America and produced the 1994 Bolivia quake (Mw 8.2, 631 km) and the 1970 Colombia quake (Mw 8.0, 645 km).

A few anomalous deep zones show the picture is not uniform. Beneath Granada, Spain, the Alboran slab—oceanic lithosphere subducted unusually fast by the African and Eurasian plates and flipped over, trapping water-rich rock—produces quakes to roughly 600–630 km, with dehydration of hydrated magnesium silicates a likely cause. The Tyrrhenian Sea west of Italy hosts deep events clustered around 250–300 km beneath a region that stopped subducting tens of millions of years ago, a fossil slab still failing under internal stress. Northeastern Afghanistan records quakes to 400 km from the Indian plate diving under Eurasia, and the South Sandwich Islands see events to 320 km from the South American plate descending beneath the South Sandwich plate.
