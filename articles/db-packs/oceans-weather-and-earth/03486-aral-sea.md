# Aral Sea

The Aral Sea was the world's third-largest lake, an endorheic (closed-basin) salt lake straddling Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan. Between the 1960s and 2014 it shrank to about 10% of its original size as the two rivers that fed it were diverted to irrigation. The collapse is the clearest modern case of destroying a large lake by cutting off its inflows.

## Why it shrank

The Aral Sea had no outlet. Its only inputs were the Amu Darya and the Syr Darya, and its only output was evaporation. Soviet planners in the 1960s diverted both rivers into canals to irrigate desert for cotton, then called "white gold." By 1960, 20 to 60 km³ of water that would have reached the sea each year was instead being sent to farmland. Engineer Aleksandr Asarin warned as early as 1964 that the lake was being written off as a deliberate cost of state agricultural plans. Between 1960 and 1998 the sea's surface area fell 60% and its volume fell 80%. As the water thinned, salinity climbed from 10 g/L to roughly 30 g/L by 1990.

The crisis is therefore a deliberate hydrological redirection, made irreversible by the basin's lack of an outflow: once the rivers are gone, no mechanism can refill the lake.

## What broke

The lake had low overall biodiversity but harboured many endemic fish subspecies, including all native sturgeon species and the Aral trout. Rising salinity and habitat loss drove these to local extinction. Salt-tolerant herring and gobies were introduced in the 1960s as a substitute fishery, but their populations later collapsed too.

The receding shoreline left seabed coated in salt and in agricultural and industrial pollutants. Wind lifted this mixture into dust storms, which the source links to high rates of cancer, respiratory illness, and child mortality in surrounding communities. The regional climate shifted toward hotter summers, colder winters, and more dust events. A fishing industry that once employed about 40,000 people vanished; the port of Moynaq in Uzbekistan now sits tens of kilometres from open water.

## The split

As the lake shrank it fragmented, which marked the collapse and created the conditions for partial recovery:

- 1987: the sea split into the North Aral Sea and the South Aral Sea.
- 2003: the South Aral divided into an eastern and a western basin.
- 2005: the Dike Kokaral, a 13 km dam built with Kazakh and World Bank support, sealed the North Aral off from the South.
- 2007: the whole system was at 10% of its 1960 size.
- 2008: with the dike holding, the North Aral's level rose about 12 m, salinity fell, and fish populations began to recover, restarting a small fishery. A second, smaller dike is planned to push water closer to the Kazakh port of Aralsk.
- 2009: the southeastern basin of the South Aral disappeared.
- 2014: NASA imagery confirmed the eastern basin had fully dried into the Aralkum Desert.

The North Aral today supports a recovering freshwater and introduced fish community. The South Aral, largely in Uzbekistan, remains hypersaline, sustains only halotolerant organisms such as brine shrimp, and has been left to desiccate. Uzbekistan is exploring oil on the exposed bed and has largely abandoned restoration there, focusing instead on planting vegetation on the dried seabed to reduce dust.

## Debates and limits

The International Fund for Saving the Aral Sea (IFAS) coordinates regional programmes (ASBP-1, -2, -3) covering water management, environmental protection, and socio-economic recovery, but the central tension is between upstream agricultural water use and downstream ecological needs. Grand proposals to refill the basin by diverting water from Siberian rivers such as the Ob and Irtysh remain technically and politically unfeasible. Restoration has succeeded only in the smaller, politically cooperative northern lobe.
