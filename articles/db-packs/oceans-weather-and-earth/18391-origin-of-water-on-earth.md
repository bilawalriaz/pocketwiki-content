# Origin of water on Earth

Earth is the only known planet with stable liquid water on its surface, because it orbits in the habitable zone, the range of distances from the Sun where surface temperatures let water remain liquid. The ocean mass today is about 1.37 × 10²¹ kg, roughly 0.023 % of Earth's mass (6.0 × 10²⁴ kg), with another 5 × 10²⁰ kg tied up in ice, groundwater, rivers, lakes, and atmospheric vapor. Far larger quantities are stored inside the planet: the mantle can hold about three modern oceans' worth of water bound in hydrated silicates, and the core may hold another four to five oceans' worth of hydrogen.

## Where the water came from

Three families of explanation dominate the debate.

*Built in from the start.* Hydrogen inside the early Earth could have combined with oxygen during the planet's hot accretion to make water directly. Miozzi et al. (2025) showed experimentally that hydrogen dissolves readily into a magma melt at around 4000 K and, when it reduces iron oxide, generates large amounts of water. Work in 2020 on enstatite chondrites, a class of stony meteorite once thought dry, also showed they contain enough hydrogen to fill the oceans several times over, raising the possibility that Earth accreted already wet.

*Delivered by asteroids.* The region of the protoplanetary disk inside the frost line, the distance beyond which water could condense as ice (now 2.7–3.1 AU), was too hot (500–1500 K) for water to condense, so Earth could not have formed with its water already in place. Beyond that boundary, ice condensed onto planetesimals. The strongest chemical match to Earth's ocean comes from carbonaceous chondrites, especially CI and CM subtypes, whose hydrogen and nitrogen isotopes closely match seawater. Eucrite chondrites from asteroid Vesta also match. One hypothesis places the delivery at 4.5 billion years ago, when Earth was 60–90 % built; another, the late veneer model, places it after the Moon-forming impact, but it requires the late-arriving material to have been very water-rich, since less than 1 % of Earth's mass was added after the Moon formed.

*Delivered by the impact that made the Moon.* Budde et al. (2019) argued from molybdenum isotopes that the Earth's mantle received material from the outer Solar System during the giant impact with Theia, the Mars-sized body whose collision with the proto-Earth is thought to have formed the Moon about 4.5 billion years ago. If Theia originated beyond the frost line, it would have brought water with it.

Comets are a poor fit: D/H ratios (deuterium-to-hydrogen) in comets Halley, Hyakutake, Hale–Bopp, C/2002 T7 (LINEAR), 8P/Tuttle, and 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko are roughly twice that of Earth's seawater, so models assign them less than 10 % of Earth's water. Comet 103P/Hartley 2 is an exception whose D/H matches Earth's, but its nitrogen isotopes do not. Earth's ocean D/H is precisely (1.5576 ± 0.0005) × 10⁻⁴. No known process lowers this ratio, but lighter hydrogen preferentially escapes to space during atmospheric loss, so the ratio has risen 2–9 times since formation. Subsurface hydrogen trapped in recently erupted lava is about 218 ‰ richer in deuterium than the modern ocean, suggesting the primordial Earth was wetter still. Venus, whose runaway greenhouse effect boiled away its surface water, shows the same loss mechanism as a strongly elevated D/H ratio.

## Timeline of liquid water

The Moon-forming impact around 4.5 billion years ago would have vaporised much of Earth's crust and upper mantle, producing a rock-vapor atmosphere that condensed within about two thousand years. The leftover hot volatiles formed a CO₂-rich atmosphere at high pressure that may have allowed liquid water even at a surface temperature near 230 °C. As the planet cooled, CO₂ was drawn down by subduction and dissolution in seawater.

Three independent geological clocks push the appearance of liquid water back near the planet's birth:

- Detrital zircons from the Jack Hills of Australia show that liquid water and an atmosphere existed 4.404 ± 0.008 billion years ago.
- Pillow basalts, rocks whose characteristic bulbous shapes only form when lava erupts underwater, from the Isua Greenstone Belt in Greenland indicate liquid oceans 3.8 billion years ago.
- Rocks from the Nuvvuagittuq Greenstone Belt in Quebec are dated 3.8 billion years old by one study and 4.28 billion years old by another, and show signs of contact with water at those ages.

No earlier direct oceanic evidence has survived, probably because plate tectonics and crustal recycling have erased it. The cool early Earth hypothesis and the possibility of plate tectonics as far back as 4 billion years ago allow surface water at temperatures consistent with the zircon evidence.
