# Geologic record

The geologic record is the full stack of rock layers (strata) at and below Earth's surface, together with the fossils and environmental signals those layers preserve. Strata form in two ways: settling of weathered debris such as clays and sands, and accumulation of volcanic ash or lava. As fresh material buries older material, the layers harden into a solid rock column that records the planet's past climate, geography, geology, and the evolution of life.

## Law of superposition

The working principle is the law of superposition: in any undisturbed stack, younger sedimentary and volcanic layers sit on top of older ones. At a single outcrop the column gives a cross section of local natural history covering ages, epochs, or sometimes several geologic periods. The full record is never preserved in one place, because a region that once collected sediment as a low basin can later be uplifted into a ridge and eroded away. Tectonic folding and faulting that raise one block generally lower a neighbouring block into a structural basin, where new layers accumulate. By comparing formations, structures, and especially layers that are geographically widespread, geologists have stitched a nearly complete record together since the 17th century. Sediment cores drilled at the mouths of large river systems, some reaching 7 miles (11 km) deep, support this picture.

## Correlation and biostratigraphy

When the local sequence is broken, geologists correlate it with distant, better-exposed sequences. The core technique is biostratigraphy, which uses diagnostic fossils to match layers of the same or different geologic ages. In South Carolina three marker species of fossil algae appear in a rock core, while in Virginia only two of the three occur in the Eocene Series (37.2–55.8 million years ago). The absent species marks an early part of the middle Eocene that is missing in Virginia. The two surviving marker species still let geologists correlate the early Eocene and late middle Eocene layers between the two states and place the local column into its slot in the global record.

## Hierarchy of rock and time units

Geologists organise both the physical layers and the time spans they represent into a matched hierarchy:

| Rock unit (chronostratigraphy) | Time unit (geochronology) | Defined units and duration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eonothem | Eon | 4 total; half a billion years or more |
| Erathem | Era | 10 defined; several hundred million years |
| System | Period | 22 defined; tens to ~100 million years |
| Series | Epoch | 38 defined; tens of millions of years |
| Stage | Age | 101 defined; millions of years |
| Chronozone | Chron | subdivision of an age; not used by the ICS timescale |

Pairing a physical layer with its corresponding slice of geologic time turns a stack of rocks into a dated history of Earth.

## Discordances and incomplete preservation

Discordances, the gaps left when erosion, non-deposition, or tectonic activity removed layers that exist elsewhere, appear throughout the record. Cyclic sediments such as the Oxfordian limestones and marls near Tavannes, Switzerland, record repeated 200,000-year environmental cycles visible as alternating colour bands. A million-year South Atlantic sediment core taken by the research vessel Polarstern shows light and dark layers driven by Quaternary climate swings.
