# Earthquake early warning system

An earthquake early warning (EEW) system is a network of seismic sensors, computers, and communication links that detects an earthquake seconds after it begins and broadcasts an alert before the strongest shaking arrives. This is not prediction: the ground has already started moving, and the alert simply outruns the shockwaves. As of January 2026, six territories operate nationwide public systems (Japan, Taiwan, South Korea, China, Israel, Transnistria), several others run regional or app-based versions, and global smartphone-based systems add coverage where dedicated infrastructure is absent.

## Why a few seconds of warning are possible

An earthquake begins at a hypocentre and tears outward along the fault at a speed slower than the seismic waves it produces. Two wave types matter. The P wave (a compressional pressure wave) arrives first; it is fast but usually too small to feel. The S wave (a shear wave) travels more slowly but carries the large, destructive motion that damages buildings, especially structures roughly eight storeys tall whose natural resonance matches the wave periods. Because radio and cellular signals travel far faster than either wave, sensors close to the hypocentre can transmit a detection ahead of the S wave's arrival at more distant sites. Local soil conditions can amplify or reduce shaking, so regional models refine the expected intensity before alerts are issued.

The head start is small. Near the epicentre there may be almost no warning; locations tens to over a hundred kilometres away can receive ten to twenty seconds, occasionally more. That is enough time for trains to brake, surgeons to pause, gas valves to close, fire-station doors to open, and people to take cover.

## Detection algorithms and newer signals

Traditional EEW algorithms estimate magnitude and location from the first few seconds of P-wave data. Peru's coastal SASPe system issues its first alert using only three seconds of P-wave data from the nearest station, with 111 strong-motion stations spaced about 30 km apart. False alarms remain a persistent problem: in October 2024, Israel issued a nationwide alert when sensors mistook controlled explosions in southern Lebanon for an earthquake's initial waves.

Researchers are exploring complementary signals. After the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake, scientists observed prompt elastogravity signals (PEGS), tiny changes in Earth's gravity field that propagate at the speed of light, far faster than seismic waves. PEGS-based detection remains experimental but could, in principle, lengthen lead times for very large events.

## How alerts reach people

A detected event is pushed through public channels. Cell Broadcast delivers a one-to-many text to every compatible phone in an area without requiring an app. Television and radio interrupts, sirens, and public-address systems reach broader audiences. Dedicated apps such as MyShake, Earthquake Network, BhuDev, Kandilli Uyarı, and Indonesia's InaEEWS reach opt-in users. Since 2020, Google's Android system has used the accelerometers inside millions of stationary, charging phones to form a global crowdsourced detector.

The Android system illustrates both promise and pitfalls. In the 2023 Turkey–Syria earthquake, Google's algorithm estimated the magnitude at 4.5–4.9 instead of 7.8, so most users received a mild "Be Aware" notification instead of the full-screen "Take Action" alert. About 469 high-priority alerts went out where roughly 10 million should have. The system was disabled in Brazil in February 2025 after a false warning reached São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro.

## Nationwide and regional deployments

Japan's JMA-operated system, partly based on the 1990s UrEDAS system Japan Railways used to brake Shinkansen trains, was tested from 2004 and became the first nationwide public EEW on 1 October 2007. Taiwan followed in 2014, developed by the Central Weather Administration after the 1999 Chi-Chi earthquake killed over 2,400 people. South Korea became the third in 2015. Israel operationalized its TRUAA system in 2022, and China completed its nationwide network in 2024, with 16,000 monitoring stations managed by 3 national, 31 provincial, and 173 prefectural centres. Transnistria became the sixth in 2025.

Mexico's SASMEX is the oldest operating public system: Mexico City broadcast the first public EEW in 1993 after the 1985 earthquake, and Oaxaca joined in 2000; cell-broadcast capability was added in 2025–2026. The United States' ShakeAlert went live in California, Oregon, and Washington between 2018 and 2021, with Alaska and Nevada in development. Canada's system, built on shared software with ShakeAlert, launched in British Columbia in August 2024 and expanded to Quebec and Ontario in November 2025. India operates a state-level system in Uttarakhand. Italy's system, launched in June 2025, covers only the Rome–Naples high-speed line and automatically slows or stops trains. Romania reserves its system for critical infrastructure in Bucharest. Guatemala, El Salvador, Nicaragua, and Costa Rica, cooperating through the Swiss-supported ATTAC project, deployed app-only systems between 2020 and 2024. Indonesia and Turkey are in alpha testing with limited user bases.

EEW works only where the fault geometry, sensor density, and communication latency allow detection to precede the S wave. Coverage is densest along the Pacific Ring of Fire and in countries that have suffered recent deadly quakes, and it remains absent across much of Africa, Central Asia, and the Middle East, where catastrophic earthquakes could strike with no automated notice.
