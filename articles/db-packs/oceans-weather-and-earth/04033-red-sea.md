# Red Sea

The Red Sea is a narrow tropical waterway, roughly 2,250 km long and 355 km at its widest, wedged between Africa and the Arabian Peninsula and joined to the Indian Ocean through the Bab-el-Mandeb Strait. It is a young ocean in the making, one of the busiest shipping lanes on Earth, and one of the warmest and saltiest seas anywhere.

Geologically, the Red Sea is a rift valley flooded by seawater. The Arabian Peninsula is slowly pulling away from Africa along the same tectonic boundary that has already torn East Africa open and that geologists expect will eventually split a new ocean basin from the continent. About 40% of the sea is shallower than 100 m, so much of this rifting sits in accessible water. The seabed shows what that future looks like: hot, super-salty brine pools and metal-rich muds vented from the rifting crust, plus oil and gas trapped in folded layers beneath Miocene salt deposits (Miocene: a geological epoch roughly 23 to 5 million years ago).

Intense evaporation, almost no rainfall, and minimal river inflow give the Red Sea exceptional warmth and salinity. Seasonal monsoon winds, blowing from opposite directions in summer and winter, reverse the surface currents. The central basin is nearly tideless, though currents squeeze hard through narrow straits.

The Red Sea hosts more than 1,200 fish species, roughly 10% of which are found nowhere else, and extensive coral reefs that remain among the world's healthiest because human pressure is comparatively low. Reefs take three main forms: fringing reefs clinging to the shore, offshore atolls ringing central lagoons, and seagrass meadows that feed dugongs and turtles. Egypt's Ras Mohammed National Park is a key protected area. Since the Suez Canal opened in 1869, more than 1,000 marine species have migrated from the Red Sea into the Mediterranean, one of the largest natural marine range shifts on record.

Egyptian expeditions reached the Red Sea around 2500 BCE, and in the 6th century BCE Persian king Darius I cut a canal linking the Nile to the sea. A 1st-century Greek sailor's handbook, the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*, mapped its ports. The Suez Canal opened in 1869, roughly halving the sea route between Asia and Europe and turning the Red Sea into a chokepoint that today carries about 12% of global trade and 30% of all container traffic.

Since late 2023, that chokepoint has become a war zone. Houthi forces in Yemen, responding to the Gaza conflict, have attacked commercial and naval vessels, and shipping through the Suez Canal fell by about 75% in 2024. Vessels now divert around the Cape of Good Hope, lengthening voyages and raising fuel use and CO₂ output. Strikes have also produced oil spills, notably the sinking of the MV Rubymar, and chemical leaks that threaten the reefs the sea is famous for. The episode has shown how regional conservation bodies such as PERSGA lose capacity once active conflict begins.

The name itself is uncertain: it may come from seasonal blooms of the cyanobacterium *Trichodesmium erythraeum*, which can tint surface water reddish, or from an older linguistic habit in which "red" simply meant "south."
