# Water cycle

The water cycle is the continuous movement of water on, above, and below Earth's surface, driven by solar energy and gravity. Water shifts between liquid, solid, and vapor phases as it moves among four main reservoirs: oceans, ice, fresh water, and the atmosphere. Total mass stays roughly constant, but the partitioning shifts with climate.

## How water moves

Solar energy lifts water into the atmosphere through three pathways: evaporation from oceans, lakes, and soil; transpiration from plants (combined with evaporation called evapotranspiration); and sublimation from snow and ice. Water vapor rises because its molecules are lighter than the major atmospheric gases, nitrogen and oxygen. As vapor climbs into cooler, lower-pressure air, it condenses into droplets that form clouds. Ground-level condensation is fog. Droplets grow, collide, and fall as precipitation: mostly rain, but also snow, hail, sleet, and graupel.

Atmospheric circulation redistributes moisture globally. Atmospheric rivers, narrow corridors of concentrated vapor, advect large water volumes over long distances. Water that falls on land returns to the ocean through surface runoff, river channel flow, and subsurface flow through soil and aquifers (underground layers that hold groundwater). Infiltration is the entry of surface water into the ground; percolation is its downward movement through soil and rock under gravity. Shallow groundwater can return to streams and lakes within months to years; deep groundwater may sit underground for millennia. Some of it, fossil water, is older than 10,000 years. Groundwater exits through surface discharge, springs, or direct seepage into the sea.

## The ocean's role

The ocean dominates the system. Of Earth's roughly 1,386,000,000 km³ of water, about 1,338,000,000 km³ (97%) sits in the oceans. The ocean is the source of 86% of global evaporation and receives 78% of global precipitation. Evaporation exceeds precipitation over the ocean, and precipitation plus runoff returning to the sea exceeds evapotranspiration over land; atmospheric transport balances the two. Globally, about 505,000 km³ evaporates and a matching 505,000 km³ falls as precipitation each year. Of those totals, 434,000 km³ of evaporation and 398,000 km³ of precipitation occur over the oceans.

The remaining 3% is freshwater, most of it locked in ice. Ice caps, glaciers, and permanent snow hold about 24,064,000 km³, roughly 1.7% of all water but 68.7% of freshwater. Surface fresh water in lakes and rivers is a tiny share.

## Residence times

Residence time is the average time a water molecule spends in a reservoir, estimated by dividing the reservoir's volume by its inflow or outflow rate. The range is enormous. Atmospheric vapor lasts about 9 days before falling. Soil moisture and rivers turn over in 1–6 months. Lakes take 50–100 years. Shallow groundwater sits for 100–200 years; deep groundwater, up to 10,000 years. Ice sheets are the longest-term store: ice in Antarctica has been dated to 800,000 years before present.

## Energy and chemistry

Phase changes move enormous quantities of heat. Evaporation absorbs the latent heat of vaporization from the surface, cooling it; condensation and freezing release that heat aloft. This vertical heat transport, combined with ocean currents, carries warmth from the tropics toward the poles. Evaporation also purifies water by leaving salts and other dissolved matter behind, which is why atmospheric condensation is the planet's primary source of freshwater. Flowing water simultaneously transports minerals, erodes landscapes, and reshapes geology through weathering, erosion, and deposition.

## Human pressures

Two distinct forces reshape the cycle.

Land use alters local hydrology. Urbanization and agriculture replace porous soils with impervious surfaces, raising surface runoff and cutting infiltration. Deforestation lowers local soil moisture, evaporation, and rainfall. Dams change natural flow timing and habitat, while stormwater and sewage infrastructure reroutes water across the landscape. Groundwater is being pumped out faster than aquifers replenish, an unsustainable drawdown in many regions.

Climate change intensifies the whole cycle. A warmer atmosphere holds more water vapor, because saturation vapor pressure rises about 7% per 1 °C of warming (the Clausius-Clapeyron relationship). The result is heavier rain and snow events when they occur, longer droughts between them, and shifting precipitation patterns. The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report and earlier assessments confirm these changes have been observable since at least 1980 and will strengthen through the 21st century, with cascading effects on ecosystems, freshwater supplies, agriculture, and the frequency of extreme weather.

Runoff transports nearly all eroded sediment and phosphorus from land to water bodies, which is why fertilizer carried by the Mississippi River fuels the Gulf of Mexico dead zone. Nitrogen, carbon, and dissolved salts ride the same pathways, linking the water cycle to every other biogeochemical cycle.
