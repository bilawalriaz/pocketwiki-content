# Foliation (geology)

Foliation is the repetitive, sheet-like layering found in many metamorphic rocks. The name comes from the Latin *folium*, meaning "leaf," because each layer can be as thin as a sheet of paper or thicker than a metre. Technically, foliation is any penetrative planar fabric in a metamorphic rock, meaning the layering runs through the body of the rock rather than sitting on its surface.

The layering is produced by directed pressure during metamorphism. In simple cases it forms perpendicular to the maximum principal stress, so a foliation surface records the direction of shortening the rock experienced. In sheared zones, however, the planar fabric can rotate away from that perpendicular orientation because of mass transport and shortening within the shear.

Two physical processes create the fabric. The first is mechanical rotation of platy minerals already present in the rock, especially micas and clays, which spin into alignment under stress. The second is the growth of new platy minerals, typically micas, during prograde metamorphism, the progressive heating and deformation as a rock is buried and squeezed. Retrograde metamorphism, the cooling and decompression that follows when a metamorphic belt is unroofed, generally does not produce foliation because the compressive stress that drives mineral alignment is absent. Thermal metamorphism in the hot aureole around a granite intrusion similarly lacks strong directed stress, so new micas tend to overprint an existing foliation rather than create one.

Foliation may run parallel to original sedimentary bedding, but more often it lies at an angle to it. The familiar prograde sequence of mudrocks illustrates the full range: slate shows an extremely fine grained alignment of clay flakes called slaty cleavage; phyllite has small mica flakes that give a silky sheen known as phyllitic luster (from Greek *phyllon*, also "leaf"); schist shows coarse, visible mica flakes; and gneiss displays compositional banding produced by metamorphic differentiation, the segregation of minerals into distinct light and dark layers. Other named examples include schistosity, the flattened pancake-shaped clasts of a metaconglomerate, and S-tectonite, the term for foliated rock within sheared masses.

Foliation requires differential pressure or shear. Rocks metamorphosed without either do not develop it and are called nonfoliated, with quartzite as a common example. Foliation is not limited to metamorphic rocks. Igneous rocks can carry a planar fabric when cumulate crystals align during convection in large magma chambers, when frictional drag against wall rocks shears a viscous granite, or when a viscous felsic lava preserves a flow foliation, including the compressed eutaxitic texture of welded tuffs and pyroclastic surge deposits.

Because foliation forms perpendicular to the principal stress, it records the orientation of shortening and the axis of folds, whose axial-planar foliation lies within the axial region. Measuring the intersection between a fold's axial plane and any planar surface gives the fold plunge. Where a foliation does not match the plunge observed in a fold, it usually belongs to a different deformation event. In shear zones and along thrust faults the foliation also indicates transport direction: the acute angle of intersection, or the way foliations curve into a shear, points the way material moved.

At a regional scale, foliations bend around rigid, incompressible bodies such as granite plutons, so they are not strictly planar everywhere and can locally violate the rule of perpendicularity to the regional stress. This megascopic deflection mirrors what happens at the microscopic scale around porphyroblasts, large new crystals that grow within the foliation. In geotechnical engineering a foliation plane is a discontinuity that introduces anisotropy into a rock mass, governing its strength and deformation in tunnels, foundations, and slopes.
