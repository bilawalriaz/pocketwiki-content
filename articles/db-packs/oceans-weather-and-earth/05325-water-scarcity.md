# Water scarcity

Water scarcity is the lack of freshwater available to meet demand in a region. Global freshwater is physically abundant: only about 0.014% of Earth's water is both fresh and easily accessible, totalling around 14,000 cubic kilometers, of which humanity uses roughly 5,000. Scarcity is therefore a problem of distribution in time and place, and of the capacity to deliver water, not absolute planetary shortage.

## Two main types

**Physical water scarcity** occurs when natural supplies are insufficient to meet all demands, including the water ecosystems need to function. It is characteristic of arid and densely populated regions such as Central Asia, West Asia, and North Africa, where available water can fall below 1,000 cubic meters per person per year. Symptoms include severe environmental degradation, declining groundwater, and allocations that favour some groups over others. Physical scarcity can also appear where water seems abundant but resources are over-committed, for example through overdevelopment of irrigation or energy infrastructure.

**Economic water scarcity** occurs where natural supplies exist but investment, infrastructure, technology, or institutional capacity is lacking to draw and deliver water from rivers and aquifers. A quarter of the world's population lives under economic scarcity, much of it in sub-Saharan Africa, where people often travel long distances to fetch water that may be unclean. The United Nations Development Programme calls economic scarcity the most common form worldwide, because most affected regions physically have enough water but cannot provide it accessibly. Around a fifth of the global population lives under physical scarcity, so the two categories together cover roughly half the world.

## Measurement

The **Falkenmark Water Stress Indicator** sets thresholds on annual renewable water per person: below 1,700 cubic meters signals water stress, 1,000 to 1,700 indicates periodic shortages, and below 1,000 marks scarcity. A broader approach used for Sustainable Development Goal 6 is the water stress ratio, defined by the FAO as total freshwater withdrawn by all sectors divided by total renewable freshwater resources after environmental flow requirements, the minimum flows needed to sustain freshwater and estuarine ecosystems. Below 10% indicates low stress, above 80% very high.

These indicators have limits. They average across countries and years, ignore green water (soil moisture), water quality, and uneven precipitation, and do not capture how easily households or governments can actually access water. Canada and Brazil have high renewable water yet face serious local problems. Since the early 2000s, assessments have grown more sophisticated, incorporating green-blue water scarcity, footprint-based metrics, and integrated quantity–quality models that combine hydrology, ecosystem science, and social science.

## Scale and trajectory

About half the world's population experiences severe water scarcity for at least part of the year, and around half a billion people face it all year round. Half of the world's largest cities experience scarcity, and 2.3 billion people live in countries with per-capita supplies below 1,700 cubic meters per year. A 2016 study traced those affected rising from 0.24 billion (14% of global population) in 1906, to 3.8 billion (58%) in 2004, to 6.2 billion (75%) by 2025. The World Economic Forum projects global water demand will exceed supply by 40% by 2030.

## Causes and contributing factors

Scarcity is driven by a mismatch between where and when water is needed and where and when it is available. Population growth (from under 4 billion in 1970 toward a projected 9 billion by 2050), rising meat consumption, expansion of irrigated agriculture, urbanisation, and industrial supply chains all push demand upward. Scientists have found that population change is roughly four times more important than long-term climate change in driving scarcity.

Groundwater supplies nearly half of all drinking water worldwide. Aggregate abstraction is about 1,000 cubic kilometers per year, of which 67% goes to irrigation, 22% to domestic use, and 11% to industry, with India, China, the United States, Pakistan, Iran, and Bangladesh among the largest users. Many aquifers are over-pumped: cities such as Mexico City, Bangkok, Beijing, Chennai, and Shanghai have seen water tables drop 10 to 50 meters, and a study found 6 to 20% of roughly 39 million groundwater wells are at high risk of running dry if local levels fall by only a few meters. Over half of Earth's wetlands have been lost in the past century, the Aral Sea has lost more than 58,000 square kilometers in three decades, and in the United States subsidence from groundwater pumping has affected more than 17,000 square miles across 45 states.

Climate change intensifies these pressures by raising evaporation, shifting precipitation, reducing snowpack and glacier storage, and making both droughts and floods more frequent. A 2019 study by more than 200 researchers projected that Himalayan glaciers, which feed the Ganges, Indus, Brahmaputra, Yangtze, Mekong, Salween, and Yellow rivers, could lose 66% of their ice by 2100, affecting the 2.4 billion people in their drainage basins. Water pollution adds a quality dimension: contamination drives diarrheal and waterborne diseases that account for roughly 80% of illnesses globally, and 2.4 billion people live with contaminated water and poor sanitation.

## Responses

Because scarcity is a mismatch problem, responses span supply and demand. **Supply-side options** include expanding reservoirs and pipelines, treating and reusing wastewater, and desalination, though desalination is energy-intensive. **Demand-side management** improves efficiency across agriculture, industry, and households and is often more cost-effective than large infrastructure projects. **Virtual water trade** shifts the water embedded in food and goods from scarce to water-rich regions, a pattern already embedded in globalised supply chains. **Cooperation between countries** sharing rivers is increasingly important; the dispute between Egypt and Ethiopia over the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam escalated in 2020, with Egypt treating the dam as an existential threat to its Nile allocation.

Australia shows that decoupling water use from economic growth is possible: between 2001 and 2009, national water consumption fell by 40% while the economy grew by more than 30%. Sustainable Development Goal 6, target 6.4, calls by 2030 for substantial increases in water-use efficiency, sustainable withdrawals, and reductions in the number of people suffering from scarcity, targets that more than half the world is currently far from meeting.
