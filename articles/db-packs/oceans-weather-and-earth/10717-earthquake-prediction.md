# Earthquake prediction

Earthquake prediction means specifying the time, location, and magnitude of a future earthquake tightly enough to justify immediate public action. It sits at the demanding end of a continuum: probabilistic *forecasting* (long-term hazard estimates such as "a 10% chance in 50 years") is broadly useful, while *prediction* requires enough certainty to warrant drastic measures. No prediction has been demonstrated to succeed beyond chance.

## Why prediction is so hard

Earthquakes occur because tectonic stress slowly deforms rock until a fault slips, releasing energy as seismic waves in the *elastic rebound* cycle described by Reid in 1910. A single large earthquake displaces rock by only meters, but accumulated across centuries produces the hundreds of miles of offset visible along major faults.

Two features defeat prediction. First, earthquake occurrence is clustered in space and time rather than random. In southern California, about 6% of M≥3.0 quakes are followed by a larger one within 5 days and 10 km; in central Italy, 9.5% within 48 hours and 30 km. Analyses assuming a random *Poisson process* (events occurring independently at a constant average rate) are misled, because simple clustering-based guesses "predict" around 5% of earthquakes, better than chance. Second, the Earth may behave as a *self-organized criticality*, a system that naturally evolves toward a threshold where any small disturbance can cascade into a large event, so rupture is governed by tiny heterogeneities along the fault. Repeated null results since the 1970s optimism that "routine prediction is on the verge" have pushed most seismologists toward viewing prediction as either fiendishly difficult or fundamentally impossible.

## The social cost of false alarms

A 1997 cost-benefit study in Greece found earthquakes kill fewer than ten people per year on average, mostly in identifiable unsafe buildings, while Greek highways kill more than 2,300. Stiros argued the money would save more lives if spent on building upgrades and road safety. Missed warnings have led to criminal liability (the 2009 L'Aquila manslaughter convictions) and political purges (after the 1976 Tangshan earthquake). False alarms also carry costs: 30+ inaccurate unofficial warnings in China during the late 1990s triggered new regulations and erode public trust in future alerts.

## The two research approaches

Researchers pursue two strategies. *Precursor methods* look for anomalous signals before an earthquake, aiming at short-term prediction (hours to months). *Trend methods* look for statistical patterns in *seismicity* (the distribution of earthquakes in space, time, and magnitude), aiming at long-term (10–100 years) or intermediate-term (1–10 years) forecasting.

### Precursors

About 400 reports of roughly 20 precursor types, ranging from radon emission to animal behavior, appear in the literature; none has proven reliable. The 2011 International Commission on Earthquake Forecasting for Civil Protection (ICEF) found "considerable room for methodological improvements." Many reports are contradictory, lack amplitude measurements, or are unsuitable for statistical testing, and published results are biased toward positive findings.

Radon spikes have been linked to earthquakes up to 1,000 km away, months later, and across all magnitudes; ICEF found "no significant correlation." A 2018 review of animal behavior across 130+ species found no evidence of warning hours, days, or weeks in advance. Apparent animal precursors likely reflect foreshocks (smaller quakes preceding a main event) and the *flashbulb memory effect*, the tendency for emotionally charged events to make coincidental details feel significant afterward. The dilatancy–diffusion hypothesis of the 1970s predicted that rocks would expand, change the *Vp/Vs ratio* (the ratio of the faster P-wave speed to the slower S-wave speed, sensitive to cracking), and alter electrical resistivity before failure; it collapsed under failed replications and the recognition that laboratory rock samples do not behave like fault zones.

### Trends and patterns

The *characteristic earthquake* model holds that individual fault segments rupture quasi-periodically with similar magnitudes, with Parkfield, California as the test case. Six quakes (1857, 1881, 1901, 1922, 1934, 1966) suggested recurrence every ~22 years, predicting the next by 1988 (95% confidence by 1993). The actual M 6.0 event came on 28 September 2004, a decade late, undermining the quasi-periodic claim. The *seismic gap* model, which expects the next big quake in quiet segments, underlies long-term hazard maps like California's UCERF3, though tests show it "did not forecast large earthquakes well." The M8 algorithm issues "Time of Increased Probability" warnings, indicating a large quake is more likely within a broad area and time window, and produced an unfulfilled 2004 Southern California TIP; a 2008 study judged it no better than guessing from historical rates. *Accelerating moment release*, the hypothesis that foreshock energy ramps up exponentially before a mainshock, is now seen as a statistical artifact of how curves are fitted.

## Notable predictions and their fates

The 1975 Haicheng earthquake (M 7.3) is the most cited "success," yet a 2006 study concluded there was no official short-term prediction, the evacuation was probably spontaneous after a M 4.7 foreshock, and about 2,041 people still died. The 1985–1993 Parkfield experiment deployed one of the densest instrument networks ever and caught nothing until a decade after the predicted window. The VAN method (1981–present) claims to predict quakes from geoelectric "seismic electric signals"; an unprecedented 1996 public peer review found it flawed, and a 2020 review concluded it produces too many false positives to be usable. The Brady (1981 Lima), Browning (1990 New Madrid), and Giuliani (2009 L'Aquila) cases ended with "NO PASÓ NADA," a quiet December, and manslaughter convictions, respectively.

## The current picture

Most seismologists treat short-term prediction as unachieved and possibly unachievable. Some hold that prediction might emerge from sustained study of non-seismic precursors, and machine-learning work since 2017 (neural networks predicting aftershock locations, random forests forecasting fault failure from acoustic signals) represents a developing frontier. The decisive evidence against the "intrinsically impossible" claim would be a single reproducible, tightly specified, validated prediction, a demonstration the field has been unable to produce.
