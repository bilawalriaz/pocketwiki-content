# Niger River

The Niger River is West Africa's principal river, running about 4,180 km (2,600 mi) from the Guinea Highlands to the Gulf of Guinea in a distinctive boomerang shape. It is the world's 14th-longest river and Africa's third-longest, after the Nile and the Congo. Its drainage basin — the land area from which all water drains into the river — covers 2,117,700 km² across ten countries. The countries of Niger and Nigeria both take their names from the river.

## An unusual course

The Niger's boomerang shape puzzled Europeans for centuries. The river flows northeast from the Guinea Highlands into the Sahara, then turns sharply southeast toward the Gulf of Guinea. Geologists attribute this path to stream capture, in which one river erodes headward and diverts the flow of another; the ancient upper Niger was captured by the lower Niger, creating the bend. Europeans long confused the river with the Nile or the Senegal. Mungo Park became the first European since antiquity to see the middle Niger in 1796, and the Lander brothers traced its full course to the ocean in 1830.

## Hydrology and key features

The Inner Niger Delta in Mali is a defining feature, where the river's gradient drops sharply and it fans into a vast network of braided streams, marshes, and lakes. The river loses nearly two-thirds of its flow there to evaporation and seepage, yet the area is highly productive for fishing, farming, and pastoralism. The main tributary is the Benue River, which joins the Niger in Nigeria. The river carries little sediment because its source lies in ancient, resistant rocks.

## Human history

The fertile Niger bend region drew early settlement after the Sahara began drying around 3500 BC, at the end of the African humid period. Yams, African rice, and pearl millet were domesticated in the region around 2000 BC, and cities like Djenné-Djenno flourished. The river was the artery of trans-Saharan trade, fueling the Ghana, Mali, and Songhai Empires and carrying Islam into West Africa by the 14th century. Local names reflect this heritage: *Joliba* ("great river" in Manding) and *Kwara* (Hausa). The European name "Niger," first published by Leo Africanus in 1550, possibly derives from a Berber phrase meaning "river of rivers," though the exact origin is debated.

## Modern pressures

Late 20th-century dams such as Sélingué in Mali and Kainji in Nigeria generate hydropower and supply irrigation, but the basin faces mounting stress from increased water abstraction, additional dam construction, climate change, and pollution. The Niger Basin Authority coordinates management across the ten riparian countries, promoting sustainable use of a river system on which tens of millions depend.
