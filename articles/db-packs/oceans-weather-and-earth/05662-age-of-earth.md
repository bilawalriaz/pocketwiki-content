# Age of Earth

Earth is 4.54 ± 0.05 billion years old. The age marks the final stages of Earth's accretion (the gradual accumulation of planetesimals into a planet) and planetary differentiation (the separation of core, mantle, and crust) during the early Hadean eon, and is established by radiometric dating of meteorites, cross-checked by lunar samples and the oldest known terrestrial minerals.

## Why earlier estimates fell short

Before radiometric dating existed, attempts to date Earth relied on cooling rates, sediment accumulation, or ocean salinity, all of which assumed Earth loses heat steadily and changes at constant rates. Newton (1687) modeled a cooling iron sphere and got about 50,000 years; Buffon (1779) measured a cooling globe and got 74,832 years. Charles Lyell popularized uniformitarianism, the idea that geological change is slow and steady, but without a clock this produced only relative ages.

In 1862, William Thomson (later Lord Kelvin) calculated 20–400 million years from the time a fully molten Earth would need to cool to its present surface heat gradient. His calculation missed two things: convection in the mantle, which keeps deep rock hot far longer, and radioactive decay, which continuously generates new heat. Kelvin also estimated the Sun at about 20 million years old based on gravitational collapse, conflicting with the time Darwin's evolution required. By 1903, after the Curies showed radium melts its own weight in ice in under an hour, geologists including George Darwin and John Joly recognized that internal heat was being replenished, and Kelvin's cooling argument collapsed.

## Radiometric dating

A radioactive isotope is an unstable variant of an element that decays into a stable "daughter" element at a fixed rate, the half-life, the time for half the atoms in a sample to decay. By measuring the ratio of parent isotope to daughter product in a rock, and knowing the half-life, the time since the rock solidified can be calculated. Common pairs include uranium decaying to lead and potassium-40 decaying to argon. A nuclide is an atom identified by its number of protons and neutrons. Melting resets the clock because daughter atoms escape, so the date records the rock's last solidification.

Rutherford proposed helium accumulation as a clock in 1904 and dated a sample to 40 million years. Bertram Boltwood then used the uranium-to-lead ratio and in 1907 published ages up to about 2.2 billion years for 26 samples. Arthur Holmes continued lead dating through the 1910s and 1920s, facing resistance from geologists who distrusted physicists. By 1931, a U.S. National Research Council committee, largely written by Holmes, declared radiometric dating the only reliable method for a geological time scale.

## Why meteorites give the best answer

Earth's rocks are poor record-keepers of Earth's birth because plate tectonics, weathering, and hydrothermal circulation continuously mix the crust and mantle, so no terrestrial rock has remained a closed system (a sample that has neither gained nor lost parent or daughter atoms) since formation. Meteorites, by contrast, are leftover planetary building blocks that have not been reprocessed. The Canyon Diablo meteorite contains three distinct mineral phases, sulfide, nickel-iron metal, and silicate, in which lead and uranium are segregated very differently. This large separation between parent and daughter concentrations makes lead-lead dating far more precise.

In 1956, Clair Cameron Patterson used uranium-lead dating on the Canyon Diablo meteorite and found 4.55 ± 0.07 billion years, very close to today's accepted value. Hundreds of other meteorite analyses cluster between 4.53 and 4.58 billion years; that ~50-million-year spread is interpreted as the time it took the solar nebula to collapse and accrete into the Sun and planets. Lunar rocks, dated to 4.51 billion years, and calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions (CAIs) in meteorites at 4.5673 ± 0.00016 billion years, the oldest known solid Solar System material, cross-check the figure. CAIs also set a lower limit for the age of the Solar System, since Earth's accretion began soon after they formed; model estimates of accretion's duration range from 30 to 100 million years. The oldest surviving terrestrial minerals, zircon crystals from the Jack Hills of Western Australia, are at least 4.404 billion years old, and Archaean lead ores independently yield about 4.54 billion years.
