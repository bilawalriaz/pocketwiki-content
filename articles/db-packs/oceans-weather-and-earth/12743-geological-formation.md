# Geological formation

A geological formation is a body of rock with a consistent set of physical characteristics, called its lithology, that distinguishes it from the rock bodies next to it and occupies a specific position in the stratigraphic column, the vertical sequence of rock layers exposed in a region. Formations are the fundamental unit of lithostratigraphy, the study of rock layers.

A formation must be large enough to be mapped at the surface or traced underground, but thickness is not part of the definition: a formation can be less than a meter thick or several thousand meters thick. Formations are usually, though not always, tabular in shape, and they may consist of a single rock type, alternating beds of two or more rock types, or a heterogeneous mixture, provided the package is recognisably distinct from the bodies above and below it.

Lithology includes chemical and mineralogical composition, texture, color, primary depositional structures (features formed as the sediment was laid down), and fossils only when they behave as rock-forming particles, such as coal or kerogen (solid organic matter in source rocks). Pure fossil taxonomy is not a valid basis for defining a formation. Boundaries are drawn where lithology changes enough to justify a new unit, and the threshold of contrast varies with the geological complexity of the region. Formations cannot be defined by any criterion other than lithology, though biostratigraphic, chronostratigraphic, and chemostratigraphic units exist as separate classifications based on fossils, rock age, and chemical signatures.

## Naming and formal designation

Formations are named after a permanent natural or artificial feature in the area where they were first described, followed by the word "Formation" or a rock-type term. The Morrison Formation, named for the town of Morrison, Colorado, and the Kaibab Limestone, named for the Kaibab Plateau of Arizona, are classic examples. Each name must be unique, and the first published use has permanent precedence over later attempts to reuse it.

A formal designation requires a stratotype, normally a type section: a single, well-exposed outcrop that ideally shows the full thickness of the formation. Where no complete exposure exists or where lateral variation is significant, reference sections are added. Long-established formations that predate modern codification, and non-tabular units such as volcanic masses, may use a type locality, a geographic point, instead of a type section. The describing geologist must record enough detail for any later worker to recognise the unit unambiguously.

## Examples

Uluru (Ayers Rock) in Australia rests on the Mutitjulu Arkose, a formation made almost entirely of one rock type, arkosic sandstone. The Summerville Formation shows the opposite pattern: thin alternating beds of mudstone and sandstone cut by veins of gypsum, three lithologies in one package. On the Colorado Plateau in southeastern Utah, formations from top to bottom include the Navajo Sandstone (rounded tan domes), the red layered Kayenta Formation, the cliff-forming Wingate Sandstone, the slope-forming Chinle Formation, the lighter-red Moenkopi Formation, and the white Cutler Formation.

## Relationships and uses

Formations can be grouped into larger units called groups, or subdivided into smaller, lithologically distinctive members that need not be mappable at full formation scale. The formation concept lets geologists correlate rock layers across continents by matching lithology between distant outcrops, so the same body of rock can be traced from one exposure to another even when buried in between.

Formations were originally used as the primary markers of relative geologic time, because the law of superposition (younger layers sit on top of older ones) lets their order be read directly from the stratigraphic column. The divisions of the geological time scale were assembled in the 18th and 19th centuries using this approach.

The concept has deep roots: Abraham Gottlob Werner used the term in his theory of the Earth's origin from 1774 until his death in 1817. The term was progressively formalised through the 20th century, culminating in codes such as the North American Stratigraphic Code of 2005, with equivalents in other regions.

Geological maps that show where each formation reaches the surface are central to structural geology, because the geometry of folded, faulted, and tilted formations reveals the tectonic history of a region and points toward likely locations for buried mineral resources.

## Where the concept applies and where it does not

Formations can be usefully defined for sedimentary rocks, low-grade metamorphic rocks, and volcanic rocks. Intrusive igneous rocks and highly metamorphosed rocks are generally excluded and are described instead as lithodemes, units defined by similar lithology but without stratigraphic position.

Informally, the word "formation" is also used for shapes produced by erosion or deposition, such as stalactites and stalagmites in caves, and these informal formations persist only as long as the geologic agent that produced them remains active.
