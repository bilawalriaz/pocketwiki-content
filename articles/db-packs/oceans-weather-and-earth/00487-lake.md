# Lake

A lake is a body of standing water sitting in a basin on land. Lakes look permanent on a human timescale but are temporary on geological ones, because every basin eventually either fills with sediment or breaks open and drains. They cover about 2.5 million km², under 2% of Earth's surface, yet hold almost all of its liquid surface freshwater. Most are freshwater, but some inland basins lose more water to evaporation than they receive from rivers, so salts build up and the lake becomes saline, in extreme cases saltier than the sea. Endorheic basins have no outlet; exorheic basins drain to the sea, which is why the first concentrate salts and the second stay fresh.

## How lakes form

Hutchinson's 1957 classification, still the standard, groups lakes into eleven major types by origin. Tectonic lakes sit in basins warped or broken by crustal movement: rift lakes such as Baikal occupy down-faulted valleys, while ancient uplifted sea floors host the Caspian and Aral seas. Crustal extension in dry regions produces endorheic basins where the Great Salt Lake and the Dead Sea concentrate brines. Volcanic lakes fill craters, maars, or calderas; Crater Lake in Oregon dropped into the hole left by Mount Mazama around 4860 BC. Glacial action is the most common sculptor worldwide, because the last ice age gouged, dammed, and buried most high-latitude basins of the Northern Hemisphere. Fluvial lakes include the crescent-shaped oxbow lakes that get stranded when a meandering river cuts off a bend. Solution lakes drop into dissolved limestone (karst), landslide lakes form when rock or mud blocks a valley, and aeolian lakes sit in basins excavated or dammed by wind. Shoreline processes seal off estuaries with sand bars; plants and animals, especially beavers, create small organic lakes; and impacts leave round crater lakes that preserve long, undisturbed sediment records. People add reservoirs, which behave ecologically like natural lakes.

## Layering and mixing

Water is densest at 4 °C, and that quirk structures lake physics. In temperate lakes, the warm surface layer (epilimnion) floats above a cold bottom layer (hypolimnion), separated by a sharp transition (metalimnion or thermocline). Twice a year the surface cools to 4 °C, the layers equalize in density, and the lake turns over, sending oxygen to the depths. Lakes that mix at least once a year are holomictic; meromictic lakes have a deep layer that never mixes, runs out of oxygen, and lays down undisturbed annual sediment couplets called varves. In the tropics, where surface water never reaches 4 °C, deep lakes stratify permanently and their bottoms can go anoxic and CO₂-saturated. Rare landslides or quakes can break that stratification and trigger a limnic eruption, releasing a wave of CO₂ that flows downhill; in 1986 this asphyxiated villages near Lake Nyos in Cameroon.

Nutrient supply controls productivity. Oligotrophic lakes are clear and nutrient-poor; eutrophic lakes are rich and bloom with algae; hypertrophic lakes, often fed by fertilizer runoff, suffer oxygen crashes.

## Living zones

Limnology, the study of inland waters, divides a lake into zones. The shallow, sloping littoral is rooted in plants; the open, well-lit photic zone is dominated by phytoplankton; the dark profundal or benthic zone sits over the sediment. Light penetration is measured with a Secchi disk, a 20 cm black-and-white plate lowered until it vanishes, giving the Secchi depth, a proxy for turbidity from algae, detritus, and suspended sediment. A simple food-web rule: removing top predatory fish lets small fish flourish, which graze down zooplankton, which releases algae from control, a trophic cascade.

## Climate moderators and casualties

Because water has a high specific heat (about 4,186 J·kg⁻¹·K⁻¹), large lakes warm and cool slowly, generating daytime sea breezes and nighttime land breezes that soften local climate. Varved sediments and relict shorelines act as thermometers for past climate. That record is now being disrupted. Between 1990 and 2020, more than half of the world's large lakes shrank, driven by climate change and water diversion. Permafrost thaw drains hundreds of large Arctic lakes in western Siberia as the frozen ground that sealed them melts. The Aral Sea, starved of river inflow by irrigation, has fragmented into saline remnants. Climate-driven evaporation can shrink lakes even where rivers still flow.

## Disappearance

Most lakes fill in gradually: sediment and plant debris accumulate, reeds advance, peat forms, and an open lake succeeds to marsh, then to forest. Shallow lakes with many herbivorous fish disappear faster; clear, deep ones last longer. Some disappear in a moment: in 2005 Lake Beloye in Russia drained in minutes through a subsurface collapse into a nearby river. In karst terrain, lakes appear and vanish seasonally as groundwater rises and falls, and in deserts a single heavy rain can briefly fill a playa before summer heat evaporates it away.

## Lakes beyond Earth

Titan, Saturn's largest moon, is the only world with confirmed surface lakes, holding liquid ethane and methane rather than water. Kraken Mare spans about 400,000 km², roughly five times the area of Lake Superior, and Ligeia Mare is about half that. Jupiter's moon Io may hold lakes of molten sulfur in its volcanic calderas. Mars has one confirmed subglacial lake near its south pole and widespread geological traces of ancient surface lakes that have since dried.

## Records that frame the range

The Caspian Sea, at 371,000 km², is the largest lake by area; Lake Baikal, 1,637 m deep and holding 23,600 km³ of water, is the deepest, the most voluminous freshwater lake, and at roughly 25–30 million years the oldest, which is why it hosts an unmatched set of endemic species. The Dead Sea, at 418 m below sea level, is the lowest point on any lake's surface. Manitoulin Island in Lake Michigan–Huron, at 2,766 km², is the largest island in a lake, and on it Lake Manitou is the largest lake on an island in a lake. Despite the catalogue, fewer than a hundred large lakes hold more than 99% of all lake water on Earth, because volume scales faster than area.
