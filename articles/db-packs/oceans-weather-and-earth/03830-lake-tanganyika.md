# Lake Tanganyika

Lake Tanganyika is an ancient African Great Lake that holds about 16% of the world's available surface fresh water, making it the second-largest freshwater lake by volume and the second-deepest. It is the largest rift lake in Africa, formed inside the Albertine Rift, where continental crust is being pulled apart. That tectonic setting explains its extraordinary depth, narrow shape, and isolation, all of which shape its biology. Four countries share the lake, and millions of people depend on it for protein.

## Formation and Shape

The lake formed in three stages between roughly 9 and 2 million years ago: the central basin first (9–12 million years ago), then the northern basin (7–8 million years ago), then the southern basin (2–4 million years ago). Its great age is the key to everything else, because it gave evolution time to work.

The lake is long and narrow, with a mean depth of 572 m and a maximum depth of 1,471 m. The water is alkaline, with a surface pH of about 9, and strongly stratified, meaning it forms stable layers that do not mix. The deep water is anoxic (oxygen-free) and contains hydrogen sulfide, so aerobic life is confined to roughly the upper 100–250 m. Over 90% of the water input comes from direct rainfall. The Lukuga River, the main outlet, flows west into the Congo system, but it only runs when lake levels are high, so the lake is often described as "practically endorheic" (effectively without an outflow). The Ruzizi River, a major northern inflow, took shape only around 10,000 years ago.

## Biodiversity and Endemism

Because the lake is old, deep, and ecologically layered, it has become one of the clearest examples of adaptive radiation, the rapid splitting of one lineage into many species that exploit different niches. At least 250 cichlid species (a family of spiny-finned fishes) are endemic, meaning found nowhere else on Earth, and they form the most morphologically and genetically diverse cichlid assemblage in Africa. These fish range from algae-scrapers and other herbivores to large predators, occupying nearly every feeding role available in the lake.

The open-water pelagic zone is dominated by two endemic species known as "Tanganyika sardines," which in turn feed four endemic *Lates*, predatory perch-like fish. Invertebrate diversity matches this pattern: 65 endemic snail species, many with thick, sculptured shells that resemble those of marine snails (a form called thalassoid), and over 200 crustacean species, with crabs and shrimp showing especially high endemism.

## Human Use and Recent History

Swahili-Arab traders ran caravan routes to the lake's shores in the mid-19th century. European exploration began in 1858, when British explorers Richard Burton and John Speke became the first known Westerners to reach the lake. David Livingstone explored its shores from 1866 to 1873, meeting Henry Morton Stanley at Ujiji on the eastern shore in 1871. In the late 19th century, Britain, Germany, and Belgium carved the surrounding territory into colonies. During World War I, British forces fought a notable naval campaign here, even transporting two motor boats overland to challenge German control of the lake.

Major commercial fishing began in the mid-1950s and now supplies 25–60% of the animal protein for the regional population, though it has also driven declines in fish stocks. Since the 1990s, the lake's water level has risen by about 2 metres, flooding shoreline areas and displacing communities. Researchers have also debated whether Tanganyika once connected to Lake Rukwa, Lake Malawi, or the Nile, and how some deep-water cichlids survive in the near-anoxic depths remains unclear.
