# Environmental geology

Environmental geology applies the principles of geology to environmental problems caused by human activity. It overlaps with engineering geology, hydrogeology, and environmental geography, and concerns the interaction of people with the biosphere, lithosphere, hydrosphere, and atmosphere. As population and industry expand, the strain on natural systems makes this field increasingly central.

## Scope

Environmental geology covers the management of geological and hydrogeological resources (fossil fuels, minerals, surface water, groundwater, and land), the study of the Earth's surface through geomorphology and edaphology, the definition and mitigation of natural hazards, the management of industrial and domestic waste, and work that often involves litigation.

## Hydrogeology and groundwater

Hydrogeology is the study of groundwater in soil and rock. The main groundwater concern is pollution, the impairment of groundwater by heat, bacteria, or chemicals. Surface sources such as fertilizers, leaking sewers, polluted streams, and mining or mineral wastes contribute most. Environmental geology addresses this through monitoring objectives: determining the nature, extent, and degree of contamination; characterizing the propagation mechanism and hydrological parameters; detecting movement into critical areas; assessing countermeasures; recording data for long-term evaluation; and validating the models those countermeasures rely on.

## Soil science

Soil science treats soil as a surface natural resource. With rising population, rising per capita food consumption, and land degradation, soil preservation is a concern. Environmental geology contributes through soil surveys, which assess properties such as erodibility, depth, slope, shrink-swell potential, rock outcrop frequency, salinization risk, fertilizer response, and crop variety. The results feed land-use planning, agriculture, and forestry.

## Mining

Mining has been practiced since the Stone Age but is restricted to locations where a deposit is economically viable, and ends when the deposit is exhausted. Modern practice reduces impacts, yet accidental releases of harmful metals, processing chemicals, and reactive waste still contaminate the surrounding environment. Common effects include rock displacements that release fine dust into surface waters, landscape defacement, and large volumes of reactive waste. Environmental geology supports pre-mining impact assessment, mandatory environmental management plans, and post-extraction rehabilitation. In Brazil and Australia, by law, mined sites must be rehabilitated after operations cease.

## Recycling and reuse

Resources fall into three classes: nonrenewable, potentially renewable, and perpetual. Nonrenewable resources such as fossil fuels and metals are finite and are being depleted faster than they form, yet remain central to heating, transport, and infrastructure. Environmental geology supports recycling and reuse to stretch supply and cut waste. Practices include composting, upcycling, freecycling, and industrial ecology, the dismantling of large artifacts for reuse as inputs.

## Land use

Land-use planning reduces hazard exposure and limits human impact on land, water, air, and resources. New sites are needed for mining, waste disposal, and industry, and approval depends on demonstrating barriers that stop contaminant migration. Site investigation has two phases. The orientating phase uses maps and archived data on topography, vegetation, settlements, transport, climate, hydrology, groundwater, geology, and ecology. The detailed phase collects field data on stratigraphy, groundwater flow and conductivity, geochemistry, geotechnical stability, natural hazards such as faults, karst, earthquakes, subsidence, and landslides, and any past human disturbance. Environmental geology produces land-use maps that guide settlement away from flood zones, unstable ground, and wildfire risk, and that buffer rivers with vegetation.
