# Deposition (geology)

Deposition is the geological process by which sediments, soil, and rocks are added to a landform. Wind, ice, water, and gravity transport weathered surface material, and where the fluid loses enough kinetic energy for gravity and friction to overcome it, the material settles and builds up in layers. The term also covers sediment built from organic matter, such as plant debris that becomes coal under anaerobic conditions, and from chemical processes, such as the calcium carbonate skeletons of marine plankton, whose accumulation drives diagenesis and the further deposition of calcium carbonate in chalk.

The central idea for predicting where a grain ends up on a shore is the null point hypothesis, proposed by Cornaglia in 1889. Each grain size migrates across the shore profile until the wave- and flow-driven forces exactly balance gravity. That balance point is the null point. Coarse grains require more hydraulic energy to move than fine grains, so coarse sediment stays in the high-energy upper shore while fine sediment is carried into the low-energy deep water, producing a seaward fining of the sediment.

Two physical principles produce this pattern. First, gravity: a grain's settling velocity controls where suspended fines come to rest, while a grain's internal angle of friction controls where larger grains lodge on the slope. Second, wave asymmetry. As each wave passes over a ripple on the seabed, the onshore stroke traps an eddy on the lee side; the offshore stroke lifts that eddy and its sediment cloud into the water column and pushes it seaward. Repeated for every wave, this moves finer material offshore and leaves a coarse lag behind.

The balance for a non-cohesive grain settling through still water is a force equality, with downward weight on the left and upward buoyancy plus fluid drag on the right:

(4/3)πR³ρₛg = (4/3)πR³ρg + ½ C_d ρπR² wₛ²

R is the grain radius, ρₛ and ρ are the densities of the grain and the fluid, g is gravitational acceleration, C_d is the drag coefficient, and wₛ is the settling velocity. C_d is found from the grain's Reynolds number, which indicates whether the flow around the settling particle is laminar, turbulent, or in between. For very small grains or high viscosities, Stokes' law gives the settling velocity directly. Bedload and saltating grains stop when bed shear stress and turbulence fall below what is needed to keep them moving; suspended grains stop after falling through the full water column.

Cohesive sediment, silt and clay smaller than 4φ on the phi grain-size scale, behaves differently. In fresh water these fines obey Stokes' law as individual particles, but seawater is a strong electrolyte. The face of a clay platelet carries a slight negative charge and its edge a slight positive charge, so when two platelets come close, face-to-edge electrostatic bonds form flocs. Each floc has higher mass and a higher fall velocity, so flocs deposit closer to shore than the same clay grains would as isolated particles, which is why muddy estuaries and tidal flats accumulate sediment quickly.

The null point pattern has been documented in the field. Akaroa Harbour on Banks Peninsula in New Zealand is a flooded volcanic caldera about 16 km long and 2 km wide, open to storm waves from the south but sheltered within. Hart et al. (2009) surveyed the seabed and ran sieve and pipette analyses on the subtidal sediment. Grain size decreases with depth, with distance from shore, and with distance along the harbour's central axis, moving from silty sands in the intertidal zone, through sandy silts in the inner nearshore, to silts in the outer bays, and to mud below about 6 m of water. The same winnowing by hydrodynamic energy has been recorded on the low-energy clayey flats of Bohai Bay in China, the moderate silty Jiangsu coast, and the high-energy sandy flats of The Wash in the United Kingdom, and in laboratory work by Ippen and Eagleson (1955), Eagleson and Dean (1959, 1961), and Miller and Zeigler (1958, 1964).

Where fines are repeatedly reworked, a related feature appears. Waves and currents winnow out the mud and silt, leaving shelly lag deposits of bivalve and gastropod shells, then heap those shells into chenier ridges along the tidal zone. Cheniers occur at many levels on the foreshore and characterise an erosion-dominated regime.

The null point theory describes a dynamic equilibrium, and many field and laboratory studies have failed to reproduce a true null point for every grain size at once, because coastal systems mix many interacting variables. Even so, grain-size analysis across a profile lets coastal managers and engineers estimate how shoreline changes such as beach nourishment, new coastal defence structures, or building consents will alter erosion and accretion, and theoretical or modelled results should be combined with direct observational data before any shore profile is modified.
