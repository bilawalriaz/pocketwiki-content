# Indus River

The Indus is a transboundary river that originates in Tibet as the Sengge Zangbo ("Lion's Mouth") and travels about 3,180 km north-west through Ladakh and Gilgit-Baltistan, then bends south into the Punjab plains of Pakistan before reaching a large delta on the Arabian Sea. Its flow depends on Himalayan and Karakoram glaciers and snowmelt, swelling each summer monsoon. The basin supports dense agriculture and one of the world's largest irrigation networks, making it central to Pakistan's food supply and to South Asia's water politics.

## Geological origin and course

The river is antecedent: it existed before the Himalayas and cut downward fast enough to keep its course as the mountains rose. Its upper valley still traces the Indus-Yarlung suture zone, the seam where the Indian plate collided with Eurasia about 50 million years ago. The five rivers of the Punjab (Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) once drained east into the Ganga; geological evidence shows they were captured into the Indus system only about five million years ago, which is why "Punjab" means "land of five rivers." Today those five merge into the Panjnad before joining the main stem. Main tributaries above the Punjab include the Zanskar, Shyok, and Kabul.

Eroded sediment from the Karakoram and Himalaya is carried to the Arabian Sea floor as the Indus submarine fan, the second-largest sediment body on Earth. The Indus is one of the few rivers to display a tidal bore, a wave that pushes upriver against the current.

## Historical role

By about 3300 BC the river's banks hosted the Indus Valley (or Harappan) Civilisation, a Bronze Age urban culture that built planned cities such as Harappa and Mohenjo-daro. The basin was later described in the Rigveda as the "Sapta Sindhu" and in the Avesta as "Hapta Həndu," both meaning "seven rivers." The word "India" derives from the river's name. The valley has repeatedly served as a corridor for movement and invasion, from Darius I of Persia, who sent the Greek explorer Scylax of Caryanda down the river around 515 BC, and Alexander the Great, to later Muslim and Mughal armies.

## Modern economy and infrastructure

In Pakistan the Indus irrigates the cotton, wheat, and sugarcane belts of Punjab and Sindh. The Indus Basin Irrigation System, fed by major structures such as the Tarbela and Mangla dams and a chain of barrages including Sukkur and Kotri, is the world's largest contiguous irrigation network. British engineers began adding modern canal works from 1850 onward, converting the floodplain into year-round farmland but also reducing the freshwater and silt that once reached the delta.

## The Indus Waters Treaty and current disputes

Because the basin spans India and Pakistan, water sharing has long been contentious. The 1960 Indus Waters Treaty, brokered by the World Bank, divided the system into the three "eastern rivers" (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) allocated to India and the three "western rivers" (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) allocated to Pakistan, while allowing limited Indian agricultural and non-consumptive uses on the western rivers. In 2025 India suspended the treaty after a terrorist attack in Kashmir, injecting uncertainty into agriculture and power generation in both countries.

## Ecology and modern stresses

The river supports over 180 freshwater fish species, including 22 endemics, plus the endangered Indus river dolphin and two otter species. The delta is a mangrove and fishery zone, but reduced downstream flow and sediment trapping behind dams have caused coastal erosion, salt-water intrusion, and the loss of farmland to salinisation.

Three pressures now dominate the basin's future. First, climate-driven glacial retreat in the Karakoram and Himalaya could reduce long-term dry-season flow once the ice reservoir thins, though heavy rainfall events tend to intensify, as in the 2010 floods that inundated large areas of Pakistan and the 2011 event when the Indus briefly re-entered India and recharged the Rann of Kutch. Second, untreated industrial and agricultural runoff pollutes stretches of the river and canal network. Third, large upstream diversions continue to starve the delta of fresh water and silt, shrinking mangroves and fisheries.
