# Cobble (geology)

A cobble is a rock clast defined by its size: 64–256 mm (2.5–10.1 in) across on the Udden–Wentworth scale, sitting between pebbles (4–64 mm) and boulders (256–4,096 mm). The same range maps to −6 to −8 φ on the Krumbein phi scale, where larger negative values mean coarser grains. Loose cobbles are classified as gravel; a lithified deposit made mostly of cobbles is a conglomerate. The word comes from *cob*, a rounded lump, related to German *Kopf*, "head." Chester Wentworth called them "cobble bowlders" in his 1922 paper that became the basis for the Udden–Wentworth scale.

## Competing size definitions

Before the Udden–Wentworth scale spread in the mid-twentieth century, anything over 2 mm was usually lumped together as gravel or stones. After the standard settled, agencies drew the cobble boundaries slightly differently:

| Source | Cobble diameter (mm) |
|---|---|
| Udden–Wentworth / Krumbein | 64–256 |
| ISO 14688 | 63–200 |
| British Standards Institution | 60–200 |
| US Department of Agriculture | 75–250 |

D. J. Doeglas proposed in 1968 to split cobbles into small (64–125 mm) and large (125–250 mm). Blair and McPherson argued in 1999 that the original scales favoured sand grains and proposed fine cobbles (64–128 mm, −6 to −7 φ) and coarse cobbles (128–256 mm, −7 to −8 φ). Blott and Pye suggested in 2012 that the cobble label be dropped entirely, replaced by "very small boulder" and "small boulder." None of these revisions has displaced the original 64–256 mm range in general use.

## Where cobbles occur and how they move

Cobbles accumulate where flowing water, ice, or gravity can move coarse fragments but not the largest boulders. In rivers they are most common in moderately steep mountain-valley streambeds. Glaciers transport cobbles with other sediment and deposit them as till; water-reworked till washes out finer sand and pebbles, leaving a lag of boulders and cobbles. Beaches covered with small to medium cobbles or pebbles, rather than sand, are shingle beaches. Glacially carried cobbles tend to have a tabular shape with diagonal striations on their sides.

Cobble conglomerates form in two main ways. Alluvial conglomerates sit in a matrix of gravel and coarse sand, because flowing water removes mud. Stone avalanches, a type of debris flow, also pile up cobbles, but their deposits are held together by a muddy matrix. In those avalanches, well-rounded cobbles travel farthest because they roll rather than grind, with lower friction than angular clasts of the same size.
