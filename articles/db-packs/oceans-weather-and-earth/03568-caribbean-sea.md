# Caribbean Sea

The Caribbean Sea is a tropical arm of the North Atlantic, framed by Central America, South America, and two curving island chains. It began forming roughly 160–180 million years ago during the Mesozoic Era, as the seafloor that became the Caribbean Plate pulled away from neighbouring crust. Today the sea covers about 2,754,000 km², drops to 7,686 m in the Cayman Trough, and holds roughly 9% of the world's coral reefs.

## A Sea Made by Two Arcs

The floor sits on the Caribbean Plate, caught between the North and South American plates. The squeeze built the islands in two stages. The older northern arc carries the four largest islands: Cuba, Hispaniola, Jamaica, and Puerto Rico. The younger Lesser Antilles arc to the east is still rising where the South American Plate slides beneath the Caribbean Plate, a subduction zone that feeds active volcanoes such as Mount Pelée on Martinique, whose 1902 eruption killed roughly 30,000 people. Underwater ridges divide the seafloor into five basins, and two oceanic trenches cut through the region, which is why earthquakes and tsunamis are routine: a magnitude 7.1 quake struck Haiti in 2010. The International Hydrographic Organization places Trinidad, Tobago, and Barbados in the Atlantic rather than the Caribbean because they sit on the South American continental shelf.

## Climate and Water

The sea straddles the tropics, so surface temperatures stay nearly uniform year-round and the surrounding climate runs from rainforest to savanna to arid scrub. A natural cooling phase that ended in 1974 gave way to steady warming, and the warmer water is now the main threat to the reefs. Tropical cyclones, hurricanes in local usage, arrive between June and November, drawing energy from the same warm surface that stresses the corals.

## Reefs and Biodiversity

The Caribbean holds about 9% of the world's coral reef, including the Mesoamerican Barrier Reef, the second largest on Earth; the Belize portion became a World Heritage Site in 1996. Reefs depend on a partnership: coral polyps host microscopic algae called zooxanthellae, which photosynthesise and feed the coral while giving it colour. When water stays too warm for too long the algae are expelled, the coral turns white, and it can starve; this is coral bleaching. The first modern Caribbean bleaching event began in 2005 and has intensified since, and a 2012 regional study detected heavy metals in coastal sediments. The region also has high biodiversity, with many species found nowhere else; several, including sea turtles and certain iguanas, are now endangered. The Convention for the Protection and Development of the Marine Environment of the Wider Caribbean Region, which came into force in 1986, was an early attempt to coordinate protection across the basin's many countries.

## People, Economy, and Pressure

Humans have reshaped the basin since 1492, when Columbus reached these waters. Sixteenth-century European cartographers named the body the "North Sea" to set it apart from the Pacific, the "South Sea," and the name stuck. The sea now supports three large industries: oil production, fishing, and tourism, with cruise ships and reef diving the largest earners. Rum distilling is a smaller but culturally defining product. The economic weight carries costs. As of 1993, only about 10% of sewage from Central American and Caribbean island states was treated before reaching the sea, a figure that captures the scale of nutrient and pathogen pollution still feeding the reefs. Heavy metals, untreated sewage, oil, and steadily warming water now act together on a reef system that supports the region's largest source of income.
