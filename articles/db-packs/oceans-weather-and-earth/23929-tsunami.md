# Tsunami

A tsunami is a series of waves in a body of water caused by the sudden displacement of a large volume of water, most often in an ocean or large lake. Unlike wind-driven waves or tides, which are produced by wind and by the gravitational pull of the Moon and Sun, tsunamis are generated by earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, landslides, underwater explosions, glacier calvings, or meteorite impacts.

## Why they behave differently

Normal wind waves have a wavelength of about 30–100 metres. A tsunami in the deep ocean has a wavelength of up to 200 kilometres and an amplitude of only about 1 metre, so ships at sea cannot feel one passing. The deep ocean acts as "shallow water" for a tsunami because depth is small compared to wavelength. Shallow-water speed is √(depth × gravity), so a tsunami crossing 5,000-metre-deep Pacific water moves at about 224 m/s, roughly 800 km/h.

## How they formMost tsunamis come from thrust-fault movement at convergent plate boundaries beneath the sea. When the seafloor abruptly deforms and vertically displaces the overlying water, the energy becomes a wave train of crests spaced minutes to hours apart. Megathrust earthquakes such as 1960 Chile (M 9.5), 1964 Alaska (M 9.2), 2004 Indian Ocean (M 9.2), and 2011 Tōhoku (M 9.0) produced teletsunamis that crossed entire ocean basins. Submarine landslides can produce megatsunamis; in 1958 a landslide into Alaska's Lituya Bay produced a 524-metre wave, the tallest ever recorded. Volcanic eruptions have produced tsunamis through sector collapse, pyroclastic flows entering water, caldera collapse, and underwater explosions, including the 1883 Krakatoa and 2022 Hunga Tonga events. Rapid atmospheric pressure changes can generate meteotsunamis, dynamically similar but locally generated and weaker. About 80% of tsunamis occur in the Pacific.

## What happens at the coast

As a tsunami enters shallower water, wave shoaling compresses it: speed drops below 80 km/h, wavelength shrinks to under 20 km, and amplitude rises in line with Green's law. The wave usually looks like a fast-rising tide rather than a curling breaker. A large tsunami arrives as multiple crests spaced about twelve minutes apart, and the first is often not the largest. Damage comes from the impact of the moving water and from the return flow dragging debris back to sea.

Because every tsunami has a crest and a trough, either may reach shore first. A trough arriving first causes drawback, a dramatic retreat of the sea exposing the seabed. A large drawback is one of the few reliable natural warnings; people who see it should move immediately to high ground. This warning saved the Halawa Valley village on Molokai during the 1946 Aleutian tsunami.

## Warning and limits

Tsunamis cannot be precisely predicted even when an earthquake's magnitude and location are known, and a major research question is why some large earthquakes produce almost no tsunami while smaller ones do. Detection systems such as DART use bottom-pressure sensors on buoys to confirm the wave in real time; computer models using seismic data and bathymetry estimate arrival time and surge height. Regional systems such as the Pacific Tsunami Warning Center in Honolulu monitor seismic activity across the Pacific. Warning signs, sirens, and evacuation routes are standard in Japan, Chile, and the U.S. west coast. Coastal defences include seawalls up to about 12 metres and floodgates up to 15.5 metres, but tsunamis frequently overtop them, as at the Fukushima Daiichi plant and along the Taro seawall in Iwate Prefecture during the 2011 Tōhoku event.

## History

The Greek historian Thucydides argued in the 5th century BC that submarine earthquakes cause tsunamis, and the oldest human record dates to 479 BC at Potidaea. The 2004 Indian Ocean event killed around 230,000 people across 14 countries. The 1755 Lisbon and 1908 Messina events, each killing tens of thousands, show that tsunamis remain a serious, often underestimated hazard in the Mediterranean.
