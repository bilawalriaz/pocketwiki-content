# Godavari River

The Godavari is India's second-longest river after the Ganges and the largest in peninsular India, earning it the title "Dakshina Ganga," or Southern Ganges. It begins in the Western Ghats at Trimbakeshwar, Maharashtra, only 80 km from the Arabian Sea, then flows roughly 1,465 km eastward across the Deccan Plateau before turning southeast to empty into the Bay of Bengal. Its 312,812 km² drainage basin covers nearly one-tenth of India's land area and spans Maharashtra, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, and Odisha.

The basin divides into three sections: Upper, from the source to the Manjira confluence; Middle, from Manjira to the Pranhita; and Lower, from Pranhita to the sea. Tributary contribution is sharply asymmetric. The left-bank rivers (Purna, Pranhita, Indravati, Sabari) drain 59.7% of the catchment, while the right-bank rivers (Pravara, Manjira, Manair) drain only 16.1%. The Pranhita is the largest tributary by area. At Rajamahendravaram, the river splits into distributaries, smaller channels branching off the main stem, forming a broad delta. Traditionally, the delta has seven mouths considered sacred to Hindus, though some original branches have silted shut.

The Godavari records the highest flood flows of any Indian river: a normal annual flood already reaches about 1.0 million cusecs (cubic feet per second, a measure of flow rate), and a 1986 flood peaked at 3.6 million cusecs. Mean annual inflow is roughly 110 billion cubic metres, of which about half has been harnessed. The basin holds more dams than any other in India, including the Gangapur and Jayakwadi dams in Maharashtra and the Sriram Sagar Dam in Telangana. Interstate water sharing is managed by the Godavari Water Disputes Tribunal.

The basin sits on Deccan basalt, a volcanic rock from ancient lava flows that underlies about half its area. The river carries the largest sediment load of any peninsular Indian river, but that load has fallen roughly threefold between the 1970s and 2000s, because dams trap sediment before it reaches the coast. In the delta, the Coringa mangroves form India's third-largest mangrove forest, and the coast hosts nesting olive ridley sea turtles, an endangered species.

Sediment loss drives the basin's main ecological crisis. The delta is sinking relative to the sea faster than fresh sediment can rebuild it, leaving it exposed to flooding and erosion. In lean months, long stretches of the river regularly dry out, largely because of heavy damming and water-intensive sugarcane irrigation in Maharashtra. Water quality has also degraded: alkalinity rises both from natural basalt weathering and from coal-fired power stations, and in 2013 the river reached an all-time low in Nizamabad district, Telangana. A 2015 link to the Krishna River through the Polavaram right-bank canal and the Pattiseema lift scheme began moving water between the two basins, but about 2,490 tmcft of Godavari water still flows unused to the sea each year, and new inland reservoirs to capture it are blocked by land submergence and displacement.

Two projects show the political tensions over this water. The Babli project in Maharashtra has drawn opposition from Telangana over reduced downstream flow. The Polavaram project in Andhra Pradesh has been criticised for unclear environmental clearance, community displacement, safety questions about its dam design, and loss of forest cover.
