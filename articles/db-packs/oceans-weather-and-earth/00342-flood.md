# Flood

A flood is an overflow of water that submerges land that is normally dry. It begins when more water arrives at a place than the ground, the river channel, or the drainage system can absorb or carry away. The single most important determinant of a flood's size is the upstream watershed, the total land area whose runoff feeds the affected point. For small watersheds (under about 30 square miles, or 80 km²), rainfall intensity ranks second; for large ones, the main channel's slope ranks second. Because small watersheds respond in minutes and large basins in days, the critical rainfall duration varies accordingly, a quantity called the time of concentration.

Floods have always reshaped human life. Communities began managing floodplains at least 6,000 years ago, and the earliest civilizations grew on rivers whose periodic floods deposited nutrient-rich silt: the Tigris-Euphrates, Nile, Indus, Ganges, and Yellow River. Floods remain the most destructive hazard for global agriculture, causing more than USD 1.5 trillion in losses between 1991 and 2023, and risks are escalating as climate change intensifies the water cycle and lifts sea levels.

## Types

Floods take several forms, each defined by where and why the water exceeds capacity:

- Areal flooding covers flat or low-lying land when rainfall or snowmelt outpaces infiltration. Saturated, frozen, or paved ground stops absorption, and depressions fill. In endorheic basins, where water has no outlet, pooling continues until evaporation catches up.
- River (fluvial) flooding occurs when streamflow exceeds channel capacity. Slow-rising floods dominate large rivers, fed by sustained rain, snowmelt, or monsoons. Flash floods strike small, steep, or impermeable basins in minutes to hours after intense thunderstorms, dam failures, landslides, or sudden glacial release. A hybrid, overland flooding, happens on extremely flat terrain such as the Red River Valley, where spring melt spills from the channel and spreads unpredictably across an ancient lakebed.
- Coastal flooding arises when storm surges from tropical or extratropical cyclones combine with high tides and waves, or when tsunamis drive water inland. Estuaries add low pressure, large waves, and high river flow.
- Urban flooding happens when intense rain overwhelms drainage on impervious surfaces. On pavement, roughly one inch (25 mm) of rain in an hour is enough to produce significant ponding.

Intentional flooding is a separate category: a hydraulic engineering tool used in agriculture (paddy rice), river management (diverting water through lowered levee sections called overlaten in Dutch practice to sacrificial areas), and warfare (controlled inundation, as in the historic Dutch Water Lines, or uncontrolled, as in the WWII flooding of Walcheren).

## Causes and amplification

Three layers of factors determine severity. Upslope, the watershed's area sets the scale; rainfall intensity and channel slope refine it; and the proportion of precipitation converted to runoff ranges from near zero (light rain on dry ground) to 170 percent (warm rain falling on snowpack). Downslope, channel geometry (depth, slope, sediment), restrictions like bridges or canyons, and boundary conditions at the coast or receiving lake set the water's elevation. Vegetation, ice, debris, and built structures alter that geometry. Field data from the 2010–2011 Queensland floods showed that hazard models using only flow velocity and depth missed the danger from large, mobile debris entrained by the water.

Human and climate drivers amplify these processes. Draining wetlands removes natural sponges; paving surfaces speeds runoff. Climate change intensifies the water cycle and raises sea levels, pushing coastal and riverine risks upward. Coincidence of factors, such as warm rain on snow, ice jams, or beaver dam failures, routinely produces extremes that simple statistical models underestimate.

## Impacts

Floods demolish infrastructure, disrupt utilities, and cause direct fatalities by drowning and trauma. Indirect deaths follow when medical access is cut: dehydration, heat stroke, heart attacks. Waterborne diseases (cholera, hepatitis A and E, typhoid, giardia, cryptosporidium) surge from sewage contamination; vector-borne illnesses (malaria, dengue, West Nile, yellow fever) rise in standing water. Mold in chronically wet buildings increases adverse respiratory outcomes 30 to 50 percent in coastal and wetland areas. The economic toll runs into billions of US dollars annually; in the United States more than $7 billion a year, and roughly 40 percent of small businesses never reopen after a flood. Bangladesh lost more than a million houses in the 2007 flood alone.

Yet floods also confer benefits when they recur at smaller scales: they recharge groundwater, deposit fertile sediment, kill pests, maintain floodplain and river-corridor ecosystems, boost fisheries, and favor hydropower.

## Management and prediction

Responses combine structural defenses (levees, dams, detention basins, sea walls, beach nourishment) with green infrastructure that restores natural absorption: porous paving, wetlands, sustainable urban drainage systems (SUDS), and converting flood-prone land to parks. Property-level measures include rain barrels, sump pumps, and elevated or flood-proofed buildings. Emergency responses lean on sandbags, portable inflatable tubes, and the US National Weather Service's "Turn Around, Don't Drown" warning. Effective planning rests on historical observation, statistical and hydraulic modeling, inundation mapping, long-term regulation, engineering design, forecasting and emergency response, and real-time warning.

Prediction blends statistics and physics. Annual maximum flow series estimate recurrence intervals such as the 100-year flood (about a 1 percent chance in any given year, or roughly 63 percent over a century). Hydraulic models, including HEC-RAS and TUFLOW, simulate one-dimensional channel levels, two-dimensional floodplain depths, or coupled systems; some can invert observed inundation to estimate flow rate. Basin models couple land-surface processes with channel routing to forecast runoff hydrographs and test land-use or climate scenarios. Operationally, the US National Weather Service integrates USGS stream gauges, automated sensors, snowpack data, and Quantitative Precipitation Forecasts, coordinating with Environment Canada on transboundary rivers such as the St. Lawrence. Globally, NASA's Global Flood Monitoring System (GFMS) merges satellite precipitation with a land-surface model to produce streamflow, depth, and flood statistics every three hours at 12 km resolution, with five-day forecasts and 1 km inundation maps.

Full-basin physical models remain incomplete, and flash-flood forecasting in cities needs sub-hourly rainfall and detailed drainage data not yet universal.
