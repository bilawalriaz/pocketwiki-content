# Congo River

The Congo River is Africa's second-longest river, the world's deepest, and by discharge the third largest after the Amazon and the Ganges–Brahmaputra–Meghna system. It is the only major river to cross the equator twice, and that equatorial position guarantees that rainfall always falls somewhere in its basin. The result is an unusually stable flow that averages 41,000 cubic metres per second and sustains the world's second-largest rainforest.

The Congo's drainage basin covers about 4,014,500 km², roughly 13% of Africa and nearly the size of the European Union. Its headwaters rise in the East African Rift highlands, with the Chambeshi River in Zambia conventionally treated as the ultimate source, though the exact origin is debated. From there the main stem, called the Lualaba above Boyoma Falls, runs northwest then southwest, passes through Pool Malebo, a wide, lake-like reach where Kinshasa and Brazzaville stand on opposite banks, and then plunges through the Livingstone Falls, a series of cataracts that block navigation from the sea.

The river's present course dates back only 1.5–2 million years. That is recent enough on evolutionary timescales that a single ancestral ape population was split by the river into populations north and south of its channel, a case of allopatric speciation, the formation of new species through geographic isolation. The descendants are the common chimpanzee and the bonobo. The basin also harbours exceptional aquatic biodiversity, with nearly 800 recorded fish species, many endemic, restricted to that single region. The Livingstone Falls reach alone holds over 300 fish species, including deep-water and cave-dwelling endemics such as *Lamprologus lethops* and *Caecobarbus geertsii*. The basin's ecological diversity is grouped into multiple ecoregions, ecologically distinct subunits of a larger biome defined by their characteristic species and habitats.

Human history along the Congo is long. Bantu-speaking peoples had reached the middle basin by around 500 BC, and the Kingdom of Kongo, which gives both the river and two modern states their name, had formed on the southern bank by the late 14th century from the merger of the Mpemba Kasi and Mbata kingdoms. The name "Zaire," used for the river in the 16th and 17th centuries, is a Portuguese rendering of a Kikongo word for "river," and it was briefly revived for both river and country during the Mobutu regime from 1971 to 1997. Europeans reached the estuary in 1482 with the Portuguese explorer Diogo Cão, but the river's full course was not traced until Henry Morton Stanley's 1876–77 expedition, which confirmed that the Lualaba belongs to the Congo system rather than the Nile. Stanley later founded Léopoldville, today's Kinshasa, as a trading post in 1881, and in 1885 Leopold II of Belgium claimed the basin as his private Congo Free State, a colonial regime marked by widespread atrocities.

The river remains Central Africa's main transport artery above the Livingstone Falls, carrying copper, palm oil, sugar, coffee, and cotton. It is also Africa's most powerful river for hydropower, with technical potential estimated at about 13% of the global total. The existing Inga dam complex southwest of Kinshasa generates 1,776 MW, and a long-standing Eskom proposal would expand capacity toward 40,000 MW. Because most of the affected species are narrow endemics with nowhere else to live, such projects raise unresolved conservation questions, alongside the practical uncertainty over how much of the basin's biodiversity remains undocumented, even in nominally protected areas like parts of Salonga National Park. Severe floods in 2019–2020 displaced large populations across the Democratic Republic of the Congo and the Republic of the Congo.
