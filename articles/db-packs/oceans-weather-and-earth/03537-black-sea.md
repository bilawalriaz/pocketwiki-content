# Black Sea

The Black Sea is a marginal sea, a semi-enclosed basin attached to a larger sea, sitting between Europe and Asia, bordered by six countries. It connects to the Mediterranean only through the narrow Turkish Straits (the Bosporus and Dardanelles) and to the Sea of Azov via the Kerch Strait. Its defining feature is hydrological: a permanent anoxic (oxygen-free) deep layer that preserves ancient shipwrecks and has shaped the basin's ecology, history, and strategic importance.

## A layered sea

The Black Sea covers 436,400 km² and reaches a maximum depth of 2,212 m. Major rivers including the Danube, Dnieper, and Dniester feed it across a drainage basin spanning 24 countries. The water balance is positive, with a net outflow through the Bosporus into the Mediterranean. Denser, saltier Mediterranean water intrudes below, flowing out again as a deep counter-current. In 2010, scientists mapped this as a true undersea river of saline water running along the Bosporus seabed.

The sea is meromictic: its deep waters do not mix with the surface. A halocline (a sharp increase in salinity with depth) and a matching pycnocline (a sharp increase in density) trap oxygen-rich surface water above roughly 100–200 m, leaving the deeper basin permanently anoxic. Surface circulation is cyclonic (counter-clockwise), driven by the Rim Current, a coastal jet looping the basin's perimeter. The anoxic layer preserves organic material and wooden shipwrecks for millennia.

The basin formed from two relict back-arc basins (ocean basins left behind when their volcanic arc went extinct) during the Mesozoic era, and its connection to the world ocean has been intermittent. During the last ice age, sea levels more than 100 m lower than today likely turned the Black Sea into a landlocked freshwater lake, an endorheic basin with no outflow. The Black Sea deluge hypothesis proposes a catastrophic re-flooding from the Mediterranean around 5600 BC; the timing and violence remain debated, but the geological record confirms the basin has shifted between isolated lake and connected sea. Tectonic activity from the convergence of the Eurasian and African plates continues to shape the basin.

## An ecosystem under stress

Surface waters are brackish and nutrient-rich, with biodiversity about one-third that of the Mediterranean. Seasonal phytoplankton blooms draw on diatoms, dinoflagellates, and coccolithophores (single-celled algae with calcium carbonate plates), including *Emiliania huxleyi*. Marine mammals include endemic subspecies of common and bottlenose dolphins and the harbour porpoise, all endangered; the Mediterranean monk seal is likely extinct in the basin.

Since the 1960s, industrial expansion, dam-building, and agricultural runoff have driven more frequent algal blooms and expanded anoxia. Overfishing and the invasive comb-jelly *Mnemiopsis leidyi* compounded the damage. The 1990s brought partial recovery as pollution fell and EU-aligned sewage treatment improved, but ecological decline was again noted in the early 21st century, leaving the long-term trajectory contested. Past blooms left thick sapropel layers, organic-rich muds that record the basin's biological history.

## A contested crossroads

Greeks, Persians, and Romans all met on these waters in antiquity. Greek colonies founded from the 9th century BC along the southern coast developed a distinct Pontic culture. After 1479, when the Republic of Genoa lost Crimea, the Ottomans turned the sea into an Ottoman-controlled lake, a position they held for centuries.

Three events reshaped that order. Imperial Russia established the Black Sea Fleet and occupied Crimea in 1783. The Crimean War (1853–1856) ended with the Treaty of Paris demilitarizing the sea. In 1936 the Montreux Convention gave Turkey regulatory control over the Bosporus and Dardanelles, governing how warships of non-littoral states, nations without their own Black Sea coastline, may pass.

Both world wars brought major naval operations, including the Siege of Sevastopol (1941–1945). In the 21st century the sea has become a contested zone again. After Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine, Ukrainian forces sank the cruiser *Moskva* and fought over Snake Island; in 2023 a Russian Su-27 damaged an American MQ-9 Reaper drone over the water. As of January 2025, neither side holds full control, and coastal zones are more exposed to land-based weapons than open water.

The basin supports fishing, including Turkey's large anchovy catch, coastal tourism, and hydrocarbon exploration, with Turkey reporting a 320 billion m³ natural gas discovery in 2020.
