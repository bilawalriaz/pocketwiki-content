# Yellow River

The Yellow River (Huanghe) is China's second-longest river and the sixth-longest on Earth. It is the historical cradle of Chinese civilization, but it is equally known as "China's Sorrow" for catastrophic floods that have killed millions and reshaped the region's politics, warfare, and engineering for over four thousand years.

## Why the river floods and why it matters

The river's defining feature is its enormous silt load, picked up as it cuts through the Loess Plateau, a highland in north-central China blanketed in fine, wind-deposited soil that erodes easily. The middle reaches through this plateau contribute 92% of the river's sediment, staining the water yellow and, over centuries, building up the riverbed itself.

The lower reaches therefore sit on a raised bed of the river's own deposited silt, several meters above the surrounding North China Plain. This is the "Earth Suspended River," confined by artificial embankments called levees. When those levees fail, the escaping water flows downhill onto populated land below, which is why a single breach can kill hundreds of thousands.

Between 595 BC and 1946 AD, the river flooded 1,593 times and shifted its main course 26 times. The heaviest disasters in the modern era were often human-triggered. In 1642, a man-made flood meant to block rebels instead destroyed the city of Kaifeng, killing an estimated 300,000. In 1887 a flood killed 900,000 to 2 million. In 1931 floods across China killed 1–4 million. In 1938, Nationalist troops deliberately breached the dikes to slow the Japanese advance, causing a flood that killed 500,000–900,000. The 1938 breach was not closed until 1947, restoring the river's pre-1938 course.

## A river that keeps changing course

The river's path is not fixed; it can jump to a new channel, a process called avulsion. The first major recorded course change was in 602 BC, when the river shifted east from its earlier "Yu River" path. In 1128, Song-dynasty troops breached the dikes to block invaders, sending the river south where it merged with the Huai River for over 700 years. It returned to a northern course in 1855, adopting the path it follows today.

Traditional accounts place the Xia dynasty's founding around 2100 BC on the river's banks. Control of strategic passes such as Hangu Pass repeatedly decided the fate of dynasties. In 1344, a major flood helped trigger the rebellions that ended the Yuan dynasty. "China's Pride" captures the fertility; "China's Sorrow" captures the price.

Flood control itself became a long-running policy debate, traditionally framed as "Confucian" high levees that protected maximum farmland versus "Taoist" wider, lower levees that let the river spread. Modern China has combined levees, overflow basins, and large dams in a comprehensive flood-control system.

## Water, people, and pollution today

The basin supports over 120 million people but receives only about 2% of China's total water runoff, a stark supply mismatch. Heavy irrigation in the twentieth century caused the lower river to run dry before reaching the sea in most years, peaking in the 1990s. Since 1999, unified water-flow regulation has kept water in the channel continuously; as of 2024, the river has reached the sea every year for 25 years.

The South–North Water Transfer Project, a massive infrastructure effort to divert water from the Yangtze to the arid north including the Yellow River basin, is meant to ease chronic shortages. Pollution remains severe: as of 2007, more than a third of the river system was classified as unfit for any use. The basin once held over 160 native fish species, but dams, pollution, and introduced species have pushed many toward extinction; fishing bans and habitat restoration are the main recovery tools.

Geographically, the river flows through seven provinces and two autonomous regions, conventionally divided into upper, middle, and lower reaches. The large rectangular bend through Inner Mongolia is called the Ordos Loop. At its headwaters in Qinghai, the "Three Rivers' Sources" reserve (Sanjiangyuan) protects the shared birthplace of the Yellow, Yangtze, and Mekong rivers.
