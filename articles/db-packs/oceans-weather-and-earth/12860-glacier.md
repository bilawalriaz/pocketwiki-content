# Glacier

A glacier is a persistent body of ice that forms where snowfall exceeds melting over many years, then flows downhill under its own weight. Glaciers carve valleys, transport rock, and store about 69% of Earth's freshwater, mostly in the ice sheets of Antarctica and Greenland.

## Formation

Snow collects in a cirque, an armchair-shaped mountain hollow, and compacts in stages: granular snow (névé) becomes firn, then denser glacial ice as air is squeezed out. When the mass thickens enough, gravity drives it through the cirque's lip and downslope. The point where snow gained equals ice lost is the equilibrium line, which divides the upper accumulation zone (typically 60–70% of the surface area) from the lower ablation zone, where melting, calving, and sublimation remove ice.

## Types

Ice bodies are classed by area. Bodies under 50,000 km² are ice caps or ice fields; larger ones are ice sheets (continental glaciers), of which only two exist today. Alpine or valley glaciers form on mountain slopes; tidewater glaciers end in the sea and calve icebergs. Narrow fast-flowing corridors in ice sheets are ice streams; floating margins are ice shelves or ice tongues.

Glaciers are also classified by temperature. Temperate glaciers sit at the melting point year-round; polar glaciers stay below freezing throughout; subpolar and polythermal glaciers mix the two. Whether the base is at or below freezing matters for erosion: cold-based glaciers are frozen to the bed and barely erode, while warm-based glaciers slide and pluck rock. Basal melting is driven by geothermal heat, friction, and pressure, which lowers the melting point under thick ice.

## Motion

Ice flows by internal plastic deformation and by basal sliding, described by the Glen–Nye flow law: strain rate Σ = kτⁿ, where n is typically 3 and k depends on temperature. Ice begins to deform once it is about 30 m thick and flows faster above roughly 50 m. Velocity is lowest at the base and edges, where friction acts, and highest at the surface, where it sums the motion of all layers below. Mean speed is around 1 m per day, but Jakobshavn Isbræ in Greenland reaches 20–30 m per day, and surges have hit 90 m per day. Surges are driven by feedback between thickness, basal melting, and friction, sometimes triggered by meltwater pooling. Where ice moves faster than about 1 km per year, glacial earthquakes of magnitude up to 6.1 occur, mostly in Greenland in summer.

The top ~50 m of a glacier is brittle. Where ice accelerates, spreads, or shears against valley walls, it cracks open into crevasses, typically under 46 m deep but occasionally 300 m. Below that, ice is plastic and cracks seal. Intersecting crevasses isolate ice towers called seracs. Moulins are vertical shafts that carry surface meltwater down to englacial and subglacial drainage systems.

## Erosion and landforms

Two processes do the sculpting. Plucking freezes meltwater into bedrock cracks and levers blocks loose; abrasion drags embedded rock fragments across the floor like sandpaper, producing rock flour and long scratches called striations. Together they carve cirques, U-shaped valleys, truncated spurs, hanging valleys, arêtes, horns, fjords, drumlins (canoe-shaped hills 15–50 m high), and roches moutonnées (asymmetric knobs, smooth on the up-glacier face and plucked on the lee). Deposits include till (unsorted), moraines (terminal, lateral, medial, ground), erratics, eskers, and kames. Wind-blown rock flour forms loess deposits hundreds of metres thick.

The blue tint of glacial ice comes from water molecules absorbing red light via an infrared overtone, the same reason liquid water looks blue, and is enhanced because pressure has squeezed out most air bubbles that would otherwise scatter light.

## Geography

Glaciers exist on every continent except mainland Australia and cover about 10% of Earth's land. With over 7,000 known glaciers, Pakistan holds more glacial ice than any country outside the poles. Between 35°N and 35°S they occur only in the Himalayas, Andes, and a few East African peaks; within 19° of the equator they require mountains above about 5,000 m.

## Modern change

Since the end of the Little Ice Age around 1850, glaciers worldwide have retreated substantially; a brief cool period produced advances from 1950 to 1985, but loss has accelerated since. Reference glaciers tracked by the World Glacier Monitoring Service have lost mass every year since 1988, and 2023 alone cost Switzerland 4% of its glacier volume. Under current national pledges, roughly half of Earth's glaciers (excluding the ice sheets) are projected to disappear by 2100, contributing about 115 mm of sea-level rise.

Ice cores trap ancient air bubbles, allowing reconstruction of past atmospheric composition and confirming that global temperature and carbon dioxide concentration have tracked each other for at least a million years. Heavy ice loads also depress the crust, producing slow post-glacial rebound still measurable in Scandinavia and around the Great Lakes. On Mars, rock-covered glaciers (lobate debris aprons) and polar ice caps show signs of past and present glacial activity, and on Pluto, nitrogen ice in Sputnik Planitia appears to convect and flow much like water ice does on Earth.
