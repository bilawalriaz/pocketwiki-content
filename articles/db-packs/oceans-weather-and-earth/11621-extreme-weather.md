# Extreme weather

Extreme weather is weather at the statistical tails of a location's recorded history. The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report defines an extreme weather event as one "as rare as or rarer than the 10th or 90th percentile" of observed values for that place and time of year. *Severe weather* is the subset of extreme weather that threatens life or property. The main categories are heat waves, cold waves, droughts, tropical cyclones, tornadoes, and heavy precipitation.

## Heat waves and cold waves

A heat wave is a sustained period of abnormally high temperature and humidity, defined relative to local norms so that thresholds vary by region. In the United States, heat waves have increased in frequency, average duration, and seasonal length over recent decades. The 2003 European heat wave caused roughly 30,000 excess deaths; the 1936 US heat wave killed more than 5,000 people directly. Cities amplify heat through the urban heat island effect, where impervious surfaces and stored anthropogenic heat raise nighttime temperatures.

A cold wave, as defined by the US National Weather Service, is a rapid fall in temperature within 24 hours requiring substantially increased protection for agriculture, commerce, and social activity. Extreme cold injures livestock through hypothermia, freezes and ruptures water pipes, and kills crops during vulnerable growing stages. The 1816 "Year Without a Summer," triggered by volcanic ash reducing sunlight, illustrates how cold extremes can collapse harvests and cause famine.

## Tropical cyclones

A tropical cyclone is a rapidly rotating low-pressure system with a closed circulation, strong winds, and spiral thunderstorms. The same phenomenon has different names by region: hurricane in the Atlantic and northeastern Pacific, typhoon in the northwestern Pacific, and tropical cyclone in the Indian Ocean and South Pacific. Roughly 80 to 90 named tropical cyclones form globally each year, with diameters between 100 and 2,000 km.

Cyclones form over warm water and draw energy from evaporation and condensation, which differs from mid-latitude storms powered by horizontal temperature contrasts. Earth's rotation imparts angular momentum to inflowing air, so cyclones rarely form within 5° of the equator. Warmer seas supply more fuel. Between 1979 and 2017 the global share of Category 3+ cyclones rose, and in the Arabian Sea cyclone frequency increased 52% while very severe cyclones increased 150% during 1982–2019. A 2020 NOAA projection expected 25% fewer Atlantic tropical storms by late century but 5% higher peak intensity.

## Heavy rain, floods, and drought

Atmospheric water vapor has risen with global warming because warmer air holds more moisture. The result is more severe rainfall events, greater flood and landslide risk when storms saturate soils, and, in regions with higher temperatures and unchanged rainfall, more drought through increased evaporation. Total precipitation can rise on average while dry regions get drier.

## Why extremes are changing

The atmosphere is naturally variable. Oscillations such as ENSO (El Niño-Southern Oscillation) and the North Atlantic Oscillation shift temperature and precipitation patterns worldwide, and record-breaking extremes often occur when natural variability aligns with human-induced warming.

Climate models project more frequent and more intense extremes with rising global temperatures. The IPCC AR6 projects more than a 5°C increase in extreme heat events at 4°C of global warming. Cold extremes are a partial exception: average freezing days have declined across most US cities since the 1950s, and the JRC PESETA IV project concluded that overall cold extremes will weaken, though some regions may see more. Linking rapid Arctic warming to mid-latitude cold extremes remains scientifically contested.

## Human factors that worsen impacts

Human choices amplify damage. Urban flooding worsens where impervious surfaces (roads, roofs, sidewalks) prevent absorption and where destroyed wetlands can no longer buffer surges. Building on floodplains or below sea level increases exposure. The same impervious surfaces, together with waste heat from buildings and vehicles, drive urban heat islands and intensify heat waves.

## Costs and casualties

Reported climate-related events nearly doubled from 3,656 in 1980–1999 to 6,681 in 2000–2019. A 2023 study estimated that extreme weather attributable to climate change cost about US$2.86 trillion over 2000–2019, roughly $143 billion per year, and billion-dollar Atlantic hurricanes have become more than elevenfold costlier since the 1980s.

Death tolls have fallen even as exposure has grown. Despite population quadrupling and 1.3°C of warming, deaths from natural disasters fell over 90% since the 1920s, from 5.4 million in the 1920s to about 400,000 per year in the 2010s. Early-warning systems cut cyclone mortality sharply: a 1970 cyclone killed 300,000 people, a 1991 Bangladesh cyclone killed 135,000, and the comparable 2020 Cyclone Amphan killed about 120. A 2021 study found extreme temperatures cause about 5 million deaths annually, with cold-related deaths declining and heat-related deaths rising. On agriculture, a global study found that combined drought and extreme heat reduced national cereal production by about 10% over 1964–2007.
