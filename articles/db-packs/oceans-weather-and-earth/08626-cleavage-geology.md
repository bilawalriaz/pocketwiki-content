# Cleavage (geology)

Cleavage in structural geology and petrology is a planar fabric that develops in fine-grained rocks when deformation and metamorphism align mineral grains, separate them into layers, or dissolve material so that a consistent orientation runs through the rock. The kind of cleavage that forms depends on how much the rock has been deformed and metamorphosed, on the minerals present, and on the grain size of the original rock. In practical terms, cleavage is the visible or microscopic "grain" that records which direction a rock was squeezed.

Cleavage is one variety of **foliation**, the broader term for any planar fabric in a rock. Foliation is either primary (formed in igneous or sedimentary rocks during original deposition or cooling) or secondary (produced by later metamorphism and deformation). Cleavage is a secondary foliation found in fine-grained rocks; the analogous feature in coarser metamorphic rocks is called **schistosity**. Terminology varies between authors; the definitions used here follow Passchier and Trouw (2005): cleavage is a secondary foliation in fine-grained rocks marked by planar fabric elements arranged in a preferred orientation.

## Continuous and spaced cleavage

Cleavage divides first into **continuous** (penetrative) and **spaced** forms, based on whether fabric elements such as aligned platy minerals, compositional layering, or grain-size variations are distributed evenly or in separated zones.

**Continuous cleavage** appears in fine-grained rocks where platy minerals are evenly distributed and share a common orientation. The minerals present control the appearance: undeformed platy grains such as micas and amphiboles simply rotate into alignment, while minerals like quartz or calcite deform into shapes that share that alignment. The classification is scale dependent, so a rock that shows continuous cleavage under a microscope can show spaced cleavage in outcrop.

**Slaty cleavage** is the most familiar continuous variety and the first cleavage to form once deformation begins, usually after diagenesis (the low-temperature changes that turn sediment into rock). By definition, the spacing between successive cleavage planes is 0.01 mm or less. Tectonic strain strong enough to impose a new, pervasive foliation is required, which is why slaty cleavage is characteristic of low-grade metamorphic rocks such as slate.

**Spaced cleavage** forms where minerals are not evenly distributed, so the rock develops separated layers or lenses of different mineralogy. It contains two structural components: **cleavage domains**, planar boundaries oriented sub-parallel to the overall fabric, and **microlithons**, the lenses of rock between them. Spaced cleavages are further classified by what happens inside the microlithons, by spacing size, and by the shape and percentage of cleavage domains.

Two notable subtypes are widely recognised:

- **Crenulation cleavage**, in which microlithons contain microfolds from an earlier foliation warped by a later phase of deformation. The pattern depends on rock composition and the intensity of deformation and metamorphism.
- **Disjunctive cleavage**, in which microlithons are undeformed and the cleavage forms without reference to any earlier foliation. The older term "fracture cleavage" is discouraged because it suggests a purely brittle origin that misrepresents the process.

A separate term, **transposition cleavage**, applies when an older foliation is erased and replaced by a younger, stronger one, recording multiple deformation events.

## How cleavage forms

Four mechanisms, operating in different combinations, control cleavage development. Which dominates depends on rock composition, tectonic setting, and the pressure and temperature conditions of metamorphism. Cleavages develop approximately parallel to the **X–Y plane** of tectonic strain, the flattening plane of the strain ellipsoid (a geometric model of how a rock body is shortened and stretched during deformation).

1. **Mechanical rotation of mineral grains.** During ductile (non-brittle, flowing) deformation, grains with a high aspect ratio rotate so that their mean orientation lies parallel to the X–Y plane. Grains oriented perpendicular to the shortening direction may instead fold.
2. **Solution transfer.** Stress-induced pressure solution redistributes material; inequant grains dissolve where stress is highest and recrystallise where stress is lower. Micas align in a preferred orientation, and grains that deform plastically become extended along the X–Y plane.
3. **Dynamic recrystallisation.** Under metamorphic conditions, deformed grains lower their stored strain energy by recrystallising. Deformed micas can store enough strain energy to allow this, allowing old and new minerals to regrow with a shared orientation and reinforcing the cleavage.
4. **Static recrystallisation.** This occurs after deformation ceases, or without dynamic deformation. Mild heat strengthens an existing foliation by growing micas parallel to it; intense heat weakens it by nucleating new, randomly oriented crystals and converts the rock into a hornfels (a hard, fine-grained rock baked by contact heat).

## Relationship to folds

Cleavage commonly forms parallel to the axial plane of folds and is then called an **axial-planar foliation**. In folded alternations of sandstone and mudstone, the cleavage fans through the layers, divergent in the mudstone and convergent in the sandstone, a geometry called **foliation fanning**. The pattern arises because competent sandstone layers buckle while the weaker mudstone fills the intervening space, so cleavage orientation adjusts to strain in each layer.

## Engineering considerations

In geotechnical engineering a cleavage plane is treated as a discontinuity that can dominate the mechanical behaviour of a rock mass. The strength and deformation of the rock in tunnels, foundations, and slopes depend strongly on the orientation and spacing of these planes, which is why cleavage is mapped and measured whenever a rock mass is assessed for construction.
