# Coral Sea

The Coral Sea is a marginal sea of the South Pacific, sitting off the northeast coast of Australia. Its defining feature is the Great Barrier Reef, the world's largest reef system and a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1981. Warm, clear waters feed the reef's enormous biodiversity, but those same waters are increasingly stressed by climate change, shipping, and pollution, which is why a sequence of large marine protected areas now covers much of the region.

## Geography and origins

The sea is bounded by Queensland to the west, Vanuatu and New Caledonia to the east, and the Solomon Islands to the northeast, merging southward into the Tasman Sea and eastward into the open Pacific. The basin formed about 58 to 48 million years ago through geological uplift and subsidence. The seafloor combines the shallow continental shelf that carries the Great Barrier Reef, the broad Coral Sea Plateau, and several deep basins and ridges. Earthquakes occur but are generally less intense than those further east and north.

## Water, weather, and circulation

Currents sweep around the basin in a counter-clockwise gyre, with the East Australian Current carrying warm tropical water southward along the coast. The climate is subtropical: surface waters stay near 27–28°C in the north year-round, while the south shifts between roughly 19°C and 24°C with the seasons. Tropical cyclones are common from January through April. Annual rainfall is high, between 1,000 and 3,000 mm, and climate change has made record-breaking sea surface temperatures — the trigger for coral bleaching — significantly more likely. Bleaching is a stress response, usually to heat, in which corals expel their symbiotic algae, turn white, and often die.

## Life on the reef

The Great Barrier Reef alone holds over 2,000 plant species, 400 coral species, and more than 1,500 fish species. The wider sea adds whales, dolphins, sea turtles, more than 200 bird species, and many sharks, of which the grey reef shark is the most common. The crown-of-thorns starfish (*Acanthaster planci*), a multi-armed predator of coral polyps, is a natural but disruptive force; periodic outbreaks can drive significant reef loss.

## Human pressure and protection

People have lived along the coast for at least 40,000 years. Today's activities include shipping, fishing, tourism, and limited petroleum exploration, though oil exploration on the reef itself was halted in 1975. Tourism contributed about A$5.1 billion to the Australian economy in 2006–2007. Land runoff, shipping accidents, and warming seas remain the main environmental pressures. In 2010 the Chinese bulk carrier *Shen Neng 1* ran aground on the reef, causing the largest recorded oil-spill damage to the GBR, with recovery estimated at 10 to 20 years. Australia has layered protections in response: the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park from 1975, a 2004 decision that fully protected roughly one-third of it from fishing, and the Coral Sea Commonwealth Marine Reserve proclaimed in 2013 and renamed the Coral Sea Marine Park in 2017, one of the largest marine protected areas in the world.

## Open questions

How protective those reserves actually are remains contested. The Coral Sea Marine Park's 2018 management plan cut the area under its strictest tier (IUCN category II, the international standard for nature reserves) from 50.78% down to 24%, against the advice of the government's own expert review panel, and the long shadow of the 2010 spill continues to shape assessments of the reef's resilience.
