# Rock (geology)

A rock is a naturally occurring solid mass or aggregate of minerals or mineraloid matter. Minerals are crystalline solids with orderly atomic structures; mineraloids lack that crystallinity, and volcanic glass is the standard example. Together, these materials make up virtually all of Earth's solid exterior and interior, and the study of rocks through petrology and mineralogy underpins reconstruction of Earth's history, human prehistory, and the geology of other planets.

## The three rock classes

Geologists sort rocks into three main classes by origin: igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic. The boundaries between them are not sharp but pass through continuous gradations, so classifying an unusual specimen can be a judgement call.

- Igneous: about 65% of Earth's crust by volume. Forms when magma (molten rock beneath the surface) or lava (magma that has reached the surface) cools and solidifies. Melting is triggered by rising temperature, falling pressure, or a change in composition. Slow cooling underground produces coarse plutonic (intrusive) rocks such as granite. Rapid cooling at the surface produces fine-grained or glassy volcanic (extrusive) rocks such as basalt and pumice. As magma rises, earlier-forming crystals settle out and the remaining melt grows richer in silica, a process called magma differentiation and the reason silica content is the main axis for classifying igneous rocks.
- Sedimentary: about 7.9% of the crust. Forms at the surface where weathered fragments of older rock, mineral grains, or organic remains accumulate, then are compacted and cemented through diagenesis and lithification into stone. Grains range from clay to boulders and are transported by water, wind, ice, or glaciers before deposition. Shale dominates at roughly 82% of all sedimentary rock, sandstone and arkose together about 12%, and limestone about 6%. Because they build up in layers at low temperatures, sedimentary rocks are the class that commonly preserves fossils.
- Metamorphic: about 27.4% of the crust. Forms when an existing rock, the protolith, recrystallises into new minerals under heat and pressure without melting. The threshold is usually taken as temperatures above 150–200 °C and pressures above about 1500 bars. Contact metamorphism is driven mainly by heat from a nearby magma intrusion, burial metamorphism mainly by pressure, and regional metamorphism by both, typically in mountain-building belts. Rocks with aligned mineral grains are foliated (schist, gneiss); those without banding are non-foliated (marble from limestone, quartzite from sandstone).

Silicate minerals, built around the silica tetrahedron, dominate the crust, making up about 95% of it and roughly one-third of all known mineral species.

## Rocks beyond Earth

The definition extends across the Solar System: rocks make up Mars, Venus, Mercury, the Moon, many moons, asteroids, and meteoroids. Meteorites that fall to Earth are direct samples of this extraterrestrial material and tend to be denser than typical terrestrial rocks. Returned samples, including lunar rocks, Martian meteorites, and material gathered by missions such as Hayabusa, let geologists compare compositions across bodies and test ideas about how planets formed.

## Human use of rock

People have used stone for at least 2.5 million years, starting with lithic (stone) tools. Soft sedimentary rock was quarried for construction in Egypt by 4000 BCE, and stone fortifications appear in Inner Mongolia by 2800 BCE. Mining extracts the metals and minerals on which technology depends, with output shaped by regional geology. Rock strength varies enormously, from friable, crumbly material to quartzites whose tensile strength exceeds 300 MPa.

A modern addition is anthropic rock, a synthetic or human-restructured material such as concrete, made since Ancient Rome, or epoxy granite used for machine bases. Whether anthropic rock should be counted as a fourth rock class, a proposal by geologist James R. Underwood, is still debated. Two later advances reshaped the field: the 1896 discovery of radioactive decay opened radiometric dating of rocks, and plate tectonics, developed in the 1950s and 1960s, gave the whole rock cycle its modern explanatory frame.
