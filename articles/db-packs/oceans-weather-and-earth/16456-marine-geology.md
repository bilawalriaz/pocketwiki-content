# Marine geology

Marine geology (also called geological oceanography) is the study of the history and structure of the ocean floor. It combines geophysical, geochemical, sedimentological, and paleontological investigations of the seafloor and coastal zone, with strong ties to physical oceanography and to plate tectonics.

## Why the field exists

Modern marine geology was built around the discovery that the seafloor spreads. The HMS Challenger expedition (late 1800s) made some of the first deep-ocean soundings and recorded parts of what is now known as the mid-ocean ridge. In the early 20th century, dedicated institutions (Scripps on the U.S. west coast, Woods Hole on the east) made systematic study possible. After WWII, ships equipped with sonar surveyed the Atlantic. In 1953, cartographer Marie Tharp turned those soundings into the first three-dimensional relief map of the ocean floor, revealing the Mid-Atlantic Ridge as a continuous underwater mountain range. In 1960, geophysicist Harry H. Hess proposed that new crust forms at the ridge and moves outward, driving the plates apart. Magnetometer surveys then showed symmetrical magnetic "stripes" on either side of the ridge, and later dating confirmed that rocks closest to the ridge are youngest. The deep ocean floor remains the least explored part of the planet, and detailed mapping supports both science and economic interests such as mining and hazard mitigation.

## How the seafloor is studied

Most modern marine-geology data is collected remotely, using sound and magnetism.

| Method | What it does | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Side-scan sonar | Acoustic pulses sent sideways; hard rock reflects strongly (dark), soft sediment weakly (light) | Cannot measure depth; usually paired with another sonar |
| Multibeam bathymetry | Hull-mounted array measures two-way travel time to compute depth and records backscatter for sediment type | Resolution drops as altitude increases; deep surveys need ROVs, AUVs, or towed sonars |
| Sub-bottom profiler | Low-frequency pulses penetrate the seabed, resolving sediment layers and buried features to over 1,000 m | Lower spatial resolution than surface sonars |
| Marine magnetometer | Passive sensor, towed or mounted on an AUV, measures local disturbances in Earth's magnetic field from ferrous material (basalts, wrecks) | Resolution falls off with distance to target |

The magnetic field itself is generated by circulation of liquid iron and nickel in the outer core. Local anomalies recorded from a moving ship can reveal basaltic crust and buried wrecks.

## The plate-tectonic framework

Plate tectonics, formalized in the 1960s, explains the seafloor. The lithosphere (crust and uppermost mantle) is broken into plates that ride on the partially molten asthenosphere and move 2–15 cm per year. Plate boundaries come in three types, each with its own marine expression.

- Divergent boundaries: plates move apart, magma rises, and new oceanic crust forms. This builds the global mid-ocean ridge system, a nearly 60,000 km chain of underwater volcanic mountains. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge marks where the North American, Eurasian, African, and South American plates separate; it began forming over 200 million years ago as Pangea broke apart and was the birthplace of the seafloor-spreading hypothesis.
- Convergent boundaries: oceanic crust dives beneath continental crust, producing subduction zones, deep ocean trenches, and explosive volcanism. The Mariana Trench, the deepest point on Earth at nearly 11,000 m (about 2 km deeper below sea level than Everest is above it), marks where the Pacific Plate subducts under the Mariana Plate. The Pacific Ring of Fire is a chain of volcanic arcs and trenches encircling the Pacific.
- Transform boundaries: plates slide sideways, generating earthquakes and crustal deformation.

## Economic and societal applications

Marine geology underwrites both resource extraction and risk reduction. Deep-sea mining targets metals including nickel, copper, cobalt, manganese, zinc, and gold, often formed around hydrothermal vents (where super-heated, metal-rich fluids precipitate sulfur and mineral chimneys on the cold seafloor) and polymetallic (manganese) nodules, rounded ores that accrete over millions of years across abyssal plains. The Clarion–Clipperton Zone in the Pacific, about 4,500,000 km², holds an estimated 21 billion tons of nodules, containing roughly 5.95 Bt of manganese, 0.27 Bt of nickel, 0.23 Bt of copper, and 0.05 Bt of cobalt. The International Seabed Authority has divided the area into 16 mining claims and 9 conservation sections; the zone hosts over 5,000 species, with about 90% still undescribed. Deep-sea mining is high-risk and high-reward, with significant environmental costs.

Seafloor mapping is equally critical for offshore energy. Wind turbines are usually anchored with monopiles in water deeper than 15 m, so sites must avoid unstable sediment, erosion, and tectonic activity. Wave and tidal projects depend on local bathymetry, currents, and sediment transport.

Marine geology also supports hazard and environmental work. Coastal erosion in the United States causes roughly $500 million in property damage each year, with another $150 million spent annually on mitigation. The 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake (magnitude 9.1) triggered a tsunami with waves of at least 30 ft, killing about 230,000 people across 13 countries, underscoring why plate-boundary mapping feeds early-warning systems. Bottom trawling damages coral, sponge, and burrowing communities and reshuffles sediment, so trawled areas are mapped to prioritize restoration and to identify intact zones for protection.

## The unfinished map

According to NOAA, only about 23% of the seafloor has been mapped in detail. NOAA's Okeanos Explorer alone has mapped over 2 million km² with multibeam sonar since 2008. The international Nippon Foundation–GEBCO Seabed 2030 Project aims to complete a high-resolution map of the entire ocean floor by 2030, equipping both crewed ships and autonomous vehicles with sonar, magnetometers, and other sensors to fill in the remaining three-quarters of the planet hidden beneath the waves.
