# Arabian Sea

The Arabian Sea is the northwestern arm of the Indian Ocean, a roughly 3.86 million km² basin bounded by the Arabian Peninsula, Iran, Pakistan, India, and the Horn of Africa, reaching a maximum depth of about 5,395 meters. For at least five thousand years its monsoon-driven winds and central position between Mesopotamia, the Indus Valley, and East Africa have made it one of the world's busiest trade corridors, and today it still carries about a quarter of global seaborne oil and roughly a fifth of seaborne LNG through the Strait of Hormuz. The same geography that funnels ships also concentrates heat, nutrients, and pollution, producing a uniquely productive but increasingly stressed marine ecosystem.

## Geography and Connections

The southern and central floor is the relatively flat Arabian Basin, deeper than 4,500 meters in most places, and is cut by the Alula-Fartak Trough and the Owen Fracture Zone, which marks the boundary where the Indian Plate slides past the Arabian Plate. To the northwest the Gulf of Aden and the narrow Strait of Bab-el-Mandeb link the Arabian Sea to the Red Sea, while to the west the Gulf of Oman and the Strait of Hormuz connect it to the Persian Gulf; the Strait of Hormuz handles about 25% of global seaborne oil and 19% of LNG, most of it bound for Asia. The continental shelf is narrow along the Makran and East African coasts but extends more than 300 km off India. At the foot of Pakistan lies the Indus Fan, a vast underwater cone of sediment dumped by the Indus River since the end of the last ice age.

## Monsoon Climate and Warming

A seasonal wind reversal, the monsoon, dominates the climate. In summer the Findlater Jet, a ribbon of strong southwesterly winds, pulls warm, moist air from the sea toward the Indian subcontinent, producing the Indian summer rains while leaving Arabia and the Horn of Africa dry. In winter, cold, dry air flows outward from the Himalayas toward the equator. Sailors exploited this rhythm for at least three millennia: a ship could sail northeast on the summer monsoon and return on the winter monsoon, with voyages effectively waiting at each end until the winds reversed. Sea surface temperatures have risen by about 0.84°C over the past century, with a sharp jump between 1985 and 1987 that established the present warming trend. Warmer water lengthens the season of high sea-surface temperature, intensifies the monsoon, and drives more erratic cyclones and heavier rainfall along the coast.

## Productivity and a Growing Dead Zone

The Arabian Sea Large Marine Ecosystem is one of the most biologically productive ocean regions on Earth, hosting at least 330 coral species, 550 mollusk species, and more than 1,200 fish species, along with protected marine mammals such as blue whales and humpback whales. That productivity is paid for in oxygen. Surface waters are warm and stratified, and agricultural runoff and sewage pour nutrients into the sea, so algae bloom and then decompose in a mid-depth layer that barely exchanges air with the surface. The result is one of the world's largest oxygen minimum zones (OMZs), an oxygen-starved layer in which concentrations fall to near zero. Eutrophication, the over-enrichment of water by nutrients, and continued warming are expanding this zone in the 2000s, which squeezes fish habitats and helps explain why more than half of the sea's fish stocks are collapsed or overexploited and about 80% of the catch comes from fully exploited stocks. Destructive fishing gear, bycatch, the limited extent of coastal mangroves, and mass coral bleaching from repeated marine heatwaves add to the pressure. The knifetooth sawfish is critically endangered here, and dugongs are classed as vulnerable.

## Five Thousand Years of Trade

Trade across the Arabian Sea began around 3000 BCE between Mesopotamia, the Indus Valley, and East Africa, and by the 1st millennium BCE a Greek-speaking Egyptian merchant could describe its ports and anchorages in the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*. From the 2nd century BCE the sea became the central hub of the Maritime Silk Road, gradually eclipsing the overland Silk Road because sea transport was cheaper per ton. The adoption of the lateen sail, a triangular sail on a long angled yard, in the 1st millennium CE let ships tack against the wind and loosen the rigid "trapped by the monsoon" rhythm. Between roughly 600 and 1498 CE the rise of Islam gave the basin a shared language and legal framework, and Arabic became the lingua franca of Indian Ocean commerce, while Chinese Admiral Zheng He's seven voyages between 1405 and 1433 showed how far a single fleet could reach. The arrival of the Portuguese in the 15th and 16th centuries shifted political control of the network, while the trade itself continued to spread crops such as millet and sorghum, technologies, Islam, and at times disease.

## Modern Economy and Stress

The sea's economic life still runs through a few chokepoints and a few ports. The Strait of Hormuz, the Gulf of Aden, and the Strait of Bab-el-Mandeb funnel oil, gas, and container traffic toward Asian markets, with Karachi, Gwadar, Jawaharlal Nehru, and Salalah as the largest handling hubs. Commercial fish catches quadrupled between 1950 and 1990 and have since plateaued, with recent growth driven largely by distant-water Chinese fleets. Geopolitical risk remains high, from Somali piracy in the southwest to conflicts that intermittently shut nearby ports, and coastal waters receive large volumes of untreated industrial wastewater and sewage.

## Open Questions

Two factual disputes matter for any precise definition of the sea. The International Hydrographic Organization fixes its eastern limit at Sadashivgad Village on the Indian coast, while the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration uses Devgad Island, a few hundred kilometers to the north. Scholars also increasingly doubt that the *Periplus* really describes active Roman traders in the 1st millennium CE; it may instead reflect a thin sliver of Egyptian-Mediterranean knowledge of a much larger Indian Ocean world run by others. The trajectory of the oxygen minimum zone is similarly unsettled: some global models project a long-term easing of ocean de-oxygenation, but the Arabian Sea's dead zone is presently expanding and no one can yet say when, or whether, it will recover.
