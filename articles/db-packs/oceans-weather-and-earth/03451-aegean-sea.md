# Aegean Sea

The Aegean Sea is an elongated arm of the Mediterranean, set between the Greek mainland to the west and the Anatolian peninsula of Turkey to the east, with Crete closing off its southern edge. It covers about 214,000 km² and reaches a maximum depth of 2,639 m west of Karpathos. To the north it connects through the Dardanelles and the Bosphorus to the Black Sea, a position that has long made it a maritime bridge between Europe and Asia.

The sea's water moves in a two-layer pattern. Warm, salty Mediterranean water enters from the south, while cooler, fresher Black Sea surface water exits northward through the Dardanelles. The denser Mediterranean water sinks beneath it, producing a slow counterclockwise circulation. The Etesian winds, strong dry northerlies, scour the basin each summer under a Mediterranean climate of hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters. Three island groups dominate: the Cyclades and Dodecanese in the central and southeastern Aegean, and the Sporades along the eastern mainland, with Crete standing apart as the largest single island and the southern boundary.

Human history on the Aegean begins with the Minoan civilization on Crete (c. 3000–1450 BC), Europe's first advanced culture, followed by the mainland Mycenaeans (c. 1600–1100 BC), whose palace-states and Linear B script provide the earliest written Greek. After the Bronze Age collapse, city-states such as Athens and Sparta emerged, and the Aegean became the stage for the Greco-Persian Wars. The Greek fleet's victory at Salamis in 480 BC halted Persian expansion westward. A century later, Philip II and his son Alexander the Great carried Macedonian power across the Aegean, unifying the region for the first time.

Rome absorbed the Aegean in the 2nd century BC through the Macedonian Wars, and after the empire split, the Byzantine East guarded the sea for nearly a millennium against Arab, Bulgarian, and Seljuk Turkish raids. That continuity broke in 1204, when the Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople and let Venice carve out the Duchy of the Archipelago and other Aegean colonies. Ottoman forces gradually absorbed the coast over the 14th and 15th centuries; Venetian Crete held out until 1669. Ottoman control then lasted until the Greek War of Independence (1821–1829) created a Greek state on the Aegean shore. The Balkan Wars (1912–1913) and 1920s border settlements fixed the present Greek and Turkish coastlines.

Since the 1970s the same waters have been the focus of the Aegean dispute between Greece and Turkey. The arguments centre on four interlocking questions: how wide each country's territorial sea may be, where national airspace ends, how the seabed and exclusive economic zones should be divided, and which small islands and islets carry their own maritime entitlements. Greek islands dotting the eastern Aegean sit close to the Turkish coast, so the extent of their surrounding waters and seabed rights directly affects Turkey's access to resources and open sea.
