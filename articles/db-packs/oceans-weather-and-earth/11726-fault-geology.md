# Fault (geology)

A fault is a planar fracture in rock across which the two sides have moved past each other. Most large faults form at the boundaries of Earth's tectonic plates, where slow grinding produces sudden slip; the energy released is the cause of most earthquakes. Some faults also creep slowly without shaking, a behaviour called aseismic creep.

Several terms describe how a fault appears in the field. The **fault plane** is the fracture surface itself. The **fault trace** (or fault line) is where the plane meets the ground surface and is the line drawn on geological maps. A **fault zone** is a band of closely spaced parallel faults; prolonged motion crushes the rock between them into fault-bound lenses until, in places, the zone becomes a single broad band.

## Why faults slip

Friction keeps the two sides from sliding smoothly. Sticky patches called **asperities** lock the surfaces together while tectonic forces continue to push the surrounding rock. Stress builds until it overcomes the strength of the asperities; the fault ruptures and releases stored strain energy as seismic waves. In the brittle upper crust this release is sudden and produces earthquakes, while in the hot, ductile lower crust and mantle rock deforms gradually by shearing. Even in ductile rock, a very high strain rate can trigger sudden rupture.

## Measuring motion: slip, heave, throw

The relative movement of the two sides is **slip**, a vector with a direction and a magnitude. Two components matter most: the **throw**, the vertical displacement, and the **heave**, the horizontal component ("throw up, heave out"). Slip direction can often be read from drag folds, the small folds near a fault produced by frictional drag, but exact heave and throw require matching a feature that crosses the fault on both sides, a **piercing point**.

## Hanging wall and footwall

On any non-vertical fault the upper side is the **hanging wall** and the lower side the **footwall**. Miners coined the terms because they stood on the footwall with the hanging wall above their heads. The terms are needed to name dip-slip faults: in a **normal fault** the hanging wall drops; in a **reverse fault** it rises. The same distinction tells a geologist whether the region is being pulled apart (extensional) or squeezed (compressional).

## Fault types by slip direction

Geologists classify faults by **dip**, the angle the fault plane makes with the surface, and by the direction of slip.

- **Strike-slip** faults: the plane is near-vertical and the two sides slide horizontally past each other. Left-lateral (sinistral) motion means the far side moves left; right-lateral (dextral) means it moves right. When a strike-slip fault forms a plate boundary it is a **transform fault**, along which lithosphere is neither created nor destroyed. The Dead Sea Transform and New Zealand's Alpine Fault are examples.
- **Dip-slip** faults: motion is mainly along the dip direction. Normal faults (dip usually 60° or more, sometimes less than 45°) come from extension; reverse faults from compression. A **thrust fault** is a reverse fault with dip less than 45°, often forming ramps and flats that climb through rock layers and build folds and nappes. Subduction zones are giant thrusts and produce Earth's largest earthquakes.
- **Oblique-slip** faults carry components of both dip-slip and strike-slip; nearly every fault does to some degree.

Extensional terrains show characteristic landforms. A down-dropped block between two inward-dipping normal faults is a **graben**; a block left up between two outward-dipping faults is a **horst**. Alternating grabens and horsts create the basin-and-range topography seen in the western United States.

Some normal faults curve with depth, steep near the surface and flattening downward; these are **listric faults**. Where the curved plane flattens fully it becomes a **décollement**, and a large décollement of regional scale is a **detachment fault**. The gap opened as the hanging wall slides can be filled by folded rock (rollover folding) or by broken blocks in **imbrication fans** or **domino faulting**.

**Ring faults** are the circular fractures around collapsed volcanic calderas and large impact craters, such as the Chesapeake Bay structure; they may be intruded by ring dikes. Minor faults associated with a major one are **synthetic** if they dip the same way and **antithetic** if they dip the opposite way. Stress directions can flip through geologic time, and a normal fault formed under extension can be reactivated as a reverse fault when compression takes over, a process called **fault inversion**.

## Fault rocks

Every fault has thickness. Slip grinds and transforms the rock along the surface into distinctive **fault rocks** whose texture records depth, temperature, and fluid conditions. **Cataclasite** is broken but cohesive rock; **fault breccia** is coarse cataclasite with more than 30% visible fragments; **fault gouge** is a clay-rich, incohesive powder with less than 30% fragments; **clay smear** forms when clay layers are dragged into a gouge. **Mylonite** is a cohesive, finely layered rock produced by ductile shearing at depth. **Pseudotachylyte** is a dark glassy vein material that forms only during true seismic slip, and it can be used to confirm ancient earthquakes on faults that no longer move.

## Why faults matter

A fault is a discontinuity that controls the strength and deformation of soil and rock, which matters for tunnels, foundations, and slopes. In California new buildings are prohibited on or near faults that have moved within the Holocene (the last 11,700 years), and faults active during the Holocene plus the Pleistocene (the last 2.6 million years) receive special scrutiny for dams, hospitals, and power plants. Geologists date past rupture with radiocarbon on buried organic material and study soil profiles and aerial photographs to distinguish active from inactive faults; this work underpins paleoseismology and seismic-hazard assessment.

Fractured fault zones also channel mineral-bearing fluids and ascending magma, so their intersections are common sites for ore deposits; the Domeyko Fault in northern Chile hosts several giant porphyry copper deposits. The same permeability makes fault zones efficient aquifers, enhanced by chemical weathering that widens the fractured rock.
