# Water purification

Water purification removes chemicals, biological contaminants, suspended solids, and gases from water so it is fit for a specific purpose, most commonly drinking. The source determines what treatment is needed. Deep groundwater, often rain that fell centuries ago, is filtered by soil and rock to high clarity and usually needs only a secondary disinfectant, though it can carry dissolved minerals such as calcium, magnesium, iron, and manganese. Surface waters (rivers, lowland reservoirs) carry high bacterial loads, suspended solids, and dissolved contaminants; upland reservoirs are cleaner but may be acidic or coloured by humic acids from peaty soils. Seawater is desalinated by distillation or reverse osmosis. Visual inspection cannot establish safety; only chemical and microbiological analysis reveals what a given source needs.

Conventional plants combine a sequence of stages, each removing a different class of impurity.

**pH adjustment.** Pure water has a pH near 7; seawater runs 7.5–8.4, fresh water varies widely. Lime, soda ash, or caustic soda raise the pH of acidic water, precipitating calcium and magnesium carbonates and improving later coagulation. Correct pH reduces corrosion of iron pipes and suppresses lead leaching from lead pipes and solder, which is why orthophosphate is sometimes added.

**Coagulation and flocculation.** The first chemical step adds inorganic coagulants such as aluminium sulfate (alum) or iron(III) salts. Within seconds these neutralise the negative surface charges on suspended particles and precipitate as metal hydroxides. The precipitates, called floc, aggregate through Brownian motion and gentle mixing, enmeshing clay, silt, algae, bacteria, viruses, and natural organic matter. Aluminium hydroxide floc forms between pH 5.5 and 7.7; iron(III) hydroxide floc forms between 5.0 and 8.5. Plants inject the coagulant in a rapid-mix chamber, then move water into flocculation basins (15–45 minutes) where slow paddles grow larger flocs. Cationic polymers such as polyDADMAC can aid or replace metal coagulants by bridging particles.

**Sedimentation and flotation.** Water flows into large, low-velocity tanks where floc settles. Settled sludge is 3–5% of treated volume and must be removed and disposed of, a major operating cost. Lamella plates and floc blanket clarifiers improve removal in a smaller footprint. When particles settle poorly, dissolved air flotation attaches fine bubbles to floc and skims it from the surface, a method favoured for waters with algal blooms or low turbidity combined with high colour.

**Filtration.** Clarified water passes through a filter to catch remaining particles. In a rapid sand filter, water flows down through sand, often with activated carbon or anthracite on top to adsorb taste- and odour-causing organics. Although sand pores are larger than the smallest particles, most are trapped deeper in the bed by adhesion; if the surface alone captured them the filter would clog quickly. Cleaning reverses the flow (backwashing), sometimes with air scour. Slow sand filters work differently: a thin biological layer called the *Schmutzdecke* develops on top of graded sand and digests contaminants biologically, so the filters need only periodic scraping, not backwashing, but they require large land areas. Membrane filters with pores around 0.2 µm remove nearly all particles, including *Giardia* and *Cryptosporidium*, but cannot remove dissolved substances such as nitrates or heavy-metal ions.

**Dissolved-ion removal.** Ion-exchange resins swap unwanted ions (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, lead, arsenic) for benign ones, commonly Na⁺ or K⁺ in softening. Precipitative softening adds lime and soda ash to force calcium carbonate out of solution via the common-ion effect.

**Disinfection.** Filtration removes most microbes; disinfection kills the rest and leaves a residual that protects the distribution system. Target pathogens include bacteria such as *Salmonella*, *Vibrio cholerae*, *Campylobacter*, and *Shigella*, plus protozoa such as *Giardia* and *Cryptosporidium*.

*Chlorine and its compounds* are the most common disinfectants, supplied as gas, sodium hypochlorite, or calcium hypochlorite; levels up to 4 mg/L are considered safe. Chlorine reacts with natural organic matter to form trihalomethanes (THMs) and haloacetic acids (HAAs), both regulated because they are carcinogenic at high concentrations, and it is weak against *Giardia* and *Cryptosporidium* cysts. *Chloramine*, made by adding ammonia after chlorine, persists longer in pipes and produces fewer THMs/HAAs, but can corrode older lead service lines, an effect counteracted by adding orthophosphate. *Chlorine dioxide* acts faster but is restricted because it forms excess chlorite.

*Ozone* (O₃) is a powerful oxidant that inactivates even resistant cysts. It produces fewer halogenated by-products but reacts with bromide ions to form bromate, regulated at 10 ppb by the US EPA, and leaves no residual, so a secondary disinfectant is usually added. *Ultraviolet light* inactivates cysts in clear water but effectiveness drops sharply with turbidity and it leaves no residual.

**Other methods.** Boiling kills intestinal pathogens above about 60 °C but concentrates non-volatile solutes and leaves no residual. Distillation boils water, condenses the vapour, and leaves most solutes behind, yielding water about 99.9% pure. Reverse osmosis forces water through a semi-permeable membrane under pressure and is the most thorough large-scale method available. Activated carbon adsorption removes many organic contaminants, but the bed can itself grow bacteria and must be replaced.

Demineralised water from distillation or reverse osmosis is not ideal drinking water: low-mineral water leaches electrolytes and more readily dissolves lead and cadmium from pipes, with documented cases of infant lead poisoning. WHO recommends at least 20 mg/L calcium and 10 mg/L magnesium, with total hardness of 2–4 mmol/L; hardness above 5 mmol/L is associated with higher rates of gallstones, kidney stones, and arthropathies.

Sand filters for public supplies date from 1804 in Paisley, Scotland; in 1829 James Simpson built the first large treated public supply for London's Chelsea Waterworks. In 1854 John Snow linked the Broad Street cholera outbreak to the local pump, demonstrating waterborne transmission, and the Metropolis Water Act of 1855 required all London water to be filtered. Permanent chlorination began in 1905 at Lincoln, England, after a typhoid epidemic. The WHO estimates 1.1 billion people still lack access to improved drinking water and 1.8 million die from diarrhoeal disease each year, with 94% of cases preventable through safer water, sanitation, and hygiene.
