# Norwegian Sea

The Norwegian Sea is a deep marginal sea, partially enclosed by land and adjacent to larger oceans, lying northwest of Norway between the North Sea and the Greenland Sea. Despite its high latitude, it stays ice-free year-round because warm Atlantic water flows in continuously. That single fact explains most of the sea's climate role, its biological richness, and why Norway, Iceland, and the Faroe Islands (Denmark) share its waters.

## Formation and geography

About 250 million years ago, the Eurasian and North American tectonic plates began rifting apart, and the basin that became the Norwegian Sea opened between them. The present continental slope still marks where Norway and Greenland once joined. During the last ice age, glaciers gouged Norway's fjords and pushed the slope seaward.

The floor averages roughly 2 km deep and is split into two basins by a mid-sea ridge. The southern basin is larger and deeper, plunging to 3,500–4,000 m. Submarine sills (shallow ridges connecting deeper basins) such as the Scotland–Greenland Ridge, averaging only 500 m deep, separate the Norwegian Sea from neighbouring seas and control how water moves between them.

## Why it never freezes

Four major water masses converge here, and their interplay drives weather far beyond the region.

The warm, salty North Atlantic Current enters from the south, releases heat into the atmosphere, and that energy transfer keeps the surface above freezing even in winter. The current runs roughly twice as strong in winter as in summer, because colder, denser air intensifies the pressure gradient that pulls warm water northward. This same heat release gives Norway a milder climate than other places at the same latitude.

Cold, dense water from the Greenland Sea pours in through the Fram Strait (between Greenland and Svalbard) and sinks to form Norwegian Sea Deep Water (NSDW). NSDW sits below 0 °C, is highly saline, carries nutrients from the Arctic, but holds little oxygen because it has been isolated from the surface for a long time. Where this cold deep water meets the warm surface layer, especially in winter, the sea acts as a transition zone between boreal (mild, mid-latitude) and Arctic conditions and helps drive thermohaline circulation, the global "ocean conveyor" powered by differences in water temperature and salinity.

## A biological crossroads

Because warm and cold water meet here, the Norwegian Sea hosts species from both boreal and Arctic realms. The biological year begins with a phytoplankton bloom (a surge of microscopic drifting plants) peaking in May, dominated by diatoms, single-celled algae with silica shells. Zooplankton, especially the copepod *Calanus finmarchicus* (a tiny crustacean that is the key grazer of the North Atlantic), feed on the bloom and become the base of the food web supporting herring, cod, whales, and seabirds.

Cold-water coral reefs built by *Lophelia pertusa* grow on the continental slopes, creating habitat for other species, though bottom trawling (dragging weighted nets across the seafloor) has damaged many of them. The coastal waters, especially around Lofoten, are spawning grounds for Atlantic cod, and the entire stock of Norwegian spring-spawning herring depends on the sea's plankton productivity.

That productivity is not stable. Herring stocks rose through the early twentieth century, then collapsed in the 1960s–70s as climate shifts redistributed plankton and overfishing removed the fish. The collapse cascaded upward: seabirds such as puffins, which feed on young herring, starved in huge numbers, and whales that followed the fish moved elsewhere.

## Human stakes

Cod fishing at Lofoten has run for centuries but suffered from overfishing. Whaling was intensive from the 1600s to the early 1900s, nearly wiping out local bowhead and blue whale populations. Jan Mayen island was sighted in 1607 and became a Dutch whaling base; English Muscovy Company crews hunted walrus and whale near Spitsbergen from 1610.

Today the sea's main non-fishery resource is hydrocarbons. Norway began undersea oil production in 1993 and started gas production at the giant Ormen Lange field in 2007, sending output through long pipelines to shore. Norway's claim to these resources rests on a 200-nautical-mile exclusive economic zone (EEZ), first declared in 1976. First systematic depth soundings were taken by Constantine Phipps in 1773, and the first full oceanographic expedition ran aboard the Norwegian research vessel *Vøringen* in 1876–1878.

Two pressures define the modern era. The Northern Sea Route, an Arctic shipping lane along Russia's northern coast, is becoming more navigable as sea ice retreats, and the Norwegian Sea is part of that corridor. During the Cold War the basin became a strategic gateway: Soviet submarines, including the nuclear-powered *Komsomolets*, which sank in 1989 with its reactor and warheads still aboard, passed through on their way to the Atlantic, and the wreck remains a persistent radioactive contamination concern. Around 8,000 years ago, the Storegga Slide, a massive submarine landslide off Norway's coast, sent a tsunami across the surrounding coastlines, a reminder that the sea's geology can act as quickly as its currents.
