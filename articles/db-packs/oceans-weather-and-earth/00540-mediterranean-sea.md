# Mediterranean Sea

The Mediterranean is a small, almost-enclosed intercontinental sea bordered by Europe, Asia, and Africa, connected to the Atlantic only through the 14 km Strait of Gibraltar. That near-isolation shapes its geology, circulation, biology, and human history. Covering about 2,500,000 km² (0.7% of global ocean surface), it averages 1,500 m deep and reaches 5,109 m in the Calypso Deep.

## A sea that nearly disappeared

The basin formed as the Neotethys during African–Eurasian plate convergence. About 5.96 million years ago, the Betic and Rifian corridors closed, sealing the Mediterranean from the Atlantic. With evaporation far exceeding inflow, the sea largely desiccated, leaving evaporite deposits up to 3 km thick on the floor. This *Messinian salinity crisis* ended around 5.33 mya when the Atlantic breached the Strait of Gibraltar. The resulting *Zanclean flood*, roughly 1,000 times the Amazon's present discharge, refilled the basin in under two years. Active plate boundaries still generate the earthquakes, tsunamis, and volcanoes (Etna, Vesuvius, Thera, Campi Flegrei) that shape the region.

## Circulation driven by evaporation

Because evaporation exceeds precipitation and river input, the Mediterranean loses about 1 m of level per year without replacement. Atlantic water pours in at Gibraltar (~70,000 m³/s). Cool, fresher Atlantic surface water flows east along North Africa, warms and gains salinity in the Levantine Sea, then sinks to form *Levantine Intermediate Water* (LIW) between 50 and 600 m depth. This LIW circulates westward along Greek and Italian coasts and exits at depth through Gibraltar. Deep water forms in winter when cold, dry winds (Bora, Mistral) chill surface waters in the Adriatic, Aegean, and Gulf of Lion, allowing dense water to sink. The system can switch abruptly: the 1990s *Eastern Mediterranean Transient* shifted deep-water formation to the Aegean, and the *Bimodal Oscillating System* (BiOS) in the North Ionian Gyre periodically flips the source of inflowing water between Adriatic and Levantine origins. Average salinity is 38 PSU, rising eastward, and water residence time is roughly 100 years.

## An oligotrophic, warming sea

The Mediterranean is *oligotrophic*: nutrient-poor and low in chlorophyll, with primary productivity peaking briefly in spring when light and winter-mixed nutrients coincide. Summer stratification then cuts off the deep nutrient supply, and chlorophyll maxima sit at 80–200 m. The N:P ratio of 24–29 exceeds the global average of 16, indicating phosphorus limitation. About 17,000 marine species live here, mostly of Atlantic origin that entered after the Zanclean flood; biodiversity is highest on shallow coasts and falls with depth.

The climate is mild and wet in winter, hot and dry in summer. Since the 1950s the entire water column has warmed, and projections to 2100 forecast less rain, more evaporation, higher salinity, and stronger layering. Marine heatwaves, driven by high-pressure systems that suppress cloud cover, have intensified, with five consecutive years (2015–2019) of mass die-offs in benthic communities and coral bleaching. The eastern basin has flipped to a net source of CO₂. Sea-level rise of 3–61 cm by 2100 threatens low-lying coasts, with half a million people displaced in the Nile Delta by a 30 cm rise alone.

## The cradle of connected civilisations

Permanent settlement in the Levant dates to about 12,000 BC, and the sea became the principal corridor for trade, migration, and cultural exchange among the Egyptians, Minoans, Greeks, Phoenicians, and Romans. The Bronze Age Collapse around 1200 BC devastated eastern cities. After defeating Carthage in the Punic Wars (3rd–2nd c. BC), Rome controlled the entire coastline for 400 years, the only state ever to do so, calling it *Mare Nostrum*. The 7th-century Arab expansion introduced sugarcane, rice, citrus, cotton, spinach, eggplant, saffron, bananas, and wider cultivation of olives and pomegranates. Ottoman naval power peaked with the fall of Constantinople in 1453; the Battle of Lepanto in 1571 checked Ottoman expansion in the last great galley engagement. The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 rerouted Europe–Asia trade.

## An environment under pressure

UNEP estimates annual inputs of 650 million tonnes of sewage, 129,000 t of mineral oil, 60,000 t of mercury, and 36,000 t of phosphates; the seabed holds on average nearly 2,000 debris items per km². Shipping moves about 370 Mt of oil a year (roughly 20% of the global total) with 250–300 tankers passing daily and around ten spills annually. More than 65% of fish stocks lie outside safe biological limits, and large, long-lived species have largely vanished from catches. Over 70% of exotic decapods and about two-thirds of exotic fishes are Indo-Pacific *Lessepsian* migrants that entered through Suez and now dominate parts of the Levantine basin, while warming waters admit tropical Atlantic species at Gibraltar. Mass tourism since the 1950s accelerates coastal erosion, and the Mediterranean monk seal remains critically endangered. Earthquakes and tsunamis continue to drive disaster risk, including 198,548 deaths from such events in the 20th century.
