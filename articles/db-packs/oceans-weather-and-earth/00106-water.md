# Water

Water (H₂O) is the most abundant liquid on Earth. It covers 71% of the planet's surface, makes up 50–60% of the human body, and is the medium in which life's chemistry happens. What makes water fit for this role is one feature: its molecules form strong hydrogen bonds with each other. Nearly every unusual property of water traces back to those bonds.

## Molecular structure and hydrogen bonding

A water molecule is bent, with the two hydrogen atoms forming a 104.5° angle around the oxygen, slightly less than a perfect tetrahedron because oxygen's lone electron pairs push the bonds inward. The O–H bond length is about 0.096 nm. Because oxygen pulls electrons more strongly than hydrogen, the molecule is polar: one negative end and two positive ends.

This polarity lets each water molecule form up to four hydrogen bonds with neighbours, each about 23 kJ/mol and roughly ten times stronger than the weak Van der Waals forces that hold most other liquids together. Around 90% of a hydrogen bond's strength comes from electrostatics; the remaining 10% is partly covalent.

These bonds explain why a molecule this small behaves so unusually: high melting (0 °C) and boiling (100 °C) points at 1 atm, far above analogous compounds like hydrogen sulfide; specific heat capacity of about 4.2 J/(g·K), so oceans stabilise climate; heat of fusion of 333 J/g and heat of vaporisation of 2257 J/g, making water resistant to freezing and steam a powerful heat-transfer fluid; and high surface tension with capillary action, which lets vascular plants pull water from roots to leaves.

## Density, ice, and the phase diagram

Water is the only common substance to exist naturally as solid, liquid, and gas at Earth's surface. At 1 atm it reaches maximum density of 999.972 kg/m³ at 3.98 °C. Below that it expands, and on freezing at 0 °C it expands by about 9%, dropping to 917 kg/m³. Ice floats because of this.

The consequence for life is direct. In a freezing lake, the densest water (at 4 °C) sinks to the bottom, colder water sits above, and ice forms a floating insulating lid. Without this, lakes would freeze solid from the bottom up each winter. The same density inversion drives thermohaline circulation, the slow ocean current system that redistributes heat around the planet.

Two reference points on the phase diagram matter. The triple point, where ice, liquid, and vapour coexist, is 273.16 K and 611.657 pascals; until May 2019 it defined the Kelvin scale. The critical point is 647.096 K and 22.064 megapascals; above this water becomes a supercritical fluid, used as an ecologically benign industrial solvent. As of 2024, twenty distinct ice phases have been confirmed, including ice XVIII, a superionic phase produced by shocking a water droplet, in which oxygen forms a solid lattice while hydrogen ions flow through it.

## Water as a solvent

Because water is polar, it dissolves other polar and ionic substances easily: salts, sugars, gases such as oxygen and carbon dioxide, and the proteins, DNA, and polysaccharides of living tissue. Fats, oils, and alkanes are hydrophobic and do not dissolve.

Pure water self-ionises weakly (2H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻) and is a poor conductor; conductivity rises as dissolved ions enter. Splitting water by electrolysis requires 285.8 kJ/mol, more than the heat recovered by burning it back. Water is nearly incompressible, shrinking only about 1.8% at 4 km ocean depth. Pure water sits at pH 7, the neutral midpoint between acids (proton donors) and bases (proton acceptors).

Water participates directly in biology. Photosynthesis uses sunlight to split water, releasing oxygen and combining hydrogen with CO₂ to form glucose. Cellular respiration reverses the process. Anabolism removes water to build larger molecules; catabolism adds it to break them apart.

## Water on Earth

Earth holds about 1.386 billion cubic kilometres of water, of which roughly 96.5% is in the oceans. Freshwater is a small share: an estimated 69% sits in glaciers and permanent snow, 30% in groundwater, and just 1% in lakes, rivers, the atmosphere, and living things. The water cycle, driven by evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation, and runoff, continuously moves water between atmosphere, soil, surface water, groundwater, and plants.

Seawater averages 3.5% sodium chloride, freezes at about −1.9 °C, and ranges in salinity from 0.7% in the Baltic to 4.0% in the Red Sea. Tides come from the gravitational pull of the Moon and Sun.

## Water beyond Earth

Hydrogen and oxygen are among the most abundant elements in the universe, so water is widespread. Water vapour has been detected in the atmospheres of the Sun (traces), Mercury (3.4%), Venus (0.002%), Earth (≈0.40%), and Mars (0.03%), and on many extrasolar planets. Liquid water is confirmed on Earth, present in small amounts on Mars, and strongly suspected on Saturn's moons Enceladus (a 10-km-thick ocean 30–40 km beneath the south pole) and Titan, and on Jupiter's moons Europa and Ganymede. Water ice covers parts of Mars, the Moon, Ceres, Pluto–Charon, and comets.

Earth sits near the inner edge of the Sun's habitable zone, only about 5%, or 8 million km, from the optimal distance for liquid water, and its gravity holds an atmosphere. Both conditions are prerequisites for the oceans covering 71% of the surface.

## Water and civilisation

Cities have always grown along rivers: the Tigris–Euphrates, the Nile, the Indus (c. 3300–1300 BCE), and the Tiber. Roughly 70% of human fresh-water use goes to agriculture, which can reach 80–90% of total water consumption. Other major uses include industrial cooling, nuclear-reactor cooling and neutron moderation, firefighting, food processing, and hydroelectric power.

The human body is 50–60% water. The U.S. National Academies recommend 3.7 L/day for adult men and 2.7 L/day for women, though medical literature supports lower typical intakes, around 1 L for an average male. The popular "eight glasses a day" rule has no clear scientific basis.

## The water crisis

By 2023, the UN World Water Development Report estimated 2 billion people (26% of the global population) lacked safe drinking water and 3.6 billion (46%) lacked safely managed sanitation. Roughly 30% of irrigation water comes from unsustainable groundwater withdrawals, and in developing countries 90% of municipal wastewater is discharged untreated into rivers. Poor water quality causes about 5 million deaths a year from water-related diseases.

In 2026, the UN declared humanity had entered an era of "water bankruptcy," noting that more than half of large lakes have shrunk since the 1990s, 35% of wetlands have disappeared since 1970, 75% of the world's population live in water-insecure countries, and droughts cost about $307 billion per year.
