# Bitter taste evolution

About 500 million years ago, the common ancestor of jawed vertebrates evolved the ability to taste bitterness. Most natural poisons are bitter, so rejecting bitter food usually means rejecting toxins. The receptor family behind this is TAS2R, found in jawed vertebrates but absent in jawless ones such as lampreys.

## The receptor family and how it grows

TAS2R genes encode G-protein-coupled receptors. When a bitter molecule binds one on the cell surface, a calcium-driven signal is triggered inside the cell. The same receptor type appears in taste buds and in the pancreas and gut, where it likely helps the body respond to ingested toxins. The best-known probe molecule is PTC (phenylthiocarbamide); some people taste it intensely, others taste nothing.

Genes duplicate by accident, and a freed copy can mutate freely. Most duplicates decay into pseudogenes, broken gene copies that no longer make a working protein, but occasionally a duplicate keeps working and picks up sensitivity to a new toxin. Copy number in TAS2R follows a clear pattern:

- Cartilaginous fish: 0–1 copies
- Bony fish such as zebrafish: about 7 copies
- Tetrapods (land vertebrates): dozens of copies

The jump onto land exposed animals to a far wider range of plant toxins, and the cluster expanded to keep up.

## Diet shapes the cluster

More copies means a broader detection palette, so species eating risky food tend to carry more TAS2R genes. Among primates, the plant-heavy Old World monkeys (Cercopithecidae) show especially high duplication. In the bat genus Myotis, the cluster expanded as well, and the extra copies help these insect-eating bats detect plant-derived toxins such as arbutin and salicin carried by their insect prey.

The opposite also holds. Carnivores rarely encounter plant toxins but can afford to be extremely sensitive to the few bitter compounds they do meet. When researchers tested sensitivity to the bitter marker quinine, carnivores were most sensitive, omnivores intermediate, and grazers and browsers least sensitive. Diet, not ancestry, sets the threshold.

## Detecting positive selection

To tell whether a gene is being favoured, evolutionary biologists compare two kinds of mutation. A synonymous mutation is a silent DNA change that does not alter the protein and is effectively neutral. A non-synonymous mutation changes the protein, so when such changes accumulate faster than silent ones, selection is shaping the gene. The standard ratio, Ka/Ks, equals 1 under neutrality, falls below 1 when the gene is conserved because the wrong change is harmful, and rises above 1 when new variants are spreading because they help. In TAS2R, Ka/Ks exceeds 1 in the regions that bind bitter molecules, confirming that the binding pocket is being refined to track changing plant toxins. Outside the binding pocket, relaxed and neutral evolution dominate.

## Humans: a history written in taste

Humans carry 25 functional TAS2R genes and 11 pseudogenes, an unusually modest count for a primate, and the shortfall is informative.

Roughly two million years ago, hominid diets shifted toward meat, and about 800,000 years ago controlled use of fire arrived. Cooking detoxifies food and reduces the need for a large toxin-detecting repertoire. Chimpanzees, whose diet is about 15% meat and the rest mostly ripe fruit low in toxins, show the same pattern of relaxed constraint, suggesting the slowdown began before the two lineages split.

The TAS2R16 gene illustrates regional pressures. The 172Asn allele is nearly fixed across Eurasia and in some African Pygmy groups, but rare elsewhere in Africa. In Africa, accumulated knowledge of which plants are toxic, dating to around 10,000 years ago, let populations relax selection on the gene. After migration into Eurasia, unfamiliar flora made that knowledge useless, and selection on TAS2R16 intensified again. Among small, isolated Pygmy populations, the allele's high frequency is better explained by genetic drift, random fluctuation of allele frequencies in small populations amplified by the founder effect, than by adaptation.

Pseudogenes add another chapter. African populations usually show the highest diversity at TAS2R pseudogenes, matching the rest of the genome, but at two loci (TAS2R6P and TAS2R18P) non-African populations are most diverse. The functional versions of these genes must have arisen before the out-of-Africa migration, after which relaxed selection outside Africa let broken copies persist and accumulate differences.

The dominant signal in the human TAS2R cluster is loss of constraint: cooking, culture, and diet change have steadily reduced the need for a large bitter-detection repertoire, and pseudogenization has followed. Positive selection still flickers in the binding regions, and the alleles left behind by migration, drift, and regional cuisine continue to shape why the same vegetable can taste violently bitter to one person and bland to another.
