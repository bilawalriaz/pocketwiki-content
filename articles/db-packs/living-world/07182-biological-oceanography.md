# Biological oceanography

Biological oceanography is the study of how marine organisms shape, and are shaped by, the physics, chemistry, and geology of the ocean. It is also called ocean ecology, from the Greek *oikos*, meaning "house" or "habitat." The field overlaps with marine biology but is distinct in perspective: biological oceanography works **bottom-up** through the food web, beginning with microorganisms and tracing their effects upward to larger animals, whereas marine biology typically works top-down, from big predators downward.

The central focus is plankton (microscopic drifting organisms). Three linked questions structure the field. First, their diversity: their morphology, what they eat, how they move, and how they metabolise. Second, their productivity, especially how photosynthesis by phytoplankton (plant-like plankton) fixes carbon and so drives the global carbon cycle. Third, their distribution, governed by predation and by life-cycle stages that move organisms between depths.

## Why the ocean is its own world

Oceans cover about 71% of Earth's surface, average roughly 3,800 m deep, and reach nearly 11,000 m in the deepest trenches. The marine environment has a volume of about 1.37 billion km³, roughly 300 times the living space offered by land and freshwater combined. Life is thought to have originated in these ancient oceans long before it colonised land, and the ocean's time scales differ sharply from the atmosphere: the atmosphere exchanges globally every few weeks, while the ocean can take around 1,000 years to overturn. Because of this size and inertia, models built for land or air cannot simply be transplanted to the sea.

A second motivation is climate change. The ocean makes Earth habitable by regulating climate and by hosting primary production (the conversion of carbon dioxide into organic matter by photosynthesis, which releases oxygen as a byproduct). Global stressors such as shifts in pH, temperature, salinity, and CO₂ do not act uniformly, so the biological response has to be studied directly in the ocean.

## How the field took shape

Early observations were incidental to exploration. In 325 BC, the Greek geographer Pytheas of Massalia sailed the coasts of England and Norway, worked out a method for finding latitude from the North Star, and noted a link between tides and the Moon, a connection later developed by the English monk Bede around 700 AD. Robert Boyle's 1674 work on salinity, temperature, and pressure opened the chemistry of seawater, and trade voyages through the 15th to 18th centuries added coastal and surface knowledge.

In 1843, the British naturalist Edward Forbes declared that no marine life could exist below about 300 fathoms (~550 m), even though others had already pulled animals up from deeper water. The claim was only widely dropped when a submarine cable raised from 1,830 m came up covered in living animals. That finding prompted the Royal Society to fund a systematic survey.

The **HMS Challenger expedition of 1872–1876**, led by Charles Wyville Thomson with naturalists Henry N. Moseley and John Murray, became the founding cruise of the discipline. The Royal Society's original goal was practical, to test whether seabed cables could be laid, but the expedition systematically sampled biology, chemistry, and geology. It proved that life exists in deep water down to 5,500 m and that the ocean's overall composition is remarkably consistent. Its success triggered parallel German, French, American, and British voyages.

## What biological oceanographers actually ask

Four broad threads of questions dominate. The first maps biodiversity: which organisms live where, at what depths, and why. The second measures the production of organic matter by marine life and the physical and chemical factors that control growth rates. The third studies relationships, both between organisms from microbes to whales and between organisms and the chemistry or physics of their surroundings. The fourth is more applied: how much can be harvested from the sea, and how weather, seasons, natural disasters, and long-term climate change will affect fisheries. Among these, understanding how climate change will alter ocean biota is the central question for the coming decades.
