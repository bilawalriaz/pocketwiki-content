# Biological pest control

Biological pest control (biocontrol) uses living organisms to suppress pests: insects, mites, weeds, and pathogens. It works by exploiting natural ecological relationships: predation (one animal eats another), parasitism (living on a host), parasitoidism (a parasite that ultimately kills its host), herbivory, and competition. The aim is to reduce a pest population rather than eliminate it, so the crop and the natural enemy can coexist. Biocontrol is one component of integrated pest management (IPM) programs that combine biological, cultural, and chemical tools.

## The three strategies

Importation (classical control) moves a pest's co-evolved natural enemy from its native range into a region where the pest has invaded. The first modern success was the vedalia beetle, *Rodolia cardinalis*, moved from Australia to California in 1888–1889 against the cottony cushion scale (*Icerya purchasi*); the scale population collapsed within a year. A much earlier example appears in a Chinese text from c. 304 AD, recording that citrus growers in Jiaozhi sold weaver ant (*Oecophylla smaragdina*) nests to place in orange trees. Importation works best when the agent can persist without the pest and rapidly exploit outbreaks when they return.

Augmentation boosts natural enemies already present locally. Inoculative release adds small populations that reproduce over time, keeping pests at low levels; the parasitoid wasp *Encarsia formosa* in greenhouses is a textbook case. Inundative release floods an area with large numbers for rapid knockdown, for example the egg parasitoid *Trichogramma* released at 5,000–200,000 per acre per week against the European corn borer, or entomopathogenic nematodes applied at millions to billions per acre against soil-dwelling pests. Success depends on a close ecological match between pest and agent.

Conservation protects enemies already in place. Planting nectar-producing borders in rice fields lets parasitoids and predators of planthoppers survive between outbreaks, reducing insecticide use by about 70% and raising yields by roughly 5%. Beetle banks, hedgerows, mulch, and even inverted flowerpots filled with straw for earwigs provide shelter and alternative food. Because the enemies are already adapted, conservation is usually the cheapest strategy.

## The agents

Predators consume many prey across their lifetime. Lady beetles and their larvae eat aphids, mites, and scale insects; a single hoverfly larva can eat up to 400 aphids; predatory mites such as *Phytoseiulus persimilis* and pirate bugs (*Orius insidiosus*) control spider mites and thrips; barn owls and cats are used against rodents; tilapia and copepods added to water tanks eliminated *Aedes aegypti* mosquito larvae in Honduras. Entomopathogenic nematodes, such as *Phasmarhabditis hermaphrodita* against slugs, enter a host and release bacterial symbionts that do the actual killing.

Parasitoids are mainly wasps and flies whose larvae develop on or inside a host, eventually killing it. Ichneumonid and braconid wasps target caterpillars; chalcidoid wasps attack eggs and larvae; tachinid flies attack a wide insect range. *Encarsia formosa* turns greenhouse whitefly scales black as its larvae pupate inside; *Gonatocerus ashmeadi* suppressed glassy-winged sharpshooter densities by about 95% in French Polynesia. Production and transport are delicate because high temperatures and vibrations damage them, and hosts must be at a susceptible stage on arrival.

Pathogens include bacteria, fungi, viruses, and oomycetes. *Bacillus thuringiensis* (Bt) is the most widely used, with subspecies targeting moth, beetle, and fly larvae; its toxin genes have been engineered into crops such as Bt cotton, though pest resistance is an emerging risk. *Beauveria bassiana* and *Metarhizium* spp. are deployed against whiteflies, beetles, locusts, and spider mites. Baculoviruses are highly host-specific; the Lymantria dispar nucleopolyhedrovirus is sprayed over forests to control spongy moth. The water mould *Lagenidium giganteum* parasitises mosquito larvae. The largest field trial of *Wolbachia*-infected *Aedes aegypti* mosquitoes, in Bello, Medellín, and Itagüí, Colombia, covering 135 km² and 3.3 million people, cut dengue incidence by 94–97%; the technique is not yet endorsed by WHO.

Competitors are organisms that displace pests by using the same resources. Forty-nine dung beetle species were introduced to Australia between 1965 and 1985 to bury cattle dung and remove breeding sites for the bush fly *Musca vetuestima*. The legume vine *Mucuna pruriens* suppresses *Imperata cylindrica* grass in Benin and Vietnam by outcompeting it for light and space.

## Risks and limits

The strongest candidates for biocontrol are invasive pests that arrived without their natural enemies, yet that same enemy-free condition makes the approach attractive and risky at once. Introduced agents may attack non-target species: the cane toad, released in Australia in 1935 against the greyback cane beetle, ignored the beetles and instead poisoned native goannas, snakes, and quolls; *Rhinocyllus conicus*, a weevil released against musk thistle, also fed on the native Platte thistle and shrank its gene pool; the small Asian mongoose, introduced to Hawaii to control rats, preyed on endemic bird eggs because rats are nocturnal and mongooses are diurnal. Vertebrate generalists have caused most of the worst outcomes, which is why modern regulations require host-range testing before release. Biocontrol also has practical limits: growers accustomed to fast-acting pesticides may resist slower, knowledge-intensive methods, and bacterial products such as Bt work only against insects that ingest them, so sap-feeders like aphids and scales are poor targets.
