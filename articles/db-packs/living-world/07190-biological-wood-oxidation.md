# Biological wood oxidation

Biological wood oxidation is the composting of woody material to produce heat, also called "compost heating." Bacteria and fungi break down wood in the presence of oxygen, releasing carbon dioxide, water, and heat. Feedstocks such as branches, trunks, and leaves are carbon-rich but nitrogen-poor, so nutrient-rich additives like manure, urine, and food waste are mixed in to feed the microbes. Pile temperatures usually reach 20–70 °C, a range suited to space heating, underfloor heating, and domestic hot water.

## Biomeiler

A biomeiler (compost heater) is the most common implementation. The concept was developed by French inventor Jean Pain in the 1970s and has since spread to Germany, Romania, the Netherlands, Australia, Italy, France, the United States, Belgium, and the Czech Republic. A biomeiler is a roughly cylindrical heap of shredded woodchips, several metres wide and tall, with water-filled coils of hose or pipe embedded inside. Cool water is pumped through the coils, heated by conduction from the composting mass, and passed through a heat-exchanger radiator. A proposal to use shredded banana-plant waste as the fuel has been made but not built.

## Turbomeiler

A turbomeiler is a tall silo filled with woodchips, tested in Leeuwarden and Haarlem in the Netherlands. Unlike a biomeiler, it can be loaded and unloaded continuously. Water is sprayed over the top, percolates down through the pile by gravity, and is warmed by contact with the hot chips. The water is collected at the bottom, sent to a buffer tank, passed through a heat exchanger, and recirculated. The spray also keeps the chips moist, sustaining microbial activity while extracting heat.

## Advantages

Biological wood oxidation is cheap. Material costs (excluding the wood waste) for a 90 m³ pile are about €457, with tool or machine costs of about €525. Arie van Ziel and Paolo Zampieri reported a build cost of about €3000 for a biomeiler, falling to about €700 on reuse. Jorritsma reported a total cost of €6225 for a 135 m³ biomeiler (woodchips, materials, and labour) and a saving of €6900 compared with gas heating. Turbomeilers cost more to build because of the silo, but are cheap to run afterwards, and a pile that cannot deliver high temperatures still saves energy by preheating water.

The end product is also useful: the spent wood becomes compost, a soil conditioner. Biological wood oxidation can retain roughly 70% of the carbon dioxide originally absorbed by the tree during photosynthesis, locking it into the compost. Because it displaces fossil-fuel combustion, it avoids the CO₂, SO₂, and NOₓ emissions of conventional heating and acts as a low-carbon alternative for cutting greenhouse-gas emissions.

## Disadvantages

The central limitation is low heat output. Reported heat production rates range from 0.02 to 0.95 W per kilogram of dry matter, roughly 200 to 6000 times lower than a domestic wood stove. To deliver useful hot water, piles must therefore be very large: biomeilers typically weigh 10 to 50 tonnes. Three interacting factors suppress output:

- Oxygen. The process is aerobic, and the core of a large pile exchanges air slowly. Best performance is reported at about 30% free pore volume and roughly 50% air volume; conditions in the core are usually below this. Jean Pain found that the core can turn anaerobic and produce methane.
- Temperature. Microbial activity peaks between 40 and 55 °C. The core of a biomeiler can reach 70 °C, too hot for the fungi and bacteria, while the outer layers lose heat to ambient air and may cool enough in winter to stop the pile.
- Humidity. Microbial activity falls below 40% moisture, and above 60% the air spaces flood and oxygen becomes limiting. Drier zones occupy less than 20% of the pile but about 60% of the core, so a self-reinforcing cycle of drying and falling output develops.

A turbomeiler can in principle control humidity, aeration, and temperature, but poor insulation caps its temperature at about 37 °C, too low for domestic heating, and published data on its performance remain scarce.
