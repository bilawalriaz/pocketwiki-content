# Cell biology

Cell biology (also called cellular biology or cytology) is the branch of biology that studies the structure, function, and behavior of cells. Every organism is built from cells, and a cell is the basic unit of life responsible for the living functioning of an organism. The field covers both prokaryotic cells (bacteria and archaea, which lack a nucleus) and eukaryotic cells (animals, plants, fungi, and protists, which keep their DNA inside a nucleus), and it branches into cell metabolism, cell communication, the cell cycle, biochemistry, and cell composition. Knowing how cells work is fundamental to all biological sciences and to biomedical fields such as cancer and infectious disease. Cell biology is closely connected to genetics, molecular biology, molecular genetics, medical microbiology, immunology, and cytochemistry.

## The cell theory

Three ideas, refined over about two centuries, define the field. The first two were proposed in 1838 by Matthias Schleiden and Theodor Schwann, who viewed live cells in plant and animal tissue: all living things are made of cells, and the cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life. The third was added nineteen years later by Rudolf Virchow: all cells arise from the division of pre-existing cells. Viruses are not part of cell biology, because they lack the machinery of a living cell and are studied instead in virology.

## How cells were seen

Cells became visible only after the compound microscope was invented in seventeenth-century Europe. In 1665 Robert Hooke examined a slice of cork and named the box-like compartments he saw "cells," borrowing the word from monastic rooms, but those were dead, empty walls and revealed nothing about living contents. In 1674 Anton van Leeuwenhoek became the first person to observe living cells, using improved single-lens microscopes to examine algae. The next landmark came in 1831, when Robert Brown identified the nucleus.

## Tools of the trade

Modern cell biology rests on a small toolkit of methods that make the invisible visible or measurable.

- Cell culture grows cells rapidly on nutrient media so a single type can be produced in large numbers for studying normal physiology, drug effects, mutagenesis, and carcinogenesis, and for screening medicines and manufacturing vaccines and therapeutic proteins.
- Fluorescence microscopy attaches glowing markers such as GFP to specific molecules, then excites them with a particular wavelength of light so that labeled structures light up against the rest of the cell.
- Phase-contrast microscopy turns subtle differences in how light passes through solids, liquids, and gases into brightness differences, making living, unstained cells visible.
- Confocal microscopy combines fluorescence with a focused pinhole that captures thin optical slices, building sharp three-dimensional images of cells.
- Transmission electron microscopy stains cellular structures with heavy metals and fires electrons through ultra-thin samples; where electrons are deflected, an image forms at far higher resolution than light microscopy can achieve.
- Cytometry fires a beam at cells flowing in a stream, scattering them by size and internal content, and can sort them by a fluorescence tag.
- Cell fractionation breaks cells open with heat or sonication and spins the pieces in a centrifuge, separating organelles (the specialized internal structures of a cell) by mass so each can be studied in isolation.

## Cytopathology

A direct medical offshoot is cytopathology, which diagnoses disease at the cellular level by examining free cells or small tissue fragments rather than whole tissue. It is widely used to detect cancer and certain infections. The Pap smear, which screens for cervical cancer and precancerous lesions, is its most familiar application.
