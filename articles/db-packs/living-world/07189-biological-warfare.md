# Biological warfare

Biological warfare is the deliberate use of disease-causing organisms or their toxins, including bacteria, viruses, fungi, or insects, to kill, injure, or incapacitate people, animals, or plants as an act of war. The same logic, applied by non-state actors or covertly by states, is called bioterrorism. Some agents sit at the boundary with chemical weapons; these "midspectrum" agents, including toxins, do not reproduce inside the victim and act faster than living bioweapons.

## Why states pursued it

Relative to its destructive potential, a biological weapon is extremely cheap: producing a given area of mass-casualty effect is estimated to cost about 0.05% of an equivalent conventional weapon, and the same fermentation (microbial growth in controlled vats) and spray technology used for vaccines or antibiotics can produce the agent. The pathogens are also self-replicating, so a small release can grow into a large epidemic. A state that can credibly threaten mass-casualty release can therefore shift the strategic balance, which is why biological weapons have been treated as deterrents, not merely battlefield tools. The main military weakness is delay: most agents have a 3–7 day incubation period (time between infection and first symptoms), so they rarely stop an advancing force in the short term.

## History

The earliest recorded intent appears in Hittite texts of roughly 1500–1200 BC, describing plague victims driven into enemy territory. In 1346, Mongol forces besieging the Crimean port of Kaffa catapulted plague corpses over the walls; some historians link this to the Black Death, which killed about 25 million Europeans. In 1763, during the French and Indian War, the British commander at Fort Pitt gave smallpox-contaminated blankets to a Lenape delegation, though specialists still debate how much this caused subsequent Native American outbreaks.

Modern biological warfare began with the germ theory of disease around 1900. Germany ran anthrax and glanders sabotage in World War I. In World War II, Britain's Porton Down program weaponized anthrax, tularemia, brucellosis, and botulinum toxin, contaminating the Scottish island of Gruinard with anthrax for 56 years. The United States built a parallel industrial complex at Fort Detrick, Maryland, from 1942. The most notorious program was Japan's Unit 731 in Manchuria, which conducted fatal human experiments and field-tested plague against Chinese populations, killing up to 200,000 people.

## International law

The 1925 Geneva Protocol banned the *use* of biological and chemical weapons in international armed conflicts but not their possession; many states reserved the right to retaliate, so it functioned mainly as a no-first-use pledge. The 1972 Biological Weapons Convention (BWC), in force from 26 March 1975, went further by banning the development, production, acquisition, transfer, stockpiling, and use of biological weapons, the first multilateral treaty to outlaw an entire category of weapons of mass destruction. As of March 2021, 183 states were party to it. The BWC has no formal verification regime; compliance is reinforced by export controls such as the 1985 Australia Group and by UN Security Council Resolution 1540 (2004). President Nixon ended the US offensive program in 1969, helping drive the negotiations, while the Soviet Union secretly expanded its own through a civilian-front agency called Biopreparat; a 1979 anthrax release at Sverdlovsk killed roughly 65–100 people.

## Agents, targets, and why anthrax is the model

Candidate agents include bacteria such as *Bacillus anthracis* (anthrax), *Francisella tularensis* (tularemia), *Yersinia pestis* (plague), and *Brucella*; viruses including Variola (smallpox), Ebola, Marburg, and several encephalitis viruses; and toxins such as botulinum and ricin.

Anthrax illustrates the ideal profile. Its spores (dormant, hardened forms of the bacterium) survive storage and aerosol dispersal; infection is not transmissible person-to-person, so secondary outbreaks are rare; symptoms start as ordinary flu but progress to a lethal internal hemorrhage within 3–7 days, killing 90% or more of untreated patients; and friendly forces can be protected with antibiotics. The hardest engineering problem is not growing the agent but weaponizing, storing, and delivering it to a vulnerable target.

Targets extend beyond people. Anti-agriculture programs have weaponized plant diseases such as wheat blast and rice blast against enemy farmland, and the defoliants used in Vietnam, notably Agent Orange, sit in an adjacent category. The Soviet "Ecology" program in the 1980s developed livestock agents including foot-and-mouth disease and rinderpest. Entomological warfare uses insects as direct attackers, as vectors carrying a pathogen, or as crop pests.

## Defense and modern risks

Defence rests on vaccinating at-risk military personnel, running disease surveillance that can spot unusual outbreaks early enough to treat or isolate the exposed, and deploying field detection tools. With inhalational anthrax, models suggest more than 80% of an exposed population can be reached with prophylactic antibiotics if the attack is recognised within 24–36 hours. Public-health agencies watch for warning signs such as simultaneous clusters of an unusual disease in non-contiguous areas, rare or genetically engineered strains, and unexplained animal die-offs preceding human cases.

Two technological shifts now lower the barrier to entry. CRISPR-Cas9, a cheap and widely available gene-editing tool, makes it possible to enhance virulence, evade vaccines, or alter a pathogen's host range. Synthetic biology has already demonstrated the reconstruction of an extinct poxvirus (horsepox) from commercial DNA, showing that highly dangerous agents can be assembled from scratch without access to natural stocks.
