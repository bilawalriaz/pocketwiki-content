# Biological membrane

A biological membrane is a thin, selectively permeable barrier that separates a cell from its surroundings or partitions a cell into internal compartments. Almost every organelle in a eukaryotic cell is wrapped by one. The barrier is built from a **phospholipid bilayer**: two sheets of lipid molecules whose hydrophobic tails face inward, away from water, and whose hydrophilic heads face the surrounding water. This arrangement forms spontaneously in water because burying the tails increases the entropy of the surrounding water molecules through the **hydrophobic effect**.

## Composition

A typical plasma membrane is roughly half lipid and half protein by weight, with small amounts of sugar-containing lipids and proteins. The proteins do most of the active work; the lipids provide a fluid, two-dimensional solvent in which the proteins float and rotate.

**Lipids.** Each phospholipid has a hydrophilic head and two hydrophobic hydrocarbon tails. Tail length and the proportion of saturated versus unsaturated (kinked) tails strongly influence how fluid the membrane is. Saturated tails pack tightly, producing a stiffer membrane; unsaturated tails introduce kinks that loosen packing and increase fluidity. **Cholesterol**, a short and rigid sterol, slips between phospholipids and partly fills the gaps left by kinked tails, stiffening the bilayer and reducing its permeability at warm temperatures while preventing it from solidifying at cold ones. In animal plasma membranes cholesterol makes up about 20% of the lipid by weight.

**Membrane asymmetry.** The two leaflets are not identical. Specific lipids and proteins are concentrated in one leaflet or the other, and the cell maintains this difference using enzymes called **flippases** that move selected phospholipids from the cytosolic half to the outer half after new lipids are inserted. Glycolipids, lipids with a sugar group, sit almost exclusively in the outer leaflet, where their sugars project from the cell surface and act as recognition and adhesion labels. Red blood cell membranes keep the lipid phosphatidylserine on the cytoplasmic side until clotting is needed, at which point it is flipped to the outer face to support coagulation.

**Proteins.** Two classes sit in or on the bilayer. **Integral (transmembrane) proteins** span the bilayer and are surrounded by a ring of tightly bound lipids called an **annular lipid shell**; they can usually be removed only by detergents that disrupt the membrane. **Peripheral proteins** bind loosely to one face and detach easily. Together they perform most membrane jobs: receptors that sense outside signals, transporters such as the sodium/potassium pump that moves ions against their gradient, anchors like integrins that link the cytoskeleton to the outside, and enzymes such as adenylyl cyclase that produce internal messengers.

## Function

The defining property of a biomembrane is **selective permeability**. Small, non-polar molecules (oxygen, carbon dioxide, steroid hormones) diffuse straight through the lipid core. Water crosses more slowly but does so in useful amounts. Ions, sugars, amino acids, and most other polar or charged molecules cannot cross the hydrophobic core on their own; they pass through specific transport proteins or are taken up when the membrane engulfs material as a vesicle (endocytosis). Because membranes can also change shape and fuse with one another, the cell uses them to build internal compartments with distinct chemistry, such as the peroxisome, which walls off peroxide-producing reactions from the rest of the cytoplasm.

## Fluidity

The lipid core is not a static sheet. C–C bonds in the tails rotate constantly, so the interior is fluid, allowing membrane proteins to drift laterally and meet one another, a requirement for signalling. At low temperature, lipids with long, saturated tails can pack so tightly that the bilayer sets into a gel-like solid; the temperature at which this happens depends on tail length and saturation. Cold-blooded organisms and bacteria adjust the fatty-acid makeup of their membranes to keep fluidity roughly constant as temperature changes. Without membrane fluidity, proteins could not diffuse, membranes could not fuse, and dividing cells could not distribute their components evenly between daughters.
