# Biological interaction

In ecology, a biological interaction is the effect that a pair of organisms living together in a community have on each other. The pair can be members of the same species (intraspecific) or of different species (interspecific). Some interactions last only seconds, such as a predator catching prey, while others persist across generations, such as a fungus and an alga living together as a lichen. Even brief interactions can drive long-term evolution in both partners, because natural selection acts on their outcomes.

Interactions are described by their net effect on each partner's fitness, its ability to survive and reproduce. Ecologists also distinguish direct interactions, where the organisms physically contact one another, from indirect interactions, where the link runs through something shared, such as a food resource, a territory, a toxin, or a chemical that suppresses neighbour growth. Black walnut trees, for example, secrete a root substance that harms nearby plants, a case of indirect chemical antagonism.

## Classification by effect on fitness

The standard scheme sorts interactions by whether each partner gains, loses, or is unaffected, using +, −, and 0 as shorthand.

| Interaction | Effect on species A | Effect on species B |
|---|---|---|
| Mutualism | + | + |
| Commensalism | + | 0 |
| Parasitism | + | − |
| Neutralism | 0 | 0 |
| Amensalism | 0 | − |
| Competition | − | − |

Mutualism means both partners gain. Examples include nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the root nodules of legumes, gut flora that aid digestion, and cleaner fish that remove parasites from larger fish. Within a single species, equivalent interactions are called cooperation. Some mutualisms are obligate, meaning one or both partners cannot survive without the other.

Commensalism benefits one partner while leaving the other essentially unaffected. Remoras, for instance, ride with manatees and feed on their faeces without affecting the manatee.

Parasitism involves one partner, the parasite, living on or inside the other, the host, drawing nutrition from it and causing harm. Many parasites feed directly on host tissue, while intestinal parasites consume some of the host's food.

Neutralism describes two species that interact but do not measurably affect each other. Because every organism alters its environment in some way, the term is reserved for cases where any effect is negligible.

Amensalism is one-way harm: one organism damages another while gaining nothing itself. The most studied mechanism is allelopathy, the release of inhibitory chemicals. Examples include microbes producing antibiotics, leaf litter from pines or eucalypts suppressing seedlings, and hoofed mammals crushing grass. In an asymmetric case, Spanish ibex browsing a shrub swallow weevils of the genus *Timarcha* living on it; the ibex is unaffected, but weevil numbers collapse.

Competition lowers the fitness of both partners because they share a resource in short supply, such as food, water, territory, or mates. Competition within a species is intraspecific, and between species is interspecific. The competitive exclusion principle states that two species competing for the same limiting resource cannot coexist indefinitely; the less efficient one adapts, shifts resource, or dies out.

## Short-term interactions

Short-term interactions include predation, where a predator kills and eats prey, and pollination, where an animal transfers pollen from one flower to another in return for nectar or pollen. Each event is brief, yet evolutionary consequences are durable. Predators evolve sharper senses, claws, stealth, and aggressive mimicry; prey evolve camouflage, warning coloration, alarm calls, defensive spines, and chemical defences.

Pollination has driven the coevolution of flowering plants and their animal pollinators for over 100 million years. Flowers evolved shaped structures, bright colours, patterns, scents, nectar, and sticky pollen to attract and reward visitors. Pollinators such as bees evolved colour vision tuned to flowers, bristles shaped to carry pollen, and, in honey bees, the conversion of nectar into stored honey.

Seed dispersal, the movement of seeds away from the parent plant by wind, water, ballistic ejection, gravity, or animals, is another short-term interaction. Plants depend on these vectors, and the dispersal mechanism shapes the genetic structure and migration patterns of plant populations.

Long-term interactions, called symbioses in the broad sense, are typically classified by sign of effect, with mutualism, commensalism, and parasitism as the most familiar categories.

## Strength, directness, and non-trophic effects

Ecologists also measure the strength of an interaction (how much it changes each partner's fitness) and its frequency. They distinguish incidental effects, where the interaction follows directly from one partner's behaviour, from stochastic side effects, where one individual changes another's environment without targeting it.

Recent research has highlighted non-trophic interactions, including habitat modification, competition for space, and mutualism, in shaping food webs. Foundation species such as kelp, mussels, and trees alter the physical structure of their environment, and in doing so change which predators and prey can coexist. Empirical studies on coastal systems show these effects can be large, though it remains unclear how broadly they generalise, or whether they act on particular trophic levels, functional groups, or the whole network indiscriminately. Sessile species with low trophic levels appear to benefit most from non-trophic facilitation, though more mobile and higher-level consumers also gain.
