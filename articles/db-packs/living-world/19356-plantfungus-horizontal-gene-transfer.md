# Plant–fungus horizontal gene transfer

Plant–fungus horizontal gene transfer (HGT) is the movement of genetic material between individuals in the plant and fungus kingdoms. HGT is universal in bacteria, fungi, viruses, and other eukaryotes, though research has focused on prokaryotes because more sequence data exists for them. Most plant–fungus HGT events are ancient and rare, yet they have probably contributed useful genes, broadened substrate use, and aided habitat spread. Because the events are ancient and rare, they are hard to detect. Plant–fungus contacts can also act as one link in a multi-organism HGT pathway.

## Mechanisms

Fungus–plant HGT can occur through phagotrophic routes, in which one eukaryote engulfs another, or through non-phagotrophic routes. Non-phagotrophic routes include transposable elements, plastid-derived endosymbiotic gene transfer, prokaryote-derived gene transfer, *Agrobacterium tumefaciens*-mediated DNA transfer, cross-species hybridisation, and mitochondrial gene transfer. HGT can bypass the usual eukaryotic barriers of linear chromatin-based chromosomes, intron–exon gene structure, and the nuclear envelope.

HGT is most likely between organisms that share an ecological niche or an association such as parasitism or symbiosis; this shared ecology is an understudied factor in their evolutionary histories. Most fungal-to-plant HGT events predate the rise of land plants, and detecting more will require broader genomic and taxon sampling.

## How scientists recognise past transfers

Evidence for ancient fungus–eukaryote transfers is always indirect. Researchers look for signatures such as conflicting phylogenies across genetic elements, unusually high DNA or amino-acid similarity between distantly related organisms, patchy distribution of a gene across species, similar genes shared by species in the same habitat or region but not by their relatives, and foreign-looking features such as unusual guanine–cytosine content, codon usage, or intron pattern.

Before concluding that HGT occurred, researchers must rule out alternatives: wrong species trees, comparison of paralogous sequences (duplicated genes within a genome), retention of ancestral traits, uneven substitution rates, and introgressive hybridisation.

The "complexity hypothesis" offers a complementary explanation for why informational genes (involved in transcription, translation, and related processes) are rarely transferred while operational genes (involved in day-to-day metabolism) transfer more often. Operational genes are smaller and less embedded in multi-component systems, so they integrate more easily; the related "continual hypothesis" holds that HGT is constantly acting on operational genes.

## Examples

*Multi-vector pathway.* Because fungi can infect other organisms, plant–fungus HGT can occur during infection and may involve additional vectors such as insects, producing a multi-organism pathway.

*Rice.* A fungus–plant pathway has been demonstrated in rice (*Oryza sativa*). A phylogeny was built from 1689 identified genes plus all available rice homologs across 3177 gene families. Fourteen candidate plant–fungus HGT events were found, nine showing infrequent transfer patterns. The transferred genes likely contributed to an L-fucose permease sugar transporter, a zinc-binding alcohol dehydrogenase, a membrane transporter, a phospholipase/carboxylesterase, an iucA/iucC-family siderophore biosynthesis protein, a DUF239-domain protein, a phosphate-response 1 family protein, a C2H2-type zinc-finger-like protein, and a conserved hypothetical protein.

*Ancestral shikimate pathway.* Some plants may have acquired the shikimate pathway (a route for synthesising aromatic amino acids) from symbiotic fungi. Plant shikimate enzymes resemble prokaryote homologs and could trace back to a plastid-progenitor genome. The pathway and the pentafunctional *aroM* locus may have ancient eukaryotic origins, been conveyed by eukaryote–eukaryote HGT, or been shaped by a prokaryote-to-eukaryote transfer. Ascomycetes, zygomycetes, basidiomycetes, apicomplexa, ciliates, and oomycetes all retain elements of an ancestral pathway tracing to the bikont/unikont eukaryotic root.

*Ancestral land plants and phenylpropanoids.* Fungi and bacteria probably contributed the phenylpropanoid pathway to ancestral land plants, enabling the synthesis of flavonoids and lignin. Phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL) occurs in fungi such as *Basidiomycota* yeasts (*Rhodotorula*) and *Ascomycota* (*Aspergillus*, *Neurospora*), where it catabolises phenylalanine for carbon and nitrogen. Some plant and fungal PAL also has tyrosine ammonia lyase activity, making *p*-coumaric acid and then *p*-coumaroyl-CoA. PAL likely arose in bacteria for antimicrobial defence; it then moved into a pre-*Dikarya* fungal lineage from a *Nostoc*-like soil or sediment bacterium through symbiosis, and from that fungus into a land-plant ancestor via ancient arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis, later feeding the phenylpropanoid pathway and land colonisation. Early PAL may have protected against ultraviolet radiation, acted as a light-capturing pigment, or aided antimicrobial defence.

*Secondary metabolism.* Sterigmatocystin gene transfer has been observed between *Podospora anserina* and *Aspergillus*. *Aspergillus nidulans* produces sterigmatocystin, a precursor of aflatoxins. *Podospora* and *Aspergillus* share highly conserved sterigmatocystin/aflatoxin clusters and intergenic regions with 14 binding sites for AflR, the transcription factor that activates those biosynthetic genes. The *Aspergillus*-to-*Podospora* transfer probably broadened fungal metabolic diversity. Transposable elements, plasmids, and viruses can drive chromosomal rearrangement and integration of foreign DNA, allowing HGT to reshape fungal genomes and metabolism.

*Acquired pathogenicity.* HGT gives fungi a powerful way to gain the ability to infect new plant hosts. In *Stagonospora* and *Pyrenophora*, and in *Fusarium* and *Alternaria*, HGT and interspecific hybridisation can produce hybrid offspring with an expanded host range, causing disease outbreaks when an encoded protein enables a new pathogen–host interaction. A host-selective toxin from *Stagonospora nodorum* was transferred to *Pyrenophora tritici-repentis*, conferring virulence on wheat. In *Fusarium*, a non-pathogenic strain was experimentally converted into a pathogen, suggesting HGT can reshape large genome portions. *Fusarium graminearum*, *Fusarium verticillioides*, and *Fusarium oxysporum* are maize and tomato pathogens that produce fumonisin mycotoxins that contaminate grain. These cases show that host specialisation has polyphyletic origins and that new pathogenic lineages can emerge independently of their genetic background, allowing HGT to increase disease in susceptible plant populations.
