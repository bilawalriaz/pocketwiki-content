# RNA-based evolution

RNA-based evolution is the theory that RNA is not merely an intermediate between DNA and protein but an independent regulator of phenotype (an organism's observable traits), capable of generating heritable variation that natural selection can act on. In this theory, phenotype can change without any change to the DNA sequence.

## RNA in the central dogma and in early life

The central dogma places RNA between the genome and proteins, so a DNA mutation alters the RNA transcript, which alters the phenotype. RNA is also the best-supported candidate for the genetic material of the first life on Earth, 4 billion years ago. Its building blocks form readily from amino acids, sugars, and hydroxyl acids thought to be abundant then. RNA molecules can self-replicate, catalyse basic reactions, and store heritable information. As genomes grew larger, DNA, being far more chemically stable, took over as the major carrier of genetic information, leaving RNA with regulatory and catalytic roles.

## Single-stranded folding

Single-stranded RNA folds into three-dimensional shapes by intramolecular base pairing. Secondary structure forms through hydrogen bonds between complementary regions; tertiary structure emerges when those elements fold on themselves, producing grooves and helices. The balance of disorder and order, and the catalytic capability that results, is part of why early life could have begun with RNA.

## RNA processing as a source of heritable variation

Processing events regulate how DNA is transcribed, how stable the resulting RNA is, and how efficiently messenger RNA is translated, so a single gene can yield many different proteins.

Alternative splicing is the clearest example. Splicing removes non-coding regions from a transcript, and the number and combination of splicing events varies with the transcript sequence and with environmental factors. In Drosophila melanogaster, the sex-determining Tra gene illustrates this. In males, a splicing failure retains a stop codon (a three-nucleotide signal that halts protein synthesis), truncating the transcript. In others, the stop signal is removed and a functional Tra protein is made, producing the female phenotype. The same DNA sequence thus yields different phenotypes through splicing alone.

RNA stability sets protein output. Short repetitive tails added to RNA ends protect the molecules from degradation, raising transcript numbers and so increasing protein synthesis. During mammalian liver regeneration, growth-factor RNA molecules accumulate these protective tails and increase in number, helping rebuild the organ.

RNA silencing works in the opposite direction. Double-stranded RNA is processed by a series of enzymatic reactions into fragments that degrade complementary RNA sequences, lowering protein output and shifting phenotype.

## RNA and protein

In early life, RNA acted as both blueprint and catalyst. Today it acts through proteins: mRNA, rRNA, and tRNA together underpin gene expression. In laboratory experiments, RNA selected in vitro has bound tightly to specific amino acids, and the recognised sequences are enriched for the codons (three-nucleotide units that specify an amino acid) of those same amino acids. This hints that a direct RNA–amino-acid association may have given rise to a primitive genetic code, and that information could move in both directions between the two polymers.

## Evolutionary consequence

RNA processing events work together in networks that let a single genotype produce a wider range of proteins than the DNA sequence alone would dictate. These events can be inherited from one generation to the next through reverse transcription into the genome, so networks that produce fitter phenotypes persist in a population. In vertebrates, RNA processing has driven unusually fast phenotypic change. Human genome analyses have likewise identified RNA-processing events that open significant extra "sequence space for more variability."

## RNA virus evolution

RNA viruses evolve quickly because their replication lacks a proofreading mechanism (an enzyme check that corrects copying errors), so mutations accumulate at a high rate. Genetic recombination adds a second source of change: when two viral genomes share a host cell, they can swap segments. Recombination is a major driver in the Picornaviridae (positive-sense single-stranded RNA viruses such as poliovirus), and strand switching during reverse transcription protects the Retroviridae (such as HIV) from genome damage. Recombination also occurs in the Coronaviridae (such as SARS), and it generally looks like an adaptation for coping with genome damage. When divergent animal viruses of the same species recombine, the offspring can occasionally jump into human hosts and start new outbreaks.
