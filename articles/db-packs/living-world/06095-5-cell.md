# 5-cell

The 5-cell is the four-dimensional analogue of the tetrahedron. A tetrahedron is the simplest solid in 3D (4 vertices, 4 triangular faces, 4 triangular cells), and the 5-cell is the simplest 4-polytope (a 4D solid bounded by flat 3D pieces called cells): 5 vertices, 10 edges, 10 triangular faces, and 5 tetrahedral cells, with Schläfli symbol {3,3,3}. It is also called a 4-simplex, pentachoron, pentatope, or C₅.

A regular 5-cell consists of five regular tetrahedra, each face-bonded to the other four. To see why it needs a fourth dimension, take a regular tetrahedron and try to add a fifth vertex exactly one edge length from all four existing vertices. In 3D no such point exists: a regular tetrahedron's four vertices do not have a fifth equidistant point in the same 3D space. In 4D, the extra room permits a fifth vertex at the correct distance from all four, closing the shape. Equivalently, a regular 5-cell is a 4-dimensional pyramid with a regular tetrahedron as its base plus an apex above the base's 3D hyperplane (a flat 3D slice of 4D space), giving four tetrahedral side cells.

The 5-cell continues the simplex pattern: any three non-collinear points form a triangle, any four non-coplanar points form a tetrahedron, and any five non-hyperplanar points form a 5-cell, though generally not a regular one. The regular 5-cell is self-dual, so swapping its vertices and cells yields a 5-cell again. It is one of six regular convex 4-polytopes, the 4D analogues of the Platonic solids, and the first in their sequence ordered by volume at a given radius.

## Numerical properties

For a regular 5-cell with edge length 1, the dihedral angle (angle between two adjoining faces) between adjacent tetrahedral cells is arccos(1/4) ≈ 75.52°, and between adjacent triangular faces within a cell it is arccos(1/3) ≈ 70.53°. With unit circumradius (centre-to-vertex distance), edge length is √(5/2) ≈ 1.581. The characteristic angle η = arc sec(4)/2 ≈ 37.74° controls the cell dihedral as π − 2η. Its maximal 3D cross-section is a triangular prism.

A standard set of Cartesian coordinates for a regular 5-cell in 4D with edge length 2√2 uses the golden ratio φ ≈ 1.618:
(2, 0, 0, 0), (0, 2, 0, 0), (0, 0, 2, 0), (0, 0, 0, 2), (φ, φ, φ, φ).

A simpler form places the 4-simplex in 5D: take all distinct permutations of (0, 0, 0, 0, 1) or of (0, 1, 1, 1, 1), giving edge length √2 and circumradius 1.

## Projections

5-cells are visualised by projection into 3D or 2D, each viewpoint revealing different structure.

- Coxeter plane A₄ (the 2D plane of highest symmetry): projects to a regular pentagon and pentagram.
- Coxeter plane A₃: projects to a square pyramid.
- Coxeter plane A₂: projects to a triangular bipyramid (two tetrahedra joined face-to-face).
- Vertex-first projection to 3D: a tetrahedral envelope (the convex 3D shell that wraps the projection), with the nearest vertex at the centre and the farthest cell covering the envelope.

The 5-cell's characteristic rotation is an isoclinic rotation, a rotation in which two perpendicular 2-planes turn at equal rates. It leaves invariant 10 digon central planes, 2D planes whose intersection with the 5-cell is a two-vertex segment (a digon), where each vertex pair forms an edge of the 5-cell.

## The 5-cell as a building block (orthoschemes)

An orthoscheme is an irregular simplex whose edges meet only at right angles, forming a path with three 90° turns. A 4-orthoscheme is a 5-cell whose 10 faces are all right triangles, with five vertices connected by four mutually perpendicular edges in a bent path. Each sub-simplex of an orthoscheme is itself an orthoscheme, so every triangular face is a right triangle, every tetrahedral cell is a 3-orthoscheme, and so on.

Every regular polytope has a characteristic orthoscheme that acts as its "genetic code." For the 4-cube (tesseract), the characteristic 4-orthoscheme has four perpendicular edges of unit length, giving 10 edges of length √1, √2, √3, or √4: four of length 1, three of √2, two of √3, and one of 2. The 4-cube dissects into 24 such orthoschemes surrounding each of four long diameters, and into 384 smaller copies meeting at its centre.

The regular 5-cell has a characteristic 4-orthoscheme whose four edges meeting at the centre have the four characteristic radii: distances from the centre to a vertex, an edge midpoint, a face centre, and a cell centre. The regular 5-cell dissects into 120 copies of this orthoscheme, all meeting at its centre. Reflecting a single orthoscheme in its own bounding facets generates the full polytope; the number of reflected copies, the order of the polytope, equals its symmetry group order.

## The family around the 5-cell

The 5-cell is the smallest of nine uniform polychora (4D polytopes whose cells are uniform and vertex-transitive) built from the [3,3,3] Coxeter group. Three families place it in a wider context.

- {p,3,3}: regular 4-polytopes whose cells are {p,3}. The 5-cell {3,3,3}, tesseract {4,3,3}, and 120-cell {5,3,3} are finite; the hexagonal tiling honeycomb {6,3,3} lives in hyperbolic 4-space.
- {3,3,p}: regular 4-polytopes with tetrahedral cells. The 5-cell {3,3,3}, 16-cell {3,3,4}, and 600-cell {3,3,5} are finite; {3,3,6} is a hyperbolic honeycomb.
- {3,p,3}: self-dual regular 4-polytopes with a palindromic Schläfli symbol (one that reads the same forwards and backwards). The 5-cell and 24-cell belong here.

A 5-cell also arises as a Boerdijk–Coxeter helix, a closed ring of five tetrahedra in which each shares a face with the next and the last shares a face with the first. Laid out flat, the 10 triangle faces form a triangular-tiling net with 6 triangles around each vertex, but the ring only closes in 4D. Its outer edges form the regular pentagon that is the 5-cell's Petrie polygon (a closed path traversing every other edge), and its skip-one edges form the pentagram that is its Clifford polygon, the chords of the isocline traced by the vertices during an isoclinic rotation.

The 120-cell contains a compound of 120 regular 5-cells. The convex hull of two 5-cells in dual configuration is the disphenoidal 30-cell. Lower-symmetry 5-cells include the tetrahedral pyramid, a non-regular 5-cell formed by lifting a regular tetrahedron into a 4D pyramid; such pyramids appear as the vertex figures of many uniform 4-polytopes and of the omnitruncated 4-simplex honeycomb.
