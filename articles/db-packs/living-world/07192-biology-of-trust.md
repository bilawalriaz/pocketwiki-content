# Biology of trust

The biology of trust studies the physiological mechanisms that let one animal rely on another despite the risk of being hurt. Trust is the intentional choice to believe what another person says on the assumption that they know the truth and act in one's interest, and it underwrites the social bonds found across mammals and other vertebrates. Those bonds carry a built-in trade-off: they deliver benefits but also expose an animal to judgment, physical harm, and emotional pain. The same neural and hormonal machinery that makes bonding possible is what makes trust biologically costly, and the rest of the lesson traces that machinery.

## Two molecules that act in two places

Two closely related neuropeptides, oxytocin (OT) and arginine-vasopressin (AVP), sit at the centre of the system. Each is produced by neurons in the hypothalamus, a small brain region that regulates basic drives, and each can be released in two ways. When hypothalamic neurons release the molecules directly, they act as neurotransmitters within the brain. When the pituitary gland, a hormone-secreting structure at the base of the brain, releases them into the bloodstream, they act as hormones acting on distant tissues. Because the same molecule can act in both modes, OT and AVP can simultaneously shape brain circuits and coordinate peripheral physiology, which is why both are linked to social behaviour rather than to brain or body alone.

OT is required for normal social bonding. When female rats receive OT injected directly into the brain, they show full maternal behaviour toward foster pups they did not bear, showing that OT can drive bonding on its own. In humans, blood OT rises when a participant receives a deliberate signal of trust from another player in a controlled trust game, so the same molecule is recruited by a one-off act of trust, not only by long-term attachment. Blocking OT receptors with an antagonist reduces social recognition and bonding in rats, while injecting OT into either the lateral septum or the main olfactory bulb, two regions involved in odour-guided social memory, increases social recognition and interaction, which indicates that the effect depends on receptors in specific brain regions rather than on the molecule alone.

AVP works in parallel. Brattleboro rats, which carry a mutation that prevents AVP production, show impaired social recognition, and the deficit is rescued by injecting AVP into either the brain or the periphery. The reverse also holds: an AVP receptor antagonist reduces bonding and recognition. In male titi monkeys, a monogamous primate species, AVP injection increases affiliative behaviour toward the female partner, and in humans AVP increases pro-social behaviour toward a significant other. When OT and AVP are both elevated, animals huddle more and share food, so the two molecules reinforce each other.

## Dopamine and the reward circuit

Bonding and trust depend on more than feeling close. The brain also has to treat the other individual as rewarding. Dopamine (DA) is the main neurotransmitter of the brain's reward pathways, and DA neurons act on OT receptors in the striatum, a subcortical region involved in motivation and habitual behaviour, to redirect that reward signal toward the attachment figure, converting attachment into the behavioural willingness that trust requires. AVP feeds into the same circuitry through social recognition memory, so OT, AVP, and DA operate as an integrated chemical system rather than three independent ones.

## The brain regions involved

Functional MRI studies in humans, using images or sounds of a partner, show that the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and ventral striatum, the midbrain and forebrain hubs of the dopamine reward system, light up during responses to an attachment target. Other regions consistently activated include the amygdala, the nucleus accumbens, the hypothalamus, and broader subcortical and cortical reward networks. All of these regions carry receptors for OT, AVP, and DA, so the same molecules can shape low-level responses like recognising a partner and higher-order judgements like deciding to trust. Sex hormones correlate with the same behaviours, which is one reason trust and bonding vary in magnitude and style between individuals and across life stages.

## What isolation does to the system

The dependence of trust on this circuitry shows up when the system is perturbed. People who experience social isolation show increased activity in the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, a stress network that drives release of cortisol, a hormone that mobilises the body during threat, alongside reduced activity in brain regions that handle OT and AVP. Losing social contact at the same time raises the stress hormone that erodes bonding and suppresses the bonding molecules themselves, providing a biological mechanism for the common experience that loneliness and distrust reinforce each other.
