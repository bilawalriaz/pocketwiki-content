# Botany

Botany is the scientific study of plants. In the strict sense, plants are **embryophytes** (land plants): multicellular organisms whose life cycle alternates between a haploid **gametophyte** and a diploid **sporophyte**. Historically the field also covered algae, fungi, and bacteria, which remain under the International Botanical Congress. Through oxygenic photosynthesis, plants fix carbon, release oxygen, and form the base of nearly every food web.

## Development of the discipline

Botany began as prehistoric herbalism for identifying edible, medicinal, and poisonous plants. Theophrastus (c. 371–287 BCE) wrote *Enquiry into Plants* in Greece; Dioscorides's *De materia medica* (1st century CE) served as a herbal encyclopedia for 1,500 years. Dīnawarī's *Book of Plants* (828–896 CE) advanced systematic study in the medieval Muslim world, and the 1545 Padua botanical garden shifted the focus from herbalism to academic study. Robert Hooke's discovery of cells in cork (1665) launched cytology, and Carl Linnaeus's *Species Plantarum* (1753) standardized binomial nomenclature. Darwin's *Origin of Species* (1859) required classification to reflect common descent. By the 20th century, molecular and genomic methods had transformed the field; *Arabidopsis thaliana* became the first plant with a fully sequenced genome in 2000.

## The plant cell and photosynthesis

Plants are **photoautotrophs**: they make their own food using light. They do this in chloroplasts, organelles derived from an endosymbiotic cyanobacterium that entered an early eukaryote more than 2 billion years ago, driving the Great Oxidation Event. Light reactions absorb blue-violet and red light through chlorophyll *a* (and *b* in plants and green algae), producing ATP and NADPH. The Calvin cycle then uses rubisco to fix CO₂ into glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate, the first sugar, which feeds glucose, starch (the main energy store), and sucrose (the transport sugar). Chloroplasts also synthesize fatty acids for membranes and most amino acids. Distinctive plant polymers include cellulose and pectin in cell walls, lignin (which strengthens xylem and dominates wood), and sporopollenin, a tough coat on spores and pollen that serves as a fossil marker for Ordovician land plants. Some plants evolved C4 and CAM pathways to reduce photorespiration, a wasteful side reaction of the ancestral C3 pathway, under low CO₂.

## Genetics, epigenetics, and inheritance

Plant inheritance follows Mendelian rules but with distinctive features. Weak species boundaries allow frequent hybridization, as in peppermint and wheat. Outcrossing through self-incompatibility and dioecy (separate male and female plants) masks deleterious mutations, producing heterosis; inbreeding exposes them as inbreeding depression. Asexual reproduction by apomixis, tubers, and bulbils produces clonal populations, especially in arctic and alpine habitats. **Polyploidy**, having more than two chromosome sets, is a major speciation driver: autopolyploidy within a species and allopolyploidy from hybridization produced tetraploid durum wheat, hexaploid bread wheat, and triploid banana. Chloroplasts are inherited paternally in gymnosperms but usually maternally in angiosperms, a non-Mendelian pattern whose mechanism is still debated.

Epigenetics studies heritable changes in gene function without DNA sequence change, via DNA methylation and repressor proteins. Epigenetic marks guide cellular differentiation: a totipotent zygote activates and silences specific genes to produce parenchyma, xylem, phloem, and guard cells. Unlike most animal cells, many plant parenchyma cells stay totipotent, so a single cell can regenerate a whole plant. Mature xylem cells die at maturity and enucleate sieve-tube elements cannot, so they cannot. **Paramutations**, epigenetic allele interactions that violate Mendelian rules, can persist across generations.

## Physiology, hormones, and transport

Respiration oxidizes carbohydrates, reversing photosynthesis. Vascular plants transport water and minerals upward through xylem via the transpiration stream, pulled by evaporation from leaves, and sucrose outward from leaves through phloem. Essential nutrients (N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S) come from soil mineral breakdown. **Phytohormones** coordinate growth: auxins drive tropic responses to light and gravity and enable tissue culture; cytokinins promote cell division and chloroplast greening; gibberellins break seed dormancy, elongate stems, and induce flowering; abscisic acid inhibits division, matures seeds, and closes stomata; gaseous ethylene ripens fruit and triggers abscission; jasmonates regulate wound responses. Light also acts as a signal through phytochromes, driving photomorphogenesis.

## Anatomy, morphology, and reproduction

Plant cells have primary walls of cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin, large vacuoles, and plastids (chloroplasts, amyloplasts for starch, elaioplasts for oils). Streptophytes divide using a phragmoplast and cell plate. Vascular plants have aerial shoots (stems, photosynthetic leaves, reproductive organs) and subterranean roots; non-vascular plants lack true roots and most tissues photosynthesize. Stems support, store water or food, or reproduce vegetatively via stolons. Leaves capture light. Gymnosperms bear naked seeds; angiosperms enclose seeds inside a carpel and produce flowers. Woody plants undergo secondary growth, producing wood (secondary xylem) and bark (secondary phloem plus cork). Traditional organ categories (root, stem, leaf) are linked by intermediate forms, blurring boundaries.

## Evolution of plants

Algae are polyphyletic; Charophyceae is the algal group sister to land plants, and together they form clade Streptophytina. Non-vascular bryophytes (mosses, liverworts, hornworts) lack xylem and phloem. Vascular pteridophytes, with true xylem, phloem, and spore reproduction, diversified in the Silurian and Devonian. Late Devonian plants independently evolved heterospory, separate megaspores and microspores, and endospory, the gametophyte retained inside the megasporangium. Seeds, essentially integumented megasporangia, appear in the latest Devonian. Seed plants diversified into extinct seed ferns, modern gymnosperms (conifers, cycads, *Ginkgo*, Gnetales), and angiosperms. Molecular phylogenetics places angiosperms as sister to gymnosperms; the 1998 Angiosperm Phylogeny Group DNA analysis resolved flowering-plant family relationships.

## Ecology, classification, and applied botany

Plants interact with habitats and each other through competition, mutualism, herbivory, parasitism, and carnivory. Mycorrhizal fungi and rhizobia (nitrogen-fixing bacteria in root nodules) trade nutrients for plant carbon; pollinators (bees, bats) enable reproduction; animals disperse seeds and spores. Plants both respond to and modify soil and climatic conditions, defining biomes from tundra to rainforest. Palynology (fossil pollen), stomatal density, and leaf morphology reconstruct past climates and atmospheric CO₂.

Systematic botany classifies plants by evolutionary history using Linnaean ranks and binomial names governed by the International Code of Nomenclature. Since the 1990s, molecular phylogenetics using DNA sequences has dominated, reclassifying fungi as closer to animals than plants and producing cladograms based on apomorphies, derived traits that uniquely evidence common ancestry.

Applied branches draw on this foundation. Phytochemistry studies secondary metabolites: toxins (coniine), aromatics (peppermint oil, capsaicin), and pharmaceuticals (opium, caffeine, morphine, nicotine, and aspirin from willow-bark salicylic acid). Staple crops such as maize, rice, wheat, and pulses were bred over millennia from wild ancestors, and botanists study yield, weed and pathogen control, and the role of plants in mitigating climate change and food insecurity.
