# Biological hazard

A biological hazard, or biohazard, is a biological substance that threatens the health of living organisms, chiefly humans. The category covers microorganisms (bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites), the toxins some produce, and other biological materials that can cause infection, allergic reaction, or poisoning. The same organism can be harmless on a surface and lethal in a wound, so a biohazard is a category of risk attached to a material, not an inherent property of the organism.

The word "biohazard" and its trefoil symbol (☣) function as a warning, telling anyone nearby to use protection or step away. Dow Chemical environmental-health engineer Charles Baldwin developed the symbol in 1966 while working on containment products. In Unicode the symbol is U+2623.

## How hazards are signposted

The American National Standards Institute publishes the Z535 colour and signal-word system, used worldwide and adopted into US law through OSHA. A black trefoil on a coloured background communicates severity before any text is read:

- **DANGER** (red): will cause death
- **WARNING** (orange): may cause death
- **CAUTION** (yellow): will cause injury, not death
- **NOTICE** (green): no-injury message (hygiene, cleanup, policy)

## Classification for transport

The United Nations assigns a four-digit UN number to each biohazard class, shown on shipping papers, labels, and containers.

- **UN 2814 (Category A, affects humans):** an infectious substance capable of causing permanent disability or life-threatening or fatal disease in otherwise healthy people or animals.
- **UN 2900 (Category A, affects animals only):** an infectious substance not generally capable of causing such disease in humans.
- **UN 3373 (Category B):** a biological substance transported for diagnostic or investigative purposes; lower risk than Category A.
- **UN 3291 (Regulated Medical Waste):** waste or reusable material from medical treatment of humans or animals, or from biomedical research.

The two Category A classes define the highest transport risk; the B and waste classes cover everything routine clinical and research work produces.

## The four biosafety levels

The United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) ranks pathogens into four biosafety levels (BSL 1–4, also P1–P4 for Pathogen or Protection Level). Laboratories handling a given pathogen must match or exceed its level. Each step up adds containment equipment, decontamination steps, and limits on who may enter.

- **BSL-1:** agents that do not consistently cause disease in healthy adults, such as *Bacillus subtilis*, *Escherichia coli*, and varicella (chickenpox). Precautions are minimal: gloves and a face guard.
- **BSL-2:** agents that cause only mild disease in humans, or are hard to contract through laboratory aerosol, including hepatitis A, B, and C, some influenza A strains, human respiratory syncytial virus, Lyme disease, salmonella, mumps, measles, scrapie, dengue fever, and HIV. Routine diagnostic work with clinical specimens is done here; concentrated or replicating virus is handled with BSL-3 practices inside a BSL-2 facility.
- **BSL-3:** agents that can cause severe or fatal disease in humans but for which vaccines or other treatments exist, including anthrax, West Nile virus, Venezuelan equine encephalitis, SARS coronavirus, MERS coronavirus, SARS-CoV-2, Influenza A H5N1, hantaviruses, cholera, tuberculosis, typhus, Rift Valley fever, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, yellow fever, and malaria.
- **BSL-4:** viruses that cause severe or fatal disease in humans and have no vaccine or treatment, including Bolivian hemorrhagic fever, Marburg, Ebola, Lassa fever, Crimean–Congo hemorrhagic fever, and Nipah virus. Smallpox (variola) is also handled at BSL-4 even though a vaccine exists: routine vaccination stopped after eradication, so the general population is again vulnerable. No bacteria are classified at BSL-4.

The defining shift between BSL-2 and BSL-3 is the existence of medical countermeasures against severe disease. The shift into BSL-4 is the absence of any such countermeasure, which forces physical containment to do the work that medicine cannot.

## What a BSL-4 lab looks like

BSL-4 work requires a positive-pressure personnel suit with a segregated air supply. Entry and exit pass through multiple showers, a vacuum room, an ultraviolet-light room, and an autonomous detection system, with electronically interlocked airlocks so that two doors can never open at once. All air and water entering or leaving the facility is decontaminated, a redundancy layered on top of the suit, the showers, and the air filtration.

The colour-coded warning label on the door, the UN number on the shipping container, and the biosafety level on the lab together describe a biohazard from the moment it is packaged to the moment it is destroyed.
