# Fungus

A fungus is a eukaryotic organism that feeds by absorbing dissolved nutrients rather than making them itself. It is neither plant nor animal, though molecular evidence places it closer to animals than to plants inside the group called opisthokonts, and the lineage that produced both fungi and animals split roughly a billion years ago. Fungi range from single-celled yeasts to the familiar mushrooms, molds, and truffles, and they live in nearly every environment on Earth.

Three features together separate fungi from other kingdoms: a cell wall built from chitin (the same tough polymer found in insect shells), the secretion of digestive enzymes, and a body built from thread-like filaments. Because fungi digest outside their cells, they must grow through their food, which is why their bodies are extended surfaces rather than compact masses.

## Body, Growth, and Reproduction

The filament that makes up most fungal bodies is called a hypha. Hyphae are tubular and may be divided by cross-walls into compartments (septate) or remain as a single multinucleate tube (coenocytic). Branching hyphae weave into a mycelium, the feeding network that threads through soil, wood, or living tissue. The visible mushroom is a temporary reproductive structure, not the main body; the mycelium it springs from can persist for years and extend over hectares.

Fungi spread and reproduce by spores, produced both asexually and through sexual cycles. In the two largest phyla, Ascomycota (sac fungi) and Basidiomycota (club fungi), sexual reproduction passes through a dikaryotic stage in which each cell contains two genetically distinct nuclei that coexist and divide in parallel before fusing. Spores travel by wind, water, animals, or active ejection, and germinate into new mycelia when they land on a suitable surface.

## Classification

Seven phyla are now recognized, following a 2007 collaborative revision that replaced older morphology-based systems. The dominant phyla are Ascomycota and Basidiomycota, which together include most familiar mushrooms, molds, and yeasts. Glomeromycota, elevated to phylum status in 2001, contains the mycorrhizal partners of most land plants. A fossil fungus, *Ourasphaira giraldae* dated to around a billion years ago, hints that fungi may have lived on land far earlier than the first land plants. The 2012 revision of the International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants ended the practice of giving the same fungus multiple competing names.

## Ecology

Fungi are the principal decomposers of dead organic matter, releasing carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus in forms other organisms can reuse. They also form the most widespread symbioses on land: the mycorrhizal association between fungal hyphae and plant roots, in which the fungus trades mineral nutrients and water for sugars from the plant. Over 90% of plant species depend on such partnerships, and the extra carbon that mycorrhizal plants draw down suggests a role in climate buffering that is still being quantified. Lichens are another partnership, in which a fungus shelters algae or cyanobacteria that photosynthesize; insects, including many ants and termites, culture fungi as food.

The same enzymatic power that lets fungi break down dead tissue lets them attack living hosts. Fungi cause major plant diseases that shape agriculture, including rice blast, and serious human infections such as aspergillosis and candidiasis. Some species make mycotoxins, including the carcinogenic aflatoxins produced by *Aspergillus* species on stored grain and the amatoxins of death-cap mushrooms, which are lethal in small doses.

## Human Use

Fungi have been part of human food and fermentation for thousands of years, supplying mushrooms, truffles, bread, beer, wine, and cheese. The discovery of penicillin from *Penicillium* by Alexander Fleming in 1928 launched the antibiotic era and made fungi a routine source of pharmaceuticals: statins lower cholesterol, ciclosporin suppresses immune rejection in transplant patients, and fungal enzymes are used in industry. Fungi also serve as biological pesticides and as tools for bioremediation, breaking down pollutants in contaminated soil and water. Two species, *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* (baker's yeast) and *Neurospora crassa* (bread mold), became model organisms whose genetics and cell biology underpin much of modern molecular biology.

Against these benefits, fungi still destroy a significant fraction of crops each year, spoil stored food, and poison people who misidentify wild mushrooms. The true number of fungal species is unknown, with estimates from 2.2 to 3.8 million and only about 148,000 formally described as of 2020, a gap that DNA-based phylogenies continue to reshape.
