# Biological pathway

A biological pathway is a series of molecular interactions inside a cell that produces a defined outcome, such as a new molecule, a change in gene activity, or cell movement. Pathways are how cells detect internal and external signals and convert them into biochemical responses, and they are involved in processes such as cell growth and development.

## Major types

Pathways are grouped by what they do.

- **Metabolic pathways** transform molecules into energy or building blocks. Glycolysis, the stepwise breakdown of glucose to pyruvate, is a standard example.
- **Genetic pathways** control how the information encoded in genes is read and used, regulating which proteins a cell makes and when.
- **Signal transduction pathways** carry messages from outside the cell, or from the cell surface, to the interior, where they alter behaviour.

The categories overlap: a signal often reaches the nucleus, changes gene expression, and reshapes metabolism.

## How a pathway works

Most pathways follow a common logic. A **receptor protein** on or in the cell binds an external cue such as a hormone or growth factor. Binding changes the receptor's shape or chemistry, switching on a **signaling cascade** of intracellular proteins, enzymes, and **secondary messengers** (small diffusible molecules such as cyclic AMP that relay and amplify the signal inside the cell). The cascade amplifies the original event, so a single molecular interaction can trigger effects throughout the cell, often by turning specific genes on or off through **transcription factors**, proteins that bind DNA and control gene activity.

## Canonical examples

- The **Notch signaling pathway** is a direct cell-to-cell communication system in which a receptor on one cell binds a ligand on a neighbour, triggering cleavage of the receptor and release of a fragment that enters the nucleus to regulate genes controlling cell fate and development.
- The **Hedgehog signaling pathway** directs cell growth and the spatial patterning of tissues during embryonic development.
- The **ethylene signaling pathway** in plants uses the gaseous hormone ethylene to coordinate fruit ripening, leaf abscission (the controlled shedding of leaves), and responses to environmental stress.

## Pathways and disease

Disruptions in biological pathways can lead to disease. Abnormal signaling is associated with cancer, where mutations can lock signaling components in an "on" state, or disable regulators, causing uncontrolled cell growth and division and contributing to tumour formation.

## Reconstructing and storing pathways

Because pathways branch and interact, researchers reconstruct them by combining experimental data with computational models that infer which molecules are linked. Several databases make this knowledge reusable, including KEGG, Reactome, and WikiPathways for curated reference maps; PhosphoSitePlus for protein modifications; BioCyc for organism-specific pathways; PANTHER for protein family classification; TRANSFAC for transcription factors; MiRTarBase for microRNA–target interactions; DrugBank for drug–target links; the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database for chemical–gene–disease links; and Pathway Commons, which integrates many resources through a shared format called BioPAX. Tools such as esyN let researchers visualise networks and overlay drug interactions.
