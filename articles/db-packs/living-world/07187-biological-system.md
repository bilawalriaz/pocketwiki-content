# Biological system

A biological system is a network of interacting biologically relevant entities, and what counts as a system depends on the scale being described. At the macro scale, populations of organisms form systems. At the organ and tissue scale in mammals and other animals, examples include the circulatory, respiratory, and nervous systems. At the micro to nanoscopic scale, cells, organelles, macromolecular complexes, and regulatory pathways form systems. A biological system is not the same as a living system: a living organism is alive, but a biological system is defined by the network of interacting parts, not by being alive.

## Organ and tissue systems in animals

In human anatomy, organ systems are groups of organs working together to carry out a specific function. The respiratory system moves air through the pharynx, larynx, bronchi, lungs, and diaphragm for breathing. The digestive system breaks down food using the salivary glands, oesophagus, stomach, liver, gallbladder, pancreas, intestines, rectum, and anus. The cardiovascular system, made up of the heart, blood, and blood vessels, pumps and channels blood to and from the body and lungs. The urinary system, consisting of the kidneys, ureters, bladder, and urethra, maintains fluid balance and electrolyte balance and excretes urine. The integumentary system covers the body with skin, hair, fat, and nails. The skeletal system provides structural support through bones, cartilage, ligaments, and tendons. The muscular system, which includes skeletal, smooth, and cardiac muscle, produces movement, maintains posture, and generates heat.

Communication and regulation are split between two glandular systems. The endocrine system uses hormones produced by glands such as the hypothalamus, pituitary, pineal, thyroid, parathyroid, and adrenals. The exocrine system releases substances through ducts, including sweat, mucous, lacrimal, and mammary glands.

Defence and fluid transport are handled by the lymphatic system, which moves lymph between tissues and the bloodstream through lymph, nodes, and vessels, and supports immune responses and antibody development. The immune system protects against foreign bodies. The nervous system collects, transfers, and processes information through the brain, spinal cord, peripheral nerves, and sense organs, with specialised subsystems for vision, hearing, smell, taste, touch, and balance. The reproductive system consists of sex organs such as the ovaries, fallopian tubes, uterus, vagina, mammary glands, testes, vas deferens, seminal vesicles, and prostate.

## Historical roots of the concept

The idea that a system is a set of organs with a definite function was already present in Antiquity, where Galen and Aristotle classified organs by purpose. The application of the word "system" itself is more recent. Rufus of Ephesus, around 90–120 CE, was the first to view the brain, spinal cord, and craniospinal nerves as a single anatomical unit, though he did not name it. The term "nervous system" was introduced by Monro in 1783. The principal functions, and therefore the systems themselves, have remained largely the same since Antiquity, but their classification has varied; Aristotle, Bichat, and Cuvier each grouped them differently.

In the 1820s, the French physiologist Henri Milne-Edwards introduced the notion of physiological division of labour, inspired by Adam Smith. Milne-Edwards compared the body of a living being to a factory in which the organs, like workers, produce the phenomena of life. In more differentiated organisms, functional labour is apportioned between different instruments, which he called *appareils*.

## Cellular and subcellular systems

Within a cell, organelles and macromolecular assemblies form their own networks, and the components present depend on whether the cell is a eukaryote or a prokaryote. In eukaryotes, the nucleus stores genetic material and acts as the control centre. The cytosol is the jelly-like fluid inside the cytoplasm in which organelles are suspended, and the cell membrane encloses the cell.

The endoplasmic reticulum is a continuous channel extending from the outer nuclear envelope. Its rough portion, studded with ribosomes, contains cisternae where proteins are produced. Its smooth portion stores and synthesises lipids and steroid hormones and carries out detoxification. Ribosomes are the site of biological protein synthesis and cannot be reproduced in other organelles, so they must be built from their own components. Mitochondria carry out cellular respiration, producing ATP. Lysosomes break down unwanted material, and peroxisomes break down toxic materials such as hydrogen peroxide. The Golgi apparatus, found only in eukaryotes, is a folded network that modifies, transports, and secretes proteins. In plants, the chloroplast is the site of photosynthesis and stores chlorophyll.
