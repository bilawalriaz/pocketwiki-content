# Biological anthropology

Biological anthropology, also called physical anthropology, is the natural-science branch of anthropology that studies the biology and behaviour of humans, their extinct hominin relatives, and other primates, with evolution as the unifying framework. A hominin is any species closer to modern humans than to chimpanzees, including extinct relatives such as Neanderthals and australopithecines.

## What the subfield does

Biological anthropologists treat humans as a biological species. They work with fossil bones, living primate behaviour, ancient DNA, human skeletal remains from archaeological sites, and measurable variation in living populations. The shared premise, traced to Darwin, is that human traits are products of evolution by natural selection, common descent, and speciation.

Main branches:

- Paleoanthropology studies the fossil record of human evolution, especially hominin and other primate remains.
- Primatology studies behaviour, morphology, and genetics of non-human primates, to identify which human traits are shared ancestry and which are uniquely human.
- Bioarchaeology interprets human remains from archaeological sites, mostly bones, using osteology, paleopathology, and mortuary context.
- Paleopathology reads disease, malnutrition, trauma, and biomechanical stress in past populations from bones and mummified tissue.
- Human biology covers population-level health, genetics, nutrition, and physiology.
- Human behavioural ecology studies adaptive responses, including foraging and reproduction, to environmental pressures.
- Forensic anthropology identifies decomposed or skeletal remains in legal contexts, using osteology.
- Evolutionary psychology examines human psychological traits as possible adaptations shaped by natural or sexual selection.

## A short history

Efforts to classify humans reach back to ancient Greece. Plato placed humans on the scala naturae, a hierarchical chain of being from inanimate objects to deities, which shaped Western thinking about nature for roughly 2,000 years. His student Aristotle, in his *History of Animals*, observed that humans are the only animals to walk upright and attributed regional physical differences to climate.

Scientific physical anthropology took shape in the 17th and 18th centuries with racial classification, most influentially by Carl Linnaeus and Johann Friedrich Blumenbach. Blumenbach built a large human skull collection and divided humankind into five groups, Caucasian, Mongolian, Aethiopian, Malayan, and American, a scheme now considered obsolete. In the 19th century, Paul Broca developed craniometry, the measurement of skulls, while Rudolf Virchow stressed the role of environment and disease. The field was drawn into the slavery debate of the 1830s–40s, where the British monogenist James Cowles Prichard, who held that all humans share one origin, opposed the American polygenist Samuel George Morton, who held that races were separately created.

In the late 19th century, Franz Boas shifted the field by showing that head shape is malleable, shaped by environment and nutrition rather than fixed racial inheritance, yet scientific racism persisted into the 20th century through figures such as Earnest Hooton and Aleš Hrdlička.

In 1951, Sherwood Washburn, a former student of Hooton, proposed a "new physical anthropology" that replaced racial typology with the study of evolution and process. Paleoanthropology and primatology expanded, and the modern synthesis, the 20th-century merger of Darwinian evolution with Mendelian genetics, combined with new knowledge of DNA and better dating methods, gave the field a sharper empirical base.
