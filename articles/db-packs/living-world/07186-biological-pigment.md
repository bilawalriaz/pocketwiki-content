# Biological pigment

A **biological pigment** (also called a biochrome) is a colored substance produced by a living organism. Its color comes from selective absorption of certain wavelengths of light and reflection of others, so the same pigment looks identical from every viewing angle. This distinguishes pigments from **structural color**, which is produced by microscopic layers that reflect or refract light and usually shifts with viewing angle (the iridescence of butterfly wings is the classic example). Pigments are deposited in specialized cells called **chromatophores** found in skin, scales, feathers, fur, and hair.

## The major chemical families

Most pigments belong to a small set of molecular families. Their color arises from **conjugated systems**, extended chains of alternating single and double bonds that lower the energy needed to absorb visible light:

- **Tetrapyrroles** are molecules built from four nitrogen-containing rings. The family includes **chlorophyll** (the green photosynthetic pigment of plants and algae), **heme** in hemoglobin and myoglobin, **bilirubin**, and hemocyanin.
- **Carotenoids** are yellow, orange, or red **tetraterpenoids** (40-carbon molecules built from isoprene units). The red of tomatoes is **lycopene**; the orange of carrots is α-carotene. Animals cannot synthesize carotenoids and must obtain them from food, with three known exceptions: pea aphids, two-spotted spider mites, and gall midges, which acquired carotenoid genes by horizontal transfer from fungi.
- **Flavonoids**, including **anthocyanins**, are water-soluble pigments that appear red, purple, or blue depending on pH, and give most flowers and fruits their color.
- **Betalains** are red or yellow, water-soluble like anthocyanins but synthesized from the amino acid tyrosine. They occur only in the plant order Caryophyllales (cacti, beets, bougainvillea) and never co-occur with anthocyanins in the same plant.
- **Melanins** are dark polymers derived from tyrosine; eumelanin is the black-brown form, phaeomelanin the yellow-to-red-brown form.
- **Specialized groups** include psittacofulvins (red and yellow pigments unique to parrots), turacin and turacoverdin (red and green pigments of turacos), luciferin (light-emitting), and phycobiliproteins (antenna pigments in cyanobacteria and red algae).

## Pigments in plants

The primary function of plant pigments is **photosynthesis**. Chlorophyll absorbs blue and red light and reflects green; accessory carotenoids harvest additional wavelengths and protect chlorophyll from photo-oxidative damage by dissipating excess energy as heat. Land plants and green algae use chlorophylls *a* and *b*; kelps, diatoms, and other heterokonts use chlorophyll *c*; red algae use only chlorophyll *a*. Pigments also guide pollinators: as a flower is pollinated, pigment loss or accumulation can shift its color, signaling which blossoms still carry nectar and pollen.

In **autumn leaves**, chlorophyll breaks down into colorless tetrapyrroles, revealing the yellow xanthophylls and orange β-carotene that were present year-round. Once roughly half the chlorophyll has degraded, the tree synthesizes red anthocyanins *de novo*. The amino acids released from degraded light-harvesting complexes are stored in woody tissues over winter and recycled the next spring.

## Pigments in algae and bacteria

Algae supplement chlorophyll with **phycobiliproteins** (phycoerythrin in red algae, phycocyanin in cyanobacteria) and with **fucoxanthin**, the xanthophyll that colors brown and golden algae. Cyanobacteria add the UV-screening pigment **scytonemin** and unique chlorophylls *d* and *f*. Purple sulfur bacteria use bacteriochlorophylls *a* and *b* for photosynthesis in low-light, anaerobic conditions.

## Pigments in animals

Animals use pigments for camouflage, mimicry, warning coloration, and courtship signaling. In fish, amphibians, and cephalopods, **chromatophores** enable rapid color change: each cell holds vesicles of one pigment color (erythrophores carry red, melanophores black-brown, xanthophores yellow), and stacking layers produces intermediate hues. Some pigments serve no visual role: the **photopigment rhodopsin** in the retina initiates vision, and heme in blood is colored as a side effect of its oxygen-binding chemistry.

Several disorders illustrate how central these molecules are. **Albinism** is the inherited loss of melanin; **vitiligo** is patchy loss of melanocytes; **melasma** produces dark facial patches triggered by hormonal change; **lamellar ichthyosis** involves melanin overproduction. Melanin also absorbs ultraviolet radiation and protects skin from sunburn.

## Marine animals and bioluminescence

In the ocean, carotenoids are usually bound into **carotenoproteins** (carotenoid–protein complexes) that produce colors ranging from red to blue. Lobster carapace color comes from astaxanthin bound to different proteins: **crustacyanin** (absorbance maximum 632 nm) produces slate-blue, while **crustochrin** (max 409 nm) produces yellow. Tetrapyrroles serve in electron transport, and melanins protect against UV. Roughly 90% of deep-sea animals produce **bioluminescence** through a reaction of luciferin, luciferase, salt, and oxygen, typically emitting blue or green light, the wavelengths that travel farthest through seawater. **Mycosporine-like amino acids** absorb UV at 310–360 nm and act as natural sunscreens.

Humans extract biological pigments as dyes and consume some as dietary supplements, notably astaxanthin and lycopene.
