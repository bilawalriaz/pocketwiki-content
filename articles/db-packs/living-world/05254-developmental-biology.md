# Developmental biology

Developmental biology studies how a single fertilised cell becomes a multicellular organism with the right cell types in the right places. The field also covers regeneration, metamorphosis, asexual reproduction, and the behaviour of adult stem cells.

The four processes at the core of embryogenesis are regional specification, cell differentiation, morphogenesis, and tissue growth. Regional specification is the earliest: a ball of initially similar cells is subdivided into zones, each committed to a future body part. A small group of cells inherits cytoplasmic determinants from the egg, becomes a signalling centre, and releases an inducing factor. The factor diffuses and decays, forming a concentration gradient. Cells further from the source read different threshold concentrations by switching on different combinations of transcription factors, and these combinations lock in regional identity.

Cell differentiation follows within each region. Differentiated cells such as neurons, muscle fibres, and hepatocytes produce large amounts of a few function-specific proteins. The relevant genes sit in open chromatin with bound transcription factors: NeuroD drives neurons, myogenin drives muscle, HNF4 drives hepatocytes. Within a single tissue, a lateral inhibition loop using the Notch signalling pathway picks out one cell type from a uniform precursor field, as when NeuroD-expressing neuronal precursors are carved out of the neural plate.

Morphogenesis turns flat or ball-like groups of cells into three-dimensional shape through coordinated sheet movements and individual cell migrations. The central event is gastrulation, which produces the three germ layers: ectoderm on the outside, mesoderm in the middle, endoderm on the inside. Tissue growth then enlarges the embryo, mostly by cell proliferation, and differential growth between regions (allometry, where one part grows at a different rate from another) sculpts final proportions.

In animals, the zygote first undergoes rapid cleavage divisions without growth, producing a blastula of smaller cells. Mouse primordial germ cells in the epiblast are reset to a totipotent state by genome-wide DNA demethylation, chromatin reorganisation, and erasure of epigenetic imprints, a process that uses the base-excision repair pathway. Free-living embryos cannot grow in mass because they have no external food supply, while placental and yolk-fed embryos grow quickly.

Plants follow a similar logic with one large exception: plant cells are mostly immotile, so morphogenesis comes from differential growth rather than cell migration, and the signalling molecules and genes are largely different.

Regeneration, the regrowth of a missing part, is best studied in four animal models. Hydra and planarian worms can rebuild whole bodies from small fragments, fuelled by stem cells that are at least partly pluripotent (able to give rise to many cell types). Crickets and urodele amphibians such as the axolotl regenerate only distal appendages; amphibian limb regeneration re-uses the same signals active in the embryonic limb, and connective tissues interconvert between cartilage, dermis, and tendon. It is still debated whether regeneration is a pristine ancestral trait that could in principle be enhanced in humans, or a series of separately evolved adaptations.

Metamorphosis reuses all four core processes. A frog hatches as a tadpole and remodels into an adult; a fly larva carries imaginal discs, sacs of undifferentiated cells set aside during embryogenesis, that build the adult body, the classic case being *Drosophila melanogaster*.

Plants develop continuously from meristems at root and shoot tips, so a mature plant always contains embryonic tissue, unlike an animal that fixes its body plan early. The zygote becomes an embryo with a first root, a shoot tip, and one or more cotyledons (seed leaves); further organs arise by organogenesis from root and shoot meristems, and branching starts when undifferentiated cells left behind begin to grow. Cells also elongate, and unequal elongation on opposite sides of a stem causes bending, the basis of tropisms toward light, gravity, water, and touch. Plant growth is steered by plant hormones and growth regulators whose levels depend on age, dormancy, photoperiod, drought, and temperature.

Variation between repeated parts of one plant (visible in leaves, stems, and flowers) has three causes: position, environment, and juvenility. The evolutionary shift from algal ancestors to land plants brought new transcription factor families wired into multicellular development and reproduction, allowing more complex morphogenesis. The antithetic theory, currently favoured, holds that the multicellular sporophyte (the spore-producing generation) arose from extra mitotic divisions inserted before meiosis.

Research depends on a small set of model organisms chosen for embryo supply, genetics, cost, or surgical access: the plant *Arabidopsis thaliana*; vertebrates *Xenopus*, zebrafish, chicken, and mouse; invertebrates *Drosophila melanogaster* and *Caenorhabditis elegans*; unicellulars *Chlamydomonas* and *Saccharomyces*; and for regeneration, axolotl and planarian worms such as *Schmidtea mediterranea*, with organoids (lab-grown miniature tissues) now adding a human-tissue route. These species share enough core mechanisms, especially early inductive signals and regional-identity genes, that findings in one often generalise across the animal kingdom, although fine-grained differences grow larger the closer one looks at any particular species.
