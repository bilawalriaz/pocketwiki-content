# Plant virus

Plant viruses are obligate intracellular parasites that depend on a host plant to replicate. Most have a single-stranded RNA genome, usually small, though some carry double-stranded RNA, ssDNA, or dsDNA. A few cause disease in vascular plants; in wild plants the relationship is often benign or even mutualistic. Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV), the first virus ever discovered, remains the most familiar example. Plant viruses cause an estimated US$60 billion in crop losses worldwide each year. They are classified into 73 genera and 49 families, but this count covers only cultivated plants.

## How they are built

Over half of plant viruses are rod-shaped, either flexuous or rigid, usually 300–500 nm long with a diameter of 15–20 nm. Their coat proteins assemble into discs that stack into a tube around the viral nucleic acid. The next most common shape is isometric (icosahedral), 25–50 nm across, built from 60 T protein subunits. A few genera of *Geminiviridae* look like two isometric particles fused together. Some plant viruses carry a lipid envelope taken from the host cell membrane as they bud out.

## How they move from plant to plant

Plants do not move, so plant viruses rely on vectors or direct contact to spread.

- **Sap contact.** Wounded plants touching healthy ones, through tools, hands, or animal feeding. TMV, potato viruses, and cucumber mosaic virus spread this way.
- **Insects**, most often leafhoppers, aphids, and whiteflies. The vector determines the host range, since the virus reaches only plants the insect feeds on. Non-persistent viruses cling to the stylet tip, semi-persistent viruses lodge in the foregut, and persistent viruses cross into the haemolymph and salivary glands. Propagative persistent viruses replicate inside the insect; circulative ones cannot and are shielded in aphids by the chaperone protein *symbionin*, produced by bacterial symbionts.
- **Nematodes** feed on roots and carry virions on their stylet or gut, transmitting tobacco ringspot virus and tobacco rattle virus without replicating.
- **Plasmodiophorids**, soil-borne protozoa such as *Polymyxa graminis* and *Polymyxa betae*, deliver viruses including Beet necrotic yellow vein virus and wound roots, opening doors for other pathogens.
- **Seed and pollen.** About 20% of plant viruses pass between generations, infecting generative cells and sometimes the seed coat; the route is environmentally influenced and no seed position is safe.

## How plant cells fight back

Plants detect viral double-stranded RNA and use siRNA to silence it; most plant viruses counter this by encoding a suppressor protein. Plants also tighten plasmodesmata after injury to slow cell-to-cell spread. Viruses hijack the plant's existing mRNA transport machinery to move through these channels, and they remodel intracellular membranes to traffic new virions between cells.

## How the genome is expressed

About 75% of plant viruses have ssRNA genomes; 65% are positive-sense (+ssRNA) and translate directly, 10% are negative-sense and must first be converted. Five percent are dsRNA, 3% require reverse transcriptase, 17% are ssDNA, and very few are dsDNA, the opposite of the pattern in animal or bacteriophage viruses.

Plant ribosomes translate one protein per mRNA, so viruses use special tricks to express multiple proteins from a compact genome. Some carry a leaky stop codon (in TMV, 5% of translations read past it) to produce a longer polymerase. Others generate sub-genomic mRNAs from a primary replicase transcript. A few, like *Bromoviridae*, split their genome across multipartite particles; all particles must infect the same cell for disease to start. About 45% of plant viruses make a single polyprotein that the virus itself cleaves into functional pieces. Plant mRNAs need a 5′ cap, so many viruses either encode a methyltransferase replicase to add one or steal a cap from a host mRNA by cap-snatching; others translate cap-independently using enhancers in their untranslated regions. Viral replicase also participates directly in packaging the genome, as shown in Beet necrotic yellow vein virus, whose five RNAs are each encapsidated into separate rod-shaped particles encoding movement, coat, regulatory, and symptom proteins.

## A short history

A. Mayer showed in 1886 that tobacco mosaic disease passed in sap but was destroyed by boiling, and he wrongly blamed bacteria. In 1898 Martinus Beijerinck filtered the sap through a Chamberland candle, found the agent remained infectious, and named it *contagium vivum fluidum*, the origin of the word "virus". Holmes listed 129 plant viruses in 1939; by 1999 there were 977. Wendell Stanley crystallised TMV in 1935 and won the 1946 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, though he had not yet identified RNA as the infectious material; 1950s work from two labs proved that the purified RNA alone could infect plants.

## Why the virus matters beyond disease

Plant viruses are now tools. Tulip breaking virus produced the streaked petals that drove 17th-century Dutch tulip mania, the earliest recorded application. TMV and cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) are standard in plant molecular biology, and the CaMV 35S promoter is the workhorse driver in transgenic plants. Viral vectors based on TMV underpin the magnICON and TRBO expression systems for transient protein production. Virions also serve as scaffolds for nanoparticles, nanocarriers for cargo, and nanoreactors housing enzymes. Virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS), viral delivery of CRISPR nucleases, and viral expression of *FLOWERING LOCUS T* let researchers silence genes, edit genomes, and accelerate breeding within a single growing season.
