# Natural selection

Natural selection is the differential survival and reproduction of individuals in a population, caused by heritable differences in their observable traits. Individuals whose traits fit the local environment leave more surviving offspring, so those traits become more common in the next generation. Repeated over many generations, this changes populations and produces adaptation and speciation, with no planner or goal.

## The core mechanism

Three conditions are required: heritable variation in traits that affect survival or reproduction; a struggle for existence, meaning resources, mates, or other limits prevent everyone from reproducing equally; and inheritance of those traits by offspring. Given these, variants associated with higher reproductive success automatically increase in frequency. The environment acts as a sieve, with no choice involved.

Selection acts on phenotypes, the observable body and behavior. The underlying DNA sequence is the genotype. Only heritable phenotypic differences change the genetic makeup of a population over time. A trait acquired during life, such as muscle built through exercise, is not passed on; a genetic predisposition to the same trait is. This is why selection requires heritability, not just plasticity.

## Fitness

Fitness means reproductive success, the number of gene copies contributed to the next generation, not strength, health, or longevity. A short-lived organism producing many surviving offspring is more fit than a long-lived one producing few. Fitness is an average over a population, since chance, weather, and accidents affect individuals. The phrase "survival of the fittest," coined by Herbert Spencer in 1864 and adopted by Darwin in 1869, is avoided by modern biologists because it sounds tautological: the fittest are just those who survive.

## Sources of heritable variation

Selection does not create raw material; it only sorts what already exists. New heritable variation comes mainly from random mutations, changes in DNA sequence or chromosome structure, and from genetic recombination, the reshuffling of parental chromosomes during reproduction. Most mutations are neutral or slightly harmful. A small fraction, especially changes in regulatory genes such as the HOX family that control embryonic development, can produce large morphological effects, though many are lethal. In long-lived organisms, much of the variation selection acts on is standing variation, differences already present in the population rather than waiting for new mutations.

## Modes of selection

Selection can be classified by what it does to a trait. Stabilizing selection favors intermediate values and removes extremes, holding a trait near an optimum. Directional selection favors one extreme, shifting the population toward it. Disruptive selection favors both extremes over the middle and can split a population into two forms, sometimes starting speciation. Balancing selection keeps multiple variants in circulation through heterozygote advantage, as with the sickle-cell allele in malaria-prone regions or the human ABO blood groups, or through frequency-dependent selection, where a trait's value depends on how common it is. Purifying selection continuously removes harmful variants, opposing new mutations.

Selection can also be classified by life stage. Viability selection acts on survival, fecundity selection on reproductive rate, and sexual selection on access to mates. Sexual selection can be intrasexual (competition within one sex) or intersexual (mate choice) and frequently produces strong sexual dimorphism.

## Worked example: the peppered moth

In pre-industrial Britain, the peppered moth (*Biston betularia*) was mostly pale, with dark forms rare. Soot from factories blackened tree trunks and killed the pale lichens on them; dark moths were then better camouflaged from bird predators and rose to about 90% of populations. After the Clean Air Act of 1956, lichens returned, pale trunks became common again, and the dark form collapsed back to rarity. Modern genetics traced the dark form to a transposable element inserted into the *cortex* gene, a single heritable change that produced a visible phenotypic shift, with the direction of selection reversed by a change in pollution.

A polygenic case is ash trees (*Fraxinus excelsior*) in Britain evolving resistance to the invasive ash dieback fungus (*Hymenoscyphus fraxineus*); genomic prediction shows each new generation is genetically more resistant because selection has been sorting standing variation, not waiting for new mutations.

## Speciation

Given enough divergence, selection can split one species into two. The usual first step is reproductive isolation, meaning the two populations stop interbreeding. This is easiest in allopatric speciation, where a physical barrier separates them; each population adapts to its own environment and accumulates incompatible mutations, so hybrids have lower fitness. The Bateson–Dobzhansky–Muller model formalizes this: once one population fixes a new mutation, any migrant carrying the old allele produces maladapted hybrid offspring, so selection reinforces isolation. Sympatric speciation can occur within a single area when disruptive selection favors extremes using different resources, eventually producing groups that no longer recognize each other as mates.

## Social life and arms races

Selection also shapes interactions within and between species. W. D. Hamilton's 1964 theory of kin selection showed that helping relatives can be favored because relatives carry shared copies of the same genes, explaining sterile insect castes, sentinel behavior in meerkats, and cooperation in many vertebrates. The 20th-century gene-centred view was skeptical of group selection; 21st-century work treats it as a real but weaker force.

When one species evolves a defense, its predators or parasites evolve counter-measures, producing evolutionary arms races. Antibiotic resistance in bacteria follows the same logic: each new drug selects for resistant strains that spread through hospitals and communities. A natural case unfolded in the Samoan butterfly *Hypolimnas bolina*, where a gene suppressing male-killing by *Wolbachia* bacteria spread across the population in roughly five years.

## A short history

Earlier thinkers, including Al-Jahiz in the 9th century, had fragments of a selection idea. Charles Darwin, after observing biogeography on the HMS Beagle voyage (1831–1836) and reading Thomas Malthus on populations outrunning their resources, formulated natural selection in 1838. In 1858, Alfred Russel Wallace independently sent Darwin a paper describing the same principle; the two were jointly presented to the Linnean Society, and Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* appeared in 1859. Darwin lacked a theory of heredity; Gregor Mendel's work, ignored in his lifetime, was rediscovered in 1900. Between 1930 and 1937, Ronald Fisher, J. B. S. Haldane, Sewall Wright, and Theodosius Dobzhansky merged Mendelian genetics with selection in the modern synthesis, showing that small mutant effects in large populations could produce evolutionary change. Molecular genetics and evo-devo later revealed how changes in regulatory genes reshape bodies, and 21st-century extended evolutionary syntheses have restored a role for organismal behavior, development, and cultural inheritance in shaping selection's direction.

Natural selection is distinct from artificial selection, where a human breeder chooses which individuals reproduce, and from genetic drift, the chance loss or fixation of alleles especially in small populations.
