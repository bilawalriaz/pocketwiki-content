# Biological target

A biological target is anything inside a living organism to which another entity, such as an endogenous ligand (a molecule the body produces that binds a specific partner) or a drug, can bind, producing a change in the target's behaviour or function. The most common targets are proteins, including enzymes, ion channels, and receptors, though nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) are increasingly targeted. In pharmacology, the native protein whose activity a drug modifies is called a drug target, and the modification can be a wanted therapeutic effect or an unwanted adverse effect.

## How a stimulus meets its target

The external stimulus (the drug or ligand) physically binds to the target. The interaction can follow one of three patterns:

- **Noncovalent** – a relatively weak, fully reversible interaction with no chemical bond formed.
- **Reversible covalent** – a chemical bond forms, but the reverse reaction occurs readily, so the bond can be broken.
- **Irreversible covalent** – a permanent bond forms between stimulus and target.

## What happens after binding

Two main consequences follow:

- **Blocked access.** The bound substance stops other endogenous (made by the organism itself) molecules, such as activating hormones, from reaching the binding site. Depending on the target type, this is called receptor antagonism, enzyme inhibition, or ion channel blockade.
- **Conformational change.** The binding shifts the target's three-dimensional shape, altering its function. If the new behaviour mimics the body's natural signal, the effect is receptor agonism (or channel or enzyme activation). If it produces the opposite effect, the result is inverse agonism.

## Common drug target classes

Marketed drugs act on a small set of protein families, with nucleic acids as a smaller, newer category:

- G protein-coupled receptors, the target of about 50% of current drugs
- Enzymes, especially protein kinases, proteases, esterases, and phosphatases
- Ion channels, including ligand-gated and voltage-gated types
- Nuclear hormone receptors
- Structural proteins such as tubulin
- Membrane transport proteins
- Nucleic acids

## How drug targets are discovered

Identifying the biological origin of a disease and a target that can modify it is the first step in reverse pharmacology, a strategy that starts from a known target and works backward to find an acting molecule. A valid target need not cause the disease, but it must be capable of altering its course. The opposite approach, forward pharmacology, screens compounds for a desired phenotypic effect (a visible change in cells or organisms), producing "orphan" ligands whose target is unknown; target deconvolution then identifies which protein the compound binds.

## Target conservation and ecological spillover

Because drug targets are often evolutionarily well conserved (similar in structure and function across many species), pharmaceuticals released into the environment can act on non-target species that share them. Synthetic estrogens in contraceptives, such as 17-α-ethinylestradiol, have been detected at nanogram to microgram per litre concentrations in aquatic systems and linked to feminisation of fish downstream from sewage treatment plants, unbalancing reproduction and adding a selective pressure on fish survival. Adverse effects in non-target species therefore arise from the same specific target interactions that make these drugs useful in humans, and the more conserved a target is, the higher the risk of unintended pharmacological effects elsewhere in the biosphere.
