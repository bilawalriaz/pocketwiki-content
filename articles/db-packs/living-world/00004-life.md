# Life

## Central idea

Life is the capacity of matter organized into one or more cells to carry out processes such as metabolism, growth, response to stimuli, homeostasis, adaptation, and reproduction. It is a process rather than a substance, which is why more than 123 competing definitions have been compiled and none commands consensus. Most biological definitions list these traits. Physicists describe organisms as open thermodynamic systems that exploit environmental gradients to make imperfect copies of themselves. A NASA working definition, "a self-sustained chemical system capable of undergoing Darwinian evolution," has been criticized because a single sexually reproducing individual cannot evolve in isolation. Living systems theory instead frames life through self-organization and autopoiesis, the self-production of components, organized hierarchically from molecules to the biosphere.

## Viruses and the boundary problem

Viruses possess genes, evolve by natural selection, and self-assemble, yet they cannot metabolize and require host cells to replicate. They are usually classified as gene replicators rather than living organisms, though their self-assembly has been used to argue that life may have begun with self-assembling organic molecules. Death is similarly hard to delimit because organ systems stop functioning non-simultaneously, so the boundary must be drawn without an agreed definition of life itself.

## Historical study

Three traditions dominated. Materialists treated living things as complex arrangements of matter: Empedocles (c. 430 BC) proposed four eternal elements; Democritus described the soul as fiery atoms; Descartes revived a mechanistic view, extended by Leibniz (1714) and La Mettrie. Aristotle's hylomorphism (c. 322 BC) held that living things combine matter with a soul (vegetative, animal, or rational), and that form explains their purpose, a teleological view in tension with the past-directed causality of natural selection. Vitalism, the belief in a non-material life-principle introduced by Stahl, was undermined by Wöhler's 1828 synthesis of urea from inorganic materials, Helmholtz's demonstration that energy is conserved in muscle contraction, and Buchner's cell-free fermentation. Spontaneous generation, proposed by Aristotle, persisted for two millennia until Pasteur's 1859 experiments.

## Origin and evolution

Earth is about 4.54 billion years old; molecular clocks place life's origin near 4 billion years ago. Evidence includes biogenic graphite in 3.7-billion-year-old Greenland rocks, microbial mat fossils aged 3.48 billion years, possible biotic remains at 4.1 billion years, and putative Quebec microfossils as old as 4.28 billion years, suggesting near-instantaneous emergence after ocean formation. All extant life descends from a universal common ancestor; in 2016, 355 of its genes were tentatively identified. Evolution, change in heritable characteristics by natural selection and genetic drift, generates biodiversity. Fossils older than 10,000 years form the fossil record, and over 99% of all species that ever lived are extinct.

## Environmental role and limits

Microbial metabolism reshaped Earth; cyanobacterial oxygen release posed novel challenges and enabled major animal and plant lineages. The biosphere extends from soil and hot springs to rock 19 km underground, ocean depths, and 64 km into the atmosphere. Extremophiles tolerate freezing, desiccation, radiation, nuclear reactors, space vacuum, and sediment at 120 °C. Each species lives within a range of tolerance, flanked by zones of physiological stress and intolerance.

## Classification

Aristotle divided life into plants and animals, then blooded and bloodless. Linnaeus introduced binomial nomenclature in the late 1740s, founding modern taxonomy. Fungi were later separated from plants (Whittaker's five-kingdom system), microscopy revealed protists, and Monera was split into Bacteria and Archaea, producing the three-domain system. Eukaryote classification, especially of protists, remains contested; metagenomics shows most living things are bacteria sharing a common origin.

## Composition

Life depends on carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, and sulfur; carbon's stable covalent bonds underpin organic chemistry. DNA, a double helix of nucleotides (bases A, T, C, G with deoxyribose and phosphate), carries genetic information, with complementary base pairing letting each strand template the other. Cells, the basic unit of life, arise only by division. Prokaryotes (Bacteria, Archaea) lack nuclei; eukaryotes have membrane-bound organelles, likely acquired through endosymbiosis. Proteins, built by ribosomes, are the cell's machinery. A change in the enzyme GK-PID roughly 800 million years ago may have enabled multicellularity, coordinated by cell signaling.

## Beyond Earth and artificial life

Extraterrestrial life is considered plausible, with candidate sites including Mars's subsurface, Venus's upper atmosphere, and subsurface oceans of giant-planet moons. Habitability depends on stellar mass and galactic location, and the Drake equation frames the question of technological civilizations. Artificial life simulates living processes computationally, while synthetic biology seeks to engineer functions absent in nature.
