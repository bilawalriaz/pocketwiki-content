# Microbiology

Microbiology is the study of microorganisms: living things too small to see with the naked eye. The name combines Greek *mīkros* (small), *bíos* (life), and *-logía* (study). Microorganisms can be unicellular (single-celled), multicellular (made of multiple cooperating cells), or acellular (lacking cells, as viruses are). The field covers bacteria, archaea, fungi, protists, viruses, and the immune responses they provoke, and it branches into virology, bacteriology, protistology, mycology, immunology, and parasitology.

The microbial world divides by cell type. Prokaryotes (Bacteria and Archaea) lack a membrane-bound nucleus and other internal compartments. Eukaryotes (fungi, protists) keep DNA inside a nucleus and carry other membrane-bound organelles. Viruses fit awkwardly: simple enough to be treated as complex molecules, yet they reproduce and evolve, so classification varies. Prions, infectious misfolded proteins that cause diseases such as scrapie, are not microorganisms, but virologists discovered them while chasing what looked like slow viral infections.

Early microbiologists relied on culture (growing microbes in nutrient media), staining (dyeing cells for visibility), and microscopy (lenses for magnification). A hard limit emerged: fewer than 1% of microbes in common environments grow in a laboratory dish. Modern microbiology therefore leans on molecular biology, especially sequencing the 16S rRNA gene, a slowly evolving stretch of bacterial DNA whose sequence acts as a species barcode.

## How the field took shape

People guessed at hidden life long before seeing it. Mahavira, the 6th-century BCE teacher of Jainism, taught that unseen creatures pervade earth, water, air, and fire, and Jain scriptures describe *nigodas*, sub-microscopic beings living in large clusters. The Roman scholar Marcus Terentius Varro warned that swamps bred "minute creatures which cannot be seen by the eyes" that enter the body and cause disease. In 1546, Girolamo Fracastoro proposed that epidemics spread by transferable "seedlike entities" passed through contact, indirect contact, or contaminated objects, an early statement of germ theory. Persian physicians, including Avicenna and Ibn Zuhr, made related observations, and a 10th-century Chinese Taoist text described "countless micro organic worms" in food.

The first person likely to have actually seen microbes was the Jesuit polymath Athanasius Kircher, who in 1646 wrote that vinegar and milk "abound with an innumerable multitude of worms" and, in 1658, that plague was caused by microbes, though he was probably looking at blood cells. Robert Hooke recorded microscopic mould structures in 1666. The decisive observer was Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, a Dutch draper who, beginning in 1676, used single-lens microscopes of his own making to describe bacteria, protists, and sperm cells. He is generally called the father of microbiology.

The discipline matured in the 19th century. Louis Pasteur disproved spontaneous generation, the idea that living organisms routinely arise from non-living matter, by showing that sterilised broth stays microbe-free unless contaminated, and he developed pasteurisation and vaccines for anthrax, fowl cholera, and rabies. Robert Koch proved that specific microbes cause specific diseases, formulated Koch's postulates (a four-step test linking a microbe to a disease), and isolated the tuberculosis bacillus in pure culture. Ferdinand Cohn founded bacteriology, classified bacteria, and discovered endospores, tough dormant forms that let some bacteria survive harsh conditions. Joseph Lister introduced phenol antiseptic on surgical wounds. Pasteur and Koch focused on disease-causing microbes, so they missed much of the microbial world. Martinus Beijerinck and Sergei Winogradsky filled that gap: Beijerinck discovered viruses (showing that tobacco mosaic disease is caused by an agent small enough to pass through filters that trap bacteria) and invented enrichment culture, selective growth conditions that favour organisms with a desired metabolism, while Winogradsky discovered chemolithotrophy, the ability of microbes to harvest energy from inorganic minerals rather than sunlight or organic food, and isolated bacteria that drive the nitrogen cycle. In 1917, Félix d'Herelle co-discovered bacteriophages, viruses that infect bacteria.

## Why microbes matter

Microbes run much of the planet's chemistry. Industrial fermentation uses them to make alcohol, vinegar, dairy products, amino acids, vitamins, proteins, and antibiotics; *Corynebacterium glutamicum* produces more than two million tonnes of amino acids a year, mostly L-glutamate and L-lysine, and *Streptomyces* species are the source of aminoglycoside antibiotics. Microbes also make biopolymers, naturally produced chain-like molecules such as xanthan, alginate, cellulose, and polyhydroxyalkanoates, used in tissue engineering and drug delivery. The enzyme Taq polymerase, isolated from a heat-loving bacterium, is the basis of PCR, a method for copying specific DNA sequences millions of times. Mixed communities of bacteria and fungi clean up domestic, agricultural, and industrial pollutants, since each strain tends to break down only certain compounds. Symbiotic microbes in the gut and on the skin help digest food, supply vitamins, and crowd out pathogens; probiotics (live beneficial bacteria) and prebiotics (fibre-like food for those bacteria) deliberately reshape this community. Engineered, non-pathogenic *Clostridium* strains that grow only inside oxygen-starved tumours are being tested as cancer therapies, and laboratory favourites such as *Myxococcus xanthus* reveal general principles of cell movement and multicellular development.
