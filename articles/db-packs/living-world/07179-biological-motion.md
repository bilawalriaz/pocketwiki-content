# Biological motion

Biological motion is movement produced by a living organism. The human visual system is highly sensitive to it: from another person or animal acting, we recognise the action, infer intent, sense emotion, and predict what comes next, often from very sparse information.

The central finding of the field is that this ability does not require seeing a body. A point-light display (PLD) is made by fixing small lights to an actor's major joints and filming in the dark. The viewer sees only moving dots on a black background, yet can still identify the action, the actor's gender, the emotional tone (anger, sadness, happiness), and even subtle gait differences. As long as the dots are moving, observers extract a coherent figure; once the display freezes, recognition collapses.

## How the brain does it

Biological motion perception is a distributed process. Neuroimaging and patient work converge on a network:

- The **superior temporal sulcus** is the most consistently active region during PLD viewing.
- The **premotor cortex** is also engaged, and contains **mirror neurons**, cells that fire both when an animal performs an action and when it observes the same action in another. The same population handles "doing" and "seeing done."
- Additional contributions come from the **supramarginal gyrus**, the **temporoparietal junction**, the **cerebellum**, and the **default mode network**, which helps separate biological from non-biological motion.

The same network that produces movement is part of the system that recognises it, which is the core of the **embodied cognition** view: understanding an action is partly a re-enactment of it.

## Experience shapes perception

Perception of biological motion is not a fixed low-level filter; it is tuned by what the observer already knows how to do. Expert dancers are more sensitive to the dance style they have trained in, and less sensitive to other styles. The same expertise effect appears in music, language, scientific thinking, basketball, and walking. Recognising an action and being able to perform it draw on overlapping resources.

Two further findings underline the role of experience. People recognise their own gait from PLDs more easily than other people's. And children only reach adult-level PLD recognition around age five; three- and four-year-olds struggle, with three-year-olds paradoxically best at walking-dog displays and worst at walking-human displays, likely because their small stature gives them more everyday experience of dogs at eye level than of adults walking.

## Local and global processing

Perception depends on two interacting sources of information. The **global** signal is the overall configuration of the body: posture, orientation, and the spatial relationship between joints. The **local** signal is the motion of each individual dot. When local dot directions conflict with the global body direction, observers have trouble reading the whole action, showing that the brain applies a form-based rule to both levels. As with faces and the **Thatcher effect**, an inverted local element is easy to spot in an upright figure but hard to spot in an upside-down figure, which indicates that whole-body orientation is applied very early in processing.

## Language and action share machinery

The same cognitive resources appear to handle biological motion and language. In one study, reaction times for judging whether a written word was a real verb correlated strongly with reaction times for judging whether a PLD showed a coherent action (r = 0.56), while the correlation between nouns and PLD actions was much weaker (r = 0.31). Verbs and actions are processed similarly because they describe the same kind of event, another prediction of embodied cognition.

## History

Swedish psychologist **Gunnar Johansson** documented the phenomenon in 1973, inventing point-light displays and showing observers could recognise actions from joint lights alone. The field expanded again after the 1996 discovery of mirror neurons in the premotor cortex, which gave a neural mechanism for the link between seeing and doing. Modern PLD setups use motion-capture cameras to build 3D representations of movement, but the core finding is unchanged. Today the research feeds into social and affective neuroscience, language studies, motion-capture technology, and the design of artificial agents, where the **uncanny valley** reflects how sensitive the biological-motion system is to small deviations from natural human movement.

## Lesions and what goes wrong

Studies of stroke patients show that damage to the superior temporal sulcus or premotor cortex impairs biological motion perception, with the cerebellum also contributing. A patient with developmental visual agnosia who could not recognise the shape of objects from motion could still recognise a biological figure from PLDs, indicating that the biological-motion pathway is partly independent of the ventral form-vision pathway. The brain treats "moving creature" as a special category: recognised from minimal cues, shaped by the observer's own body and experience, and built from a network that overlaps with the systems for imitation, language, and empathy.
