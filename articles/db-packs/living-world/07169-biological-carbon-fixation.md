# Biological carbon fixation

Biological carbon fixation is the process by which living organisms convert inorganic carbon, primarily carbon dioxide (CO₂), into organic compounds used to store energy and build biomolecules. It is the main route by which atmospheric CO₂ enters the biosphere and is incorporated into living biomass.

## Autotrophs and heterotrophs

Organisms that fix their own carbon are autotrophs. Photoautotrophs (plants, algae, cyanobacteria) use sunlight; lithoautotrophs use energy from inorganic chemical reactions (chemosynthesis). Heterotrophs, including animals and fungi, cannot fix CO₂ and instead grow by consuming the organic carbon made by autotrophs or by other heterotrophs.

## Global scale

Roughly 250 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide are converted by photosynthesis each year, split roughly evenly between the oceans and terrestrial ecosystems, with most land fixation in the tropics. About 40% of this gross fixation is released by respiration, so the net amount retained is smaller. Photosynthesis accounts for nearly all fixation; chemosynthesis contributes a small share, mainly in dark habitats such as hydrothermal vents.

## The seven known pathways

Seven natural autotrophic carbon fixation pathways are known. The Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle (Calvin cycle) accounts for about 90% of biological carbon fixation and dominates on land, in the ocean, and in cyanobacteria. The other six matter because they allow fixation where the Calvin cycle cannot operate, including anaerobic, high-temperature, and dark habitats:

- Reverse Krebs cycle (rTCA), in anaerobic and microaerobic bacteria and archaea, including hydrothermal vent communities
- Reductive acetyl-CoA (Wood–Ljungdahl) pathway, in acetogenic bacteria and methanogenic archaea
- 3-Hydroxypropionate (3-HP) bicycle, in green non-sulfur bacteria such as *Chloroflexus*
- 3-HP/4-hydroxybutyrate (3-HP/4-HB) cycle, in aerobic thermoacidophilic archaea
- Dicarboxylate/4-hydroxybutyrate (DC/4-HB) cycle, in anaerobic hyperthermophilic archaea
- Reductive glycine pathway, in certain bacteria

## The Calvin cycle in detail

The Calvin cycle uses ATP and NADPH to convert CO₂ into triose phosphate, a three-carbon sugar (glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate and dihydroxyacetone phosphate). The balanced reaction for one triose phosphate is:

3 CO₂ + 6 NADPH + 6 H⁺ + 9 ATP + 5 H₂O → triose phosphate + 6 NADP⁺ + 9 ADP + 8 Pᵢ

RuBisCO, the carboxylase (CO₂-attaching enzyme) that captures CO₂, preferentially binds the lighter isotope carbon-12 over carbon-13. This carbon isotope discrimination leaves a measurable ¹²C/¹³C signature in plant tissue, used to assess plant water-use efficiency and to trace carbon sources in global carbon cycle studies.

## The reverse Krebs cycle

The reverse Krebs cycle, discovered in 1966 in the green sulfur bacterium *Chlorobium limicola*, runs the standard citric acid cycle in reverse and builds acetyl-CoA from two CO₂ molecules. It is used by bacteria such as *Aquificales* and by archaea at hydrothermal vents, and supports primary production in the ocean's aphotic (lightless) zones. Some organisms, including *Gammaproteobacteria* and the tube worm symbiont *Riftia pachyptila*, switch between the Calvin cycle and rTCA in response to conditions such as H₂S concentration.

## The reductive acetyl-CoA pathway

Also called the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway, this route uses CO₂ as both electron acceptor and carbon source, and H₂ as the electron donor, to make acetyl-CoA. It occurs in acetogenic bacteria (especially *Clostridia*), in methanogenic archaea, and in anaerobic chemolithoautotrophs such as sulfate reducers. Hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis, which accounts for about 80% of global methane production, runs on this pathway. A key oxygen-sensitive enzyme, carbon monoxide dehydrogenase/acetyl-CoA synthase, reduces CO₂ to CO and assembles acetyl-CoA. The pathway is among the most ATP-efficient, requiring only one ATP per pyruvate, which suits energy-limited anaerobic chemolithoautotrophs.

## The 3-hydroxypropionate bicycle

Discovered in 1989 in *Chloroflexus aurantiacus*, the 3-HP bicycle runs two linked cycles that together fix three bicarbonate molecules. The first cycle produces glyoxylate; the second combines glyoxylate with propionyl-CoA and ultimately releases pyruvate while regenerating acetyl-CoA. The cycle uses 19 reactions catalysed by 13 multifunctional enzymes and is ATP-expensive (7 ATP per pyruvate, plus 3 more for the resulting triose phosphate). Its flexibility allows co-assimilation of organic substrates, which suits mixotrophic organisms. Variants of this cycle (3-HP/4-HB and DC/4-HB) extend fixation into archaea; in all 3-HP-type pathways, CO₂ fixation is catalysed by enoyl-CoA carboxylases/reductases.

## Non-autotrophic CO₂ use

Heterotrophs cannot grow on CO₂ alone, but several still incorporate it. Pyruvate carboxylase uses bicarbonate during gluconeogenesis, and various anaplerotic reactions consume CO₂. In *Escherichia coli* under high CO₂, 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase carboxylates ribulose 5-phosphate to 6-phosphogluconate. A latent reductive glycine pathway has also been activated experimentally in *E. coli*.

## Carbon fixation in soils

Soil bacteria and fungi fix and sequester carbon as they decompose organic matter. They incorporate carbon into microbial biomass and produce extracellular polymers and enzymes that bind soil particles into aggregates, protecting organic carbon from further decomposition. These aggregates accumulate over centuries to millennia as soil organic matter, a long-term carbon reservoir that improves soil fertility, water retention, and nutrient cycling while offsetting atmospheric CO₂.
