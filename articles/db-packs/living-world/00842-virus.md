# Virus

A virus is a submicroscopic infectious agent that replicates only inside the living cells of an organism. It infects animals, plants, bacteria, and archaea and is the most numerous biological entity on Earth. Virology, the study of viruses, is a subspecialty of microbiology. The first virus identified was tobacco mosaic virus in the 1890s, when filtered plant sap remained infectious.

## Structure

A complete virus particle is a **virion**: genetic material (DNA or RNA) inside a protein shell, the **capsid**, built from repeating subunits called capsomeres. Some viruses add a lipid envelope borrowed from the host membrane. Five common shapes occur: **helical** rods (tobacco mosaic virus), **icosahedral** 20-sided balls (most animal viruses), **prolate** elongated heads (bacteriophages), **enveloped** particles (influenza, HIV, SARS-CoV-2), and **complex** forms with extra structures like the syringe-like tail of T4 phage.

Viral genomes are more structurally diverse than those of any cellular group: DNA or RNA, circular or linear, single- or double-stranded, positive- or negative-sense, and sometimes segmented. Circoviruses carry about 2 kilobases and two proteins; pandoraviruses reach about 2 megabases and 2,500 proteins. RNA virus genomes are smaller because replication errors accumulate faster, and segmentation spreads mutational risk across separate molecules.

## Origin

Viruses probably predate the split of life into three domains, but they leave no fossils, so the question is open. Three hypotheses dominate. The **regressive hypothesis** holds that viruses descended from small parasitic cells that shed unneeded genes, paralleled today by intracellular parasites like rickettsia and chlamydia. The **escape hypothesis** holds that they arose from mobile genetic elements (plasmids, transposons) that escaped cellular control. The **co-evolution hypothesis** holds that they arose from complex protein–nucleic acid assemblies at the same time as the first cells. None is proven, and viruses likely arose more than once.

## Replication

A virus cannot reproduce on its own. It hijacks a host cell through six stages:

1. **Attachment**: capsid proteins bind specific host receptors (HIV's gp120 binds CD4).
2. **Penetration**: entry by endocytosis, membrane fusion, injection through a bacterial wall, or movement through plant plasmodesmata.
3. **Uncoating**: the capsid is removed, freeing the genome.
4. **Replication**: the genome is copied and viral proteins are made. DNA viruses usually work in the nucleus using host enzymes; RNA viruses work in the cytoplasm using their own replicases; retroviruses use reverse transcriptase (an enzyme that writes DNA from RNA) and insert a DNA copy, a **provirus**, into the host chromosome.
5. **Assembly**: new virions self-assemble.
6. **Release**: exit by lysing the cell, budding through the membrane (enveloped viruses), or, for some bacteriophages, integrating as a dormant **prophage**.

## Genetic change

Viruses evolve continuously. **Antigenic drift** is the slow accumulation of point mutations; **antigenic shift** is the sudden mixing of whole genes when two segmented strains coinfect a cell, sometimes called "viral sex." Influenza A pandemics, including 1918, arose from reassortment. RNA viruses exist as **quasispecies**, swarms of related genomes pruned by selection. Coronaviruses recombine by template switching during replication, a likely adaptation to genome damage.

## Classification

The International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV), founded in 1966, groups viruses into a 15-rank hierarchy from realm to species, with about 16,215 species recognised as of 2024. The **Baltimore classification** is a parallel system, devised by David Baltimore, that sorts viruses by how they produce messenger RNA into seven groups: I dsDNA (adenoviruses, herpesviruses), II ssDNA (parvoviruses), III dsRNA (reoviruses), IV positive-sense ssRNA (coronaviruses, picornaviruses), V negative-sense ssRNA (influenza, rabies), VI ssRNA reverse-transcribing (retroviruses), and VII dsDNA reverse-transcribing (hepatitis B).

## Disease and immunity

Damage usually comes from cell lysis, but some viruses persist harmlessly (latent herpes) or chronically (hepatitis B and C carriers). Accepted human cancer viruses include certain HPV genotypes, hepatitis B and C, Epstein–Barr virus, Kaposi's sarcoma-associated herpesvirus, human T-lymphotropic virus, and Merkel cell polyomavirus. Innate defence includes RNA interference, a dicer/RISC pathway that cuts viral RNA. Adaptive defence uses antibodies (IgM marks acute infection, IgG marks past exposure) and T cells that kill infected cells. HIV evades immunity through rapid mutation; neurotropic viruses hide in immune-privileged neural tissue. Prevention depends on vaccines (live-attenuated, killed, subunit, or RNA; smallpox was eradicated in 1980) and about 100 antiviral drugs, mostly nucleoside analogues that cause chain termination or protease inhibitors that block viral protein processing.

## Ecology

In seawater, bacteriophages reach 250 million per millilitre, kill about 20% of microbial biomass daily, and recycle nutrients through the "viral shunt." Bacteria defend with restriction enzymes (cutting foreign DNA at specific sequences) and CRISPR–Cas acquired immunity; archaea use related systems. Plants defend with R-gene-triggered localised cell death, RNA interference, and salicylic acid signalling. Plant viruses, often spread by insect vectors, can cut yields sharply; potato virus Y can reduce potato yields by 80%.

## Applications and risks

Viruses deliver genes in research and gene therapy, manufacture proteins, treat bacterial infections through phage therapy, and destroy tumours as oncolytic viruses (T-VEC was approved for melanoma in 2015). Cowpea mosaic virus serves as a nanoscale scaffold, and synthetic viral genomes have been built from scratch since 2002. Smallpox stocks exist only at two WHO-authorised centres, VECTOR in Russia and the CDC in the United States, raising bioweapon concerns because modern populations lack residual immunity. Whether viruses are alive, given that they carry genes, evolve, and reproduce yet lack metabolism and cellular structure, is an open question; they are usually described as "organisms at the edge of life."
