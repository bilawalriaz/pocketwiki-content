# Biological organisation

Life is organised as a hierarchy of nested levels, from atoms to the biosphere. Each level is built from the one below, and each level shows properties the lower levels alone do not possess. This hierarchy plus emergence is what makes biology researchable and is why a change at the smallest scale can ripple upward to reshape entire ecosystems.

## The core idea: hierarchy plus emergence

Biological organisation arranges life into a sequence of levels in which each object is primarily composed of the previous level's basic unit. Atoms form molecules, molecules form cells, cells form tissues, and so on up to the biosphere. The scheme is reductionist: to understand a level, break it into its components and study how they fit together.

The deeper principle is emergence. New functions appear at each level that the lower-level components cannot perform alone. A cell is alive in ways that no individual molecule is; a brain produces cognition that no single neuron produces. These new roles are emergent properties, and they are why biology cannot be reduced to chemistry or physics alone.

## The standard ladder of life

| Level | Basic unit | Definition / example |
|---|---|---|
| Acellular | Atom | Simplest unit, e.g. oxygen |
| Acellular | Molecule | Two or more atoms, e.g. carbon dioxide |
| Acellular | Macromolecule | Large molecule, e.g. a phospholipid |
| Sub-cellular | Organelle | Functional group of biomolecules inside a cell |
| Cellular | Cell | Basic unit of all life, e.g. a club cell |
| Super-cellular | Tissue | Functional group of cells, e.g. epithelial tissue |
| Super-cellular | Organ | Functional group of tissues, e.g. a lung |
| Super-cellular | Organ system | Functional group of organs, e.g. the respiratory system |
| Ecological | Organism | A living system, e.g. a lion |
| Ecological | Population | Same species in one area, e.g. a pride of lions |
| Ecological | Guild | Different species sharing one ecological role, e.g. herbivores |
| Ecological | Community (biocoenosis) | All guilds interacting in one place, e.g. lions and zebras |
| Ecological | Ecosystem | Communities plus the abiotic environment, e.g. a savanna |
| Ecological | Biome | Continentally contiguous ecosystems with similar climate |
| Ecological | Biosphere (ecosphere) | All life on Earth, with or without the abiotic environment |

A community includes organisms across all biological domains interacting in a specific location, while a guild is a subset of a community (species performing the same ecological function, such as all herbivores). A biome groups ecosystems by climate and geography, not by the species present.

Not every organism reaches every level. An organism cannot be organised at the tissue (histological) level if it is not composed of tissues, so the ladder is a general template, not a universal one. More complex schemes add finer intermediate levels: a molecule as a grouping of elements, an atom as a grouping of subatomic particles, a genome as a hierarchy of genes. Each level can also be described by its own components, so an organism can be analysed at the atomic, molecular, cellular, tissue, organ, or organ-system scale.

## Why the hierarchy matters in practice

Because the levels are nested, a perturbation at a low level can propagate upward. DDT acts at the subcellular level as an insecticide, yet its effects reach multiple ecosystems. In principle, a single altered atom could change the entire biosphere. The hierarchy is the fundamental premise for medical and physiological research: cognitive and behavioural neuroscience depend on the brain being built from specific cell types, and pharmacology rests on the fact that a change at the cellular level can alter the whole organism. Without this layered structure, linking chemistry and physics to disease and physiology would be far harder, perhaps impossible.

## How organisation emerged

Biological organisation is thought to have arisen in the early RNA world, when RNA chains began to satisfy the conditions for Darwinian natural selection: heritability, variation, and competition for limited resources. An RNA replicator's fitness (its per capita rate of increase) depended on three adaptive capacities encoded in its nucleotide sequence: the capacity to replicate with moderate fidelity (yielding heritability and variation), the capacity to avoid decay, and the capacity to acquire and process resources. These capacities were set by the folded configuration of the RNA, and competitive success depended on their relative values. Among later organisms, competitive success at each successive level has, in a broad sense, continued to depend on the same three capacities.

## Why hierarchies are stable

Hierarchical structure is empirically widespread in biology, and on theoretical grounds expected: complex systems that evolve from simpler ones tend to become hierarchies. The theoretical underpinning comes from thermodynamics. Modelled as physical systems, biological systems are open, self-organising thermodynamic systems whose dissipative structures form set/subset relations that can be described as a hierarchy. System-hierarchy analysis in the 1950s laid the empirical groundwork for what became, from the 1980s, the field of hierarchical ecology. Herbert Simon's hierarchical principle states that hierarchical structures are stable, so complexity built from stable, nearly decomposable sub-systems is far more likely to evolve than complexity built from tangled, interdependent parts.
