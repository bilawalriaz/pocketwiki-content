# Animal

Animals are multicellular, eukaryotic organisms in the kingdom Animalia. They consume organic material, breathe oxygen, possess muscle cells, develop from a hollow sphere of embryonic cells called the blastula, and—with few exceptions—can move. Together they form a clade, a group descended from a single common ancestor. Over 1.5 million living species are formally described, with the true total estimated near 7.77 million. The scientific study of animals is zoology; the study of animal behaviour is ethology.

## Origins and Timeline

The animal clade dates to roughly 800–650 million years ago (Mya) in the Cryogenian period, with about 6,331 shared gene groups tracing to a common ancestor near 650 Mya. The sponge-produced lipid marker 24-isopropylcholestane (24-ipc) appears in rocks of that age, the earliest chemical evidence. Body fossils first appear in the Ediacaran, including *Charnia*, *Spriggina*, and *Dickinsonia* (the latter confirmed as animal when its cholesterol was identified). The "Avalon explosion" marks this diversification, and *Auroralumina attenboroughii* (557–562 Mya) is the earliest known crown-group cnidarian and one of the earliest predators.

The Cambrian explosion began about 539 Mya, producing nearly all modern animal phyla as marine forms. By 510–471 Mya, arthropods colonised land alongside early land plants; the Ordovician radiation near 485.4 Mya saw most modern classes appear; lobe-finned fish such as *Tiktaalik* moved onto land about 375 Mya.

Some palaeontologists argue for earlier origins, citing possible sponges in the 665-Mya Trezona Formation and disputed burrow-like tubes up to 1.7 billion years old, though Tonian trace fossils may instead come from the protist *Gromia sphaerica*. The Cambrian explosion's apparent suddenness may also be a fossil-record artefact, supported by pre-Cambrian *Auroralumina*.

## Body Plan and Development

Animals lack cell walls, distinguishing them from plants, algae, and fungi. Their cells sit in an extracellular matrix of collagen and elastic glycoproteins, a flexible framework that permits cell movement and tissue differentiation, and can calcify into shells, bones, or spicules.

Most animals reproduce sexually: meiosis produces haploid gametes (motile sperm and larger non-motile ova), which fuse into zygotes. These develop through mitosis into a blastula, then invaginate into a gastrula with ectoderm and endoderm layers, usually plus a mesoderm between them. In Bilateria and Cnidaria, Hox genes control when and where body structures form. Some species reproduce asexually by fragmentation, budding (as in *Hydra*), or parthenogenesis (aphids). Repeated close-relative mating causes inbreeding depression; animals have evolved mechanisms to avoid it.

## Phylogeny

Animals are monophyletic and sister to choanoflagellates, together forming Choanozoa. Five major clades exist: Porifera (sponges), Ctenophora (comb jellies), Placozoa, Cnidaria, and Bilateria. Whether sponges or comb jellies branched first remains debated; molecular studies have supported both arrangements.

Sponges lack true tissues, nerve cells, and organs, relying on water flow for feeding and gas exchange; most adults are sessile hermaphrodites. Comb jellies are radially symmetric predators with unique colloblasts (adhesive cells for capturing prey) and a nerve net that evolved independently of other animals'. Cnidaria (jellyfish, corals, anemones) are radially symmetric with single-opening digestive chambers. Placozoans are tiny, asymmetric, and poorly placed phylogenetically.

Bilateria—around 29 phyla and over a million species—are triploblastic, bilaterally symmetric, and cephalised (with sense organs concentrated at the head). They split into protostomes (mouth-first development; arthropods, molluscs, annelids, nematodes) and deuterostomes (anus-first; echinoderms, chordates). The position of Xenacoelomorpha is uncertain. Most bilaterians have a body cavity (coelom, a fluid-filled cavity between gut and body wall, or pseudocoelom) and show opposing circular and longitudinal muscles that enable peristalsis (wave-like muscle contractions moving food through the gut).

## Diversity and Ecology

The blue whale is the largest animal ever (up to 190 tonnes, 33.6 m). The African bush elephant is the largest living land animal (12.25 tonnes, 10.67 m); extinct titanosaurs like *Argentinosaurus* (~73 tonnes) were the largest land animals ever. At the other extreme, some Myxozoa never exceed 20 μm, with *Myxobolus szekeli* at 8.5 μm.

Animals are grouped ecologically by trophic level: carnivores, herbivores, omnivores, fungivores, scavengers, detritivores, and parasites. Predator–prey interactions drive evolutionary arms races and coevolution. Most animals depend on plant or phytoplankton biomass; some near hydrothermal vents rely on chemosynthetic archaea and bacteria. Of Earth's 550 gigatonnes of carbon biomass, animals contribute just 2 gigatonnes. Few species survive constant temperatures above 50 °C or Antarctica's cold deserts.

## History of Classification

Aristotle divided animals by blood presence; Linnaeus's 1758 *Systema Naturae* created the first hierarchy (Vermes, Insecta, Pisces, Amphibia, Aves, Mammalia). Lamarck expanded it to 14 phyla by 1809. Cuvier's 1817 *Le Règne Animal* used comparative anatomy to define four embranchements. In 1874 Haeckel coined Metazoa for multicellular animals, now synonymous with Animalia. Modern classification relies on molecular phylogenetics.

## Human Uses

Humans exploit animals for food, materials (wool, silk, leather, fur), transport, hunting, and science—*Drosophila melanogaster* is a key model organism, and trabectedin is a derived medicine. Dogs are the most common pets. Humans and livestock account for over 90% of terrestrial vertebrate biomass. Animals also appear in zodiacs, cave art (Lascaux), painting, mythology, heraldry, and sports.
