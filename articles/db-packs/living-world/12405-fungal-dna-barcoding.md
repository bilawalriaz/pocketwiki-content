# Fungal DNA barcoding

Fungal DNA barcoding identifies species of fungi by sequencing short, standardised DNA regions from a sample and matching them against reference databases such as the ISHAM database or the Barcode of Life Data System (BOLD). The method relies on "universal" genes that are ideally present in all fungi, whose sequences differ more between species than within one species. Mycologists began using DNA sequences to distinguish fungal species at least ten years before Paul D. N. Hebert popularised the term "DNA barcoding" for animals in 2003.

The approach solves a foundational problem: many fungi have separate asexual (anamorphic) and sexual (teleomorphic) stages that look completely different, and species often encompass multiple strains described as different species, producing long synonym lists. DNA barcoding links these disconnected forms and collapses redundant names. Its success depends on the reference database being both taxonomically broad and reliably identified to species level; a match to a sequence labelled only at phylum level is uninformative. Equally important are voucher specimens: the physical samples from which barcode sequences were generated, labelled and deposited in herbaria (public collections of preserved specimens) so that any disputed identification can be re-examined.

## The primary barcode: ITS

The Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) region of the nuclear ribosomal DNA tandem repeat is the official primary fungal barcode. The ribosomal repeat contains the genes for the 18S, 5.8S, and 28S ribosomal RNA subunits, separated by two spacer regions, ITS1 and ITS2, with the 5.8S subunit nested between them. ITS is roughly 600 base pairs long. Because it sits between highly conserved flanking genes, "universal" primers (short synthetic DNA sequences that start PCR copying of a target region) can anchor PCR amplification across virtually all fungi. The most widely used pair, ITS1 and ITS4, was proposed by White et al. in 1990 and remains standard; primers ITS2 and ITS3 attach internally within the ITS region.

ITS works well chiefly because the ribosomal cluster is arranged in tandem repeats, giving many copies per genome. This lets Sanger sequencing (the classical method that reads DNA one base at a time) succeed even from tiny or degraded samples. PCR success ranges from 65% in non-Dikarya fungi (early-branching lineages outside the two dominant phyla) to 100% in Saccharomycotina and most Basidiomycota, though Pucciniomycotina (rust fungi) are an exception. Limitations are significant. Universal primers fail on about 10% of specimens. Because ITS is non-coding, it accumulates insertions and deletions (indels) that prevent reliable alignment (position-by-position comparison) of highly divergent species. Tandem repeats also cause intragenomic heterogeneity: a single individual can carry ITS copies that differ from each other, producing superimposed chromatograms (read-out traces from Sanger sequencing) that become unreadable. Cloning PCR products before sequencing resolves this and is standard for environmental metabarcoding (mass-sequencing DNA from a mixed sample to catalogue species), but most reference-library ITS sequences were generated without cloning, so databases may underestimate true intraspecific variation.

The weighted mean intraspecific ITS variability across fungi is 2.51%, but the range is extreme: 0% in *Serpula lacrymans*, 0.19% in *Tuber melanosporum*, 15.72% in *Rhizoctonia solani*, and 24.75% in *Pisolithus tinctorius*. The canonical 3% threshold for splitting species therefore overestimates species counts in highly variable taxa, while a stricter 2.5% threshold identifies only about 75% of medically relevant species correctly. Conversely, young species complexes or sibling species may differ, if at all, by only a few nucleotides and be misidentified or lumped together. In genera such as *Aspergillus*, *Cladosporium*, *Fusarium*, and *Penicillium*, ITS alone is insufficient. A universal intraspecific-versus-interspecific threshold for ITS has proven impossible to define, yet ITS retains high discrimination power in Dikarya, especially Basidiomycota, where even ITS1 alone often suffices.

## Secondary barcodes

Because ITS alone cannot resolve every fungal group, additional markers are used alongside it. The translational elongation factor 1α gene (TEF1α), which encodes a protein that delivers amino acids during protein synthesis, is the accepted secondary barcode. Stielow et al. (2015) identified it as the most promising supplementary marker because, despite an overall slow mutation rate, it contains regions of higher variation. A TEF1α reference database was merged with the ISHAM-ITS database. TEF1α resolved a new *Cantharellus* species from Texas and distinguishes species in many genera, though it fails to separate all species in *Ochroconis* and *Verruconis*.

The D1/D2 domain of the large ribosomal subunit (28S) is roughly 600 base pairs of coding sequence within the same tandem repeat as ITS, and is the standard marker for yeasts, where combined D1/D2 and ITS analysis became required for describing new asco- and basidiomycetous yeast taxa. The largest subunit of RNA polymerase II, RPB1, outperforms ITS in the species-rich Pezizomycotina (a subdivision of Ascomycota), with PCR success of 70–80% in Ascomycota but only 14% in early-diverging lineages; combining RPB1 with either ITS or 28S yields the highest identification rates. Other markers, including the intergenic spacer (IGS) between ribosomal repeats, β-tubulin, topoisomerase I (TOP1), and phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK), are deployed for specific genera or when ITS performs poorly.

The mitochondrial COI gene, the standard animal barcode, generally performs poorly in fungi: introns (non-coding sequences within a gene) make it hard to sequence (the *Agaricus bisporus* COI gene reaches 29,902 nucleotides because of 19 introns), paralogues (duplicate gene copies that evolved separately) exist in *Fusarium*, and discrimination power is low in *Aspergillus niger* and rust fungi.

## Applications

In food safety, ITS barcoding of dried mushrooms sold commercially in one study showed all samples correctly labelled, though GenBank and UNITE sometimes returned different identifications. A separate survey of mushroom products, mycelium powders, and dietary supplements found that only 30% carried the correct binomial (two-part genus-plus-species) name, another 30% had the right genus but wrong species, 15% had the wrong genus, and 25% yielded no ITS sequence. ITS also reliably distinguishes the valuable caterpillar fungus *Ophiocordyceps sinensis* from its counterfeits. In clinical mycology, dual ITS plus TEF1α barcoding identifies species in *Diutina* and *Pichia* straightforwardly, and TEF1α alone resolves the *Candida parapsilosis* complex and *Scedosporium* species that ITS cannot separate. Fungal DNA barcoding has also been applied to cultural heritage: ITS sequencing of foxing (reddish-brown spots that disfigure old paper) identified *Chaetomium*, *Eurotium*, *Penicillium*, and *Stachybotrys* species, and surveys of historic stone and canvas paintings have catalogued dozens of biodeteriorating fungi in sites including the Old Cathedral of Coimbra, part of the University of Coimbra, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
