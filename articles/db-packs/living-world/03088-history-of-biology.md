# History of biology

Biology began as scattered practical knowledge, mostly medicine and agriculture, and only gradually became a unified science. Its history is shaped by a few conceptual revolutions (cell theory, evolution by natural selection, the molecular basis of inheritance) and by tools that exposed what the naked eye could not see, from the microscope to DNA sequencing.

## Ancient and medieval roots

Early biology was local and utilitarian. Egyptian papyri from around 1600 BCE record surgery and pharmacology, while Mesopotamians studied animal anatomy mainly for divination. In China, Taoist alchemy and yin-yang theory offered an independent framework, and in India the medical system known as *Ayurveda* (from around 1500 BCE) classified organisms and developed surgery.

Greek natural philosophy became the dominant Western framework. Aristotle empirically observed and classified around 540 animal species, and his *scala naturae* (Great Chain of Being) imposed a hierarchical view of nature ordered by purpose, a stance called *teleology*. This purpose-driven outlook persisted for centuries. The physician Galen's authority remained unchallenged in Europe until the Renaissance, while Islamic scholars such as Avicenna preserved and extended Greek texts.

## Renaissance to Enlightenment: seeing and ordering

Empiricism revived with anatomy. Andreas Vesalius's 1543 *De humani corporis fabrica* replaced Galenic claims with direct human dissection. Microscopy then opened a hidden world. Robert Hooke published *Micrographia* in 1665, naming the "cell" from the boxlike chambers he saw in cork. Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, working in the 1670s and 1680s, built better lenses and observed bacteria, protozoa, and spermatozoa, the first direct sightings of microorganisms.

The seventeenth and eighteenth centuries also tried to impose order on nature. Carl Linnaeus's 1735 taxonomy introduced binomial nomenclature, a two-part naming system giving every species a genus and a species name. Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon, suggested that species could change over time. At the same time, the metaphor of "nature as machine" began displacing the older "nature as organism," weakening teleology in favour of physical explanations.

## The nineteenth century: unification, cells, and evolution

Biology emerged as a single field around 1800, when the term itself came into use. Three strands converged.

Evolution and deep time. Alexander von Humboldt linked organisms to their environments, Georges Cuvier established extinction through comparative anatomy, and Charles Lyell's uniformitarian geology, the view that the same slow processes operating today shaped the Earth's past, supplied the vast timescales Darwin needed. In 1859 Charles Darwin published *On the Origin of Species*, independently arrived at by Alfred Russel Wallace, proposing evolution by natural selection: organisms with favourable inherited traits leave more offspring, so populations change over time.

Cell theory and physiology. Matthias Schleiden and Theodor Schwann proposed in 1838–39 that the cell is the basic unit of life, and Rudolf Virchow added in the 1860s that all cells come from pre-existing cells. Microscopy revealed nuclei and other internal structures. Louis Pasteur's experiments disproved spontaneous generation, the old idea that living things can arise from non-living matter, leading to germ theory (formalised by Robert Koch): specific microorganisms cause specific diseases. Meanwhile, Friedrich Wöhler's 1828 synthesis of urea showed that compounds made by living organisms could be produced by ordinary chemistry, undermining *vitalism*, the belief that life required a special non-physical force.

## The twentieth century: genes, molecules, and synthesis

Gregor Mendel published his laws of inheritance in 1866, but they were ignored until 1900, when Carl Correns and others rediscovered them and genetics was born. Thomas Hunt Morgan's work on fruit flies in the 1910s linked Mendel's abstract "factors" to physical chromosomes, the threadlike structures inside cells that carry inheritance. The "modern synthesis" of the 1930s, developed by R. A. Fisher, J. B. S. Haldane, and Sewall Wright, showed that Mendelian inheritance and Darwinian selection are mathematically compatible, so small genetic changes in populations can drive large evolutionary ones.

Molecular biology then took over. James Watson and Francis Crick's 1953 double-helix model of DNA revealed how genetic information is stored and copied, since each strand can serve as a template for a new partner. The "central dogma" (DNA is transcribed into RNA, which is translated into protein) and the cracking of the genetic code between 1961 and 1966 explained how sequences of nucleotides specify proteins. In 1972 Paul Berg performed the first recombinant DNA experiment, splicing genes from different organisms in the lab. The Human Genome Project culminated in a working draft announced in 2000, giving biology its first complete readout of a human genome. Ecology became quantitative in the 1950s through work by Eugene Odum, and evolutionary theory expanded with ideas such as punctuated equilibrium and Motoo Kimura's neutral theory of molecular evolution, which holds that most genetic changes are neutral and spread by random drift rather than natural selection.

## Twenty-first century: integration

The boundary between molecular and organismal biology has largely dissolved. Organismal researchers now use molecular tools, while molecular researchers study how genes interact with environment. Genomics, proteomics (large-scale study of proteins), bioinformatics, synthetic biology, and astrobiology have all emerged, driven by cheaper sequencing, better imaging, and large-scale computation. Recombinant DNA technology, first demonstrated in 1972, now underpins mRNA vaccines, gene therapies, and engineered microbes that produce pharmaceuticals and biofuels.
