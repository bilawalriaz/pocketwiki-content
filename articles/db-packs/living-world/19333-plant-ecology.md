# Plant ecology

Plant ecology is the subdiscipline of ecology that studies where plants occur, how many there are, and why environmental factors and interactions with other organisms shape those patterns. A defining feature of plants is photosynthesis, the leaf-based process that converts carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen. Around 2 billion years ago, photosynthesising organisms began releasing oxygen at scale, creating Earth's oxygenated atmosphere. The geological signature is banded iron formations, sedimentary rocks rich in iron oxide. At the same time plants began removing carbon dioxide, starting the long climatic trend of rising oxygen and falling carbon dioxide.

## Why plants need their own ecology

Plant ecology differs from animal ecology for two reasons. First, most plants are rooted in the soil, so measuring nutrient uptake and species interactions requires below-ground methods. Second, plants often reproduce vegetatively, spreading asexually as connected ramets (modules of a single genetic individual, a genet), so the very concept of an individual is doubtful; some ecologists treat a tree as a large collection of linked meristems, the growth tissues that produce new cells. This has divided the field: some researchers apply animal-style population models to plants, while others argue plants need multiple perspectives chosen for the question, scale, and situation.

## Distribution and biomes

Plant distributions are governed by three combined filters: history, physiology, and biotic interactions. A species can only occur where it has evolved or dispersed to and has not gone locally extinct. The pool is further narrowed to species whose physiological adaptations match local conditions, and reshaped by interactions with other species.

At the global scale, plant communities group into biomes defined by the form of the dominant plant, such as grasses in grasslands or trees in forests. Biomes are set mainly by regional climate, especially temperature and precipitation, and follow broad latitudinal trends, shifting with elevation because high-elevation environments resemble those at higher latitudes. Within biomes, local communities are further shaped by soils, hydrology, and disturbance regime.

## Biological interactions

Plants draw on a small set of major elements, carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulphur (CHNOPS), plus micronutrients such as magnesium and sodium. When plants grow close together they deplete these shared resources, producing competition.

Competition runs from symmetric, where every individual gets the same share regardless of size, to size-asymmetric, where the largest take a disproportionate share. Most above-ground competition is size-asymmetric because taller plants intercept most light, and this asymmetry is thought to drive the evolutionary trend toward greater plant height. Light is usually the most contested resource, but on infertile or arid soils below-ground competition for water, nitrogen, or phosphorus can dominate; along natural soil-fertility gradients the ratio shifts, with above-ground competition stronger on fertile soils and below-ground competition stronger on poor ones. Weak competitors can persist by escaping in time, as buried seeds in the soil seed bank, or in space, by dispersing to sites with fewer strong competitors. In many natural ecosystems plants are co-limited by several resources at once, so competition studies usually infer the responsible resource indirectly from negative neighbour effects rather than measuring uptake directly.

The other major interaction types are mutualism, parasitism, commensalism, and herbivory. The most widespread plant mutualism is the mycorrhizal partnership with fungi, in which the fungus gains carbohydrates and the plant gains nutrients, mainly phosphate; fossil mycorrhizae appear on some of the earliest known land plants. Flowering plants added two further mutualisms: insect pollination, which originated when beetles fed on early flowers, and seed dispersal by fruit-eating animals. Plant-to-plant facilitation, where one species buffers another from stress, is more common in physically harsh environments than in benign ones, where competition dominates. Epiphytes, plants that grow on other plants without rooting in soil, such as mosses on deciduous trees, are classic commensals, benefiting from a host that is unaffected; nearly 10% of all vascular plant species are epiphytes, rising to 25% in tropical forests.

Parasitic plants attach to a host's xylem and phloem, the water- and sugar-conducting tissues, through a haustorium, drawing off water, nutrients, and carbon and reducing host growth and reproduction. Some parasitic plants are generalists that attack multiple hosts at once.

Herbivory is the consumption of plants by animals. Plants support the base of the food web by producing organic compounds for herbivores, and many plant traits, from thorns to chemical defences, reflect herbivore pressure. Large herbivores can remove selected species, open regeneration gaps, recycle nutrients, and disperse seeds, and in some ecosystems such as grasslands they shape the community as strongly as fire does. In extreme cases herbivores can nearly strip a site, such as geese in the Hudson Bay Lowlands of Canada or nutria in Louisiana marshes, but usually their impact is selective, especially when large predators keep their numbers in check. The standard method for measuring these effects is the exclosure experiment, fencing out herbivores and comparing fenced and unfenced plots over many years.

## Abundance, colonisation, and reproduction

The ecological success of a plant species can be measured by density, biomass, or percentage plant cover. Abundance changes in response to abiotic factors such as climate and biotic factors such as herbivory or interspecific competition. Whether a species occurs at a site depends on the balance between colonisation and local extinction: colonisation probability falls with distance from source habitats and rises with the source population's size, fecundity, and dispersal distance, while local extinction falls as abundance rises, both of living plants and of seeds in the soil seed bank.

Plants reproduce through parthenogenesis, producing genetically identical clones; cross-fertilisation, combining egg and sperm from different individuals to form a seed of embryo and nutritive endosperm; self-fertilisation, combining egg and sperm from the same self-compatible individual; and vegetative reproduction, the asexual spread of ramets described above. These varied modes, especially vegetative reproduction, shape the kinds of population models plant ecologists can usefully build.

## Origins

Plant ecology grew from early nineteenth-century plant geography. Carl Ludwig Willdenow noticed that similar climates produced similar vegetation on different continents, and his student Alexander von Humboldt used physiognomy, the classification of plants by growth form, to link vegetation types to environmental factors. Eugenius Warming's 1895 *Plantesamfund* synthesised plant morphology, physiology, taxonomy, and biogeography into the field of plant ecology, and Henry Chandler Cowles' 1899 studies of succession on Lake Michigan sand dunes, followed by Frederic Clements' 1916 monograph, introduced temporal change into the discipline. The first professional society, the British Ecological Society, was founded in 1913, with the Ecological Society of America following in 1917.
