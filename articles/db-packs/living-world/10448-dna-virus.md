# DNA virus

A DNA virus is a virus whose genome is made of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), replicated inside host cells by a DNA polymerase. The two main varieties are double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) viruses and single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) viruses. A separate, smaller group, the reverse-transcribing DNA viruses, copies its genome through an RNA intermediate using a reverse transcriptase.

DNA viruses are widespread, especially in the oceans, where they shape marine ecosystems. They infect both prokaryotes (bacteria and archaea) and eukaryotes (animals, plants, fungi). Their evolutionary origins are multiple: members of the realm Monodnaviria appear to have emerged independently several times from archaeal and bacterial plasmids, which are small, self-replicating rings of DNA separate from the host chromosome. The origins of the other realms are less clear, and some lineages may predate the last universal common ancestor (LUCA) of cellular life.

## Baltimore classification

The Baltimore classification sorts viruses by how they produce messenger RNA (mRNA), the molecule that carries a gene's instructions to the cell's protein-building machinery. It is often used alongside standard virus taxonomy, which is based on evolutionary history; viruses in the same Baltimore group typically share a replication strategy even when they are not closely related.

DNA viruses occupy two of the seven Baltimore groups: Group I, the dsDNA viruses with two strands in the usual double helix, and Group II, the ssDNA viruses with a single strand. Reverse-transcribing viruses, whose DNA genomes are copied via an RNA intermediate, form a third group sometimes discussed alongside DNA viruses.

## Double-stranded DNA viruses

All dsDNA viruses make mRNA the same way. A transcription preinitiation complex binds DNA upstream of a gene, a host RNA polymerase is recruited, reads the negative-sense strand as a template, and stops at a signal such as a polyadenylation site.

Four main mechanisms replicate dsDNA genomes. In bidirectional replication, two replication forks start at one origin and travel in opposite directions, the most common method. In rolling circle replication, a polymerase loops around a circular genome and peels off a linear copy. In strand displacement, a new strand is copied from a template and is then used as the template for its partner. In replicative transposition, a viral genome already inserted in host DNA is copied to a new site in the host genome.

dsDNA viruses split in two useful ways. By replication site, they are nuclear, relying heavily on host machinery, or cytoplasmic, carrying their own transcription and replication enzymes. By capsid lineage, they are the tailed viruses of the realm Duplodnaviria (tailed bacteriophages and herpesviruses) or the tailless viruses of the realm Varidnaviria.

## Single-stranded DNA viruses

Because the genome is single-stranded, it cannot be read directly. A host DNA polymerase first makes a complementary strand, producing a temporary double-stranded form, from which mRNA is then transcribed. The double-stranded intermediate can form either upon cell entry or as part of replication.

Eukaryotic ssDNA viruses replicate in the nucleus. Most have circular genomes and use rolling circle replication (RCR). An endonuclease nicks the positive strand, a DNA polymerase extends the 3' end using the negative strand as template, and as the polymerase loops around the circle it displaces the old positive strand. The endonuclease cuts again to release a new genome, which is ligated into a circle.

Parvoviruses are the main exception, with linear ssDNA genomes. They use rolling hairpin replication (RHR), in which hairpin loops at each end repeatedly unfold and refold to reverse the direction of synthesis, producing many genome copies from a single long molecule. Individual genomes are then excised by the viral endonuclease. Parvoviruses may package either strand, depending on the virus.

Most ssDNA viruses are positive-sense, meaning the strand can be read directly as mRNA. Exceptions: Anelloviridae has negative-sense circular genomes, and bidnaviruses package both positive and negative linear strands. The family Finnlakeviridae is unassigned to a realm but is a proposed member of Varidnaviria.

## ICTV realms

The International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) groups viruses at the highest rank, the realm. Unlike cellular domains, virus realms need not share a common ancestor, so each realm represents at least one independent origin of viruses. Within a realm, members are grouped by characteristics highly conserved over long evolutionary timescales.

Three realms contain DNA viruses.

**Duplodnaviria** contains dsDNA viruses whose major capsid protein has the HK97 fold, a protein shape first seen in bacteriophage HK97. Members share an icosahedral capsid and a terminase enzyme that packages DNA into it. The realm has two major groups: tailed bacteriophages of the order Caudovirales, infecting prokaryotes, and herpesviruses of the order Herpesvirales, infecting animals. Whether they share an ancestor, or herpesviruses are a divergent offshoot of tailed phages, is unresolved. A common trait is the ability to cause latent infections, long dormant periods in which the genome persists without producing new viruses but can later reactivate. The realm may predate LUCA.

**Monodnaviria** contains ssDNA viruses that encode an HUH-family endonuclease that initiates rolling circle replication, and all viruses descended from such viruses. The prototypical members, called CRESS-DNA viruses, have circular ssDNA genomes; linear ssDNA viruses descended from them, and the dsDNA class Papovaviricetes descended from linear ssDNA ancestors. The realm emerged multiple times from archaeal and bacterial plasmids, and the kingdom Shotokuvirae probably arose when plasmid DNA recombined with capsid genes from RNA viruses. Eukaryotic members cause major diseases: papillomaviruses and polyomaviruses cause many cancers, and geminiviruses damage staple crops.

**Varidnaviria** contains dsDNA viruses whose major capsid proteins carry a jelly roll fold oriented perpendicular to the capsid surface. Many members also share a minor capsid protein with a single jelly roll fold, an ATPase that powers genome packaging, and a common DNA polymerase. The realm has two kingdoms: Helvetiavirae, with a single vertical jelly roll fold, and Bamfordvirae, with two, likely derived by fusion of the single-fold version. The realm may be monophyletic or polyphyletic and may predate LUCA. Marine members are abundant and ecologically important, and most known eukaryotic DNA viruses belong here. Notable disease-causing members include adenoviruses, poxviruses such as Variola virus (the cause of smallpox), and African swine fever virus. Some varidnaviruses can become endogenized, permanently integrated into the host genome; virophages, which after infecting a host can protect it against giant viruses, are a striking example.

Many DNA viruses remain unassigned to any realm. dsDNA-only unassigned lineages include the order Ligamenvirales and families such as Baculoviridae, Plasmaviridae, and Polydnaviridae; ssDNA-only unassigned lineages include Anelloviridae and Spiraviridae.
