# Plant evolution

Plant evolution is the subset of evolutionary change that has produced the diversity of plants across geologic time. It is distinct from plant development, which follows the changes a single organism goes through during its life. Evolution is described statistically, using averages, medians, and distributions of traits in populations, and is depicted as a phylogeny, a branching tree whose branches represent clades, groups descended from a common ancestor.

## How plants differ from animals as evolving lineages

Plant cells are totipotent: most retain the capacity to rebuild a whole organism, making asexual reproduction routine. Plants also tolerate polyploidy, inheriting more than two chromosome sets. A failed meiosis can produce diploid gametes that fuse into a tetraploid zygote, an instant reproductive barrier and a burst of evolutionary change. Seed dormancy adds a third feature: long dormant phases let seed plants "sit out" harsh intervals, lowering extinction risk.

The combined effect shows in mass extinctions. Terrestrial animal families lost between 6% and 62% of their members during past events, but plant family loss was described as "negligible." What did change was ecosystem structure: species-level extinctions were highly selective. Insect-pollinated, specialised taxa were hit harder than wind-pollinated ones, and the survivors were usually generalists, rare before the event, poor competitors in good times, but ready to expand into the niches vacated when specialists died.

## Polyploidy as an engine of plant diversity

Polyploidy is pervasive. Estimates put 30–80% of living plant species as polyploid, and many lineages carry paleopolyploidy, ancient whole-genome duplication detectable in their genomes. Roughly 15% of angiosperm and 31% of fern speciation events are accompanied by a ploidy increase, and large angiosperm diversity explosions appear to coincide with ancient duplications shared by many species.

Newly formed polyploids usually show heterosis relative to their parents, plus morphological novelty that can drive speciation and the colonisation of new niches. Mechanisms behind that novelty include gene dosage effects from extra genome copies, the reunion of divergent regulatory networks from the parent species, chromosomal rearrangements, and epigenetic remodelling that alters expression. All eukaryotes probably passed through at least one polyploidy event, often inferable only by comparing sequenced genomes; confirmed examples include the thale cress *Arabidopsis thaliana* and rice (*Oryza sativa*).

## Photosynthesis and the origin of chloroplasts

Cyanobacteria were the principal primary producers through the Proterozoic Eon (2500–543 million years ago). Green algae joined them on continental shelves near the end of the Proterozoic, but marine primary production took its modern form only with the Mesozoic radiations (251–65 Ma) of dinoflagellates, coccolithophorids, and diatoms. Cyanobacteria still drive oceanic gyres and nitrogen fixation, and persist in modified form as the plastids of marine algae.

Chloroplasts retain several cyanobacterial features: a circular chromosome, prokaryote-style ribosomes, and similar photosynthetic reaction-centre proteins. The endosymbiotic theory holds that an early eukaryotic cell engulfed a photosynthetic bacterium, which became the chloroplast; chloroplast DNA is still separate from the plant nuclear DNA and its genes resemble cyanobacterial ones. The CoRR hypothesis proposes that keeping these genes inside the chloroplast is necessary because redox regulation of those proteins must occur where they are made.

## Regulatory and reproductive evolution

During the colonisation of land, many novel transcription-factor families emerged in plants and were wired into the networks controlling multicellular development, reproduction, and organ growth, producing the more complex morphology of land plants.

Flowers evolved as an adaptation that promotes cross-fertilisation. The benefit comes from genetic complementation: outcrossing masks deleterious recessive mutations in progeny, the same effect called hybrid vigour or heterosis. Once a lineage depends on cross-fertilisation, switching to inbreeding becomes disadvantageous because it unmasks those recessive mutations, producing inbreeding depression.
