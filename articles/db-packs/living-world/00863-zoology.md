# Zoology

Zoology is the branch of biology devoted to animals: their structure, classification, behavior, development, distribution, and ecological interactions.

Systematic zoology begins with Aristotle in the 4th century BC, who studied animal structure, development, and adaptation and divided animals into blooded vertebrates and bloodless invertebrates, a split that still organizes the field. Roman physician Galen advanced anatomy through animal dissection in the 2nd century AD because human dissection was prohibited, and his errors persisted for centuries because of those same taboos. Islamic scholars preserved and extended Greek knowledge through the medieval period, and the Renaissance revived direct observation through figures such as Vesalius and Harvey. Linnaeus in 1758 introduced binomial nomenclature, the two-part Latin naming system (genus and species, such as *Homo sapiens*), together with a hierarchical classification (domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species) that still anchors animal taxonomy. Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859) reframed biology by proposing evolution through natural selection, in which heritable traits that improve survival and reproduction spread through populations. Mendel's 1865 laws of inheritance were ignored for decades and rediscovered around 1900. The 1930s modern synthesis combined Mendelian genetics with Darwinian selection, and Watson and Crick's 1953 description of the DNA double helix opened molecular biology, allowing phylogenetic relationships to be read directly from genetic sequences.

Zoology now covers roughly 1.5 million described species, with estimates as high as 8 million. Modern classification relies on cladistics, which groups organisms by shared ancestry and depicts relationships as branching cladograms built from DNA data. The three-domain model (Archaea, Bacteria, Eukaryota) competes with the older five-kingdom scheme, and some molecular evidence suggests eukaryotes arose from symbiotic mergers rather than clean divergence from a single ancestor. The International Code of Zoological Nomenclature standardizes naming so each species has a unique, stable identifier usable across languages and centuries.

The field divides by what is studied. Vertebrate zoology splits into mammalogy (mammals), ornithology (birds), herpetology (reptiles and amphibians), and ichthyology (fish); invertebrate zoology covers the far more numerous phyla such as arthropods, molluscs, and sponges. Structural work spans cell biology, histology, and gross anatomy, all asking how structure produces function. Physiology examines how organs and systems operate, while developmental biology traces growth, reproduction, and regeneration. Evolutionary biology studies speciation, adaptation, and phylogeny using both fossils and molecular data, and is increasingly central because DNA-based phylogenetics both classifies animals and dates when lineages diverged.

Two further strands treat behavior and place. Ethology studies behavior in natural settings; behavioral ecology applies Tinbergen's four questions, asking what a behavior is for, how it develops, how it evolved, and what mechanism produces it. Biogeography asks why species live where they do, linking distributions to climate, geography, and evolutionary history.

Contemporary zoology feeds directly into conservation biology, where work on behavior, genetics, and habitat requirements underpins species protection, ecosystem restoration, and responses to climate change and habitat loss. The classification of life continues to shift as molecular data revise traditional groupings, and debates persist over whether animal cognition grades continuously into human intelligence or differs in kind.
