# Granule (cell biology)

A granule is a small particle inside a cell, barely visible by light microscopy. In cell biology the term most often refers to a secretory vesicle, a tiny membrane-bound packet that stores cargo such as a protein, hormone, or enzyme and releases it on demand. Granules are considered cell organelles.

Granules fall into two structural classes. Membrane-bound granules include eosinophilic granules, basophilic granules, and insulin granules. Non-membrane-bound granules, sometimes called biomolecular condensates, form through protein-protein and protein-RNA interactions and include P-granules and stress granules. Membrane status changes how a granule assembles and releases its contents.

## Immune-cell granules

Granulocytes are white blood cells named for their prominent cytoplasmic granules. The three types, neutrophils, eosinophils, and basophils, each carry enzymes tailored to their role: neutrophils attack bacteria, eosinophils and basophils respond to parasites and allergens.

Neutrophils contain several granule populations. Primary (azurophilic) granules carry hydrolytic enzymes such as elastase, myeloperoxidase, cathepsins, and defensins that degrade pathogens. Secondary (specific) granules contain lactoferrin, an iron-binding protein that deprives bacteria of iron. Tertiary granules carry matrix metalloproteinases for tissue remodelling. Natural killer cells use a different mechanism: their granules hold perforin and proteases that punch holes in neighbouring cells, causing lysis.

Release of granule contents is called degranulation. It is triggered by immune signals and works by moving granules to the cell surface where their membrane fuses with the plasma membrane and expels the cargo.

## Platelet granules

Platelets contain several granule types that drive clotting and wound repair. Alpha (α) granules are the most abundant, with 50 to 80 per platelet, measuring 200 to 500 nm and accounting for roughly 10 percent of platelet volume. Proteomic studies have identified more than 300 soluble proteins, functionally mixed: hemostasis factors including von Willebrand factor (VWF) and factor V, inflammatory chemokines such as CXCL1 and interleukin-8, and growth factors such as VEGF and FGF. Whether α-granules are structurally uniform is debated; newer work with freeze substitution shows them as largely homogeneous ovoids. Exocytosis is measured by surface P-selectin or by released cargo such as PF4.

Dense (δ) granules number 3 to 8 per platelet at about 150 nm. They belong to the lysosome-related organelle family, which also includes melanosomes and cytotoxic T-cell granules. Their electron-dense appearance comes from high cation, especially calcium, concentrations. They store serotonin, histamine, adenine nucleotides, and polyphosphates.

Platelets also carry 1 to 3 lysosomes, peroxisomes of unclear function, and a controversial T granule defined by TLR9 and PDI whose existence is disputed.

## Insulin granules

Pancreatic beta cells package insulin in secretory granules. Rising blood glucose raises intracellular calcium, which triggers exocytosis. Release is biphasic: granules docked near the plasma membrane fuse first, then deeper reserves are mobilised for a slower sustained phase.

Insulin granules mature step by step. Proinsulin is made in the endoplasmic reticulum, packaged in the Golgi, and buds from the trans-Golgi network in clathrin-coated vesicles. Acidification activates endoproteases PC1/3 and PC2, which cleave proinsulin into mature insulin. The clathrin coat is shed, and mature granules travel along actin filaments and microtubules to the cell surface.

## Germline granules

Germline granules carry inherited material that defines eggs and sperm across nearly all animals. The unifying name *nuage* (French for "cloud") was coined in 1957 by André and Rouiller. The same structure appears under several names: P-granules in *C. elegans*, germinal granules in *Xenopus*, chromatoid bodies in mice, polar granules in fruit flies.

Nuage sits on the cytoplasmic face of the nuclear envelope in cells destined to become germ cells. It is a dense network of RNA-binding proteins that captures specific mRNAs for asymmetric segregation during cell division, differential splicing, or delayed translation. Many nuage components belong to the piRNA pathway, which silences transposable elements and protects the genome across generations.

## Starch granules in plants

Plants store glucose as starch granules, an insoluble crystalline form built from amylopectin, a branched glucose polymer, and amylose, a linear one. Amylopectin forms the scaffold; amylose fills the spaces. Under a microscope starch granules show concentric growth rings and contain starch synthase.

Two functional forms exist. Transitionary starch is made by photosynthesis in leaves and used quickly. Storage starch accumulates in non-photosynthetic tissues such as roots and stems and is mobilised during germination, regrowth, or when energy demand exceeds photosynthetic supply. Granule size and shape vary by species and tissue.

## Stress granules

Stress granules are non-membrane-bound assemblies of protein and RNA that form when a cell faces oxidative damage, heat shock, toxins, or osmotic pressure. They trap mRNAs that have not started translation, along with translation initiation factors and RNA-binding proteins, which make up roughly half the granule's mass. A dense core concentrates the mRNA and proteins, surrounded by a looser shell.

Stress granules assemble through protein-protein interactions driven by methylation and phosphorylation of RNA-binding proteins. They are dynamic, docking with processing bodies, fusing, and fissioning in the cytoplasm. When stress ends, the granules disassemble and the trapped mRNAs return to translation or are degraded. Stress granules participate in mRNA triage, cell signalling, and antiviral defence, and defects in their formation or clearance are linked to neurodegenerative diseases such as ALS and FTLD, though their full physiological roles remain under investigation.
