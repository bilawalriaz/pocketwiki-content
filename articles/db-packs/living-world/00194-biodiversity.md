# Biodiversity

Biodiversity is the variability of life, measured at four nested levels: genetic variation within a species, species richness within an ecosystem, ecosystem diversity across a landscape, and phylogenetic diversity, the total branching history of life. Mora et al. (2011) estimate roughly 8.7 million species exist today, with about 1.2–1.5 million formally described; a 2016 microbial analysis pushed the upper estimate toward 1 trillion.

## Where life concentrates

Biodiversity is distributed unevenly. Tropical forests, covering under 20% of Earth's land, hold roughly half of all species. The latitudinal gradient runs from species-poor polar zones to species-rich equatorial regions, with the equator's higher mean temperature as the main driver. Marine biodiversity is about 25 times lower than terrestrial. Bacterial diversity breaks the pattern by peaking in temperate soils, driven by carbon inputs and habitat connectivity.

Biodiversity hotspots combine high endemism (species found nowhere else) with severe habitat loss. Most are tropical forests. Examples: Colombia (~10% of Earth's species, 1,900+ birds), Indonesia (10% of flowering plants across 17,000 islands), Madagascar (isolated since separating from Africa 66 million years ago), and Brazil's Atlantic Forest. Specialized habitats such as alpine zones and peat bogs also drive endemism.

## The deep history

The earliest undisputed life evidence dates to 3.7 billion years ago. For roughly the first billion years, only microorganisms existed. The Phanerozoic eon, the last 540 million years, opened with the Cambrian explosion, when most multicellular body plans appeared. The fossil record suggests the last few million years held the greatest macroscopic diversity, though recent strata are better preserved and the bias is debated. Species that have ever lived are estimated at 5 to 50 billion; if 50 million are alive today, more than 99% of all species went extinct before humans arrived.

Six major mass extinctions punctuate the record. The Permian–Triassic event 251 million years ago was the worst, requiring 30 million years for vertebrate communities to recover. The Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction 66 million years ago ended the non-avian dinosaurs. After each collapse, diversification resumes and re-expands the tree of life.

## The sixth extinction

Human activity has driven an ongoing sixth mass extinction, the Holocene extinction, since the late Pleistocene. Current loss rates run 100 to 10,000 times the natural background. Monitored vertebrate populations declined roughly 50% between 1970 and 2016 (WWF Living Planet Report 2020); freshwater populations fell 76%, Latin American populations 83%. In Germany, insect biomass dropped 75% over 25 years. As of late 2022, 40% of the 40,177 species assessed by the IUCN Red List were threatened with extinction.

| Driver | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Habitat destruction (mainly agriculture) | Largest single driver; removes the space species need. |
| Overexploitation | Hunting, fishing, logging beyond renewal rates. |
| Climate change | Shifts ranges and timing; coupled to biodiversity but not always the dominant short-term driver. |
| Invasive species | Breach natural barriers, outcompete natives, cause extirpation. |
| Pollution | Pesticides, nutrients, plastics degrade habitat quality. |

High-income nations show modest local biodiversity gains, offset by importing resource-intensive goods whose production drives habitat loss abroad.

## Why biodiversity matters

Biodiversity underpins ecosystem services in three groups: provisioning (food, wood, fresh water), regulating (climate stabilization, pollination, pest and disease control, flood and storm buffering), and cultural (aesthetic, spiritual, recreational value). Wild-insect pollination was valued at $2.1–14.6 billion in 2003. The global value of ecosystem services is estimated near $33 trillion per year (Costanza et al., 1997), and a 2021 Boston Consulting Group analysis put biodiversity's annual economic contribution near $150 trillion, about twice global GDP. Annual losses from biodiversity degradation run near $5 trillion.

The mechanisms are specific. Genetic diversity within crops raises yields and buffers disease. Species-rich plant communities use resources more fully because differing root depths and leaf traits exploit different niches. Higher species richness stabilizes productivity under climate stress. More than 80% of the global population relies on nature-derived medicines, and at least half of US pharmaceuticals originate from biological sources, yet only a tiny fraction of wild species have been screened.

## How biodiversity changes

Geographic barriers (rivers, mountains, oceans) isolate populations and drive speciation. Humans breach these barriers by moving species around the globe. Invasive species often increase local variety while reducing turnover between sites, and can drive regional diversity down through the extinctions they cause. Hybridization with introduced species can genetically swamp rare endemics even when hybrids look normal.

## Conservation in practice

Modern conservation biology matured in the mid-20th century. Its ethic is managing resources so species, ecosystems, and evolutionary processes persist alongside human culture. Strategies fall into three families:

- Protection: legally designated areas. At COP15 in December 2022, roughly 200 countries adopted the 30×30 target, protecting 30% of land and sea by 2030. At adoption, about 17% of land and 10% of ocean were formally protected. Scientists have called for 50% by 2050.
- Restoration: removing invasives (guided by DNA barcoding), reintroducing natives from gene banks, building wildlife corridors, and cutting pesticide use.
- Transformative change: shifting consumption, reforming agriculture and fisheries, embedding biodiversity costs into business. IPBES's 2019 Global Assessment concluded that incremental action is insufficient.

Citizen-science platforms such as iNaturalist and eBird now supply nearly half of the occurrence records feeding the Global Biodiversity Information Facility.

Internationally, the 1992 UN Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) grants nations sovereign rights over their biological resources and commits them to conserve, use sustainably, and share benefits. The 2023 UN High Seas Treaty (BBNJ) extends this to marine areas beyond national jurisdiction. The European Union's 2020 Biodiversity Strategy targets 30% protected area, 3 billion trees planted, 50% pesticide reduction, and 25% organic farming by 2030.
