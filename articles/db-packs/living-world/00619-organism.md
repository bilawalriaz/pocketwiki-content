# Organism

An organism is a living entity that functions as a single individual, but biology has no universally accepted definition. The standard requirements of autonomous reproduction, growth, and metabolism exclude viruses, which lack metabolism, and mishandle colonial creatures such as ant colonies and siphonophores, where specialised units divide labour. To solve this, evolutionary biologists David Queller and John Strassmann proposed *organismality*: a spectrum defined by how strongly the parts of a biological entity cooperate against how fiercely they conflict. On this view a lichen (fungus plus alga or cyanobacterium), a sexual pair of anglerfish, and a eusocial ant colony all count as organisms because their components work together with little internal competition. Individuality becomes an evolved social trait, not a binary switch, and the same logic stretches from self-replicating RNA molecules to synthetic chimaeras built in the lab.

## Why the usual definitions fail

The classic checklist seems intuitive, but it rejects viruses outright and says nothing useful about a siphonophore. Richard Dawkins offered a different test: **noncompartmentability**, the claim that an organism is heterogeneous enough that cutting it in half destroys it. Animals such as hydra and planarians, which regenerate whole bodies from fragments, break that rule. Demanding **genetic uniqueness, genetic homogeneity, and autonomy all at once** is another option, but clonal colonies fail it because every member is genetically identical. Proposed alternatives include **immune self/non-self discrimination** (the body recognising invaders), Schrödinger's **anti-entropy** (the tendency of living systems to maintain internal order by exporting disorder to their surroundings), and Claude Shannon's **information theory** (treating an organism as a self-maintaining pattern of information). None commands consensus, and some philosophers argue the category is dispensable. A flexible alternative treats autonomy, genetic homogeneity, and uniqueness as independent axes, so a biological individual can score high on one and low on another.

## Organismality as cooperation and conflict

Queller and Strassmann argue that what makes an entity an organism is the balance between cooperation and conflict among its parts, not any single property. A lichen is a partnership in which a fungus provides structure and minerals while an alga or cyanobacterium supplies sugar from photosynthesis, and the two grow as a single body in habitats neither could colonise alone. Eusocial ant colonies show extreme division of labour, with specialised workers supporting a small set of breeders, much like cells in a body. Samuel Díaz-Muñoz and colleagues extended the idea in 2016: organismality is a continuous, context-dependent variable, so the same kind of entity can sit closer to "organism" or closer to "loose association" depending on circumstances. Highly integrated animals such as vertebrates may represent the far end of a gradient that runs from independent cells, through cooperating groups, to inseparable wholes.

## Levels of biological organisation

- **Unicellular organisms**: bacteria, archaea, and protists living as single cells, often carrying internal membrane-bound **organelles** such as mitochondria.
- **Multicellular organisms**: animals, plants, fungi, and algae built from many specialised cells.
- **Colonial organisms**: siphonophores, where the colony functions as one individual but consists of communicating modules called **zooids**, each handling a specialised task.
- **Superorganisms**: eusocial colonies, especially of ants, bees, and termites, where many animals act as a single functional unit.
- **Mutualisms**: cross-species partnerships such as lichens, where each partner supplies something the other cannot obtain alone, and the combined organism occupies habitats inaccessible to either partner singly.

## Boundary cases

**Viruses** carry genes and evolve by natural selection, but they cannot reproduce, metabolise, or maintain themselves without a host cell, so most biologists place them outside life. The few viral genes linked to energy metabolism appear to have been borrowed from host genomes through **horizontal gene transfer** (movement of genetic material between organisms outside of parent-to-offspring inheritance), reinforcing the view that viruses are parasites of cellular life rather than degenerate organisms. Under the **RNA world** hypothesis, the first "organisms" were self-replicating RNA chains that combined heredity, variation, and differential reproduction, the three ingredients of Darwinian selection. Their fitness depended on replication accuracy, resistance to chemical decay, and the ability to grab resources, all shaped by how the RNA folded. Sponges, slime moulds, and naked mole-rat colonies sit in a grey zone between simple aggregation and true organism. Synthetic biology is pushing the boundary further with **chimaeras** (tissues made from cells of more than one species), **cyborgs** (living tissue merged with electronic devices), and **hybrots** (robots controlled partly by biological neurons). All three display **teleonomic** behaviour, goal-directed activity with built-in error correction.

## Open questions

The cooperation–conflict metric still needs a way to be measured quantitatively across very different systems, and whether viruses should be reclassified as living remains contested. As engineered chimaeras, cyborgs, and hybrots spread, the distinction between evolved goal-seeking and designed goal-seeking becomes a regulatory and philosophical problem, because both kinds of system correct their own errors and pursue goals.
