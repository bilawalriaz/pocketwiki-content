# Plant

Plants are eukaryotic, predominantly photosynthetic organisms that make up the kingdom Plantae. They capture sunlight with the green pigment chlorophyll inside organelles called chloroplasts, using that energy to build sugars from carbon dioxide and water, a process that also releases the oxygen that fills Earth's atmosphere. A few lineages have secondarily lost photosynthesis and live as parasites. About 380,000 species are known, of which roughly 283,000 are seed plants and 85–90 percent of all plant species are flowering plants. Green plants supply the chemical energy that runs nearly every ecosystem on land and in the sunlit ocean.

The article defines plants as the clade Viridiplantae, the green plants, comprising green algae and land plants, the embryophytes. Earlier systems lumped algae and fungi into "plants" simply because they were not animals; modern classification excludes them. A broader genome-based grouping, Archaeplastida, adds red algae and glaucophytes because they share the same ancient chloroplast acquisition.

## How a plant cell differs

Three features set plant cells apart from other eukaryotes. A large central vacuole fills most of the cell with water, keeping it turgid. Chloroplasts carry out photosynthesis. Outside the cell membrane sits a stiff cell wall made mostly of cellulose, strong enough to resist the pressure of the swollen vacuole so the cell does not burst. Chloroplasts originated when an ancestral non-photosynthetic cell engulfed a cyanobacterium, and the partnership became permanent; the same event gave rise to chloroplasts in algae. These cellular features allow plant cells to differentiate into specialised tissues, including vascular tissue with xylem and phloem that transport water and sugars, which in turn build organs such as roots, stems, leaves, and flowers.

## The plant life cycle

Plants reproduce sexually through alternation of generations, a life cycle in which two multicellular stages alternate. The diploid sporophyte produces spores that grow into haploid gametophytes, which produce gametes; fertilisation then yields a new sporophyte. In mosses and ferns the gametophyte is the familiar green plant, but in seed plants the sporophyte dominates and the gametophyte is reduced to a few cells inside pollen or inside an ovule. Flowering plants concentrate sexual reproduction in flowers: stamens shed pollen, and carpels contain egg cells that, once fertilised, develop into seeds enclosed in fruits. Many plants also reproduce asexually via runners, tubers, bulbs, or gemmae, small clumps of cells that detach and grow.

## Diversity and scale

Plants range from single-celled desmids about 10 micrometres across to the coast redwood, *Sequoia sempervirens*, reaching 120 metres, and *Eucalyptus regnans* at around 100 metres. Genome size varies widely. The bladderwort *Utricularia gibba* holds the smallest published plant genome at 82 megabases, while the Norway spruce *Picea abies* has a 19.6 gigabase genome, and wheat encodes about 94,000 genes, among the largest gene counts known.

## A short evolutionary history

Land plants evolved from green algae. Multicellular freshwater eukaryotes existed over a billion years ago, but the first land plants, resembling today's bryophytes, appear in the fossil record around 450 million years ago in the Ordovician. Primitive forms diversified through the Silurian, and by the end of the Devonian most basic features of modern plants, including roots, leaves, and secondary wood, were in place. The Rhynie chert in Scotland preserves early plant anatomy in cellular detail. The Carboniferous brought vast swampy forests of clubmosses and horsetails alongside the earliest gymnosperms, the first seed plants. The Permo-Triassic extinction reshaped plant communities, and conifers diversified from the Late Triassic, dominating Jurassic landscapes. Flowering plants radiated so quickly in the Cretaceous that Darwin called the event "an abominable mystery." A 2019 phylogeny based on genomes and transcriptomes from 1,153 plant species now anchors the modern tree of plant life.

## Ecology

Plants shape the planet. Photosynthesis, first by cyanobacteria and later by photosynthetic eukaryotes, turned Earth's early anoxic atmosphere into one that is now about 21 percent oxygen. Plants form roughly 80 percent of the world's biomass, around 450 gigatonnes of carbon, and almost every terrestrial food web traces its energy back to plants.

Plants also drive intimate partnerships. Mycorrhizal fungi thread through most plant roots, trading water and mineral nutrients for sugars. Legumes house *Rhizobium* bacteria in root nodules that fix atmospheric nitrogen. About 1 percent of plant species are parasites, from semi-parasitic mistletoe to fully parasitic broomrape. Epiphytes grow on other plants without harming them, while hemiepiphytes such as the strangler fig eventually kill their host. Around 630 species trap animals to supplement nitrogen and phosphorus in poor soils. Pollination syndromes, suites of flower colour, shape, and scent, evolved alongside insects and birds, and many fruits exist to be eaten and have their seeds passed through animal guts or carried on fur.

## Why plants matter to humans

Cultivation of plants is the foundation of agriculture. About 7,000 species have been used as food, but modern diets draw mainly from around 30, dominated by cereals such as rice and wheat, starchy roots like cassava and potato, and legumes such as peas and beans. Plants supply most medicines: Dioscorides' *De materia medica* (50–70 CE) catalogued about 600 species and remained in use for 1,500 years, and modern drugs derived from plants include aspirin, morphine, quinine, taxol, vincristine, and digitalis. Nonfood products range from timber, paper, rubber, and cotton to dyes, resins, cork, and biofuels. Fossil fuels derive from the remains of ancient aquatic organisms including phytoplankton, not from wood. Plant biology also founded genetics: Gregor Mendel derived the laws of inheritance from peas, and Barbara McClintock showed that chromosomes carry inherited traits by studying maize. Tree rings date archaeological sites and record past climates. Plants also cause harm as weeds, invasive species, allergenic pollen, and toxins to livestock and people.
