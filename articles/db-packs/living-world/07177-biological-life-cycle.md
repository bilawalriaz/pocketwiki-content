# Biological life cycle

A biological life cycle is the repeating sequence of stages an organism passes through from one reproductive event to the next. It begins with a zygote (a single cell formed by the fusion of two gametes) and ends when that organism produces offspring that start the same sequence again. Related terms *life history* and *ontogeny* (the development of an individual from embryo to adult) describe development within one generation; the life cycle differs by stressing renewal across generations.

## Ploidy and the three sexual cycles

Sexual life cycles alternate between haploid cells (one set of chromosomes, written **n**) and diploid cells (two sets, **2n**). The shift from 2n back to n occurs only through meiosis, a cell division that halves the chromosome number and shuffles genes. Biologists recognise three basic cycle types based on when meiosis occurs and which phase is multicellular:

- **Haplontic (zygotic meiosis).** The zygote is the only diploid cell and undergoes meiosis immediately. Mitosis (ordinary cell division) occurs only in the haploid phase, producing a multicellular haploid body. Gametes later form by mitosis and fuse to make a new zygote. This cycle occurs in many algae, dinoflagellates, the malaria parasite *Plasmodium*, and most fungi.
- **Diplontic (gametic meiosis).** The zygote divides by mitosis into a multicellular diploid body. Only the gametes are haploid, produced directly by meiosis. Animals and some green algae such as *Cladophora* follow this pattern.
- **Haplodiplontic (sporic meiosis).** Both phases are multicellular. The diploid sporophyte produces haploid spores by meiosis; each spore grows by mitosis into a haploid gametophyte, which then makes gametes by mitosis. The two generations may look similar or very different. Land plants and most brown algae use this cycle; the familiar leafy body is the sporophyte in ferns and seed plants, but the gametophyte in mosses and their relatives.

Cycles with a single mitotic phase (haplontic and diplontic) are termed *haplobiontic*; those with two (haplodiplontic) are *diplobiontic*. Some red and green algae show *vegetative meiosis*, in which ordinary body cells of a diploid individual undergo meiosis instead of specialised spore-producing cells.

## Parasitic life cycles

For parasites, the life cycle is defined by the sequence of hosts it moves through. A *direct* cycle uses one host, as in the canine hookworm *Ancylostoma caninum*, whose larvae penetrate a dog's skin and mature in the small intestine. An *indirect* cycle requires two or more hosts, as in the heartworm *Dirofilaria immitis*: microfilariae circulating in a dog's blood are taken up by a feeding mosquito, develop to an infective larval stage inside the mosquito, and are passed to a new dog at the next bite, after which the worms settle in the pulmonary artery and reproduce.

The host in which sexual reproduction occurs is the *definitive* host; *intermediate* hosts are those in which the parasite grows but does not reproduce sexually. A *paratenic* host carries the parasite without any development, raising the chance of transmission to the definitive host. A parasite that must infect a given host to complete its cycle is an *obligate* parasite of that host; one that can sometimes finish without it is *facultative*; an *accidental* host is one in which the parasite cannot complete development.

## Evolution, asexuality, and the germ line

The earliest eukaryotic life cycles were probably haploid and asexual; bacteria and archaea still follow that basic pattern. Many eukaryotes reproduce asexually either by loss of sex or as a facultative part of the cycle, though molecular evidence indicates that true sexual cycles with meiosis and fertilisation are ancient across eukaryotes.

A consequence of separating germ cells (set aside to produce the next generation) from somatic cells (the body) is that the body ages and dies while the cell lineage carrying DNA forward can persist without limit. Recombination and DNA repair during meiosis in germ cells can fix damage that accumulates in somatic cells, and the ancestry of every living cell traces back in an unbroken line of more than 3 billion years to the origin of life.
