# Buddhist views on evolution

Buddhism and evolutionary science are widely compatible. A 2007 Pew Forum survey of religious groups in the United States found Buddhists had the highest acceptance rate of evolution as the best explanation for the origin of human life, at 81%, with Hindus close behind at 80%. The U.S. population as a whole accepted it at 48%, and Jehovah's Witnesses at the bottom, at 8%. Two factors drive this high acceptance: a pragmatic indifference to cosmological speculation, and a doctrinal core that aligns with the ideas of change and interdependence.

## Why cosmology is not central

The pragmatic case rests on the *fourteen unanswerable questions* (*avyākata*), a set of metaphysical puzzles — including whether the universe is eternal, whether it had a beginning, and what happens to a Tathāgata after death — that the Buddha explicitly set aside. The *parable of the poisoned arrow* makes the point: a man shot by a poisoned arrow should let the physician remove it rather than insist on first learning who shot him, what wood the bow was made from, or what metal tipped the arrow. The aim is to end suffering, not to resolve every speculative question. Since evolution is a question about the origin and development of life, and Buddhism is not committed to a particular cosmology, there is no doctrinal pressure to reject it. The philosopher Stephen T. Asma summarises the prevailing attitude: the historical Buddha shunned metaphysical speculation, and most Buddhists take this as an invitation to embrace the sciences.

## Doctrinal alignment

The doctrinal case rests on three core teachings. *Impermanence* (*anicca*) holds that all conditioned phenomena arise, change, and pass away. *Dependent origination* (*pratītyasamutpāda*) holds that everything arises in dependence on causes and conditions, with no autonomous starting point. *Non-self* (*anattā*) denies a fixed personal essence behind experience. Together these describe a world of interconnected processes, which fits with biological evolution as a story of populations changing under natural selection.

## Scriptural anchor

A scriptural anchor is the *Aggañña Sutta*, the 27th discourse of the *Digha Nikaya* in the Pali Canon. It narrates a long cyclic process of world contraction and expansion in which beings begin as luminous, mind-made forms and progressively become more material, earth-bound creatures. Most modern scholars read this as an allegorical anticipation of evolutionary transformation, and the sutta's moral — that ethical conduct and wisdom, not birth or social class, define a person's worth — reinforces the lesson that the narrative is about values as much as origins.

## Modern philosophical positions

The 14th Dalai Lama, in *The Universe in a Single Atom: The Convergence of Science and Spirituality*, supports evolution while noting one philosophical reservation: from a Buddhist perspective, the idea that mutations are purely random is unsatisfying as a complete account of life's origin. Donald S. Lopez Jr. has argued that the doctrines of karma and rebirth lead many Buddhists to suspect that consciousness and ethical action play a role in how life unfolds. The Zen teacher Albert Low rejects strict neo-Darwinism and selfish-gene theory as too materialistic, proposing instead a "spiritual evolution" in which consciousness, ethics, and awareness develop alongside biology. Buddhists who find Darwinian metaphysics unsatisfying rarely deny the empirical findings of evolutionary biology; they contest the sufficiency of a purely gene-centred story.

## Limits of acceptance

Acceptance is not universal. Some traditionalists read Buddhist cosmology, including its Mount Meru cosmology and the six realms of rebirth (gods, demigods, humans, animals, hungry ghosts, and hell beings), as literal descriptions of the world. In these traditions, rejection of evolution sometimes accompanies a broader rejection of materialism and modern science, on the grounds that a literal cosmology cannot accommodate gradual biological change. From the early twentieth century, reformers such as Anagariga Dharmapala in Sri Lanka argued that Buddhism's understanding of natural change is compatible with scientific observation, and engagement with modern science has been a feature of Buddhist scholarship in Sri Lanka, Myanmar, and Tibet.

## Practical consequences

In countries with significant Buddhist populations, evolution is generally taught alongside religious education without conflict, and surveys consistently show Buddhists among the highest accepters of evolution across religious groups. The Buddhist emphasis on interdependence has also made evolution a natural ally of Buddhist environmentalism, since recognising the shared ancestry and ecological entanglement of living beings reinforces the precepts of compassion and non-harming, and supplies an empirical grounding for ecological responsibility.
