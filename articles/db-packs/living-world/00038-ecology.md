# Ecology

Ecology is the natural science of relationships among living organisms and their environment. The name combines Greek *oîkos* ("house") and *-logía* ("study of"). It examines the abundance, biomass, and distribution of organisms; the movement of materials and energy through living communities; cooperation, competition, and predation; and biodiversity. It overlaps with biogeography, evolutionary biology, genetics, ethology, and natural history, and underpins conservation biology, wetland and natural resource management, and human ecology. Ernst Haeckel coined the term in 1866; modern ecology began with American botanists in the 1890s.

## Hierarchy and Scale

Ecosystems range from a single leaf supporting aphid generations and their bacteria to entire biomes. Ecological patterns are emergent: a single tree matters little to forest classification but is critical to the organisms on it, and connections cannot be explained by species in isolation. The biological world is organized hierarchically as organisms → populations → guilds → communities → ecosystems → biomes → biosphere. This hierarchy is non-linear: small changes, such as shifts in nitrogen-fixing microbes, can cause disproportionate, possibly irreversible effects. Population and community ecology focuses on distribution and abundance; ecosystem ecology focuses on material and energy fluxes.

## Biodiversity, Habitat, and Niche

Biodiversity spans genes, species, and ecosystems and supports the services on which human life depends. A habitat is the environment over which a species occurs. G. Evelyn Hutchinson defined the niche in 1957 as the set of biotic and abiotic conditions allowing stable persistence, divided into the fundamental niche (environmental conditions alone) and the realized niche (including ecological interactions). Organisms modify habitats through niche construction, from beaver ponds to decaying logs; ecosystem engineering refers to physical modifications, while niche construction also includes evolutionary feedback on natural selection.

## Biomes and Biosphere

Biomes categorize Earth's regions mainly by vegetation, limited by climate and precipitation: tropical rainforest, temperate forests, taiga, tundra, and hot and polar deserts. The biosphere regulates planetary energy, nutrients, and climate; atmospheric CO₂ and O₂ history reflects respiration and photosynthesis. The Gaia hypothesis applies holism at planetary scale: emergent feedback from organismal metabolism is proposed to maintain Earth's temperature and atmosphere within ranges compatible with life.

## Population Ecology

A population comprises same-species individuals sharing niche and habitat. The Malthusian model holds exponential growth under constant conditions, expressed for a closed population as dN/dt = rN(t). Pierre Verhulst added a crowding coefficient; equilibrium is written K, the carrying capacity. Real populations are analyzed with life-history, fecundity, and survivorship data, applied to wildlife management and harvest quotas; when simple models fail, ecologists use information-criterion methods. Metapopulations (defined 1969) are sets of local populations that go extinct and recolonize, linked by migration. Migration is seasonal return; dispersal is one-way permanent movement.

## Food Webs, Trophic Levels, and Keystone Species

Plants capture solar energy via photosynthesis; feeding chains interconnect into food webs. Trophic levels group organisms acquiring most energy from the adjacent lower level. Species divide into autotrophs (producers), heterotrophs (herbivores and higher consumers), and detritivores. Omnivores cross levels, and discrete trophic categories have been called "fiction," though empirical work shows real levels exist, with webs above herbivores better described as tangled. Keystone species (term coined by Robert Paine, 1969) have disproportionate connections relative to their biomass; their loss can trigger trophic cascades. Sea otters limit sea-urchin grazing on kelp; removing otters collapses kelp beds.

## Relation to Evolution

Ecology and evolutionary biology are sister disciplines with no sharp boundary, and evolution can occur on timescales as short as one generation. Behavioural ecology treats behaviors as heritable adaptive traits. Social behaviors evolve through kin selection, in which altruism is rewarded genetically through relatives (as in social insects whose male drones are clones), or through group selection. Mutualisms such as gut bacteria, lichens, and coral symbioses are widespread; biogeography studies how these distributions arise through vicariance and dispersal. r/K selection predicts life-history shifts with density: rapid growth at low density, competitive efficiency near carrying capacity.

## Environment, Disturbance, and the Early Atmosphere

The environment includes abiotic factors (temperature, radiation, light, chemistry, climate) and biotic ones. Disturbances such as fire, flood, and drought both cause and result from community fluctuations, and biodiversity can buffer ecosystems against them. Earth formed roughly 4.5 billion years ago with a hydrogen-dominated atmosphere that became methane- and ammonia-rich, then CO₂- and water-vapor-rich through metabolism. Photosynthesis began 0.3–1 billion years before the Great Oxidation at roughly 2.4 billion years ago. By about 350 million years ago, atmospheric oxygen exceeded 17%, enabling combustion.

## History

Herodotus described mutualism c. 425 BC. George Perkins Marsh published *Man and Nature* in 1864, an early ecosystem concept. Haeckel coined "Ökologie" in 1866; Ellen Swallow Richards adopted "oekology" in the U.S. by 1892. Frederic Clements published the first American ecology book in 1905. Raymond Lindeman's 1942 paper founded trophic dynamics. Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* (1962) mobilized environmentalism; MacArthur and Wilson's island biogeography appeared in 1967. Clements's superorganism view sparked a holism-versus-individualism debate that lasted into the 1970s. Edward O. Wilson predicted in 1992 that the 21st century would be "the era of restoration in ecology."
