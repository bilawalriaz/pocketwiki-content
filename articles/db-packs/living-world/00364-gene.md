# Gene

A gene is the basic unit of heredity and the working blueprint for biological function. In modern biology it has two principal definitions.

The **Mendelian gene** is the classical unit of inheritance that Gregor Mendel inferred from pea-plant experiments: a discrete factor passed from parent to offspring that determines a trait.

The **molecular gene** is a specific DNA sequence that is transcribed into a functional product, either a protein or a non-coding RNA such as tRNA or rRNA. This molecular definition has been the textbook standard for over fifty years; restricting "gene" to protein-coding sequences is a common error.

A single gene can exist in different versions called **alleles**, produced when the DNA sequence mutates. Alleles can produce different observable traits, or **phenotypes**. The complete set of alleles an organism carries is its **genotype**.

## How genes are built

A gene is a stretch of DNA, a double-helix polymer of **nucleotides** (the building blocks of DNA: A, T, C, G). In eukaryotes, DNA is wrapped around histone proteins and packaged into **chromosomes**, with each gene sitting at a specific physical address called its **locus**. A eukaryotic gene's working parts include:

- **Exons**, the segments retained in the final RNA.
- **Introns**, segments cut out before the RNA is used.
- **Regulatory sequences** such as **promoters** and **enhancers**, which control when and how strongly the gene is read. These regulators sit next to the gene but are not themselves part of it.

In bacteria and archaea, genes are often clustered into **operons** and transcribed together as a single unit.

## How genes work

A gene functions through **expression**, a two-step process. First, **transcription** copies the gene's DNA into messenger RNA. For protein-coding genes, **translation** then reads that RNA in three-letter **codons**, each specifying one amino acid or a stop signal, to build a protein. The genetic code is essentially universal across life.

Expression is tightly regulated rather than constantly active. A classic example is the **lac operon**, in which the genes that metabolise lactose are switched on only when lactose is present and glucose is scarce.

## Inheritance

Organisms inherit their genes from their parents. In sexual reproduction, a specialised cell division called meiosis produces **haploid gametes** (sex cells carrying one copy of each chromosome), and the exchange of DNA between paired chromosomes, known as recombination, shuffles alleles so offspring carry new combinations. Mendel's laws of dominance and independent assortment describe the statistical outcomes of this process, with the important caveat that genes physically close together on the same chromosome tend to be inherited as a block, a phenomenon called linkage.

## Evolution and the gene's role in it

During the twentieth century, Mendelian genetics was merged with Darwinian evolution in the modern synthesis. Williams, Hamilton, and Maynard Smith then pushed the **gene-centric view**, in which natural selection acts primarily on alleles that affect reproductive success. Kimura's **neutral theory** added that many sequence changes spread through populations by drift rather than because they aid survival.

## The genome and genetic engineering

An organism's complete set of DNA is its **genome**. Gene counts vary widely between species. The Human Genome Project began in the 1990s, and the early expectation of roughly 100,000 human genes fell sharply as sequencing improved; current estimates are about 20,000 protein-coding genes and roughly 26,000 non-coding genes. Only a small fraction of these are **essential**, meaning required for survival in the laboratory.

**Genetic engineering** modifies DNA directly and is used in research, agriculture, and medicine, including **gene therapy**, which treats disease by correcting or replacing defective alleles inside a patient's cells.

## Open frontiers

Two live debates shape how "gene" is understood today. First, edge cases such as overlapping reading frames and trans-splicing resist any single neat definition. Second, large surveys suggest that around 80 percent of the human genome is transcribed, so DNA once dismissed as "junk" is now recognised as a likely source of regulatory and functional RNA.
