# Molecular biology

Molecular biology studies the molecular structures and chemical processes inside cells, focused on nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) and proteins. It explains how these macromolecules direct replication, transcription, translation, and protein synthesis, and how they interact. The field grew where biochemistry and genetics converged in the twentieth century, once physics and chemistry offered tools sharp enough to dissect biological molecules. William Astbury coined the term "molecular biology" in 1945 for this approach of explaining classical biology at molecular scales. Applications now include drug targeting, disease diagnosis, and molecular medicine such as gene therapy.

## Establishing DNA as the genetic material

Before DNA's role was clear, scientists debated whether protein or DNA carried heredity. Mendel's 1866 law of segregation established that diploid organisms pass one of two alleles per gene to offspring, defining genes as hypothetical units of inheritance. Friedrich Miescher isolated "nuclein" (DNA) from pus in 1869 by exploiting its high phosphorus content. Phoebus Levene proposed the polynucleotide model in 1919, and Erwin Chargaff showed in 1950 that nucleic acid composition varies by species but total purines (adenine, guanine) always equal total pyrimidines (cytosine, thymine).

Two experiments settled the question. In 1928, Frederick Griffith found that heat-killed virulent type III-S *Streptococcus pneumoniae* transformed live avirulent type II-R bacteria into deadly III-S cells. The smooth III-S strain's polysaccharide capsule, a glucose–glucuronic acid polymer, hides it from host immunity, and capsule production is genetic. In 1944, Avery, MacLeod, and McCarty purified the "transforming principle" and identified it as DNA, not protein. Hershey and Chase confirmed this by labeling phage protein coats with radioactive sulfur and DNA with radioactive phosphorus: only phosphorus entered *E. coli*, proving that injected viral DNA alone directs new phage assembly. The phenomenon is a form of horizontal gene transfer (HGT), movement of genes between contemporaries rather than parent to offspring; transduction, in which a bacteriophage carries bacterial DNA between cells, is another HGT route.

## Structure and replication

In 1953, Watson and Crick proposed the double helix model, drawing on Rosalind Franklin's X-ray crystallography shared by Maurice Wilkins. The model immediately suggested a copying mechanism. The Meselson–Stahl experiment of 1958 tested this by labeling parental DNA with a heavy nitrogen isotope: after replication, each new double helix contained one original strand and one new strand, establishing semiconservative replication. By 1961, experiments showed the genetic code is a triplet code: three sequential bases, a codon, specify each amino acid, read non-overlapping from a fixed start. Watson, Crick, and Wilkins shared the 1962 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the structure work.

## Core techniques

Molecular cloning, developed in the 1960s, inserts a gene, cut with restriction enzymes or amplified by PCR, into a plasmid vector carrying an origin of replication, multiple cloning site, promoter, and selective marker. DNA enters bacteria by transformation, conjugation, or transduction; eukaryotic transfection uses calcium phosphate, electroporation, microinjection, or liposomes. Expressed proteins can be tested for activity, crystallized, or screened against drug candidates.

PCR copies specific DNA sequences exponentially, amplifying a single molecule to over a billion copies in under two hours under ideal conditions, and supports gene expression studies, pathogen detection, mutation screening, site-directed mutagenesis, and cDNA library work. Gel electrophoresis separates DNA fragments by size, since negatively charged phosphate groups migrate toward the positive electrode; SDS-PAGE separates proteins by size, and 2D gels add charge. The Bradford assay (1975) quantifies protein by the color shift of Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 from reddish-brown at 465 nm to bright blue at 595 nm on binding, with readings taken within 5–20 minutes; it tolerates ethanol, sodium chloride, and magnesium chloride but is distorted by strong alkaline buffers such as SDS.

Blotting detects specific molecules. Southern blotting probes membrane-transferred DNA with labeled complementary sequences, now less common than PCR but still used to count transgene copies and verify knockout stem cells. Northern blotting (the name, like Western and Eastern, plays on Southern) measures RNA size and amount to gauge gene expression timing, though interpretation assumes no post-transcriptional regulation. Western blotting uses labeled antibodies against proteins transferred to PVDF, nitrocellulose, or nylon membranes, with detection by colorimetry, chemiluminescence, or autoradiography. Microarrays, carrying over 10,000 short oligonucleotide spots around 100 micrometres across, hybridize labeled cDNA to profile expression, for example comparing healthy and tumour tissue. Allele-specific oligonucleotide probes detect single-base mutations without PCR or gels, because a single mismatch blocks hybridization of a 20–25 nt probe.

## Modern state and connections

Since the early 2020s, real-time atomic-scale monitoring and inexpensive deep sequencing have enabled genetic work in non-model organisms and synthetic production of molecules through engineered metabolic pathways, while CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing has become accessible to individuals for under $10,000. Molecular biology overlaps with biochemistry, which catalogues proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids, and with genetics, which links mutations and genes to phenotype; its techniques now feed cell biology, developmental biology, population genetics, phylogenetics, and biophysics, with molecular genetics among the most prominent sub-fields since the early 2000s.
