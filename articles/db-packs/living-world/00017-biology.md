# Biology

Biology is the scientific study of life and living organisms, encompassing their structure, function, growth, origin, evolution, and distribution. It rests on five unifying themes: the cell as life's basic unit, genes as the basis of inheritance, evolution as the driver of diversity, energy transformation, and homeostasis (the maintenance of internal stability). Modern biology, grounded in Darwinian evolution and DNA-based genetics, spans levels from molecules to ecosystems and underpins medicine, agriculture, biotechnology, and environmental science.

## Etymology

The word "biology" combines Greek *bíos* ("life") with *logia* ("study of"). The compound first appears in Michael Christoph Hanow's 1766 work. Its modern sense was introduced independently by Thomas Beddoes (1799), Karl Friedrich Burdach (1800), Gottfried Reinhold Treviranus (1802), and Jean-Baptiste Lamarck (1802).

## History

Science, including medicine, traces to ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia (c. 3000–1200 BCE), shaping Greek natural philosophy. Aristotle explored biological causation and life's diversity; Theophrastus began scientific plant study. Medieval Islamic scholars al-Jahiz and Al-Dīnawarī wrote on biology and botany. Carl Linnaeus published a basic taxonomy in 1735 and binomial species names in the 1750s. Anton van Leeuwenhoek's microscope improvements revealed spermatozoa, bacteria, and infusoria, while Jan Swammerdam advanced entomology and microscopic techniques.

In 1838 Schleiden and Schwann promoted two cell-theory tenets (the cell as organisms' basic unit, and individual cells having all characteristics of life) while still supporting spontaneous generation. Robert Remak and Rudolf Virchow established the third tenet (cells come only from cell division); by the 1860s all three were accepted.

Lamarck presented an early evolutionary theory, but Darwin, synthesizing Humboldt's biogeography, Lyell's uniformitarian geology, Malthus on population, and his own observations, forged natural selection (reached independently by Alfred Russel Wallace). Mendel's 1865 work founded genetics but was recognized only in the early 20th century, when the modern synthesis reconciled Darwinian evolution with classical genetics. The Hershey–Chase experiments in the 1940s–early 1950s identified DNA as the gene-bearing material. Watson and Crick's 1953 double-helix discovery opened molecular genetics, and Khorana, Holley, and Nirenberg later cracked the genetic code. The Human Genome Project launched in 1990.

## Fields

**Biochemistry** studies chemical processes within organisms. **Molecular biology** examines nucleic acids and proteins, including replication, transcription, translation, and protein synthesis. The 1953 Miller–Urey experiment showed that organic compounds could form abiotically under early-Earth-like conditions, informing abiogenesis research.

**Cell biology** investigates cell structure, function, and behavior. **Bioenergetics** concerns energy flow through living systems, including respiration and enzymatic metabolism.

**Genetics** studies inheritance. Classical genetics examines Mendelian inheritance, with Punnett squares predicting test-cross results. The chromosome theory of inheritance was supported by Thomas Morgan's fruit-fly experiments, which established sex linkage of eye color.

**Evolutionary developmental biology (evo-devo)** compares developmental processes across organisms. Recombinant DNA technology in the 1970s united embryology with molecular genetics. Key findings include homeotic genes regulating development across eukaryotes and deep homology: dissimilar organs (insect, vertebrate, and cephalopod eyes) are controlled by similar toolkit genes.

**Evolutionary biology** analyzes mechanisms of evolution, which accounts for life's unity and diversity (Dobzhansky: "nothing in biology makes sense except in the light of evolution"). Population genetics studies how variation develops and shapes populations. Some research addresses phenomena poorly covered by the modern synthesis, including speciation, sexual reproduction, cooperation, ageing, and evolvability.

**Ecology** studies the distribution and abundance of life and organism–environment interactions. **Systematics** studies diversification using phylogenetics to visualize evolutionary relationships and unify taxonomy.

**Conservation biology** aims to protect species, habitats, and ecosystems from extinction and the erosion of biotic interactions. It studies the maintenance, loss, and restoration of biodiversity, with estimates suggesting up to 50% of species may disappear within 50 years, contributing to poverty and starvation and resetting evolution's course.

## Key terms

- **Cell**: the basic unit of organisms; individual cells have all characteristics of life.
- **Gene**: trait-carrying unit found on chromosomes.
- **DNA**: double-helical molecule encoding genes via codons.
- **Homeostasis**: maintenance of internal stability.
- **Natural selection**: Darwin's mechanism driving evolution.
- **Mendelian inheritance**: principles of how genes and traits pass from parents to offspring.
- **Phylogenetics**: study of evolutionary relationships, visualized as trees.
- **Deep homology**: dissimilar organs controlled by similar developmental genes.
- **Abiogenesis**: spontaneous arising of complex organic molecules on early Earth.
- **Modern synthesis**: reconciliation of Darwinian evolution with classical genetics.

## Open questions

Evolutionary research continues addressing phenomena poorly accounted for by the modern synthesis, including speciation, the evolution of sexual reproduction, cooperation, ageing, and evolvability. Conservation biologists debate biodiversity-loss trends and their effects on human well-being, given estimates that up to 50% of species may vanish within 50 years.
