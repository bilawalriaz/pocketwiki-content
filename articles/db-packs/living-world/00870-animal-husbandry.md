# Animal husbandry

Animal husbandry is the agricultural practice of raising domesticated animals for meat, milk, eggs, fiber, and labor. It underwrote the shift from nomadic hunting to settled farming, and it remains a major global industry whose environmental and ethical footprint now shapes food, climate, and animal welfare debates.

## How domestication unfolded

Domestication began with the dog more than 17,500 years ago, valued for companionship and hunting help. The Neolithic Revolution around 13,000 BC brought the first systematic crop cultivation and animal taming. By roughly 8,500 BC, sheep, goats, pigs, and cattle were domesticated in the Fertile Crescent and the Near East, the species that still dominate temperate livestock. The following millennia added regionally distinct animals: elephants in southern Asia by 6,000 BC, chickens in northeastern China and managed beekeeping in Egypt by about 5,000 BC, horses and the wild ass around 3,000 BC in Central Asia and Egypt, and camels soon after, with Bactrian camels in Mongolia and Arabian camels becoming beasts of burden. In the Andes, llamas and alpacas were domesticated before 3,000 BC. By 1,000 BC, camel caravans were linking India with Mesopotamia and the Mediterranean, a transport role livestock would fill for thousands of years.

A pivotal institutional change came in 18th-century Britain, when Robert Bakewell pioneered selective breeding, the controlled mating of animals to enhance traits such as growth rate, yield, or temperament. This made possible the modern intensive breeds whose productivity rests on genetic selection rather than on feeding and care alone.

## Systems, feed, and reproduction

Traditional systems were subsistence-based and integrated: a single herd or flock provided meat, milk, wool, draft power, manure, and breeding stock. Transhumance, the seasonal movement of herds between pastures, is the classic extensive pattern and survives in many regions. Modern systems span the opposite extreme, with intensive feedlots and battery cages concentrating animals at high density. The species' natural diet sets the basic rule: herbivores graze or receive forage, while omnivores such as pigs and chickens require high-protein diets. Artificial insemination propagates the genetic gains from selective breeding across large populations cheaply and with lower disease risk than moving live animals.

## Health, welfare, and disease

Productive animals depend on adequate nutrition, hygiene, and disease control. Veterinary practice relies on vaccines and antibiotics, but routine antibiotic use is declining because resistant bacteria can pass back to humans, one expression of the wider concern over zoonoses, diseases transmissible from animals to people, such as avian flu and rabies. The 1999 Nipah virus outbreak in Malaysia traced the path clearly: fruit bats passed the virus to pigs, which amplified it and passed it to humans, showing how a single farm interface can move a wildlife pathogen into the human population. Governments respond to outbreaks with quarantines and movement controls. Welfare debates center on a recurring tension between using animals for food and labor and minimising their suffering, and these debates have driven laws on housing, transport, and slaughter in many countries.

## Environmental footprint

The environmental costs of animal husbandry are large and concentrated in a few channels. Cattle produce on the order of 570 million cubic metres of methane per day, roughly 35–40% of global methane emissions, a significant lever for climate because methane is a potent greenhouse gas. Livestock are responsible for about 65% of all human-related emissions of nitrous oxide, mainly from manure and fertilised feed crops. About 70% of agricultural land and 30% of Earth's land surface is used directly or indirectly for animal husbandry, and the resulting demand for pasture and feed crops drives deforestation, especially in the Amazon, and is a leading contributor to the ongoing loss of species driven by human activity, often called the Holocene extinction. Aquaculture, the cultivation of fish and shellfish, has expanded as wild fisheries have been depleted by overharvesting, but it carries its own pollution and habitat costs.

## Culture, economy, and open questions

Beyond commodities, animals serve as transport (horses, oxen, camels), security (dogs), and companions, roles that shape cultural imagery of farming, often at odds with industrial reality. The Columbian Exchange after 1492 redistributed livestock worldwide, moving Old World species to the Americas and New World species such as the turkey back to Europe and Africa. The field now faces unresolved questions: whether productivity can be sustained while cutting greenhouse gases and water use, how to weigh growth-promoting antibiotic use against antimicrobial resistance, whether humane treatment is sufficient or animals have rights that preclude use, where to draw the line on what counts as livestock as insects, fish, and reptiles enter commercial production, and whether consumer pressure and regulation will push intensive systems toward more sustainable and humane models. A 2003 New Zealand ban on live animal exports, introduced for welfare reasons, illustrates the kind of policy lever these debates are producing.
