# Biological dispersal

Biological dispersal is the movement of organisms or their propagules (any structure capable of producing a new individual, such as a seed, spore, egg, or larva) away from their origin, in a way that can lead to gene flow (the transfer of genetic variation between populations through migration and breeding). It covers two distinct journeys: natal dispersal, the move from birth site to first breeding site, and breeding dispersal, the move between successive breeding sites. The underlying process has three phases, departure, transfer, and settlement, each carrying its own fitness (an organism's reproductive success measured by the genes it passes on) costs and benefits.

## Passive and active dispersal

Dispersal splits into two broad types. In passive dispersal, the organism cannot move itself; it exploits kinetic energy already present in the environment. Wind, water currents, and other animals carry seeds, spores, larvae, and gametes (reproductive cells such as sperm and eggs). In active dispersal, the organism moves under its own power, using flight, swimming, or walking. The extent of active dispersal depends on local population density, resource competition, habitat quality, and habitat size, and is therefore often density-dependent: how many individuals leave a patch depends on how crowded it is. Walking costs more energy than flying or swimming, so terrestrial animals face the strongest barriers.

## Why organisms disperse: costs and benefits

Dispersal occurs when the benefits of moving exceed the costs. Benefits include reaching new resources, escaping unfavourable conditions, avoiding competition with siblings, and avoiding inbreeding depression (reduced fitness caused by breeding with close relatives, which brings harmful mutations to light). The costs are paid in four currencies: energy used in travel and in building dispersal machinery such as wings; risk of injury, death, or settling in a poor site; time lost from growth and reproduction; and opportunity costs, including the chance of outbreeding depression (reduced fitness from breeding with individuals so different that offspring are poorly adapted to either parent's environment) or, in social animals, loss of social rank in a new group.

## Range, barriers, and consequences

Dispersal range is the distance a species can move from an existing population, and is usually smaller than the species' full distribution because of barriers. Natural barriers include mountain ranges and rivers; the Congo River separates the two chimpanzee species. Human activity cuts range through habitat fragmentation, while it can also extend range through new vectors such as ship ballast water (water carried in ships' tanks to stabilise them, which transports aquatic organisms across oceans), cars, shoes, and tunnels, often producing invasive species, sometimes beneficial ones like honeybees and earthworms.

Plants rely on five main seed-dispersal modes: gravity, wind, ballistic (mechanical ejection from the parent plant), water, and animals. Sessile (permanently attached) marine invertebrates such as sponges, corals, and bryozoans release gametes or larvae into the water; the three-dimensional water column brings food within reach, which is why stationary life is feasible for them. Many freshwater invertebrates travel as dormant eggs, embryos, or resistant adults, surviving desiccation, digestive enzymes, and temperature extremes while hitching rides on birds or in wind currents.

Dispersal has consequences beyond the individual. It links sub-populations into metapopulations (networks of local populations connected by occasional migration), allowing recolonisation after local extinction and stabilising ecosystems. Over evolutionary time, geographic separation created by dispersal barriers is a primary route to allopatric speciation (the formation of new species when populations are isolated by a physical barrier and diverge genetically). When climate shifts, long-lived specialists such as the Southern Rockhopper Penguin, whose foraging behaviour took years to shape, are predicted to respond by shifting their range rather than by evolving.

## Quantifying dispersal

Ecologists measure dispersal in two ways. The dispersal rate is the probability that an individual leaves a patch. The dispersal kernel is the probability distribution of distances travelled by those that do; empirical data are usually best fit by leptokurtic, fat-tailed distributions (curves with a sharp central peak and heavy tails, meaning most movements are short but a few are very long), often modelled with inverse power or exponential power functions. These long tails are what allow occasional individuals to colonise distant habitat and drive rapid range expansion.
