# Biodiversity loss

Biodiversity is the variety of genes, species, and ecosystems in a region. Biodiversity loss occurs when species disappear from a place, either locally or forever through extinction, and ecosystems simplify. The current rate of loss runs roughly 100 to 1,000 times faster than the natural background rate and is faster than at any other time in human history. Many scientists call this the sixth mass extinction, or Holocene extinction.

## Five direct drivers

Conservation biology groups the immediate causes into the acronym HIPPO, coined by biologist E. O. Wilson: Habitat destruction, Invasive species, Pollution, human over-Population, and Overharvesting. The 2019 IPBES Global Assessment ranked them for land and sea.

| Driver | How it operates | Where it dominates |
|---|---|---|
| Habitat loss, fragmentation, degradation | Clearing forests, draining wetlands, and converting land to farms, cities, roads, or mines | Largest single driver on land; about 70% of projected terrestrial biodiversity loss is tied to agriculture |
| Overexploitation | Hunting, fishing, and harvesting wild species faster than they can recover | Leading driver in oceans; fish and marine mammal biomass is down about 60% since the 1800s |
| Pollution | Nutrient runoff, pesticides, plastics, noise, light, and acidifying emissions damage water, soil, and air | Drives eutrophication, dead zones, and insect declines |
| Invasive species | Non-native organisms that outcompete, prey on, or hybridise with locals, homogenising communities | Especially damaging on islands and in fresh water; spreading with global trade |
| Climate change | Warming, shifting seasons, and extreme weather push species outside their climate niches; ocean acidification bleaches coral | Currently smaller than habitat loss, but rising fast |

Infrastructure cuts across these drivers: roads, pipelines, and lights fragment habitat, kill animals directly, and block species from migrating to track suitable climates.

## How much has been lost

About 28% of the 134,400 species assessed by the IUCN Red List were classified as threatened with extinction in 2021, up from roughly 16,000 species in 2006. The WWF Living Planet Report found an average 68 to 69% decline in monitored wildlife populations between 1970 and the late 2010s, with freshwater populations hit hardest. As of 2018, wild land mammals made up just 4% of mammalian biomass on Earth (the total weight of all wild mammal individuals), compared with 60% for livestock and 36% for humans. On current trends, 70 to 90% of warm-water coral reefs will disappear even if warming is held to 1.5°C, and Caribbean reefs could be lost by 2100.

Insects illustrate the speed. German nature reserves recorded about 75% loss of flying insect biomass over 26 years, roughly 5.2% per year, and global estimates suggest 10 to 40% of insect species are at risk, though some groups remain poorly studied.

## The debate over the ultimate driver

Most scientists, including the IPBES report, identify a growing human population combined with rising per-capita consumption as the underlying driver. A 2022 study argued that conservation efforts will continue to fail without addressing population size. Others counter that habitat loss is driven mainly by the growth of export commodities, especially soy and palm oil for livestock feed and biofuel, tied to GDP and wealth rather than population size. Both sides agree that all contemporary biodiversity loss is caused by human activity; only the proximate mix is disputed.

## Why it matters

Biodiversity underpins the ecosystem services humans depend on: crop pollination, water and air purification, soil fertility, flood buffering, carbon storage, and a reservoir of genetic material for medicines. The WHO estimates about 60,000 plant species are used medicinally. The loss of one species can ripple through food webs, triggering coextinctions, and the FAO's 2019 report warned that the genetic, species, and ecosystem diversity underpinning agriculture is in decline.

## What is being done

The 2010 Aichi Biodiversity Targets under the UN Convention on Biological Diversity largely failed: only six of twenty goals were partially achieved by 2020. In December 2022, every country except the United States and the Holy See signed the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, which sets a "30 by 30" goal of protecting 30% of land and sea by 2030. The targets are not legally binding, and as of signing, only 17% of land and 10% of ocean were formally protected. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity_loss
