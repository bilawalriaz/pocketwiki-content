# Adoptive cell transfer

Adoptive cell transfer (ACT) is a medical therapy in which living cells, usually immune cells taken from the patient or a donor, are grown, modified, or selected outside the body, then infused back into the patient to fight disease. Cancer is the dominant target.

## Core idea

Cells that should already be fighting the cancer are removed, expanded or engineered into more potent versions, and returned. In autologous therapy, T cells (white blood cells that coordinate and kill abnormal cells) are taken from the patient and returned to the same individual. In allogeneic therapy, cells come from a donor. Most modern work uses T cells: CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, which directly kill target cells, and CD4+ helper T cells, which coordinate immune responses.

## The three strategies

**Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs):** T cells already inside a resected tumor, harvested and expanded in interleukin-2 (IL-2, a T cell growth factor). These naturally recognize the cancer but are too few to control it.

**T cell receptor (TCR) engineering:** A patient's normal T cells receive a new TCR gene that recognizes a specific tumor antigen, such as MART-1 in melanoma or NY-ESO-1 in sarcoma.

**Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells:** A patient's T cells receive a synthetic receptor built from an antibody's antigen-binding fragment fused to intracellular T cell signaling domains. CARs recognize surface proteins directly, without needing the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) that TCRs require. The 1989 work by Zelig Eshhar first showed a T cell's targeting receptor could be swapped, the foundation of all CAR-T therapy.

CAR-T cells against CD19, a protein on most B cell malignancies and on healthy B cells, produced the first dramatic, reproducible human regressions, demonstrated in 2010 in B cell lymphoma and extended to acute lymphoblastic leukemia, where complete response rates of 67–100% have been reported.

## How a treatment course works

A melanoma TIL course illustrates the process. A resected tumor is digested into fragments and grown in IL-2. Lymphocytes overgrow the tumor cells within 2–3 weeks, yielding pure cultures. These are tested against the patient's tumor, then massively expanded with IL-2 plus anti-CD3 antibody, which activates T cells by engaging the CD3 component of the TCR complex. After 5–6 weeks, up to 10^11 lymphocytes are ready. Before infusion, the patient receives a lymphodepleting chemotherapy (cyclophosphamide 60 mg/kg for 2 days and fludarabine 25 mg/m² for 5 days) that empties immune niches, letting the transferred cells engraft, expand, and persist. IL-2 at 720,000 IU/kg is infused with the cells to support survival.

## Why youth matters

CD8+ T cells progress from naive through stem cell memory and central memory to effector memory and terminally differentiated effectors, and antitumor potency declines along that path. T cells that look most active in a dish, producing interferon-γ and lysing targets, often do worse in patients than less differentiated, stem-like cells that persist and keep dividing. Patient age also correlates negatively with response, since older patients supply fewer naive T cells. CD4+ helper T cells, including Th17 cells, can independently drive tumor rejection and support CD8+ function.

## Solid tumors and limits

Blood cancers responded first because they offer clean targets like CD19. Solid tumors present three barriers: few antigens confined to the tumor, since most candidates also sit on essential normal tissues; physical stromal barriers that block T cell entry; and a suppressive tumor microenvironment.

## Manufacturing and approvals

Each course is patient-specific: cells are grown, tested, and released under current Good Manufacturing Practice (cGMP), the regulatory standard for clinical-grade biological products. Early engineered T cell lots cost about $75,000 to manufacture. As of February 2024, 27 advanced cell therapy products were FDA-approved, including six CAR-T products (Kymriah, Yescarta, Tecartus, Breyanzi, Abecma, Carvykti), several hematopoietic stem cell products, and gene-modified cell therapies.

## Safety

On-target/off-tumor toxicity strikes when the chosen antigen exists on healthy tissue. High-avidity TCRs against MART-1 or gp100 damaged melanocytes, and an anti-ERBB2 CAR caused patient deaths. Cross-reactivity is unpredictable: an HLA-A*0201–restricted MAGE-A3 TCR also recognized a related brain epitope and destroyed gray matter. Cytokine release syndrome, a systemic inflammatory reaction triggered when infused T cells kill large numbers of tumor cells and release cytokines, has killed trial participants. B cell loss after CD19 therapy is managed with periodic immunoglobulin infusions, and suicide switches let clinicians eliminate engineered T cells in emergencies.

Regulatory T cells, which suppress rather than activate immune responses, have been transferred in type 1 diabetes and rheumatoid arthritis to restore tolerance.
