# On the Origin of Species

Charles Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859) introduced the theory that populations change over generations through natural selection, accounting for the diversity of life through common descent from shared ancestors. The first printing of 1,250 copies sold out immediately and the book became the foundation of evolutionary biology.

## The Core Theory

Darwin's argument rests on three observations and one inference:

1. Populations breed beyond the resources that can support them, yet numbers stay roughly stable, producing a *struggle for existence* among individuals competing for limited food, shelter, and mates.
2. Individuals within a species differ from one another (*variation*), and much of that variation is inherited.
3. In that struggle, individuals whose inherited traits suit their environment survive and reproduce at higher rates, passing those traits to offspring.

The inference: when this differential survival acts generation after generation, populations gradually adapt, and over long stretches of time they split into new species. Darwin called the process **natural selection**, using the *artificial selection* practiced by animal and plant breeders as his everyday analogy. The same logic that lets a pigeon fancier turn one rock dove into many breeds can, given vast spans of time and no human hand, turn a single ancestral form into a branching tree of species.

## How Darwin Got There

Darwin spent more than twenty years gathering evidence. He returned from the 1831–1836 *Beagle* voyage convinced that species change. In 1838, reading Thomas Malthus on population gave him the key insight: competition for limited resources is what makes some inherited variants more successful than others. A 230-page essay of 1844 set out the theory privately. Darwin might have delayed publication indefinitely had Alfred Russel Wallace not independently sent him a near-identical theory in 1858. Their papers were read jointly at the Linnean Society, and Darwin then condensed his notes into the book-length "abstract" that became *Origin*.

## The Book's Argument

*Origin* is written for general readers, not specialists. Darwin opens with the mechanism, using domestic breeding to make selection intuitive, then marshals evidence from the fossil record, the geographic distribution of species, embryology, and comparative anatomy to support a branching *tree of life* in which all organisms share common ancestors. He deliberately avoids applying his theory to humans, only remarking that his work would "throw light on the origin of man." *The Descent of Man* (1871) made the application explicit.

## Reception and Aftermath

The book drew sharp scientific, philosophical, and religious criticism, even as many scientists accepted evolution itself by the 1870s. The 1860 Oxford debate between Bishop Wilberforce and T. H. Huxley became its most famous public clash. Two serious objections took decades to answer:

- **Heredity.** Darwin knew nothing of the laws of inheritance, and critics such as engineer Fleeming Jenkin argued that favourable variations would be diluted by blending with ordinary individuals. The problem was resolved when Mendelian genetics was rediscovered in the early twentieth century.
- **Time.** Physicist Lord Kelvin calculated an Earth too young for gradual evolution, an estimate overturned after radioactivity was discovered.

From the 1880s to the 1930s many biologists accepted that life had evolved but rejected natural selection in favour of alternatives such as Lamarckism (the inheritance of acquired traits) and orthogenesis, a period called the *eclipse of Darwinism*. Full acceptance of selection came with the **modern evolutionary synthesis** of the 1930s–40s, which wedded Darwin's mechanism of differential survival to Mendelian genetics and produced the framework still used in biology today. The theory's prestige also led to its misappropriation by movements such as Social Darwinism, a use Darwin neither endorsed nor controlled.
