# Anatomical plane

An anatomical plane is an imaginary flat surface that transects the body so that the location of structures, the direction of movement, or the orientation of medical images can be described precisely. Planes are a coordinate system, not physical cuts.

## The three principal planes

In human anatomy three orthogonal planes organise nearly every spatial description, all defined for a body in the standard anatomical position, which is standing upright and facing forward.

- Sagittal plane: vertical, runs front to back, divides the body into left and right portions. Any vertical slice parallel to the midline counts as sagittal, including the midline slice itself.
- Median (midsagittal) plane: the single sagittal plane that passes through the midline, dividing the body into mirror-image left and right halves and passing through the navel and the spine.
- Coronal (frontal) plane: vertical, runs side to side, divides the body into front (anterior, or ventral) from back (posterior, or dorsal).
- Transverse (axial, or horizontal) plane: horizontal, parallel to the ground, divides the body into upper (superior) from lower (inferior), head from feet.

A parasagittal plane is any sagittal plane that is not the median and produces unequal left and right sections. The word "sagittal" comes from Latin for "like an arrow", a reference either to the spine dividing the body into equal halves or to the arrow-shaped sagittal suture of the skull.

## Planes and motion

When describing movement, a plane names the axis of action. A person jumping straight up and down moves through a transverse plane, with the motion itself running in the coronal and sagittal planes. A longitudinal plane is any plane perpendicular to the transverse, so the coronal and sagittal planes are both longitudinal.

## Quadrupeds and rodents

In animals with a horizontal spine the orientations of the coronal and transverse planes switch. The coronal plane is then called the dorsal plane, separating upper (dorsal, toward the backbone) from lower (ventral, toward the belly), while the transverse plane separates front from rear. The sagittal plane and the body's length axis are unchanged.

In rodent neuroscience, brain sections are named by analogy with the human brain rather than by the animal's true body axes. Because the human brain's length axis is rotated roughly 90 degrees ventrally relative to the body axis, a section that is geometrically transverse in a rat is often called a coronal brain section, and vice versa. The brain's internal flexures (cervical, cephalic, pontine) bend this axis further, so a section plane should always be paired with the brain region it crosses, for example transverse to the midbrain.

## Medical imaging

Cross-sectional imaging such as CT, MRI, sonography, and PET follows the DICOM coordinate standard. With the body in the anatomical position, the x-axis runs right to left, the y-axis front to back, and the z-axis toe to head. This is a right-handed Cartesian system, meaning the axes follow the right-hand rule so the cross product of x and y points along z. Plane names are applied relative to this reference, so equipment axes that do not match the body's orientation must be reconciled before reading the image.

## Reference lines and landmarks

Planes extend into imaginary lines drawn on the body's surface for locating internal structures without a scan. Examples include the midaxillary line, dropping vertically from the armpit apex, with the anterior and posterior axillary lines parallel to it; the mid-clavicular line, dropping from the midpoint of the clavicle; and named transverse planes such as the transpyloric plane, which passes through the pylorus. Spinal levels, such as the fourth cervical vertebra, are used the same way.
