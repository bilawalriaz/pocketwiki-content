# Sickle cell disease

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a group of inherited blood disorders caused by a mutation in the gene for β-globin, one of the protein chains that make up haemoglobin, the oxygen-carrying molecule inside red blood cells. Under low-oxygen conditions, the abnormal haemoglobin (haemoglobin S, HbS) polymerises into long rigid fibres that distort red blood cells from their flexible disc shape into stiff crescents. These sickled cells lodge in small blood vessels, cutting off blood flow and damaging tissues.

## Inheritance

SCD follows autosomal recessive inheritance: a child must inherit two mutated copies of the HBB gene, one from each parent, to develop the disease. A person with one mutated copy has sickle cell *trait* and usually has no symptoms, though extreme conditions (high altitude, severe dehydration) can trigger sickling. When both parents carry the trait, each child has a 25% chance of having the disease, a 50% chance of being a carrier, and a 25% chance of inheriting no mutation.

The molecular defect is a single nucleotide change that substitutes valine for glutamate at position 6 of the β-globin chain. Several haplotypes exist (Cameroon, Senegal, Benin, Bantu, Saudi-Asian); the Senegal and Saudi-Asian variants are associated with higher foetal haemoglobin and milder disease.

## Clinical picture

Symptoms typically begin around 5–6 months of age, as foetal haemoglobin (HbF) declines and HbS becomes dominant. The central features are:

- **Vaso-occlusive crisis (VOC):** severe pain, usually in the limbs, back, or chest, caused by sickled cells blocking capillaries. Triggers include cold, dehydration, stress, and high altitude.
- **Chronic haemolytic anaemia:** sickled cells last 10–20 days instead of the normal 90–120, so the bone marrow cannot keep up with destruction. Jaundice and fatigue follow.
- **Splenic dysfunction:** repeated sickling scars and atrophies the spleen, usually before age 5, leaving patients vulnerable to encapsulated bacteria, especially *Streptococcus pneumoniae*.
- **Acute chest syndrome:** a VOC in the lungs with fever, chest pain, and hypoxia, accounting for roughly 25% of SCD deaths.
- **Aplastic crisis:** sudden worsening of anaemia triggered by parvovirus B19, which temporarily halts red blood cell production.
- **Chronic organ damage:** stroke (10–15% of children, silent strokes more common), avascular necrosis of bones, kidney failure, pulmonary hypertension, retinopathy, leg ulcers, and priapism.

## Why the gene persists: malaria

The sickle mutation is common in people with ancestry from sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East, India, and the Mediterranean. Heterozygous carriers are protected against severe *Plasmodium falciparum* malaria: parasite growth is impaired inside their red cells, and infected cells are cleared by the spleen. Because heterozygotes survive malaria better while homozygous individuals still suffer SCD, the mutation persists in malarial regions, a classic heterozygote advantage. About 80% of SCD cases occur in sub-Saharan Africa.

## Diagnosis and treatment

Newborn screening is routine in the US, UK, and many other countries, typically a heel-prick blood test followed by haemoglobin electrophoresis or HPLC to identify HbS. Antenatal diagnosis is possible through chorionic villus sampling.

Management combines prevention and crisis treatment:

| Goal | Approach |
|---|---|
| Prevent infection | Daily penicillin through childhood, full vaccination schedule |
| Reduce sickling triggers | High fluid intake, avoid cold, dehydration, high altitude |
| Reduce crises | Hydroxyurea (hydroxycarbamide): reactivates foetal haemoglobin, cuts VOC frequency by ~44% |
| Treat anaemia and crises | Blood transfusions (simple or exchange); exchange for emergencies |
| Prevent stroke | Transcranial Doppler screening; chronic transfusion when blood flow is abnormal |
| Cure | Allogeneic bone marrow transplant, or gene therapy (exagamglogene autotemcel, brand name Casgevy, approved 2023), which edits the patient's own stem cells to switch on foetal haemoglobin |

Casgevy works by CRISPR-editing the BCL11A gene to lift the natural brake on foetal haemoglobin, so red cells produce HbF instead of HbS.

## Prognosis and burden

In high-income countries, median life expectancy now reaches roughly 43 years in the US and 67 years in the UK. In low-resource settings, 50–90% of affected children still die before age 5. As of 2021, an estimated 7.7 million people live with SCD, directly causing about 34,000 deaths annually and contributing to a further 376,000. Roughly 150,000 affected children are born each year in Nigeria alone, where carrier frequency reaches 24%, and gene therapies such as Casgevy remain accessible only to a small fraction of patients worldwide.
