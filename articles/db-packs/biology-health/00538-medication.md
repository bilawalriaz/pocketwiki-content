# Medication

A medication is a substance used to diagnose, cure, treat, or prevent disease. Drug therapy, called pharmacotherapy, depends on pharmacology for how drugs act on the body and on pharmacy for how they are prepared and supplied. A placebo looks like a medication but contains no active ingredient and is used in research to separate a drug's pharmacological effect from the patient's expectation.

Drugs are sorted in overlapping ways: by control level (prescription, over-the-counter, or behind-the-counter sold only by a pharmacist), by the organ system they act on, by route of administration, and by therapeutic effect. Two foundational categories run through the field. Small molecule drugs are low-molecular-weight organic compounds, usually made by chemical synthesis, that regulate a biological process. Biological medical products come from biological sources and include recombinant proteins, vaccines, blood products, and gene or cell therapies. The Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) system organises drugs worldwide by the organ or system acted on, therapeutic use, and chemical properties.

## Discovery and development

Historically, medicines came from plant remedies or chance discovery. Classical pharmacology tested large libraries of chemicals in whole animals. Reverse pharmacology, practical after the human genome was sequenced, starts with a single biological target thought to drive a disease and screens compounds against that target in isolation. The first step is a hit with some activity; researchers then optimise it for affinity (binding strength), selectivity (so it does not act on unintended targets, which causes side effects), metabolic stability (half-life), and oral bioavailability (how much of an oral dose reaches the bloodstream). The output of successful discovery is a patent on a new molecular entity (NME), a novel active substance not previously approved for marketing. Clinical trials in Phases I–III then test safety and efficacy in humans, and most candidates fail. Small firms often discover leads, then license or sell them to large firms with the resources to run full trials.

Development turns a lead into a product: formulation into pills, injections, or other dosage forms; preclinical research in cells and animals for safety; clinical trials in humans; regulatory review by agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA); and post-market surveillance, the ongoing safety monitoring that continues after a drug is on sale. Biological products follow the same legal pathway but differ in manufacturing because they are made by biological rather than purely chemical processes. As of 2010, the average research and development cost per approved NME was estimated at about US$1.8 billion.

## Regulation and access

Regulation aims to protect public health by ensuring that marketed drugs are safe, of high quality, and actually work. The European Union defines a "medicinal product" as any substance presented for treating or preventing disease or for modifying physiological functions through pharmacological, immunological, or metabolic action. U.S. law defines a "drug" as a substance other than food intended to affect the body's structure or function or to diagnose, cure, treat, or prevent disease. Most jurisdictions require registration before a drug can be sold, and the International Narcotics Control Board enforces global prohibitions on controlled substances.

A physician may legally prescribe an approved drug for an unapproved indication, dose, or patient group; this is off-label use. Medical errors include over-prescription, polypharmacy (the simultaneous use of many drugs by one patient, which raises the risk of harmful interactions), and ambiguous dosing instructions.

In the United States, prices are set by negotiations between manufacturers and insurers and are among the highest in the world, attributed to government-granted monopolies from patents and to rising research costs. A blockbuster drug, whose manufacturer earns more than US$1 billion per year from it, almost always targets a highly prevalent chronic condition so that long-term use sustains sales.

## History

Drug use is ancient. The Kahun Gynaecological Papyrus, from about 1800 BC in Egypt, is the earliest known record. *De materia medica*, written by Pedanius Dioscorides around 50–70 CE, remained the standard pharmacological text for more than fifteen centuries. In medieval Islamic scholarship, Al-Kindi and Ibn Sina cataloged drugs, with Avicenna's *The Canon of Medicine* the most influential. Gerhard Domagk introduced the first sulfa antibiotics in 1932, and mass production of penicillin during the Second World War made antimicrobial therapy routine. The U.S. Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938, passed after the Elixir Sulfanilamide disaster killed more than a hundred people, required safety filings before marketing. The 1951 Humphrey-Durham Amendment required a prescription for certain drugs. The 1962 Kefauver-Harris Amendment, written after the thalidomide birth-defect tragedy in Europe, required proof of efficacy as well as safety.

## Controversies

"Compassionate use" or "expanded access" programs (treatment INDs in the United States, Named Patient Programs elsewhere) let patients with serious conditions and no alternatives try drugs before approval. The TRIPS international trade agreement protects drug patents, while the Doha Declaration lets governments issue compulsory licenses, which are government authorisations that allow generic production of a patented drug without the patent holder's consent, balancing innovation incentives against public health. Critics argue that drug marketing budgets often exceed research budgets, undermining the claim that high prices are needed to fund innovation.

Pharmaceuticals have been measured in water and soil worldwide at nanograms to micrograms per litre, from municipal and industrial wastewater and from antibiotics running off farmland. They are classed as Environmentally Persistent Pharmaceutical Pollutants (EPPPs): inherently biodegradable, but constantly replenished, so they behave as if permanent, a state called pseudopersistence. They accumulate in aquatic species and act as Ecological Disrupting Compounds, with slow, food-web-level effects, and regulators have not yet agreed on how to set limits for them.
