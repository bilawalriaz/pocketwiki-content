# Human body

The human body is a hierarchical biological system: chemical elements assemble into cells, cells into tissues, tissues into organs, and organs into systems that together maintain a stable internal environment called **homeostasis**—a steady state of blood sugar, oxygen, pH, temperature, and pressure. An adult is roughly 60% water (about 42 liters), houses around 30 trillion human cells alongside some 38 trillion bacteria, and depends on DNA-directed protein synthesis to function and repair itself. Studying the body divides into anatomy (structure), physiology (function), histology (tissues), and embryology (development).

## The cellular foundation

Every cell's activity is governed by DNA in the nucleus. Segments of DNA are transcribed into RNA, which directs protein synthesis; proteins carry out work and regulate which genes are active, letting cells self-tune their output. Mature red blood cells are an exception: they lack a nucleus and DNA, which is why they cannot divide or repair themselves. Each cell endures tens of thousands of DNA damage events daily, and unrepaired or misrepaired damage can produce mutations that threaten the cell.

Tissues are groups of cells specialised for a shared task. Four principal types exist: **epithelia** lining surfaces and cavities (including endothelium lining blood vessels), **connective tissue** providing support, **nerve tissue** for signalling, and **muscle tissue** for contraction. Organs are organised collections of tissues with specific roles, packed into body cavities: the thoracic cavity holds the heart and lungs, the abdominal cavity holds the stomach, liver, spleen, and intestines, and the skin is the major external organ.

## Organ systems

The body coordinates its functions through eleven major systems.

The **circulatory system**—heart and blood vessels—runs two loops: a pulmonary circuit carrying blood between heart and lungs for gas exchange, and a systemic circuit serving the rest of the body. Blood cells originate in bone marrow and the spleen.

The **digestive system** (mouth, esophagus, stomach, intestines, liver, pancreas, gallbladder, salivary glands) breaks food into absorbable molecules. **Peristalsis**—rhythmic smooth-muscle contraction—propels contents; mechanical and enzymatic digestion begins in the mouth, gastric acid forms chyme (a semi-liquid mixture) in the stomach, the small intestine absorbs nutrients, and the large intestine compacts waste.

The **respiratory system** (nose, pharynx, trachea, lungs) moves air by diaphragm contraction into **alveoli**—microscopic air sacs where oxygen diffuses into blood and carbon dioxide and water vapour exit.

The **nervous system** divides structurally into the central nervous system (brain and spinal cord) and the peripheral nervous system (nerves and ganglia outside the CNS), and functionally into the somatic (voluntary movement) and autonomic (involuntary regulation) branches. Neurons and glial cells form the signalling network; the brain governs thought, memory, sensory processing, and system control.

The **endocrine system** uses hormones as inter-system signals. Principal glands—pituitary, thyroid, adrenals, pancreas, parathyroids, and gonads—coordinate functions such as blood pressure, metabolism, and growth.

The **musculoskeletal system** (skeleton plus attached muscles) provides structure and movement. Long bones contain marrow for blood-cell production; all bones store calcium and phosphate.

The **integumentary system** (skin, hair, nails, glands) provides containment, protection, and a major sensory interface.

The **immune system** (white blood cells, thymus, lymph nodes) distinguishes self from non-self and neutralises pathogens using antibodies, **cytokines** (signalling proteins), and toll-like receptors (pattern-recognition proteins).

The **lymphatic system** extracts, transports, and metabolises interstitial fluid, structurally analogous to the circulatory system but carrying lymph.

The **urinary system** (two kidneys, ureters, bladder, urethra) filters about 170 liters of blood daily through **nephrons**—the kidney's microscopic filtering units—returning most of it to the bloodstream and excreting 1–2 liters as urine containing urea, creatinine, and excess ions.

The **reproductive system** produces germ cells and sex hormones. A female is born with around 70,000 immature oocytes, falling to about 40,000 at puberty; no new oocytes form later. A male's testes produce sperm continuously from puberty onward.

## A closer look at two organs

The **heart** sits in the thoracic cavity between the lungs, enclosed by the pericardium. Two atria receive blood; two ventricles pump it—the right ventricle to the lungs, the left to the body. An electrical conduction system (sinoatrial node then atrioventricular node) coordinates contraction. The atria act as collection chambers that keep venous blood flowing into the heart during ventricular contraction, so enough blood is on hand when the atria contract; this arrangement raises cardiac output by roughly 75% compared with a heart lacking atria. Coronary artery disease—plaque narrowing the coronary arteries—is the leading global cause of death at 16% of all deaths, with risk factors including obesity, smoking, high cholesterol, hypertension, inactivity, and diabetes.

The **gallbladder**, a pear-shaped sac behind the liver's right lobe, stores bile and releases it into the duodenum to emulsify fats. Gallstones are common and can obstruct bile flow, causing biliary colic, nausea, and vomiting; the standard treatment is cholecystectomy (surgical removal). Although gallbladder cancer is rare, it is rapidly fatal when missed.

## How systems integrate

The **neuroendocrine system** illustrates integration: nerves send impulses and neurotransmitters to the brain while endocrine glands release hormones, and together they regulate the internal environment. Claude Bernard's 19th-century concept of the *milieu interieur* (the extracellular fluid bathing every cell) and Walter Cannon's later coinage of *homeostasis* capture the same insight, that the body actively defends a stable internal chemistry.

## Development and study

Development starts at fertilization, when a sperm penetrates an ovum. The resulting zygote implants in the uterus, becoming an embryo, then a fetus until birth; postnatal growth then unfolds through childhood, adolescence, adulthood, and old age, driven by genetic, hormonal, and environmental factors.

The study of the body has a long history: Hippocrates initiated physiology in Ancient Greece around 420 BCE, and Aristotle emphasised the link between structure and function. Galen of Pergamum (2nd century CE) compiled classical anatomy and pioneered experimentation on body functions. Andreas Vesalius (1514–1564) established modern anatomy through human dissection, publishing *De humani corporis fabrica*. William Harvey described the circulatory system in 1628, Schleiden and Schwann formulated cell theory in 1838, and modern imaging (MRI, CT, ultrasound) now reveals internal structure in living patients. Whether the central nervous system directly generates consciousness remains an open question, and cell-count estimates (~30 trillion human, ~38 trillion bacterial) carry real uncertainty.
