# Plant disease epidemiology

Plant disease epidemiology is the study of disease in plant populations. Like diseases of humans and animals, plant diseases are caused by pathogens: bacteria, viruses, fungi, oomycetes, nematodes, phytoplasmas, protozoa, and parasitic plants. Epidemiologists aim to understand cause and effect and to design interventions that keep crop losses acceptable relative to the crop's value.

The field is multi-disciplinary. Biology explains the pathogen's life cycle and the crop's physiology. Agronomy shapes whether farming practices suppress or encourage disease. Ecology matters because wild plants can act as reservoirs of crop pathogens. Statistics summarises this complexity and lets researchers compare disease-progress patterns across cultivars, climates, and management strategies. Policy adds another lever, for example import restrictions that keep a pathogen out of regions that lack it.

The modern theoretical framework dates from 1963, when J. E. van der Plank published *Plant Diseases: Epidemics and Control*. Drawing on experiments across many host-pathogen systems, the book gave researchers tools to model epidemics and estimate thresholds, especially for fungal foliar diseases in uniform monocultural fields.

## The disease triangle and time

The classic disease triangle names the three elements required for any plant disease: a susceptible host, a pathogen, and a conducive environment. If any one is absent, no disease occurs. Corn planted into residue harbouring *Cercospora zea-maydis*, the fungus that causes grey leaf spot, stays healthy in a dry spell because its spores need leaf wetness to germinate. The same corn in a ploughed, residue-free field stays healthy in a wet spell because the pathogen is missing.

Time turns a one-off infection into an epidemic. An epidemic requires all three elements to persist, and any of them can be interrupted: the host may outgrow susceptibility through high-temperature adult-plant resistance, a form of ontogenic resistance where maturing tissue becomes less vulnerable; the weather may shift; or a fungicide may remove the pathogen. If a pathogen depends on a vector, that vector must also be plentiful and active.

## Types of epidemics

Epidemics are classified by how many infection cycles a pathogen completes per season. Monocyclic pathogens, typical of soil-borne diseases such as Fusarium wilt of flax, complete one cycle per season. Polycyclic pathogens, typical of airborne diseases such as powdery mildew, complete many; brown rot of stone fruits is bimodal, infecting blossoms and later fruits. Polyetic epidemics build across multiple seasons because inoculum carries over, common in perennials or in the tropics where growing seasons blur. Apple powdery mildew is polyetic and polycyclic; Dutch elm disease is polyetic and monocyclic.

## Detecting disease

Detection methods are either destructive or non-destructive. Non-destructive methods are preferred because they leave the plant intact and can be repeated.

Four non-destructive families dominate. Image processing extracts colour, texture, and shape features from photographs after segmenting the image into diseased and healthy tissue. Imaging-based methods include fluorescence imaging, which probes the metabolic state of chlorophyll, and hyperspectral imaging, which uses spectral information divergence to compare reflectance across wavelengths. Spectroscopy covers visible, infrared, fluorescence, and electrical-impedance techniques that read how matter interacts with electromagnetic radiation. Remote sensing extends these ideas from the field to the landscape, with hyperspectral sensors giving fine spectral and spatial resolution and multispectral sensors grading severity at larger scales.

As of 2015 the persistent gap was the shortage of antibody- and molecular-marker tests for new pathogens and for known pathogens in new hosts, alongside weak global integration of quarantine and surveillance.

## The plant immune system

Plants lack mobile defender cells and a somatic adaptive immune system, so they rely on innate immunity in every cell plus systemic signals. Visible symptoms of infection range from obvious rusts and moulds to no change at all in some viral diseases.

The innate system has two branches. The first uses pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on the cell surface to detect broadly conserved microbial molecules (PAMPs or MAMPs), triggering PAMP-triggered immunity (PTI). The second uses resistance (R) proteins inside the cell to detect specific pathogen effectors, triggering effector-triggered immunity (ETI). Both PTI and ETI release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and act through ion-channel gating, oxidative bursts, shifts in cellular redox state, and protein-kinase cascades to reprogram transcription and restrict colonisation.

## Consequences

Through 2013, invasive tree diseases had killed roughly 100 million elm trees across the United Kingdom and United States, and 3.5 billion American chestnut trees. The potato late blight epidemic caused by *Phytophthora infestans* drove the Great Irish Famine, and Dutch elm disease showed how an introduced pathogen can functionally eliminate a tree species.
