# Anatomy

Anatomy is the study of the structure of organisms—their organs, tissues, and the relationships between them. The word comes from Greek *anatomē*, meaning dissection. Anatomy describes form; physiology describes function; biochemistry describes chemical processes. A liver anatomist records the organ's size, position, and blood supply; a liver physiologist studies bile production. The field is a foundational natural science, tied to embryology (development from fertilised egg), comparative anatomy, and evolutionary biology, and human anatomy is the basic science of clinical medicine.

Two scale divisions frame the work. Gross (macroscopic) anatomy examines structures visible to the naked eye, including surface features; microscopic anatomy covers histology (tissues) and cytology (cells). Anatomists study a body region by region, such as the abdomen, or system by system, such as the digestive tract. Standard tools include dissection of cadavers, endoscopy (a camera-equipped tube through a small incision), and imaging with X-rays, ultrasound, MRI, and CT. *Gray's Anatomy* shifted from a systems to a regional format.

All animals are built from four tissue types. Connective tissue scatters fibrous cells in an extracellular matrix whose chief protein is collagen; the matrix mineralises into crustacean shells, stiffens with chitin in insect exoskeletons, or forms vertebrate bone. Epithelium packs cells tightly on a basal lamina, forming keratinised skin, ciliated airway linings, and the microvilli- and villi-studded gut surface that absorbs nutrients. Muscle has three main types: smooth (slow, sustained, unstriated, in blood vessels and gut), skeletal (rapid, striated, moving the body), and cardiac (striated, in the heart). Nervous tissue is built of neurons; simple animals have diffuse nerve nets, while vertebrates concentrate neurons in a central nervous system (brain and spinal cord) connected to a peripheral system split into voluntary (somatic) and involuntary (autonomic) branches.

Vertebrates are animals with a backbone. They share a chordate body plan at some life stage: a notochord, a dorsal hollow nerve tube, pharyngeal arches, and a post-anal tail. In nearly all vertebrates the notochord is reduced to the jelly-like nucleus pulposus of the intervertebral discs; sturgeon and coelacanth keep it as a true rod into adulthood. Vertebrate limbs are homologous, inherited from a common ancestor—the evidence Darwin used to argue for evolution.

The five vertebrate groups form a familiar sequence. Fish are water-bound, with a two-chambered heart and a single circulatory loop, gills for gas exchange, and a lateral line system that senses water vibrations. Cartilaginous fish (sharks, rays) have placoid scales and no swim bladder; bony fish have an operculum covering the gills, a swim bladder for buoyancy, and overlapping scales. Amphibians (frogs, salamanders, caecilians) venture onto land with three-chambered hearts, light hollow bones, moist skins used for gas exchange, and buccal pumping for breathing; they excrete urea. Reptiles are more fully terrestrial: better-ossified skeletons, horny scales, chest-wall breathing, partially separated heart chambers, the amniotic egg laid on land, and uric acid as nitrogenous waste. Crocodilians uniquely have fully four-chambered hearts. Birds are endothermic tetrapods whose hollow bones contain air sacs, a keeled sternum for flight muscles, toothless beaks, and feathers—epidermal outgrowths waterproofed by a single uropygial gland. Mammals combine enamel-coated differentiated teeth, three middle-ear bones, hair, sweat and mammary glands, a muscular diaphragm, and a four-chambered heart. Most bear live young with placentas; monotremes lay eggs; marsupials finish development in an external pouch.

Invertebrates, animals without backbones, make up roughly 95% of animal species. Single-celled protozoans perform tissue-like roles and move using cilia, flagella, or pseudopodia. Even simple multicellular animals already combine epithelium and connective tissue. Exoskeletons arise from the epidermis: arthropods use chitin, molluscs use calcium carbonate, and diatoms and radiolarians use silica. Arthropods are the largest animal phylum. Insects divide the body into head, thorax, and abdomen under a chitinous exoskeleton; spiders fuse head and thorax into a cephalothorax, carry venomous chelicerae, and use pedipalps as taste and smell organs.

The discipline's history follows the rise of direct observation. The Edwin Smith Papyrus (c. 1600 BCE) already named the heart, vessels, brain, meninges, liver, spleen, kidneys, uterus, and bladder. Herophilus and Erasistratus performed the first systematic human dissections in Ptolemaic Alexandria in the 4th–3rd centuries BCE: Herophilus showed arteries have thicker walls than veins, named the meninges, prostate, and ventricles, and classified the pulse; Erasistratus distinguished cerebrum from cerebellum and described the heart's valves including the tricuspid. Galen's animal-based treatise closed antiquity and dominated for a millennium, reaching Renaissance Europe through Islamic medicine. Systematic human dissection resumed at Bologna with Mondino de Luzzi (1275–1326); his 1316 *Anatomy* was the standard text for a century. Vesalius's 1543 *De humani corporis fabrica*, probably illustrated by Jan van Calcar, founded modern human anatomy. Cadaver shortages in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries drove grave-robbing; Britain's Anatomy Act of 1832 and later US legislation after Forbes's 1882 conviction ended the trade by providing legal bodies. Microscopy then pushed anatomy deeper: Schleiden and Schwann's cell theory (c. 1839) made the cell life's fundamental unit; microtomes, stains, and electron microscopy revealed ultrastructure; and X-ray diffraction in the 1950s opened molecular anatomy, the shape of proteins and nucleic acids.
