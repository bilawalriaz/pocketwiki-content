# Public health surveillance

Public health surveillance is the continuous, systematic collection, analysis, and interpretation of health-related data used to plan, implement, and evaluate public health practice (World Health Organization definition). Its core job is to answer three questions about a health problem: when and where it is occurring, and who is affected. Early signals, raised before a physician or laboratory confirms a diagnosis, drive timely action.

## Passive and active systems

Surveillance systems come in two basic forms. A **passive** system relies on health facilities to report diseases and conditions on a regular, ongoing basis across a territory. It is cheap and low-effort to run, but it risks under-reporting. An **active** system sends people into facilities to review cases and medical records looking for a specific disease. Active systems are more expensive and time-consuming, so they are reserved for epidemics or for diseases targeted for elimination.

Large institutions such as the WHO and the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) maintain the databases and informatics infrastructure that make these systems work. Cancer registries track cancer incidence to spot trends and possible causes; broader disease registries now cover stroke, diabetes, and domestic violence. A cost–benefit analysis on each registry determines whether governments fund ongoing research and prevention. Automation is increasingly replacing handwritten reports of adverse drug events, a shift endorsed by hospital accreditation bodies such as JCAHO (the Joint Commission on Accreditation of Healthcare Organizations).

## Syndromic surveillance

Syndromic surveillance watches health-related signals that appear *before* a definitive diagnosis. In an influenza outbreak, the first signs are not lab results but school absences, over-the-counter pharmacy sales, doctor visits, and emergency-department arrivals. Systems that monitor these streams, including absenteeism logs, emergency-call volumes, drug-sale records, and search queries, raise an alarm when activity spikes.

Early warning matters most for bioterrorism, where the 2001 U.S. anthrax attacks showed how fast an intentional outbreak can spread. Effective syndromic systems run in real time, require no extra data entry by clinicians, aggregate across geographic boundaries, and alert epidemiologists automatically.

The idea of using search queries as a syndromic signal was proposed by Gunther Eysenbach in 2004. Google Flu Trends, launched in 2008, was the first large-scale test: more flu-related searches were taken to mean more flu in the population. Early results published in *Nature* matched CDC data and led it by one to two weeks, but later analysis showed the original model had serious flaws. Subsequent linear and nonlinear methods, including the University of Bristol's Flu Detector, which analyzes Twitter content, have improved on that approach.

## Digital surveillance methods

Modern public health surveillance draws on three kinds of digital data:

| Source type | Examples | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| Search aggregates | Google, Wikipedia | Indirect proxy of symptoms or concern |
| Social media | Twitter, Facebook | Direct signals from user posts and surveys |
| Participatory sites | Flu Near You (US), Influenzanet (10 European countries; predecessor Grote Griepmeting, 2003–2004), Dengue na Web (Bahia, Brazil) | Self-reported symptoms from volunteers |

Twitter is the most-used platform because its policies allow public access to 1% samples of raw tweets, which can also be geolocated to model disease spread. During COVID-19, Facebook supplied aggregated, anonymized movement data to disease models and partnered with Carnegie Mellon University on a symptom survey. Wikipedia traffic has been used as a search signal but is noisy, since healthy readers also search for illness topics.

## Laboratory-based surveillance

Some surveillance pulls from clinical lab results rather than symptoms. Because laboratory information systems in Europe and the US automatically process test results, those records can be aggregated cheaply into disease registries. Unlike most syndromic data, lab records for a chronic condition can be linked at the individual patient level if identifiers match, building a chronological history that can also be rolled up to population statistics.

The Vermedx Diabetes Information System, funded by NIH, held years of laboratory results: glycated hemoglobin A1c for blood-sugar control, cholesterol, and serum creatinine plus urine protein for kidney function, for diabetic adults in Vermont and northern New York. Because each record carried a name and address, the system could mail patients when results went out of range, remind them of overdue tests, and feed guideline-based alerts back to providers. Clinical and economic evaluations, including a large randomized trial, showed better guideline adherence and fewer emergency-department visits and hospital admissions, lowering per-patient costs. The system has since been commercialized and extended to chronic kidney disease.

The New York City A1C Registry covers an estimated 600,000 diabetic patients; by early 2012 it held over 10 million test results on 3.6 million individuals, and the NYC Department of Health and Mental Hygiene used it to route patients to information and services. Unlike Vermedx, it offers no opt-out. A similar A1C registry launched in Bexar County, Texas in May 2008 through the San Antonio Metropolitan Health District, drawing from every major clinical lab in the city, but was discontinued in 2010 for lack of funds.

Laboratory surveillance sees only patients already in care and getting tested. That makes it well suited to quality management and care improvement for known patients, but poorly suited to tracking an entire population or catchment area.
