# RNA

Ribonucleic acid is a polymeric molecule built from four bases (G, U, A, C) attached to a ribose-phosphate backbone. It is one of the two nucleic acids (the other being DNA) and one of the four major macromolecules of life. RNA either acts directly as a functional, non-coding molecule or carries genetic instructions from DNA to the ribosome as messenger RNA (mRNA), where the base sequence specifies the amino acid sequence of a protein. Many viruses instead store their genomes in RNA.

RNA can both store information and catalyse chemical reactions. The ribosome's active site is built entirely from RNA, not protein. This dual capacity underpins the "RNA world" hypothesis: that early self-replicating life may have used RNA before DNA-based genomes and before protein enzymes evolved.

## Chemical structure

Each RNA nucleotide has a ribose sugar with a base at the 1' position and a phosphate linking the 3' of one sugar to the 5' of the next. Bases pair canonically as C–G and A–U; G can also pair with U in a flexible "wobble" pair. The backbone's negatively charged phosphates make RNA a polyanion, and ions such as Mg²⁺ help stabilise folded structures.

The defining chemical difference from DNA is a hydroxyl group at the ribose 2' position. This extra oxygen forces most RNA to adopt a compact A-form geometry (deep narrow major groove, shallow wide minor groove) and gives flexible regions the ability to cleave the RNA backbone itself, the basis of catalytic activity by ribozymes. Most functional RNAs are single-stranded but fold into short self-complementary helices and loops, producing protein-like tertiary shapes. Four bases appear sufficient to design any given secondary structure, which may explain why evolution settled on a four-letter alphabet.

Although RNA is transcribed with only four standard bases, over 100 modified nucleosides exist in nature, most diversely in tRNA. Pseudouridine and 2'-O-methylribose are common in rRNA; inosine enables wobble pairing that loosens the genetic code at the third codon position. Many rRNA modifications cluster at functional sites such as the peptidyl transferase centre, implying they matter for catalysis.

## Types of RNA

mRNA carries the protein-coding instructions from DNA to the ribosome. Non-coding RNAs carry out diverse roles: tRNA (~80 nucleotides) delivers amino acids via an anticodon; rRNA forms the catalytic and structural core of the ribosome, with eukaryotic ribosomes containing the 18S, 5.8S, 28S, and 5S rRNAs (the first three made in the nucleolus). A typical eukaryotic cell is mostly rRNA by mass. Bacterial tmRNA tags proteins made from broken mRNAs for degradation and prevents ribosome stalling. By length, small RNAs are <200 nt (tRNA, miRNA, siRNA, snoRNA, piRNA) and long RNAs are >200 nt (mRNA, lncRNA). About 97% of eukaryotic transcription is non-protein-coding.

## Regulatory RNA

RNA interference (RNAi) represses genes post-transcriptionally: miRNAs base-pair with target mRNAs, and the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) then degrades them or blocks translation. Long non-coding RNAs such as Xist recruit Polycomb complexes to silence whole chromatin blocks. Enhancer RNAs are transcribed from enhancers and up-regulate nearby genes, though whether they form a distinct class is unsettled. Small regulatory RNAs exist in bacteria too, often acting by antisense pairing to down-regulate translation under stress; their fast turnover allows rapid response. Riboswitches are cis-acting allosteric sequences that change shape when a metabolite binds, regulating the adjacent gene. Archaea use CRISPR RNAs for antiviral defence, a system now repurposed for DNA editing.

## Synthesis and processing

Transcription is performed by RNA polymerase, which uses DNA as a template, starts at a promoter, unwinds the helix, and elongates 5'→3' while reading the template 3'→5'. Eukaryotic pre-mRNA receives a 5' cap and a poly(A) tail, and spliceosomes (built from snRNAs) or self-splicing ribozymes remove introns. snoRNAs (60–300 nt) base-pair with target RNAs to guide modification enzymes. RNA-dependent RNA polymerases replicate viral RNA genomes such as poliovirus and contribute to RNAi. Reverse-transcribing viruses, retrotransposons, and the enzyme telomerase all copy information between RNA and DNA or use an RNA template. Double-stranded RNA, present in some viral genomes, triggers RNAi and innate interferon defences. Circular RNAs, made by back-splicing, have largely unknown functions, though a few sponge up miRNAs.

## Medical applications

RNA was once dismissed as too unstable to be a drug, but chemical stabilisation changed that. mRNA vaccines work by instructing cells to make a chosen protein, mounting an immune response, an approach first deployed at scale during the COVID-19 pandemic. Small molecules such as ribavirin, branaplam, and ataluren bind structured RNAs to control splicing in genetic disease. In vitro-transcribed mRNA has been used in animal models to deliver proteins for bone repair, pluripotency reprogramming, and heart function. Synthetic siRNAs are routinely introduced into cells to silence specific genes for research and drug validation.
