# Callus (cell biology)

A callus is a growing mass of unorganized plant parenchyma cells. In a living plant, callus forms over a wound. In research, callus is induced in vitro by placing a surface-sterilized piece of plant tissue, called an explant, onto a nutrient gel in a closed vessel such as a Petri dish.

The culture medium is an agar gel containing a basal salt mixture, vitamins, and plant growth regulators. Common basal media include modified Murashige and Skoog medium, White's medium, and woody plant medium. Enrichment with nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium supports plant cell growth. The hormones most often added are auxin, cytokinin, and gibberellin; their balance determines whether the tissue remains unorganized callus or proceeds to somatic embryogenesis.

Callus can be initiated from tissues of all major groups of land plants. The starting tissue is usually somatic and either already dividing or partially undifferentiated, such as meristem. In alfalfa, mesophyll cells dedifferentiate and give rise to callus and somatic embryos.

## Hormone balance and morphology

Specific auxin-to-cytokinin ratios steer development. The classical experiment by Folke Skoog and Carlos O. Miller on tobacco pith showed that a high auxin-to-cytokinin ratio induces roots, a balanced ratio sustains callus, and a ratio favoring cytokinin induces shoots. Changing the hormone mix after callus has formed can shift development toward root formation, shoot growth, or somatic embryogenesis, producing organ primordia that regenerate mature plants.

Callus cultures are usually classified as compact or friable. Compact calli are green and sturdy. Friable calli are white to creamy yellow, fall apart easily, and are the starting material for cell suspension cultures and for generating somatic embryos. In maize these are called type I (compact) and type II (friable).

Callus can brown and die in culture. The main cause is oxidation of phenolic compounds released from damaged cells, which turns the tissue brown and disorganizes it. In rice, conditions that favor induction of scutellar callus also tend to cause necrosis.

## Regeneration, totipotency, and transformation

The ability of a callus, derived from many cell types, to regenerate a whole plant is called totipotency. Because a callus is often produced from structural tissue rather than a single cell, its cells are not necessarily genetically homogeneous, but they are usually similar enough to treat the callus as a single experimental subject. For example, half of a callus can serve as the experimental treatment and the other half as the control.

Regeneration from a single transformed cell yields a whole plant in which every cell carries the transgene. Regeneration from a mixture of transformed and untransformed cells yields a chimera, which is generally not useful for genetic research or agriculture. Genes are typically introduced into callus cells with a gene gun (biolistic bombardment) or with Agrobacterium tumefaciens, and transformed cells are recovered into whole plants by adjusting the plant hormones in the medium.

Callus is also the basis of micropropagation, in which genetically identical copies of a plant with desirable traits are produced. Protocol details matter: in maize the auxin 2,4-D induces callus more effectively than NAA or IAA, while in prune the auxin IBA inhibits callus formation whereas IAA does not.

## History

Henri-Louis Duhamel du Monceau first reported callus forming on live plants while studying wound healing in elm trees. In 1908, E. F. Simon induced callus from poplar stems that also produced roots and buds. The first in vitro callus inductions were reported independently in 1939 by P. White, who grew hormone-independent callus from tumor-derived procambial tissue of hybrid Nicotiana glauca, and by Gautheret and Nobécourt, who maintained carrot callus with added auxin.
