# Cell membrane

The cell membrane (also called the plasma membrane) is the thin barrier that separates a cell's interior from the outside world. It holds the cell together, controls what passes in and out, and lets the cell communicate with its neighbors. Nearly every cell on Earth uses this same basic design.

## Composition

The core of the membrane is a **lipid bilayer**: two sheets of phospholipid molecules arranged tail-to-tail. Each phospholipid has a hydrophilic head and two hydrophobic tails. In water, they spontaneously line up with heads facing out and tails tucked inside, forming a sealed sheet. This self-assembly is driven by the hydrophobic effect.

Three classes of lipid make up the bilayer:

- **Phospholipids**, usually over half the lipid, with fatty acid tails of 16 or 18 carbons. Unsaturated (kinked) tails make the membrane more fluid; saturated (straight) tails make it stiffer. Organisms adjust this balance to suit temperature (homeoviscous adaptation).
- **Cholesterol** (in animal cells) and related sterols in plants and fungi sit between the phospholipid tails. At high temperatures they stiffen the membrane; at low temperatures they prevent it from gelling.
- **Glycolipids**, about 2% of the lipid, carry sugar chains that project outward and help cells recognize one another.

Floating in and on this lipid sea are **membrane proteins**, typically about half the membrane by weight:

- **Integral (transmembrane) proteins** span the bilayer, with hydrophobic middles and hydrophilic ends. Examples include ion channels, proton pumps, and G-protein-coupled receptors.
- **Peripheral proteins** attach to the membrane surface, often as enzymes.
- **Lipid-anchored proteins** are tethered by a covalently attached lipid.

On the outer surface, sugar chains on lipids and proteins form the **glycocalyx**. The terminal sugar, sialic acid, carries a negative charge that helps repel unwanted particles. Viruses and immune cells use this coat to identify their targets.

The membrane is not rigid. Lipids drift sideways through the bilayer (lateral diffusion) very quickly, and proteins are also free to move, though the cytoskeleton beneath often fences them into particular regions. The **fluid mosaic model** (Singer and Nicolson, 1972) captures this picture: a two-dimensional fluid of lipids in which proteins float and interact. It replaced the earlier Davson-Danielli model and remains the standard view.

## Function

The bilayer's hydrophobic core is impermeable to ions and most polar molecules, so the membrane acts as a selective filter.

Small, uncharged molecules such as oxygen, carbon dioxide, and water slip directly through the lipid by simple diffusion. Water can also pass through dedicated **aquaporin** channels.

Larger or charged molecules need protein help. **Channels and transporters** ferry specific sugars, amino acids, and ions across. Some provide a pore (facilitated diffusion); others couple movement to ATP (active transport) or to another molecule flowing down its gradient.

**Endocytosis** pulls material in: the membrane folds inward, pinches off, and forms a vesicle inside the cell, used to engulf nutrients or larger particles. **Exocytosis** is the reverse: a vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane and releases its contents outside, the way nerve cells release neurotransmitters and secretory cells release hormones.

The membrane is also the cell's communication hub. Surface receptors bind signaling molecules and trigger internal responses, adhesion proteins hold cells together to form tissues, and anchoring proteins connect the membrane to the cytoskeleton, giving the cell its shape and letting it move.

## Variations across life

- **Gram-negative bacteria** have two membranes: the inner plasma membrane and an outer membrane rich in lipopolysaccharides, separated by a periplasm. Porins make the outer membrane porous.
- **Gram-positive bacteria and archaea** have only a plasma membrane, surrounded by a thick cell wall.
- **Mitochondria and chloroplasts** have double membranes, supporting the theory that they evolved from engulfed bacteria.
- **Muscle cells** have a specialized membrane called the sarcolemma, about 10 nm thick (compared with roughly 4 nm for a typical cell), with T-tubules that carry action potentials deep into the cell.
- **Oocytes** lack a typical lipid bilayer, using a glycoprotein coat called the zona pellucida instead.

## Permeability

The permeability of a membrane depends mainly on a molecule's charge and polarity, and to a lesser extent its size. Because the bilayer's interior is hydrophobic, small neutral molecules cross easily, while charged ions are blocked unless a channel or pump carries them. This selective barrier is what allows a cell to maintain its internal chemistry, hold a voltage across its surface, and respond to its environment.
