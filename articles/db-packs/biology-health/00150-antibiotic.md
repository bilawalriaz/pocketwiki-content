# Antibiotic

An antibiotic is a substance that kills bacteria or stops them from growing. The word combines Greek *anti* ("against") and *bios* ("life"). In strict medical usage an antibiotic is a natural product made by one microorganism to fight another, such as penicillin from the *Penicillium rubens* mould. In everyday use the word covers any drug that acts on bacteria, including fully synthetic ones such as sulfonamides. Antibiotics belong to antimicrobial chemotherapy but do not work against viruses or fungi, with a few exceptions that also act on protozoa.

Bactericidal drugs kill bacteria; bacteriostatic drugs only hold growth in check. The distinction matters because bactericidal activity usually requires actively dividing cells, and combining a bacteriostatic with a bactericidal drug is often antagonistic, for example tetracyclines blocking penicillins.

Antibiotics fall into a small number of mechanism families. Cell-wall agents (penicillins, cephalosporins) and membrane disrupters (polymyxins) kill. Protein-synthesis inhibitors (macrolides, tetracyclines, lincosamides) mostly only slow growth, with aminoglycosides as the exception. Enzyme-targeting drugs (rifamycins, quinolones, sulfonamides) usually kill. Two refinements extend useful life: a β-lactamase inhibitor such as clavulanic acid neutralises the bacterial enzymes that destroy penicillins, and semisynthetic chemical tweaks turn natural scaffolds into more potent medicines. Almost all modern antibacterials start life as a natural molecule, and producing them at scale means fermenting microbes under strongly aerobic conditions, a method Florey and Chain pioneered in 1939 to bring penicillin to wartime medicine.

A drug's potency against a strain is described by the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), the lowest dose that halts visible growth. A drug is broad-spectrum if it hits many bacterial types, narrow-spectrum if it targets a defined group such as gram-positive or gram-negative organisms.

In the clinic, doctors often start with empiric broad-spectrum therapy while waiting for lab identification of the pathogen, then switch to a narrow-spectrum drug once the culprit is known. Narrow drugs cost less, hit the bug more precisely, and slow the spread of resistance. Antibiotics are also given prophylactically to immunocompromised patients, surgical patients to protect the incision, people with neutropenia, and in some dental procedures to prevent infective endocarditis. Common side effects come from collateral damage to the body's own microbes: diarrhoea when gut flora are disrupted, sometimes allowing *Clostridioides difficile* to overgrow (probiotics can help prevent this), and vaginal candidiasis. Rare but dangerous effects include photodermatitis and anaphylaxis, and some drugs damage the host's mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum. A few specific interactions matter: alcohol with metronidazole, tinidazole, some cephalosporins, or furazolidone triggers a disulfiram-like reaction, while alcohol may blunt the effect of doxycycline. Most studies find that antibiotics do not meaningfully raise the failure rate of oral contraceptives beyond about 1%, except where rifampicin or other strong enzyme-inducers are also taken. Use of antibiotics for secondary prevention of coronary heart disease is unsupported and may increase mortality.

The history runs from observation to industrial production. In 1877 Pasteur and Koch noticed an airborne bacillus stopping *Bacillus anthracis* from growing, the first recorded antibiosis. Paul Ehrlich opened the era of synthetic antibacterials in the late 1880s, and in 1907 his team produced salvarsan, the first synthetic cure for syphilis. In 1928 Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin, but Florey, Chain, and Abraham's 1942 purification is what brought the drug to patients; the three shared the 1945 Nobel Prize. René Dubos isolated tyrothricin from *Bacillus brevis* in 1939, the first antibiotic derived directly from another microbe. Domagk's 1932 discovery of Prontosil launched the sulfonamides. Waksman coined "antibiotic" in 1942 and the term stuck by 1947. Earlier evidence suggests tetracycline was already present in fermented Nubian grain drinks in 350–550 CE. After roughly forty years with no new chemical classes, four new families appeared in the late 2000s and early 2010s: cyclic lipopeptides (daptomycin), glycylcyclines (tigecycline), oxazolidinones (linezolid), and lipiarmycins (fidaxomicin).

The reason new classes matter is antimicrobial resistance (AMR). When a course of treatment wipes out susceptible bacteria, resistant mutants survive and multiply; Luria and Delbrück demonstrated this selection in 1943. Resistance spreads three ways: by mutation, by vertical inheritance, and by horizontal gene transfer, in which plasmids carrying several resistance genes jump between strains and even between species. The fitness cost of resistance is real, but compensatory mutations can erase it. The result is a global crisis. The WHO has called AMR a serious threat happening in every region, with around 1.27 million deaths directly attributable to bacterial AMR in 2019 and nearly 5 million associated deaths each year. Concrete examples show the scale: almost half a million new multidrug-resistant tuberculosis cases per year, the NDM-1 enzyme giving resistance to nearly all β-lactams, colistin-resistant *E. coli* reported in the United States in May 2016, and *Bacteroides* penicillin resistance over 90%. Drivers are predictable: self-prescribing, treating viral infections, wrong doses, incomplete courses, and prescribing to placate patients who expect a drug, all amplified by a lack of rapid diagnostic tests.

The development pipeline is thin. As of May 2017, only 51 antibiotic candidates were in clinical phases 1–3, and recent approvals (ceftaroline, oritavancin, telavancin, dalbavancin, tedizolid, fidaxomicin, and the β-lactamase-inhibitor combinations ceftazidime-avibactam and ceftolozane-avibactam) are mostly variants of existing classes. The US ADAPT Act tries to speed approval through smaller trials. Alternative strategies are being explored: screening soil actinomycetes, which still supply 70–80% of current antibiotics, for new molecules; bezlotoxumab antibody therapy against recurrent *C. difficile*; phage therapy using bacteria-specific viruses that spare the microbiota; fecal microbiota transplantation, which clears around 90% of resistant *C. difficile* infections; antisense RNA that silences resistance genes such as *mecA*; CRISPR-Cas9 editing of those genes; and vaccines. Public-health responses such as stewardship programmes are essential, though simple measures like antibiotic cycling have so far failed to slow resistance, and farm antibiotic use keeps climbing despite pledges. Public campaigns can work: France's 2002 "Antibiotics are not automatic" drive cut prescriptions, the United Kingdom tightened rules after the 1969 Swann report, and the European Union banned growth-promoting antibiotic use in 2003.
