# First aid kit

A first-aid kit, also called a medkit or med kit, is a portable collection of supplies used to give immediate medical care for minor injuries and emergencies until professional treatment is available. Contents vary with the setting (home, vehicle, workplace, wilderness, or combat), the training of the user, and the regulations of the country.

## Regulation and identification

Workplace and vehicle kits are governed by national standards. In the United States, employers must provide first-aid equipment under OSHA (Occupational Safety and Health Administration) rules, and ANSI/ISEA Z308.1-2021 is the most widely cited content standard, classifying kits by hazard type and container durability. Germany requires motor-vehicle kits to meet DIN 13164, which lists supplies for passenger cars. Canada regulates workplace kits federally, with provinces adding rules; Nova Scotia requires a Type 2 kit in vehicles used to transport employees.

The ISO 7010 symbol, a white cross on a green background, identifies first-aid kits worldwide and has been adopted across the EU. The red cross emblem is reserved under the First Geneva Convention for the International Committee of the Red Cross and military medical services; use by anyone else is illegal in signatory countries. Johnson & Johnson holds a U.S. trademark on the red cross for medicinal plasters dating to 1905, an exception that predates most treaty obligations.

## Core contents

Most personal and household kits share a basic set for minor wounds:

- Adhesive bandages in various shapes, plus moleskin for blisters
- Sterile gauze pads, non-adherent pads, and petrolatum gauze as an occlusive (air-tight) dressing for chest wounds
- Hypoallergenic adhesive tape, roller or elastic bandages, and triangular bandages for slings and tourniquets
- Antiseptic wipes or spray, usually benzalkonium chloride; povidone iodine is also common. Hydrogen peroxide is a poor choice because it damages tissue and slows healing
- Saline for flushing wounds and eyes, cotton swabs, tweezers, scissors, and disposable gloves
- A cold pack, an emergency blanket, and a torch
- Simple analgesics such as paracetamol (acetaminophen) or ibuprofen

Workplace kits add a CPR breathing barrier to reduce infection during rescue breaths. Vehicle kits add reflective vests. Industrial kits may include eye-wash solutions and burn dressings.

## Trauma, ABCs, and PPE

Trauma care (controlling bleeding, dressing burns, and stabilizing fractures) is the focus of most kits, so bandages and dressings dominate their contents. Higher-level kits may add hemostatic agents that promote clotting for severe bleeding and butterfly closures to pull wound edges together, though closures can trap infection in uncleaned wounds.

The ABC framework, meaning Airway, Breathing, and Circulation, organizes first-aid priorities. Equipment that supports it includes pocket masks, face shields, bag-valve masks (handheld devices that squeeze air into the lungs), and oropharyngeal or nasopharyngeal airways (tubes inserted through the mouth or nose to keep the airway open). Advanced kits carried by event responders may include bottled oxygen.

Personal protective equipment (PPE) protects both patient and responder. Disposable gloves are standard; goggles, surgical or N95 masks, and aprons appear where splash exposure is likely. A mask placed on a patient should not have an exhale valve, since valves vent unfiltered breath outward.

## Specialized kits

Trauma, combat, and tactical kits concentrate on stopping major bleeding. The Stop the Bleed campaign, launched after the 2012 Sandy Hook shooting by the American College of Surgeons, the Hartford Consensus, and federal agencies, has spread the use of Individual First Aid Kits (IFAKs) containing tourniquets, chest seals, hemostatic gauze, and pressure bandages. Wilderness and expedition kits add supplies for delayed evacuation, including epinephrine autoinjectors (spring-loaded syringes delivering a single dose), oral rehydration salts, and anti-diarrheal medication such as loperamide.

## Medications and their limits

- Aspirin is included mainly for suspected heart-attack chest pain, where its anti-platelet effect can be lifesaving.
- Epinephrine autoinjectors (EpiPen) temporarily open a swelling airway during anaphylactic shock but do not treat the reaction itself, and effects wear off within minutes, so additional doses may be needed.
- Diphenhydramine (Benadryl) treats anaphylactic symptoms; once the airway closes, oral drugs cannot be absorbed, so an EpiPen must be used first. A common adult dose is two 25 mg pills.
- Paracetamol and NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) such as ibuprofen handle routine pain from sprains, strains, and fractures.
- Hydrocortisone cream, calamine lotion, and burn gel address skin inflammation and minor burns.

Activated charcoal absorbs ingested toxins. Syrup of ipecac, an emetic that induces vomiting, appears in older kits but first-aid manuals now advise against it.

## Maintenance and history

Kits should be kept clean, dry, easily accessible, and clearly marked. Routine inspection replaces missing, damaged, or expired items, especially medications and sterile supplies.

Kit contents have changed as medical understanding has advanced. Older US federal specifications included incision-and-suction snakebite kits and mercurochrome antiseptic; the snakebite kit is no longer recommended, and the FDA removed mercurochrome from the generally-recognized-as-safe category in 1998 over its mercury content. Modern additions include CPR face shields and body-fluid barriers that protect against bloodborne pathogens such as HIV during rescue breathing.
