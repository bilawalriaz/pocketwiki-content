# Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification System

The Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) Classification System is a World Health Organization system that sorts the active ingredients of medicines by the organ or system on which they act and by their therapeutic, pharmacological, and chemical properties. Maintained by the WHO Collaborating Centre for Drug Statistics Methodology (WHOCC) and first published in 1976, it is a tool for monitoring drug use and supporting research on medication quality. It makes no claim about whether a drug should be used or how well it works.

The design is a strict five-level hierarchy in which every code, except the 14 at the top, has exactly one parent. The codes are semantic: each carries its own complete ancestry. As of 7 May 2020 the system contains 6,331 codes, with 14 at level 1, 94 at level 2, 267 at level 3, 889 at level 4, and 5,067 at level 5.

Building a code is cumulative. Level 1 is one letter for the anatomical main group. Level 2 adds two digits for the therapeutic subgroup. Level 3 adds one letter for the therapeutic or pharmacological subgroup. Level 4 adds another letter for the chemical, therapeutic, or pharmacological subgroup. Level 5 adds two digits naming the chemical substance. The diuretic example makes the structure concrete: **C** is the cardiovascular system; **C03** narrows to diuretics; **C03C** specifies high-ceiling diuretics; **C03CA** is the sulfonamide family; **C03CA01** identifies furosemide.

The 14 first-level groups are A (alimentary tract and metabolism), B (blood and blood forming organs), C (cardiovascular system), D (dermatologicals), G (genito-urinary system and sex hormones), H (systemic hormonal preparations, excluding sex hormones and insulins), J (antiinfectives for systemic use), L (antineoplastic and immunomodulating agents), M (musculo-skeletal system), N (nervous system), P (antiparasitic products, insecticides and repellents), R (respiratory system), S (sensory organs), and V (various).

A drug can carry more than one code when it has more than one use. Acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin) illustrates this: A01AD05 for local oral treatment, B01AC06 as a platelet inhibitor, and N02BA01 as an analgesic and antipyretic. A code can also represent more than one active ingredient in a fixed combination, so C09BB04 names perindopril with amlodipine, while C09AA04 and C08CA01 are the codes for each drug alone. Brands with the same active substance in the same indication share a code; different uses of the same molecule produce different codes.

Two derived systems extend the same idea. The veterinary system (ATCvet) places a Q in front of most human codes, giving furosemide for animals the code QC03CA01; a few codes, such as QI for immunologicals, are veterinary only. The Herbal ATC system (HATC) uses four digits instead of two at level 5, is maintained by the Uppsala Monitoring Centre, and is not adopted by WHO.

The defined daily dose (DDD) is the assumed average maintenance dose per day for a drug used for its main indication in adults, and it is the standard unit for measuring drug consumption. A DDD appears only when the WHOCC has made an assignment.

ATC follows published guidelines for change. A sponsor submits an application to WHO for an ATC classification and DDD assignment; a preliminary code is posted on the WHOCC website and in WHO Drug Information for comment or objection. The semi-annual Working Group meeting decides whether a code becomes final, and new codes take effect on January 1 each year. Alterations to existing codes apply only in the coming annual update, so original codes must continue until the end of the year. National adaptations, such as the German Anatomisch-therapeutisch-chemische Klassifikation mit Tagesdosen, can add extra codes and DDDs the WHO version omits.

The WHO system is an extension of an earlier four-level Anatomical Classification System that the European Pharmaceutical Market Research Association (EphMRA) introduced in 1971 for the pharmaceutical industry. The two share the ATC name, which can confuse: the WHO version is five-level and built for drug-use statistics, while the EphMRA version is shallower and oriented toward market analysis.
