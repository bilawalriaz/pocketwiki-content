# Altruism (biology)

In biology, altruism is behaviour that lowers the actor's own reproductive fitness while raising that of another individual. The consequences for fitness decide whether an action counts as altruistic; intentions or moral judgements are irrelevant. The term was coined by Auguste Comte, from Italian *altrui* and Latin *alteri*, meaning "other people".

Two forms are recognised. *Obligate altruism* is a permanent loss of direct reproduction, as in honey bee workers that forage for the colony and never breed. *Facultative altruism* is temporary: a Florida scrub jay may help at a parent's nest and later inherit a territory of its own.

## The puzzle

Altruism looks paradoxical because natural selection acts on individuals, and a self-sacrificing individual should leave fewer offspring than a selfish one. If selfishness is heritable, the whole population should turn selfish. Group selection, the idea that groups of altruists outcompete groups of selfish individuals, was first suggested by Darwin in 1871 but lost credibility in the 1960s after critiques by George C. Williams, John Maynard Smith, and Richard Dawkins.

## Kin selection and Hamilton's rule

The key resolution came from W. D. Hamilton's 1960s theory of kin selection. Because parents and offspring share on average half their genes, and full siblings likewise share half, helping raise full siblings is genetically equivalent to raising one's own young. A gene for helping relatives can therefore spread even at some cost to the helper.

Hamilton's rule formalises the trade-off: a behaviour is favoured when the benefit to the recipient (B), weighted by the relatedness between actor and recipient (r), exceeds the cost to the actor (C), that is, when rB − C > 0. The higher the relatedness, the smaller the benefit a helper needs to give, or the larger the cost it can bear. Kin selection is a special case of *inclusive fitness*, the sum of an individual's own offspring plus those extra descendants it helps relatives produce. Extreme examples include *Stegodyphus* spiders, whose young consume the mother, and male spiders that let a fertilising female eat them.

## Reciprocity and the prisoner's dilemma

When the recipient is not kin, altruism can still evolve through reciprocity: one animal grooms another, or a vampire bat shares regurgitated blood with a starving roost-mate, and the favour is later returned. Robert Trivers formalised this as reciprocal altruism in 1971, arguing that helpers benefit if cheats are punished and cooperation continues.

The prisoner's dilemma captures why reciprocity is hard. Two players who would both gain by cooperating can each gain even more by defecting, so pure defection is the only stable outcome in a single encounter. Cooperation becomes possible when the same players meet repeatedly. In Axelrod and Hamilton's 1981 analysis of the iterated prisoner's dilemma, *tit-for-tat*, a strategy that begins by cooperating and then copies the partner's last move, outperforms nearly every alternative. Tit-for-tat is, however, fragile: a single mistake triggers mutual retaliation, and over long runs of play, mutant strategies (such as "generous tit-for-tat") keep invading, so no single strategy is evolutionarily stable.

## The handicap principle and signalling

Amotz Zahavi proposed that costly acts function as honest signals of quality, much as a peacock's heavy tail honestly advertises a male's vigour. A weak individual cannot afford the cost of an altruistic act or a large ornament without its fitness falling still further, so the cost makes the signal hard to fake. A closely related idea, *koinophilia*, holds that mates prefer partners who look most like the population average, since rare or unusual features are usually mutations with negative effects. Both effects can stabilise cooperation, because a selfish freeloader stands out and is penalised at mating.

## Reciprocity mechanisms in practice

Across species, benefits are returned through three common mechanisms. *Symmetry-based* reciprocity, a long-running "buddy system" with no close accounting, appears in many primates and humans. *Attitudinal* reciprocity, mirroring a partner's friendliness on the spot, governs interactions with strangers. *Calculated* reciprocity, keeping track of favours given and received, is typical of chimpanzees and human relationships, though it has proven hard to elicit in laboratory experiments. Biological market theory (Noë and Hammerstein, 1994) extends these ideas by treating the exchange of food, grooming, warning calls, and shelter as a market in which supply, demand, and partner choice set the value of each commodity. Cleaner wrasse and their reef-fish clients illustrate the system: choosy clients that can visit several stations switch partners if cheated, while cleaners give them priority.

## Examples across the animal kingdom

Vertebrate examples are abundant: wolves and wild dogs bringing meat back to pack-mates, meerkats posting sentinels, vampire bats sharing blood, vervet monkeys giving alarm calls, dolphins supporting sick pod-mates, and humpback whales intervening against killer whales attacking other species. Invertebrate cases include ants and *Globitermes sulphureus* termites that rupture a gland to release a sticky defensive secretion, dying in the act; the eusocial shrimp *Synalpheus regalis*, whose helpers defend a sponge shared by close kin; and the cellular slime mould *Dictyostelium mucoroides*, in which starving amoebae aggregate into a fruiting body where some cells sacrifice themselves so others can disperse. Even in plants, cultivated soybeans have been shown to recognise kin and adjust growth accordingly, with mixed individual and group selection in breeding programs apparently able to favour altruistic tendencies.

## What explains the variety

No single mechanism accounts for every case. Kin selection dominates where relatedness is high, reciprocity dominates where partners meet repeatedly, costly signals and conformity to the average phenotype stabilise cooperation in populations, and biological market dynamics govern exchanges between unrelated individuals whose choices determine the value of services offered. The shared logic is that altruism evolves whenever the genetic or social pay-off to the helper, directly or through relatives, outweighs the personal reproductive cost.
