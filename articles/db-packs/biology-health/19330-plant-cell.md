# Plant cell

A plant cell is a eukaryotic cell of the kingdom Plantae, defined by features that animal, fungal, and bacterial cells lack. The most distinctive are a rigid cellulose cell wall built outside the cell membrane, plastids that include the photosynthetic chloroplast, a single dominant central vacuole that controls turgor, and a mode of division that uses a cell plate rather than a cleavage furrow. Plant cells also lack centrioles, and the only flagellated cells in land plants are the motile sperm of bryophytes, pteridophytes, cycads, and Ginkgo.

## The cell wall

Every plant cell deposits a primary cell wall of cellulose, hemicelluloses, and pectin. This composition is diagnostic: fungi use chitin, bacteria use peptidoglycan, and archaea use pseudopeptidoglycan. The wall gives the cell a defined shape, permits controlled expansion during growth, and mediates intercellular communication. Pores called plasmodesmata cross the primary wall and are lined by the plasma membrane and endoplasmic reticulum of adjacent cells, which are continuous through the pore, so that nutrients, hormones, and signalling molecules pass directly from cell to cell.

Secondary wall layers may be deposited inside the primary wall. Lignin stiffens mature cells, and suberin waterproofs them. Cutin is secreted to the outside of the epidermal wall, where it combines with epicuticular waxes to form the plant cuticle, the waterproof skin that seals leaves and stems against water loss.

## Plastids

Plastids are a family of membrane-bound organelles. The chloroplast, coloured green by chlorophyll, converts sunlight, water, and carbon dioxide into sugars. Other plastid types store particular substances: amyloplasts accumulate starch, elaioplasts store fats, and chromoplasts synthesise and store pigments such as carotenoids. Like mitochondria, plastids carry their own genomes of roughly 100–120 unique genes, a remnant of their origin as prokaryotic endosymbionts inside an early eukaryotic host.

## The central vacuole

Most plant cells contain one large vacuole, a watery compartment called the sap, bounded by a membrane called the tonoplast. The vacuole generates turgor pressure by osmotic uptake of water, pushing the protoplast against the wall and keeping soft tissues stiff. It also regulates traffic between cytosol and sap, stores phosphorus and nitrogen, and digests waste proteins and worn-out organelles.

## Cell division

When a plant cell divides, a phragmoplast forms across the equator and acts as a scaffold for building a cell plate. The plate becomes the new middle lamella and the new wall separating the daughter cells. This mode of cytokinesis is shared by land plants and a few algae, notably the charophytes and the chlorophyte order Trentepohliales.

## Tissues and cell types

Plant cells differentiate from meristematic cells, the plant equivalent of stem cells, into a small set of mature types, each suited to a specific role.

| Cell or tissue | Wall | Living at maturity | Main function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parenchyma | thin primary wall | yes | storage, photosynthesis (mesophyll, chlorenchyma), nectar secretion, defence compounds; some retain totipotency |
| Collenchyma | unevenly thickened primary wall, pectin- and hemicellulose-rich | yes | flexible, plastic support in growing shoots and petioles; lacks lignin |
| Sclerenchyma (sclereids, fibres) | thick lignified secondary wall | no; cytoplasm lost | rigid mechanical support and protection; stone cells give fruits their grit, fibres carry tensile load |
| Xylem tracheids and vessel elements | lignified secondary walls, often ringed or pitted | no | water conduction under tension; tracheids define the vascular plants |
| Phloem sieve tubes with companion cells | thin primary wall | sieve tubes lose nuclei and ribosomes; companion cells remain alive | translocation of sucrose along osmotic pressure gradients |

The epidermis is a parenchyma-based tissue covering leaves, stems, and roots. It contains stomatal guard cells, which are the only shoot epidermal cells with chloroplasts and which regulate gas exchange, along with trichomes and root hairs. Conifers and flowering plants lack both flagella and centrioles, and their sperm cells are non-motile, relying on pollen tubes instead.
