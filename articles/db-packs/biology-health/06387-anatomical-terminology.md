# Anatomical terminology

Anatomical terminology is a standardised vocabulary of Greek and Latin origin used by anatomists, zoologists, and health professionals to describe body structures and functions. Everyday phrases such as "above the wrist" are ambiguous, so precise terms like *proximal* (closer to the trunk) and *distal* (farther from it) remove that ambiguity. The international standard is *Terminologia Anatomica* (TA), published in 1998 by the Federative Committee on Anatomical Terminology and the International Federation of Associations of Anatomists. It replaces the older *Nomina Anatomica* and lists about 7,500 gross human structures. Sister standards cover microanatomy (*Terminologia Histologica*) and embryology (*Terminologia Embryologica*).

## How terms are built

Anatomical terms follow a regular morphology. A typical three-part name gives the structure, its type, and its location. *Musculus biceps brachii* parses as "two-headed muscle of the arm": *musculus* (muscle), *biceps* (two-headed), *brachii* (of the arm). A *root* names an organ or tissue, while consistent *prefixes* and *suffixes* modify meaning across terms.

## Standard anatomical position

Every location term is defined from one reference pose: the body standing upright, feet shoulder-width apart and parallel, toes forward, upper limbs at the sides, palms facing forward with thumbs pointing outward. Because descriptions always assume this pose, structures keep the same relative names no matter how the actual body is oriented. Right and left always refer to the subject, never the observer.

## Regions

The body is divided into named regions. The head is the *cephalic* region (subdivisions include *cranium*, *facies*, *oculus*, *mentum*); the neck is *cervix*. In front the trunk has the *thorax* and *abdomen*; in back, the *dorsum* and *lumbus*. The arm is the *brachium*, the forearm the *antebrachium*, the wrist the *carpus*, and the hand the *manus*. In the lower limb, *thigh* means hip to knee, while *leg* means strictly knee to ankle; the calf is the *sura* and the sole the *planta*.

The abdomen is mapped clinically in two ways. Four quadrants are drawn with one horizontal and one vertical line through the umbilicus, naming the right and left upper and lower quadrants. A finer scheme uses nine regions, defined by two vertical midclavicular lines and two horizontal lines, the subcostal just below the ribs and the intertubercular across the iliac tubercles, producing the right and left hypochondriac, epigastric, right and left lumbar, umbilical, right and left iliac, and hypogastric regions.

## Directional terms

Directional terms come as opposing pairs anchored in the standard position:

- *Anterior* and *posterior*: front and back. The toes are anterior to the heel.
- *Superior* and *inferior*: above and below. The pelvis is inferior to the abdomen.
- *Proximal* and *distal*: closer to and farther from the trunk or point of attachment. The shoulder is proximal to the arm; the foot is distal to the knee.
- *Superficial* and *deep*: closer to and farther from the body surface. Skin is superficial to bone.
- *Medial* and *lateral*: closer to and farther from the midline. The umbilicus is medial to the hips.
- *Dorsal* and *ventral*: back and front, often used for embryonic structures before limb rotation.
- *Rostral* and *caudal*: toward the nose and toward the tail end. *Cranial* and *caudal* mean toward the skull and toward the base of the spine respectively.

## Planes

Three standard planes section the body. The *sagittal* plane is vertical, dividing right from left; the midline version is *midsagittal*. The *frontal* (or *coronal*) plane divides front from back, named from Latin *corona*, "crown". The *transverse* plane divides upper from lower and produces cross sections.

## Movement

Movement terms are paired opposites and always described from the anatomical position. *Flexion* decreases the angle between body parts, *extension* increases it. *Abduction* moves a structure away from the midline, *adduction* toward it. *Internal* (medial) rotation turns toward the body's centre; *external* (lateral) rotation turns away. *Elevation* and *depression* move a structure superiorly or inferiorly, mainly the scapula and mandible.

The hands and feet use specialised terms. *Dorsiflexion* and *plantarflexion* flex or extend the foot at the ankle. *Pronation* and *supination* rotate the forearm so the palm faces posteriorly or anteriorly. *Eversion* and *inversion* tilt the sole of the foot away from or toward the midline.

## Muscles

Skeletal muscles cross a *joint* and attach to bone at an *origin* and an *insertion*; the insertion moves toward the origin during contraction. The muscle producing a movement is the *agonist* (the triceps extends the forearm); the *antagonist* produces the opposite action (the biceps flexes it). *Synergists* assist the agonist, so the *brachialis* and *brachioradialis* help the biceps flex the forearm. Most movement occurs at *synovial joints*, though *symphysis* joints such as those between vertebrae also allow slight movement through compression of the intervertebral discs.

## Functional state

Some terms describe whether a structure is open or connected. *Patent* means a vessel, duct, or lumen remains open when it should not, as in a *patent ductus arteriosus*, a fetal vessel that normally closes within three weeks of birth. *Anatomical variation* covers normal differences between individuals, such as the presence or absence of the *palmaris longus* tendon, and is distinct from *congenital anomalies*, which are disorders.

## Body cavities and membranes

The body is partitioned into cavities. The *ventral cavity* holds the thoracic and abdominopelvic cavities; the *dorsal cavity* holds the cranial and spinal cavities. A *serous membrane* lines these cavities in two layers: the *parietal* layer on the cavity wall and the *visceral* layer covering the organ, separated by a thin fluid-filled space. The *pericardium* is the serous cavity that surrounds the heart.
