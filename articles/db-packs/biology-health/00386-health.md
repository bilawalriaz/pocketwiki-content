# Health

Health is the state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being, a condition far broader than the mere absence of disease. This definition comes from the World Health Organization's 1948 constitution, which moved the idea of health beyond a biomedical view of the body running normally. The 1948 formulation was criticized as vague, and through the 1980s the WHO refined it. In 1984 the organization described health as "the extent to which an individual or group is able to realize aspirations and satisfy needs and to change or cope with the environment," and the 1986 Ottawa Charter called health "a resource for everyday life." The shift recasts health as something a person uses to live, not a static state of not being ill.

What determines a person's health is a web of factors. The 1974 Lalonde report from Canada grouped them into three fields: biomedical (genetics and internal body function), environmental (clean water, safe housing, and natural spaces), and lifestyle (personal choices such as diet, exercise, smoking, and sleep). The WHO groups them similarly, listing the social and economic environment, the physical environment, and individual characteristics and behaviors as the main categories. Individual factors such as gender, ethnicity, and income interact with these, and the environment matters especially for children, whose developing bodies are more sensitive to contaminants and stress. Genetics sets predispositions for some diseases and shapes how a person responds to stress, but the Alameda County Study and similar research have shown that people can still shift their health trajectory through exercise, adequate sleep, and other lifestyle choices. The relative weight of genes versus environment and behavior remains debated.

The world's biggest health problems split into two camps. Non-communicable diseases (illnesses that cannot pass from person to person, such as cardiovascular disease and cancer) cause roughly 36 million deaths each year. Communicable diseases such as AIDS/HIV, tuberculosis, and malaria still cause millions more, and malnutrition, especially in children, plus injuries add further burden. The leading individual risk factors are smoking, poor diet, and inactivity. Mental health sits inside the same picture. The WHO defines it as a state of well-being in which a person can cope with ordinary life stresses, work productively, and contribute to the community. Mental disorders are a leading cause of disability in both the United States and Canada, with causes that mix genetics, family history, and life experience, including trauma.

Three behaviors do most of the work in maintaining health. A balanced diet, drawing on both plant- and animal-based foods, supplies macronutrients (carbohydrates, proteins, fats) and micronutrients (vitamins, minerals) that regulate body processes and lower disease risk. Regular physical exercise strengthens the body and cuts the risk of heart disease, cancer, and diabetes. Sleep is similarly foundational: chronic deprivation makes people more vulnerable to illness and to long-term conditions, and the International Agency for Research on Cancer has judged that shiftwork, which disrupts the body's circadian rhythm (the internal 24-hour cycle governing sleep, hormone release, and other functions), is probably carcinogenic.

The way societies organize care also shapes health. Health systems are usually layered into primary care (the first-contact clinician for most problems and for prevention), secondary care (specialists), and tertiary care (advanced treatment at specialized centers). Public health works one level up, at the population scale, using surveillance, education, and policy to prevent disease before it starts; it has helped raise American life expectancy by about 30 years since 1900. Since the 2000s, health has also been framed as a civic responsibility, aided by digital wellness tools that help people track diet, activity, and sleep, a direction the U.S. Healthy People 2020 framework carried forward by adding a focus on social determinants and a digital interface. Self-care, including hygiene and stress management, remains a daily complement to clinical and public-health systems.
