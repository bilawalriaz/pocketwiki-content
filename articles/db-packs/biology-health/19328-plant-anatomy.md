# Plant anatomy

Plant anatomy, or phytotomy, is the study of the internal structure of plants. Originally the term also covered plant morphology (the description of external form and shape), but since the mid-twentieth century the two have been separated, and plant anatomy refers only to what lies beneath the surface. Modern work is usually done at the cellular level, by cutting thin sections of tissue and examining them under a microscope.

## How the field is organised

Two organising schemes are in use. A functional approach groups structures by what the plant does with them: nutrient transport, flowering, pollination, embryogenesis (the formation of the embryo inside the seed), and seed development. A more classical structural approach divides the subject by organ:

- Flower anatomy: calyx (sepals), corolla (petals), androecium (stamens), gynoecium (carpels).
- Leaf anatomy: epidermis, stomata (pores for gas exchange), palisade cells (columnar photosynthetic cells beneath the upper epidermis).
- Stem anatomy: vascular tissues, buds, shoot apex.
- Fruit and seed anatomy: ovule, seed, pericarp (fruit wall), accessory fruit (one formed from tissue outside the ovary).
- Wood anatomy: bark, cork, xylem (water-conducting tissue), phloem (sugar-conducting tissue), vascular cambium (the lateral meristem that adds wood), heartwood and sapwood, branch collar.
- Root anatomy: root, root tip, endodermis (the selective inner cortex layer).

The two systems reflect two longstanding aims of the discipline: understanding how plants work, and classifying them by their structure.

## A short history

About 300 BC Theophrastus wrote plant treatises, of which *Enquiry into Plants* and *On the Causes of Plants* survive. Plant anatomy became a modern science in the late 1600s through two founders. The Italian Marcello Malpighi published *Anatomia Plantarum* in 1671, the first major advance in plant internal structure since Aristotle. The British Nehemiah Grew published *An Idea of a Philosophical History of Plants* in 1672 and is credited with recognising plant cells, though he called them "vesicles" and "bladders."

In the eighteenth century Carl Linnaeus built taxonomy on structure. In 1802 Charles-François Brisseau de Mirbel helped found plant cytology (the study of plant cells). In 1838 Matthias Jakob Schleiden confirmed that lower plants are single-celled while higher plants are multicellular. Eduard Strasburger described mitosis (nuclear division) in plant cells and showed that new nuclei arise only from existing ones. In 1884 Gottlieb Haberlandt classified tissues by function, naming twelve tissue systems such as absorptive, mechanical, and photosynthetic. Katherine Esau's *Plant Anatomy* (1953), followed by *Anatomy of Seed Plants* (1960), became the standard textbook in North American universities and remained in print more than fifty years later.
