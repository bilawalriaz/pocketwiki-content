# Nutrition

Nutrition is the biochemical and physiological process by which organisms use food and water to obtain energy and build cellular structures, supporting survival, growth, and reproduction. The scientific study of this process, nutritional science, evolved from late-18th-century chemical analysis into a modern understanding of how specific nutrients sustain health. Because human nutrition shapes public health, agriculture, and disease prevention, the field emphasises people, but the same principles govern all living things.

## Nutrients and Their Roles

Nutrients supply energy and structural material. The major classes are macronutrients, needed in large quantities, and micronutrients, required in trace amounts. Macronutrients include carbohydrates, lipids (fats), and proteins; micronutrients split into organic vitamins and inorganic minerals. When metabolised, carbohydrates and proteins each yield 4 kcal per gram and fats 9 kcal per gram.

A subset of nutrients is essential: the body cannot synthesise them, so they must come from food. The absence or excess of any essential nutrient causes malnutrition, which differs from simply eating too little. Chronic imbalance contributes to obesity, type 2 diabetes, and deficiency diseases such as scurvy, anaemia, and cretinism. Deficiency can result from reduced intake or from impaired absorption during illness; the body responds by lowering energy expenditure and drawing on stored reserves. Daily Reference Values estimate population needs, though individual requirements vary with age, sex, activity, and health.

## How Organisms Get Their Food

Biologists classify organisms along several independent axes that reflect adaptations to different environments:

- Autotrophs build nutrients from inorganic carbon (such as CO₂); heterotrophs obtain carbon by consuming other organisms. Mixotrophs do both.
- Phototrophs use light as an energy source; chemotrophs harvest energy from chemical reactions.
- Organotrophs take electrons from organic compounds; lithotrophs from inorganic ones.
- Prototrophs synthesise all nutrients they need; auxotrophs cannot, a feature exploited in laboratory research.

Animals are heterotrophs that need carbohydrates, essential amino acids, and fatty acids. Herbivores rely on microbial fermentation to digest cellulose; obligate carnivores depend on meat for nutrients such as taurine, which cats require and cannot manufacture. Migration, breeding cycles, conditioned aversions, and nutrient-specific hunger all tie behaviour to food supply. Optimal foraging theory models feeding as a cost-benefit trade-off that maximises energy gain per unit effort. Specialists exploit one food type; generalists use many.

Plants are autotrophs, drawing carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen from air and water through stomata, and absorbing nitrogen, sulphur, phosphorus, and potassium from soil through roots. Root hairs take up mineral ions by cation exchange, releasing hydrogen ions to displace nutrient cations from soil particles. Atmospheric nitrogen becomes biologically usable only after bacteria fix it.

Fungi are chemoheterotrophs that secrete enzymes to digest matter externally and absorb the products through thread-like mycelia, living as decomposers, parasites, or symbionts. Protists include photosynthetic algae, predatory or parasitic protozoa, and mixotrophs that switch strategies with conditions. Bacteria and archaea span the full range, including phototrophs, lithotrophs, predatory strains that use chemotaxis to hunt, and extremophiles that exploit energy sources in nutrient-poor habitats.

## Nutrient Cycles

Nutrients move through ecosystems in continuous cycles, unlike energy, which flows one way and is lost as heat. Carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulphur, and water cycle through soil, air, water, and living tissue, sustaining ecosystem function and regulating climate and atmospheric oxygen. Disrupting these cycles, for instance through fossil fuel combustion or excessive fertiliser use, undermines both environmental stability and food security.

## Human Nutrition in Context

The Neolithic Revolution around 10,000 years ago shifted human diets from foraging to farming. The late-18th-century Chemical Revolution made systematic nutrient analysis possible, and the 1910s brought modern nutrition science with the identification of individual micronutrients. Thiamine became the first vitamin chemically identified in 1926, and vitamin C was linked to scurvy prevention in 1932. Industrialisation enabled mass food production, fortification, and processed foods, which improve safety and shelf-life but may lack essential nutrients. Open questions remain about how much social determinants versus biology drive nutrition-related disease, and how climate change will alter nutrient cycling and global food security.
