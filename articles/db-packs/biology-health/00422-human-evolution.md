# Human evolution

Human evolution is the roughly 7‑million‑year branching history by which *Homo sapiens* emerged from earlier primates. Four physical changes define the hominin lineage (the human side of the split with chimpanzees): habitual two‑legged walking (bipedalism), expanding brain size relative to body (encephalization), reduced size difference between males and females, and a precision grip. These traits appeared in a mosaic across species and regions, linked to toolmaking, diet, and social behavior through gene‑culture coevolution, in which cultural practices reshape genetic selection and vice versa.

## Primate background and the hominin split

Primates diverged from other mammals around 85 million years ago. Gibbons split off 18–12 mya; orangutans separated from African great apes around 14 mya. Molecular clock estimates place the hominin–gorilla split at 8–9 mya and the hominin–chimpanzee split at 4–7 mya, though a 2006 genomic study suggested possible hybridization, finalizing around 5–6 mya.

## Earliest hominins and the rise of bipedalism

Bipedalism, the foundational hominin adaptation, appears by 6–7 mya. Candidate earliest hominins include *Sahelanthropus tchadensis* (7 Ma, Chad) and *Orrorin tugenensis* (6 Ma, Kenya). *Ardipithecus* (5.5–4.4 Ma, Ethiopia) combines habitual bipedality with reduced canine size and a skull retaining juvenile features (paedomorphy). Some researchers read this as a self‑domestication pattern more like bonobos than chimpanzees, implying lower aggression, stronger maternal care, and active female mate choice.

Bipedalism reshaped the skeleton: a valgus (inward‑angled) knee, robust ankle, S‑shaped spine, shortened bowl‑shaped pelvis, and a forward‑shifted foramen magnum that places the skull atop the spine. The narrower birth canal limited neonatal brain size, favoring helpless infants, longer childhoods, shared childcare beyond the mother, and late‑life loss of fertility (menopause). The grandmother hypothesis explains this: older women boost descendants' survival by helping feed them.

## *Australopithecus* and the origin of *Homo*

*Australopithecus* evolved in East Africa around 4 mya and spread across the continent before disappearing about 2 mya. Lucy, an *A. afarensis* from 3.2 Ma, is the best‑known specimen. A "robust" branch, *Paranthropus*, evolved heavy chewing apparatus and went extinct. The genus *Homo* appears by 2.8 Ma (Ledi jaw, Ethiopia). *H. habilis* (2.8–1.4 Ma) had smaller molars, a brain around 600 cm³, and is associated with the earliest widespread stone tools (Oldowan), though its skeleton retained tree‑climbing features.

## *Homo erectus* and the first global dispersal

*H. erectus* (sometimes *H. ergaster* in Africa) appears around 1.9 Ma with a cranial capacity of 800–1100 cm³, nearly double earlier *Homo*. These populations mastered Acheulean handaxes, probably controlled fire, and were the first hominins to leave Africa: Dmanisi, Georgia, 1.85 Ma; Java, 1.5 Ma. In Europe, descendants evolved into *H. antecessor* and then *H. heidelbergensis* (800,000–300,000 years ago), which most researchers view as the last common ancestor of Neanderthals, Denisovans, and modern humans.

## Neanderthals, Denisovans, and island relatives

Neanderthals (*H. neanderthalensis*, 400,000–40,000 years ago) lived in Europe and western Asia, with cold‑adapted bodies, brains of 1200–1900 cm³, and an expanded visual cortex. Genome sequencing in 2010 showed that all non‑African modern humans carry 1–4% Neanderthal DNA from admixture roughly 45,000–80,000 years ago. Denisovans, known mainly from a finger bone and teeth in Denisova Cave (Altai), are a sister group to Neanderthals; they contributed 4–6% of Melanesian DNA and a high‑altitude‑adapted *EPAS1* allele to Tibetans. On islands, *H. floresiensis* and *H. luzonensis* show insular dwarfism, the evolutionary shrinkage of large‑bodied species on resource‑limited islands. *H. naledi* (around 250,000 years ago, South Africa) mixes primitive and derived features.

## Origin and spread of *Homo sapiens*

Anatomically modern humans appear in Africa around 300,000 years ago at sites such as Jebel Irhoud (Morocco), Omo and Herto (Ethiopia), and Florisbad (South Africa). The recent African origin model is strongly supported by mitochondrial DNA (Mitochondrial Eve, around 200,000 years ago), Y‑chromosome data, and the highest genetic diversity in living humans found in southern African San peoples. A major dispersal out of Africa occurred 70,000–50,000 years ago, with modern humans largely replacing archaic populations, with limited admixture from Neanderthal, Denisovan, and unidentified "ghost" lineages.

## The biology of modern humans

Brain size grew across the genus: *H. habilis* (~600 cm³) → *H. erectus* (~900 cm³) → Neanderthals and modern humans (~1300–1400 cm³). Modern brain shape, with a globular skull and expanded parietal, temporal, and prefrontal regions, appeared only 100,000–35,000 years ago. Likely drivers include higher‑quality food from cooking and meat, plus the social demands of larger groups, each feeding back on the other.

Other derived traits: canine reduction with ~15% body‑size difference between sexes, concealed ovulation paired with year‑round receptivity (favoring long‑term pair bonds), a precision grip enabled by ulnar opposition and a third‑metacarpal styloid process appearing around 1.8 Ma, a parabolic dental arch, a chin unique to *H. sapiens*, a descended larynx, dense eccrine sweat glands, and shoulders adapted for throwing.

## Tools and behavioral modernity

Stone tools predate *Homo*, with the Lomekwi industry at 3.3 Ma, but the Oldowan tradition at 2.6 Ma is associated with *H. habilis*. Acheulean handaxes (1.7 Ma–300 ka) accompany *H. erectus*; the Levallois prepared‑core technique (around 350 ka) marks Middle Paleolithic Neanderthals and *H. heidelbergensis*; Upper Paleolithic blade and bone toolkits (from 50 ka) accompany modern humans.

Behavioral modernity, defined by symbolic art, personal ornaments, ritual burial, specialized hunting, and long‑distance trade, appears abruptly in Eurasia around 50,000 years ago but has deeper African roots: ochre use, shell beads, and microliths appear by 100,000–70,000 years ago. Neanderthals made Châtelperronian assemblages imitating *H. sapiens* Aurignacian technology, evidence of cultural contact.

## Ongoing evolution in the Holocene

Since about 10,000 years ago, humans have continued to evolve through natural selection, genetic drift, and cultural niche construction, the process by which organisms modify their own environment and thereby change the selection pressures they face. Domesticated animals and cereals drove lactase persistence, extra amylase gene copies for starch digestion, and disease‑resistance alleles such as *HLA* variants from archaic humans. Tibetans carry a Denisovan‑derived *EPAS1* allele for high‑altitude tolerance; Andeans and Ethiopians use different pathways. Malaria maintains the sickle‑cell allele through heterozygote advantage, and historical kuru epidemics selected for a *PRNP* variant.

## How the field learned this

Linnaeus placed humans in *Homo* within Primates in 1758 on morphological grounds. Darwin supplied natural and sexual selection (1859, 1871); Huxley argued for ape ancestry in 1863. African fossils closed the "missing link" debate: *Australopithecus africanus* (Taung Child, 1925), Leakey discoveries at Olduvai, Lucy (1974), and *Ardipithecus* (1990s). In 1967 Sarich and Wilson used immunological distances to date the human–chimp split at 4–5 Ma, far younger than the fossil estimate. Whole‑genome sequencing after 2010 overturned strict replacement models by revealing pervasive Neanderthal and Denisovan admixture.
