# Stem cell

A stem cell is an undifferentiated or partially differentiated cell that can both make more of itself and change into specialised cell types. These two abilities, self-renewal and potency, are the defining features. They sit at the start of any cell lineage and are distinguished from progenitor cells, which can divide only a limited number of times, and from precursor or blast cells, which are usually committed to becoming one specific cell type.

## Potency

Potency describes how many kinds of cells a stem cell can produce, and it forms a hierarchy. Totipotent cells can form a complete organism, including extraembryonic tissues, and exist only as the fertilised egg and the first few cells from its division. Pluripotent cells can give rise to nearly all cell types derived from any of the three germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm), but not extraembryonic structures. Multipotent cells are restricted to a closely related family, such as blood cells. Oligopotent cells can become only a few types, like lymphoid or myeloid. Unipotent cells produce a single cell type but still self-renew, which separates them from ordinary cells.

## Self-renewal

Stem cells keep their numbers through two modes of division. Asymmetric division produces one stem cell and one differentiating daughter. Symmetric division produces two identical stem cells. In adults, stem cells also depend on signals from their niche, specialised microenvironments such as bone marrow or gonads. Leaving the niche or losing those signals triggers differentiation. In the Drosophila germarium, the signals decapentaplegic and adherens junctions keep germline stem cells undifferentiated.

To keep dividing past the Hayflick limit that stops ordinary cells, stem cells use telomerase, an enzyme that restores the telomeres that cap and protect chromosome ends.

## Where stem cells live

In mammals, about 50 to 150 cells form the inner cell mass of a blastocyst around days 5–14 of development. In the embryo, these cells are pluripotent and give rise to all derivatives of the three germ layers; in the lab they can be held in the stem-cell stage as embryonic stem cell (ESC) lines. ESCs in culture are identified by surface markers SSEA-3, SSEA-4, Tra-1-60 and Tra-1-81, and by core transcription factors Oct-4, Nanog and Sox2 that suppress differentiation genes and maintain pluripotency. Mouse ESCs need gelatin and LIF, or a GSK3B and MAPK/ERK inhibitor cocktail called 2i; human ESCs need a mouse embryonic fibroblast feeder layer and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF).

Adult (somatic) stem cells are rare and sit in specific niches including bone marrow, adipose tissue, blood, and the gonads. They replenish tissues that turn over rapidly, such as blood, skin epithelium, bone, cartilage, muscle and fat, and are usually multipotent or unipotent. Hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), which produce all blood and immune cells, are the best-studied example. Fetal stem cells from the fetus itself or from extraembryonic membranes divide actively but are not immortal. Amniotic fluid and umbilical cord blood contain multipotent perinatal stem cells, an active research focus partly because using them avoids the ethical objections attached to embryonic sources.

## History

The term stem cell was coined by Theodor Boveri and Valentin Haecker in the late 19th century. The defining properties were established in the early 1960s by Ernest McCulloch and James Till at the University of Toronto, who showed that single bone marrow cells could form colonies in the spleens of irradiated mice. Joined by Andrew Becker and Louis Siminovitch, they proved each colony came from a single cell and published in Nature in 1963. The first clinical use came in 1956 when French oncologist Georges Mathé performed a bone marrow transplant on five workers irradiated at the Vinča Nuclear Institute in Yugoslavia; all survived.

In 1981, Martin Evans and Matthew Kaufman first isolated and cultured mouse ESCs from blastocysts. James Thomson isolated human ESCs in 1998, enabling new transplantation and cell-testing methods. In 2006, Shinya Yamanaka's team in Kyoto converted adult fibroblasts into pluripotent cells by switching on four genes (Oct3/4, Sox2, c-Myc, Klf4), creating induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). iPSCs behave much like ESCs but sidestep the embryo-destruction controversy and, because they can be made from a patient's own cells, carry almost no risk of immune rejection. They retain some epigenetic memory of their tissue of origin, and their chromatin is more methylated than that of ESCs.

## Cell cycle

Embryonic stem cells divide every 8 to 10 hours, much faster than somatic cells at around 20 hours, because of a compressed G1 phase, no G0 phase, and altered checkpoints that keep cells mostly in S phase. In mouse ESCs, Cyclin E/Cdk2 stays constantly active, keeping retinoblastoma protein (pRb) hyperphosphorylated and inactive, which removes the D-type cyclin requirement and shortens G1. In human ESCs, G1 is shortened but still functional, driven by high Cyclin D2 and Cdk4 and low levels of inhibitors like p21, p27, p57 and Ink-family members. ESCs also lack a working G1 DNA-damage checkpoint and rely on S and G2/M checkpoints or apoptosis instead, which removes damaged cells quickly and protects the next generation from mutations. Manipulating the same regulators, for example overexpressing Cyclin D/Cdk4 or knocking down Rb, raises the efficiency of reprogramming adult cells into iPSCs.

## Therapies and limits

The only established medical therapy using stem cells is hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, bone marrow or umbilical cord blood, used for over 50 years against leukaemia, lymphoma and related blood cancers. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) from bone marrow, fat, muscle or liver, which can become bone, cartilage or fat cells and modulate inflammation, are widely studied in clinical trials for wound healing and tissue repair. No treatments using ESCs are approved; an early US trial for spinal cord injury by Geron Corporation started in October 2010 and was discontinued in November 2011.

Undifferentiated pluripotent cells can form teratomas after transplantation, and pluripotency itself is linked to tumour formation, including in some fetal and induced pluripotent cells. Donor cells often require immunosuppression. The "stem cell tourism" industry markets unproven procedures that have caused spinal tumours and deaths. ESC research remains contentious because isolating ESCs usually destroys the blastocyst, and several countries restrict the practice while others, including the UK and China, have supported it. Adult and iPSC-based approaches avoid that destruction, but adult cells are hard to expand without replicative senescence, and iPSC reprogramming remains slow and sometimes incomplete.
