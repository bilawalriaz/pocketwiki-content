# Immune system

The immune system is a layered biological network that defends organisms against pathogens, cancer, and foreign material by recognising molecules as self or non-self. Failures of regulation produce immunodeficiency, autoimmunity, or hypersensitivity.

## Layered architecture

Defence is organised in three tiers, each slower and more specific than the one before. Surface barriers — skin, mucous membranes, and their chemical secretions — block most invaders. If those are breached, the innate immune system responds within minutes in a generic way to anything foreign. If the innate response is evaded, vertebrates deploy the adaptive immune system, which targets specific molecules and remembers them. Both internal systems depend on detecting molecular patterns that mark something as non-self.

## Innate immunity

The innate system is the main defence in most organisms and is ancient in evolutionary terms. Its cells carry pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), a class of sensors that includes toll-like receptors (TLRs). These bind conserved molecular signatures: pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) on microbes, or damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released by injured host cells.

The main cellular actors are phagocytes — macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells — which engulf and digest invaders, and natural killer (NK) cells, which destroy the body's own cells that have become infected or abnormal. Inflammation, the hallmark early response, is driven by lipid messengers called eicosanoids and signalling proteins called cytokines. The complement system, a biochemical cascade, damages foreign cells and tags them for destruction.

## Adaptive immunity

Found in jawed vertebrates, the adaptive system adds two capacities the innate system lacks: antigen specificity and immunological memory. Its actors are lymphocytes, divided into B cells and T cells.

B cells produce antibodies — circulating proteins that bind a specific non-self molecule, called an antigen, and neutralise or mark it. This branch is humoral immunity because the effectors travel in body fluids. T cells act by direct contact. Killer T cells destroy infected or abnormal host cells; helper T cells coordinate the response of other immune cells.

A key step is antigen presentation. Most cells continuously display fragments of their internal proteins on surface molecules called MHC (major histocompatibility complex). When a cell is infected or cancerous, those fragments include foreign or abnormal peptides, which T cells detect. MHC is central to distinguishing self from non-self.

After an infection, a subset of activated B and T cells becomes memory cells. On re-exposure to the same pathogen, these cells mount a faster and stronger response — the basis of vaccination.

## Regulation and disorders

The immune system is not autonomous. Hormones such as cortisol and sex hormones modulate its activity, and the nervous system influences it as well. Sleep deprivation and intense exercise transiently impair immune function, and chronic stress shifts it toward suppression or inappropriate activation.

Three broad classes of disorder follow from the system's design. Immunodeficiency — a weakened response — can be inherited, caused by infections such as HIV, or produced by malnutrition. Autoimmunity arises when the system mistakes self for non-self and attacks healthy tissue, as in rheumatoid arthritis. Hypersensitivity is an excessive or misdirected response that damages the host, as in allergies.

## Manipulation and medical use

Medicine exploits the immune system in several directions. Immunosuppressive drugs control autoimmunity and prevent transplant rejection. Vaccination deliberately induces immunological memory against specific pathogens, typically by exposing the immune system to a harmless version of the target. The system performs ongoing tumour surveillance, though successful tumours evolve evasion strategies. Immune-like defence is ancient: bacteria use CRISPR-based systems against viruses. Pathogens have co-evolved countermeasures including antigenic variation — repeatedly changing surface molecules so memory cells no longer recognise them — which is why influenza requires a new vaccine each season.

## Open questions

The conditions that activate gamma-delta T cells, an unusual subset that straddles innate and adaptive immunity, are not fully understood. The role of vitamin D in immunity is debated, with clinical trials giving conflicting results. Whether highly regenerative animals such as axolotls have a less competent immune system remains an active hypothesis. And claims that everyday habits can "boost" immunity beyond its normal capacity lack strong evidence.
