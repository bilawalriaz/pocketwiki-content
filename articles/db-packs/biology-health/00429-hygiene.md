# Hygiene

Hygiene is the set of practices that prevent the spread of disease-causing organisms. Cleaning (handwashing, disinfecting surfaces) is usually the means; hygiene is the goal of interrupting transmission. The mental shorthand is the "chain of infection" — a path from pathogen source to susceptible host. Hygiene matters most where sanitation is weak, because breaking one link cuts infectious disease sharply.

## How infection spreads at home

Main infection sources at home are people, raw foods, water, and domestic animals. Pathogens concentrate in moist reservoirs: sinks, toilets, and damp cleaning cloths. Spread occurs mainly on hands, food-contact surfaces, and cleaning cloths via the fecal–oral route, with secondary spread through clothing, linens, and air (respiratory viruses, fungal spores). Some pathogens need only 10–100 viable units to infect; some viruses need fewer.

"Targeted hygiene" intervenes at the moments and places where transmission is most likely, using a risk-based method borrowed from the Hazard Analysis Critical Control Point (HACCP) framework from food safety. Hygienic cleaning either removes pathogens with soap and rinsing, kills them in place with disinfectants, heat, or alcohol sanitizers, or does both, as laundry does.

## Routine practices

Handwashing with soap substantially cuts gastrointestinal and respiratory infections. Respiratory hygiene means catching coughs and sneezes in tissues or the elbow. Routine cleaning of hands, food, and high-touch surfaces (toilet seats, flush handles, taps, worktops) reduces spread; flush toilets are low-risk if maintained, though splashing and aerosols occur during diarrheal illness. Damp, poorly ventilated tiles, curtains, and carpets grow molds that cause infection, allergy, and odor.

Laundry targets items that touch the body (underwear, towels, nappies) and cloths used for vomit or feces cleanup. Laundering removes microbes physically through rinsing and spinning, thermally through hot water, and chemically with surfactants, oxygen bleach, or hypochlorite; sun-drying further reduces pathogens. A 2013 review of 30 laundering studies found standardization so poor that confident guidelines remain impossible, which is why agencies disagree on temperatures and detergents. Laundry hygiene limits antibiotic-resistant strains such as MRSA and NDM-1- or ESBL-producing enterobacteria; healthy carriers can infect themselves after surgery, and US experience shows MRSA spreading within families, prisons, schools, and sports teams through skin contact and shared objects.

About one in five people (mostly the elderly with other illnesses, the very young, discharged patients, and those on immunosuppressants or with invasive devices) has reduced immunity. Carers may need extra procedures such as catheter or dressing changes; antiseptics on wounds prevent sepsis-causing bacteria. Daily practices are otherwise the same as for everyone.

## Products and terminology

A disinfectant kills pathogens; all are bactericidal, and some are also fungicidal, sporicidal, or virucidal. The label should say "disinfectant" or "kills." An "antibacterial" product is less specific: some kill bacteria, others only slow their growth. "Sanitizer" now usually means an alcohol hand rub, which does not work on visibly soiled hands. "Biocide" is the umbrella term covering antiseptics, disinfectants, and pesticides.

## Personal and sleep hygiene

Personal hygiene combines avoiding illness, feeling well, social acceptance, and protecting others; specific norms are culture-dependent. Common practices include bathing, handwashing before food handling, dental care, nail trimming, clean clothing, menstrual hygiene, anal cleansing, and covering coughs. Ear canals are mostly self-cleaning; pushing wax inward causes irritation or infection. For teeth, dentists recommend brushing twice daily with a soft brush replaced roughly every three months, not rinsing after brushing so fluoride stays on the teeth, avoiding brushing immediately after acidic drinks, flossing daily, and annual check-ups; the benefit of amorphous calcium phosphate products is debated. Grooming choices such as deodorant or shaving concern appearance, not disease prevention.

Sleep hygiene was developed in the late 1970s for mild-to-moderate insomnia. Recommendations include a regular sleep schedule, limited napping, no late exercise, avoiding alcohol, nicotine, and caffeine near bedtime, limiting evening light and worry, leaving bed if unable to sleep, using the bed only for sleep, and a dark, comfortable environment. Evidence for each recommendation was "limited and inconclusive" in a 2014 assessment.

## The hygiene hypothesis

A popular idea that "dirt is healthy" worries clinicians. The actual evidence is narrower: early-childhood microbial exposure does seem to protect against allergies, but harmful infections are not required, and handwashing has not been shown to raise allergy rates. Researchers suspect modern lifestyles reduced contact with immuno-regulatory organisms such as intestinal helminths, though which specific changes mattered remains uncertain.

## Services and low-resource settings

Medical hygiene covers isolation, instrument sterilization, wound dressing, medical-waste disposal, scrubbing up, and alcohol sanitizers. It mostly developed in the 19th century and was tightened after the AIDS and Ebola outbreaks. Food hygiene covers safe handling, storage, preparation, and serving. Personal-care services such as piercing and tattooing require sterilized instruments, ideally by autoclave.

In low- and middle-income countries, the WASH agenda under Sustainable Development Goal 6 (target 2030) promotes universal water access, sanitation, and hygiene. About two million people, most children under five, die yearly from diarrheal diseases. Handwashing with soap can roughly halve diarrhea and cut respiratory infections by about a quarter, with further effects on skin diseases, trachoma, and the worm infections ascariasis and trichuriasis. Household water treatment options include chlorine, iodine, boiling, ceramic filters, solar disinfection, UV light, flocculation sachets, and portable purifiers, with safe storage preventing recontamination. Roughly 120 million people in Europe still lack safe drinking water.

## History and origins

Oracle bones from Shang-dynasty China (1600–1046 BCE) describe hair and body washing; the later Book of Rites prescribed a hot shower every five days and a hair wash every three days. Ibn Sina's 1025 *Canon of Medicine* proposed contagion through breath, water, and dirt. Mid-19th-century figures — Semmelweis and Nightingale for handwashing, Snow for linking cholera to fecal-contaminated water — joined Pasteur's germ theory and Lister's antisepsis to make modern hygiene possible. The word "hygiene" entered English in 1676 from French *hygiène*, itself from Greek *hygieinē technē*, "the art of health," named for Hygeia, the Greek personification of health and cleanliness.
