# Disease ecology

Disease ecology is the sub-discipline of ecology that studies how hosts, pathogens, and the environment interact to produce patterns of infection in wild and human populations. A pathogen is a microorganism or parasite that causes disease; a host is the organism it infects; a vector is an animal, often an insect or tick, that transmits a pathogen between hosts. Because emerging and re-emerging infectious diseases now appear at unprecedented rates, the field matters for public health, ecosystem health, and biodiversity.

## How the field took shape

The roots lie in 19th-century germ theory, but ecologists quickly realised that environmental conditions, not just microbes, govern who gets sick. Malaria work showed transmission depends on the parasite *Plasmodium*, the *Anopheles* mosquito, and the mosquito's aquatic breeding habitats, and Ronald Ross's early mathematical models began quantifying those links. By the mid-20th century, researchers such as Charles Elton treated pathogens as ecological forces shaping population dynamics, a view reinforced when myxomatosis swept through European rabbits. Public-health scientists studying vector-borne disease then added ecological methods that tracked land use, climate, and species interactions. Anderson and May's 1970s–80s models, which combined host density, parasite reproduction, immunity, and population regulation, gave the field its unifying theoretical core and still anchor modern epidemiology and conservation biology.

From the 1990s on, HIV, SARS, Ebola, West Nile virus, and COVID-19 made clear that wildlife ecology, biodiversity loss, and environmental change drive spillover, the jump of a pathogen from animals into humans. The field became deeply interdisciplinary, merging conservation science, global health, climate research, and veterinary medicine, and molecular tools, satellite imaging, and climate modelling now make it possible to map reservoirs, track pathogen evolution, and forecast outbreak risk.

## What controls disease in nature

Three classes of factors determine whether a disease persists and spreads.

Ecological factors include the size, density, and composition of host populations. A disease needs enough susceptible hosts to keep transmitting, and competition and predation reshape that pool. Predators often remove sick individuals before they pass infection on, and predators can spread parasites through their faeces or, in some cases, activate dormant pathogens in disturbed prey.

Biological factors operate at the individual level. Larger or more mobile animals tend to encounter more parasites, and juveniles, lacking acquired immunity, are similarly vulnerable.

Anthropogenic factors (human-caused ones) include translocating wildlife, fragmenting habitats, building roads, and trading species globally, all of which create "novel species associations" that pathogens exploit.

## Human-driven change as a disease driver

Urbanization and biodiversity loss. More than 65% of people will live in cities by 2025. As biodiversity declines, large, slow-reproducing species disappear first, leaving small, adaptable ones such as bats, rats, and mice, many of which are competent reservoir hosts, species that maintain a pathogen long-term, for human pathogens.

Invasive species. Global trade and travel move pathogens and their hosts into new regions, where escapees disrupt local ecosystems and the services such as clean water and nutrient cycling that those ecosystems provide.

Habitat fragmentation. Roads and forest clearing create edge habitats that increase contact among wildlife, vectors, and people. The 2013–2015 West African Ebola outbreak is thought to have begun when deforestation placed frugivorous and insectivorous bats closer to dense human settlements, enabling direct contact and spillover.

Climate change. Warmer temperatures, altered rainfall, and shifting humidity expand the ranges of vectors such as mosquitoes and ticks, lengthen transmission seasons, and let parasites such as *Plasmodium* complete their development inside the vector. Pathogens are moving into higher latitudes and into populations with no prior exposure.

Modelling these effects uses process-based tools such as MIASMA, which links climate scenarios to vector reproduction, and statistical tools such as CLIMEX, which maps vector ranges. Predictions of El Niño (ENSO) can anticipate disease peaks up to two months ahead because ocean temperature anomalies, rainfall, and plankton blooms all shape the ecology of waterborne and vector-borne pathogens.

## Four cases that show the principles at work

Malaria is tightly regulated by climate. Warmer temperatures speed *Plasmodium*'s development inside *Anopheles* mosquitoes, ensuring they become infectious within their lifespan, while changed rainfall expands breeding habitat.

Lyme disease is the most common tick-borne illness in the United States and Europe, with roughly 476,000 European and 200,000 US cases per year. Milder winters and longer springs let the tick *Ixodes scapularis* expand into southern Canada, aided by migratory birds and dense deer populations. A non-native tick, *Haemaphysalis punctata*, has spread in Britain via migratory birds and sheep movements.

West Nile virus circulates between mosquitoes and birds of prey such as eagles, hawks, falcons, and owls. Climate-driven hybridisation of the mosquito vectors *Culex pipiens* and *Culex quinquefasciatus* in New York and Connecticut produces hybrids better adapted to varied conditions and more efficient at transmitting the virus.

White-nose syndrome, first found in New York bats in 2006, is caused by the cold-loving fungus *Pseudogymnoascus destructans*. It disturbs hibernation, depleting fat reserves and killing more than 90% of some populations, one of the most dramatic wildlife declines ever recorded. Cholera shows the marine side: *Vibrio cholerae* attaches to copepods (tiny crustaceans) and blooms with phytoplankton, so satellite readings of chlorophyll and sea-surface temperature serve as early-warning signals, and simple cloth filtration of drinking water can cut cholera risk by more than half.

## From understanding to action

Zoonotic diseases such as Ebola, hantavirus, and COVID-19 spill over when deforestation, climate change, and fragmentation bring wildlife, livestock, and people into new contact. The One Health approach, which combines human medicine, veterinary medicine, and environmental science, is now central to rabies control, malaria prevention, and pandemic preparedness. Treating ecosystems as part of the disease system has also made interventions cheaper: filtering copepods from water, managing predator and reservoir populations, and protecting biodiversity all reduce transmission by addressing the ecological conditions that sustain pathogens.
