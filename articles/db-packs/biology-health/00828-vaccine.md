# Vaccine

A vaccine is a biological preparation that trains the immune system to recognise and destroy a specific infectious agent before it causes serious illness. Vaccination is the most effective method of preventing infectious disease: it eradicated smallpox and has sharply restricted polio, measles, and tetanus. The World Health Organization reports licensed vaccines for 25 preventable infections.

## How vaccines work

The immune system treats vaccine components as foreign, destroys them, and retains a memory. On later exposure to the virulent pathogen, antibodies and T cells neutralise it before it can establish infection. Innate immunity activates within roughly 12 hours, but adaptive immunity takes one to two weeks, so infection can still occur in the interval. Protection may be partial or temporary; older people mount weaker responses, a decline called immunosenescence, so adjuvants are commonly added to strengthen the reaction.

When a high share of a population is vaccinated, transmission chains break and outbreaks become hard to sustain. This community effect is herd immunity. Measles illustrates the scale: US cases fell from about 763,000 in 1958, with 552 deaths, to fewer than 150 per year after vaccination, and the measles vaccine is estimated to prevent roughly a million deaths worldwide each year. Polio eradication has confined endemic transmission to parts of Afghanistan, Nigeria, and Pakistan; missed target dates reflect access problems and disinformation rather than flaws in the vaccines.

## Effectiveness and limits

Vaccine failure has two sources. Vaccine-related causes include weak attenuation, poor regimens, or administration errors. Host-related causes account for two to ten percent of inadequate responses and stem from genetics, immune status, age, health, and nutrition; X-linked agammaglobulinemia, a genetic disorder that prevents antibody production, is one example. Breakthrough infections in vaccinated people typically cause less severe illness and are less transmissible.

Vaccines are generally safe. Common reactions are mild: fever, injection-site pain, and muscle aches. Severe events are extremely rare: the varicella vaccine can rarely complicate immunodeficient individuals, and rotavirus vaccines are moderately associated with intussusception, a bowel-folding condition. At least 19 countries operate no-fault compensation schemes, including the US National Childhood Vaccine Injury Act and the UK Vaccine Damage Payment. Concerns that thiomersal, a mercury-containing preservative, causes autism lack scientific support; a 10–11 year study of 657,461 children found no link between MMR and autism.

## Types of vaccine

Eight design strategies cover nearly all licensed products.

- Attenuated: live but weakened microbes such as measles, mumps, rubella, yellow fever, and BCG. They elicit durable cellular and antibody responses but are unsafe for immunocompromised people and can rarely revert to virulence.
- Inactivated: killed organisms including inactivated polio, hepatitis A, rabies, and most influenza vaccines.
- Toxoid: inactivated bacterial toxins, used for tetanus, diphtheria, and a rattlesnake vaccine for dogs.
- Subunit: fragments only, such as the hepatitis B surface protein now made by inserting viral genes into yeast, HPV virus-like particles in Gardasil, and influenza surface proteins.
- Conjugate: polysaccharide capsules linked to a protein carrier, used for Haemophilus influenzae type b.
- Outer membrane vesicle: hollow bacterial envelopes, as in serogroup B meningococcal vaccines.
- Heterotypic, or Jennerian: animal pathogens causing mild human disease, originally cowpox for smallpox and BCG for tuberculosis.
- Genetic: nucleic acids that instruct cells to make the antigen, including viral vectors such as adenovirus and vaccinia, mRNA wrapped in lipid nanoparticles as in the Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna COVID-19 vaccines, and DNA plasmids. Genetic vaccines stimulate B- and T-cell responses and contain no infectious agent.

Monovalent vaccines target one strain; multivalent products cover several, described as bivalent, trivalent, or tetravalent. Adjuvants boost immune response and permit lower doses, with aluminium salts the standard. A 1928 unpreserved diphtheria vaccine killed 12 of 21 children through Staphylococcus contamination, a case that drove routine use of preservatives such as 2-phenoxyethanol.

## Regulation, economics, and production

Licensure requires Phase I–III trials showing safety, immunogenicity, effectiveness, and durability, overseen by bodies such as the EMA, the US FDA, and India's Drugs Controller General. The WHO prequalifies products distributed through UNICEF under Good Manufacturing Practice standards, and Phase IV postmarketing surveillance continues after release. Combination products like MMRV and Pentavalent reduce the burden of up to 24 injections by age two.

Diseases that most need vaccines, including HIV, malaria, and tuberculosis, disproportionately burden poor countries with low commercial return, so development depends on government and non-profit funding. The WHO notes that patents are not the largest barrier; financial, infrastructure, and workforce needs dominate, and there are no true generic vaccines because each facility must fully test its own product. A production plant costs 50 to 500 million US dollars and takes 4 to 6 years to build, with full development running 10 to 15 years.

## History

Variolation, the deliberate inoculation of people with smallpox material to induce mild disease and immunity, preceded true vaccination; Lady Mary Wortley Montagu brought the practice from Turkey to Britain in 1721. In 1796 Edward Jenner vaccinated James Phipps with cowpox pus and then variolated him without causing illness, and his 1798 *Inquiry into the Variolae vaccinae Known as the Cow Pox* spread the technique worldwide. Louis Pasteur extended the term "vaccine" beyond smallpox in 1881, and his chicken cholera and anthrax products followed. Egg-based virus culture, which began with Woodruff and Goodpasture's 1931 fowlpox work and gave way to cell culture in 1959, enabled the polio vaccines and the final eradication of smallpox. First-generation vaccines are whole organisms; second-generation are subunit proteins; third-generation are nucleic-acid based, with Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman receiving Columbia's Horwitz Prize in 2021 for foundational mRNA work that enabled the 2020 deployment of mRNA COVID-19 vaccines. Effective vaccines remain elusive for herpes simplex, malaria, gonorrhoea, and HIV.

Vaccine hesitancy, the delay or refusal of available vaccines, causes outbreaks and deaths despite the scientific consensus on safety and efficacy, and the WHO named it one of the top ten global health threats in 2019.
