# Cell physiology

Cell physiology is the study of the activities that keep a cell alive. Animal, plant, and microorganism cells differ in structure but share core functions: they all maintain boundaries, transform energy, and move materials in and out.

## Two cell lineages

Every cell is either a prokaryote or a eukaryote. Prokaryotes appeared first and have no membrane-bound nucleus; their DNA sits in an open region called the nucleoid. Eukaryotes evolved later, are usually larger, and wrap their DNA inside a nucleus along with most of their other organelles. Prokaryotes split into two groups, bacteria and archaea, and carry distinctive surface projections: fimbriae (finger-like, used for attachment) and flagella (threadlike, used for movement). Both lineages share a plasma membrane that encloses the cytoplasm, and ribosomes, the particles that synthesize proteins by linking amino acids into chains.

## Eukaryotic compartments and their jobs

The nucleus holds the DNA inside a nuclear envelope and contains the nucleolus, where ribosomal subunits are built, and chromatin, the DNA-protein fibre. Surrounding it, the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) comes in two forms: rough ER, studded with ribosomes, which makes membrane proteins and secretory proteins, and smooth ER, which synthesises lipids and carries out other metabolic work.

The Golgi apparatus is a stack of flattened sacs that finishes, sorts, and packages molecules received from the ER, then ships them out in vesicles, small membrane-bound sacs. Lysosomes are vesicles filled with hydrolytic enzymes (enzymes that cut other molecules by adding water) that digest material delivered to them. Mitochondria run cellular respiration, the set of reactions that convert nutrients into ATP, the cell's main energy currency. A cytoskeleton of protein fibres holds the cell's shape and drives its movement.

## Moving substances across the membrane

Cells move material across their plasma membrane in two ways. Passive transport requires no energy: substances flow down their concentration gradient, from a region where they are more concentrated to one where they are less concentrated. Active transport is powered by ATP and can move a substance against its gradient.

Ions cross the membrane through three kinds of protein machinery. Channel proteins let ions flow down an electrochemical gradient, a combined difference in concentration and electrical charge, producing electrical signals. Transporter proteins couple an ion's movement to another gradient to generate signals. Pumps keep the gradients themselves in place. The dominant pump is the Na/K pump, which moves three sodium ions out of the cell and two potassium ions in for each ATP it breaks down into ADP and phosphate, leaving the inside electrically negative relative to the outside.

## Three ways to take material in

Endocytosis is active transport inward: the plasma membrane wraps around material and pinches off as a vesicle inside the cell. It takes three forms.

In phagocytosis, the cell extends pseudopods, temporary bulges of membrane and cytoplasm, around solid particles such as food, enclosing them in a food vacuole. A lysosome then fuses with that vacuole, and its hydrolytic enzymes digest the contents.

In pinocytosis, the membrane folds inward to take in droplets of extracellular fluid, bringing whatever molecules happen to be dissolved there.

In receptor-mediated endocytosis, receptor proteins on the membrane first bind specific target molecules. The loaded receptors cluster into coated pits that sink inward and seal into vesicles. The vesicle releases its cargo inside the cell, and the empty receptors return to the membrane for reuse.

## The protein pipeline

Proteins destined for export or for insertion into membranes follow a defined route. Lipids and proteins are first synthesised in the ER, where carbohydrates are attached to form glycoproteins. The Golgi apparatus then modifies them further, sometimes adding lipids to make glycolipids, and packages them into vesicles that travel to the plasma membrane. There the vesicles fuse with the membrane and release their contents to the outside, a step called exocytosis, the same machinery as endocytosis running in reverse.
