# Adaptive immune system

The adaptive immune system is the branch of vertebrate immunity that recognises specific pathogens and retains memory of them, producing a faster, stronger response on re-exposure. It works alongside the innate immune system, which supplies general, immediate defence. Both arms use two strategies: humoral immunity, in which soluble antibodies patrol body fluids, and cell-mediated immunity, in which specialised white blood cells kill infected or abnormal cells directly.

What sets the adaptive system apart is specificity and memory. Each lymphocyte bears receptors that fit one particular antigen, and any structure carrying that antigen is targeted. After a first encounter, a fraction of the activated lymphocytes persist as long-lived memory cells, so a second infection is cleared in days rather than weeks. Vaccination uses this principle: a harmless version of a pathogen trains the system without causing disease.

## How specificity is generated

The human genome has only about 25,000 genes, yet more than a trillion distinct antibody shapes can be made. Diversity arises from two mechanisms. In V(D)J recombination, each developing lymphocyte randomly joins one Variable, one Diversity, and one Joining gene segment to assemble a unique receptor. The rearranged gene is permanent, so every daughter cell of that lymphocyte inherits the same specificity. Somatic hypermutation, which occurs after activation, introduces extra point mutations into antibody genes, refining receptors until they bind their target more tightly. Because the gene rearrangement is irreversible, the chosen specificity passes to the memory cells responsible for long-term protection.

## The two lymphocyte classes

Both B cells and T cells originate in the bone marrow from the same stem cell. B cells mature in the bone marrow and mediate humoral immunity; T cell progenitors migrate to the thymus, where they mature and are screened to remove strongly self-reactive cells.

At any time, B and T cells exist in three functional states: naive cells that have not yet met their antigen, effector cells actively fighting an infection, and memory cells that persist afterward. The human body holds roughly two trillion lymphocytes, which make up 20–40% of white blood cells; about 1–2% recirculate through the bloodstream each hour, raising the chance that the rare cell bearing a matching receptor will encounter its antigen, usually in a lymph node.

## Antigen presentation: the bridge to T cells

A T cell cannot recognise a free antigen. It only sees peptide fragments displayed on the surface of another cell by MHC (major histocompatibility complex) molecules, also called HLA in humans. MHC class I is found on almost all nucleated cells and shows fragments from inside the cell, including viral proteins. MHC class II is restricted to specialised antigen-presenting cells (dendritic cells, macrophages, and B cells) and shows fragments engulfed from outside. T cells that recognise class I become cytotoxic; those that recognise class II become helpers. Every cell also displays MHC loaded with self peptides, and this self-display is what allows the system to leave normal tissue alone. Failures of this distinction underlie autoimmune disease.

## T cell functions

CD8+ cytotoxic T cells kill infected or damaged cells. Activation requires strong binding to an MHC class I–peptide complex, often with help from CD4+ cells. Once active, they release perforin to puncture the target membrane, allowing granzymes to enter and trigger apoptosis. Most effector cells die after the infection resolves, leaving a memory pool.

CD4+ helper T cells have no killing activity of their own; they direct the response by releasing cytokines. Two classic subsets dominate. Th1 cells release interferon-gamma, which activates macrophages and drives antibodies that opsonise pathogens; this response is most effective against intracellular microbes. Th2 cells release interleukins 4 and 5, which drive antibody class switching and recruit eosinophils, and work best against extracellular parasites and toxins. Other CD4+ subsets include regulatory T cells, which suppress overactive responses to self-antigens, and follicular helper T cells, which assist B cells in producing high-affinity antibodies. HIV is severe because it specifically destroys CD4+ T cells, removing the immune system's organisers.

Gamma-delta T cells straddle innate and adaptive immunity: they rearrange their receptors like other T cells, yet some subsets respond within hours to common microbial patterns without prior exposure.

## B cells and antibodies

B cells bind antigens in their native form, not as processed peptides. A naive B cell that meets its matching antigen and receives help from a CD4+ T cell proliferates and differentiates into a plasma cell, a short-lived factory that secretes antibodies for two to three days, and into long-lived memory B cells (about 10% of the activated pool) ready for rapid response on reinfection.

Antibodies (immunoglobulins) are Y-shaped proteins of five classes in mammals: IgA, IgD, IgE, IgG, and IgM. They do not destroy targets directly but tag them in five ways: clumping infectious units together, activating the complement cascade to lyse cells, opsonising pathogens for phagocytes, flagging infected cells for destruction by natural killer cells, and blocking microbes from binding to mucosal surfaces.

## Immunological memory and vaccination

A first adaptive response in humans takes 4–7 days to reach effective strength. On re-exposure, memory cells short-circuit that delay, often so completely that the host notices nothing. Immunisation exploits this by presenting a harmless version of a pathogen (a live-attenuated virus, a killed organism, or a fragment, often combined with an adjuvant to stimulate innate cells) so the body generates memory without disease.

A separate, short-term form of protection comes from passive immunity, in which antibodies rather than memory are transferred: IgG crosses the placenta before birth, and IgA passes through breast milk afterwards. Antibody-rich serum can also be given artificially for immediate but short-lived protection.

## Beyond mammals

Vertebrate adaptive immunity originated in jawed fish roughly 500 million years ago, after a whole-genome duplication supplied spare genes that were co-opted for receptor diversity. Most core components (immunoglobulins, T cell receptors, MHC, RAG) were already present in cartilaginous fish. Jawless vertebrates such as lampreys and hagfish lack immunoglobulin-based receptors and instead use variable lymphocyte receptors built from LRR gene segments to achieve comparable diversity, a parallel solution to antigen recognition. Insects show immune priming and specific memory without dedicated adaptive cells, using the Dscam gene and RNA interference to store pathogen-specific information. In bacteria, CRISPR-Cas provides a heritable, sequence-specific defence against bacteriophages, a separate molecular route to the same problem of remembering past invaders.

Innate and adaptive systems are interdependent. Dendritic cells sense danger and present antigen to activate T cells; T cells then recruit B cells and macrophages. Without innate instruction, adaptive lymphocytes would not know when to act; without adaptive precision, innate responses would be overrun.
