# Animal disease model

An animal disease model is a living, non-human animal, often genetically engineered, used to study a human disease. Researchers can perform procedures on the animal that would be unethical in a patient. Activity in an animal does not guarantee an effect in humans, but many drugs, treatments, and cures have been developed with guidance from animal models. When the focus is on developmental biology in a specific taxonomic group, the animals are also called model organisms.

Models are classified in two overlapping ways. By closeness to the human condition: **homologous** models share causes, symptoms, and treatment response; **isomorphic** models share symptoms and treatment response only; **predictive** models match a human disease in a few features and are used to isolate specific mechanisms. By origin: **experimental** models, the most common, are induced in the lab, for example injecting blood into a mouse's basal ganglia to model hemorrhagic stroke, or using the ovariectomized rat to study osteoporosis; **spontaneous** models are animals that naturally develop an analogous condition, rare but informative; **negative** models are healthy controls used to validate results; **orphan** models are diseases with no human counterpart, found only in the studied species.

The use of animals in research rests on evolutionary relatedness. Humans and chimpanzees last shared a common ancestor about 6 million years ago, and humans and rodents about 80 to 100 million years ago. Despite the distance, humans share roughly 99% of their genome with chimpanzees (98.7% with bonobos) and over 90% with the mouse, because large portions of the genome are conserved. The differences between humans and mice come down to roughly six thousand of the approximately thirty thousand genes. This conservation, combined with the development of genetically engineered tissues, organs, and species that express human diseases, makes vertebrate animals productive stand-ins. Rodents are the most common subjects; chimpanzees, the closest relatives, are rarely used and protected from invasive procedures.

Animal models of mental disorders show both the power and the limits of the approach. Mental disorders such as depression consist of **endophenotypes**, biological components that can be reproduced and measured independently, so researchers can examine molecular, genetic, and epigenetic mechanisms and screen drugs in animals even when subjective features such as depressed mood, low self-esteem, or suicidality cannot be accessed. The approach fails where a human symptom has no animal analogue: a rat with insomnia and weight loss cannot reliably be called depressed, and a mouse cannot model the language deficits central to autism. Laboratory rodents are often sedentary, obese, and glucose intolerant, which can confound studies of metabolism. Behavioral assessments risk observer bias, and diagnostic criteria for psychiatric illness are imprecise. A 2000 review found animal toxicity agreed with human outcomes in 71% of cases overall, 63% for non-rodents and 43% for rodents alone, showing that even well-chosen models frequently mislead.

Ethics shape how models are used. British law first regulated animal research in 1822, and the Cruelty to Animals Act 1876 established three principles still in force: experiments must be necessary to save or prolong life, animals must be anesthetized, and animals must be killed when the experiment ends. In the United States, the Animal Welfare Act of 1970 sets standards enforced by APHIS's Animal Care program. At institutions receiving NIH funding, every protocol involving live animals must be approved by an Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and justified under three principles. **Replacement** favors non-animal alternatives such as cell cultures, computer models, or lower species wherever possible. **Reduction** uses the minimum number of animals that yield a statistically significant result. **Refinement** minimizes pain and distress. Public concern follows a rough cognitive hierarchy, from worms and flies to rodents to dogs and non-human primates, a pattern critics call speciesism. Alternatives continue to grow, including in vitro cell culture, induced pluripotent stem cells, non-invasive imaging such as MRI and PET, and genomics-based identification of disease-associated genes, each reducing the need for whole-animal studies without fully replacing them.
