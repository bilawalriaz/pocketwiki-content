# Mental health

Mental health is a positive state of well-being in which a person can realise their abilities, cope with ordinary stress, work productively, and contribute to their community. It is not the absence of mental illness but a continuum blending emotional, psychological, and social well-being. The World Health Organization treats it as a positive resource. Positive psychology frames it as a measurable attribute, including subjective well-being, self-efficacy, autonomy, and resilience. The **tripartite model** operationalises this by separating well-being into three components, measured by the MHC-SF scale: *emotional well-being* (high positive affect), *psychological well-being* (skills for optimal functioning), and *social well-being* (integration and contribution).

Mental disorders are health conditions that disrupt cognition, emotion, and behaviour. As of 2019, about 970 million people worldwide lived with one, making mental illness more prevalent than cancer, diabetes, or heart disease. Anxiety and depression are the most common. In the US, over 22% of adults met criteria for a mental illness in 2021, and major depression ranked third in global disease burden, projected to become first by 2030. Suicide accounts for more than 700,000 deaths each year and roughly 14 million attempts. Prevalence climbs in younger cohorts: US adults aged 18–25 report 33.7% prevalence compared with about 15% in those over 50. Women show higher rates of depression, anxiety, and eating disorders; men show higher rates of substance use. The global economic cost was about US$2.5 trillion in 2010, two-thirds of it indirect, and is projected to exceed US$6 trillion by 2030.

Mental health is shaped by interacting biological, psychological, and social forces. Neurotransmitter disruptions in dopamine, glutamate, and norepinephrine are implicated in conditions such as schizophrenia. Childhood trauma, unhappy marriage or divorce (which can multiply depression risk 3–25 times), unemployment, poverty, and poor diet all increase risk. The **vulnerability-stress model** holds that stress activates latent vulnerabilities to produce illness. The **differential susceptibility hypothesis** adds that people sensitive to stress suffer more in harsh environments yet benefit more from supportive ones, so the same individual can be harmed or helped depending on context.

Three forms of stigma undermine recovery in every culture studied. **Public stigma** drives discrimination such as withholding help, avoidance, coercion, and segregation. **Structural stigma** operates through policies that underfund care and research. **Self-stigma** is the internalised shame that discourages treatment-seeking. Disparities layer on top: Asian Americans face self-stigma tied to Confucian notions of "face" and achievement; Latino Americans encounter documentation, language, and machismo barriers; men absorb stoicism norms that promote isolation; rural residents face higher stigma and severe provider shortages; and refugees, LGBTQ+ people, and the homeless experience compounded access barriers. Half of all mental conditions begin before age 20, and in 2019 about 165 million children and adolescents aged 10–19 had a disorder, with suicide the fourth-leading cause of death in 15–19-year-olds.

Care has moved dramatically over nearly two centuries. Dorothea Dix's 1840s movement built more than 30 US state psychiatric hospitals by 1880, but many became understaffed and abusive. Clifford Beers' 1908 memoir *A Mind That Found Itself* triggered the modern mental-hygiene movement. Emil Kraepelin's 1896 taxonomy of mental disorders dominated classification for roughly 80 years. The 1963 US Community Mental Health Centers Act then drove **deinstitutionalisation**, restricting state hospital admissions to cases of imminent danger and shifting care into the community. The 1939 **Penrose Hypothesis** named the predictable side-effect: as psychiatric beds fell, prison populations rose, a shift called **transinstitutionalisation**, visible today in people cycling between homelessness, untreated illness, and reoffending. Whether community care has failed because the model is flawed or because it has been chronically underfunded remains debated.

A wide range of treatments now exists. Pharmacotherapy (antidepressants, benzodiazepines, lithium) is prescribed by psychiatrists. Physical activity matches antidepressants for some depression and anxiety through endorphin release, and stopping exercise worsens outcomes. Activity and occupational therapy rebuild daily functioning through self-care, work, and leisure. Expressive therapies such as music, art, drama, dance, and poetry are used, with music therapy NICE-approved for psychosis. Evidence-based psychotherapies include cognitive-behavioural therapy, dialectical behaviour therapy, psychoanalysis, gestalt, psychedelic-assisted, and transpersonal approaches. Meditation and mindfulness reduce depression, anxiety, and stress and support recovery from substance use. Self-compassion (self-kindness, common humanity, mindfulness) predicts well-being. Self-management, often called "mental fitness", builds resilience through routine, sleep, exercise, mindfulness, and social contact. Care navigation guides patients through fragmented systems; US treatment-seeking tripled between 1990 and 2003 even though prevalence stayed flat, showing that access and engagement, not just illness, drive demand.

Persistent structural problems remain. The US has the highest annual prevalence (26%) among 14 comparable high-income nations. The average delay to treatment is about a decade, fewer than one-third of patients receive minimally adequate care, community programmes are underfunded, and homelessness and incarceration cycles continue. Globally, WHO and partners have begun expanding investment, while East Asia, Africa, and Latin America each confront distinct combinations of cultural stigma, workforce shortages, and competing health priorities, leaving the central question open: how much of mental illness is driven by brain chemistry versus social conditions such as poverty, trauma, and inequality, and how resources should be split between prevention and treatment.
