# Respiratory syncytial virus vaccine

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infects roughly 64 million people and kills about 160,000 each year worldwide. After decades of failed attempts, three vaccines that prevent RSV lower respiratory tract disease in older adults were approved between 2023 and 2024: Arexvy (GSK), Abrysvo (Pfizer), and Mresvia (Moderna). Abrysvo is also approved to protect infants through maternal vaccination. All three are listed on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines, and the structural-biology techniques behind them accelerated COVID-19 vaccine development.

## Why the field stalled

Work began in the 1960s with a formalin-inactivated vaccine. In RSV-naive children, that vaccine caused vaccine-associated enhanced respiratory disease (VAERD): when later exposed to natural RSV, about 80% of vaccinated children were hospitalized (versus 5% of unvaccinated controls), and two died of lung inflammation. The disaster halted RSV vaccine development for decades. Infant immunity is also complicated by immature immune systems and maternal antibodies, which interfere with direct infant vaccination.

## The scientific breakthrough

Progress restarted with a 2013 study that solved the crystal structure of the RSV fusion (F) protein and showed how to lock it into its prefusion shape, the form that provokes the strongest neutralizing antibodies. Pfizer engineered 400 F protein variants to identify the most immunogenic, and both Arexvy and Abrysvo are protein subunit vaccines built on this prefusion-stabilized F antigen. Abrysvo (RSVpreF) is bivalent, containing equal amounts of prefusion F from the two major RSV subgroups, A and B. Mresvia is an mRNA vaccine (mRNA-1345) delivered in a lipid nanoparticle that encodes the stabilized prefusion F protein.

## The three approved vaccines

| Vaccine | Manufacturer | Type | Approved indications |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arexvy | GSK | Protein subunit, adjuvanted | Adults 60 and older; at-risk adults 50–59 |
| Abrysvo | Pfizer | Bivalent protein subunit | Adults 60 and older; at-risk adults 18–59; pregnant individuals at 32–36 weeks gestation to protect infants |
| Mresvia | Moderna | mRNA | Adults 60 and older; at-risk adults 18–59 |

Arexvy was first approved by the US FDA in May 2023 for adults 60 and older, then expanded in June 2024 to at-risk adults 50–59. Abrysvo was FDA-approved in May 2023 and authorized in the EU in August 2023. Mresvia was FDA-approved in May 2024 and EU-authorized in August 2024. The June 2024 CDC recommendation advises a single dose for adults 75 and older, and for adults 60–74 who have chronic lung or heart disease or live in nursing homes. Pregnant women should receive Abrysvo between 32 and 36 weeks of gestation in the US; EU guidelines permit 24–36 weeks and UK guidelines 28–36 weeks.

## Efficacy

The pivotal trial of about 25,000 adults aged 60 and older found one dose of Arexvy 82.6% effective against RSV lower respiratory tract disease and 94.1% effective against severe disease. Pfizer's bivalent trial of more than 34,000 older adults reported 67% efficacy against RSV infection with two or more symptoms and 86% against illness with three related symptoms. In roughly 7,200 pregnant women, one Abrysvo dose was 81% effective against severe infant RSV within 90 days of birth and 69% effective within 180 days. Moderna's phase 2/3 trial of about 35,000 older adults showed mRNA-1345 raised neutralizing antibody titres 8.4-fold against RSV-A and 5.1-fold against RSV-B at 29 days, with seroresponse rates of 74% and 57%. Common side effects across all three products are injection-site pain, headache, muscle pain, and nausea.

## Safety and durability

GSK's earlier maternal trial was halted in February 2022 because vaccinated women had more preterm births, a signal that resurfaced when the FDA panel considered Abrysvo in pregnancy. A revaccination study of mRNA-1345, given as a second 50 μg dose 12 months after the first to adults 50 and older, found the booster safe and well tolerated, with neutralizing antibody geometric mean titre ratios of 1.08 (RSV-A) and 0.91 (RSV-B) versus the primary dose and seroresponse rates of 77.5% and 47.5%. Because natural RSV infection does not confer lifelong immunity, annual revaccination is under study.
