# DNA

DNA, or deoxyribonucleic acid, is the molecule that stores genetic information in nearly all living things. A nucleotide, the basic unit, joins three parts: a phosphate group, a deoxyribose sugar, and one of four nitrogenous bases (adenine, thymine, cytosine, or guanine). Nucleotides link into a single strand via phosphodiester bonds, with a 5' end and a 3' end that define chemical directionality. Two strands then pair into a double helix: the backbones run antiparallel (opposite directions), and bases pair complementarily, A with T and C with G, held together by hydrogen bonds. B-DNA, the common form in cells, measures about 2 nm wide with roughly 10 base pairs per turn, exposing a major groove and a narrower minor groove that proteins read without unwinding the helix. Stacking interactions between adjacent bases add substantial stability. Alternative conformations exist in special contexts, including the shorter, wider A-DNA seen in dehydrated samples, the left-handed Z-DNA found in alternating purine-pyrimidine runs, and G-quadruplexes, four-stranded stacks of guanine-rich sequences implicated in telomere maintenance and gene regulation.

Replication copies DNA before cell division. It is semiconservative: each daughter molecule keeps one parental strand and gains one new strand, proven by Meselson and Stahl in 1958 using nitrogen isotope labeling. Initiation begins at origins of replication, where helicase unwinds the helix and primase lays down a short RNA primer. The enzyme DNA polymerase reads each parental strand as a template and synthesizes a new strand only in the 5' to 3' direction. Because the two strands run antiparallel, one new strand, the leading strand, is made continuously toward the replication fork; the other, the lagging strand, is made as short Okazaki fragments (short pieces copied backward relative to fork movement), later joined by DNA ligase into a continuous strand. Polymerase proofreads by excising mismatched bases, giving replication a low error rate.

In transcription, RNA polymerase reads a gene and produces a messenger RNA copy. RNA uses uracil in place of thymine and is usually single-stranded. Eukaryotic genes contain introns that are spliced out of the pre-mRNA before export. In translation, ribosomes read mRNA three bases at a time. Each triplet is a codon (64 possible from four bases taken three at a time), and transfer RNA molecules carry matching anticodons and the corresponding amino acids. Three stop codons, UAG, UAA, and UGA, terminate the chain; the other 61 codons encode 20 amino acids, with most amino acids specified by more than one codon, a redundancy called degeneracy. Crick summarized this information flow, DNA to RNA to protein, as the central dogma in 1957, while recognizing that retroviruses use reverse transcriptase (an enzyme that writes DNA from an RNA template) to copy RNA back into DNA.

Inside eukaryotic cells, DNA is packaged with histone proteins. About 147 base pairs wrap each histone octamer to form a nucleosome, the basic chromatin unit; nucleosomes fold further into higher-order fibers and loops. Chemical modifications to histones, such as methylation or acetylation of their tails, alter gene expression without changing the underlying sequence, a phenomenon termed epigenetics, and can be inherited through cell division. Linear chromosomes end in telomeres, repetitive TTAGGG sequences that shorten with each division in most somatic cells and are maintained by the enzyme telomerase in stem cells and germ cells. Prokaryotes lack histones: their DNA forms a denser nucleoid region, often a single circular chromosome, compacted by nucleoid-associated proteins. Mitochondria and chloroplasts carry their own small circular DNA, remnants of their bacterial ancestors.

DNA is constantly damaged by ultraviolet light, which fuses adjacent thymines into cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers, by reactive oxygen species from metabolism, and by double-strand breaks from ionizing radiation or replication stress. Repair pathways exist: nucleotide excision repair cuts out UV lesions, base excision repair removes oxidized bases, mismatch repair fixes replication slip-outs, and homologous recombination or non-homologous end joining mends double-strand breaks. Unrepaired damage drives mutation, aging, and cancer. In the RNA world hypothesis, early life used self-replicating RNA for both information storage and catalysis before DNA took over as the more stable archive, explaining why all modern cells still use RNA and protein machinery to read DNA.

Humans have exploited DNA as a tool. Recombinant DNA technology, developed in the early 1970s, splices genes between organisms to produce medicines like insulin. The polymerase chain reaction (PCR), invented by Kary Mullis in 1983, amplifies specific sequences exponentially using heat-stable polymerase and primers. DNA fingerprinting, developed by Alec Jeffreys in 1984 and first used in a criminal case in 1988, identifies individuals from unique non-coding repeat lengths. DNA sequencing, first automated in the 1980s and scaled massively by next-generation methods after 2005, reads entire genomes. DNAzymes, catalytic DNA molecules selected by in vitro evolution in 1994, and DNA origami, in which short staple strands fold a long scaffold into designed two- and three-dimensional nanostructures, extend the molecule's reach into chemistry and materials science.

The human nuclear genome contains roughly 3 billion base pairs arranged into 46 chromosomes (22 pairs of autosomes plus the sex chromosomes XX or XY). Less than 10% of those bases code for protein; the rest includes introns, regulatory sequences, transposable elements, and thousands of non-coding RNAs. Mitochondrial DNA is a separate, maternally inherited circle of 16,569 base pairs encoding 37 genes.

Friedrich Miescher isolated DNA from pus-soaked bandages in 1869 and called it "nuclein." In 1928, Griffith showed that a "transforming principle" could pass between dead and live pneumococcus, heritable across generations. Avery, MacLeod, and McCarty identified that principle as DNA in 1943. Chargaff's 1950 rules showed that A equals T and C equals G in any genome. The Hershey-Chase experiment of 1952 confirmed DNA as the genetic material using phages labeled with radioactive sulfur and phosphorus. Rosalind Franklin's X-ray diffraction image known as Photo 51, taken in May 1952 by Franklin and Raymond Gosling, revealed helix geometry; Franklin died of ovarian cancer in 1958, four years before the prize was awarded. Watson and Crick proposed the double-helix model in February 1953, drawing on Franklin's data, and published the structure in Nature on 25 April 1953, sharing the 1962 Nobel Prize with Wilkins. A 2023 reassessment by Matthew Cobb and Nathaniel Comfort re-examined the contested credit shared among Franklin, Wilkins, Watson, and Crick. Because DNA decays hydrolytically over roughly a million years, no dinosaur genome can be read directly, only traces preserved in exceptional fossils and inferences from descendant species.
