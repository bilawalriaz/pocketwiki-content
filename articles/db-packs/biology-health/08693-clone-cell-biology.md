# Clone (cell biology)

A **clone** is a group of identical cells that share a common ancestry, meaning they are all derived from the same parent cell. Because every cell in a clone inherits the same DNA from that ancestor, the cells also share the same **genotype** (the complete set of genes carried in the cell's DNA).

## Clonality as a spectrum

The term *clonality* describes where a population sits on a spectrum of ancestry:

- **Monoclonal** — derived from a single clone (one ancestor cell).
- **Oligoclonal** — derived from a few clones.
- **Polyclonal** — derived from many clones.

These distinctions matter most when analysing **antibodies** or **immunocytes** (immune system cells such as B cells and T cells), because the clonality of a population reveals how narrowly or broadly the immune system is responding.

## Clones in the immune system

B cells provide the clearest example. The body's B cells exist in two functional forms (**phenotypes**). Plasma cells are antibody-secreting cells that have terminally differentiated, meaning they have matured into a final form that can no longer divide. Memory and naive B cells, by contrast, retain the ability to proliferate. A clone of B cells therefore consists of genetically identical descendants that may be scattered across the body, for example in cervical (neck) and inguinal (groin) lymph nodes, yet all trace back to one original cell.

## Clones in tumours and other tissues

Many tumours originate from a single cell that has accumulated enough mutations to grow uncontrollably, so the tumour is technically one clone. During further division, a daughter cell may acquire additional mutations and diverge as a new sub-clone, producing genetic diversity within a single cancer (**tumour heterogeneity**). This single-clone view has been challenged: some cancers, including malignant mesothelioma, appear to have a polyclonal origin, arising from two or more distinct cells or clones.

Clonality also appears in healthy tissue. All the **granulosa cells** (the support cells surrounding the developing egg) in a **Graafian follicle** (a mature ovarian follicle ready to release an egg) are clones of one another. In **paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria**, a bone marrow disorder that shortens red blood cell lifespan, all the diseased cells descend from a single mutant cell, and this abnormal clone compromises the functioning of the surrounding normal bone marrow cells.

## Why clones can keep dividing: telomerase

Most human cells cannot divide forever. With each division, the DNA at the chromosome ends (**telomeres**, protective repetitive sequences that cap chromosomes) shortens, and when telomeres become critically short the cell stops dividing. **Telomerase** is the enzyme that rebuilds these ends by replacing the lost **nucleotides** (DNA building blocks). In cells with elevated telomerase expression, telomere length is maintained, allowing almost unlimited cycles of cell division. When one such cell produces many descendants, the process is called **clonal expansion**.

## Clonal colonies versus cellular clones

A **clonal colony**, also called a **genet**, is a related but distinct concept. It refers to unicellular organisms, such as bacteria in a culture dish or in infected tissue, that share common ancestry *and* remain physically together. Cellular clones in multicellular organisms, by contrast, consist of specialised cells (usually in vertebrates) that may be dispersed throughout the body.

## Clonal reproduction and aging in *Paramecium*

The single-celled eukaryote *Paramecium tetraurelia* reproduces asexually by **binary fission**, in which the cell divides in two using chromosome behaviour similar to **mitosis** (the standard cell division in higher organisms). It can also reproduce sexually through **autogamy** (self-fertilisation) or **conjugation** (exchange of genetic material with another cell). Clonal asexual reproduction can resume after either sexual process.

Asexually dividing *P. tetraurelia* gradually accumulates DNA damage and loses vitality after about 200 divisions unless another round of autogamy or conjugation occurs. This decline is called **clonal aging**, and experiments indicate that DNA damage accumulation is its likely cause. The same DNA-damage mechanism has been proposed to underlie aging in multicellular eukaryotes, a hypothesis known as the **DNA damage theory of aging**.
