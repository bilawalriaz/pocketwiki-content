# Fragmentation (cell biology)

In cell biology, fragmentation is the splitting of DNA or an entire cell into smaller pieces. It underlies two normal processes, DNA cloning and apoptosis, and also describes pathological breakage in red blood cells and sperm. The pieces are smaller units of the original, and the word itself does not distinguish helpful from harmful breakage.

## DNA cloning

DNA cloning is asexual reproduction at the molecular level, either performed by an organism that splits into fragments which each grow into a clone, or by a researcher who copies a specific gene many times.

In the laboratory, cloning requires a small, discrete piece of DNA. A single gene is the typical target because the standard vectors, carrier molecules used to propagate DNA inside a host cell, only hold short inserts. A long chromosome therefore has to be cut into fragments first, and once inside the host the vector replicates on its own and copies the inserted gene along with it.

Two enzymes do the cutting and pasting:

- **Restriction enzymes** are endonucleases, enzymes that cut inside DNA strands, produced by bacteria. Each recognises a short restriction site, usually a palindromic sequence (the same bases read 5' to 3' on both strands). The bacterium that makes a given enzyme also makes a matching modification enzyme that methylates its own DNA at those sites; the methyl group blocks cleavage there, so the host's genome is protected from its own weapon. Many enzymes make staggered cuts, leaving single-stranded sticky ends. Others make straight cuts, leaving blunt ends.
- **DNA ligase** normally joins Okazaki fragments, the short pieces produced on the lagging strand during DNA replication, end-to-end. In cloning, purified ligase seals a restriction fragment into a vector through standard 3' to 5' phosphodiester bonds. It can join both sticky and blunt ends, but blunt-end ligation is inefficient and needs higher concentrations of DNA and ligase, which is why most workflows favour enzymes that leave sticky ends.

The basic scheme:

```
Vector + DNA fragment
        ↓
Recombinant DNA
        ↓
Replication inside a host cell
        ↓
Isolation, sequencing, manipulation of the insert
```

Variations exist, but every cloning protocol rests on these steps: cut with a restriction enzyme, paste with ligase into a vector, replicate inside a host, then isolate the copies.

## Apoptosis

Apoptosis is programmed cell death with a defined sequence of morphological changes: cellular and nuclear shrinkage, chromatin condensation and fragmentation, formation of apoptotic bodies (membrane-bound packages of the dying cell's contents), and phagocytosis by neighbours. These changes ensure dying cells leave minimal impact on surrounding tissue. Fragmentation of chromosomal DNA is a biochemical hallmark of the process.

Three functional classes of proteins carry out the programme. Killer proteins initiate apoptosis. Destruction proteins, including DNA-cleaving enzymes, dismantle the cell. Engulfment proteins let a neighbour phagocytose the dying cell corpse.

The DNA is cleaved by **Caspase-Activated DNase (CAD)**, held inactive by its partner **Inhibitor of CAD (ICAD)**. When apoptosis begins, the protease **caspase 3** cleaves ICAD, freeing CAD to act. Because CAD can only reach the linker regions between nucleosomes, the spool-like complexes around which DNA is wound, which sit at roughly 180 base-pair intervals, cleavage produces a characteristic ladder of fragments in multiples of 180 bp (180, 360, 540). This pattern shows up as a DNA ladder on a gel and underpins detection methods such as DNA laddering, TUNEL, and the Nicoletti assay.

The mechanism explains both the timing and the pattern. CAD waits bound to ICAD until caspase 3 removes the brake; CAD then attacks only the exposed linker DNA, so the result is a regular ladder rather than random smearing.

## Irregularities

Fragmentation can also be a sign of damage or disease.

A **schistocyte** is a fragmented red blood cell caused by mechanical injury inside the circulation. Schistocytes appear when the normally smooth endothelium, the inner lining of blood vessels, is roughened or crossed by fibrin strands, and they are a classic finding in hemolytic anemia. They also occur in advanced iron deficiency anemia, where the fragility of poorly made cells themselves is the likely cause.

Sperm DNA fragmentation is measured as the percentage of sperm with broken DNA. In an average male fewer than 4% of sperm carry fragmented DNA. Smoking and similar exposures raise that fraction. Higher fragmentation correlates negatively with sperm motility, morphology, and concentration, and with fertilisation rate and embryo cleavage rate.
