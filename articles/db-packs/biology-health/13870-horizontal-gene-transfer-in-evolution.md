# Horizontal gene transfer in evolution

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) is the movement of genetic material between organisms outside the normal parent-to-offspring route. Where standard inheritance produces a branching tree of descent, HGT links distant branches and scrambles the signal needed to reconstruct that tree. At the level of any single cell the event is improbable, but across billions of years of microbial evolution it has happened often enough to matter.

The tree of life divides cellular organisms into three domains: Bacteria, Archaea (single-celled microbes superficially resembling bacteria but biochemically distinct), and Eukarya (organisms whose cells contain a nucleus, including fungi, protists, plants, and animals). Carl Woese established this framework in 1977 using the gene for the small subunit of ribosomal RNA (SSU rRNA, also called 16S rRNA in prokaryotes), chosen because it is conserved enough to align across all life yet variable enough to measure divergence.

SSU rRNA was assumed to be vertically inherited, but cases of horizontal transfer of even this gene have been documented, and many other genes, including those for core metabolism, have moved between lineages. A clear example is the archaeon *Archaeoglobus fulgidus*: every other marker identifies it as an archaeon, but its HMG-CoA reductase gene is of bacterial origin. Older textbook cases of HGT are the origins of mitochondria from an alpha-proteobacterial ancestor and chloroplasts from a cyanobacterial ancestor, now housed inside eukaryotic cells.

The deeper problem is that if different genes have different histories, no single gene tree reliably tracks the species tree. Two distantly related bacteria that have swapped a gene will look like close relatives on a tree built from that gene alone, even when the rest of their genomes have diverged. Sequence comparisons across diverse species, including across the three domain boundaries, confirm recent horizontal movement of many genes, so a species' history cannot be read off one gene alone.

Two responses have grown from this. W. Ford Doolittle argued in 2000 that the Last Universal Common Ancestor (LUCA) cannot have been a single cell. Early life was probably a loose community of primitive cells, each carrying few genes, freely swapping molecules, and the three domains became distinct only because most later gene transfer stays within domains. He and Peter Gogarten proposed replacing the tree with a mosaic for individual genomes and a net for the web of microbial exchange.

The opposing view, supported by phylogenomics (phylogenetic inference from whole genomes rather than single genes), holds that the tree metaphor survives. Jonathan Eisen and Claire Fraser showed that whole-genome data can separate true phylogenetic signal from HGT noise, correcting earlier single-gene errors such as placing hyperthermophiles (heat-loving organisms) at the deepest branches. A 2005 study of more than 40 microbial genomes estimated HGT at roughly 2% of core genes per genome, frequent enough to matter but low enough that vertical inheritance still dominates. Their preferred image is a tree "adorned with cobwebs" of horizontally transferred genes.

Practical fixes exist. Multi-locus sequencing, which uses several genes, resolves relationships among bacteria that exchange DNA, such as meningitis-causing *Neisseria*. Whole-genome analyses have moved *Encephalitozoon cuniculi*, once classed as a deep-branching protist, into the fungal kingdom, and have supported a two-domain fusion hypothesis in which eukaryotes arose when a bacterium and an archaeon merged whole genomes, a fusion earlier proposed by Lynn Margulis from symbiosis reasoning. This turns HGT into a positive source of evolutionary information: a transferred gene behaves like a DNA mutation that can be modeled in tree reconstruction, it requires the donor and recipient to have lived at the same time or the recipient later, and transfers from lineages with no fossil record offer traces of extinct biodiversity where the fossil record is poorest.
