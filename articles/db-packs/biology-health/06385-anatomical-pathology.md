# Anatomical pathology

Anatomical pathology (anatomic pathology in the U.S.) is the medical specialty that diagnoses disease by direct examination of organs and tissues, at macroscopic, microscopic, biochemical, immunologic, and molecular levels. The other main branch of pathology, clinical pathology, diagnoses disease through laboratory analysis of body fluids and tissues. Many pathologists practise both, a combination called general pathology. The discipline's modern founder was the Italian physician Giovanni Battista Morgagni (1682–1771). Over the 20th century, the field shifted from whole-body autopsy toward the diagnosis and prognosis of cancer in surgical and biopsy specimens, which now guides treatment decisions in oncology.

## Core skills and procedures

An anatomical pathologist's toolkit is a short list of complementary techniques, each answering a different question about the tissue.

- **Gross examination**: inspecting the specimen with the naked eye, sometimes aided by a magnifying glass or stereo microscope. This step identifies visible disease and selects the areas that will be processed for microscopy.
- **Histopathology**: microscopic examination of stained tissue sections. The standard stain is haematoxylin and eosin, though many others exist. Reading H&E slides by morphology is the core skill of the specialty. The science of staining tissue sections is called histochemistry.
- **Immunohistochemistry**: using antibodies to detect specific proteins, which distinguishes disorders that look similar under the microscope and reveals molecular features of cancers, such as HER2 overexpression in some breast cancers.
- **In situ hybridization**: localising specific DNA or RNA sequences in tissue. When the probe carries a fluorescent label, the technique is called FISH.
- **Cytopathology**: examining loose, individual cells spread on slides, obtained by exfoliation or fine-needle aspiration. The Pap smear is a familiar example, and cytopathologists can often give a diagnosis during the patient's visit.
- **Electron microscopy**: magnifying tissue far beyond the limits of light microscopy to visualise organelles. Once central, it is now mostly used for specific problems such as kidney disease and immotile cilia syndrome.
- **Tissue cytogenetics**: visualising chromosomes to detect genetic defects such as chromosomal translocations.
- **Flow immunophenotyping**: classifying cells by their proteins using flow cytometry, essential for diagnosing leukaemias and lymphomas.

## Subspecialties

Most anatomical pathologists spend most of their time in **surgical pathology**, the gross and microscopic examination of surgical specimens and biopsies sent by surgeons, internists, dermatologists, interventional radiologists, and other clinicians. **Oral and maxillofacial pathology** is a U.S. subspecialty in which board certification is open to doctors of dentistry as well as medicine. **Molecular pathology** applies nucleic-acid techniques such as in-situ hybridization, reverse-transcriptase PCR, and microarrays; it overlaps both branches of pathology and is sometimes treated as a crossover discipline. **Forensic pathology** focuses on sudden, unexpected, or suspicious deaths, combining autopsy with toxicology, histology, and genetic testing, and frequently involves court testimony and public-health work such as investigating deaths in custody or sudden infant deaths.

## Training and certification

Training follows a medical-degree pathway, with national variation. In the United States, the American Board of Pathology offers certification in anatomic pathology (AP) after four years of medical school and three years of residency; many pathologists add clinical pathology (CP) for a combined four-year residency, followed by optional fellowship subspecialisation. In Canada, the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons grants AP certification after medical school plus five years of residency, alongside related certificates in general pathology, hematopathology, and neuropathology. In Australasia and several associated regions, the Royal College of Pathologists of Australasia (RCPA) requires a medical qualification, at least two years of clinical experience, and a minimum five-year training program across at least two laboratories, with staged examinations. Pathologists' Assistants are a separate, highly trained profession in anatomic and forensic pathology, prepared through NAACLS-accredited programs and ASCP certification.

## Practice settings

Anatomical pathologists work in academic medical centres, combining diagnosis with teaching, research, and administration; in group private practices, often partnerships that own histology labs and contract with hospitals; as employees of large corporate providers; and in multispecialty groups that include clinical specialties and radiology. Corporate and "pod-lab" arrangements have drawn criticism for weakening physician independence.
