# Genome

A genome is the complete set of genetic information in an organism, encoded in DNA (or RNA in some viruses) as a sequence of four nucleotide bases: adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G), and thymine (T), with uracil (U) replacing T in RNA. In eukaryotes, the term usually refers only to the nuclear DNA, excluding the separate genomes in mitochondria and, in plants, chloroplasts. The term was coined in 1920 by botanist Hans Winkler, blending "gene" and "chromosome."

## Components of a eukaryotic genome

A typical human nuclear genome has roughly 3.1 billion base pairs distributed across 24 linear chromosomes (22 autosomes plus X and Y), each present in two copies in most cells (diploid). Gametes (sperm, eggs) carry one copy of each (haploid). A reference human genome lists one copy of each autosome plus one X and one Y.

Only a small fraction of the genome codes for protein. Noncoding DNA includes:

- Introns, sequences removed from gene transcripts before translation.
- Sequences for noncoding RNAs.
- Regulatory regions that switch genes on or off.
- Repetitive DNA, the majority of the genome in mammals and plants.

In humans, noncoding sequences make up about 98% of the genome. Repetitive DNA comes in two forms: tandem repeats (short head-to-tail blocks) and interspersed repeats (copies of transposable elements scattered through the genome).

### Transposable elements

Transposable elements (TEs) are DNA sequences that can move within the genome. They fall into two classes:

- Retrotransposons (copy-and-paste): transcribed into RNA, then reverse-transcribed into DNA at a new site by reverse transcriptase. They include LINEs (long interspersed nuclear elements, encoding their own reverse transcriptase), SINEs (short elements that rely on LINE-encoded machinery; the Alu element is the most common SINE in primates), and LTR retrotransposons, derived from ancient retroviruses.
- DNA transposons (cut-and-paste): encode a transposase that excises the element and reinserts it, usually within 100 kilobases of the original site.

TEs make up more than 45% of human DNA (LINEs ~17%, SINEs including Alu ~11%, DNA transposons ~3%). Their movement reshapes genomes: insertion can disrupt genes, recombination between TE copies can produce duplications, and TEs can shuffle exons and regulatory sequences to new positions.

### Tandem repeats and disease

Tandem repeats are short sequences repeated head-to-tail, including microsatellites (2–5 base pair units) and minisatellites (30–35 bp). Telomeres, the protective chromosome ends, consist of TTAGGG tandem repeats in mammals. In humans, tandem repeats make up about 4% of the genome; in fruit flies, about 9%.

Expansion of CAG repeats in the huntingtin (Htt) gene beyond 36 copies (the normal range is 6–29) causes Huntington's disease. About twenty human disorders result from similar repeat expansions, often producing misfolded proteins that aggregate in neurons.

## Prokaryotic and viral genomes

Prokaryotes (bacteria and archaea) usually have a single circular chromosome, though some bacteria have linear or multiple chromosomes. They carry little repetitive DNA. Plasmids, small extrachromosomal DNA molecules carrying useful but nonessential genes, are part of the genome in a broad sense but are not chromosomes. Some symbiotic bacteria, such as *Serratia symbiotica*, have reduced genomes where only about 40% of DNA encodes protein, the rest being pseudogenes.

Viral genomes can be DNA or RNA, single-stranded or double-stranded, and may be a single molecule or segmented (multipartite).

## Genome size

Genome size is the total DNA in one haploid copy. Eukaryotic genomes vary enormously, from a fern at 720 chromosome pairs to single-pair species like the Jack jumper ant. Size variation is driven mainly by repetitive DNA and transposable elements, and a larger genome does not necessarily mean more genes. There is no consistent link between genome size and morphological complexity.

The human nuclear genome's 24 chromosomes range from 45 million to 248 million base pairs. In birds, DNA methylation enabled genome expansion, after which a substantial portion was lost during the transition to flight.

## Sequencing milestones

- 1976: Walter Fiers sequenced the first complete RNA genome (bacteriophage MS2).
- 1977: Fred Sanger sequenced the first DNA genome, φX174, at 5,386 base pairs.
- 1995: *Haemophilus influenzae*, the first bacterial genome.
- 1996: *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, the first eukaryotic genome (16 chromosomes); *Methanococcus jannaschii*, the first archaeal genome.
- 1990–2003: the Human Genome Project produced a first draft in April 2003.
- 2013: the first Neanderthal genome, from a 130,000-year-old toe bone in Siberia.

## How genomes change

All somatic cells of an organism start from one fertilized cell, so they share a genome, but mutations accumulate. Somatic mutations from replication errors or environmental mutagens can cause cancer. In immune cells, V(D)J recombination shuffles gene segments so each lymphocyte makes a unique receptor. During meiosis, recombination between paired chromosomes reshuffles alleles, so every gamete has a slightly different genome.

Over evolutionary time, genomes grow by duplication (tandem repeats, gene clusters, whole chromosomes, or whole genomes) and shrink by loss of redundant sequences. Horizontal gene transfer moves DNA between distantly related microbes, and early in eukaryotic evolution many genes moved from mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes into the nucleus.
