# Disease

A disease is an abnormal condition that adversely affects the structure or function of part or all of an organism and is not immediately due to external injury. Diseases are medical conditions with specific signs and symptoms, caused by external factors such as pathogens or internal dysfunctions like immune system problems leading to immunodeficiency, allergies, or autoimmune disorders. The study of disease is **pathology**, which includes **etiology**, the study of cause. The deadliest diseases in humans are ischemic heart disease, stroke, and COPD.

## Core Concepts and Terminology

"Disease" broadly means any condition impairing normal body functioning, but it often denotes infectious diseases caused by pathogens: viruses, bacteria, fungi, protozoa, multicellular organisms, and prions (aberrant proteins). An infection with no clinically evident impairment, such as normal gut bacteria, is not a disease, though an asymptomatic infection expected to cause symptoms later is. Non-infectious diseases include most cancers, heart disease, and genetic diseases.

Key distinctions organize the field. An **acquired disease** begins during life; a **congenital disease** is present at birth. An **acute disease** is short-term; a **chronic disease** persists, often at least six months. A **genetic disease** results from one or more mutations, sometimes inherited, sometimes de novo; a **hereditary disease** is specifically passed through families. An **iatrogenic disease** is caused by medical intervention; an **idiopathic disease** has unknown cause. An **incurable disease** cannot be cured but is not necessarily terminal; a **terminal disease** inevitably ends in death. A **primary disease** is the root cause; a **secondary disease** is a **sequela** of it.

**Illness** and **sickness** are generally synonyms, but "illness" can refer to the patient's subjective experience. A **disorder** is a functional abnormality that may or may not show signs, and is more value-neutral than "disease." A **medical condition** is broader, covering all diseases, lesions, and disorders plus non-pathological states receiving medical treatment, such as pregnancy. **Morbidity** (from Latin *morbidus*, "sick") is a diseased state; **comorbidity** is the presence of two or more conditions together. A **syndrome** is an association of signs and symptoms that often occur together, whether or not the cause is known. **Predisease** describes a subclinical or prodromal stage, such as prediabetes, though its identification is contested.

Symptoms often reflect evolved "sickness behavior," including lethargy, loss of appetite, sleepiness, hyperalgesia (increased pain sensitivity), and poor concentration, which helps clear infection.

## Types and Stages

By extent, a **localized disease** affects one part, a **disseminated disease** has spread, as in metastatic cancer, and a **systemic disease** affects the entire body. In infectious disease, the **incubation period** is the time between infection and symptom onset, while the **latency period** is the time between infection and the ability to spread to others. Some viruses, such as varicella zoster, exhibit **viral latency**, hiding dormant before later causing shingles. A **clinical disease** produces characteristic signs and symptoms; AIDS is the clinical stage of HIV infection.

## Classification, Causes, and Prevention

Diseases are classified by cause, pathogenesis, symptoms, or organ system. A chief difficulty in **nosology** (classification) is that diseases often cannot be clearly defined when cause or mechanism is unknown, so diagnostic terms often reflect only symptoms. The most widely used system is the WHO's **ICD**, now in its eleventh edition.

Causes include microorganisms, genetics, environment, or combinations. Infectious diseases spread through hand-to-mouth contact, insect bites, contaminated food or water, or sexual contact. Social determinants of health strongly influence well-being, and climate change can worsen infectious disease transmission. A pathogen is the cause, not the disease: West Nile virus causes West Nile fever. Many diseases can be prevented through sanitation, nutrition, exercise, vaccinations, and other self-care and public health measures.

## Treatment, Epidemiology, and Burden

Treatments include medications, surgery, medical devices, and self-care. A **cure** ends a condition; **remission** is the disappearance, possibly temporary, of symptoms. Epidemiology studies the factors that cause or encourage diseases, from outbreak investigation to statistical modeling, and examines **syndemics**, the interactions of diseases within populations.

Disease burden is measured by financial cost, mortality, morbidity, or other indicators. **Years of potential life lost (YPLL)** estimates years shortened by disease but ignores pre-death disability. **Quality-adjusted life years (QALY)** and **disability-adjusted life years (DALY)** account for whether a person was healthy after diagnosis. In the developed world, heart disease and stroke cause the most loss of life, but neuropsychiatric conditions such as major depressive disorder cause the most years lost to sickness.

## Society, Culture, and Open Debates

Medical sociology studies how society responds to disease. A condition may be considered a disease in some cultures but not others: obesity was associated with prosperity in parts of Africa, and epilepsy is seen as a spiritual gift among the Hmong. Language shapes the experience of disease through military metaphors (disease as enemy) and journey metaphors (the "road to recovery"). Diseases are also used as metaphors for social ills.

The classification of predisease remains contested: there is seldom a clear line between legitimate concern for subclinical status and over-medicalization driven by conflicts of interest. One review proposed three criteria for predisease: high risk of progression, actionability for risk reduction, and benefit outweighing harm.
