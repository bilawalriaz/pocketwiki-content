# Anatomical terms of muscle

Anatomical terminology for muscle describes three things about each muscle: what it is made of, how it acts on the body, and how it is named. A short term such as *gluteus maximus* or *biceps brachii* carries information about structure, location, and function at a glance.

## The three muscle tissues

The body contains three types of muscle tissue, each with a distinct role.

| Tissue | Control | Location | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skeletal | Voluntary | Attached to bone by tendons | Moves bones, maintains posture |
| Smooth | Involuntary | Digestive and urinary tracts, uterus, eye (pupillary sphincter) | Propels food, chyme, feces, urine; aids birth; adjusts pupil size |
| Cardiac | Involuntary, self-excitatory | Heart only | Contracts without external stimulation |

In skeletal muscle, the widest portion that pulls on the tendons is called the belly. A muscle slip is either an anatomical variant or a branch such as the rib attachments of the serratus anterior. A composite (or hybrid) muscle contains more than one set of fibres performing the same function, often supplied by different nerves; the tongue is a composite of longitudinal, transverse, and horizontal components.

## How muscles act: agonists, antagonists, and synergists

Most skeletal movement involves three roles working together.

An agonist (or prime mover) produces most of the force for a movement. The triceps brachii is the agonist during both phases of a push-up: it shortens concentrically during the up phase, then lengthens eccentrically while still controlling elbow flexion during the down phase. The agonist is defined by which muscle is currently producing or controlling the action, not by the direction of fibre shortening.

An antagonist produces an opposing joint torque and can slow movement, especially during ballistic actions such as throwing a dart, where the triceps fires briefly to accelerate elbow extension and the elbow flexors fire immediately after to decelerate it. Antagonism is a role, not a fixed property: a muscle is an antagonist only when another muscle is acting as agonist.

Common agonist–antagonist pairs are called antagonistic pairs. A typical example is biceps and triceps at the elbow: as one contracts, the other relaxes. Abductor–adductor and flexor–extensor pairs work on the same principle. Two complications matter. First, agonists and antagonists sometimes co-activate, stiffening the joint mechanically. Second, not all muscles pair this way; the deltoid is an exception.

A synergist (also called a fixator) acts around a joint to help the agonist. As a neutralizer, it cancels unwanted extra motion so the agonist's force stays in the desired plane. The hamstrings illustrate this: the semitendinosus and semimembranosus flex the knee and internally rotate it, while the biceps femoris flexes and externally rotates it. For pure knee flexion without rotation, all three contract together to neutralise rotation.

Muscle fibres can contract to only about 40 percent of their fully stretched length, which limits any muscle crossing multiple joints. Active insufficiency is when the prime mover cannot shorten enough to move all joints it crosses (fingers cannot fully flex when the wrist is already flexed). Passive insufficiency is when the opposing muscle cannot stretch enough. Synergists often fix one joint so another can move through its full range, as when the wrist is stabilised during fist-clenching.

## Naming muscles

Muscle names come from a small set of Latin descriptors, each encoding a single visible property.

- Size: brevis (short), longus (long), major (large), minor (small), maximus (largest), minimus (smallest). Examples: gluteus maximus, gluteus minimus.
- Shape: deltoid (triangular), quadratus (four-sided), rhomboideus (rhomboid), teres (round), trapezius (trapezoid), rectus (straight). Examples: pronator teres, rectus abdominis.
- Action: abductor (away from midline), adductor (toward midline), depressor (downward), elevator (upward), flexor (decreases joint angle), extensor (increases it), pronator (palm down), supinator (palm up), internal and external rotator.
- Fibre direction: fusiform muscles have parallel fibres and a spindle shape (pronator teres). Unipennate muscles have fibres along one side of a central tendon, like a quill pen (fibularis muscles). Bipennate muscles have two diagonal rows meeting a central tendon (rectus femoris, stapedius), giving a larger physiological cross-sectional area and greater tension, but less shortening and earlier fatigue.

## Where muscles attach

Every muscle has two attachment points. The origin is the typically proximal, more massive, and more stable bone. The insertion is the typically distal attachment, often less massive and more mobile, connected to bone or tendon. The connective tissue at either attachment is an enthesis. With the latissimus dorsi, the origin is the torso and the insertion is the arm, so the arm normally moves toward the torso when the muscle contracts; in a chin-up, the role reverses and the torso moves toward the arm. The head (caput musculi) of a muscle is the part at its origin; muscles such as the biceps have more than one head.

Intrinsic muscles have their origin inside the body part they act on; extrinsic muscles originate outside that part. The tongue and the hand each contain both types.

## Change in muscle size

Hypertrophy is an increase in muscle size caused by enlargement of individual muscle cells, usually the result of exercise. Atrophy is the opposite, a reduction in muscle size.
