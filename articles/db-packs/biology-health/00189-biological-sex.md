# Biological sex

Sex is the division of sexually reproducing organisms into two kinds based on the gametes they produce. Males make small, motile gametes (sperm); females make large, non-motile gametes (ova). This asymmetry, called anisogamy, is the foundation of "male" and "female" across animals and plants, and it drives the anatomical, physiological, and behavioural differences between the sexes.

## Why gametes differ, and what sex is for

In sexual reproduction, a diploid cell (one with two sets of chromosomes) undergoes meiosis, a division that halves the chromosome number and shuffles parental genes through crossover, producing haploid gametes (cells with one set). Fertilization then fuses two gametes to restore the diploid state in the offspring. This cycle is exclusive to eukaryotes (organisms with a nucleus and other membrane-bound compartments).

The earliest eukaryotes used isogamy, fusion of similar-sized gametes, and had no males or females, only "mating types." Anisogamy evolved independently in many lineages (protists, algae, plants, animals). The extreme version, oogamy, pairs a tiny, swimming sperm with a large, nutrient-storing ovum. The oldest fossil showing distinct male and female forms is *Bangiomorpha pubescens*, a 1.2-billion-year-old red alga.

Sex is maintained because meiotic recombination and outcrossing (mating between unrelated individuals) repair DNA damage and mask harmful recessive mutations, preserving genome integrity across generations.

## How organisms reproduce

Animals are diploid throughout life. In males, testes produce sperm, stripped-down cells built to find and fuse with an egg. In females, ovaries produce ova, large, immobile cells packed with nutrients for the embryo. Aquatic animals often release gametes into water (external fertilization); land animals usually mate internally so gametes do not dry out. Birds transfer sperm by brief cloacal contact; mammals use a penis to deposit sperm in the vagina, where fertilization occurs and the embryo grows inside the uterus.

Land plants make male gametes inside tough-coated pollen grains and female gametes inside ovules. In flowering plants, stamens hold pollen in their anthers and pistils hold ovules in their ovaries. Most flowers carry both sexes; about 5% of flowering plant species are dioecious (separate male and female individuals), compared with roughly 65% of gymnosperms, though most conifers keep both sexes on one tree. Many plants block self-fertilization through molecular, morphological, or timing tricks, and rely on wind or animals to move pollen because they cannot seek mates.

Fungi typically lack a male/female split. They are isogamous and use mating-type genes, sometimes allelic variants at one locus, to decide which cells can fuse.

## How sex is distributed among individuals

About 95% of animal species are gonochoric, meaning individuals are either male or female; the other 5% are hermaphrodites. Almost all vertebrates are gonochoric, and hermaphroditic vertebrates are almost entirely fishes. Most plants are bisexual (hermaphroditic or monoecious, with separate male and female flowers on the same plant). Some fish and plants change sex during life, called sequential hermaphroditism: clownfish turn female when they become dominant, while wrasses turn male when they grow large enough.

## How sex gets determined

Sex determination varies. In chromosomal systems, sex is fixed at fertilization.

- XY (mammals, some insects, some plants): the Y carries a male-determining gene such as *SRY*, so XY is male and XX is female. The platypus is exceptional, with ten sex chromosomes (5X5Y in males, 10X in females). In fruit flies, sex depends on X-chromosome number, so XO is male and XX is female.
- ZW (birds, some fish and crustaceans, butterflies and moths): the W carries female determinants, so ZW is female and ZZ is male; the female gamete decides the offspring's sex.
- XO (many arachnids, insects, nematodes): males have one X chromosome; females have two. In *C. elegans*, XX individuals are self-fertilizing hermaphrodites and XO individuals are males.
- Haplodiploidy (bees, ants): fertilized (diploid) eggs become female, unfertilized (haploid) eggs become male. Fertilization status, not chromosome assortment, sets the sex.

Some species ignore chromosomes. In all crocodiles and most turtles, temperature during embryonic development decides sex: in some turtles, cool nests yield males and warm nests yield females, while in the alligator snapping turtle (*Macroclemys*), females arise below 22 °C or above 28 °C and males in between. A few species rely on social or chemical cues: the fern *Ceratopteris* defaults to hermaphrodite but a pheromone from a nearby hermaphrodite can push larvae toward becoming male, and larvae of the marine worm *Bonellia* only become male after contacting a female.

## Layers of sex in one body

A single individual can be classified in several ways, and the layers sometimes disagree.

- Genetic sex: presence of key genes such as *SRY*; fixed at fertilization.
- Chromosomal sex: the sex-chromosome combination; fixed at fertilization.
- Gonadal sex: whether testes or ovaries develop.
- Gametic sex: which gametes the body actually produces; the most specific test for mosaics.
- Phenotypic sex: the anatomy that forms, including internal and external genitalia.
- Hormonal sex: the influence of sex hormones, which can reshape anatomy and secondary traits (caponized chickens lose male features) but cannot rewrite genetic or gametic sex.

## Where sex differences come from

Anisogamy is the root. Because eggs are costly and few, females usually invest more in each offspring and tend to be the choosier sex; because sperm are cheap and numerous, males usually compete for mating opportunities. These pressures, summed up under sexual selection, produce sexual dimorphism, the systematic differences in body size, colour, and ornament between males and females of a species.

In most animals, females are larger because eggs demand so much nutrition (a black widow female is twice a male's length). Males are larger in polygynous mammals such as elephant seals, where males fight for harems. In anglerfish, tiny males live as parasites on the female's body. In many birds, males are colourful and ornamented; one explanation, the handicap principle, holds that costly ornaments honestly signal genetic fitness because only a healthy individual can survive while carrying them.

## Open questions

Sperm and eggs leave no fossil trace, so whether anisogamy first led to separate sexes or to combined-sex ancestors is unresolved. What pushes a lineage to switch between XY, ZW, XO, temperature-dependent, and haplodiploid systems remains unclear. The twofold cost of producing males, whose cheap sperm can flood a population, against the long-term benefits of genetic mixing is a long-standing evolutionary problem. And in species with genetic sex determination, how often the chromosomal, gonadal, gametic, and anatomical layers fail to align, and what that costs in fitness, is only partly measured.
