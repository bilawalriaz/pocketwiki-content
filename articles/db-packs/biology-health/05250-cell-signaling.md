# Cell signaling

Cell signaling is how a cell communicates with itself, with neighbors, and with distant targets, a property found in every form of life. A signal travels from a first messenger (the ligand) to a receptor, and the cell responds. Most signals are chemical, though pressure, voltage, temperature, and light also act as signals. Specificity comes from the match between ligand and receptor; the same chemical can trigger different effects in cells that express different receptors.

## How the ligand reaches its receptor

A signal works only if the ligand can reach a compatible receptor. Polar, hydrophilic molecules such as peptides and most protein hormones cannot cross the lipid bilayer, so they bind receptors on the cell surface. Small, lipid-soluble molecules such as steroid hormones, thyroid hormones, and retinoic acid diffuse across the membrane and bind receptors inside the cell.

Ligands include ions (Na+, K+, Ca2+), lipids, peptides, carbohydrates, glycoproteins, and gases. Three gases signal in humans: nitric oxide, carbon monoxide, and hydrogen sulfide. Many signals leave the cell by exocytosis through porosomes, cup-shaped lipoprotein structures in the plasma membrane where secretory vesicles dock and fuse.

## Range of signaling

Signals are classified by how far they travel and which cells they reach.

- Autocrine: a cell signals itself.
- Intracrine: a signal made inside the cell binds a receptor in the same cell's cytoplasm or nucleus.
- Juxtacrine: signaling between physically adjacent cells, by membrane-bound ligand and receptor, through gap junctions, or via extracellular matrix contacts. Notch signaling is the classic example.
- Paracrine: a signal diffuses to nearby cells; neurotransmitters and retinoic acid are examples.
- Endocrine: hormones travel through the blood to distant target cells; the hypothalamus controls vertebrate endocrine systems, with the thyroid and adrenal glands as major examples.

The same molecule can act at different ranges. Epinephrine and norepinephrine function as hormones when released by the adrenal gland, but norepinephrine also acts as a neurotransmitter. Estrogen released by the ovary acts as a hormone but can also signal locally.

## Receptors

Receptors are proteins (often multimeric) on the plasma membrane or inside the cell (cytoplasm, organelles, nucleus). They detect a signal by binding a specific ligand or by changing shape in response to a physical cue. The two main classes are cell surface receptors and intracellular receptors.

Cell surface receptors fall into three groups:

- Ion channel–linked receptors (ligand-gated ion channels): a ligand opens a pore that lets specific ions cross. They mediate fast synaptic transmission; GABA's activation of a chloride channel is a standard example.
- G protein–coupled receptors (GPCRs): seven-transmembrane proteins that, when bound, activate an associated G protein by exchanging GDP for GTP; the G protein then triggers downstream cascades such as the cAMP or phosphatidylinositol pathways. GPCRs are the largest family of drug targets, with about 34% of FDA-approved drugs acting on roughly 108 GPCRs.
- Enzyme-linked receptors: ligand binding activates an intracellular enzymatic domain, often a kinase. Receptor tyrosine kinases such as the EGF receptor are the most common type.

Intracellular receptors bind lipid-soluble ligands that have crossed the membrane. The receptor–ligand complex moves to the nucleus and alters gene expression, producing slower but longer-lasting effects over hours to days. Steroid hormone receptors, including those for estrogen and other 3-ketosteroids, work this way.

## Signal transduction

After the receptor is activated, the signal is carried into the cell through signal transduction, a chain of relay molecules whose activities change the cell's behavior. A few receptors, like ligand-gated ion channels, are directly coupled to the response; most set off a cascade. The MAPK/ERK pathway is a well-studied example: EGF binds EGFR, the receptor phosphorylates itself, an adaptor protein (GRB2) binds the phosphorylated site, and the signal passes through a kinase cascade that ultimately phosphorylates transcription factors such as MYC, driving cell-cycle progression.

Cascades amplify the signal, so a few receptors release many second messengers, and provide places for feedback, regulation, and cross-talk. Downstream chemical modifications include phosphorylation, methylation, ubiquitinylation, and proteolytic cleavage. The final response may be a change in ion flow, enzyme activity, cytoskeletal rearrangement, or altered gene transcription.

## Turning signals off

Cells also stop responding. Receptor-mediated endocytosis pulls activated receptors into the cell via coated pits and vesicles. Phosphorylation of a receptor can lower its affinity for its ligand. Prolonged exposure can desensitize a receptor, so it stops responding while the ligand is still present.

## Signaling in other organisms

Each cell responds to specific extracellular signals, and this response underlies development, tissue repair, immunity, and homeostasis. Errors in signaling contribute to cancer, autoimmunity, and diabetes. In bacteria, quorum sensing lets cells coordinate behavior such as bioluminescence (first observed in *Aliivibrio fischeri*) once a population is dense enough, using autoinducers that can create positive feedback loops. Slime molds use diffusible signals, including cyclic AMP in *Dictyostelium* and the dipeptide glorin in *Polysphondylium violaceum*, to aggregate into fruiting bodies through chemotaxis.
