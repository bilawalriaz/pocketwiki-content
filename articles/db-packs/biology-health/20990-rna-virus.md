# RNA virus

An RNA virus is a virus whose genome is built from ribonucleic acid rather than DNA. RNA viral genomes are either single-stranded (ssRNA) or double-stranded (dsRNA). Notable human diseases caused by RNA viruses include influenza, SARS, MERS, COVID-19, dengue, hepatitis C, hepatitis E, West Nile fever, Ebola, rabies, polio, mumps, and measles.

## Genome types and replication

All RNA viruses replicate using a viral enzyme called RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, because host cells lack an enzyme that copies RNA. The polymerase is encoded by the virus and enters the cell inside the incoming particle.

Positive-sense ssRNA resembles messenger RNA (mRNA), the molecule host ribosomes read to build proteins. A positive-sense genome can be translated directly on entry, then copied into a negative-sense template that produces new positive-sense genomes. Purified positive-sense RNA can cause infection by itself, though less efficiently than a whole particle.

Negative-sense ssRNA is the complement of mRNA and cannot be read by ribosomes. The viral polymerase must first copy it into positive-sense RNA, which is both translated and used as a template for new genomes. Purified negative-sense RNA is not infectious on its own. Ambisense viruses carry genes on both strands and translate some proteins before the polymerase acts.

Double-stranded RNA viruses carry an RNA polymerase that transcribes the negative-sense strands inside the capsid into positive-sense mRNA, which exits for translation. Rotaviruses, the most common cause of gastroenteritis in young children, belong to this group.

Retroviruses have an ssRNA genome but replicate through a DNA intermediate. The viral enzyme reverse transcriptase copies the RNA into DNA, and another viral enzyme, integrase, inserts that DNA into the host genome. HIV is a retrovirus.

The International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) places RNA viruses in Baltimore Groups III (dsRNA), IV ((+)ssRNA), V ((−)ssRNA), and VI (retroviruses), all within the realm Riboviria. The Baltimore system sorts by genome type and replication strategy rather than evolutionary descent.

## Mutation rates

RNA viruses mutate far faster than DNA viruses because viral RNA polymerases lack the proofreading activity of DNA polymerases. Retroviruses keep mutating quickly even after their DNA integrates into the host genome, because errors made during reverse transcription are fixed into both DNA strands before integration. HIV has the highest known mutation rate of any microorganism, around 4.1 × 10⁻³ substitutions per base pair. This genetic diversity is a major reason vaccines against RNA viruses are hard to design, though some regions cannot tolerate change. The core-protein coding region of hepatitis C virus is highly conserved because it folds into an internal ribosome entry site, a structure that lets ribosomes start translation without the usual cap.

## Recombination

When two RNA virus genomes infect the same cell, they can exchange segments. Recombination occurs in poliovirus and other picornaviruses ((+)ssRNA), in reoviruses (dsRNA), in influenza ((−)ssRNA), and in coronaviruses such as SARS-CoV ((+)ssRNA). HIV uses strand switching during reverse transcription to repair genome damage. Recombination between animal viruses of different lineages occasionally produces human outbreaks, and it appears to function mainly as a repair mechanism for damaged genomes.

## Classification

ICTV recognises five orders and forty-seven families of RNA viruses, plus many unassigned species. Phylogenetic analyses of RNA-dependent RNA polymerases divide positive-sense RNA viruses into picorna-like, flavi-like, and alpha-like supergroups. Negative-sense RNA viruses appear to have originated in arthropods. Viroids and satellite nucleic acids, including hepatitis D (Deltavirus), are related RNA agents but are not RNA viruses; they were briefly placed in Riboviria in 2019 by clerical error and removed in 2020.

Major families include Coronaviridae (SARS-CoV-1, SARS-CoV-2, MERS-CoV), Flaviviridae (yellow fever, West Nile, dengue, Zika, hepatitis C), Picornaviridae (poliovirus, rhinovirus, hepatitis A), Paramyxoviridae (measles, mumps, Nipah, Hendra), Filoviridae (Ebola, Marburg), and Rhabdoviridae (rabies).
