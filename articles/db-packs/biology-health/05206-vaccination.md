# Vaccination

Vaccination is the deliberate exposure of the immune system to a harmless form of an infectious agent, training it to recognise and repel the real pathogen later. A vaccine is the preparation itself: a weakened (attenuated) or killed microbe, or pieces of it such as proteins or toxins. The goal is adaptive immunity, a tailored defence built around antibodies and memory cells that respond faster and more accurately than they could to a first-time natural infection.

## How the protection works

A first vaccination primes the immune system: it produces antibodies and, more importantly, memory cells. A later encounter with the true pathogen triggers a rapid, large response, often preventing infection entirely or blunting the disease to a mild case. Protection takes weeks to mature. Some vaccines are given after exposure (rabies, smallpox), where they shorten or soften the illness. Most are given before exposure.

Vaccines fail occasionally. In a primary vaccine failure the recipient's immune system does not respond to the first doses. Failure can also follow a full series, because individuals vary. The label "vaccine failure" does not mean the product is defective.

A few vaccines, like smallpox, produce sterilising immunity: they block infection itself, which is why eradication is possible when no animal reservoir exists. Many modern vaccines, including those for COVID-19, instead reduce the chance of severe disease without reliably preventing infection or transmission.

## Vaccination vs. inoculation

The terms are often used as synonyms, but historically they differ. Inoculation (for smallpox, also called variolation) used the unweakened live variola virus scratched into the skin, a risky practice that sometimes caused severe smallpox. Vaccination uses an attenuated agent or a related but harmless one. The word comes from *vacca*, Latin for cow, because Jenner's 1796 smallpox vaccine began with cowpox.

## Herd immunity

When a large enough share of a population is immune, transmission chains break. This protects the few who cannot be vaccinated, including infants, pregnant women, and immunocompromised people for whom even an attenuated vaccine would be dangerous. The threshold depends on how contagious the disease is; measles is so transmissible that public health experts treat near-100% coverage as the only reliable control.

## Scale of impact

The World Health Organization estimates that vaccination prevents 3.5 to 5 million deaths per year, and a 50-year Lancet study credited vaccination with preventing 154 million deaths from 1974 onward, 146 million of them in children under five. Vaccines against ten diseases averted about 50 million deaths between 2000 and 2019, and COVID-19 vaccination averted an additional 14.4 to 19.8 million deaths in 2021 alone. Childhood immunisation is among the most cost-effective health interventions, with benefit-to-cost ratios as high as 27 to 1 for diphtheria and pertussis.

## Safety, testing, and surveillance

No vaccine is risk-free for every recipient. Minor side effects (soreness, low fever) are common; serious effects, usually allergic, occur in roughly 1 per 100,000 doses. Each candidate passes cell-culture and animal tests, then a three-phase human programme: Phase I (~20 people, safety), Phase II (50 to several hundred, dose and efficacy), Phase III (hundreds to thousands, efficacy versus unvaccinated controls). Regulators such as the US FDA review trial data, manufacturing, and purity, then continue monitoring production batches and run Phase IV post-licensure studies across populations and years. In the US, suspected injuries can be compensated through the National Vaccine Injury Compensation Program.

Two historical ingredient concerns are worth understanding. Aluminium salts are adjuvants, substances that boost the immune response; they have been used since the 1930s and only increase local reactions. Thimerosal, a mercury-containing preservative once used in multi-dose vials, contains ethylmercury, which the body clears faster than the methylmercury in contaminated fish and is not linked to autism; it was removed from US childhood vaccines in 2001 as a precaution, not because of demonstrated harm.

## Variolation to vaccination: a compressed history

Smallpox killed 20–60% of infected adults and over 80% of infected children, and an estimated 300–500 million people in the 20th century before eradication. Early Chinese practitioners used variolation, blowing powdered scabs up the nose; the first documented Chinese manual dates to 1549. In 1796 the English physician Edward Jenner showed that cowpox infection protected an eight-year-old from smallpox, and by 1801 his report had reached six languages and 100,000 vaccinations. The word *vaccination* was coined by the surgeon Richard Dunning in 1800. Mass campaigns and compulsory laws followed through the 19th century, and the US Supreme Court upheld compulsory vaccination in *Jacobson v. Massachusetts* (1905). A 1959 WHO eradication drive confined smallpox to a few countries; the last natural case was recorded in Somalia in 1977, and in 1980 the WHO declared the world free of smallpox.

In 1974 the WHO launched a universal childhood immunisation programme against measles, polio, diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, and tuberculosis. WHO data show that wherever measles vaccination rates fall, cases rebound, and polio persists where water and sanitation are poor. A 2024 WHO/UNICEF report noted that DTP3 coverage stalled at 84% in 2023, with 14.5 million children receiving no dose at all, more than half of them in fragile or conflict-affected countries.

## A note on opposition

Resistance to vaccination is as old as vaccination itself and rests on scientific, ethical, political, religious, and safety objections, though no major religion forbids it. The 1998 claim by Andrew Wakefield that the MMR vaccine causes autism was later shown to rest on falsified data; *The Lancet* retracted it in 2010, and Wakefield lost his medical licence in 2011. Every validated study since has found no link.
