# Epstein–Barr virus vaccine

Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) is a herpesvirus that infects two host cell types, B cells and epithelial cells, a property called dual tropism. It establishes latent infection, in which the virus persists inside cells without producing new virus, causes infectious mononucleosis, and is increasingly linked to multiple sclerosis. A vaccine against EBV is not yet available.

The central difficulty is that EBV looks like a different virus at different stages. During the lytic phase the virus replicates and exposes surface glycoproteins used to enter cells; during the latent phase it expresses a different set of proteins and hides inside B cells. A vaccine that only targets lytic-entry proteins on B cells, such as gp350/220, leaves epithelial-cell infection untouched. The protein gB plus the gH/gL complex are shared entry machinery, so targeting them can cover both cell types. Moderna's mRNA candidate mRNA-1189 reflects this logic: it encodes gp350, gH/gL/gp42 to block B-cell entry, adds gH/gL for epithelial cells, and includes gB for protection of all cells. The viral proteins are produced in their native membrane-bound form so the immune system sees them as they appear on infected cells. A second candidate, mRNA-1195, adds antigens aimed at the longer-term complications of EBV infection, including EBV-associated cancers.

Earlier work explored other strategies. Trials of gp350-based vaccines ran in 2006–2008. MVA-EL was proposed for EBV-positive cancers rather than prevention of infection. Virus-like particle (VLP) vaccines, which are empty shells that mimic the virus without containing genetic material, are also under investigation. Antiviral drugs that block viral DNA replication have shown little effectiveness against EBV, are costly, drive resistance, and produce side effects in 1–10% of patients.

In April 2018 researchers described AMMO1, the first human antibody shown to neutralize EBV. AMMO1 binds gH/gL and blocks infection of both B cells and epithelial cells, defining new vulnerability on the virus. Phase I trials of mRNA-1189 began on 5 January 2022 and Phase I trials of mRNA-1195 began in early 2023.
