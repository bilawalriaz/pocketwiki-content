# Transfusion therapy (Sickle-cell disease)

In sickle cell disease, transfused donor red blood cells carry normal adult haemoglobin (HbA), so adding them lowers the proportion of the patient's own red cells that contain the abnormal sickle haemoglobin (HbS). The value of a transfusion depends on how much HbS it displaces from the circulation, not just on raising the total haemoglobin.

## Simple versus exchange transfusion

The two main approaches differ in whether any of the patient's blood is removed.

**Simple transfusion** adds donor red cells without removing any of the patient's blood. It is the standard treatment when the patient is suddenly very anaemic, for example in an aplastic crisis, and in splenic or hepatic sequestration.

**Exchange transfusion** removes the patient's blood and replaces it with donor red cells. It is used for life-threatening complications such as stroke and acute chest crisis. By drawing off HbS-containing cells, an exchange achieves three things a simple transfusion cannot:

- A higher final percentage of donor HbA-containing red cells.
- Larger volumes of donor blood without pushing the haematocrit high enough to thicken the blood.
- A smaller net gain in red cell volume, which limits iron accumulation.

The trade-off is greater donor exposure, which raises the risk of alloimmunisation, plus higher cost, specialised equipment, and good venous access. Exchange can be performed by an automated cell-separator (apheresis) or manually, by phlebotomising part of the patient's blood before or during transfusion. The automated route lowers sickle cell concentration quickly without raising overall haematocrit or viscosity.

## Episodic versus chronic transfusion

**Episodic** transfusion is used acutely, in response to a complication such as acute chest syndrome, or shortly before surgery. **Chronic** transfusion maintains a continuously low HbS level and is most commonly used in children to prevent stroke.

## Indications

Transfusion treats and prevents complications by reducing the number of red cells able to sickle.

*Stroke.* In children, prophylactic chronic transfusion reduces the risk of a first stroke or silent stroke once transcranial Doppler ultrasonography shows abnormally high cerebral blood flow velocities, and lowers the risk of recurrence in children who have already had a stroke. Evidence for primary stroke prevention in adults is lacking, though transfusion is recommended to prevent a second stroke.

*Surgery.* Raising haemoglobin to 100 g/L with transfusion decreases sickle cell-related complications in children and adults, demonstrated only for African haplotypes of HbSS and not in every study.

*Respiratory problems.* In children transfused to prevent stroke, acute chest crises also become less common. Whether transfusions prevent chronic lung complications is unknown.

*Pregnancy.* High-quality evidence is scarce, so American Society of Hematology recommendations are conditional. Patients with prior pregnancy complications, frequent admissions, or preterm deliveries appear to benefit from scheduled partial exchange transfusions.

*Treatment of specific complications.*
- **Aplastic crisis:** suspected when haemoglobin drops well below the patient's usual level together with low reticulocytes, most often due to erythrovirus B19 (parvovirus B19). Anaemia is severe, averaging a 40 g/L fall, and is treated with a simple transfusion.
- **Splenic and hepatic sequestration:** severe anaemia is corrected with a simple transfusion.
- **Acute chest crisis with respiratory compromise:** exchange transfusion is preferred when baseline haemoglobin is already above 90 g/L, when a simple transfusion has not helped, or when respiratory compromise is severe.

## Complications of transfusion

**Alloimmunisation** is common in patients transfused in Europe and North America, because donor pools are predominantly Caucasian while recipients are usually of African or Afro-Caribbean ancestry, and blood group antigens differ between these groups. Extended phenotype matching of Rh and Kell groups as well as ABO reduces the risk but does not eliminate it.

**Iron overload** accumulates because each unit of blood delivers about 250 mg of iron and the body cannot excrete the excess, so tissue iron builds up with each chronic transfusion and adds to long-term illness and death. Iron chelation therapy counters it, and guidelines recommend monitoring patients on regular or intermittent transfusions for overload.
