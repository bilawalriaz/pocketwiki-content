# Gene-centered view of evolution

The gene-centered view of evolution, also called selfish gene theory or the gene's eye view, holds that adaptive evolution is driven by the differential survival of competing genes. Alleles whose phenotypic effects most successfully promote their own propagation become more frequent in the population. Because heritable information passes between generations almost exclusively through DNA, natural selection is best understood from the gene's perspective.

Under this view, genes are the unit of selection. Genomes, organisms, and groups are temporary. A particular genotype lives only for one generation, after which meiosis and recombination shuffle and destroy it. Only the gene, replicated as a lineage of near-identical DNA sequences, persists long enough for selection to act on it cumulatively. Since 1954, DNA has been known as the main physical substrate of heredity, and it can copy itself with high fidelity across many generations.

Organisms are survival machines built by genes to propagate themselves. Genes group into genomes because cooperation in replication and metabolism lets them do far more together than alone. A gene's effect on the body that carries it depends on its environment, including the other genes it shares a genome with. Selection acts on phenotypic differences between alleles, and through those differences, on the underlying genes.

A selfish gene, in this framework, is one that spreads without regard for the welfare of the organism, the group, or the species. Adaptations are the phenotypic effects by which genes maximize their representation in future generations. Whether a behaviour counts as selfish depends on its net effect on the gene's transmission, not on any motive.

## Altruism and kin selection

A gene that makes its bearer help another organism at a cost to itself can still spread if the recipient is likely to carry a copy. The standard condition is Hamilton's rule: a costly helping action is favoured when pB > C, where B is the benefit to the recipient, C is the cost to the helper, and p is the probability that the recipient carries a copy of the same gene.

Relatedness, the probability that a relative carries an identical-by-descent copy, is the usual source of a high p. For humans, p is 1/2 for a full sibling and 1/8 for a first cousin, so siblings should generally receive more help than cousins. J. B. S. Haldane summarised the arithmetic: he would not die for one brother, but would for two brothers or eight cousins. Kin selection theory predicts that organisms calibrate altruism by relatedness, learning to recognise kin through cues such as parental care or shared nest location.

Relatedness is not the only way to raise p. Green-beard effects, named by Bill Hamilton and popularised by Richard Dawkins, occur when a single gene produces a recognisable trait, such as a green beard, and a tendency to direct benefits toward others bearing the same trait. Real examples include the Gp-9 system in fire ants, csA in the social amoeba Dictyostelium discoideum, and FLO1 in budding yeast.

## Intragenomic conflict

Genomes can contain selfish elements that cheat at the expense of the rest of the genome. Segregation distorter genes manipulate meiosis or gametogenesis so that they end up in more than half of functional gametes, and can persist even though they reduce the fertility of their carriers. Egbert Leigh compared the genome to a parliament of genes, each acting in its own interest but liable to be suppressed by the others if its actions harm them, which helps explain why such conflicts are usually kept in check.

## Theoretical tools

The Price equation, derived by George R. Price while re-deriving Hamilton's work on kin selection, is a covariance equation that gives a general mathematical description of how selection acting on phenotypes changes gene frequencies across generations. It remains a central tool for analysing both kin selection and multilevel selection.

## Criticisms and alternatives

Critics argue that the gene-centred view is too narrowly adaptationist. It treats natural selection as the default explanation and downplays neutral traits fixed by genetic drift, or even deleterious traits that persist for other reasons. Stephen Jay Gould characterised the view as confusing bookkeeping with causality, holding that it is phenotypes, not genes, that interact directly with the environment, so genes register evolutionary change rather than cause it. Ernst Mayr went further, calling Dawkins's claim that the gene is the object of evolution totally non-Darwinian. Niles Eldredge has continued these arguments after Gould's death.

The main alternative is multilevel selection, which holds that selection can act simultaneously on genes, cells, organisms, groups, and species, and that no single level has automatic priority. Proponents such as E. O. Wilson, David Sloan Wilson, Elliott Sober, and Samir Okasha develop the mathematical and conceptual tools for treating these levels together.

Defenders of the gene-centred view, including Dawkins in The Extended Phenotype, reply that gene effects on developing phenotypes blend during an individual's life, but the genes themselves do not blend as they replicate and recombine across generations, so the gene remains the entity whose frequencies selection changes.
