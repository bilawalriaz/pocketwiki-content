# Cell (biology)

A cell is the basic structural and functional unit of all life. The term comes from the Latin *cellula* ("small room"). Every cell consists of cytoplasm bounded by a semipermeable cell membrane that encloses genetic material. Most cells are microscopic, and most can replicate and synthesize proteins. Cells emerged roughly four billion years ago. Every organism belongs to one of two groups: prokaryotes (single-celled bacteria and archaea) and eukaryotes (single-celled or multicellular, with a true nucleus).

## Prokaryotes: bacteria and archaea

Prokaryotes are the simpler and smaller cells, typically 0.1–5.0 μm in diameter. They lack a nucleus, holding DNA in a nucleoid region, and generally lack membrane-bound organelles. The largest known bacterium, *Thiomargarita magnifica*, averages about 1 cm and is visible to the naked eye.

**Bacteria** have a plasma membrane covered by a cell wall of peptidoglycan, sometimes wrapped in a gelatinous capsule. The wall prevents cytolysis under osmotic pressure. DNA is usually a single circular chromosome, sometimes supplemented by plasmids that can carry traits such as antibiotic resistance. Surface appendages include flagella for movement and pili for attachment. Some species contain protein microcompartments or membrane-bound organelles such as magnetosomes and anammoxosomes.

**Archaea** share a membrane-and-wall envelope (except *Thermoplasma*, which has only a membrane), but their lipids are uniquely ether-linked. Many are extremophiles, and methanogenesis is uniquely archaean. No archaea are known pathogens.

## Eukaryotes

A eukaryotic cell can be 2–100 times larger in diameter than a prokaryote. Its defining feature is a membrane-bound nucleus (Greek for "true nut") that houses chromosomes and is the site of nearly all DNA replication and transcription. Other organelles include the endoplasmic reticulum, ribosomes, the Golgi apparatus, mitochondria, lysosomes, peroxisomes, endosomes, and vesicles. The cell membrane is a phospholipid bilayer described by the fluid mosaic model; together with the membranes of most organelles it forms the endomembrane system, excluding mitochondria. The cytoplasm contains the cytosol (30–50% of cell volume) and a cytoskeleton of microtubules, intermediate filaments, and microfilaments that maintains cell shape. Mitochondria generate ATP through oxidative phosphorylation, carry their own DNA, and divide by binary fission because they descend from free-living bacteria. Chloroplasts, found in plants and algae, also carry their own DNA and descend from cyanobacteria captured by an early eukaryotic cell.

## Specialized cells

**Animal cells** develop from a totipotent diploid zygote. Ectoderm forms epithelial and nervous tissue; triploblastic animals (Bilateria) add a mesodermal layer. The human body contains about 200 cell types and roughly 30 trillion cells. Centrosomes are unique to animal cells and act as the main microtubule-organizing center.

**Plant cells** add walls of cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin, with lignin in secondary walls. Plasmodesmata connect adjacent cells, and chloroplasts carry out photosynthesis. Large water-storage vacuoles dominate the interior. Specialized peroxisomes handle photorespiration, and glyoxysomes convert stored fatty acids to sugars in germinating seeds.

**Protist cells** vary widely in covering: a plain membrane, a wall, a pellicle (ciliates), a test (testate amoebae), or a frustule (diatoms). Ciliates uniquely carry both a generative micronucleus and a vegetative ampliploid macronucleus. Fungal cells have chitin–glucan walls and a spitzenkörper that guides hyphal growth.

## Cellular processes

Prokaryotes divide by binary fission; eukaryotes undergo mitosis followed by cytokinesis. Diploid cells can enter meiosis, producing four haploid gametes from one DNA replication and two divisions. DNA repair uses nucleotide excision repair, mismatch repair, non-homologous end joining, recombinational repair, and photoreactivation. Metabolism splits into catabolism and anabolism. Protein synthesis runs from transcription (DNA → mRNA) to translation (mRNA → polypeptide at ribosomes using tRNA). Movement uses flagella, cilia, or pseudopodia. Cell death takes the form of necrosis from injury or programmed death by apoptosis and autophagy, with further pathways including anoikis, pyroptosis, mitoptosis, parthanatos, and necroptosis. Cells also signal through a three-part system of ligand, receptor, and signal; errors in this system are linked to cancer, autoimmunity, and diabetes.

## Origins and multicellularity

The origin of the first cells is debated. Small precursor molecules may have arrived on meteorites, formed at deep-sea hydrothermal vents, or been synthesized by lightning in a reducing atmosphere. RNA is the leading candidate for the earliest self-replicating molecule because it both stores information and catalyzes reactions. The first cells were likely heterotrophs with permeable membranes. About 2.2 billion years ago, symbiogenesis between an archaean and a bacterium produced the first eukaryotic common ancestor, which had a nucleus, aerobic mitochondria, at least one centriole and cilium, sex, peroxisomes, and a dormant cyst. A later symbiosis, around 1.6 billion years ago, added chloroplasts and produced the green plants. The earliest evidence of multicellularity appears 3–3.5 billion years ago in cyanobacteria-like organisms with differentiated cells, an arrangement later reproduced in laboratory evolution experiments using predation as a selective pressure.
