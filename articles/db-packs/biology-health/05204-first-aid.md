# First aid

First aid is the immediate assistance given to someone with a medical emergency before professional care arrives. It preserves life, prevents the situation from worsening, and starts recovery, bridging the gap until an ambulance or doctor takes over. First aid is not medical treatment: it relies on common-sense decisions and basic training.

## Aims: the three Ps

- **Preserve life.** Stop what kills fastest: bleeding, blocked airway, stopped heart.
- **Prevent further harm.** Move the person from danger, immobilise a damaged spine, avoid worsening the injury.
- **Promote recovery.** Clean a small wound, support a fracture, calm the patient.

## General procedure: ABCDE

Most protocols share a single order of priorities, ABCDE, in which the fastest killers are addressed first:

1. **Airway.** An open passage from mouth and nose to the lungs. If blocked, nothing else helps.
2. **Breathing.** Confirm air moves in and out. If not, begin rescue breathing.
3. **Circulation.** Check for severe bleeding and a pulse. If the heart has stopped, start chest compressions; if bleeding is heavy, press on the wound. Automated external defibrillation (AED, a device that reads heart rhythm and delivers a shock to restart it) belongs here for cardiac arrest.
4. **Disability.** A quick neurological check: consciousness, pupil response, and blood glucose if diabetic collapse is suspected.
5. **Exposure.** Examine the patient for missed injuries while preventing hypothermia.

Modern variants (csABCDE, xABCDE) prepend **catastrophic bleeding** and **spine protection**, both critical in trauma and combat care.

For cardiac arrest, the American Heart Association and the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation teach a simplified sequence, **CABD**: compressions first, then airway (head-tilt, chin-lift; do not tilt an infant's head), then breathing, then defibrillation.

## Wider protocols

The clinical core is wrapped by surrounding tasks. The European protocol adds protection, evaluation, alerting services, and care. The Red Cross Check–Call–Care method reduces this to three steps: check the scene and patient, call emergency services, care for the patient. The Czech construction-industry SAFE-POINT protocol lists safety, alerting, bleeding, airway, resuscitation, and warmth.

## Key skills

**Moving a patient.** Move only if the scene is dangerous or care cannot be given in place. Suspected spinal injury requires minimal, careful movement; the patient ends face-up on a firm surface so compressions are possible.

**Checking responsiveness.** Touch a shoulder and call out. No reaction means checking the carotid pulse (side of the neck) or, if needed, the radial pulse (wrist), and watching for chest rise. Trained rescuers spend no more than ten seconds on this.

**Clearing the airway.** An unconscious person with a Glasgow Coma Scale below 8 (a score measuring eye, verbal, and motor response) cannot protect their own airway. The recovery position (lying on the side) lets the tongue fall forward and vomit drain. For a foreign object, use back slaps, chest thrusts, or abdominal thrusts. For an infant, give five firm blows to the upper back with the baby face-down on the forearm, then two-finger chest thrusts; if the infant can cough or cry, the airway is partly open and no assistance is given.

**CPR.** Compressions force blood through the body; rescue breaths force air into the lungs. An AED reads the rhythm and shocks the heart. Permanent brain damage begins after roughly five minutes without oxygen, which is why rapid bystander action matters.

**Bleeding control.** Direct pressure is the first step; a tourniquet or haemostatic dressing (one designed to clot blood on contact) handles catastrophic limb bleeding when pressure fails.

## First aid kits and symbols

A first aid kit is a durable bag or clear box marked with a white cross on a green background, the international symbol for first aid. A red cross on white belongs to the International Red Cross and Red Crescent Movement and, under the Geneva Conventions, may not be used by anyone else. Contents vary by setting; British Standard BS 8599 gives one workplace example.

## Training

Effective CPR, defibrillation, and trauma care require instruction and practice, ideally before an emergency. In many countries, dispatchers give first-aid instructions by phone while an ambulance is en route. Courses are run by community organisations such as the Red Cross and St John Ambulance, and by commercial providers, often for workplace compliance. Certificates expire, so regular refreshers keep skills current.

## Specialised branches

- **Battlefield first aid** treats casualties under fire, prioritising catastrophic bleeding and rapid evacuation.
- **Aquatic or marine first aid** covers problems after water rescue and delayed evacuation.
- **Wilderness first aid** prepares responders to care for a patient for hours or days when terrain, weather, or distance delays help.
- **Hyperbaric and oxygen first aid** treat diving injuries such as decompression sickness, in which gas bubbles form in tissues.
- **Mental health first aid** is taught separately and covers supporting someone in crisis and recognising early signs of mental illness.
- **Conflict first aid**, piloted in Canada, focuses on preserving and restoring social and relationship well-being.

## A short history

Bandaging appears on Greek pottery from about 500 BC, and the Good Samaritan parable describes wound dressing. Roman legions had capsarii, soldiers dedicated to first aid and forerunners of modern combat medics. In 1774 the Society for the Recovery of Persons Apparently Drowned (later the Royal Humane Society) popularised artificial respiration. Napoleon's surgeon Dominique-Jean Larrey created flying ambulance units for the battlefield. After the Battle of Solferino in 1859, Jean-Henri Dunant founded the Red Cross. In 1870 Friedrich von Esmarch coined the German term *erste Hilfe* and trained Prussian soldiers in bandaging and splinting with his Esmarch bandage. The Order of St John began civilian teaching in 1878, and the first recorded training in the United States took place in Jermyn, Pennsylvania, in 1899.
