# Anatomical terms of bone

Anatomical terms for bone are mostly derived from Greek and Latin, and a few roots carry through the whole vocabulary. A *condyle* comes from the Greek for "knuckle," a *fossa* from the Latin for "ditch," a *ramus* from the Latin for "branch." The root usually names what the feature looks like.

## Bone shapes

Bones fall into five shape categories, and the category predicts the function.

- **Long bones** are cylindrical, longer than they are wide, and act as levers moved by muscles. Examples: humerus, ulna, radius, femur, tibia, fibula, metacarpals, metatarsals, phalanges.
- **Short bones** are cube-like, roughly equal in length, width, and thickness. The carpals (wrist) and tarsals (ankle) are the only short bones, and they give stability with limited motion.
- **Flat bones** are thin and often curved rather than truly flat. Examples: cranial bones, scapulae, sternum, ribs. They anchor muscles and shield organs. They have no medullary cavity because they are too thin.
- **Irregular bones** resist neat description and have complex shapes, such as the vertebrae and many facial bones that contain sinuses.
- **Sesamoid bones** are small, round, sesame-seed-shaped bones that form inside tendons where a joint generates heavy pressure, and they protect tendons from compressive forces. The kneecap (patella) is the only sesamoid bone everyone has, and the largest.

## Surface features

Most terms for bumps and projections describe the size, shape, or feel of the feature.

| Rounded | Pointed | Rough-surfaced |
|---|---|---|
| Condyle: round joint prominence at a bone's end | Line: long, thin projection | Tubercle: small rough bump |
| Epicondyle: projection near a condyle | Ridge or crest: long, narrow raised line | Tuberosity: larger rough bump |
| Eminence: small bump | Spine: long, thin projection | Protuberance: roughly synonymous with tuberosity |
| Process or promontory: large projection | | |
| Facet: small, flattened articular surface | | |
| Ramus: an extension or branch of bone | | |

Two terms are tied to specific places. The **malleolus** ("small hammer" in Latin) is the bony prominence on each side of the ankle: the medial malleolus is the lower end of the tibia, the lateral malleolus the lower end of the fibula. The **trochanters** are muscle attachment sites on the femur, named greater, lesser, or third.

## Cavities and openings

Cavities fall into two groups based on whether they connect regions or end in a dead end.

- **Openings that connect regions.** A *foramen* is any opening in bone; foramina let muscles, nerves, arteries, or veins pass between body regions, as with the *foramen magnum* of the occipital bone. A *canal* is a long, tunnel-like foramen, such as the auditory canal.
- **Blind-ended cavities.** A *fossa* (Latin "ditch") is a hollow or depression, such as the hypophyseal fossa in the sphenoid bone. A *meatus* is a short canal that opens into another part of the body, like the external auditory meatus. A *fovea* ("pit") is a small pit, usually on a bone's head, such as the fovea capitis of the femur.

Two further terms describe cavity walls: a *labyrinth* names the fine, complex passages of the inner ear, and a *sinus* names a bony cavity, usually inside the skull.

## Joints and long-bone regions

A **joint**, or **articulation**, is where adjacent bones meet, such as the elbow, shoulder, or a costovertebral joint. An **articular process** is a projection that contacts an adjacent bone, and a **suture** is the articulation between cranial bones.

Long bones are described with standard regional terms: the **head** is usually the proximal end, the **base** the opposite end, the **shaft** (or body) the long middle section, and the **neck** the segment between head and shaft. Named growth regions follow Greek prefixes:

| Region | Prefix meaning | Location and role |
|---|---|---|
| Diaphysis | *dia-*, "between" | The long main shaft; the site of primary ossification |
| Epiphysis | *epi-*, "on top of" | The end regions; sites of secondary ossification |
| Physis (epiphyseal plate) | *physis*, "growth" | A disc of hyaline cartilage between epiphysis and metaphysis where bone lengthens after birth; it disappears by about age twenty |
| Metaphysis | *meta-*, "transitional" | The zone between epiphysis and diaphysis |
| Apophysis | *apo-*, "outward from" | Any process or outgrowth from a bone |

Internally, the **cortex** is a bone's outer wall and the **medulla** its inner surface. Red marrow, which produces blood, sits in spongy bone and the medullary cavity; yellow marrow, which is fatty, sits mainly in the medullary cavity. Flat bones lack a medullary cavity because they are too thin to contain one.
