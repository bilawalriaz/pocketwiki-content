# Activated charcoal (medication)

Activated charcoal, also called activated carbon, is a medication used to treat poisonings that were swallowed. It works by binding toxins to its surface so the body cannot absorb them. The charcoal is biologically inert; it does not enter the body's chemistry. Its effectiveness comes from a large internal surface area packed into a small volume, which lets it adsorb many molecules before they cross the gut wall into the bloodstream. It sits on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

## When and how it is used

Toxicologists recommend giving activated charcoal within about an hour of the poison being swallowed, before absorption is complete. It can be taken by mouth as a slurry, or delivered through a nasogastric tube when the patient cannot swallow. Activated charcoal is also packed into cartridges in hemoperfusion machines, where blood is circulated outside the body so the charcoal can adsorb toxins directly.

Activated charcoal binds many orally ingested drugs, including phenobarbital and carbamazepine. It does not work against a clinically important list of substances. Cyanide, strong acids and alkalis, iron, lithium, arsenic, methanol, ethanol, ethylene glycol, and the pesticide malathion either do not adsorb well onto charcoal or cause further injury when they do. For acids, alkalis, and petroleum products, charcoal is contraindicated because it can provoke vomiting and additional damage. A study in Sri Lanka of acute self-poisoning with agricultural pesticides and yellow oleander seeds found that multiple-dose activated charcoal did not improve survival, so clinicians use discretion before giving it.

## Side effects and risks

Common side effects are vomiting, black stools, diarrhea, and constipation. The more dangerous complications come from misuse. If charcoal is inhaled into the lungs, aspiration pneumonitis can develop, and in some cases progresses to bronchiolitis obliterans, a scarring of the small airways. Rarely, the bulk of charcoal in the gut causes gastrointestinal obstruction or ileus. Use during pregnancy and breastfeeding is considered safe. Charcoal can also bind other medications taken at the same time and can produce false readings on the guaiac card test for blood in the stool.

## Other medical uses

Outside emergency poisoning, activated charcoal is sold as tablets or capsules for diarrhea, indigestion, and flatulence. There is modest evidence that it reduces diarrhea in cancer patients receiving the chemotherapy drug irinotecan. Radiologists give it before abdominal imaging to reduce intestinal gas and improve the visibility of bile, pancreatic, and renal stones. In dentistry, practitioners using the Safe Mercury Amalgam Removal Technique give patients a charcoal or chlorella slurry to rinse and swallow before drilling out amalgam fillings, on the assumption that any released mercury will be adsorbed before absorption. Popular claims that activated charcoal whitens teeth, cures hangovers, prevents bloating, or "detoxifies" the body are not supported by evidence, and charcoal cleanses are considered pseudoscience.

## History

Charcoal has been used medicinally for millennia. Egyptians applied it to wounds around 1500 BC to absorb odours, and Phoenicians stored drinking water in charred barrels by about 400 BC. The activated form familiar today was developed in the 18th century during sugar refining, where it removed coloured impurities from raw syrup. Medical use began in the early 19th century. A frequently cited demonstration came in 1835, when strychnine mixed with activated charcoal produced no signs of poisoning in the test subject, providing early experimental evidence for the adsorption mechanism.
