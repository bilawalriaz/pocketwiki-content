# Zika virus vaccine

A Zika virus vaccine is designed to prevent infection by Zika virus and, critically, to protect pregnant women from passing the virus to the fetus, where it can cause congenital Zika syndrome, a cluster of birth defects that includes microcephaly (an abnormally small head and brain). As of April 2019, no Zika vaccine is approved for clinical use, though several candidates are in clinical trials.

## Why it is hard to design

A successful vaccine must train the immune system to produce specific antibodies against Zika. Two biological complications make this unusually difficult.

First, Zika infection itself can trigger Guillain-Barré syndrome, a rare condition in which the immune system attacks the body's own peripheral nerves, causing muscle weakness and sometimes paralysis. Any vaccine must therefore avoid provoking that same autoimmune response.

Second, Zika is closely related to dengue virus. Antibodies raised against Zika can sometimes bind to dengue without neutralising it, and the bound antibody actually helps dengue enter new cells. This phenomenon is called antibody-dependent enhancement, and it makes dengue infection worse, not better. A Zika vaccine must avoid generating exactly this kind of cross-reactive antibody.

## Main vaccine platforms in development

Researchers are pursuing several distinct strategies, each a different way of presenting Zika proteins to the immune system.

**DNA vaccine.** As of 31 March 2017, a DNA vaccine entered Phase 2 trials. It delivers a small circular piece of DNA (a plasmid) that instructs the body's own cells to make two Zika surface proteins, E and PrM, which form the outer coat of the virus. The cells then display these proteins, triggering an antibody response. The approach reuses a platform previously used to develop a West Nile virus vaccine.

**Purified inactivated vaccine (ZPIV).** The Walter Reed Army Institute of Research developed ZPIV using killed Zika particles. Because the virus is inactivated, it cannot replicate or cause disease. The technology mirrors the existing Japanese encephalitis vaccine. Initial Phase 1 results at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center were considered promising, but a planned partnership with Sanofi was halted by protest in Congress.

**Live attenuated vaccine.** This candidate uses a genetically weakened Zika virus that can still infect cells but cannot cause disease. It builds on Dengvaxia, the approved dengue vaccine.

**mRNA vaccine.** Developed with Moderna Therapeutics, this vaccine delivers messenger RNA (mRNA) encoding the E and PrM proteins, instructing cells to make the Zika surface proteins directly. It was running concurrent Phase 1 and 2 trials as of 2017.

**Viral vector vaccines.** These use harmless viruses to deliver Zika genes into cells. A measles-virus-based candidate completed a Phase 1 trial in April 2018; an adenovirus-based candidate was completing Phase 1 in 2019. Adenovirus vectors were previously used for HIV vaccine candidates and provoke strong immune responses. A chimeric vaccine, a hybrid that fuses Zika surface proteins onto an unrelated viral scaffold called Binjari virus, has proven highly effective at immunising mice.

All of these candidates aim to elicit antibodies against the E and PrM surface proteins while avoiding the two known hazards: triggering Guillain-Barré syndrome and worsening dengue through antibody-dependent enhancement. Reaching that balance safely, particularly for use in pregnant women, remains the field's central challenge.
