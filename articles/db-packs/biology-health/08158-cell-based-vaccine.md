# Cell-based vaccine

A cell-based vaccine is one whose viral antigen is grown in cultured mammalian, avian, or insect cell lines instead of the more familiar embryonic chicken eggs. Cell culture was investigated through the 2000s as a complementary and alternative platform to egg-based production.

## How production works

A candidate vaccine virus is grown in cells with a finite lifespan, most commonly Madin-Darby Canine Kidney (MDCK) cells, along with Vero cells from monkeys and several human lines such as HEK 293, MRC-5, Per.C6, and WI-38. The virus replicates inside these cells, the virus-containing fluid is collected, and the antigen is purified. For inactivated vaccines the virus is then killed, and the final product is tested before release. The same facilities can be retooled for different viral vaccines, and cells grown in synthetic media avoid animal serum, removing the risk of transmitting spongiform encephalopathies.

## Advantages over egg-based production

Eggs yield only one or two doses each, so cell-based production scales faster and is not capped by the supply of fertilized eggs, which matters when a pandemic strain outpaces egg supply. A shorter growth phase gives the virus less chance to mutate away from the intended strain, so the antibody response is more likely to match the target. Cell-based vaccines can be used by people with egg allergies, and they open a route for strains that grow poorly in eggs.

## Approved examples

Cell-based vaccines are approved for influenza, rotavirus, measles, smallpox, polio, rabies, and Japanese encephalitis. Flucelvax, grown in MDCK cells by Novartis, was the first mammalian cell-based influenza vaccine approved in the United States (FDA, 2012); it targets H1N1, H3N2, and two influenza B lineages and is approved for people over three years of age. Optaflu, made the same way by Novartis, was approved by the European Medicines Agency in 2009, with the differences from Flucelvax limited to release specifications and assays. Flublok, grown in insect cells by Protein Sciences, was approved by the FDA in 2013 and is suitable for people with egg allergies. Rotarix and RotaTeq (Vero cells) cover rotavirus; Verorab (Vero cells) covers rabies; Ixiaro (Vero cells) covers Japanese encephalitis.
