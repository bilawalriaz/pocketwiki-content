# Physiology

Physiology is the scientific study of how living things function, from molecules to whole organisms. The name comes from Greek *phúsis* ("nature") and *-logía* ("study"). As a subdiscipline of biology, it examines the chemical and physical processes carried out by biomolecules, cells, tissues, organs, organ systems, and organisms. Its organising principle is **homeostasis**: the active maintenance of a stable internal environment despite a changing external one. A second guiding idea is the form–function link: structure dictates function, and function shapes structure, which is why anatomy and physiology are taught together in medicine.

## Partner disciplines

The field draws on several neighbours. **Anatomy** supplies the structure that physiology explains. **Biochemistry** supplies the reactions that power cells. **Biophysics** explains how organisms sense stimuli (light, sound, temperature) and how electrical signals travel. **Genetics** shows how genes and environment together produce traits. **Evolutionary biology** explains why physiological traits are shaped as they are. None is sufficient alone; each supplies one lens on the same underlying processes.

## Levels and subdisciplines

Because organisms are built in layers (molecules, cells, tissues, organs, systems, whole bodies, populations), physiology operates at every layer, and each layer is built from the one below. Subdisciplines are organised four ways: by taxon (human, animal, plant, microbial, viral), by level of organisation (molecular to integrative), by cause of variation (developmental, environmental, evolutionary), and by research goal (applied, such as medicine, versus non-applied or comparative).

**Cell physiology** studies division, signalling, growth, and metabolism in animal, plant, and microbial cells. **Plant physiology** covers photosynthesis, respiration, water and nutrient transport, growth responses to light and gravity, daily and seasonal rhythms, seed germination and dormancy, and the opening and closing of leaf pores. **Human physiology** focuses on organs and systems (nervous, endocrine, cardiovascular, respiratory, digestive, urinary) and on how they coordinate; the nervous system uses electrical signals, the endocrine system uses hormones, and their interaction keeps the body stable. **Comparative physiology** uses differences between species to illuminate general principles.

## How the field developed

Physiology began in classical Greece. Hippocrates introduced the theory of the four humors (black bile, phlegm, blood, yellow bile) into medicine in the late 5th century BC, and Aristotle linked structure to function. Galen (c. 130–200 AD) was the first to experiment on bodily function, located humoral imbalances in specific organs, and added four temperaments (sanguine, phlegmatic, choleric, melancholic). His framework dominated medicine for roughly 1,400 years.

The early modern period broke that grip. Jean Fernel coined the word "physiology" between 1497 and 1558. Santorio Santorio invented the pulsilogium and thermoscope in the 1610s, the first instruments to measure pulse rate and body temperature. In 1791 Luigi Galvani showed that electricity is involved in frog nerves. Between 1811 and 1824, Bell and Magendie proved that the dorsal and ventral roots of the spinal cord carry different kinds of signals. In 1838 Schleiden and Schwann established cell theory, giving physiology a common unit of analysis. Lister's 1858 work on coagulation and inflammation led to antiseptic surgery and a sharp fall in surgical deaths. Pavlov's study of conditional reflexes in dogs began in 1891.

Two 20th-century ideas did the most to organise the field. Claude Bernard's earlier concept of the *milieu intérieur*, the body's internal environment, was championed by Walter B. Cannon in 1929 under the name "homeostasis," defined as "maintenance of steady states in the body." In 1954 Andrew Huxley and Hugh Huxley discovered the sliding-filament mechanism of skeletal muscle, showing that the same logic that governs whole organisms also governs single proteins.

## Women in the discipline

Women were initially excluded from the founding physiological societies. The American Physiological Society, founded in 1887, elected Ida Hyde as its first female member in 1902. Six women were admitted to The Physiological Society on 3 July 1915 after J. S. Haldane's 1913 proposal, and Bodil Schmidt-Nielsen became the APS's first woman president in 1975. Recognition followed: Gerty Cori in 1947 (the cycle that converts muscle glycogen to lactic acid), Barbara McClintock in 1983 (genetic transposition, still the only unshared Nobel awarded to a woman), and Elizabeth Blackburn in 2009 (telomeres and telomerase).

## Current debates

The field's visibility has fallen since the 19th century because it spawned the now-separate disciplines of neuroscience, endocrinology, and immunology. Whether physiology is "dead or alive" as a named discipline is therefore an open question, even as integrative work continues and the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine is still awarded for discoveries rooted in its traditions.
