# Bacteria

A bacterium is a single-celled organism whose interior is not divided into compartments: no nucleus holds the DNA, and there are no membrane-wrapped organelles, only a shared cytoplasm. Most bacteria are a few micrometres long, about one-tenth the size of an animal or plant cell. They were the first life on Earth, appearing around four billion years ago, long before any nucleated cell existed.

## Two prokaryotic domains

Bacteria share ancestry with archaea, and the two groups look alike under a microscope, but they differ at the molecular level. Since 1977, biologists have separated all prokaryotes into two domains, Bacteria and Archaea, based on Carl Woese's sequencing of a ribosomal gene called 16S rRNA. The split is ancient, probably 2.5 to 3.2 billion years ago, and the last common ancestor of both groups was likely a heat-loving microbe. The clearest chemical difference is the cell wall: bacteria build theirs from peptidoglycan, a mesh of sugars cross-linked by short peptides, while archaea do not. This is why certain antibiotics, such as vancomycin, kill bacteria without harming us, and why no common antibiotic attacks archaea.

## How bacteria shaped other life

Bacteria did not just coexist with evolving life; they built it. A eukaryotic cell, defined by having a nucleus, arose when an ancestral cell engulfed a free-living bacterium and kept it as an energy-producing organelle, the mitochondrion. A later engulfment of a cyanobacterium-like cell produced the chloroplast, and therefore all green tissue. Every animal, plant, and fungus carries descendants of those two bacterial partners.

Bacteria also dominate the planet by numbers. An estimated 2×10³⁰ bacteria live on Earth, with combined biomass exceeded only by plants. A single gram of soil holds roughly a billion of them, and ocean bacteria may supply up to half the oxygen in the atmosphere.

## Structure, shape, and behaviour

Bacteria have shapes, usually spheres (cocci) or rods (bacilli), set by the cell wall and an internal scaffold. They swim using rotating hair-like flagella and crawl using extendable pili. Many form coordinated groups: myxobacteria build fruiting bodies of up to about 100,000 cells when starved, communicating by releasing and sensing small molecules in a process called quorum sensing. Many species also form biofilms, dense surface-attached communities encased in slime where cells can show over 500-fold increased resistance to antibiotics compared with free-floating cells, a major reason chronic infections persist.

A few Gram-positive genera, notably *Bacillus* and *Clostridium*, form endospores: dried-out, metabolically dormant shells that survive boiling, radiation, and exposure to outer space, possibly for millions of years. Endospores are why anthrax, tetanus, and botulism are so hard to eradicate.

## Reproduction and genetics

Bacteria reproduce by binary fission, splitting in two, with some strains doubling in 17 minutes. Most carry a single circular chromosome as one copy, so any disabling mutation immediately shows its effect, and populations can adapt in days. Three mechanisms move genes sideways between bacteria, not just from parent to offspring: transformation (picking up loose DNA from the environment), transduction (bacteriophages carrying bacterial DNA from one cell to another), and conjugation (direct DNA transfer through pili). This horizontal gene transfer occasionally crosses species and is the main route by which antibiotic resistance spreads. Bacteria defend against phages using restriction enzymes and a stored-memory system called CRISPR that slices incoming viral DNA.

## How bacteria make a living

Bacteria harvest energy from light (phototrophs) or chemical reactions (chemotrophs), pull electrons from minerals (lithotrophs) or organic matter (organotrophs), and use oxygen, other molecules, or minerals as electron acceptors. Some, called diazotrophs, are the only living things that take nitrogen gas from the air and fix it into biologically usable forms, a step on which all plants and animals depend. Bacteria and a few archaea are the only organisms that synthesise vitamin B12, making nearly all animal life dependent on them. Gut bacteria also produce vitamin K, biotin, and folic acid, and selected strains ferment milk into cheese, cabbage into sauerkraut, and grape juice into wine.

## Disease and resistance

A small fraction of bacterial species cause disease: tuberculosis, cholera, tetanus, typhoid, diphtheria, syphilis, and leprosy are all bacterial. Some pathogens secrete exotoxins, which can sometimes be neutralised into vaccines, while others release endotoxins, toxic cell-wall fragments released when cells die. Since Paul Ehrlich's 1910 syphilis treatment, antibiotics have exploited chemical differences between bacterial and human cells, but routine agricultural antibiotic use accelerates the spread of resistance genes through horizontal gene transfer.

## What remains unknown

Bacterial fossils, often stromatolites, lack distinctive shapes, so the family tree is reconstructed from gene sequences. Only about two percent of bacterial species have been studied in any depth; ten species account for half of all research papers, and roughly three-quarters of named species have no research at all. Estimates of total bacterial diversity span seven to nine orders of magnitude, anywhere from 10 million to 1 billion species. The bacteria that built mitochondria, fix our nitrogen, and cycle our nutrients are mostly unknown to science.
