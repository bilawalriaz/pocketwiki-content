# DNA vaccine

A DNA vaccine delivers a circular piece of engineered DNA, called a plasmid, into the body's cells. Once inside the nucleus, the plasmid instructs those cells to manufacture a specific viral or bacterial protein (an antigen, a substance the immune system recognizes as foreign). The immune system mounts a defensive response and stores a memory of that protein for future encounters, so the vaccinated organism becomes its own antigen factory.

The concept emerged in 1983, when Enzo Paoletti and Dennis Panicali at the New York Department of Health inserted foreign genes into cowpox virus DNA to make recombinant vaccines. In 1993, Jeffrey Ulmer and colleagues at Merck Research Laboratories showed that injecting mice with a plasmid encoding a flu antigen protected them against influenza. By August 2021, Indian regulators granted emergency approval to ZyCoV-D, developed by Cadila Healthcare, the first DNA vaccine authorized for human use, designed against COVID-19. No DNA vaccine has been approved for humans in the United States as of 2021, though a veterinary West Nile virus vaccine for horses has been approved.

## How it works

Once a plasmid reaches the cell nucleus, the cell's machinery reads the gene and produces the encoded antigen. The protein is processed and displayed on the cell surface bound to MHC (major histocompatibility complex) class I and class II molecules, which present peptide fragments to T cells. An antigen-presenting cell carries these fragments to the lymph nodes, where it primes T cells and B cells, triggering both antibody and cytotoxic responses.

Plasmids typically include a strong viral promoter (the CMV immediate-early promoter is now preferred over older SV40 or RSV versions), a polyadenylation signal, and sometimes an intron to boost expression. Structural instability is a concern for any plasmid used in vaccines or gene therapy, because repeated sequences in the backbone can recombine, so vector design tries to minimize extraneous noncoding DNA.

## Delivery and dosage

Two delivery methods dominate. **Saline injection** into muscle or skin is often assisted by electroporation (brief electrical pulses that temporarily open cell membranes), myotoxins like bupivacaine, or hypertonic solutions. This typically biases immunity toward a TH1 profile, the cellular, pro-inflammatory branch. The **gene gun** fires DNA-coated gold or tungsten microparticles directly into cells using compressed helium, uses 100 to 1,000 times less DNA, and tends to bias toward a TH2 profile, the humoral, antibody-dominated branch.

Saline injection needs roughly 10 μg to 1 mg; gene gun delivery works with 0.2 to 20 μg, and as little as 16 ng has produced responses. Mice require about ten times less DNA than primates, because saline-injected DNA must cross the basal lamina and connective tissue before reaching cells, while gene gun delivery forces DNA directly into them.

## Immune response characteristics

DNA vaccines activate both arms of adaptive immunity. They induce cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) that kill infected cells, and helper T cells (CD4+) that coordinate the broader response. Antibody titres rise more slowly than with recombinant proteins, as long as 12 weeks to peak in mice, but are long-lived, sustained for over 74 weeks without boosting against hepatitis B surface antigen. Compared to recombinant protein immunization, DNA-raised antibodies are often lower in quantity but show higher affinity for native viral epitopes.

## Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include no risk of infection, antigen presentation through both MHC pathways, ease of production, good storage stability, and the ability to bias toward TH1 or TH2 responses. The plasmid itself also acts as an adjuvant: bacterial DNA contains unmethylated CpG motifs (cytosine-guanine dinucleotides rare in vertebrates), which trigger dendritic cells to release IL-12 and drive TH1 cytokine production.

Disadvantages include limited effectiveness for non-protein antigens such as bacterial polysaccharides, potential for atypical antigen processing, and a safety concern: nasal administration of plasmid DNA nanoparticles has been shown to transfect brain cells in rats, an off-target risk that constrains delivery routes.

## Prime-boost strategies

Because DNA vaccines alone often produce modest responses in humans, researchers combine a DNA prime with a recombinant viral boost, such as a poxvirus or canarypox vector. This approach achieved partial protection against *Plasmodium knowlesi* malaria in rhesus monkeys, with higher antibody levels, CTL activity, and IFN-γ secretion than DNA-only regimens. A trial in chronic hepatitis B carriers found that a DNA vaccine encoding HBV envelope proteins induced specific IFN-γ-producing T cells, but the response was too weak to control the established infection.

Most immunostimulatory CpG data comes from murine studies and may not transfer directly to humans or livestock; ruminants show reduced sensitivity, likely because of their large gastrointestinal microbial load.
