# Global Public Health Intelligence Network

The Global Public Health Intelligence Network (GPHIN) is an electronic public health early warning system that Canada's Public Health Agency created in 1994 and now operates inside the World Health Organization's Global Outbreak Alert and Response Network (GOARN). The system continuously scans internet media, news wires and websites in nine languages to detect rumours of disease outbreaks before official reports appear. Analysts flag clusters of unusual illness and forward alerts to WHO and other partners so field teams and laboratories can investigate. WHO once credited GPHIN with roughly 20 percent of its epidemiological intelligence and described it as the foundation of a global pandemic early-warning system. Ronald St. John, then a government epidemiologist, started the network, which grew alongside the older ProMED-mail system.

GPHIN's value is speed. Formal case reports from national ministries move slowly through official channels, while open-source media can surface an outbreak within hours. The system is credited with early detection of the 2009 swine flu pandemic in Mexico, Zika in West Africa, H5N1 in Iran, MERS, and Ebola. After the 2003 SARS outbreak showed how fast a respiratory pathogen could cross borders, GPHIN became central to Canada's pandemic preparedness and was placed under the Centre for Emergency Preparedness and Response (CEPR) inside the Public Health Agency of Canada (PHAC).

The system's most consequential episode is its 2019 to 2020 silence. Alert output had been falling for a decade, from 877 alerts in 2009 to 198 in 2013 and 21 in 2018. In May 2019 the federal government directed PHAC to refocus on domestic priorities; analysts were reassigned, international scanning stopped, and the final alert was issued on May 24, 2019. In early 2020, as COVID-19 spread from China, PHAC scientists were told to rely on official Chinese sources rather than the unofficial intelligence GPHIN was built to gather. Some reported difficulty moving urgent findings up the chain of command.

The Globe and Mail revealed the shutdown in July 2020. In March 2021 the Auditor General of Canada, Karen Hogan, reported that PHAC was not adequately prepared for a pandemic and that silencing GPHIN, combined with inaccurate risk assessments that replaced it, hurt Canada's early COVID-19 response. Hogan added that the counterfactual, what an active GPHIN might have changed, cannot be established. Health Minister Patty Hajdu commissioned an independent review led by Margaret Bloodworth, Paul Gully, and Mylaine Breton that also examined complaints dissenting scientists had been marginalized. Chief Public Health Officer Theresa Tam supported the review, while Prime Minister Justin Trudeau blamed earlier funding cuts under Stephen Harper's Conservative government for leaving PHAC weakened. CEPR leadership was replaced, and in August 2020, more than 400 days after going dark, GPHIN resumed issuing alerts.

GPHIN shows that an early warning system depends on the institution that runs it. Automated multilingual scanning of open media can flag outbreaks days before official channels, but only if analysts are staffed continuously, alerts reach decision-makers, and the signal is protected from political redirection. Canada's experience is that a decade of declining output, a quiet reassignment of staff, and a shift toward official foreign sources turned a globally respected surveillance asset into a near-silent one.
