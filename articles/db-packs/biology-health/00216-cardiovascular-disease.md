# Cardiovascular disease

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a cluster of disorders of the heart and blood vessels, including coronary artery disease, stroke, peripheral artery disease, heart failure, cardiomyopathy, and arrhythmia. It is the leading cause of death worldwide except in sub-Saharan Africa, responsible for 17.9 million deaths in 2015, about 32% of the global total. Up to 90% of cases are preventable, which makes CVD essentially a story about risk.

The shared mechanism behind the most common forms (coronary artery disease, stroke, peripheral artery disease) is atherosclerosis, the slow buildup of fatty plaques inside artery walls, narrowing them and sometimes rupturing to block blood flow. This process can start in childhood. A second distinct pathway is rheumatic heart disease, in which an untreated strep throat infection triggers immune damage to the heart valves, now rare in wealthy countries but still significant globally.

Risk factors fall into two groups. The modifiable ones carry most of the weight: tobacco use, physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and high cholesterol. A 10 mmHg reduction in blood pressure lowers CVD risk by about 20%, and statins reduce cardiovascular mortality by about 31%, showing how strongly these factors drive the disease. Non-modifiable factors matter too: risk roughly triples with each decade of age, men are about 50% more likely than women of the same age to develop CVD and are diagnosed 7–10 years earlier, and a family history triples risk. Other contributors include poor sleep, chronic psychological stress, air pollution (especially PM2.5 particles, 2.5 micrometers or smaller), and socioeconomic disadvantage. A 2021 WHO study found working 55 or more hours per week raised stroke risk by 35% and heart disease death risk by 17%, and 2018 WHO estimates tied trans fats to over 500,000 deaths per year. A 2015 study found women with two or more divorces had CVD risk comparable to that of a smoker or diabetic, illustrating how social stress can translate into physical risk.

Prevention centres on lifestyle: a diet such as the Mediterranean pattern, at least 150 minutes of moderate exercise per week, avoiding tobacco, and limiting alcohol. Where lifestyle is not enough, medications that treat the underlying risk factors (statins for cholesterol, antihypertensives for blood pressure) are the next layer. Aspirin is not recommended for primary prevention in low-risk people because the bleeding risk outweighs the cardiovascular benefit. When prevention fails, treatment combines lifestyle, drugs, and sometimes surgery such as angioplasty (opening a blocked vessel with a balloon or stent) or bypass grafting (rerouting blood around a blockage). The American Heart Association's 2023 PREVENT calculator refines how clinicians estimate an individual's 10- and 30-year risk.

The disease burden is unevenly distributed. Over 80% of CVD deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries, and the average age of death from coronary artery disease is around 80 in the developed world versus 68 in the developing world, reflecting both earlier onset and less complete treatment. Open questions remain: the precise role of saturated versus unsaturated fat (replacing saturated with polyunsaturated fat is the standard advice), the value of omega-3 and antioxidant supplements (not conclusively beneficial), and whether telling asymptomatic people their risk score changes their behaviour.

The mental model: CVD is mostly one slow process (atherosclerosis) acting on the blood vessels of the heart, brain, and limbs, accelerated by a well-mapped set of modifiable risks, and it kills more people than any other disease because those risks are so common and so cumulative across a lifetime.
