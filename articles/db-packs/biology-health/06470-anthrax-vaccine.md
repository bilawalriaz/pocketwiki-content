# Anthrax vaccine

Anthrax is a disease of livestock and humans caused by the bacterium *Bacillus anthracis*. Anthrax vaccines train the immune system to recognise either the bacterium or its toxins, so that exposure does not progress to fatal disease.

## How anthrax harms the body

*B. anthracis* kills by secreting toxins. In 1948, the British biochemist Harry Smith, working at Porton Down, identified three anthrax toxins. Modern acellular (cell-free) vaccines target one of those components, the protective antigen (PA): antibodies against PA neutralise the toxin complex and prevent disease. The 2005 U.S. FDA review confirmed that the licensed American vaccine is effective regardless of whether anthrax enters through the skin, gut, or lungs.

## Three generations of vaccine design

A **first generation** is a live, weakened vaccine. Pasteur's 1881 public trial at Pouilly-le-Fort showed the principle: vaccinated sheep and cows survived injection with live anthrax, while unvaccinated controls died. Pasteur publicly claimed he had weakened the bacilli with oxygen, but his laboratory notebooks show he used a chemical method (potassium dichromate) developed by the Toulouse veterinary surgeon Jean-Joseph-Henri Toussaint, a controversy reassessed in 1995 by the historian Gerald L. Geison. In 1935, the immunologist Max Sterne isolated a more reliably attenuated strain, the Sterne strain 34F2, which remains the basis of most livestock anthrax vaccines today.

A **second generation** is a killed or cell-free vaccine. The British Anthrax Vaccine Precipitated (AVP), licensed in 1954, contains a sterile filtrate of the Sterne strain precipitated with alum, given as three injections three weeks apart, a booster at six months, and yearly boosters. The American Anthrax Vaccine Adsorbed (AVA, trade name BioThrax), licensed in 1970, is made from culture filtrates of the avirulent V770-NP1-R strain and requires 3 to 6 doses for protective immunity. Because they contain no live organisms, these vaccines are safer than live vaccines but cause considerable local reactogenicity (redness, swelling, soreness, sometimes fever); serious adverse reactions occur in about 1% of recipients.

A **third generation**, still experimental, uses recombinant protective antigen, the PA protein produced by genetic engineering, combined with adjuvants such as aluminium hydroxide, saponin QS-21, or monophosphoryl lipid A. In rhesus macaques, a single dose has given over 90% protection against inhaled anthrax.

## Live vaccines used in humans

The Soviet Union developed a live spore vaccine in the 1930s, available for humans by 1940. The current Russian formulation, STI-1, uses freeze-dried spores of a non-encapsulated strain and is given in two doses; reports from the 1970s and 1980s suggest it was more protective than the British and American killed vaccines, but serious side-effects restrict its use to healthy adults. China uses a different live attenuated strain, A16R, given by scarification with a booster at 6 or 12 months and annual boosters thereafter.

## Why anthrax vaccination is unusual

Anthrax is rare in civilian life, so most human vaccine is bought by governments preparing for biological warfare or bioterrorism. The United States maintains ten million doses in the Strategic National Stockpile. In 1997 the Clinton administration made vaccination mandatory for active-duty service members; the requirement was halted by court order in 2004 after questions about safety and efficacy, reinstated in 2006, and remains required for personnel deployed to Iraq, Afghanistan, or South Korea. Live vaccines should not be given during antibiotic treatment, and vaccinated animals have a withholding period before slaughter, though no such period is defined for milk and no human infections from vaccinated-animal products have been reported.

All currently used human anthrax vaccines, live spore (Russia) or acellular (USA, UK), share two limitations: they require multiple doses, and they produce substantial local reactions.
