# Plant nutrition

Plants need 17 essential elements to complete a normal life cycle. Three come from air and water; the rest come from the soil as dissolved ions. Liebig's law of the minimum, proved in 1840, says growth is limited by whichever nutrient is scarcest, not by the total supply. Adding fertilizer only helps when that scarcest nutrient is actually limiting.

## The 17 essential elements

**From air and water:** carbon and oxygen enter through leaves as CO₂ via stomata; hydrogen comes almost entirely from water absorbed by roots. Together with nitrogen, these four elements make up over 95% of a plant's dry weight. Carbon forms the backbone of proteins, starches, and cellulose. Hydrogen ions drive the proton gradient that powers ATP synthesis in photosynthesis and respiration. Oxygen is taken in as O₂, CO₂, H₂O, and various anions from soil water.

**From soil, as ions:**

| Macronutrients (>0.1% dry weight) | Micronutrients (0.1–200 ppm) |
|---|---|
| Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), Potassium (K), Calcium (Ca), Sulfur (S), Magnesium (Mg) | Iron (Fe), Boron (B), Chlorine (Cl), Manganese (Mn), Zinc (Zn), Copper (Cu), Molybdenum (Mo), Nickel (Ni) |

Micronutrients act at parts-per-million levels but are just as essential as macronutrients. Excess of one nutrient can induce deficiency of another, and toxicity begins only slightly above each element's optimum.

## What each major nutrient does

**Nitrogen** is the most common limiting nutrient. It makes up 40–50% of protoplasm dry matter, forms the building blocks of amino acids and proteins, and is part of chlorophyll.

**Phosphorus** structures nucleic acids (DNA, RNA) and phospholipid membranes. Every cellular energy transfer depends on ATP, which contains phosphorus. It concentrates at growing points and seeds.

**Potassium** is not built into any structural molecule. It activates enzymes, regulates stomatal opening through a K⁺ ion pump, controls turgor, and improves drought tolerance. It is the most mobile nutrient in plant tissue.

**Calcium** forms part of cell walls, balances anions in the vacuole, and acts as an intracellular messenger. Deficiency stunts growth and causes blossom end rot in tomatoes and peppers.

**Magnesium** sits at the center of the chlorophyll molecule and activates many enzymes, especially those linked to phosphorus metabolism.

**Sulfur** is part of the amino acids cysteine and methionine, supports chloroplast function via iron-sulfur complexes, and is needed for nitrogen fixation in legumes.

**Iron** is required for chlorophyll synthesis; deficiency causes interveinal chlorosis. **Molybdenum** is part of nitrate reductase and nitrogenase. **Boron** affects flowering, pollen germination, and sugar transport and is the second most limiting micronutrient in crops after zinc. **Manganese** is required for chloroplast construction. **Copper** is needed for lignin and photosynthesis. **Zinc** is required for DNA transcription. **Nickel** activates urease. Silicon and cobalt benefit some species but are not universally essential.

## How plants take up nutrients

Roots, especially the fine root hairs, absorb nutrient ions from soil water. Two processes dominate:

1. **Cation exchange.** Root hairs pump H⁺ into the soil through proton pumps. The H⁺ displaces positively charged nutrient ions (K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺) from negatively charged soil particles, freeing them for uptake.
2. **Movement toward the stele.** Ions then cross the **Casparian strip**, a waterproof band in the root that blocks passive flow and forces nutrients through selective membranes. From the central stele they enter the **xylem**, which carries water and minerals upward, and the **phloem**, which distributes organic compounds. Water potential drives the flow: when the plant's internal water potential is more negative than the soil's, water and dissolved nutrients move in.

Three transport modes cross root cell membranes: simple diffusion for nonpolar molecules (O₂, CO₂, NH₃), facilitated diffusion through transport proteins, and active transport, which uses ATP to pump ions against a concentration gradient.

## Symbiosis and nitrogen fixation

The atmosphere is 78% N₂, but the triple bond makes it inert. Plants need it reduced to ammonium (NH₄⁺) or nitrate (NO₃⁻). **Legumes** (alfalfa, soybeans, peas, clovers) host *rhizobia* bacteria that perform biological nitrogen fixation, converting atmospheric N₂ into NH₄⁺. Non-legumes (wheat, corn, rice) rely on soil nitrogen from organic matter breakdown, animal waste, lightning, or fertilizer. **Mycorrhizal fungi** form a second key symbiosis: their hyphae extend the effective root surface area, especially helping phosphorus uptake, which is otherwise limited because soil phosphates dissolve very slowly.

## Mobility and deficiency symptoms

Whether a nutrient can be relocated within the plant determines where deficiency shows first.

**Mobile nutrients** (N, P, K, Mg): the plant moves them from old leaves to young tissue, so symptoms such as chlorosis or necrosis appear on **older leaves** first.

**Immobile nutrients** (Ca, B, S): cannot be relocated, so deficiency shows in **youngest tissues**. Calcium deficiency causes blossom end rot; boron deficiency kills growing points.

Nitrogen deficiency is the most common: stunted growth, pale chlorosis, and purple anthocyanin accumulation on stems and leaf undersides. Phosphorus deficiency is harder to diagnose and often shows only as poor vigor with no color change, because soil phosphate must drop to extremely low levels before visible symptoms appear.

## Hydroponics

Because the essential elements are known, plants can be grown without soil in a nutrient solution. The standard formula is Hoagland solution, which supplies all 17 elements in the correct ratios. In the nutrient film technique, a thin layer of aerated solution flows past the roots, preventing hypoxia, which would otherwise shut down root respiration and block nutrient uptake.

Boron illustrates how narrow the safe range can be. Below 1 ppm in soil solution causes deficiency; above 1 ppm is toxic to most plants, though boron-tolerant crops such as sugar beet tolerate up to 200 ppm. Excess boron is leached by irrigation combined with sulphur to lower soil pH.
