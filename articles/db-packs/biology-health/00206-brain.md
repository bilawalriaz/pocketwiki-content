# Brain

The brain is the central organ of the nervous system, a dense network of cells that turns sensory input into behaviour, memory, and thought. It sits at the head end of the body, near the eyes, ears, and nose, because evolution placed decision-making tissue close to the organs that first detect the world. Every brain, from a worm's to a human's, is built from the same basic ingredients: excitable cells that pass signals at specialised junctions, rewiring those connections with experience.

A brain contains two cell classes. Neurons transmit signals; glial cells support them, insulate them, regulate their chemistry, and guide them during development. A typical neuron has a cell body, branching dendrites that receive input, and a single axon that sends output. Many axons are wrapped in a fatty myelin sheath, which speeds conduction to 1–100 metres per second. Where an axon meets its target, a synapse forms. An arriving action potential, an electrical pulse lasting about a millisecond, triggers release of a neurotransmitter that binds receptors on the next cell and either excites or inhibits it. The human brain contains roughly 100 trillion synapses, and the strength of each can change with use. This activity-dependent rewiring, synaptic plasticity, is the leading explanation for how the brain stores information.

Two neurotransmitters dominate: glutamate, which excites, and GABA, which inhibits. Their balance lets circuits generate rhythms, filter signals, and avoid runaway firing; when inhibition fails, seizures result. Other transmitters, including serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine, and acetylcholine, are released by small localised groups of neurons that modulate whole-brain states like arousal, mood, and reward. Most of the brain's energy maintains the electrical gradient across neuronal membranes rather than driving signals, which is why the human brain uses 20–25 percent of the body's resting metabolism even though it is only 2–8 percent of body mass in most animals. Glucose is the main fuel, with ketones and lactate as backups. A blood–brain barrier of tightly joined vessel walls keeps toxins out, and a separate glymphatic system compensates for the lack of ordinary lymphatic drainage.

Brains evolved from a common bilaterally symmetric ancestor that lived roughly 700–650 million years ago, probably a worm with a nerve cord and a swelling of neurons at the front end. Invertebrates such as insects, octopuses, and nematodes built complex brains from this starting point, often from paired ventral nerve cords. The nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans*, with exactly 302 neurons, became the first animal whose complete connectome, or wiring diagram, was mapped in 1986; the fruit fly *Drosophila* followed in the 2010s and 2020s.

In vertebrates, the embryonic neural tube forms three swellings, the forebrain, midbrain, and hindbrain, which then divide into five. This plan is conserved across every fish, reptile, bird, and mammal. Within it, regions took on specialised roles. The medulla controls heartbeat and breathing; the pons manages sleep and respiration; the cerebellum, packed with roughly half of all the brain's neurons despite taking about a tenth of its volume, refines movement and contributes to cognition; the thalamus relays sensory information; the hypothalamus keeps internal variables like temperature and salt balance on target; the hippocampus stores spatial and episodic memories; and the basal ganglia select actions by holding motor systems under tonic inhibition and releasing them, with dopamine encoding the difference between expected and received reward. All vertebrate forebrains contain a layered pallium used for higher processing; in mammals this became a six-layered neocortex that folded into gyri and sulci as it expanded, while in reptiles and birds it remains three-layered. Mammals also developed a corpus callosum linking the two hemispheres, and their brains run about twice the size of a bird's and ten times a reptile's at equal body mass.

Brain size scales with body size according to a power law with an exponent near 0.75. Primates sit well above this line, with brains 5–10 times larger than the prediction, and humans further still: the encephalisation quotient reaches 7–8 in humans, compared with 2–3 in other primates. The difference is concentrated in the prefrontal cortex, the seat of planning and abstract thought, and in an expanded visual cortex with more than thirty distinct areas.

Development follows the conserved three-vesicle plan. Neurons are born in proliferative zones near the ventricles and migrate outward, guided by molecular cues and later by patterned electrical activity, such as retinal waves that align the visual map. In humans, most neurons are produced before birth, though the olfactory bulb and the hippocampal dentate gyrus keep generating neurons throughout life. Early overproduction of synapses is trimmed by activity-dependent pruning, leaving experience-shaped circuitry.

In function, the brain is organised hierarchically. Reflexes are handled in the spinal cord; stereotyped actions like breathing and swallowing in the brainstem; limb coordination in the midbrain; and fine voluntary movement in the motor cortex, with the cerebellum adjusting precision and timing and the basal ganglia choosing which action to attempt. Perception flows from receptors through brainstem and thalamus to the cortex. A circadian clock in the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus, driven by clock genes that cycle every 24 hours and reset by light, organises sleep and wakefulness into alternating REM and NREM phases. Memory comes in distinct forms: working memory held by transient cell assemblies, episodic memory stored in the hippocampus, semantic knowledge distributed across the cortex, and motor skills refined in the cerebellum, with BDNF and physical activity boosting the plasticity that underlies all of them.

Major research methods now include staining and microscopy to map anatomy, electrodes and EEG to record activity, targeted lesions in animals and natural strokes in humans to infer function, computer simulations of neurons, and single-cell sequencing, which in 2024 profiled nearly three million nuclei from the prefrontal cortex of 388 people and identified 28 cell types. Open questions remain: whether the last common ancestor of bilaterians truly had a brain or only a sensory patch, how to match vertebrate and invertebrate brain regions to each other, how large populations of neurons encode information, how much adult neurogenesis really matters, and how physical processes give rise to conscious experience.
