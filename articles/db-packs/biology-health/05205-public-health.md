# Public health

Public health is the science and practice of improving the health of populations rather than individual patients, through organised efforts by society, organisations, communities, and individuals. Its aim is to prevent disease, prolong life, and promote physical, mental, and social well-being. The World Health Organization defines health as "a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity." Public health is interdisciplinary, drawing on epidemiology (the study of disease patterns in populations), biostatistics, social sciences, environmental health, health economics, and health policy, and it sits alongside primary, secondary, and tertiary care as a distinct part of a country's overall health system.

## How it works

Public health aims are achieved through two main mechanisms: surveillance of disease cases and health indicators, and the promotion of healthy behaviours, communities, and environments. Surveillance is the systematic collection and analysis of health data. Analysts study the determinants of health, meaning the social, environmental, and behavioural factors that shape outcomes, and the threats a population faces, then design interventions. Many interventions are non-medical. Hand-washing with soap prevents contagious disease; seat-belt laws reduce injury deaths; clean water and sanitation interrupt the transmission of waterborne pathogens. Public health also uses geographic information systems, because risk, vulnerability, and exposure vary by place. The workforce is multidisciplinary, typically including epidemiologists, biostatisticians, public health nurses, microbiologists, environmental health officers, economists, sociologists, pharmacists, and physicians.

A core tension in the field is between individual liberty and population health. Interventions such as vaccination, tobacco taxation, and seat-belt laws restrict personal choice in order to reduce collective harm. Research during the COVID-19 pandemic found that perceived duty to prevent harm best predicted compliance with measures like hand-washing, mask-wearing, and staying home, while valuing liberty over equality best predicted flouting them.

## Evolution of priorities

Until the 20th century, public health in industrialising nations focused on three core problems: clean water and sanitation, control of infectious disease through vaccination and quarantine, and the build-up of supporting sciences such as statistics, microbiology, and epidemiology. Great Britain led this work in the 19th century because it was the first modern urban nation, with more than half its population living in settlements of over 2,000 people by 1851. Edwin Chadwick's 1842 report on the sanitary condition of the labouring population documented the link between squalid housing and death rates, helping drive the Public Health Act of 1848, which centralised water, sewerage, and drainage under a General Board of Health. James Newlands designed the world's first integrated sewerage system in Liverpool beginning in 1848, and Joseph Bazalgette later built London's (1858–1875). The Vaccination Act of 1853 made smallpox vaccination compulsory in England and Wales.

The transition from infectious to chronic disease defined the 20th century. Britain's infant mortality fell from over 15% in 1870 to 7% by 1930 through preventive measures. In wealthy nations today, non-communicable diseases dominate, with the WHO reporting at least 220 million people with diabetes worldwide and projecting that diabetes deaths will double by 2030. A 2010 Lancet editorial called type 2 diabetes, a largely preventable disorder, "a public health humiliation." The WHO estimated that in 2014, 1.9 billion adults and 41 million children under five were overweight globally, with the problem rising sharply in low-income urban settings. Antibiotic resistance was the theme of World Health Day 2011 because it allows controlled infectious diseases to re-emerge.

## Global disparities

Public health infrastructures in many developing countries are still forming, and shortages of trained workers, money, or knowledge leave large populations without basic care. Poor maternal and child health, aggravated by malnutrition and poverty, is a leading concern, and the WHO reports that lack of exclusive breastfeeding in the first six months of life contributes to over a million avoidable child deaths each year. Health aid from developed countries rose from $5.5 billion in 1990 to $26.87 billion in 2010, though critics argue it can foster dependence or be misdirected. One study associated $1 billion in aid with 364,000 fewer deaths among children under five in 2011. Some African governments spend less than $100 per person per year on health care, compared with roughly $10,600 per capita of US federal health spending in 2019, though clinical spending differs from public health spending, since regulations, sanitation, and education sit outside clinical care.

The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals for 2030, particularly Goals 1 to 6, address poverty, hunger, health, education, gender equality, and water and sanitation as structural drivers of population health. The social gradient in health, the stepwise improvement in outcomes as social position rises, shapes modern population health, and reducing health inequity is now a central concern, with critics noting that public health organisations sometimes reproduce the very inequities they aim to fix.
