# Genome evolution

Genome evolution is the process by which a genome changes in structure or size over time. It is studied through comparative genomics and by cataloguing events such as gene duplication, polyploidy, and the activity of genomic parasites. Since the first genomes were sequenced in the late 1970s, and the full human genome was published in 2001, the field has expanded alongside the growth of public sequence databases.

## Forces of change

Mutation, horizontal gene transfer, and genetic exchange related to sex reshape DNA in every lineage.

- Mutation introduces small sequence changes.
- Horizontal gene transfer moves DNA between cells without parent-to-offspring inheritance, through bacterial conjugation (plasmid or chromosome transfer, including antibiotic-resistance plasmids), transduction (bacteriophages carry DNA into a bacterium), and natural genetic transformation (uptake of free DNA; at least 67 prokaryotic species are competent for this).
- Sexual reproduction shuffles existing variation into new combinations, producing far more diversity among offspring than prokaryotic replication alone.

## Prokaryotic versus eukaryotic genomes

Prokaryotic genomes are compact and circular (bacteria) or circular (archaea), with most DNA coding for protein and few introns. Eukaryotic genomes are linear, split across multiple chromosomes, packaged with histones in a nucleus, and dominated by introns, non-coding segments removed by RNA splicing before translation. *E. coli* carries about 4,288 genes in 4.6 Mb on one circular chromosome; humans carry about 20,000 genes in 3.2 Gb on 46 linear chromosomes, with an average gene size near 27,000 bp.

Free-living prokaryotes often evolve larger genomes with more genes, allowing adaptation to variable environments. Parasites usually lose genes, because the host supplies nutrients the bacterium no longer needs to make. *Mycobacterium leprae*, the leprosy agent, has lost many once-functional genes compared with its relative *M. tuberculosis*; surviving as pseudogenes, they make replication faster and cheaper. The opposite pattern appears in filamentous plant pathogens, whose repeat-rich regions expand over time, encoding host-interaction proteins and creating more chances for new virulence factors.

## The C-value paradox

Genome size is measured in base pairs. The C-value is the total DNA content of a genome. In prokaryotes, C-value correlates positively with gene number, so size tracks gene content. In eukaryotes there is a paradox: genome size does not correlate with gene number. Many eukaryotes carry far more DNA than the count of protein-coding genes would predict, because most eukaryotic DNA is non-coding.

## Chromosomal evolution

Chromosome number and structure can change over time. Human chromosome 2 arose when two ancestral chromosomes, still separate in chimpanzees as 2A and 2B, fused. Such changes can be visualised with fluorescence in situ hybridisation.

## Mechanisms that reshape genomes

- *Gene duplication.* A DNA region is copied, often by recombination error or retrotransposition. Because the duplicate is buffered against selection, it can accumulate mutations and sometimes evolve a new function.
- *Whole genome duplication (polyploidy).* The entire genome is copied. Wolfe and Shields showed in 1997 that *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* carries 32 pairs of homologous chromosomal regions covering over half its genome, evidence of an ancient whole-genome duplication after the yeast split from *Kluyveromyces*. Whole-genome duplication is common in fungi and plants; the dodecaploid *Spartina anglica* has 12 chromosome sets, compared with two in humans. A comparison of teleost fish with duplicated genomes against holostean relatives without them found little difference in evolutionary rate for roughly 150 million years.
- *Transposable elements.* DNA segments that move within a genome by cut-and-paste (excising and relocating) or copy-and-paste (duplicating into a new site). The Alu sequence is the most common in humans, with over one million copies.
- *Mutation.* Spontaneous changes can substitute, add, or delete nucleotides. Insertions or deletions can cause frameshifts that scramble the downstream reading frame. Mutations in promoters, enhancers, or transcription-factor binding sites can raise or lower gene expression.
- *Pseudogenes.* Non-functional copies of former genes, created by disabling mutations such as frameshifts, premature stop codons, or disrupted promoters. Human olfactory gene families contain many pseudogenes, accounting for our weaker sense of smell relative to most other mammals.
- *Exon shuffling.* Exons from different genes are combined or duplicated to form new genes, driven by transposons, sexual recombination, or illegitimate (non-homologous) recombination.
- *De novo gene birth.* New protein-coding genes can arise from non-coding DNA if an open reading frame and a transcription-factor binding site both appear. Five such genes were reported in *Drosophila melanogaster*, and around 60 putative human-specific de novo genes have been identified, almost all short and single-exon. In bacteria, grounded prophages (integrated phage DNA that can no longer produce phage) tolerate variation, raising the chance of new gene formation, and also serve as entry points for horizontal gene transfer.

## Genome reduction and speciation

Genome reduction is most extreme in obligate parasites and endosymbionts. The endosymbiont *Polynucleobacter necessarius*, required for survival of its ciliate host *Euplotes aediculatus*, has a reduced genome of 1.56 Mbp compared with 2.16 Mbp in closely related free-living relatives.

Speciation involves changes in behaviour, morphology, physiology, or metabolism, and recent work with next-generation sequencing shows genomes change quickly during this process. In African cichlid fish, comparisons across five species that diverged 100,000 to a few million years ago found that both gene sequences and expression patterns changed rapidly. About 20% of duplicate gene pairs acquired new tissue-specific expression patterns, showing that regulatory changes alone can produce new functions without major protein changes.

## Composition and originDNA is built from four bases: adenine (A), guanine (G), cytosine (C), and thymine (T). GC-content, the percentage of G and C bases, varies between organisms. Coding regions, longer genes, and promoters tend to have higher GC-content, because G-C base pairs are held by three hydrogen bonds versus two for A-T, giving greater stability. Organisms in extreme environments, including hydrothermal vents, often show high GC-content, and broader GC-content spread within a genus tends to reflect deeper evolutionary divergence. Both glycine and alanine are encoded by codons beginning with G-C, and it has been hypothesised that the earliest life, evolving under high heat and pressure, depended on this extra stability.

Under the RNA world hypothesis, free ribonucleotides in the primitive environment joined into the first RNA genomes. Subsequent work has shown plausible prebiotic routes to purine and pyrimidine ribonucleotides, including direct condensation of nucleobases with ribose inside aqueous microdroplets and wet-dry cycle chemistry.
