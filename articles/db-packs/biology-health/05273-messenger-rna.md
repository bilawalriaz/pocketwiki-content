# Messenger RNA

Messenger RNA (mRNA) is a single-stranded RNA copy of a gene. A ribosome reads it to build a protein. The gene stays in the DNA; the mRNA carries the instructions to the ribosome.

The flow of information runs DNA → mRNA → protein, a principle known as the central dogma of molecular biology. Copying DNA into mRNA is called transcription, and reading mRNA to make a protein is called translation.

## How the code is read

Like DNA, mRNA is a chain of four nucleotides (A, U, G, C), where uracil replaces thymine. The ribosome reads the chain three nucleotides at a time. Each triplet is a codon. Most codons specify one of the twenty amino acids; three (UAG, UAA, UGA) are stop codons that terminate the protein. The start codon is AUG, which also codes for methionine. Translation needs two other RNAs: transfer RNA (tRNA), which recognises a codon and delivers the matching amino acid, and ribosomal RNA (rRNA), the catalytic core of the ribosome.

## From gene to usable mRNA

Making an mRNA begins when RNA polymerase binds a promoter on the DNA and synthesises a complementary RNA strand. The raw product is pre-mRNA.

In prokaryotes, transcription and translation both occur in the cytoplasm, and ribosomes can begin translating an mRNA while it is still being transcribed. Prokaryotic mRNA is usually polycistronic, carrying several genes that are translated into related proteins, and short-lived, averaging 1–3 minutes in bacteria.

In eukaryotes, the pre-mRNA must be processed before use. Three modifications matter most:

- 5′ capping. A modified guanine (7-methylguanosine, m7G) is added to the 5′ end shortly after transcription starts. The cap protects the mRNA and is recognised by the ribosome.
- Splicing. Non-coding regions called introns are removed; the remaining coding regions, called exons, are joined. The result is mature mRNA.
- 3′ polyadenylation. About 150–250 adenosine residues are added to the 3′ end, forming the poly(A) tail. The tail aids nuclear export, promotes translation, and protects against exonucleases.

Mature eukaryotic mRNA is monocistronic, encoding one protein, and is exported from the nucleus to the cytoplasm through nuclear pores. Once in the cytoplasm, it is translated on free ribosomes or on ribosomes docked at the endoplasmic reticulum, depending on the protein.

## Eukaryotic mRNA structure

A mature eukaryotic mRNA has three parts:

- 5′ untranslated region (5′ UTR), between the cap and the start codon. It influences ribosome recruitment and translation efficiency.
- The coding region, read as a series of codons.
- 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR), between the stop codon and the poly(A) tail. It controls mRNA stability, localisation, and translational efficiency. MicroRNAs and AU-rich elements bind here, often to promote degradation.

The m7G cap and poly(A) tail are bridged by proteins (eIF4E on the cap, poly(A)-binding protein on the tail, and eIF4G connecting them). This circularisation allows ribosomes to recycle efficiently.

## mRNA degradation

mRNA lifetime sets how much protein a cell can make from a transcript. In mammalian cells, lifetimes range from minutes to days. In bacteria, they average 1–3 minutes, letting the cell reshape its proteome quickly. Shortening of the poly(A) tail is usually the first step in eukaryotic decay, leading to decapping or attack by the exosome. AU-rich elements in the 3′ UTR of transcripts such as TNF promote rapid deadenylation and turnover. Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) and microRNAs (miRNAs), loaded into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), guide destruction or translational silencing of complementary messages. Nonsense-mediated decay destroys transcripts containing premature stop codons, which would otherwise produce truncated proteins. In prokaryotes, endonucleases and exonucleases degrade mRNA, sometimes guided by small regulatory RNAs that base-pair with their targets.

## Discovery and application

The idea of mRNA emerged from the PaJaMo experiment of Arthur Pardee, François Jacob, and Jacques Monod, which hinted at a short-lived informational intermediate. On 15 April 1960, Sydney Brenner and Francis Crick heard Jacob describe the result at Cambridge, and with Matthew Meselson at Caltech they ran the experiment that proved mRNA existed. Jacob and Monod coined the name "messenger RNA" later that year. Two back-to-back papers in May 1961, one by Brenner, Jacob, and Meselson, the other by Watson and Gros, experimentally characterised the molecule. The 2023 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman for nucleoside-modified mRNA that worked as a vaccine, the same class deployed against COVID-19 by Pfizer–BioNTech and Moderna.
