# Anatomical terms of motion

Anatomists describe body movement with a shared vocabulary built around the **anatomical position**: standing upright, facing forward, arms at the sides, palms facing forward. Every motion term is defined relative to that reference posture, which is why the same action can be called "flexion" in one joint and "extension" in another depending on which way the limb is oriented. Most terms come in opposite pairs (flexion/extension, abduction/adduction), and most real movements combine several motions at once across different planes.

Motions fall into a few basic types. **Angular** motions change the angle between two bones, as when the elbow bends. **Gliding** motions slide flat surfaces across each other, as between the vertebrae or the small bones of the wrist. **Rotational** motions spin a structure around its own long axis, like turning the head to look sideways. Movements can also be described as **linear** (straight-line) or **curvilinear** (along a curve), and the study of human movement as a whole is called **kinesiology**.

## The general motion pairs

**Flexion and extension** change the angle between body segments. Flexion decreases the angle, extension increases it. Bending the elbow, clenching a fist, and sitting down (knees flexed) are flexion; straightening the arm or standing up is extension. For joints like the neck and trunk, flexion moves the body anteriorly (chin to chest) and extension moves it posteriorly (leaning back). Shoulder and hip flexion swing the limb forward; extension swings it backward. The knee, ankle, and wrist are exceptions where the distal end must move anteriorly to be called extension.

**Abduction and adduction** describe movement toward or away from the body's midline (the midsagittal plane). *Ab-* means "away" and *ad-* means "toward" in Latin. Raising the arms sideways, spreading the fingers, and splaying the legs are abduction; dropping the arms back to the sides and bringing the knees together are adduction. For the wrist, abduction is also called **radial deviation** (toward the thumb) and adduction is **ulnar deviation** (toward the little finger).

**Elevation and depression** describe movement above and below the horizontal. Shrugging the shoulders is elevation of the scapula; lowering them is depression.

**Rotation** spins a body part around its longitudinal axis. *Internal* (medial) rotation turns toward the body's centerline; *external* (lateral) rotation turns away. The internal and external rotators of the shoulder form the **rotator cuff**, which stabilizes that joint.

**Circumduction** is a conical movement, a continuous loop combining flexion, extension, abduction, and adduction. The hip and shoulder, being ball-and-socket joints, perform it most freely; the arm traces a cone during a tennis serve or cricket bowl.

**Reduction** is the return of a bone or structure to its normal position, for example after a shoulder dislocation or hernia.

**Hyperextension** and **hyperflexion** go beyond the normal range of motion. Hyperextension (a joint "bent backward") increases stress on the ligaments and can result from trauma, or be used deliberately in surgery, or as a pain-compliance technique.

## Special motions of the hands and feet

The hand and foot need extra terms because embryological rotation of the limbs flipped their orientation. For the **foot at the ankle**, **dorsiflexion** pulls the toes up toward the shin (needed for a deep squat with heels down), while **plantar flexion** points the toes down, as when pressing a car pedal or standing on tiptoes. For the **hand at the wrist**, the labels reverse: **palmarflexion** bends the wrist toward the palm, and **dorsiflexion** bends it toward the back of the hand.

**Pronation and supination** refer to rotation of the forearm or foot. Supination of the forearm turns the palm to face anteriorly (upward in anatomical position, as when washing one's face); pronation turns it to face posteriorly (as when typing). In the foot, pronation shifts weight onto the medial edge, supination onto the lateral edge.

**Inversion and eversion** tilt the sole of the foot. Inversion turns the sole toward the midline (the motion of a twisted ankle); eversion turns it away.

## Other regions

For the **jaw**, **occlusion** is the motion that brings the mandible up to meet the maxilla so the teeth contact; **protrusion** and **retrusion** slide the jaw forward and back. For the **eye**, a **version** is a synchronized movement of both eyes in the same direction, and **torsion** is rotation around the eye's vertical axis (the movement made when looking toward the nose). **Opposition** is the thumb's unique motion of touching the fingertips, allowing grasping. **Nutation and counternutation** describe rotation of the sacrum, downward and forward with lumbar extension, upward and backward with lumbar flexion. **Protraction and retraction** slide a structure forward or backward, as with the shoulder girdle.
