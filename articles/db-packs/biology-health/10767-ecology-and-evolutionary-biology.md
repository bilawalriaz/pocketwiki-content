# Ecology and evolutionary biology

Ecology and evolutionary biology is an interdisciplinary field that studies how organisms interact with their environment and how those interactions drive evolutionary change. It unites ecology, the study of organisms in their physical and biological setting, with evolution, the change in inherited traits across generations. The two are inseparable: when a habitat changes, the relationships among animals, plants, and microorganisms shift, and populations either adapt, migrate, or decline. The field spans genes to whole ecosystems and helps explain human impacts on the global ecosystem and the path toward more sustainable development.

## How ecology shapes evolution: birdsong

Birdsong is shaped jointly by learning, genetics, and the acoustic properties of the habitat. A study of male white-crowned sparrows across regions found that birds from the same location share song traits, while birds from different locations diverge. Song structure also tracks habitat structure. In dense vegetation, high frequencies are absorbed and cannot travel far, so only slow trills at low frequencies propagate well. In open areas, fast trills at high frequencies dominate. Because lower frequencies carry further through leaves and stems, singing low gives a bird in dense cover a longer communication range with rivals and mates.

A similar acoustic pattern appears along mountain elevation. Birds living higher up sing at higher frequencies, driven by biotic noise rather than geometry: higher elevations are colder, support fewer competing species, and have less of the high-frequency sounds birds would otherwise have to talk over.

## Visual selection in snails: *Cepaea nemoralis*

The European land snail *Cepaea nemoralis* shows how visual predation and temperature jointly select shell colour and banding. Shell appearance is almost entirely genetically determined, so consistent environmental pressure shows up as a shift in morph frequencies across habitats. In the Evolution Mega-Lab, a Europe-wide citizen science project, volunteers counted snails in different habitats. Two selective pressures stand out. Bird predation favours camouflage: yellow snails, for example, blend better in dune backgrounds than brown snails. Thermal stress favours light shells in hot, exposed habitats, because dark shells absorb more heat and risk overheating the animal. The result is predictable spatial matching between shell colour, background, and climate.

## Urban evolution: rapid change in human-made environments

Urbanisation has created ecosystems of extremes, and evolution in cities is measurably faster than in natural or non-urban human landscapes. Four well-studied examples show how specific urban pressures select for specific traits.

In tropical regions, the lizard *Anolis cristatellus* lives on tree trunks, fences, and building walls. Urban surfaces are smoother and more slippery than bark, raising the risk of falling. City lizards have evolved longer limbs and more lamellae, the adhesive toe scales, improving grip on smooth substrates.

Traffic noise overlaps the frequencies of many bird songs, so urban birds have shifted to higher frequencies and shorter songs, keeping their signals audible above the anthropogenic noise floor.

In the Mediterranean weed *Crepis sancta*, seed morphology has shifted. The plant normally produces both light, wind-dispersed seeds with a pappus, a parachute-like structure, and heavier seeds without one. In the city, green patches are small and scattered, separated by asphalt and stone where wind-borne seeds are wasted. Urban plants produce more heavy, non-pappus seeds, which fall near the mother plant and are more likely to land on a viable patch and sprout.

Light pollution traps nocturnal insects that navigate by keeping a fixed angle to a distant light source, the moon, which produces a straight flight path. Near a street lamp, that same behaviour produces a tight spiral around the bulb, wasting time and energy and reducing mating and feeding opportunities. Urban moth populations have evolved reduced attraction to artificial light.

## The field and its institutional form

The label "Ecology and Evolutionary Biology" became common in North American universities as an alternative to the older "Cell and Molecular Biology" pairing and a broader frame than "Botany" or "Zoology." It signals integrated study across levels of biological organisation, from genes and genomes to species and ecosystems, with a focus on how biodiversity has arisen and is maintained. Recent advances in genetics and molecular biology have been folded into the discipline, so the field now routinely uses molecular tools to answer ecological and evolutionary questions. PhD-granting departments using this name include those at Columbia, Cornell, Princeton, Rice, the University of Arizona, UCLA, the University of Colorado, the University of Michigan, the University of Toronto, and Yale.
