# Taxonomy (biology)

Taxonomy is the science of naming, defining, and classifying living organisms. In modern practice it does more than sort specimens into boxes: it tries to reflect evolutionary history, grouping organisms so that each named taxon corresponds to a real branch on the tree of life. The discipline is rooted in the 18th-century work of Carl Linnaeus, whose system of ranked categories and two-part Latin names is still the backbone of how biologists refer to species.

A **taxon** (plural: *taxa*) is any named group of organisms, from a single species up to a kingdom. Taxa are arranged in a hierarchy of ranks. From broad to narrow, the principal ranks are **domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species**. Every named organism fits somewhere in this nested structure: humans, for example, sit in the family Hominidae, the order Primates, the class Mammalia, and so on. The rank of *species* is the basic unit, and every species name consists of two parts (the genus name plus a specific epithet), a convention called **binomial nomenclature** that Linnaeus formalised in works such as *Systema Naturae* (1735) and *Species Plantarum* (1753) for plants, with the 10th edition of *Systema Naturae* (1758) later adopted as the starting point for animal names.

Modern taxonomy differs from Linnaeus's original project in one decisive way. Before Darwin, classification was largely descriptive, sorting organisms by visible similarities. After *On the Origin of Species* (1859), the goal became to make classification reflect common ancestry. The methodology that dominates today, **cladistics**, builds classifications from the pattern of branching in evolutionary trees. Its central requirement is that every accepted group be **monophyletic**: it must include a common ancestor and *all* of that ancestor's descendants. Groups that omit some descendants are called **paraphyletic**; groups whose members do not share their most recent common ancestor are called **polyphyletic**. Cladists identify natural groups using **synapomorphies**, shared derived traits inherited from a common ancestor. A group defined this way is a **clade**.

The highest level of the modern tree is the three-domain system proposed by Carl Woese in 1977, based on comparisons of ribosomal RNA: **Archaea, Bacteria, and Eukarya**. This division was not visible in Linnaeus's time, when microscopes could not yet separate the single-celled domains. An alternative to the Linnaean ranks themselves is the **PhyloCode**, a rank-free nomenclature that names clades directly, though traditional ranks remain in everyday use.

Taxonomy is conventionally split into smaller tasks. **Alpha taxonomy** is the identification, description, and naming of new species, still largely a morphological and molecular craft. **Beta taxonomy** arranges those species into higher groups. **Microtaxonomy** tackles the "species problem," asking how to define a species in the first place, while **macrotaxonomy** deals with the ranks above genus. A **taxonomic revision** analyses variation across a group using morphology, anatomy, pollen structure, biochemistry, or DNA to determine its true boundaries, and a full **monograph** is a global revision of an entire taxon. Valid publication of a new name requires a formal description, a unique Latin binomial, a designated type specimen, and a list of features that distinguish it from relatives.

Taxonomy underpins both pure and applied biology. It supplies the names and groupings that biodiversity inventories, ecological surveys, and conservation prioritisation all depend on. Large digital resources such as the Catalogue of Life and the Encyclopedia of Life now aggregate these classifications, and taxa whose position is genuinely unknown can be labelled *incertae sedis*, "of uncertain placement."

Debates remain active. The boundary between taxonomy and its parent discipline **systematics** is still drawn differently by different authors, and the species concept itself has no single universally accepted definition. Molecular phylogenies sometimes disagree with classifications built from anatomy, and Woese's three-domain arrangement, though widely accepted, is contested by alternatives such as Cavalier-Smith's Neomura hypothesis. Standardising the vast and growing database of known life is itself an ongoing task, and new species are still described at a pace of roughly fifteen thousand per year.
