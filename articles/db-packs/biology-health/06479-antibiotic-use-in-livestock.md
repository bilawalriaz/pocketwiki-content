# Antibiotic use in livestock

Antibiotics in livestock serve four overlapping purposes: treating sick animals (therapeutic), treating a whole group once one animal is diagnosed (metaphylaxis), preventing disease in at-risk animals (prophylaxis), and, historically, promoting growth at low, sub-therapeutic doses mixed into feed. Worldwide, an estimated 73% of antimicrobials are consumed by farm animals, and global agricultural use was projected to rise 67% from 2010 to 2030, mostly in BRIC countries. The central concern is antimicrobial resistance (AMR), where bacteria withstand the drugs designed to kill them.

## How resistance spreads

Bacteria acquire resistance by mutating their own genes or, more importantly for farm-to-human spread, by acquiring foreign DNA through three processes: transformation (picking up naked DNA from the environment), transduction (DNA delivered by bacteriophages, viruses that infect bacteria), and conjugation (direct cell-to-cell DNA transfer, often via plasmids, small transferable DNA rings). Conjugative plasmids can carry multiple resistance genes at once, producing multi-drug resistant strains. Resistance predates human antibiotic use; resistant bacteria have been recovered from woolly mammoth remains, polar ice, and deep caves. Sub-therapeutic feeding pressures bacteria by killing susceptible cells while leaving naturally resistant ones to multiply, a selection effect that enriches resistant populations in the animal gut and surrounding environment.

## How resistance reaches humans

Three pathways have been identified: direct infection with resistant bacteria from an animal source, breaches in the species barrier where a livestock strain begins sustained human transmission, and transfer of resistance genes (not whole bacteria) from agricultural bacteria into human pathogens. Direct contact is the main route for livestock-associated methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* (LA-MRSA); livestock workers and veterinarians carry LA-MRSA at roughly 10 times the rate of unexposed people, and the bacteria have been detected in barn air and soil. Foodborne spread matters most for *Salmonella*, *Campylobacter*, *E. coli*, and *Listeria*, which arrive via meat, dairy, and poultry contaminated at slaughter, processing, or handling. Environmental spread is significant because roughly 70–90% of antibiotics given to livestock are excreted unmetabolised; residues and resistant bacteria enter soil and water through manure, runoff, and wastewater, and one field study found a 4-fold rise in antibiotic resistance genes in soil after manure application.

## Human and economic stakes

At least 700,000 people die each year from drug-resistant infections, and the UK Review on Antimicrobial Resistance projected 10 million annual deaths by 2050 without intervention. The U.S. Center for Infectious Disease Research and Policy estimates roughly $2.2 billion per year in U.S. resistance-related healthcare costs. A full FDA ban on livestock antibiotic use was estimated to raise consumer food costs by $1.2–2.5 billion per year, a tradeoff whose full balance is not yet quantified.

## Policy timeline

Sweden banned growth-promoter antibiotic use in 1986; the European Union followed with a region-wide ban effective 1 January 2006 under Regulation (EC) No 1831/2003. South Korea banned growth promoters in 2011. The U.S. FDA's Guidance for Industry #213, effective 1 January 2017, eliminated growth-promotion and feed-efficiency uses of medically important antibiotics, reclassified remaining uses as requiring veterinary oversight, and produced a 33% drop in domestic sales of medically important livestock antibiotics from 2016 to 2017. In 2017, the WHO recommended ending routine antibiotic use in healthy food animals. The EU's Veterinary Medicines Regulation (EU) 2019/6, in force from 28 January 2022, further restricts prophylactic and metaphylactic use. The WHO's 2019 priority list flags highest-risk drug classes for human medicine, including 3rd, 4th, and 5th generation cephalosporins, glycopeptides, macrolides, polymyxins (including colistin), and quinolones; the European Medicines Agency similarly classifies these as "Restrict" or "Avoid" in food animals. Surveillance gaps remain significant: no U.S. agency systematically collects species-specific antibiotic-use data, a 2022 study found roughly 15% of U.S. beef lots marketed as "raised without antibiotics" tested positive for residues, and a 2023 USDA study raised that figure to 20%.

## Alternatives and substitutes

Researchers are testing probiotics, prebiotics such as fructooligosaccharides and mannanoligosaccharides, bacteriophages, bacteriocins, antimicrobial peptides, competitive exclusion cultures, vaccines, and improved biosecurity and husbandry. The Scottish salmon sector's 1994 introduction of a furunculosis vaccine (*Aeromonas salmonicida*) sharply cut antibiotic treatments, showing that disease control without routine antibiotics is feasible where effective vaccines exist.
