# Anatomical terms of neuroanatomy

Neuroanatomy borrows terms from Latin and Greek, standardised in references such as *Terminologia Anatomica*, to describe the brain, brainstem, spinal cord, and nerves with minimal ambiguity. Most of the unusual vocabulary exists for one reason: the human central nervous system bends roughly 90° at the midbrain–diencephalic junction, so directions that work for a fish or lizard have to be redefined once you cross that bend.

## Position and planes

Descriptions assume the body is standing erect, facing the observer, arms at sides, palms forward. Structures are then located using general anatomical terms, or with stereotactic coordinates defined by planes and axes.

The body and brain are sliced by three planes:

| Plane | Orientation | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Axial (transverse, horizontal) | Parallel to the ground | Contains the lateral and medial axes |
| Coronal | Vertical, through both ears | Divides front from back |
| Sagittal | Vertical, between the nostrils and between the cerebral hemispheres | Divides left from right; the midline is the *median plane*; any parallel plane is *parasagittal* |

## General directional terms

These apply across the body:

- **Anterior / posterior**: front / back
- **Superior / inferior**: above / below
- **Medial / lateral**: closer to / farther from the midline
- **Proximal / distal**: closer to / farther from a reference point
- **Contralateral / ipsilateral**: on the opposite / same side of the sagittal plane

## Neuroanatomy-specific terms

Four terms, ordinary in animal anatomy, shift meaning in the human brain because the neural tube is bent at the midbrain–diencephalic junction:

- **Rostral** (Latin *rostrum*, "beak") and **caudal** (*cauda*, "tail"). Above the junction, rostral = anterior and caudal = posterior, as in a fish. Below the junction, rostral = superior and caudal = inferior, because the spinal cord runs downward.
- **Dorsal** (*dorsum*, "back") and **ventral** (*venter*, "belly"). Above the junction, dorsal = superior and ventral = inferior. Below the junction, dorsal = posterior and ventral = anterior.

The back of the head and the occipital lobes are *posterior* but not *caudal* to the forehead and frontal lobes, because the rostro-caudal axis bends rather than running straight back. In quadrupeds, the same brain is anterior to the spinal cord; in humans it is superior to it.

The three brain axes sit inside the three planes: the axial (superior–inferior) axis runs in the axial plane, the left–right lateral axis runs in the axial and coronal planes, and the median (anterior–posterior) axis runs in the sagittal plane.

## Nerves: direction and crossings

An **afferent** fibre originates at a given point (a *striatal afferent* leaves the striatum); an **efferent** fibre arrives at it (a *cortical efferent* terminates in the cortex). Efferent thus points opposite to the direction the fibre conducts signals.

Crossings of nerve fibres are named by where they occur and what they connect:

- **Chiasm** (from Greek *chi*): a crossing of *peripheral* nerve fibres. The optic chiasm, where the optic nerves partially swap sides, is the major human example.
- **Decussation** (from Latin *decussis*, "ten", written as a capital X): a crossing inside the *central* nervous system, where fibres cross the sagittal plane. Type 1 decussations cross within the same brain region or spinal segment as the cell body, as in Mauthner cells of fish and amphibians, and this pattern also occurs in invertebrates. Type 2 decussations cross in a different region, as in the pyramidal decussations, and occur only in vertebrates; this type is thought to result from an axial twist that causes each forebrain hemisphere to represent the contralateral side of the body.
- **Commissure**: a tract connecting the *same* brain region across the two hemispheres, such as the corpus callosum, anterior commissure, posterior commissure, hippocampal commissure, and habenular commissure. The anterior white commissure is the spinal equivalent. A commissure links matching regions; a decussation links different ones.
- **Ganglion**: a cluster of neuron cell bodies and synapses. Chiasm, decussation, and commissure crossings contain axons but not synapses or neuronal cell bodies.

## Surface features of the brain

The folded cortical surface has its own vocabulary:

- **Gyrus**: an outward fold, such as the precentral gyrus.
- **Sulcus**: an inward fold, with its bottom called the *sulcal fundus*. A small gyrus hidden deep in a sulcus is an *annectent gyrus*. The central sulcus separates major motor and sensory regions.
- **Fissure**: a deeper groove, either produced by *opercularisation* during development (the Sylvian fissure) or by the separation of the telencephalic vesicles (the longitudinal fissure between the hemispheres).

Imaging studies add their own shorthand, with MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging) and CT (Computed Tomography) the most common acronyms.
