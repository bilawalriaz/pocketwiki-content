# Plant physiology

Plant physiology is the subdiscipline of botany that studies how plants function: how they capture light and raw materials, build their tissues, move substances, time their development, and cope with stress. A practitioner is called a plant physiologist. The field spans molecular events inside a single cell to whole-plant behaviour across seasons, and overlaps with plant morphology, ecology, phytochemistry, cell biology, genetics, and molecular biology.

## Plant chemistry

Plants are built from the same elements as other life, mainly carbon, oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur. What sets them apart is the vast array of special compounds they synthesise. Pigments absorb specific wavelengths of light. Toxins and bitter or foul-smelling compounds deter herbivores and competing plants. Antimicrobials fight disease. Attractants draw pollinators or seed dispersers. Humans exploit this chemistry in dyes, rubber, biofuel, and drugs such as salicylic acid (the source of aspirin), morphine, and digoxin.

Nutrients required in large amounts are macronutrients; those required only in trace amounts are micronutrients. Micronutrients are usually absorbed as ions dissolved in soil water, though carnivorous plants supplement them by digesting prey.

### Pigments

Pigments selectively absorb certain wavelengths and reflect others. The reflected light gives the pigment its colour; the absorbed light can drive chemical reactions. Chlorophyll is the primary photosynthetic pigment, a porphyrin that absorbs red and blue light and reflects green, which is why plants look green. Land plants and green algae carry chlorophyll a and b; kelps and diatoms carry chlorophyll c instead of b; red algae carry only chlorophyll a. Carotenoids are red, orange, or yellow accessory pigments (carotene in carrots, lycopene in tomatoes) that harvest wavelengths chlorophyll misses and act as antioxidants. Anthocyanins are water-soluble flavonoid pigments that appear red, purple, or blue depending on pH; in shade plants such as *Tradescantia zebrina* they reflect transmitted light back into chlorophyll-rich tissue to make better use of dim light. Betalains are red or yellow indole-derived pigments restricted to the order Caryophyllales (cacti, amaranth, beets); they never co-occur with anthocyanins, and their function in plants is uncertain.

## Cells and transport

Plant cells differ from animal cells in two consequential ways. A rigid cell wall holds cell shape, and chloroplasts containing chlorophyll let plants manufacture their own sugars from light and carbon dioxide. Roots absorb water and dissolved minerals; leaves capture light and fix carbon. Both organs stay alive only if these materials are exchanged, and plants achieve this through vascular tissue.

## Hormones and light signals

Plant hormones, also called phytohormones or plant growth regulators, are signal molecules that in tiny amounts influence growth and development. Unlike animals, plants have no dedicated hormone-producing organs, so hormone synthesis is distributed and hormones often act near where they are made. The five main groups are abscisic acid (ABA), auxins, ethylene, gibberellins, and cytokinins. They govern flowering, seed development, dormancy, germination, the direction of growth, leaf and stem formation, fruit ripening, leaf abscission, and programmed cell death. Ethylene, a gas, controls fruit ripening and seasonal leaf drop.

Plants also use light to shape their structure, a process called photomorphogenesis, through photoreceptor pigments. Phytochrome, the best-understood, absorbs red and far-red light and regulates flowering time, seed germination, seedling elongation, leaf size and number, chlorophyll synthesis, and circadian rhythms. Cryptochrome is the UV-A receptor; a UV-B receptor exists but its identity is uncertain; protochlorophyllide a is a chlorophyll precursor.

Flowering plants use phytochrome to sense day length. Long-day plants need a minimum length of daylight and flower in spring or summer. Short-day plants need a minimum length of continuous darkness per 24-hour cycle, so a flash of light during the night prevents flowering. Day-neutral plants ignore day length, and some rely on temperature cues (vernalization). Greenhouse growers manipulate day length to force crops such as the poinsettia (*Euphorbia pulcherrima*) to bloom out of season.

## Environmental physiology

Environmental physiology covers responses to physical factors such as radiation, temperature, wind, fire, water, gas exchange, and nutrient cycles, and to biological factors such as competition, herbivory, disease, mutualism, and pollination. Although plants perceive mechanical and chemical stimuli, they lack nerves, a brain, and pain receptors, so they do not feel pain in the animal sense.

Tropisms are directional growth responses caused by unequal cell elongation under hormonal control, such as phototropism (bending toward light) and geotropism (roots growing downward). Nastic movements are non-directional responses to stimuli such as touch, temperature, or humidity, and can be rapid because they rely on changes in cell turgor pressure. The Venus flytrap snap is a thigmonastic response: trigger hairs on a modified leaf fire when touched, the leaf closes by turgor change, and the plant slowly resets through new growth before it can fire again.

## Plant disease

Plants face viruses, bacteria, fungi, insects, and roundworms. Because they cannot flee, they rely on chemical defences and abscission, shedding infected leaves or flowers to isolate pathogens. Most plant pathogens spread by spores or animal vectors rather than casual contact. The first known fungicide, Bordeaux mixture, combines copper sulfate and lime and was developed in the nineteenth century to save the French wine industry from downy mildew.

## Brief history

Jan Baptist van Helmont's 1648 willow experiment is the first quantitative experiment in plant physiology: a willow grew in 200 pounds of oven-dry soil for five years and the soil lost only two ounces, leading him to conclude that plant mass comes from water, not soil. Stephen Hales is called the father of plant physiology for his 1727 *Vegetable Staticks*, and Julius von Sachs later unified the discipline through his *Lehrbuch der Botanik*. Nineteenth-century researchers established that plants absorb mineral nutrients as inorganic ions in water, the basis of hydroponics.
