# Species

A species is a group of organisms treated as a distinct kind in biological classification. The traditional biological species concept, formalised by Ernst Mayr in 1942, defines a species as a population that can interbreed in nature and produce fertile offspring while remaining reproductively isolated from other such groups. This definition dominates textbooks but fails for asexual organisms, fossils, and hybrids, and biologists have proposed roughly two dozen species concepts to handle these cases. The unresolved question of which to use is called the "species problem."

# Why reproductive isolation fails as a universal test

It cannot be applied to asexual organisms such as bacteria or parthenogenetic animals, nor to fossils, which are extinct. Fertile hybrids let genes flow between otherwise separate groups, and ring species, in which neighbouring populations interbreed but the distant ends of the ring cannot, blur reproductive boundaries. Bacteria further complicate the picture through horizontal gene transfer, in which genes move sideways between unrelated lineages rather than passing only from parent to offspring, undermining the idea of a closed gene pool. Discordance between molecular and morphological evidence produces two recurring patterns: cryptic species, where one morphology hides several genetic lineages, and phenotypic plasticity, where one lineage produces several forms.

# Major species concepts

Different research traditions emphasise different evidence.

- Morphospecies, sometimes called typological species, are defined by physical appearance, the approach Linnaeus used and that field guides and iNaturalist still rely on. A four-winged mutant of a normally two-winged *Drosophila* is not a new species, showing that phenotypic variation alone does not mark species boundaries.
- Phenetic species, introduced by Sokal, Crovello, and Sneath in the 1970s, use numerical similarity measured across many traits.
- Cladistic species are the smallest population groupings diagnosable by unique character states. This approach handles both sexual and asexual organisms but encourages splitting, and critics argue it inflates species counts and dilutes conservation resources.
- Evolutionary species, proposed by George Gaylord Simpson in 1951, are lineages that keep their identity through time and follow independent evolutionary fates.
- Ecological species are clusters of organisms adapted to discrete niches.
- Genetic species are defined by measurable genetic isolation rather than breeding behaviour.

# Molecular and microbial species

For microorganisms, morphology and breeding tests are useless, so DNA-based methods dominate. Eukaryotes are commonly identified with a stretch of mitochondrial DNA encoding cytochrome c oxidase, while bacteria are identified with 16S rRNA, a small ribosomal gene that evolves slowly enough to compare closely related lineages. A similarity threshold of 98.7% in 16S rRNA, adopted in 2006, and a later method, whole-genome average nucleotide identity (ANI), were used to categorise all sequenced bacteria and archaea by 2020. Whole-genome ANI shows a gap between 85% and 95% identity that appears to mark a prokaryotic species boundary.

# How species change

Speciation most often begins with geographic separation, a process called allopatric speciation. The Bateson-Dobzhansky-Muller model explains how populations that diverge in isolation accumulate incompatible gene combinations, so when they meet again their hybrids are inviable or sterile. Phyletic speciation describes gradual transformation of a whole lineage without branching. Over geological time, more than 99% of all species that have ever existed have gone extinct, in five major mass extinction events among many smaller ones.

# Naming and classification

Scientific names follow binomial nomenclature, two words: the genus and the specific epithet. A type specimen anchors each name in a permanent collection. The abbreviations *sp.* (singular), *spp.* (plural), *cf.* (confirmation needed), *nr.* (near), and *aff.* (affine) qualify uncertain identifications. When more than one name has been published for the same species, the older valid name has priority and the others become synonyms, a problem called synonymy. Taxonomists fall into two camps: lumpers, who use broad species concepts, and splitters, who use narrow ones, with the qualifiers *sensu stricto* (narrow sense) and *sensu lato* (broad sense) marking the choice.

# Why the matter stays open

Darwin called the line between species and varieties "vague and arbitrary." A 2011 estimate put the number of eukaryotic species at more than eight million, but only about 14% had been formally described, and phylogenetic species concepts tend to recognise roughly 48% more species than older approaches, a divergence called taxonomic inflation. Conservation law can clash with biology when protected species hybridise with unprotected relatives, as the northern spotted owl does, and proposed alternatives such as phylogenetic diversity try to count evolutionary distinctness rather than raw species numbers. The so-called cynical species concept, that a species is whatever a qualified community of biologists agrees to treat as one, captures the practical reality that classification must keep working while a universal definition remains out of reach.
