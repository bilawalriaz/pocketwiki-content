# Merovingian dynasty

The Merovingians were the ruling family of the Franks from the mid-5th century to 751, the first major Germanic dynasty to rule a unified kingdom in Western Europe after the fall of Rome. Their realm covered most of Gaul and parts of Germania, laying the political and territorial foundations for France and Germany.

## Origins and identity

The dynasty takes its name from Merovech, a Frankish king and father of Childeric I. Unlike many Germanic royal lines, the Merovingians did not claim divine descent. They were identified by long hair: a Merovingian whose hair was cut lost the right to rule, so rivals could be removed from succession by being tonsured and confined to a monastery.

## Clovis I and unification

Clovis I united the Franks, defeated the last Roman authority in Gaul (Syagrius, in 486), and converted to Nicene Christianity around 496, the form of Christianity defined by adherence to the Nicene Creed and distinct from the Arian Christianity of the Visigoths. His conversion brought ecclesiastical support. Clovis defeated the Visigoths at Vouillé (507) and absorbed Burgundy by 534, leaving the Merovingians in control of most of Gaul. He died in 511, and his kingdom was divided among his four sons.

## Division and reunification

The Frankish realm was treated as a single patrimony divisible among heirs, so it was repeatedly split and recombined. The main sub-kingdoms were Austrasia (northeast, Frankish heartland), Neustria (northwest), Burgundy, and Aquitaine. Periods of reunification occurred, most notably under Clotaire II in 613, who reunited the whole realm.

## Government and law

The Merovingian king redistributed conquered land and wealth among his followers, and the royal domain (the fisc) was expected to support him. Counts (*comites*) were appointed for local defense and administration. Law was personal rather than territorial, applied according to a person's ethnic origin: Salian Franks lived under the *Lex Salica* (Salic Law), codified c. 511, while Gallo-Romans kept Roman law. Legal officials called *rachimburgs* memorized precedents, since making new law was not part of the tradition.

## Weakening royal power

From the 7th century, royal authority declined as the aristocracy grew stronger. The mayor of the palace, the king's chief official, increasingly held real power. Late Merovingian kings became ceremonial, later mocked as *rois fainéants* ("do-nothing kings"). In 687, Pepin of Herstal, Mayor of Austrasia, won the Battle of Tertry and became the de facto ruler. His son Charles Martel continued the pattern, ruling without a king in his final years and defeating the Moors at Tours in 732. In 751, his son Pepin the Short deposed the last Merovingian king, Childeric III, and founded the Carolingian dynasty.

## Religion and culture

Christianity, adopted through contact with Gallo-Roman society, was used pragmatically: monasteries and bishoprics were granted to supporters, and royal family members were appointed as abbots. The period produced hagiographies (saints' lives) that shaped sanctity and popular devotion. The Frankish language gave way to Vulgar Latin across most regions by the 7th century, though some Germanic probably survived as a second language among officials into the 9th century.

## Sources and debates

The main contemporary accounts are Gregory of Tours' *Decem Libri Historiarum* (to 594) and the *Chronicle of Fredegar* (584–641, later extended to 768), with the *Liber Historiae Francorum* as a third narrative. Childeric I's tomb, opened in 1653, provides key archaeological evidence of Merovingian material culture. A legendary origin linking Merovech to a sea-beast (quinotaur) survives, but is now usually read as a folk etymology for the name ("sea-bull") rather than a real claim of supernatural descent. Calling the late kings *rois fainéants* is also an oversimplification: only Childeric II and the last two kings were truly inactive.
