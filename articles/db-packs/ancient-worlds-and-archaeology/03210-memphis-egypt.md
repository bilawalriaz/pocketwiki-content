# Memphis, Egypt

Memphis was the capital of ancient Egypt from the 31st century BC through the Old Kingdom, founded according to tradition by King Menes, who united Upper and Lower Egypt. For more than a millennium it served as the political, religious, and commercial centre of the Nile valley, anchoring unification at the southern tip of the Nile Delta. Its state religion centred on Ptah, the creator god, and its Egyptian name — Hut-ka-Ptah, "Enclosure of the ka of Ptah" (the ka being the vital spirit of a person or deity) — is the most likely source of the word "Egypt."

## Location and Names

Memphis sat at the apex of the Nile Delta, where the river fans toward the Mediterranean. This placed it at the natural junction between Upper Egypt (the southern valley) and Lower Egypt (the delta), giving it command over north–south trade. The modern village of Mit Rahina, about 20 km south of Cairo on the west bank of the Nile, lies within the ancient city's administrative borders.

The city carried three names that mark its shifting identity:

- **Inebu-hedj** ("the white walls") — its original name, probably referring to the whitewashed enclosure around the royal compound.
- **Ankh-Tawy** ("Life of the Two Lands") — used in the Middle Kingdom, advertising Memphis as the binding point of a unified Egypt.
- **Men-nefer** ("enduring and beautiful") — adopted at the start of the New Kingdom around 1550 BC. This name was hellenised into "Memphis."

## Rise and Golden Age

Memphis was the state capital from the First Dynasty and remained so for more than six consecutive dynasties, spanning the Early Dynastic Period and the Old Kingdom. It reached its peak under the Sixth Dynasty, when the cult of Ptah dominated Egyptian religious life. Its port, Peru-nefer, was lined with workshops and served as the hub through which goods from across the kingdom were distributed.

## Middle Kingdom to New Kingdom

When the political capital moved south to Thebes in the Middle Kingdom (c. 2055–1640 BCE), Memphis retained a role as a major commercial and artistic centre. The Hyksos besieged and captured the city around 1650 BC, but the New Kingdom brought a revival as a centre of royal education and a key port. Pharaohs including Amenhotep II, Thutmose IV, and especially Ramesses II built or expanded temples and palaces there. Ramesses II's contribution included a colossal 10-metre limestone statue, one of the site's most famous surviving monuments.

## Foreign Rule

Memphis was repeatedly contested in the Late Period. The Assyrian king Esarhaddon sacked it in 671 BC, and the Assyrians returned in 664 BC. The Persian king Cambyses II conquered it in 525 BC, after which it served as the administrative capital of the Persian satrapy of Egypt. Its religious prestige persisted: in 332 BC Alexander the Great was crowned pharaoh inside the Temple of Ptah. Two famous decrees were later issued at Memphis in 216 and 196 BC, the second of which is the Rosetta Stone inscription, written in three scripts and used in the 19th century to decipher hieroglyphs.

## Decline

Memphis's political primacy ended when Alexandria was founded and absorbed the delta's trade and administration. The Arab conquest in 641 AD and the founding of Fustat, the settlement that became Cairo, drew population and construction away from Memphis. Its stone was quarried for new buildings, and the city was largely abandoned.

Modern exploration began with Napoleon's 1798 expedition, followed by the systematic excavations of Flinders Petrie in the early 20th century, which uncovered much of the Temple of Ptah. In 1979 Memphis and its surrounding necropolis — including the Saqqara pyramids and the Serapeum, the burial complex for the sacred Apis bulls — were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and the ruins now function as an open-air museum.

Scholarly debates remain open: the identity of Menes is contested (some equate him with the historical king Narmer, others treat him as mythical), and the original layout of the Temple of Ptah is unknown, with only its perimeter gates and some structures excavated. The locations of the Temple of Apis and the larger Temple of Hathor within Memphis have not been definitively found.
