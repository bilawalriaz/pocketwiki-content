# Oyo Empire

The Oyo Empire was the largest and most politically important Yoruba state in West Africa, dominating present-day western Nigeria and Benin from the late 16th to the early 18th century. Its power rested on a professional cavalry rare among Yoruba peoples, agricultural wealth, control of trans-Saharan trade, and a political system designed to prevent any single ruler from monopolising authority.

Origins and rupture. Oral tradition credits the founding to Oranmiyan, a prince from Ile-Ife, who established the first capital at Ajaka. Early rulers included Ajaka, deposed for lacking military virtue, and his brother Shango, later worshipped as the god of thunder. By the late 14th century Oyo had grown into a formidable inland power. Around 1535 a Nupe invasion sacked the capital and drove the dynasty into roughly 80 years of exile in Borgu, a break that reshaped the state.

Rebuilding and zenith. On return in the early 17th century the Oyo rebuilt with a more centralised, militarised government, adopting cavalry and armour from their former Nupe enemies. A new capital, Oyo-Igboho, was constructed, and the original capital Oyo-Ile was repopulated. Leaders such as Eguguojo and Orompoto conquered much of Yorubaland. By 1680 the empire covered more than 150,000 square kilometres; from 1682 Oyo began raiding southward and eventually reached the coast. The Fon Kingdom of Dahomey was invaded in 1728 and finally subjugated as a tributary in 1748.

Political structure. Power was deliberately fragmented. The Alaafin ("owner of the palace") was supreme ruler but was checked by the Oyo Mesi, a seven-member council led by the Bashorun, or prime minister. The Oyo Mesi chose the Alaafin from Oranmiyan's descendants, so kingship was not purely hereditary. If the council withdrew its support, the Bashorun could compel the Alaafin to commit ritual suicide. A second check came from the Ogboni, a secret society of elders that embodied religious authority and popular opinion. A separate tradition required the crown prince, the Aremo, to take his own life on his father's death, a safeguard against succession disputes. Periodically the Bere festival publicly celebrated a successful reign and the peace it had secured.

Military. The empire's main weapon was its cavalry, commanded by the Aare-Ona-Kakanfo, a general who had to reside on a frontier. The army could field over 100,000 horsemen, supported by skilled archers. Forces combined metropolitan troops, tributary levies, and the Eso, a semi-standing body of 70 specialist cavalry war chiefs nominated by the Oyo Mesi. Victory was so culturally prized that a defeated general was often expected to commit suicide.

Decline and fall. Oyo's collapse was internal as much as external. From 1754 Prime Minister Gaha's intrigues forced several Alaafins to suicide, a corruption of the system meant to restrain them. Failed campaigns under Alaafin Abiodun and his murder by his son further eroded central authority. In 1817 Ilorin, under Afonja and aided by Fulani Muslims, seceded. Dahomey defeated Oyo in 1823, ending its regional hegemony, and Oyo-Ile itself was destroyed by the Fulani of Ilorin in 1835. Two long-running pressures compounded these blows: the decline of the Atlantic slave trade, which cut tribute and trade revenue, and the Fulani Jihad that established the Sokoto Caliphate to the north. By the mid-19th century Oyo had lost its prominence and its people became targets of slave raids by neighbouring states.

Open questions. Scholars still debate how much of Oyo's cavalry tradition was inherited from neighbours such as Nupe, Borgu, or Hausa states, and how much of its later political sophistication predated the 1535 exile rather than being forged in response to it.
