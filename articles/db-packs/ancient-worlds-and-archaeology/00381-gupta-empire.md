# Gupta Empire

The Gupta Empire was a classical Indian state that ruled much of northern India from roughly the mid-3rd to the mid-6th century CE. It is often called a "Golden Age" for its advances in art, science, and literature, though historians dispute that label. The dynasty began in the Magadha region, expanded dramatically under three powerful rulers, and then fractured under the weight of foreign invasion and internal revolt.

## Founding and early rulers

The earliest known king, Sri Gupta, ruled as an independent monarch in the late 3rd century, according to a 7th-century Chinese account and recent coin finds. Scholars still debate his social class (*varna*); both Vaishya (merchant) and Brahmin (priestly) origins have been proposed, and the evidence is inconclusive. The dynasty's first real leap in power came when Chandragupta I married a Licchavi princess around 319 CE, gaining the political and military backing of that established clan. He then adopted the title *Maharajadhiraja* ("King of Great Kings"), a deliberate claim to paramount sovereignty beyond that of a regional chief.

## Imperial expansion and zenith

Chandragupta I's son Samudragupta (c. 335–375 CE) conducted extensive military campaigns across northern and southern India, as recorded in the Allahabad Pillar inscription, and performed the *Ashvamedha*, a Vedic horse sacrifice that publicly asserted imperial sovereignty. He also established Gupta hegemony over a ring of tributary states rather than ruling them directly.

The empire reached its peak under Samudragupta's son Chandragupta II (375–415 CE), called "Vikramaditya." His decisive victory over the Western Kshatrapas, Indo-Scythian governors whom the Guptas displaced, extended control from coast to coast. His court patronized scholars and poets, most famously the playwright and poet Kalidasa, and his reign drew the Chinese Buddhist pilgrim Faxian, who visited between 399 and 411 CE and documented a prosperous, lightly administered realm. This period of relative peace and stability is sometimes called the *Pax Gupta*, and it was the foundation on which the cultural achievements rested. Kumaragupta I (415–455 CE) extended this legacy by founding Nalanda University, which would become one of the most important Buddhist centres of learning, but his reign also saw the first serious internal rebellions by the Pushyamitras.

## Military and administration

Gupta armies combined heavy cavalry archers and siege engines, relatively new elements in the subcontinent, with traditional Indian war elephants and infantry. The use of horse archers is even visible on Gupta coinage. Administratively, the empire was divided into provinces called *Bhukti*, which were subdivided into districts called *Vishaya*, each administered by an official (*Vishayapati*) working with local councils. Society still operated within the four-fold *varna* system of Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, and Shudra, though some occupational mobility existed. Gupta rulers were traditionally Hindu and patrons of Brahmanism, but they tolerated Buddhism and Jainism, and later kings such as Narasimhagupta Baladitya became strong Buddhist supporters.

## Decline and fragmentation

The collapse was not a single event. Skandagupta (c. 455–467 CE) repelled the first wave of Central Asian Huna nomads, the Kidarites, but the wars drained the treasury. In the late 490s and around 500 CE, the Alchon Huns under Toramana and his son Mihirakula invaded and overran much of the northwest, disrupting the trade networks and urban culture on which Gupta prosperity depended. Internal revolt by feudatories, the cost of these foreign wars, and possibly a catastrophic 6th-century flood all accelerated the break-up. By 528 CE, the ruler Yashodharman of Malwa had defeated the Huns and ended their direct rule in central India, but the Gupta imperium itself was already gone. Vishnugupta (540–550 CE) was the last recognized Gupta emperor. Northern India then fragmented into regional kingdoms: the Maukharis and Pushyabhutis in the Gangetic plain, and the Gurjara-Pratiharas in the west.

The *Huna* invasions delivered the final blow, but the underlying cause was structural: a loosely bound empire of tributary states and locally governed districts had always depended on strong kings to hold it together, and once Skandagupta's treasury was drained by foreign war and the line of capable rulers thinned, the centre could no longer project the *Pax Gupta* that had made the Golden Age possible.
