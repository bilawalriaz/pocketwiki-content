# Maurya Empire

The Maurya Empire (c. 320–185 BCE) was the first state to unite most of the Indian subcontinent under centralised rule. Founded from the Magadha heartland, it combined a large territorial reach with a royal administration that taxed, censused, and built infrastructure. Under Emperor Ashoka it also became the vehicle that carried Buddhism from an Indian sect into a trans-regional religion.

## Founding and Reach

Chandragupta Maurya overthrew the Nanda dynasty in Magadha around 320 BCE. After consolidating the east, he fought the Seleucid general Seleucus I Nicator around 305–303 BCE, gaining Gandhara and Arachosia in the northwest, settling the conflict with a dynastic marriage and a transfer of 500 war elephants. His son Bindusara (c. 297–273 BCE) pushed control into the Deccan plateau. Direct administration was strongest in four anchor zones: Magadha, Taxila, Ujjain, and the lower Deccan; the empire's edges were held more loosely through allied commanders and trade ties.

## How the State Worked

The Mauryan realm was a centralised monarchy in which the emperor owned all land. A population of roughly 15–30 million was governed through provinces led by royal princes (*kumaras*) and a council of ministers. Cities such as Pataliputra ran complex municipal systems, and the state conducted detailed censuses. Two long-run factors amplified state capacity: the spread of Buddhism and Jainism, which gave merchants shared ethical norms and lowered transaction costs, and royal roads such as the Uttarapath, which moved armies and trade. Riverine and maritime routes supplied metals and luxury goods.

The *Arthashastra* is often treated as the manual of Mauryan statecraft and attributed to Chandragupta's adviser Chanakya. Scholars now date it to the early centuries CE, so it reflects later ideas about governance rather than a working Mauryan blueprint.

## Ashoka and Buddhism

Ashoka's reign (268–232 BCE) is the empire's high point. The conquest of Kalinga in 261 BCE killed large numbers of soldiers and civilians, an event that pushed Ashoka toward Buddhism and a renunciation of aggressive war. He issued rock and pillar edicts across the subcontinent promoting *dhamma*, a moral code built on non-violence, tolerance, and public welfare. He sent Buddhist missions to Sri Lanka, Central Asia, and the Hellenistic courts of Antiochus II Theos and Ptolemy II Philadelphus, turning a regional teaching into a world religion.

The strategic cost of Ashoka's pacifism remains debated. Some historians argue the renunciation of war hollowed out the army; others, including Romila Thapar, hold that its practical effect has been "grossly exaggerated" and that the empire's real fragility lay elsewhere.

## Decline

After Ashoka's death around 232 BCE, weaker successors and a probable partition bled the core. Provincial governors drifted toward autonomy, and the northwest faced external pressure from the Greco-Bactrians. The dynasty ended in 185 BCE when the last emperor, Brihadratha, was killed by his own general Pushyamitra Shunga during a military parade, opening a period of smaller successor states and leaving the northern frontier exposed to later invaders.
