# Mongol Empire

The Mongol Empire (1206–1368) was the largest contiguous land empire in history, built when a single leader unified the warring nomads of the Mongolian Plateau and used steppe cavalry to conquer most of Eurasia within a century. Successor khanates lasted far longer: the last Mongol state fell to the Qing in 1705.

## Unification and military revolution (1206–1227)

In 1206, at a *kurultai* (an assembly of Mongol chiefs that elects the Great Khan and decides major campaigns), Temüjin was proclaimed Genghis Khan and announced two institutions that outlasted him.

The **decimal military system** dissolved tribal identities by grouping all adult males into units of 10 (*arban*), 100 (*zuun*), 1,000 (*mingghan*), and 10,000 (*tumen*), commanded by loyal officers regardless of birth. Every man was a soldier, expected to maintain five or six steppe horses. The **Yassa** was a written legal code criminalising theft, adultery, and inter-clan fighting, granting religious freedom, exempting clergy and the poor from taxes, and mandating the Uyghur script for record-keeping. A 10,000-strong *keshig* (imperial guard) doubled as a military academy and the empire's administrative core.

The early army (50,000–75,000 men) offset the weaknesses of steppe cavalry by combining mobility with acquired siege technology. Composite bows had an effective range of about 325 m; lamellar armour protected elites. Tactics relied on feigned retreats, encirclement (*nerge*), concentrated arrow storms, and multi-column invasions coordinated by scouting screens. Muslim and Chinese engineers supplied trebuchets, gunpowder, naphtha, and early cannon, so the empire could take walled cities that pure nomads could not.

## Expansion and its western limit (1227–1260)

By Genghis's death in 1227, Mongol territory stretched from the Pacific to the Caspian Sea. Under his son **Ögedei** (r. 1229–1241), armies under Batu Khan and Subutai destroyed Kievan Rus' (1237–1240) and invaded Poland and Hungary in 1241. The advance into Central Europe ended when Ögedei died and the princes were recalled to a *kurultai*. The conventional explanation is political; modern scholars add wetter weather, European stone fortifications, and Batu's reluctance to leave his western base.

After a regency and the brief reign of Güyük (1246–1248), **Möngke** of the Toluid line took power in 1251 after a purge of rival branches. He assigned his brothers Kublai to China and Hulagu to the Middle East. Hulagu destroyed the Nizari Ismailis (Assassins) and sacked Baghdad in 1258, ending the Abbasid Caliphate. His westward advance was halted by the Egyptian Mamluks at Ain Jalut in 1260, the first major Mongol defeat, which set the effective western frontier. Möngke died campaigning against the Song in 1259, triggering a succession crisis.

## Civil war and the four khanates (1260–1294)

With Möngke dead, rival *kurultais* elected **Kublai Khan** in China and his brother **Ariq Böke** at Karakorum. In the **Toluid Civil War (1260–1264)**, Kublai blockaded Ariq Böke into famine before Ariq Böke surrendered at Shangdu in August 1264. The war permanently fractured the empire: while the brothers fought, the western princes asserted themselves. **Berke**, Khan of the Golden Horde and a Muslim convert, allied with the Mamluks against Hulagu's Ilkhanate after the sack of Baghdad, opening the **Berke–Hulagu war (1262)**. **Kaidu**, an Ögedeid prince in Central Asia, rebelled against Kublai and enthroned his own puppets among the Chagataids, warring for decades.

By 1294, four sovereign khanates had emerged: the **Yuan dynasty** in China, the **Golden Horde** on the Russian steppes, the **Chagatai Khanate** in Central Asia, and the **Ilkhanate** in Persia. In 1271 Kublai renamed his regime the Yuan and moved the capital to Khanbaliq (Beijing), sinicising administration. He completed the conquest of the Song in 1279, the first non-Chinese dynasty to rule all of China. His fleets failed against Japan (1274, 1281), Đại Việt (defeated at Bạch Đằng), and Java.

## Pax Mongolica and khanate evolution (1294–1368)

In 1304 Kublai's successor **Temür Khan** secured a treaty in which the three western khanates accepted Yuan suzerainty, though they continued to coin money, run foreign policy, and fight each other as effectively independent states. The pact enabled roughly a century of intensified Eurasian exchange, the *Pax Mongolica* (c. 1250–1350), built on the **Yam** postal relay: stations every 40 km with fresh horses let messengers cover 200 km a day. The state-merchant **ortoq** partnership financed long-distance trade; the Polo family and the monk Rabban Bar Sauma travelled under Mongol protection. In the Ilkhanate, **Ghazan**'s conversion to Islam in 1295 turned the state toward the Muslim world. The Chagatai Khanate later fragmented under Timur (Tamerlane), and the Golden Horde dissolved during its "Great Troubles" (1359–1381) into Kazan, Astrakhan, Crimea, and Sibir.

## Collapse (1335–1705)

The empire fell sequentially. The **Ilkhanate** disintegrated between 1335 and 1353 after the death of Abu Said, splitting into successor polities across Persia and Anatolia. The **Yuan** fell in 1368 to the Red Turban Rebellion and Ming forces, who captured Dadu (Beijing); the Mongol court retreated to the Mongolian Plateau as the **Northern Yuan**, surviving until Qing conquest in the 1630s. The **Black Death**, which reached Europe in 1347 (the catapulting of plague corpses at Caffa is a documented early instance of biological warfare), severed the trade routes that had bound the empire together. The **Great Stand on the Ugra River** in 1480 ended Golden Horde suzerainty over Moscow; the Horde's last remnant, the Great Horde, fell when the Crimean Khanate sacked Sarai in 1502. The Chagatai line survived longest in the Yarkent Khanate, which fell to the Dzungars in 1680 and to the Qing in 1705.

## Knowledge, culture, and demographic legacy

Genghis's decree of religious tolerance gave the empire wide cultural reach: Ögedei built temples for Buddhists, Muslims, Christians, and Taoists at Karakorum, while successor states localised, with the Yuan favouring Tibetan Buddhism and the western khanates adopting Islam. Khans patronised astronomy and mathematics: Möngke summoned **Nasir al-Din al-Tusi** to build the Maragheh Observatory (1259) from libraries salvaged in Baghdad, producing the *Ilkhanic Tables* and the Tusi-couple, a geometric device later used in Copernican heliocentric models. The Uyghur-derived vertical Mongol script is still used in Mongolia.

Demographic estimates are contested: between 30 and 80 million people are thought to have died from massacres, famine, and plague; China's recorded population fell from about 120 million to 60 million between 1200 and 1300, though some scholars attribute the drop to Yuan administrative failure rather than mass death; Iran's population may have fallen 75% and not recovered until the 20th century.
