# Cradle of civilization

A cradle of civilization is a region where a complex society first emerged on its own, marked by a state, cities, social hierarchy, and writing. Six primary cradles are recognised: Mesopotamia, Ancient Egypt, Ancient India, Ancient China, the Caral–Supe civilisation of Peru, and the Olmec civilisation of Mexico. They arose independently, then later influenced neighbours.

## How civilisation started

The trigger was the Neolithic Revolution, the shift from hunting and gathering to farming and permanent settlement. It began in the Levant around 12,000 BC, where the Natufian culture became sedentary before plants were even domesticated. Farming produced a food surplus, and the surplus paid for specialists: soldiers, scribes, priests, and kings. Cities, states, and writing followed in a rapid, mutually reinforcing package that culminated in the Bronze Age. Writing matters because it separates historic from prehistoric time. A proto-writing system, agreed symbols that record meaning without being a full script, usually appears just before true writing, such as the cuneiform wedge-marks on clay tablets that emerged in Mesopotamia around 3300 BC.

## Mesopotamia

The earliest cradle lies in the Fertile Crescent of West Asia, with Mesopotamia (modern Iraq) at its heart. The Tigris–Euphrates river system made intensive farming possible. Urbanisation began in the Ubaid period (c. 6500–3800 BC) and accelerated in the Uruk period (4000–3100 BC), when cities like Uruk and Ur housed tens of thousands. Sumerian city-states were probably theocratic, ruled by priest-kings. By the Early Dynastic Period (c. 2900 BC), secular kings called Lugals took over. The first verifiable empire was Eannatum of Lagash (c. 2500 BC), followed by Sargon the Great's Akkadian Empire (c. 2350 BC). The Third Dynasty of Ur later collapsed, and Semitic-speaking peoples reorganised the region into Assyria and Babylonia.

## Ancient Egypt

Civilisation in the Nile Valley was shaped by the desiccation of the Sahara, which pushed populations toward the river. Agriculture was established by 5500 BC, and the Naqada culture (c. 4400–3000 BC) showed rising social complexity. Egyptian civilisation coalesced around 3500 BC and was unified under the god-king Narmer by c. 3150 BC. The Old Kingdom (c. 2686–2181 BC) is known for pyramid building at Giza and a strong central administration. Its decline, likely a mix of drought and weakened royal power, opened the First Intermediate Period.

## Ancient India

The Neolithic site of Mehrgarh (7000 BC) shows early farming in South Asia. The Indus Valley Civilisation began c. 3300 BC and reached its Mature Harappan phase around 2600 BC, with large planned cities such as Harappa and Mohenjo-daro. These cities had grid streets, advanced sanitation, and standardised weights and measures, signs of a powerful central authority. The civilisation declined around 1800–1700 BC, probably because climate change dried up the Ghaggar–Hakra river system, and the population dispersed.

## Ancient China

Chinese civilisation grew from interactions among cultures in the Yellow River, Yangtze, and Liao regions. Early millet and rice farming dates to 7000–6500 BC. The Peiligang culture (7000–5000 BC) appears to have been egalitarian, while the later Yangshao (5000–3000 BC) and Longshan (3000–1900 BC) cultures show growing hierarchy. The Erlitou culture (1900–1500 BC) is the first state-level society in East Asia and may correspond to the semi-legendary Xia dynasty. The Shang dynasty (1600–1046 BC) is the first with firm archaeological and written evidence, the oracle bone script.

## Ancient Peru

The Caral–Supe (Norte Chico) civilisation emerged c. 3200 BC on the coast of Peru. It built large platform mounds and sunken plazas but left no ceramics and no figurative art. Its economy combined agriculture with marine resources, and cotton was a key trade good. Society was probably theocratic, with elites directing labour for monumental construction. The Caral–Supe used quipu, knotted strings that encoded numeric and possibly narrative information, to keep records. The civilisation declined c. 1800 BC as rival centres rose.

## Mesoamerica

The Olmec civilisation, often called the "mother culture" of Mesoamerica, took shape c. 1600–1500 BC in the Gulf Coast lowlands. Its first major centre, San Lorenzo Tenochtitlán, flourished until c. 900 BC and is known for colossal stone heads carved from single boulders. La Venta succeeded it until c. 400 BC. The Olmec developed early forms of writing and the Mesoamerican ballgame, ideas that fed directly into the later Maya and Aztec civilisations. Their decline is tied to environmental change in the lowlands.

## Open questions

The cradles share a core pattern: agriculture produces a surplus, the surplus supports non-farmers, and non-farmers build states, cities, and scripts. The multi-regional model is dominant, but the balance of independent invention versus cultural diffusion between early societies is still argued. The exact role of climate, the Sahara drying, monsoon shifts, and the drying of Andean rivers, in both the rise and fall of civilisations is still being measured. Whether the Olmec truly seeded later Mesoamerican cultures, whether Caral–Supe leaned more on the sea than the farm, and whether the Erlitou culture is the Xia dynasty, are open questions.
