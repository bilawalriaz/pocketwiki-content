# Almoravid dynasty

The Almoravids were a Berber Muslim empire that ruled the western Maghreb (North Africa) and al-Andalus (Muslim Iberia) from the mid-11th to mid-12th century. They emerged from Saharan nomadic tribes, united the region under a strict reading of Maliki Islam (the most widespread school of Islamic law in North Africa, known for relying heavily on the practices of the people of Medina), halted Christian advances in Iberia for a time, and linked African and Iberian cultures. Their empire ended when the rival Almohad movement captured their capital.

## Origins

The Almoravids came from the Lamtuna, Gudala, and Massufa, three Sanhaja Berber confederations (kinship-based tribal alliances) living in the Sahara. Their state began as a religious reform: the scholar Abdallah ibn Yasin preached strict Maliki observance and rejected tribal loyalties, winning converts among the nomads. In 1054–55 the Almoravids seized the trans-Saharan trade hubs of Sijilmasa and Awdaghust, taking control of the gold and salt routes that crossed the desert.

Ibn Yasin died in battle against the Barghawata in 1058–59, and the movement split. Abu Bakr ibn Umar held the southern Saharan lands; his cousin Yusuf ibn Tashfin led the northern Maghreb and built a new capital at Marrakesh around 1070. The name *Almoravid* comes from Arabic *al-Murābiṭūn*, meaning "those of the ribat," a fortified religious house where warriors guarded the faith.

## Expansion in North Africa

Yusuf ibn Tashfin extended Almoravid rule across Morocco and organized the western Maghreb into provinces. He acknowledged the Abbasid caliph in Baghdad as the symbolic head of Sunni Islam and took the title *Amir al-Muslimīn* ("Prince of the Muslims"), framing his rule as a defense of orthodox Islam rather than a Berber dynasty.

## Intervention in al-Andalus

To the north, Muslim Iberia had broken into small Taifa kingdoms after the fall of the Caliphate of Córdoba in 1031. Several Taifa rulers paid *paria* (tribute) to Christian kingdoms in exchange for peace, a practice that angered both Muslims and strict jurists. When Alfonso VI of Castile threatened Toledo, the Taifas invited Yusuf ibn Tashfin to intervene.

At the Battle of Sagrajas in 1086, Yusuf ibn Tashfin's forces defeated Alfonso VI and checked the Christian advance. Between 1090 and 1094 the Almoravids conquered the Taifas, including Seville and Valencia, and governed al-Andalus directly. For the first time, the Maghreb and al-Andalus shared a single political ruler.

## Decline and Fall

The loss of Zaragoza to Aragonese King Alfonso I in 1118 marked a turning point. Almoravid legitimacy weakened as Christian forces pressed south and the dynasty reintroduced non-canonical taxes (levies not sanctioned by Maliki law) to fund defense, a reversal that undermined their claim to religious rigor.

The fatal challenge came from the Almohads, a reform movement founded in the 1120s by Ibn Tumart, who accused the Almoravids of laxity. Led after his death by Abd al-Mu'min, the Almohads conquered Almoravid territory piece by piece. The last Almoravid ruler, Ishaq ibn Ali, died when Marrakesh fell in 1147.

## Culture and Architecture

Almoravid patronage produced a shared Andalusi-Maghribi style that blended North African and Iberian traditions. Their buildings introduced or refined features such as the *muqarnas* (honeycomb vaulting, a decorative ceiling of stacked niches), the ribbed dome seen at the Great Mosque of Tlemcen, and the compact Almoravid Qubba in Marrakesh. The Maghrebi cursive script, still used across northwest Africa, was standardized in this period. The dynasty's early austerity gradually gave way to the production of fine textiles, ceramics, and illuminated manuscripts.

## Open Questions

The scale of Almoravid involvement in the Ghana Empire around 1076–77 is disputed, with some scholars reading it as a military conquest and others as a slower religious and political diffusion. The origin of the name "Almoravid" itself has several competing explanations in the sources.
