# Ghana Empire

The Ghana Empire (c. 300–1200 CE) was a West African state in the western Sahel whose power rested on taxing trans-Saharan trade in gold and salt. It grew rich by sitting between the salt deposits of the Sahara and the goldfields of the south, levying duties on every caravan that crossed its territory. Arab writers called it the "Land of Gold."

Ghana was a confederacy: a central core ruling tributary kingdoms, not a nation in the modern sense. Its people were predominantly Soninke, part of the broader Mande cultural world. The empire's own name was Wagadu; "Ghana" was the title of its ruler, meaning "war chief." The king was called the *Kaya Maghan*, "king of gold." Each tributary unit, a *kafu*, owed allegiance and tribute to the centre. Kingship passed matrilineally, to the sister's son rather than the ruler's own child, while daily society was organised around patrilineal clans, so political succession and everyday kinship ran on different tracks.

Archaeology at Dhar Tichitt shows proto-Mande agro-pastoralist chiefdoms in the region from around 300 BCE. Two developments around 300 CE transformed this landscape. A wetter period in the Sahel expanded pasture and settlement, and the camel made it practical to move bulky goods across the desert. With long-distance caravans now viable, the people of Wagadu positioned themselves at the bottleneck and began taxing the trade. Oral traditions attribute the founding to a hero named Dinga who defeated a serpent called Bida; scholars prefer the environmental and economic explanation because it is supported by material evidence.

By the eighth century Arab writers described Ghana as fabulously wealthy. The capital, almost certainly Koumbi Saleh, was a twin city: one part for the royal court, the other for Muslim merchants who ran the long-distance trade. The king did not own the gold mines, which lay further south, but he controlled who could move the metal north. He also claimed all gold nuggets as royal property, while gold dust circulated as currency, a rule that kept the treasury full. The first datable reference to the dynasty comes from al-Khwārizmī in 830. In 990 Ghana captured the trading city of Awdaghust.

In the eleventh century the Almoravids, a Berber-Muslim reform movement, swept down from the north. The old story says they conquered Ghana outright around 1076, but most modern scholars doubt a full invasion. They captured Awdaghust in 1054, disrupted trade, and pressured Ghana's rulers to convert to Islam. Al-Bakri, writing under Tunka Manin (1063–1076), gives the most detailed picture of the empire at this moment. Conversion may have caused some internal displacement, but the state survived. By 1154 al-Idrisi described a powerful, fully Muslim Ghana with an Islamic judicial system, a second peak rather than a collapse.

Ghana's fall was gradual. The Sahel was drying, which shifted trade routes south and west and strengthened the peoples Ghana had once ruled. In 1203 the Sosso, a former vassal, conquered the empire. In 1235 Sundiata Keita of Mali defeated the Sosso, and what remained of Ghana became a Mali vassal. The capital was abandoned in the fifteenth century.

The economy was fiscal: a tax state built on caravan duties, salt levies, and royal gold, not on productive agriculture or mine ownership. Muslim scribes and officials handled the commercial and legal paperwork of a largely non-Muslim court, and by the eleventh century Ghana hosted recognised centres of learning. The name "Ghana" later lent itself to the modern state, and the trade-and-tribute model Ghana pioneered was inherited, on a larger scale, by Mali.
