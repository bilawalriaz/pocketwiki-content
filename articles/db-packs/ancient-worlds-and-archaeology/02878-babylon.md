# Babylon

Babylon was a city in southern Mesopotamia (modern Iraq) whose name survives in the phrase "gate of the god(s)," though the original meaning is unknown. For roughly 1,500 years it served as the political and cultural capital of southern Mesopotamia, rising twice to imperial power before fading into a small village, a long decline now complicated by modern military damage.

## Why Babylon mattered

Babylon sat on the Euphrates River, which gave it water, fertile land, and trade access. A cuneiform tablet is a small clay slab inscribed with wedge-shaped marks, the writing system used across Mesopotamia. Over centuries the city built, lost, and rebuilt its dominance, leaving behind a written record dense enough to reconstruct its history in unusual detail.

## The two empires

Babylon reached its first peak under the **Old Babylonian Empire** (c. 1894–1595 BC), founded by the Amorite king **Hammurabi** (r. 1792–1750 BC). Amorites were a Semitic-speaking people who had migrated into Mesopotamia from the west. Hammurabi united southern Mesopotamia under one rule, after which the empire gradually fragmented.

Babylon reached its second, more famous peak under the **Neo-Babylonian Empire** (626–539 BC), also called the Chaldean Empire. Its great builder was **Nebuchadnezzar II** (r. 605–562 BC), who reconstructed the city's temples, the **Ishtar Gate**, and possibly the **Hanging Gardens**, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, whose actual existence is debated. This is also the period of the Babylonian Exile of the Jews, recorded in the Hebrew Bible.

## The long stretch between

The two imperial peaks were separated by more than a millennium of foreign rule. In 1595 BC the Hittite Empire sacked the city, and the **Kassite dynasty**, who took over after the Hittite withdrawal, governed Babylon for over 400 years. Assyrian powers then dominated the city, and in 689 BC the Assyrian king **Sennacherib** destroyed it so thoroughly that the act became a lasting outrage. Babylon was rebuilt but remained a vassal until 626 BC, when a local revolt under **Nabopolassar** threw off Assyrian rule and set the stage for the Neo-Babylonian revival.

## Conquest and decline

In 539 BC Babylon fell to **Cyrus the Great** of Persia and became part of the **Achaemenid Empire**, the first Persian Empire, remaining a major administrative and scholarly center. In 331 BC **Alexander the Great** captured it, but the city declined after his death and was later absorbed by Parthian, Sassanid, and Arab-Muslim empires. By the 11th century AD it was a small village called Babel, no longer a city of cuneiform tablets and ziggurats.

## The site and its preservation

The ruin covers about 1,000 hectares and is split by a branch of the Euphrates. The main mounds are **Kasr**, the location of the stepped temple-tower **Etemenanki** (which may have inspired the Tower of Babel story), and **Amran**, the site of the **Esagila** temple of the god **Marduk**. A ziggurat is a stepped temple-tower intended as a link between heaven and earth. Scientific excavation began with the German archaeologist **Robert Koldewey** (1899–1917).

In 2003 a US-led coalition built **Camp Alpha** on the ruins during the Iraq War, causing irreparable damage to archaeological layers. In 2019 the site was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, a status that now governs how further damage is prevented, even as the scars of the military base remain part of the site's recent history.
