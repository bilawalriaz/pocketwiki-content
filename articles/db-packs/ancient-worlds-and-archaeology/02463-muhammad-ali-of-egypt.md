# Muhammad Ali of Egypt

Muhammad Ali (1769–1849) was an Ottoman commander who arrived in Egypt in 1801 as part of a force sent to expel the French, and within four years had manoeuvred himself into the position of Wāli (governor). He spent the next four decades using that base to build an autonomous, hereditary state ruled by his family — a state powerful enough to defeat the Ottoman Empire itself, and structured enough that historians still call him the founder of modern Egypt.

## Origins and rise

Muhammad Ali was born in Kavala, in the Ottoman Empire; sources disagree on whether he was Albanian or Anatolian Turkish. He rose through the Ottoman military and joined the 1801 expedition. With the French gone, Egypt had competing power centres: the Ottomans, the Mamluks (a class of soldier-slaves who had dominated Egypt for centuries), and urban factions. Muhammad Ali built alliances with soldiers, notables, and parts of the Mamluk leadership, and in 1805 popular pressure secured his appointment as Wāli.

His decisive move against the Mamluks came in 1811 at the Cairo Citadel, where he invited their leaders to a celebration and had them massacred, then sent his army to destroy the remaining Mamluk forces. This cleared the only military-political rival inside Egypt and left him personally commanding the army — the foundation of his power.

## Building the state

Muhammad Ali's domestic programme aimed to make Egypt rich, self-sufficient, and loyal to his dynasty. The core economic change was abolishing the *iltizam*, a tax-farming system in which private individuals collected taxes for a share of revenue. He replaced it with direct state collection, creating a state monopoly on agricultural production. New cash crops, especially long-staple cotton, made this monopoly highly profitable.

He used the revenue to build an industrial base focused on weapons manufacture, a navy, and textile factories, and staffed them with a *corvée* — forced, unpaid labour that was deeply unpopular. He created a professional bureaucracy, often staffed by his own sons, and sent students to Europe. He founded the Bulaq Press in 1819/21, the first indigenous printing press in the Arab world, and supported educational missions that fed the 19th-century Arabic literary renaissance, the *Nahda*.

The economic model was: state takes the land's revenue, builds industry and army, and uses the army to expand and defend the dynasty. The corvée and the army were the two pillars, and both rested on coercion.

## Wars and foreign policy

Muhammad Ali first waged war as an Ottoman subordinate. He sent expeditions to Arabia from 1811 to 1818, recapturing the Hejaz from the House of Saud, and conquered Sudan beginning in 1820 for its resources and recruits. In 1824 he intervened in the Greek revolt on the Sultan's side, but the combined Egyptian–Ottoman fleet was destroyed at Navarino in 1827.

Denied compensation, he turned on his own overlord. In the First Egyptian–Ottoman War (1831–1833), his son Ibrahim Pasha defeated the Ottoman army and advanced toward Constantinople. The Convention of Kutahya gave Muhammad Ali control of Crete and Syria. A second war in 1839 again ended in Ottoman defeat at Nezib, but Britain, Russia, Prussia, and Austria intervened to prevent Egypt from replacing the Ottoman Empire. The Convention of London (1840) forced Muhammad Ali to withdraw from almost all his conquests. In exchange, his dynasty received something far more durable: recognition of hereditary rule over Egypt and Sudan, which his successors held until 1952.

## Social reforms and their limits

His social reforms were real but instrumental. In 1829 he passed the first modern penal legislation in Egypt and created a professional police force. In 1832 he allowed the French doctor Antoine Clot to open a School of Medicine for women, training *hakimas* (female doctors) to treat women and children — pioneering in the region. The driving purpose was military: a conscript army needs healthy soldiers, and a healthy soldier population needs medical care that male doctors could not easily provide to women. Education, medicine, and law were shaped by the same logic as the cotton monopoly and the corvée — they existed to strengthen the state.

In 1848 senility forced his retirement; his son Ibrahim briefly succeeded him, and Muhammad Ali died in Alexandria in 1849. The dynasty he founded outlived him by a century, and the centralised, export-oriented, army-first Egypt he assembled remained the basic template of the country for the rest of the 19th century.
