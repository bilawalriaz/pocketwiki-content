# Kanem–Bornu Empire

The Kanem–Bornu Empire was a long-lived African state centred on Lake Chad, conventionally dated from about 700 CE to 1902, whose control of trans-Saharan trade made it a dominant power in the central Sahel for over a millennium. Its history is a story of geographic relocation, religious conversion, and repeated recovery from political and environmental shocks.

## Geographic and economic foundation

The state originated between Lake Chad and the Bahr el Ghazal, in the region called Kanem, with the early capital at Njimi. Its wealth came from taxing caravans and exporting slaves along trans-Saharan routes. The camel had reached the region by the 5th century CE, enabling the long-distance trade that sustained the ruling class and the stratified societies built on it.

## Conversion and early imperial peak

Islam arrived from the north, carried by traders and possibly the Toubou, a Saharan people of the central Sahara. The first Muslim ruler was Mai Hu in the 11th century, just before the Sayfawa dynasty replaced the earlier Duguwa. Conversion was strategic: because Islamic law forbids enslaving fellow Muslims, rulers kept much of the population non-Muslim so the slave export trade could continue. In the 13th century, Mai Dunama II Dibalemi presided over the empire's first great peak, commanding a large cavalry and projecting power as far as the Fezzan, a Saharan region in modern Libya, through control of trade in salt, ivory, and slaves.

## Shift to Bornu and second peak

Economic strain, conflict with the Bilala people, and a drying Lake Chad cost Kanem its heartland in the 14th century. The Sayfawa relocated the polity westward into Bornu. After roughly a century of disorder, Mai Ali I Gaji founded a new permanent capital, Ngazargamu, around 1472. A second peak came in the late 16th century under Mai Idris IV Alooma, who acquired Ottoman firearms, forged diplomatic alliances, and made Bornu a centre of Islamic learning after the fall of the Songhai Empire, the large West African state centred on the Niger bend.

## Decline, collapse, and partition

From the 17th century, the empire weakened under the combined pressure of Atlantic trade routes drawing commerce away from the Sahara, Tuareg attacks on northern oases, and drought linked to the Little Ice Age. In 1807–1808, the Fula jihads, the Fulbe holy wars spreading across West Africa in the early 1800s, shattered the western provinces and destroyed Ngazargamu. The scholar Muhammad al-Amin al-Kanemi organised resistance, took effective power, and founded a new dynasty and capital at Kukawa. Even so, the state was fatally weakened, and in 1893 the Sudanese warlord Rabih az-Zubayr conquered Bornu. French forces killed Rabih in 1900 and briefly restored the al-Kanemi dynasty, but in 1902 France, Britain, and Germany partitioned the empire's territories, ending Kanem–Bornu as an independent polity.

## Key terms

- **Mai**: royal title of the sovereign, equivalent to king or emperor.
- **Kanuri**: the dominant ethnic group and language; "Kanuri" derives from the empire's name and spread with it.
- **Chima system**: the feudal land arrangement, in which all land belonged to the sovereign and was granted as fiefs to nobles and officials.
- **Girgam**: the royal chronicle, preserved orally and later written down, the main source for the empire's political history.

## Open questions

Scholars debate the founding date, with estimates ranging from about 600 to 900 CE, and have not located the original capital Njimi despite archaeological searches. The label "Kanem–Bornu Empire" is anachronistic, since the state rarely controlled both regions at once and was not called that in its own time; historian Rémi Dewière has proposed renaming it a "sultanate" to move away from colonial-era categories.
