# Achaemenid Empire

The Achaemenid Empire (550–330 BC) was the first Iranian empire and, at roughly 5.5 million km², the largest state of its time. It stretched from the Balkans and Cyrenaica to the Indus Valley, ruled from the Iranian plateau by a single royal family founded by Cyrus the Great. Its central achievement was governing this enormous, multi-ethnic territory through a small set of standardised institutions, the same template later inherited by Alexander and the Greco-Roman world.

## How one man built it in twenty years

Cyrus the Great defeated three kingdoms in a single decade. In 550 BC he overthrew his Median overlord and took Ecbatana. In 546 BC he captured Sardis and absorbed Lydia. In October 539 BC he defeated Babylon at Opis, freed the Jews of the Babylonian Captivity, and authorised the rebuilding of Jerusalem and the Second Temple. His reputation for religious tolerance grew from this act and from his policy of preserving local gods and customs. His son Cambyses II added Egypt in 525 BC by winning the Battle of Pelusium; the Libyans and Greeks of Cyrenaica then submitted without a fight.

## A succession crisis that defined the dynasty

When Cambyses died in 522 BC, a magus (Median priest) named Gaumata, according to Darius's own Behistun Inscription, impersonated Cyrus's son Bardiya and ruled for seven months. Darius I, a collateral branch of the royal family, overthrew him and recorded the version of events we still have. Greek sources give different names (Smerdis, Sphendadates), and the historian Briant argues Bardiya was probably the real claimant, but nothing is certain. What matters is that the dynasty survived and used the inscription to legitimise a new line.

Darius then pushed the empire northwest, subjugating Thrace and Macedonian tribute c. 512–511 BC, the first imperial power to absorb the southern Balkans.

## The Greco-Persian Wars: where the empire hit its ceiling

Two Persian invasions of Greece are the empire's most famous campaigns, and both failed. The first began as the Ionian Revolt (499–493 BC) of Greek cities under Persian rule, ended with Darius's punitive expedition, and finished in the Persian defeat at Marathon (490 BC). The second, under Xerxes I in 480–479 BC, sacked Athens but ended in decisive Persian defeats at Salamis and Plataea. Persia then lost all its European territories and Macedonia regained its independence. The strategic lesson Persia absorbed was to stop invading Greece and fund her enemies instead. Under Artaxerxes I, Persia backed Sparta against Athens, contributing to Athens's defeat in 404 BC. Under Artaxerxes II, the Treaty of Antalcidas (387 BC) restored Ionian Greek cities to Persia.

## How the empire actually ran

Three inventions made the size manageable.

*Satrapies.* The empire was divided into 20–30 provinces, each governed by a satrap (governor) paired with a separate military commander and a state secretary. Each official reported to the satrap and to the king, so no single provincial figure could build an independent power base.

*Standardised taxation and law.* Darius I introduced the gold *daric* and silver *siglos*, a bimetallic coinage of fixed gold-to-silver weight, tailored taxes to each satrapy's resources (Babylon paid 1,000 talents of silver plus food; Egypt paid grain plus 700 talents), and codified the *dāta*, a uniform legal framework binding the king and all subjects.

*Communications.* The Royal Road ran 2,500 km from Susa to Sardis, served by mounted couriers called the *angarium* who could reach remote provinces in fifteen days. The relay stations of this postal system, the *Chapar Khaneh*, handled imperial orders and reports across the plateau.

The king ruled from four regional capitals (Pasargadae, Susa, Ecbatana, Babylon), each suited to a season. Persepolis, built by Darius, was the ceremonial centre.

## The army as a multi-ethnic force

The army was a coalition of subject peoples. Its core was the Immortals, 10,000 heavy infantry kept at exactly that number by continuous replacement, who fought with wicker shields and scale armour under their robes. Line infantry was provided by the Sparabara (shield-wall troops in quilted linen), the Takabara (light peltasts with crescent shields and axes), and later the Cardaces. Cavalry included horse, chariot, and camel contingents; war elephants, likely introduced after the Indus conquest, appeared at Gaugamela (331 BC). The navy was a Phoenician-, Egyptian-, and Greek-crewed force under Persian command, and Persia pioneered boat-bridges across the Bosphorus, Danube, and Hellespont.

## Religion, language, and the unusual status of women

Official policy was religious tolerance. Cyrus's freeing of the Jews and his funding of temple rebuilding set the template. Zoroastrianism, which introduced the concept of free will, became the de facto state religion from Artaxerxes I onward. Persian practice was distinctive: Herodotus reports no images, temples, or altars, though Artaxerxes II later erected cult statues.

Three languages served different functions: Old Persian for royal inscriptions, Elamite as the chancellery language until 458 BC, and Imperial Aramaic as the empire-wide written vehicle, a *lingua franca* rather than a formal official language. Greek saw limited bureaucratic use.

Women's lives varied by region. The Persepolis Fortification Tablets show royal women travelling, managing estates, and supervising workforces; some female labourers earned more than male workers, while craft-qualified workers of both sexes received equal pay. No woman ruled as monarch or regent, though Atossa and Parysatis were influential at court. There is no archaeological evidence for a secluded harem.

## The last Achaemenids and the fall

Successive Artaxerxes tried to hold the empire together by terror and by keeping foreign powers balanced. Artaxerxes III reconquered Egypt in 343 BC with about 330,000 men, looted its temples, and resettled Jews in Hyrcania or Babylon. He was poisoned by his chancellor Bagoas in 338 BC; Bagoas also disposed of the next king, Arses, before installing Darius III.

Darius III lost to Alexander the Great at Granicus (334 BC), Issus (333 BC), and Gaugamela (331 BC). Persepolis was burned in early 330 BC. Darius was murdered by his satrap Bessus, whom Alexander hunted down and executed. Alexander preserved Achaemenid administration, which is why he is often called "the last of the Achaemenids." After Alexander's death in 323 BC, the Iranian plateau passed to the Seleucids, then to the Parthians (2nd century BC), and finally to the Sasanians (Ardashir I crowned 224 AD), both dynasties later claiming Achaemenid descent.
