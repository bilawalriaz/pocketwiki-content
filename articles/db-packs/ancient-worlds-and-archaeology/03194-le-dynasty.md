# Lê dynasty

The Lê dynasty (1428–1789) governed Đại Việt (the historical name for Vietnam) for over 360 years, the longest stretch of any Vietnamese ruling house. Its story falls into two halves: a brief era of strong emperors who built a Confucian state, followed by a long stretch in which Lê kings were puppets while rival families fought the country's real wars.

## Founding and the Golden Age

The dynasty began with a war of liberation. Lê Lợi spent nine years (1418–1427) leading the Lam Sơn uprising against 20 years of Ming Chinese occupation, then took the throne in 1428 as Lê Thái Tổ. He introduced the Thuận Thiên legal code and land redistribution.

The dynasty's peak came under Lê Thánh Tông (r. 1460–1497), who reorganised the state on the Chinese Confucian model. His reforms included a civil service examination system that became the main path into government, a six-ministry administration, and the compilation of the *Đại Việt sử ký toàn thư*, the national history. His 1471 conquest of the Cham capital Vijaya opened the way for *nam tiến*, the southward expansion that extended Vietnamese territory into former Cham lands.

## Usurpation and the Revival Lê

After Lê Thánh Tông's death, a run of weak or tyrannical rulers let corruption hollow out the court. In 1527, the general Mạc Đăng Dung seized the throne and founded the Mạc dynasty. Loyalist families, including the Trịnh and Nguyễn, rallied behind a surviving Lê prince and restored the house in 1533, but the new Lê kings held no real power. The Trịnh lords ruled the north in the emperors' name and fought the Mạc until 1677. From the 17th century, Vietnam was effectively split in two, with the Trịnh controlling the north and the Nguyễn lords the south (Đàng Trong) across the Gianh River. Seven campaigns between 1627 and 1672 ended in stalemate, and the partition hardened into separate northern and southern cultures and dialects.

## Downfall

Late in the 18th century, heavy taxation and concentrated landholdings drove peasant revolts. The Tây Sơn rebellion (1771), led by three brothers, toppled the Nguyễn and then the Trịnh, and its forces defeated a Qing Chinese invasion in 1789, bringing the Lê dynasty to a close.

## Culture and Foreign Contact

The Lê period fixed Confucianism as the state ideology, displacing earlier Buddhist influence. Literature flourished in both chữ Hán (Classical Chinese characters) and chữ Nôm, a script adapted to write vernacular Vietnamese. European contact began in the 16th century through Portuguese traders and Jesuit missionaries, including Alexandre de Rhodes, whose work led to chữ Quốc ngữ, the romanised script that eventually replaced both older writing systems. The dynasty kept tributary relations with the Ming and later the Qing, and trade grew through ports at Đông Kinh (Hanoi) and Hội An.

## Unresolved Questions

Historians still debate the exact nature of Lê Thánh Tông's treatment of captured Chinese sailors and Malaccan envoys, some of whom were reportedly subjected to forced castration, with one interpretation linking them to illegal trade rather than accidental castaways. Scholars also differ on how much symbolic or cultural authority the Revival Lê emperors actually retained behind the Trịnh façade, and on how deeply the 17th-century north–south partition shaped the divergent regional identities that still mark Vietnam today.
