# Spanish Empire

The Spanish Empire (1492–1976) was the first global empire, spanning the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Oceania at its peak of about 13.7 million km². It began with the 1469 dynastic marriage of Ferdinand II of Aragon and Isabella I of Castile, whose 1492 capture of Granada ended the Reconquista and freed resources for overseas expansion. That same year, Columbus's voyage opened the Americas to colonization. The empire ran on American silver and Catholic evangelization, and its decline stretched from early-19th-century independence movements to the 1898 Spanish-American War. Its lasting legacy is the worldwide spread of Spanish language, Catholic Christianity, and Iberian legal and administrative forms.

## How it was built

The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494), brokered by the pope, divided the newly "discovered" world along a meridian: Spain received most of the Americas; Portugal kept Africa and Asia. Caribbean outposts such as Santo Domingo (1496) became templates for governing the wider Americas, and the conquest of the Canary Islands (complete by 1496), where over 90% of the indigenous Guanche were killed or enslaved by 1504, rehearsed the methods later used in the Americas.

The empire's two great land conquests were Hernán Cortés against the Aztecs (Tenochtitlan fell 1521) and Francisco Pizarro against the Inca at Cajamarca (1532). Silver from mines at Potosí and Zacatecas then financed Habsburg wars in Europe for the next two centuries.

## How it was ruled

Three institutions tied the empire together. The Casa de Contratación, based in Seville, regulated all colonial trade and navigation. The flota, a convoy system, protected transatlantic silver shipments from European privateers. A labor system forced indigenous workers into mines and fields: the encomienda granted colonists tribute and labor from indigenous people in exchange for nominal Christianization, and the repartimiento was a rotational draft that supplied workers for mines and plantations. The crown skimmed one-fifth of all precious-metal output through the quinto real, a 20% tax.

A sharp social hierarchy separated peninsulares (Spaniards born in Spain), who monopolized high office, from American-born creoles, with mestizos (people of mixed European and indigenous ancestry) and indigenous people below them. Catholic missions ran in parallel with this civil structure, making the Church both a governing instrument and a unifying cultural force.

## The Habsburg peak and its costs

When Charles V inherited the throne in 1516, Spain already ruled the Americas plus Italian kingdoms, the Low Countries, and the Austrian Habsburg lands. His son Philip II (r. 1556–1598) added the Philippines (occupied from 1565) and, during the 1580–1640 Iberian Union, Portugal itself, producing the first "empire on which the sun never sets." Constant wars in Europe, the Dutch Revolt, and Mediterranean conflicts against the Ottomans drained the treasury, and the Armada's defeat in 1588 showed the limits of Spanish naval power.

The silver that paid for these wars caused sustained inflation across Europe, a problem debated by scholars at the School of Salamanca. Smuggling to evade the mercantile monopoly steadily undermined the system from within.

## Bourbon reforms and loss of the empire

The 1700 death of Charles II triggered the War of Spanish Succession (1701–1714), installing the Bourbon Philip V. The new dynasty centralised authority through the Nueva Planta decrees (which abolished the separate laws of the Crown of Aragon), moved colonial trade from Seville to Cádiz in 1717, and opened legal inter-colonial trade through the 1778 comercio libre policy. These reforms provoked resistance, notably the Túpac Amaru uprising in 1780 and the comunero revolts in New Granada, and came too late to save the empire.

Defeat in the Seven Years' War cost Spain Havana and Manila, and the 1808 Napoleonic invasion of Spain created a legitimacy vacuum that triggered wars of independence led by Simón Bolívar and José de San Martín. Mexico became independent in 1821, and by 1826 Spain retained only Cuba, Puerto Rico, the Philippines, and Guam. The 1898 Spanish-American War destroyed Spain's remaining fleets at Manila Bay and Santiago de Cuba, forcing cession of the last overseas territories. Spain then held only African possessions such as Spanish Sahara and Equatorial Guinea, relinquished in the mid-20th century.

## Open questions

Historians still debate whether colonial rule was primarily extractive or developmental: Alexander von Humboldt's early-19th-century observations suggested many indigenous New Spaniards were better off than European peasants, complicating a simple oppression narrative. The role of silver is contested, with some arguing it impoverished Spain while others emphasize its role in funding European wars. The Bourbon reforms are similarly disputed, viewed by some as too little, too late, and by others as provocations that accelerated colonial rebellion.
