# Shang dynasty

The Shang dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BC) was the earliest Chinese royal dynasty backed by both traditional written records and firm archaeological evidence. It ruled the middle and lower Yellow River valley, and its inscriptions on oracle bones provide the first direct written evidence of Chinese history, politics, and religion.

## How we know it existed

For centuries the Shang were treated as legend, known mainly from later classics like the *Book of Documents* and Sima Qian's *Shiji*. The breakthrough came in 1899, when scholars traced "dragon bones" sold by medicine sellers to inscribed turtle shells and ox bones. Between 1928 and 1937, scientific excavations at Yinxu, near Anyang, uncovered the final Shang capital of Yin: royal tombs, palace foundations, sacrificial pits, and tens of thousands of artefacts. Later work added Zhengzhou Shang City, an earlier walled centre, and in 1976 the undisturbed tomb of Fu Hao, a consort and general of King Wu Ding. This pairing of text and archaeology makes the Shang the earliest Chinese dynasty verified in both ways.

## Chronology

Traditional dates placed the Shang at 1766–1122 BC. The Xia–Shang–Zhou Chronology Project, using radiocarbon dating and astronomical records, proposes a start around 1600 BC and an end in 1046 BC, the date most widely accepted today. The dynasty was founded by Tang after he overthrew the last Xia ruler and ended when its final king, Di Xin, was defeated by the Zhou at the Battle of Muye. Key dates:

| Date | Event |
|------|-------|
| c. 1600 BC | Dynasty traditionally begins; possibly linked to the Erligang culture walled city at Yanshi. |
| c. 1500 BC | Large-scale production of ritual bronze vessels and weapons begins. |
| c. 1250 BC | Earliest surviving oracle bone inscriptions, from the reign of King Wu Ding at Yinxu. |
| c. 1046 BC | Shang defeated by the Zhou at the Battle of Muye. |

## Society, religion, and writing

Shang society was agricultural, governed by a hereditary royal line and a warrior aristocracy supported by a bureaucracy. The king was both political ruler and chief religious figure, performing divination and sacrifices to ancestors and to Di, the high god believed to control natural forces.

The dynasty's most important cultural legacy is a fully developed writing system, preserved mainly on oracle bones used for divination. Inscribers heated a shell or bone until it cracked, then read the cracks as answers from ancestors or Di, recording the question and outcome on the bone. These inscriptions, dating from King Wu Ding's reign onward, document royal activities, warfare, harvests, and rituals, and they remain the oldest substantial body of Chinese writing.

## Bronze and material culture

The Shang are the core of early Chinese Bronze Age culture. They produced elaborate ritual vessels and weapons in segmented piece-moulds, a demanding technology that required organised mining of copper and tin, long-distance trade, and a large craft labour force. Sites such as Zhengzhou and Yinxu show massive rammed-earth walls and palace platforms, a building technique in which layers of moist earth are compacted inside formwork. Bronze went to ritual and warfare, not everyday tools. Fu Hao's tomb, filled with bronzes, jades, and weapons, shows how much wealth and ritual status could be concentrated in a single elite burial.

## Military and expansion

The Shang fought frequent wars with neighbouring settlements and northern groups such as the Guifang. From around 1200 BC the army fielded chariots alongside bronze-armed infantry and could mobilise thousands of conscripts. The king served as supreme commander, and divination inscriptions often record the outcomes of campaigns, giving a rare direct view of early Chinese warfare.

## Open questions

Several issues remain unsettled. The exact start and end of the dynasty are debated, with proposed end dates ranging from 1130 to 1018 BC. The relationship with the preceding Xia is unclear, seen by some as a contemporary state and by others as a later myth. The role of shamanism in Shang religion is disputed, as is the historicity of legends that a disgraced Shang prince, Gija, founded a state in Korea.
