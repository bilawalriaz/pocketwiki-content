# Ottoman Empire

The Ottoman Empire was a six-century transcontinental state (c. 1299–1922) founded by the Turkoman leader Osman I as a small Anatolian principality. It expanded to span three continents, became the dominant Islamic power and a central Mediterranean trade hub, and ended as the Republic of Turkey.

## Origins and Expansion (c. 1299–1566)

The empire emerged from the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum's collapse into Anatolian beyliks (principalities). Osman I founded a beylik in Bithynia around 1299, taking Byzantine towns along the Sakarya River. A Byzantine defeat at Bapheus (1302) accelerated this rise. Modern scholarship largely rejects the older ghaza thesis, that the Ottomans were purely religious warriors, in favour of the demographic exhaustion of Byzantine lands after the Black Death and the economic incentive of akıncı slave-hunting raids as drivers of expansion.

Orhan captured Bursa in 1326, the first capital, then Thessaloniki (1387), and won at Kosovo (1389), opening the Balkans. Timur's defeat of Bayezid I at Ankara in 1402 caused an eleven-year civil war (1402–1413); Mehmed I restored unity, and Murad II recovered the Balkans, defeating the Crusade of Varna (1444).

Mehmed II conquered Constantinople on 29 May 1453, ending the Byzantine Empire and granting the Eastern Orthodox Church autonomy. Selim I (1512–1520) roughly doubled the empire's size by defeating the Safavids at Chaldiran (1514), conquering the Mamluk Sultanate (1516–1517), and acquiring the Caliphate. Suleiman the Magnificent (1520–1566) captured Belgrade (1521), shattered Hungary at Mohács (1526), besieged Vienna (1529, 1532), took Baghdad (1535), and partitioned the Caucasus with the Safavids (Peace of Amasya, 1555). At its peak under Suleiman, the empire spanned 877,888 mi² (2,273,720 km²) across three continents.

## Structure of the State

The Sultan headed a dual military-civil administration. The Divan (council) evolved into a bureaucracy led by the Grand Vizier (from 1320), who became de facto head of state as sultans withdrew from politics in the late 16th century. Law combined Sharia (Islamic law) with Qanun (dynastic law issued by the Sultan). Three court systems coexisted: Islamic courts (primary), non-Muslim communal courts, and trade courts.

The millet system granted non-Muslim confessional communities (Rum Millet, Armenians, Jews) autonomy over personal status, education, and taxation, while imposing legal disabilities such as the jizya tax and restrictions on arms and horses. The devshirme levy recruited Christian boys from the Balkans for the palace school (Enderûn) and Janissary corps; the source describes this as falling within modern definitions of genocide, a claim that remains debated.

The military combined Janissary infantry, Sipahi cavalry fief-holders, and Akıncı raiders, using Mongol-style tactics (feigned retreat, crescent formation) and early gunpowder falconets.

## Stagnation, Defeat, and Reform (1566–1908)

After Suleiman, the empire adapted institutionally rather than collapsed. The Celali rebellions (1590–1610) brought anarchy to Anatolia, and the Köprülü era (1656–1703) restored central authority, completing Crete's conquest (1669) and expanding into Ukraine. The failed Second Siege of Vienna (1683) and the Treaty of Karlowitz (1699), ceding Hungary and Transylvania to Austria, began permanent territorial retreat. Russia gained Azov (1739) and a protectorate over Wallachia and Moldavia through the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca (1774). Selim III's Nizam-ı Cedid reforms were blocked by the Janissaries and ulema; Mahmud II abolished the Janissaries in 1826, the Auspicious Incident, enabling modernisation.

The Tanzimat era (1839–1876) introduced secular Nizamiye courts, a penal code, banking reforms, and the 1876 Constitution (Kanûn-u Esâsî), but the First Constitutional Era lasted only two years before Sultan Abdul Hamid II suspended parliament. Nationalism eroded territory: Greece gained independence (1830), Serbia autonomy (1817, recognised 1830). The Crimean War (1853–1856) triggered foreign loans, bankruptcy came in 1875, and the 1881 Ottoman Public Debt Administration placed state revenues under European control. The 1877–1878 war cost Bulgaria, Romania, Serbia, and Montenegro, with Austria occupying Bosnia.

## Defeat and Dissolution (1908–1922)

The 1908 Young Turk Revolution restored the Constitution; the Committee of Union and Progress (CUP) soon dominated. The Balkan Wars (1912–1913) stripped the empire of nearly all European territories, and a 1913 coup established CUP dictatorship. Joining the Central Powers in October 1914 led to defensive successes at Gallipoli (1915–1916) and Kut (1916), but the Arab Revolt (1916–1918), backed by Britain, broke the Middle Eastern front. During the war, the CUP orchestrated the extermination of Armenians (up to 1.5 million), Assyrians, and Greeks through massacres, forced labour, and death marches to the Syrian desert.

The Armistice of Mudros (30 October 1918) brought Allied occupation of Istanbul and the Treaty of Sèvres (1920), which partitioned Anatolia. Mustafa Kemal Atatürk led the Turkish War of Independence (1919–1923); the Grand National Assembly abolished the sultanate on 1 November 1922 and proclaimed the Republic of Turkey on 29 October 1923. The Caliphate was abolished in 1924.

## Economy and Society

The state followed a "provisionist" policy, developing Bursa, Edirne, and Istanbul as commercial hubs, welcoming Jewish refugees from Spain, and protecting productive classes; annual income quadrupled in real terms from 1523 to 1748. The discovery of the Cape Route (1488) bypassed Ottoman land monopolies, and capitulations (treaties with France in 1536, 1673, 1740) fixed tariffs at 3%, creating a liberal trade regime that some economists argue caused deindustrialisation.

Population grew from roughly 12 million in the 1520s to 25–32 million by 1800, then fell to 19 million by 1897 (74% Muslim). As the empire shrank, 7–9 million Muslim refugees (muhacir) from the Caucasus, Crimea, and Balkans migrated to Anatolia; by 1922 half of urban Turkey's population descended from these refugees. Non-Muslims fell from a Christian majority in the 15th century to 19.1% by 1914, driven by war, plague, and migration. Sunni Islam (Hanafi school) was the state religion, with the Sultan claiming the Caliphate from Selim I, formalised in 1517. Ottoman Turkish, an Oghuz Turkic language with heavy Persian and Arabic influence, was the official language; French became the Western lingua franca after the Tanzimat. Literacy remained around 15% by 1900.

## Debates

Modern academic consensus rejects the mid-20th-century "decline paradigm" of post-Suleiman stagnation, emphasising institutional adaptation and 18th-century resilience. The early state's nature remains contested: jihad state (Paul Wittek), nomadic Turkic tribal continuation (Mehmet Fuat Köprülü), booty-seeking confederacy open to Christians and Muslims (Heath Lowry, Dimitri Kitsikis), or Byzantine state under a Turkish Muslim veneer (Edward Gibbon, Speros Vryonis, Norman Stone). Twenty-first-century scholars re-periodise the Balkan conquest into an akıncı phase of slave-hunting and destruction followed by administrative integration. The devshirme system's classification as genocide and the economic effects of capitulations continue to generate scholarly disagreement.
