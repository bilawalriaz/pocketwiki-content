# Thebes, Egypt

Thebes, known in ancient Egyptian as Waset, was a Nile city in Upper Egypt whose ruins now sit inside modern Luxor. For roughly a thousand years it alternated between being Egypt's political capital and its foremost religious center, and its surviving temple complexes and tombs mark the high point of pharaonic civilization.

## Setting and economy

Thebes lay on the Nile about 800 km south of the Delta, near the Wadi Hammamat route to the Red Sea. Around 4000 years ago a shift in the floodplain expanded the arable land, allowing a small trading post to grow into a major city. Its wealth came from controlling trade and agriculture, a prominence noted centuries later by Homer.

## Rise to power

Inhabited from about 3200 BC, Thebes was the capital of the fourth Upper Egyptian nome, an administrative district of ancient Egypt. During the First Intermediate Period, when central authority collapsed, the 11th Dynasty ruled Upper Egypt from Thebes. Around 2050 BC, Mentuhotep II reunified the country, beginning the Middle Kingdom and making Thebes a key city.

During the Second Intermediate Period, a people of Asiatic origin called the Hyksos took Lower Egypt and made Memphis their capital, while Theban rulers resisted from the south. Around 1550 BC, Ahmose I expelled the Hyksos and founded the New Kingdom with Thebes as its capital, a status the city held for roughly 500 years.

## New Kingdom peak

The New Kingdom, roughly 1550 to 1070 BC, was the era when Egypt functioned as an imperial power. Thebes flourished as the cult center of Amun, the chief god of Thebes and later considered king of the gods. The Karnak temple complex, dedicated to Amun, was expanded over more than 2,000 years of building.

Several pharaohs shaped the city:

- Thutmose I extended Egypt's southern frontier and began royal tomb construction in the Valley of the Kings.
- Hatshepsut, ruling c. 1479–1458 BC, renewed foreign trade and built the terraced mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahri, a temple for worship of a deceased pharaoh.
- Amenhotep III, c. 1388–1350 BC, expanded construction to its highest point, leaving colossal statues including the Memnon Colossi.

A sharp interruption came under Akhenaten, c. 1351–1334 BC, who moved the capital to a new city at Amarna and promoted a different god, sidelining Amun. After Akhenaten's death the court returned to Thebes. Ramesses II, c. 1279–1213 BC, resumed large-scale building, including the Ramesseum, his mortuary temple on the west bank.

## Society and culture

By around 1500 BC Thebes may have been the largest city in the world, with an estimated 75,000 inhabitants, a figure based on scholarly reconstruction rather than a hard count. The city staged more than sixty annual festivals. Two stood out: the Beautiful Feast of Opet, which linked Karnak to the Luxor temple along the processional avenue, and the Beautiful Festival of the Valley, when the living crossed to the west bank to honor the dead. The chief deities of Thebes, the Theban Triad, were Amun, his consort Mut, and their son Khonsu.

## Decline

Thebes lost political primacy after the New Kingdom. During the Third Intermediate Period the High Priests of Amun governed the region from the temple, power shared with royal women who held the priestly title God's Wife of Amun. In 721 BC the Kushite king Shabaka made Thebes the capital of his reunited kingdom, but in 663 BC the Assyrians plundered the city, ending its political life. Thebes remained an important religious center but never regained political power. A revolt against Ptolemaic rule was suppressed, and by the Roman period Thebes had dwindled to a village, remembered mainly as a sacred site.

## The two banks

The east bank of the Nile held the city and the great temples, Karnak and Luxor, both fronted by pylons, massive gateways formed by a pair of tapering towers. The west bank held the necropolis: the tombs of the Valley of the Kings, the Valley of the Queens, and mortuary temples such as the Ramesseum and Hatshepsut's Deir el-Bahri.
