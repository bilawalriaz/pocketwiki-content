# German Empire

The German Empire (1871–1918) was a federal constitutional monarchy built on the unification of German states under Prussian leadership. In forty-seven years it became Europe's leading industrial power, fought and lost the First World War, and left a constitutional structure whose authoritarian features shaped Germany's twentieth-century crises.

## Foundation and constitutional shape

On 18 January 1871, the North German Confederation merged with the south German states, excluding Austria, and the Empire was proclaimed in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles. The constitution took effect on 16 April 1871, with Wilhelm I as the first Kaiser.

Executive power sat with the Kaiser, who appointed the Chancellor and commanded the armed forces. The legislature was bicameral: the Reichstag, elected by universal male suffrage, and the Bundesrat, which represented the twenty-five constituent states. The system was a hybrid. Universal suffrage gave it a democratic veneer, but Prussia's oversized representation in the Bundesrat, combined with a voting structure that weighted rural conservative districts heavily, guaranteed conservative control. In practice, the Prussian prime minister usually served as Imperial Chancellor, so the two governments were fused.

## Bismarck's era (1871–1890)

Otto von Bismarck, the first Chancellor, ran the Empire on a doctrine he called "revolutionary conservatism." The aim was to co-opt the working class without giving it political power. In the 1880s his government introduced health, accident, and old-age insurance, the first national social-welfare system in the world, binding workers to the state while marginalising the Social Democrats.

At the same time, Bismarck launched the Kulturkampf, a campaign from 1871 to 1880 against Catholic Church influence. It backfired, mobilising Catholic voters into a coherent political bloc, and Bismarck quietly reversed course. The most durable part of his legacy was foreign policy. Through a web of treaties, including the Dual Alliance with Austria-Hungary in 1879, the Three Emperors' League of 1881, and the Triple Alliance of 1882, Bismarck kept France isolated and preserved European peace. He resisted colonial expansion, but public pressure forced the acquisition of territories in Africa and the Pacific in 1884–1885.

## The Wilhelmine turn

1888, the "Year of the Three Emperors," reset the political climate. Wilhelm I died, Frederick III reigned for ninety-nine days before dying of cancer, and Wilhelm II acceded at age twenty-nine. In 1890 he dismissed Bismarck and announced Weltpolitik, a "world politics" of colonial expansion, a battle fleet built to challenge Britain, and a louder German voice in global affairs. This abandoned Bismarck's careful balance of power. Germany's relations with Britain, France, and Russia all deteriorated.

Domestically, the economy boomed; by 1913 Germany was the largest economy in Europe, and its science and industry led the continent. Yet political modernisation lagged. The Social Democratic Party (SPD) became the largest faction in the Reichstag by 1912, despite persistent repression and surveillance.

## The Herero and Nama genocide

Between 1904 and 1907, German colonial forces in South West Africa (modern Namibia) carried out a deliberate extermination of the Herero and Nama peoples, including mass executions, forced labour in concentration camps, and the driving of survivors into the desert. It is considered the first genocide of the twentieth century and a direct expression of the racial logic that accompanied Weltpolitik.

## The First World War and collapse

In 1914, after the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand, Germany offered Austria-Hungary a "blank check" of unconditional support. When the Austro-Serbian conflict escalated, Germany entered a war whose conduct exceeded anyone's planning. The Schlieffen Plan, a rapid sweep through Belgium to knock France out, failed and produced a four-year stalemate on the Western Front. In the east, Germany and Austria-Hungary prevailed, and the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (1918) stripped enormous territory from revolutionary Russia.

Two strategic decisions doomed the outcome. The Allied naval blockade starved Germany of food and raw materials. Unrestricted submarine warfare, resumed in 1917, sank American shipping and pulled the United States into the war. By autumn 1918, German offensives had failed, allied states were collapsing, and a naval mutiny in Kiel ignited the November Revolution. On 9 November 1918, Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicated and fled to the Netherlands. The Empire was succeeded by the Weimar Republic. The Treaty of Versailles, signed on 28 June 1919, imposed heavy reparations and territorial losses, and assigned Germany primary responsibility for the war.

The Sonderweg thesis, the argument that Germany followed a "special path" to modernity in which authoritarian structures never yielded to parliamentary democracy, remains a major historical debate, as does the question of German responsibility for the war, sharpened by Fritz Fischer's claim that Berlin's aims went far beyond a blank check. What is settled is the basic trajectory: a state built for rapid industrial success and continental dominance, ruled by a constitution that could neither liberalise nor win a total war.
