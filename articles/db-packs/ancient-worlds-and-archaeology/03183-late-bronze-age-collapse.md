# Late Bronze Age collapse

Around 1200 BC, the palace‑based kingdoms of the Eastern Mediterranean and Near East broke apart within a few decades. Egypt, the Hittite Empire, Mycenaean Greece, Ugarit, and other centres lost the interlinked trade and administrative systems that had sustained them. The result was a sharp material decline, the loss of writing in Greece, and several centuries of poorer, more isolated village life.

## What made the system fragile

Late Bronze Age states were organised as **palace economies** — central administrations that collected goods, kept records, and redistributed them. The same palaces anchored diplomacy: the Amarna letters (a 14th‑century BC cache of diplomatic correspondence found in Egypt) and Hittite–Egyptian treaties show kings writing each other in Akkadian, a shared diplomatic language of the era, exchanging embassies, gifts, and wives. Bronze, copper, tin, grain, olive oil, timber, and luxury goods moved along routes that linked Greece, Anatolia, Cyprus, the Levantine coast, and the Nile. Because each kingdom depended on partners for raw materials and finished goods, the system had little slack. Disrupting a few hubs — a harbour, a tin shipment, a capital — could cascade through the whole network.

## What changed, region by region

The breakdown was uneven rather than total.

- **Mycenaean Greece.** Every major palace, including Mycenae, Tiryns, and Pylos, was destroyed. Surveys of the Peloponnese suggest roughly 90% of smaller sites were abandoned, and literacy in Linear B (the Mycenaean script used to keep palace inventories) disappeared. Greece then entered a long "Dark Age" of small, isolated villages.
- **Anatolia.** The Hittite Empire fragmented and its capital Hattusa was burned and never rebuilt.
- **Levant and Cyprus.** Ugarit, a key trade hub, was destroyed by about 1178 BC. Some inland cities, like Lachish, were rebuilt. Cyprus was less damaged; sites such as Kition show cultural continuity.
- **Egypt.** Under Ramesses III (c. 1186–1155 BC) Egypt fought off two invasions, probably by the so‑called **Sea Peoples**, but the cost was heavy. Egypt later withdrew from the Levant.
- **Syria and Mesopotamia.** Aramean and other groups migrated into the vacuum. The Middle Assyrian Empire began to weaken after about 1056 BC, and Phoenician city‑states gained autonomy once Egyptian and Assyrian power waned.

## Why it happened

No single cause is accepted; most scholars now argue for a "perfect storm" of interacting factors.

- **Drought.** Climate records from the Eastern Mediterranean point to severe dry conditions around 1198–1196 BC. Harvest failures would have hit redistributive palace economies hard.
- **Sea Peoples.** Egyptian inscriptions, especially those of Ramesses III, record naval raids and land attacks by a loose confederation labelled the "Sea Peoples." Their origin is unknown, and their exact role is debated.
- **Technology and warfare.** Iron tools and weapons spread, and new infantry tactics undercut the chariot warfare on which the great kingdoms had relied.
- **Earthquakes, disease, and complexity.** Earthquakes, possibly an early bubonic plague, and the strain of running overly complex bureaucracies are also implicated.

## How big was the collapse

The scale is itself a live debate. Robert Drews argued for near‑universal violent destruction from Pylos to Gaza around 1200 BC. Jesse Millek's recent reassessment of 153 destruction events found that more than 60% were misdated, unsupported, or never happened. The collapse was real and severe, but more patchy and more localised than the older cataclysm model suggests — closer to a series of regional crises whose effects rippled through a tightly coupled system.

## Open questions

- Was the collapse a single cataclysm or overlapping regional crises? Current evidence favours the latter.
- Was the primary driver climate, invasion, internal revolt, or systemic failure? Most researchers now treat these as reinforcing rather than competing.
- Were the Sea Peoples a cause or raiders profiting from weakness?
- How much cultural and economic life continued unbroken in places such as Cyprus and parts of the Levant, even as political centres fell?
