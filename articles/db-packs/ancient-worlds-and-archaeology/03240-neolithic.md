# Neolithic

The Neolithic, or New Stone Age, is the final period of the Stone Age, running roughly from 10,000 BC to 2,000 BC depending on the region. It is defined by the Neolithic Revolution: a shift from mobile hunting and gathering to permanent villages sustained by farming and herding. Farming produced a storable food surplus, which let populations grow, settlements persist year-round, and labour specialisation, social stratification, and durable infrastructure develop. The period's end is not a single date but a regional transition, generally marked by the onset of metal use: the Chalcolithic, or Copper Age, around 4500 BC in the Near East, with Egypt exiting the Neolithic near 3150 BC and parts of China and Scandinavia as late as 2000 BC.

## Origins in the Levant

The transition began in the Levant, the eastern Mediterranean coastlands, on the edge of the Fertile Crescent. The earlier Natufian culture (c. 12,500–9,500 BC) had already settled around stands of wild cereal and exploited them intensively, so the deeper cause of the change is debated. A leading explanation is climatic stress: the Younger Dryas, a cold-and-dry snap around 10,000 BC, may have compressed wild food supplies enough to make deliberate sowing worthwhile.

Pre-Pottery Neolithic A (PPNA), beginning c. 10,200 BC, marks the first clear farming villages in the region. Three things characterise it. First, the cultivation of founder crops, wild species whose harvest first justified sowing: wild emmer and einkorn wheat, barley, pulses such as lentils and peas, and fibre crops like flax. These remained the staples of Near Eastern farming for millennia. Second, animal management, beginning with captured wild herds and reaching full domestication of sheep, goats, cattle, and pigs around 8000 BC. Third, durable architecture and ritual life: Pre-Pottery Neolithic B (PPNB, from c. 8800 BC) added rectangular mud-brick houses, and the stone enclosures at Göbekli Tepe in southeastern Turkey were built around 9500 BC by communities whose subsistence still relied on wild and only partly cultivated resources, evidence that large ritual investment did not require full farming. Pottery spread across the region only with the Pottery Neolithic, c. 6400 BC, in cultures such as Halaf.

## How the Neolithic Spread

The new way of life radiated from the Fertile Crescent at very different speeds. In Europe, the principal mechanism was the movement of Anatolian farmers beginning around 9,000 years ago, shown by the close similarity of their crops, livestock, and pottery to those of western sites; the incoming farmers mixed with local foragers rather than simply replacing them. In Northeast Africa, domestic plants and animals appear by the early fifth millennium BC, though whether by migration from the Near East or by local invention is contested. In Sub-Saharan Africa the first food-producing economy was mobile pastoralism, the Pastoral Neolithic, in which livestock, especially cattle, moved with their keepers. In East Asia, sites such as Pengtoushan (c. 7500 BC) point to an independent origin of rice cultivation, and elsewhere in the region pottery was in use before farming, the opposite sequence to the Near East.

## Society, Tools, and Settlement

Neolithic material culture is summed up by two signatures. Mud-brick architecture, seen at Çatalhöyük in Anatolia, indicates permanent, year-round occupation. Polished stone tools, axes ground smooth rather than just knapped, are the diagnostic artefact: a polished axe can fell a small tree in minutes where a knapped one cannot, which is what is needed to clear forest for fields, and their spread tracks the spread of farming. Pottery, once it appears, lets grain be cooked, stored, and traded in standardised containers.

Neolithic societies are often described as small, relatively egalitarian tribes, and for most of the period this is roughly correct, but the pattern changed. The domestication of large animals around 8000 BC created the first heritable wealth, since a herd could be passed on, and social inequality grew with it. By the late Neolithic some regions had complex chiefdoms, and fortified settlements show that inter-group violence had become routine. Two open questions follow. The "Neolithic decline", a population fall across Europe after 5000 BC, has been attributed to climate change, plague, and mass migration, with no consensus. The sharp drop in Y-chromosome diversity during the same period, meaning fewer distinct male lineages survived, is usually explained by social factors such as patrilineal inheritance and polygyny, where a few men father most offspring, rather than by any single catastrophe.

The change to metal is gradual and regional, which is why a single end-date is impossible. The period from c. 10,000 BC to c. 2,000 BC is the span in which most of humanity traded a mobile foraging life for a settled, agricultural, ultimately urban one.
