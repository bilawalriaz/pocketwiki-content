# Byzantine Empire

The Byzantine Empire was the medieval continuation of the Roman state, centred on Constantinople. It survived the 5th-century fall of the Western Roman Empire and endured until the Ottoman conquest of 1453. Its citizens never called themselves "Byzantine"; they were *Romaioi* (Romans), inhabiting *Rhomanía* (Romanland). The label "Byzantine" was coined in the 16th century by scholars like Hieronymus Wolf, drawing on *Byzantion*, the pre-Constantinian settlement. Western Europeans, asserting their own Roman legitimacy through the Pope and the Holy Roman Empire (after 800), called the empire "Greece."

The empire fused three inheritances: Roman law and administration, the Greek language, and Nicene Christianity as state religion. Its core function was to act as a military and cultural buffer between Europe and the Persian, Arab, and Turkish powers of the East, while preserving classical learning for transmission to the Islamic world and, later, the Italian Renaissance.

## Foundations and Apogee

Constantine I refounded Byzantium as Constantinople in 330, choosing a site commanding the Bosphorus and the wealthy eastern provinces. He introduced the gold *solidus*, which stabilised Mediterranean trade for centuries. Theodosius I (379–395) made Nicene Christianity the state religion and was the last emperor to rule a united Roman world. When the Western Empire collapsed in 476, the East survived by avoiding barbarian warlord rule through diplomacy (Zeno redirecting the Ostrogoths to Italy) and through Anastasius I's fiscal reforms.

Justinian I (527–565) marked the apogee. His *Corpus Juris Civilis* codified Roman law, anchoring the legal tradition of Byzantium and later Europe. The Hagia Sophia (532–537) established the pendentive-dome model replicated from Novgorod to Kiev. He briefly reconquered the western Mediterranean. Yet overextension, the Plague of Justinian (541), and the Lombard invasion of Italy exhausted the empire. The 7th-century Arab conquests permanently removed the Levant, Egypt, and North Africa, cutting state revenue by roughly three-quarters and dropping the population from a 6th-century peak near 27 million to about 12 million by 800.

## Survival Through Reorganisation

The empire endured by retreating into Anatolia and reorganising. Provinces became *themata*, military-administrative districts where soldier-farmers held land in exchange for hereditary service under a *strategos* (general-governor). The Arab sieges of Constantinople (674–678, 717–718) were broken using Greek fire, a petroleum-based incendiary that burned on water. The Isaurian dynasty stabilised the frontiers but triggered Iconoclasm (726–843), a policy banning religious icons that destroyed vast amounts of art and caused internal strife.

The Macedonian dynasty (867–1056) presided over a two-century renaissance. Basil II (976–1025) annexed Bulgaria and pushed to the Euphrates. Administrative reform was neglected, and after 1025 civil wars eroded the position. The 1071 Battle of Manzikert opened Anatolia to Seljuk Turkish settlement, while the Norman capture of Bari ended Byzantine Italy. The Komnenian dynasty (1081–1180) restored regional power through diplomacy, the *pronoia* system (state revenue grants for military service), and Crusader cooperation, but Manuel I's defeat at Myriokephalon (1176) ended offensive capacity. Dynastic infighting then invited the Fourth Crusade, which sacked Constantinople in 1204.

## Fragmentation and End

The 1204 sack fragmented the empire into the Empire of Nicaea, the Empire of Trebizond, and the Despotate of Epirus. Michael VIII Palaiologos recaptured Constantinople in 1261, but the state was now a rump, dependent on Venetian and Genoese naval power. Civil wars and the rise of the Ottomans accelerated collapse. The Ottomans took Adrianople (1369) and Thessalonica (1387) and besieged Constantinople in 1394, lifted only by Timur's intervention. By 1453 the city's population had fallen to roughly 50,000; Mehmed II's final siege ended the empire on 29 May, killing Constantine XI.

## State, Law, and Church

Diocletian and Constantine separated civil and military administration. After the 7th century the theme system concentrated authority in the emperor and the *strategoi*, as cities lost autonomy to warfare. Roman law was codified in the *Codex Theodosianus* (438) and definitively in Justinian's *Corpus Juris Civilis* (529–534). Greek gradually replaced Latin in administration; Leo III's *Ekloge* (741) emphasised humane penalties, and Leo VI's *Basilika* (c. 900) became the standard Byzantine code.

Theological disputes divided eastern churches. Chalcedonian Orthodoxy dominated imperial territory, while Miaphysite and Nestorian communities largely fell under Muslim rule after the 7th century. The East–West Schism of 1054 formalised centuries of divergence over papal authority, the *Filioque* clause, and ritual differences, cementing the Orthodox Church as a pillar of Byzantine identity and a key diplomatic instrument.

## Military Evolution

The 7th-century collapse forced a shift from professional frontier and field armies to *thematic* militias of part-time soldier-farmers. By the 11th century, professional *tagmata* regiments, including the Varangian Guard, handled offensive operations. The late 10th-century army of around 140,000 may have been the highest-quality force the empire ever fielded, but defensive capacities were neglected during 11th-century civil wars, and the loss of Anatolia to the Seljuks followed. Post-1081, the *pronoia* system funded a mercenary-heavy force. The Palaiologan army never exceeded 5,000 field troops; fewer than 8,000 defended Constantinople in 1453. The navy, organised into provincial fleets with *dromons* firing Greek fire, dominated the eastern Mediterranean until Italian reliance eroded indigenous capability; the fleet was disbanded in 1284.

## Society, Economy, and Culture

Latin persisted in law and military until about 400; Greek then became dominant. A diglossia emerged between literary Attic Greek and vernacular *Koine*. Slavery declined; *coloni*, tenant farmers bound to the land, formed an intermediate dependency. Constantinople shrank from over 500,000 to 50,000 by 1453.

Maritime geography and the gold solidus supported trade. The 6th-century plagues and wars collapsed the ancient economy; fortified cities replaced open ones, and rural areas became hamlets. A 9th-century revival brought agricultural growth and urban expansion, but by the 11th–12th centuries Italian merchants dominated international trade. The 1204 sack destroyed accumulated wealth, opened the Black Sea to Western merchants, and made governance inefficient under competing rump factions.

Art was Christian and non-naturalistic, with gold-ground mosaics at Hagia Sophia and San Vitale as hallmarks. Macedonian art standardised cross-in-square churches and symmetrical frontality. Palaiologan works, looted in 1204, influenced the Italo-Byzantine style of Cimabue and Giotto. Literature blended Hellenism, Christianity, and classicising revival, with romance epics like *Digenes Akritas*. Late Greek-to-Latin translations by scholars such as Plethon and Bessarion fed the Italian Renaissance. Byzantine scholars transmitted classical knowledge to Islam and Italy, with figures like John Philoponus, whose critique of Aristotle influenced Galileo, bridging antiquity and the medieval world.
