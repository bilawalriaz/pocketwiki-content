# Spanish conquest of the Inca Empire

In 1532 a Spanish force of 168 men under Francisco Pizarro ambushed and captured Atahualpa, the Inca emperor, at Cajamarca. Within forty years the largest pre-Columbian state in the Americas, home to an estimated 12–16 million people, had been dismantled, its last emperor executed, and a new Spanish colonial capital, Lima, founded. Four forces converged: an Inca civil war that had already broken the empire from inside, Spanish steel and gunpowder weapons, alliances with peoples the Incas had recently subjugated, and Old World disease, above all smallpox, that killed the previous emperor and his heir and left the succession in chaos.

## The empire on the eve of conquest

The Inca state, called Tawantinsuyu in Quechua, had grown in roughly eighty years from about 400,000 km² around Cuzco in 1448 to about 1,800,000 km² stretching from modern Colombia into Chile by 1528. Administration ran through a hierarchy of local leaders watched by Inca officials, all of whom answered to the Sapa Inca, the emperor, who was treated as divine. Centralised rule held at this scale only loosely: many subject peoples had been incorporated by force within living memory and stayed loyal only while watched. That structural fragility mattered once the centre cracked.

## The fracture: civil war and epidemic

Huayna Capac, the reigning Sapa Inca, died in 1528, very probably of smallpox introduced by earlier Spanish contact, and his designated heir died with him. Two sons, Huáscar in Cuzco and Atahualpa in the north, fought for the throne. Atahualpa, commanding the army that had just put down a northern rebellion, won and imprisoned Huáscar, but the five-year war exhausted the realm, depleted its armies, and left provincial garrisons weakened. Pizarro arrived in 1532 not against a unified empire but against one already broken from within. Historians still debate whether the 1520s epidemic was truly smallpox or a different disease such as Carrion's disease; either way, the demographic and political blow was real.

## Cajamarca and the capture of Atahualpa

On 16 November 1532 Pizarro's small force, equipped with cavalry, steel swords, firearms, and crossbows, lured Atahualpa into a closed plaza at Cajamarca and opened fire on an unarmed ceremonial retinue. About 2,000 Incas were killed and Atahualpa was seized. Indigenous auxiliaries fought with the Spanish, notably the Huanca and the Cañari, peoples the Incas had recently conquered who saw a chance for revenge or independence; their role complicates any simple European-versus-American story.

## Ransom, trial, and execution

From captivity Atahualpa offered a ransom of gold and silver in exchange for his release. The Spanish collected the treasure by May 1533. Pizarro then had Atahualpa tried, on charges including polygamy and idolatry, convicted, and garrotted on 29 August 1533 after a forced conversion to Christianity. A captive Sapa Inca could have served as a puppet, but a dead one removed any chance of a legitimate native successor, and the Spanish moved immediately to install compliant rulers while pressing on Cuzco, which fell the same year.

## Rebellion, civil war among the Spanish, and the long endgame

In 1535 Manco Inca, originally a Spanish client, rebelled after abuse by Pizarro's brothers, besieged Cuzco in 1536, and was repulsed. He withdrew to Vilcabamba in the eastern cloud forest and founded the Neo-Inca State, a rump Inca kingdom that survived for decades. The conquest was prolonged less by Inca resistance than by Spanish factionalism. Pizarro and his former partner Diego de Almagro fought a civil war ending with Almagro's defeat at the Battle of Las Salinas and his execution in 1538; Pizarro in turn was assassinated in 1541 by Almagro's supporters. The Spanish crown responded with direct royal control: Lima was founded in 1535, and in 1542 Charles V created the Viceroyalty of Peru. The Neo-Inca State held out at Vilcabamba until 1572, when the last Sapa Inca, Túpac Amaru, was captured and executed, forty years after the first Spanish landing.

## The demographic catastrophe

The political conquest was overshadowed by a biological one. Estimates put the Andean population at 10–15 million in 1520; by 1570 it had fallen to under 3 million, from smallpox and measles, the warfare and forced resettlement of conquest, and the Spanish mita, the conscript labour draft adapted from an Inca institution and redirected to the silver mines of Potosí. Mercury pollution from the amalgamation process used to refine silver added an environmental injury that lingered for centuries. Some historians argue that the empire was already in structural decline before Pizarro arrived, weakened by the very size that had made it great; the conquest, in this view, finished a process the Incas had begun. Spanish colonization involved forced labor (mita), the imposition of Christianity, and the exploitation of resources, especially silver from Potosí, which caused significant environmental damage through mercury pollution.
