# Mughal Empire

The Mughal Empire (1526–1857) was an early-modern South Asian state built by a Central Asian dynasty, governed through a centralised bureaucracy, and at its peak produced roughly a quarter of the world's manufactured goods. It grew from a single battlefield victory into the largest economy of its era, fragmented within a generation of its strongest ruler's death, and was dissolved by a British-led rebellion.

## Origins and identity

The founder, **Babur**, was a **Chagatai Turk** descended from Timur on his father's side and from Genghis Khan on his mother's. "Mughal" is Persian for "Mongol", a label applied in India to set the dynasty apart from the Afghan **Lodi** sultans they displaced. The dynasty's own name was **Gurkani**, "sons-in-law", marking Timur's marriage into Genghis Khan's line. Official documents called the realm *Hindustan*. Some historians, notably Marshall Hodgson, prefer "Timurid" or "Indo-Timurid" because the rulers were Turks, not Mongols.

## Expansion and peak (1526–1707)

Babur seized the empire in 1526 at the **First Battle of Panipat** using Ottoman-style wagon-laager artillery and consolidated his position at Khanwa (1527), but died before a stable administration took shape. His son **Humayun** (r. 1530–1556) lost the realm to the Afghan **Sher Shah Suri** in 1540, spent fifteen years in exile at the Safavid court in Persia, and returned in 1555, dying the following year.

The decisive builder was **Akbar** (r. 1556–1605), who took power as a teenager. Akbar created the institutions that held the empire together for 150 years: a merit-based ranking system, the **mansabdari**, that assigned every officer a number fixing both his salary and the cavalry he had to supply, with non-hereditary ranks; the **zabt** land-revenue system, which measured cultivated area and levied cash taxes in silver rupees, pushing peasants toward cash crops such as cotton, indigo and opium; and a policy of *sulh-i-kul* ("universal tolerance") that drew Hindus and Muslims into the administration, culminating in *Din-i-Ilahi*, a syncretic court cult.

His three successors extended the realm to its greatest territorial extent. **Jahangir** (r. 1605–1627) expanded tax-free grants to religious figures and executed Sikh Guru Arjan, beginning Mughal-Sikh conflict. **Shah Jahan** (r. 1628–1658) presided over the architectural climax (the Taj Mahal), but court spending outran tax income. **Aurangzeb** (r. 1658–1707) seized the throne from his liberal brother Dara Shikoh, annexed the Deccan sultanates of Bijapur and Golconda, compiled the **Fatawa 'Alamgiri** as a comprehensive code of Hanafi law, and reinstated the **jizya**, a poll tax on non-Muslims that Akbar had removed. His twenty-five-year Deccan war drained the treasury and left the north under-governed.

## Collapse (1707–1857)

The collapse outpaced the rise. Between 1707 and 1720 four different emperors sat on the throne in 1719 alone; courtiers known as the **Sayyid Brothers** chose whom to crown. In 1739 the Persian invader **Nader Shah** sacked Delhi, stripping the treasury and the Peacock Throne. Provincial governors stopped forwarding revenue: **Bengal**, **Awadh** and **Hyderabad** became practically independent successor states while still sending ceremonial greetings to the emperor. The **Marathas**, a Hindu confederacy from the western Deccan, took central India.

Into this vacuum stepped the **British East India Company**. After the **Battle of Plassey** (1757) it took Bengal, the richest province. The 1803 Anglo-Maratha war made the company the emperor's nominal "protector". The fiction of Mughal sovereignty ended with the **Indian Rebellion of 1857**, after which the last emperor, **Bahadur Shah Zafar**, was deposed and exiled to Rangoon.

## How the state worked

The emperor sat above four ministries: the **Diwan** (finance, the most powerful), the **Mir Bakhshi** (military and mansabdari rolls), the **Sadr as-Sudr** (law and religion) and the **Mir Saman** (household and royal workshops, the *karkhanas*). Territory ran Subahs (provinces) under **Subadars** → **Sarkars** (districts) → **Parganas** (village clusters with a judge, *qadi*, and a tax collector, the basic unit). Law was hybrid: **Hanafi** Islamic jurisprudence for Muslims, with custom and village councils (*panchayats*) for Hindus, and the emperor as the final authority above all courts. The court was mobile; the **imperial camp** functioned as a moving capital, with **Kabul** as summer capital until 1681, and later **Shahjahanabad** (Old Delhi) as the fixed seat.

## Economy

The economy was **proto-industrial**, producing roughly **24.5% of world manufacturing output** before 1750. Agriculture was the tax base: peasants paid more than half of each harvest, assessed in silver rupees of over 96% purity minted from bullion reaching Asia through trade surpluses. **Bengal** alone supplied about a quarter of global textile trade in the early 1700s and built ships at over 200,000 tons a year, far more than colonial North America. Roughly 82% of people lived in the countryside, but with about 15% urbanisation the empire was more citied than contemporary Europe.

## Culture, military and technology

The Mughals fused Persian, Indian and Central Asian traditions. **Persian** was the language of court and government; **Hindustani** (later Urdu) became the everyday speech of Delhi's Muslims under Shah Jahan. Architecture mixed bulbous domes and pointed arches from Iran with Indian red sandstone and white marble, visible in the Taj Mahal, Agra Fort, Fatehpur Sikri, the Red Fort and Humayun's Tomb. Painting flourished in illustrated manuscripts such as the *Baburnama* and *Akbarnama* until Aurangzeb cut patronage around 1668. **Mughlai** cuisine and the wrestling tradition **pehlwani** (Indian *malla-yuddha* plus Persian *varzesh-e bastani*) show the same blending.

The military was a gunpowder army. Babur's artillery won Panipat; Akbar's engineer Fathullah Shirazi built an early multi-barrel volley gun, and rockets were used against war elephants by Akbar's reign. The state's **karkhanas** also produced seamless metal celestial globes cast in Kashmir and Lahore in 1589–90 and 1668, an achievement long thought technically impossible.

## Open questions

Two unresolved debates shape any picture of the empire. First, why did it fall: internal causes, including the **jagirdari crisis** (a shortage of revenue assignments that pitted officials against each other), succession wars, and Aurangzeb's religious and Deccan policies, weighed against external shocks such as Maratha expansion, Nader Shah and the East India Company. Second, the post-1707 emperor's status: successor states still acknowledged him ceremonially, which is why the British kept a pensioner Mughal on the throne until 1857 rather than declaring their own rule openly.
