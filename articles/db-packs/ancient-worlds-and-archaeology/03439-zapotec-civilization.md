# Zapotec civilization

The Zapotec civilization was a pre-Columbian indigenous culture of Mexico centered in the Valley of Oaxaca from roughly 700 BC to the Spanish conquest in 1521. They built one of Mesoamerica's earliest cities, Monte Albán, developed a logosyllabic writing system (symbols standing for whole words and syllables), and their descendants still speak Zapotec languages.

## Origins and the founding of Monte Albán

Zapotec civilization emerged in the late 6th century BC in the Y-shaped Central Valleys of Oaxaca, where three sub-valleys were originally divided among competing societies. After earlier centers such as San José Mogote lost population, around 500 BC a new city, Monte Albán was founded on a mountaintop in a "no-man's-land" between the valleys. Its population likely migrated from San José Mogote and surrounding villages. Archaeologists Joyce Marcus and Kent V. Flannery compare this consolidation to synoikism, a Greek process in which scattered populations merge into a single defensible city against external threats; early fortifications at Monte Albán support that reading.

## Expansion and the Zapotec state

From 400 BC to AD 200 the state based at Monte Albán expanded sharply. Zapotec colonists and soldiers took over or settled provinces far beyond Oaxaca, a shift visible in the sudden appearance of Zapotec-style pottery across neighboring regions. By about AD 200 Zapotec influence stretched from Quiotepec to Ocelotepec. Building J at Monte Albán, a structure whose carved stone slabs depict place-names and the names of conquered rulers, is read by many archaeologists as a visual record of this expansion. The state's power rested on colonization and military conquest.

Around AD 700 Monte Albán ceased to be the largest city in the southern Mexican highlands, and its political dominance faded. The causes of this decline remain incompletely understood.

## Writing, language, and religion

The Zapotecs developed one of the earliest logosyllabic scripts in Mesoamerica. The oldest known example, the "Danzante" stone (c. 500–600 BC) from San José Mogote, already uses glyphs for syllables. The script recorded calendar dates and historical events but is still largely undeciphered. The Zapotec languages belong to the Oto-Manguean family and are tonal, using up to four distinct pitch patterns (tonemes) to mark meaning.

Religion was polytheistic and tied to agriculture, with Cocijo as the rain god and Pitao Cozobi as the maize god. The Zapotecs practiced human sacrifice and dedicated new buildings by burying caches of symbolic offerings: jade for power, obsidian for sacrifice, and marine shells for the underworld. Society became increasingly stratified and centralized as the population grew.

## Geography and technology

The Zapotec heartland was the fertile Central Valleys of Oaxaca. They were sedentary, building stone-and-mortar houses. To support population growth they dug canal irrigation systems across the valley floor and foothills, an agricultural innovation that sustained their cities and produced tribute for the state.

## Aztec pressure and the Spanish conquest

In 1458 the Aztec Empire conquered the Valley of Oaxaca, and the last major Zapotec-Aztec battle was fought at Guiengola between 1497 and 1502. After the Spanish defeated the Aztecs, Zapotec king Cosijoeza, on hearing of that defeat, ordered his people not to resist. In 1521 the Zapotecs peacefully submitted to Hernán Cortés, sparing the Valley of Oaxaca the destruction suffered by the Aztecs. In 1526 the region became part of the Marquessate of the Valley of Oaxaca within New Spain, and in 1535 the Dominican-led Bishopric of Oaxaca was established.

The conquest set off a demographic catastrophe. Introduced diseases, chiefly smallpox and measles, cut the indigenous population from an estimated 1.5 million in 1520 to roughly 150,000 by 1650. Spanish settlers and friars arrived alongside new domestic animals, and a long process of mestizaje (racial and cultural mixing between Indigenous peoples and Europeans) began. The name "Zapotec" is an exonym derived from the Nahuatl *tzapotēcah*; the people call themselves *Be'ena'a*, "The Cloud People."

## The origin of Mesoamerican writing

Whether the Zapotec script (c. 500 BC) or earlier Olmec symbols (c. 650 BC) represent the first true writing in Mesoamerica remains debated, since it is still unclear whether the Olmec signs encode language in the same way the Zapotec glyphs do.
