# Greek mythology

Greek mythology is the body of myths and teachings belonging to the ancient Greeks, concerning their gods, heroes, and the nature of the world. The stories began in oral tradition and were later fixed in epic poetry, drama, and philosophy, where they explained natural phenomena, cultural practices, and Greek origins. They remain a foundational layer of Western literature and art.

The earliest literary sources are Homer's epics and Hesiod's *Theogony*. Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey* (c. 8th century BC) gave the canonical shape to the Trojan War and the hero's return. Hesiod's *Theogony* gives the fullest account of how the gods came into being. The *Homeric Hymns*, lyric poetry, Athenian tragedy and comedy, and later works by Hellenistic and Roman authors carried the tradition forward. Archaeological finds at Mycenaean and Minoan sites, and geometric pottery from c. 900–800 BC, often show earlier or parallel versions of the stories.

Hesiod's cosmogony begins with Chaos, a void or formlessness, from which Gaia (Earth), Uranus (Sky), and the Titans emerge. A cycle of succession runs through the myths: Uranus is overthrown by Cronus, and Cronus is overthrown by his son Zeus, who rules the Olympian gods from Mount Olympus. The Olympians are anthropomorphic, having human shape, emotions, and weaknesses, though they are immortal. Each governs a domain: Zeus the sky, Aphrodite love, Ares war. The strict count of twelve Olympians is a late idea; earlier worship included a wider crowd of minor deities and spirits.

Mythic history divides into three ages. The Age of Gods covers the Theogony and the rise of the Olympians. Next comes an age of mingling between gods and mortals, producing demigod offspring. Then the Heroic Age opens with the labours of Heracles, runs through legends such as the Argonauts, and ends with the Trojan War and its aftermath. Heroes like Heracles and Achilles were honoured in a hero cult, a religious practice of venerating the dead as figures who retain power over the living. Hero cults grounded local identity and gave epic narrative its religious weight.

From the 6th century BC, philosophers challenged the tradition. Xenophanes attacked the anthropomorphic gods as immoral; Plato rejected mythology's place in education and preferred allegorical readings. Later, Euhemerus and the Stoics argued that myths were distorted memories of real people and events, a view called Euhemerism. This rationalist thread ran alongside continuing popular belief and artistic use.

Roman culture absorbed Greek mythology through Ovid and Virgil, producing a syncretic tradition in which Greek and Roman gods were identified. After the Renaissance and Romantic revivals, modern scholars read the myths through new lenses. Comparative mythology set them beside Near Eastern and Indo-European parallels. Freud, Jung, and the structuralists treated them as expressions of universal psychology or social structure. A live debate remains about whether myths preserve kernels of real history, as some argue for the Trojan War, or whether their function was primarily religious, social, or pedagogic, but the corpus itself, fixed in Hesiod, Homer, and the tragedians, continues to supply the working material for every later interpretation.
