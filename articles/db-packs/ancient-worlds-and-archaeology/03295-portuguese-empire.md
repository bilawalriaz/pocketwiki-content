# Portuguese Empire

The Portuguese Empire (c. 1415–1999) was the first and longest-lasting European colonial empire. It began with the conquest of Ceuta in 1415 and ended when Portugal handed Macau back to China in 1999. Its core was a chain of fortified trading posts, called *feitorias*, that stretched from West Africa to East Asia, with Brazil added in 1500. Across nearly six centuries it took four distinct forms: an Asian trading empire, a Brazil-centred gold and sugar economy, a coerced African colonial system, and finally a brief post-1974 transition out of empire.

## Why Portugal Went Overseas

The starting impulse was the Reconquista, the long Christian reconquest of the Iberian Peninsula from Muslim rule. Once that ended in Portugal, energy turned outward, driven by crusading zeal, royal sponsorship, and the lure of gold and Asian spices. The caravel, a small, manoeuvrable lateen-rigged ship, let Portuguese crews push down the African Atlantic coast and return safely against prevailing winds.

Two voyages turned a coastal foothold into a global empire. In 1488 Bartolomeu Dias rounded the Cape of Good Hope, proving Africa could be circumnavigated by sea. In 1498 Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in India, opening the maritime route to Asian spices. Within a few years Portugal had strung forts from East Africa through the Indian Ocean to as far as Nagasaki in Japan, taxing and reshipping pepper, cinnamon, and cloves. In 1494 the Treaty of Tordesillas divided the non-European world with Spain along a north–south line, with Portugal getting the eastern hemisphere plus the eastern bulge of South America, which Pedro Álvares Cabral reached in 1500.

## The Spanish Shadow and the Turn to Brazil

In 1580 the Portuguese crown was absorbed into Spain during the Iberian Union. Portuguese colonies were now targets of Spain's enemies, chiefly the Dutch and English. The Dutch seized much of the Asian network, and the first empire effectively collapsed. Independence was restored in 1640 and recognised by Spain in 1663, and the empire was rebuilt around Brazil.

The 1693 discovery of gold in Minas Gerais turned Brazil into the engine of the Portuguese economy, with diamonds found in 1729 adding to the flow. Eighteenth-century reforms under the Marquis of Pombal centralised administration, expelled the Jesuits, and tightened the metropolitan grip. When Napoleon invaded Portugal in 1807, the royal court fled to Rio de Janeiro; Brazil was raised to a kingdom in 1815 and became independent in 1822 under Pedro I.

## Late Empire and the End

Shorn of Brazil, Portugal concentrated on Africa, especially Angola and Mozambique, and briefly hoped to link them coast to coast in a "Pink Map." The 1890 British Ultimatum killed the plan and forced Portugal to back down, exposing its dependence on British goodwill. The Estado Novo dictatorship under António de Oliveira Salazar (1933–1974) refused to follow the global trend toward decolonisation. It renamed colonies "overseas provinces" and justified rule with *pluricontinentalism*, the idea that Portugal was a single transcontinental state rather than a colonial power, while relying on forced labour.

The cost broke the regime. The Portuguese Colonial War (1961–1974) bled resources across Angola, Mozambique, and Guinea-Bissau; India annexed Goa in 1961; the 1974 Carnation Revolution, a nearly bloodless military coup on 25 April, overthrew the dictatorship and triggered a hasty withdrawal from Africa. The Indonesian invasion of East Timor followed in 1975. The empire's last act was the 1999 handover of Macau to China, after more than four centuries of continuous Portuguese presence in Asia.

## What the Empire Did and Did Not Do

At its height between 1500 and 1800, trade with the empire contributed roughly a fifth of Portuguese per-capita income, an enormous share for a small European kingdom, though that wealth did not prevent Portugal's relative decline within Europe. The empire's long reach depended on the caravel, the *feitoria*, and control of narrow sea lanes rather than mass settlement, and on coerced labour, including enslaved Africans shipped both to Brazil and across the Atlantic. After 1974, the mass return of settlers and mixed-race Portuguese, the *retornados*, swelled Portugal's population by roughly a tenth within a single generation.
