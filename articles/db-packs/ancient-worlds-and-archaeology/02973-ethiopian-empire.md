# Ethiopian Empire

The Ethiopian Empire, also called Abyssinia, was a Christian state in the Horn of Africa that lasted from roughly 1270 to 1974. Two facts frame its history: it is one of the oldest Christian states in the world, and it is the only major African polity that defeated a European colonial power in a major war, at Adwa in 1896.

## Solomonic foundation

Around 1270, Yekuno Amlak overthrew the Zagwe dynasty and founded the Solomonic dynasty, which would rule until 1974. To legitimise the new line, Yekuno Amlak claimed descent from the last Aksumite king and, beyond that, from King Solomon and the Queen of Sheba through a legendary son named Menelik I. This genealogy was codified in the 14th-century text *Kebra Nagast* ("Glory of the Kings") and fused political authority with biblical ancestry. From a small highland base, the empire grew rapidly. Amda Seyon I (r. 1314–1344) roughly doubled its territory against neighbouring Muslim sultanates, anchoring a Christian regional power that would outlast its rivals.

## A short golden age, then near-destruction

The empire's high point of centralised power came under Zara Yaqob (r. 1434–1468), who tightened administration, built churches, and projected hegemony across the Horn. Beginning in 1529, the imam Ahmad Gran, ruler of the Adal Sultanate and supplied with firearms by the Ottoman Empire, invaded Ethiopia in the name of jihad. The war devastated the highlands and forced the emperor into hiding. Ethiopia survived because Portuguese soldiers armed with muskets intervened, ending the campaign with the Ethiopian-Portuguese victory at the Battle of Wayna Daga in 1543.

## Recovery and fragmentation

After the Adal war, Ethiopia absorbed Ottoman pressure on its coast and large-scale Oromo migrations into the south. Emperor Fasilides founded Gondar as a new capital in 1636, opening a 17th–18th-century cultural golden age, the Gondarine period, known for castles, painted churches, and new literary traditions.

That order collapsed in the 18th century. After the deaths of Emperors Iyasu II and Iyoas I, the monarchy became ceremonial while regional warlords called *Ras* fought for territory. The 1769–1855 stretch is the *Zemene Mesafint*, the "Era of the Princes," roughly 85 years of decentralised warlord rule rather than an empire in any normal sense.

## Reunification and modern expansion

Tewodros II (r. 1855–1868) ended the *Zemene Mesafint* by force, reuniting the empire and beginning cautious modernisation. His successor Yohannes IV repulsed Egyptian and Mahdist invasions. Then Menelik II (r. 1889–1913) did two things that defined modern Ethiopia: he conquered the southern and western kingdoms that today form the country's borders, and in 1896 he defeated an Italian invasion at the Battle of Adwa. The Adwa victory, won with modern imported rifles, secured Ethiopian independence and made Ethiopia a diplomatic exception on a colonised continent.

## Occupation, restoration, fall

Italy returned in 1935, occupied Ethiopia in 1936, and merged it with Eritrea and Italian Somaliland into Italian East Africa. Emperor Haile Selassie fled into exile. British forces restored him to the throne in 1941, after which Ethiopia joined the United Nations, and in 1952 Eritrea was federated under Ethiopian rule. The monarchy issued constitutions in 1931 and 1955, but late in Selassie's reign the 1973 Wollo famine, a stalled economy, and student unrest drained legitimacy. In 1974 a military committee called the Derg deposed Selassie and formally abolished the monarchy in 1975, ending the 700-year Solomonic line.

## What holds the arc together

A Christian, Solomonic identity anchored by the *Kebra Nagast* let successive emperors claim authority that transcended any single region, and the *Zemene Mesafint* exposed the limit of that claim: when the line weakened, the ideology could not hold the *Ras* in check. Geography and firepower set the other constraint. The Ethiopian Highlands were defensible enough to resist Muslim neighbours for centuries, but the 16th-century jihad and the 20th-century Italian war both showed that firearms, and later artillery, could overwhelm terrain advantage. At Adwa, Ethiopian access to modern rifles turned the highlands into a weapon rather than just a refuge.

A few terms are worth keeping distinct. The Solomonic dynasty (c. 1270–1974) is the ruling house; the Aksumite Empire is its much older predecessor, and the Zagwe dynasty sits in between. The *Chewa* were regiments that made up the imperial army, paid through land grants called *Gult*, which is why decentralisation after Iyasu II also meant military fragmentation. Scholars still debate who exactly the last Zagwe king was, how disruptive the Oromo migrations really were, and how to weigh the religious, dynastic, and military causes of the *Zemene Mesafint*, but the broad arc, Christian highland empire, near-destruction by jihad, warlord interlude, reunification, anti-colonial victory, and junta-led collapse, is well established.
