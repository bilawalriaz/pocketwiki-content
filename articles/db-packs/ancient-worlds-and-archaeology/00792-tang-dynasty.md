# Tang dynasty

The Tang dynasty (618–907 CE) was a Chinese imperial state that combined wide territorial reach, a cosmopolitan capital, and a working merit-based bureaucracy. Its founder, Li Yuan (Emperor Gaozu), seized power in 618 during the collapse of the preceding Sui dynasty. Across its 289 years the Tang set administrative, cultural, and technological patterns that shaped China and, through transmission to Japan, Korea, and Vietnam, much of East Asia.

## How the dynasty was built

The first three reigns established the institutional and territorial base. Gaozu's son Li Shimin seized the throne in 626 at the Xuanwu Gate Incident, killing two brothers, and ruled as Emperor Taizong (626–649). Taizong's campaigns destroyed the Eastern Turkic Khaganate and brought Inner Asia under Tang control, reopened the Silk Road, and sponsored a revised legal code and Buddhist institutions. A civil-service examination system, testing candidates on the Confucian classics and literary composition, supplied officials alongside older aristocratic recruitment. The Three Departments and Six Ministries became the template for Chinese imperial administration for the next millennium.

## The cosmopolitan high point

Emperor Xuanzong's 44-year reign (712–756) was the dynasty's peak. He patronised Buddhism, Taoism, and foreign trade, and in 747 temporarily abolished the death penalty. Chang'an, the capital, was the largest city in the world, hosting Sogdian, Persian, Arab, Korean, and Indian communities alongside Chinese residents. Tang poetry by Li Bai, Du Fu, and Wang Wei belongs to this period. Woodblock printing emerged in this era, allowing scriptures and literature to circulate in large volume.

Two administrative features underpinned this prosperity. The equal-field system allotted state land to households in return for tax and labour obligations, limiting landlessness and large private estates. The fubing system recruited soldiers from farming households, who drilled in rotation and returned to their fields, tying military manpower to the agricultural economy. These systems kept tax revenue, military strength, and peasant livelihood in balance.

## Wu Zetian and the interruption

Between 690 and 705 the dynasty was interrupted by the Wu Zhou, a short-lived separate state founded by Empress Wu Zetian, the only woman to rule China as emperor in her own right. She had risen from concubine to regent, used Buddhist prophecies such as the *Great Cloud Sutra* to legitimise her claim, and purged rivals at court. Her rule weakened the old northwestern aristocratic clans and widened access to office through the examinations, but factional struggle continued after her deposition in 705.

## The An Lushan turning point

The An Lushan rebellion (755–763) is the dynasty's decisive break. An Lushan, a general of Sogdian-Turkic origin, turned against Xuanzong; the fighting devastated the north, killed tens of thousands, and forced the court to flee Chang'an. Suppression required granting regional military governors, the *jiedushi*, autonomous command and hereditary succession. Once the war ended, central tax revenues shrank, the equal-field system broke down as commanders and aristocrats accumulated land, and displaced peasants lost the basis for livelihood and military service.

## Slow decline and fall

From the 760s the dynasty governed a shrinking core. Emperor Wuzong's 845 suppression of Buddhism closed monasteries, confiscated their lands, and forced tens of thousands of monks and nuns to return to secular life, partly to refill the state treasury. The Huang Chao rebellion (874–884) drew on agrarian unrest after decades of drought, tax flight, and banditry. After Huang Chao's defeat the regional militarists who had suppressed him, most notably Zhu Wen, held the real power. Zhu Wen seized Chang'an in 901, assassinated the emperor in 904, and in 907 deposed Emperor Ai to found the Later Liang, the first of the Five Dynasties that succeeded the Tang in the north.

## Culture, technology, and reach

Tang poetry set standards still used to define literary Chinese. Chang'an functioned as a hub of trans-Asian trade, with Chinese goods reaching the Middle East and Africa, and Persian and Arab merchants resident in the capital. Tang armies fought Tibet to the southwest and met the Abbasid Caliphate at the Battle of Talas in 751; a defeat there is sometimes credited with transmitting papermaking to the Islamic world. Sea routes through the South China Sea linked Chinese ports to Southeast Asia, India, and the Persian Gulf, and the Grand Canal moved grain and revenue from the productive south to the northern frontier. Tang influence reached Japan through Buddhism, written Chinese, and city planning at Nara and Kyoto, though the degree of direct imitation versus independent adoption remains debated.

## Open questions

Population figures for the Tang remain uncertain: household registers appear to undercount, and modern reconstructions vary by tens of millions. Traditional Confucian historiography vilified Wu Zetian, while some modern scholars reassess her administrative reach positively.
