# Chola dynasty

The Chola dynasty was a Tamil imperial power from southern India whose peak, between the 9th and 13th centuries CE, produced a maritime empire that reshaped trade and culture across South and Southeast Asia. The dynasty is best understood as the force that converted a regional Tamil kingdom into the most powerful Indian Ocean state of its era, built on military conquest, naval projection, temple patronage, and administrative reach into the Kaveri River delta.

## Origins and the long interregnum

The earliest datable references to the Cholas appear in Ashokan Edicts of the 3rd century BCE. Their early history is mostly preserved in Sangam literature, roughly 600 BCE to 300 CE, which records kings such as Karikala and Kocengannan, though their exact chronology remains uncertain. The Chola heartland lay in the fertile Kaveri valley, with early capitals at Uraiyur and Kaveripattinam.

After the Sangam period ended around 300 CE, Chola power collapsed into a long interregnum lasting roughly until 848 CE. During this time they were displaced by the Kalabhras, an obscure dynasty that invaded Tamil country, and later overshadowed by the Pallavas and Pandyas. A branch called the Telugu-Cholas ruled in the Rayalaseema region while the main line faded from prominence.

## Imperial Cholas: rebuilding an empire

Vijayalaya Chola re-founded the dynasty in 848 CE, beginning the Imperial Chola period. His successors Aditya I and Parantaka I expanded territory by defeating the Pallavas and Pandyas in the early 10th century, restoring Chola rule across much of south India.

The empire reached its zenith in the 11th century under Rajaraja I and Rajendra I. Rajaraja I consolidated the conquests of his predecessors and built the Brihadeeswarar Temple at Thanjavur, a monumental Shiva temple that remains the architectural signature of the dynasty. Rajendra I pushed Chola power to its furthest extent: he campaigned north as far as the Ganges, founded a new capital at Gangaikonda Cholapuram to celebrate those victories, and in 1025 CE launched naval raids on the Srivijaya Empire, a maritime power based in modern Indonesia and Malaysia that had been a major Indian Ocean trade rival. These expeditions extended Chola influence into Southeast Asia and the Maldives, making the dynasty the dominant naval and economic force in the region.

## Later Cholas and decline

In 1070 CE Kulothunga Chola I, of mixed Chola-Chalukya lineage, ascended the throne and began the Later Chola period. Capable rulers such as Kulothunga III held the core territories, but Chola power came under growing pressure from the resurgent Pandya dynasty and the Hoysalas. Kulothunga III was defeated by the Pandyas in 1215–1216 CE, after which Chola control collapsed rapidly. They lost Sri Lanka and their traditional mainland territories, and by 1279 CE the dynasty had been fully absorbed into the Pandyan Empire.

## Administration, culture, and religion

Chola administration was highly organized, centered on the wealthy Kaveri delta. The dynasty was a major patron of Hinduism, particularly Shaivism, the tradition devoted to Shiva, and its temple-building program was both a religious act and a political statement of imperial reach. Chola conquests carried Hindu cultural influence into Southeast Asia, contributing to the wider spread of Indian religious and artistic forms across the region.

The Imperial Chola era was a golden age for Tamil literature, producing works such as the *Periapuranam*, a Tamil hagiography of Shaiva saints, and *Nannul*, an influential grammatical text. Through their feudatories, subordinate rulers who owed allegiance to the Chola crown, the dynasty also patronized Telugu literature, extending its cultural footprint beyond the Tamil-speaking core.

## Open questions

The internal chronology and succession of the early Chola kings from Sangam literature remain unsettled, and the interregnum between roughly 300 and 848 CE is poorly documented, leaving the dynasty's fate during those centuries largely unknown. A modern debate in which some Tamil film personalities claim the Cholas were not Hindu conflicts with the historical record of their Shaiva patronage and temple construction, including the Brihadeeswarar Temple.
