# Isma'il Pasha of Egypt

Isma'il Pasha (1830–1895), known as Ismail the Magnificent, was the Khedive (a hereditary title meaning "sovereign prince," secured from the Ottoman Sultan in 1867) of Egypt and Sudan from 1863 to 1879. He aimed to align Egypt with Europe through rapid reforms and territorial expansion, but the cost of that ambition produced a debt crisis that removed him and handed Egyptian finances to European powers.

## Reforms: Building a European Egypt

Isma'il poured borrowed money into infrastructure. He built more than 900 miles of railroads, 5,000 miles of telegraph lines, 8,000 miles of irrigation canals, and 4,500 schools. Education spending rose tenfold, a national library was founded in 1871, and a new Cairo quarter was laid out on a Parisian model. He remodelled the customs service, created a sugar industry, and expanded cotton exports. In 1866 he established an advisory assembly of delegates that gained influence over government, and that year he replaced Ottoman Turkish with Egyptian Arabic as the language of administration, a break with Cairo's former imperial culture.

## Imperial Reach and the Ethiopian Defeats

Isma'il pushed Egypt's African frontiers past the Nile valley. After the Ottoman Empire ceded the Habesh Eyalet (its old Red Sea province) to him in 1865, his general Werner Munzinger Pasha annexed the Bogos region in 1872, and Isma'il took Darfur in 1874. His attempt to seize the Ethiopian highlands, a step needed to control the Nile basin, ended in defeat at Gundet on 16 November 1875, where his son was captured, and again at Gura in March 1876. A cold war followed until the 1884 Hewett Treaty returned Bogos to Ethiopia. The Red Sea Province he created later fell to Italy and became the nucleus of Eritrea; he also held the northern Somali coast until 1884.

## Suez, Debt, and the Loss of Independence

The Suez Canal was the centrepiece of his reign. He renegotiated the original concession (with Emperor Napoleon III as arbitrator), oversaw Egypt's share of the construction, and staged a lavish opening festival in 1869. The prestige was real, and so was the cost: spending, wars, and borrowing pushed the national debt from £3 million to more than £100 million. In 1875, he sold Egypt's Suez Canal shares to Britain for £3,976,582, giving Britain a strategic stake in the route to India and costing Egypt its most valuable asset.

In 1876, Britain and France imposed joint control over Egyptian finances, creating the Caisse de la Dette, an international Debt Commission that serviced creditors, and Mixed Courts in which Europeans were tried by judges from their own countries rather than Egyptian courts. By 1878, Isma'il was reduced to a ceremonial role inside a cabinet of European ministers.

## The Urabi Revolt and Deposition

The squeeze triggered the Urabi Revolt, a nationalist uprising (1879–1882) led by Colonel Ahmed Urabi against the Khedive and European financial control. Isma'il, hoping to use the movement to recover real power, did little to suppress it. When Britain and France demanded the reinstatement of their ministers in 1879, he refused. On 26 June 1879, Ottoman Sultan Abdülhamid II, pressured by those two powers, deposed him and installed his son Tewfik Pasha. Isma'il was exiled first to Italy, then to Constantinople, where he died on 2 March 1895. Four years after his fall, Britain occupied Egypt in 1882.
