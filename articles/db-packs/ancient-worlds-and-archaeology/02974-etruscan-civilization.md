# Etruscan civilization

The Etruscans were an indigenous Iron Age civilization of central Italy who flourished from roughly 900 BC until Rome absorbed their last territories in 27 BC. They formed a federation of independent city-states in a region called Etruria, spoke a non-Indo-European language, and influenced early Roman religion, art, engineering, and government more than any other pre-Roman people. The central point is that the Etruscans were not a migrant people from the east, as Greek and Roman writers long claimed, but a local population whose distinctive culture grew out of Italy's earlier Bronze Age Villanovan culture.

## Origins and identity

Greek and Roman authors insisted the Etruscans had come from Lydia in Anatolia or from Greek settlers, but this was propaganda or guesswork. Modern archaeology shows continuous development from the Villanovan culture of the early Iron Age, and recent DNA studies confirm that Etruscans were genetically similar to their Latin and other Italic neighbors, with no Anatolian or eastern Mediterranean signal. They called themselves the *Rasenna*, "the people." Greeks knew them as Tyrrhenians; Romans as *Tusci* or *Etrusci*. Indigenous origin explains why the Etruscan language, although written in a Greek-derived alphabet, is a true isolate unrelated to any Indo-European tongue.

## Language and writing

Around 700 BC the Etruscans borrowed the alphabet from Greek colonies in southern Italy, which is why their surviving texts are readable letter by letter even when the words are not. The language is a pre-Indo-European isolate, linked only to two small relatives, Raetic in the Alps and Lemnian in the Aegean, that together form the proposed Tyrsenian family. About 13,000 inscriptions survive, most of them short epitaphs, dedications, or labels, so the language is only partly deciphered and Etruscan literature, which Roman writers claimed was substantial, is effectively lost. The single longest text, the *Liber Linteus*, is a ritual calendar written on linen wrappings of an Egyptian mummy.

## Society and government

Etruscan politics was a patchwork of independent city-states loosely bound by a shared religious league, the Dodecapolis, traditionally twelve major cities that sent representatives to an annual meeting at the Fanum Voltumnae sanctuary. Each city was governed by an aristocratic elite, and its leader, the *lucomo*, doubled as chief priest. Religion and politics were therefore fused: magistrates read the will of the gods through haruspicy, the inspection of sacrificed animal entrails, and augury, the reading of bird flights, rather than ruling on a purely secular basis. Etruscan women appear in tomb paintings and inscriptions participating in banquets and public life to a degree unusual for the ancient Mediterranean, more visible than in contemporary Greece or early Rome.

## Economy and reach

Etruria sat on rich deposits of copper and iron, and its cities lay on coastal and river routes that tied it into Mediterranean exchange with Greeks, Phoenicians, and Carthaginians. From this base the Etruscans expanded north into the Po Valley and south into Campania, and around 500 BC Etruscan power reached its maximum extent, stretching from the Tiber valley to the Alps.

## Decline and absorption by Rome

Etruscan fortunes reversed in the fifth century BC. Around 540 BC a joint Etruscan-Carthaginian fleet fought the inconclusive Battle of Alalia against Greek colonists in Corsica, and in 474 BC the Etruscans were decisively beaten by the Syracusan Greeks at the Battle of Cumae, costing them Campania and their naval standing. The expanding Roman Republic then dismantled Etruria city by city: Rome overthrew the last Etruscan monarchy on the Capitoline in 508 BC, sacked the great city of Veii in 396 BC, and crushed Etruscan forces at Lake Vadimone in 310 BC. By 90 BC the survivors held Roman citizenship, and the formal end came in 27 BC when Augustus incorporated the last Etruscan communities into the Roman Empire.

## Cultural legacy

Etruscan art fused Greek models with local traditions, producing the painted tombs of Tarquinia and the great terracotta statues and sarcophagi that still survive because the Etruscans built cities of wood, not stone. Their religious ideas, the household cult of the dead, the temple layout with frontal steps, the ritual calendar, the reading of entrails, passed directly into Rome, and the last three kings of Rome before the Republic were Etruscan. The asymmetry between Etruscan visual culture, which speaks clearly, and its language and inner life, which remain only partly legible, is what makes the civilization hard to study.
