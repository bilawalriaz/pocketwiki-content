# Roman–Persian wars

The Roman–Persian wars were a series of intermittent conflicts, spanning roughly seven centuries (54 BC to 628 AD), between the Greco-Roman world (the Roman Republic and Empire, later the Byzantine Empire) and the Iranian world (the Parthian Empire, then the Sasanian Empire). They were fought mainly over frontier territories such as Armenia and Mesopotamia, and despite enormous cost they produced almost no permanent territorial change. The mutual exhaustion of both empires left them unable to resist the Arab Rashidun Caliphate in the 7th century.

## The two sides and the stakes

Rome and Parthia first met at the Euphrates River in 64 BC, after the Parthians absorbed the old Seleucid lands in Iran and Mesopotamia. The first great shock was Roman: at Carrhae in 53 BC the Parthians killed the Roman general Crassus, fixing a pattern in which Roman armies could invade deep into Iran but could not hold what they took. The Armenian highlands, sandwiched between the two empires, became a permanent bone of contention, with each side installing client kings backed by subsidies and troops.

Parthian armies were cavalry-based, mobile and strong in defence, but weak in siege warfare, which mattered because Roman cities in the east were heavily fortified. The result was a built-in stalemate: Persia held the strategic initiative whenever it stayed on the defence, while Rome had to attack across Mesopotamia to keep its frontier, and attacking was always expensive.

## A new Persian dynasty: the Sasanians (224 AD)

In 224 AD a more centralised and aggressive dynasty, the Sasanians, overthrew the Parthians, and war intensified. In 244 the Sasanian king Shapur I defeated the Romans at Misiche, and the next emperor had to buy peace. In 260, at the Battle of Edessa, Shapur captured the Roman emperor Valerian himself. After this disaster a local Palmyrene leader named Odaenathus forced the Persians to withdraw from Roman territory, but the prestige damage to Rome was lasting.

The sack of Ctesiphon, the Sasanian capital, by the Romans in 165 AD carried disease back to the Roman army: the Antonine Plague killed large numbers of soldiers and civilians and raged for a generation, weakening Roman eastern operations for decades.

## Tactical evolution and the long frontier

Across these centuries both armies converged on heavy armoured cavalry called **cataphracts**, effective against infantry but expensive to equip and maintain. The Sasanians also learned Roman-style siegecraft, erasing one of Rome's main advantages.

Because direct war was so costly, both empires increasingly fought through proxies: Arab client kingdoms such as the Lakhmids for Persia and the Ghassanids for Rome, plus nomadic groups like the Huns. Rome built a fortified eastern frontier known as the *limes*, a chain of forts and frontier cities such as Dara, manned by garrison troops called *limitanei*. The Persian equivalent was the *marzoban*, a frontier commander governing a border province.

## Key turning points

- **63 AD**: A treaty ended a war over Armenia; a Parthian prince named Tiridates ruled Armenia as a Roman vassal.
- **114 to 117 AD**: Emperor Trajan annexed Armenia and Mesopotamia, but revolts forced a withdrawal.
- **298 AD**: The emperor Galerius defeated the Sasanian king Narseh, securing Roman control of territory east of the Tigris and over Armenia.
- **363 AD**: Emperor Julian was killed invading Persia; his successor Jovian had to cede territory just to let the Roman army march home.
- **387 AD**: A treaty divided Armenia between the two empires.
- **502 to 506 AD**: The Anastasian War ended the longest period of peace; the Byzantines built the fortress of Dara to hold the new line.
- **532 AD**: Justinian I and Khosrau I signed the "Perpetual Peace," which returned occupied territory and included a Roman payment.
- **540 to 562 AD**: The Lazic War over the Caucasus kingdom of Lazica ended with the Fifty-Year Peace; Persia gave up Lazica in return for an annual Byzantine payment.
- **591 AD**: Byzantines helped restore Khosrau II to the Sasanian throne, and he returned territory as thanks.
- **602 to 628 AD**: The final war began after the Byzantine usurper Phocas seized the throne, giving Khosrau II a pretext to invade. Sasanian armies conquered Syria, Egypt, and most of Anatolia, bringing Byzantium close to collapse. Emperor Heraclius then mounted a counter-offensive, allied with Turkic horsemen from the steppe, and at the **Battle of Nineveh in 627** shattered the Persian army. Khosrau II was overthrown, and the peace of 628 restored the pre-war borders.

## Why the wars were so inconclusive

Geography and logistics kept the border roughly fixed. Mesopotamia and the Armenian highlands formed a natural defensive belt: any army marching across them had to live off thin land, while defenders could concentrate at river crossings. Neither side had the logistical base to occupy the other's heartland; Trajan reached deep into Mesopotamia, but his conquests were abandoned within three years. Victory was rarely total: even after capturing Valerian, the Sasanians could not hold Roman provinces for long, and even after deep Sasanian advances in 602 to 620, Heraclius reversed them.

Treaties, not conquest, were the normal endpoint. The settlements of 63, 298, 363, 387, 532, 562, and 591 all returned prisoners, fixed frontiers, and arranged payments or subsidies in lieu of land. The "Perpetual Peace" of 532 and the "Fifty-Year Peace" of 562 show the same bargain: no annexation, but regular money flowing from one side to the other.

## Religion and the limits of sources

Religious difference sharpened but did not cause the wars. Zoroastrian Persia and Christian Rome used each other's faith as propaganda: Roman emperors styled themselves protectors of Eastern Christians living under Persian rule, while Sasanian kings stressed their divine right. Both sides repeatedly allied with or against co-religionists, so religion was a tool rather than the engine. A practical problem for historians is that Parthian history is known mainly from Roman and Greek writers, while Sasanian history survives mainly in later Arabic, Armenian, and Syriac texts, so the Persian side is often seen through hostile or distant eyes.

## The cost: a power vacuum for Islam

The Byzantine–Sasanian war of 602 to 628 was the most destructive of all. Decades of total mobilisation drained treasuries, depopulated villages, and left veteran armies dead. The Sasanian state collapsed into civil war after 628 and fell to the Arab Rashidun Caliphate within a generation. Byzantium survived, but lost Syria, Egypt, and North Africa, shrinking to a smaller, poorer state centred on Anatolia.
