# Song dynasty

The Song dynasty (960–1279) was an imperial Chinese dynasty that reunified much of China after the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period. It is split into two halves: the Northern Song (960–1127), based at Kaifeng, and the Southern Song (1127–1279), based at Lin'an (modern Hangzhou), separated by the loss of the north to the Jurchen Jin. Its economy, technology, and philosophy were unusually productive, while its military position remained weak, and the dynasty ended in conquest by the Mongols.

The founding moment was the usurpation of the Later Zhou by Emperor Taizu in 960, who reunified the realm and built a strong central state with a civil service recruited by merit. The defining external problem was militarily strong neighbours to the north. The Khitan Liao dynasty was bought off rather than defeated: the Chanyuan Treaty of 1005 fixed peace in exchange for annual Song tribute, a formula later repeated with the Tangut Western Xia. The same arrangement collapsed when the Song allied with the Jurchen Jin to destroy the Liao; the Jin turned on its partner, and in 1127 the Jingkang Incident saw the Jin sack Kaifeng and capture the court, forcing the dynasty south of the Yangtze. The Southern Song rebuilt a robust economy and a powerful navy, with a permanent headquarters at Dinghai from 1132, to hold the Yangtze line, but the northern frontier never recovered. After the Mongols destroyed the Jin in 1234, they inherited the same threat, and in 1279 the Battle of Yamen extinguished the last Song resistance, completing the Yuan conquest.

The economy grew on agricultural foundations. Early-ripening Champa rice from Vietnam spread northward, lifting yields and helping population roughly double between the 10th and 11th centuries. Cities such as Kaifeng and Hangzhou grew past a million residents, and one modern estimate puts 12th-century Song GDP at around three times that of Europe. The state withdrew from direct economic control, leaving room for a private sector, merchant guilds, and long-distance maritime trade. The Song also issued *jiaozi*, the world's first government paper money.

Society became more literate and mobile. Competitive civil service examinations, rooted in Confucian classics, opened office to those who could pass them, and a scholar-official class supplemented by local gentry took over much of regional administration. Cities developed entertainment quarters and clubs around poetry, tea, and art. Women held limited legal standing, including property inheritance under specific conditions, and produced figures such as the *ci* poet Li Qingzhao.

Culture and technology flourished in the same period. Landscape painting and the *ci* lyric form reached their classical peak. The dominant philosophy became Neo-Confucianism, which Zhu Xi systematised by weaving Buddhist and Daoist ideas into a Confucian framework; his commentaries on the Four Books were later made the standard examination curriculum. The era produced a dense cluster of practical inventions: gunpowder weapons such as fire arrows, bombs, and the fire lance, with the 1044 military manual *Wujing Zongyao* providing the earliest known written formulas; the magnetic compass for navigation; movable type printing, devised by Bi Sheng around 1040; the pound lock for canals; and Su Song's astronomical clock tower. Polymaths like Shen Kuo advanced science, mathematics, and engineering alongside these.

Internal politics were unsettled. Chancellor Wang Anshi's New Policies (1069–1085) provoked factional conflict at court that long outlasted the reforms themselves. Scholars still debate how meritocratic the examination system really was: the exams opened a path upward, but the cost of the education they required gave land-owning families a continuing edge, and the balance between local gentry power and central authority remains a live question. The combination of tribute, defence, and retreat let the Song preserve commercial and intellectual life through nearly three centuries of pressure from successive steppe empires.
