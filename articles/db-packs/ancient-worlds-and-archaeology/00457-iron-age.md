# Iron Age

The Iron Age is the last of the three Metal Ages (after Copper and Bronze), defined by smelted iron and steel replacing bronze as the dominant material for tools and weapons. Because smelting, diffusion, and written records proceeded at different speeds in different places, the epoch begins and ends at very different dates depending on the region. The label is an archaeological convention tied to tool material, not a universal calendar period.

The shift rests on a specific technological change. Smelting iron means extracting it from iron ore by heating, which requires temperatures above 1,250 °C (2,280 °F), beyond what Neolithic kilns could reach. The metal then has to be purified, adjusted in carbon content to become steel, and hot-worked to gain useful hardness. Bronze melts at much lower temperatures and was far easier to produce with earlier kilns. The bottleneck was furnace capability, not the idea of using metal.

A common confusion is meteoric iron. Beads of meteoric iron, a natural iron–nickel alloy from fallen meteorites, appear in Egyptian burials at Gerzeh c. 3200 BC, and tentative iron-working traces exist at Kaman-Kalehöyük in Turkey c. 2200–2000 BC. These are not Iron Age iron. The age only begins when smelted iron enters common use.

The chronology is approximate and regional:

- c. 1300 BC: Iron Age begins in Anatolia, the Caucasus, and Southeast Europe.
- c. 1200 BC: Iron replaces bronze in the ancient Near East, coinciding with the Late Bronze Age collapse.
- c. 1200–1100 BC: Iron technology spreads across the Mediterranean and into South Asia.
- c. 800 BC: Central and Western Europe enter the Iron Age, marked by the Hallstatt culture.
- c. 500 BC: Iron Age reaches Northern Europe and Scandinavia.
- 1st century BC: Roman conquests end the Iron Age across much of Central and Western Europe.
- c. AD 800: The Germanic Iron Age ends in Scandinavia, giving way to the Viking Age.

In the ancient Near East the epoch is conventionally bounded by c. 1200 BC (collapse) and c. 550 BC (the Achaemenid Empire), though the end date is debated.

Regional shape matters. In Europe the Iron Age divides into Hallstatt (c. 800–450 BC) and the succeeding La Tène (c. 450 BC until Roman conquest). In China, written records predate smelted iron, so the term is rarely used. In India the period is associated with the Painted Grey Ware culture (c. 1200 BC to the 3rd century BC), and iron reached Japan and Korea via the Korean Peninsula and China in the last centuries BC. Across much of sub-Saharan Africa, populations moved directly from stone tools to iron without a Bronze Age stage, and some archaeologists argue for independent invention of iron metallurgy there as early as 2000 BC, a claim still under investigation.

Two older ideas should be set aside. The "Hittite monopoly" on iron in the Late Bronze Age is no longer a scholarly consensus. And the tidy notion that the Iron Age ends where written history begins has not generalised, because literacy and metallurgy arrived independently in different societies. The three-age system itself (Stone, Bronze, Iron) was a 19th-century European framework, a shorthand for dominant tool material.
