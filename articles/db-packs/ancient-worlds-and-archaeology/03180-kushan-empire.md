# Kushan Empire

The Kushan Empire was a Central Asian state built by the Yuezhi, an Indo-European nomadic people who migrated west after defeat by the Xiongnu around 160 BC and settled in Bactria (modern Afghanistan and Uzbekistan). From roughly 30 to 375 CE, the Kushans controlled the land bridge between Han China, the Roman Empire, and India, taxing Silk Road caravans and transmitting Buddhism along the way. A **nomadic people** is one who moves seasonally with herds rather than living in fixed cities. The Yuezhi were one such group, and the Kushans emerged as one of their five branches.

## How the empire was built

Kujula Kadphises united the Yuezhi tribes around 30 CE and conquered Kabul, Paktiya, and Gandhara. **Gandhara** is a region in the northwest of modern Pakistan, long a meeting point of Greek, Iranian, and Indian cultures. The empire reached its maximum size under Kanishka the Great (r. c. 127–c. 150), whose rule, confirmed by the Rabatak inscription, stretched from parts of Afghanistan, Pakistan, and Central Asia deep into the Indian Gangetic plain, including Saketa, Kausambi, and Pataliputra. Administration used Kshatrapas (provincial governors, a title borrowed from older Iranian practice) and Mahakshatrapas (great satraps), with twin capitals at Purushapura (modern Peshawar) and Mathura. This long stretch of stability and trade is called the Pax Kushana.

## A syncretic culture

The Kushans blended Greek, Iranian, and Indian traditions. They wrote the Bactrian language, an Eastern Iranian tongue, in a modified Greek alphabet left over from the Hellenistic kingdoms that had ruled Bactria after Alexander the Great. Their coins depicted more than thirty deities drawn from all three worlds. Art from Gandhara fused Hellenistic naturalism (Western-style drapery and curly hair) with Buddhist iconography, giving the Buddha a body that looks Greek but a meaning that is Indian. Coins were struck in gold, silver, and copper. The gold pieces, called Dinara (a name borrowed from the Roman *denarius*), were once thought to be melted-down Roman currency, but modern analysis shows the metal has a distinct origin, with high platinum and palladium content.

## Patronage of Buddhism

The Kushans were the chief sponsors of Buddhism's expansion into Central Asia and China. Kanishka is remembered in Buddhist tradition for convening a great council. Emperor Huvishka (r. c. 150–c. 190) is linked to some of the earliest epigraphic evidence for the Buddha Amitabha and may have followed Mahayana Buddhism, the "Great Vehicle" branch that emphasises bodhisattvas and universal salvation. Kushan-era coins show the Buddha and bodhisattvas such as Maitreya, evidence that the faith had become respectable enough for imperial imagery. Missionaries like Lokaksema worked in Chinese capitals, translating scriptures.

## Decline and successors

After Vasudeva I (r. c. 190–c. 230), the last of the "Great Kushans," the empire split. The Sasanian Empire, the Persian power that replaced the Parthians, invaded around 240 CE and installed vassal rulers called the Kushano-Sasanians in the west. The eastern rump, known as the Little Kushans, held the Punjab until roughly 270–350 CE before absorption by the Gupta Empire. The Kidarites overran the Kushano-Sasanians around 350 CE, ending the last major Kushan polity, and Alchon Huns finished the remaining fragments in the 5th century.

## What remains uncertain

The exact boundary between the broader Yuezhi and the Kushan branch is still debated. The source of the gold in Kushan coinage is unknown, though it is not Roman. The start of Kanishka's reign was long contested, but 127 CE is now generally accepted following the research of Harry Falk.
