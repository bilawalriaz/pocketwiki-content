# Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire

The Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire (1519–1521) destroyed the dominant power in Mesoamerica. A few hundred Spanish soldiers, thousands of Indigenous allies, and European disease brought down Tenochtitlan, the Aztec island-capital, and replaced Aztec rule with the Spanish colony of New Spain, permanently reshaping the region's demographics, politics, and culture.

The Aztecs ruled through hegemony, not direct administration. The Triple Alliance of Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan, formed in 1428, projected power through tributary city-states that kept their own rulers. This arrangement rested on the perception of military invincibility and was fragile: subject peoples resented Aztec tribute, and any sign of Aztec weakness could unravel the system.

Cortés landed on the Gulf coast in 1519 with a small expedition, defying Governor Diego Velázquez of Cuba, who had ordered him not to sail. He founded Villa Rica de la Vera Cruz and exploited the empire's structural weakness by allying with the Tlaxcalans and Tetzcocans, city-states hostile to Aztec rule. A second decisive asset was Doña Marina (La Malinche), a multilingual Nahua woman who translated Nahua and Maya, mediated between Spanish and Indigenous worldviews, and served as strategist and diplomat throughout the campaign.

As Cortés marched inland, he massacred the population of Cholula, a major religious center, demonstrating Spanish willingness to use overwhelming violence and deterring resistance from other cities. On 8 November 1519, the Spanish entered Tenochtitlan and were initially received by Emperor Moctezuma II, elected *huey tlatoani* (emperor) in 1502. The Spanish took Moctezuma captive and governed through him as a puppet. The arrangement collapsed in mid-1520 when Pedro de Alvarado, commanding in Cortés's absence, massacred Aztec nobles during a religious festival, sparking a citywide uprising in which Moctezuma died. Sources disagree on whether the Spanish or his own people killed him.

On 30 June 1520 ("La Noche Triste"), the Spanish and their allies fled Tenochtitlan at heavy cost and regrouped in Tlaxcala. Smallpox, arriving with the Spanish and devastating a population with no immunity, peaked in Tenochtitlan in mid-October 1520 and killed the new emperor, Cuitláhuac. Cortés returned in late December 1520 with a reinforced army, and in late April 1521 launched 13 purpose-built brigantines, small maneuverable sailing ships, onto Lake Texcoco, taking naval control around the island city.

The siege of Tenochtitlan began on 10 May 1521. The Spanish cut food and water supplies and methodically destroyed the city. Cuauhtémoc, the last Aztec emperor, surrendered on 13 August 1521 after roughly eight months of fighting. The city was almost totally destroyed; its ruins became the site of Mexico City. In October 1522, Charles V named Cortés captain-general of New Spain.

The aftermath reorganized Mesoamerican society. The Spanish crown authorized the *encomienda* system, granting colonists the right to Indigenous labor and tribute, and built on pre-existing patterns of forced service. Catholic missionaries worked to convert the population; the Aztec education system was replaced by limited church education, and many Indigenous cultural and religious practices were suppressed. Because most Spanish colonists were single men, widespread union with Indigenous women produced a large *mestizo* population, the demographic foundation of modern Mexico.

Historians continue to debate several points. Modern scholars are skeptical that Cortés immediately imprisoned Moctezuma on entry to Tenochtitlan, suggesting captivity came later. Indigenous accounts describe eight supernatural omens foretelling the conquest, which most scholars read as retrospective explanations after the defeat rather than literal prophecy. The claim that Moctezuma believed Cortés was the returning god Quetzalcoatl is disputed; many ethnohistorians argue Aztec leaders perceived the Spanish as powerful but rational outsiders, and the myth appears mainly in later European writings. Scholars still weigh the relative weight of Spanish steel, cavalry, and gunpowder versus Indigenous alliances versus the catastrophic smallpox epidemic, with most modern accounts treating all three as jointly necessary and none individually sufficient.
