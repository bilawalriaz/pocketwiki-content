# First Bulgarian Empire

The First Bulgarian Empire (681–1018 AD) was a medieval Southeastern European state that grew from a pagan Bulgar khanate into a major Christian empire and the principal rival of Byzantium. It absorbed Slavic culture, adopted Christianity, formalised the Cyrillic script, and at its peak was the foremost cultural and spiritual centre of Slavic Europe.

## Foundation and Early Centuries

The state was founded in 681 when Bulgar tribes under Asparuh settled south of the Danube, defeated Byzantine forces, and won recognition plus annual tribute. The Bulgars were Turkic semi-nomadic warriors; the local population was Slavic. The Slavs were politically subjugated but kept their customs in exchange for tribute and military service. The early centuries were defined by war with Byzantium. Khan Tervel helped Justinian II regain his throne in 705, earning the title "Caesar," and in 717–718 a Bulgarian army helped break the Arab siege of Constantinople. After the Dulo dynasty ended in 753, Emperor Constantine V exploited Bulgarian instability with nine campaigns. Stability returned when Khan Kardam won the Battle of Marcellae in 792.

Khan Krum (r. 803–814) roughly doubled Bulgaria's territory, destroyed the Avar Khaganate, and pushed into Thrace and Macedonia. In 811 he killed Emperor Nicephorus I at the Battle of Varbitsa Pass; Krum reportedly had Nicephorus's skull lined with silver and used it as a drinking cup. This was the first time a Byzantine emperor had fallen in battle against a Balkan power.

## Christianisation and the Golden Age

In 864 Khan Boris I converted to Christianity, taking the name Michael, aligning Bulgaria with the Christian world and strengthening royal authority. The Council of Preslav in 893 made Old Bulgarian the language of church and state and moved the capital to Preslav.

Simeon I (r. 893–927) brought the empire to its zenith. He aimed to absorb Byzantium into a joint Bulgarian-led state. In 895–896 he defeated the Magyars, forcing their migration into Pannonia, and routed the Byzantines at Boulgarophygon. His crushing victory at Anchialus in 917 made Bulgaria the dominant military power in the Balkans. In 927 Byzantium recognised his title of Tsar (Emperor) and an independent Bulgarian Patriarchate, the highest rank of an autocephalous Orthodox church.

## Decline and Fall

After Simeon's death the empire held a long peace under Peter I, but the strategic position worsened: Bulgaria was ringed by Magyars, Pechenegs, Kievan Rus', and Byzantium. In 968 Byzantium incited Sviatoslav I of Kiev to invade; Preslav fell in 971 and Emperor Boris II was forced to abdicate. Western resistance continued under the Cometopuli brothers, with Samuel eventually becoming emperor. He opened a long war against Byzantium, winning the Battle of the Gates of Trajan in 986.

The decisive reversal came under Basil II. After years of attrition he won the Battle of Kleidion in 1014 and blinded roughly 15,000 Bulgarian prisoners, leaving one in every hundred with one eye to lead the rest back to Samuel, who reportedly died of shock. By 1018 the last strongholds had surrendered and the empire was annexed by Byzantium, ending a state that had lasted 337 years.

## Society, Government, Economy

Bulgaria was a hereditary monarchy whose ruler's title evolved from Khan (tribal chief) to Knyaz (prince) to Tsar (emperor). High officials included the *kavhan* (first minister) and the *ichirgu-boila*. Society was stratified into the *boila* (highest nobility, later known as *bolyar*), the *bagains* (a second aristocratic class), and the common people. The *zadruga*, a rural commune of kin-related peasants bound by collective responsibility, formed the base of the rural economy, which also relied on animal husbandry, mining, and trade with Byzantium, the Rus', and Central Europe. Pliska and later Preslav were major cities; Bulgaria was unusually urbanised for a 9th–10th century inland state.

## Religion and Culture

Before 864 the Bulgars worshipped the sky god Tangra and the Slavs deities such as Perun. After Christianisation Bulgaria developed an autonomous church, elevated to a Patriarchate in 927, and a cultural golden age followed. Saints Cyril and Methodius had earlier created the Glagolitic alphabet for Slavonic; the Preslav Literary School then developed the Cyrillic alphabet from Greek, adding letters for Slavic sounds. Old Church Slavonic, based on the Thessaloniki dialect, became the literary language of liturgy and learning across Slavic Europe. Distinctive art, including the Madara Rider relief, and architecture marked the Pliska-Preslav culture. A dualist heresy, Bogomilism, also arose in 10th-century Bulgaria, teaching that the material world was the creation of an evil god.
