# Songhai Empire

The Songhai Empire was a major West African state that dominated the Sahel (the semi-arid belt just south of the Sahara) during the 15th and 16th centuries, growing wealthy by taxing and protecting the trans-Saharan caravan trade in gold, salt, and other goods. Its history runs from a small Niger River kingdom, through expansion under a warrior conqueror and a peak of administrative sophistication under an Islamic reformer, to a sudden collapse when a Moroccan gunpowder army defeated a much larger Songhai force.

## Origins and Rise

The Songhai formed from several Niger River peoples, including the Sorko, Gao, and Do, with chiefs based at Gao controlling regional trade by the 10th century. Around 1300 the expanding Mali Empire absorbed Gao, and for roughly 130 years the city sat inside Mali's sphere. When Mali splintered in the late 14th century, local Songhai leaders reasserted independence and began expanding into the power vacuum.

## The Sonni Dynasty and Expansion

Sonni Ali (r. 1464–1492) built the empire into a major power by conquering the great Sahelian trading cities, taking Timbuktu in 1468 and, after a seven-year siege, Djenné in 1475. Control of these cities gave Songhai command of the upper Niger's trade routes, and the customs and tribute funded further campaigns.

His rule was politically double-edged. Islamic scholars in Timbuktu, whose wealth and prestige depended on patronage from a pious ruler, disliked his adherence to traditional religion and his rough handling of their scholarly elite; their accounts largely produced the "tyrant" label. He was also a capable organiser of cavalry and riverine forces, and without him the later Islamic reform programme would have had no state to reform.

## The Askia Dynasty and Golden Age

After Sonni Ali died, his general Muhammad Ture seized power in 1493 as Askia the Great, founding the Askia dynasty. Askia reoriented the empire along Islamic lines: he took the hajj to Mecca, replaced traditional-court officials with a bureaucracy built around tax farming (granting private collectors the right to gather state revenue in exchange for a fixed payment) and Islamic judges called qadis, codified weights and measures, built mosques and schools, and turned the army into a salaried, professional force. He also pushed the empire to its greatest territorial extent.

The contrast between the two rulers explains the golden age. Military conquest created the territory; Islamic administration gave it durable institutions, reliable revenue, and cultural prestige across the Muslim world.

## Decline and Fall

The empire's weakness was dynastic. Askia the Great was overthrown by his own son in 1528, and from then on the throne was contested repeatedly. A brief revival under Askia Daoud ended with his death in 1583, which triggered another war of succession and left the empire politically fractured.

In 1591 the Moroccan sultan Ahmad I al-Mansur, seeking to seize the trans-Saharan trade directly, sent an army under Judar Pasha across the Sahara. At the Battle of Tondibi the Songhai fielded a much larger force but lost to Moroccan gunpowder weapons, primarily arquebuses (early shoulder-fired muskets). The defeat destroyed the empire's core cities, broke its control of the trade routes, and its leading nobles withdrew south to found smaller successor kingdoms around Gao and in Hausa country. The extent to which firearms rather than internal decay decided the battle remains debated, though the two clearly reinforced each other.
