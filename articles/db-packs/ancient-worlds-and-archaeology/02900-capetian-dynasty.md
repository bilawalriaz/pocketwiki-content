# Capetian dynasty

The Capetian dynasty is the royal house that ruled France almost continuously from 987 to 1848, founded when Hugh Capet was elected King of the Franks. Its members and their cadet branches went on to supply monarchs across Europe, including in Spain and Luxembourg, and its long arc explains how a relatively weak tenth-century regional lord came to anchor the centralised French state.

## The Capetian miracle

When Hugh Capet took the throne in 987, the kings of France were elected and his personal power was small. He ruled only the Île-de-France, a small region around Paris, and faced rivals who held far more land, chief among them the Anglo-Norman kings of England. The dynasty survived and prevailed through a single mechanism: the early Capetians crowned their eldest sons as associate king during their own lifetimes, then handed over the crown at death. This practice, known as associate kingship, converted an elective office into a hereditary one, removing the succession disputes that weakened other medieval families. Father-to-son inheritance held without interruption from Hugh Capet until 1328, three unbroken centuries. Philip II Augustus (r. c. 1180–1223) dropped the practice of associate kingship once hereditary succession was no longer in question. The meaning of "Capet" itself is unknown, though folk etymology has linked it to the Latin *caput* ("head").

## Expansion and the logic of cadet branches

The Capetians built power by aligning with the Church, joining the Crusades, and absorbing the royal demesne, the land directly controlled by the monarch. As the realm grew, younger sons received appanages, territorial grants that gave them wealth and status without threatening the eldest son's inheritance. A cadet branch is a line descended from a younger son. The Capetian habit of sending younger sons outward produced dynasties in Portugal, Spain, Naples, Sicily, Hungary, and Poland. The two reigning Capetian monarchs today, King Felipe VI of Spain and Grand Duke Guillaume V of Luxembourg (since 2025), both descend from the Bourbon branch.

## Salic law and the shift to cadet lines

The succession crisis of 1328 turned on a legal question, not a military one. The senior Capetian line ended with the death of Charles IV without a male heir. The nearest male-line Capetian was Philip, Count of Valois, whose claim rested on a re-asserted Frankish tradition, Salic law, barring women and their descendants from the French throne. The rival claimant through the female line was Edward III of England, whose exclusion helped trigger the Hundred Years' War. The French barons accepted Salic law and chose Philip as Philip VI, placing the House of Valois, a cadet branch, on the throne. The same logic returned in 1589, when the Valois line died out and Henry IV, a Bourbon and therefore another cadet branch, took the crown after the Wars of Religion. From 1328 onward the French kings were always cadets of the original Capetian line.

## Decline and modern survival

The French monarchy was abolished during the Revolution in 1792, and Louis XVI was executed the following year. The Bourbon restoration of 1814 briefly revived the line, but the July Revolution of 1830 replaced Charles X with Louis Philippe I of the Orléans branch, the last Capetian to sit on the French throne, and the monarchy finally ended in 1848. Capetians survive in two ways today. First, through cadet branches still reigning: the Spanish and Luxembourg Bourbons. Second, through pretenders to defunct thrones, most prominently Louis Alphonse, Duke of Anjou, the Legitimist claimant to France, along with others in Brazil, Portugal, and Italy. Philip IV, who brought the papacy under French control, shows the dynasty's reach even at its peak.
