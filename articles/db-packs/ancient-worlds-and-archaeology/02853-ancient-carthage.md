# Ancient Carthage

Carthage was a Phoenician maritime empire in North Africa that controlled the western Mediterranean for centuries through naval power and commerce, then was destroyed by Rome in 146 BC. Its story shows how a single trading colony outgrew its mother city, dominated an entire sea, and provoked the war that ended Phoenician political power.

## Origins and rise

Carthage was founded around 814 BC by merchants from Tyre, a Phoenician city-state on the coast of modern Lebanon. Its site on the Gulf of Tunis gave access to fertile farmland, natural harbours, and the sea lanes connecting the western Mediterranean. For most of its early existence it was a colony subordinate to Tyre.

That changed when Phoenicia's homeland was conquered by the Neo-Assyrian Empire in the 7th century BC. Cut off from its mother city, Carthage became independent, and by the mid-6th century BC it was the leading Phoenician state. It built a network of colonies, trading posts, and tributary relationships across North Africa, southern Iberia, and the islands of Sicily, Sardinia, and the Balearics. A thalassocracy is a state whose power rests on control of the sea, and Carthage monopolised the tin trade that moved from Atlantic Europe toward the eastern Mediterranean, manufacturing purple dye, textiles, and metalwork for export.

## Government

Carthage ran a mixed republic, unusual in the ancient world. Two **sufetes**, elected annually from the wealthy elite, served as chief magistrates with executive and judicial authority, but they did not command armies. A council of elders called the **Adirim** ("the mighty ones") held broad administrative and financial power. A popular assembly could break deadlocks. A separate court, the **One Hundred and Four**, reviewed generals after campaigns and could punish them, even with death.

This system separated political office from military command, which Aristotle praised for its balance. The same separation let powerful families like the Barcids dominate by winning wars and elections in parallel. The exact moment Carthage shifted from monarchy to this oligarchic republic is debated, but the reforms probably occurred in the 7th or 6th century BC.

## Society and economy

Carthaginian society was urban, wealthy, and sharply stratified. At the top stood a merchant aristocracy; below them citizen-soldiers; at the bottom a large population of foreign mercenaries, slaves, and subject peoples, especially Libyans, who provided most agricultural labour. Agriculture grew more important after the Second Punic War, when Carthage lost overseas territory and had to extract more from its African hinterland, driving innovations in farming technique. The Carthaginian writer Mago produced a treatise on farming that was influential in the ancient Mediterranean.

## Military

Carthage fielded one of the largest navies of the ancient world, built to protect trade routes and blockade enemies. Its land armies were mostly mercenary: Iberians, Gauls, Numidians, Libyans, and other subjects hired for each campaign and led by professional Carthaginian generals. The **Sacred Band** was the main unit of citizen-soldiers, and war elephants were deployed in several battles. Because the One Hundred and Four could prosecute any general on return, successful commanders like Hannibal lived under permanent political risk.

## Religion

Carthaginians worshipped a Phoenician pantheon headed by **Baal Hammon** (a sky and fertility god) and **Tanit** (a war and fertility goddess often called the "Face of Baal"). They absorbed deities from conquered and trading peoples, a process called religious syncretism. Sacred precincts called **tophets** held urns with the cremated remains of children, and the meaning of these burials is one of the most contested questions about Carthaginian culture. Greek and Roman sources describe child sacrifice, especially during crises, while modern archaeologists debate whether the children died naturally and were ritually interred or were killed as offerings.

## Conflict with Rome and destruction

Carthage and Rome first clashed over Sicily. A 509 BC treaty had already divided spheres of influence, and Carthage lost the First Sicilian War to Syracuse in 480 BC. The three **Punic Wars** ("Punic" being the Roman term for Phoenician) settled the rivalry:

| Date | Event |
|------|-------|
| 264–241 BC | First Punic War: Rome takes Sicily. |
| 241–238 BC | Mercenant War: unpaid troops revolt. |
| 218–201 BC | Second Punic War: Hannibal invades Italy; Rome wins at Zama. |
| 149–146 BC | Third Punic War: Rome besieges, captures, and razes Carthage; survivors enslaved. |
| 44 BC | Julius Caesar refounds the site as a Roman city. |

After 146 BC no Phoenician state remained politically independent, and Rome absorbed the western Mediterranean trade networks Carthage had built. The site lay in ruins until Julius Caesar refounded it as a Roman city in 44 BC.

## Open questions

Three issues remain unresolved. First, the tophets: ritual child sacrifice, mass infant death, or some mixture. Second, the degree of direct rule Carthage exercised over its empire, which most scholars now treat as a hegemonic network of treaties and tribute (an empire held together by local allies and payment rather than governors) rather than a centrally administered state. Third, who counted as a citizen, and what political rights subject peoples, including Libyans, actually held.
