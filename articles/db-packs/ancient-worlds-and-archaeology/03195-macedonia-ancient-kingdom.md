# Macedonia (ancient kingdom)

Ancient Macedonia was a kingdom on the northern edge of the Classical Greek world that, in a single generation, conquered the Greek states, overthrew the Achaemenid Persian Empire, and spread Greek culture across the Near East. That trajectory, from peripheral backwater to the core of a Hellenistic world, is the central story of the kingdom.

## Origins and early subordination

The Argead dynasty founded the kingdom with its capital at Aigai (modern Vergina) around the 7th century BC. The Macedonians spoke a Greek dialect, later replaced by the standardized Koine Greek that emerged in the Hellenistic period, and they worshipped the Greek gods, with the king serving as high priest. Even so, Macedonia sat outside the urbanised, city-state world of southern Greece, and its aristocracy was built around hunting and warfare rather than the civic life of Athens or Thebes. By 511/510 BC the kingdom had become a vassal of the Achaemenid Persian Empire under King Amyntas I, and it remained a minor, often-overrun polity through the 5th century.

## The transformation under Philip II

The turning point was the reign of Philip II (359–336 BC). Macedonia was still a small kingdom when he came to power, but he converted it into the dominant Greek state through three interlocking changes.

He reformed the army. His core innovation was the Macedonian phalanx, a close-order rectangular infantry block armed with the *sarissa*, a pike roughly 18 feet long. Longer reach meant the phalanx could engage the enemy before the enemy's shorter spears could reach it, giving Philip a decisive tactical edge. Revenues from gold, silver, and timber mining funded this professional force and a Macedonian navy.

He subdued mainland Greece. At the Battle of Chaeronea in 338 BC he defeated Athens and Thebes, the two leading Greek city-states, and created the League of Corinth, a federation of Greek states under his leadership, nominally to invade Persia.

He centralised royal power. His rule rested on the *hetairoi*, the Companions, an elite cavalry force drawn from the Macedonian aristocracy that also formed the king's advisory council, and on a court of royal pages and bodyguards. Kings were technically answerable to the army assembly, but in practice they ruled as autocrats.

## Alexander and the Hellenistic world

Philip was assassinated in 336 BC and was succeeded by his son Alexander III, Alexander the Great (r. 336–323 BC), whose tutor had been the philosopher Aristotle. Alexander first destroyed Thebes in 335 BC to deter Greek revolt, then carried out the Persian invasion his father had prepared. In little more than a decade he destroyed the Achaemenid Empire, reaching from Egypt to the Indus River. The result was a vast Hellenistic state that spread Greek art, literature, philosophy, and science across the Near East and inaugurated the Hellenistic period, conventionally dated 323–31 BC.

The cultural impact mattered as much as the conquest. A cosmopolitan world took shape in which the standardised Koine Greek served as a *lingua franca* alongside local languages and institutions, and this fusion of Greek and Eastern elements laid groundwork for both the Roman and Byzantine civilisations.

## The Successor Wars and the Antigonid restoration

Alexander died in 323 BC with no capable heir. His generals, the *Diadochi* (Successors), fought a series of wars over his empire. Three major Hellenistic kingdoms emerged: Macedonia itself, Ptolemaic Egypt, and the Seleucid Empire. Macedonia was initially ruled by the Antipatrid dynasty and then, from 277 BC, by the Antigonid dynasty, after Antigonus II Gonatas defeated an invading Gallic force. The capital had moved to Pella, and new cities such as Thessalonica were founded. For about a century Macedonia remained a major Greek power, but its position steadily weakened as it clashed with a rising Roman Republic.

## Conflict with Rome and the end of the kingdom

Macedonia's rivalry with Rome produced four wars. The First Macedonian War (215–205 BC) ended inconclusively. The Second (200–197 BC) ended at the Battle of Cynoscephalae, where Rome defeated Philip V and forced Macedonia to surrender its Greek territories. The Third Macedonian War (171–168 BC) was decisive: the Roman general Aemilius Paullus defeated King Perseus at the Battle of Pydna, abolished the Macedonian monarchy, and divided the territory into four client republics. A brief revival under Andriscus was crushed in the Fourth Macedonian War (150–148 BC), and in 148 BC Rome converted the region into the Roman province of Macedonia, ending the kingdom's independence.

## Open questions

Two debates remain active. First, ethnic identity: the dominant view treats ancient Macedonians as Greeks with an unusually archaic, rural culture, but ancient sources contradict each other, and some label them as "barbarians" alongside Thracians and Illyrians. Second, the royal tombs at Vergina, including one widely attributed to Philip II after forensic work in 1977–78, continue to attract competing identifications, and the exact extent of Philip's military originality, given his exposure to the Theban general Epaminondas during a hostage period, is also disputed.
