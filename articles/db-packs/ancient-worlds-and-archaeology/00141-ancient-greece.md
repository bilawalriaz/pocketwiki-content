# Ancient Greece

Ancient Greece was a civilisation of independent city-states in the northeastern Mediterranean, lasting from the Greek Dark Ages (c. 1200–800 BC) through classical antiquity to about 600 AD. It produced the West's first comprehensive historical writing, its first democracy, and the philosophical, scientific, and artistic foundations that Rome, and later Europe, inherited. Greece was politically a patchwork of hundreds of small poleis (city-states) that shared language, religion, and festivals but rarely united; the only period of political unity was under Macedon (338–323 BC), followed by Roman rule.

## Why Greece stayed fragmented

After the Mycenaean palaces collapsed around 1200 BC, no centralised states survived. When population recovered, mountainous terrain and the absence of a regional power favoured the polis: a small city governing its surrounding farmland. The Archaic period (c. 800–500 BC) saw the Greek alphabet adopted, coinage introduced, and colonies founded as far west as Ischia (by 775 BC) and east to Al-Mina (by 800 BC). Within Greece, power passed from kings to aristocratic oligarchies, sometimes interrupted by tyrants (sole rulers, not necessarily oppressive) such as those at Corinth from 657 BC.

The two most important poleis developed opposite political systems. Athens moved toward democracy in stages: Draco's law code (621 BC), Solon's reforms balancing rich and poor (594 BC), Pisistratus' tyranny (mid-6th century), and Cleisthenes' democratic constitution after the overthrow of Hippias. Sparta built a militarised dual kingdom (diarchy), traditionally attributed to the lawgiver Lycurgus, and reduced its neighbour Messenia to a serf population called helots, who farmed for Spartan citizens and gained freedom only after Thebes defeated Sparta at Leuctra in 371 BC.

## Persia, Athens, and the Classical crisis

The defining conflict of the 5th century was between the Greek poleis and the Achaemenid Persian Empire. The Ionian Revolt (499–494 BC), backed by Athens and Eretria, drew Darius I into Greece; he was defeated at Marathon in 490 BC. Xerxes' second invasion in 480 BC was met by a coalition of about 31 Greek states. The Greek victories at Salamis (sea) and Plataea (land) preserved Greek independence, and Athens, leading the Delian League from 477 BC, gradually converted that alliance into an empire.

Athenian power provoked Sparta. The Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC) ended with Spartan victory; the disaster at Syracuse (415–413) and the loss at Aegospotami (405) forced Athens to surrender, and Sparta briefly installed the oligarchic Thirty Tyrants. In the 4th century, power rotated: Thebes under Epaminondas freed Messenia after Leuctra, then Macedon under Philip II defeated a Greek alliance at Chaeronea (338 BC) and bound the poleis into the League of Corinth. His son Alexander the Great then destroyed the Achaemenid Empire between 334 and 323 BC, defeating Darius III at Issus (333 BC) and Gaugamela (331 BC).

## The Hellenistic world and Roman absorption

Alexander's death in 323 BC opened the Hellenistic period. His generals carved out kingdoms: the Ptolemies in Egypt, the Seleucids in Syria and the former Persian east, and the Antigonids in Macedon. Greek settlers, language, and city life spread as far as Afghanistan and Pakistan; Alexandria and Antioch became the new cultural capitals. Greek independence effectively ended at the Battle of Corinth in 146 BC; mainland Greece became the Roman province of Achaea in 27 BC, while Greek remained the lingua franca of the eastern Mediterranean.

## Society, thought, and legacy

Full citizenship was narrow: only free, native-born, land-owning men. In Athens, women were confined mostly to domestic life, metics (resident foreigners) had no political rights, and slaves (perhaps 40–80% of the Athenian population at its peak) performed much of the labour, though they could not legally be killed. Education was private except in Sparta, with wealthy boys taught reading, writing, music, and athletics. Athens' urban economy was unusually prosperous, with unskilled wages reaching 7–12 kg of wheat per day in the 5th–4th centuries BC, although this measure likely overstates the standard of living of the enslaved majority.

Herodotus (writing in the 450s–420s BC) is called the "father of history" for his narrative inquiries, though his accuracy is debated; Thucydides, Plato, and Aristotle built on this tradition. Pre-Socratics, then Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle, founded Western philosophy. Hellenistic thinkers added Stoicism (Zeno of Citium), Cynicism (Antisthenes), and Neoplatonism (Plotinus). Greek mathematicians, including Pythagoras, Euclid, and Archimedes, created formal geometry and proof. Aristarchus of Samos proposed a heliocentric universe, Eratosthenes measured Earth's circumference, Hipparchus catalogued the stars and discovered precession, and the Antikythera mechanism (c. 80 BC) used a differential gear to calculate planetary positions, a technology not seen again until the 16th century. Hippocrates established medicine as a discipline separate from philosophy and religion. Greek sculpture, and a polytheistic religion centred on animal sacrifice, were transmitted through Rome and the Renaissance into European culture. Justinian's closure of the Academy of Athens in 529 AD is conventionally taken as the end of ancient Greek intellectual life.
