# Qin dynasty

The Qin dynasty (221–206 BC) was the first imperial dynasty of China. It lasted 14 years but replaced the Warring States system with a centralized empire whose administrative framework endured for two millennia.

## Origins and Rise

Qin began as a minor Zhou fief in the 9th century BC, granted to Feizi. In 770 BC, Duke Xiang escorted the Zhou court east, establishing Qin as a major vassal. In the 4th century BC, the statesman Shang Yang reformed Qin between 359 and 338 BC. These reforms created a strong centralized state with a large army supported by a stable agrarian economy, made possible by the Guanzhong region's fertile farmland and mountain-protected geography.

During the Warring States period (c. 475–221 BC), Qin developed a more effective military than its rivals. Other states fought ceremonial wars, while Qin used ruthless tactics. From 266 BC, Prime Minister Fan Sui pursued an expansionist policy. Between 230 and 221 BC, Qin conquered the six rival states one by one, beginning with Han.

## Unification and Rule

In 221 BC, King Zheng completed unification and took the title Qin Shi Huang, or First Emperor. The new empire ended aristocratic rule by dividing the realm into 36 commanderies administered by appointed officials. The state directly administered the peasant majority through a household registration system.

A wide standardization program unified currency (the round banliang bronze coin), weights, measures, and writing. Chancellor Li Si consolidated scripts into the small seal script, though variant characters remained in everyday use. These standards enabled the state to mobilize labor at a scale scholars identify as a turning point in East Asian environmental history.

Major construction projects included the Great Wall, a national road system, and the emperor's mausoleum guarded by the Terracotta Army. The Qin pushed south into Baiyue territories (214–210 BC) and campaigned north against the Xiongnu nomads. A censorate supervised officials.

Qin rule followed Legalism, a philosophy of strict laws, centralized power, and harsh punishments. Corvée labor and heavy taxes funded public works. Merchants were viewed with suspicion and legally restricted. Qin law focused on theft, and included forced labor for public works as a punishment, though evidence shows pardons and fines were also used. The state promoted maximizing cultivated land, and over 90% of the population farmed millet, wheat, and barley.

## Collapse

Qin Shi Huang died in 210 BC. Chief eunuch Zhao Gao and Prime Minister Li Si placed the weak Qin Er Shi on the throne. Their internal conflicts, combined with heavy taxation, forced labor, and suppression of dissent, sparked revolts in 209 BC. Zhao Gao later forced Qin Er Shi to suicide. The last ruler, Ziying, surrendered to rebel leader Liu Bang in 207 BC. After the capital Xianyang was destroyed in 206 BC, the Chu–Han Contention followed, ending in 202 BC when Liu Bang founded the Han dynasty.

## Disputed Claims

The "burning of books and burying of scholars" under Qin Shi Huang in 213–212 BC is recorded mainly in the Shiji (c. 91 BC), compiled more than a century later, and some modern scholars doubt the events. The claim that the English name "China" derives from "Qin" is also questioned, since the Sanskrit term "Sina" predates the dynasty. Qin rulers notably did not claim the Zhou-era Mandate of Heaven, a doctrine holding that a ruler's authority depended on divine approval.
