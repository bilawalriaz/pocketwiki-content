# Mycenaean Greece

Mycenaean Greece (c. 1750–1050 BC) was the first advanced, literate civilization on the Greek mainland and the final phase of the Bronze Age Aegean before its collapse. Its myths, gods, and engineering seeded Classical Greek culture.

## Origins and Identity

The Mycenaeans were mainland Greeks whose civilization evolved from local Bronze Age populations with strong influence from Minoan Crete. Their Greek identity is confirmed by Linear B, a syllabic script adapted from Minoan Linear A and used for palace records in the earliest attested form of Greek. Neighbors knew them by other names: the Hittites called them *Ahhiyawa*, the Egyptians *Danaya*, and Homer later called them *Achaeans*. Genetic studies show Mycenaeans resembled Minoans but carried additional ancestry from steppe-related populations, consistent with Indo-European Greek-speaking groups migrating into the Aegean.

## Palatial Society and Economy

Mycenaean Greece was not a single kingdom but a network of palace-centered states, with Mycenae, Pylos, and Thebes among the most important. Each palace was ruled by a king called the *wanax*, the supreme political, military, and religious authority, supported by a bureaucratic elite. The economy was redistributive: agricultural produce, craft output, and trade goods flowed into the palace and were then allocated outward. Linear B tablets record flocks of sheep, rations of olive oil, textile quotas, and land distribution. Common villagers, called *da-mo*, lived in communities subordinate to the palace.

Long-distance trade was essential because Greece lacked key raw materials. Palaces imported metals and ivory, and exported olive oil, pottery, and textiles across the Mediterranean. Between roughly 1400 and 1200 BC, shared pottery, weapons, and burial practices spread across the Mycenaean world, a phase called the *Koine*.

## Material Culture

Mycenaean elites lived behind massive fortifications of unworked boulders, a style later Greeks called *Cyclopean* because they believed only the mythical Cyclopes could have moved such stones. The central room of each palace was the *megaron*, a throne room built around a circular hearth. High-status burials took two forms: early *shaft graves*, deep pits often rich with gold and weapons, and later *tholos* tombs, large circular stone chambers with corbelled vaulted roofs.

## Collapse and Legacy

Around 1250 BC the first wave of destruction struck mainland centers including Thebes and Mycenae. Mycenae fell finally near 1190 BC, and the palace of Pylos was burned around 1180 BC, an event that preserved its Linear B archive by baking the clay tablets hard. By about 1050 BC Mycenaean culture had ended and Greece entered the Dark Ages, a period of lost literacy and reduced population. The cause of the collapse is debated: Dorian Greek migration, the so-called Sea Peoples, internal warfare, and environmental stress are all implicated, and most scholars favor a combination.

The Mycenaean legacy shaped what followed. Their stories of the Trojan War became the Trojan Epic Cycle and the core of Homer's epics, and their gods became the leading figures of the Olympian pantheon. Their roads, fortification techniques, and artistic motifs passed into the toolkit of Classical Greece once literacy returned.
