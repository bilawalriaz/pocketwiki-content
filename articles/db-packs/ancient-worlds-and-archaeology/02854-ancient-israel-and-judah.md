# Ancient Israel and Judah

Two Israelite kingdoms emerged in the southern Levant during the Iron Age from the indigenous Canaanite population. Their roughly 600-year arc, from highland villages to exiled communities, produced the foundational events of Judaism and the Hebrew Bible.

## Origins

Archaeological evidence shows a rapid rise of small, unwalled highland villages in Canaan from the 13th century BCE onward. These settlements shared Canaanite culture but introduced distinct markers: collared-rim jars, four-room houses, and an absence of pig bones. The earliest non-biblical reference to "Israel" appears on the Egyptian Merneptah Stele, dated c. 1208 BCE, confirming the group existed as a recognizable entity before any monarchy. Early Israelites organized into kinship-based tribes forming a loose confederation, not a state.

## The United Monarchy Question

The Hebrew Bible describes a unified kingdom under Saul, David, and Solomon (c. 1000–930 BCE) with Jerusalem as capital. Its historicity is heavily debated. Skeptics note the lack of archaeological evidence for a large centralized state in the 10th century BCE; Jerusalem itself was then a modest settlement. The Tel Dan Stele (9th century BCE) references the "House of David," proving the Davidic dynasty was recognized early, and Khirbet Qeiyafa suggests a centralized polity in Judah by the early 10th century. Most scholars treat the biblical account as a blend of early memories and later theological elaboration.

## Division and the Two Kingdoms

After Solomon's death (c. 930 BCE), the monarchy split. The northern Kingdom of Israel was larger and richer but politically unstable, with frequent coups. The southern Kingdom of Judah was smaller and poorer but enjoyed dynastic continuity under the Davidic line. Israel peaked under the Omride dynasty before Assyria destroyed it in 722/720 BCE and deported much of its population. Judah survived as an Assyrian vassal, growing significantly, likely absorbing Israelite refugees.

## The Babylonian Exile

Judah revived under King Josiah's religious reforms in 622 BCE, which centralized worship in Jerusalem. After Josiah's death, Judah was caught between Egypt and the rising Neo-Babylonian Empire. Following revolts, Nebuchadnezzar II deported Judahite elites in 597 BCE, then destroyed Jerusalem and Solomon's Temple in 587/586 BCE, exiling much of the population. Interpreted as divine punishment, this catastrophe forced a theological reckoning. In exile, biblical texts were compiled and edited, and Israelite religion crystallized into strict monotheism, with intensified emphasis on law, Sabbath, and circumcision as identity markers. The Persian Edict of Cyrus (c. 538 BCE) later permitted return.

## Religion and Economy

Israelite religion evolved from Canaanite polytheism through henotheism, the worship of a single god (Yahweh) as supreme while not yet denying others' existence, toward full monotheism. Worship shifted from local shrines to centralized Jerusalem, especially after the reforms of Hezekiah and Josiah. The economy was agricultural, with olive oil and wine as major exports, particularly in the north. Under Assyrian dominance, Judah's economy specialized regionally.

## Core Debates

Four questions remain open. First, whether the United Monarchy was a real centralized state or a later idealization of a smaller chiefdom. Second, whether Israelites emerged purely from Canaanites or included outside groups, possibly including Egyptian refugees. Third, the demographic impact of the Babylonian Exile: some scholars argue for severe population collapse, others for substantial continuity among those who remained. Fourth, the exact timeline by which henotheism became strict monotheism.
