# Ming dynasty

The Ming dynasty (1368–1644) was the last Han Chinese imperial dynasty, founded after the overthrow of the Mongol-led Yuan. It combined cultural flourishing with global engagement, then collapsed under fiscal strain, climate shocks, and invasion. Its legacy includes provincial borders still recognizably modern, a flourishing of vernacular literature, and a bureaucratic model that shaped later Chinese states.

## Founding and Consolidation

Zhu Yuanzhang, a peasant rebel, founded the dynasty and as the Hongwu Emperor rebuilt state infrastructure, issued the Great Ming Code, and imposed a rigid social order. He abolished the Chancellorship after executing Chancellor Hu Weiyong in 1380, concentrating authority in the emperor. He deployed the Embroidered Uniform Guard, a secret police force, carried out large purges, and extended control into Inner Asia and the southwest, resettling Han colonists into Yunnan.

After Hongwu's death in 1398, his grandson Jianwen briefly reigned until his uncle Zhu Di seized the throne in 1402 through the Jingnan campaign. As the Yongle Emperor, Zhu Di is often called a "second founder."

## The Yongle Reign

Yongle moved the primary capital to Beijing, where the Forbidden City was completed in 1421. He sponsored seven major voyages by Admiral Zheng He between 1405 and 1433, reaching the Indian Ocean. To balance the scholar-bureaucrats, he expanded eunuch power, an institutional pattern that would outlive his reign.

## Mid-Ming Crises and Adaptation

The 1449 Tumu Crisis, in which Oirat Mongols captured Emperor Yingzong, ended major northern campaigns. The dynasty turned inward: it reinforced the Great Wall and allowed the navy to decay. The economy monetized as New World silver replaced paper currency, and New World crops such as maize and potatoes supported population growth. Tax collection, however, failed to keep pace, and rival eunuch factions produced corruption and factional conflict.

## Decline and Fall

The late Ming faced three overlapping pressures. The Little Ice Age, a period of global cooling, caused repeated crop failures. Silver import disruptions collapsed the fiscal system that depended on them. Peasant rebellions spread, led by Li Zicheng. To the north, Nurhaci unified the Jurchen tribes in 1616 and founded the Later Jin, a rising Manchu power. In 1644, Li Zicheng's forces captured Beijing, and the Chongzhen Emperor committed suicide. General Wu Sangui then opened the Great Wall to Manchu troops, who allied with him against Li Zicheng and established the Qing dynasty. Loyalist Ming regimes in the south, known as Southern Ming, continued resistance until 1662.

## Government and Society

The Ming governed through a centralized bureaucracy staffed by scholar-officials recruited via civil service examinations on the Confucian classics. In practice the system favored the gentry, and upward mobility was limited. Authority was shared and contested among the emperor, the Grand Secretariat (an advisory body that became the de facto top executive organ after the Chancellery's abolition), the civil bureaucracy, and eunuch agencies. Society was stratified under sumptuary laws, and a vibrant urban culture emerged, especially in the Jiangnan region of the south.

## Culture and Thought

Ming vernacular literature reached its peak with novels such as *Water Margin* and *Journey to the West*. Porcelain and painting achieved new refinement. The philosopher Wang Yangming challenged orthodox Neo-Confucianism by teaching that moral knowledge is innate. Jesuit missionaries from Europe introduced new scientific ideas, though their direct impact on the broader culture remained limited.

## Open Questions

Historians debate whether Ming control over Tibet amounted to real authority or largely a religious tie through the Tibetan Buddhist clergy. Population figures are uncertain because late Ming census data underreported; estimates range from roughly 160 to 200 million. The relative weight of fiscal collapse, climate-driven famine, rebellion, and Manchu invasion in the dynasty's fall remains contested. The examination system probably expanded literacy more than it broadened elite access, since preparing for the exams required wealth and leisure.
