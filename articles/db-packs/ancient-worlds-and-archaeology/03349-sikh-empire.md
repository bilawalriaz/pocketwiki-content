# Sikh Empire

The Sikh Empire was a South Asian power centred on the Punjab, existing for fifty years, from 1799 to 1849. Founded by Maharaja Ranjit Singh, it was the last major Indian subcontinent state to be conquered by the British East India Company. At its peak, the empire was religiously diverse and militarily formidable, stretching from the Sutlej River in the east to the Khyber Pass in the west and including Kashmir and Ladakh.

## Origins and the Misl System

Before the empire, the Punjab was governed by a loose confederacy of Sikh chiefdoms called *misls*. Each *misl* was a sovereign mini-state led by a warrior chieftain; the confederacy was loosely held together by the Khalsa, the collective body of initiated Sikhs established by Guru Gobind Singh. Ranjit Singh, leader of the Sukerchakia misl, captured Lahore in 1799 and was crowned Maharaja on 12 April 1801.

Between 1801 and 1818, Ranjit Singh absorbed the remaining misls and expelled Afghan Durrani influence from the Punjab. Key milestones: the capture of Attock Fort in 1813 ended Afghan power east of the Indus; the conquest of Multan in 1818 completed Sikh control of the whole Punjab. Expansion continued into Kashmir (annexed 1819) and west to Peshawar, formally taken in 1834, and the Khyber Pass. The Treaty of Amritsar (1809) with the British fixed the Sutlej as the eastern border, an uneasy peace that held until Ranjit Singh's death.

## The State Under Ranjit Singh

The capital was the Lahore Darbar, the royal court and government, run on Persian administrative models inherited from the Mughals and Afghans. A *suba* was a province; a *kardar* a local tax collector; a *jagir* a land-revenue grant to an official in place of a salary. The court employed news-writers (*waqia-navis*) and diplomatic agents (*vakils*), and the term *Sarkar-i-Khalsa*, "Rule of the Khalsa," named the government itself.

Despite Sikh religious foundations, the government was secular in practice. Court and army appointments were made on merit and loyalty rather than faith, and Sikhs, Hindus, Muslims, and European officers all served in senior roles. The Muslim Fakir brothers were among Ranjit Singh's most powerful advisors. Land revenue was the principal source of state income.

Pluralism extended to Ranjit Singh's personal religious policy: he visited shrines of other faiths and ordered equal treatment of all religions. Two policies caused lasting resentment among Punjabi Muslims: a ban on cow slaughter and the seizure of mosques in Kashmir after its conquest.

## Military

Ranjit Singh's most distinctive achievement was the Sikh Khalsa Army. Combining traditional Sikh cavalry with European-drilled infantry and modern artillery, it became, by the 1830s, the most formidable indigenous military force in South Asia, strong enough to deter the British from open war.

## Decline and the Anglo-Sikh Wars

Ranjit Singh died in 1839. His successors were children or weak rulers dominated by rival courtiers and generals. The court fractured into factions; several potential heirs were assassinated; the state relied increasingly on troops of uncertain loyalty. The British East India Company exploited these divisions.

The First Anglo-Sikh War (1845–1846) ended in defeat and costly territorial concessions. The Second Anglo-Sikh War (1848–1849), triggered by a rebellion in Multan that the British blamed on the Sikh court, ended with the empire's dissolution. On 30 March 1849, the empire was formally annexed, and Maharaja Duleep Singh, Ranjit Singh's young son, was deposed and exiled.

## Why the Empire Fell So Fast

Two explanations dominate the debate. The first emphasises British manipulation and the betrayal of Sikh commanders such as Lal Singh and Tej Singh during the wars. The second, advanced by the historian Patwant Singh, locates the cause internally: Ranjit Singh built a personal autocracy on top of the Khalsa's older, quasi-republican traditions of collective leadership, and because he never institutionalised a stable succession, the empire had no constitutional mechanism to survive him. The army was strong enough to threaten the British but politically divided enough to be defeated.
