# Maratha Empire

The Maratha Empire was an early modern Indian power founded in the 17th century that grew to dominate much of the subcontinent before being destroyed by the British in 1818. Its arc shows a recurring political problem: a state that begins centralised can, within a few generations, drift into a loose federation of ambitious subordinates, and that drift makes it vulnerable to a more disciplined rival.

## Founders and first kingdom

The Marathas were Marathi-speaking warriors from the western Deccan Plateau. Their founder, Shivaji (1630–1680), started by raiding the Bijapur Sultanate, capturing Fort Torna in 1645. He framed his revolt as "Hindavi Swarajya," a slogan meaning self-rule for Hindus against Muslim-ruled empires. In 1674 he was crowned *Chhatrapati* (sovereign), founding an independent kingdom. At his death his realm covered about 4.1% of the subcontinent, defended by roughly 40,000 cavalry and 50,000 infantry.

Two institutions defined the early state. The *Ashtapradhan* was a council of eight ministers that ran the civil administration. The *Peshwa* was the prime minister who led that council; after 1749 the office became hereditary and the de facto centre of power.

## From kingdom to confederacy

Shivaji's sons Sambhaji and Rajaram fought the Mughals, and Sambhaji was captured and executed by Aurangzeb in 1689. After Aurangzeb died in 1707, his grandson Shahu was released, claimed the throne, and fought a civil war against his aunt Tarabai. Shahu's appointment of Balaji Vishwanath as Peshwa in 1713 made the post hereditary and shifted real power away from the Chhatrapati.

Under Peshwa Baji Rao I (1720–1740) the state expanded roughly tenfold, winning decisive victories at Palkhed (1728) and Bhopal (1737), and raiding Delhi in 1737. To fund this growth the Marathas extracted two taxes from defeated or tributary rulers: *chauth*, a fixed share of revenue (commonly one-quarter), and *sardeshmukhi*, an extra 10% levied as nominal head of the Deccan. The empire reached its greatest extent around 1760, spanning about 2.5 million km².

By then the Maratha state was no longer a kingdom but a confederacy of four great houses: the Scindia, Holkar, Gaekwad, and Bhonsle. They operated semi-autonomously under the Peshwa's nominal leadership and kept their own armies. This structure made rapid expansion possible, because each house could campaign independently, but it also meant the Peshwa could not reliably command them in a crisis.

## The Panipat shock and revival

The confederacy's high point ended at the Third Battle of Panipat in 1761, where the Maratha army was decisively defeated by the Durrani Empire (modern Afghanistan). The loss was political as well as military: other Hindu groups such as the Jats and Rajputs, who had earlier been raided for tribute, refused to support the Marathas, and the army suffered from poor supplies and strategic errors. Power revived in the 1770s under Peshwa Madhavrao I and the general Mahadaji Shinde, who recaptured Delhi in 1771 and restored the Mughal emperor as a puppet. A further success came at the Battle of Kharda in 1795, where the Marathas jointly defeated the Nizam of Hyderabad. After 1800, however, the five great houses (the four plus the Peshwa) fought each other as often as they fought outsiders.

## British conquest

The British East India Company exploited these internal divisions directly. The First Anglo-Maratha War (1775–1782) ended in stalemate. The Second (1803–1805) cost the Marathas Delhi and large territories. In 1802, Peshwa Baji Rao II signed the Treaty of Bassein, accepting British protection in return for being restored against a rival. The Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817–1818) saw the great houses defeated one by one; the Peshwa was exiled, the Maratha heartland was annexed, and the remaining princely states passed under British paramountcy. The confederacy ceased to exist in 1818.

## A note on terminology

Historians still debate what to call the polity. "Empire" implies a centralised state, which the later Marathas were not. "Confederacy" suggests a stable power-sharing arrangement, which also does not fit, because the balance between the Peshwa and the great houses shifted constantly. The accurate picture is a kingdom that centralised under Shivaji, expanded through semi-autonomous commanders, and then fractured into competing warlords just as a better-organised colonial adversary arrived.
