# Pharaoh

A pharaoh was the monarch of ancient Egypt, combining supreme political power with a sacred religious role. The office ruled Egypt from the unification of the kingdom around 3150 BCE until Roman annexation in 30 BCE. The word "pharaoh" comes from the Egyptian *pr ꜥꜣ*, meaning "great house," and originally referred to the royal palace, not the king. From the Twelfth Dynasty onward, scribes used phrases like "Great House, May it Live," still addressing the building. Only during the New Kingdom did the term start shifting to the ruler personally, sitting alongside older titles such as Horus and Nesu Bity. The earliest confirmed letter that calls a king "Pharaoh" dates to Akhenaten's reign, around 1353–1336 BCE; an inscription possibly referring to Thutmose III (c. 1479–1425 BCE) may be older, though scholars disagree. The first dated use attached to a ruler's name is Year 17 of Siamun in the 10th century BCE, and the term only became the standard epithet for the king during the Third Intermediate Period in the 8th and 7th centuries BCE.

The pharaoh's core duty was to maintain Maat, an Egyptian idea bundling cosmic order, truth, balance, and justice. Because Egypt was understood as the world ordered by the gods, the role was both priestly and political. The pharaoh was the obligatory intermediary between gods and humanity: he performed the highest temple rituals, and in exchange the gods granted prosperity, defence, and justice. Practically, that meant owning all land, making laws, collecting taxes, and commanding the army. War was part of maintaining Maat, whether defending Egypt or extending its reach for resources.

Authority rested on a claim of divinity that long predates the title. Before 3000 BCE, the king was treated as the living incarnation of Horus, the falcon god of kingship. By the 26th century BCE, under Djedefre of the Fourth Dynasty, royal theology went further: his mother was said to have been impregnated by the sun god Ra, giving the king a divine origin as well as a divine role. At New Kingdom coronations, the pharaoh received the "living royal ka," a spiritual essence, from Amun-Re, the chief state god. This divine status survived foreign conquests, continuing under Persian and Ptolemaic rule, even though later observers such as Herodotus claimed Egyptian priests denied the king's divinity. The Egyptian sources themselves treat divinity as built into the office.

Symbols of the office were carefully layered. The pharaoh carried sceptres such as the *heqa* (a crook) and the *was*, wore the *uraeus* (a rearing cobra) on the brow for divine protection, and changed crowns to signal which part of Egypt he embodied. The Red Crown (*Deshret*) stood for Lower Egypt, the White Crown (*Hedjet*) for Upper Egypt, and the Double Crown (*Pschent*) for unified rule. Later headdresses included the striped *Nemes*, the *Atef*, the triple-plumed *Hemhem*, and the blue war crown *Khepresh*. No physical ancient Egyptian crown has ever been found, suggesting these were sacred regalia passed from one ruler to the next rather than buried as personal property.

By the Middle Kingdom, a pharaoh carried five official names. The Horus name, the oldest, was written in a *serekh*, a rectangular frame meant to look like a palace façade. The *Nebty*, or Two Ladies, name tied the king to two protective goddesses. The Golden Horus name, whose exact meaning remains unclear, came next. Finally came the *prenomen* and *nomen*, both usually enclosed in cartouches, the oval rings that mark a royal name. The *prenomen* (throne name), often incorporating Re, was typically prefixed with "King of Upper and Lower Egypt" (*Nesu Bity*); the *nomen* was the personal name, prefixed with "Son of Re." Some scholars treat the *Nesu Bity* as a birth name, but the distinction is uncertain.

The office changed shape over more than three thousand years but kept the same foundation: a single ruler, treated as a living link between gods and people, whose job was to keep Maat intact through ritual, law, taxation, and war. Its effective end came in 30 BCE, when Rome absorbed Egypt and the last Ptolemaic queen, Cleopatra VII, died; with her, the institution of pharaoh passed out of independent existence.
