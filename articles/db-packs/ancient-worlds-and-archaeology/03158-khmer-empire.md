# Khmer Empire

The Khmer Empire was a mainland Southeast Asian state that lasted from 802 to 1431 AD, ruled from a sequence of capitals in the Angkor region of modern Cambodia. At its 13th-century peak, the Greater Angkor area held 700,000–900,000 people, one of the largest pre-industrial urban complexes on Earth. The empire is remembered for monumental stone temples, a state-managed hydraulic network, and a religious framework that fused kingship with divinity.

## Origins and the Devaraja System

Jayavarman II unified the former Chenla polities and in 802 declared independence on Phnom Kulen, taking the Sanskrit title *chakravartin* (universal ruler). The "Java" he claimed to be breaking from is debated: the Indonesian island, the Cham kingdom, or another polity.

To legitimise the new kingship, Jayavarman II instituted the *devaraja* cult, in which the monarch was treated as an earthly embodiment of a Hindu deity, usually Vishnu or Shiva. The claim justified the corvée labor and taxation that funded temple construction, reservoir digging, and territorial expansion. His successors Indravarman I and Yasovarman I extended Khmer power and built the first great temple-mountains (stepped pyramids representing Mount Meru, the cosmic mountain of the gods) and *barays* (vast rectangular artificial reservoirs used for irrigation and flood control).

## The Hydraulic City

Angkor's defining feature was its water management. A network of canals, moats, and barays stored monsoon water and redistributed it across the dry season, allowing multiple rice crops per year in a climate where rainfall alone is unreliable. The state, not local lords, controlled this infrastructure. The system was repeatedly expanded, modified, and patched for over five centuries, and its dependence on intact upland forests links the empire's later collapse to environmental strain. The 1296–1297 visit of the Chinese diplomat Zhou Daguan gives a rare eyewitness record of daily life in the hydraulic capital.

## Golden Age: Angkor Wat and Angkor Thom

The 12th century was both the empire's zenith and its most violent. Suryavarman II (r. 1113–1150) built Angkor Wat, the largest religious monument in the world. In 1177 the Cham of present-day Vietnam sailed up the Tonle Sap and sacked the capital. Jayavarman VII (r. 1181–1219) avenged this by conquering Champa, then launched a building program on a scale never repeated: the walled capital Angkor Thom, the Bayon temple with its face-towers, a network of roads, and a chain of state hospitals.

Under Jayavarman VII the state religion shifted toward Mahayana Buddhism, though the devaraja framework persisted. By the 13th–14th centuries a second religious change had taken hold: Theravada Buddhism, the school emphasising monastic discipline and individual enlightenment, had arrived from Sri Lanka and become dominant across all social classes, replacing Hinduism as the everyday faith of ordinary Khmer.

## Society and Economy

Khmer society was hierarchical, structured around a Hindu-derived caste model. Rice farming was the economic foundation, sustained by the irrigation system and the corvée labor the devaraja cult authorised. Women played a significant role in trade.

## Decline and Fall

The empire's decline from the 14th century came from a combination of factors rather than any single event. Internal power struggles weakened central authority. The hydraulic network suffered ecological strain: deforestation in the upland catchments led to sedimentation of canals and barays, and extreme floods and droughts damaged the system, reducing rice yields. The shift to Theravada Buddhism may have eroded the devaraja framework that justified mass labor mobilisation, though the causal weight of religious change is disputed. External pressure grew from the rising Siamese Ayutthaya Kingdom, which besieged Angkor in 1352–1353. In 1431, under King Ponhea Yat, Angkor was abandoned and the capital moved southwest to the Phnom Penh region.
