# Sasanian Empire

The Sasanian Empire (224–651 AD) was the last great Persian empire before Islam, ruling from Ctesiphon in modern Iraq. It succeeded the Parthian (Arsacid) state, lasted over four centuries, and was a serious rival to Rome and Byzantium. Its administrative templates, court culture, and Zoroastrian framework later passed into the early Islamic world.

## Origins and Expansion

Ardashir I, a local ruler in Pars, defeated the last Parthian king Artabanus IV at Hormozdgan in 224, ending the Arsacid dynasty. His son Shapur I (r. 241–271) expanded aggressively, capturing Roman Emperor Valerian in 260. These conquests stretched Sasanian power from Mesopotamia deep into Roman territory.

## Government and Society

The emperor bore the title *shahanshah* ("King of Kings") and ruled from Ctesiphon. Beneath him stood a centralised bureaucracy headed by a vizier called the *wuzurg framadar*. Society was divided into four classes: priests (*asronan*), warriors (*arteshtaran*), commoners (*wastaryoshan*), and artisans (*hutukhshan*). The *dehqans*, landowning knights, supplied cavalry officers and provincial governors, the administrative and military backbone of the empire.

## Military

Sasanian armies depended on the *clibanarii* and *cataphracts*: heavily armoured noble cavalry whose charge could break infantry lines. Ordinary foot soldiers (*paygan*), war elephants, and a Persian Gulf navy rounded out the forces. These troops defended long frontiers against nomadic peoples such as the Hephthalites and Kidarites to the east, and repeatedly clashed with Rome and Byzantium over Armenia and Mesopotamia.

## Religion and Culture

Zoroastrianism was the state faith, its orthodoxy policed by powerful priests such as Kartir. Under Sasanian patronage the Avesta was compiled and the Avestan alphabet created. Other faiths were often tolerated: Christianity split into Nestorian and Jacobite branches inside Sasanian territory, Manichaeism briefly flourished with state favour under Shapur I, and Jewish communities remained. The era was a cultural renaissance, marked by the great arch of Ctesiphon, the *Khwaday-Namag* (a Book of Kings later used as source material for the *Shahnameh*), and the Academy of Gondishapur, a hub for medical and philosophical learning.

## Neighbours

Rome and then Byzantium were the chief western rivals, fighting frequent wars over frontier cities and client kingdoms. To the east lay the Hephthalites, the Kidarites, and diplomatic and trading contact with India, China, and Aksumite Yemen.

## Decline and Fall

The Byzantine–Sasanian War of 602–628 was the turning point. After initial Sasanian gains, a Byzantine counter-offensive stripped the empire of territory, treasure, and manpower. A rapid succession of weak rulers followed, leaving no firm central authority. When Muslim Arab armies invaded in the 630s, the depleted state collapsed. The Battle of al-Qādisiyyah in 637 was the decisive defeat; the last *shahanshah* Yazdegerd III was killed in 651, and Sasanian lands were absorbed into the Islamic Caliphate.

## Key Terms

- **Shahanshah**: "King of Kings," the imperial title.
- **Dehqan**: Landowning knight supplying provincial officers and cavalry.
- **Clibanarii / Cataphracts**: Elite heavy armoured cavalry.
- **Avesta**: The Zoroastrian scriptural collection, compiled under Sasanian rule.
- **Ctesiphon**: The Sasanian capital on the Tigris, in modern Iraq.

Its four-class social model, bureaucratic offices, and *shahanshah* idiom were inherited and adapted by the Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates, while Zoroastrian, Christian, and Greek intellectual traditions fed directly into early Islamic scholarship.
