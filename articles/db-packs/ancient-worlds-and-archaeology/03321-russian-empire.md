# Russian Empire

The Russian Empire was the final period of Russian monarchy, lasting from 1721, when Peter I took the title of Emperor, until the abdication of Nicholas II in 1917. At its peak it covered roughly one sixth of the world's land surface, a size made possible by two centuries of continuous conquest. Its history turns on a persistent tension between Westernising reform and conservative autocracy, a tension that gave way under the strain of industrialisation, war, and revolution.

## Foundations under Peter and Catherine

The modern empire was the work of two rulers. Peter the Great, reigning 1682 to 1725, defeated Sweden in the Great Northern War, gained access to the Baltic Sea, and built a new capital, Saint Petersburg, as a deliberate window to the west. He reorganised the army, government, and church along Western models, and in 1721 adopted the imperial title, formally founding the empire. Catherine the Great, reigning 1762 to 1796, extended the same project eastward and southward. Wars against the Ottoman Empire and the Partitions of Poland added Finland, the Crimea, and large Polish territories, while she promoted Enlightenment ideas among the nobility without loosening her own grip on power.

## The 19th Century: Reform, Reaction, and Defeat

The century followed a recurring pattern. A defeat or crisis exposed the empire's backwardness, triggered reforms, and was followed by a conservative reaction that rolled them back. Napoleon invaded in 1812 and lost catastrophically, after which Alexander I helped form the Holy Alliance to suppress liberalism across Europe. The failed Decembrist revolt of 1825, an uprising by reform-minded army officers, brought Nicholas I to power and inaugurated three decades of repression under the slogan "Orthodoxy, Autocracy, and Nationality."

The cycle repeated after the Crimean War of 1853 to 1856, a defeat that exposed military and administrative weakness against Britain, France, and the Ottoman Empire. Alexander II responded with sweeping reforms, the most important being the Emancipation Reform of 1861, which freed roughly 23 million serfs, peasants who had been legally bound to the land and to their masters since 1649. The reform was limited. Former serfs received allotments of land but had to pay heavy redemption taxes over many years, leaving many indebted and impoverished. After Alexander II was assassinated in 1881, his son Alexander III reversed much of the programme, pursuing aggressive Russification, the imposition of Russian language, Orthodox Christianity, and administration on non-Russian peoples, along with renewed repression of religious minorities, especially Jews, who were largely confined to a designated Pale of Settlement established in 1791.

## Society and Economy

By 1897 the empire had about 126 million people, of whom ethnic Russians made up roughly 45 percent. Society was divided into legally defined estates, called *sosloviyes*, with peasants forming over 80 percent of the population. The economy remained overwhelmingly agricultural, though late 19th-century industrialisation, aided by the Trans-Siberian Railway, produced significant urban growth. The Russian Orthodox Church was the state religion, and the emperor served as its supreme defender.

## Revolution and Collapse

Two shocks broke the autocracy's monopoly on force. Defeat in the Russo-Japanese War of 1904 to 1905, combined with the massacre of protesters on Bloody Sunday in Saint Petersburg, triggered a nationwide uprising. Nicholas II granted the October Manifesto, creating a constitution and the State Duma, an elected national parliament with limited powers, but retained ultimate authority. World War I made the underlying weaknesses fatal. Russian armies suffered massive casualties, food shortages spread, and the economy collapsed. Strikes and army mutinies in Petrograd, the name Saint Petersburg had been given in 1914, forced Nicholas II to abdicate in February 1917. A Provisional Government lasted only months before the Bolshevik-led October Revolution established a Soviet republic, ending the empire and Russian participation in the war.
