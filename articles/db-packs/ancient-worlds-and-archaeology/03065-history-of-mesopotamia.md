# History of Mesopotamia

Mesopotamia, "the land between the rivers" (the Tigris and Euphrates), is a historical region in the modern Middle East, mostly modern Iraq. It is called a "cradle of civilization" because it hosted some of the world's earliest cities, the first writing, and the first known law codes. Its history runs from prehistoric farming villages to the Arab Muslim conquests of the 630s–651 AD, and must be reconstructed from archaeology combined with later written records.

## Foundations: From Farming to Cities (c. 10,000–3100 BC)

Human settlement began in the foothills of the Taurus and Zagros Mountains, where people gradually shifted from hunting and gathering to agriculture and animal herding. The Pre-Pottery Neolithic periods (c. 10,000–6800 BC) mark this transition. Monumental structures such as Göbekli Tepe (c. 10,000 BC) show that hunter-gatherers could organize large building projects before farming took hold.

Three later cultures mattered most. The Halaf culture (c. 6500–5500 BC) is known for its painted pottery. The Ubaid culture spread northward from the southern alluvial plain, where it is the earliest known culture, possibly through adoption rather than invasion, since there is no archaeological break between the preceding Halaf and the later Northern Ubaid. The Uruk period (4th millennium BC) saw the rise of true cities, including Uruk itself, and the invention of **cuneiform**, a system of wedge-shaped marks on clay tablets that began as pictographs and by about 2600 BC had become a syllabic script, where each sign represents a syllable.

## The Third Millennium BC: Sumer, Akkad, and the First Empire

In the Early Dynastic period (c. 2900–2350 BC), southern Mesopotamia was divided into competing Sumerian city-states such as Ur and Uruk. The Sumerian language is a **language isolate**, meaning it has no known relatives, so where the Sumerians came from remains unknown. Their kingship lists are now considered unreliable for reconstructing real political history.

The Akkadian Empire (c. 2350–2170 BC), founded by Sargon, was the first large empire. Akkadian, a **Semitic language** (a family that later includes Hebrew and Arabic), became the region's common administrative language, a *lingua franca*, a shared language used across different native tongues, and was written in cuneiform. After Sargon's dynasty fell to the Guti, the Ur III Empire (c. 2112–2004 BC) briefly restored Sumerian rule with unusually heavy state control, before being overthrown by Elamites and Amorites.

## The Second Millennium BC: Babylon, Assyria, and Kassites

Amorite dynasties dominated the south during the Isin-Larsa period. One Amorite chief founded an independent Babylon in 1894 BC, which rose to power under Hammurabi (c. 1792–1750 BC), who unified much of Mesopotamia and issued the famous law code. To the north, Assyria eventually threw off Amorite rule and grew into a major power during the Middle Assyrian period (c. 1720–1076 BC), overthrowing the Hurrian-speaking Mitanni Empire, a northern Mesopotamian state that had been the earlier regional power. Farther south, the Kassites, a people of unknown linguistic origin, ruled Babylon for over four centuries (c. 1650–1155 BC), the longest-lasting Babylonian dynasty, though they left few records. The millennium ended with widespread invasions and collapse around 1200 BC.

## The First Millennium BC: Superpowers and Conquest

The Neo-Assyrian Empire (911–612 BC) was the largest empire of its time, conquering Egypt, Babylon, and the Levant, the eastern Mediterranean coast. It fell in 612 BC when a coalition of Babylonians, Medes, and Scythians destroyed its capital, Nineveh. The Neo-Babylonian Empire (620–539 BC), whose rulers included Nebuchadnezzar II, took over but was itself conquered by the Achaemenid Persians in 539 BC.

After that, Mesopotamia was ruled in turn by the Achaemenids, the Hellenistic Seleucids (Greek successors of Alexander), the Parthians, and the Sassanid Persians, who ruled from the 3rd to 7th centuries AD and under whom Christianity spread widely in Assyria. In the 630s–651 AD, Arab Muslim armies absorbed the region, ending the ancient political entities of Mesopotamia. Across all these layers of rule, Mesopotamia remained a wealthy, densely populated, and culturally productive satrapy, a province of a larger empire, under the Achaemenids and later empires, contributing to art, science, law, and trade.

## Chronology and Open Questions

Absolute dates for the 3rd and 2nd millennia BC are genuinely uncertain. Scholars use Ultra-long, Long, Middle, Short, and Ultra-short chronologies that disagree by up to 150 years; the Middle Chronology is most common in textbooks but is no more certainly correct than the others. Three other questions remain open: the unknown origins of the Sumerians, because their language is an isolate; the debated mechanism of the Ubaid expansion, adoption versus invasion; and the unreliability of the Sumerian King List for early political history. These uncertainties mean that Mesopotamia's chronology is a working framework, not a settled sequence.
