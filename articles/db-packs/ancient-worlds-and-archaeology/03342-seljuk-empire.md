# Seljuk Empire

The Seljuk Empire (1037–1308) was a high medieval Turco-Persian state that briefly reunified the eastern Islamic world, stretching from Anatolia to Central Asia. Within one dynasty's lifetime it passed from obscurity on the Syr Darya to guardianship of the Abbasid Caliph, then fractured into rival sultanates and was swept away by the Khwarazmians and Mongols. Its two longest legacies are the Turkification of Anatolia and the institutional framework of medieval Sunni Islam.

## Origins and Rise

The dynasty's founder, Seljuk, led the Qiniq branch of the Oghuz Turks, a confederation of Turkic nomads. Near the Syr Darya in 985 he converted to Islam, and his descendants migrated into Khorasan and Transoxiana, the Iranian-speaking lands just beyond the Islamic heartland. His grandsons Tughril and Chaghri captured Merv and Nishapur in 1037 and shattered the Ghaznavid army at the Battle of Dandanaqan in 1040. In 1055 Tughril entered Baghdad, displacing the Shia Buyid dynasty that had held the Abbasid caliphs as ceremonial figureheads. The Seljuks inherited that role without deposing the caliphs, ruling as their *temporal protectors*, meaning military guarantors of the Sunni caliphal institution.

## Golden Age

Under Malik-Shah I (r. 1072–1092) and his Persian vizier Nizam al-Mulk, the empire reached its greatest extent, touching China in the east and the Byzantine frontier in the west. Two institutional innovations anchored this peak. The *iqta'* system reassigned land revenue to military officers in place of cash salary, funding a standing army loyal to the sultan rather than to tribal kin. The *Nizamiyya* network of madrasas (Sunni colleges, usually built beside a mosque) trained jurists and bureaucrats in state-sponsored Sunni orthodoxy, explicitly to check Shia influence. The capital shifted to Isfahan, and Persian became the language of administration and court literature, even as Turkish remained the spoken language of the dynasty and the army. The result was a hybrid court culture historians call *Persianate*: Persian forms of statecraft, poetry, and architecture carried by a Turkic ruling class.

In 1071 Malik-Shah's father Alp Arslan defeated the Byzantine emperor at Manzikert in eastern Anatolia. The battle did not destroy Byzantium, but it opened the Anatolian plateau to mass settlement by Turkomans, the nomadic Turkmen tribal followers of the dynasty. These settlers were organised into *beyliks*, semi-autonomous principalities ruled by a *bey* (Turkic for chief or lord), and Anatolia's demographics shifted permanently from Greek-speaking Christian to Turkish-speaking Muslim.

## Fragmentation

Malik-Shah's death in 1092 triggered a civil war among his sons and brother, and the empire never cohered again. Real power drifted to *atabegs*, officials originally appointed as tutors and regents for young princes, who began ruling provinces in their own names while owing only nominal obedience to the sultan. The eastern provinces were lost at Qatwan in 1141 to the Qara-Khitai, a sinicised Buddhist dynasty from northern China. In 1153 the last effective Great Seljuk sultan, Ahmad Sanjar, was captured by his own rebellious Oghuz followers, and the eastern rump collapsed.

The first crusaders marched through this fractured landscape in 1096–1099. The Seljuks crushed the People's Crusade, a popular mob led by the preacher Peter the Hermit that crossed into Anatolia ahead of the main armies, but could not mount a coordinated defence of the Holy Land, and Jerusalem fell in 1099. In the western provinces Seljuk authority was progressively supplanted first by the Zengids, a dynasty of atabegs based in Mosul and Aleppo, and then by Saladin's Ayyubids, who restored Muslim unity in Egypt and the Levant.

## End of the Great Seljuks and Anatolian Survival

The Great Seljuk line ended in 1194, when Toghrul III was defeated by the Khwarazmian Empire, a northeastern Iranian state that briefly became the dominant power in the Islamic east before it was overrun by the Mongols in the 1220s. The Anatolian branch, the Sultanate of Rum (a name reflecting the Seljuk claim to the former Byzantine heartland), survived until the Mongol incursions of the 1260s, after which it fragmented into beyliks. One of these, the Ottoman beylik, would within two centuries absorb the others and inherit the Seljuk claim to Anatolia. The last Seljuk successor state fell in 1308.

## Culture and Religion

The Seljuks sponsored a recognisable architectural style, most distinctively the four-iwan mosque, a rectangular courtyard with a vaulted hall open on each of its four sides, often combined with *muqarnas* decoration, a form of three-dimensional honeycomb vaulting that creates a tiered, stalactite-like surface. Their court workshops pioneered the *Mina'i* technique of overglaze painted ceramics and produced illuminated manuscripts that blended Turkic, Persian, and Arab Islamic motifs. Persian remained the literary language of the court, and Seljuk chancery documents are the earliest extensive use of the *tughra*, a calligraphic imperial monogram later associated with the Ottomans. Mamluk slave-soldiers, enslaved men often of Turkic origin trained for military service, provided a professional core loyal to the sultan rather than to competing tribes.

In Anatolia, Manzikert converted a Christian Byzantine province into the future Turkish homeland. Across the wider Islamic world, the Nizamiyya madrasas and the iqta' system set templates for military, educational, and religious organisation that later powers, including the Ottomans, inherited and adapted.
