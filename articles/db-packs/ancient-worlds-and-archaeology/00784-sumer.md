# Sumer

Sumer was the earliest known civilization, established in southern Mesopotamia (modern Iraq) during the 5th–4th millennia BC. It introduced cuneiform writing, urban planning, legal codes, and large-scale irrigation, and is counted among the world's six cradles of civilization.

The Sumerians spoke a non-Semitic, non-Indo-European language, a linguistic isolate unrelated to any known family. They called their land *Kengir* and their language *Emegir*; the name "Sumer" comes from the later Akkadians. Most scholars place their settlement between c. 5500 and 3300 BC, though some propose origins in the Green Sahara or Dilmun (Bahrain). Before them, the Ubaidians developed agriculture, trade, and early towns, and were gradually absorbed into Sumerian society.

## Periods of Sumerian history

- **Ubaid and Uruk (c. 6500–3100 BC)**: Painted pottery and temple-centered settlements spread across the south. Uruk grew into the world's first city, exceeding 50,000 inhabitants, and proto-writing appeared.
- **Early Dynastic (c. 2900–2334 BC)**: Secular kingship (*lugal*) emerged alongside priest-governors (*ensi*). Legendary rulers like Gilgamesh and Enmerkar appear in early texts. Eannatum of Lagash built one of the first empires and commissioned the Stele of the Vultures.
- **Akkadian Empire (c. 2334–2154 BC)**: Sargon of Akkad unified much of Mesopotamia. Akkadian became the language of administration; Sumerian survived in scholarship and ritual. The empire fell to Gutian invasions and internal collapse.
- **Ur III (c. 2112–2004 BC)**: Ur-Nammu and Shulgi briefly restored Sumerian rule. Amorite incursions then broke central authority, and Amorite-led Babylon rose under Hammurabi by c. 1800 BC.

Sumerian survived as a liturgical language in temples and scribal schools for more than two thousand years after Ur III fell.

## Cities, government, and society

By the late 4th millennium BC, Sumer was a patchwork of independent city-states: Ur, Uruk, Lagash, Kish, and others. Each centered on a temple to its patron deity and was ruled by an *ensi* (priest-governor) or *lugal* (king, literally "great man") whose authority rested on religious sanction. The Sumerian King List mixes legendary pre-flood rulers with later historical ones.

Society was rigidly stratified. The Code of Ur-Nammu records early legal norms. Women could serve as priestesses and hold property, but their legal standing declined after 2300 BC. Marriage was typically arranged, with bridal gifts exchanged, and sexual ethics were framed around ritual purity rather than moral judgment.

## Writing, mathematics, and technology

Cuneiform began c. 3500 BC, evolving from pictographic tokens into wedge-shaped signs pressed into damp clay with a reed stylus. Thousands of tablets survive, covering law, commerce, literature, and astronomy.

Sumerians pioneered the wheel, the plow, the sailboat, and bronze tools. Their number system was *sexagesimal* (base-60), the direct ancestor of the 60-minute hour, the 60-second minute, and the 360-degree circle. Irrigation canals, shadufs (counterweighted bucket lifts), and organized *corvée* labor supported a barley-based agriculture that also produced surpluses of wool, textiles, and beer for trade with the Indus Valley, Anatolia, and the Persian Gulf. Barley and silver circulated as currency.

## Religion

Sumerian religion was anthropomorphic polytheism. Each city had a patron god: An of Uruk ruled the sky, Enlil of Nippur the storms, Enki of Eridu wisdom, Inanna of Uruk love and war. Creation myths centered on *hieros gamos* (sacred marriage) between divine pairs. The dead descended to a dark underworld ruled by Ereshkigal, a realm of gloom rather than moral judgment. Temple platforms, the stepped structures later called *ziggurats*, anchored both worship and the city's grain economy.

## Why Sumer ended

The same irrigation that built Sumer's wealth gradually poisoned it. Salts carried by river water accumulated in the fields, reducing yields between 2100 and 1700 BC. Combined with political fragmentation after the fall of Ur III, this prepared the ground for Amorite and Babylonian rule. Its cuneiform, legal, and administrative traditions shaped Assyria, Babylon, and Egypt for millennia.
