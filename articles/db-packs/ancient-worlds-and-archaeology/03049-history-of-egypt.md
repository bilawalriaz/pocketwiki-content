# History of Egypt

Egypt's story is the longest continuous record of any major civilization, built on the predictable flood-and-drought rhythm of the Nile, which let farmers settle, store surplus, and support a central state. That relationship between river, peasant, and ruler produced a unified kingdom around 3150 BC and, over the next 3,500 years, a string of native dynasties that built the pyramids, expanded an empire, and invented one of humanity's first writing systems. After losing independence in 525 BC, Egypt spent roughly 2,500 years absorbing wave after wave of foreign rulers, each leaving a cultural and religious layer, until a military-led republic in 1953 and an authoritarian order after 2013.

## The Three Kingdoms and the Age of Empire

Before unification, the Nile Valley held distinct cultures in Upper (southern) and Lower (northern) Egypt, including the Badari and Naqada, and developed hieroglyphic writing around 3200 BC. Narmer, often identified with the traditional figure Menes, merged the two lands around 3150 BC, opening the dynastic era.

The dynastic sequence divides into three peaks separated by collapse, a pattern driven by Nile floods, famine, and invasion:

| Period | Dates (BC) | Character |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Old Kingdom | c. 2700–2200 | "Golden age" of pyramid construction under pharaohs treated as living gods. |
| Middle Kingdom | from c. 2040 | Reunification and renewed prosperity after a First Intermediate collapse. |
| New Kingdom | c. 1550–1070 | Imperial expansion into Syria and Nubia; pharaohs such as Ramesses II; the religious revolution of Atenism, the worship of a single sun-disk god, under Akhenaten. |

Between the Middle and New Kingdoms, the Second Intermediate Period brought the Hyksos, a Semitic-Asiatic people who conquered Lower Egypt around 1650 BC. Their expulsion by Ahmose I founded the New Kingdom and brought chariot warfare and bronze weapons into Egyptian armies. After the New Kingdom collapsed, Egypt was ruled in turn by Libyans, Nubians, and Assyrians before a final period of native rule.

## Foreign Conquests and Cultural Layering

From 525 BC onward, Egypt was rarely independent for long. Each conqueror left a religious or institutional mark, layering Greek, Coptic Christian (the Egyptian form of Christianity), and Islamic Arab institutions over the pharaonic base.

- **Persians (Achaemenids).** Conquered Egypt in 525 BC, lost it, then reconquered it in 343 BC.
- **Greeks.** Alexander the Great took Egypt in 332 BC, founding Alexandria. His Ptolemaic successors, a Greek dynasty that adopted Egyptian titulature and temples, ruled until Cleopatra VII's suicide in 30 BC.
- **Romans.** Annexed Egypt as a personal province of the emperor; its grain fed Rome, and Alexandria remained a major intellectual center. Christianity took root early, and after the Council of Chalcedon in 451 AD most Egyptian Christians split from the Byzantine church to form the Coptic Church.
- **Arabs.** The conquest of 639–642 AD brought Sunni Islam, which blended with local traditions and made Egypt a center of the Islamic world, especially under the Fatimid Caliphate based in Cairo.
- **Mamluks and Ottomans.** A military caste of Turkic and Circassian slave-soldiers, the Mamluks, seized power around 1250 and ruled until the Ottoman conquest in 1517. Ottoman rule brought economic decline; Napoleon's brief 1798 invasion was a military failure but launched modern Egyptology.

## From Muhammad Ali to the Republic

After the French withdrew, an Albanian Ottoman officer, Muhammad Ali, seized control in 1805 and founded a dynasty that modernized the army, irrigation, and industry while incurring heavy European debt. The Suez Canal, completed in 1869, made Egypt strategically vital to Britain, which crushed a nationalist revolt and installed a protectorate in 1882. A 1919 revolt produced nominal independence in 1922, but British military presence continued.

The Free Officers Movement, a secret group of Egyptian army officers, overthrew the king in 1952, and a republic was declared in 1953.

- **Gamal Abdel Nasser (1956–1970).** Nationalized the canal, triggering the 1956 Suez Crisis; briefly united Egypt with Syria in the United Arab Republic; lost the 1967 Six-Day War to Israel.
- **Anwar Sadat (1970–1981).** Switched Egypt from Soviet to American alignment, launched the 1973 October War, and signed the 1979 peace treaty with Israel. Islamists assassinated him in 1981.
- **Hosni Mubarak (1981–2011).** Presided over three decades of stability, state-of-emergency rule, and a violent Islamist insurgency in the 1990s and 2000s.

## Twenty-First-Century Upheaval

In 2011, mass protests across Cairo's Tahrir Square forced Mubarak to resign. The Muslim Brotherhood's Mohamed Morsi won the presidency in 2012, but mass protests returned in 2013 and the military, led by General Abdel Fattah el-Sisi, removed him in July 2013. El-Sisi won the presidency in 2014 and again in 2018, backed by constitutional changes that strengthened the military's political and economic role and returned Egypt to authoritarian governance.

## Open Questions

Menes, traditionally credited with unifying Egypt, may be a later literary figure merged with the historically attested Narmer. Akhenaten's Atenism is read by some scholars as true monotheism and by others as monolatry, devotion to one god while not denying others. Genetic studies disagree on the ancestry of ancient Egyptians: a 2017 mummy survey found close affinity to Near Eastern populations, while a 2025 full-genome study of a single Old Kingdom individual proposed a two-source model with major contributions from both the Levant and Mesopotamia.
