# Seleucid Empire

The Seleucid Empire was the largest of the Hellenistic states carved out of Alexander the Great's realm, ruling West Asia from 312 BC until Rome absorbed it in 63 BC. At its peak it stretched from Anatolia to the borders of India, a multi-ethnic territory in which Greek language, cities, and administration sat on top of older Near Eastern societies. That scale made it the chief vehicle for Greek culture east of the Mediterranean, but it also made the state structurally fragile, because long frontiers, restless subject peoples, and ambitious neighbours could all be exploited at once.

A short term helps here: the *Diadochi* ("successors") were Alexander's rival generals, who divided his empire after his death in 323 BC. One of them, Seleucus I Nicator, seized Babylon in 312 BC, the conventional founding date of the empire. Over the next decade he fought the other Diadochi and pushed Seleucid authority across the old Achaemenid lands, from Anatolia to the eastern Iranian plateau. He also fought the Mauryan emperor Chandragupta around 305–303 BC; ancient sources do not name a clear victor. The settlement gave Seleucus 500 war elephants in return for ceding territory east of the Indus (parts of modern Afghanistan and Pakistan). Those elephants helped him win the Battle of Ipsus in 301 BC, securing eastern Anatolia and northern Syria. He founded two new capitals, Antioch in Syria and Seleucia on the Tigris.

How the empire worked depended on a layered arrangement. A Greek urban elite, reinforced by immigration from the Aegean, formed the ruling class, while the mass of the population kept its local language, religion, and customs. The state founded hundreds of Greek-style cities called *poleis*, which acted as administrative nodes, trading hubs, and garrisons. Local elites who adopted Greek names, language, and education were absorbed into this system, a process called *Hellenization*. The army was built around the Macedonian *phalanx* (a block of spearmen using long pikes called sarissas), filled out with native and mercenary troops. Land was given to military settlers, the *katoikoi*, in return for service, tying the soldier to the state and the state to the land. The empire inherited the Persian *satrapy*, a province run by a governor, as its basic unit of administration.

The economy was overwhelmingly agricultural, with roughly 80–90% of the population working the land. Wealth came in through tribute from cities and temples and a proportional tax on royal estates, while irrigation works, especially in Mesopotamia, kept farmland productive. Coinage was sophisticated but uneven: coastal regions used the Greek *Attic standard* of weights, while Mesopotamia kept the older shekel system. The army was paid in silver, and bronze coins handled everyday trade. Babylonian astronomical diaries that recorded barley prices show markets responding to supply and demand, so the economy is now seen as a hybrid, partly market-oriented rather than purely autarkic or fully modern.

The same scale that made the empire rich also broke it. Two shocks hit at once in the second century BC. In the west, Antiochus III the Great's attempt to recover lost ground ended in defeat by Rome at Magnesia in 190 BC; the Treaty of Apamea in 188 BC stripped the Seleucids of everything west of the Taurus Mountains and imposed a heavy indemnity. In the east, provinces peeled away. Diodotus had set up an independent Greco-Bactrian kingdom around 245 BC, and the Arsacid dynasty under Arsaces had taken Parthia around 238 BC. The indemnity starved the treasury, and a weakened centre could no longer hold the Iranian frontier. The death of Antiochus VII Sidetes in 129 BC marked the final loss of the eastern provinces to Parthia.

Religion finished the south. Antiochus IV Epiphanes tried to impose Greek cult practice on Judea, triggering the Maccabean Revolt of 167–160 BC, which fused political resistance with a defence of Jewish law and permanently cost the Seleucids loyalty in the region. After a century of civil war, the empire shrank to a rump in Syria; Tigranes of Armenia overran that remnant around 83 BC, and in 63 BC the Roman general Pompey annexed Syria, formally ending the empire. Defeat by Rome clipped its western wing, the Parthians and Greco-Bactrians tore off its eastern wing, and internal revolt and civil war prevented it from rebuilding either.
