# Qing dynasty

The Qing dynasty (1636/1644–1912) was the last imperial dynasty of China, founded by the Manchus, a Tungusic people from Manchuria. At its 18th-century peak it was the largest empire in Chinese history, holding roughly 300 million people and the territorial base of modern China. It then declined under foreign pressure and internal rebellion until republican revolution ended imperial rule in 1912.

## Origins and conquest

The Manchus began as the Jurchen tribes of Manchuria. In 1616 their leader Nurhaci unified the Jurchens, created the Eight Banners, a combined military, social, and administrative system that organised Manchu, Mongol, and Han Chinese populations into units, and proclaimed the Later Jin dynasty. His son Hong Taiji renamed the people "Manchu" and rebranded the state "Great Qing" in 1636, building a Chinese-style bureaucracy on top of Manchu institutions.

In 1644 peasant rebels captured Beijing and toppled the Ming. The Qing allied with Ming general Wu Sangui at Shanhai Pass, the mountain gateway between Manchuria and the North China Plain, entered China proper, and presented themselves as heirs to the Mandate of Heaven, the traditional Chinese belief that heaven grants a ruler the right to govern, lost through misrule and transferable by rebellion. Conquest of the rest of China took nearly forty more years, finishing in 1683 when Ming loyalists in Taiwan surrendered. A 1645 Queue Order forced Han Chinese men to shave the front of the head and braid the rest as a sign of submission, a physical marker of Manchu rule that lasted until 1912.

## The High Qing (1683–1796)

The reigns of the Kangxi, Yongzheng, and Qianlong emperors formed the dynasty's zenith. Kangxi suppressed the Revolt of the Three Feudatories, three southern Han Chinese generals granted semi-autonomous fiefdoms to help conquer the Ming, and expanded into Central Asia and Taiwan. Yongzheng tightened central administration and finances. Qianlong oversaw the "Ten Great Campaigns" that pushed the empire to its greatest extent and patronised massive encyclopaedic projects. By the 1790s the empire held some 300 million people.

Rule rested on a deliberately multi-ethnic framework. The emperor simultaneously acted as Confucian sage to Han Chinese, Mongol khan on the steppe, and patron of Tibetan Buddhism. The Lifan Yuan, a government agency for Mongolia, Tibet, and other Inner Asian territories, handled these affairs outside the normal Chinese bureaucracy. The Grand Council, an Inner Court advisory body that functioned as the de facto cabinet, coordinated daily governance. In 1689 the Treaty of Nerchinsk with Russia, the first formal agreement China signed with a European power, stabilised the northern border and set the Amur boundary.

## Decline and collapse

After Qianlong the dynasty entered a long decline. The First Opium War (1839–42), fought over Britain's opium exports, ended with the Treaty of Nanjing, which opened treaty ports. The Second Opium War (1856–60) deepened the concessions. Trade had previously run in China's favour through silver inflows; the opium trade reversed this, draining silver and stressing the fiscal system.

Internal collapse was more damaging still. The Taiping Rebellion (1850–64), a civil war that killed millions and devastated the south, forced the dynasty to rely on regional Han Chinese armies that increasingly acted independently. The Self-Strengthening Movement of the 1860s–1890s tried to adopt Western military and industrial technology while preserving Confucian institutions, but it could not prevent defeat by a modernising Japan in the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95), which stripped Taiwan from the empire. The Hundred Days' Reform of 1898 attempted deeper institutional change but was blocked by conservatives. The Boxer Uprising of 1900, an anti-foreign movement the court initially supported, provoked invasion by the Eight-Nation Alliance and a crushing indemnity.

By 1905 the imperial examination system was abolished and provincial assemblies were created, reforms that admitted the old order's failure. The Wuchang Uprising in October 1911 spread from the Han-controlled New Army in Wuhan across the provinces, and in early 1912 the Xuantong Emperor (Puyi) abdicated, ending the dynasty.

## Causes of decline

Three structural weaknesses compounded over the century. Government revenue stayed low relative to population, leaving the state unable to fund modern military or industrial capacity. The Manchu ruling elite grew increasingly isolated and unwilling to share real power with the Han Chinese gentry who supplied most administrators. And the 19th-century world order, industrialised, imperialist, and willing to impose Unequal Treaties granting extraterritoriality, low tariffs, and indemnities to foreign powers, demanded a centralised, technologically adaptive state the Qing had not built and could not quickly build. Foreign pressure set the conditions; internal rigidity decided the outcome.
