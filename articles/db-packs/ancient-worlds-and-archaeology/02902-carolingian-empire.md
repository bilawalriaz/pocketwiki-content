# Carolingian Empire

The Carolingian Empire (800–887) was a Frankish-dominated state in Western and Central Europe, the largest western political entity after Rome's fall and the first phase of what later became the Holy Roman Empire. Its significance lies in three things it created: a Western imperial tradition independent of Byzantium, a template for medieval Christian governance, and the political fault lines that became France and Germany.

## Origins and Charlemagne

The dynasty rose through military success. Charles Martel's victory at Tours in 732 halted a Muslim army advancing into Francia and made his family the effective rulers. His son Pepin III secured the throne in 751 with papal approval, formalising what the battle had made real. Charlemagne took power in 768 and expanded the empire through near-constant warfare, absorbing Lombardy, Bavaria, Saxony, and lands up to the Elbe.

On Christmas Day 800, Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne Roman Emperor. The act bypassed the weakened Byzantine Empire's claim to imperial authority and established a new Western line. It also created a lasting ambiguity: was the emperor's authority sacred, derived through the pope, or secular, derived from conquest and Frankish tradition? The Carolingians never resolved this tension.

## The Empire's Structure

Charlemagne ruled a vast territory with a deliberately simple apparatus. A small **itinerant court** moved between royal palaces, with Aachen as the most important. Local administration rested on **counts**, appointed officials who governed districts and presided over the **mallus**, a public assembly for routine justice. To check the counts, Charlemagne sent out **missi dominici**, royal emissaries who travelled in pairs (one secular, one ecclesiastical) to audit provincial government and enforce royal orders.

A defining legal feature was the **plurality of law**: Franks, Lombards, Saxons, and others were tried under their own traditional codes rather than a single imperial law. On top of this customary layer, Charlemagne issued **capitularies**, written royal decrees that set standards and announced reforms. Historians still debate whether these were effective law or a "royal wish-list," since evidence of consistent enforcement is thin.

Charlemagne reformed the silver coinage to imitate Roman models, placing his image and the title *imperator augustus* on the coins, a visual claim to imperial continuity. The economy remained agrarian; soldiers in annual campaigns supplied their own weapons and provisions, and the empire fielded no standing professional army.

## Christianity as Governing Ideology

Charlemagne treated his realm as a Christian empire (*Imperium Christianum*). The *Admonitio Generalis* of 789 set standards for clerical education and made the emperor personally responsible for his subjects' salvation. This fused religious and political authority: bishops and abbots became key royal officials, not merely spiritual leaders. The accompanying Carolingian Renaissance brought scholars like Alcuin to court, promoted learning, and preserved classical texts that might otherwise have been lost.

## Division and Collapse

Charlemagne died in 814, and his son Louis the Pious inherited an empire that had outgrown any mechanism for transferring whole. Louis's attempt to include a fourth son in the succession triggered civil war among his surviving sons. After Louis's death in 840, his eldest son Lothar claimed the whole realm, reigniting the conflict. The **Treaty of Verdun (843)** ended the fighting by splitting the empire into three autonomous kingdoms: a central middle realm (including Italy and the imperial title) for Lothar, an eastern kingdom for Louis the German, and a western kingdom for Charles the Bald. This three-way split is the political moment that produced the separate kingdoms of France and Germany.

After 843, the imperial title passed between Carolingian branches, but real power fragmented. Viking, Magyar, and Muslim raids added external pressure that local authorities could not always meet. Charles the Fat briefly reunited the three kingdoms in 884, but his failure to confront the Vikings led to his deposition in 887, and his death in 888 ended any hope of restoration. Regional nobles then elected kings outside the Carolingian line, though a legitimate Carolingian branch returned in the west in 898 and held the throne until 987.

## What the Empire Left Behind

Three legacies outlasted the polity. Politically, the Treaty of Verdun fixed a central, eastern, and western division on the map that hardened into modern Europe's core states. Religiously, the model of a Christian emperor ruling with bishops as administrators shaped medieval kingship for centuries. Culturally, the Carolingian Renaissance preserved a critical mass of classical learning, including much of what survived of Latin literature, that fed directly into the twelfth-century intellectual revival. The collapse was a fragmentation into the regional, Christian, militarised kingdoms that defined the rest of the Middle Ages.
