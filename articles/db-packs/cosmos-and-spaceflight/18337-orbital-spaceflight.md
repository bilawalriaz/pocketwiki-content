# Orbital spaceflight

An orbital spaceflight is any spaceflight that places a spacecraft on a trajectory capable of completing at least one full revolution around an astronomical body. To distinguish it from sub-orbital flights, which briefly reach space but whose perigee sits inside the atmosphere, an orbital flight must cross an altitude threshold where the vehicle can sustain itself for an entire orbit without further propulsion.

## Where space begins

Different institutions draw the boundary at different altitudes. NASA, the US Air Force, and the FAA put it near80 km. At that height, an object needs a circular-orbit speed of about 7.8 km/s. Atmospheric drag at 80 km is so intense that the practical minimum for completing one full orbit without thrust is closer to 150 km. The Fédération Aéronautique Internationale instead uses100 km, the Kármán line, named for Theodore von Kármán, who showed that above this altitude a craft must already travel faster than orbital velocity just to derive aerodynamic lift from the thinning air. The 100 km line is the conventional marker between aeronautics and astronautics.

Going higher reduces drag and lengthens the orbit but costs more energy, because the velocity change required, called delta-v (the total change in speed a propulsion system must deliver), rises with altitude.

## The three main orbital bands

Orbits around Earth fall into three broad regions:

- Low Earth orbit (LEO): up to roughly 2000 km. Most crewed missions and Earth observation satellites live here.
- Medium Earth orbit (MEO): between LEO and GEO.
- Geostationary orbit (GEO): about 35,786 km above the equator, where a satellite's orbital period matches the Earth's rotation and so appears fixed over one point on the ground.

Every orbit lies in a plane through the Earth's centre, inclined to the equator. That plane combined with Earth's rotation underneath sets what part of the planet a spacecraft can see and where it appears in the sky from the ground; this projection is the ground track.

## Launching to orbit

Only rocket engines have ever put a payload into orbit from Earth. The total delta-v the rocket must deliver to its payload is about 9.3 to 10 km/s. About 7.8 km/s of that is the horizontal velocity needed for orbit; the rest covers atmospheric drag (around 300 m/s for a dense 20 m rocket), gravity losses during the climb, and the climb itself. The standard technique is a brief vertical rise, then a gravity turn (a slow tilt from vertical toward horizontal so the rocket's path follows a curved descent it would have flown anyway), then a 5 to 8 minute horizontal acceleration burn at 170 km or higher until orbital velocity is reached. Most rockets are expendable, and two to four stages are usually needed to hit the required delta-v.

In special cases the rocket is not the first stage. The Pegasus, built for small satellites, is dropped from an aircraft at 12 km altitude so its rocket only has to do part of the climb. Far more ambitious concepts, including space elevators, rotovators, launch loops, scramjet and SABRE-class spaceplanes, and gun launch for cargo, have been proposed as cheaper routes to orbit, though several depend on materials stronger than any now manufactured. Since 2015 SpaceX has taken a more incremental path, using propulsive landing and reuse of the first-stage booster, the Dragon capsule, and payload fairings; early estimates suggested the changes could cut the cost of an orbital launch by roughly an order of magnitude.

## Staying in orbit

A satellite below about 200 km is unstable; atmospheric drag pulls it down within days. About 350 km is the usual lower limit for sustained low Earth orbit. Explorer 1, launched on 1 February 1958, used a perigee of 358 km and stayed aloft more than 12 years before re-entering over the Pacific on 31 March 1970. Exact lifetimes depend on altitude, the satellite's ballistic coefficient (its mass-to-drag ratio), and space weather, which swells or shrinks the upper atmosphere.

Long-term stability still requires work. Orbital station-keeping uses small thruster burns to counter non-Keplerian forces, meaning the perturbations beyond simple Earth-as-uniform-sphere gravity: the slight lumpiness of Earth's gravity, pulls from the Sun and Moon, solar radiation pressure, and residual air drag. At the Lagrangian points (positions in a two-body system where a third small body can orbit in step with both), a halo orbit is naturally unstable, and station-keeping becomes essential rather than optional, because any tiny deviation causes the spacecraft to drift away.

## Returning from orbit

To come back, a craft must lose most of its orbital speed. Crewed vehicles fire retrorockets to lower perigee into the atmosphere, then use aerobraking: a blunt heat shield pushes a shockwave of compressed air ahead of the vehicle, dissipating kinetic energy as heat in that shockwave rather than in the spacecraft itself. Small or unpowered satellites in LEO, including many nanosatellites, rely on natural drag to slow them. Once perigee dips into the mesosphere, even unshielded objects are stripped of nearly all remaining speed. Sub-orbital flights re-enter far more slowly and so generate far less heating. Most space agencies now prefer controlled re-entries, even for expendable hardware, to limit debris striking people or property on the ground.

## A short history

Sputnik 1, launched by the Soviet Union on 4 October 1957, was the first human-made object to reach orbit. The first human to do so was Yuri Gagarin aboard Vostok 1 on 12 April 1961, followed by Valentina Tereshkova aboard Vostok 6 on 16 June 1963, the first woman in orbit. Crew Dragon Demo-2, launched on 30 May 2020 by SpaceX and the United States, was the first crewed orbital flight operated by a private company. Nine orbital crewed spacecraft programmes have flown, from Vostok (1961–1963) to Crew Dragon (2020 onward), for a combined 333 launches.
