# Space

Space is the arena in which objects have positions and directions, traditionally understood as a three‑dimensional continuum and now treated as part of a four‑dimensional spacetime woven with time. Across physics, philosophy, and mathematics, the same word covers three live questions: what space is (a thing, a set of relations, or a mental framework), what its shape is (flat or curved), and how it behaves under gravity and motion.

## Philosophical foundations: container, relation, or intuition

In the fourth century BCE, Plato's *Timaeus* introduced *khôra*, an elusive receptacle in which things come to be, while Aristotle's *Physics* defined *topos* (place) as the inner boundary of a surrounding body. A millennium later, Alhazen's *Discourse on Place* continued the debate over whether place belongs to an object or to the world.

The question sharpened in the seventeenth century, when Isaac Newton argued that space is **absolute**: an infinite, uniform container that exists whether or not anything occupies it. He supported this with the rotating bucket: water in a spinning bucket climbs the sides and becomes concave, and the concavity persists briefly after the bucket stops, so the water curves relative to space itself, not merely to the bucket. Gottfried Leibniz replied that space is only "that which results from places taken together," a network of relations among objects. His identity of indiscernibles sharpened the point: if two universes contained the same matter in the same arrangement, an extra, undetectable absolute space would make no difference.

Immanuel Kant offered a third route. Space, he argued, is not learned from experience but is a *form of intuition* built into the mind, the framework that organises every spatial perception. Because even people without sight perceive space through touch, hearing, and smell, spatial knowledge feels given rather than acquired, yet it is informative about the world; Kant called this status *synthetic a priori*.

## The shape of space: from Euclid to curved geometry

For two thousand years, Euclidean geometry was taken to describe the actual shape of space, resting on the **parallel postulate** that exactly one line parallel to a given line passes through any point not on it. In the 1830s, János Bolyai and Nikolai Lobachevsky dropped the postulate, producing **hyperbolic geometry** in which infinitely many parallels pass through a point and the angles of a triangle sum to less than 180°. In the 1850s, Bernhard Riemann developed **elliptical geometry**, in which no parallel lines exist and triangle angles exceed 180°.

Whether the physical world follows Euclid or one of these alternatives is a separate question. Carl Friedrich Gauss proposed measuring stellar triangles to test it. Henri Poincaré countered with his "sphere‑world" thought experiment: scientists living on a sphere whose material expands with temperature could warm a flat disc into a curved one yet have no experiment to tell curvature from flatness. The choice of geometry, Poincaré concluded, is conventional, and Euclidean geometry wins on simplicity.

## Relativity: space and time as one entity

In 1905, Einstein's **special relativity** showed that measurements of space and time depend on the observer's motion, fusing the two into a four‑dimensional **spacetime**. The speed of light is the same for every observer, simultaneity is relative, and moving clocks run slow. In 1915, **general relativity** deepened the picture: gravity is not a force but the curvature of spacetime produced by mass and energy. Objects follow **geodesics**, the straightest possible paths through this curved geometry, which near Earth looks like Newton's falling apple. The theory predicted that starlight bends as it passes the Sun, that clocks deeper in a gravitational well tick more slowly, and that accelerating masses send out **gravitational waves**, ripples in spacetime. In 2015, LIGO recorded the first direct detection of these waves.

## The modern view

Three‑dimensional space is now understood as a slice of four‑dimensional **Minkowski spacetime** (the flat spacetime of special relativity, extended by gravity into general relativity). The universe began in the Big Bang around 13.8 billion years ago and expanded rapidly through cosmic inflation; its large‑scale shape is still unknown, and whether spacetime is fundamentally continuous or built from discrete pieces remains unresolved. Reconciling that picture with quantum mechanics is the central open problem in theoretical physics.

The metre is defined as the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 seconds, tying length to the constant speed at the heart of relativity. In mathematics, a "space" is any set equipped with added structure, whether topological, metric, or vector. In geography and the social sciences, scholars such as Henri Lefebvre (*The Production of Space*, 1974), Edward Soja ("Thirdspace"), and Homi Bhabha ("Third Space") treat space as a product of social forces, including capitalism, colonialism, and globalisation, sitting alongside its physical meaning.
