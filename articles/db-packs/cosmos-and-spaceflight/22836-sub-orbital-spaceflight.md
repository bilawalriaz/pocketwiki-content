# Sub-orbital spaceflight

A sub-orbital spaceflight is a spaceflight in which the vehicle reaches outer space but its trajectory intersects the surface of the gravitating body from which it was launched. It never completes a full orbit, never becomes an artificial satellite, and never reaches escape velocity. Newton's cannonball shows why: a projectile fired fast enough falls around the Earth in a closed ellipse (an orbit); fired slower, the ellipse is so elongated that the ground cuts through it, so the projectile rises, arcs over, and falls back. Both paths in that diagram are sub-orbital. The boundary between sub-orbital and orbital is the orbital energy threshold, not speed in isolation.

## Where "space" begins

By international convention (Fédération Aéronautique Internationale), a sub-orbital spaceflight must exceed 100 km (62 mi) above sea level, the Kármán line. That altitude is roughly where a vehicle would have to fly faster than orbital speed just to hold itself up with aerodynamic lift, so wings stop making sense. The US military and NASA award astronaut wings at 50 mi (80 km), so SpaceShipTwo flights in the 80 to 100 km band count as spaceflight under US rules but not FAI rules. Flights that never reach 80 km, however high, are not officially sub-orbital spaceflights.

A vehicle that reaches orbital speed, completes most of an orbit, and then de-orbits (the source's example is the Fractional Orbital Bombardment System) is not sub-orbital, because it achieved orbit first. Reaching space is necessary but not sufficient.

## Energy, speed, and altitude

The extra specific energy a sub-orbital flight needs, beyond simply lifting the spacecraft to space, is less than the energy of the lowest circular orbit. The required launch speeds sort out as:

| Mission | Δv needed |
|---|---|
| Vertical hop to 100 km (Ansari X Prize target) | about 1.4 km/s |
| V-2 style flight, 330 km downrange | about 1.6 km/s |
| Low Earth orbit near 300 km altitude | about 9.2 km/s (8.1 km/s theoretical minimum without drag, less from the equator) |

To save fuel, the rocket cuts off well below peak altitude and the vehicle coasts up and down in free-fall; maximum speed occurs at the lowest points of the trajectory (start and end), not the top. Because the coasting phase traces an ellipse with one focus at Earth's centre, longer ranges climb higher: an intercontinental missile on a 10,000 km path peaks above 1,300 km, and the asymptotic Δv limit of about 7.9 km/s is reached as range approaches 20,000 km (halfway around the world). Re-entry heating scales with that maximum speed, so a 1 km/s hop barely heats the vehicle while an intercontinental sub-orbital trajectory faces heating comparable to a returning orbital capsule.

## Duration

A vertical 1 km/s hop lasts about three and a half minutes. An intercontinental missile spends 3 to 5 minutes in boost, about 25 minutes in free-fall, and about 2 minutes in re-entry. The minimum-Δv trajectory for any range comes from the geometry of an ellipse with one focus at Earth's centre and the other halfway between launch and target; flight time for a 10,000 km hop is about 32 minutes, about 42 minutes for halfway around the world. Sub-orbital flights have ranged from a few seconds (sounding rockets) to 43 hours (Pioneer 1, whose Moon-bound upper stage failed and re-entered).

## History and uses

The first sub-orbital spaceflight was MW 18014, a German V-2 test rocket launched from Peenemünde on 20 June 1944, reaching 176 km. The V-2 became the ancestor of every ICBM and IRBM, and captured V-2s were converted into sounding rockets that seeded modern scientific sub-orbital flight. The first living creature in space was Albert II, a rhesus macaque, on a US V-2 sounding rocket in June 1949.

Crewed sub-orbital flight began with Alan Shepard on Mercury-Redstone 3 on 5 May 1961, then the X-15 (air-launched to roughly 100 km, gliding back). Most later crewed sub-orbital flights were either X-15-style air-launched gliders or unintended results of failed orbital launches, including Soyuz 18a (1975), the Challenger disaster (1986), and Soyuz MS-10 (2018). The first commercial sub-orbital spaceflight was SpaceShipOne flight 15P on 21 June 2004; two further SpaceShipOne flights won the Ansari X Prize that October. From 2021 onward, Blue Origin's New Shepard has flown tourist flights out of Corn Ranch, Texas, while Virgin Galactic's SpaceShipTwo has flown a parallel stream of paying passengers from Spaceport America. Mike Hughes's self-built rockets (2014 to 2020) reached only fractions of a kilometre and ended with his death on the final launch.

The main current uses are scientific sounding rockets (microgravity and upper-atmosphere experiments), military systems, and space tourism, which is the only use now in routine commercial service. Sub-orbital point-to-point transport (Europe to North America in under an hour, studied for X-20 Dyna-Soar and now proposed for SpaceX Starship) remains a concept: a transcontinental sub-orbital rocket needs a launcher only marginally smaller than an ICBM, with Δv sitting between that of a vertical hop and low Earth orbit, so the price advantage over orbit is real but small, and the binding constraint is reusable, reliable engines rather than raw energy.
