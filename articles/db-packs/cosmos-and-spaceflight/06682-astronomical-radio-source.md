# Astronomical radio source

An astronomical radio source is any object in outer space that emits strong radio waves. Radio emission comes from planets, stars, supernova remnants, distant galaxies, and the faint afterglow of the Big Bang itself, so radio astronomy samples an unusually wide range of the universe.

## How the field began

In 1932, Karl Jansky of Bell Laboratories, investigating radio interference, detected a steady hiss that turned out to come from the centre of the Milky Way. This was the first radio wave detection from space. Grote Reber completed the first radio sky survey in 1941, and in the 1970s individual stars such as the unusual binary MWC 349 were identified as radio emitters.

## Solar System sources

The Sun is the brightest radio source in the sky down to about 300 MHz (1 m wavelength). When the Sun is quiet, the diffuse galactic background dominates at longer wavelengths, but during geomagnetic storms the Sun can dominate even at those frequencies.

Jupiter produces intense, beamed radio emission from its polar regions. Volcanic activity on the moon Io injects gas into Jupiter's magnetosphere, forming a torus of particles. As Io moves through this torus, the interaction launches Alfvén waves that carry ionised material down Jupiter's magnetic field lines to the poles, where a cyclotron maser mechanism converts the energy into radio waves beamed along a cone. When Earth crosses that cone, Jupiter can briefly outshine the Sun in radio output. The Juno spacecraft, in orbit since 2016, detected an FM-like signal from Ganymede at the points where its magnetic field lines link to Jupiter's, produced by the same cyclotron maser instability.

## Galactic sources

The Galactic Centre was the first radio source ever detected. It hosts Sagittarius A, a compact region that includes Sagittarius A*, the Milky Way's supermassive black hole, and its accretion disc, which flares brightly in radio waves. In the 2000s three Galactic Centre Radio Transients (GCRTs) were identified, and a fourth candidate was detected six times in 2020. In 2021 astronomers reported highly circularly polarised, intermittent radio waves from near the Galactic Centre whose source could represent a new class of object that GCRTs do not fully explain.

Supernova remnants often glow diffusely in radio light; Cassiopeia A is the brightest extrasolar radio source in the sky, with the Crab Nebula as another well-known example. A supernova that leaves behind a dense, spinning neutron star produces a pulsar, which fires jets of charged particles that radiate synchrotron emission (broadband radiation from particles spiralling in a magnetic field) in the radio band. The Crab Pulsar was the first pulsar discovered, and in 2003 the Parkes telescope found the first two pulsars orbiting each other. Rotating Radio Transients (RRATs), discovered in 2006 by Maura McLaughlin's team at Jodrell Bank, are a related class whose flashes last less than one second per day and are easily mistaken for terrestrial interference.

In star-forming regions, complex molecules in dense gas clouds emit short radio waves, while spiral galaxies contain neutral hydrogen and carbon monoxide clouds whose characteristic radio frequencies have been used to map much of the Milky Way.

## Extragalactic sources

Many galaxies, called radio galaxies, are powerful radio emitters, with Centaurus A and Messier 87 as prominent examples. Quasars ("quasi-stellar radio sources") were among the first point-like radio sources identified; their large redshifts showed they are extremely distant active galactic nuclei powered by supermassive black holes, whose jets of charged particles produce synchrotron radiation. 3C 273 is the optically brightest quasar in the sky. Merging galaxy clusters also show diffuse radio emission.

The cosmic microwave background is faint blackbody radiation (radiation whose spectrum is set solely by temperature) left over from the Big Bang, around 13.8 billion years ago.

Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) are millisecond-long, highly energetic radio flashes from outside our galaxy. The first, the Lorimer burst, was a 30-jansky dispersed signal under 5 ms long, located 3° from the Small Magellanic Cloud and inferred to originate more than 1 gigaparsec away. Hundreds of similar events may occur daily, and if routinely detected they could serve as cosmological probes. Possible progenitors include neutron star–neutron star, neutron star–black hole, or black hole–black hole mergers. In 2010 sixteen similar pulses from Parkes were shown to be terrestrial, but in 2013 four genuine extragalactic sources were confirmed. Blitzars are one proposed explanation.

## Hypothetical and unconfirmed sources

According to the Big Bang model, density fluctuations in the early universe could have created primordial black holes, smaller than known stellar black holes, that would survive cosmic expansion. Martin Rees theorised that a black hole evaporating via Hawking radiation would not radiate radio waves directly but would produce an expanding fireball of gamma rays and particles that pushes on surrounding magnetic fields and so generates a radio signal, which the Astropulse project aims to detect. SETI projects, beginning with Project Ozma, have traditionally searched for narrow-band signals analogous to human broadcasts, and Astropulse's broader transient search can run in parallel with them.
