# Absolute space and time

Absolute space and time is the idea that space and time form a fixed, unchanging backdrop against which all physical events occur. Every object then has a true state of motion measured against space itself, so a body is either genuinely at rest or moving at some absolute speed, independent of any observer. Time, on the same account, flows uniformly everywhere in the universe regardless of what is happening in it.

## Newton's formulation

Isaac Newton introduced the concept in the *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica* (1687) as the foundation for his mechanics. He defined absolute time as something that "flows equably without regard to anything external," a mathematical duration that exists whether or not anything perceives it, while relative time is the imperfect, sensible measure we read off clocks and celestial motion. Absolute space, in his words, "removes always similar and immovable," while relative space is the movable measure our senses fix by reference to bodies. Absolute motion is the change of a body from one absolute place to another.

Because the underlying frame is imperceptible, Newton offered empirical tests. A solitary spinning sphere bulges at its equator, which he took as evidence of rotation relative to absolute space. Two isolated spheres joined by a rope sit under tension when they rotate about their common centre, again revealing absolute, not merely relative, motion.

## Objections and the rise of inertial frames

The strongest rival view came from Gottfried Leibniz, who held that space is nothing but the relative position of bodies, and time nothing but their relative motion. George Berkeley pushed the point further: a lone sphere in an empty universe could not meaningfully be said to rotate, and a pair could rotate only with respect to each other, never about their own barycentre. Ernst Mach sharpened this into Mach's principle, the claim that mechanics is entirely about relations among masses, so a single particle in a universe with no other bodies would have zero mass. In Mach's words, saying a body keeps its direction and velocity is "nothing more or less than an abbreviated reference to the entire universe."

These critiques are read today as early attempts to give space and time operational definitions, definitions tied to what observers can actually measure. Within Newtonian mechanics itself the modern replacement is the inertial frame, a family of reference frames moving uniformly relative to one another, connected by Galilean relativity, in which the laws of physics take the same form. Absolute space is no longer required, and the fixed stars serve as a convenient practical stand-in for an inertial frame. Milutin Blagojević put the internal pressure this creates in three points: absolute space contradicts Galilean relativity because no inertial frame can be privileged, it does not explain why inertial forces depend on acceleration relative to any inertial frame, and it acts on objects without being acted on in return.

## Mathematical form

In the Newtonian setting, space is three-dimensional and Euclidean (denoted E³), each point fixed by a radius vector **r** from a chosen origin O, with vectors forming a linear space R³ endowed with a scalar product (the operation that gives the length of a vector and the angle between two vectors). Time is a single scalar t, the same everywhere in E³, and a trajectory is a function **r**(t) mapping each moment in an interval to a radius vector. Newton himself treated space, time, place, and motion as "well known to all" and left them undefined.

## Relativity and the end of absolute structure

Special relativity fused space and time into spacetime and showed that simultaneity is frame-dependent: two events simultaneous in one inertial frame need not be simultaneous in another, so there is no absolute time across the universe. General relativity went further by making spacetime curved, with geodesics (the straightest possible paths through curved spacetime) shaped by the distribution of mass and energy. The distant stars still appear to define an approximate inertial background, because their mass and motion shape the local geodesics everywhere, but no separate absolute space is needed to describe any system's physics.

Einstein captured the shift in his later writing by relabelling the Newtonian backdrop the "aether" and declaring it no longer absolute, since the metric it provides is set locally by matter. In Newtonian mechanics and special relativity the aether, if invoked, was absolute; in general relativity it is determined point by point by mass-energy, and inertial and gravitational effects are unified as different aspects of geodesic motion.
