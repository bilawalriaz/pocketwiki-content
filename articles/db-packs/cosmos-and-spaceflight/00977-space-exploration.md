# Space exploration

Space exploration is the physical investigation of outer space using uncrewed robotic probes and human spaceflight. The Kármán line at 100 km altitude marks the conventional edge of space. Modern activity rests on mid‑20th‑century rocket technology and is driven by scientific discovery, national competition, and the long‑term survival of humanity.

## Origins and early tools

Systematic observation began with the telescope. Hans Lippershey built the first practical refracting telescope in 1608; Galileo used an improved version in 1609. Isaac Newton's 1668 reflecting telescope replaced lenses with mirrors. These instruments revealed lunar mountains, Jupiter's moons, Saturn's rings, and later Uranus and Neptune, making the sky a testable physical domain. The first artificial object to reach space was a German V‑2 rocket in 1944, reaching 176 km.

## The Space Race

The Cold War turned rockets into instruments of prestige. The Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1 in 1957, the first human‑made object to orbit Earth. The USSR then accumulated a sequence of firsts: first living being in orbit (1957), first human in space, Yuri Gagarin aboard Vostok 1 (1961), first spacewalk, Alexei Leonov (1965), first lunar impact, Luna 2 (1959), first soft lunar landing, Luna 9 (1966), and first space station, Salyut 1 (1971). The United States responded with the Apollo program, which landed humans on the Moon on 20 July 1969 and conducted six crewed lunar landings between 1969 and 1972.

## Cooperation and reusable hardware

The 1970s shifted emphasis from competition to cooperation and from expendable to reusable systems. The Space Shuttle introduced reusability, and the International Space Station, built between 1998 and 2011, became the leading symbol of international collaboration, continuously inhabited since 2000.

## Robotic exploration of the Solar System

Robotic missions extended reach where humans could not yet go. Mariner 2 made the first successful planetary flyby at Venus in 1962. Venera 7 became the first spacecraft to land on another planet and return data from Venus in 1970. Mars 3 made the first Mars landing in 1971. Later milestones include the first asteroid flyby, Galileo at Gaspra (1991), the first asteroid landing, NEAR Shoemaker on Eros (2000), and the first comet landing, Philae on 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko (2014). Voyager 2 visited Uranus in 1986 and Neptune in 1989; New Horizons conducted the first Pluto flyby in 2015 and an Arrokoth flyby in 2019; Voyager 1 entered interstellar space in 2012. In 2019, China's Chang'e 4 became the first spacecraft to land on the Moon's far side.

## Modern programs and private industry

The 2000s brought serious activity from China, the European Union, Japan, and India. The 2010s added private spaceflight, led by SpaceX and Blue Origin, which are cutting costs through reusable launch vehicles. Current lunar efforts include the Chinese‑led International Lunar Research Station and the U.S.‑led Artemis program, with Artemis II planned to complete a crewed lunar flyby in 2026 and longer‑term goals of south‑pole landings and a sustainable lunar presence. The "Von Braun Paradigm" frames this as a stepwise path: reusable spacecraft, space stations, lunar bases, then Mars colonisation.

## Why it is hard for humans

Long‑duration spaceflight degrades the body through bone loss, muscle atrophy, vision changes, radiation exposure, and circadian disruption. The ISS is a research platform for countermeasures such as exercise equipment and medical monitoring. As missions move outward, communication delays grow, so crews must handle emergencies autonomously rather than wait for ground control.

## Open questions

More than two‑thirds of Mars missions have failed, a pattern called the "Mars Curse" and attributed to mission complexity rather than any exotic cause. The Outer Space Treaty forbids territorial claims, but property rights for resources and bases remain legally unclear. Effective radiation shielding for deep space is still technically difficult, and the economic case for permanent lunar bases is unproven. Interstellar propulsion, including anti‑matter, nuclear, or beamed‑energy concepts, is largely theoretical, which is why projects such as Breakthrough Starshot remain conceptual.
