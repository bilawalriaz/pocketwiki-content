# Astronomical unit

The **astronomical unit** (symbol: **au**) is a unit of length defined since 2012 as exactly **149,597,870,700 metres**. Historically it was the average Earth–Sun distance, roughly the mean of perihelion and aphelion. Earth's orbit is elliptical, so that average is a derived quantity, not an observable point, and high-precision work needed a number that would not shift with new measurements.

One au equals about **499 light-seconds**, or **8 minutes 19 seconds** of light travel. It is the natural yardstick for distances inside the Solar System and around other stars, but is too small for interstellar work, where the **parsec** and **light-year** take over. The parsec is defined in terms of the au: 1 pc = 1 au / tan(1″) ≈ 206,265 au.

## Symbol and definition history

The IAU used **A** in 1976, the BIPM recommended **ua** in 2006 (from French *unité astronomique*), and in 2012 the IAU settled on **au**, since adopted by the American Astronomical Society, Royal Astronomical Society, and BIPM.

The 1976 IAU definition tied the au to the **Gaussian gravitational constant** *k* = 0.01720209895, which appears in Kepler's third law when distances are in au, masses in solar masses, and time in days. Equivalently, 1 au was the radius of a circular Newtonian orbit about the Sun with angular frequency 0.01720209895 rad/day. Only the product *G × M*☉ is needed to compute planetary positions, and that product is known far more precisely than *G* and *M*☉ separately, which is why ephemerides were built in au.

The 1976 definition did not specify a reference frame, and general relativity makes the length of a "metre" depend on the observer's gravitational environment. The Earth–Sun distance also varies between about 0.9833 and 1.0167 au over the year. In 1983 the metre was redefined as the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 s, fixing *c* exactly. Combining that with radar and telemetry of planetary positions, the IAU in 2009 fixed the light-crossing time of 1 au at 499.0047838061 s, and in 2012 redefined the au as exactly 149,597,870,700 m. The au is now a conventional constant; the rest of the Solar System scale is measured in SI.

## How it is used

Planetary distances are simple numbers in au: Mercury 0.39, Venus 0.72, Earth 1.00, Mars 1.52, Jupiter 5.2, Saturn 9.5, Uranus 19.2, Neptune 30.1. The Kuiper belt begins near 30 au and the inner Oort cloud reaches tens of thousands of au. Proxima Centauri is about 268,000 au away, and the Galactic centre about 1.7 × 10⁹ au.

Because the au keeps Solar System numbers near 1, it reduces floating-point error in numerical integrations. A 2004 study claimed the au is lengthening by about 15 m per century as the Sun loses mass through radiation, but other analyses disagree and the result is controversial. Since 2010, planetary ephemerides are no longer tied to the au; the value is fixed and the rest of the system is solved in SI units.
