# Fractal cosmology

Fractal cosmology is a minority group of physical cosmology theories in which the distribution of matter in the Universe, or the geometry of space itself, takes a fractal form across a wide range of scales. A **fractal** is a pattern whose statistical structure repeats at different magnifications, so no single characteristic scale dominates. The central observational question is the **fractal dimension** of matter distribution: a number describing how a structure fills space, equal to 3 for a uniformly filled 3D volume, 2 for a flat surface, and an intermediate value for a true fractal.

## The observational debate

The first attempt to model galaxies with a fractal pattern was made by Luciano Pietronero and his team in 1987, and a more detailed view of the universe's large-scale structure emerged over the following decade as galaxy catalogues grew. Pietronero argued that the universe exhibits a clear fractal aspect over a fairly wide range of scales, with a fractal dimension of about 2.

This claim sits in tension with the standard view. Big Bang and Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker cosmology, and most interpretations of the Lambda-Cold Dark Matter (ΛCDM) model, predict that the universe becomes homogeneous (uniform in density) and isotropic (the same in every direction) at very large scales. A 2004 analysis of the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) wrote that the **power spectrum**, a graph of how density fluctuations vary with scale, "is not well-characterized by a single power law but unambiguously shows curvature … thereby driving yet another nail into the coffin of the fractal universe hypothesis." A 2005 SDSS study of luminous red galaxies calculated a fractal dimension of 3, consistent with homogeneity, on scales from 70 to 100 Mpc/h (megaparsecs divided by the Hubble parameter h), but a dimension of 2 out to roughly 20 h⁻¹ Mpc. In 2012, Scrimgeour et al. showed that the large-scale structure of galaxies becomes homogeneous beyond roughly 70 Mpc/h. The scientific consensus reads SDSS data as showing that structure smooths out above about 100 Mpc.

## Fractals at the largest and smallest scales

On the theoretical side, fractals entered cosmology with Andrei Linde's 1986 "Eternally Existing Self-Reproducing Chaotic Inflationary Universe" theory, a form of chaotic inflation in which quantum fluctuations in a **scalar field**, an underlying quantity filling space, produce peaks that become nucleation points, spawning inflating patches of space that grow into "bubble universes." In Linde's picture the universe is fractal on scales likely larger than the observable universe, since the branching structure of bubble universes never ends. Alan Guth's 2007 paper "Eternal Inflation and its implications" treats this picture as still under serious consideration, and some form of inflation is widely regarded as the best available cosmological model.

At the opposite extreme, near the **Planck scale** (roughly 10⁻³⁵ m, the smallest scale at which current physics gives meaningful predictions), approaches to quantum gravity describe a fractal structure for spacetime itself. **Causal dynamical triangulation** and the **asymptotic safety** approach both suggest that the effective dimensionality of space evolves with scale: spacetime is effectively 2D at the Planck scale and gradually becomes 4D at larger scales.

French mathematician Alain Connes has worked to reconcile general relativity with quantum mechanics using **noncommutative geometry**, a framework in which the coordinates of space do not commute, so the product of two position measurements depends on their order. Fractality also arises in this approach; Connes described the next important step as understanding how space with fractional dimensions couples with gravitation. Connes and physicist Carlo Rovelli further suggest that time is an emergent property, whereas in causal dynamical triangulation time emerges by selecting configurations where adjacent building blocks share the same direction in time. Both approaches nonetheless conclude that the fabric of space itself is fractal.

The disagreement is therefore sharpest at intermediate, galactic scales, where fractal models predict persistent dimension-2 clustering but SDSS analyses report a transition to homogeneity near 70–100 Mpc/h. At the Planck and multiverse extremes, the competing theories simply place their fractals where current observation cannot reach.
