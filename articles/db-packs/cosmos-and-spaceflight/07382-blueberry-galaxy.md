# Blueberry galaxy

A blueberry galaxy is a dwarf starburst galaxy: a tiny, intensely star-forming system and one of the youngest, lowest-mass, most metal-poor galaxy classes in the local universe. Stellar masses cluster around 10⁷ solar masses, gas metallicities sit at roughly 3–10% of solar, and the visible light is dominated by the strong [O III] λ5007 emission line from ionised oxygen, which gives the galaxies their blue colour in SDSS imaging.

Blueberry galaxies (BBs) are the nearby counterpart of Green Pea galaxies (GPs). Where GPs lie at redshifts 0.112 < z < 0.36, with the [O III] line falling in the SDSS r-band, BBs sit at z < 0.05 and the same line falls in the g-band. Purple Grape galaxies fill the gap at 0.05 ≤ z < 0.112, where [O III] lands in the i-band and the ultraviolet continuum is shifted into the g-band. The three classes are the same kind of object, distinguished by apparent colour. BBs are physically small (less than 1/3000 the size of the Milky Way), low in luminosity, and live in low-density local environments.

Star formation in BBs is young and intense. Median stellar ages are around 70 Myr, with sample ranges of roughly 30–370 Myr. Specific star-formation rates reach 10–100 M☉ per year per unit mass. A sample of 48 BBs (Kouroumpatzakis et al. 2024) is the most intensely star-forming set among local dwarf galaxies: bluer in visible light, redder in the infrared, and more strongly ionised than typical star-forming galaxies, with higher equivalent widths and lower metallicities than even GPs. Two BBs are among the most metal-poor galaxies known locally.

Their stellar populations are so young that BBs are extreme ionising sources. Their O32 ratios and Hβ equivalent widths match or exceed those of high-redshift galaxies, and their ionising radiation is thought to leak into intergalactic space, which is why they are used as nearby analogs of galaxies in the epoch of reionization. Radio observations with the GMRT find that star-formation rates inferred from radio continuum are about a factor of 3 lower than those from optical emission lines, attributed either to the youth of the stellar population or to cosmic-ray electrons escaping through diffusion and outflows. XMM-Newton detected significant X-ray emission from only 2 of 7 BBs, consistent with populations too young to have produced many neutron-star or black-hole binaries. FAST H I studies detected neutral hydrogen in only about 7% of a sample of 28 BBs, far below the fraction in main-sequence galaxies, with two detections showing substantial undepleted gas reservoirs; most BBs have already converted their gas into stars.

The most massive BB studied in detail, SHOC 579 at z ≈ 0.047, hosts an old stellar disk of about 7 Gyr alongside a younger blueberry component of about 5 Gyr, the most massive, metal-rich BB with direct evidence of an old population. Other work has found older stars in some BBs and GPs, complicating the picture of them as pristine infant-universe analogs. H I imaging of BB J1509+3731 with the GMRT shows a neutral-hydrogen reservoir offset from the optical body, with a depletion time of 0.2 Gyr indicating a star-formation rate above that of comparable blue compact galaxies, suggesting that a merger triggered the starburst and may help ionising photons escape.

Comparisons with JWST data have made BBs a benchmark for the early universe. The JADES survey at z ~ 5.5–9.5 found that the ionisation and metallicity properties of distant galaxies occupy line-ratio regions offset from typical local galaxies but aligned with extreme low-redshift populations such as BBs and GPs. Langeroodi et al. (2023) found that 11 JWST galaxies at z ≈ 8 have metallicities similar to BBs, but at fixed stellar mass the z ≈ 8 galaxies are systematically more metal-poor, so BBs resemble but do not exactly match the earliest galaxies. NGDEEP observations of 178 star-forming galaxies at 1.7 < z < 3.4 show an evolutionary trend in O32 and Hβ equivalent width from z ~ 0 to z ~ 5, with higher redshift giving higher ionisation and stronger emission, and BBs and GPs anchoring the local end. A J-PLUS survey for extreme [O III] emitters at z < 0.06 increased the number of known BB-class objects roughly tenfold using a narrow-band method more efficient than the broadband colour cuts of Yang et al. (2017), who named the class after the Galaxy Zoo citizen-science label.

The terminology is unstable. Yang et al. (2017) defined BBs as a subset of GPs at very low redshift, but some authors broaden the GP definition to include them, while Izotov et al. (2011) called the same objects Luminous Compact Galaxies. Hidalgo-Gámez et al. (2017) distinguished "blue marbles," "purple marbles," and other coloured analogues and argued that the scaling relations differ, so the three colours should not be treated as identical. "Little Blue Dots" identified in HST Frontier Fields are even less massive analogs, with stellar populations that could have built up in roughly 1% of the present age of the universe and that are candidate progenitors of the metal-poor globular clusters now found in larger galaxies.
