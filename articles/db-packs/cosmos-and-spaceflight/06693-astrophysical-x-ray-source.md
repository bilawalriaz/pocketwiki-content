# Astrophysical X-ray source

An astrophysical X-ray source is any astronomical object whose physical conditions produce X-rays, a band of electromagnetic radiation from about 0.008 to 8 nm that is blocked by Earth's atmosphere. The diffuse X-ray background seen across the sky is the summed light of countless unresolved sources.

## Emission mechanisms

Six processes produce the observed X-ray continuum. **Thermal bremsstrahlung**, or braking radiation, is emitted when free electrons are deflected by ions in plasma at roughly 10^7 to 10^8 K, the temperature of gas in galaxy clusters shock-heated by infalling material. **Black-body radiation** comes from optically thick surfaces, including accretion disks and the photospheres of hot white dwarfs. **Synchrotron radiation** is produced by electrons spiraling along magnetic fields, as in quasar jets and supernova remnants. **Inverse Compton scattering** boosts low-energy photons to X-ray or gamma-ray energies when they collide with relativistic electrons, and dominates the hard X-ray output near the supermassive black holes of active galactic nuclei (AGN). **Recombination and line emission** from highly ionized metals add sharp spectral features in hot thin plasma. **Charge exchange**, in which a highly charged ion captures an electron from a neutral atom, releases X-rays even at low temperatures, and is how comets glow in X-rays when the solar wind strikes their neutral gas.

## Compact objects and accretion

When matter falls toward a compact remnant, gravitational energy is released far more efficiently than by nuclear burning. Infalling gas carries angular momentum, so it spreads into an **accretion disk** whose inner regions reach X-ray-emitting temperatures from friction. Around black holes, no surface exists, so radiation emerges from the disk and a surrounding corona. Around **neutron stars** and **white dwarfs**, the inner disk adds the extra shock of impact onto a hard surface, where infall speeds can reach a sizable fraction of the speed of light. A magnetic field strong enough to channel the flow concentrates the impact near the magnetic poles, producing a pulsing X-ray source whose spin can be timed.

X-ray emission is variable on very short timescales, and that variability carries information. Rapid flickering sets an upper limit on the size of the emitting region, and the asymmetric broadening of iron lines seen by XMM-Newton and Suzaku in systems such as Serpens X-1 traces gas whipping around just outside the neutron star, giving a maximum neutron-star diameter of about 18 to 20.5 miles.

## Binaries and stellar end states

Compact X-ray sources are usually members of a binary. **Low-mass X-ray binaries** (LMXBs) have a small donor; the first extrasolar X-ray source, **Scorpius X-1**, discovered in 1962 and located about 9,000 light-years away, is an LMXB whose roughly 1.4-solar-mass neutron star pulls gas from a 0.42-solar-mass companion, releasing 2.3 × 10^31 W, about 60,000 times the Sun's total luminosity. **High-mass X-ray binaries** (HMXBs) accrete from a massive companion's stellar wind; Vela X-1 is the prototype. A **super soft X-ray source** radiates in the 0.09 to 2.5 keV band, thought to come from steady nuclear fusion of hydrogen-rich material on a white dwarf's surface pulled from a companion.

An **ultraluminous X-ray source** (ULX) is a nonnuclear point source whose luminosity exceeds the **Eddington limit** of about 3 × 10^32 W for a 20-solar-mass black hole, the brightness above which outward radiation pressure should blow away accreting matter. ULXs can be beamed stellar-mass black holes, or, when their variability and spectrum indicate otherwise, intermediate-mass black holes of thousands of solar masses.

**Magnetars** are neutron stars whose magnetic fields, decaying over time, power bursts of X-rays and gamma rays; the strongest known are hundreds of thousands of times stronger than any man-made magnet. Observations of PSR J1846-0258, a young pulsar that produced magnetar-like bursts in 2006, suggest pulsars and magnetars may be stages of a single life cycle rather than separate species.

## Galaxies and clusters

In **galaxy clusters**, infall shocks heat intergalactic gas to 10^7–10^8 K, and this plasma emits by thermal bremsstrahlung and metal line emission. The **Bullet Cluster** is the case in which the separation of total mass from baryonic mass was detected at 8σ, evidence that dark matter is not simply a modification of gravity. Active galactic nuclei power some of the most luminous X-ray sources; a **quasar** is a distant, energetic AGN whose central supermassive black hole drives an accretion disk and jets, while **Seyfert galaxies** are a subclass whose nuclei show strong emission lines from highly ionized gas. The summed emission of unresolved AGN across cosmic time produces most of the cosmic X-ray background.

## Supernova remnants, stars, and the Sun

A **supernova remnant** (SNR) shines in X-rays when the blast wave and reverse shock heat ejecta and swept-up interstellar gas to millions of degrees. **Type Ia supernovae**, the thermonuclear explosions of white dwarfs that have reached the **Chandrasekhar mass** of about 1.4 solar masses, serve as standard candles for cosmology; SN 2005ke was the first Type Ia detected in X-rays.

Most main-sequence stars are hot enough in their coronae to emit some X-rays, with surface flux rising sharply across spectral type A7–F0 where convection zones deepen. The Sun's corona varies with the roughly 11-year solar cycle, and bright coronal loops trace magnetic flux tubes anchored at sunspots.

## Brown dwarfs and solar-system X-rays

Below about 8% of the Sun's mass, an object cannot sustain hydrogen fusion and is a **brown dwarf**; below about 1% (roughly 13 Jupiter masses), it cannot fuse deuterium and is conventionally a planet. The brown dwarf LP 944-20 flared in X-rays when observed by Chandra but was X-ray dark in quiescence, showing that coronae fail once surface temperature drops below about 2500 °C.

X-ray reflection and fluorescence dominate the signal from solar-system bodies other than the Sun. The Moon's X-ray luminosity of about 1.2 × 10^5 W makes it one of the weakest known non-terrestrial X-ray sources, almost entirely reflected sunlight. Saturn's X-ray spectrum matches the Sun's, while Jupiter's intense polar X-ray auroras require electric potentials near 10 million volts. Earth's own X-ray auroras, first imaged in 1996 by the Polar satellite, are produced when solar charged particles funnel into the ionosphere along the geomagnetic field.

## Exotic transients

**Microquasars** such as SS 433 are X-ray binaries that also drive resolved radio jets, with material in SS 433's jet moving at about 26% of the speed of light. **Be X-ray binaries** show periodic outbursts tied to the eccentric orbit of a compact object around a rapidly rotating B star. **Supergiant fast X-ray transients** are defined by flares that rise in tens of minutes and last only hours, much shorter than classical HMXB outbursts.
