# Black hole information paradox

General relativity predicts black holes, and in the 1970s Stephen Hawking showed (using quantum field theory in curved spacetime) that an isolated black hole radiates. This Hawking radiation is nearly thermal: its spectrum depends only on the black hole's mass, electric charge, and angular momentum, not on what fell in. A black hole formed from any of many different initial states would therefore emit the same radiation and evaporate into a featureless gas of particles, making the initial details unrecoverable. This loss conflicts with unitarity, the quantum-mechanical principle that the wave function's evolution preserves all information about the initial state, so the system can in principle be run backward. The clash is the information paradox.

The paradox sharpens once it is quantified. A pure quantum state has von Neumann entropy 0; a mixed state has positive entropy. Unitarity keeps entropy constant, so a pure state must remain pure. Hawking's calculation suggests the post-evaporation state is mixed, with entropy equal to the black hole's initial Bekenstein-Hawking entropy. The trouble is microscopic, not the coarse-grained irreversibility familiar from thermodynamics.

In 1993 Don Page argued that if evaporation is unitary, the entanglement entropy of the Hawking radiation must climb, peak at roughly the halfway point of the black hole's life, then fall back to zero as the black hole disappears. This shape, the Page curve, is the benchmark for any proposed resolution; many researchers treat reproducing it as equivalent to solving the paradox.

The prevailing view today is that information is preserved. The dominant approach, rooted in string theory and the holographic principle (especially the AdS/CFT correspondence), holds that Hawking's calculation omits small quantum corrections: the radiation is almost thermal, but subtle correlations across many emitted particles encode the missing details, much as the smoke from a burning book is thermal in bulk yet carries information about every page. A 2019 replica-wormhole computation derived the Page curve directly. Hawking accepted the conclusion in 2004, conceding a 1997 bet with John Preskill; Kip Thorne did not.

Competing ideas modify the picture differently. The fuzzball and firewall proposals give the horizon structure that stores and releases information. Loop-quantum-gravity approaches trust Hawking's calculation until the end, then rely on quantum-gravity effects near the singularity or on a long-lived remnant. A minority, including Roger Penrose, argues that information is genuinely lost, accepting a breakdown of unitarity tied to gravity. The 2012 firewall paradox sharpened the stakes: unitarity forces late Hawking quanta to be entangled with earlier radiation, but quantum field theory also entangles them with interior partners, and monogamy of entanglement forbids both, implying a wall of high energy at the horizon that violates the equivalence principle of general relativity.
