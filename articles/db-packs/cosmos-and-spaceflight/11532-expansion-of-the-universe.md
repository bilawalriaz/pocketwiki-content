# Expansion of the universe

The expansion of the universe is the increase in distance between gravitationally unbound parts of the observable universe with time. To any observer, all but the nearest galaxies (bound by gravity) recede at speeds proportional to their distance, on average. The expansion is intrinsic: the universe does not expand into anything, and no preferred rest frame exists outside it.

## The core law

The recession speed of a distant galaxy is proportional to its distance from the observer:

$$\vec{v} = H_0 \vec{x}$$

This is **Hubble's law**, now also called the Hubble–Lemaître law. The proportionality constant $H_0$, the **Hubble constant**, quantifies today's expansion rate. Current supernova-based measurements give roughly $H_0 \approx 73$ km/s/Mpc, meaning every million parsecs of distance adds about 73 km/s of recession speed. Because the universe is homogeneous and isotropic on large scales, the same linear relationship holds from any observer's viewpoint, and the distance between any two galaxies grows over time by the same factor.

## Discovery

Vesto Slipher found in 1912–1914 that light from remote galaxies was redshifted. Alexander Friedmann derived expanding-universe solutions to Einstein's field equations in 1922. Georges Lemaître applied the equations to Slipher's and Hubble's data in 1927, proposing a linear distance–velocity relation. Edwin Hubble established the law observationally in 1929, though he did not link it to expansion and his value of the constant was about seven times too large. Successive refinements through the 20th and early 21st centuries tightened the measurement, though two principal methods now disagree (see "Hubble tension" below).

## What the expansion does

The expansion stretches light traveling through it. Photon wavelengths lengthen toward the red end of the spectrum, producing the **cosmological redshift** that is the main observational signature of expansion. The shift is more naturally described as a Doppler effect than as space itself stretching; both descriptions predict the same numbers.

As the universe expands, its contents thin out. For a fluid with pressure $p = w\rho$ (where $\rho$ is energy density and $w$ is the equation-of-state parameter), energy density falls as $\rho \propto a^{-3(1+w)}$, with $a$ the **scale factor**. Nonrelativistic matter has $w = 0$ and dilutes as $a^{-3}$; radiation (ultrarelativistic particles) has $w = 1/3$ and dilutes as $a^{-4}$ because each particle also loses energy as its momentum redshifts. Radiation temperature falls as $T \propto a^{-1}$.

The scale factor $a$ is a function of time, conventionally set to $a = 1$ today, smaller in the past and larger in the future. Extrapolating back gives $a = 0$ about $13.787 \pm 0.020$ billion years ago, marking the Big Bang.

## Why the rate changes

The expansion rate is governed by the second Friedmann equation:

$$\frac{\ddot{a}}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left(\rho + \frac{3p}{c^2}\right) + \frac{\Lambda c^2}{3}$$

Here $G$ is the gravitational constant, $c$ the speed of light, and $\Lambda$ the **cosmological constant**. Positive energy density and positive pressure decelerate expansion. Ordinary matter has negligible pressure, so it slows expansion through gravity alone, but its density is too low to halt it. The cosmological constant and any fluid with sufficiently negative pressure ($p < -\rho c^2/3$) accelerate it. **Dark energy** is the inferred cause of the present acceleration; if it is a true cosmological constant, its energy density stays constant as the universe expands, rather than diluting like matter or radiation.

## Expansion history

The standard picture divides cosmic history into epochs with different dominant contents.

- **Inflation.** Around $10^{-32}$ seconds after the Big Bang, a hypothetical field called the inflaton drove the scale factor to grow by at least $e^{60}$ (about $10^{26}$). This rapid expansion diluted predicted exotic relics, solved the horizon and flatness problems, and stretched quantum fluctuations into the seed variations that later grew into galaxies.
- **Radiation epoch.** By neutrino decoupling at about 1 second, the universe was dominated by radiation. Expansion decelerated with $a \propto t^{1/2}$.
- **Matter epoch.** Because radiation dilutes faster than matter, nonrelativistic matter came to dominate about 50,000 years after the Big Bang. Expansion continued to decelerate with $a \propto t^{2/3}$. Galaxies and large-scale structure formed during this epoch.
- **Dark energy era.** Around 3 billion years ago, dark energy began to dominate, and expansion began to accelerate again, with the scale factor growing exponentially.

## Measuring the expansion rate

The most direct method measures recession velocities (from redshifts) and distances (using standard candles such as Cepheid variables and Type Ia supernovae) independently; the ratio gives $H_0$. Supernovae are visible so far away that their light approaches the age of the universe, letting astronomers track how the expansion rate has changed. The 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics recognized the discovery of late-time acceleration through supernova observations.

A second method infers $H_0$ from the characteristic size of fluctuations in the cosmic microwave background, assuming a cosmological model (typically Lambda-CDM). The Planck collaboration obtained $H_0 = 67.4 \pm 0.5$ km/s/Mpc this way. Independent methods include time-delay cosmography using strongly lensed quasars and supernovae, and gravitational-wave standard sirens from neutron-star mergers.

The supernova-based value near 73 km/s/Mpc and the CMB-based value near 67 km/s/Mpc differ by several standard deviations, a discrepancy known as the **Hubble tension**. No measurement method has resolved it.

## Common misconceptions

- **Expansion as a force pushing galaxies apart.** Cosmic expansion describes how objects are already separating due to their inertial motion; it is not itself a force. The cosmological constant, by contrast, does produce a repulsive effect.
- **Expansion of space as a privileged rest frame.** Describing galaxies as stationary while space stretches violates the principle of relativity. The consistent view is that galaxies move apart through curved spacetime.
- **Superluminal recession violating special relativity.** Objects beyond the Hubble horizon do recede faster than light, but this is a feature of expansion in curved spacetime over large distances. Special relativity governs local motion, where nearby relative velocities remain well below $c$. The relative velocity of very distant objects is not even well defined in general relativity.
