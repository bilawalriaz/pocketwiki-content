# Cosmological horizon

A cosmological horizon is a boundary in three-dimensional space that limits what we can see of the universe. Two such boundaries exist, and they answer different questions. The **particle horizon** separates points whose light has had time to reach us from points whose past light signals could not yet have arrived. It marks the edge of the observable universe: the farthest distance from which we could have received information. The **event horizon** encloses everything we will ever be able to observe in the future; points beyond it can never send us light, no matter how long we wait.

In practice, when cosmologists say "horizon" they almost always mean the particle horizon.

Both horizons follow from general relativity applied to an expanding universe and the Big Bang model, which describes a hot, dense early state that has been expanding ever since.

## Particle horizon

The particle horizon, also called the comoving particle horizon, is the maximum distance from which particles could have emitted light that reaches an observer today. Its distance at the present epoch defines the size of the observable universe, and it represents the farthest distance from which we can retrieve information from the past.

For an empty, homogeneous, and isotropic universe, the proper distance to the particle horizon at time $t$ is

$$d_H(t) = R(t)\int_0^t \frac{dt'}{R(t')},$$

where $R$ is the cosmological scale factor, a quantity with dimensions of length that describes how the universe expands.

In comoving coordinates, which stretch with the expansion so that galaxies sit at fixed coordinates, the particle horizon equals the conformal time elapsed since the Big Bang multiplied by the speed of light. Conformal time is a reparametrization of ordinary time chosen so that light travels at constant speed on the resulting time axis. The normalized scale factor is $a = R(t)/R(t=0)$, and conformal time is

$$\eta(t) = \int_0^t \frac{dt'}{a(t')}.$$

The particle horizon is a boundary at a single instant, dividing events already observed from those that cannot yet have been observed.

## Event horizon

The event horizon is the largest comoving distance from which light emitted now can ever reach the observer in the future. It bounds all future observable universes, not just the present one. In a universe that expands forever, the proper distance to the event horizon at time $t$ is

$$d_e(t) = a(t)\int_t^{t_{\max}} \frac{c\,dt'}{a(t')},$$

where $t_{\max}$ is the end time of the universe, infinite in a forever-expanding case.

Our cosmic event horizon lies about 5 gigaparsecs, or roughly 16 billion light-years, away, well inside the particle horizon. If dark energy is a true cosmological constant $\Lambda$, the event horizon saturates at a finite maximum

$$\max(d_e) = \frac{c}{H_e} = c\sqrt{\frac{3}{\Lambda}} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda}\,H_0} \approx 17.55\ \text{Gly},$$

where $H_0$ is the present Hubble parameter, $H_e$ is its minimum value over the universe's future evolution, and $\Omega_\Lambda$ is the fraction of the universe's energy density in the form of a cosmological constant. This maximum is sometimes called the only particle horizon.

## Future horizon

The standard Lambda-CDM model, which combines cold dark matter with a cosmological constant, assumes an accelerating universe. Extrapolated into the far future, distant galaxies recede so fast that their light becomes infinitely redshifted and effectively invisible. The Milky Way ends up as the only visible galaxy, and almost all current observational evidence for cosmology, including features tied to the particle horizon, becomes unverifiable.

## Other horizons

A few related surfaces are not true cosmological horizons in the relativistic sense, but act as practical observation limits. The **optical horizon** is set by the surface of last scattering, the moment about 380,000 years after the Big Bang when the hot plasma of the early universe became transparent, releasing the cosmic microwave background. This is the farthest distance any photon can freely stream. The **Hubble sphere**, also called the photon horizon, marks a similar boundary tied to the Hubble expansion rate. There is also a **neutrino horizon** for the farthest distance a neutrino can freely stream and a **gravitational-wave horizon** for the farthest distance gravitational waves can freely stream; the latter is predicted to be a direct probe of the end of cosmic inflation, the brief period of extremely rapid expansion in the universe's first fraction of a second.

## History

The nature of cosmological horizons was clarified in 1956 by Wolfgang Rindler. He distinguished instantaneous events, such as a supernova explosion, from world lines, the continuous strings of events produced by enduring objects such as galaxies. The behavior of those world lines became the basis for splitting the universe into observable and unobservable regions.
