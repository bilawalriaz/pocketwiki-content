# History of astronomy

Astronomy began as a practical effort to track the sky for timekeeping, navigation, and ritual, then matured into a mathematical science that explained how the heavens actually work.

## How it started

Long before writing, people recorded the sky. A 32,500-year-old ivory tusk may carry lunar phase markings, and a bone stick from roughly 35,000 BC has been read the same way, though both interpretations are debated. Around 5000 BC, monuments such as the Goseck circle and Stonehenge were built with alignments to the Sun and bright stars. The oldest known calendar, the Warren Field calendar from Scotland (c. 8,000 BC), gave the year a fixed structure. By 1600 BC the Nebra sky disc in Germany encoded a lunisolar calendar, a system that keeps months tied to lunar cycles while inserting extra months to stay in step with the seasons.

Independent traditions appeared worldwide. In Mesopotamia, Babylonian astronomers after about 1200 BC built the first mathematical models to predict eclipses and planetary events, working in a base-60 (sexagesimal) numeral system. Their compendium *Enūma Anu Enlil* fed directly into Greek and later Islamic astronomy. In India the *Vedanga Jyotisha* (c. 1350 BC) gave rules for following the Sun and Moon, and Aryabhata (5th–6th century AD) proposed that Earth rotates and built a computational model of the planets. Chinese court astronomers logged the first recorded supernova in 185 AD, and Maya astronomers in Mesoamerica built calendars tied to Venus and other planetary cycles. Each tradition watched the sky closely enough to produce numbers that predicted what came next, even before anyone agreed on what the numbers meant.

## The Greek turn

The Greeks converted that arithmetic into geometry. In the 4th century BC Eudoxus and Callippus modelled the cosmos with nested spheres, each carrying a planet around Earth. Aristarchus of Samos went further in the 3rd century BC and put the Sun at the centre, the first heliocentric model, but it did not catch on. Hipparchus, working in the 2nd century BC, compiled the first star catalogue and discovered precession, the slow wobble of Earth's axis that shifts the coordinates of the stars over centuries. The Antikythera mechanism (c. 150–100 BC), a geared bronze device recovered from a shipwreck, is the oldest known astronomical computer, capable of predicting the positions of the Sun, Moon, and planets. Ptolemy closed this era in the 2nd century AD with the *Almagest*, a geocentric model so mathematically complete that it remained authoritative for more than a thousand years.

## The Copernican Revolution

In 1543 Nicolaus Copernicus published *De revolutionibus*, placing the Sun at the centre. The model still used circular orbits and was not yet more accurate than Ptolemy's, but it changed the question being asked. Tycho Brahe's precise naked-eye observations revealed that the heavens change: a new star in 1572 and a comet showed the sky was not the immutable realm Aristotle had claimed. Using Brahe's data, Johannes Kepler showed in 1609 that planets move in ellipses with the Sun at one focus, and that a planet sweeps equal areas in equal times (Kepler's first two laws). In 1610 Galileo Galilei turned the newly invented telescope (1608) on the sky, saw four moons orbiting Jupiter, and watched Venus show a full set of phases, evidence that Venus circles the Sun rather than Earth. Isaac Newton closed the loop in 1687 with the *Principia*: the same law of universal gravitation that pulls the Moon around Earth also pulls the planets around the Sun, and it produces Kepler's elliptical orbits as a consequence.

## The modern picture

Nineteenth-century instruments changed what astronomers could measure. Spectroscopy, the splitting of starlight into its component colours, revealed the temperature and chemical makeup of stars and planets, while photography made the sky permanently recordable. In 1838 Friedrich Bessel measured the first stellar parallax, the tiny apparent shift of a nearby star against the distant background as Earth orbits the Sun, confirming that the stars are far away in the same three-dimensional space the Sun inhabits.

The twentieth century redrew the scale of the universe. The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram (1913) classifies stars by luminosity and surface temperature. Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin showed in 1925 that stars are mostly hydrogen and helium, overturning the assumption that the Sun shares Earth's chemistry. The "Great Debate" of the 1920s asked whether the spiral nebulae were nearby objects or distant galaxies. Edwin Hubble resolved it: the Milky Way is one galaxy among many, and the light from those galaxies is redshifted in proportion to distance, meaning the universe is expanding, the empirical foundation of the Big Bang model. The field then opened beyond visible light to radio, X-ray, and other wavelengths, with cosmology emerging as a subfield of its own.

Open questions remain. Stonehenge's exact alignments, the Marshack bone interpretation, and the cultural purpose of megalithic monuments are still argued over, and the universe's ultimate fate and composition are unresolved.
