# Calorimetric Electron Telescope

The Calorimetric Electron Telescope (CALET) is a space telescope aboard the International Space Station that directly measures the highest-energy cosmic ray electrons and gamma rays, with the goal of identifying their sources in the local galaxy and probing the nature of dark matter.

Cosmic rays are subatomic particles — electrons, protons, and heavier atomic nuclei — that travel through space at nearly the speed of light. Their charge, mass, and energy encode information about where and how they were accelerated. Most natural particle accelerators in the galaxy, such as supernova remnants, push particles to energies that Earth-based accelerators cannot reach, so a space-borne instrument is the only way to measure them directly.

CALET was developed and sponsored by the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA), with teams from Japan, Italy, and the United States. It launched aboard JAXA's H-II Transfer Vehicle Kounotori 5 (HTV-5) on 19 August 2015 and was installed on the station's Japanese Kibo module. Shoji Torii of Waseda University is the Principal Investigator, with John Wefel as US co-investigator and Pier S. Marrocchesi as Italy co-investigator.

Rather than forming an image, CALET operates in scanning mode: every time a cosmic ray enters its field of view and triggers its detectors, it records a single event. For each event it reconstructs the incoming particle's trajectory, identifies its charge, and measures its total energy. The instrument is optimised for the teraelectronvolt (TeV) region, where 1 TeV equals one trillion electronvolts. Telemetry is downlinked to a ground station at Waseda University for analysis. A sub-payload, the Compact Infrared Camera (CIRC), shares the platform and observes Earth to detect forest fires.

The mission pursues three linked objectives: the origin and acceleration mechanisms of high-energy cosmic rays and gamma rays; how cosmic rays propagate through the galaxy after being accelerated; and the identity of dark matter. These are approached from two directions. In particle physics, CALET searches for signatures of dark matter particle creation and annihilation, including rare matter–dark matter interactions that would appear as excesses in the electron or positron (the electron's antimatter counterpart) spectrum. In space physics, it studies the acceleration and propagation of charged particles through the galaxy. The spectral index — the exponent describing how the number of particles falls with increasing energy — gives a sensitive benchmark for both.

CALET works in synergy with the Alpha Magnetic Spectrometer (AMS), another ISS instrument that separately measures positrons and antiprotons (antimatter counterparts of protons) to search for dark matter. A real dark matter signal should appear in both datasets, so the comparison strengthens any candidate detection.

In November 2017, the collaboration published its first results: roughly half a million electron and positron events spanning 10 GeV to 3 TeV. The measured electron spectral index above 30 GeV was −3.152 ± 0.016, a precise benchmark for how steeply the electron spectrum falls at high energies. Observations were planned to continue for more than five years.
