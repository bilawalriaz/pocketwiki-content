# Carbon star

A carbon star is a cool giant whose atmosphere holds more carbon than oxygen, giving it a deep ruby-red colour and a chemically "sooty" appearance. In most stars the reverse is true, and those that are cool enough to form carbon monoxide yet still oxygen-rich are called oxygen-rich stars.

The defining chemistry is straightforward. Carbon and oxygen in the upper layers combine into carbon monoxide, a tightly bound molecule that consumes nearly all the available oxygen. The leftover carbon atoms build other carbon compounds, including the C₂ molecules whose Swan bands dominate the visible spectrum. The same chemistry explains the red colour and the strong molecular fingerprints at visible, millimetre and submillimetre wavelengths. In the carbon star CW Leonis, more than fifty distinct circumstellar molecules have been detected, which is why astronomers use it to search for new interstellar chemistry.

## How carbon stars get their carbon

Classical carbon stars, the C-R and C-N types, are asymptotic giant branch (AGB) stars near the end of their lives. Carbon is forged in the core by the triple-alpha process, in which three helium nuclei fuse into one carbon nucleus, then reaches the surface through repeated convective episodes called the third dredge-up. The mechanism is cyclic: a hydrogen-burning shell normally drives the star, but every 10⁴ to 10⁵ years the star switches to a helium-burning shell flash. The flash lifts luminosity, swells the star, shuts off the helium shell, and lets the hydrogen shell reignite. Each cycle dredges more freshly made carbon upward, and the heavy mass loss that accompanies the flashes gradually strips the envelope away. The exposed core eventually becomes a hot white dwarf, and the discarded atmosphere seeds a planetary nebula.

Non-classical carbon stars, the C-J and C-H types, are binaries. A now-white-dwarf companion was once a classical carbon star; while it was an AGB giant, it transferred carbon-rich material onto its partner, which was still on the main sequence at the time. The polluted star has since evolved into a red giant, but the carbon it shows was not made by it. This same binary mass-transfer story explains the chemically similar barium stars, whose strong carbon and barium features once seemed mysterious until their binary nature was uncovered. Astronomers call the AGB producers "intrinsic" and the polluted partners "extrinsic." A third, much rarer group, the hydrogen-deficient C-Hd stars, is linked to R Coronae Borealis variables, though only five are known and none has been shown to be binary.

## Spectral classification

Carbon stars were first singled out by Angelo Secchi in the 1860s as Secchi class IV, later renamed Harvard class N. The Harvard scheme added a class R for less deeply red stars with similar carbon bands; together R and N formed a temperature sequence from roughly G7 to M4. This single sequence proved misleading, because carbon abundance shifts the spectrum independently of temperature.

The Morgan–Keenan C system replaced the R–N classes between 1960 and 1993. Each C-type star receives a two-part label such as C5,4: the first digit (0–7) tracks temperature-like features from about 4500 K down to roughly 2600 K, and the second digit (0–9) tracks the strength of the C₂ Swan bands. A revised system published by Philip Keenan in 1993 splits carbon stars by astrophysical origin: C-R and C-N (the classical AGB stars, distinguished by blue visibility and s-process element line strength), plus non-classical C-J (strong isotopic bands), C-H (very strong CH, all of which are binaries), and the rare C-Hd (hydrogen-deficient).

## Dust, variability and distance measurement

Carbon stars are prodigious dust factories. With weak surface gravity, a classical carbon star can shed half or more of its total mass in stellar winds, leaving graphite-like carbon grains that enrich the interstellar medium. In the early Solar System, silicon carbide grains condensed in carbon-star outflows and survived inside primitive chondritic meteorites, where isotopic analysis still reveals the chemistry of the 1–3 M☉ stars that produced them. In extreme cases, surrounding dust absorbs all visible starlight.

Most classical carbon stars are also long-period variables. Because the human eye is poorly sensitive to deep red and adapts slowly to it, magnitude estimates are distorted by the Purkinje effect, in which the eye's peak sensitivity shifts toward blue at low light levels and red stars look fainter than they are.

Their bright near-infrared emission and red near-infrared colours make carbon stars useful as standard candles in nearby galaxies. Individual luminosities vary, but a large sample in a similar galaxy yields a near-infrared luminosity function with a nearly constant median. Calibrated against galaxies whose distances are known by other means, that median becomes a distance indicator, sensitive in practice to the average metallicity of the AGB population.
