# Astronomical constant

An astronomical constant is a physical constant used in astronomy whose value has been officially adopted for calculations. The International Astronomical Union (IAU) maintains the authoritative list. Formal sets were issued in 1964 and 1976 (updated 1994), and most recently in 2009. Since 2009 the IAU has not fixed the numbers: its Working Group on Numerical Standards for Fundamental Astronomy continuously publishes Current Best Estimates, so that improved measurements replace older ones without a new resolution. The values are republished in the *Astronomical Almanac* produced by the United States Naval Observatory and Her Majesty's Nautical Almanac Office. The International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service (IERS) maintains a parallel set for Earth's orientation and rotation.

## Two values, one constant

A quantity can be quoted in two ways, depending on the observer's frame of reference. Values in barycentric dynamical time (TDB), or the equivalent Jet Propulsion Laboratory ephemeris time T_eph, are the mean values an observer on Earth's geoid would measure over time. Values in SI units are the proper-length and proper-time values an observer at the Solar System barycentre would measure. The IAU gives exact conversion factors; three are defining:

τ_A(SI) = (1 + L_B)^(1/3) · τ_A(TDB)
GE(SI) = (1 + L_B) · GE(TDB)
GS(SI) = (1 + L_B) · GS(TDB)

Here τ_A is the light-time for one astronomical unit, GE and GS are the geocentric and heliocentric gravitational constants, and L_B = 1.550 519 767 72 × 10⁻⁸ relates TDB to the barycentric coordinate time TCB. Using the wrong frame introduces an error of order 10⁻⁸, small absolutely but large compared with the best modern measurements.

## The astronomical system of units

The IAU defines three base units and fixes length by anchoring it to a classical constant:

- **Time** (D): one day of 86 400 seconds.
- **Mass** (S): the mass of the Sun.
- **Length** (A): the length for which the Gaussian gravitational constant k takes the value 0.017 202 098 95, when A, S, and D are the units.

The speed of light c = 299 792 458 m/s and the light-time τ_A = 499.004 783 836 156 s are also defining, so the astronomical unit has the exact modern value A = 149 597 870 700 m. Because c is fixed in SI units, A and the metre are now defined together; older systems in which A was measured independently are obsolete.

Defining constants (k, c, the frame conversions) have zero uncertainty by fiat. Constants tied to Earth's gravity field and shape are known to a few parts in 10⁹. G and the mass of the Sun, computed as S = GS/G, are known only to about 1.5 × 10⁻⁴, since both depend on laboratory measurement of G, and the Sun's mass limits every derived Solar System quantity.

## Constants outside the formal IAU system

A few widely used quantities are not part of the IAU's adopted list but appear in the same tables because distance calculations rely on them:

- **Parsec** pc = A / tan(1″) = 3.085 677 581 28 × 10¹⁶ m (4.0 × 10⁻¹¹).
- **Light-year** ly = 365.25 · c · D = 9.460 730 472 580 8 × 10¹⁵ m (defined).
- **Hubble constant** H₀ ≈ 70.1 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹, uncertain at the 2 % level, one of the most contested numbers in cosmology.
- **Solar luminosity** L_☉ = 3.939 × 10²⁶ W, variable to about ±0.1 % over the solar cycle.

## A limitation of the 1976 values

The tabulated precession, obliquity, aberration, and nutation values were fixed with the 1976 system. Modern theories of precession and nutation have improved, and the definition of the ecliptic has shifted with them. The numbers above remain correct within the older framework, but current work uses a larger set of constants tied to present models.
