# Satellite

An artificial satellite is a spacecraft placed into orbit around a celestial body, most often Earth, to do work the surface cannot: relaying communications, monitoring weather, navigating vehicles, or observing the universe. The defining idea is orbit. A satellite is not flying somewhere; it is falling continuously around the planet, with its sideways speed balanced against gravity's pull so that it never re-enters the atmosphere. The same physical insight Isaac Newton captured in his 1687 cannonball thought experiment still explains every working satellite in orbit today.

Once a satellite reaches the correct velocity, it stays in free fall around Earth with no engine power needed to maintain altitude. Only occasional thrust is required, to correct drift, dodge debris, or, at the end of life, lower the orbit so the craft burns up on re-entry. Because space has no air, there is no friction to slow a satellite down, which is why an object placed in orbit in 1957 can still circle Earth decades later.

### A Short History

The theoretical groundwork was laid in three steps. In 1687 Newton used a cannonball imagined on a tall mountain to show that a fast enough projectile would miss the curving Earth and circle it indefinitely. In 1903 Konstantin Tsiolkovsky calculated, on paper, the velocities liquid-fueled rockets would need to reach orbit, and in 1928 Herman Potočnik described satellites parked in a geostationary orbit, one where the orbital period matches Earth's 24-hour rotation so the craft appears fixed above a single point on the equator. Arthur C. Clarke's 1945 paper extended that into the proposal of three such satellites spaced around the equator for continuous global communications.

The theoretical became physical on 4 October 1957, when the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1 under Sergei Korolev's leadership, triggering the Sputnik Crisis in the United States and the start of the Space Race. Sputnik 2 followed a month later with the dog Laika, the first living creature in orbit. The U.S. answered on 31 January 1958 with Explorer 1, whose instruments discovered the Van Allen radiation belts, zones of charged particles trapped by Earth's magnetic field. On 1 April 1960 TIROS-1 returned the first weather images from space. By 2025 there were 12,952 satellites in Earth orbit, of which 8,530 belonged to the U.S., 1,559 to Russia, and 908 to China.

### How Satellites Are Built

Early satellites were one-of-a-kind craft. As launches grew routine, engineers converged on a satellite bus, a standardised chassis that supplies power, propulsion, structure, and attitude control, onto which a mission-specific payload is bolted. The HS-333, launched in 1972 as a geosynchronous communications satellite, was the first such design. A modern bus uses solar panels for electricity, chemical thrusters (usually hydrazine) or electric ion thrusters (Hall-effect engines running on xenon) for manoeuvring, and reaction wheels, spinning masses whose torque turns the spacecraft, for fine pointing. For deep-space missions far from the Sun, radioisotope thermoelectric generators supply power instead. CubeSats and other small satellites, built around a 10×10×11.35 cm unit, have lowered the cost of access so far that universities and small companies can now fly experiments. The flip side is the rise of large constellations such as SpaceX's Starlink, which passed 1,000 active satellites in 2020.

### Orbits and Their Uses

Different jobs call for different orbits, and the choice shapes both the satellite's design and its lifetime.

| Orbit | Approximate altitude | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Low Earth orbit (LEO) | below 2,000 km | Earth observation, the ISS, most Starlink satellites |
| Geostationary (GEO) | ~35,786 km, equatorial | Communications, weather, TV broadcast |
| Sun-synchronous | polar, ~600–800 km | Imaging, because local solar time is the same on every pass, so lighting is constant |

GPS satellites orbit at about 20,200 km in a medium orbit; receivers on the ground measure the travel time of their signals to compute position. Communications satellites carry transponders that receive an uplink signal, shift its frequency, amplify it, and re-broadcast it, so a single craft can route many calls or data streams at once.

### Costs and Consequences

The same density that makes satellites useful also makes them a problem. Collisions create debris, which creates more debris in a cascade called the Kessler Syndrome, a scenario in which runaway impacts could render some orbits unusable for generations. The Chinese anti-satellite test in 2007, which destroyed a weather satellite, produced a large share of the dangerous fragments now tracked in orbit.

Rocket launches inject black carbon, nitrogen oxides, and water vapour into the stratosphere, where they can persist for years and may affect the ozone layer. At end of life, about 70% of de-orbited satellites fall into the ocean and are rarely recovered. Bright satellite streaks also contaminate astronomical images and disturb wildlife, and the rapid growth of constellations is measurably brightening the night sky.

Mitigation is now built into satellite programmes: designs are tested for demisability, the ability to burn up completely on re-entry, and agencies are exploring materials that char rather than shed metal fragments. In 2024 the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA) and NASA planned to launch LingoSat, a wooden satellite prototype, after earlier tests in 2021 showed wood samples could survive the thermal and radiation environment of low Earth orbit.

International law has not kept pace: there is no binding global treaty on space-debris removal, anti-satellite weapons, or the allocation of crowded orbits, leaving the sustainability of large constellations an open question.
