# Centaur (small Solar System body)

A centaur is a small Solar System body whose orbit crosses the region between Jupiter and Neptune. Because no orbital resonance shields them, their trajectories are chaotic, with dynamic lifetimes of only a few million years. They ultimately collide with the Sun, strike a planet, or are ejected into interstellar space. Centaurs share traits of both asteroids and comets, which is why they are named for the mythological half-horse, half-human creatures.

## Orbit and definition

The Jet Propulsion Laboratory defines a centaur by semi-major axis: 5.5 AU ≤ a ≤ 30.1 AU. The Minor Planet Center instead uses a perihelion cut-off (q > 5.2 AU) with a < 30.1 AU, while the Deep Ecliptic Survey applies a dynamical criterion based on 10-million-year orbital simulations. The Solar System Beyond Neptune (2008) uses Jupiter-relative Tisserand's parameter above 3.05. Because the criteria differ, the same object can be classified as a centaur, a scattered-disc object, or a Jupiter-family comet depending on the catalogue. The JPL Small-Body Database lists 910 centaurs.

A 10-million-year simulation of Kuiper belt objects passing through the centaur zone identified a short-lived "orbital gateway" between roughly 5.4 and 7.8 AU. About 21% of all centaurs transit this region, including 72% of those that become Jupiter-family comets. Only four objects are currently observed there, but models suggest roughly a thousand bodies larger than 1 km in radius may exist undetected.

## Dynamical life cycle

Centaurs are a transitional population. Objects perturbed out of the Kuiper belt become Neptune-crossing, lose orbital stability, and are reclassified as centaurs. Repeated close encounters with the outer planets drive chaotic evolution: some migrate inward, where smaller perihelia allow volatile outgassing and reclassification as Jupiter-family comets; others are scattered outward or ejected. Amycus sits near the unstable 3:4 resonance of Uranus, and the largest known centaur, 10199 Chariklo at about 250 km across (discovered 1997, with its own ring system), has a forward-simulated half-life near 10 million years, far longer than most.

## Physical properties

Sizes range up to a few hundred kilometres; the largest confirmed centaurs exceed 300 km and tend to orbit beyond 20 AU. Light-curve analysis gives Chiron a rotation period of 5.5 hours and Chariklo 7.0 hours. Spectra confirm water ice on Chiron, Chariklo and Pholus, with tholins, amorphous carbon, olivine, methanol ice, and kerogens proposed on various surfaces. Colours split centaurs into two broad groups, very red (like Pholus) and blue-grey (like Chiron), possibly reflecting different surface ages, radiation weathering, or origin.

## Centaurs as comets

Because any centaur perturbed sunward can outgas, about 30 centaurs have shown comet-like comas. Three, 2060 Chiron, 60558 Echeclus, and 29P/Schwassmann–Wachmann 1, produce detectable volatiles entirely beyond Jupiter, and the first two are officially dual-classified as both centaurs and comets. Carbon monoxide has been detected in Chiron and Echeclus at rates that can account for their comas. The boundary between centaurs and Jupiter-family comets is current activity, not orbit type.

## Origin, naming, and notable objects

Most centaurs are thought to scatter inward from the Kuiper belt, but 2013 VZ70 may be a fragment of a Saturnian irregular moon, and the high-inclination retrograde orbits of some centaurs have been controversially linked to interstellar capture. The first centaur recognised under the JPL definition was 944 Hidalgo in 1920, but the population was only identified after 2060 Chiron was found in 1977. Saturn's moon Phoebe, imaged by Cassini in 2004, may itself be a captured centaur from the Kuiper belt. Under International Astronomical Union rules, centaurs with a < 30 AU and q > 5.5 AU are named after mythological centaurs, while Neptune-crossing trans-Neptunian objects sharing the centaur label are named after chimeras.
