# Telescope

A telescope is a device that collects and focuses electromagnetic radiation so that distant objects can be observed. The word originally meant an optical instrument built from glass lenses or mirrors, but it now covers instruments that detect radio waves, infrared, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays. The 20th-century expansion across the electromagnetic spectrum turned the telescope from a single-purpose optical tool into a family of instruments, each matched to a particular slice of the spectrum.

## The two basic optical designs

The first practical telescopes were refractors, which use a glass lens to bend and focus incoming light. The earliest known patent was filed in 1608 by the Middelburg spectacle maker Hans Lipperhey, though the true inventor is unknown. In 1609 Galileo Galilei independently built a refractor and used it to observe the Moon's craters and the moons of Jupiter, the first major astronomical discoveries made with the instrument. Refractors are limited by chromatic aberration, the colored fringes produced because a single lens focuses different wavelengths at slightly different distances, and physically they cannot be made much larger than about 1 meter in aperture. The 1733 achromatic lens, a compound lens made of two glasses with different refractive properties, partly corrected this color error but did not remove the size limit.

Reflectors sidestep both problems. Isaac Newton built the first practical reflecting telescope in 1668; the design, now called the Newtonian, uses a curved primary mirror to collect light and a small flat secondary to redirect it. Reflectors avoid chromatic aberration entirely and can in principle be made arbitrarily large. Their mirrors evolved from speculum metal, a copper-tin alloy that tarnished quickly, to silver-coated glass in 1857 and aluminized coatings in 1932, both of which reflect far more light and last longer. Today's largest reflectors have mirrors wider than 10 meters, and several 30–40 m designs are in development. A third family, catadioptric telescopes, combines lenses and mirrors in one tube. John Dobson's 1956 Dobsonian mount made large reflectors cheap and portable by replacing an equatorial mount, a tilted axis that tracks the sky's rotation, with a simple swivel on a box.

## Beyond visible light

The same idea, collect radiation and focus it, applies to every part of the electromagnetic spectrum, but the hardware changes with wavelength.

Radio telescopes use large parabolic dishes. Individual dishes can be linked by a technique called aperture synthesis, in which signals from widely separated antennas are combined to simulate a single much larger aperture. The method achieves angular resolution finer than any single dish could produce and is now also used at optical wavelengths with optical interferometers, instruments that combine light from several telescopes directly. Radio waves penetrate Earth's atmosphere and interstellar gas, so radio observatories can sit on the ground and see through dust clouds that block visible light. Arrays such as the Allen Telescope Array and the former Arecibo dish have been used by programs like SETI to search for radio signals from extraterrestrial civilizations.

Infrared telescopes, developed in the 1960s, work much like optical ones but detect longer wavelengths. They can be ground-based for the near-infrared, though the atmosphere glows and absorbs much of the signal, so the most sensitive instruments, including the James Webb Space Telescope, operate in space.

Ultraviolet, X-ray, and gamma-ray photons are blocked by the atmosphere, so these telescopes must fly. X-rays are too energetic to bounce off a mirror at normal angles; instead, X-ray telescopes use Wolter optics, nested cylindrical mirrors at very shallow grazing incidence, to steer photons toward a focus. The design was outlined by Hans Wolter in 1952 and is used by Chandra, NuSTAR, and other space observatories. Gamma-ray telescopes rely on coded aperture masks, patterned screens whose shadow is decoded into an image, or on detectors such as water Cherenkov tanks that catch the particle showers gamma rays produce in the atmosphere. The reward for going to a larger aperture is twofold: more photons are collected, and the achievable angular resolution improves, which is why the push toward larger ground-based reflectors and ever-larger space observatories continues.

## Why some telescopes must leave the ground

Earth's atmosphere is transparent to only two broad windows: visible and near-infrared light, and a large portion of the radio band. X-ray, far-infrared, ultraviolet, and gamma-ray astronomy are possible only from orbit. Even within the transparent windows, space offers steadier images, no atmospheric turbulence, no clouds, and no light pollution, which is why the Hubble Space Telescope, the Spitzer Space Telescope, and the Kepler Space Telescope all flew. The James Webb Space Telescope, launched on 25 December 2021 from Kourou on an Ariane 5 rocket, detects infrared and orbits the L2 Lagrange point, a gravitationally stable spot about 1.5 million kilometers beyond Earth where a spacecraft can stay aligned with the planet as it orbits the Sun. Space telescopes cost far more than ground-based ones, cannot be easily repaired, and are constrained in size by the launch vehicle, which is why a parallel ground program of giant reflectors continues alongside them.

Telescopes are sorted in three overlapping ways: by location (ground, space, or airborne), by operator (professional or amateur), and by optical design (refracting, reflecting, or catadioptric). Specialized subtypes include astrographs for photography, comet seekers with wide fields, and solar telescopes built to handle the Sun's brightness.
