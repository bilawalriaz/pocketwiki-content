# Satellite galaxy

A satellite galaxy is a smaller companion galaxy that travels on a bound orbit inside the gravitational potential of a more massive host galaxy, the same way planets are bound to the Sun. The Milky Way has about 50 known satellite galaxies, and the largest is the Large Magellanic Cloud. Most satellite galaxies are dwarf galaxies, though satellites of galaxy clusters can be far more massive.

## Why they are not globular clusters

A galaxy is defined as a gravitationally bound collection of stars whose internal motions cannot be explained by ordinary matter and Newton's laws alone, because the orbital speeds of material in the outer parts of spiral galaxies exceed what visible matter can supply. This mismatch is the main evidence for dark matter. Globular clusters orbit the Milky Way too, but they are not galaxies: they are more compact, less diffuse, and lack the massive dark matter halos that satellites acquired during their formation.

## A turbulent life

Satellites are reshaped by their environment. Tidal forces and ram pressure from the host strip cold gas out of the satellite, removing the fuel for star formation and leaving the satellite quiescent. Satellites can also fall into the host in a minor merger, where a small satellite is consumed by a much larger galaxy, or collide with each other in a major merger, where the participants are of comparable mass. Because galaxies are mostly empty space, mergers do not require direct collisions between individual stars, but they redistribute gas and stars and build larger galaxies over cosmic time.

## Historical milestones

Whether galaxies existed beyond the Milky Way was unresolved before the 20th century. Harlow Shapley and Heber Curtis debated the question at the National Academy of Sciences in 1920. Shapley held that the Milky Way was the whole universe, while Curtis argued that the spiral nebulae were separate galaxies. Edwin Hubble settled the question in late 1923 by measuring the distance to the Andromeda galaxy using Cepheid variable stars, stars whose intrinsic brightness can be inferred from their pulsation period. Combining the inferred luminosity with the apparent brightness gave a distance of about 300 kpc, an order of magnitude larger than Shapley's estimate of the size of the universe, proving that nebulae are distant galaxies.

For most of the 20th century only a handful of Milky Way satellites were known. Surveys such as SDSS and DES pushed the total to 59 satellites. Two of them, the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds, have been visible to the naked eye in the Southern Hemisphere since antiquity. The current count is still below what cosmological models predict, the missing satellites problem, but higher resolution simulations show this tension is not fatal to the standard theory of galaxy formation.

## Classification

Satellites are usually dwarfs and follow the Hubble classification of normal galaxies with a lowercase "d" prefix. The main types are:

| Type | Key properties | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Dwarf irregular (dI) | Chaotic shape, low gas fraction, high star formation, low metallicity | Small Magellanic Cloud, Canis Major Dwarf, Antlia 2 |
| Dwarf elliptical (dE) | Oval shape, disordered stellar motions, old stars, low gas | NGC 147, NGC 185, NGC 205 (around Andromeda) |
| Dwarf spheroidal (dSph) | Diffuse, low surface brightness, high mass-to-light ratio (dark matter dominated), old stars | Hercules, Pisces II, Leo IV |

Dwarf spirals (dS) are believed to live only in the field, not as satellites. Transitional types exist: Phoenix and LGS3 appear to be moving from irregular to spheroidal, and the Large Magellanic Cloud is thought to be shifting from a dwarf spiral to a dwarf irregular. Dwarf spheroidals dominate the known Milky Way satellite population numerically.

## Formation in the standard model

In the ΛCDM model, structure builds from the bottom up. After recombination, when free electrons and protons combined into neutral hydrogen, small baryonic density fluctuations grew alongside dark matter fluctuations. The smallest overdensities collapsed and virialized, reaching gravitational equilibrium, then clustered into successively larger halos. Cold gas condensed into these dark matter halos, cooled, and formed stars. Superclusters like the Virgo Supercluster sit at the top of this hierarchy, with clusters of galaxies inside them, and dwarf galaxies around the galaxies. Dwarfs are the fundamental building blocks of larger galaxies, and surviving satellites are the dwarfs that have not yet been absorbed by their host.

The extended Press-Schechter (EPS) formalism estimates how dark matter halos gain mass through mergers of smaller halos. It yields a progenitor mass function, a formula giving the average number of smaller halos that merged to build a halo of a given mass at a later time. The formula matches numerical simulations only when the time interval between snapshots is small; for long intervals it underestimates high mass progenitors because it assumes spherical collapse and a linear density field.

## Galactic cannibalism

A satellite orbiting inside the host's dark matter halo loses kinetic energy and angular momentum to dynamical friction, the gravitational drag exerted as the satellite pulls on the surrounding mass. Its orbit shrinks through orbital decay, and tidal forces from the host strip stars from the satellite's outskirts. The stripped stars form long stellar streams visible around galaxies such as NGC 5907. The process ends when the satellite is fully disrupted and incorporated into the host, producing a minor merger. Major mergers, between comparable galaxies, are rarer but more violent.

The timescale for orbital decay, assuming both host and satellite are singular isothermal spheres, is approximately t_fric = (2.7 Gyr / ln Λ) × (r_i / 30 kpc) × (σ_M / 200 km s⁻¹)² × (100 km s⁻¹ / σ_s)³, where r_i is the initial orbital radius, σ_M and σ_s are the host and satellite velocity dispersions, and ln Λ is the Coulomb logarithm depending on the maximum impact parameter. Using the Faber-Jackson relation, which links a galaxy's velocity dispersion to its luminosity, the merger time can be estimated from the observed brightnesses of the two galaxies.

## Why mergers matter

Work in 1978 by Beatrice Tinsley and Richard Larson on the colors of merger remnants showed that mergers are associated with young, massive, blue stars, implying that they trigger bursts of star formation. Major mergers drive the most vigorous starbursts, but minor mergers are far more common, so their cumulative effect over cosmic time is also significant. Tidal heating during a minor merger can puff up a host's preexisting thin disk into a thicker component, one explanation for the thick disks seen in most edge-on galaxies like NGC 4565.
