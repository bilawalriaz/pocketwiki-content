# Cosmology

Cosmology is the study of the origin, large-scale structure, and evolution of the universe as a whole. The dominant branch, physical cosmology, is built on Einstein's general theory of relativity and treats the universe as a physical system whose history can be modelled and tested. Cosmogony studies the origin of the universe; cosmography maps its features. Religious or mythological cosmology is a separate tradition of creation narratives and eschatologies grounded in sacred texts rather than observation.

## A historical science

Because light travels at a finite speed, looking far away in space is the same as looking back in time. Cosmology is therefore a historical science: the present sky is a layered record of different epochs, and telescopes act as time machines.

Two observations anchor the modern picture. First, distant galaxies show a *redshift*, a lengthening of their light's wavelength. Interpreted through general relativity, this means space itself is expanding and carrying galaxies apart. Edwin Hubble measured this expansion in 1929, building on Georges Lemaître's 1927 model. Second, the *cosmic microwave background* (CMB) is a faint microwave glow filling the sky, detected in 1964 by Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson. It is the cooled remnant of a much hotter, denser early universe. Together, expansion and the CMB define the Big Bang model.

## The standard model: Lambda-CDM

The current standard parameterisation of the Big Bang is the *Lambda-CDM model*, which fits a wide range of observations: the CMB mapped by the COBE, WMAP, and Planck satellites; large galaxy redshift surveys such as 2dF and the Sloan Digital Sky Survey; distant supernovae; and gravitational lensing.

The Planck 2014 results give the universe's age as 13.8 billion years and its energy content as roughly 68.5% dark energy, 26.6% dark matter, and 4.9% ordinary atomic matter. *Dark energy* is the name given to whatever drives the expansion to accelerate. *Dark matter* is invisible mass inferred from gravitational effects on galaxies and clusters. Neither has been identified directly in laboratories, but their combined behaviour matches observation precisely. The standard age of 13.799 ± 0.021 billion years comes from fitting this model to the CMB.

## Brief history of ideas

Anaximander proposed the first mechanical model in the 6th century BCE: a cylindrical Earth floating in infinite space, with celestial bodies explained by rings of fire. Later Greeks including Plato, Eudoxus, Aristotle, and Ptolemy built geocentric systems with Earth at the centre and bodies moving on spheres or epicycles. Aristarchus of Samos suggested a heliocentric alternative around 280 BCE, but it did not take hold.

The Ptolemaic system remained dominant until the 16th century, when Copernicus, then Kepler and Galileo, displaced it with a Sun-centred model. Newton's *Principia Mathematica* (1687) supplied universal gravitation, giving the Copernican principle, that Earth obeys the same laws as the heavens, a physical basis.

In the 19th century, Newtonian cosmology ran into paradoxes: Olbers' paradox (why the night sky is dark), the Bentley–Seeliger paradox (why gravity does not collapse an infinite universe), and the heat death paradox (why the universe has not reached thermal equilibrium). All three pointed to a finite, evolving cosmos rather than a static, infinite one.

The 20th century delivered that evolving cosmos. General relativity replaced Newton's static backdrop with curved, dynamic spacetime. Einstein applied it to the whole universe in 1917; Friedmann derived expanding-universe solutions in 1922–1924; Lemaître proposed a primeval-atom model in 1927; and Hubble's 1929 redshift measurements confirmed expansion. The CMB detection in 1964 and the development of cosmic inflation by Guth and Linde in the 1980s turned the Big Bang into a precise framework. Since around 1990, satellite and survey data have made cosmology a precision science, sometimes called the "golden age of cosmology." A 2014 claim by the BICEP2 experiment of gravitational-wave imprints in the CMB was later attributed to interstellar dust, showing the field's sensitivity to systematic error.

Hubble's identification of Cepheid variable stars in the Andromeda Galaxy in 1923–1924 resolved the Great Debate of 1920 by showing that the Milky Way is one galaxy among roughly 2 trillion in the observable universe.
