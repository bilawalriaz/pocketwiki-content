# Babylonian astronomy

Babylonian astronomy was the first attempt to give a refined mathematical description of celestial phenomena, and every later tradition of scientific astronomy, in the Hellenistic world, in India, in Islam, and in the West, depends on it in fundamental ways. Its practitioners were priest-scribes in Mesopotamia who recorded celestial events on clay tablets in cuneiform, computed planetary positions, and treated the sky as a source of omens that could be read and, in their belief, sometimes averted.

The numerical foundation was the sexagesimal system (base 60), which made it easy to record both very large and very small numbers and to divide the 360-degree sky evenly. Babylonians split the sky into three bands of 30 degrees and assigned 12 zodiacal signs to the constellations along the ecliptic, the apparent path of the Sun through the sky.

## How it was recorded

Three kinds of texts survive in fragmentary form:

- **Astronomical diaries**: systematic night-by-night logs of lunar and planetary positions, used to detect repeating patterns such as the 18-year Saros cycle of lunar eclipses.
- **Ephemerides**: arithmetical tables that compute future positions of planets and the Moon, giving orbital speeds.
- **Procedure texts**: the rules for generating those tables.

Because almost no theoretical writing survives alongside them, modern knowledge of Babylonian planetary theory remains fragmentary. What does survive shows that Babylonian methods were mostly arithmetical rather than geometric, in contrast to the Greeks, who favoured circles and uniform rotation. Recent work on unpublished British Museum tablets (350–50 BC) shows that some Babylonian astronomers also used geometrical methods, including a time–velocity graph for Jupiter that prefigured later European work.

## Key early works

The **Venus tablet of Ammisaduqa** (a 7th-century BC copy of observations probably going back to the second millennium BC) is the oldest surviving planetary text. The **Enuma Anu Enlil**, a Neo-Assyrian omen series from the 7th century BC, links omens to planetary motions. The **MUL.APIN** tablets catalogue stars and constellations and give schemes for predicting heliacal risings (the first annual dawn appearance of a star), settings, and daylight length measured by water clocks and shadows. The earlier **astrolabes** (c. 1800–1100 BC) list 36 stars tied to the months, drawn from three city-state traditions (Elam, Akkad, Amurru), each contributing 12; under Hammurabi these were merged into a single system arranged by the paths of Ea, Anu, and Enlil.

## The empirical turn

During the 8th and 7th centuries BC, Babylonian astronomers developed an empirical approach based on internal logic inside predictive planetary systems, detached from myth, that some modern scholars call the first scientific revolution. Chaldean astronomers of the Neo-Babylonian, Achaemenid, Seleucid, and Parthian periods extended this by maintaining long observational records, from which they extracted the Saros eclipse cycle and noticed that the Sun's motion along the ecliptic was not uniform, an effect now explained by Earth's elliptical orbit.

## Mathematics, calendar, and astronomy

A lunar calendar required occasional 13th-month corrections to track the seasons, and computing lunar and planetary positions drove Babylonian mathematics forward. Naburimannu, a priest of the moon god and the first documented Babylonian astronomer, wrote lunar and eclipse computation tables whose methods were reused by Seleucid-era astronomers.

## Omens

Mesopotamians believed the gods sent signs through the sky. Since planetary omens arose without human action they were considered the most powerful, though also preventable through rituals (namburbu, "[the evil] loosening"). The god Ea sent omens, eclipses were treated as the most dangerous, and the Omen Compendia record this belief from the early second millennium BC onward.

## Transmission to Greek astronomy

Babylonian astronomy reached Greece through the Hellenistic period and is responsible for the spread of astrology across the Graeco-Roman world. Herodotus credits the Babylonians with transmitting the gnomon (a vertical rod whose shadow tells time and direction) and the 12-hour division of the day. Franz Xaver Kugler showed that Ptolemy's Almagest attributes lunar periods to Hipparchus that were already in Babylonian ephemerides ("System B", sometimes credited to Kidinnu); a 2nd-century AD Greek papyrus using the same System B confirms the transmission. The only surviving planetary model from this school is Seleucus of Seleucia (b. 190 BC), who uniquely supported Aristarchus's heliocentric model, argued that tides are caused by the Moon's attraction, and may have proved heliocentrism geometrically. His original writings survive only in fragments of Arabic translation cited by al-Razi.
