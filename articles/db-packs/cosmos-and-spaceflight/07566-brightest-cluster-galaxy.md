# Brightest cluster galaxy

A brightest cluster galaxy (BCG) is the brightest galaxy in a galaxy cluster, a gravitationally bound group of hundreds to thousands of galaxies. BCGs are typically giant elliptical galaxies and include the most massive galaxies in the universe, with masses around 100 billion suns and reaching much higher values. They sit close to the geometric and kinematical center of their host cluster, at the bottom of the cluster potential well, the region where the combined gravity of all cluster matter is deepest. This central position makes them coincident with the peak of the cluster X-ray emission, which traces the hot gas filling the space between cluster galaxies. The BCG of the galaxy cluster Abell S0740, the giant elliptical ESO 325-G004, illustrates the scale involved.

## How BCGs form

Three formation scenarios have been proposed.

- Cooling flow. Star formation is driven by gas cooling out of the central region of the X-ray halo. The gas must cool below a key entropy threshold, a measure of how disordered the hot gas is, for this to occur. Studies of accretion populations and radiative cooling clusters have found no evidence that cooling flows operate as once proposed.
- Galactic cannibalism. Galaxies lose orbital energy through dynamical friction, the gravitational drag produced as they pass through the cluster, and are stripped of their outer stars by tidal forces. The remnants sink to the cluster center and add to the BCG. This model predicts many small galaxies still present in the evolved cluster.
- Galactic merger. Multiple galaxies merge rapidly during the collapse of the cluster itself. This hierarchical picture predicts that the BCG assembled late, as smaller pieces came together.

Simulations have shown that orbital decay through dynamical friction is too slow to account for BCG growth, weakening the cannibalism model. The merging model is now the most widely accepted framework, though it has its own problems.

## Tension with observations

Hierarchical simulations predict that BCGs grew late, but observations show that BCG stellar mass was already in place by redshift z roughly 1.5 to 2.0, a cosmic age of only a few billion years. BCGs show little change in size, luminosity, or structure across a wide range of redshifts, evidence for passive evolution after early assembly. Any later growth comes mostly from minor mergers and gas accretion rather than major collisions. This discrepancy has prompted calls for revisions to hierarchical models so they better account for early mass assembly.

## Types of BCGs

BCGs fall into several classes: giant ellipticals (gE), D galaxies, and cD galaxies. D and cD galaxies share an extended diffuse envelope of stars surrounding an elliptical-like nucleus, similar in inner structure to ordinary ellipticals. Their light profiles are often described by a Sersic surface brightness law, a double Sersic profile, or a de Vaucouleurs law, mathematical fits to how brightness falls off with distance from the center. Different choices of fit, and the faintness of the outer envelope, lead to reported sizes that disagree by significant factors.

## Disturbed high-redshift clusters

An X-ray study of ten high-redshift clusters (0.81 < z < 1.17) found that nine of the ten BCGs sit offset from the X-ray centroid and peak, signs that the galaxies are dynamically disturbed. A comparison with lower-redshift clusters (0.14 < z < 0.75) shows that these high-redshift systems are younger and that the early universe had a higher cluster merger rate. The same clusters show higher active galactic nuclei (AGN) power fractions at higher redshift, with substantial AGN feedback reported even in outer cluster regions.

## Precursors and the role of AGN

Precursors of BCGs may have been very large early-universe galaxies in relatively dense regions, already hosting supermassive black holes (SMBH) and active nuclei. These configurations can gather mass through mergers of smaller systems. Radio-jet turbulence, radiation pressure on the interstellar medium, and possible dual SMBH systems in adjacent galaxies may all push surrounding galaxies toward the central BCG, accelerating cluster assembly.
