# Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope

The Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope is a NASA space observatory in low Earth orbit that surveys the sky in gamma rays, the most energetic form of light. Launched on 11 June 2008 from Cape Canaveral aboard a Delta II rocket, it became the most sensitive gamma-ray telescope in orbit, succeeding the EGRET instrument on NASA's 1990s Compton Gamma Ray Observatory. Originally called the Gamma-ray Large Area Space Telescope (GLAST), it was renamed in August 2008 for Enrico Fermi, the physicist who first described high-energy particle behaviour.

The spacecraft carries two complementary instruments. The Large Area Telescope (LAT) is the primary imager: a pair-conversion detector sensitive to photons from 20 MeV to 300 GeV (roughly 20 million to 300 billion electronvolts). A gamma ray entering LAT strikes thin metal sheets and converts into an electron-positron pair; silicon microstrip trackers record the particles' paths, and a calorimeter of caesium iodide crystals measures their total energy. From these, researchers reconstruct the photon's arrival direction and energy. LAT sees about 20% of the sky at any moment, with angular resolution of a few arc minutes at the highest energies and about 3° at 100 MeV. The Gamma-ray Burst Monitor (GBM) wraps the spacecraft in 14 scintillation detectors: twelve sodium iodide crystals covering 8 keV to 1 MeV, and two bismuth germanate crystals covering 150 keV to 30 MeV. The side-mounted design lets GBM watch the sky unocculted by Earth for short gamma-ray flashes. Together the two span roughly eight orders of magnitude in photon energy, from medium X-rays to very-high-energy gamma rays.

Fermi travels in a near-circular orbit at about 550 km altitude with a 95-minute period. It normally rocks back and forth so its instruments sweep across most of the sky about 16 times per day, building up an all-sky map while remaining pointed away from Earth. Since June 2008 it has operated primarily in this sky-survey mode.

The scientific objectives centre on extreme cosmic accelerators and fundamental physics. Fermi probes how active galactic nuclei, pulsars, and supernova remnants accelerate particles to near-light speeds; resolves the unidentified sources that make up the cosmic gamma-ray background; characterises gamma-ray bursts across a wider energy range than previously possible; and searches for indirect signatures of dark matter, such as a gamma-ray excess from the Galactic Center or sharp spectral lines from particle annihilation. It has also tested whether the speed of light in vacuum depends on photon energy, a prediction on which general relativity and some quantum-gravity models disagree.

Major findings include the first gamma-ray-only pulsar, found in the CTA 1 supernova remnant, spinning every 316.86 milliseconds and lying about 4,600 light-years away; the record-setting burst GRB 080916C, whose jet moved at over 99.9999% of light speed and released the energy of roughly 9,000 supernovae; confirmation in 2010 that supernova remnants accelerate cosmic rays; two enormous gamma-ray "Fermi bubbles" extending about 25,000 light-years above and below the Galactic Center; the highest-energy light ever recorded from a solar flare, about 4 GeV, or two billion times visible-light energy; detection of gamma rays from thunderstorms that produce around 100 trillion positrons per flash; and a 94 GeV photon from GRB 130427A, the highest-energy photon yet recorded from a burst.

Fermi is also a key player in multi-messenger astronomy, where observations across different signal types are combined. In 2017 its GBM detected GRB 170817A about 1.7 seconds after a LIGO gravitational-wave signal from a binary neutron star merger, the first confirmed joint detection of electromagnetic and gravitational radiation from a single event. This established that short gamma-ray bursts come from such mergers. An earlier claim of a weak gamma-ray counterpart to the black-hole merger GW150914 remains contested, with INTEGRAL and AGILE finding no significant emission.

The mission was designed for a five-year prime phase with a ten-year goal; it has now operated for over 18 years. A 2018 solar-array drive failure forced near-exclusive reliance on survey mode, and the President's FY2026 and FY2027 budget requests proposed zeroing out the programme. The entire dataset has nevertheless been reprocessed multiple times, most notably with the Pass 8 software upgrade in 2015, which sharpened angular resolution, expanded the usable energy range, and recovered previously missed photons.
