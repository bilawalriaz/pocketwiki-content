# Hubble Space Telescope

The Hubble Space Telescope is a 2.4 m reflecting space telescope in low Earth orbit, operated jointly by NASA and the European Space Agency. Launched in April 1990, it observes in near-infrared, visible, and ultraviolet light from above Earth's atmosphere, where turbulence and absorption would otherwise blur images and block UV.

## Design and instruments

Hubble is a Cassegrain reflector of Ritchey–Chrétien design: two hyperbolic mirrors with a 57.6 m focal length. It carries five instruments in interchangeable bays. The original set, the Wide Field and Planetary Camera (WF/PC), Goddard High Resolution Spectrograph, Faint Object Camera, Faint Object Spectrograph, and High Speed Photometer, was replaced over time. Since the 2009 servicing mission the active instruments are Wide Field Camera 3 (WFC3), Cosmic Origins Spectrograph (COS), Space Telescope Imaging Spectrograph (STIS), and the Advanced Camera for Surveys (ACS); the Near Infrared Camera (NICMOS) has been in hibernation since 2008.

Goddard Space Flight Center flies the spacecraft, while the Space Telescope Science Institute (STScI) in Baltimore selects targets and processes data. ESA contributed solar panels and the original Faint Object Camera and receives about 15% of observing time in return.

## A flawed mirror

Within weeks of launch, images were blurrier than expected. The primary mirror had been polished to the wrong curvature, about 2200 nanometers too flat at the edge. This introduced spherical aberration, in which light from the mirror's edge and center focuses at different points, smearing each star into a halo roughly one arcsecond wide instead of concentrating it. The mirror had been ground precisely to a wrong specification because a custom reflective null corrector had a lens misplaced by 1.3 mm.

The flaw was stable and well-characterized, so astronomers partially compensated with image-processing techniques such as deconvolution. The full fix came in December 1993 on the first servicing mission: astronauts installed COSTAR, a module of small mirrors that exactly counteracted the aberration, and swapped in a replacement camera (WFPC2) whose internal optics carried the opposite error built in.

## Servicing missions

Hubble is the only telescope designed to be serviced in space. Five Space Shuttle missions (1993, 1997, 1999, 2002, 2009) replaced failed components, upgraded instruments, and swapped corrective optics. The fifth mission was canceled in 2004 after the Columbia disaster, because shuttle flights without a Space Station refuge were deemed unsafe. It was reinstated after NASA administrator Michael D. Griffin approved a crewed flight, and it flew in May 2009 on Atlantis. That mission installed WFC3 and COS, replaced all six gyroscopes and the batteries, and added the Soft Capture Mechanism for future deorbit. It was the last servicing visit; none is currently planned.

## Major discoveries

Hubble's results transformed several areas of astrophysics.

Measuring Cepheid variable stars in distant galaxies pinned down the Hubble constant, the universe's expansion rate, to within ±10%, narrowing the age of the universe to roughly 13.7 billion years; earlier estimates spanned 10–20 billion. Observations of distant Type Ia supernovae by Hubble and ground telescopes showed that expansion is accelerating, driven by something still unknown called dark energy. Three researchers received Nobel Prizes for this.

High-resolution spectroscopy showed that supermassive black holes are common at the centers of galaxies, and that a galaxy's black hole mass correlates with its properties.

Long exposures of "empty" patches of sky, the Hubble Deep Field, Ultra-Deep Field, and Extreme Deep Field, revealed thousands of galaxies extending back to within a few hundred million years of the Big Bang. GN-z11, the most distant galaxy confirmed by Hubble, dates to about 400 million years after the Big Bang.

## Costs, impact, and end of mission

Hubble's cumulative cost is roughly $11.3 billion in 2015 dollars, making it NASA's most expensive science mission. As of 2025, over 22,000 papers based on Hubble data have appeared in peer-reviewed journals, and such papers are cited about twice as often as the average astronomy paper. Specialized ground techniques such as aperture-masking interferometry and adaptive optics now match some of Hubble's resolution, but only over narrow fields and mainly in the near-infrared.

The Space Shuttle fleet's retirement in 2011 ended crewed servicing. Orbital drag steadily lowers Hubble's altitude; if it re-entered uncontrolled, parts of the 2.4 m mirror could survive and cause ground damage. The 2009 Soft Capture Mechanism was installed so a future robotic or crewed mission could attach a propulsion module and guide the telescope to a safe reentry over an ocean. Natural reentry is projected for sometime between 2028 and 2040. NASA and SpaceX studied a Crew Dragon servicing flight in 2022–2024, but NASA rejected the proposal in June 2024 because of damage risk. Hubble's mid-infrared successor, the James Webb Space Telescope, launched in December 2021 and concentrates on longer infrared wavelengths rather than duplicating Hubble's ultraviolet coverage.
