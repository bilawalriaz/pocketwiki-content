# Cosmic ray astronomy

Cosmic ray astronomy is a branch of observational astronomy that studies the most energetic charged particles arriving at Earth from outer space. These cosmic rays span energies from about 1 MeV (a million electron volts) to more than 1 EeV (a billion billion, or 10^18, electron volts) and consist mainly of protons, electrons, positrons, and atomic nuclei (mostly helium, but potentially all chemical elements). They travel at nearly the speed of light; the record holder is the "Oh-My-God particle," an ultra-high-energy event. Unlike visible-light, radio, or neutrino astronomy, this field uses charged particles as its carriers of information, which both enables unique measurements and creates a defining problem: magnetic fields bend their paths, so the arrival direction rarely points back to the source.

How detection works

When a high-energy cosmic ray hits the atmosphere, it collides with air nuclei and produces an *extensive air shower*: a cascade of secondary particles (electrons, positrons, photons, muons) that shower down through the air. Astronomers cannot observe the original particle directly; they infer its energy, direction, and composition from the measured properties of these secondaries.

Two complementary ground-based techniques exist. *Surface detector arrays* sample the shower at one altitude, while *air fluorescence detectors* record the faint light emitted when shower particles excite atmospheric nitrogen. Hybrid observatories combine both techniques. The Pierre Auger Observatory in Argentina, the world's largest cosmic ray observatory, is a hybrid array spread over 3,000 square kilometers. The Large High Altitude Air Shower Observatory (LHAASO) in Sichuan, China, applies the same approach. At very high altitude, scientific balloons (such as those used in the Cosmic Ray Energetics and Mass Experiment) and satellites (such as China's DAMPE/Dark Matter Particle Explorer) measure cosmic rays directly in space, before they interact with air.

What cosmic rays reveal

By measuring the energy, direction, and composition of cosmic rays, scientists identify the sources and acceleration mechanisms that produce them, including supernova explosions, black-hole accretion, and galactic magnetic fields. The composition of arriving nuclei also encodes the output of stellar nucleosynthesis, the nuclear reactions that forge the elements. Cosmic ray observations in the 1930s revealed subatomic particles beyond the then-known proton, neutron, and electron, including the positron and the muon, laying the groundwork for modern particle physics. They also established the presence of magnetic fields and radiation within the Solar System and supplied estimates of the amount and composition of matter in the universe. Some cosmic rays originate from beyond the Solar System or galaxy.

Closer to home, cosmic rays alter the isotopic composition (the mix of atomic variants) of meteorites; measuring those isotopes reveals when the parent bodies formed and fell to Earth, providing a timeline for Solar System history. The same interactions produce isotopes used in carbon-14 dating, which sets ages for archaeological artefacts and geological formations, and the secondary particles enable ground-based monitoring of soil moisture for agriculture and irrigation.

Selected milestones

- 1912: Austrian-American Victor Hess discovers cosmic rays with balloon flights.
- 1930s: Positron and muon identified in cosmic ray showers, opening particle physics.
- 1930s–40s: Pierre Auger discovers extensive atmospheric particle showers.
- 1940s–50s: Ground-based detectors measure cosmic ray flux and energy spectrum.
- 1960s: Volcano Ranch cosmic ray observatory established; first detection of cosmic ray anisotropy, the fact that arrival directions are not uniform across the sky.
- 1980s–90s: High-energy gamma-ray telescopes observe gamma rays from cosmic ray interactions.
- 2000s: Space-based detectors such as AMS-02 on the International Space Station extend measurements beyond the atmosphere.
- 2010s: Multi-messenger astronomy integrates cosmic rays with photons, neutrinos, and gravitational waves.

Open problem and future instruments

The central difficulty is that charged particles are deflected by galactic and intergalactic magnetic fields, so tracing a cosmic ray back to its source is rarely possible. Astronomers rely on modelling these fields and combine cosmic ray data with multi-messenger signals (gamma rays, neutrinos, gravitational waves) to localise origins. Future instruments sharpen this approach: the Cherenkov Telescope Array will detect gamma rays produced by cosmic ray interactions in Earth's atmosphere, a channel expected to be the most sensitive probe of cosmic rays near their source, enabling precision studies of their sources, acceleration mechanisms, and propagation.
