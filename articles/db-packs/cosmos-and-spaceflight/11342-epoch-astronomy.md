# Epoch (astronomy)

An epoch in astronomy is a moment in time used as a reference point for time-varying quantities such as celestial coordinates and orbital elements. Because gravitational perturbations cause orbits and coordinate frames to drift, quoting a value without saying *when* it was measured would be ambiguous. The main use of an epoch is to calculate future positions and velocities by plugging the time elapsed since the epoch into equations of motion, producing an ephemeris. A quantity is usually written as a polynomial in time from the epoch, though older tables simply froze the value at its epoch and applied separate corrections.

In 18th-century usage the word "epoch" sometimes meant the *value* of a quantity at a reference date rather than the date itself, so "correcting the epochs" referred to small adjustments of tabulated numbers, not a change of reference date. Modern usage reserves the term for the moment.

Epoch is not the same as equinox. The epoch fixes the moment the *values* apply to; the equinox, together with an equator or ecliptic, fixes the *coordinate frame* in which those values are expressed. Earth's rotation axis and orbital plane move through precession, so the frame itself moves, and there is an infinity of such frames. A single dataset can therefore carry two dates: one for the epoch of the numbers, another for the equinox of the frame. Orbital elements of minor planet (5145) Pholus, for instance, are listed for epoch 2010 January 4.0 TT, but the angular elements (argument of perihelion, longitude of the ascending node, inclination) are referred to the equinox and ecliptic of 2000.0, known as J2000; the mean anomaly, semi-major axis, eccentricity, and mean daily motion are frame-independent. When a frame is described as "of date," the coordinate system used in the polynomial is the one valid at the time of evaluation, so its date equals the epoch plus the elapsed interval t.

Frames can be reused indefinitely, but the tabulated values age. Osculating elements match the true orbit only near their epoch; a few decades later, accumulated perturbations make them unreliable, whereas a full analytical theory written as polynomials with periodic correction terms can remain valid for centuries. Some frames persist by convention even when their numerical data do not: the IAU constellation boundaries are defined in the equinox of B1875, so a comet's current position must be transformed into that 1875 frame before its constellation can be read off. For accurate pointing, observers need a frame close to the date of observation, since precession error grows with the gap, and stars drift relative to one another through proper motion. Once data are old enough, switching to a newer catalog is easier than stacking corrections.

The common ways of writing an epoch are Julian dates, Besselian years, and Julian years, all in Terrestrial Time (TT). Besselian years were defined so each year begins when the Sun's mean longitude, including aberration and measured from the mean equinox of date, reaches 280°, falling near the start of the corresponding Gregorian year. The definition relied on Newcomb's 1895 solar theory, which is now obsolete, so Besselian years are largely historical; B1900.0 is 1900 January 0.8135 TT and B1950.0 is 1950 January 0.9235 TT, written with a "B" prefix or a trailing ".0" to distinguish them from calendar years. Julian years are exactly 365.25 days and do not by themselves define a calendar date; the prefix "J" simply marks the conventional reference. The current standard is J2000: 12:00 TT on January 1, 2000, equivalent to Julian Date 2451545.0 TT, which fell about 64 seconds before noon UT1. A J-epoch written as a decimal year, J2000 + x, denotes an instant x Julian years from J2000 along the Gregorian calendar. Standard epochs are set by the IAU so data from different groups can be compared without ad hoc conversion, though raw observations are often archived in their original form so future reductions remain possible.

Astronomers have also disagreed about when a day begins. The civil day starts at midnight, but until 1925 astronomical days began at noon, when the mean Sun crossed the meridian, which is why J2000 is defined at noon. Older traditions started the day at sunrise, as in ancient Egypt where the year was tied to the heliacal rising of Sirius, or at sunset, as in Jewish, Islamic, and medieval European liturgical calendars, while some calendars such as the Hindu and Buddhist followed another morning epoch.
