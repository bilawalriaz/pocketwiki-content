# Space telescope

A space telescope is a telescope placed in outer space to observe astronomical objects, avoiding the absorption and distortion caused by Earth's atmosphere. Also called a space observatory, it differs from Earth-imaging satellites, which point downward for weather analysis, espionage, and other information-gathering tasks.

## Why space

Earth's atmosphere interferes with most electromagnetic radiation in three ways. It absorbs certain wavelengths outright, particularly ultraviolet, X-ray, and much of the infrared, so telescopes sensitive to those bands must operate above the air. It refracts incoming light, causing stars to twinkle and smearing fine detail. It also scatters shorter wavelengths and reflects city light upward, so ground-based observatories struggle to see faint objects near artificial lights and cannot observe during daytime.

Above the atmosphere, a telescope avoids twinkling, light pollution, and daytime sky brightness. Its angular resolution is then far better than a ground-based instrument of the same aperture, the diameter of the primary light-collecting mirror. Large ground-based telescopes partly compensate with adaptive optics, deformable mirrors that correct atmospheric distortion in real time, but no ground technique can recover wavelengths the air itself absorbs.

Space telescopes are therefore most valuable outside the optical window (visible light) and the radio window, the only two ranges where the atmosphere is largely transparent. Instruments such as Chandra (X-ray), the James Webb Space Telescope (infrared), XMM-Newton (X-ray), and the now-deactivated International Ultraviolet Explorer orbit above the air specifically because their target wavelengths cannot reach the ground.

## A short history

American astrophysicist Lyman Spitzer, later nicknamed "father of Hubble," proposed placing a telescope in space in 1946, arguing that a large instrument above the atmosphere would reveal far more detail than any ground-based observatory. His vision materialized as the Hubble Space Telescope, launched on April 24, 1990, aboard Space Shuttle Discovery on mission STS-31. The project owed much to Nancy Grace Roman, NASA's first Chief of Astronomy, known as "mother of Hubble," who lobbied NASA, Congress, and others to fund it.

Before Hubble, two smaller space telescopes had already operated: NASA's Orbiting Astronomical Observatory OAO-2, launched in 1968, and the Soviet Orion 1 ultraviolet telescope, carried to the Salyut 1 space station in 1971.

## Types and trade-offs

Space telescopes fall into two categories. Survey satellites map the entire sky systematically, while targeted observatories focus on selected objects, sky regions, or specific wavelengths. Operators include NASA, ESA, JAXA, ISRO, CNSA, and formerly the Soviet space program (now succeeded by Roscosmos).

The trade-offs are significant. Space telescopes cost far more to build than ground-based ones and, once launched, are extremely difficult or impossible to service. Hubble is the rare exception, having been repaired and upgraded by Space Shuttle missions. Most orbit beyond astronaut reach and must be replaced rather than repaired when a component fails.

Future programs face a recurring concern: as older observatories are decommissioned before successors launch, astronomers risk losing access to parts of the spectrum. On 16 January 2023, NASA announced preliminary development of successors including the Habitable Worlds Observatory and the New Great Observatories program, intended in part to prevent gaps in fundamental-science research.
