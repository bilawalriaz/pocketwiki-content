# Astrophysical jet

An astrophysical jet is a pair of narrow, oppositely directed streams of ionised matter that shoot away from a rotating astronomical object along its spin axis. When the flow approaches the speed of light, the jet is called relativistic, and its appearance is shaped by special-relativistic effects, most notably relativistic beaming, which makes a beam aimed near the observer look far brighter than an identical one pointing away.

Jets are observed across a remarkable range of objects. Supermassive black holes at the centres of active galaxies, quasars, and radio galaxies produce the largest examples, some extending millions of parsecs, far larger than the host galaxy itself. Centaurus A, the closest active radio galaxy to Earth, shows this on a smaller scale with plasma jets over a million light-years long. Stellar-mass black holes, neutron stars, and pulsars launch jets that are physically similar but far smaller, earning the name microquasars. Young stellar objects such as T Tauri stars and Herbig–Haro objects drive parsec-scale outflows, and bipolar jets are also seen around protostars, post-AGB stars, and planetary nebulae. Cataclysmic variable stars, X-ray binaries, and gamma-ray bursts are jet-associated, with gamma-ray bursts hosting the most extreme ultrarelativistic flows known.

## How jets are launched

The exact cause is not settled, but the leading picture ties jets to accretion disks. As the disk spins, it drags and twists magnetic field lines, and the twisted fields funnel disk material into two narrow beams and drive it outward along the rotation axis. Frame-dragging, a general-relativity effect in which a rotating massive body twists the surrounding spacetime, can also help collimate and power the flow.

Two influential energy-extraction mechanisms are:

- The Blandford–Znajek process extracts rotational energy from a spinning black hole through magnetic fields anchored in the surrounding disk; the black hole's spin tightens the field lines and the wound-up fields fling relativistic material outward.
- The Penrose mechanism extracts energy from a rotating black hole via frame-dragging, a process later shown to be capable of launching relativistic particles and producing collimated jets.

The frequency of high-energy sources with jets, together with X-ray emission from the launching region, suggests that real jets draw on a combination of accretion-disk energy and black-hole spin, rather than any single mechanism. Jet composition remains uncertain: some models favour a neutral mix of nuclei, electrons, and positrons, while others fit a purely electron–positron plasma. Any heavy nuclei embedded in a positron–electron jet would be dragged up to the same bulk velocity, so they would carry unusually high energy.

## Relativistic effects and key examples

When jets move at a sizable fraction of the speed of light, relativistic beaming means that jets aimed near our line of sight look dramatically brighter and can even appear to move faster than light, an illusion caused by the near-light speed combined with the small angle to us. The microquasar SS 433 ejects matter at a mean speed of about 0.26c, a clear relativistic signature in a stellar-mass system. Across the full population, beam lengths range from thousands to millions of parsecs.

Pulsar jets show that rotation is not the whole story. The pulsar IGR J11014-6103 drives the largest jet yet seen in the Milky Way, with material moving at roughly 0.8c. It is seen only in X-rays, with no detected radio emission and no accretion disk, and its spin rate is a modest 15.9 Hz. Because that rotation and the absence of inflowing material cannot power the outflow on their own, the jet must rely on an additional mechanism, even though it still emerges aligned with the rotation axis and perpendicular to the pulsar's actual motion through space.

Jets therefore link black holes, neutron stars, and young stars through a shared geometry: a central spinning engine, a surrounding accretion disk or rotating surface, twisted magnetic field lines, and two narrow beams aligned with the rotation axis. The same template produces outflows from a neutron star a few kilometres across and from a supermassive black hole billions of times more massive, with the only meaningful difference being the energy budget available to feed the jet.
