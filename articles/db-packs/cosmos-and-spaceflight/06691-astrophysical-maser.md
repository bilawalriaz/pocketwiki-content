# Astrophysical maser

An astrophysical maser is a naturally occurring source of stimulated spectral line emission, usually in the microwave portion of the electromagnetic spectrum. Like a laboratory laser, the radiation is monochromatic and stimulated at a frequency matching the energy difference between two quantum levels of a molecule whose population has been pumped into a non‑thermal distribution, with more molecules in the upper state than the lower one. Unlike a laboratory maser, an astrophysical maser has no engineered resonant cavity: the radiation takes a single pass through the gain medium, so the output generally lacks the spatial coherence and mode purity of a laboratory device. The emission is technically amplified spontaneous emission, the same process used in single‑pass laboratory amplifiers such as a regeneratively amplified Ti:Sapph stage.

Whether the "m" in maser means "microwave" or "molecule" is unsettled. Charles Townes argued for "molecule," since the gain transition usually lies in a rotational or vibrational state of a molecule. The terms *iraser* and *taser* are used for transitions at micrometre and terahertz wavelengths, but the boundary between laser and maser is drawn loosely in practice.

## Conditions required

A pumped population inversion is not, by itself, enough to make a detectable maser. The gas along the line of sight must be velocity coherent, so that Doppler shifts in different parts of the cloud do not prevent inverted molecules from radiatively coupling. Surrounding unpumped molecules of the same species can absorb the signal, and astronomers partially overcome this with interferometric techniques such as very long baseline interferometry (VLBI). Polarisation, where present, arises from a magnetic field in the gain medium or from a pump that prefers certain magnetic sub‑transitions, sometimes via the Zeeman effect.

## Consequences of exponential gain

The gain of a maser cloud is exponential in the population inversion and the velocity‑coherent path length, and this produces the observed radiation properties:

- **Beaming.** A slightly longer path through the irregular cloud produces a much brighter spot, so maser features appear as compact bright points much smaller than their parent cloud, with most radiation emerging along the longest coherent path.
- **Rapid variability.** Any change in the inversion or path length produces an exponential change in output, so masers flicker on timescales of days to years.
- **Line narrowing.** The centre of the line profile is amplified more than the wings, producing a tall, narrow spike rather than a broad bump.
- **Saturation.** Once pumping can no longer keep up with stimulated emission, the maser is saturated and further amplification becomes roughly linear in path length. Saturation in one transition can steal inversion from another, an effect called competitive gain.
- **High brightness temperature.** Strong interstellar OH masers at 1665 MHz correspond to a blackbody of roughly 10⁹ K, and some masers reach 10¹²–10¹⁴ K. This is not a kinetic temperature: at 10⁹ K the OH bond would already break. The figure describes brightness, not heat.
- **Polarisation.** Many astronomical masers are strongly polarised, up to 100% circularly in some OH sources, with weaker linear polarisation, tracing magnetic fields in the source region.

## Where they live

- **Comets.** Volatiles released from the nucleus can form inversions. The 1994 impact of Shoemaker‑Levy 9 on Jupiter produced 22 GHz water‑maser emission, and 1667 MHz OH maser emission was detected in comet Hale‑Bopp in 1997 from water broken down by sunlight.
- **Planetary atmospheres and moons.** Cyclotron masers occur at Jupiter's north pole. Water masers were reported in 2009 in the plumes of the Saturnian moons Hyperion, Titan, Enceladus, and Atlas.
- **Stellar atmospheres.** Around late‑type stars such as the Mira variable S Orionis, shock waves from pulsations drive the gas outward, placing SiO masers at about 5–10 AU, H₂O masers at 100–400 AU, and OH masers at 1000–10 000 AU from the star, with the outer SiO limit set by condensation of SiO onto dust.
- **Star‑forming regions.** Young stellar objects and ultra‑compact H II regions inside molecular clouds host most known masers, including rarer ammonia and formaldehyde transitions.
- **Supernova remnants.** The 1720 MHz OH transition traces supernova remnants that are interacting with molecular clouds.
- **Active galactic nuclei.** Megamasers arise in molecular disks, roughly 0.5 parsec across, accreting onto supermassive black holes, or in jets, where OH, H₂O, and H₂CO can mase at unusually high luminosities.

## Discovery

In 1965 Weaver and colleagues detected an unexplained emission line at 1665 MHz, briefly attributed to a hypothetical "mysterium" before being identified as hydroxyl (OH) from compact sources in molecular clouds. Water masers were found in 1969, methanol in 1970, and silicon monoxide in 1974. Masers were then found around evolved late‑type stars (the OH/IR stars), in other galaxies from 1973 onward, and in cometary halos within the Solar System. In 1982 an extragalactic source about a million times more luminous than any previously known was labelled a *megamaser*. A disk maser was found in 1995 in MWC 349A using NASA's Kuiper Airborne Observatory.

## Why they matter

Masers are extraordinarily bright, compact, and spectrally narrow, which makes them precision probes of otherwise inaccessible gas. Their Doppler shifts give line‑of‑sight velocities, and their proper motions, tracked by VLBI over years, give parallaxes and transverse speeds. The most dramatic result of this technique has been a geometric distance to the galaxy NGC 4258 derived from water masers orbiting its central black hole. Similar work has yielded distances and proper motions for Local Group galaxies, including the Triangulum Galaxy, and has tightened the galactic distance scale by pinning down the positions of spiral arms. Many pumping schemes are still inferred empirically, and some predicted transitions, such as the magnetic dipole transitions of OH near 53 MHz, remain undetected, possibly because the telescopes are not yet sensitive enough.
