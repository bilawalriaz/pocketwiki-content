# James Webb Space Telescope

The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) is a 6,500 kg infrared space telescope launched on 25 December 2021 aboard an Ariane 5 from French Guiana. It reached its station about 1.5 million km from Earth, near the Sun–Earth L2 Lagrange point, in January 2022 and began science operations on 12 July 2022. It is the largest telescope in space, built by NASA with the European and Canadian space agencies, managed by the Space Telescope Science Institute in Baltimore, and constructed primarily by Northrop Grumman.

## Why infrared, and why in space

Webb observes near- to mid-infrared light from 0.6 to 28.5 μm. Distant galaxies recede so quickly that their visible light is redshifted into the infrared. Infrared also passes through dust clouds that block visible light, revealing star-forming regions and planetary systems, and cool objects such as brown dwarfs, exoplanets, and debris disks emit most of their radiation in the infrared. Earth's atmosphere absorbs most infrared bands and glows in them, so observations must happen from space. Hubble, which observes ultraviolet through near-infrared (0.1–2.5 μm), cannot reach these mid-infrared bands, and its own warm optics emit infrared radiation that would swamp the faint signals Webb is designed to detect.

## The mirror and the cold

The primary mirror is 6.5 m across, built from 18 gold-coated beryllium hexagons that fold for launch and unfold in space. Segmented optics, pioneered by Guido Horn d'Arturo, lets a mirror too large for any rocket fairing be launched folded and assembled on the way to orbit. The segments are positioned by 132 actuators with roughly 10 nanometre accuracy, fine-tuned every few days. The collecting area is 25.4 m², more than six times Hubble's 4.0 m², and infrared resolution is comparable to Hubble's visible-light resolution because longer wavelengths require a larger aperture to achieve the same sharpness.

Because the telescope's own heat would glow in the infrared and drown out the targets, Webb must stay below 50 K (−223 °C). A five-layer sunshield blocks heat from the Sun, Earth, and Moon. Each layer is a thin Kapton E film coated with aluminium, with the outermost two layers also coated with doped silicon to reflect sunlight; the system has an effective SPF of about 1,000,000. The shield was folded 12 times to fit the Ariane 5 fairing. MIRI, the mid-infrared instrument, is cooled further to under 6 K by a helium gas mechanical cryocooler.

## Orbit at L2

Webb does not actually orbit L2, because L2 is an unstable equilibrium with no gravity. Instead it traces a halo orbit around L2, drifting between 250,000 km and 832,000 km from the point and completing one circuit every six months. The combined pull of the Sun and Earth lets a spacecraft there orbit the Sun in exactly one Earth year, so the telescope stays at nearly constant distance from Earth. This keeps the spacecraft bus and high-gain antenna on the Sun-facing side powered and in contact with Earth, while the cold side sits permanently in the sunshield's shadow. The halo orbit avoids both Earth's and Moon's shadows, stabilising temperature. Station-keeping uses about 2.5 m/s of Δv per year from a budget of 93 m/s, and a precise launch left enough propellant to extend the mission from a planned 10 years to roughly 20.

## Instruments

Four instruments sit in the Integrated Science Instrument Module behind the mirrors. NIRCam (0.6–5 μm) is the primary imager and acts as the wavefront sensor that aligns the 18 mirror segments. NIRSpec (0.6–5 μm) is a near-infrared spectrograph built by ESA that can take spectra of hundreds of objects at once through a microshutter array. MIRI (5–27 μm) covers the mid-infrared with a camera and spectrograph and requires active cooling to 6 K. FGS-NIRISS, provided by Canada, is the fine guidance sensor that stabilises the line of sight; the attached NIRISS module provides imaging and slitless spectroscopy. NIRCam and MIRI include coronagraphs that block starlight when imaging exoplanets and dust disks.

## Mission goals and performance

Webb was designed to search for light from the first stars and galaxies, to study galaxy and planet formation, and to characterise exoplanet atmospheres, including searching for biosignatures such as methane. It can detect objects up to 100 times fainter than Hubble, reaching back to a redshift of about z ≈ 20, around 180 million years after the Big Bang, when the first stars are thought to have formed. Hubble's deepest pre-Webb view reached only z ≈ 11.1 (galaxy GN-z11, about 400 million years after the Big Bang).

## Development history and cost

The project began in 1996 as the Next Generation Space Telescope with an 8 m mirror and a 2007 launch target. It was renamed in 2002 for James E. Webb, who led NASA from 1961 to 1968 through Mercury, Gemini, and most of Apollo. A 2005 redesign cut the mirror to 6.5 m and pushed the schedule. Final assembly finished in 2016, and sunshield tears during 2018 testing forced further delays before the 2021 launch. Total NASA cost reached about US$9.7 billion, with ESA contributing roughly €700 million and CSA about CA$200 million, for a combined total near US$10 billion. A 2010 Nature article called it "the telescope that ate astronomy" because overruns consumed budget from other programmes.

## First results

Within two weeks of the first images in July 2022, preprint papers reported galaxies at redshifts up to z ≈ 16.7, far earlier than expected, though follow-up spectroscopy revised some candidates downward. In May 2024 JWST identified JADES-GS-z14-0 at z = 14.32, seen 290 million years after the Big Bang. By 2025, observations of compact "little red dot" galaxies revealed supermassive black holes of 10⁷ to 10⁸ solar masses within400 million years of the Big Bang, including GHZ2 at z = 12.34 and a ~50 million solar mass black hole in UHZ1 at z = 7.04, findings that challenge models in which black holes grow only from stellar remnants and favour massive direct-collapse seeds. A 2026 study used JWST to detect a repeating daily cloud cycle on the hot Jupiter WASP-94 A b, with magnesium silicate clouds forming on the morning side and evaporating on the evening side.
