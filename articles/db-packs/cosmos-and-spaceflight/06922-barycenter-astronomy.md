# Barycenter (astronomy)

In astronomy, the **barycenter** is the center of mass around which two or more bodies orbit. The term comes from Ancient Greek *barús* ("heavy") and *kéntron* ("center"). A barycenter is a dynamical point, not a physical object: it has no mass, cannot be touched, and exists only as the balance point of the bodies' gravitational motions.

## Position in a two-body system

For two bodies, the distance from body 1's center to the barycenter is

$$r_1 = a \cdot \frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}$$

where $a$ is the center-to-center distance and $m_1$, $m_2$ are the masses. The secondary's distance is $r_2 = a - r_1$. The barycenter also lies at one of the two foci of each body's elliptical orbit, which is why Kepler's laws still apply.

Where the barycenter falls depends on the mass ratio and the separation.

- **Inside the larger body.** The small body visibly orbits the large one, and the large body only wobbles. In the Earth–Moon system, the barycenter lies 4,671 km from Earth's center, about 74% of Earth's radius (6,371 km), so the Moon orbits Earth while Earth shimmies in a small circle.
- **Between the bodies.** Both bodies swing around a point in empty space. Pluto and Charon have a mass ratio of about 8.5 to 1, so their barycenter sits well outside Pluto, and the pair is often treated as a double planet rather than a planet and moon.
- **Outside the larger body.** This requires the smaller body to be both massive and far. The Sun is about 1,000 times more massive than Jupiter, but Jupiter is distant enough that their barycenter lies 742,370 km from the Sun's center, just above the solar surface (solar radius 695,700 km). When the four giant planets line up on one side of the Sun, the combined barycenter of the Solar System reaches about 1.17 solar radii from the Sun's center.

For any Sun–planet pair, the barycenter lies outside the Sun only when

$$\frac{a}{R_\odot} \cdot \frac{m_\text{planet}}{M_\odot} > 1,$$

which is equivalent to $a \cdot m_\text{planet} > R_\odot \cdot M_\odot \approx 1530\;M_\oplus\;\text{AU}$. If Jupiter were at Mercury's orbital distance (0.387 AU), the Sun–Jupiter barycenter would still sit only about 55,000 km from the Sun's center. Even Earth at Eris's orbital distance (68 AU) would leave the Sun–Earth barycenter just over 30,000 km from the Sun's center.

## Moving with elliptical orbits

All real orbits are ellipses, so $a$ varies between apoapsis and periapsis and the barycenter shifts with it. In borderline systems the barycenter can pass from inside the larger body to outside it and back. The condition for this oscillation is

$$\frac{1}{1-e} > \frac{r_1}{R_1} > \frac{1}{1+e}.$$

The Sun–Jupiter system, with $e = 0.0484$, gives $1.05 < 1.07 > 0.954$ and fails the inner bound by a small margin.

## Barycentric coordinates and time

Astronomers use barycentric coordinates, a non-rotating frame with its origin at the system's barycenter. The International Celestial Reference System (ICRS) is defined this way, anchored to the Solar System's barycenter. For highly eccentric objects such as long-period comets, barycentric orbital elements are more stable than heliocentric ones because the barycentric osculating orbit is less disturbed by Jupiter's 11.8-year position.

In Newtonian mechanics barycentric coordinates are straightforward. In general relativity, clocks at different gravitational potentials and velocities tick at different rates, so the barycentric frame is paired with Barycentric Coordinate Time (TCB): a global time defined by an ideal clock assumed to be far from all gravitating matter and synchronized by telemetry. TCB is the standard for precise Solar System ephemerides.
