# Natural satellite

A natural satellite is a body that orbits a planet, dwarf planet, or small Solar System body. Colloquially called "moons," they range from kilometer-scale moonlets to worlds larger than Mercury. The Solar System has 455 confirmed moons across six planetary satellite systems, plus 625 minor planets known to host satellites. Kepler coined the word "satellite" (Latin *satelles*, attendant) in 1610, shortly after Galileo discovered Jupiter's four large moons. After Sputnik in 1957, "satellite" came to mean artificial objects in everyday use, and "moon" became the standard scientific term for natural ones.

Two formation pathways explain most moons. **Regular satellites** orbit close to their primary on prograde, low-inclination, near-circular paths and condensed *in situ* from the same protoplanetary disk as the planet. **Irregular satellites** sit far out on inclined, eccentric, often retrograde paths and are captured asteroids, possibly fragmented by later collisions. Most large moons are regular; most small ones are irregular. Earth's Moon and the Pluto system are exceptions that formed from giant impacts, with debris reaccreting in orbit. Neptune's large, circular, retrograde moon Triton is a captured dwarf planet. A few small moons share a larger moon's orbit by librating around the L4 or L5 Lagrangian points, 60° ahead or behind, and are called **Trojan satellites**; Telesto and Calypso orbit with Tethys, and Helene and Polydeuces with Dione. Temporary satellites are asteroids briefly captured from solar orbits; 2006 RH120 lingered near Earth for nine months, and simulations show such captures are common.

Tidal forces from the primary gradually brake a close moon's spin until its rotation period matches its orbital period, fixing one hemisphere permanently toward the planet. This state is **tidal locking**. Saturn's Hyperion and Pluto's four small moons rotate chaotically instead, Hyperion because Titan perturbs it, Pluto's moons because Charon tugs them. Irregular satellites are too distant to lock; Jupiter's Himalia, Saturn's Phoebe, and Neptune's Nereid each spin roughly every ten hours despite orbital periods of hundreds of days.

Gravity pulls any moon large enough for self-gravity to overcome rock or ice strength into a rounded ellipsoid, the state of **hydrostatic equilibrium**. Twenty known moons reach this shape and are sometimes called "satellite planets." Neptune's Proteus, about 420 km across, is the largest irregularly shaped moon. Tidally locked moons also develop ovoid shapes, squat at the poles with the long equatorial axis pointed at the primary; Saturn's Mimas is about 9% longer than its polar axis, and the 3 km moon Methone is visibly egg-shaped. The effect shrinks for larger moons with stronger self-gravity.

Several of the round moons are geologically active. **Io** is the most volcanically active body in the Solar System. **Europa, Enceladus, and Triton** show active tectonics and **cryovolcanism**, the eruption of water, ammonia, or methane instead of silicate lava. For Io, Europa, and Enceladus, the energy source is **tidal heating**: friction from the moon's eccentric orbit flexing its interior. Triton was likely heated the same way before its orbit circularized. Enceladus and Triton vent geyser-like plumes. Titan and Triton hold thick atmospheres; Titan alone hosts stable surface lakes of liquid hydrocarbons. Four Galilean moons carry thin atmospheres, and Europa, Ganymede, Callisto, Titan, and Enceladus are inferred to hide subsurface liquid-water oceans beneath their icy shells, based on magnetic and gravity data rather than direct sampling.

The giant planets host the richest systems. Seven moons exceed 2,500 km across: the four Galilean moons, Saturn's Titan, Neptune's Triton, and Earth's Moon. Ganymede and Titan are both larger than Mercury; Callisto matches Mercury in diameter. Mercury and Venus have no moons, Earth has one, and Mars has two small captured-asteroid moons, Phobos and Deimos. Pluto's system is unusual because Charon is so massive, about 0.52 of Pluto's diameter and 12.2% of its mass, that the two bodies orbit a **barycenter** (their shared center of mass) located in empty space between them rather than inside Pluto; some astronomers call it a double dwarf planet. No other planetary moon comes close to this ratio: Triton's mass is 1/4790 of Neptune's, Titan's 1/4225 of Saturn's, Ganymede's 1/12810 of Jupiter's. Dwarf planets such as Haumea, Orcus, Quaoar, Makemake, Gonggong, and Eris each host one or two small moons, and hundreds of asteroids, Mars-crossers, near-Earth objects, and trans-Neptunian objects do as well; tracking these binaries lets astronomers compute bulk density without a spacecraft visit.

No moon of a moon has been confirmed. Tidal forces from the host planet usually destabilize such orbits, though large, wide-orbit moons like Titan, Iapetus, Callisto, and Earth's Moon remain theoretical candidates. A 2008 hint of rings around Rhea that would have implied shepherd moonlets did not survive later Cassini imaging. Iapetus's equatorial ridge has been proposed as debris from a lost subsatellite, but the origin is unresolved. As of 2024, numerous exomoon candidates have been catalogued, mostly through transit timing variations on exoplanets, yet none has been statistically confirmed.
