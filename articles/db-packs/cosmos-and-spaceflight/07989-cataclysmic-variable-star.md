# Cataclysmic variable star

A cataclysmic variable (CV) is a close binary star in which a white dwarf pulls hydrogen-rich gas from a less massive companion, brightening by many magnitudes before fading back to a quiet state. The name comes from when these objects were called "novae" (Latin for "new"), because an outbursting CV can look like a new star in the sky.

## How the system works

The two stars orbit so closely that the white dwarf's gravity stretches the companion. The companion, called the **donor star**, fills its **Roche lobe**, the region around a star in a binary where material is still bound to that star rather than its partner, and spills gas through the inner Lagrange point. That gas almost always forms a flat, swirling **accretion disk** around the white dwarf. Viscous friction in the disk drains the gas inward, releasing **gravitational potential energy** as strong ultraviolet and X-ray emission. The shortest orbit measured in a hydrogen-rich CV is 51 minutes, in ZTF J1813+4251.

Gas that reaches the white dwarf piles up as a hydrogen-rich layer. When the base of that layer becomes dense and hot enough, **runaway hydrogen fusion** ignites, converting the hydrogen to helium in a thermonuclear flash. This is a **classical nova**: a single violent brightening of 6 to 19 magnitudes. If accretion continues long enough to push the white dwarf toward the **Chandrasekhar limit** (about 1.4 solar masses, the maximum a white dwarf can support against its own gravity), carbon fusion can ignite and the white dwarf is destroyed as a **Type Ia supernova**.

## Dwarf-nova outbursts

The accretion disk itself is unstable. The outer disk can switch from a cool, dim state to a hotter, brighter one and back, producing **dwarf-nova outbursts** that recur on timescales of days to decades. These brightenings are smaller than classical novae because they come from the disk, not from a thermonuclear flash on the white dwarf.

## Magnetic systems

In some CVs the white dwarf's magnetic field is strong enough to disrupt the inner disk or prevent one forming. In **polars**, the field locks the white dwarf's spin to the binary orbit and channels the accretion stream onto the magnetic poles; the systems show strong optical polarisation, which gives them their name. **Intermediate polars** (DQ Herculis stars) have weaker fields, so a disk still forms but is threaded and warped by field lines. **VY Sculptoris stars** are likely a magnetic subclass, dropping by more than a magnitude with rare dwarf-nova-type outbursts during the dim state.

## Main subclasses

- **Classical novae**: single thermonuclear outbursts of 6 to 19 magnitudes.
- **Recurrent novae**: outbursts of 4 to 9 magnitudes, repeating every 10 to 80 years; examples include T Pyxidis and RS Ophiuchi.
- **Dwarf novae** (U Geminorum stars): repeated small outbursts from disk instability. Z Camelopardalis stars "halt" at an intermediate brightness; SU Ursae Majoris stars show brighter "superoutbursts"; SS Cygni stars show outbursts of two distinct lengths.
- **Luminous red novae**: stellar mergers that become very red after outburst.
- **AM Canum Venaticorum stars**: both stars are white dwarfs, the disk is helium-rich, and they are candidate **gravitational-wave** sources.
- **SW Sextantis stars**: dwarf-nova-like systems with the disk in a steady, outburst-free state that emits non-uniformly.
- **Z Andromedae stars (symbiotic variables)**: a cool giant losing mass to a hotter compact companion with its own accretion disk.

More than 1,600 CVs were catalogued by 1 February 2006, and new ones continue to be found.

## Discovery and identification

CVs are popular amateur targets because an outbursting system is bright enough to see with modest equipment, and the only common false alarm is an asteroid, which moves noticeably from night to night. Confirmed CVs are blue, highly variable on short timescales, and show strong **emission lines** in their spectra. They radiate in the ultraviolet and X-ray bands and are expected to emit gamma rays from positron annihilation in fusion debris, though this has not yet been detected.

About six galactic novae are discovered each year, even though models predict 20 to 50; the gap is explained by interstellar dust hiding some, by sparse observing in the southern hemisphere, and by the impossibility of seeing novae near the Sun or in full moonlight. **Superhumps**, extra periodic brightenings seen in some CVs, arise when the accretion disk precesses in resonance with the binary orbit, distorting the disk and changing how much light it sends toward Earth.
