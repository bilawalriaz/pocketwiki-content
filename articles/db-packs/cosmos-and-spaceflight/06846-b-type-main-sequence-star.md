# B-type main-sequence star

A B-type main-sequence star is a core hydrogen-burning star of spectral type B with luminosity class V: a hot, blue-white star on the upper main sequence. They are also called B-type dwarfs. The term "blue-white dwarf" can be confused with a white dwarf, an unrelated compact stellar remnant rather than a main-sequence star. Within the Harvard sequence O, B, A, F, G, K, M, B-type stars sit just below the rare O-type stars and far above the Sun (a G-type star). Achernar is the brightest as seen from Earth; Rigel is brighter but is a blue supergiant, not a main-sequence star.

## Spectral definition

The B class was introduced in the Revised Harvard photometry catalogue, originally defined by neutral helium (He I) absorption lines together with the absence of singly ionised helium (He II) lines in the blue-violet spectrum. A numerical suffix from 0 to 9 marks how far a star lies toward the next class, so B2 sits one-fifth of the way from B0 to A0. Later high-resolution spectra showed He II lines in B0 stars and weak He I lines in A0 stars, so the modern MK system classifies by relative line strengths instead, for example defining B0 by the 439 nm helium line being stronger than the 420 nm line. A typical B-type spectrum is dominated by strong He I absorption (peaking at B2) and moderately strong hydrogen Balmer lines, which grow through the B class and peak at A2. Ionised silicon lines set the sub-type within B, while magnesium lines help separate temperature classes.

## Physical properties

B-type main-sequence stars span roughly 2 to 18 solar masses, surface temperatures of about 10,000–30,000 K, and luminosities from tens to tens of thousands of Suns. Representative values:

| Spectral type | Mass (M☉) | Radius (R☉) | Luminosity (L☉) | Temperature (K) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| B0V | 17.70 | 7.16 | 44,875 | 31,400 |
| B2V | 7.30 | 4.06 | 2,679 | 20,600 |
| B5V | 4.70 | 3.36 | 592 | 15,700 |
| B8V | 3.38 | 2.86 | 154 | 12,300 |
| B9V | 2.75 | 2.49 | 73 | 10,700 |

Familiar examples include Regulus, Algol A and Acrux.

## Internal structure, winds, and Be stars

B-type stars have no outer convection zone and no corona. Their cores are hot enough that hydrogen is fused mainly through the CNO cycle, a hydrogen-burning chain that uses carbon, nitrogen and oxygen as catalysts. The CNO cycle is extremely temperature-sensitive, so nearly all the energy is released near the centre, producing a convective core wrapped in a radiative envelope. This keeps fresh hydrogen mixed into the fuel and helium stirred through the core. Many B-type stars also spin rapidly, with equatorial velocities around 200 km/s, and they drive stellar winds of roughly 3,000 km/s that strip mass at a higher rate than the Sun does. Be stars are a non-supergiant subset showing one or more Balmer hydrogen lines in emission, produced by unusually strong winds, high temperatures and rapid rotation ejecting material into view. B[e] stars are a related but distinct group whose forbidden emission lines, transitions normally suppressed by quantum-mechanical selection rules, appear in square brackets; the class includes blue giants and blue supergiants, not only dwarfs.

## Chemical peculiarities

Some B0–B3 stars show anomalously strong neutral helium lines and are called helium-strong stars, often with strong photospheric magnetic fields. The opposite case, helium-weak B stars with understrength helium and strong hydrogen, also exists. Mercury-manganese stars occupy the cooler B7–B9 sub-types.

## Standards

The MK classification is anchored by long-stable reference stars whose types have not changed since the 1940s: Upsilon Orionis (B0 V), Eta Aurigae (B3 V) and Alkaid (B3 V). Later catalogues added further standards such as Tau Scorpii, Omega Scorpii, 42 Orionis, 22 Scorpii, Rho Aurigae, 18 Tauri, Acrab, 29 Persei, HD 36936, HD 21071, Omega Fornacis, HR 2328, and the sole B4 V standard, 90 Leonis. Because they are massive and luminous, B-type main-sequence stars exhaust their core hydrogen in only millions to tens of millions of years, a small fraction of the Sun's ten-billion-year main-sequence lifetime.
