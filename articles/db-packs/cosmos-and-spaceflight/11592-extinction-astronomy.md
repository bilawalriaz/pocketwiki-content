# Extinction (astronomy)

In astronomy, **extinction** is the absorption and scattering of electromagnetic radiation by dust and gas lying between an emitting object and the observer. It dims the object, shifts its colour toward the red, and distorts its spectrum. Robert Julius Trumpler formally documented the effect in 1930, though Friedrich Georg Wilhelm von Struve noted it in 1847, and several earlier observers had noticed that stars near the Milky Way's plane looked redder than expected without linking this to intervening dust.

Extinction has three sources. **Interstellar extinction** comes from dust and gas between stars. **Circumstellar dust** around an observed object adds a local contribution. For ground-based observers, **atmospheric extinction** — Rayleigh scattering by air molecules, scattering by particulates, and telluric absorption by oxygen, ozone, and water — attenuates incoming light and renders X-ray, ultraviolet, and much of the infrared completely opaque, which is why space-based observatories are required for those bands.

## Reddening and the colour excess

Interstellar dust scatters and absorbs short-wavelength (blue) photons more efficiently than long-wavelength (red) ones, so a star seen through dust looks dimmer and redder than it actually is. This shift is **interstellar reddening**, distinct from **redshift**: reddening distorts relative intensities while leaving spectral line positions unchanged, whereas redshift shifts every frequency by the same proportional factor. The colour of sunsets arises from the same physical mechanism in Earth's atmosphere.

Reddening is quantified as a *colour excess*, the difference between an observed colour index and the intrinsic one. In the UBV photometric system, the standard blue-minus-visual colour excess is

$$E_{B-V} = (B-V)_\text{observed} - (B-V)_\text{intrinsic}.$$

An A0-type main-sequence star has intrinsic $(B-V) = 0$ (±0.02), giving the reference against which reddening is measured. Comparing two to five passbands (U, B, V, R, I) and subtracting expected intrinsic differences recovers the amount of dust along the line of sight.

## How much extinction is typical

In the solar neighbourhood, V-band (centred near 540 nm) extinction averages about **0.7–1.0 mag/kpc**, but the dust is clumpy so values vary along each sight line. Roughly1 magnitude corresponds to a factor of 2.512 in brightness, giving about a 2× dimming per kiloparsec in V. Toward the broader plane of the Milky Way, visual extinction reaches roughly **1.8 mag/kpc**. Toward the Galactic Centre, extinction can exceed **30 magnitudes in the optical**, allowing fewer than one optical photon in 10¹² to pass through, producing the *zone of avoidance* in which background galaxies such as Dwingeloo 1 stayed hidden until radio and infrared surveys revealed them.

The general extinction curve rises steeply into the ultraviolet. It is parameterised by

$$R_V = \frac{A_V}{E_{B-V}},$$

the total V-band extinction divided by the colour excess. $R_V$ tracks the average size of dust grains along the sight line; the Milky Way's typical value is **3.1**, varying with direction. Beyond the near-infrared the curve becomes hard to measure, so extinction corrections shift to longer-wavelength bands (such as $K_s$) where both the amount and variability of extinction are smaller. Empirically, gas and dust track each other: in the Milky Way, $N_H/A_V \approx 1.8 \times 10^{21}$ atoms cm⁻² mag⁻¹.

## Measuring extinction

For a star, astronomers compare its spectrum to that of a similar, unreddened star (or occasionally to a theoretical model) and attribute the differences to dust. For an emission nebula, intrinsic line ratios are predictable, so comparing two hydrogen recombination lines gives extinction directly. The intrinsic Hα/Hβ ratio is about **2.85** under a wide range of nebular conditions, so any deviation from 2.85 must come from intervening dust.

## The 2175-Å bump and galaxy-to-galaxy variation

A broad absorption feature appears at about **2175 Å** in many Milky Way sight lines. First observed in the 1960s, its carrier is unidentified, with candidates including graphitic grains and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) molecules; interplanetary dust particles carrying this feature suggest organic carbon and amorphous silicates as components.

The extinction curve is not universal. In the **Large Magellanic Cloud** (LMC), the 2175-Å bump is weaker and the far-ultraviolet extinction stronger, especially toward the LMC2 supershell near 30 Doradus. In the **Small Magellanic Cloud** (SMC), the bump is essentially absent in the star-forming Bar but appears in the quiescent Wing. Initially these differences were attributed to metallicity (LMC about 40%, SMC about 10% of the Milky Way's value), but the discovery of Milky Way sight lines resembling LMC and SMC curves, combined with the absence of the bump in starburst galaxies, points instead to dust processing by nearby star formation. The dust lies mainly along spiral arms, consistent with other spiral galaxies.

## Atmospheric extinction

Atmospheric extinction gives the rising or setting Sun its orange-red colour. It is smallest at the zenith and greatest near the horizon, so an object's altitude and the corresponding air mass are the main geometric variables; a dry site reduces infrared extinction. Observatories characterise their local atmospheric extinction curve and apply corrections, but for many wavelength regimes only satellite observatories can bypass it.
