# Astrophysics

Astrophysics is the branch of science that applies the methods of physics and chemistry to understand what astronomical objects are, how they work, and how they change. Where traditional astronomy, called celestial mechanics, asks where objects are and how they move, astrophysics asks what they are made of, what powers them, and how they evolve. The same physics also underlies the plotting of spacecraft trajectories.

The discipline studies an enormous range of objects: the Sun, other stars, galaxies, extrasolar planets, the diffuse gas and dust between stars (the interstellar medium), and the cosmic microwave background, the faint, redshifted relic radiation from the early universe. Its targets also include dark matter, dark energy, black holes, and the origin and ultimate fate of the universe. Because the subject is so wide, astrophysicists draw on nearly every corner of physics: classical mechanics, electromagnetism, statistical mechanics, thermodynamics, quantum mechanics, relativity, nuclear and particle physics, and atomic and molecular physics.

## How the field began

For most of history the heavens were treated as fundamentally different from the Earth. Aristotle held the sky to be made of a special substance called aether, whose only motion was circular, while Earthly matter grew, decayed, and moved in straight lines. During the seventeenth century Galileo, Descartes, and Newton argued that the same natural laws govern both realms, but the tools to prove it did not yet exist.

The breakthrough came from light. In the early nineteenth century William Hyde Wollaston and Joseph von Fraunhofer independently noticed that the Sun's spectrum was crossed by dark lines where specific colors were missing. By 1860 the physicist Gustav Kirchhoff and the chemist Robert Bunsen showed that those dark lines matched bright emission lines of known gases, and Kirchhoff deduced that the Sun's atmosphere absorbs light at the same wavelengths that each element emits. Stars, it turned out, are made of the same chemical elements as the Earth.

A direct consequence was the discovery of helium. In 1868 Norman Lockyer, working with chemist Edward Frankland, found a bright yellow line in the solar spectrum that matched no known element. He proposed a new one and named it helium, after the Greek sun god Helios. The element was only isolated on Earth decades later.

By 1885 Edward C. Pickering at Harvard had launched a large program of classifying stellar spectra on photographic plates. A team of women computers, notably Williamina Fleming, Antonia Maury, and Annie Jump Cannon, sorted stars into spectral types. By 1924 Cannon had expanded the catalog to nine volumes covering over a quarter of a million stars, producing the Harvard Classification Scheme adopted worldwide in 1922. Around 1920 Arthur Eddington, using the newly popular Hertzsprung–Russell diagram (a scatter plot of stellar luminosity against temperature), proposed in *The Internal Constitution of the Stars* that stars shine by fusing hydrogen into helium. He appealed to Einstein's E = mc² to explain the enormous energy released, a remarkable guess at a time when the structure of the atomic nucleus and the very composition of stars were still unknown.

The payoff came in 1925, when Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin, in a doctoral thesis at Radcliffe College, applied Meghnad Saha's ionization theory to stellar atmospheres and concluded that hydrogen and helium are the dominant constituents of stars, contradicting the assumption that stars resemble the Earth. The conclusion was so unexpected that her dissertation readers, including Henry Norris Russell, persuaded her to soften it before publication; later research proved her right. By the late twentieth century spectroscopy had been extended across the entire electromagnetic spectrum, and the twenty-first century added gravitational waves as a new observational channel.

## How astrophysicists observe

Observational astrophysics is the practice of gathering light, and a few other signals, from space. Most observations use the electromagnetic spectrum, divided by wavelength into regions that require very different instruments.

Radio astronomy detects long-wavelength radiation from cold gas, dust, and pulsars, and studies the cosmic microwave background using large radio dishes. Infrared astronomy catches cooler objects such as planets and dusty star-forming regions using optical-style mirrors. Optical astronomy, the oldest kind, is dominated by stars and the chemical spectra of galaxies and nebulae, with charge-coupled devices and spectroscopes as workhorse instruments; the blurring of light by the Earth's atmosphere is countered with adaptive optics and space telescopes. Ultraviolet, X-ray, and gamma-ray astronomy probe the most energetic sources, including binary pulsars, black holes, and magnetars; because these wavelengths are absorbed by the atmosphere, they require space observatories such as RXTE, Chandra, and the Compton Gamma Ray Observatory, or ground-based imaging atmospheric Cherenkov telescopes such as H.E.S.S. and MAGIC that catch the brief optical flashes made when gamma rays hit the air.

Other messengers include gravitational waves, which are extremely difficult to detect, and neutrinos, used mainly to study the Sun. Cosmic rays, very high-energy particles from space, can be observed hitting the upper atmosphere. Observations also differ in timescale: optical work usually samples minutes to hours, while radio studies of millisecond pulsars resolve events on millisecond scales, and historical records span centuries.

The Sun holds a special place because it is the only star close enough to study in fine detail, so it serves as the reference point for understanding all others. Stellar evolution, the life cycle of stars, is read off the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram, which can be followed from birth to destruction.

## How astrophysicists theorize

Theoretical astrophysicists build mathematical and computational models and compare their predictions with observation. Analytic models, such as polytropes, are simplified treatments of stellar structure that give physical insight. Numerical simulations handle complex situations such as galaxy collisions or supernova explosions and can reveal phenomena too tangled for closed-form analysis. When a model fails, the usual practice is to modify it minimally; if enough data contradict it, the model is abandoned.

Modern theoretical work includes stellar dynamics and evolution, galaxy formation and evolution, magnetohydrodynamics, the large-scale structure of matter in the universe, the origin of cosmic rays, general relativity, and physical cosmology. The Lambda-CDM model, the standard framework of Big Bang cosmology, incorporates cosmic inflation, dark matter, and dark energy. Relativistic astrophysics is the essential tool wherever gravity dominates, from black holes to gravitational waves. Closely related fields are astroparticle physics, which studies particles from space, and astrochemistry, which studies molecules in interstellar space.
