# Solar System

The Solar System is the Sun and everything held in orbit around it by gravity, from the smallest dust grains to the largest gas giants, extending outward for more than two thousand billion kilometers. Formed about 4.568 billion years ago, it consists of four rocky planets close to the Sun, four giant planets farther out, dwarf planets, moons, and swarms of small icy and rocky bodies, all tracing nearly circular paths in roughly the same plane.

The Sun holds 99.86 percent of all the mass. The four giant planets contain 99 percent of what remains, with Jupiter and Saturn alone accounting for more than 90 percent of that. Everything else, all terrestrial planets, moons, asteroids, and comets combined, amounts to less than 0.002 percent of the total.

## How the Solar System Formed

The system began as a cold, rotating region inside a large molecular cloud, likely several light-years across, that collapsed under its own gravity. As the cloud contracted, conservation of angular momentum, the principle that a spinning system spins faster as it shrinks, caused it to flatten into a protoplanetary disk roughly 200 AU across, with a hot protostar at the center. An AU, or astronomical unit, is the Earth–Sun distance, the standard ruler for the system.

Within about 50 million years, the protostar grew hot and dense enough to begin thermonuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium, becoming a main-sequence star. In the spinning disk, dust and gas collided and stuck together, building larger and larger bodies through accretion. Hundreds of protoplanets formed, but most merged, were destroyed, or were flung out of the system.

Temperature in the disk set the composition of the planets. Close to the Sun, beyond the soot line, temperatures stayed too high for hydrocarbon molecules to survive, so inner planets formed from rocky material with high melting points. Farther out, beyond the frost line at about 5 AU, it was cold enough for water, ammonia, and methane to remain as ice. These volatile compounds let cores grow large enough to capture massive envelopes of hydrogen and helium, creating the gas and ice giants. This is why inner planets are small and rocky while outer planets are enormous and gaseous.

## Structure of the System

The inner Solar System contains Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars, the terrestrial planets, dense rocky worlds with few or no moons. The asteroid belt lies between Mars and Jupiter, holding tens of thousands of objects larger than a kilometer; its total mass is less than a thousandth of Earth's. The dwarf planet Ceres orbits there.

Beyond the belt, the outer Solar System contains the four giant planets. Jupiter is the largest, with 115 confirmed satellites. Saturn has 293 confirmed satellites and a famous ring system. Uranus rotates on its side, with axial tilt greater than 90 degrees, likely from an ancient collision. Neptune, the farthest planet, has 16 confirmed satellites, including Triton, which orbits backward and shows nitrogen geysers, evidence of internal geological activity.

Past Neptune lies the Kuiper belt, from about 30 to 50 AU, holding more than 100,000 icy objects larger than 50 km, including the dwarf planets Pluto, Haumea, Makemake, and Quaoar. Beyond it, the scattered disc stretches to nearly 500 AU, home to Eris and Gonggong, while extreme trans-Neptunian objects like Sedna follow orbits reaching hundreds of AU. Theorized to extend from roughly 2,000 AU to 200,000 AU, the Oort cloud is a vast spherical shell of icy bodies, the source of long-period comets that occasionally fall inward toward the Sun.

## Notable Worlds

Mercury is the smallest planet, with no atmosphere and surface temperatures swinging from −170 °C to 420 °C between night and day. Venus has a carbon dioxide atmosphere about ninety times denser than Earth's sea-level pressure, producing surface temperatures above 400 °C through greenhouse heating. Earth is the only known world with life and surface liquid water, with an atmosphere of 78 percent nitrogen and 21 percent oxygen. Mars has a thin carbon dioxide atmosphere and two small captured moons, Phobos and Deimos. Mars lost its magnetosphere about 4 billion years ago, leaving its surface exposed to solar wind.

## Future of the Sun

The Sun has been a stable main-sequence star for about 4.568 billion years and has roughly 5 billion years of hydrogen fusion remaining. When its core hydrogen runs out, it will swell into a red giant about 260 times its current diameter, hot enough to vaporize Mercury and Venus and to make Earth and Mars uninhabitable. It will then shed its outer layers and collapse into a dense white dwarf about half its original mass but compressed to roughly the size of Earth.

## Discovery and Comparison

In 1543, Copernicus proposed a mathematically predictive heliocentric system. In 1687, Newton showed that the same laws of motion and gravity governing falling objects on Earth also govern planets and moons, unifying celestial and terrestrial physics. In 1838, Bessel measured stellar parallax, the apparent shift of a nearby star against the background as Earth orbits the Sun, the first direct proof of Earth's motion around the Sun. By 1989, all eight planets had been visited by space probes.

Compared with other planetary systems, the Solar System is unusually orderly. About 37 percent of known systems are "ordered" like ours, meaning planet masses increase with distance from the star; 59 percent are "similar" and 4 percent "mixed." Our system lacks super-Earths and lacks any planet orbiting closer to the Sun than Mercury, and it has unusually small rocky planets and unusually large gas giants, with no intermediate sizes. Whether the near-circular shape of our planets' orbits reflects a real formation advantage or a detection bias remains open.

Open questions include whether the clustering of some extreme trans-Neptunian orbits points to a hypothetical Planet Nine, whether the Solar System passes through the Local Interstellar Cloud or just outside it, and what shape the outer edge of the heliosphere, the bubble the Sun carves in interstellar space through its solar wind, actually takes. The model to keep is simple: a vast spinning disk of gas and dust collapsed around a young star, and the disk's temperature at each distance dictated the composition, size, and character of every world that formed there.
