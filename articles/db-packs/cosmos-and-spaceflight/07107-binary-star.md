# Binary star

A binary star is a pair of stars bound by gravity that orbit a shared center of mass, the barycenter. Roughly one third of the star systems in the Milky Way are binary or multiple, and the fraction rises with stellar mass, from about 22% for red dwarfs below 0.1 solar masses to over 80% for stars above 16 solar masses. Binaries matter because they let astronomers do something almost impossible for isolated stars: directly measure stellar masses, radii, and densities by watching two bodies move under their mutual gravity. For most other stars, mass is inferred indirectly from light.

## How binaries are observed

Astronomers classify a pair by how it is detected, and the same system can fall into several classes.

**Visual binaries** are wide enough that a telescope, or high-power binoculars, separates the two stars. The brighter star is the *primary*, the dimmer the *secondary* (called a *comes* in older texts). By plotting the secondary's position relative to the primary over decades, observers trace the orbit projected onto the sky and solve Kepler's laws for the ellipse. Many visual binaries have periods of centuries or more, so only a curved arc of the orbit has been seen.

**Spectroscopic binaries** are too close to resolve visually, so they reveal themselves through the Doppler shift of their spectral lines. As each star alternately moves toward and away from Earth, its absorption lines oscillate between blue and red with the orbital period. If lines from both stars are visible the system is *SB2*; if only one set shifts, it is *SB1*. The radial-velocity curve is a sine wave for a circular orbit and a skewed curve for an eccentric one. The product *a sin i* (semi-major axis times the sine of the orbital inclination) is measurable, but the individual values of *a* and *i* are not unless another method is available.

**Eclipsing binaries** sit nearly edge-on to our line of sight, so the stars periodically pass in front of each other and the combined light dips. A light curve shows flat maxima with two minima per orbit, a deeper *primary eclipse* (usually the hotter star being occulted) and a shallower *secondary eclipse*. The shape of the dips encodes the relative sizes and temperatures of the two stars. When an eclipsing binary is also spectroscopic, the full set of orbital elements is known and individual masses, radii, and densities can be derived. Since about 1995, 8-meter-class telescopes have applied this technique to eclipsing binaries in the Magellanic Clouds, Andromeda, and the Triangulum Galaxy, giving direct distance estimates accurate to about 5%, more accurate than standard-candle methods.

**Astrometric binaries** show a visible star wobbling around a point in empty space. The unseen companion can be a faint star, a neutron star, or a black hole, and its mass is inferred from the wobble. The technique works best on nearby stars, within about 10 parsecs, and is the same method used to find extrasolar planets.

## How binaries are configured

Separation matters physically as well as observationally. The boundary is set by the *Roche lobe*, the region around each star within which its own gravity dominates. In a **detached** binary both stars sit inside their Roche lobes and evolve almost independently, the most common situation. In a **semidetached** binary one star fills its lobe and pours gas onto the other, often through an *accretion disk*. In a **contact** binary both stars overfill their lobes and share a common envelope; friction in that envelope shrinks the orbit until the two stars merge. W Ursae Majoris is the textbook example.

## How binaries change each other

A star expands as it ages, and if it grows past its Roche lobe, gas streams across the *first Lagrangian point* to the companion. This *Roche-lobe overflow* drives some of the most energetic binary phenomena.

**Cataclysmic variables** are white dwarfs accreting from a companion. Hydrogen piling on the white dwarf's surface ignites in a thermonuclear flash that blows the unburned gas away as a *nova*. If enough mass accumulates to push the white dwarf past the Chandrasekhar limit, the entire object detonates as a *type Ia supernova*. **X-ray binaries** work the same way with a neutron star or black hole as the accretor; the infalling gas releases gravitational energy that heats to millions of kelvins and radiates in X-rays. The unseen companion of Cygnus X-1 weighs about nine solar masses, well above the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff limit for a neutron star, making it the first widely accepted stellar-mass black hole. Mergers of compact binaries, two neutron stars, two black holes, or one of each, are sources of gravitational waves.

The *Algol paradox* shows how mass transfer scrambles the expected order of stellar evolution. Algol A and Algol B formed together, yet the originally more massive star, now the dimmer Algol B, evolved off the main sequence first, filled its Roche lobe, and dumped most of its mass onto its companion, which is now the brighter main-sequence Algol A. The "evolved" star is the lower-mass one.

## Brief history

Double stars were first noticed through telescopes in the 17th century, with Mizar resolved in 1650 and Acrux in 1685. In 1767 John Michell applied statistics to the Pleiades and showed the chance of so many close pairs by random alignment was about one in half a million, the first evidence that these pairs are physically bound. William Herschel, observing from 1779, catalogued about 700 pairs hoping to measure parallax; by 1803 he realized the slow relative motions were actually orbital, and in 1802 he coined the term "binary" for stars revolving around a common center of mass. The first computed binary orbit was Félix Savary's 1827 solution for Xi Ursae Majoris. The Washington Double Star Catalog, maintained by the US Naval Observatory, now lists more than 100,000 pairs, though full orbits are known for only a few thousand. About 40 systems are rare enough to be both visual and spectroscopic binaries, and these are the richest sources of stellar parameters.

## Planets around binaries

Planets in binary systems are rarer than around single stars. Kepler found planets around most Sun-like single stars but only about a third of comparable binaries, because a companion's gravity tends to disrupt the disks from which protoplanets form. Planets that orbit just one component sit on *S-type* orbits; those that orbit the whole pair are *P-type*, or circumbinary. Simulations suggest 50 to 60% of binary systems could in principle support a stable habitable zone, but the gravitational environment is delicate and easy to destabilize.
