# Spaceflight

Spaceflight moves crewed or uncrewed vehicles into and through outer space using rocket propulsion, orbital mechanics, and managed launch, transit, and reentry phases.

The theoretical foundation is the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation, published in 1903. It relates delta-v (the change in velocity a spacecraft needs for a maneuver) to exhaust velocity (how fast propellant leaves the engine) and mass ratio (the ratio of a rocket's initial mass to its final mass after propellant is burned). Higher exhaust velocity or a larger mass fraction yields more delta-v for the same propellant. Robert Goddard's 1919 work on liquid-fuel rockets and the de Laval nozzle (a converging-diverging nozzle that accelerates exhaust to supersonic speeds) made practical spaceflight possible. The V-2 rocket, developed by Nazi Germany, became the first human-made object to reach space in June 1944. After the war, captured German engineers, including Wernher von Braun, advanced U.S. missile and launcher programs. The Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1 in 1957 using an R-7 booster, the first artificial satellite. The U.S. responded with Explorer 1 in 1958, beginning the Space Race. Yuri Gagarin orbited Earth on Vostok 1 in 1961, and the Apollo program landed humans on the Moon between 1969 and 1972.

A launch starts at a spaceport with a multistage rocket, a vehicle whose lower stages drop away in flight as their propellant is exhausted, shedding dead weight so the upper stage accelerates more efficiently. The Kármán line, at 100 km altitude, is the conventional boundary of space. Reaching orbit requires high tangential velocity, meaning speed parallel to Earth's surface, rather than just climbing straight up. From low Earth orbit, missions to the Moon or planets may use parking orbits (temporary waiting orbits from which a spacecraft later departs) or direct injection (a single burn that sends the spacecraft onto its final trajectory without first circling in a parking orbit). Rendezvous and docking let crewed vehicles reach space stations. Reentry exposes vehicles to intense heat from atmospheric compression; blunt-body designs, shaped so that the spacecraft's broad bottom faces the airflow, push the shock wave away from the vehicle and dissipate heat into the surrounding plasma, a strategy derived from Harry Julian Allen's 1950s work.

Spaceflight spans suborbital trajectories (brief flights that reach space but do not complete an orbit), orbital flight, interplanetary missions, and proposed interstellar travel. Crewed programs have included Vostok, Apollo, Soyuz, the Space Shuttle, and Shenzhou. Uncrewed missions include communications and Earth-observation satellites, planetary probes, and deep-space explorers such as Voyager and New Horizons. Point-to-point suborbital transport, using a suborbital trajectory to cross between two distant points on Earth in under an hour, has been proposed for rapid travel.

Rockets dominate propulsion, while ion drives (engines that accelerate a gas such as xenon with electric fields for high efficiency but low thrust) and solar sails (thin reflective sheets pushed by the pressure of sunlight) offer higher efficiency for long-duration, low-thrust missions. Launch systems are either expendable, such as the Falcon 9 first stage on missions where it is not recovered, or reusable. SpaceX first landed a reusable orbital-class booster in December 2015, and the Space Shuttle and Starship also reuse major hardware. Reusability lowers the marginal cost per launch.

Spaceflight faces safety risks from launch failures and the space environment. In orbit, apparent weightlessness from continuous free fall causes bone loss and muscle atrophy. The Van Allen belts, two donut-shaped zones of charged particles trapped by Earth's magnetic field, and cosmic rays (high-energy particles originating outside the Solar System) raise long-term radiation risks for crew. Life-support systems, often called ECLSS, recycle air and water, manage waste, and regulate temperature. Space weather, driven by solar activity such as flares and coronal mass ejections (large eruptions of plasma from the Sun's outer atmosphere), can disrupt satellites and accelerate orbital decay (the gradual loss of altitude from atmospheric drag). Rocket exhaust contributes to atmospheric pollution, and orbital debris is a growing concern. Kessler syndrome describes a cascade in which one collision creates fragments that trigger further collisions, potentially rendering entire orbital regions unusable. The 1967 Outer Space Treaty, ratified by most spacefaring nations, sets the basic legal framework for national responsibility, peaceful use, and non-appropriation of celestial bodies.

Applications include Earth observation, communications, navigation, scientific research, and space tourism. Russia, the United States, and China have independent crewed launch capability; many other nations operate uncrewed missions. The United Nations Office for Outer Space Affairs (UNOOSA) coordinates international cooperation on space activity.
