# Gravitational-wave astronomy

Gravitational-wave astronomy observes the universe through ripples in spacetime produced when massive objects accelerate violently. These waves propagate outward at the speed of light and interact only weakly with matter, so they travel through regions that scatter or absorb light, such as nebulae, dust near galactic cores, and the interiors of exploding stars. That same weak interaction makes them extraordinarily faint.

## What the waves are and where they come from

Gravitational waves are fluctuations of the gravitational field, analogous to electromagnetic waves but far weaker. Their main sources are tight binaries of compact objects (white dwarfs, neutron stars, or black holes) spiraling together and merging, along with supernovae and processes in the early universe shortly after the Big Bang. To produce a detectable signal, the source must involve extremely massive objects moving at a significant fraction of the speed of light.

## From prediction to first detection

Oliver Heaviside suggested gravitational waves in 1893 and Henri Poincaré in 1905. Albert Einstein derived them from general relativity in 1916, though he later briefly doubted them. Indirect evidence came in 1974, when Russell Hulse and Joseph Taylor observed a binary pulsar whose orbit decayed exactly as general relativity predicts when energy is lost to gravitational radiation, work honoured by the 1993 Nobel Prize in Physics. Direct detection came on 11 February 2016, when the twin LIGO interferometers in Hanford, Washington, and Livingston, Louisiana recorded the first signal: the merger of two stellar-mass black holes about 1.3 billion light-years away. The 2017 Nobel Prize went to Rainer Weiss, Kip Thorne, and Barry Barish. Virgo in Italy and KAGRA in Japan later joined joint observation runs, and detection by three or more detectors makes it possible to triangulate a source's sky position.

## How the detectors work

Modern observatories use laser interferometry, splitting a laser beam along two perpendicular arms several kilometres long. A passing gravitational wave slightly stretches one arm while squeezing the other, and the interference pattern reveals the difference. Three frequency regimes exist, each with its own technique and sources.

| Frequency band | Technique | Key sources |
|---|---|---|
| High (about 10–10000 Hz) | Ground-based interferometers: LIGO, Virgo, KAGRA, plus GEO600 for testing | Binary black holes, binary neutron stars, supernovae, rotating neutron stars |
| Low (millihertz) | Future space-based interferometers, notably LISA (ESA L3, 2030s) and DECIGO | White-dwarf binaries, extreme-mass-ratio inspirals, supermassive black hole mergers |
| Nanohertz | Pulsar timing arrays (EPTA, NANOGrav, PPTA, the Chinese Pulsar Timing Array, together the International Pulsar Timing Array) | Stochastic background from supermassive black hole binaries |

Pulsar timing arrays track many pulsars over years, looking for correlated timing shifts produced by very long gravitational waves. In June 2023, four independent PTA collaborations reported the first evidence for a stochastic nanohertz gravitational-wave background, each measuring the characteristic Hellings–Downs quadrupolar correlation pattern that distinguishes gravitational waves from local noise. Supermassive black hole binaries in merging galaxies are the leading candidate source.

## Why the waves matter

Gravitational-wave observations complement electromagnetic ones in multi-messenger astronomy, combining, for example, gravitational and light signals from the same neutron-star merger to reconstruct the event. Because the early universe was opaque to light before recombination but transparent to gravitational waves, a background of primordial waves could in principle reveal conditions during inflation in the first roughly 10⁻³⁶ seconds, with an imprint on the polarisation of the cosmic microwave background. Extreme-mass-ratio inspirals, a small compact object spiraling into a supermassive black hole, can be tracked over many orbits, providing precision tests of general relativity in strong-field gravity. Wave detections also constrain the Hubble constant, the distribution of dark matter and dark energy, and physics beyond the Standard Model.

## Limits and outlook

Seismic noise, finite arm length set by Earth's curvature, and the inherent weakness of the signals cap ground-based sensitivity. Even supernova explosions produce strains as small as an atomic diameter, requiring detectors calibrated to noise levels near 2 × 10⁻²² Hz⁻¹ᐟ². Localising a source on the sky is hard because detectors monitor the whole sky rather than pointing like telescopes; gravitational lensing combined with machine-learning triangulation may eventually sharpen localisation. The field's next stages are upgraded ground detectors, the space-based LISA mission adopted by ESA for launch in the 2030s, and continued pulsar timing, extending gravitational-wave observations across roughly twenty orders of magnitude in frequency.
