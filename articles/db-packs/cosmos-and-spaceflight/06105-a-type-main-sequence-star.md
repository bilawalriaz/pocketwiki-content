# A-type main-sequence star

An A-type main-sequence star is a core-hydrogen-burning star of spectral type A and luminosity class V. These stars sit in the middle of the Morgan-Keenan spectral classification, defined by strong hydrogen Balmer absorption lines, with surface temperatures of 7,400–9,700 K. They are 1.6–2.3 times the Sun's mass and 8–38 times its luminosity, so they appear white or bluish-white.

## Core properties

A-type dwarfs span subtypes A0 through A9, running from hotter to cooler. An A0V star has an effective temperature of 9,700 K and a luminosity of about 38 L☉, while an A9V star cools to 7,400 K with a luminosity near 8 L☉. The mass range is narrow, yet luminosity varies by nearly a factor of five because luminosity scales steeply with temperature for stars of similar mass.

| Type | Mass (M☉) | Radius (R☉) | Luminosity (L☉) | Temperature (K) |
|------|-----------|-------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| A0V  | 2.33      | 2.19        | 38.37           | 9,700           |
| A5V  | 1.86      | 1.79        | 12.36           | 8,100           |
| A9V  | 1.63      | 1.75        | 8.24            | 7,400           |

Higher mass drives faster fuel consumption, so A-type stars live for roughly a quarter of the Sun's lifetime, typically a few hundred million to about a billion years.

## Internal structure and magnetic activity

A-type stars lack a deep outer convective zone; energy transport near the surface occurs mostly through radiation. The Sun's magnetic dynamo depends on convection coupled with rotation, so without a convective zone A-type stars do not sustain strong magnetic dynamos, lack significant stellar winds, and produce essentially no X-ray emission.

## Spectral standards

The Morgan-Keenan system anchors each subtype to reference stars whose classifications have remained stable over decades. For A-type dwarfs the surviving anchors are Vega (A0V), Phecda (A0V), and Fomalhaut (A3V). The 1973 Morgan and Keenan review provided no standards between A3V and F2V. HD 23886 was suggested as an A5V standard in 1978, and Gray and Garrison added further standards in 1987 and 1989, including HD 45320 (A1V), HD 88955 (A2V), 2 Hydri (A7V), 21 Leonis Minoris (A7V), and 44 Ceti (A9V). No published A6V or A8V standards exist.

## Planets and debris disks

A-type stars are young enough that many still host the dusty debris disks where planets form. These disks emit infrared beyond what the star alone would produce, marking active planet formation. Doppler surveys show that massive planets form commonly around A-type stars: about one in six stars with twice the Sun's mass hosts at least one Jupiter-sized planet, compared to roughly one in sixteen for Sun-like stars.

Detecting these planets through Doppler spectroscopy is hard because A-type stars rotate rapidly, often hundreds of km/s at the equator, which broadens spectral lines and washes out the small shifts an orbiting planet induces. When an A-type star exhausts core hydrogen it expands and cools into a red giant that rotates more slowly, so its radial velocity becomes easier to measure. By early 2011, about 30 Jupiter-class planets had been found around evolved K-type giants, including Pollux, Gamma Cephei, and Iota Draconis. Known A-type systems with planets include HR 8799 (four confirmed planets), Beta Pictoris (three confirmed, with a debris disk extending to about 1,835 AU), and Fomalhaut (one controversial candidate plus an unnamed candidate).

## Nearby examples

A-type stars are common in naked-eye astronomy because they are intrinsically bright. Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, is classified A0mA1 Va with a mass of about 2.06 M☉ and a luminosity near 25 L☉ at 8.6 light-years. Other nearby examples are Vega (A0V, 25 light-years, 47 L☉), Fomalhaut (A3V, 25 light-years, 17 L☉), Altair (A7V, 17 light-years, 11 L☉), and Denebola (A3V, 36 light-years, 13 L☉).
