# Astronomical object

An **astronomical object** is any naturally occurring physical entity, association, or structure that exists in the universe. Astronomy uses *object* and *body* almost interchangeably, but they are not identical. A **body**, also called a celestial or heavenly body, is a single, tightly bound, contiguous physical entity, such as a planet, moon, star, or asteroid. An **object** is a looser term that can also refer to extended, less cohesive structures such as planetary systems, star clusters, nebulae, and entire galaxies, and it may contain other objects. A comet illustrates the overlap: its solid ice-and-dust nucleus qualifies it as a body, while the whole comet with its diffuse coma and tail is treated as an object.

## A short history of how we learned to see them

According to NASA astrophysicists, the first astronomical objects emerged roughly 13.6 billion years ago, about 200 million years after the Big Bang, once gravity could pull enough material together to ignite stars and assemble galaxies. Humans have watched the brighter examples for thousands of years, first as deities, then as practical markers for navigation, seasons, and agriculture.

The modern picture took shape during the Scientific Revolution. In 1543 Nicolaus Copernicus placed the Sun, not the Earth, at the center of the Solar System, and Johannes Kepler soon derived his laws of planetary motion describing how the planets travel around it. In 1584 Giordano Bruno proposed that the distant stars are suns in their own right, an idea no one in Europe had taken seriously for centuries. Galileo Galilei turned a telescope to the sky in 1610, discovering Jupiter's four largest moons, Venus's phases, lunar craters, and sunspots. William Herschel discovered Uranus in 1781, the first planet found that is not visible to the naked eye.

From the 19th century onward, spectroscopy, pioneered by Joseph von Fraunhofer and Angelo Secchi, let astronomers read the chemical composition of stars and nebulae. Photography, photometry, and telescopes sensitive to radio and other invisible wavelengths vastly extended what could be detected. The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram, developed independently in 1913 by Ejnar Hertzsprung and Henry Norris Russell, plots stellar luminosity against surface temperature and remains the standard map of how stars live and die. Edwin Hubble resolved the long debate over whether anything lay beyond the Milky Way by showing that the Andromeda nebula is a separate galaxy, with countless others beside it.

## The hierarchy from the cosmos down to a planet

The universe is built in nested layers. At the largest scale, **galaxies** are the building blocks. They gather into groups and clusters, then into superclusters strung along great filaments that surround enormous voids, producing a web-like structure across the observable universe. Galaxies come in several shapes, including irregular, elliptical, and disk-like, and many grow or change shape by colliding and merging. Disk galaxies include lenticular and spiral types, with spiral arms and a surrounding halo. Most large galaxies host a supermassive black hole at their core, and a fraction of those power an active galactic nucleus. Smaller companion dwarf galaxies and globular clusters often orbit as satellites.

Inside a galaxy, gas clouds collapse under their own gravity to form stars, usually in clusters, and the resulting stars follow evolutionary tracks that depend mainly on their mass, composition, and life stage. Stars can live alone or in multiple-star systems on hierarchical orbits, and the leftover material around a young star can accrete into a planetary system of planets, moons, asteroids, and comets. When a star ends its life, its mass and any companion determine whether it leaves behind a white dwarf, neutron star, or black hole. Stars that pass through the instability strip of the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram, including Cepheids, RR Lyrae, and Delta Scuti variables, rhythmically change brightness, a property that became one of the key tools for measuring distances across the universe.

## Why so many things are round

Gravity pulls matter inward, and once a body has enough mass, its own internal heat lets the material flow until the object settles into a roughly spherical shape called **hydrostatic equilibrium**. For stars, that heat comes mostly from ongoing nuclear fusion; for planets, it comes from accretion and residual heat of formation. The IAU's definition of a planet and a dwarf planet requires this rounded shape for any Sun-orbiting body, the same spheroidal form seen on Mars and on gas giants such as Jupiter. Bodies that have not accumulated enough mass to round out, classified by the IAU as small Solar System bodies, keep lumpy, irregular shapes. A few, such as 4 Vesta, are large enough to have partially differentiated into layers without becoming fully round.

## A practical map of the categories

Astronomical objects are usually sorted by where they sit and how they are structured. Within the Solar System lie the Sun, terrestrial and giant planets, dwarf planets such as Pluto, Eris, Ceres, Makemake, Haumea, Quaoar, Orcus, Gonggong, and Sedna, plus moons, ring systems, asteroids, comets, meteoroids, and distant trans-Neptunian and Kuiper belt populations. Extrasolar objects include exoplanets of many kinds (hot Jupiters, super-Earths, rogue planets, and theoretical types such as ocean or eyeball planets), brown and sub-brown dwarfs, and the full range of stars, from protostars and pre-main-sequence objects through main-sequence, giant, supergiant, and hypergiant phases, down to compact remnants such as white dwarfs, neutron stars (including pulsars and magnetars), and stellar black holes. Beyond single stars lie stellar groupings such as open and globular clusters, and beyond those the galaxies themselves, their groups, clusters, and superclusters, all embedded in interplanetary, interstellar, and intergalactic media and crossed by dark matter, cosmic dust, and the cosmic microwave background.
