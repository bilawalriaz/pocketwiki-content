# Gamma-ray astronomy

Gamma-ray astronomy observes the highest-energy photons in the electromagnetic spectrum. *Photons* are individual packets of light; *gamma rays* are those with energies above about 100 keV (thousand electron-volts), shorter in wavelength than X-rays, which sit just below that threshold. Because no single mechanism produces all cosmic gamma rays, the field samples the most violent environments in the universe, regions of extreme temperature, density, magnetic fields, or particle acceleration, where visible light cannot escape but gamma rays can.

Most of the Milky Way's diffuse gamma-ray glow comes from collisions between high-energy *cosmic rays* (fast charged particles travelling through space) and interstellar hydrogen gas, often through neutral *pion* decay: when a cosmic-ray proton smashes into gas it produces short-lived *pions*, which decay into gamma rays. Other production mechanisms include the *inverse Compton effect* (a fast electron transfers energy to a lower-energy photon on collision), *electron–positron annihilation* (the electron meets its antimatter twin and both vanish into gamma rays), and the radioactive decay of nuclei forged in supernovae, such as cobalt-56 in SN 1987A, whose decay produced the characteristic 847 keV and 1238 keV gamma-ray lines.

These photons reveal objects no other wavelength probes as cleanly. Detected sources include the Crab Nebula, the Vela Pulsar (the brightest steady source so far), neutron stars, black holes, stellar coronas, white dwarfs, supernova remnants, and active galactic nuclei. A galaxy-scale diffuse background traces the plane of the Milky Way, and in 2010 the Fermi telescope revealed two enormous gamma-ray bubbles, each roughly 25,000 light-years across, rising from the galactic centre, possibly the fossil record of past activity by the central black hole or a burst of star formation.

At still higher energies, *gamma-ray bursts* (GRBs) appear as sudden, brief flashes lasting from microseconds to several hundred seconds. GRB 221009A, detected in October 2022 at 2.4 billion light-years' distance in the constellation Sagitta, was the most energetic observed, with individual photons reaching 18 TeV (trillion electron-volts), interpreted as a long burst from a massive star collapsing to a black hole. China's LHAASO observatory has since reported photons of 1.4 PeV (peta-electron-volt, a thousand trillion electron-volts) in 2021 and 2.5 PeV from the Cygnus X region in 2024, defining a class of sources called PeVatrons.

Earth's atmosphere is opaque to gamma rays, so detectors must fly high. Balloons made the first observations in the 1960s; Explorer 11 in 1961 collected under 100 cosmic gamma-ray photons. Solar flares then yielded the first true astrophysical detection, the 2.223 MeV line predicted by Philip Morrison in 1958, emitted when a neutron and proton fuse into deuterium inside the flare. The military Vela satellites of the late 1960s and early 1970s, built to watch for nuclear tests, instead discovered cosmic GRBs. Dedicated satellites followed, most recently Swift (2004), INTEGRAL (2002), AGILE (2007), and Fermi (2008), whose Large Area Telescope catalogued 1,873 sources, 57% of them blazars (galaxies whose central black hole's jet points at Earth).

Above about 30 GeV (giga-electron-volts, billion electron-volts) photons are too rare for small satellites, so observatories detect the *Cherenkov light* (a faint blue flash produced when charged particles travel through air faster than light does in air) from the particle showers these gamma rays trigger in the atmosphere. Ground-based arrays such as H.E.S.S., MAGIC, VERITAS, HAWC, and the Whipple Observatory, which first detected TeV gamma rays from the Crab Nebula in 1989, exploit this technique. The Cherenkov Telescope Array (CTA), planned to be ten times more sensitive, is the next generation.

Gamma-ray images remain coarse, around 6 arcminutes (1/60 of a degree) in the GeV range compared with 0.5 arcseconds for low-energy X-rays, because gamma rays are hard to focus. Silicon 'prism' designs proposed since 2012 aim to sharpen that view, and combining gamma-ray data with gravitational-wave and neutrino detections is now a routine part of tracking neutron-star mergers.
