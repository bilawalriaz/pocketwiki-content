# Chinese astronomy

Chinese astronomy is an observational tradition spanning more than 3,000 years. It differs from Western astronomy in one structural choice: where Hellenistic astronomy mapped the sky along the ecliptic (the Sun's apparent annual path) using zodiac constellations, Chinese astronomy was equatorial, anchored to the north celestial pole, with positions measured by distance from the pole rather than along the Sun's track.

## Origins and the mansion system

Oracle bones from Anyang, dating to King Wu Ding's reign (1250–1192 BCE), already carry Chinese star names later grouped into the twenty-eight lunar mansions (宿, *xiù*). A lacquered box from the Tomb of Marquis Yi of Zeng (433 BCE) is the oldest surviving object clearly showing all 28 mansions on a star map.

Detailed observational records began in the Warring States period (fourth century BCE) and grew rapidly under the Han (202 BCE – 220 CE). The Chinese year is lunisolar, so leap months had to be inserted regularly. The calendar was treated as a political symbol: each new dynasty commissioned one to mark its legitimacy.

## Constellations and catalogues

The sky was organised around the Northern Dipper and the 28 mansions. Most other constellations trace back to two Warring States astrologers, Shi Shen and Gan De, plus a disputed third figure, Wu Xian, sometimes grouped with them as the "Three Schools". Sima Qian's *Shiji* (late 2nd century BCE) listed 90 constellations. Zhang Heng's catalogue of 120 CE listed 124 constellations and about 2,500 stars. By the Three Kingdoms period, Chen Zhuo merged the schools into 283 constellations with 1,464 stars. The Qing *Imperial Astronomical Instruments* (1757) catalogued 3,083 stars.

The oldest printed star map is Su Song's celestial atlas (1092). The Dunhuang map, drawn on paper and uncovered by Marc Aurel Stein in 1907, shows more than 1,350 stars and is dated to 705–710 CE under Emperor Zhongzong of Tang.

## Cosmology

Before Western contact, Chinese thinkers proposed three main models. **Gai Tian** ("canopy heaven"), as described in the *Zhoubi Suanjing*, placed a hemispherical sky over a flat, water-surrounded Earth, with the Sun tracing a seasonally changing circle. **Hun Tian** ("entire sky") kept the geometry but made the sky a full sphere, with seasons explained by a shifting pole. **Xuan Ye** held that the heavens are infinite, bodies float freely, and the speed of each luminary depends on its own nature. Xuan Ye's void cosmology would later clash with the Aristotelian solid-spheres model the Jesuits introduced.

## Eclipses and moonlight

Chinese records include about 1,600 observations of solar and lunar eclipses from 750 BCE. Shi Shen (4th century BCE) knew a solar eclipse is the Moon blocking the Sun and gave instructions for predicting them.

The question of whether the Moon shines by its own light was debated for centuries. Jing Fang (78–37 BCE) argued for reflection: the Moon and planets have shape but no light, and receive it only when the Sun illuminates them. Zhang Heng (78–139 CE) explained lunar eclipses as the Earth blocking sunlight from the Moon, and wrote that the Sun is like fire and the Moon like water, which reflects it. Shen Kuo (1031–1095 CE) used the same reasoning in his *Dream Pool Essays* (1088) to argue celestial bodies are spherical, since the Moon's phases and the round shadow during eclipses are what a reflective ball would produce. He also explained why eclipses do not occur at every conjunction: the ecliptic and the Moon's orbital path are slightly tilted, so the bodies usually miss.

## Instruments

The armillary sphere, a framework of rings representing the celestial equator, ecliptic, and meridian, was assembled in stages during the Han. Geng Shou-chang added a fixed equatorial ring in 52 BCE, Fu An and Jia Kui added an elliptical ring by 84 CE, and Zhang Heng completed the device in 125 CE with horizon and meridian rings. Zhang also built the first water-driven armillary sphere, turned by an inflow clepsydra.

The abridged armilla (*jianyi*), designed by Guo Shoujing in 1276, simplified the design into two perpendicular rings holding a sighting tube. The Gaocheng Observatory, built the same year, shows traces of Islamic design in its gnomon and simplified instrument.

Su Song's astronomical clock tower (1086–1092) combined an armillary sphere, a celestial globe, and a mechanical timekeeper driven by an escapement and the earliest known chain drive. A Jurchen army dismantled the tower in 1127 after capturing Kaifeng, and it was never rebuilt.

## Foreign influences

Indian astronomy reached China with Buddhism after the Eastern Han, with the deepest influence during the Tang (618–907 CE). The *Kaiyuan Zhanjing* (718 CE), compiled by the Indian-born astronomer Gautama Siddha, translated Aryabhata's sine table into Chinese and listed over 800 star names.

Islamic astronomers came to China under the Mongols. Kublai Khan brought Iranian astronomers to Beijing, where an Islamic Astronomical Bureau operated alongside the Chinese one for about four centuries. Jamal ad-Din, formerly at Maragheh, presented seven Persian instruments and the *Wannian Li* calendar in 1267 and directed the Beijing Islamic observatory from 1271. Islamic planetary latitude theory and eclipse prediction, absent from Chinese astronomy at the time, gave the Islamic bureau a strong reputation.

Jesuits brought Western astronomy from the late 16th century. The telescope was first described in Chinese by Manuel Dias in 1615, and Johann Adam Schall von Bell published a Chinese treatise on it in 1626. After the Galileo affair, the Jesuits officially taught Ptolemaic–Aristotelian geocentrism and introduced Tycho Brahe's geoheliocentric model rather than Copernicus. Copernican heliocentrism did not become mainstream in China until Protestant missionaries spread it in the early 19th century. Ferdinand Verbiest redesigned the Peking observatory in 1669, and his 1673 celestial globe was the first Chinese globe to use 360 degrees and to show southern celestial pole stars.

## Imperial astronomy in the Ming

Under the Ming (1368–1644), the traditional Chinese and Muslim Astronomical Bureaus were merged in 1370 into the *Qintianjian*. Astronomer was a hereditary post, with children trained by the Ministry of Rites. The Bureau made calendars, reported celestial abnormalities to the emperor, and chose auspicious dates; most day-to-day work was routine recording of star and planet positions. Punishments for corruption ranged from salary loss to beatings.
