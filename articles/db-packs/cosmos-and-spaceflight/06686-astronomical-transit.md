# Astronomical transit

A transit is the passage of a celestial body directly between a larger body and the observer, so the nearer object moves across the face of the farther one. The term applies only when the nearer object looks smaller than the distant one; if it covers the whole disk, the event is an occultation, such as a total solar eclipse. Transits are rare from any given vantage point because they require a near-perfect alignment of three bodies.

## Transits within the Solar System

The most familiar transits are passages of Mercury and Venus across the Sun. These are called *inferior planets* because their orbits lie inside Earth's. A transit of the Moon across the Sun produces an *annular* solar eclipse, in which the Moon's disk looks smaller than the Sun's and leaves a bright ring; when the Moon looks larger it covers the Sun completely, producing a total solar eclipse. The classification depends on the observer: from Mars, Earth itself can transit the Sun, and from Saturn, Cassini recorded Venus transiting the Sun on 21 December 2012. The first planetary transit photographed from another world was Mercury crossing the Sun, imaged by the Mars rover Curiosity on 3 June 2014. The next opportunity to observe Earth transiting the Sun from Mars will come in 2084.

Satellites can also transit their parent planet. The four Galilean moons of Jupiter (Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto) regularly cross Jupiter's disk as seen from Earth, with their shadows trailing slightly behind because the Sun and Earth are not on the same line. In rare cases one planet can pass in front of another, producing a *mutual planetary transit*.

## Transits beyond the Solar System

The transit method is now the leading way to discover exoplanets. When a planet crosses the face of its host star, it blocks a small fraction of the star's light. Sensitive charge-coupled devices record this dip and produce a *light curve*, a graph of brightness against time. From the depth, shape, and spacing of repeated dips, astronomers infer the planet's size, orbital period, and density. A single dip can be caused by starspots or instrumental noise, so multiple transits at a regular interval are needed to confirm a planet. Several planets around the same star can also be detected through *transit-timing variations* (TTV), small shifts in the predicted mid-transit times caused by the gravitational pulls of the other planets.

The probability that a given planet will transit its star as seen from Earth is

P_transit = (R_star + R_planet) / a

where R_star and R_planet are the stellar and planetary radii and *a* is the orbital semi-major axis. The probability is small for Earth-like systems, so survey telescopes monitor hundreds of thousands of stars. *Hot Jupiters* (large planets on tight orbits) are over-represented in discoveries because their deep, frequent dips are easy to detect, while Earth-sized planets are best sought around *red dwarf* stars, whose small radii make the relative dip larger. HD 209458b, detected in this way, was the first confirmed transiting exoplanet. As of July 2025, 2,782 planets have been confirmed using light curves from the Kepler mission.

## Contact points and timing

A transit is divided into four *contacts*, the moments when the circumference of the smaller disk touches the circumference of the larger at a single point. First contact marks the start of *exterior ingress*; at second contact the small body is fully inside the larger disk (*interior ingress*); at third contact it begins to leave (*interior egress*); and at fourth contact the transit ends (*exterior egress*). A fifth named point, *greatest transit*, occurs midway, when the apparent centres of the two bodies are closest. In the 18th and 19th centuries, observers timed these contacts during transits of Venus to compute the Earth–Sun distance, but the *black drop effect*, an optical smearing near contact, limited the precision. Those same transits also let Lomonosov detect Venus's atmosphere from the refractive halo at ingress and egress.

## Survey missions

The transit method's strength is that it scans wide fields of sky with straightforward photometry, so it has dominated exoplanet discovery for two decades. Ground-based projects such as HATNet and HATSouth (small telescopes spread across multiple continents for continuous coverage), KELT-North and KELT-South (sensitive to flux dips as small as 1%), and WASP have contributed many detections. The Kepler space telescope, operating from 7 March 2009 to 15 November 2018, monitored a 115-square-degree field around Cygnus, Lyra, and Draco. After a reaction-wheel failure in 2013 it continued as K2, shifting fields along the ecliptic roughly every 75 days. Its successor TESS, launched 18 April 2018, surveys strips along right-ascension lines, 27 by 90 degrees each for 27 days, giving nearly full-sky coverage and longer coverage near its rotational poles for planets with longer orbital periods.
