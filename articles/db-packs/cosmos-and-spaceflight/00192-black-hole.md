# Black hole

A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity is so intense that nothing, not even light, can escape once it crosses a boundary called the event horizon. The prediction follows from Einstein's 1915 general relativity, which describes gravity as the curvature of spacetime produced by mass and energy. A black hole is defined by two features: an event horizon, the one-way surface beyond which no signal can reach the outside, and a central singularity, where curvature becomes infinite and the known laws of physics break down.

## How they form and what they are made of

Black holes form wherever mass is compressed past the point at which an escape velocity would need to exceed the speed of light. Stellar-mass black holes, a few to roughly a hundred times the mass of the Sun, arise when massive stars exhaust their fuel and their cores collapse, sometimes through a supernova and sometimes through direct implosion. The lower mass limit is set by the maximum mass a neutron star can support, around two to three solar masses. Supermassive black holes, millions to billions of solar masses, sit at the centers of most large galaxies. Intermediate-mass black holes are a hypothesised, rarely observed class. Primordial black holes, if they exist, would have formed from extreme density fluctuations in the early universe and could span an enormous mass range.

Once formed, a stationary black hole is simple. The no-hair theorem states that it is fully described by just three numbers: its mass, its electric charge, and its angular momentum. Every other detail of what fell in is, in principle, erased.

## Geometry around a black hole

For a non-rotating, uncharged black hole the event horizon sits at the Schwarzschild radius, $r_s = 2GM/c^2$. Real black holes are usually rotating. A rotating (Kerr) black hole has an oblate horizon, an inner Cauchy horizon, and an ergosphere, a region outside the horizon where the dragging of spacetime forces everything to co-rotate. The Penrose process can extract rotational energy from this region, and the Blandford–Znajek mechanism uses magnetic fields threading the horizon to launch the relativistic jets seen from some black holes.

A few key distances structure the surrounding space. The photon sphere at $1.5\,r_s$ for a non-rotating hole is where light itself can orbit, and it sets the apparent size of the black hole's shadow. The innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO), at $3\,r_s$ for Schwarzschild and closer for prograde Kerr orbits, marks the inner edge of a thin accretion disk. Inside the horizon, every possible path leads inward to the singularity, a point in the non-rotating case and a ring in the rotating case, where curvature diverges and general relativity gives way to an unknown quantum-gravitational description.

## How they grow and radiate

Black holes grow by accreting matter and by merging with other black holes. Accretion disks convert gravitational potential energy into radiation with efficiencies of roughly 6–42% of the rest mass, far higher than nuclear fusion, which is why black-hole accretion powers quasars and X-ray binaries. Merging supermassive black holes face a final-parsec problem: after they have eaten or scattered the nearby stars, further inspiral by gravitational wave emission slows, and they need extra interactions with gas, a third black hole, or other perturbers to merge within a Hubble time.

A black hole is not entirely black. Quantum effects at the horizon produce Hawking radiation, a thermal glow whose temperature is inversely proportional to mass. A solar-mass hole has a Hawking temperature near 62 nanokelvin, colder than today's 2.7 K cosmic microwave background, so it currently gains energy from the CMB faster than it radiates. Evaporation matters only for very small black holes, if primordial ones exist at low masses.

## How we know they are real

Black holes were long treated as mathematical curiosities. Skeptics including Einstein and Eddington suspected that realistic pressure or rotation would halt collapse. Oppenheimer and Snyder's 1939 model showed that a pressureless sphere does collapse as seen by an infalling observer, even though an outside watcher sees it appear to freeze at the horizon because of gravitational time dilation. Finkelstein's 1958 re-interpretation of the Schwarzschild surface as a one-way event horizon convinced most theorists.

Observational evidence accumulated in stages. Cygnus X-1, identified between 1971 and 1974 as an X-ray binary whose compact partner exceeded the neutron-star mass limit, became the first widely accepted stellar-mass candidate. Stellar orbits tracked for decades around Sagittarius A* established a mass of about 4.3 million Suns at the Milky Way's center. Water-maser observations of NGC 4258 in 1995 ruled out a dense star-cluster alternative and confirmed a supermassive black hole there. The masses of supermassive black holes correlate tightly with the velocity dispersion of stars in their host-galaxy bulges, a relationship called the M–sigma relation, tying black-hole growth to galaxy evolution. In 2015 LIGO detected gravitational waves from a binary black-hole merger, the first direct observation of black holes in motion. The Event Horizon Telescope then imaged the shadows of M87* in 2019 and Sagittarius A* in 2022, resolving horizon-scale structure for the first time.

## What remains unresolved

The most famous puzzle is the information loss paradox: Hawking radiation looks thermal, so if a black hole evaporates completely, the quantum information that fell in appears to be destroyed, violating unitarity. Proposed resolutions include remnants, firewalls, and holographic ideas such as AdS/CFT. Whether the singularity is replaced by some smooth quantum-gravitational object is unknown. And the question of how billion-solar-mass black holes appeared by redshift 7, when the universe was under a billion years old, drives active research into direct-collapse and super-Eddington seed mechanisms.
