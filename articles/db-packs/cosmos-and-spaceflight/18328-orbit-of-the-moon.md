# Orbit of the Moon

The Moon orbits Earth once every 27.32 days relative to the stars (sidereal month) and once every 29.53 days relative to the Sun (synodic month). At a mean distance of about 384,400 km, or 60 Earth radii, it traces an ellipse slightly tipped from Earth's orbital plane around the Sun. The Sun pulls harder on the Moon than Earth does, more than twice as strongly, yet the Moon stays bound to Earth inside Earth's sphere of influence. From a heliocentric view, the Moon's path around the Sun is therefore Earth-perturbed rather than Earth-centered: it is always convex, never loops backward, and bends continuously toward the Sun because the Moon's 1 km/s speed around Earth is small compared to Earth's 30 km/s around the Sun.

## Shape and distance

The Moon's orbit is an ellipse with a mean eccentricity of 0.0549. Perigee averages 363,300 km and apogee 405,507 km, a spread of about 12% that lets a near-perigee full Moon (a "supermoon") appear up to 12% larger in diameter and 25% brighter than one near apogee. Mean orbital speed is about 1.022 km/s, with a maximum near 1,082 m/s and a minimum near 964 m/s. The lunar plane is inclined about 5.15° to the ecliptic, while Earth's equator is tilted 23.4° from it, so the Moon's path sits close to Earth's orbital plane rather than close to Earth's equatorial plane, unlike most regular satellites.

## Three-body dynamics

The Moon–Earth–Sun system is the oldest three-body problem in astronomy. The Earth–Moon pair shares a barycentre about 4,670 km from Earth's centre, roughly 73% of Earth's radius, putting it inside Earth. Over long timescales tidal friction pushes that barycentre outward as the Moon recedes. Although the Sun dominates the Moon's gravity, the Moon stays trapped within Earth's Hill sphere, whose radius is only about 60,000 km, less than one-sixth of the Earth–Moon distance. This stable capture means the Moon oscillates around the shared heliocentric velocity of 30 km/s without drifting into the Sun or escaping, and without ever reversing direction in its solar orbit.

## Direction and precession

Viewed from the north celestial pole, the Moon orbits Earth anticlockwise, Earth orbits the Sun anticlockwise, and both bodies rotate anticlockwise. Two slow rotations deform the orbit over time. Apsidal precession swings the major axis eastward every 8.85 years (3,232.6 days), while nodal precession drags the line of nodes westward every 18.6 years (19.35° per year). Because the nodes drift, the eclipse season returns every 173.3 days, and the "draconic year" on the Moon (the interval between successive returns of the Sun to the same node) is only 347 days.

The Moon's rotational axis is tilted 6.69° from the perpendicular to its orbital plane. As Cassini noted in 1722, this axis precesses at the same rate as the orbital plane but 180° out of phase, locking the angle between the lunar equator and the ecliptic at a constant 1.54%. The result is latitude libration of about 6.7°, while an observer at Earth's surface sees an additional ~8° of longitude libration from the mismatch between the Moon's elliptical orbital speed and its constant rotation.

| Anomalistic | 27.5546 | perigee |
| Draconic | 27.2122 | ascending node |

The anomalistic month differs from the sidereal because perigee itself advances every 8.85 years; the draconic month differs because the nodes regress every 18.6 years.

## Tidal evolution

Tidal friction transfers angular momentum from Earth's spin to the Earth–Moon orbit. The Moon is currently receding at about 38 mm per year, and Earth's day is lengthening by about 24 microseconds per year, though both figures depend on ocean basin geometry. Tidal rhythmites 620 million years old show the long-term rate was closer to 22 mm per year and 12 microseconds per day. If the oceans were to evaporate in roughly 2.3 billion years, this braking would largely stop; otherwise full tidal locking would arrive near 50 billion years from now, with the Moon showing a single face and a month lasting about 47 current days.

## Inclination and observers on Earth

Earth's 23.4° axial tilt plus the Moon's 5.15° orbital tilt lets the Moon's declination swing from ±18°20′ to ±28°36′ over the 18.6-year nodal cycle. At a minor standstill the Moon rises every day from latitudes below 70°43′; at a major standstill the cap shrinks to 61°27′. Near the poles, this gives two weeks of continuous moonlight each month even during the six-month polar night, light on which Arctic zooplankton rely.

## History of the measurement

Babylonian clay tablets from about 1000 BC contain the earliest systematic records of moonrise, moonset, and the three main lunar periods, and were used to predict future lunar behaviour. Greek astronomers, especially Ptolemy with epicycles and evection, gave the first geometric models. Newton's theory of motion was first tested against the observed lunar motion, and modern lunar theory still refines it through series like ELP2000, which supply the semi-major axis of 384,748 km and other constants used today.
