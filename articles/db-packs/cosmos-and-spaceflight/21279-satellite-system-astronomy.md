# Satellite system (astronomy)

A satellite system is a gravitationally bound set of natural satellites, rings, moonlets, and other small bodies orbiting a single primary, which may be a planet, dwarf planet, asteroid, sub-brown dwarf, rogue planet, or a shared barycenter. Artificial satellites can form secondary systems. Quasi-satellites are excluded because, although the primary's gravity bends their paths, they do not revolve around it.

## In the Solar System

Every giant planet hosts a large, diverse satellite system. Jupiter has 115 known moons including the four Galilean moons discovered in 1609–1610. Saturn has 293 known moons and the Solar System's brightest rings. Neptune and Uranus, each with their own moons and faint rings, complete the pattern. Farther out, Pluto and several Kuiper-belt objects orbit a common centre of mass. Among terrestrial planets, only Earth–Moon and Mars (with two tiny captured moons) qualify; Mercury and Venus have none. Beyond the Solar System, possible exomoons have been reported around Kepler-1625b, and rogue planets ejected from their systems could plausibly retain moons.

## Where satellites can exist

A body's region of gravitational dominance is its Hill sphere; outside it the Sun (or a more massive neighbour) takes over. Neptune and Uranus have the largest Hill spheres because they orbit far from the Sun; Mercury and Ceres have very small ones. The L4 and L5 Lagrangian points, the third corners of equilateral triangles with the two masses, are stable when the larger mass exceeds the smaller by at least about 24.96. Bodies nudged from these points trace small kidney-bean-shaped loops around them rather than escaping.

Most natural moons follow regular, prograde orbits aligned with their planet's spin. Irregular moons travel on tilted or retrograde paths and are thought to have been captured later.

## How satellite systems form

No single mechanism explains every system; major moons are studied case by case, and several processes usually contribute.

**Accretion from a disk.** Gas and dust around a young planet form a circumplanetary (proto-satellite) disk. Moons condense from this material. Across the giant planets, the planet-to-moons mass ratio is close to 10,000:1; Neptune is a notable exception.

**Accretion from debris.** Moons shredded near the Roche limit, the distance inside which tidal forces overcome a body's self-gravity, spread into a ring that can reaccumulate into new moons. Some researchers propose this cycle explains the Galilean moons as a younger generation.

**Giant impact.** Simulations show a Mars-sized impact on the early Earth likely produced the Moon. A similar collision is the leading explanation for Charon and Pluto's smaller moons, and is invoked for the unusual sideways spin of Uranus, where 2018 models favour an oblique strike by a body about twice Earth's size.

**Gravitational capture.** A passing body slowed by an extended atmosphere or three-body interaction can fall into orbit. Capture is the leading theory for Neptune's retrograde Triton, Saturn's Phoebe, and Mars's moons. A tell-tale sign is a retrograde orbit. Capture struggles to account for the Moon, since Earth and lunar rocks share nearly identical isotope ratios.

Temporary captures occur: Jupiter has been observed to hold small objects for years. The longest confirmed capture lasted about twelve years, and comet 111P/Helin-Roman-Crockett is predicted to be captured for roughly eighteen years starting in 2068.

## Features and interactions

**Ring systems** are the most visible feature. Saturn hosts the largest; Jupiter, Uranus, and Neptune also have them, and rings have been found around the dwarf planet Haumea, the centaur 10199 Chariklo (the smallest ringed object known, about 250 km across, with two narrow bands 4–6 km wide), and possibly Saturn's moon Rhea. Some rings appear ancient; others may be transient, repeatedly accreting into moons that break up again at the Roche limit.

**Orbital resonances** lock moon periods into simple integer ratios. Examples include the 1:2:4 Laplace resonance of Io, Europa, and Ganymede, and the 2:4 Tethys–Mimas and 1:2 Dione–Enceladus pairs at Saturn. **Co-orbital** moons such as Janus and Epimetheus at Saturn share nearly the same path, swapping positions every few orbits. **Libration** makes orbiting bodies appear to oscillate relative to each other, prominent in the Earth–Moon system. Binaries, two bodies orbiting their common centre of mass, include Pluto–Charon and asteroid pair 90 Antiope.

**Tidal interactions** deform bodies, generate heat, and slowly change orbits. Io's volcanism comes from tidal flexing during its resonance with Europa and Ganymede. Triton spirals inward toward Neptune and in roughly 3.5 billion years will cross Neptune's Roche limit, crashing or forming a Saturn-like ring. Phobos spirals into Mars on a timescale of about 50 million years, while Earth's Moon recedes by tidal acceleration and may eventually escape Earth's gravitational grip.

**Gas and magnetic coupling** connect moons to primaries and to each other. Io feeds oxygen and sulfur into a plasma torus around Jupiter; Enceladus supplies water to Saturn's E ring; nitrogen may transfer between Pluto and Charon. Jupiter's magnetic field interacts with those of Ganymede and Io, stripping atmospheres from moons and driving intense auroras.

## Habitability

Tidal heating models define a circumplanetary habitable zone where a moon could keep liquid water. For giant planets the most promising band lies between roughly 5 and 20 planetary radii, and host stars need a minimum mass near 0.2 solar masses for moons in their stellar habitable zones to remain stable. The planet's magnetosphere strongly modulates whether such moons can actually retain an atmosphere.
