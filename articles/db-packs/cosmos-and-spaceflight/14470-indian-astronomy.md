# Indian astronomy

Indian astronomy is one of the oldest continuous scientific traditions on the subcontinent, stretching from the Indus Valley Civilisation to modern space missions. It absorbed Greek, Islamic, and European ideas while exporting its own across Eurasia.

## Origins and early development

Astronomy in India grew from ritual needs. The Vedic period (1500 BCE or older) embedded cosmological ideas in its hymns, and the *Rigveda* describes a 360-day solar year with 12 months. The oldest surviving astronomical text, the *Vedanga Jyotisha* (1400–1200 BCE, extant form possibly 700–600 BCE), tracks the Sun and Moon for calendars and sacrifices. It defines a *yuga* of 5 solar years containing 1,830 days, 1,835 sidereal days, 62 synodic months, and 67 lunar sidereal cycles. The *Shulba Sutras*, manuals for altar construction, mix geometry with basic astronomy.

Early astronomers marked time using *nakshatras* (lunar mansions), each spanning 13° 20′ of the ecliptic (the Sun's apparent annual path), with 27 or 28 such divisions. Calendars were built around the lunisolar year, with an intercalary leap month added periodically to keep lunar months aligned with the solar year.

## Greek influence and the classical era

Greek astronomical ideas entered from the 4th century BCE after Alexander's conquests, reaching India through the *Yavanajataka* (3rd century CE) and the *Romaka Siddhanta* (2nd century), a Sanskrit rendering of a Greek text. Ujjain became a centre for this Hellenistic-influenced work.

The classical era began in the 5th–6th centuries with **Aryabhata** (476–550 CE), whose *Aryabhatiya* treated planetary motion as elliptical, argued that Earth's rotation causes the apparent westward drift of the stars, gave Earth's circumference as 24,835 miles (39,967 km), and explained moonlight as reflected sunlight. It is among the earliest works to begin each day at midnight.

**Brahmagupta** (598–668 CE), in *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* (628 CE), argued that bodies with mass are attracted toward the Earth, computed instantaneous planetary motion, gave correct parallax equations, and reinforced the midnight start of the day. Translated into Arabic around 771, the work shaped Islamic astronomy. **Varahamihira** (505 CE) compiled Greek, Egyptian, Roman, and Indian systems in his *Pañcasiddhāntikā*, which approximates the meridian direction from three shadow positions of a gnomon (a vertical rod whose shadow indicates direction and time).

## The Kerala school

Medieval India remained active, especially in the Kerala school of astronomy and mathematics (c. 1380–1632). **Parameshvara** (1380–1460) used eclipse observations to refine Aryabhata's parameters. **Nilakantha Somayaji** (1444–1544) revised Aryabhata's models for Mercury and Venus; his equation of the centre (a correction for a planet's varying orbital speed) remained the most accurate until Kepler in the 17th century. In his *Āryabhaṭīyabhāṣya*, Nilakantha proposed a partly heliocentric model in which Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn orbit the Sun, which in turn orbits the Earth, mathematically more efficient than Tycho Brahe's later Tychonic system.

## Calendars and international transmission

Indian astronomers used several eras: the Kali Yuga epoch (18 February 3102 BCE Julian), the Vikram Samvat (begins 56–57 BCE), the Saka Era (near the vernal equinox of 78 CE), and the Saptarishi calendar (traditionally 3076 BCE). The *sine function*, developed in Indian mathematics, replaced the Greek chord-of-arc method in Arabic astronomy. Muhammad al-Fazari's *Great Sindhind*, based on the *Surya Siddhanta* and Brahmagupta, was translated into Latin in 1126. Indian astronomy also reached China during the Later Han period (25–220 CE), with detailed incorporation during the Tang dynasty (618–907 CE).

## Instruments and observatories

Indian astronomers used the gnomon (*shanku*) for cardinal directions and time, the cross-staff (*yasti-yantra*) for angles, the clepsydra (water clock), and the armillary sphere (a skeletal model of celestial circles, here equatorial rather than ecliptic). The astrolabe (a graduated brass disc used to measure star positions and time) arrived under Firuz Shah Tughluq (1351–1388). In the early 18th century, **Jai Singh II** built five stone observatories (*Jantar Mantars*) at Delhi, Jaipur, Ujjain, Varanasi, and Mathura; the Jaipur *samrat yantra* is the world's largest sundial. Mughal metallurgists in Lahore and Kashmir, including Ali Kashmiri ibn Luqman (1589–90) and Muhammad Salih Tahtawi (1659–60), produced seamless celestial globes using lost-wax casting; 21 survive and were long considered metallurgically impossible to make.

## Modern era

The Madras Observatory (1786), the first modern Indian observatory, helped detect helium in the solar spectrum and evolved into the Kodaikanal Solar Observatory (1899) and the Indian Institute of Astrophysics (1971). The Ooty Radio Telescope (1970) led to the Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope. ISRO's AstroSat (2015) detected a gamma-ray burst in January 2017, and Chandrayaan-3 soft-landed near the lunar south pole on 23 August 2023.
