# Exoplanet

An exoplanet, or extrasolar planet, is a planet outside the Solar System. The first confirmed detection came in 1992, when Aleksander Wolszczan and Dale Frail found two planets orbiting the pulsar PSR B1257+12. The first planet around a main-sequence star (a star like the Sun that fuses hydrogen steadily in its core), 51 Pegasi b, was confirmed in 1995 by Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz, work that earned a share of the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics. As of August 2026,6,354 exoplanets have been confirmed across 4,756 planetary systems, with 1,060 systems hosting more than one planet.

## How astronomers find them

Most exoplanets cannot be photographed directly because a Sun-like star is roughly a billion times brighter than the light reflected from any orbiting planet. Indirect methods dominate the census. Transit photometry measures tiny dips in starlight when a planet crosses the star's face; it favors large planets in tight orbits with edge-on geometry. Radial velocity (Doppler spectroscopy) detects the star's small wobble toward and away from Earth through shifts in its spectral lines, with sensitivity as fine as 1 m/s. Gravitational microlensing captures brightness spikes when a foreground planet's gravity lenses a background star, and works best for planets at 1–10 AU, a region (the Earth–Sun distance) where other methods struggle. Direct imaging blocks the star's glare to capture light from the planet itself, but only for young, massive gas giants on very wide orbits.

Other techniques, including pulsar timing, astrometry, transit-timing variations, and polarimetry, have produced smaller numbers of detections. Pulsar timing made the very first confirmed discovery but has yielded only about five planets, because so few suitable pulsars exist. Every method is biased toward certain kinds of planets, so the census is incomplete.

## What exoplanets are like

Known masses range from about twice the Moon's mass (Draugr) to roughly 30 times Jupiter's mass (HR 2562 b), though the most massive objects shade into brown dwarfs, a category whose boundary is debated. The official IAU (International Astronomical Union) working definition draws the line at 13 Jupiter masses, the threshold above which deuterium (a heavy isotope of hydrogen) fusion becomes possible, but several catalogs use higher cutoffs because this threshold has no sharp physical signature.

Orbital periods span more than ten orders of magnitude, from under an hour for planets skimming their star's surface to thousands of years for those at hundreds of AU. The nearest known exoplanet, Proxima Centauri b, lies 4.2 light-years away. At the other extreme, a candidate planet, M51-ULS-1b, was reported in 2020 inside the Whirlpool Galaxy, suggesting that extragalactic planets exist as well.

Most known planets are gas giants comparable to Jupiter or Neptune, but this is largely a selection effect. Surveys such as Kepler (2009–2018) revealed that small planets between the sizes of Earth and Neptune are common. They also uncovered the "Fulton gap," a scarcity of planets with radii between 1.5 and 2 times Earth's, which hints that two distinct formation pathways produce rocky super-Earths and gaseous sub-Neptunes.

A small fraction, called hot Jupiters, orbit in days or hours at temperatures above 1,000 K. These worlds are thought to have formed farther out and migrated inward, sometimes ending up in orbits opposite their star's spin, a sign that gravitational interactions with other bodies reshaped the system. Direct imaging has shown systems such as HR 8799, where four giant planets trace slow, decades-long orbits. Tidal forces, volcanism, magnetic fields, evaporating atmospheres, and comet-like dust tails have all been observed on exoplanets, so they share many of the same physical processes as the planets in our own Solar System.

## Habitability and the search for life

The habitable zone, sometimes called the "Goldilocks zone," is the orbital range where starlight is strong enough to keep surface water liquid but not so strong that it boils away. About one in five Sun-like stars is estimated to host an Earth-sized planet (1–2 Earth radii) in this zone. Including red dwarfs, the most common stars in the galaxy, the figure rises substantially. Assuming 200 billion stars in the Milky Way, that implies roughly 11 billion potentially habitable Earth-sized planets around Sun-like stars, climbing to about 40 billion once red dwarfs are counted. Rogue planets, ejected from their systems and drifting through interstellar space, may add billions more.

No confirmed exomoon yet exists, though candidates around Kepler-1625b and Kepler-1708b have been reported. The James Webb Space Telescope is now measuring exoplanet atmospheres in detail, characterizing composition, temperature, and cloud structure, with the long-term aim of detecting biosignatures (such as atmospheric oxygen) that cannot be explained by non-biological processes alone.
