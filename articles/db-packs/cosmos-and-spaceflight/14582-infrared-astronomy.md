# Infrared astronomy

Infrared astronomy observes astronomical objects using infrared (IR) radiation, a form of electromagnetic radiation with longer wavelengths than visible light. The infrared band spans 0.75 to 300 micrometers, sitting between visible light (380 to 750 nanometers) and submillimeter waves. Because infrared penetrates dust that blocks visible light and reveals objects too cool or too distant to emit strongly in visible wavelengths, it gives astronomers information unavailable through optical observation alone.

## Why infrared requires different instruments

Near-infrared behaves much like visible light and can be detected with similar solid-state devices, which is why many optical telescopes also operate in the near-infrared. Far-infrared approaches submillimeter wavelengths and needs dedicated instruments.

All infrared detectors share one problem: warm objects emit strongly at infrared wavelengths. A detector itself glows at room temperature, producing noise that would overwhelm faint cosmic signals. For this reason, infrared detectors must be chilled with liquid nitrogen or similar coolants, and the instruments must be shielded from nearby warm surfaces. Coolant supplies run out, so space missions end or shift to "warm" observation when the cryogen is exhausted. Common detector arrays include mercury cadmium telluride (HgCdTe) arrays for0.6–5 micrometer observations, narrow-gap semiconductor detectors, bolometer arrays, and superconducting tunnel junction arrays. Special requirements include very low dark currents, low-noise readout circuits, and high pixel counts.

Atmospheric water vapor absorbs much of the infrared, and the atmosphere itself emits infrared radiation. Ground-based infrared telescopes are therefore built at high altitude in dry locations, including Mauna Kea (4205 m), Paranal (2635 m), and Dome C in Antarctica. Even there, the atmosphere is transparent only in specific infrared windows, wavelength bands where absorption is low. Space avoids both absorption and atmospheric blurring, but space telescopes face the same cooling requirement. Airborne observatories such as SOFIA and the Kuiper Airborne Observatory offer a compromise, flying above most but not all water vapor.

## The infrared spectrum and observing bands

The infrared range divides into near (roughly 0.75–5 micrometers), mid (5–30 micrometers), and far (30–300 micrometers) regions, with standard astronomical bands named R, I, J, H, K, L, M, N, Q, and Z. The J, H, and K bands are the most used from the ground because they fall inside the cleanest atmospheric windows. Space telescopes can observe across the full infrared range, including the mid- and far-infrared where the ground is opaque.

## What infrared astronomy reveals

Because infrared passes through interstellar dust, it reveals embedded star clusters, protostars, and stellar nurseries hidden from optical telescopes. Surveys such as 2MASS and WISE have used infrared to discover many previously unknown clusters. Infrared also detects cool objects such as brown dwarfs and planets, and it captures light from the most distant galaxies, whose visible light has been redshifted into the infrared.

In 2008, astronomers showed that intergalactic dust dims distant galaxies more than expected: galaxies are nearly twice as bright as they appear in visible light, because dust absorbs visible photons and re-emits the energy in the infrared.

## HistoryWilliam Herschel discovered infrared radiation in 1800 by passing sunlight through a prism and finding the strongest heating effect just beyond the red end of the visible spectrum. He called these "calorific rays" and showed they could be reflected, refracted, and absorbed like visible light.

Early infrared observations in the 1830s and through the 19th century struggled against weak detectors. Charles Piazzi Smyth detected infrared radiation from the Moon in 1856 during an expedition to Tenerife. Ernest Fox Nichols attempted to detect infrared from the stars Arcturus and Vega; the flux ratio he reported matches modern values, so George Rieke credits him with the first infrared detection of a star other than the Sun.

The field grew slowly through the early 20th century as Seth Barnes Nicholson and Edison Pettit developed thermopile detectors sensitive to a few hundred stars. Most practitioners were trained as physicists rather than astronomers. The success of radio astronomy in the 1950s and 1960s, combined with improved infrared detectors, brought infrared astronomy into the astronomical mainstream.

Space-based infrared astronomy began with United States Air Force sky surveys using sounding rockets. IRAS made the first all-sky infrared survey in 1983. The Infrared Space Observatory (ISO,1995–1998) discovered protostars and detected water in the universe. NASA's Spitzer Space Telescope (2003–2020) operated in the infrared until it exhausted its liquid helium in 2009, then continued at shorter infrared wavelengths. The James Webb Space Telescope (operating 2022–) is currently the most capable infrared observatory.

## Resolution and interferometry

Angular resolution depends on telescope diameter and observing wavelength, so single infrared telescopes resolve less detail than optical instruments of the same size. To overcome this, infrared telescopes are combined into astronomical interferometers, in which the effective resolution is set by the separation between telescopes rather than by their individual apertures. When used with adaptive optics, interferometers such as the pair of 10-meter Keck telescopes or the four 8.2-meter telescopes of the Very Large Telescope Interferometer achieve much higher resolution than any single dish.
