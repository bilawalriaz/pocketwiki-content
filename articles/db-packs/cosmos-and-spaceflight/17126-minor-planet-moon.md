# Minor-planet moon

A minor-planet moon is a natural satellite orbiting a minor planet (an asteroid, Kuiper belt object, or similar small body). As of May 2026, 625 minor planets are known or suspected to have moons. Tracking these orbits lets astronomers calculate the primary's mass and density, properties that direct imaging of the small body alone cannot reveal.

## Terminology

Systems with one moon are called binary, those with two triple, and the sole known quadruple is 130 Elektra. The larger body is the primary, the smaller the secondary. "Double asteroid" specifically describes pairs of roughly equal size orbiting a barycenter (the shared center of mass of the system) located outside the larger body. A good example is 90 Antiope, where both components are about 87.8 km across, identified as a true binary in August 2000. Very small satellites are sometimes called moonlets.

## Discovery history

Early searches relied on Earth-based observations. In 1978, a stellar occultation (one body blocking a star's light) was claimed as evidence of a satellite around 532 Herculina, but later Hubble imaging found no moon. Also in 1978, Pluto's moon Charon was discovered, though Pluto was still classified as a major planet then.

The first confirmed asteroid moon was Dactyl, found orbiting 243 Ida by the Galileo probe in 1993. The second, Petit-Prince around 45 Eugenia, followed in 1998. In 2001, 617 Patroclus and Menoetius became the first known binary among Jupiter trojans (asteroids sharing Jupiter's orbit around the Sun). The first binary trans-Neptunian object (TNO, an icy body orbiting beyond Neptune) after Pluto–Charon, 1998 WW31, was resolved in 2002.

## Multiple systems and populations

The first triple asteroid, 87 Sylvia with two moons, was recognized in 2005. A second moon was then found around 45 Eugenia, and dwarf planet Haumea was found with two moons. As of 2025, 18 multiple systems are known. Pluto has six bodies total (five moons plus itself); 130 Elektra is the only quadruple.

By orbital class: 86 near-Earth systems, 31 Mars-crossing, 212 main-belt, 6 Jupiter trojans, and 122 TNOs. Roughly 2% of asteroids have satellites; among TNOs the figure reaches about 11%, and all four IAU-listed dwarf planets have at least one moon. Two binaries are known among centaurs (small icy bodies orbiting between Saturn and Neptune): double ring systems around 2060 Chiron and 10199 Chariklo.

## Regional characteristics

Typical properties depend on location. Near-Earth binaries have moons orbiting 3–7 primary radii away, with diameters two to several times smaller than the primary; tidal stresses during close planetary passes likely form many of them. Main-belt moons are usually much smaller than the primary (Antiope being an exception) and orbit roughly 10 primary radii out; many are fragments from a shared collision.

Among TNOs, components are often comparable in size, with orbits 100–1000 primary radii apart, and many are thought to be primordial (formed alongside the primary, not later). The eight largest TNOs with moons are Pluto, Haumea, Makemake, Eris, Quaoar, Gonggong, Orcus, and Salacia. Pluto's Charon has a radius more than half that of Pluto, so the barycenter lies outside Pluto's surface, making it formally a double dwarf planet. Haumea has two moons (Hiʻiaka ~155 km, Namaka ~85 km radius), Makemake one (~160 km diameter), and Eris one (Dysnomia, 150–350 km radius). The TNO 47171 Lempo is unusual: Lempo and its near-equal-mass moon Hiisi orbit closely (~867 km apart), while Paha orbits the pair at ~7411 km. Component separations range from a few hundred km (243 Ida, 3749 Balam) to over 3000 km (379 Huenna) for asteroids, and 3000–50,000 km for TNOs.

## Origin

The origin of minor-planet moons remains uncertain. One leading model is collision: debris knocked off the primary later becomes a companion. This fits close binaries like Pluto–Charon, where angular momentum (the rotational momentum of the orbiting masses) is constrained by mass and separation. More distant pairs with comparable-size components are harder to explain by collision alone unless substantial mass was lost. Gravitational capture is an alternative, and tidal disruption during planetary flybys likely contributes to near-Earth binaries.

A moon's orbit reveals the primary's mass via Kepler's third law (the orbital period squared is proportional to the orbital radius cubed, scaled by total mass), which combined with size yields density, distinguishing rocky from icy composition.
