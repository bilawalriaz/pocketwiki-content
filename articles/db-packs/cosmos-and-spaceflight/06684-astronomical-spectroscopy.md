# Astronomical spectroscopy

Astronomical spectroscopy measures how celestial objects emit, absorb, and reflect light across the electromagnetic spectrum: visible, ultraviolet, infrared, radio, and X-ray. Splitting an object's light into its component wavelengths reveals its chemical composition, temperature, density, mass, distance, motion, and luminosity, often from a single observation. The physics is shared across bands, but the equipment changes with wavelength, because the Earth's atmosphere blocks ultraviolet and X-rays, partially blocks infrared, and passes visible light and most radio waves.

## How a spectrograph works

A prism or diffraction grating splits incoming light into a spectrum. In the early 1800s, Joseph von Fraunhofer used high-purity prisms to record 574 dark lines in the Sun's spectrum. In the early 1900s, J.S. Plaskett developed blazed reflection gratings at the Dominion Observatory; these resolve finer detail than prisms with less light, and tilting them focuses a chosen spectral region. Holographic gratings reach up to 6000 lines/mm (versus about 1000 for blazed) and last for decades. Modern optical spectrographs record dispersed light on charge-coupled devices (CCDs). Gas-discharge lamps of known emission lines calibrate the wavelength scale, and a standard star calibrates the flux scale, a procedure called spectrophotometry.

Radio spectroscopy began in the early 1930s when Karl Jansky, working for Bell Labs, detected radio noise from the centre of the Milky Way. In 1942, J.S. Hey recorded the Sun at radio frequencies. The 1951 discovery of the 21-centimetre neutral hydrogen line opened the field. Radio interferometry, pioneered in 1946, combines signals from multiple antennas; in 1960, Martin Ryle and Antony Hewish introduced aperture synthesis, autocorrelating and Fourier-transforming the signals to produce a three-dimensional image whose third axis is frequency, work for which they shared the 1974 Nobel Prize in Physics.

X-ray and ultraviolet spectroscopy require detectors on rockets or satellites, because ozone and molecular oxygen absorb wavelengths below 300 nm. Infrared spectroscopy depends on satellites for much of its range, since water vapour and carbon dioxide block ground observation, though the optics resemble those of visible-light work.

## What a spectrum reveals

Three basic spectral shapes exist. A hot solid or dense gas produces a continuous spectrum. A hot low-density gas produces emission lines at specific wavelengths. A continuous spectrum passing through a cooler gas produces absorption lines at those same wavelengths, because atoms in the cooler gas absorb them. Kirchhoff and Bunsen established this framework in the 1850s. Fraunhofer's dark lines are absorption lines, and matching them to laboratory spectra identifies the absorbing elements. Over 20,000 absorption lines have been catalogued for the Sun between 293.5 and 877.0 nm, but only about 75% are linked to known elements.

A black body radiates across all wavelengths, with a peak that shifts with temperature. Wien's displacement law, λ_max · T = b (b ≈ 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K), links peak wavelength to temperature: a peak at 502 nm corresponds to 5772 K, the Sun's surface temperature. The Stefan–Boltzmann law, L = 4πR²σT⁴, links luminosity L, temperature T, and radius R. Measuring L and T gives R.

In 1868, Norman Lockyer and Pierre Janssen independently observed a yellow line near the sodium doublet; Lockyer named the new element helium, 27 years before it was isolated on Earth. In 1869, a green coronal line observed during an eclipse was attributed to a phantom "coronium"; in the 1930s, Walter Grotrian and Bengt Edlén showed it came from highly ionised iron (Fe¹³⁺), a state produced by the corona's extreme temperature.

## Motion, distance, and redshift

The Doppler effect shifts spectral lines: recession stretches them toward longer wavelengths, approach shortens them. The relation is (λ − λ₀) / λ₀ = v / c, where positive v is recession. In 1913, Vesto Slipher found the Andromeda Galaxy is blueshifted, while 16 of 20 other galaxies he measured were redshifted. Edwin Hubble combined such velocities with distances to define Hubble's law: v = H₀d, where H₀ is the Hubble constant. Redshift z is defined as (λ_observed − λ_emitted) / λ_emitted, and for small z, z ≈ v/c. The largest known galaxy redshift, z ≈ 12, corresponds to light emitted over 13 billion years ago, when the universe (now ≈13.82 billion years old) was young.

Gravitationally bound systems have peculiar velocities that add to or subtract from the Hubble flow, so observed redshift is the sum of cosmological recession and local motion. In spectroscopic binary stars, two stars orbit too closely to resolve visually, but their composite spectrum's lines shift in opposite directions as they orbit, revealing the orbit. The radial-velocity shift vanishes if the orbital plane is exactly face-on.

## Stars, nebulae, and galaxies

William Huggins showed in 1864 that some nebulae have only emission lines, proving they are gaseous. Bright lines at 495.9 nm and 500.7 nm, once attributed to a phantom element called "nebulium", were explained by Ira Bowen in 1927 as forbidden transitions of doubly ionised oxygen (O²⁺). These transitions are suppressed in laboratories, where collisions dominate at high density, but in a nebula's ~1 atom/cm³ environment, metastable ions decay radiatively via these forbidden lines.

Neutral hydrogen's 21 cm line, produced when the electron flips its spin relative to the proton, is a radio transition that reveals the velocity, density, and temperature of hydrogen clouds. Mapping this line across the sky showed the Milky Way is a spiral galaxy.

Galaxies' combined stellar spectra show Doppler shifts from their stars' motion. In 1937, Fritz Zwicky found that galaxies in clusters orbit faster than their visible mass can explain, inferring the presence of dark matter. In 2003, four galaxies (NGC 821, NGC 3379, NGC 4494, NGC 4697) were found to contain almost no dark matter; the reason remains unknown. Quasars, named by Hong-Yee Chiu in 1964, were identified when strong radio sources showed otherwise unidentifiable spectral lines; the lines turned out to be highly redshifted ordinary galactic spectra, indicating extreme distance and energy output now attributed to supermassive black holes.

## Planets, asteroids, and comets

Planets and asteroids primarily reflect sunlight, but their thermal emission peaks in the infrared, and any atmosphere adds absorption and emission bands. Spectroscopy of over 3,500 exoplanets has detected alkali metals, water vapour, carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, and methane. The Tholen classification (1984) sorts asteroids by spectral type: C-types are carbonaceous, S-types are silicate-rich, and X-types are metallic; the later SMASS scheme expanded this to 26 categories. Comets show a reflected solar spectrum from dust, plus emission lines from gases fluorescing in sunlight, notably cyanogen (CN) and the C₂ and C₃ carbon molecules. Nearby comets also emit X-rays when solar-wind ions are neutralised in the coma.
