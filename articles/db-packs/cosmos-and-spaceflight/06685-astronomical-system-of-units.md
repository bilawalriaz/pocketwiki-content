# Astronomical system of units

The astronomical system of units is a measurement system adopted by the International Astronomical Union (IAU) in 1976 (Resolution No. 1) and significantly updated in 1994 and 2009. It exists because the International System of Units (SI) handles astronomical data poorly: precise positional data for Solar System objects is awkward to express in SI, and through successive revisions the system now explicitly recognises general relativity, which SI does not, yet which is required to treat astronomical observations accurately.

The system is three-dimensional, fixing units of length, mass, and time, and the associated astronomical constants also define the reference frames in which observations are reported. A reference frame is the coordinate system against which positions and motions are measured; the two principal ones are the barycentric celestial reference system (BCRS), centred on the Solar System barycentre, and the geocentric celestial reference system (GCRS), centred on the Earth's centre of mass. Neither the unit of length nor the unit of mass is a true physical constant, and at least three different measures of time are in use, making the system conventional rather than natural.

## Unit of time

The astronomical unit of time is the day, defined as exactly 86 400 seconds. 365.25 days make one Julian year. The symbol is D.

## Unit of mass

The astronomical unit of mass is the solar mass, symbol M☉, equal to 1.988 92 × 10³⁰ kg. The Sun is about 333 000 times more massive than the Earth and 1 048 times more massive than Jupiter, and the solar mass is the standard yardstick for expressing stellar and galactic masses.

In Solar System dynamics, masses enter only through the product GM, where G is the gravitational constant. Historically GM☉ could be measured only to limited precision; its best measured value is 1.327 124 420 99(10) × 10²⁰ m³ s⁻². The IAU now recommends a fixed nominal value, (𝒢ℳ)ᴺ☉ = 1.327 1244 × 10²⁰ m³ s⁻², for use in astrophysics where the uncertainty of measurement would otherwise propagate into every dynamical calculation.

Two auxiliary mass units are common. The Jupiter mass (M_J), 1.898 × 10²⁷ kg, is used for gas giants, exoplanets, and brown dwarfs, with nominal (𝒢ℳ)ᴺ_J = 1.266 8653 × 10¹⁷ m³ s⁻². The Earth mass (M🜨), 5.974 2 × 10²⁴ kg, is used for rocky terrestrial planets up to roughly Neptune-mass bodies, with nominal (𝒢ℳ)ᴺ_E = 3.986 004 × 10¹⁴ m³ s⁻².

## Unit of length

The astronomical unit (au) is now defined as exactly 149 597 870 700 m, approximately the mean Earth–Sun distance. Its old definition made it the length for which the Gaussian gravitational constant k takes the value 0.017 202 098 95 when length, mass, and time are expressed in astronomical units. An equivalent formulation was the radius of an unperturbed Newtonian circular orbit around the Sun of a massless particle with mean motion 0.017 202 098 95 radians per day.

The speed of light used by the IAU is the SI defined value c₀ = 299 792 458 m/s. Under the old definition, the accepted length was 1 au = c₀ τ_A = (149 597 870 700 ± 3) m, where τ_A is the light-transit time across the unit. The au was pinned down by demanding that ephemeris calculations match observations, which in turn fixes τ_A.

## Other distance units and redshift

Different scales suit different regimes: kilometres for satellites, lunar distance for near-Earth objects, astronomical units for planetary distances, parsecs and light-years for nearby stars, kiloparsecs for galactic-scale distances, and megaparsecs for nearby galaxies. Distances to remote galaxies are usually quoted as redshift rather than as a length, because converting redshift to distance requires the Hubble constant, which was not accurately measured until the early 21st century, and because at cosmological distances the curvature of spacetime permits several inequivalent definitions of distance, so a single number does not capture separation.
