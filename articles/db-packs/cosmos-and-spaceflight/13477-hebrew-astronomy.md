# Hebrew astronomy

Hebrew astronomy names a continuous tradition of astronomical writing in Hebrew, by Hebrew speakers, or in Judeo-Arabic (Arabic written in Hebrew script by Jews). Persian and Arabian traditions ascribe its origins to Adam, Seth, and Enoch, and Flavius Josephus writes that Seth and his offspring preserved astronomical knowledge on stone pillars. The tradition runs from the Bible through the Talmud and into the medieval and early modern periods.

## Biblical sources

The Hebrew Bible names only a handful of stars and constellations, and several identifications remain uncertain. *Kesil* is usually Orion; *Kimā* may be the Pleiades, Aldebaran, Arcturus, or Sirius; *ʿĀš* may be the Hyades, Arcturus, Ursa Major, or the Evening Star; *Mezarim* may mean Ursa Major and Minor or, if read as a synonym of *mazzarot*, the planets or zodiac.

Two planets are named explicitly. "Sikkuth and Kiyyun" (Amos 5:26) refers to the Assyrian deities Sakkut and Kayawanu, names for Saturn. Venus appears as the "Queen of Heaven" in Jeremiah 7:18, an identification reinforced by the "bread of Ishtar" cake offerings in Assyria and Babylonia. *Hēlēl*, the "son of the morning" in Isaiah 14:12, is the Morning Star, better known in English by its Latin name Lucifer (literally "light-bearer").

Genesis 1:14 places lights in the heavens to mark seasons, days, years, and festivals, and the seven-branched menorah's seven lamps were taken to correspond to the seven classical planets (Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Sun, Venus, Mercury, Moon). The number seven recurs: the four lunar phases average about 7.4 days each, and Genesis 1:1 contains seven words and twenty-eight letters in Hebrew.

## The Talmud and its cosmology

The Talmud accumulated material over at least four centuries from varied authorities in the Jerusalem and Babylonian compilations, many inclined to mysticism. Astronomy had religious weight: rabbinic writers held that computing the solstice and calendar was a "dessert" of wisdom, and neglecting the courses of the Sun and planets amounted to disregarding the work of God. Most scientific observations came from Samuel of Nehardea, trained in Babylonian schools, who claimed to know the heavens as well as the streets of his city.

Two competing cosmologies sit inside the Talmud. One is a flat Earth picture resembling ancient Near Eastern mythology: the heavens are a firm, lustrous plate two or three fingers thick resting over the Earth, with stars of fire, and the Earth itself floating on water. The other is geocentric, with the stars revolving around the Earth. A Talmudic passage stages a debate:

> The learned of Israel say, "The sphere stands firm, and the planets revolve"; the learned of the nations say, "The sphere moves, and the planets stand firm." The learned of Israel say, "The sun moves by day beneath the firmament, and by night above the firmament"; the learned of the nations say, "The sun moves by day beneath the firmament, and by night beneath the earth."

The Talmud also specifies that the Sun has 365 windows and traverses its yearly course in 12 months, while Jupiter takes 12 years, Saturn 30, and Venus and Mars 480 (though a gloss objects to the last figure). A 28-year solar cycle and a 19-year lunar cycle are mentioned.

The seven classical planets carry Hebrew names derived from their characters: *Shabbatay* (Saturn, "of Shabbat"), *Tzedeq* (Jupiter, "righteousness"), *Ma'adim* (Mars, "the red one"), *Ḥamma* (Sun, "the hot one"), *Noga* (Venus, "the bright one"), *Kokhav* (Mercury, "the star"), and *Levanah* (Moon, "the white one"). Because weekdays were ruled by planets but Jews opposed planetary worship, Hebrew numbers the days rather than naming them; only Shabbat keeps a planetary name.

The zodiac functioned as a calendar scaffold. The twelve signs were paired with the twelve Hebrew months (Aries with Nisan, Taurus with Iyar, and so on through Pisces with Adar) and, by some scholars, with the twelve tribes of Israel. The four solstices (Tekufot of Nisan, Tammuz, Tishrei, and Tevet) fixed the seasons. The Milky Way was called "Fire-Stream" from Daniel 7:10, and Rabbi Joshua ben Hananiah warned that a comet appearing every seventy years could lead mariners astray.

## Medieval Hebrew astronomy

With the Islamic Golden Age, Jews became central translators of Greek and Arabic astronomy. The *Almagest* (Ptolemy's great astronomical treatise) was rendered into Arabic by Sahal ibn Tabari around AD 800; one of the earliest independent Arab astronomers was Mashallah ibn Athari (754–873). Jews contributed to nearly every major table set of the Mediterranean: Sind ben Ali to Caliph al-Mamun's tables (c. 830), twelve Jews to the Tables of Toledo (c. 1080), Isaac ibn Sid to the Alfonsine Tables, and Abraham Zacuto's *Almanac* and *Tables* were carried by Columbus. Abraham bar Ḥiyya wrote the first systematic Hebrew treatise on astronomy at Marseilles around 1134, and Levi ben Gershon (Gersonides) proposed a new planetary system in *Milḥamot Adonai*.

Practical instrumentation flourished: Jacob ben Makir (Profiat Tibbon), apparently professor of astronomy at Montpellier around 1300, invented a quadrant replacing the astrolabe; Levi ben Gershon invented his own instrument; Bonet de Lattes invented an astronomical ring.

## The Renaissance decline

After the Renaissance, Jewish work in astronomy faded as Christian scholars could read Greek sources directly. David Gans of Prague (d. 1613) corresponded with Kepler and Tycho Brahe, knew the Copernican system, but still preferred Ptolemy; David Nieto of London was opposing Copernicus as late as 1714. Later Jewish astronomers include H. Goldschmidt, who discovered 14 asteroids in the nineteenth century, and Moritz Loewy, director of the Paris Observatory and inventor of the coudé (elbow) telescope. The Academy of the Hebrew Language voted the name *Rahab* for Neptune in 2009.
