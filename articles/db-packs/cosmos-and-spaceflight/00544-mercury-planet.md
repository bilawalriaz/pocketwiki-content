# Mercury (planet)

Mercury is the smallest planet in the Solar System and the closest to the Sun, an iron-rich rocky world whose extreme environment and unusual motion have made it central to testing both planetary formation and Einstein's general relativity. It is a dense, airless, scarred body whose surface bakes at 700 K by day and freezes at 110 K by night, yet harbours water ice in permanently shadowed polar craters.

## A planet dominated by its core

Mercury's equatorial radius is 2,440 km, smaller than Jupiter's moons Ganymede and Titan, yet its density of 5.43 g/cm³ is the second highest of any planet. The reason is internal: an iron core with a radius near 2,020 km occupies about 57% of the planet's volume, compared with 17% for Earth. The silicate mantle is only about 420 km thick, with a crust estimated at 26–35 km.

Three hypotheses compete to explain the oversized core. A giant impact could have stripped the primordial mantle; extreme heat from the young Sun could have vaporised surface rock and driven it away; or solar nebula drag could have preferentially removed lighter material as the planet accreted. MESSENGER's detection of volatiles such as potassium and sulfur, which would have been lost under the first two scenarios, favours nebular drag, though BepiColombo, due to enter orbit in 2026, will test this.

The mantle's chemical homogeneity, together with large-scale surface compositional variations, suggests Mercury once had a global magma ocean, like the Moon, in which minerals crystallised and convective overturn produced today's layered crust.

## A surface that records contraction

Mercury's surface resembles the Moon's: heavily cratered, with ancient inter-crater plains and younger smooth plains that are volcanic. The largest feature, Caloris Planitia, is a 1,550 km multi-ring impact basin whose smooth interior is filled with volcanic material; its antipode hosts "Weird Terrain," hilly, fractured ground shaped by converging seismic waves or ejecta from the impact. Forty-six impact basins are known.

Lobate scarps, cliff-like thrust faults up to 1,000 km long and 3 km high, wrinkle the surface. They formed as the interior cooled and contracted, with most major faulting ending 3.6–3.7 billion years ago, though small scarps less than 50 million years old show contraction continues. The mapped scarps imply a radius reduction of 1–7 km. Volcanism was long-lived: 51 pyroclastic deposits, 90% inside crater rims, and a compound volcano with at least nine overlapping vents inside Caloris.

## An exosphere, not an atmosphere

Mercury has no real atmosphere. Instead, a tenuous exosphere of surface pressure below 0.5 nPa, where atoms follow individual ballistic paths without colliding, is made and lost continuously. Its constituents include hydrogen, helium, oxygen, sodium, calcium, potassium, magnesium, silicon, and hydroxyl, supplied by solar wind, radioactive decay, micrometeorite vaporisation (currently enhanced by dust from Comet Encke), and water-ice sublimation.

Despite being nearer the Sun, Mercury is only the second-hottest planet after Venus, whose thick atmosphere traps heat. Surface temperature ranges from −170 °C on the nightside to 420 °C at the subsolar point at perihelion.

## Ice at the poles

Mercury's axial tilt is only 0.027°, so sunlight never reaches the floors of deep polar craters. Radar observations in 1991 revealed bright deposits there; MESSENGER confirmed them as water ice, with an estimated mass of 10¹⁴–10¹⁵ kg, possibly delivered by comets or released from the interior, and possibly insulated by a regolith cover.

## Spin–orbit resonance

Mercury rotates three times for every two orbits, a 3:2 spin–orbit resonance. Its sidereal rotation is 58.65 days and its orbital period is 87.97 days, giving a solar day of 176 days. At perihelion, where Mercury moves fastest, tidal torque peaks and locks the long axis toward the Sun. Radar observations in 1965 confirmed this resonance, overturning the earlier belief that Mercury rotated synchronously with one side always facing the Sun.

The resonance creates two "hot poles" at longitudes 0° and 180° W, where the Sun reverses direction near perihelion and passes overhead three times over about 16 Earth days, producing the hottest surface points. Two "cold poles" at 90° and 270° W see the Sun overhead only at aphelion. Longitude zero is defined at the hot pole containing the crater Hun Kal, which lies at 20°W.

## Orbit and relativity

Mercury has the most eccentric planetary orbit, with eccentricity 0.21, swinging between 46 and 70 million km from the Sun. Its orbital inclination of 7° is the highest of any planet, so transits of the Sun occur only in May or November, roughly every seven years. In 1859, Urbain Le Verrier found that Mercury's perihelion precesses by 5,600 arcseconds per century relative to Earth, while Newtonian mechanics predicts only 5,557. The 43-arcsecond discrepancy was explained by Einstein's general relativity in 1915, making Mercury's perihelion precession one of the theory's first confirmed predictions.

## Magnetic field and the solar wind

Mercury has a global, dipolar magnetic field about 1.1% of Earth's strength (300 nT at the equator), tilted only 10° from the spin axis. It is generated by a dynamo in the liquid outer core, maintained by tidal heating driven by the planet's eccentric orbit. The magnetosphere deflects the solar wind but is leaky: MESSENGER discovered flux transfer events, twisted magnetic tubes up to 800 km wide, where magnetic reconnection lets solar wind reach the surface at roughly ten times the rate seen at Earth.

## Why so few missions

Reaching Mercury demands an exceptionally high delta-v because a spacecraft must decelerate against the Sun's deep gravity well, and aerobraking is impossible without an atmosphere. Only three missions have visited. Mariner 10 (1974–75) made three flybys using a Venus gravity assist and mapped under 45% of the surface, discovering the magnetic field. MESSENGER (2004–2015) used multiple gravity assists, orbited Mercury from 2011 to 2015, mapped 100% of the surface, confirmed polar ice, and measured core structure before crashing on 30 April 2015. BepiColombo, a joint ESA/JAXA mission launched in 2018, completed six flybys by 2025 and will enter orbit in 2026 to test formation hypotheses and measure surface composition.

## Future of the planet

Mercury's orbit is not permanently stable. Simulations show its eccentricity can vary chaotically from 0 to 0.45, and a future resonance with Jupiter gives about a 1% chance of orbital destabilisation over the next five billion years, through collision with the Sun, collision with Venus, ejection from the Solar System, or disruption of the inner planets. When the Sun becomes a red giant in roughly seven to eight billion years, Mercury and Venus are expected to be destroyed, with Earth and the Moon possibly sharing that fate.
