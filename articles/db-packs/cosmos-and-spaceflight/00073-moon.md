# Moon

The Moon is a rocky, planetary-mass body orbiting Earth at an average distance of 384,399 km. It is the largest moon relative to its host planet in the Solar System, tidally locked so the same face always points at Earth, and the only world beyond Earth that humans have visited. Its gravity drives Earth's tides, and its cratered surface preserves a record of early Solar System bombardment.

## Origin: a giant impact

The Moon formed about 4.51 billion years ago, roughly 50 million years after the Solar System. The leading explanation is the giant-impact hypothesis: a Mars-sized body called Theia struck the young Earth obliquely, blasting a disk of vaporized debris into orbit. That material coalesced just outside Earth's Roche limit, the distance inside which tidal forces tear a loosely bound body apart, at about 2.56 Earth radii.

Two facts make the theory compelling. The Earth–Moon system has unusually high angular momentum, which older ideas (fission from a spinning Earth, gravitational capture, or co-formation) cannot explain. Earth and Moon also share nearly identical isotopic compositions, suggesting thorough mixing of vaporized rock after the collision. The main open question is how much of the Moon came from Theia versus from proto-Earth, and whether remnants of Theia remain inside Earth.

## Interior and gravity

The Moon is differentiated, meaning heavier material sank into layers: a solid iron-rich inner core (radius possibly as small as 240 km), a fluid iron outer core about 300 km in radius, and a partially molten boundary layer about 500 km thick. This layering froze in from a global magma ocean within the first few hundred million years. The crust is mostly anorthosite, averaging about 50 km thick.

Surface gravity is 1.62 m/s², one-sixth of Earth's and the second-highest among Solar System moons after Io. The gravity field is lumpy, with mascons (positive gravitational anomalies tied to giant impact basins, partly filled in by dense basaltic lava). The Moon has no global magnetic field today; only patches of crustal magnetization remain. Paleomagnetic measurements show an early strong field (up to ~100 microteslas, 4.5–3.56 billion years ago) and a later weak one (5–7 microteslas) until about 1 billion years ago.

## Surface and conditions

The Moon has only an exosphere, an atmosphere so thin that molecules almost never collide. Total mass is under 10 tonnes and surface pressure about 3 × 10⁻¹⁵ atm. Daytime radiation averages 1.369 millisieverts per day, roughly 2.6 times the dose on the ISS. Surface temperatures swing from 120 °C in sunlight to −171 °C in darkness, and permanently shadowed polar craters drop to 26 K, the coldest temperature measured by a spacecraft.

The surface is buried in regolith, a powdery, impact-shattered layer 10–15 m thick in the highlands and 4–5 m in the dark plains. The far side averages about 1.9 km higher than the near side, and lobate scarps show the Moon has shrunk by roughly 90 m over the past billion years as its interior cooled.

## Two terrains: maria and highlands

The dark plains visible from Earth are the maria, vast basaltic lava flows that flooded ancient impact basins between about 3.7 and 3.3 billion years ago. Lunar basalts are richer in iron than Earth's and contain no water-altered minerals, a sign they erupted dry. Most maria lie on the near side (31% of its surface versus 2% on the far side), likely because heat-producing radioactive elements concentrated under the near-side crust, thinning it.

The lighter highlands are older crustal rock, dated to 4.4 billion years ago. The largest single feature is the South Pole–Aitken basin on the far side, 2,240 km across, the second-largest confirmed impact crater in the Solar System. Roughly 300,000 craters wider than 1 km scar the near side, and crater counts are the standard way to estimate surface ages. The lunar geologic timescale is anchored to basins like Imbrium, Nectaris, and Orientale.

## Water

Liquid water cannot survive on the surface, because solar radiation breaks water molecules apart. Ice is stable inside permanently shadowed polar craters. The 2008 Chandrayaan-1 mission confirmed surface water ice, with concentrations up to about 1,000 ppm. A 2009 impact experiment (LCROSS) detected at least 100 kg of water vapor in a plume from a shadowed crater, and in 2018 further evidence showed water ice is more abundant at the South Pole. In 2020 molecular water was detected on sunlit surface by SOFIA.

## Orbit, tides, and eclipses

The Moon's orbit is slightly elliptical (eccentricity 0.055), ranging from 356,400 km at perigee to 406,700 km at apogee. The sidereal period is 27.3 days; the synodic period (new moon to new moon) is 29.5 days. The Earth–Moon barycenter lies about 5,000 km from Earth's center, inside Earth.

Because the Moon is tidally locked, it rotates once per orbit. A subtle wobble called libration lets observers see about 59% of the surface over time. The Moon's gravity raises ocean tides, two high and two low each ~24 hours 50 minutes, with the Sun adding up to 40% of the tidal force. Tidal friction is lengthening Earth's day by about 17 microseconds per year and pushing the Moon outward by 38 mm per year.

Eclipses require the Sun, Earth, and Moon to align (syzygy): solar eclipses at new moon, lunar eclipses at full moon. Because the lunar orbit is tilted ~5.145° to Earth's, alignments require the Moon to be near a node, the point where its orbit crosses the ecliptic. The saros cycle of roughly 18 years describes when nearly identical eclipses recur. The Moon's angular size (~0.52°) closely matches the Sun's, which is why both total and annular solar eclipses occur. Its albedo is only 0.12, comparable to worn asphalt.

## Exploration

The Soviet Union's Luna program achieved the first flyby (1959), crash impact, far-side photos, soft landing (1966), and lunar orbit. Apollo 8 carried the first humans around the Moon in 1968, and Apollo 11 landed the first crew on July 20, 1969. Six Apollo missions placed twelve people on the surface and returned 380 kg of samples.

After a long gap, renewed exploration began with Japan's Hiten in 1990. Subsequent missions mapped the gravity field, confirmed water ice, and returned samples. China's Chang'e 4 made the first far-side landing in 2019, and Chang'e 6 returned the first far-side samples in 2024. Artemis II is planned as the first crewed lunar flyby since Apollo. No nation owns any part of the Moon: the 1967 Outer Space Treaty designates it the "province of all mankind" and restricts its use to peaceful purposes.
