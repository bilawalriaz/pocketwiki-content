# Formation and evolution of the Solar System

Roughly 4.57 billion years ago, a fragment of a giant molecular cloud, seeded with heavy elements from earlier generations of stars, collapsed under its own gravity and flattened into a disk around a young protostar. This **nebular hypothesis**, first proposed in the 18th century, fell from favour because it could not explain why the Sun holds so little of the system's angular momentum, the total spin carried by orbiting bodies. Observations of young stars in the 1980s showed the cool, dusty disks the hypothesis predicted, and it returned to favour.

## From cloud to protostar

The collapse was triggered from outside. Iron-60, a radioactive isotope forged only in a short-lived massive star, is spread evenly through meteorites and the early Solar System, indicating that a nearby supernova injected it before dust clumped into planets. The shock wave from that supernova probably squeezed part of the cloud hard enough to make it collapse. The Sun likely formed inside a cluster of 1,000–10,000 stars, which broke apart 135–535 million years later.

As the cloud contracted, conservation of angular momentum spun it faster. Over about 100,000 years, gravity, gas pressure, magnetic fields, and rotation flattened the nebula into a spinning **protoplanetary disk** roughly 200 AU across, where one AU is the Earth–Sun distance (about 150 million km). For ~50 million years the Sun was a **T Tauri star**, a young phase marked by fierce stellar winds and surface temperatures near 1,000 K. Hydrogen fusion then ignited, and the Sun settled into its **main-sequence** phase, where it remains today.

## Building the planets

Dust grains stuck together into pebbles, then ~10 km **planetesimals**, then Moon-to-Mars-sized protoplanets. The inner disk, inside ~4 AU, was too hot for water and methane to freeze, so only refractory metals and silicates condensed there, producing the small rocky **terrestrial planets**: Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars. The four giants grew beyond the **frost line**, where water and other volatiles freeze solid. There, abundant ices built ~10 Earth-mass cores that then swept up huge envelopes of hydrogen and helium.

A pressure gap near 5 AU piled up material, explaining why Jupiter sits where it does. Its core reached ~10 Earth masses, then rapidly pulled in gas to ~318 Earth masses within ~10⁵ years. Saturn formed a few million years later, when less gas remained. Uranus and Neptune probably formed closer in, since accretion is too slow at their present distance, and were scattered outward by Jupiter and Saturn.

Within 3–10 million years, the T Tauri wind blew away the remaining gas and dust. Over the next ~100 million years, collisions and mergers among 50–100 Moon-to-Mars-sized protoplanets built the four terrestrial planets. The Moon is thought to be mantle debris from a single late impact of a Mars-sized body on the proto-Earth. The asteroid belt once held enough mass for 2–3 Earths; Jupiter's gravity and **orbital resonances**, gravitational synchronizations between bodies whose orbital periods form simple ratios, shattered or ejected planetesimals instead of letting them accrete, leaving it at less than 1% of Earth's mass.

## Migration and the late heavy bombardment

The giant planets did not stay put. According to the **Nice model**, interactions with the remaining planetesimal disk slowly shifted their orbits. About 4.1–4.0 billion years ago, Jupiter and Saturn fell into a 2:1 mean-motion resonance, with Saturn completing one orbit for every two of Jupiter's, flinging Neptune outward into the ancient Kuiper belt and scattering icy bodies inward. This may have triggered the **Late Heavy Bombardment**, leaving most of the craters still visible on the Moon and Mercury, though recent cosmochemical work questions whether such a spike really occurred. Some scattered bodies were hurled thousands of AU outward to form the spherical **Oort cloud**, the Solar System's most distant cometary reservoir; others were trapped into resonances with Neptune, producing the present Kuiper belt and scattered disc.

Mars is anomalously small. The **Grand tack hypothesis** proposes that Jupiter once migrated inward to 1.5 AU before being hauled back out by Saturn, starving Mars of accretion material.

## Moons and tides

Most large moons of the giant planets formed from miniature disks. Smaller, outer moons with irregular tilted orbits were captured. Earth's Moon is the product of a giant impact; Mars's tiny moons are probably captured asteroids; Neptune's Triton is almost certainly a captured Kuiper belt object.

Tidal forces reshape moon systems. If a moon orbits faster than its planet rotates, tidal friction drags it inward; Phobos will break up or crash in 30–50 million years. If a moon orbits more slowly, it spirals outward while the planet's spin slows, which is why the Moon is receding and Earth's spin is still slowing. **Tidal locking**, in which a moon's rotation period equals its orbital period, halts this transfer.

Planetary orbits are chaotic on million- to billion-year timescales. Pluto's position cannot be predicted beyond ~10–20 million years, a window called the **Lyapunov time**. Within a similar span, a close encounter with Venus could plausibly eject Mercury from the Solar System.

## Death of the Sun

The Sun has been brightening ~10% every 1.1 billion years. In ~600 million years, rising output will disrupt Earth's carbon cycle; in ~1.1 billion years the surface will lose all liquid water; by 3.5 billion years from now, Earth's surface will resemble present Venus.

In about 5 billion years the Sun exhausts core hydrogen and swells into a **red giant**, eventually reaching ~1.2 AU in radius, 256 times its present size, engulfing Mercury and Venus. Earth's fate is contested: tidal interaction with the Sun's outer envelope may pull it in, but a 2026 modelling study argues it will likely survive. Luminosity will rise to ~2,700 times today's and surface temperature fall to ~2,600 K. After a **helium flash**, a brief runaway ignition of helium in the core, the Sun shrinks, then expands again as an **asymptotic giant** before shedding its outer layers as a **planetary nebula**. The exposed core, a **white dwarf** of 54% of the Sun's mass but only Earth's size, cools for trillions of years. All remaining planets drift outward into dark, frigid orbits as the Sun loses mass.

After roughly one quadrillion (10¹⁵) years, the cumulative gravity of passing stars will strip the Sun of its last planets.
