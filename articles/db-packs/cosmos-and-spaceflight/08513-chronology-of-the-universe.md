# Chronology of the universe

The universe began 13.8 billion years ago as a hot, dense state and has been expanding and cooling ever since. Its history divides into four phases tied to a shifting balance of contents: a brief inflationary flash, the hot Big Bang, a long stretch where gravity builds cosmic structure, and a final stretch where expansion accelerates.

**Inflation (first ~10⁻³⁶ to 10⁻³² seconds).** The earliest epoch supported by direct evidence. Space expanded by a factor of roughly 10²⁶, stretching quantum fluctuations into the seeds of all future structure. The engine was a scalar field, the "inflaton," whose energy then dumped into particles in a process called reheating, creating a hot plasma and launching the hot Big Bang.

**Hot Big Bang (~10⁻³² s to ~370,000 years).** As the fireball cooled, particle interactions switched on and off in sequence:

- *Electroweak transition (~10⁻¹² s, ~10¹⁵ K):* the electromagnetic and weak forces separated, and particles acquired mass via the Higgs mechanism.
- *Quark-hadron transition (~10⁻⁵ s):* free quarks bound into protons and neutrons.
- *Neutrino decoupling (~1 s, ~10¹⁰ K):* neutrinos stopped interacting and began free-streaming. They form a faint cosmic neutrino background, today at about 1.95 K, whose existence is confirmed by its imprint on the CMB.
- *Electron-positron annihilation (~6 s):* most electrons and positrons wiped out, dumping energy into photons.
- *Big Bang nucleosynthesis (~3–20 minutes):* the hot plasma cooled below the fusion threshold, fusing protons and neutrons into roughly 75% hydrogen, 25% helium-4 by mass, with trace deuterium, helium-3, and lithium-7. The helium ratio is one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the model, and the unfused deuterium left over is hard to explain any other way.

Around 47,000 years the energy density of matter overtook that of radiation, ending the radiation-dominated era and letting gravity begin pulling structure together. Dark matter, about 84.5% of all matter, clumped first because it cannot radiate away energy; ordinary atomic matter fell into those wells.

**Recombination and the cosmic microwave background (~370,000 years).** Electrons and nuclei combined into neutral atoms, mostly hydrogen and helium. Photons, no longer scattered by free electrons, decoupled and streamed freely. That light is still visible today as the cosmic microwave background, a near-uniform 2.725 K glow whose tiny temperature variations encode the density fluctuations of the infant universe. This is the oldest light we can directly see.

**Dark Ages and first light (~370 ka to ~1 billion years).** With no stars, the universe was dark except for the CMB, redshifted to infrared, and a faint 21 cm hydrogen signal. The first stars, Population III, lit up a few hundred million years after the Big Bang, probably around 150–300 million years, and began forging heavier elements. By about 400 million years the earliest confirmed galaxies had formed; as of 2025 the most distant observed is MoM-z14, at redshift z = 14.44. Their ultraviolet radiation reionized the intergalactic hydrogen, mostly complete by about 1 billion years.

**Structure building (1 to ~9.8 billion years).** Gravity assembled dark-matter halos into a cosmic web of filaments, clusters, and superclusters, with galaxies condensing at the nodes. Within galaxies, successive stellar generations (Population II, then Population I like our Sun) built the periodic table through fusion and supernovae. The Solar System formed at about 9.2 billion years, 4.6 billion years ago, and life appeared on Earth by 4 billion years ago.

**Dark-energy-dominated era (from ~9.8 billion years).** As matter thinned, a constant energy density of empty space, dark energy, came to dominate, about 68.3% of the universe's mass-energy today. Unlike gravity, its repulsive effect does not weaken with distance, so expansion that had been slowing began accelerating roughly 5–6 billion years ago and continues to speed up.

**The far future.** The Stelliferous Era of ongoing star formation is expected to end near 100 trillion years after the Big Bang, when fuel runs out. Beyond that, the long-term outcome depends on physics not yet measured: heat death, an indefinite fade to thermal equilibrium; Big Rip, accelerating expansion shredding galaxies, stars, atoms, and eventually spacetime itself; Big Crunch, a reversal of expansion now thought unlikely; or vacuum decay, a bubble of lower-energy "true vacuum" expanding outward at light speed.
