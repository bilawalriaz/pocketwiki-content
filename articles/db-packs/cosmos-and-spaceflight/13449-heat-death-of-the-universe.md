# Heat death of the universe

The heat death of the universe is a hypothesis about the universe's far future in which the cosmos reaches thermodynamic equilibrium: a state where energy is spread so evenly that no temperature differences remain, so no further work can be extracted and no thermodynamic process can continue. The name refers to the running-down of useful energy, not to a particular temperature. A heat death can occur at any temperature, including one above absolute zero, provided no work is possible.

## What "heat death" means

Thermodynamic processes, from a steam engine to a star, run because hot regions and cold regions exist side by side. Heat flows from hot to cold, and that flow can be tapped to do work. The second law of thermodynamics states that entropy, a measure of how dispersed energy is, never decreases in an isolated system. In practice this means usable energy steadily degrades into heat that is spread uniformly, and the system moves toward a state where no further work is possible.

Applied to the whole universe, the same logic says that if the universe lasts long enough, every temperature difference will smooth out. Stars will burn out. Black holes will evaporate. Eventually only a thin, nearly uniform bath of low-energy particles remains. At that point the universe is in thermodynamic equilibrium. That final state is the heat death.

## Origin of the idea

Lord Kelvin (William Thomson) gave the hypothesis its modern form in the 1850s by extending the laws of thermodynamics to cosmic scales. In 1852 he published a paper on "a universal tendency in nature to the dissipation of mechanical energy," arguing that motion and the ability to do work naturally run down. Hermann von Helmholtz and William Rankine elaborated the idea over the following decade.

A precursor appears earlier: the French astronomer Jean Sylvain Bailly wrote in 1777 that planets are cooling bodies at different stages, with Venus still too hot for life and Mars already too cold, ending in a state of "equilibrium" in which all motion ceases. The thermodynamic version is Kelvin's.

Kelvin also drew a paradox from the hypothesis. If the universe were infinitely old, it should already have reached thermodynamic equilibrium, and no stars or temperature differences should exist. Because stars and temperature differences clearly do exist, the universe cannot be infinitely old. This is the heat death paradox.

## When heat death is expected

Whether heat death is the actual fate depends on the geometry of space and the nature of dark energy.

If the universe is open or flat, or if dark energy is a positive cosmological constant, expansion continues forever and heat death is expected. If the cosmological constant is zero, the temperature asymptotically approaches absolute zero. If the cosmological constant is positive, the temperature instead asymptotes to a small but nonzero value, while the universe still approaches maximum entropy.

Alternatives exist in some cosmological models: a Big Crunch (recollapse), a Big Rip (accelerating expansion that tears structures apart), or a cyclic model. These are not the heat-death scenario, though some cyclic models route through a heat-death-like phase between cycles.

## Timescales

Heat death is not imminent. The most durable reservoirs of usable energy are supermassive black holes. A black hole of roughly one galactic mass, around 10^11 solar masses, decays by Hawking radiation on the order of 10^100 years. Some supermassive black holes may grow to roughly 10^14 solar masses during the collapse of superclusters and would still evaporate within roughly 10^106 years.

After the last black holes evaporate, the universe enters the Dark Era: a dilute gas of photons and leptons with extremely low energy and extremely long timescales between events. Heat death marks the end of that era, when entropy production effectively stops.

A speculative addition: the Poincaré recurrence theorem and fluctuation theorems allow, in principle, for entropy to spontaneously decrease and a new Big Bang-like configuration to arise from quantum fluctuations. Such an event would take on the order of 10^(10^(10^56)) years, an unimaginably large number. This is not a tested prediction but a mathematical possibility within the framework.

## Status and objections

The hypothesis rests on treating the universe as a closed thermodynamic system, an assumption several physicists have challenged. Max Planck argued that the phrase "entropy of the universe" has no precise meaning. László Tisza noted that an isolated system out of equilibrium cannot be assigned an entropy. Hans Adolf Buchdahl called the closed-system assumption "entirely unjustifiable." Giovanni Gallavotti has written that no universally accepted notion of entropy exists for systems out of equilibrium. Elliott Lieb and Jakob Yngvason have stated that despite most physicists believing in a nonequilibrium entropy, no clearly satisfactory definition has been produced.

Lee Smolin has argued a more concrete objection: gravity keeps the universe out of thermal equilibrium. Gravitationally bound systems have negative specific heat, meaning they grow hotter as they lose energy, fragmenting into denser structures rather than smoothing out. On this view the universe becomes more structured over time, not less.

Recent work also notes that gravitational entropy is difficult to quantify, and that supermassive black holes may contribute far more entropy to the observable universe than older estimates suggested.

Quantum extensions are possible. Even after thermodynamic entropy has reached its maximum, other quantities such as quantum circuit complexity may continue to grow toward their own maximums, giving a richer end state than the classical picture.

## What remains

The classical heat-death hypothesis is one well-defined scenario for the far future of an expanding universe, grounded in the second law of thermodynamics and in Kelvin's nineteenth-century reasoning. Its weaknesses are real: the universe is not obviously a closed equilibrium system, gravitational entropy is poorly understood, and gravity appears to drive structure formation rather than dissipation. Whether the universe actually ends in thermodynamic equilibrium depends on the geometry of space, the value of the cosmological constant, and whether gravity is correctly included in the thermodynamic accounting.
