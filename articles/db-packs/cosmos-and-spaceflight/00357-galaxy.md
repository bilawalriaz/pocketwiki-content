# Galaxy

A galaxy is a gravitationally bound system of stars, stellar remnants, interstellar gas, dust, and dark matter. The name comes from the Greek for "milky," a reference to our own galaxy, the Milky Way. Galaxies range from dwarfs with fewer than a thousand stars to supergiants with up to one hundred trillion stars. They are the basic building blocks of the universe, holding most of its visible matter and hosting nearly all star formation. Most of a galaxy's mass is dark matter, a non-luminous form of matter that exerts gravity but does not interact with light; only a few percent is in stars and nebulae. A supermassive black hole, with a mass millions to billions of times that of the Sun, sits at the center of most large galaxies. Astronomers estimate 200 billion to 2 trillion galaxies in the observable universe, typically separated by millions of parsecs (about 3.26 light-years each).

## How the picture developed

Democritus proposed around 450 BCE that the Milky Way consists of distant stars, and Galileo confirmed it with a telescope in 1610 by resolving faint stars. Thomas Wright suggested in 1750 that the Milky Way is a rotating disk and that some nebulae are separate "island universes." Vesto Slipher's 1912 measurements of high redshifts (shifts of spectral lines toward longer wavelengths, caused by motion away from the observer) for spiral nebulae showed these objects were receding. Edwin Hubble settled the question of their nature in 1926 by resolving individual stars in Andromeda, proving it lies far outside the Milky Way; he also introduced the first classification scheme by shape, now called the Hubble sequence. Vera Rubin's 1970s measurements of galactic rotation showed outer stars orbit faster than visible matter alone can explain, providing the first strong evidence for dark matter halos surrounding galaxies.

## Types of galaxies

Galaxies are classified by shape. **Elliptical galaxies** (E0–E7) are ellipsoidal, contain mostly older stars, and hold little gas for new star formation; the largest are supergiant type-cD galaxies. **Spiral galaxies** (S) have a rotating disk with spiral arms that host active star formation, surrounding a central bulge. Many spirals, including the Milky Way, are **barred spirals** (SB), with a bar of stars running through the center. **Lenticular galaxies** (S0) sit between ellipticals and spirals, with a disk but no prominent arms. **Irregular galaxies** lack a defined shape, and **dwarf galaxies**, the most common type, are small systems with relatively few stars.

## Activity and interactions

**Interacting galaxies** feel tidal forces from a near neighbor or are merging, and these events can trigger a **starburst**, a brief phase of exceptionally high star formation. **Active galaxies** host an **active galactic nucleus (AGN)**, a compact, highly luminous central region powered by matter accreting onto a supermassive black hole. The main AGN types are Seyfert galaxies, quasars (AGNs luminous enough to outshine their host galaxy), and radio galaxies, which launch giant jets of radio-emitting material from their nuclei. **Luminous infrared galaxies (LIRGs)** release most of their energy in the infrared, often because dust from a starburst or merger absorbs starlight and re-emits it at longer wavelengths.

## Large-scale structure

Galaxies are not scattered randomly. Most live in **groups**, such as our Local Group, or in larger **clusters** containing hundreds to thousands of galaxies. Clusters are gravitationally bound and contain hot gas and dark matter along with their galaxies. Clusters gather into **superclusters**, such as the Virgo Supercluster, which are arranged in sheets and filaments surrounding vast cosmic voids.

## Formation and evolution

Galaxies formed from small density fluctuations in the early universe. Dark matter halos pulled in baryonic (ordinary atomic) gas, which cooled and condensed into protogalaxies. The first stars, Population III, formed, reionized the universe, and seeded the interstellar medium with heavy elements through their supernovae. Over billions of years, disks, bulges, and central supermassive black holes built up through gas accretion, ongoing star formation, and mergers. The Milky Way and Andromeda are approaching each other at about 130 km/s and are likely to merge in roughly five to six billion years; eventually, star formation will cease as galaxies exhaust their gas supplies. Open questions remain about the exact formation of the first galaxies, the particle nature of dark matter, how supermassive black holes and their host galaxies co-evolve, and the true count of galaxies in the observable universe, with estimates depending strongly on detection limits and definitions.
