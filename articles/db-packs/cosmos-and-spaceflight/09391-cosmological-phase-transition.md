# Cosmological phase transition

A cosmological phase transition is an overall change in the state of matter across the whole universe. The success of the Big Bang model led researchers to conjecture such transitions in the very early universe, when it was far hotter and denser than today. Any cosmological phase transition may have left signals observable now, even if it occurred in the first moments after the Big Bang, when the universe was opaque to light.

## How phase transitions arise

The Standard Model of particle physics, parameterised by laboratory measurements, can be used to predict the character of cosmic phase transitions. As the universe expanded and cooled, a system sitting in one ground state at high temperature could find a new ground state more favourable, and the shift between states is a phase transition.

A phase transition is tied to a difference in symmetry between the two states. Water is isotropic, the same in every direction, while ice has directions with different properties. The two states also have different energy, with ice holding less than liquid water. An iron bar cooled below its Curie temperature offers another example: above that temperature the magnetic moments are isotropic, but below it the bar becomes magnetic, with two opposite alignments at the same energy. This process is called spontaneous symmetry breaking, meaning the equations describing the system stay symmetric while the state it settles into does not.

## Transition order

Phase transitions are sorted by their order. First-order transitions proceed via bubble nucleation, the spontaneous formation of small pockets of the new phase, and release latent heat as the bubbles expand. After the hot Big Bang, such a transition would have released huge amounts of energy, both as heat and as the kinetic energy of growing bubbles. In a strongly first-order transition, the bubble walls can grow at near the speed of light, which would produce a stochastic background of gravitational waves, a random, persistent hum of ripples in spacetime. Experiments such as NANOGrav and LISA may detect this signal. Simulations show the sequence: bubbles first nucleate, then expand and collide, eventually converting the universe from one phase to another.

Second-order transitions are continuous rather than abrupt and are less likely to leave observable imprints on cosmic structures.

## Transitions within the Standard Model

The Standard Model contains the electromagnetic, weak, and strong forces. Shortly after the Big Bang, extremely high temperatures may have modified their character. Although the three forces act differently today, it has been conjectured that they were once unified at the highest temperatures of the early universe.

**QCD phase transition.** The strong force binds quarks into protons and neutrons through colour confinement, the requirement that quarks only exist inside bound combinations. At sufficiently high temperatures, protons and neutrons disassociate into free quarks. This shift, also called the quark–hadron transition, has been studied with lattice QCD, a numerical method that solves the strong-force equations on a discrete space-time grid. Those studies indicate it took place at about 155 MeV (with the simplest assumptions giving roughly 170 MeV) and was a smooth crossover, a gradual change with no sharp boundary. First- or second-order behaviour is still possible if a quark, baryon, or neutrino chemical potential, a measure of how many more particles than antiparticles are present, is nonzero, or under strong magnetic fields.

**Electroweak phase transition.** This marks the moment when the Higgs mechanism breaks the SU(2)⊗U(1) symmetry of the Standard Model. The SU(2) and U(1) labels name the mathematical symmetry groups that describe the weak and electromagnetic interactions before the Higgs field acquires a value. Lattice studies find this to be a smooth crossover at 159.5 ± 1.5 GeV. This conclusion assumes the minimal scenario and is modified by additional fields or particles. Models that account for dark matter or achieve successful baryogenesis, the generation of more matter than antimatter, may predict a strongly first-order electroweak transition. The electroweak baryogenesis model may explain the baryon asymmetry, the observation that matter vastly exceeds antimatter.

## Beyond the Standard Model

If the three Standard Model forces are unified in a Grand Unified Theory, a cosmological phase transition at even higher temperatures would correspond to the moment when the forces first separated. A GUT transition breaking the unified SU(3)⊗SU(2)⊗U(1) symmetry into the Standard Model symmetry may be responsible for the observed excess of matter over antimatter. Cosmological phase transitions may also have occurred in a dark or hidden sector, among particles and fields only very weakly coupled to visible matter.

## Observational consequences

Cosmological phase transitions can leave two measurable traces: primordial gravitational waves and the baryon asymmetry. Adequate confirmation has not yet been achieved.
