# Exoplanet orbital and physical parameters

An exoplanet's orbit needs six numbers to be fully defined: semi-major axis, eccentricity, longitude of periastron, time of periastron, inclination, and longitude of the ascending node. Indirect detection methods recover only some of them. Radial velocity measures four (semi-major axis, eccentricity, longitude of periastron, time of periastron); inclination and the node usually stay hidden, which is why most radial-velocity masses are reported as minimum values, written M sin i (true mass times the sine of the line-of-sight inclination).

## Distances, periods, and detection biases

Known exoplanet orbits span an enormous range. The shortest periods are a few hours, and five planets in the Kepler-11 system orbit closer to their star than Mercury orbits the Sun. At the other extreme, planets like GU Piscium b sit thousands of AU out with periods of tens of thousands of years. A 2019 study showed WASP-12b's orbit is decaying through tidal interactions and the planet will be consumed by its star in 2.90 ± 0.14 million years.

Each discovery method samples a different slice of this range. Radial velocity and transit are most sensitive to small orbits; transit also requires the orbit to be nearly edge-on. Direct imaging finds large orbits, with planets detected hundreds of AU from their stars. Microlensing has caught a few planets in the 1–10 AU range. The middle range, equivalent to where our gas giants live, was largely unexplored until instruments like Gemini Planet Imager and VLT-SPHERE began work in 2014. Core-accretion models predict giant planets form inside about 10 AU, so the distant ones detected by imaging may be captured rogue planets, scattered giants, or unusual gravitational-instability products.

## Orbital shape (eccentricity)

Eccentricity (e) measures how stretched an ellipse is; 0 is a circle. Solar System planets except Mercury have e < 0.1. Exoplanets split into two populations: short-period planets have near-circular orbits because tides from the star have circularized them, while giant planets on longer periods often show high eccentricities. As of July 2010, 55% of radial-velocity giants had e > 0.2 and 17% had e > 0.5. Low eccentricities also correlate with multi-planet systems.

Weak signals near detection limits often produce overestimated eccentricities. Simulations suggest about 35% of apparently single planets in eccentric orbits are statistically indistinguishable from a pair of planets in a 2:1 resonance, and another 40% cannot be distinguished from a circular orbit.

## Inclination and spin–orbit angle

For exoplanets, "inclination" means the line-of-sight angle from Earth, so transit-detected planets sit near 90°. The angle between a planet's orbit and its star's spin axis is a separate quantity, the spin–orbit angle. Kepler's multi-planet systems generally show coplanar orbits like the Solar System, but more than half of hot Jupiters are substantially misaligned with their star's spin, and some orbit backwards (retrograde). One proposed explanation: the star itself flipped early on through magnetic interactions with the planet-forming disk, not the planet's orbit being disturbed.

## Precession and close-in dynamics

For very close planets, tidal forces and general relativity drive measurable orbital precession (a slow rotation of the orbit's orientation in space). Periastron precession is the rotation of the ellipse within its plane, and for tight orbits the relativistic contribution can be orders of magnitude larger than Mercury's and detectable in under a decade. Nodal precession is the wobble of the orbital plane itself; WASP-33b, in an almost polar orbit around a fast-rotating star with a quadrupole moment (a measure of how non-spherical the star's mass distribution is) about 1900 times the Sun's, shows large classical and relativistic nodal precession because of the star's oblateness and the Lense–Thirring effect (a general-relativity frame-dragging term produced by a rotating body's angular momentum).

## Rotation and axial tilt

In 2014, Beta Pictoris b became the first exoplanet with a measured rotation: about 8 hours per spin at 25 km/s, faster than any Solar System giant. It is young and still warm; as it cools and shrinks over hundreds of millions of years, conservation of angular momentum will shorten its day to roughly 3 hours and push rotation to about 40 km/s. A 2017 survey of several gas giants, however, found no clear mass-rotation correlation. As of 2024, four exoplanets have measured axial tilts, including VHS 1256 b at a Uranus-like 90° ± 25°.

For terrestrial planets, the final few giant impacts during formation dominate the spin, averaging about 70% of break-up velocity. Tidal effects then matter: for short-period planets, gravitational tides drive the spin toward tidal lock (the same face always toward the star) and push the axial tilt toward zero over time, though multi-planet interactions can lock a planet into a Cassini state (a resonance between the planet's spin-axis precession and its orbital precession). Hot Jupiters in circular orbits tend toward synchronous rotation; those on eccentric orbits, like HD 80606 b (predicted 1.9-day rotation), stay non-synchronous because they are fluid bodies.

## Mass, radius, and what density reveals

Radial velocity alone gives only M sin i, but the geometric probability of an inclination under 30° is about 13%, so most true masses sit close to the minimum. Adding a transit detection fixes the inclination and yields the true mass; transit-timing variations can also pin down masses dynamically.

Combining true mass and radius gives density, the main handle on bulk composition: low density implies mostly hydrogen and helium, intermediate density implies water or volatiles, high density implies rocky. Before Kepler, most known planets were gas giants because they are easiest to detect. Kepler shifted the sample toward planets between Earth and Neptune in size. Below about 1.5 Earth radii, planets grow denser with size from gravitational compression; between 1.5 and 4 Earth radii density falls with size as volatiles and gas envelopes take over. Statistical studies suggest dividing lines near 1.7 Earth radii (rocky planets versus gas dwarfs) and 3.9 Earth radii (gas dwarfs versus gas giants), though the picture is debated; one reanalysis sees a continuum rather than sharp boundaries.

Two oddities stand out. Hot Jupiters are often far larger than their mass and heating would predict. Super-puffs like the Kepler-51 planets have only a few Earth masses but radii larger than Neptune, making them extraordinarily diffuse. At the other extreme, Kepler-10c at 17 Earth masses is denser than Earth, a "mega-Earth" of rock with possibly some high-pressure water ice. Objects like CoRoT-20b (4.24 Jupiter masses, 0.84 Jupiter radii) imply heavy-element cores of hundreds of Earth masses. Around massive B- and O-type stars, protoplanetary disks may hold enough heavy elements to build solid planets up to thousands of Earth masses before stellar UV and winds strip the gas.
