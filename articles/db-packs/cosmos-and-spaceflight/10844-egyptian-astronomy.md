# Egyptian astronomy

Egyptian astronomy is a continuous tradition spanning roughly 4,500 years, from prehistoric stone alignments through Hellenistic Alexandria and into the Islamic era when Cairo overtook Baghdad as a center of observation. It served three practical needs: forecasting the Nile flood, fixing religious festivals to solar, lunar, and stellar cycles, and aligning monumental architecture to the sky.

## Origins and the calendar problem

The Nile flood governed Egyptian agriculture, so predicting it was the first job of astronomy. A *heliacal rising* is the first morning a star becomes visible just before sunrise after months lost in the Sun's glare. The bright star Sirius (Egyptian *Sopdet*, Greek *Sothis*) had its heliacal rising each year just before the inundation, marking the new year.

A *365-day civil calendar* of 12 months of 30 days plus 5 epagomenal days was already in use by the start of the Dynastic Period in the 3rd millennium BCE. Ignoring the extra quarter-day caused it to drift about one day every four years against the true solar year, and Sirius's heliacal rising cycled through it on a roughly 1,460-year cycle, the *Sothic cycle*.

Stone circles at Nabta Playa in Lower Nubia, dated to the 5th millennium BCE, are the earliest known Egyptian astronomical structures.

## Aligning architecture to the sky

Egyptian builders encoded sky knowledge into stone. The 3rd-millennium-BCE pyramids align to the north celestial pole. Because of the slow wobble of Earth's axis (*precession of the equinoxes*), the north pole star then was Thuban, a faint star in Draco, not today's Polaris. The Great Temple of Amun-Re at Karnak aligns to the rising of the midwinter Sun, with a corridor long enough that sunlight reached the inner sanctuary only near the solstice.

## Telling time at night

Egyptian timekeeping divided the night into 12 hours whose length changed with the seasons. Because no single star could mark every hour year-round, observers used a rotating roster of 36 stars called *decans*, each rising at a fixed interval on a given date. From the 9th Dynasty onward, decanal schedules were painted as "diagonal star tables" on coffin lids, later appearing on tomb and temple ceilings. New Kingdom star charts (tombs of Ramesses VI and IX) show the method: an observer sat facing the astrologer with the pole star over the middle of his head, and a star crossing a specific meridian position marked the hour.

Two standard instruments appear in temple reliefs: the *merkhet*, a plumb-line handle used to mark the north–south line, and the *bay*, a palm branch with a sighting slit held to the eye.

## Stars, kings, and the Book of Nut

Religion drove much of the observing. Sirius's rising opened the inundation; the Sun, Moon, and five naked-eye planets were watched for conjunctions and phases to fix festival dates. The *Book of Nut*, from the Middle Kingdom or earlier, is the most important Egyptian astronomical text, depicting the sky goddess Nut swallowing the Sun each evening and giving birth to it each dawn. The Pyramid Texts describe the dead king ascending to join the *Imperishable Stars*, circumpolar stars that never set.

## Hellenistic and Roman Egypt

After Alexander the Great's conquest, Alexandria in Lower Egypt became the scientific capital of the Hellenistic world. Eratosthenes (c. 276–195 BCE) measured Earth's circumference from the angle of the noon Sun at two latitudes. Claudius Ptolemy (90–168 CE), a Roman citizen from Upper Egypt, wrote the *Almagest*, which modeled planetary motion using the *equant*, a point offset from Earth's center from which a planet's motion appears uniform. The equant is a mathematical device: the planet's angular speed as seen from the equant is constant, letting predictions match observations over centuries. The Almagest remained the standard astronomical textbook in the Islamic world and medieval Europe until the 16th century, when Maragha, Copernican, and Tychonic systems displaced it. Late antique commentators included Pappus, Theon of Alexandria, and his daughter Hypatia.

## Islamic Egypt

After the Muslim conquest, Egypt came under the Rashidun, Umayyad, and Abbasid caliphates, and from the 10th century under the Fatimids, who founded Cairo. By the 13th century Cairo had overtaken Baghdad as the chief intellectual center of the Islamic world.

Three Egyptian contributions stand out. Ibn Yunus (c. 950–1009) compiled over 10,000 solar observations with an astrolabe nearly 1.4 m in diameter; his eclipse data were still used by Simon Newcomb in the 19th century to study lunar motion. In 1006, Ali ibn Ridwan observed SN 1006, the brightest recorded supernova, describing it as two to three times the apparent diameter of Venus and about a quarter the brightness of the Moon, low on the southern horizon. Najm al-Din al-Misri (c. 1325) catalogued over 100 scientific and astronomical instruments, many of his own design; the astrolabic quadrant, invented in Egypt during the 11th or 12th century, later reached Europe as the *Quadrans Vetus*.

Each generation inherited the same core problem of tracking the Sun, Moon, stars, and Nile against a shifting sky, and each rebuilt the solution with the mathematics and instruments of its time.
