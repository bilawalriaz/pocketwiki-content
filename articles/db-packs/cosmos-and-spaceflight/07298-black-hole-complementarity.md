# Black hole complementarity

Black hole complementarity is a conjectured solution to the black hole information paradox, proposed by Leonard Susskind, Lárus Thorlacius, John Uglum, and Gerard 't Hooft. It reframes a deep tension in quantum gravity rather than answering it with a single picture.

The paradox begins with Stephen Hawking's claim that information falling through the event horizon is destroyed at the singularity, turning pure quantum states into mixed states and violating unitarity, the principle that total probability is conserved. The problem is that information cannot escape the horizon without exceeding light speed, yet Hawking radiation carries no obvious trace of it. The horizon also looks locally unremarkable, so there is nothing there to "reflect" the information back.

Complementarity resolves this by allowing two mutually exclusive stories to be true, provided no single observer can confirm both at once. From outside, infalling information never crosses the horizon. Infinite time dilation at the horizon means an external observer watches the infalling object asymptotically freeze and redden, never actually falling in. The information is instead absorbed by a **stretched horizon**, a hot, physical membrane hovering about a Planck length outside the true horizon. There the information is scrambled and later reradiated as Hawking radiation in a way that is consistent with unitarity.

From inside, nothing special happens at the horizon. The infalling observer and the information they carry pass through smoothly and eventually reach the singularity.

There are not two copies of the information, one outside and one inside, because that would violate the no-cloning theorem, which forbids duplicating an unknown quantum state. The two descriptions are complementary in the quantum-mechanical sense of noncommuting observables: an observer can verify the information at the horizon or inside, but never both at once.

A concrete difference: the infalling observer sees their information localised at a single point on the horizon, while the external observer sees the same information delocalised across the entire stretched horizon, smeared out and re-emitted. To the external observer, the horizon behaves as a dynamical membrane, a conducting surface whose charges spread out logarithmically and which carries entropy, viscosity, and electrical conductivity, as catalogued in the membrane paradigm.

A central feature of this stretched-horizon membrane is that it is the fastest scrambler in nature, as conjectured by Patrick Hayden, John Preskill, Yasuhiro Sekino, and Leonard Susskind. A single bit of information falling onto the horizon is rapidly delocalised through quantum scrambling across the entire surface. The characteristic time is the scrambling time

t_s = (β / 2π) log S,

where β is the inverse Hawking temperature and S is the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy, proportional to the horizon's area. The logarithmic dependence on entropy makes scrambling far faster than any classical transport process.

To achieve this, the stretched-horizon dynamics must be profoundly non-local. A local theory spreads information diffusively, with a timescale proportional to R² (R being the black-hole radius), which is far too slow. The horizon's degrees of freedom must instead interact in an "all-to-all" fashion, with every degree effectively coupled to every other. The Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev (SYK) model, a toy quantum-mechanical model with random all-to-all interactions, exhibits this behaviour, saturates the scrambling-time bound, and serves as a theoretical laboratory for stretched-horizon dynamics. This non-local scrambling is the mechanism by which information from infalling matter is transferred to the horizon and later encoded in outgoing Hawking radiation, restoring unitarity.

A tension remains. It has been suggested that combining effective field theory near the horizon with the monogamy of entanglement, the rule that quantum correlations cannot be freely shared, implies the existence of an AMPS "firewall", a region of high-energy, short-wavelength photons at the horizon that would incinerate the infalling observer and so reintroduce a sharp local violation of the equivalence principle.
