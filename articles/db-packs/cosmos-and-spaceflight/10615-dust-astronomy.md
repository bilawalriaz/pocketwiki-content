# Dust astronomy

Dust astronomy is the study of individual cosmic dust particles to learn about the astronomical objects they came from. Each grain carries information in its motion, its isotopes, its elements, molecules, and minerals, so it can be analysed in a laboratory in ways a photon cannot. The discipline overlaps with planetary science, cosmochemistry, and astrobiology, and uses dedicated "dust telescopes" flown on space observatories.

## Where cosmic dust comes from

After the Big Bang, the universe contained only hydrogen, helium, and a trace of lithium. Every heavier element, from carbon to plutonium, was forged by stellar nucleosynthesis and supernova explosions. Mature stars shed this material as stellar winds, and massive stars explode. The ejected gas cools in the interstellar medium, where molecules and dust condense. Comets, asteroids, and planets are later aggregates of that stardust, mixed and reprocessed. A small fraction of grains, called presolar grains, survived unmodified inside primitive meteorites and are recognisable by their non-Solar isotopic ratios. Examples include silicon carbide with red-giant isotopic signatures, and grains rich in ⁴⁴Ca, the decay product of ⁴⁴Ti made in Type II supernovae.

In the present Solar System, several processes keep producing dust. Jupiter-family comets shed micrometre grains when their ices sublimate near the Sun. Asteroids collide and grind each other down, generating the dust bands seen by IRAS from families such as Veritas and Koronis. Volcanically active moons and ring systems also contribute. The icy plumes of Saturn's moon Enceladus, fed by a subsurface salt-water ocean, supply the E ring with salt-rich ice grains. Jupiter's moon Io ejects nanometre dust that becomes positively charged in the Io plasma torus; Jupiter's magnetic field then accelerates these grains to several hundred kilometres per second and flings them out of the Jovian system.

## How dust is collected and analysed

Stratospheric aircraft collect dust that survives atmospheric entry, and high-altitude collectors in the 1970s first confirmed the extraterrestrial nature of these particles by their chondritic composition. In situ spacecraft instruments use impact ionisation to measure composition. Dust composition analysers on the 1986 Halley fly-bys (Vega 1, Vega 2, Giotto) showed that cometary dust is chondritic in composition but enriched in light elements (H, C, N, O), more primitive than carbonaceous chondrites. The Cassini Cosmic Dust Analyzer went further: it measured charges, mapped trajectories before impact, identified sodium chloride and sulphur-rich grains from Io, and detected magnesium-rich silicate grains arriving from the local interstellar medium. The Stardust mission returned the first cometary samples from 81P/Wild in 2006 along with seven probable interstellar grains. Hayabusa returned particles from asteroid Itokawa, Hayabusa2 from Ryugu, and OSIRIS-REx from Bennu.

These capabilities are combined in the modern "dust telescope," which pairs a dust trajectory sensor with a large-area composition analyser. Future instruments include SUDA on Europa Clipper, which will sample ice ejecta for biosignatures, and IDEX on IMAP, which will analyse interstellar and interplanetary dust at the Sun–Earth L1 point.

## Forces that move dust

Four forces govern a grain's orbit.

- Gravity follows Newton's law and produces the Keplerian orbits that bind large particles to the Sun. Within planetary systems, orbital resonances with massive planets can stabilise or destabilise dust orbits, and close encounters scatter grains between reservoirs.
- Solar radiation pressure pushes grains away from the Sun. The dimensionless ratio β = radiation force / gravity grows as particles become smaller or less dense. Grains with β > 0.1 drift far from their parent comet, those with β > 0.5 escape the Solar System on hyperbolic orbits, and "β-meteoroids" with β > 1 are repelled outward.
- Poynting–Robertson drag, a tangential component of radiation pressure caused by the finite speed of light, removes angular momentum and makes small grains spiral inward. A centimetre-sized particle at 1 AU takes about four million years to fall into the Sun, so all small interplanetary dust has been supplied recently by comets, asteroids, or interstellar infall.
- Electromagnetic forces. Dust is charged by ambient plasma, solar UV, and energetic particles, typically to a few volts positive in the solar wind, but to negative values inside cold planetary magnetospheres. Charged grains feel the Lorentz force F = Q(E + v × B). For grains smaller than about 0.1 µm, this force exceeds gravity, and the interplanetary magnetic field alternately focuses and defocuses interstellar dust depending on the phase of the 22-year solar magnetic cycle.

## How dust evolves

In the diffuse interstellar medium, micron-sized grains couple to the gas by collisional drag. Inside denser molecular clouds, icy mantles form, with H₂O, NH₃, CO₂, CO, CH₃OH, and complex organics condensing at low temperature. These grains shield interior molecules from UV and seed star formation.

In a protoplanetary disk, micron grains grow by Brownian-motion collisions to millimetre aggregates, then to centimetre pebbles. Outside the snow line at roughly 150 K, ices of water, ammonia, methane, and CO₂ condense, providing the raw material of comets and icy moons; inside it, rocky planetesimals form the cores of terrestrial planets. Chondrules, the millimetre spheres in primitive meteorites, are believed to have been flash-heated by nebular shocks or lightning. Once the central star becomes a T Tauri object, powerful winds disperse the remaining gas and dust.

In the inner Solar System, interplanetary dust at 1 AU is about 80–90% cometary and 10–20% asteroidal, based on the lifetimes required to match the observed flux. Grains smaller than 1 µm are depleted because radiation pressure and Poynting–Robertson drag remove them quickly. High-speed impacts (mean ~20 km/s at 1 AU) dominate destruction: for rocky targets the catastrophic-disruption threshold is about a million times the projectile mass, while for porous cometary material it is only a few thousand times. Sputtering by solar-wind ions erodes surfaces over hundreds of millions of years, and sublimation removes material inside a dust-free zone within about 5 solar radii, where silicate and carbon grains reach roughly 2000 K.
