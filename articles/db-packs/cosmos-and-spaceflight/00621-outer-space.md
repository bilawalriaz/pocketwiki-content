# Outer space

Outer space is the near-perfect vacuum beyond Earth's atmosphere, stretching between celestial bodies. Intergalactic regions hold only a few hydrogen atoms per cubic metre, compared with about 10²⁵ molecules per cubic metre in sea-level air. Gas and radiation are not in thermodynamic equilibrium: the cosmic microwave background (CMB) sits at 2.7 K, while gas temperatures range from 1 K in the Boomerang Nebula to 1.2–2.6 million K in the solar corona.

## Where it came from

The universe began about 13.8 billion years ago in a hot, dense state that expanded rapidly—the Big Bang. For the first 380,000 years matter and radiation were coupled, with light scattering constantly off free electrons and protons. As the universe cooled, protons and electrons combined into neutral hydrogen during the recombination epoch, decoupling matter from radiation. Those released photons still travel through space as the CMB, the faint glow that sets space's baseline temperature.

Tiny density variations grew under gravity and collapsed into stars, galaxies, and the empty regions between them. Space has flat geometry—parallel light rays stay parallel—which implies a non-zero vacuum energy called dark energy. Ordinary (baryonic) matter is only about 4.6 percent of the total mass–energy; the rest is dark matter and dark energy, neither detected directly. Average energy density is about 5.9 protons per cubic metre, but atoms contribute roughly one proton per four cubic metres. Within the Milky Way, dark energy's pull is five orders of magnitude weaker than gravity from matter and dark matter.

## Why it is dangerous

The vacuum causes rapid physiological collapse. At the Armstrong line, about 19.14 km altitude, ambient pressure equals the vapour pressure of water at body temperature, so saliva, tears, and lung-lining fluid begin to boil. Above 6.3 kPa, blood can form gas bubbles, a condition called ebullism. Rapid decompression can rupture lungs, eardrums, and sinuses, and hypoxia causes unconsciousness within minutes. Pressure suits hold 30–39 kPa of pure oxygen to prevent ebullism, though decompression sickness from dissolved nitrogen remains a risk.

More than half of astronauts experience space motion sickness in the first three days, and long-duration microgravity produces muscle atrophy, bone loss, fluid shifts toward the head, cardiovascular deconditioning, and immune weakening. Cosmic rays—mostly protons with energies up to 10²⁰ electronvolts—stream through unimpeded, causing acute fatigue, nausea, long-term elevated cancer risk, and damage to eyes, nervous system, lungs, and gut. Spacecraft walls and bulk shielding absorb some dose but also generate secondary radiation. These hazards have so far limited crewed flight to low Earth orbit (LEO) and the Moon; robotic craft have reached all Solar System planets, and Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause in 2012 into interstellar space.

## The regions

The atmosphere has no sharp edge. The Kármán line at 100 km is a conventional boundary where aerodynamic lift can no longer support flight; a 2009 sounding rocket found the midpoint of the charged-particle transition at 118 km.

Above the thermopause, around 250–500 km, lies the exosphere, where molecular collisions become rare. Near-Earth space runs from LEO out to geostationary orbit at roughly 35,786 km and holds most satellites and most space debris. Within this region the Van Allen belts form two toroidal zones that trap charged particles; the inner belt peaks near 3,000 km and overlaps upper LEO, with the South Atlantic Anomaly bringing it closest to Earth's surface. Geospace includes the magnetosphere, bounded sunward by the magnetopause at about 10 Earth radii and trailing into a magnetotail of 100–200 radii on the night side.

The Sun's magnetic field carves out the heliosphere, a bubble filled with the solar wind—5–10 protons per cubic centimetre moving at 350–400 km/s—extending to the heliopause at 110–160 astronomical units, past Neptune. Beyond lies the interstellar medium, roughly 70 percent hydrogen and 28 percent helium by mass, enriched with heavier elements from stellar winds and supernovae. Cold molecular clouds reach 10⁸ to 10¹² particles per cubic metre and host more than 200 detected molecules; dust grains about 0.1 micrometres across drive organic chemistry under cosmic-ray ionisation.

Between galaxies the intergalactic medium forms a foam of filaments and voids. Filaments hold galaxy groups and clusters at roughly one atom per cubic metre and contain about 76 percent hydrogen by mass. Gas falling into filaments heats to 10⁵–10⁷ K, forming the warm–hot intergalactic medium (WHIM), which may hold up to half the universe's ordinary matter.

## How humans reached it

The idea that a vacuum could exist was resisted for two thousand years by the ancient Greek doctrine of *horror vacui*—nature abhors a vacuum. Torricelli produced the first partial vacuum with a mercury barometer in 1643; Périer showed in 1648 that atmospheric pressure drops with altitude; Guericke built the first vacuum pump in 1650 and concluded a vacuum lay between Earth and the Moon. The Michelson–Morley experiment in 1887 found no evidence for the luminiferous aether, paving the way for special relativity, in which the speed of light in vacuum is a universal constant.

Bessel measured the first stellar parallax in 1838, putting 61 Cygni about 10 light-years away. Hubble used Cepheid variable stars in 1923 to show that Andromeda is a galaxy beyond the Milky Way, establishing an expanding universe. Lemaître proposed the Big Bang in 1931. The German A-4 rocket reached about 80 km in 1942. Sputnik 1 orbited in 1957, Gagarin followed in 1961, Apollo 8 left LEO for lunar orbit in 1968, and Voyager 1 crossed into interstellar space in 2012.

## Who governs it

The 1967 Outer Space Treaty declares space the "province of all mankind," free for exploration by all states and not subject to national sovereignty; it bans nuclear weapons in space but not conventional ones. Anti-satellite weapons have been tested by the United States, Russia, China, and India. The 1979 Moon Treaty placed celestial bodies under international jurisdiction but has not been ratified by any major spacefaring nation. Since 2020 the US-led Artemis Accords have tried to align lunar governance, especially around resource use, but no single framework is accepted. The physical altitude at which national airspace ends remains legally undefined.

## What it is used for

Artificial satellites carry most of the world's communications, navigation, weather forecasting, and Earth observation. The vacuum allows telescopes to observe across the entire electromagnetic spectrum; Hubble has seen light from more than 13 billion years ago. On Earth, the ultracold temperature of space enables passive daytime radiative cooling through the atmospheric infrared window, a property photonic metamaterials can enhance.
