# History of spaceflight

Spaceflight is the use of rockets and other space technology to send people and instruments beyond Earth's atmosphere. Its modern story is shaped by three forces that took turns dominating: a Cold War rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union, a long period of international cooperation, and a more recent mix of renewed national competition and private companies.

## Theoretical roots and the first rockets

Spaceflight became possible because a small group of pioneers worked out the basic physics in the early 20th century. Konstantin Tsiolkovsky, Robert H. Goddard, and Hermann Oberth published foundational theories of rocketry, and Goddard's 1919 paper on liquid-fueled rockets was especially influential. A liquid-fueled rocket burns a propellant and an oxidizer stored separately, releasing far more energy than a solid fuel. The first vehicle to reach space was the German V-2 rocket MW 18014, on a vertical test flight in June 1944. After the war, captured V-2 hardware and engineers seeded rocket programs in both the US and the USSR, which used the technology to send the first animals into orbit in the late 1940s and early 1950s.

## The Space Race

The "Space Race" was the 20th-century competition between the US and USSR for dominance in spaceflight capability. It began in earnest on 4 October 1957, when the USSR launched Sputnik 1, the first artificial satellite. The shock prompted the United States to create NASA in 1958. The Soviet Union kept its lead for years: Sputnik 2 carried the dog Laika, and on 12 April 1961, Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space aboard Vostok 1, completing one orbit. The United States responded with Alan Shepard's suborbital flight, and the USSR then recorded more firsts, including the first woman in space (Valentina Tereshkova, Vostok 6, 16 June 1963) and the first spacewalk (Alexei Leonov, 1965). Through the Gemini program, the US mastered rendezvous and docking, the two manoeuvres needed to link spacecraft in orbit, which were prerequisites for a lunar mission.

The race culminated on 20 July 1969, when Apollo 11 landed the first humans on the Moon, fulfilling President Kennedy's stated goal. Six successful Apollo landings followed through 1972, with Apollo 13 surviving a near-disaster. The Soviet lunar program, crippled by repeated failures of its N1 rocket, was officially canceled in 1974. Alongside the Moon contest, both countries explored other planets. The Soviet Venera probe made the first soft landing on another planet (Venus, 1970) and returned images from its surface, while the US Mariner 2 performed the first successful planetary flyby (Venus, 1962).

## From rivalry to cooperation

The handshake between an Apollo astronaut and a Soyuz cosmonaut on 17 July 1975, the Apollo-Soyuz Test Project, symbolically closed the Space Race era. The US and USSR then developed common docking standards, and the Shuttle-Mir program linked American shuttles with the Russian space station Mir. This work laid the groundwork for the International Space Station (ISS), a modular space station in low Earth orbit, a region roughly 160 to 2,000 km above the surface where most satellites orbit. The first ISS module launched on 20 November 1998, and the station became the primary destination for human spaceflight after the US Space Shuttle retired in 2011. Other nations built independent capacity, with France reaching orbit in 1965 and China in 2003, when Shenzhou 5 made China the third nation with independent crewed spaceflight. China later assembled its own Tiangong station, fully operational in November 2022.

## The reusable era and new competition

Beginning with the first Space Shuttle mission on 12 April 1981, engineers designed rockets to be recovered and reflown, a class known as reusable launch vehicles. This significantly reduces the cost per kilogram delivered to orbit, and SpaceX's Falcon 9 turned the idea into routine practice through the 2010s. On 30 May 2020, SpaceX's Crew Dragon Demo-2 became the first commercial crewed mission to the ISS, opening human spaceflight to private providers under NASA's Commercial Crew Program. Companies such as Blue Origin and Northrop Grumman now build lunar landers and cargo vehicles alongside government agencies. A "New Space Race" has taken shape, driven by national programs like the US-led Artemis initiative and a China–Russia lunar base plan on one side, and a competitive commercial market on the other. The future of lunar exploration in particular is contested, with Artemis, the China–Russia partnership, and private ventures all aiming to establish a sustained human presence, and the long-term balance between renewed national competition and international or commercial cooperation unresolved.
