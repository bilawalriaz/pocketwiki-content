# Astronomy in the medieval Islamic world

Between the 8th and 15th centuries, astronomers working in Arabic from Baghdad to Samarkand to Córdoba turned a borrowed science into a new one. They absorbed Greek, Indian, and Persian material, built the first research observatories in the Islamic world, exposed mathematical flaws in Ptolemy's geocentric model, and transmitted their tools and tables to Latin Europe and to China.

## Foundations

The first Arabic astronomical texts were Indian and Persian. Around 770, Muhammad al-Fazari and Yaʿqūb ibn Ṭāriq produced the *Zij al-Sindhind*, based on an 8th-century Indian work brought to the caliph al-Mansur's court. Arab astronomers adopted the Indian *sine* in place of the Greek *chord*. Ptolemy's *Almagest* (the dominant geocentric spherical-Earth model) was translated at least five times in the late 8th and 9th centuries and became the main authority.

Practical religious needs pushed the science forward. Islam requires knowing the five daily prayer times and the *qibla* (direction toward the Kaaba in Mecca), so astronomers built accurate solar models and portable instruments.

The turning point came under the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun (early 9th century), who funded the House of Wisdom in Baghdad and built the first observatory there. His court's *Zij al-Sindhind* of 830, by al-Khwarizmi, tabulated the Sun, Moon, and the five planets Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn, and introduced Ptolemaic concepts. Arabic astronomy then stopped merely translating and began producing original work.

## Instruments

The **astrolabe**, a flat brass disc engraved with a star map and rotating plates, was the signature instrument. The earliest known Islamic example dates to 927/8 CE. Given a latitude, a user could read the time, find the qibla, and locate any visible celestial body. It spread to Europe by the 10th century, where it stimulated Latin interest in mathematics. A mariner's astrolabe later allowed sailors to fix latitude at sea. Islamic astronomers also refined **celestial globes** (126 survive, oldest from the 11th century), **armillary spheres**, **quadrants**, and **equatoria** (geometric calculators for planetary positions, earliest planetary example from the 11th-century Ibn al-Samh). Al-Biruni designed a geared mechanical lunisolar calendar.

## Criticising Ptolemy

By the 10th century, Muslim astronomers were openly questioning the *Almagest*. In 850, al-Farghani's *Kitab fi Jawami* revised Ptolemy's values for the obliquity of the ecliptic, the precession of the apogees, and the Earth's circumference. Ibn Yunus found that the rate of axial precession was one degree every 70¼ years, not Ptolemy's one degree per 100 years. Between 1025 and 1028, Ibn al-Haytham's *Al-Shukuk ala Batlamyus* ("Doubts on Ptolemy") catalogued problems without rejecting geocentrism.

The deepest response came from the Maragha school, named after the observatory built under Hulagu Khan at Maragha in 1259, directed by Nasir al-Din al-Tusi. Two mathematical devices mattered most. The **Tusi couple**: a small circle rolling inside a larger circle of twice the diameter. A point on the small circle's circumference oscillates in a straight line, producing linear motion from pure circles, a requirement of Aristotelian physics. The **Urdi lemma** (Mu'ayyad al-Din al-Urdi, c. 1266): a geometric construction that replaces the Ptolemaic *equant* (an off-centre reference point that made planetary distances swing wildly) while preserving predictions. Both devices let later astronomers, including Ibn al-Shatir of Damascus (c. 1375), build planetary models without the equant's absurd distance changes. Tusi's 1261 *Tadkhira* listed 16 problems in Ptolemy and set off a 50-year reform program. None of these astronomers abandoned geocentrism.

## Earth in motion?

The rotation question was debated but not resolved. Abu Rayhan al-Biruni (b. 973) noted that a rotating Earth would be consistent with his parameters but classified the question as natural philosophy, not mathematics, and in his *Masudic Canon* left the Earth stationary at the universe's centre. His contemporary Abu Sa'id al-Sijzi accepted axial rotation and built an astrolabe on that premise. Tusi and Ali Qushji (b. 1403) at Maragha and Samarkand produced arguments for Earth's motion resembling those later used by Copernicus, but the community did not commit to a moving Earth.

In 12th-century al-Andalus, Nur al-Din al-Bitruji proposed a planetary theory avoiding both epicycles and eccentrics, but his numerical predictions were less accurate than Ptolemy's.

## Transmission to Europe and China

From the 12th century, Arabic works were translated into Latin. al-Battani's *Kitab az-Zij* (d. 929) was frequently cited; Copernicus mentioned al-Battani at least 23 times in *De revolutionibus* and again in the *Commentariolus*. Michael Scot's 1217 Latin translation of al-Bitruji's *Book of Cosmology* circulated in scholastic circles, and Copernicus cited the system while discussing the order of the inferior planets.

The possible link to Copernicus is sharper. His replacement of the equant by two epicycles in the *Commentariolus* matches Ibn al-Shatir's earlier construction, and his lunar and Mercury models are identical to Ibn al-Shatir's. Routes of transmission proposed include Byzantine Greek translations of Tusi (manuscripts still extant in Italy), Proclus's *Commentary on Euclid*, and Nicole Oresme's *Questiones de Spera*. Some historians hold the influence direct; others argue Copernicus could have reached the same ideas independently. The question, first raised by Otto Neugebauer in 1957, remains open. Copernicus explicitly names al-Battani, Averroes, Thabit ibn Qurra, al-Zarqali, and al-Bitruji in *De revolutionibus*, but shows no awareness of the later Maragha astronomers.

In China, Islamic astronomy arrived through the Mongols. Kublai Khan brought Iranian astronomers to Beijing, and in 1267 Jamal ad-Din, formerly of Maragha, presented seven Persian astronomical instruments, a terrestrial globe, an armillary sphere, and an almanac known as the *Wannian Li*. He became first director of the Islamic Astronomical Bureau in Beijing, which operated alongside the Chinese bureau for about four centuries. The Chinese astronomer Guo Shoujing's instruments and his *Shoushili* calendar of 1281 show traces of Islamic influence, including use of decimals and cubic interpolation. The *Huihui Lifa* (Muslim System of Calendrical Astronomy) was compiled in Chinese around 1384 and reprinted into the early 18th century, though the Qing court officially ended the tradition in 1659. In early Joseon Korea, a Korean translation of the *Huihui Lifa* was studied under Sejong the Great in the 15th century.

## Observatories

Malik Shah I established the first large observatory, probably at Isfahan, where Omar Khayyam and collaborators produced the *jalali* (Persian Solar) calendar, ancestor of the modern Solar Hijri calendar still used in Iran and Afghanistan. Maragha (1259), under Tusi, ran for 50 years. Ulugh Beg built his Samarkand observatory in 1420, which yielded a new set of planetary tables before its remains were excavated in 1908. In Ottoman Constantinople, Taqi al-Din founded a comparably scaled observatory in 1577; it was destroyed in 1580 after opponents argued against its use for astrological prognostication, since Ottoman clergy accepted astronomy but opposed astrology.
