# Physical cosmology

Physical cosmology is the branch of physics that applies physical laws to the universe as a whole, asking where it came from, how it is structured, how it changes, and how it will end. The guiding idea is that the cosmos is a single evolving system whose largest behavior follows from the same gravity that drops an apple to the floor.

The modern subject rests on Einstein's 1915 general relativity, which describes gravity as the curvature of spacetime. Mass and energy tell spacetime how to bend, and that bending tells matter how to move. Einstein initially wanted a static universe, so in 1917 he added a uniform energy density, the cosmological constant (Λ), to hold the cosmos up against its own gravity. The patch was unstable, and Alexander Friedmann soon showed that the equations themselves prefer expanding or contracting solutions, described by the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) metric. The FLRW universe is homogeneous, the same everywhere on large scales, and isotropic, the same in every direction, with a spatial geometry that can be open, flat, or closed.

The mathematics predicted expansion, and telescopes soon confirmed it. In the 1920s Vesto Slipher measured redshifts in spiral nebulae, shifts of spectral lines toward longer wavelengths that arise from motion away from the observer. Edwin Hubble then proved these nebulae were external galaxies and found that recession speed grows with distance: a galaxy twice as far moves away twice as fast, a relation now called Hubble's law. Georges Lemaître argued that a presently expanding universe must have begun from an extremely dense, hot state, the "primeval atom" that became the Big Bang. A rival steady-state picture, championed by Fred Hoyle, held that new matter continuously appears to fill the gaps as space expands, so the cosmos has no beginning. The decisive evidence came in 1965, when Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson discovered the cosmic microwave background (CMB), a faint thermal glow of about 2.7 kelvin that fills all of space. The CMB is the stretched remnant of the hot early fireball, exactly what the Big Bang predicts and what steady-state models struggle to reproduce.

Since the 1990s, precision observations have hardened these ideas into a standard model called ΛCDM, Lambda Cold Dark Matter. About 68% of the universe's energy content is dark energy, the modern reading of Einstein's old cosmological constant and the cause of an accelerated expansion discovered in 1998 through distant supernovae whose faintness showed they were farther away than a decelerating universe would allow. About 27% is cold dark matter, a non-luminous substance that clumps into halos around galaxies and was originally inferred from galaxy rotation: stars at the outer edges of spirals move as fast as stars near the center, which is impossible if only visible matter, whose gravity falls off rapidly with distance, were present. The remaining 5% is the ordinary atoms that make up stars, planets, and people. The model is tested through the CMB mapped by COBE and WMAP, through large galaxy surveys like the Sloan Digital Sky Survey, and through measurements of how fast the universe expands at different epochs.

Cosmic history is written in the cooling of the expanding fireball. In the first minutes the cosmos was so hot that nuclei could not hold together, and Big Bang nucleosynthesis forged almost all of its hydrogen and helium along with a trace of lithium. As expansion cooled the universe, radiation lost out to non-relativistic matter as the dominant energy component, and gravity slowly pulled that matter into stars, galaxies, and quasars. Much later, dark energy came to dominate and began driving an ever-faster expansion.

Several big questions remain. No one has directly detected a dark matter particle, so its nature is unknown. Dark energy may be the vacuum energy of empty space, but the standard quantum estimate is about 120 orders of magnitude larger than what the expansion actually requires, the cosmological constant problem. Baryogenesis, the early process that left the universe with more matter than antimatter, requires CP-violation, an asymmetry between matter and antimatter in physical laws, beyond what particle colliders have measured. Cosmic inflation, a brief episode of exponential expansion a fraction of a second after the Big Bang, explains why the universe is so flat and so uniform across regions that were never in causal contact, yet it still lacks direct confirmation. Under current evidence the expansion will continue accelerating toward a cold, dilute Big Freeze, with a Big Rip or recollapse as alternatives.

A new window opened in 2016, when LIGO detected gravitational waves, ripples in spacetime produced by merging black holes, confirming a 1915 prediction of general relativity and offering a fresh way to probe black holes and the early universe. Competing frameworks, such as MOND, which tweaks Newtonian dynamics instead of adding dark matter, and string-inspired models, continue to challenge the standard picture, keeping physical cosmology a science where its most basic constituents are still unknown.
