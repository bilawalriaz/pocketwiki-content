# Intermediate-mass black hole

An intermediate-mass black hole (IMBH) is a black hole whose mass sits in the gap between two known populations: stellar black holes, formed from collapsing massive stars at a few to about a hundred solar masses, and supermassive black holes, hundreds of thousands to billions of solar masses, sitting at the centres of large galaxies. IMBHs occupy the range from about 100 to 100,000 solar masses, too heavy to be made by ordinary stellar collapse but too light to be central engines of large galaxies.

Evidence for IMBHs is weaker than for either neighbouring class. No IMBH has yet been confirmed through a direct dynamical mass measurement, an estimate obtained by measuring the gravitational pull of the black hole on nearby objects whose motions can be tracked. Candidates rest on indirect signatures, and many have later been disputed. The strongest individual detections come from gravitational waves, ripples in space produced when masses accelerate violently, because those events allow the masses of the merging black holes to be read directly from the waveform, the shape of the signal over time.

The first such event, GW190521, was observed on 21 May 2019 and published on 2 September 2020. Two black holes of about 85 and 65 solar masses merged to form a remnant of about 142 solar masses, with roughly 8 solar masses radiated away as gravitational waves, placing the final mass in the IMBH range. A second IMBH merger, GW231123, was observed on 23 November 2023 and announced by the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA collaborations in July 2025.

Other lines of evidence are more tentative. Low-luminosity active galactic nuclei (AGN), galaxies whose central black hole is actively pulling in matter and emitting radiation, contain accreting black holes whose masses can be estimated by reverberation mapping, a technique that infers mass by measuring delays between fluctuations in the AGN's light and the response of surrounding gas clouds. The spiral galaxy NGC 4395, about 4 megaparsecs away, hosts a black hole of about 3.6 × 10⁵ solar masses, near the top of the IMBH range. A 2018 analysis of one million optical spectra from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey identified 305 candidate IMBHs, of which 10 showed X-ray emission consistent with accretion. Some ultraluminous X-ray sources (ULXs), point-like objects shining brighter in X-rays than any known stellar process, in nearby star-forming galaxies such as M82 are suspected IMBHs of a few hundred to a thousand solar masses, though only a dynamical mass measurement from the optical spectrum of a companion star could confirm the identification.

Globular clusters, compact, gravitationally bound assemblies of roughly 100,000 old stars, have also been claimed as IMBH homes based on high stellar velocities near their centres, but none of these claims has survived scrutiny. M31 G1 in Andromeda was once a leading candidate, yet its stellar velocities can be fit equally well without any central black hole. A 2017 claim of a few-thousand-solar-mass IMBH in 47 Tucanae was overturned by a re-analysis with fuller pulsar data, where pulsars are rapidly spinning neutron stars that act as precise local clocks. In 2023, motions in the nearby cluster Messier 4 revealed roughly 800 solar masses of unexplained central mass, and a July 2024 study of fast-moving stars in Omega Centauri pointed to an IMBH of at least 8,200 solar masses; both remain candidates rather than confirmations.

Near the Milky Way's centre, several candidate IMBHs have been proposed. The star cluster IRS 13E was reported in 2004 to host an IMBH of about 1,300 solar masses, three light-years from Sagittarius A*, the Milky Way's own supermassive black hole, though a 2005 dynamical study cast doubt on the claim. In 2015, a high-velocity gas cloud called CO-0.40-0.22 was best fit by a black hole of around 100,000 solar masses, although competing explanations such as supernovae have since complicated the picture. A 2018 Keio University study of molecular gas streams near the galactic centre suggested an invisible object of about 32,000 solar masses, designated HCN-0.009-0.044.

Three formation scenarios are widely discussed. The first is accretion-driven growth: stellar-mass black holes in a dense environment merge with other compact objects and pull in surrounding gas, gradually climbing into the IMBH range. The second is runaway stellar collisions inside dense young star clusters, where repeated mergers build up a single very massive star that then collapses. The third treats IMBHs as primordial, born directly from the hot, dense conditions of the early universe. A related possibility is direct collapse of a single star whose pre-supernova helium core exceeds about 133 solar masses, large enough to avoid a pair-instability supernova that would otherwise completely blow the star apart, which would require an initial stellar mass above roughly 260 solar masses; such stars could form through repeated collisions in young star clusters.

If IMBHs exist in large numbers, they offer a natural route by which supermassive black holes could grow: smaller black holes fall toward galactic centres, merge, and accrete, steadily accumulating mass over cosmic time. The very existence of supermassive black holes in the early universe has motivated searches for their IMBH seeds. The M–sigma relation, which links black hole mass to the velocity dispersion of stars in a galaxy's central bulge, predicts IMBHs of 10⁴ to 10⁶ solar masses in low-luminosity galaxies, with the smallest predicted example at about 50,000 solar masses in the nucleus of the galaxy RGG 118.
