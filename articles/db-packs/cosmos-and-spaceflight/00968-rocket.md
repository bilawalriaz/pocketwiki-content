# Rocket

A rocket is a vehicle that produces thrust by expelling propellant at high speed. Because it carries its own oxidiser, a rocket does not need atmospheric oxygen and works in the vacuum of space. This self-contained propulsion enabled the Space Age, making satellites, human spaceflight, and exploration beyond Earth possible.

## How a rocket works

A rocket stores propellant in tanks and burns it inside an engine, then accelerates the hot gas through a de Laval nozzle, a convergent-divergent nozzle that expands the gas to supersonic speed. Thrust arises from Newton's third law: ejecting mass rearward pushes the vehicle forward. Propellant combinations include liquid pairs such as RP-1/LOX (kerosene and liquid oxygen), solid mixtures, hybrid systems, and monopropellants.

**Specific impulse (Isp)** measures how efficiently a rocket uses propellant: the impulse delivered per unit weight of propellant consumed. **Delta-v (Δv)** is the total velocity change a vehicle can deliver and therefore the budget for any mission. These are linked by the **Tsiolkovsky rocket equation**:

Δv = ve · ln(m₀ / m₁)

where ve is exhaust velocity, m₀ is the initial mass (full tanks), and m₁ is the final mass (after propellant is spent). Because the relationship is logarithmic, doubling the propellant does not double the delta-v; high **mass ratios** (m₀/m₁) and high exhaust velocities are needed to reach orbit, which requires roughly 9.7 km/s of delta-v.

A single stage struggles to achieve this because tanks and engines needed for later burns are dead weight during the early burn. **Multistaging** discards empty tanks and spent engines during ascent, keeping each stage's mass ratio reasonable. The **Oberth effect** makes a burn more energy-efficient at higher speed, so upper stages fire near apogee. Propulsive efficiency peaks when vehicle speed equals exhaust speed, which is why rockets excel at hypersonic regimes but are inefficient for slow atmospheric flight.

## A condensed history

Gunpowder rockets appeared in Song Dynasty China by the 13th century, and the *Huolongjing* (c. 1350) by Jiao Yu described the first known multistage rocket, the "fire-dragon issuing from the water." The Mongols spread the technology westward; Hasan al-Rammah compiled 22 gunpowder rocket recipes in *al-furusiyyah wa al-manasib al-harbiyya* between 1270 and 1280, and Konrad Kyeser illustrated rockets in *Bellifortis* around 1405. In late-18th-century India, Mysorean rockets under Hyder Ali introduced iron casings, raising effective range to about 2,000 yards from a roughly 100-yard baseline.

Modern rocketry began with **Konstantin Tsiolkovsky's** 1903 theoretical framework and **Robert Goddard's** 1926 launch of the first liquid-fuel rocket, which used a supersonic de Laval nozzle and raised propellant efficiency from about 2% to 64%. The Soviet Gas Dynamics Laboratory developed solid-propellant rockets from 1921, with a 1928 launch reaching roughly 1,300 metres; these became prototypes for the Katyusha launcher and powered the first rocket-assisted aircraft takeoff in 1931. The German **V-2** became the first human-made object in space on 20 June 1944, crossing the Kármán line. After the war, Operation Paperclip brought scientists including Wernher von Braun to the United States, and Cold War competition produced ICBMs and orbital launchers such as Vostok, Soyuz, and Proton in the Soviet Union. The Saturn V's three stages delivered Apollo 11 to the Moon in 1969, demonstrating the multistage approach at escape-velocity scale.

## Where rockets are used

Beyond spaceflight, rockets serve as military missiles and ICBMs, as **sounding rockets** for atmospheric and microgravity research, as launch-escape systems and ejection seats for crew safety, and as model rockets for hobbyists. The Space Shuttle carried roughly 100,000 kg to orbit at about 16% energy efficiency. Since the 2010s, private companies have introduced reusable boosters and mass production, lowering per-launch cost.

Two engineering questions remain open. **Single-stage-to-orbit (SSTO)** vehicles are theoretically possible but impractical, because the exponential growth of propellant mass leaves negligible payload without staging. **Reusability versus expendability** is an economic trade-off: reusable systems need more upfront investment but spread that cost across many flights. Liquid and solid propellants balance performance, storability, and complexity differently, and the cumulative environmental effect of frequent large launches is an emerging constraint on future growth.
