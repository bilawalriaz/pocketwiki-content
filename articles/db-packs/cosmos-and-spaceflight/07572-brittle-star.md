# Brittle star

Brittle stars are echinoderms in the class Ophiuroidea, distinguished from sea stars by a sharply centralised body plan. They diverged in the Early Ordovician, over 480 million years ago, and now include more than 2,000 living species, with over 1,200 found deeper than 200 m. Total modern diversity may exceed 3,000 species, making Ophiuroidea the most species-rich living echinoderm class. The name comes from Greek *óphis* (serpent) and *ourá* (tail), describing the snaking arms.

The body is a flat central disk with five long, slender, whip-like arms reaching up to 60 cm in the largest specimens. Two clades sit within the class: Ophiurida, the true brittle stars, and Euryalida, the basket stars, whose arms are forked and branched. The disk is sharply distinct from the arms, and all internal organs, including the digestive and reproductive systems, remain confined to the disk, unlike in sea stars where these tissues extend into the arms.

The mouth sits on the underside of the disk, ringed by five toothed jaws, and this same opening also expels waste. A short oesophagus leads to a stomach with ten digestive pouches lined with hepatic cells, pouches that essentially never reach into the arms. The coelomic cavity is strongly reduced compared with other echinoderms. The water vascular system ends in tube feet, but these lack suckers and ampullae, and in adults they function mainly as sensory organs rather than for locomotion. The madreporite, the entry point for water, usually lies inside one of the jaw plates rather than on the upper surface as in sea stars. A nerve ring around the disk sends radial nerves down each arm, passing through a canal at the base of the vertebral ossicles.

Brittle stars show the strongest five-fold radial symmetry of any echinoderm, yet they move as if bilaterally symmetrical. One arm acts as the leading axis while the other four work as two pairs of opposite levers, propelling the animal in rapid rowing jerks. The leading arm can be swapped at any direction change because the nervous system treats all five arms as equivalent. Ophiurida scuttle horizontally, while Euryalida move vertically, coiling their arms around structures and remaining attached even after death.

The skeleton is calcium carbonate as calcite, organised as fused ossicles (small skeletal plates). The central disk is armoured by a test, while each arm is built from a chain of vertebral ossicles linked by ball-and-socket joints and longitudinal muscles, giving the serpentine flexibility. Joints allow side-to-side bending but not upward bending, except in basket stars, whose arms flex in all directions. Lateral plates bear spines that grip the substrate; in Euryalida these become hook-like clubs.

Most ophiuroids are scavengers or detritivores, using tube feet to move particles into the mouth. Many in the family Ophiuridae are active carnivores, preying on small crustaceans and worms. Basket stars extend one arm while anchoring with the other four, trapping plankton and bacteria in mucus on the arm surface. Some cling to coral to browse on polyps, and *Ophiopsammus maculata* in New Zealand fjords feeds on *Nothofagus* pollen falling from overhanging trees. Respiration and excretion occur through ten cilia-lined bursal sacs opening at the arm bases, with oxygen carried by a separate hemal system distinct from the water vascular system.

Most species have separate sexes, with gonads inside the disk releasing gametes through the bursae into open water. Some species brood larvae in the bursae, and a few, such as *Amphipholis squamata*, are viviparous (giving live birth), nourishing embryos through the bursal wall. Other species release an ophiopluteus larva, a free-swimming form with four pairs of ciliated arms that develops directly into a juvenile without the attached stage seen in sea star larvae. Brittle stars typically mature in 2–3 years, reach full size in 3–4, and live around 5 years.

Brittle stars shed and regenerate arms to escape predators, similar to lizard tail autotomy (self-amputation). Unless all arms are lost, the missing parts regrow, and in the family Amphiuridae, gut and gonad tissue can also regenerate. Detached arms cannot themselves regrow a body. A few species reproduce asexually by fissiparity, splitting the disk in half. In *Ophiocomella ophiactoides* this cycle takes about 89 days, so a single individual can theoretically yield around 15 clones per year.

Brittle stars occupy nearly every marine habitat from poles to tropics, including abyssal depths beyond 6,000 m, and a few even tolerate brackish water, an ability almost unknown among echinoderms. Over 60 species are bioluminescent, most producing green light. Their strong calcitic skeleton makes them unsuitable as food, and none are venomous; their defences are fast movement and arm autotomy, though they actively hunt small crustaceans.
