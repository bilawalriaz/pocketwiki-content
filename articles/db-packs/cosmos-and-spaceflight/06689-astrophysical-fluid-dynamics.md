# Astrophysical fluid dynamics

Astrophysical fluid dynamics applies fluid mechanics to the gases and plasmas in outer space, including the material that makes up the Sun and other stars. The governing equations are the same ones used in ordinary fluid dynamics: the continuity equation, the Navier–Stokes equations, and Euler's equations for collisional fluids. What differs is the setting. Most calculations concern fluids moving through a near vacuum with negligible gravity, where the behaviour is shaped by magnetic fields, supersonic motion, and self-gravity.

The fluid is almost always compressible, electrically conducting, and threaded by a magnetic field. When the electrical conductivity is high, the ideal magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) equations are a good approximation, a treatment that treats the fluid and the magnetic field as a single coupled system. In dense, weakly ionised star-forming regions this assumption fails and non-ideal MHD effects such as ambipolar diffusion (in which neutral particles slip past the magnetic field lines threaded through them) become important.

## The interstellar medium as a fluid

The interstellar medium is not at rest. Supernova explosions, stellar winds, radiation pressure, and the time-varying gravitational field of spiral density waves drive the gas into supersonic motion. Because supersonic flow almost always produces shock waves, shocks must be included in any realistic calculation. The galactic magnetic field is dynamically significant, so the equations used are those of compressible magnetohydrodynamics rather than ordinary hydrodynamics.

Applications of the subject include stellar dynamics, accretion disks around compact objects, astrophysical jets, the behaviour of Newtonian fluids in astrophysical settings, and the large-scale fluid dynamics of galaxies.

## Star formation as a worked example

Star formation shows how these tools combine. Stars condense out of the interstellar medium, mostly inside giant molecular clouds such as the Rosette Nebula. An ordinary cloud collapses under its own self-gravity only if its mass reaches several thousand solar masses, far larger than any single star. Smaller clouds still form stars, but only through a sequence of linked processes.

In a giant molecular cloud the magnetic pressure is much larger than the thermal pressure, and the gas is threaded by a magnetic field strong enough to resist compression. MHD waves travelling through such a medium can squeeze gas into denser regions, but on their own they cannot raise the density to the point where self-gravity takes over. The missing piece is the thermal behaviour of the gas. Cosmic rays heat the gas while radiative processes cool it, and the resulting thermal equilibrium admits three coexisting phases at a single pressure: a warm, low-density phase; an intermediate, unstable phase; and a cold, dense phase.

A supernova shock or a spiral density wave compresses the gas from the warm phase into the unstable one. MHD waves then drive fragments of that gas into the cold phase, where self-gravity is finally strong enough for collapse into stars. The cold-phase fragments are the immediate precursors of individual stars.

## Core equations in brief

The continuity equation expresses conservation of mass in a flowing fluid. For steady flow through a fixed volume, the mass flow rate is $\dot{m} = \rho v A$, where $\rho$ is density, $v$ is velocity, and $A$ is cross-sectional area. Equating the flow at two points gives $\rho_1 v_1 A_1 = \rho_2 v_2 A_2$. For an incompressible fluid ($\rho_1 = \rho_2$) this reduces to $v_1 A_1 = v_2 A_2$, the familiar rule that constricted flow speeds up. The same conservation principle, written in differential form, underlies calculations ranging from accretion onto neutron stars to the large-scale circulation of gas in galaxies, and serves as the starting point from which the Navier–Stokes and Euler equations are derived.
