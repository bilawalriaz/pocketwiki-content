# Old Kingdom of Egypt

The Old Kingdom (c. 2700–2200 BC) was the first of three "golden ages" in ancient Egyptian history, a sustained peak of civilization in the lower Nile Valley and the "Age of the Pyramids." It was defined by centralized royal power, monumental stone architecture, and the pharaoh as a living god, a model that shaped Egyptian culture for roughly two thousand years.

## Political Foundation

The Old Kingdom began with the Third Dynasty under King Djoser, whose architect Imhotep designed the Step Pyramid at Saqqara, the first large stone building in Egypt. The project showed that the king could mobilize permanent state labor and skilled craft at a scale previously impossible. Formerly independent territories were reorganized into **nomes**, provinces governed by officials called **nomarchs** who answered to the crown. The pharaoh was believed to be the earthly incarnation of the god Horus, with the primary duty of maintaining *ma'at*, the cosmic order that kept the Nile flooding, the harvests coming, and society stable.

## The Pyramid Age

The Fourth Dynasty (c. 2613–2494 BC) marked the political and architectural zenith. Sneferu, the founder, experimented with pyramid design and built three major structures at Dahshur, including the Bent Pyramid. His son Khufu, grandson Khafre, and great-grandson Menkaure then built the Great Pyramids of Giza. These projects drew on the king's absolute authority over labor, seasonal flooding cycles that freed farmers for construction, and a centralized bureaucracy capable of feeding and housing large workforces. The same dynasty pushed Egypt's military frontier south into Nubia, securing gold and granite quarries, and north into Canaan.

## Religion, Trade, and Art

The Fifth Dynasty (c. 2494–2345 BC) saw the cult of the sun god **Ra** rise to prominence alongside the older Horus cult, and pharaohs built dedicated sun temples in addition to their pyramids. Egypt extended its trade networks, sending ships down the Red Sea to the Kingdom of Punt for ebony, incense, and other luxury goods, and importing cedar from Lebanon.

Old Kingdom art was functional, built to serve religious ritual and royal ideology rather than aesthetics. It followed three rules: **frontality** (figures face the viewer squarely), **composite composition** (artists combined multiple viewpoints, such as a face in profile with a torso facing forward, to show each body part most clearly), and **hierarchy of scale** (the most important figure is drawn largest, regardless of position). Sculptors favored hard stones like granite, and colors carried symbolic meaning: black evoked the fertile Nile silt, green signaled rebirth and vegetation.

## Decline and Collapse

Power shifted during the Sixth Dynasty (c. 2345–2181 BC). The nomarchs, originally royal appointees, became hereditary lords who kept tax revenue locally and built their own tombs. Central authority weakened. Pepi II, who reigned from c. 2278 to 2214 BC, had one of the longest reigns in recorded history; his later years saw succession disputes and decentralized power. A severe, multi-decadal drought around 2200–2150 BC, recorded in Nile flood records and wider paleoclimate data, reduced agricultural output and triggered famine. Central government could not respond, and the Old Kingdom dissolved into the First Intermediate Period, an era of civil war and local rule.

## Population Origins

Genetic analysis of a high-status Old Kingdom individual dating to c. 2855–2570 BC, published in 2025, shows ancestry primarily from North African Neolithic populations, with about 20% traceable to the eastern Fertile Crescent. This provides direct biological evidence of early population movement from Mesopotamia into Egypt, paralleling the archaeological record of shared crops, animals, and writing between the two regions. Egyptologists still debate the relative weight of drought and political fragmentation in the collapse, the attribution of the Great Sphinx (traditionally Khafre, though Djedefre has been proposed), and whether the Seventh and Eighth Dynasties belong to the Old Kingdom or to the First Intermediate Period that followed.
