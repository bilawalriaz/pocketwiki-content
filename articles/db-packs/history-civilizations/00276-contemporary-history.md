# Contemporary history

Contemporary history is the period from about 1945 to the present. Its defining feature is a long ideological contest between two superpowers, the United States and the Soviet Union, fought through alliances, proxy wars, and economic rivalry rather than direct combat. Around this contest clustered almost every other large story of the era: the dismantling of European empires, an economic boom broken by repeated shocks, the rise of the internet, and new global problems such as climate change and pandemic disease.

## The Cold War and the world it shaped

The Cold War (1947–1991) divided the globe into a US-led Western Bloc and a Soviet-led Eastern Bloc. Both sides possessed nuclear weapons, so direct war was too costly; instead they competed through military alliances (NATO from 1949, the Warsaw Pact from 1955), arms races, and proxy conflicts such as Korea (1950–1953) and Vietnam. The United Nations, founded in 1945, sat above both blocs as a forum for diplomacy, though it could not prevent superpower confrontations. Most newly independent states, unwilling to pick a side, joined the Non-Aligned Movement. The contest ended with the Revolutions of 1989 and the Soviet Union's dissolution in 1991, leaving the United States as the sole superpower and opening the way for democratic transitions across Eastern Europe.

## Decolonisation and regional conflict

Between 1945 and 1975, almost every European colonial empire in Asia and Africa was dissolved, creating dozens of new states. Many inherited borders and institutions drawn for colonial convenience, which helps explain later political instability. The Middle East became a particular flashpoint, shaped by the Arab–Israeli wars of 1948, 1967, and 1973, the 1979 Iranian Revolution, and the rise of oil as a strategic commodity. After 1991, new conflicts emerged in the Balkans, the 2003 Iraq War reshaped the Middle East, and the Arab Spring of 2010–2012 spread popular uprisings across the region. The September 2001 attacks on the United States launched a long "War on Terror" that reshaped security policy worldwide.

## Economic boom, crisis, and globalisation

The Western post-war decades saw sustained growth, underpinned by US Marshall Plan aid to rebuild Europe and the Bretton Woods system of fixed exchange rates, the IMF, and the World Bank. Growth spread: Japan's "economic miracle" and the Four Asian Tigers (Hong Kong, Singapore, South Korea, Taiwan) shifted wealth toward Asia, and China's reform programme from 1978 turned it into a major exporter. This long boom was punctuated by the 1973 and 1979 oil crises and broken by the 2008 global financial crisis, which triggered the Great Recession. After 1991, trade liberalisation, the expansion of the European Union, and digital technology accelerated globalisation, knitting economies together more tightly than before.

## Information, science, and society

A cluster of breakthroughs defined modern life: the Apollo 11 Moon landing in 1969, the contraceptive pill from 1960, and from the 1990s onward the spread of the internet and mobile phones, which together produced the Information Age. Major scientific milestones included the Human Genome Project and the discovery of the Higgs boson in 2012. Culturally, English consolidated its role as a global lingua franca, and Western popular culture spread through television, film, and later social media. Urbanisation accelerated worldwide as populations shifted from countryside to city.

## New global risks

Two challenges now sit at the centre of contemporary history. Climate change, driven by human activity, is producing global warming, rising seas, and more extreme weather, and its management is contested. The COVID-19 pandemic, which spread globally from late 2019, exposed how deeply interconnected the world had become, causing a sharp recession and reshaping work and travel. Earlier health crises, including the HIV/AIDS epidemic from 1981 to the present, had already shown that disease could travel with globalised trade and movement.

## Open questions

The post-1991 belief that liberal democracy had become the permanent default ("the end of history") has been complicated by the rise of authoritarian and populist movements. Whether globalisation has chiefly spread prosperity or widened inequality remains debated, as does whether current climate agreements are sufficient. Decolonisation's long shadow, in unstable borders and institutions, is still being assessed.
