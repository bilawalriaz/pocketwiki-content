# Ancient Greek astronomy

Greek astronomers treated the sky as a geometric problem to be solved with circles, spheres, and arithmetic, and over roughly eight centuries they built a predictive model of the heavens that survived, in its essentials, until the sixteenth century.

## The shared starting picture

All serious Greek astronomy assumed, following Ptolemy's summary in the *Almagest* (1.2), that the heavens are spherical, the celestial sphere rotates, the Earth is a sphere, the Earth sits at the cosmic centre and is effectively a point compared to the stellar sphere, and the Earth does not move. Geocentrism, the doctrine that the Earth is stationary at the centre of the cosmos, is the name for this picture; heliocentrism places the Sun at the centre instead. From these axioms everything else was derived.

Two technical terms shaped the work. A **planet** was any *planētēs*, or "wanderer", a point of light that moved against the fixed stars; the naked-eye planets are Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn. A **constellation** is a named pattern of fixed stars; the 48-figure scheme standardised by Eudoxus, transmitted through Aratus's poem *Phaenomena* (c. 270 BC), survives in modern star maps largely via Latin translations.

## The two-sphere model

The first geometric framework was Plato and Eudoxus's two-sphere model in the early fourth century BC: an outer celestial sphere carrying the stars, Sun, Moon, and planets, and an inner terrestrial sphere fixed at the common centre. The Sun's annual path on the celestial sphere, the **ecliptic**, is tilted about 23° to the celestial equator (the projection of the Earth's equator onto the sky), and the two intersection points mark the equinoxes while the two extreme tilts mark the solstices.

Eudoxus then tried to explain the irregular motions of the planets by nesting several rotating spheres around each one, each with its own axis and speed, the homocentric theory. The model failed observationally: Autolycus noted that the Moon's apparent size changes, which a fixed-distance Earth–Moon system cannot produce.

## Eccentrics and epicycles

The fix, developed by Apollonius of Perga (c. 240–190 BC) and elaborated by Hipparchus and Ptolemy, was to abandon the single circle and combine two. An **eccentric** places the observer off-centre, so a body moving on a true circle appears to speed up and slow down. An **epicycle** puts the body on a small circle whose centre rides on a larger circle (the **deferent**) around the Earth, so the planet traces a looping path. The two devices are mathematically equivalent for the Sun; for the Moon and planets they are combined, and for Mercury and Venus Ptolemy required that the epicycle's centre stay on the line between Earth and Sun, which keeps them within a fixed angular distance, their **bounded elongation**.

Hipparchus (2nd century BC) used these tools, drew heavily on Babylonian records no earlier Greek writer seems to have had, compiled the first systematic star catalogue, discovered **precession of the equinoxes** (the slow westward drift of the equinox points along the ecliptic), and showed that the tropical year (the time between successive returns of the Sun to the same equinox) is slightly less than 365.25 days while the sidereal year (measured against the fixed stars) is slightly more.

## The one exception: Aristarchus

Geocentrism was nearly universal, but two variants are recorded. Philolaus's fifth-century Pythagorean system posited an unseen Central Fire. Heraclides Ponticus proposed a geo-heliocentric system, with the Sun circling the Earth and the other planets circling the Sun. In the third century BC Aristarchus of Samos, sometimes called the "ancient Copernicus", placed the Sun at the centre; he was the only premodern author to do so, and his model did not survive. In a separate work he used geometry to estimate the relative sizes and distances of the Sun and Moon in Earth-radii; both he and Hipparchus drastically underestimated the Sun's distance. Eratosthenes later measured the Earth's circumference at 252,000 *stades* (an ancient Greek unit of about 157 m), around 39,690 km, close to the true 40,120 km.

## Ptolemy's *Almagest*

Claudius Ptolemy, working in Roman Alexandria in the 2nd century AD, gathered the tradition into the *Almagest* (originally *Mathematical Composition*), a 13-book treatise. Books 1–2 set out the spherical framework, Books 3–5 give theories of the Sun and Moon and predict eclipses, Books 7–8 catalogue 1,022 stars in 48 constellations, and Books 9–13 treat the five planets, including retrograde motion (the apparent backward loop planets make against the stars) and departures from the ecliptic. The *Almagest* dominated European and Islamic astronomy until the sixteenth century and entered India through translations such as the *Yavanajataka* (2nd century AD), seeding the *Romaka* and *Paulisa Siddhantas* of Varahamihira's *Pañca-siddhāntikā*.

## Limits and legacy

The Ptolemaic system accounted for changing planetary brightness and retrograde loops using only uniform circular motion combined through eccentrics and epicycles, and its predictive accuracy held for roughly 1,400 years until Maragha, Copernican, and Tychonic astronomers showed that equivalent fits could be obtained with the Earth in motion. Greek astronomy's enduring contribution is the mathematical apparatus: ecliptic and equatorial coordinates, the 48 constellations, the naked-eye planet list, and the demonstration that the apparent sky can be modelled geometrically rather than explained mythologically.
