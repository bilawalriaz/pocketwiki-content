# New Kingdom of Egypt

The New Kingdom (c. 1570–1069 BC) was ancient Egypt's imperial climax, sandwiched between two weaker eras: the Hyksos-ruled Second Intermediate Period before it and the fragmented Third Intermediate Period after. It began when a native Egyptian dynasty expelled foreign occupiers, pushed Egypt's borders to their greatest extent, and accumulated unprecedented wealth; it ended when state finances, royal authority, and external defenses unraveled in a single generation.

The founding cause was a security lesson. The Hyksos, a people of mixed Asiatic origin who had ruled Lower Egypt, introduced the horse-drawn chariot and composite bow. Once Ahmose I (c. 1550–1525 BC) completed their expulsion and founded the Eighteenth Dynasty, he and his successors concluded that Egypt could never feel secure until its frontiers sat deep inside the territories that had spawned its attackers.

That logic produced the empire. Thutmose I extended Egyptian control into Nubia (the gold-rich Nile region south of Egypt) and the Levant (the eastern Mediterranean coast including modern Syria, Lebanon, Israel, and Palestine). Thutmose III (c. 1479–1425 BC) turned conquest into a system: at least 16 campaigns in roughly 20 years, about 350 cities taken, and territory held from Nubia to the Euphrates. Conquering the routes meant taxing them, so the same armies paid for themselves by controlling trade in gold, incense, timber, and bronze.

Concentrated royal power funded the monuments. Hatshepsut, ruling as a female pharaoh (c. 1479–1458 BC), reopened foreign trade and built extensively at Karnak. Amenhotep III (c. 1390–1352 BC) presided over the period of greatest wealth, expressed in projects like the Luxor Temple.

The Eighteenth Dynasty then nearly broke itself on a religious experiment. Akhenaten (c. 1353–1336 BC), originally Amenhotep IV, promoted the Aten, the visible solar disc associated with Ra, as a supreme or sole deity, a break now often called the Amarna Period after his new capital, Akhetaten (modern Amarna). Whether this was true monotheism, henotheism, or monolatry is still debated; the political cost is not. Akhenaten's absorption in religious reform distracted him from Asia, and the Hittites, an Anatolian people based in modern Turkey, filled the vacuum in the Levant. Tutankhamun (c. 1332–1323 BC) restored the traditional gods and moved the court away from Akhetaten, but the dynasty's military momentum never fully recovered.

The Nineteenth Dynasty rebuilt it. Seti I recaptured the Syrian town of Kadesh from the Hittites. His son Ramesses II (c. 1279–1213 BC) then fought the Hittite king Muwatalli II at the Battle of Kadesh. The battle itself was inconclusive, but it produced the world's first recorded peace treaty, a formal border between two great powers that held for the rest of the Bronze Age. Ramesses II also used architecture as propaganda, cutting the temples of Abu Simbel and the Ramesseum into cliffs to broadcast his victories.

The end began with a single generation of strain. Ramesses III (c. 1186–1155 BC) was the last great pharaoh, defeating the Sea Peoples, a confederation of naval raiders who attacked the eastern Mediterranean in the late Bronze Age, along with Libyan tribes migrating into the region during the wider Late Bronze Age collapse. The cost drained the treasury, and the first recorded labor strike broke out during his reign, triggered by food shortages that may have been worsened by the Hekla 3 volcanic eruption around 1159 BC, which may have cooled the climate and reduced Nile floods. Ramesses III was assassinated in a palace conspiracy; a 2012 CT scan of his mummy confirmed a fatal throat wound. After his death, weak pharaohs, famine, and the rising power of the High Priests of Amun, who came to dominate Thebes and Upper Egypt, left Smendes ruling only Lower Egypt. He founded the Twenty-first Dynasty around 1077 BC, and the New Kingdom dissolved into the Third Intermediate Period.
