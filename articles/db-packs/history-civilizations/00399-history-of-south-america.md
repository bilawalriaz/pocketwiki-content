# History of South America

The continent was once joined to Africa in Gondwana, part of the supercontinent Pangaea. Around 110 million years ago South America and Africa began pulling apart along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. By 35 million years ago Antarctica had split off too, leaving South America an isolated island-continent for roughly 30 million years, which produced distinctive animals. The dinosaur extinction 66 million years ago had already cleared space for neotropical rainforests, including the Amazon. About 3 million years ago the Panamanian land bridge rose, joining North and South America and triggering the Great American Interchange. Ground sloths moved north while North American carnivores, rodents, and ungulates moved south; northern arrivals out-competed many southern forms. About 60% of present-day South American mammal species descend from North American ancestors.

Humans came from the north in waves over millennia, crossing the Bering Land Bridge to Alaska. The oldest firm evidence of human presence is the Monte Verde II site in Chile, about 14,500 years old. The Fishtail projectile point style spread from about 13,000 years ago and disappeared around 11,000 years ago, coinciding with the Quaternary extinction event that killed most large Ice Age mammals.

Agriculture began in the Amazon Basin around 6500 BC with potatoes, chilies, and beans; manioc followed by 2000 BC. In the Andean highlands llamas and alpacas were domesticated by about 3500 BC for transport, meat, and fibre. By 2000 BC agrarian villages with irrigation dotted the Andes, growing maize, quinoa, beans, peanuts, sweet potatoes, potatoes, oca, squashes, and cotton. Permanent settlements such as Huaca Prieta in Peru (4700 BC) and the Valdivia culture in Ecuador (3500 BC) anchored this period.

The most striking early civilization was Caral-Supe, or Norte Chico, on Peru's northwest coast around 3500 BC. One of only six civilizations worldwide to develop independently, it was roughly contemporary with the Egyptian pyramids and preceded Mesoamerican urbanism by two millennia. Unlike the others, it may have relied on fishing more than farming, and its 27th-century BC sites show no signs of warfare.

Two later Andean cultures show how advanced pre-Columbian society could become. The Chavín culture (900–200 BC), centered at Chavín de Huantar in Peru, built trade networks and a shared religious style. The Moche (1st–9th centuries AD) left elaborate burials and pottery depicting daily life. The Inca Empire, Tawantin suyu or "land of the four regions", ruled from Cuzco between 1438 and 1533 and governed 9 to 14 million people across nearly a hundred linguistic communities. It held the Andes together with a 25,000-kilometre road system, terrace farming, and advanced metalwork. The Inca had no written language but recorded information in quipu, knotted strings that may encode a phonetic system.

The Muisca of Colombia formed a loose confederation on the Altiplano Cundiboyacense from about 800 AD, known as "The Salt People" for trading halite. Their goldwork inspired the El Dorado legend. The Spanish conquered them between1536 and 1537.

The Amazon was long dismissed as a sparse hunter-gatherer wilderness, but new evidence has reversed that picture: geoglyphs dating 0–1250 AD, the widespread terra preta "black earth" understood as an indigenous soil-management product, and large planned settlements with roads, bridges, and plazas in the Xinguanos territory all show dense, organized habitation. Disease drove most of the post-contact collapse: the population may have fallen from around 5 million in 1500 to 1 million by 1900 and under 200,000 by the early 1980s.

European contact transformed the continent within decades. Before 1492 perhaps 20 to 30 million people lived in South America. The 1494 Treaty of Tordesillas drew a meridian 370 leagues west of Cape Verde, granting the west to Spain and the east to Portugal; loose enforcement let Portugal expand Brazil westward. Disease, above all smallpox, was the main cause of depopulation, amplified by forced-labour systems such as the Spanish encomienda and mita. Population losses ranged from 20–50% on lower estimates to90% in the worst-hit areas. Enslaved Africans, who had some immunity, replaced native labour, especially on Brazilian plantations. Catholic missionaries taught indigenous languages including Quechua and Guarani in order to preach, expanding those languages by giving them writing systems. Spaniards and natives intermixed into a Mestizo class that was taxed harshly and excluded from many offices.

In the 17th and 18th centuries, escaped slaves formed independent forest communities called Maroons in Spanish America and quilombos in Brazil. Revolts tested colonial rule, including Tupac Amaru II's revolt in 1780, continued by Tupac Katari in Upper Peru.

Independence came in the early 19th century. Spanish America was liberated largely by Simón Bolívar, who tried to knit his victories into a single state called Greater Colombia, and by José de San Martín and Bernardo O'Higgins in the south. Bolívar's dream of unity dissolved quickly. Brazil's path was different and unusually peaceful: when Napoleon's armies invaded Portugal, the Portuguese court fled to Rio de Janeiro, and the regent Pedro declared independence in 1822. The War of the Pacific (1879–1883) made Chile the dominant Pacific power, and the War of the Triple Alliance (1864–1870) crushed Paraguay.

The 20th century brought the United States to the centre. Theodore Roosevelt's Big Stick Doctrine turned the Monroe Doctrine into a licence for intervention. The Great Depression crushed demand for South American raw materials, pushing countries toward import-substitution industrialisation, the building of self-sufficient domestic industries. In the Cold War, democracies in Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Uruguay, and Paraguay were overthrown by military dictatorships in the 1960s and 1970s. These regimes detained tens of thousands, tortured and killed prisoners, and coordinated repression across borders through Operation Condor. By the early 1990s every country had restored democracy. Colombia's conflict began in 1964 with the FARC-EP guerrillas, later funded in part by the narcotics trade.

The 21st century opened with a "pink tide" of left-wing governments: Hugo Chávez in Venezuela from 1998, Lula da Silva in Brazil from 2002, Evo Morales in Bolivia from 2005, the Kirchners in Argentina, and Rafael Correa in Ecuador, breaking with the Washington Consensus of market reforms promoted by the IMF, World Bank, and US Treasury. In 2008 the Union of South American Nations (USAN) was founded to merge Mercosur and the Andean Community. By2023 Argentina had swung to the right, and several other countries followed.
