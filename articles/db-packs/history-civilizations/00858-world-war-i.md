# World War I

World War I (28 July 1914 – 11 November 1918) was a global conflict between the Allied Powers and the Central Powers that killed an estimated 15 to 22 million people, including victims of war crimes and genocides, and helped spread the Spanish flu pandemic. It introduced the first large-scale use of machine guns, heavy artillery, aircraft, chemical weapons, and tanks.

## How two rival blocs formed

For decades after 1871, the German Empire under Otto von Bismarck kept continental peace by isolating France through a web of treaties, including the Dual Alliance with Austria-Hungary and the Reinsurance Treaty with Russia. After Bismarck's dismissal in 1890, Kaiser Wilhelm II pursued an aggressive naval build-up under Admiral Tirpitz, which triggered an Anglo-German naval arms race. Germany let the Reinsurance Treaty lapse, pushing Russia toward France; the Franco-Russian Alliance of 1894 and the Anglo-French Entente Cordiale of 1904 then created the Triple Entente opposing the Central Powers' Triple Alliance. Europe had split into two armed camps, so a dispute between any two members could drag in all the others.

## The July Crisis

On 28 June 1914, Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb nationalist, assassinated Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo. Austria-Hungary, blaming Serbian intelligence, used the killing as a pretext to crush Serbian influence in Bosnia. On 23 July it delivered an ultimatum whose demands were drafted to be unacceptable; Serbia accepted everything except a clause letting Austrians suppress "subversive elements" inside Serbia. Austria declared war on 28 July and shelled Belgrade. Russia mobilised on 30 July; Germany declared war on Russia and France on 1 and 3 August, then invaded Belgium to outflank France under the Schlieffen Plan. Britain, bound by treaty to protect Belgian neutrality, declared war on 4 August. A Balkan crisis had become a continental war in eleven days.

## Why the war stalled

Germany's Schlieffen Plan called for a rapid sweep through Belgium into France, but French and British forces halted the advance at the First Battle of the Marne in September 1914. Both sides dug in, and the Western Front became a line of trenches that neither artillery nor infantry assaults could break. Major battles at Verdun, the Somme, and Passchendaele inflicted enormous casualties for almost no territorial gain. The Eastern Front was more mobile, producing large manoeuvres such as the Brusilov Offensive, but elsewhere fighting bogged down in mountains (the Italian Front), the Dardanelles (Gallipoli), the Middle East, and the colonies of Africa and the Pacific.

## Why the Central Powers lost

By 1917 the war had become a contest of resources. Germany's decision to resume unrestricted submarine warfare, sinking neutral and passenger ships without warning, pulled the United States into the conflict in April 1917, bringing fresh manpower under General Pershing's American Expeditionary Forces. On the Eastern Front, the October Revolution let the Bolsheviks seize power and withdraw Russia from the war, freeing German troops for the west. Germany's Spring Offensive of 21 March 1918 (Operation Michael) achieved tactical surprise but exhausted its reserves. The Allied counter-attack, the Hundred Days Offensive beginning 8 August 1918, broke the German line. Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicated on 9 November 1918, and the Armistice of 11 November ended hostilities on the Western Front.

## Consequences

The Treaty of Versailles, signed 28 June 1919, imposed harsh terms on Germany. Article 231, the "War Guilt Clause," assigned Germany and its allies responsibility for "all the loss and damage… as a consequence of the war" and was used to justify massive reparations. The war toppled four empires (German, Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman, and Russian) and created new states such as Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Yugoslavia. The League of Nations was founded to keep the peace, but its weakness, combined with German resentment over Versailles, helped make a second world war possible two decades later.
