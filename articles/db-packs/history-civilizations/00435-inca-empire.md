# Inca Empire

The Inca built the largest pre-Columbian American empire in roughly a century, ruling about 12 million people across the Andes without the wheel, iron, or writing. The official name, Tawantinsuyu, means "Realm of the Four Parts," referring to four provinces (suyu) that radiated from the capital, Cusco, like quarters of a wheel.

## From kingdom to empire

Around 1200, the Inca were a small highland kingdom centered at Cusco. The transformation began under the 9th Sapa Inca, Pachacuti (r. 1438–1471), who reorganized the state into the four suyu (Chinchaysuyu, Antisuyu, Kuntisuyu, Qullasuyu) and launched sustained conquest. His preferred tactic was to offer generous terms to rulers who submitted peacefully, then resort to military force against those who refused; in both cases, conquered elites were relocated to Cusco for assimilation. Pachacuti's son Topa Inca Yupanqui (r. 1463–1471) extended control to the Chimor Kingdom on the north coast, and Topa's son Huayna Capac pushed the empire north into modern Ecuador and Colombia and south into Chile and Argentina. A 1527 push into the Amazon Basin was halted by the Shuar. By the early 1500s the empire stretched roughly 4,000 km along the Andes, making it the largest state in the Americas at the time.

## How the state ran

The economy had no money and no markets. Land and labor were organized through the **ayllu**, a kinship community that held land collectively, and through **mit'a**, a rotating labor tax owed by households to the state. In return, the state built roads, terraces, and storehouses, and redistributed food from those storehouses during famine or war. Reciprocity, not profit, was the operating principle: a family helped neighbors build a roof expecting the same in return, and the same logic scaled up to the empire itself.

The political system was a centralized theocracy. The Sapa Inca was treated as the son of Inti, the sun god, which gave rulers sacred authority and let the state absorb local deities rather than suppress them. Society was governed by three moral precepts: do not steal, do not lie, do not be lazy. Gender roles were complementary, with both men and women contributing to weaving and agriculture. Locally, hereditary leaders called **kuraka** administered units of 10, 100, 1,000, and 10,000 households (a decimal system), and officials kept accounts on **quipu**, knotted-cord recording devices. Quipus reliably encoded numbers, but the full extent of what they could record, including possible narratives, is unknown because the knowledge to read them was lost after the conquest. Quechua was promoted as a lingua franca across a population that spoke dozens of languages.

## Engineering and adaptation

The Qhapaq Ñan road network tied the empire together, and stonework like Machu Picchu used precisely fitted blocks assembled without mortar, a technique that lets walls flex during earthquakes rather than crack. Farmers built stone-faced terraces (**andenes**) on steep slopes to create flat, irrigated fields, and the state ran experimental agricultural stations. Inca surgeons performed trepanation (cutting a hole in the skull) with high survival rates, and herbal medicine was highly developed. A central storehouse at Cusco held surplus from across the empire, allowing the state to feed armies, support drought years, and reward loyal communities. Over generations, high-altitude populations developed larger lung capacity and higher hemoglobin counts, biological adaptations to living above 3,000 m. Culturally, the Inca produced intricate textiles (cumbi), polychrome ceramics, and pentatonic music.

## Collapse

Three forces destroyed the empire in roughly a generation.

First, disease. Smallpox arrived around 1527, likely killing Huayna Capac and triggering a succession crisis.

Second, civil war. Huayna Capac's death split the empire between his sons Huáscar and Atahualpa; the resulting war (1527–1532) killed tens of thousands and left both sides exhausted by the time the Spanish appeared.

Third, conquest. A band of about 180 Spanish soldiers under Francisco Pizarro, armed with steel swords, firearms, and cavalry, captured Atahualpa at Cajamarca in 1532 through ambush rather than open battle. After a ransom of gold and silver filled a 22 by 17 m room to a height of about 1 m, the Spanish executed him in 1533 and installed Manco Inca as a puppet ruler. Manco briefly recaptured Cusco in 1536 before retreating to Vilcabamba in the jungle, where Inca resistance continued under his successors until the last ruler, Topa Amaru, was captured and executed in 1572. (Portuguese explorer Aleixo Garcia had reached the empire in 1524, the first European to do so.)

## Open debates

Scholars still debate what the Inca economy actually was. The standard view treats it as a non-market, redistributive system built on reciprocity, sometimes compared to socialism. Others argue hidden markets and price-like exchanges persisted despite official ideology. A related debate concerns the Maule River battle, traditionally placed in the late 1400s as the southern limit of Inca expansion. Historian Osvaldo Silva has argued the battle actually occurred in 1532 and that the Inca simply had no reason to keep pushing into Mapuche territory, suggesting imperial borders were a deliberate choice rather than a military ceiling. The rainbow flag associated with Tawantinsuyu today is a 20th-century invention, with no contemporary evidence that the Inca used a national flag.
