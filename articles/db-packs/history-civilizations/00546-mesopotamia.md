# Mesopotamia

Mesopotamia, the "land between rivers," is the historical region within the Tigris–Euphrates system, roughly modern Iraq. Both rivers rise in the Armenian highlands. The north (Upper Mesopotamia) gets enough rain for dry farming; the south (Lower Mesopotamia) is alluvial plain with a high water table and a 15,000 km² marshland, productive only with irrigation fed by mountain snowmelt. Lacking stone, timber, and metal ore, the region traded agricultural surplus for those materials. Irrigation demanded organised canal labour, which pushed villages toward cities and cities toward centralised states. The same geography that forced cooperation also fragmented the south into rival city-states, and the cycle of salinisation, climate stress, invasion, and local rivalry kept producing collapse and reunification for three thousand years.

Mesopotamia is one of the four riverine cradles where writing and urban civilisation appear independently (Nile, Indus, Yellow River), and the earliest, beginning with the Neolithic Revolution around 10,000 BC. Cuneiform, the "wedge-shaped" script pressed into wet clay with a reed stylus, was invented in Sumer during the mid-4th millennium BC and evolved from pictograms into a mixed logographic-syllabic system. Sumerian, a language isolate, was the first written language; the Semitic Akkadian replaced it as the spoken vernacular by the early 2nd millennium BC, while Sumerian survived in scholarship and liturgy until about the 1st century AD. Sargon of Akkad's syllabic standardisation (c. 2350 BC) broadened literacy beyond the narrow scribal class.

The political story is a long oscillation between fragmentation and empire. Sumerian city-states dominated until Sargon of Akkad founded the first multi-city empire around 2350 BC. The Third Dynasty of Ur (2112–2004 BC) centralised administration and irrigation, with temples holding up to a third of the land and *ensi* officials managing it. The early 2nd millennium split into Assyria in the north and Babylonia in the south; Hammurabi (c. 1780 BC) produced the most famous of the early law codes, with 200+ laws showing progressively harsher treatment of slaves and women. Later empires swung wider: the Neo-Assyrian (911–612 BC) ruled much of the Near East through provinces named for capitals like Nineveh, Samaria, and Damascus; the Neo-Babylonian (626–539 BC) was the last independent Mesopotamian state before Cyrus the Great took Babylon in 539 BC. Alexander conquered the region in 332 BC, after which Seleucids, Parthians, Romans, and Sassanids fought over it. The 7th-century Muslim conquests closed the ancient period and gave the land the name Iraq.

Two intellectual legacies outlived every empire. The sexagesimal (base-60) numeral system still gives the 60-minute hour, 24-hour day, and 360° circle. Babylonian mathematicians worked algorithmically: π approximated as 3 (or 25/8 on one tablet), circle area as 1/12 of circumference squared, quadratics solved step by step. Tablet YBC 7289 (c. 1800–1600 BC) records √2 to six decimal places; Plimpton 322 (c. 1900–1600 BC) lists Pythagorean triples. Babylonian astronomy, run by temple priests, developed eclipse prediction, a 12-month lunar calendar, and by the 8th–7th centuries BC a predictive planetary theory using internal mathematical rules, sometimes called the "first scientific revolution." Assyrian *Limmu* eponym lists gave absolute chronology by naming a different official for each year alongside celestial events. Seleucus of Seleucia (b. 190 BC) is the one ancient figure reported to have supported heliocentrism, though only his tidal argument survives. The *Diagnostic Handbook* (Esagil-kin-apli, c. 1069–1046 BC) applied empirical observation and logic to symptom–disease mapping, including detailed epilepsy descriptions, with treatments mixing pharmacology and exorcism for incurable cases. This combination of open-systems reasoning and dialectic in wisdom literature fed directly into early Greek philosophy and, through Genesis, into the Abrahamic traditions.

Material culture followed the same long arc. Ziggurats, the massive terraced temple platforms, are the signature Mesopotamian building, since the alluvial south had only mud-brick. Assyrian palaces paired colossal narrative reliefs with *lamassu*, human-headed winged bulls or lions carved with five legs so they read correctly from both front and side. The Ishtar Gate's polychrome brickwork marks the peak of Neo-Babylonian monumentality. The wheel, copper/bronze/iron metallurgy, glass, textile weaving, and large-scale water control all passed through Mesopotamia into the wider Near East and Mediterranean.

Modern Iraqi groups (Arabs, Assyrians, Kurds, Yazidis, Marsh Arabs) show significant genetic continuity, with Y-chromosome haplogroups J and R dominant and little differentiation among them, reflecting deep Near Eastern ancestry. Marsh Arabs are hypothesised to descend most directly from ancient Sumerians; Assyrians and Yazidis may preserve the strongest ancient Mesopotamian signal.
