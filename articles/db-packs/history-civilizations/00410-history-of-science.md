# History of science

Science originated as practical problem-solving in ancient civilizations. In Egypt and Mesopotamia, between roughly 3000 and 1200 BCE, people developed mathematics, geometry, astronomy, and medicine for tasks such as tracking Nile floods, surveying land, predicting seasons, and treating illness. These fields worked empirically but were often mixed with religious ritual and magical thinking, and they did not yet attempt to explain nature through natural causes.

## Greek natural philosophy

Between about 600 and 300 BCE, Greek thinkers in the Mediterranean transformed inquiry. The Pre-Socratics, then Plato and Aristotle, sought explanations grounded in natural causes rather than in the actions of gods. Aristotle emphasized empirical observation and careful classification of plants, animals, and physical phenomena. This shift from "what the gods do" to "what nature does" is the first major step toward modern science, even though many Greek conclusions, including the geocentric universe and the four elements, later proved wrong. The whole programme was called **natural philosophy**, the historical name for the study of nature and the physical universe and the direct ancestor of modern science.

## The Islamic Golden Age

After the fall of the Western Roman Empire, Greek learning survived in the Byzantine Empire and was taken up and expanded by scholars in the Islamic world from the 8th to the 14th centuries. Working in centres from Baghdad to Córdoba, they translated Greek texts, refined algebra, advanced optics and medicine, and kept inquiry alive when Western Europe had fewer institutional centres for learning. Without this preservation, many Greek works would have been lost to Europe.

## Medieval Europe

From the 12th century onward, Western Europeans translated Greek and Arabic texts, often from Arabic versions of Greek originals, and founded the first universities. Thinkers such as Roger Bacon pushed for greater reliance on observation and experiment, laying institutional groundwork for later work.

## The Scientific Revolution

The 16th and 17th centuries produced the largest change in how science was done. The key shift was from trusting ancient authorities to trusting evidence and mathematics.

In 1543, Copernicus published *De Revolutionibus*, arguing that the Earth orbits the Sun (heliocentrism) rather than the other way around. In the same year, Vesalius's *De humani corporis fabrica* corrected many ancient ideas about human anatomy. Between 1609 and 1619, Kepler's three laws of planetary motion, derived from Tycho Brahe's precise observations, and Galileo's telescopic discoveries, including the moons of Jupiter and the phases of Venus, established a new astronomy. In 1687, Newton's *Principia Mathematica* unified celestial and terrestrial mechanics under a single mathematical framework: the same laws that govern falling apples govern orbiting planets.

This new science rested on three things: a **mechanistic worldview** in which the universe behaves like a predictable machine, heavy use of mathematics, and a new emphasis on experimentation, formalized in institutions such as the Royal Society of London, founded in 1660. The **scientific method**, a systematic cycle of observation, hypothesis, experimentation, and verification, became the standard approach.

## The 18th and 19th centuries

Chemistry emerged from alchemy; Lavoisier established the law of conservation of mass, the principle that matter is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. Geology developed theories of Earth's deep age and slow processes. Biology was reshaped by Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection in 1859 and by Mendel's laws of inheritance in 1866, the rules governing how traits pass from parents to offspring. Physics unified electricity and magnetism through Maxwell's equations and produced the laws of thermodynamics, the science of heat, work, and energy. The social sciences also took shape, with Adam Smith's *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) founding classical economics and Auguste Comte founding sociology. By 1833, William Whewell had coined the word "scientist," replacing "natural philosopher" and marking science as a profession.

## The 20th and 21st centuries

The 20th century began with two revolutions in physics. Einstein's theories of special and general relativity (1905–1915) replaced Newton's absolute space and time with a flexible spacetime shaped by mass and energy. Quantum mechanics, developed by Heisenberg and Schrödinger between 1925 and 1927, showed that at atomic scales nature is fundamentally probabilistic rather than deterministic, the opposite of the clockwork universe the Scientific Revolution had imagined.

After World War II, science changed scale. Large, expensive, government-funded projects such as the Manhattan Project and particle accelerators gave rise to what is now called **Big Science**. Biology was transformed in 1953 when Watson and Crick described the double-helix structure of DNA. Later milestones included the completion of the Human Genome Project in 2003, the development of CRISPR gene editing in 2012, the confirmation of the Big Bang theory, the acceptance of plate tectonics, and the first direct detection of gravitational waves in 2015. Historians also study how social and cultural factors, including gender, race, economics, and historical exclusion from scientific communities, have shaped which questions get asked and who gets to ask them. Science has become increasingly global, collaborative, and dependent on advanced technology.
