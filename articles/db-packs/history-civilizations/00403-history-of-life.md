# History of life

Life on Earth emerged by at least 3.7 billion years ago (Ga) and has since grown in complexity, surviving and transforming through a series of major environmental and biological revolutions. The earliest clear evidence comes from stromatolites (layered mats built by microbes) and biogenic carbon in 3.7-billion-year-old rocks from western Greenland, with possible signs as early as 4.28 Ga. The first organisms were simple single-celled microbes, and microbial mats of bacteria and archaea dominated early ecosystems for billions of years.

## Oxygen and the first great change

Around 3.5 Ga, cyanobacteria evolved oxygenic photosynthesis, releasing oxygen as a waste product. For a long time this oxygen was consumed by reacting with reduced minerals dissolved in the oceans. Once those chemical sinks were exhausted, free oxygen accumulated in the atmosphere in the Great Oxygenation Event around 2.4 Ga. This oxygenation was a prerequisite for more complex, energy-intensive life: aerobic respiration (using oxygen to extract energy from food) yields far more energy than anaerobic metabolism, and reactive oxygen shaped the chemistry of cells and rocks alike.

## Eukaryotes, sex, and multicellularity

Around 1.85 Ga, fossils record the first eukaryotes, cells with a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. Their defining features arose largely through endosymbiosis, in which one prokaryote (a simple cell without a nucleus) lived inside another and became an organelle. Mitochondria descended from an engulfed aerobic bacterium, and chloroplasts from a cyanobacterium, giving eukaryotes the metabolic capacity to power larger, more complex cells. Around 1.7 Ga, multicellularity evolved independently in several lineages, allowing cells to specialise into tissues. Sexual reproduction, which mixes the genes of two parents, likely arose early in eukaryotes; by generating genetic variation in offspring, it accelerated adaptation and enabled complex multicellular life.

## Colonisation of land and the animal radiation

Microbes may have lived on land as early as 3.22 Ga, but complex terrestrial ecosystems appeared much later. Land plants evolved from freshwater algae around 1 billion years ago and spread widely in the Devonian. Their growth drew down atmospheric CO₂, helping to cool the planet, while their roots broke down rock and built soils. Animals followed: the earliest widely accepted animal fossils are cnidarians (the phylum of jellyfish and corals) from about 580 Ma, and the Ediacaran biota (c. 575–542 Ma) included the first large, complex body forms. Bilateria, animals with left–right mirror symmetry, appear around 555 Ma, just before the Cambrian explosion around 525 Ma saw the rapid appearance of most modern animal phyla, including the first vertebrates (animals with backbones), within a geologically brief window.

## Mass extinctions reroute evolution

Mass extinctions periodically wiped out a large share of species in a geologically short time. The Late Devonian extinction around 370 Ma coincided with the spread of early forests, which altered climate, soils, and freshwater systems. The Permian–Triassic extinction 252 Ma eliminated most complex species and cleared the way for archosaurs, the reptile lineage that includes crocodilians, pterosaurs, and dinosaurs, to dominate the Mesozoic Era. The Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction 66 Ma killed the non-avian dinosaurs, opening ecological space for mammals to diversify in size and form. Removing dominant groups creates vacant niches (ecological roles) that surviving lineages radiate into.

## Mammals, humans, and the species yet to be found

After the dinosaur extinction, mammals diversified, and hominins (the lineage of modern humans and their immediate ancestors after the split from chimpanzees) appear in the fossil record over 6 Ma ago. The genus *Homo* emerged around 2.5 Ma, with significant brain-size growth over the last 3 million years. *Homo sapiens* evolved in Africa less than 200,000 years ago and then spread across the globe. One unresolved debate is whether modern humans descend largely from a single African population that later replaced other hominin species, or whether genetic contributions from multiple regional populations were substantial. Only a small fraction of an estimated 1 trillion species has been formally described, so the count of living kinds remains far from complete.
