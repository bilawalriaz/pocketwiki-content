# Han dynasty

The Han dynasty (202 BC – 220 AD, with a brief interruption) was the second unified imperial dynasty of China and the one that fixed what most people mean by "Chinese." A peasant-born rebel, Liu Bang, took the throne in 202 BC after defeating rival Xiang Yu in the Chu–Han Contention. Over the next four centuries, his family built a state so durable that the majority ethnic group of China still calls itself the *Han people* and its writing system *Han characters*.

The dynasty has two halves. The **Western Han** (202 BC – 9 AD) ruled from Chang'an. The **Eastern Han** (25 – 220 AD) moved the capital east to Luoyang after a usurper named Wang Mang briefly founded the Xin dynasty (9 – 25 AD). The split matters because most long-term institutions — Confucian state ideology, the Silk Road, paper — belong to specific phases, and the collapse looks very different in each half.

## Government and social order

The emperor was supreme, but a bureaucracy of Confucian scholar-officials ran the day-to-day state. Under the Western Han, officials were recruited mainly by local recommendation and reputation for filial piety (the *xiaolian* system), not by examination. The empire was carved into centrally ruled **commanderies** and semi-independent **kingdoms**; the kingdoms were progressively stripped of military power after the Rebellion of the Seven States in 154 BC.

Society was legally ranked: imperial Liu clan and nobles, then scholar-bureaucrats, then farmers, artisans, and (lowest) merchants, who paid heavier taxes and could not wear silk. The Eastern Han saw land concentrate into the hands of a hereditary gentry, while palace eunuchs accumulated so much power that they repeatedly purged reform-minded officials in events called the **Partisan Prohibitions**.

## Ideology

In 124 BC Emperor Wu founded an Imperial University to train officials in the classics. Under the philosopher Dong Zhongshu, Confucianism was fused with yin-yang and Five Phases correlative cosmology — the idea that heaven responds to the ruler's virtue through natural signs — and made the official state ideology. This merger turned a moral philosophy into a civil-service creed that would outlast every dynasty that followed.

Taoism also hardened from a loose philosophy into organised religion, including the Way of the Five Pecks of Rice, a millenarian movement that blended Taoist ritual with peasant grievance. Buddhism arrived later, filtering in along Central Asian trade routes during the Eastern Han.

## Economy and technology

The economy was agricultural, monetised by bronze coinage. Emperor Wu's first attempt at a standard coin, the **wuzhu** (introduced 120 BC, withdrawn a year later), was only made permanent in 40 AD; the wuzhu remained China's currency standard until the Tang dynasty, nearly six centuries later.

To pay for war, the state nationalised salt and iron in 117 BC; the monopolies were later repealed after a court debate recorded in the *Discourses on Salt and Iron*. The period produced several inventions with global reach:

| Innovation | Date | Significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Papermaking (standardised by Cai Lun) | 105 AD | First practical cheap writing surface |
| Seismometer (Zhang Heng) | c. 132 AD | First instrument to detect the direction of distant earthquakes |
| Iron seed drill, mouldboard plough | Han era | Raised grain yields per worker |
| Rudder for ships | Han era | Allowed large vessels to steer reliably |
| Negative numbers in mathematics | Han era | First systematic use in any civilisation |

## Expansion and the Silk Road

China's main external threat was the **Xiongnu**, a nomadic confederation on the northern steppe. After decades of paying tribute and sending princesses in marriage (a policy called **heqin**), Emperor Wu switched to all-out war. The 119 BC Battle of Mobei drove the Xiongnu court north of the Gobi Desert, and in 60 BC the Han set up a Protectorate of the Western Regions to control the Tarim Basin oases. Safe passage across Central Asia opened the **Silk Road** — caravan trade linking China to Parthia, the Kushan Empire, and ultimately Rome.

Southern expansion absorbed Nanyue (111 BC) and Dian (109 BC); northern expansion reached the Korean Peninsula in 108 BC. In 166 AD, a party the Roman sources call an "embassy" reached the court of Emperor Huan — most historians read this as Roman or Greco-Roman merchants seeking trade, not an official mission.

## Decline and fall

The Eastern Han was slowly hollowed out. Eunuch-factionalism, corruption, and a succession of weak child emperors eroded central authority. In 184 AD two huge uprisings broke out at once: the **Yellow Turban Rebellion**, a Taoist millenarian movement led by the Zhang brothers, and the related Five Pecks of Rice Rebellion. The court suppressed them only by granting regional generals autonomous command, which created warlords.

After Emperor Ling died in 189 AD, the warlord Dong Zhuo seized the capital. A coalition failed to dislodge him, and a generation of civil war followed. In 220 AD the warlord Cao Pi forced the last Han emperor, Xian, to abdicate and took the title of ruler of Wei, opening the Three Kingdoms period. By that point, the dynasty's institutional inheritance — centralised bureaucracy, Confucian statecraft, an agrarian tax base — had already been claimed by every regional successor.
