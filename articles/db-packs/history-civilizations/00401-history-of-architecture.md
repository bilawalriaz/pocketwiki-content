# History of architecture

Architecture is the record of how human societies have solved the shelter problem while expressing beliefs, displaying power, and inventing new materials and techniques. Each major period inherits the engineering and formal vocabulary of the one before it, then adds, abandons, or recombines elements, producing a chain of reactions: a new material or social need opens possibilities, a style crystallises around them, and a later generation rebels against what feels stale or excessive.

The earliest confirmed purpose-built structures are the huts at Terra Amata in France, dated to around 400,000 BC. With the Neolithic period (10,000–2000 BC) and the domestication of plants and animals, permanent settlements such as Göbekli Tepe in Turkey and Çatalhöyük appeared. From roughly 3100 BC the Egyptian pharaonic state developed formal architectural styles built for the afterlife and the gods, and by about 2667–2648 BC the Step Pyramid of Djoser, designed by Imhotep, marked the first pyramid.

Classical Antiquity introduced the systems that would dominate Western building for two thousand years. Greek architecture, flourishing from about 850 BC to 300 AD, established principles of order and proportion through temple design and the three classical *orders* of column: Doric, Ionic, and Corinthian, which defined the proportions and detailing of column and entablature. Roman architecture (509 BC–476 AD) absorbed Greek and Etruscan models and added decisive engineering innovations: the arch, the dome, and concrete. These made possible the Pantheon and the Colosseum, and were codified in the 1st century BC by Vitruvius in *De architectura*, the only architectural treatise to survive from antiquity.

When the Roman Empire fell, building did not stop; it fragmented. Byzantine architecture, roughly 330–1453, developed domed churches such as Hagia Sophia. In Western Europe the Romanesque style of the 10th to 12th centuries used thick walls and round arches. It evolved into the Gothic style (12th to 16th century), which replaced round arches with pointed arches and added ribbed vaults and *flying buttresses*—external masonry struts that transfer the sideways thrust of a vault to a heavy pier away from the wall, freeing the wall to carry large stained-glass windows. The rebuilding of the Basilica of St. Denis between about 1135 and 1344 is traditionally treated as the starting point of Gothic.

The Renaissance, from about 1400 to 1600, consciously revived Greek and Roman ideals of symmetry, proportion, and geometry, restated by Leon Battista Alberti in *De Re Aedificatoria* (1452). The Baroque (c. 1580–1800) reacted against this restraint with dramatic light, curved forms, and opulent decoration, especially in Catholic churches projecting power. Neoclassicism in the mid-18th to 19th centuries swung back, rejecting Baroque ornament in favour of perceived Classical purity, an impulse reinforced by the excavations at Pompeii and Herculaneum.

The 19th-century Industrial Revolution changed the materials available to architects. Iron, steel, and later reinforced concrete made possible the Crystal Palace in London in 1851, a landmark of iron-and-glass construction, and fed a wave of revival styles such as Gothic Revival. Art Nouveau (c. 1890–1914) used organic forms and new materials to break from this historicism, the conscious borrowing of visual styles from earlier eras. Modernism, emerging in the early 20th century, rejected ornament in favour of *functionalism*, the idea that a building's form should follow its purpose; the International Style was named in 1932 by Philip Johnson and Henry-Russell Hitchcock, and Brutalism later used raw, exposed concrete (*béton brut*). Postmodernism, in the late 20th century, reacted against Modernist austerity by reintroducing ornament, historical references, and complexity, and was followed by Deconstructivism, which fragments and distorts forms to create unpredictable shapes.

Several caveats complicate the neat story. "Romanesque" is itself a debated label: it covers wide regional variation, its boundaries with neighbouring styles are unclear, and medieval builders may not have recognised the categories modern scholars impose. The origins of Gothic are similarly diffuse, evolving from a combination of existing techniques rather than a single invention. From the 19th century onward, the link between architectural style and national identity became contested political terrain, with revivalism used to project an idealised "glorious past."
