# Eastern Front (World War I)

The Eastern Front was the theater of the First World War fought between the Central Powers (Germany, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire, and Bulgaria) and the Entente, principally Russia and later Romania. It stretched from the Baltic to the Black Sea, with combat and occupation spanning more than 100,000 square miles. Its outcome was a decisive Central Powers victory: Russia left the war, but the territorial gains were erased by the Allied victory in the West.

## Origins and war plans

The war began when Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia on 28 July 1914. Russia, bound by treaty to Serbia, ordered full mobilisation, which Germany treated as an act of war. Two opposing plans then collided. Germany's *Schlieffen Plan* called for a rapid defeat of France before turning east against the slower-mobilising Russian army. Russia's *Schedule 19A*, adopted in 1912, committed it to an immediate simultaneous offensive against both Germany and Austria-Hungary to support France. When Russia attacked early, it forced Germany to divert divisions from the West, but it also exposed Russian forces to a coordinated Central Powers response. The result was a war of movement rather than trench deadlock.

## 1914–1915: mobile warfare and the Great Retreat

The opening campaign produced a striking asymmetry. In August 1914, Germany encircled and destroyed the Russian Second Army at Tannenberg, halting the invasion of East Prussia. A month later, Russia defeated Austria-Hungary in the Battle of Galicia, occupying most of the region. In May 1915, Germany shifted its focus east and launched the Gorlice–Tarnów Offensive, which broke through Russian lines in Galicia and triggered the "Great Retreat." Russia abandoned Poland, Lithuania, and parts of Belarus and Ukraine, losing more than 300,000 square kilometres. The retreat was driven by critical shortages of artillery and ammunition, systemic command failures, and corruption. Russia's territorial depth prevented total collapse, and by late 1915 a stable front line had formed from Riga to Tarnopol.

## 1916: Brusilov Offensive and Romania

In June 1916, General Aleksei Brusilov launched an offensive against Austria-Hungary that became the war's largest Entente victory, inflicting more than a million casualties on Austria-Hungary and forcing Germany to divert divisions from Verdun. The success persuaded Romania to join the Entente in August, but the Central Powers counterattacked rapidly. By December they had captured Bucharest and the Ploiești oilfields, stripping Romania of two-thirds of its territory.

## 1917: revolution and collapse

The February Revolution, driven by food shortages and war weariness, toppled Tsar Nicholas II in March 1917. The Provisional Government continued the war, but the Kerensky Offensive of July 1917 ended in failure and army discipline crumbled. In November, the Bolsheviks seized power in the October Revolution on a platform of immediate peace. The army largely stayed at the front into early 1918, but the Bolsheviks began dissolving units while negotiating with the Central Powers.

## 1918: Brest-Litovsk and its annulment

In March 1918, the German Operation Faustschlag forced the new Soviet government to sign the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, ceding Ukraine, Poland, the Baltic states, and other vast territories. This allowed Germany to redeploy forces west for its spring offensive, which ultimately failed. The November Armistice annulled Brest-Litovsk, and Romania, which had signed a separate peace in May 1918, re-entered the war on the Allied side in November.

## Open questions

Historians continue to debate three points. The condition of the Russian army after 1916: General Brusilov and the historian Sergei Oldenburg argued it remained well-supplied, numerically superior, and capable of fighting defensively, suggesting its collapse stemmed chiefly from political revolution rather than military defeat. The Eastern Front's effect on the West: significant German cavalry and resources remained tied down in the east even after Russia's exit, a factor in the failure of Germany's 1918 spring offensive. Casualty totals: Russian losses are estimated at 7.9 to 9 million, and Central Powers losses on the front at 5.1 to 6 million, with some sources placing Eastern Front casualties above those of the Western Front.
