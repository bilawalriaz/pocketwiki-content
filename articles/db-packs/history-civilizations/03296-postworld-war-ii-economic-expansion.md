# Post–World War II economic expansion

The post–World War II economic expansion, also called the Golden Age of Capitalism, ran from the late 1940s to 1973, when the industrialized world grew faster, more steadily, and more evenly than before or since. OECD members averaged over 4% real GDP growth annually in the 1950s and nearly 5% in the 1960s, with full employment, fewer financial crises than in surrounding periods, and rising living standards in both developed and developing countries. The boom ended abruptly in 1971–75 when the Bretton Woods fixed-exchange-rate system collapsed (currencies had been pegged to the dollar, and the dollar to gold), the 1973 Arab oil embargo roughly doubled oil prices, and a 1973–75 recession and stock market crash closed the era.

## Why growth was so fast and so stable

Several mechanisms reinforced one another:

- **Catch-up and technology.** Wartime R&D spilled into civilian use (automation, new agricultural methods), and war-damaged economies rebuilt with the latest plant and equipment, closing the productivity gap with the United States. U.S. GDP itself grew from $228 billion in 1945 to nearly $1.7 trillion in 1975.
- **Keynesian demand management.** Governments smoothed the cycle through fiscal policy (spending and taxation), accepting budget deficits to sustain demand. *Keynesian economics* is the theory that government intervention can manage aggregate demand and maintain full employment.
- **Cheap, directed credit.** "Financial repression" — keeping nominal interest rates low, often below inflation — let states service and gradually erode large war debts while channelling savings into investment.
- **International cooperation.** The *Bretton Woods system*, a postwar monetary order of fixed exchange rates backed by institutions like the IMF, curbed competitive devaluations. The Marshall Plan injected over $12 billion into Western Europe from 1947, accelerating reconstruction.
- **Redistribution.** Progressive taxation and wartime rationing, which lingered into peacetime, narrowed inequality and widened the consumer base, so productivity gains translated into broad-based demand.
- **Geopolitical backdrop.** Cold War military spending propped up aggregate demand, and decolonization opened new markets and labour pools.

## National patterns

The expansion was global, with each country following a distinct trajectory. West Germany's *Wirtschaftswunder* (economic miracle) roughly doubled industrial production between 1950 and 1957, aided by a 1948 currency reform. France's *Trente Glorieuses* ("Thirty Glorious Years," 1947–1973) averaged about 5% annual growth. Japan's recovery, accelerated by Korean War procurement, produced the "Japanese economic miracle" in steel, cars, and electronics. Italy's *Miracolo economico* peaked in the early 1960s. South Korea and Austria, war-ravaged, and Sweden, relatively untouched, also grew rapidly. The Soviet Union expanded quickly — launching the first satellite in 1957 — until the mid-1960s, when resources tilted toward military projects and growth slowed.

## Social consequences

Sustained growth and full employment financed the baby boom, the rise of the welfare state, mass suburbanization in the United States, decolonization, the space race, and the 1960s counterculture. Technocratic planning — the idea that experts could engineer social outcomes — gained credibility. The 1956 U.S. Federal Aid Highway Act, which authorized the Interstate Highway System, shows how growth, policy, and social change intertwined.

## Why it ended

The early 1970s delivered three simultaneous shocks: the breakdown of Bretton Woods in 1971, when Nixon ended dollar–gold convertibility; the 1973 oil embargo that roughly doubled oil prices; and a 1973–75 recession and stock-market crash. The crisis exposed vulnerabilities in Western manufacturing to new global competition, accelerated the shift toward a post-industrial, services-led economy, and marked the close of the golden age.

## What historians argue about

The main open question is causation. Keynesian economists credit deliberate demand management and the welfare state. Structuralists point to reconstruction, technology catch-up, and the unique Cold War environment. Some stress "military Keynesianism" — the stabilizing effect of high military outlays. A second debate asks whether the golden age was a one-off anomaly tied to postwar conditions, or whether its policy lessons can be reproduced today.
