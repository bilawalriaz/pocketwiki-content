# Caral–Supe civilization

Caral–Supe, also called Norte Chico, was a complex pre-Columbian society that flourished on the arid north-central coast of Peru between the 4th and 2nd millennia BCE. It is the oldest-known civilization in the Americas and one of six places worldwide where civilization arose independently. Its combination of monumental architecture and a complete absence of ceramics overturns the standard assumption that pottery, sculpture, and warfare are necessary ingredients of a "civilization."

The society was anchored in the Supe, Pativilca, and Fortaleza river valleys, with related sites along the Huaura. Although the coast is one of the driest regions on Earth, snowmelt from the Andes feeds more than fifty small rivers, and sites cluster near irrigation canals. Agriculture and fishing were locked together: inland fields produced cotton used for fishing nets, and coastal communities needed inland crops and labor to maintain the canals.

## Economy and diet

The food base was mixed. People ate domesticated squash, beans, and sweet potato, caught anchovies, sardines, and clams from the Pacific, and grew maize from roughly 3000 to 1800 BCE. Isotopic and dietary analyses show plant foods dominated at both coastal and inland sites, undercutting the older view that Caral–Supe was a purely maritime society. Cotton (*Gossypium barbadense*) was the critical non-food crop, supplying fiber for fishing nets and textiles and binding inland farming villages to the coastal fishing economy in a single trading network.

## Architecture and social organization

Settlement is unusually dense, with many large sites featuring rectangular platform mounds (flat-topped earthen or stone structures used for ceremony or elite housing) and sunken circular plazas (depressed, round ceremonial spaces typically placed beside a mound). The principal pyramid at Caral measures 160 m by 150 m at the base and rises 18 m. Builders used quarried stone and river cobbles packed inside reed "shicra-bags." Monuments of this scale imply centralized leadership able to marshal large labor forces, yet there is no evidence of warfare, defensive walls, or physical coercion. Power appears to have rested on controlling cotton and long-distance trade, while ideological cohesion may have drawn on shared religious symbols, including the earliest known depiction of the Staff God, a staff-bearing figure that appears in later Andean cultures.

## What was missing, and what substituted for it

Caral–Supe had no ceramics and no sculpture, yet it produced sophisticated textiles, flutes carved from pelican bone, and possibly used quipus, knotted-string devices that may have stored numeric and logographic data, for record-keeping. Quipus suggest organized administration without an alphabet, although their full function remains uncertain.

## Open questions

The Maritime Foundation of Andean Civilization (MFAC) theory holds that the civilization rested on a maritime subsistence economy, but dietary evidence showing plant foods dominated has pushed most researchers toward an agricultural core with a strong marine supplement. A second dispute asks whether the coast, represented by sites such as Aspero, led the way and influenced the inland, or whether inland centers like Caral were primary from the start; radiocarbon dates show the two zones were contemporaneous, so the direction of influence is contested. A separate, more personal controversy concerns the discovery itself, pitting Peruvian archaeologist Ruth Shady against American researchers Jonathan Haas and Winifred Creamer in competing claims over credit and allegations of plagiarism.
