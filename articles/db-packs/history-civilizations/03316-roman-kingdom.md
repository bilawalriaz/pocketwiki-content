# Roman Kingdom

The Roman Kingdom was the earliest period of Roman history, conventionally dated c. 753–509 BC. Rome began as a small settlement on the Palatine Hill near a ford of the Tiber and, by tradition, was ruled by seven successive kings over roughly 244 years. Almost everything known about these kings comes from writers like Livy and Plutarch, who lived centuries later and relied on oral tradition, so the narrative is best read as a useful organising legend rather than a verified chronology. Contemporary records existed but were largely destroyed when the Gauls sacked Rome in 390 BC, leaving modern historians with legend, archaeology, and inference.

## The king's power

Rome's king held *imperium*, supreme authority over military, executive, judicial, and religious affairs, for life. *Imperium* was the formal power that later magistrates would also hold. The king was elected by the people, but when a reign ended the Senate governed during an *interregnum* (a vacancy between reigns) and effectively chose the next king, so aristocratic agreement mattered as much as popular vote. The king appointed his own subordinates, including the *tribunus celerum* (bodyguard commander) and the *praefectus urbi* (city warden), and he personally served as chief priest, the *pontifex*, controlling the calendar and every public religious rite. The Senate and the Curiate Assembly existed, but their roles were narrow: the Senate advised, the Assembly voted on laws the king proposed and formally granted *imperium*, and the king set the agenda for both.

## The supporting institutions

The Senate was a council of 300 men drawn from the noble families and appointed by the king; it had prestige and continuity across reigns but no power to overrule him. The Curiate Assembly organised citizens into 30 *curiae* (voting units) and could pass laws the king proposed, but it was not an independent legislature. Servius Tullius, the sixth king, layered a second popular body on top of this structure, the Centuriate Assembly, organised by military centuries and wealth classes rather than by kinship. He also instituted the *census*, a periodic count of citizens and their property, which fed military recruitment and tied voting weight to economic class. These two reforms, a property-classified citizenry and a second assembly, outlived the monarchy and shaped the politics of the later Republic.

## The seven kings

Roman tradition lists seven kings and pairs each with a defining contribution, so the sequence works as a compressed history of state-formation.

| King (reign c. BC) | Defining contribution |
| :--- | :--- |
| Romulus (from c. 753) | Founder; Rome built on the Palatine Hill near a Tiber ford. |
| Numa Pompilius (c. 715–673) | Religious reform; a peaceful interval between warrior kings. |
| Tullus Hostilius (c. 673–642) | Warrior; destroyed Alba Longa, Rome's Latin rival. |
| Ancus Marcius (c. 642–617) | Builder; founded Ostia, Rome's coastal port, and the first Tiber bridge. |
| Tarquinius Priscus (c. 616–579) | First Etruscan king; major building projects. |
| Servius Tullius (c. 578–535) | Constitutional reformer; census and Centuriate Assembly. |
| Tarquinius Superbus (c. 535–509) | Tyrannical last king. |

The last three kings were Etruscan in origin, marking a phase of strong cultural influence from northern Italy before the monarchy was expelled.

## The fall of the monarchy

The dynasty ended in scandal. Sextus Tarquinius, son of the last king, raped the noblewoman Lucretia, whose suicide triggered a revolt led by Lucius Junius Brutus. In 509 BC Brutus deposed Tarquinius Superbus and, with Lucius Tarquinius Collatinus, became the first two consuls of the Roman Republic. An attempt by the Etruscan ruler Lars Porsena to restore the Tarquins failed, though a minority of sources claim he briefly captured Rome.
