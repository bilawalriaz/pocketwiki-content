# Archaeology

Archaeology is the study of human activity through material remains: artifacts, architecture, biofacts, sites, and cultural landscapes. Because writing only emerged in the 4th millennium BC among a few civilizations while *Homo sapiens* has existed for at least 200,000 years, more than 99% of the human past is prehistoric, and archaeology is often the only source of evidence. The discipline sits at the intersection of social science and the humanities, classified in different traditions as part of anthropology (the North American four-field model), history, or geography. The working model is straightforward: every object people made, used, or built leaves a trace, and the location, depth, and associations of that trace can be read to recover behavior, belief, and change. Methods, theory, and ethics exist to make that reading accurate and honest.

## How the discipline formed

Curiosity about buried ruins is ancient, but the transformation into a science is recent. The Babylonian king Nabonidus (c. 550 BC) excavated temple foundation deposits at Sippar and Harran and dated Naram-Sin's temple, off by roughly 1,500 years but remarkable without scientific dating. In 12th-century Kashmir, Kalhana compiled the *Rajatarangini*, a systematic record of traditions, inscriptions, and coins. Song dynasty scholars Ouyang Xiu and Zhao Mingcheng established Chinese epigraphy between 960 and 1279, though it remained a branch of historiography. Renaissance figures like Cyriacus of Ancona and Flavio Biondo systematically recorded classical ruins; the term "archaeologist" first appears in 1824, with Jacob Spon's 1685 *archaeologia* as an earlier definition. Early descriptive work by non-professionals is called archaeography.

The shift to modern archaeology happened in waves. The 18th and 19th centuries brought controlled excavation: Herculaneum (1738) and Pompeii (1748), buried by Vesuvius in AD 79; Thomas Jefferson's 1784 trench excavation of a Virginia mound, correctly attributed to Native Americans; and William Cunnington's meticulous recording of Wiltshire barrows, funded by Richard Colt Hoare, who wrote "We speak from facts, not theory." Late 19th-century figures then turned excavation into a method. Schliemann, Calvert, and Dörpfeld demonstrated stratigraphic excavation at Hissarlik (Troy) in the 1870s. Augustus Pitt Rivers collected every artifact rather than only beautiful ones, arranged them by typology, and is widely regarded as the first scientific archaeologist. Flinders Petrie brought pottery-based seriation (ordering artifacts by stylistic change over time) to Egypt and mentored Howard Carter, the discoverer of Tutankhamun's tomb. Mortimer Wheeler's grid system, refined by Kathleen Kenyon, became the standard disciplined approach, and by the late 20th century nearly all professional archaeologists were university-trained.

Theory followed excavation. The mid-20th-century "New Archaeology" of Binford and Flannery, called processualism, treated archaeological questions as hypotheses to be tested with the scientific method. The 1980s post-processual movement (Hodder and others) pushed back, demanding reflexivity and questioning scientific objectivity. Historical processualism has since tried to synthesize the two, while modern work also draws on Marxism, feminism, queer theory, postcolonialism, and cognitive science.

## How archaeology actually works

A project moves through survey, excavation, and post-excavation analysis. Remote sensing uses passive tools like satellite imagery and active tools like lidar, laser altimeters, and drones, deployed in Peru and at Aphrodisias for 3D mapping, to locate features without breaking ground. Field surveys may be surface (walking the ground), aerial (reading cropmarks and shadow traces), geophysical (magnetometry, electrical resistivity), or underwater (sonar, marine magnetometers). Gordon Willey pioneered regional settlement survey in Peru's Virú Valley in 1949.

Excavation is destructive, expensive, and almost never total, so the central discipline is recording exactly where each artifact came from. Stratigraphy, the principle that deeper overlapping layers correspond to earlier periods, borrowed from geologists like Smith, Hutton, and Lyell, provides relative chronology. Each feature is described through its cut and fill, meaning the boundary edge dug into subsoil and the material that later settled into it. Permanent records include plans, sections, photographs, and context sheets. Post-excavation work (cleaning, cataloguing, typological comparison, plus zooarchaeology, paleoethnobotany, palynology, and stable isotope analysis) usually consumes more time than digging.

Subfields include historical, ethnoarchaeology, experimental, archaeometry, digital (3D modeling and agent-based simulation), maritime, and urban archaeology. Cultural resources management (CRM) now dominates US practice, mandated by the National Historic Preservation Act of 1966, with parallel mitigation in the UK under PPG16 from 1990. CRM saves sites threatened by development but is criticized for time pressure and lowest-bid contracts.

## Current pressures

Pseudoarchaeology, epitomized by Erich von Däniken's ancient-astronaut claims, misrepresents the past in violation of accepted scientific practice. Looting feeds the illicit antiquities trade. Climate change both destroys sites and opens frozen ones to a new field of glacial archaeology. Tension between descendant communities and researchers is partly addressed by NAGPRA (1990), though repatriations can end scientific study entirely, as with the 2005 Kitigan Zibi reburial. AI offers new tools for pattern recognition and translation but risks oversimplifying complex data and perpetuating outdated frameworks. Public outreach programs such as *Time Team* and Passport in Time build the popular support that protects sites from looting.
