# Ancient maritime history

The earliest known boats were rafts and dugout canoes, with the Pesse canoe surviving from roughly 10,000 years ago. Because rafts and hide-covered boats rarely survive archaeologically, early seafaring is inferred from human migration and from materials like obsidian that moved between islands.

Seafaring first flourished in Island Southeast Asia and the Mediterranean, both warm regions dotted with intervisible islands, and along rivers such as the Nile. By at least 5,000 years ago, Egyptians imported spices from the "Land of Punt" and Arabia, and Sumerians traded with the Indus Valley civilisation.

## The sail and its slow spread

The sail was a late invention. Egyptian iconographic evidence dates to the late fourth millennium BC, with suggestive evidence in the eastern Mediterranean and Persian Gulf by the late third millennium. Sailing spread from those centres around 2500 BC to the central Mediterranean and western India by 2000 BC, and to Island Southeast Asia and the western Mediterranean around 1000 BC. Northern Europe adopted sails in the early centuries AD, Scandinavia late in the first millennium, and the Caribbean only with Columbus in 1492. Whether the sail was invented once or in several places is debated; the Guayaquil river region of Ecuador is a likely case of independent invention, since prevailing winds blow opposite to the current, as on the Nile.

## Crossing open water in the Paleolithic

Stone tools and marine shells on Salibabu Island, North Sulawesi, dated 32,000–35,000 years ago, record a voyage of at least 100 km, probably the longest Paleolithic sea crossing known. The first crossing to Sahul (Australia and New Guinea) from Sundaland occurred 53,000–65,000 years ago, covering more than 55 miles out of sight of land, likely on large bamboo rafts. Favourable Northern Australian wet-season winds made this possible before 58,000 BP; their later weakening matches the single prehistoric colonisation phase of Australia.

## The Austronesian expansion

Austronesians developed the catamaran, outrigger ship, tanja sail, and crab claw sail. Between roughly 3000 and 1500 BC they used these to colonise most of the Indo-Pacific, reaching Easter Island and New Zealand between the 10th and 13th centuries AD. Before the 16th century they were the most widespread ethnolinguistic group on Earth, stretching from Madagascar to Easter Island.

Outrigger technology spread to Sri Lanka and southern India around 1000–600 BC, visible in borrowed words for "ship" such as Tamil paṭavu and Telugu paḍava, all derived from Proto-Hesperonesian *padaw. Bornean colonists reached Madagascar and the Comoros by around 500 AD. In the east, the Lapita culture spread from the Northern Marianas through Island Melanesia to Tonga and Samoa; after about two thousand years Polynesians continued to the Cook Islands, Hawaii, Easter Island, and New Zealand between roughly AD 700 and 1200.

## Ancient sea routes and the Indian Ocean

Maritime trade began as coastal cabotage and expanded with mastery of monsoon winds, allowing crossings of the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal. South Asia's many port choices made maritime monopoly difficult, and by the 2nd century BC Roman merchants could cross the Mediterranean by sea at one-sixtieth the cost of overland transport.

Austronesians built the earliest large Asian trade networks. The Maritime Jade Road moved raw jade from Taiwan to the Philippines, where it was carved into lingling-o ornaments, lasting about 3,000 years from 2000 BCE to 1000 CE. By 1500–600 BCE Austronesians traded with Dravidian South Asia, exchanging crops and outrigger boats. By the 2nd century BCE their networks merged with South Asian, Middle Eastern, East African, and Mediterranean routes to form the Maritime Silk Road. Srivijaya, a Hindu-Buddhist Austronesian polity founded at Palembang in 682 CE, dominated the Malacca and Sunda straits, controlling the spice trade between India and China.

## The Mediterranean world

Cardium pottery marks the earliest Mediterranean seafaring culture, spreading 2,000 km from Genoa to the Mondego estuary between roughly 6000 and 5500 BC in only 100–200 years, suggesting deliberate coastal colonisation. Minoan Crete was active in the eastern Mediterranean by the 2nd millennium BC. Phoenicia, centred on modern Lebanon, pioneered the bireme and spread maritime trade across the Mediterranean during the early first millennium BC. The Greek trireme, powered by oarsmen, became the dominant warship, and the Lighthouse of Alexandria, built 285–247 BC on Pharos island, marked the peak of ancient navigational infrastructure. Piracy was endemic; by the 1st century BC, pirate states along the Anatolian coast threatened Roman commerce.

## Egypt and the Sea Peoples

Egypt's first warships appeared in the Middle Kingdom, with a 16th-century-BC tomb inscription for Amenhotep I describing a 41-metre vessel rowed by 108 oarsmen. Thutmose III's warships displaced up to 360 tons. Around 1200–1150 BC the Sea Peoples, a confederacy of seafaring raiders Merneptah called "the foreign-countries of the sea," attacked the eastern Mediterranean, contributing to the collapse of the Bronze Age.

According to Herodotus, Pharaoh Necho II sponsored a Phoenician expedition, possibly between 610 and 594 BC, that sailed from the Red Sea around Africa and returned to the Nile after three years. Herodotus cited as evidence the Phoenicians' report that the sun stood on their right as they sailed west around southern Africa, credible only if Africa were circumnavigable from the south. Strabo, Polybius, and Ptolemy doubted the account, and Egyptologist A. B. Lloyd considers it "extremely unlikely" that an Egyptian king would have authorised such a voyage.

## Northern Europe, India, and Japan

The coracle, a small hide-covered frame boat, was used in Britain before the Roman invasion. The Dover Bronze Age Boat, dated to about 1600 BC, is one of the oldest known seagoing vessels: 9.5 m long, built of oak planks stitched with yew lashings, capable of 4–5 knots in Force 4 winds. Norse seafarers from southern and central Scandinavia established states across northern Europe from the late 8th to 11th centuries, with Leif Erikson reaching North America around the year 1000.

The Harappan civilisation built the world's first tidal dock at Lothal on the Gujarat coast, trading with Mesopotamia, where the Indus Valley may have been known as Meluhha. Kautilya's Arthashastra describes a state department of waterways under the nāvādhyakṣa. Japan had a navy by the 6th century, and in 663 AD a Tang–Silla alliance defeated Japanese forces at the Battle of Baekgang, expelling them from Korea.
