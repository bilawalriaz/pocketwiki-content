# Mediterranean and Middle East theatre of World War II

The Mediterranean and Middle East theatre was the longest-running of World War II, lasting from Italy's declaration of war in June 1940 to the final Axis surrender in Italy in May 1945. It covered a single interconnected arena from Southern Europe through North Africa to the Middle East and the Horn of Africa, with land, sea, and air operations feeding into one another. Its outcome destroyed the Italian Empire and tied down German forces that Germany would later need elsewhere.

## Italian aims and the opening campaigns

Fascist Italy under Benito Mussolini entered the war to build an empire from Gibraltar to the Persian Gulf, seeing itself as a "prisoner of the Mediterranean" hemmed in by Britain and France. After earlier conquests in Ethiopia (1935) and Albania (1939), Italy attacked in several directions: into France, against British shipping and the island of Malta, into Egypt from Libya, and into Greece in October 1940.

Nearly all of these openings failed. The invasion of Greece was halted and reversed by the Greeks, forcing Germany to intervene. In North Africa, the British Operation Compass destroyed the Italian 10th Army by February 1941, pushing Italy out of Egypt and deep into Libya. The single theatre thus opened in June 1940 with a string of Italian defeats.

## Axis recovery and the widening war (1941)

Germany rescued its ally in three regions. In North Africa, the Afrika Korps under Erwin Rommel arrived in 1941 and, though technically subordinate to Italian command, drove the British back toward the Egyptian border. In the Balkans, the April 1941 invasions of Yugoslavia and Greece were swift, leading to a British evacuation and the airborne Battle of Crete. In East Africa, British forces from Kenya and Sudan destroyed the Italian empire there by May 1941, restoring the Ethiopian Emperor.

The war also spread into the Middle East. A pro-Axis coup in Iraq in April 1941 triggered the Anglo-Iraqi War, which Britain won within weeks. Britain and Free France then invaded Vichy-controlled Syria and Lebanon in June–July 1941 to block further Axis influence. In August 1941, Britain and the Soviet Union jointly invaded neutral Iran to secure a supply route to the USSR and protect its oil fields, deposing the pro-German Shah.

## The Mediterranean sea lanes and the Allied turnaround

Control of the Mediterranean Sea determined whether armies in North Africa could be supplied. The British island fortress of Malta, under constant Axis air attack, was the key Allied base for attacking the convoys that fed Rommel's forces.

The turning point came in late 1942. The Second Battle of El Alamein (23 October–November 1942) ended the Axis threat to Egypt. Eight days later, Operation Torch landed American and British forces in Morocco and Algeria, opening a second front. Axis forces in Tunisia, caught between two Allied armies advancing from east and west, surrendered in May 1943, ending the North African war.

## The Italian collapse and civil war

Allied forces invaded Sicily in July 1943. Within weeks Mussolini was deposed, and in September Italy signed an armistice with the Allies. Germany reacted instantly, occupying most of the peninsula, rescuing Mussolini, and installing him as head of a puppet state in the north (the Italian Social Republic, also called the Republic of Salò). The rest of Italy joined the Allies as the Italian Co-Belligerent Army.

What followed was an Italian Civil War fought alongside the Allied advance. Germans and Italian Fascists held successive defensive lines across the mountainous peninsula. The Gustav Line halted the Allies for months in 1943–44, and the Gothic Line held until the spring 1945 offensive. Rome fell in June 1944, but the war in Italy ended only when partisans captured and executed Mussolini on 28 April 1945 and all German forces in Italy surrendered at Caserta on 2 May 1945.

## Strategic debates

Three debates remain about the theatre's significance. Historians still argue whether the German rescue of Italy in the Balkans in spring 1941, by forcing a delay to the invasion of the Soviet Union, helped doom Barbarossa. Others question whether the Allied "soft underbelly" strategy through the Mediterranean was wiser than an earlier direct cross-Channel invasion of France. The label "Italian Civil War" for 1943–45, popularised by Claudio Pavone in 1991, is also still contested in Italian memory politics.
