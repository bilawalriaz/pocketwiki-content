# History of the Peloponnesian War

Thucydides, an Athenian general born around 460 BC, wrote a historical account of the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC) that became a founding text of Western historical writing and of political realism, the analysis of politics in terms of power, interest, and necessity. The work is unfinished, its narrative breaking off mid-sentence in 411 BC, six years before the war ended, and it has been continuously studied since antiquity.

The war was fought between two coalitions: the Peloponnesian League, led by Sparta and built on land power, and the Delian League, led by Athens and built on naval supremacy. Thucydides traces the primary cause to the growth of Athenian power after the Persian Wars and the alarm this rise inspired in Sparta. In the preceding fifty years, a span he calls the *pentecontaetia*, Athens transformed a defensive alliance of Greek city-states into what amounted to an Athenian empire, and the resulting fear on both sides made conflict, in his analysis, almost inevitable.

What distinguishes Thucydides from his predecessor Herodotus, the earlier Greek historian who emphasised legend and divine causation, is method. He organises events year by year, dividing each year into a summer campaign season and a winter period, a strict chronological framework new to Greek history-writing. He inserts long set-piece speeches, the most famous being Pericles' Funeral Oration, to reveal the motives and values of the participants. Some of these speeches he heard himself; others he composed from eyewitness reports or from his own judgment of what a speaker "was called for" to say in a given situation, and the speeches should therefore be read as plausible reconstructions rather than verbatim transcripts.

A second innovation is secular causation. The gods never act as agents in the *History*; events are driven by human decisions, fear, interest, and the clash between justice and power. Religion still motivates individual characters, especially the Spartans, but it does not move the plot.

The narrative emphasises several themes that follow from this human-centred view. War corrupts society from within, producing *stasis*, the violent factional conflict within a single city-state that recurs throughout the book. Power overrides morality, most starkly in the Melian Dialogue, a fictional exchange between Athenian and Melian envoys in which the Athenians argue that the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must, often cited as the earliest extended example of realpolitik, politics based on practical and material calculation rather than ethics, in Western literature. Sea power is the material foundation of empire: Athens' triremes, warships with three banks of oars that dominated Mediterranean warfare for centuries, are treated as the instruments of its rise. Thucydides also pauses to record an early scientific correlation, noting that an earthquake in the Malian Gulf coincided with a tsunami and inferring a causal link.

Thucydides was not a detached observer. He held military command, was exiled in 424 BC after a failure in Thrace, and returned only after the war. The text ends mid-sentence in 411 BC, and he probably died around 400 BC, leaving the work incomplete. The question of how to read the surviving text remains open. "Analysts" treat apparent contradictions as evidence that Thucydides revised his views over time, producing different "strata" of composition; "unitarians" hold that the work is a single, coherent whole. A separate debate concerns his objectivity: although he claims to have written "to last for ever," some scholars detect a pro-Athenian or oligarchic sympathy, while others read the text as epic literature shaped by deliberate literary aims. His analytical vocabulary of fear, interest, and honour shaped political thought from Thomas Hobbes, whose 1628 English translation made the work accessible in English, through later statesmen including Woodrow Wilson.
