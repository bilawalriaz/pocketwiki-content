# History of Africa

Africa is the oldest continuously inhabited continent and the evolutionary cradle of *Homo sapiens*, which arose there roughly 350,000–260,000 years ago. Its history is a layered sequence of regional civilisations, long-distance trade, and—late but decisively—European colonisation that redrew the continent's political map in a single generation.

Prehistory and the first states. The Sahara was once fertile, supporting human settlement before rapid desertification around 3500 BCE. In the Nile Valley, ranked societies coalesced into the unified Kingdom of Egypt under the 1st dynasty around 3100 BCE, while the Kingdom of Kerma arose in Nubia to the south. These two powers traded and warred with each other for millennia. Northeast Africa is the earliest documented zone of state formation on the continent.

A continent-wide expansion. From about 3000 BCE to 500 CE, the Bantu expansion carried Bantu-speaking agriculturalists out of Central Africa. As they moved into Eastern and Southern Africa, they spread farming, ironworking, and a related family of languages over much of sub-Saharan Africa. This is why a band of related Bantu languages stretches from Cameroon to South Africa today.

Trade-based empires. West Africa grew wealthy by moving goods across the Sahara—gold southward, salt northward. Tichitt culture (c. 4000 BCE) preceded a succession of Sahelian states, of which Ghana (Wagadu), Mali, and Songhai were the most powerful, with Mali and Songhai peaking between the 13th and 16th centuries. Far to the east, the Aksumite kingdom in the Horn of Africa became a major trading power and adopted Christianity in the 4th century, while Swahili city-states such as Kilwa and Mombasa linked East Africa to Indian Ocean commerce. North Africa cycled through Carthage, Berber kingdoms, and Islamic dynasties (Fatimids, Almohads) after 7th-century Arab conquests carried Islam across the region.

The Atlantic slave trade. Beginning in the 15th century, European traders along the African coast initially sought gold and ivory, then increasingly enslaved people to work American plantations. The trade reoriented many African economies toward capturing and selling captives, stimulated new states such as Asante and Dahomey, and inflicted deep demographic loss. Internal jihads such as the Sokoto Caliphate tried to reorder West African politics in the 19th century.

The Scramble. Between 1870 and 1914, European powers—armed with quinine, machine guns, and mutual rivalry—partitioned almost the entire continent. The 1884–1885 Berlin Conference formalised these claims, drawing the borders that largely survive today. Colonial rule disrupted existing institutions, often governed through traditional leaders (indirect rule), and spread Christianity.

Independence. After the Second World War, African nationalism surged. Decolonisation peaked in 1960, the "Year of Africa," when 17 nations became independent. The Organisation of African Unity, founded in 1963, preserved the colonial borders to avoid border wars. Namibia's independence in 1990 was among the last. Post-independence states have inherited those borders and face internal conflict, neocolonial economic dependence, and climate stress.
