# Middle Ages

The Middle Ages (c. 5th–late 15th centuries) are the millennium between classical antiquity and the modern era, beginning with the fragmentation of the Western Roman Empire and ending amid the Renaissance, Reformation, and Age of Discovery. Roman law and administration, Germanic custom, and Christian institutions fused into new political and cultural forms. Historians divide the period into the Early Middle Ages (c. 5th–10th centuries), the High Middle Ages (c. 1000–1300), and the Late Middle Ages (c. 1300–1500).

## Why the Roman order collapsed

The empire fractured under the Crisis of the Third Century, then was permanently split east–west by Diocletian (286). The Battle of Adrianople (378), where Goths killed Emperor Valens, showed the frontier could no longer be held. Successive Germanic groups entered: Visigoths sacked Rome (410); Vandals, Alans, and Suevi crossed the Rhine (406); Huns under Attila raided the 440s–450s. By 476, when Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus, the Western Empire had dissolved into Germanic kingdoms (Ostrogoths in Italy, Visigoths in Spain, Franks in Gaul, Vandals in Africa). The Eastern (Byzantine) Empire survived, preserving Roman law through Justinian I's *Corpus Juris Civilis* (529–534).

## The Early Middle Ages: new kingdoms, Byzantium, and Islam

Successor kingdoms fused tribal custom with surviving Roman institutions. Taxation withered as armies were paid through land grants; slavery declined; society became rural. The Merovingian Franks under Clovis (r. 509–511) converted to Catholic Christianity, gaining papal alliance. Latin evolved into Romance languages; Greek persisted in the East; Slavic languages spread through the Balkans.

Byzantium revived under the Macedonian dynasty (Basil I, Basil II, 9th–10th centuries), expanding borders and sponsoring a Macedonian Renaissance. The Avars and Slavs pressured the Balkans for centuries.

The decisive external event was the rise of Islam. After Muhammad's death (632), Arab armies conquered Syria, Persia, Egypt, North Africa, and reached Iberia by 711. The Umayyad Caliphate ruled from Damascus; its defeat at Tours (732) by Charles Martel halted northward expansion. The Abbasid revolution (750) moved the capital to Baghdad and fragmented Muslim rule, while Mediterranean trade rerouted: Franks exchanged timber, furs, swords, and slaves for silks, spices, and metals.

In the West, the Carolingians built the first post-Roman empire. Pippin III deposed the Merovingians (753) with papal support; Charlemagne (r. 768–814) conquered Lombardy and Saxony and was crowned Emperor by Pope Leo III in Rome (800), asserting parity with Byzantium. His court at Aachen sponsored a Carolingian Renaissance under Alcuin of York that standardised script (Carolingian minuscule, ancestor of modern lowercase letters), imposed Roman liturgy, and preserved classical Latin texts through monastic copying. Charlemagne's empire broke apart at Verdun (843) into West Francia, East Francia, and a Middle Kingdom, then absorbed Viking, Magyar, and Saracen raids. Otto I's victory over the Magyars at Lechfeld (955) and his imperial coronation (962) founded the Ottonian Holy Roman Empire.

## The High Middle Ages: expansion and institutions

Europe's population roughly doubled (35–80 million) between 1000 and 1347, driven by the Medieval Warm Period and three agricultural innovations: the heavy plough, the horse collar, and the three-field system, which together raised land in use from one-half to two-thirds.

Two interlocking institutions organised this growth. *Manorialism* bound peasants to manors in return for labour, rents, and protection. *Feudalism* structured the military elite: lords granted *fiefs* to vassal knights in return for cavalry service, and stone castles anchored noble power. Towns grew and trade revived through the Hanseatic League in the Baltic and the Italian maritime republics.

Centralised monarchies emerged. The Norman Conquest (1066) created a cross-Channel English empire; Henry II (r. 1154–1189) ruled England and western France through marriage to Eleanor of Aquitaine. When John lost Normandy (1204), the barons forced him to seal Magna Carta (1215), the first sustained limit on royal power in Europe. The Capetian dynasty expanded from Île-de-France. In Iberia the *Reconquista* advanced against the Almohads, and by 1150 five Christian kingdoms (León, Castile, Aragon, Navarre, Portugal) coexisted with Muslim power. In Germany the Investiture Controversy (1076–1122) between Pope Gregory VII and Emperor Henry IV ended with the Concordat of Worms (1122), permanently weakening imperial control over bishops.

The Crusades, preached by Urban II in 1095 after the Seljuk defeat of Byzantium at Manzikert (1071), captured Jerusalem in 1099 and founded crusader states and military orders (Templars, Hospitallers). Later expeditions failed; the Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople (1204), fatally weakening Byzantium. Crusading logic extended to Baltic pagans (Teutonic Knights) and heretics in France (Albigensian Crusade, 1209).

Intellectually, the recovery of Aristotle through Arabic translations produced *Scholasticism*: Abelard and then Aquinas's *Summa Theologica* fused faith and reason. Universities emerged at Bologna (law), Paris (theology), and Oxford; Gratian's *Decretum* (c. 1140) systematised canon law; Arabic and Greek sources introduced Hindu-Arabic numerals and algebra. Architecture shifted from the massive round-arched Romanesque to the pointed-arch Gothic, exemplified at Chartres and Reims. Vernacular literature blossomed: *chansons de geste*, Arthurian legends, Dante, and Chaucer.

## The Late Middle Ages: crisis and transition

The Medieval Warm Period ended, and the Great Famine (1315–1317) presaged worse. The Black Death (1347–1350) killed roughly one-third of Europe. Labour scarcity raised wages; lords commuted labour services to cash rents; serfdom declined in the West but intensified in the East. Peasant revolts erupted alongside flagellant movements and anti-Jewish expulsions (England 1290, France 1306, Spain 1492).

War reshaped states. The Hundred Years' War (1337–1453) opened with English longbow victories at Crécy (1346) and Poitiers (1356), then turned under Joan of Arc (1429–1431) until France expelled the English. War drove taxation and representative assemblies (English Parliament, French Estates General). The Avignon Papacy (1309–1376) and Western Schism (1378–1417) with rival popes were resolved at the Council of Constance (1414–1418). Byzantium never recovered from 1204; the Ottoman capture of Constantinople (1453) under Mehmed II ended the Eastern Roman Empire.

Three transformations closed the era. The printing press with movable type, developed by Gutenberg around 1450, produced some 30,000 *incunabula* editions by 1500. Iberian exploration, led by Henry the Navigator, Dias (1488), da Gama (1498), and Columbus (1492), connected Europe to Africa, Asia, and the Atlantic world. Nominalist philosophy (Ockham, Duns Scotus) separated reason from faith in ways that shaped Protestant theology and early modern science.

## What remains contested

Periodisation itself is open: whether the medieval ends in 1453, 1492, 1517 (Reformation), or 1648 (Westphalia). The 19th-century "Dark Ages" label is now restricted to the Early Middle Ages, given cultural continuity preserved in monasteries and the Carolingian Renaissance. Whether "feudalism" names a coherent system or a retrospective construct, and how exactly the 14th-century crisis tied climate, population, and money, remain live questions.
