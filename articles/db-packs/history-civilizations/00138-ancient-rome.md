# Ancient Rome

Ancient Rome was a single Italian city that grew into the dominant power of the Mediterranean, then of Europe, North Africa, and the Middle East, ruling roughly 5 million km² and an estimated 50–90 million people (about a fifth of the world's population) at its peak. Its lasting contribution is a model of organised state power: codified law, professional armies, road and urban infrastructure, and a Latin language whose descendants still carry Roman vocabulary.

## Phases

| Phase | Dates | Core mechanism | Outcome |
|-------|-------|----------------|---------|
| Kingdom | c. 753–509 BC | Hereditary kings over a Latin hill settlement of perhaps 35,000 people | Overthrown; replaced by elected rule |
| Republic | 509–27 BC | Two annually elected consuls plus the Senate; citizen-soldier armies; tax farming (private contractors collecting public revenue) | Conquered Italy, then the Mediterranean; collapsed into civil war |
| Empire (Principate) | 27 BC–284 AD | One emperor (princeps, "first citizen") holding imperium (supreme military and civil authority) | Greatest territorial extent, then slow crisis |
| Late Empire (Dominate) and Fall | 284–476 AD | Tetrarchy (rule by four co-emperors); Christianity legalised | Western half fell in 476; Eastern half survived to 1453 |

Founding dates are traditional. Varro placed the city at 753 BC; archaeological settlement appears c. 1000 BC, large-scale organisation c. 800 BC. The Republic's start in 509 BC rests on later accounts, primarily Livy, writing 500 years after the event, so the early narrative blends legend (Romulus and Remus, Aeneas) with recoverable fact.

## How the Republic Worked, and Why It Broke

The Republic distributed power. Two consuls, elected for one-year terms, held imperium and commanded the army. Below them stood the Senate, an advisory body of former magistrates, and popular assemblies that passed laws and elected officials. This system ran a city, then a peninsula. It struggled with an ocean.

Three pressures broke it. Conquest flooded Rome with wealth and slaves, enriching senators and creating a new mercantile class called equestrians, while citizen-soldiers lost their farms during long campaigns abroad. Reformers who tried to redistribute land, notably the Gracchi brothers, were killed, showing the Senate would not yield peacefully. Commanders who led professional armies grew more powerful than the state they served. Marius opened recruitment to the poor, creating soldiers loyal to their general. Sulla marched on Rome itself. Caesar formed a triumvirate, conquered Gaul, crossed the Rubicon in 49 BC, and was assassinated in 44 BC. A final round of civil war left Octavian, Caesar's adopted heir, as sole victor. In 27 BC he took the name Augustus and held real power behind Republican forms.

## The Empire: Stability, Expansion, Fracture

Augustus created the Praetorian Guard (the emperor's household troops) and a standing professional army of 28 legions. The first dynasty, Julio-Claudian, fixed imperial tradition; the Flavians built the Colosseum and destroyed Jerusalem in 70 AD. The Nerva–Antonine dynasty produced the "five good emperors" (Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, Antoninus Pius, Marcus Aurelius), under whom the empire reached its greatest extent. In 212 AD the Edict of Caracalla extended citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants, turning a city-state's privilege into a universal legal status.

Crisis arrived after 235 AD: 26 emperors in 49 years, civil war, invasion, economic depression. Diocletian's response, the Tetrarchy, split rule among four co-emperors to manage the unwieldy territory. Christianity, intermittently persecuted, was legalised by the Edict of Milan in 313 AD and later became the official state religion. The Western half could not hold; in 476 AD the Germanic leader Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustus, the last Western emperor. Constantinople and the Eastern (Byzantine) Empire continued until 1453.

## Society, Law, and the Economy

Roman society was legally hierarchical: slaves, then freedmen (manumitted former slaves), then free-born citizens. The old patrician–plebeian divide faded during the late Republic. Women held property and could divorce but could not vote. Roman law, beginning with the Law of the Twelve Tables (449 BC) and reaching a codified form under Justinian (c. 530 AD), supplied concepts such as *jus gentium* ("law of nations," governing relations between Romans and foreigners) that shaped European legal systems into the 17th century.

The economy rested on farming and trade, with slave labour critical. Slaves were roughly a fifth of the empire's population and around 40% of the city of Rome. Tax collection was farmed out to private contractors, workable for a city but inefficient at imperial scale. Latin was the administrative language and evolved into the Romance languages; engineering feats such as roads, aqueducts, and concrete, invented in the late 3rd century BC, were not matched again until the 19th and 20th centuries.

## Why the Fall Is Still Debated

Historians have proposed loss of republican virtue, moral decay, military tyranny, class conflict, slavery, economic stagnation, climate change, disease, lead plumbing, and the natural life-cycle of civilisations. No single cause is accepted, because the Western Empire did not fall in one event but eroded over two centuries, while its Eastern half endured.
