# History of technology

Technology, from Greek *techne* (craft) and *logos* (word), is the body of tools and techniques a society uses to shape its environment. Its history is the record of humans widening what they can harness: stone, fire, animals, water, wind, fossil fuels, atoms, and information.

## How historians measure progress

Lewis H. Morgan divided human development into three stages (savagery, barbarism, civilization), separated by milestones such as the control of fire. Leslie White argued that culture's core job is to "harness and control energy," ranking societies by muscle, animal, agricultural, fossil-fuel, and nuclear stages, and summarizing the idea as P = E/T (development equals energy consumed divided by the efficiency of technical means). Nikolai Kardashev extended this into a scale for hypothetical advanced civilizations. Gerhard Lenski focused on information, distinguishing societies by whether they communicated through genes, learned experience, signs and logic, or symbols and writing, and classifying them as hunter-gatherer, simple or advanced agricultural, industrial, or specialist.

Economists track progress through productivity: output per unit of input (labor, capital, sometimes energy or materials). In the United States, manufacturing employment fell from over 30 percent in the 1940s to barely above 10 percent seventy years later, the empirical core of what Alvin Toffler, Daniel Bell, and John Naisbitt in the late 1970s called the post-industrial society, where services and information outweigh industry. Productivity growth in developed economies has slowed since the late 1970s.

## Prehistory: the long stone age

The earliest direct evidence of tool use, stone choppers and scrapers, comes from Ethiopia's Great Rift Valley about 2.5 million years ago. Toolmaking then refined itself: Acheulian shaped hand axes around 1.65 million years ago, Middle Paleolithic prepared-core blades around 300,000 years ago (when *Homo sapiens* emerged), Upper Paleolithic pressure flaking around 40,000 years ago, and Mesolithic microliths after the Ice Age ended about 10,000 years ago. Fire, clothing, and outrigger canoes extended reach across the Malay Archipelago, the Indian Ocean to Madagascar, and the Pacific, requiring knowledge of currents, weather, sailing, and stars. With no written record, archaeology (tools, cave paintings, the Venus of Willendorf, bones, mummies) is the only evidence for this span.

## Ancient civilization

The Neolithic revolution around 12,000 years ago added polished stone axes, crop farming, and the first mining tunnels. Mesopotamian cities from roughly 4000 BC used mud-brick arches, canals, and cuneiform writing, and produced the potter's wheel (5th millennium BC), the lever and shadoof crane (c. 3000 BC), early pulleys, and the screw. Babylonian astronomical diaries spanning 800 years allowed prediction of eclipses and planetary motion. Water wheels and watermills, the first motive force beyond muscle and sail, appear before 350 BC in the Persian Empire.

Egypt built the pyramids with inclined planes, wedges, and levers, moving limestone blocks of roughly 3.5 tons; Egyptians also developed papyrus, sundials, a calendar still in use, and stem-mounted rudders. The Indus Valley is noted for city planning and hydrology. China contributed cast iron, the multi-tube seed drill, the wheelbarrow, compass, rudder, crossbow, and gunpowder; deep wells reached 900 meters for brine and natural gas. Hellenistic Alexandria, from the 4th century BC, combined Greek and Near Eastern knowledge in Archimedes, Ctesibius, and Hero, yielding Archimedes' screw, the water organ (the first keyboard instrument), the Antikythera mechanism (an analog computer with a differential gear), the astrolabe, and the lighthouse. Rome added volcanic-sand concrete still intact 2,000 years later, glassblowing, the codex book, and roads unmatched until the 19th century. In the Americas, Inca masons fitted one-ton stone blocks so precisely that a blade cannot pass between them, and Maya engineers built the first pressurized Mesoamerican water system at Palenque; without draft animals or wheels, Aztec roads served foot travel.

The three-age system (Stone, Bronze, Iron) describes a Eurasian sequence in which smelting likely emerged from pottery kilns, bronze enabled stronger ships and long-distance trade, and iron displaced bronze because its ores were more abundant. The system is misleading outside Eurasia and meaningless for isolated peoples such as the Spinifex, Sentinelese, and some Amazonian groups, who still use stone tools.

## Medieval and early modern

Medieval economies shifted from muscle to water and wind power for grinding grain; the 1086 Domesday Book records 5,624 English water mills, about one for every thirty families. The Islamic Golden Age (8th–16th centuries) inherited earlier empires' science and spread crops, hydropower, tidal and wind power, paper mills (Baghdad 794–795), the spinning wheel, and pure potassium nitrate gunpowder formulas. Al-Jazari's 1206 crankshaft and camshaft became central to later engines, and he built programmable automata and a hydropowered castle clock.

Medieval Europe, once dismissed as stagnant, produced mechanical clocks, spectacles, vertical windmills, plate armor, cannons, and the pintle-and-gudgeon rudder, lateen sail, and dry compass that made the Age of Exploration possible. The movable cast-metal type printing press (c. 1441) multiplied book output, helping fuel the Scientific Revolution. By the late 17th century, deep mines needed up to 500 horses for pumping until Thomas Savery's1698 steam pump and Thomas Newcomen's 1712 engine.

## The industrial revolutions

From 1760 to the 1830s, cheap British coal powered textile machines (flying shuttle, spinning jenny, water frame, spinning mule, power loom), steam engines that drained mines, and coke-fired blast furnaces that raised iron output. The 1830 Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened with Stephenson's Rocket. Portsmouth's block mills (1803) introduced sustained mass production with metal machine tools.

The Second Industrial Revolution (1860s–1914) added chemicals, electricity, petroleum, and steel. Bell patented the telephone in March 1876 (who built the first working version is debated), and Edison's networks made electric light practical. Electrification, later judged the 20th century's most important technical development, enabled multiple factory shifts.

The 20th century brought mass-produced automobiles, nuclear power after the Manhattan Project, jet engines, and the space age. The Human Genome Project, conceived in 1985 and completed in 2003, sequenced the three billion base pairs of human DNA; its data demands drove adoption of Internet protocols and motivated Tim Berners-Lee's World Wide Web. Optical amplifiers and wave-division multiplexing (1996) multiplied fiber-optic capacity, enabling commercial Internet scale.

The 21st century extends this trajectory with quantum computers, gene therapy (introduced 1990), 3D printing (1981), nanotechnology (1985), graphene, drones, advanced batteries, and AI. The Large Hadron Collider, built 1998–2008, is the largest single machine ever constructed; LIGO now detects gravitational waves; and spaceflight continues with the James Webb Space Telescope (launched December 2021), the completed International Space Station, and planned Mars missions in the 2030s.
