# Allies of World War II

The Allies of World War II were an international military coalition formed to oppose the Axis powers (Germany, Italy, Japan). Formally renamed the "United Nations" in 1942, the alliance shifted from a small European coalition in 1939 to a global coalition of more than two dozen governments by war's end, and it became the institutional foundation of the modern United Nations.

## Core membership: the Big Four

The alliance revolved around four principal powers. The **United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, the United States, and China** were designated the "Four Powers" in the 1942 Declaration by United Nations, dominating strategic decision-making throughout the war. Three of them, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, and the United States, are usually called the "Big Three," reflecting their far greater industrial and military weight than China.

A **declaration of war** on the Axis was the first step to joining, but full participation required signing the Declaration by United Nations on 1 January 1942, which 26 states did. Signing also made a state eligible for membership in the postwar United Nations, a deliberate design by US President Franklin Roosevelt to bind wartime cooperation to a permanent international body.

## How the coalition formed

When Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939, the Allied side consisted of Poland, the United Kingdom and its empire (including Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, and India), and France, essentially the same coalition that had won the First World War. After Germany overran western Europe in 1940, occupied governments relocated to London as governments-in-exile, where they continued to function as Allied members.

The Soviet Union joined on 22 June 1941 after Hitler broke the 1939 non-aggression pact, a dramatic reversal: Stalin had previously co-operated with Germany in partitioning Poland and the Baltic states. The United States entered on 8 December 1941 following Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor, and China formally declared war on the Axis the next day, though it had been fighting Japan since the 1937 Marco Polo Bridge incident.

## Strategy and tensions

Military direction came from a series of Anglo-American summits, then Big Three summits once the Soviet Union was fighting. The Atlantic Charter (August 1941) defined the Anglo-American war aim, the "final destruction of Nazi tyranny." Key decisions:

- **Casablanca (1943)**: delay a cross-Channel invasion of France until mid-1944.
- **Cairo (1943)**: Roosevelt, Churchill, and Chiang Kai-shek planned operations against Japan.
- **Tehran (1943)**: the Big Three agreed the cross-Channel invasion was the top priority for 1944; Stalin promised to enter the Pacific war once Germany was beaten.
- **Yalta (February 1945)**: zones of occupation for defeated Germany and the postwar United Nations.
- **Potsdam (July–August 1945)**: Truman and Attlee (replacing Roosevelt and Churchill) demanded Japan's unconditional surrender.

Two recurring tensions shaped the alliance. First, Britain and the US repeatedly delayed the cross-Channel invasion, a decision that Stalin saw as indifference to Soviet losses: roughly 5 million Axis soldiers were killed or went missing on the Eastern Front. Second, the US and Britain clashed over the Pacific: Washington wanted to support Nationalist China and to undermine European empires, while London prioritised recovering Burma, Malaya, Hong Kong, and Singapore. These disputes never broke the alliance, but they prefigured the Cold War split.

## Material cooperation

Lend-Lease was the principal material link. The US delivered roughly $42–50 billion in war materiel, of which the British Commonwealth and empire received about half, and supplied the Soviet Union with about $10 billion (7% of Soviet war production). In return, Allied countries provided about $8 billion in Reverse Lend-Lease, including food and forward bases. Lend-Lease kept Britain in the war before US entry and sustained the Soviet war effort throughout.

## Other Allies and late switchers

Beyond the Big Four, the Allies included governments-in-exile in London (Poland, Czechoslovakia, Norway, the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg, Greece, and Yugoslavia), Free France under Charles de Gaulle from June 1940, and colonial forces, especially British India, which by 1945 had 2.5 million volunteers under arms. Latin American participants included Brazil, which sent a 25,700-strong expeditionary force to Italy.

Four former Axis states switched sides in 1943–44, becoming "co-belligerents" rather than full Allies. Italy switched after Mussolini's fall in July 1943; Romania, Bulgaria, and Hungary switched as the Red Army approached. Finland, which had fought alongside Germany to recover territory lost in 1940, was forced to expel German troops in 1944. None of these states was recognised as a full Allied power, a legal distinction with lasting consequences for the 1947 peace treaties.

## Legacy: the United Nations

The alliance directly produced the United Nations. The 1942 Declaration was its seed; the 1944 Dumbarton Oaks Conference (US, UK, USSR, China) designed the Security Council; the San Francisco Conference of April–June 1945 produced the Charter, signed by 50 states on 26 June 1945. The five permanent Security Council seats (US, UK, USSR, France, China) reflect the Big Four plus France, rehabilitated after liberation. The wartime coalition thus evolved, within four years, from an improvised military alliance into the permanent framework of the postwar international order.
