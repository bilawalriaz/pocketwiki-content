# American Revolution

The American Revolution (1765–1789) turned a colonial tax dispute into the first working modern federal republic. Colonists went from demanding their rights as Britons to declaring independence, fighting a war, and then writing a constitution that bound thirteen states into one nation. The core idea: legitimate government rests on the consent of the governed, and when a government acts without that consent the people may form a new one.

## How it began

The Thirteen Colonies had grown used to running their own affairs. Under the policy of salutary neglect, Parliament largely left colonial trade and taxes alone. After the French and Indian War ended in 1763, Britain faced a national debt of about £133 million and stationed 10,000 soldiers in North America. To pay for both, Parliament decided the colonies should help cover the cost.

The turning point was a constitutional argument. Colonists accepted trade regulation but rejected direct taxes imposed by a Parliament in which they had no elected seats. Their slogan, "No taxation without representation," captured the principle. When Parliament passed the Stamp Act in 1765, the first direct tax on the colonies (on documents, newspapers, even playing cards), nine colonies sent delegates to the Stamp Act Congress. Britain repealed the tax in 1766 but reaffirmed its authority in the Declaratory Act. The pattern repeated: new taxes (Townshend Acts of 1767 on paper, glass, and tea), partial repeal, new provocations, escalating resistance.

## From protest to rebellion

Resistance built its own institutions. The Sons of Liberty organized boycotts and intimidation. Samuel Adams's Committees of Correspondence linked Patriots across all 13 colonies, creating the first continental political network. When the Tea Act of 1773 let the British East India Company undercut smugglers, colonists responded with the Boston Tea Party: dumping £10,000 of tea into Boston Harbor.

Parliament's reply, the four Intolerable Acts of 1774, closed Boston's port, restricted town meetings, allowed troops to be quartered in private homes, and moved British soldiers' trials to Britain. Twelve colonies sent delegates to the First Continental Congress, which organized a continent-wide boycott. Shots at Lexington and Concord on April 19, 1775 began the war. The Second Continental Congress created the Continental Army and appointed George Washington commander.

## Ideology

The push for independence rested on Enlightenment ideas filtered through British Whig politics. John Locke's *Two Treatises of Government* argued that people are naturally free and equal and that legitimate government needs their consent. American republicanism combined this with the classical ideal of civic virtue: citizens putting public duty above self-interest. Protestant dissenters preached these themes from their pulpits, while most Church of England clergy preached loyalty to the crown, a split that helps explain who became a Patriot and who a Loyalist.

The Declaration of Independence (July 4, 1776), drafted largely by Thomas Jefferson, translated these ideas into a founding document. "All men are created equal" was a political claim about natural rights that made revolution philosophically legitimate; it was not a description of colonial society, since slavery persisted and women could not vote.

## Winning the war

The war's outcome turned on a few critical moments. After losing New York in 1776, Washington revived his army with surprise victories at Trenton and Princeton. The British surrender at Saratoga in 1777 was the pivot: it convinced France to sign the 1778 Treaties of Amity and Commerce and Alliance, becoming the first nation to recognize American independence. Spain and the Dutch Republic entered as French allies by 1780. At Yorktown in October 1781, a combined French and American force trapped Cornwallis's army. Britain still held New York, Charleston, and Savannah with 26,000 troops, but public support at home collapsed, and no further offensives were launched. The 1783 Treaty of Paris recognized American sovereignty and gave the new nation all land east of the Mississippi.

The war also widened the conflict. About 200,000 Indigenous people east of the Mississippi mostly sided with Britain, hoping to limit colonial expansion; after the war Britain ceded their lands without consulting them. Tens of thousands of enslaved people escaped to British lines, and roughly 20,000 Black men fought on one side or the other. About 46,000 Loyalists, 15–20% of the white population, eventually relocated to Canada.

## Building a nation

Independence was only the first problem. The Articles of Confederation, ratified in 1781, gave the central government no power to tax, so Congress financed the war with French loans, requisitions, loans from the wealthy, and "Continental" paper money that became so worthless that "not worth a Continental" entered the language. Robert Morris, Superintendent of Finance, used a French loan to establish the Bank of North America in 1781.

Shays's Rebellion in 1786 convinced nationalists like Washington and Alexander Hamilton that a stronger union was needed. The 1787 Philadelphia Convention drafted a Constitution giving the federal government authority to tax and laying out a republic with separated powers. It took effect in 1789, with Washington as the first president. The Bill of Rights (first ten amendments) was ratified in 1791. In 1790, Congress consolidated about $80 million in state, foreign, and domestic debts into a single national obligation under Hamilton's plan, giving the new government creditworthiness.

## What it changed

The Revolution made democratic self-government thinkable as a working system rather than a theoretical idea. The U.S. Constitution is the world's oldest written constitution still in use. The example directly inspired the French Revolution, the Haitian Revolution, and the Latin American wars of independence. By 1804, every northern state had begun abolishing slavery; no southern state did. Women gained some property and divorce rights but remained legally subordinate under coverture, unable to vote or hold office. The democratic promise of 1776 was real, partial, and contested from the start, and it set the terms on which later Americans would keep arguing.
