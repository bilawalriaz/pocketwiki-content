# World War I cryptography

Wireless telegraphy let armies send messages by radio, but any receiver in range could also hear them. That made codes and ciphers essential. The most consequential break of the war was British Naval Intelligence's Room 40 decoding of the Zimmermann telegram, which helped draw the United States into the conflict.

## Common methods

Field armies on both sides relied on simple substitution ciphers. These were fast and required little training, but every unit needed a copy of the codebook, and a captured or copied book handed the enemy the entire system.

More important messages used mathematical ciphers for additional protection. The Russian army used a heavy variant of the Vigenère cipher; Austro-Hungarian cryptanalyst Hermann Pokorny broke it in three days. The French diplomatic cipher was the Tableau de Concordance. British military intelligence also used the Playfair cipher.

## Field conditions

The Imperial German Army regulated which cipher could be used at what distance from the front. Within three kilometers, a danger zone, the only permitted system was the three-number code. Behind that, the three-letter code was allowed. Communications between divisions, corps, and army headquarters used ADFGVX, a Polybius square with columnar transposition and letters chosen for clean Morse transmission.

The Germans intercepted Russian traffic during the 1914 invasion of East Prussia. Because Russian corps sent some messages in plaintext, German cryptanalysts could read them directly; they read the enciphered ones too. The interception at Tannenberg confirmed where the Russian armies were moving.

## Codebreaking bureaus

Britain ran two. Room 40, part of the Royal Navy, was led first by James Alfred Ewing, then by William R. Hall ("Blinker" Hall), and staffed by figures including Nigel de Grey, Dilly Knox, William Montgomery, and Alastair Denniston. MI1(b), the Army's section, was headed by Malcolm Vivian Hay from 1915 and included Oliver Strachey. Ernst Fetterlein, a former Tsarist cryptographer, joined Room 40 in June 1918 to work on Austrian and Bolshevik material.

France was better prepared than any other belligerent because of prewar cryptanalytic work by Georges Painvin and Étienne Bazeries, the latter recalled from retirement. France began the war with eight intercept stations and built many more, including one in the Eiffel Tower. Colonel Cartier of the War Ministry recorded over 100 million words of intercepted German radio traffic during the war.

The German Abhorchdienst, staffed mainly by mathematicians, was set up in 1916. Its French counterpart, Painvin, broke ADFGVX in 1918. The decrypt revealed that Germany was moving munitions for a planned offensive. Knowing the place and date, the French stopped the attack. The intercepted message became known as "The Radiogram of Victory." This was the second time in the war that cryptanalysis shaped events on a major scale, after the Zimmermann decrypt.

## United States

Herbert Yardley began as a State Department code clerk, then headed Military Intelligence Section MI-8 during the war and later led the Cipher Bureau, founded in 1919. Private work was done at Riverbank Laboratories in Chicago, owned by Colonel George Fabyan, where Elizebeth Friedman, William F. Friedman, and Agnes Meyer Driscoll were employed. The Navy used code A-1 and later created OP-20-G in 1922. The United States also used Choctaw and Cherokee speakers as code talkers, the first large-scale use of Indigenous languages for this purpose.
