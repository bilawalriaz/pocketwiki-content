# Akkadian Empire

The Akkadian Empire (c. 2334–2154 BC) was the first empire in world history, unifying the independent Sumerian city-states of Mesopotamia under a single Semitic-speaking dynasty. Centered on the city of Akkad, which has never been archaeologically located, it created a centralized state that stretched from the Persian Gulf (the "Lower Sea") to the Mediterranean (the "Upper Sea"), establishing a model for future Mesopotamian states and demonstrating the possibility of multi-ethnic imperial rule.

## Founding and Expansion
Sargon of Akkad rose from being a cupbearer to the king of Kish to conquer all of Sumer and then expand aggressively. He replaced local rulers, called ensis, with loyal Akkadian governors, establishing a centralized administration. His conquests reached the Lower and Upper Seas, and he organized naval expeditions to Magan (Oman) and Dilmun (Bahrain). His successors Rimush and Manishtushu faced constant revolts and were both assassinated.

## Zenith under Naram-Sin
Naram-Sin, Sargon's grandson, brought the empire to its greatest extent. He adopted the title "King of the Four Quarters" and was deified during his lifetime, a break from Sumerian tradition, where kings were divine representatives rather than gods. He campaigned in Syria, Anatolia, and against the Lullubi and Gutians, victories recorded on his Victory Stele. His reign also saw the first known named author, his daughter Enheduanna, high priestess of the moon god Nanna at Ur.

## Administration and Economy
Akkadian government set a classical standard for Mesopotamian states, with the king as a supreme, often divine, authority. The economy rested on agricultural surpluses from irrigated southern lands and rain-fed northern regions, supporting a large bureaucracy and military. Trade networks imported metals, timber, and stone from Anatolia, Lebanon, and the Indus Valley (Meluhha), and Akkadian became the lingua franca, the shared language of administration and diplomacy, across the Middle East.

## Collapse
Internal instability followed Naram-Sin's death, with weak successors and frequent revolts. A prolonged drought tied to the 4.2-kiloyear climate event (c. 2200 BC) caused crop failures and the collapse of the rain-fed northern agricultural subsidies. This economic crisis, combined with pressure from Amorite groups and a Gutian invasion, produced a "Dark Age" that lasted until the Ur III dynasty. The cause of collapse remains debated, with some scholars emphasizing the drought and others citing internal political weakness, and the exact role of the climate event is still uncertain.
