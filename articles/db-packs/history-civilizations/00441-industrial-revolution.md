# Industrial Revolution

The Industrial Revolution was a transition, beginning in Great Britain around 1760 and spreading over the following decades, from economies built on farming and handicraft production to economies built on industry, machine manufacturing, and factory work. In scale of material impact, historians compare it only to the original adoption of agriculture. It produced economic growth, population increase, and urbanisation on a scale that had no earlier precedent.

The core mechanism was a cluster of inventions that dramatically raised the output of a single worker. In textiles, mechanised spinning, through the spinning jenny (1764), the water frame (1769), and the spinning mule (1779), multiplied per-worker spinning output by roughly 500 over the earlier hand-spinning baseline, while the power loom (1785) multiplied weaving output by about 40. The American cotton gin (1793) made raw cotton about 50 times cheaper to prepare by mechanically separating fibre from seed. Steam power provided a new, transportable source of energy. Thomas Newcomen's early engine was efficient only enough to pump water out of mines, but James Watt's separate condenser (1778) cut coal use to roughly 20–25% of Newcomen's level, and later high-pressure engines could drive locomotives and steamboats. In iron-making, replacing scarce charcoal with coke lowered fuel cost, Henry Cort's puddling process (1784) produced structural-grade iron cheaply, and James Beaumont Neilson's hot blast (1828) preheated the air entering the blast furnace, again cutting fuel use. Machine tools, including John Wilkinson's cylinder borer (1774) and Henry Maudslay's slide-rest lathe, made it possible to cut metal accurately enough that identical parts could be swapped between machines, the basis of interchangeable-parts manufacturing.

These technologies were combined inside the factory. Earlier, cloth had been made under the putting-out system, in which merchants handed raw material to families who spun and wove at home. The new machines were too large and too expensive for cottage use, so production moved into water- and then steam-powered mills that concentrated workers in one place, which in turn drew people off the land and into growing industrial cities such as Manchester, nicknamed "Cottonopolis". Richard Arkwright was the leading example, building integrated cotton mills that became the model of the modern factory. British raw cotton imports rose from about 2.5 million pounds in 1750 to roughly 588 million pounds in 1850, a measure of how much the new system could absorb.

Britain had a head start because it combined abundant coal and iron ore, an agricultural revolution that freed up workers, a large internal market, and a legal climate that protected property and contracts. Industrialisation reached Belgium and France in the early nineteenth century and Germany, the United States, and Japan somewhat later. The United States developed interchangeable parts particularly aggressively in firearms production, an approach sometimes called the "American system". Outside the West, hand manufacturing in places such as India was undercut, the global centre of manufacturing shifted towards North America and Western Europe, and the demand for raw cotton encouraged the expansion of slavery in the American South.

The social consequences were as large as the technical ones. The share of people living in cities, about 3% of the world in 1800, rose to about 50% by 2000, driven by factory work. Sustained growth in average income began, but whether ordinary workers' living standards improved in the early decades is still genuinely debated; some historians argue that real wages and life expectancy did not rise meaningfully until late in the nineteenth century. Early factories demanded long hours at low pay, employed many children, and concentrated pollution and disease, which eventually produced trade unions, political movements such as Chartism, and public-health reforms culminating in the British Public Health Act of 1875. A new middle class of factory owners, managers, and professionals appeared, and everyday consumption shifted as mass-produced goods replaced individually made ones.

Historians disagree on exact start and end dates, on whether the word "revolution" is appropriate for what may have been a gradual shift, and on its chief causes, which have been attributed to high wages and cheap energy in Britain, to cultural factors such as a Protestant work ethic, to Europe's political fragmentation, or to earlier proto-industrialisation. There is also active debate over how much of Britain's early capital came from the Atlantic slave trade, a thesis most associated with Eric Williams.
