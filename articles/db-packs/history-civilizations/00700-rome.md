# Rome

Rome is a continuously inhabited city of more than 28 centuries, set on the Tiber River in west-central Italy, and the only place in the world that contains an independent country, Vatican City, within its boundaries. It has served successively as the capital of the Roman Kingdom, the Roman Republic, the Roman Empire, the Papal States, the Kingdom of Italy, and the present Italian Republic. That sequence of political roles, from ancient monarchy to modern nation-state, makes Rome a focal point of Western history rather than simply a large European city.

## From villages to city

Humans lived in the area of Rome as long as 14,000 years ago, but the city itself formed through synoecism, the gradual merging of separate hilltop villages into a single urban centre. By the mid-eighth century BC this process had produced a settlement on the Palatine Hill. Roman tradition dated the city's founding to 753 BC, attributing it to the twin brothers Romulus and Remus, suckled by a she-wolf, and marked time from that year using the expression *Ab Urbe Condita*, "from the founding of the City." The name "Roma" has no settled origin: candidates include the Tiber (whose old name was *Rumon*), an Etruscan word for "teat" (referring to the she-wolf image), and the Greek word for "strength."

## Monarchy and Republic

Tradition records that seven kings ruled Rome over 244 years, the last being the Etruscan Lucius Tarquinius Superbus. In 509 BC the Romans expelled him and established a republic led by two consuls elected for one-year terms. The republic's internal life was shaped by conflict between patricians (old aristocratic families) and plebeians (common citizens), and its external life by war: victories over the Etruscans, Latins, and other Italic peoples extended Italian control, while the Punic Wars against Carthage (264–146 BC) and the Macedonian Wars gave Rome dominance over the Mediterranean. Rival political factions, the optimates and populares, produced civil war, brought Julius Caesar to power, and after his assassination in 44 BC paved the way for his heir Octavian to take the title Augustus in 27 BC and end the republic.

## Empire and its transformation

Augustus founded the Principate, a constitutional arrangement in which the emperor and the Senate shared formal power while real authority rested with the emperor. The empire reached its greatest extent under Trajan in the second century AD, from Britain to Mesopotamia. Two crises reshaped it. Nero's Great Fire of 64 AD was followed by the first Roman persecutions of Christians. The Crisis of the Third Century (AD 235–284) brought civil war, economic collapse, and near disintegration. Diocletian responded in 284–285 by introducing the Dominate, an openly autocratic system, and dividing the empire into eastern and western administrative halves. Constantine legalized Christianity with the Edict of Milan in 313 AD. After Theodosius I died in 395 AD, the two halves never reunited. The Visigoths sacked Rome in 410 AD, the Vandals in 455 AD, and in 476 AD the Germanic chieftain Odoacer deposed the boy emperor Romulus Augustus, the conventional marker for the end of the Western Roman Empire.

## Medieval and Renaissance Rome

When the western empire collapsed, political authority over Rome passed to the papacy. Pepin the Short granted the Pope temporal jurisdiction over central Italy in 756 AD, founding the Papal States, which lasted until Italian unification. The papacy was weakened in the 14th century when it moved to Avignon in France (1309–1377), an episode known as the Avignon Papacy, and later split by the Western Schism of 1377–1418, when rival claimants created a crisis of legitimacy. The city revived in the 15th century when popes such as Nicholas V sponsored large-scale building and patronage, turning Rome into a leading centre of Renaissance art through figures including Michelangelo and Raphael. The 16th-century Counter-Reformation, the Catholic Church's response to the Protestant Reformation, reinforced Rome's role as the spiritual capital of Catholicism. In 1527, troops of Emperor Charles V known as Landsknechts sacked the city.

## Modern Rome

In 1798 a short-lived Roman Republic was declared during the French Revolutionary Wars, and from 1809 to 1814 Napoleon annexed Rome to France. Italian unification in 1861 left Rome outside the new kingdom; Italian forces captured it in 1870 and made it the capital of the Kingdom of Italy in 1871. The Pope refused to recognise the new state until the Lateran Treaty of 1929 established Vatican City as a sovereign state. The 20th century brought rapid growth, including the 1960 Summer Olympics and the rise of Cinecittà Studios, founded in 1927, as a major film centre. Today Rome hosts United Nations agencies and remains a leading tourist destination, with its historic centre still organised into 22 traditional *rioni* whose layout descends from the 14 regions Augustus imposed.
