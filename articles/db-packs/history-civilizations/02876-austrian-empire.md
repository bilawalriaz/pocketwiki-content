# Austrian Empire

The Austrian Empire (1804–1867) was a multinational European great power built from the Habsburg dynasty's patchwork of territories. It was created to preserve the dynasty's imperial standing against Napoleon, dominated European conservative politics for a generation under Metternich, and was eventually dismantled by a combination of nationalist revolts and military defeats, which forced it to reorganise as the dual monarchy of Austria-Hungary.

## Origins and the Napoleonic Crisis

In 1804, Holy Roman Emperor Francis II proclaimed the Austrian Empire to give his dynasty a sovereign title independent of the now-fragile Holy Roman framework and to pre-empt Napoleon, who was about to declare himself Emperor of the French. The new title legally merged the Habsburg composite monarchy's separate realms, with one major exception: the Kingdom of Hungary kept its own institutions as a *Regnum Independens*, governed by its own Diet and monarch.

The empire continued inside the Holy Roman Empire until 1806, when Francis abdicated as Holy Roman Emperor and the Holy Roman Empire formally dissolved. Austria then fought Napoleon through the rest of the Napoleonic Wars, taking heavy defeats at Austerlitz (1805) and Wagram (1809) and losing territory by the Treaty of Pressburg (1806). These losses were reversed when Austria played a decisive part in Napoleon's final defeat in 1813–14, restoring it to great-power status.

## The Metternich System (1809–1848)

Klemens von Metternich became Foreign Minister in 1809 and, especially after 1815, became the dominant figure in Austrian and European politics. He was a committed conservative who believed that suppressing revolution and balancing the great powers were the surest guarantees of order. The Congress of Vienna (1815) confirmed Austria as a great power and made it president of the new German Confederation, a loose association of 39 German states.

Metternich's international method, often called the "Metternich system," relied on periodic congresses (Aix-la-Chapelle, Troppau, and others) to coordinate conservative powers against liberalism and nationalism. Domestically, he used censorship, a spy network, and police surveillance to muzzle dissent. The period brought political stability, economic growth, and a population rise to 37.5 million by 1843, but it also froze national aspirations in Italy, Germany, and the empire's own subject peoples.

## 1848, Neo-Absolutism, and the Turn Southward

The revolutions of 1848 swept the empire with nationalist, liberal, and socialist uprisings. Most were suppressed, but the shock forced Metternich to resign and produced lasting reforms, above all the abolition of serfdom (the binding of peasants to landlords and their land), along with a promised constitution that was never fully honoured.

From 1852 to 1859, Minister Alexander von Bach imposed neo-absolutism: a centralised, bureaucratised state that rejected constitutionalism and allied itself with the Catholic Church through the 1855 Concordat (an agreement between the state and the Vatican regulating Church affairs). The decade also brought economic modernisation, notably the abolition of internal customs duties, but not political freedom. Austria lost Lombardy in 1859 to France and Sardinia, and diplomatic isolation in the wake of its ambiguous behaviour during the Crimean War (1854–56) had already weakened its position.

## Defeat and the Compromise of 1867

The decisive blow came in 1866, when Prussia defeated Austria in the Austro-Prussian War, expelled it from German affairs, and dissolved the German Confederation. With the army spent and the German sphere lost, Hungary's demand for constitutional parity became impossible to refuse. The Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867 transformed the Austrian Empire into the dual monarchy of Austria-Hungary, in which the Empire of Austria and the Kingdom of Hungary shared a single monarch, foreign policy, and defence budget but kept separate parliaments, governments, and legal systems as constitutionally equal partners.
