# Ancient Roman technology

The Romans were antiquity's great borrowers and organisers of technology. They inherited techniques from the Greeks, Etruscans, Celts, and others, then combined them with disciplined logistics, large labour forces, and abundant volcanic materials to build roads, aqueducts, and harbours that remained unmatched until the 19th century. Most knowledge lived inside trade guilds, passed from master to apprentice; written technical sources survive chiefly from Vitruvius, Pliny the Elder, and Frontinus.

## Power

Without engines, the Romans drew on five sources. Human power drove cranes via treadwheels (shown on Trajan's Column) and ships' oars in battle; a windlass used pulleys and handspikes so teams could lift together. Animal power came from oxen for slow, heavy haulage, horses where speed mattered (cavalry, scouts), and donkeys or mules for light loads; animals also turned rotary mills. *De rebus bellicis* describes an ox-driven paddle ship, almost certainly never built.

Water power came from undershot wheels (a stream pushing submerged paddles) and overshot wheels (water dropping into buckets from an aqueduct-fed channel above). Undershot wheels were preferred despite being roughly 30 percent less efficient, because the aqueduct cost more than the extra power was worth. Wheels ground grain, raised water, and occasionally powered saws. Wind drove sails only; windmills did not exist. Thermae used passive solar gain through large southwest-facing windows. Steam existed only on paper: Hero of Alexandria's spinning-ball device turned at roughly 1500 rpm, but the labour and fuel made industrial use impossible.

## Materials and construction

Roman builders used wood, stone, marble, and concrete. Stone came from the nearest quarry. Cutters drilled lines of holes, drove in wooden wedges, soaked them, and let swelling wood split the bedrock. Imperial-age saws, at first hand-cranked then water-powered, replaced chisels for finishing. Pozzolana mortar, two parts volcanic clay from near Naples to one part lime, cured underwater and reached the hardness of natural rock. Lime mortars were tailored to the sand: river or sea sand mixed 2:1:1 with lime and powdered shell; inland sand 3:1 with lime. A later variant using crushed brick dust instead of volcanic ash reached about 3000 kilopascals in tensile strength, roughly fifteen times the 200 kPa of plain lime mortar, which is why builders could make thick joints. Cranes lifted 6–7 tons at a time.

The Pantheon's dome embeds 28 coffers, a reference to the perfect number 28 (1+2+4+7+14=28). Its concrete was laid in thick walls that did not need to be fully dry to cure; Delaine estimates about 400,000 man-days of labour. The later Hagia Sophia used the same pozzolana recipe; its arches sagged during construction because the mortar cures slowly, forcing engineers to remove decorative walls until it set.

## Waterworks

Eleven limestone aqueducts delivered over one million cubic metres of water per day through 350 km of channel, enough for roughly 3.5 million people by modern standards. The longest, supplying Carthage, ran 178 km; Constantinople's drew from 120 km away along a sinuous 336 km route. Water travelled in slightly sloped stone channels from mountain springs to distribution tanks, then through pipes to fountains and latrines. Where valleys deeper than about 50 m interrupted the line, inverted siphons forced water uphill. Only around 5 percent of any aqueduct ran on arched bridges; the rest flowed at or below ground level because bridges cost more to build and repair. Channels were coated with *opus signinum*, a pozzolana-lime plaster with crushed terracotta, to prevent erosion. Aqueducts also fed the sixteen overshot mills at Barbegal in Gaul, the largest concentration of mechanical power in the ancient world, and drove hydraulic mining at Las Medulas in Spain, where jets stripped hillsides to expose gold ore, and at Dolaucothi in Wales.

The Romans built at least 72 dams in Spain alone, plus earth dams in Britain (one at Longovicium probably served a smelting works) and masonry dams in North Africa, where they scaled up Nabataean wadi irrigation to capture seasonal floodwater for dry-season use.

## Sanitation and baths

Plumbing and toilets were borrowed from the Minoans, whose systems dated to about 3100 BCE. Public thermae combined hygiene with social life. Bathers moved from the changing room (*apodyterium*) to the warm dry *tepidarium*, then to the humid *caldarium* (up to 40 °C), and finally to the cold *frigidarium*. Doors were hung on inward-sloping posts so they swung shut automatically; bathers sat on wood rather than stone, since wood conducts heat away more slowly.

## Roads and vehicles

Over 400,000 km of road were built, about 80,500 km stone-paved, mainly for military use. Construction began with a pit dug to bedrock, filled with rubble, gravel, or sand, topped with concrete, and finished with fitted polygonal slabs. Relay stations let a courier cover up to 800 km in 24 hours. Bridges used the stone arch: the Pons Aemilius (142 BC) is Rome's oldest surviving stone bridge, and Trajan's Bridge over the Danube, by Apollodorus of Damascus, was the world's longest bridge for over a thousand years and stood at least 18 m above the water. Carts ran on stone-paved roads in grooved railed systems, with block axles, wooden wheels banded in metal, brakes, leather-and-bronze elastic suspension, and Celtic stone-ring bearings lubricated with mud.

## Military technology

The standard legionary javelin was the pilum, about 2.25 kg, designed to be unusable after one throw so enemies could not recover it; each soldier carried two, plus a wooden hand-guard. Combined with the *gladius* sword, this enabled the throw-and-charge tactic Polybius recorded. Body armour centred on *lorica segmentata*, articulated iron plates that gave better protection than chain mail (*lorica hamata*) but cost more, wore out faster, and were harder to repair in the field, which is why mail remained the everyday choice. The *testudo* ("tortoise") formation locked shields overhead to deflect missiles during sieges but required near-perfect discipline. Siege engines copied Greek designs, though the Romans were the first to mount ballistas on carts for mobility. The manuballista, a two-armed torsion engine resembling a giant crossbow, and the onager, a single-arm stone-thrower named for the wild ass's kick, softened fortifications before assault. The *corvus*, a hinged boarding bridge developed in the First Punic War, let legionaries fight naval battles as if they were land battles; the *helepolis*, a 15 m armoured tower on wheels, moved on capstan power or a counterweight. Greek fire, adopted in the 7th century AD and treated as a state secret, was a precursor to napalm used mainly at sea.

## Medicine

Roman military surgeons pioneered the battlefield surgical unit and developed instruments still in use today, including hemostatic tourniquets and arterial clamps, alongside a rudimentary antiseptic technique.
