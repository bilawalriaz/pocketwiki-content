# Eastern Front (World War II)

The Eastern Front, fought from 22 June 1941 to 8 May 1945 between Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union, was the largest and deadliest theatre of the Second World War, producing over 30 million deaths. It was a *Vernichtungskrieg*, a war of annihilation, in which Nazi ideology framed the campaign as a racial crusade against "Jewish Bolshevism" and the Slavic *Untermenschen* (subhumans). Nazi planners drew up *Generalplan Ost*, a programme envisaging the ethnic cleansing, enslavement, and starvation of over 100 million Eastern Europeans to secure *Lebensraum*, or "living space," for German settlement.

A short prelude made the surprise invasion possible. The Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact of 1939 was a non-aggression agreement that secretly divided Eastern Europe into German and Soviet spheres, enabling the joint invasion of Poland and the Soviet annexation of the Baltic states. On 22 June 1941, Germany tore it up and invaded the USSR with Operation Barbarossa, catching Stalin's forces largely unprepared despite repeated warnings.

The first phase, 1941 to 1943, was defined by brutality on both sides. German *Blitzkrieg* ("lightning war"), meaning rapid, concentrated armoured and air strikes to encircle and destroy enemy formations, drove deep into Soviet territory and trapped millions of Red Army troops in vast pockets. German troops and SS units carried out mass shootings of Jews, Communist officials, and prisoners. Between 45 and 57 percent of Soviet prisoners of war died in German captivity, killed by shooting, starvation, or exposure. By December 1941 the German advance had stalled at the outskirts of Moscow, and a Soviet counter-offensive marked the first clear German defeat.

The middle years saw the strategic balance shift against Germany. In summer 1942, Operation Fall Blau (Case Blue) drove German forces toward the Caucasus oilfields and the city of Stalingrad. On 19 November 1942, Operation Uranus, a Soviet pincer attack, encircled the German 6th Army in the city. Its surrender on 2 February 1943 cost Germany an entire field army. Six months later the Battle of Kursk (5 July 1943), the largest tank engagement in history, broke the last German offensive capacity on the Eastern Front, and the Red Army held the strategic initiative for the remainder of the war.

The final phase, 1943 to 1945, was a long Soviet advance westward. Operation Bagration, launched on 22 June 1944 on the third anniversary of Barbarossa, destroyed Army Group Centre, liberated Belarus, and accelerated the collapse of Germany's eastern alliances. Romania defected to the Allies on 27 August 1944, and Bulgaria followed. The Red Army fought through Poland, entered Germany in early 1945, and captured Berlin between 16 April and 2 May 1945. Germany signed unconditional surrender on 8 May 1945, ending the war in Europe.

Soviet victory rested on two material pillars. The first was industrial relocation: Soviet factories were dismantled and moved east of the Urals, and by 1943 Soviet production outpaced German output in tanks and aircraft. The second was Western Lend-Lease, a programme in which the United States supplied war materials to the Allies. The USSR received 58 percent of its high-octane aviation fuel, 93 percent of its railway equipment, and large quantities of food and raw materials through this channel, freeing Soviet production for tanks, guns, and ammunition. Historians continue to debate the precise weight of Lend-Lease versus Soviet domestic production, but neither factor alone explains the outcome.

The human cost exceeded any previous conflict. Roughly 30 million people died as a result of the fighting, the occupation, and deliberate murder, including an estimated 9 million children. Soviet war-related deaths totalled about 26.5 million, comprising 10.6 million military and 15.9 million civilian dead; German military deaths on the Eastern Front approached 4 million. The Holocaust, the siege starvation of cities such as Leningrad, and reciprocal scorched-earth policies all contributed. Scholarship has also dismantled the "Clean Wehrmacht" myth, the claim that the regular German army was largely unblemished by Nazi crimes. The Wehrmacht was deeply complicit in war crimes, in the starvation of Soviet POWs, and in supporting the Holocaust throughout the eastern campaign.
