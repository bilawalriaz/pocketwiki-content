# Cold War

The Cold War was a geopolitical, ideological, and economic rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union, fought without direct large-scale military conflict between them. It shaped international relations from 1945 to 1991, the year the Soviet Union dissolved. Because the two superpowers never fought each other directly, competition took the form of proxy wars, arms races, espionage, and propaganda, creating opposing military alliances and a constant risk of nuclear war.

## Origins (1945–1953)

The wartime alliance between the US and USSR collapsed once World War II ended, as ideological differences reasserted themselves. The US adopted **containment**, a strategy of preventing the spread of communism beyond its existing borders, articulated in the 1947 Truman Doctrine and backed by the Marshall Plan's economic aid to Western Europe. The Soviet Union responded by installing Communist governments in Eastern Europe, a boundary Winston Churchill called an "Iron Curtain."

Two early crises hardened this division. The 1948–49 Berlin Blockade, in which the USSR cut off West Berlin, prompted the founding of NATO in 1949 and a sustained airlift. The 1950–53 Korean War ended in stalemate and locked in opposing alliance structures, with the Warsaw Pact formalizing the Soviet side in 1955.

## Nuclear Arms Race and Global Proxy Wars (1953–1979)

Both sides built up nuclear arsenals. The Soviet Union tested its first nuclear device in 1949 and a thermonuclear (hydrogen) bomb in 1953; the United States adopted a "massive retaliation" doctrine, threatening overwhelming nuclear response to any Soviet attack. The competition extended into space, beginning with the Soviet launch of Sputnik in 1957.

Because direct war was too dangerous, the superpowers fought through **proxy wars**: conflicts in which each side supported opposing local forces rather than its own troops. Vietnam and Cuba were the defining arenas. The 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis, triggered by Soviet missiles placed in Cuba, brought the world closer to nuclear war than at any other point in the Cold War. The rivalry also fractured the communist bloc: the Sino-Soviet split, a breakdown in relations between the USSR and the People's Republic of China, escalated to a border conflict in 1969, pushing China toward a separate relationship with the United States.

A period of **détente** (relaxed tensions) in the 1970s produced arms control agreements, including SALT I in 1972, which capped the number of strategic nuclear launchers. Détente ended when the Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan in 1979, an action the United States read as expansionism.

## Renewed Tensions and Collapse (1979–1991)

The 1980s brought renewed confrontation. The Reagan administration pursued a major military buildup, including the Strategic Defense Initiative ("Star Wars"), a proposed missile-defense shield that Soviet leaders feared would upset the nuclear balance. Soviet military spending, combined with the costly Afghan war, deepened the USSR's economic stagnation. The 1983 Soviet shootdown of a civilian airliner, Korean Air Lines Flight 007, increased Western support for rearmament.

In 1985, Mikhail Gorbachev took power and introduced **perestroika** (economic restructuring) and **glasnost** (political openness). These reforms loosened central control and exposed the scale of the Soviet system's problems. Combined with US-Soviet summits and the 1987 INF Treaty, which eliminated intermediate-range nuclear forces, they reduced tensions sharply. When communist governments fell across Eastern Europe in 1989 and the Berlin Wall was opened, Gorbachev refused to use Soviet force to hold the bloc together. The Soviet Union itself dissolved in December 1991.

## Causes and Causal Chain

Three structural factors drove the conflict. First, ideological incompatibility: the US promoted liberal capitalism and democracy, while the USSR promoted a one-party communist state, and each viewed the other as an existential threat. Second, the power vacuum in Europe and Asia after World War II left both superpowers competing to fill the same space. Third, the existence of nuclear weapons made direct war suicidal, channeling competition into proxy conflicts and arms races that, by their nature, could not be fully resolved without that risk.

Two turning points show how these factors interacted. The Cuban Missile Crisis demonstrated the danger of nuclear brinkmanship and produced a shared incentive to manage the arms race, which détente attempted to do. Gorbachev's reforms showed the opposite dynamic: a Soviet system that could not sustain military spending, Afghan occupation, and domestic reform simultaneously, and whose internal contradictions the Cold War had long masked.

## Competing Explanations

Historians divide over who bears primary responsibility. The **orthodox** school places it on Soviet expansionism and communist ideology. **Revisionists** argue US economic and diplomatic policies after 1945 provoked the conflict. **Post-revisionists** emphasize mutual misperception, domestic politics, and the self-reinforcing cycle of actions by both sides, a view consistent with how the rivalry actually unfolded.
