# Mali Empire

The Mali Empire (c. 1235–1610) was a West African state built on controlling trans-Saharan trade. Founded by Sundiata Keita after defeating the Sosso Empire at the Battle of Kirina around 1235, it grew into the largest empire in West African history, spreading Mande language, law, and culture across the region. It declined through succession crises, raids, and rival powers, ending in defeat and fragmentation around 1600.

## Foundation and Constitutional Structure

Mali emerged from the Manding region of the upper Niger River, once a province of the older Ghana Empire (Wagadu). As Ghana collapsed in the 11th–12th centuries, the Sosso (Kaniaga) kingdom under the sorcerer-king Soumaoro Kanté (from 1203) took over much of the area. Sundiata, an exiled Keita prince, built a coalition of Mema, Wagadu, and Mandinka city-states and defeated Sosso at Kirina.

His victory was followed by the **Kouroukan Fouga**, a constitutional charter that organised the empire as three allied states (Mali, Mema, Wagadou) plus the **Twelve Doors of Mali**, a coalition of conquered or allied territories. Door rulers, called *farbas*, planted their spears before Sundiata's throne to relinquish their kingdoms, then governed ancestral lands as great commanders in the name of the *mansa* (emperor), keeping most prior authority. This pact made Mali a hegemonic alliance held together by the Keita dynasty, tribute, and shared trade rules rather than a centralised bureaucracy.

## Golden Age Under Mansa Musa

After a turbulent succession including the usurper Sakura (c. 1285–1300), the Keita line stabilised. **Mansa Musa (r. c. 1312–1337)** presided over the golden age. A devout Muslim, he annexed Timbuktu in 1324 and developed the Sankore madrasah into a major Islamic university. His pilgrimage (*hajj*) to Mecca between 1324 and 1326 distributed so much gold in Cairo that the dinar depreciated for roughly twelve years.

Mansa Musa secured the Taghaza salt mines, built mosques and palaces, and secured trans-Saharan trade. His brother Suleyman (r. 1341–1360) maintained this prosperity and hosted Ibn Battuta, whose eyewitness account is the empire's most detailed contemporary record. By about 1350 the empire covered approximately 478,819 square miles (1,240,140 km²); by 1387 it still held roughly 1.1 million km² despite losing Gao and Dyolof.

## Government and Administration

The *mansa* held ultimate authority, exercised through strict court protocol. A **Gbara** (Great Assembly) of 32 clan delegates acted as a cabinet with portfolios covering war, justice, and the economy. Administration layered central control over local autonomy: provinces (*dyamani-tigui*) ruled by customary chiefs approved by the *mansa*; conquered or strategic zones governed by *farins*, military governors appointed by the *mansa* who ran garrisons, taxes, and courts; and vassal kingdoms, including the Twelve Doors *farbas*, which kept high autonomy in ancestral lands in exchange for tribute. On the Saharan frontier, Berber groups held vassal status, with garrisons at Oualata, Timbuktu, Koumbi, and Gao overseen by the *sura farin*. The 14th-century writer al-ʿUmari described the realm as square and an eight-month journey across, listing fourteen provinces, though Arabic manuscripts lack diacritics and many identifications remain uncertain. The capital's location is unresolved: candidates include Niani, Dakajalan, Manikoro, or a peripatetic court, and archaeology at Niani confirms 15th-century trade but not 13th–14th-century royal occupation.

## Economy: Gold, Salt, and Trade

Mali's wealth rested on **tributary control of goldfields** at Bambuk, Boure, and Galam rather than direct mining. Gold nuggets were crown property; only gold dust circulated, weighed in *mithqals* (4.5 g, interchangeable with the dinar), a policy that curbed inflation from nugget hoarding. Salt from Taghaza, transported south in camel-carried slabs, equalled or exceeded gold's value in the forest zone. Copper from Takedda traded south for gold at roughly 60 bars per 100 dinars. The empire taxed trade goods, including gold, salt, copper, and kola nuts, at staging posts such as Oualata and Timbuktu, and used slaves as porters and trade goods. Agriculture (millet, sorghum, cotton) supported dense populations, and crafts included ironworking, indigo dyeing, and goldsmithing.

## Military

Mali maintained a semi-professional standing army peaking at around 100,000 men, including about 10,000 cavalry, though this figure may be a conventional exaggeration repeated in Arabic sources. Clans provided quotas of *horon* (freemen) with their own arms; the state equipped *sofa* slave troops with bows and poisoned arrows. Command was split between two elite cavalry generals, both *ton-tigi* (quiver-masters) of the *farari* corps: the **Farim-Soura** in the north and the **Sankar-Zouma** in the south. Infantry used spears and shields in the north, bows in the south. War canoes moved troops on the Niger and Senegal waterways, projecting power and protecting trade routes.

## Religion: A Dual Structure

Mali combined Islam in the Sahel with traditional animism in the south. The Keita dynasty cultivated legitimacy in both spheres, claiming descent from Bilal (the Prophet Muhammad's muezzin) while also leading the *donso-ton* hunter brotherhood and the *Kòmò* secret power society. The *Epic of Sundiata* portrays him as a sorcerer-hunter; Mansa Musa built mosques and madrasas yet imported sacred objects (*boliw*) from Mecca for *Kòmò* rites. Jakhanke clerics administered theocratic enclaves such as Dia and Goundiourou under the quietist tradition of Al-Hajj Salim Suwari.

## Decline and Collapse

After Suleyman's death in 1360, a run of short-lived or weak rulers handed real power to the *kankoro-sigui* (chief minister). External pressure mounted in parallel: Mossi raids in the 1370s and 1477 seized Macina and BaGhana; Tuareg forces captured Timbuktu in 1433 and Oualata in 1436; the rising Songhai Empire took Mema in 1465, Timbuktu in 1468, and Diafunu in 1502. Mali rallied briefly around 1550–1576, regaining access to the Bighu goldfields and reasserting control over Gambia vassals such as Wuli, but the 1591 Moroccan invasion of Songhai created a power vacuum Mali could not fill. At the **Battle of Jenne on 26 April 1599**, Mansa Mahmud IV's army was crushed by Moroccan firearms and the betrayal of his ally Bukar of Kala. Mahmud died around 1610, and his three sons partitioned the Keita heartland, ending the empire.

## Legacy

Mali's expansion spread Mande languages and Dyula trade networks from the Gambia mouth to Burkina Faso and the Gold Coast, and its political vocabulary (*mansa*, *farin*, *farba*, *Gbara*, *Kouroukan Fouga*) survived in successor states such as Kaabu, Bamana, and Kenedougou into the colonial era. Sudano-Sahelian architecture, built of mudbrick and adobe with protruding wooden support beams (*toron*), peaked under Mansa Musa in the Great Mosque of Gao and the Timbuktu palace; the Great Mosque of Djenné, rebuilt in 1907 on 13th- or 14th-century foundations, remains the style's most famous example.
