# Ancient Greek architecture

Ancient Greek architecture is the tradition of temple and civic building that flourished from roughly 900 BC into the Hellenistic and Roman periods, producing a formal vocabulary of columnar "orders" and proportional systems that became the foundation of Western architectural practice. Its character was shaped by a rocky landscape that supplied limestone and high‑quality marble, a maritime climate that pushed daily life outdoors, and a humanist philosophy that placed reasoned order at the centre of the world. The result was architecture conceived less as shelter than as a sculpted object in a landscape, refined with optical corrections so that it would appear perfectly balanced to the eye.

## Chronology

After the collapse of the Minoan and Mycenaean palatial cultures around 1100 BC, much technical knowledge was lost in the Greek Dark Age. Substantial stone architecture re‑emerges around 600 BC, by which time the Doric order was already well defined; the earliest surviving open‑air theatres date to about 525–480 BC. The Parthenon was built on the Athenian Acropolis between 448 and 432 BC as the high point of the Classical period. Alexander the Great's death in 323 BC marks the transition to the Hellenistic age; the Corinthian order's first known external use is the Choragic Monument of Lysicrates in 334 BC, and the Temple of Zeus Olympius in Athens, begun in 174 BC and not completed until 132 AD, shows the order in a major temple setting.

## Structural logic

The core system is trabeated, meaning columns carry a horizontal lintel, a descendant of earlier timber construction translated into stone. A Greek temple is a rectangular room (the *cella*) housing a cult statue and acting as a treasury; worship happened at an outdoor altar. The building sits on a stepped base, the *crepidoma*, whose top step, the *stylobate*, carries the columns. Above the columns runs the *entablature*, a horizontal band of the *architrave* (plain lintel), the *frieze*, and the projecting *cornice*. The shallow gable roof ends in a triangular *pediment*, normally filled with sculpture. A *peripteral* temple has a single ring of columns around the cella, a *dipteral* temple a double ring.

Stone was laid in ashlar masonry, precisely cut blocks fitted with minimal mortar and often pinned with metal clamps. Because long straight lines and slender stone columns can look to the eye as if they sag or hollow inward, Greek architects introduced subtle optical refinements. The shafts of columns were given a faint convex swelling called *entasis*. Major horizontal lines such as the stylobate were gently curved upward at the centre; on the Parthenon this rise is about 65 mm across the building's width. These adjustments correct the way the eye reads stone.

## The three orders

Greek formal architecture is organised by three orders, each specifying the proportions, profile, and decoration of the column and entablature together. An order governs the whole design; the orders are not mixed on a single building.

The **Doric order** is the oldest and heaviest. Columns have no base, sit directly on the stylobate, and end in a simple cushion‑like capital. The Doric frieze is divided by raised vertical blocks called *triglyphs* alternating with square panels called *metopes*, which carry relief sculpture.

The **Ionic order**, associated with the Greek cities of Ionia on the eastern Aegean coast, is more slender. Columns rest on moulded bases and are crowned by *volute* capitals, paired spirals that unroll like scrolls at the corners. The Ionic frieze is typically a continuous sculpted band.

The **Corinthian order** is a later elaboration of the Ionic, distinguished by a tall, bell‑shaped capital sheathed in two tiers of acanthus leaves, giving it a more ornate profile.

## Civic buildings and the house

Beyond the temple, the Greek civic repertoire included the open‑air theatre, carved into a natural hillside for sightlines and acoustics; the *agora*, a public square lined with a *stoa*, a long covered colonnade used for commerce and conversation; the *bouleuterion*, the council building; and the stadium. Domestic architecture followed a different logic: houses turned inward, with rooms arranged around a central courtyard.

## Sculpture as architectural decoration

Decoration was part of the structural vocabulary rather than applied ornament. Early wooden temples were sheathed in painted terracotta revetments; as construction moved to stone, sculpture took their place in the same zones. In Doric buildings, figural relief appeared in the metopes and the two pediments; Ionic buildings carried continuous friezes. Archaic sculpture is stiff and frontal; the High Classical period (the Parthenon sculptures overseen by Phidias) reached an idealised, lifelike naturalism; the Hellenistic period (the Pergamon Altar frieze) turned toward dramatic, emotionally charged composition.

## Open questions

The function of the great Minoan palaces on Crete remains debated, since their scale and layout are unlike those of a purely residential building, and it is unclear whether they were domestic in any simple sense. The orientation of some Greek temples appears to track astronomical events, but the consistency and intent of that alignment are still under discussion.
