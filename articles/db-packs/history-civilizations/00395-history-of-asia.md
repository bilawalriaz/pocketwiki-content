# History of Asia

Asia is the stage on which humanity first built cities, states, and world religions, and its history explains the long arcs that still shape global politics and culture. Two of the world's oldest continuous civilizations, Chinese and Indian, emerged here, alongside influential centers of state-building in West and Southeast Asia. Across roughly eleven thousand years, four patterns recur: power concentrates around fertile lowlands or steppe trade routes and radiates outward through religion, technology, and commerce; new empires absorb and transform earlier cultures rather than erase them; long-distance trade networks carry goods, ideas, and pathogens across the continent; and contact with seafaring Europeans from the sixteenth century onward disrupts Asian states and dissolves their empires by the twentieth century.

The deepest roots are agricultural. Göbekli Tepe in Anatolia (c. 9500–8000 BC) is the world's oldest known megalithic site, predating settled farming in the region. In South Asia, Lahuradewa shows rice cultivation by 9000–8000 BC, which fed the Indus Valley Civilization (c. 3300–1300 BC), a Bronze Age urban culture that shared technologies such as the wheel and mathematics with Mesopotamia, though writing likely arose independently in each region.

Classical empires layered administration and ideology on top of these foundations. The Zhou dynasty in China (1029–258 BC) introduced the **Mandate of Heaven**, a doctrine that legitimized the emperor as divinely sanctioned but could be withdrawn if the ruler governed badly, and the framework justified dynastic change for the next two thousand years. The Qin (221–207 BC) completed China's first unification, and the Han (206 BC–220 AD) opened a golden age that promoted Confucianism and pushed the Silk Road deep into Central Asia. In West Asia, the Achaemenid Empire (6th–4th centuries BC) pioneered efficient administration and spread **Zoroastrianism**, an early monotheistic Iranian religion with concepts of heaven, hell, and final judgment. In South Asia, the Maurya (321–185 BC) and later Gupta (c. 320–550 AD) empires spread Hinduism and Buddhism while standardizing coinage, law, and Sanskrit scholarship.

Religion and trade drove the medieval centuries. From 622 AD, Islam spread rapidly out of West Asia, creating a new political and cultural sphere and triggering an Islamic Golden Age of science and learning. In East Asia, the Tang (618–907) and Song (960–1279) dynasties refined **Neo-Confucianism**, a Song-era revival of Confucian thought stressing ethics and social order, and pioneered gunpowder weapons, movable-type printing, and paper money. The Mongol Empire (1206–1368) imposed the **Pax Mongolica**, a century of relative stability that let goods, missionaries, and the Black Death travel Eurasia simultaneously. In Japan, the Heian period (794–1185) produced classical literature and court culture, and the 1185 establishment of the **shogunate** placed a military ruler, the shōgun, in effective power while the emperor remained a figurehead.

The early modern era was defined by gunpowder empires and European contact. The Ming (1368–1644) and Qing (1644–1912) dynasties ruled a reunified China, with Zheng He's early-fifteenth-century voyages reaching the Indian Ocean before Chinese maritime power was deliberately withdrawn. The Mughal Empire, founded by Babur in 1526, unified much of the subcontinent until British colonization fragmented it. From the sixteenth century onward, Portuguese, Dutch, and especially British trading companies set up coastal enclaves; the British East India Company eventually controlled most of India, and Western gunboats forced the **Unequal Treaties** on Qing China, granting extraterritorial rights and trade concessions that hollowed out imperial sovereignty.

The modern period is the story of imperial rivalry, modernization, and decolonization. Britain and Russia fought the cold **Great Game** for influence in Central Asia (1828–1907); historians still debate whether Russia genuinely planned to invade India, and archival evidence suggests it did not. Japan restored imperial rule under the Meiji in 1868, industrialized rapidly, and annexed Korea in 1910. After World War II, decolonization produced the partition of British India into India and Pakistan in 1947 and Indonesian independence in 1949, while the Cold War split the region into proxy conflicts, including the Korean War (1950–1953) and the Vietnam War (1955–1975). Scholars still argue whether the Indus Valley Civilization fell around 1700 BC mainly because of flooding and climate shifts, and how much the Silk Road moved pathogens relative to technologies and ideas.
