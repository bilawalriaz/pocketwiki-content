# Post-classical history

Between roughly 500 and 1500 CE, the world reorganized around three forces: expanding civilizations, universalizing religions, and trade networks binding Afro-Eurasia. Population roughly doubled, from about 210 million in 500 to 461 million in 1500, despite two plague pandemics and the Mongol conquests. World historians call this the post-classical period, the global counterpart to Europe's Middle Ages, though its start and end depend on which civilization you track.

## What defines the period

The post-classical era sits between classical empires and the early modern world. Classical society ended on different dates: Han China in 220 CE, the Western Roman Empire in 476 CE, the Gupta Empire in 543 CE, and the Sasanian Empire in 651 CE. The label is debated. Applying a Europe-centered timeline to the Americas, where societies followed different rhythms, is awkward, and some scholars call the term Eurocentric. Still, three large trends genuinely crossed most of the Old World.

## The three global trends

Civilizations expanded into new territory. China's dynastic cycle continued with improving bureaucracy. Islamic empires filled the Middle East, North Africa, and Central Asia. West Africa saw Ghana, Mali, and Songhai. Western Europe developed feudalism (authority fragmented into personal lord–vassal bonds) and manorialism, while Byzantium preserved Roman institutions. In the Americas, Teotihuacan declined, the Maya flourished and collapsed around 900 CE, and the Aztec and Inca Empires later emerged. Polynesians colonized Hawaii, New Zealand, and Easter Island.

Universalizing religions spread across the Old World. Buddhism moved from India into China, Japan, Korea, and Vietnam. Christianity expanded into northern and eastern Europe, with the Catholic–Orthodox split adding lasting diversity. Islam, after Muhammad's revelations beginning in 610 CE, unified Arabian tribes and spread through conquest across North Africa, Iberia, Persia, Central Asia, India, and Indonesia, often absorbing rather than replacing local traditions.

Long-distance trade grew dramatically. The Silk Road carried goods, ideas, religion, and disease between China and the Mediterranean. Camels enabled trans-Saharan trade connecting West Africa to Eurasia. Islamic empires translated and transmitted Greek, Roman, and Indian knowledge. The Mongol Empire, the largest continuous land empire in history, briefly enforced the Pax Mongolica, allowing goods, missionaries, and pathogens to cross Asia.

## Plague as a recurring shock

Disease shaped this era as much as religion or trade did. The bacterium *Yersinia pestis* caused both pandemic waves. The Plague of Justinian (541–549), possibly originating in the Tian Shan mountains or East Africa, killed roughly a quarter of the Mediterranean's people, weakened Byzantium and Persia, and helped clear the way for early Muslim conquests. The Black Death (1347–1351) killed 25–50% of affected populations and reached Sicily via merchant ships. In Western Europe it broke the feudal labor system, raised wages, and pulled survivors into market towns. The Silk Road carried both pandemics; once sea routes replaced overland trade after 1500, this conveyor lost its central role.

## The Mongol pivot

Genghis Khan unified the nomadic tribes in 1206, and his successors built an empire stretching from Central Europe to the Sea of Japan. Under Kublai Khan, who completed the conquest of China in 1279 and founded the Yuan dynasty, the empire briefly functioned as a single political space. The Pax Mongolica let Chinese astronomers, Islamic cartographers, and European merchants share knowledge; the 1303 Yuan world map fused Chinese and Islamic geography.

The empire could not hold. Succession disputes after Möngke Khan's death split it into four khanates: the Golden Horde, Chagatai Khanate, Ilkhanate, and Yuan dynasty. The Ming overthrew the Yuan in 1368, and by 1500 all original khanates had collapsed, though Chagatai descendants founded the Mughal Empire in India. The Mongol interruption of the 13th and 14th centuries reshaped Eurasian politics, and its consequences, including the spread of the Black Death and the stimulus to European maritime exploration, echoed for centuries.

## Regional trajectories

Africa: Islam spread along the Sudan and Swahili coast, while traditional beliefs persisted among commoners. Mansa Musa of Mali was probably the wealthiest person alive in the early 14th century, and Timbuktu's University of Sankoré drew scholars from across the Muslim world.

Europe: Charlemagne's coronation in 800 launched a 500-year contest between pope and emperor that defined European politics. The High Middle Ages (c. 1000–1300) brought the Crusades, Scholasticism, and chivalry, then the Black Death transformed feudalism and fed the Renaissance.

West and Central Asia: Rapid Muslim conquests toppled the Sasanian and Byzantine empires, and the Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates sparked an Islamic Golden Age of science and translation. The Seljuk Turks took Baghdad in 1055; the Mongols sacked it in 1258; Timur's raids and the Ottoman rise followed.

South Asia: The Delhi Sultanate (from 1206) conquered the north, Buddhism declined, and Hinduism survived. The Vijayanagara Empire held the south unconquered.

East Asia: China under the Sui, Tang, and Song (581–1279) was the world's largest economy, with gunpowder, woodblock printing, and the magnetic compass. The Song's commercial expansion has been called an early industrial revolution. Korea and Japan adopted Chinese institutions voluntarily, and Sejong the Great created Hangul in the 15th century.

Americas and Oceania: These regions developed in relative isolation. The Maya independently invented the concept of zero and a 365-day calendar. Polynesian sailors colonized Pacific islands using knowledge of wind and currents, sometimes causing mass extinctions through hunting and the introduction of the Polynesian rat; Easter Island's ecology collapsed completely.

## Why the period ended

The post-classical world unraveled in the 15th and 16th centuries. The Ottoman capture of Constantinople in 1453 disrupted overland routes between Asia and Europe, pushing Europeans toward the sea. Columbus reached the Americas in 1492, Vasco da Gama reached India by sea in 1498, and Magellan's crew completed the first circumnavigation in 1521. These voyages triggered the Columbian exchange, the transfer of crops, livestock, people, and diseases that bound the Americas into the Afro-Eurasian system for the first time. The post-classical era ended because the world it described, one of regional civilizations linked mainly by overland routes, gave way to an oceanic world in which every continent was connected.
