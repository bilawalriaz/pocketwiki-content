# History

History is the systematic study of the human past. As an academic discipline, it collects and interprets evidence to construct narratives explaining what happened, why, and in what context. "History" can also mean past events themselves or individual written accounts; some scholars distinguish interpretative *histories* from chronologically arranged *chronicles*. Traditionally centered on written documents, the field left the unwritten deep past to prehistory and archaeology, though the 20th century broadened this scope. *Pseudohistory* departs from scholarly standards, often for ideological ends; *historical denialism* rejects documented events such as the Holocaust, the Armenian genocide, the Nanjing Massacre, and the Holodomor.

Theorists debate both classification (social science, humanity, or hybrid) and purpose. Proposed aims include disinterested truth-seeking, practical lessons, appreciation of human diversity, collective identity, and, for Whig historians and E. H. Carr, understanding or shaping the present and future. Politicians have invoked history to justify tradition or promote change.

## Etymology

From Greek *histōr* ("learned, wise man") to *historiā* ("inquiry" or "testimony") and into Latin *historia*. In Hellenistic and Roman times the meaning shifted toward narrative presentation. It entered Middle English via Old French *histoire* in the 14th century as "story, tale," conflating fact and fiction, gained a knowledge-branch sense in the 15th century, and became tied to evidence-based accounts in the 18th–19th centuries. The dual sense of "past events" and "accounts of them" persists in French (*histoire*), Italian (*storia*), and German (*Geschichte*).

## Methods

Research begins with a question and draws on *primary sources* (evidence from the studied period: documents, letters, eyewitness accounts, artefacts) and *secondary sources* (analyses of other sources; classification depends on use). *Source criticism* proceeds in two stages: *external criticism* assesses authenticity (origin, authorship, modifications, forgeries), and *internal criticism* evaluates meaning, accuracy, reliability, bias, and cross-references.

Validated statements are synthesized into narratives using tools such as *periodization* (themed periods like the Stone/Bronze/Iron Age system), analysis of *silences* (gaps in the record), and quantitative methods. Schools differ: positivists seek objective empirical truths; postmodernists reject grand narratives; Marxists stress economic forces and class struggle; the Annales school emphasizes long-term, interdisciplinary, quantitative trends; feminist historians foreground gender and women's experience.

## Periods and Regions

A traditional scheme runs prehistory → ancient → post-classical → early modern → modern. Prehistory spans human evolution to anatomically modern humans (~200,000 years ago), the African diaspora, and sedentary agriculture. Ancient history begins ~3500 BCE with the first civilizations in Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus Valley, China, and Peru, alongside writing, urbanization, empires, and formative religions and philosophies. Post-classical history (c. 500 CE) saw missionary world religions, flourishing trade, and the 13th–14th-century Mongol Empire. Early modern history (c. 1500) brought European gunpowder empires, colonization, the Columbian exchange, the Scientific Revolution, and Enlightenment secularization. Modern history (late 18th c.) encompasses industrialization, colonial empires, globalization, democratizing revolutions, and two world wars ending European dominance.

Regionally: Africa includes Egyptian civilization (4th millennium BCE), kingdoms such as Nubia, Axum, Ghana, Mali, and Songhay, the 7th-century spread of Islam, the Atlantic slave trade, colonization, and post-WWII independence. Asia covers early humans, cradle civilizations, all major world religions, the Silk Road, the Mongol Empire, and colonial rule until WWII. Europe begins ~45,000 years ago, with Greek contributions, the 5th-century fall of Western Rome, Christianization, and the world wars and Cold War that ended its dominance. The Americas saw first humans 20,000–15,000 years ago, civilizations including Maya, Olmec, Teotihuacan, Aztec, and Inca, European disease and colonization, independence movements, and US rise. Oceania was settled 60,000–50,000 years ago, came under Western control by the late 19th century, and decolonized after WWII.

## Evolution of the Discipline

Early writing mixed fact and myth (*Epic of Gilgamesh*, *Odyssey*). Herodotus's 5th-century BCE *Histories* emphasized evidence-based inquiry; Thucydides refined it with political and military focus. Romans added moral judgment, shifting from annalistic to narrative styles. The Chinese tradition valued annals and source verification tied to dynasties, with Sima Qian especially influential; in 629 CE the Tang established a history bureau producing the Veritable Records, and Japan adopted the model in the 8th century. Medieval European chronicles by clergy (Eusebius, Bede, Augustine) framed history as divine plan; Islamic historiography (al-Tabari, Ibn Khaldun) stressed chains of transmission. The Renaissance and printing press secularized and broadened interest, and Enlightenment rationalism expanded topics. The 19th century brought professionalization through Ranke's source criticism, Comte's positivism, and Marx's historical materialism. The 20th century added the Annales school, postcolonialism, postmodernism, and quantitative and digital methods.

## Related Fields and Debates

*Historiography* is the metatheoretical study of historical methods; *philosophy of history* examines what entities make up history, whether general laws or contingent factors drive it, and whether it is cyclic or teleological. Objectivity is contested: realist and empiricist views are challenged by perspectivist, relativist, and postmodernist claims that all history is biased (Trevelyan, Jenkins), while neo-realists reassert empirically reachable truth. History education spans schools, where "history wars" over textbooks can erupt, and informal settings such as museums, popular media, and oral traditions. Interdisciplinary links connect history with archaeology, geology, genetics, anthropology, linguistics, politics, and historical religions such as Christianity. Unresolved questions concern the field's disciplinary status, its purpose, the possibility of objectivity, general laws versus unique causation, the shape of historical time, and social history's precise definition.
