# Renaissance

The Renaissance was a European cultural movement (c. 1300–1600), born in Italy, that revived classical Greek and Roman learning and reshaped how Europeans studied the world, depicted it, and challenged religious authority. Its core was humanism, a philological method: the disciplined study of ancient Greek and Latin texts in their original languages to recover the "genius of man." This method replaced medieval scholasticism (formal logical debate rooted in Christian doctrine) with empirical observation and civic engagement. It produced breakthroughs in perspective, anatomy, navigation, and astronomy, and humanist textual criticism of the New Testament directly fueled the Protestant Reformation.

## Why Italy, Why Florence

Italian city-republics (Florence, Venice, Genoa, Milan) were unusually urbanized and wealthy compared to feudal northern Europe, ruled by merchant elites who patronized arts and learning. The Medici family of Florence is the most famous such patron. The cities sat among Roman ruins, giving artists and scholars physical contact with antiquity. Political thinkers such as Coluccio Salutati and Leonardo Bruni celebrated civic liberty and republican virtue. The Black Death (1348–50) killed roughly half of Florence's population, raising wages, lowering rents, and tilting attention toward earthly life. Petrarch (1304–1374) is often called the "father of humanism" for rediscovering Cicero's letters.

## Humanism as Method

Humanism's curriculum, the *Studia Humanitatis*, had five parts: poetry, grammar, history, moral philosophy, and rhetoric. Its educational goal was the *uomo universale* ("universal man"), a person combining intellectual, physical, and civic excellence. Libraries built around this method, such as the Vatican and the Bibliotheca Corviniana in Buda, became public institutions for exchanging secular classics alongside theology.

## The Greek Dimension

Greek knowledge entered Italy in two waves. In 1396 the Byzantine diplomat Manuel Chrysoloras was invited to teach Greek in Florence, launching the first. The second came in 1453, when the Ottoman capture of Constantinople sent Greek scholars and manuscripts westward. With the original New Testament Greek in hand, humanists like Lorenzo Valla and Erasmus exposed errors in the Latin Vulgate, the standard medieval Bible translation. This work undermined papal doctrinal authority, a precondition for Luther's Reformation.

## Art and Architecture

Medieval art used symbols to point toward heaven. Renaissance art aimed at naturalism, depicting the visible world convincingly. Giotto (d. 1337) treated painting as a "window into space." Around 1420, Filippo Brunelleschi formalized linear perspective, a mathematical system for representing three-dimensional space on a flat surface by having parallel lines converge at a vanishing point, codified in Leon Battista Alberti's *De pictura* (1435). In the Low Countries, Jan van Eyck perfected oil painting on canvas, giving artworks luminous color and durability. Brunelleschi's dome for Florence Cathedral (1420–36) revived Roman architectural principles such as classical columns, pilasters (rectangular columns attached to walls), and entablatures (horizontal structures resting on a column's capital). The High Renaissance peaked in the collaborative rebuilding of St. Peter's Basilica in Rome.

## Science, Navigation, and Medicine

Humanist reverence for Aristotle and Ptolemy initially reinforced the geocentric view (Earth at the center of the universe), but direct observation eroded it. Nicholas of Cusa (c. 1450) argued for an infinite, centerless universe; Copernicus's *De revolutionibus* (1543) placed the Sun at the center, a heliocentric model. Leonardo da Vinci ran controlled experiments in hydraulics, anatomy, and aerodynamics. In medicine, Andreas Vesalius's *De humani corporis fabrica* (1543) corrected Galen by dissecting human cadavers directly, while the Paracelsian movement rejected Galen's humoral theory, the idea that health depends on balancing four bodily fluids (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile), in favor of chemical remedies. Luca Pacioli's 1494 treatise on double-entry bookkeeping, where every transaction is recorded as both a debit and a credit so the books always balance, founded modern accounting. Gutenberg's movable-type printing press (c. 1440) made all of this rapidly shareable: by 1500, 270 European cities had presses, producing more than 20 million books.

## Reformation as Outgrowth

Renaissance humanism and the Protestant Reformation were inseparable. Valla's *Annotations on the New Testament* and Erasmus's Greek New Testament (1516) exposed corruption in Church doctrine and practice. The Western Schism (1378–1417), when rival popes claimed authority simultaneously, had already weakened the papacy. When Martin Luther posted his Ninety-five Theses in 1517 attacking the sale of indulgences (certificates promising to reduce punishment for sins), the textual tools humanism had sharpened made the break possible. The Catholic Counter-Reformation, the Church's own reform movement launched at the Council of Trent (1545), responded, but Western Christendom never reunited.

## Spread North and Beyond

The Italian Renaissance reached other regions on its own terms. France absorbed it after Charles VIII's 1494–95 invasion of Italy; Francis I brought Leonardo da Vinci to his court. In Germany and the Low Countries, the printing press and the artist Albrecht Dürer preceded Italian influence, and humanism fused directly with the Reformation. England's Elizabethan era produced Shakespeare, Marlowe, and Spenser, while Francis Bacon pioneered empirical science. Hungary under King Matthias Corvinus (r. 1458–90) became the first northern kingdom to embrace the Renaissance, building the Bibliotheca Corviniana. Portugal led ocean navigation with figures like Pedro Nunes and Abraham Zacuto, and developed the *Manueline* style, an early-16th-century Portuguese architecture blending Gothic, Renaissance, and maritime motifs. Spain produced Cervantes, whose *Don Quixote* is often called the first modern novel, and the mystics Teresa of Ávila and John of the Cross.

## The Central Debate

Historians still disagree about what the Renaissance actually was. Jacob Burckhardt (1860) framed it as a sharp break: the discovery of the individual. The medievalist Erwin Panofsky answered that "a thousand ties" linked the two eras. The Dutch historian Johan Huizinga argued the Renaissance actually declined from the High Middle Ages by rigidifying Latin and ignoring vernacular languages. Giorgio Vasari (1550) had earlier used *rinascita* (rebirth) to describe art's revival in three phases; Jules Michelet (1855) coined "Renaissance" in its modern sense. Many political and economic historians now prefer "early modern" (c. 1500–1800), which avoids the teleological baggage, the assumption that history moves toward a predetermined goal, built into "rebirth." Critics also point to the movement's failures: intensified witch-hunts, religious wars, the Machiavellian turn in politics, and severe gender restriction, as Joan Kelly-Gadol famously argued.
