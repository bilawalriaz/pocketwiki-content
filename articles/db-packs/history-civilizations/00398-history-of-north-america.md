# History of North America

The history of North America is the story of how a sparsely populated landmass was reshaped by successive waves of migration, imperial competition, and political revolution into a handful of large, economically dominant states. The central mechanism is layered contact: each new population overlaid new institutions, economies, and identities onto what was already there, often displacing earlier ones.

## First peoples and early civilizations

Humans first reached the continent on foot during the last Ice Age, when falling sea levels exposed a land bridge called Beringia that connected Siberia to Alaska. Traditional estimates place these Paleo-Indians in the Americas between roughly 40,000 and 17,000 years ago, though newer evidence hints at earlier arrivals and the timing remains debated. Over millennia, their descendants developed everything from small hunting bands to large agricultural states, adapting to deserts, woodlands, plains, and tropical lowlands.

Agriculture was invented independently in two regions: the Eastern Woodlands and Mesoamerica, the cultural area of central Mexico and northern Central America. Southern groups, including the Maya and Aztec, went furthest, building cities with writing, architecture, and calendars and domesticating staple crops such as maize. Around 1500, on the eve of sustained European contact, the Maddison Project estimates the combined population at about 9.75 million: roughly 7.5 million in Mexico, 2 million in what is now the United States, and about 250,000 in Canada.

## European contact and colonial rivalry

A Norse expedition led by Leif Eriksson reached Newfoundland around 1000 AD, but left no lasting footprint. Sustained contact began in 1492 when Christopher Columbus reached the Bahamas. Three empires came to dominate. Spain conquered the Aztec Empire and claimed territory from the southwestern United States through Mexico and Central America. France built a thinner but enormous empire centred on the St. Lawrence River, Great Lakes, and Mississippi, sustained primarily by the fur trade, the exchange of animal pelts for European goods. England established permanent agricultural settlements beginning with Jamestown, Virginia in 1607 and Plymouth in 1620, and later developed plantation colonies in the Caribbean and American South worked by enslaved Africans. This pattern of settler colonialism, in which migrants maintained ties to their home country while permanently displacing indigenous inhabitants, defined the Anglo-American colonies.

Competition produced repeated wars. The decisive conflict was the French and Indian War, ending in 1763 with Britain taking over nearly all of France's North American holdings.

## Revolutions and new nations

The removal of a French counterweight destabilised British North America. The American Revolution (1775–1783) created the independent United States, and the flight of United Empire Loyalists, colonists who remained loyal to Britain, into remaining British territory helped forge a distinct English-speaking Canada. Further south, the Mexican War of Independence (1810–1821) broke Spanish rule and produced a sovereign Mexico. The United States then expanded: it acquired the Louisiana Territory from France in 1803 and took large additional lands from Mexico after the Mexican–American War (1846–1848). Internal conflict over slavery culminated in the American Civil War (1861–1865). In response to American power, the British North American colonies federated in 1867 to form the Dominion of Canada.

## The modern era

The 20th century turned North American states into global heavyweights. The United States and Canada fought in both world wars, with the United States emerging from the Second as a superpower. Mexico was preoccupied by its own Mexican Revolution (1910–1920) and did not enter the First World War. The postwar decades brought the Cold War, rapid economic growth, and major social transformations, including the U.S. Civil Rights Movement and Quebec's Quiet Revolution, a period of rapid secularisation, modernisation, and assertiveness among French-speaking Canadians. Mexico underwent a phase of fast industrial growth known as the Mexican Miracle.

Economic integration deepened with the 1994 North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA), binding Canada, the United States, and Mexico into a free trade bloc. In 1995 Quebec held a sovereignty referendum; 51% voted no, keeping the province within Canada. Recent decades have been shaped by shocks such as the September 11, 2001 attacks, the 2008 Great Recession, and the COVID-19 pandemic, each testing the political and economic systems built over the previous centuries.

Migration routes, population figures, and the demographic consequences of colonisation continue to be revised by archaeologists and geneticists, and the legacy of displacement remains an active political and scholarly question across all three nations.
