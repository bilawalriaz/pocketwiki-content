# History of medicine

The history of medicine is the long transition from explanations of illness rooted in the supernatural or in balancing bodily fluids, to explanations grounded in observation, experiment, and germ theory. That shift did more than change which treatments doctors used; it reshaped life expectancy, the organisation of healthcare, and the kind of evidence a society demands before calling something medicine.

The story has three parts. First, a long pre-scientific era in which healing blended practical experience with religious or magical ideas. Second, humoralism, the classical framework that gave Western medicine its dominant theory for nearly two thousand years. Third, a scientific revolution from the 17th century onward, capped by germ theory in the 19th century, that replaced those older explanations with testable mechanisms. Modern medicine is the cumulative result of that third phase.

## Prehistoric and ancient medicine

Before writing, evidence of medicine is archaeological. The earliest known dental work dates to about 11,900 BCE, and Ötzi the Iceman (c. 3300 BCE) carried birch polypore fungus, moss, and poppy seeds, items interpreted as a "prehistoric medical kit." Surviving written records begin soon after. The Kahun Gynaecological Papyrus (c. 1800 BCE) is the oldest known medical text, and the Edwin Smith Papyrus (c. 1600 BCE) shows that Egyptian physicians were already practising practical surgery and describing anatomy. Mesopotamia produced the *Diagnostic Handbook* in the 11th century BCE, blending observation with magic. In East Asia, China's *Huangdi Neijing* (5th–3rd century BCE) systematised a continuous medical tradition, while Indian Ayurveda, codified in works such as the *Sushruta Samhita*, described surgery and an eight-branch system of practice. Across these civilisations, the common pattern is careful technique wrapped in a worldview that treated illness as a mix of natural and supernatural causes.

## Classical Greece and Rome

Greek medicine introduced the idea that disease has a consistent, describable cause inside the body. Hippocrates (c. 460–370 BCE) and his followers systematised humoral theory, the claim that health depends on the balance of four fluids: blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile. Disease was imbalance; treatment was restoration. The theory was attractive because it was comprehensive, but it was also unfalsifiable, meaning no observation could disprove it, which helps explain its 2,000-year grip on Western medicine. Galen of Pergamon (c. 129–216 CE) reinforced the system through animal dissection, producing an anatomical model that was treated as authoritative long after parts of it were wrong. Rome added surgical instruments and herb-based pharmacology, most famously Dioscorides' *De materia medica*.

## The post-classical transmission

After the western Roman Empire fell, medicine survived and grew elsewhere. Byzantine physicians compiled earlier Greek knowledge, and during the Islamic Golden Age scholars translated and extended it. Avicenna's *Canon of Medicine* and the work of Al-Razi became standard medical references in both Islamic and medieval European universities. Baghdad's first Islamic hospital (805 CE) introduced features still recognisable today: separate wards, pharmacies, and structured clinical teaching. In medieval Europe, universities at Salerno, Bologna, and Padua became centres of medical training, and human dissection was cautiously revived in the 13th century, setting up later anatomical corrections.

## The scientific turn

Two developments broke humoralism's authority. Andreas Vesalius's *De humani corporis fabrica* (1543) showed, through direct human dissection, that Galen had been wrong about specific anatomical structures. William Harvey's demonstration of blood circulation in 1628 showed that a core Galenic model of how the body worked was mechanically incorrect. By 1677, Antonie van Leeuwenhoek's microscope observations revealed an entire living world of microorganisms that no humoral scheme had predicted.

The decisive change came in the 19th century. Ignaz Semmelweis showed in 1847 that handwashing cut childbirth fever. Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch then proved that specific microorganisms cause specific diseases; Koch's postulates, a four-step checklist linking a microbe to a disease, gave researchers a way to test that link. Joseph Lister applied the idea to surgery with antiseptic technique (1865), and Koch identified the tuberculosis bacillus in 1882. Germ theory replaced both humoralism and miasma theory, the older belief that "bad air" caused epidemics, because it was experimentally testable in a way earlier frameworks were not. Florence Nightingale professionalised nursing, statistical methods entered public health, and psychiatry emerged as a medical specialty with its own institutions, including early "moral treatment" approaches that replaced coercion with calmer care.

## Modern medicine

The 20th and 21st centuries are the applied harvest of germ theory plus new tools. Alexander Fleming's discovery of penicillin in 1928 opened the antibiotic era, transforming infections that had been fatal. Medical imaging (X-rays, then CT scans), organ transplantation, and later genetic research extended what doctors could see and do. The World Health Organization, founded in 1948, coordinated global campaigns such as smallpox eradication. Subsequent decades have been shaped by the HIV/AIDS pandemic, the growth of personalised medicine tailored to a patient's genetics, and COVID-19, which began in 2019 and exposed both the power of modern vaccines and the fragility of health equity. Persistent problems include antibiotic resistance, the rising cost of advanced care, and uneven access to basic treatment, while open questions remain about how ancient systems such as Ayurveda and Traditional Chinese Medicine should be evaluated by modern standards, and about how historical bias by race and gender shaped both research and clinical practice.
