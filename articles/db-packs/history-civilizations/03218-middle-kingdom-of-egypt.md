# Middle Kingdom of Egypt

The Middle Kingdom (c. 2040–1782 BC) is one of ancient Egypt's classical eras, sandwiched between the fragmented First Intermediate Period and the divided Second Intermediate Period. It opened when a southern ruler reunited the country, and it closed when succession crises and poor Nile floods handed a weakened state to a new dynasty. Across roughly 260 years, Egypt rebuilt a strong central government, pushed its borders south into Nubia, expanded irrigation, and produced literature that later Egyptians treated as canonical.

## Reunification

Before reunification, Egypt was split between two rival dynasties: the Herakleopolitan Tenth Dynasty in the north and the Theban Eleventh Dynasty in the south. The Theban king Mentuhotep II ended the division by conquering Herakleopolis around 2040 BC, restoring a single pharaoh over the Nile Valley. He reasserted Egyptian control in Nubia and the Sinai and revived the state cult of the ruler, in which the king was framed as a divine or semi-divine figure. His successors Mentuhotep III and IV continued consolidation. The dynasty then ended when Amenemhat I, probably not of royal blood, took power and founded the Twelfth Dynasty around 1991 BC, moving the capital to a new city called Itjtawy.

## The Twelfth Dynasty

The Twelfth Dynasty (c. 1991–1782 BC) is the heart of the Middle Kingdom. Its early kings, Amenemhat I and Senusret I, secured borders, built frontier fortifications, and reorganised the provinces. Egypt was divided into nomes, roughly provincial districts, each governed by a hereditary nomarch. Under the old system these posts had become entrenched, with provincial families growing into local power centres. The Twelfth Dynasty kings broke that pattern by directly appointing nomarchs themselves, turning a quasi-feudal patchwork into a more centralised administration. To keep transitions smooth, pharaohs practiced coregency, installing the chosen heir as joint ruler before the king died, so the throne never sat empty.

Military expansion ran in parallel. Egyptian forces pushed into Nubia as far as the Second Cataract, a stretch of rock-strewn rapids on the Nile, and Egyptian influence extended into the Levant, the eastern Mediterranean coastlands. Senusret III, a warrior-king who reigned around 1878 BC, campaigned in Nubia, built a chain of massive forts, and fixed a formal southern boundary at Semna, a fortified checkpoint that served as a hard border. He also tightened central control, further curbing the nomarchs and reforming the bureaucracy.

## Height and Decline

The economic peak came under Amenemhat III, Senusret III's son, whose long reign beginning around 1842 BC saw mining intensify, trade expand, and a major irrigation project open up the Faiyum, a fertile depression west of the Nile developed into one of Egypt's most productive agricultural regions. After his reign the dynasty faltered. Succession disputes, possibly worsened by climate-related crop failures, hollowed out central authority. The Twelfth Dynasty ended with Sobekneferu, the first attested female pharaoh, who ruled only briefly. Power passed to the Thirteenth Dynasty around 1782 BC, beginning a gradual slide into the Second Intermediate Period. Scholars still debate whether the transition was usurpation, civil war, or slow decay.

## Administration and Society

Government was highly centralised. The vizier, the chief minister, often served in two parallel roles, one for Upper Egypt (the southern Nile Valley) and one for Lower Egypt (the Nile Delta), reporting directly to the pharaoh. The state kept registers of land and fixed provincial boundaries. The old nomarch families lost their hereditary grip, most sharply under Senusret III, though some were likely absorbed into the growing royal bureaucracy rather than destroyed. A wider scribal class and a modest middle class emerged to run the more complex state, and literacy, confined to this professional class, became a more useful career path.

## Art, Literature, and Technology

Middle Kingdom artists looked back to Old Kingdom models for their sense of order but refined them, producing works of unusual elegance. Two sculptural innovations stand out: the block statue, a tight squatting figure whose folded limbs form a compact block-like shape, and more naturalistic royal portraits. Literature is the period's most lasting legacy. Tales such as *The Story of Sinuhe*, a narrative about a courtier who flees Egypt and longs to return, and *The Satire of the Trades*, a didactic text praising the scribal profession over manual work, were treated as classics by later Egyptians and shaped how scribes were trained for centuries. Smiths routinely worked arsenical bronze, copper hardened with a small amount of arsenic, with signs of intentional alloying rather than accidental contamination.

The end date of the Middle Kingdom is itself debated. Defining it by the end of the Twelfth Dynasty gives 1782 BC, but if the Thirteenth Dynasty is folded in, the figure can be pushed to c. 1650 BC.
