# Ancient Greek medicine

Ancient Greek medicine began as a collection of theories and practices that expanded by testing ideas against observed symptoms. Early Greeks saw illness as divine punishment and healing as a gift from the gods. Over time this spiritual view was replaced by a physical, cause-and-effect framework that sought natural explanations for disease. The Greek term for medicine was *iatrikē* (ἰατρική).

## Asclepius and the Asclepieia

Asclepius, mythically the son of Apollo, was venerated as the first physician. Temples called Asclepieia (Ἀσκληπιεῖα) functioned as healing centres, offering prognosis, advice, and treatment. Patients entered a dream-like state called *enkoimesis* (ἐγκοίμησις), induced sometimes with soporific substances such as opium, in which they either received divine guidance in a dream or underwent surgery.

At Pergamum, sacred spring water, mud baths, and herbal drinks such as chamomile or peppermint were used, and patients slept in the temple so physicians could interpret their dreams and review symptoms. Three marble tablets from the Asclepieion of Epidaurus, dated to 350 BC, preserve about 70 case histories including realistic procedures like the opening of abdominal abscesses and the removal of foreign material, performed under *enkoimesis*. The Rod of Asclepius, a single snake without wings, became the universal symbol of medicine and is often confused with the Caduceus, a winged staff with two snakes belonging to Hermes.

## Humoral theory

Hippocrates, known as the "Father of Modern Medicine," founded a medical school on Cos and is the central figure of ancient Greek medicine. The Hippocratic Corpus, a collection of around seventy works probably written by several authors over decades, is the main source of his ideas. The treatise *On the Sacred Disease* argues that if all illness were supernatural, biological medicines could not work, marking a deliberate break from magical thinking.

The Corpus focused on pathology rather than a complete physiology, and its views are not perfectly consistent. Its central theory, humorism, holds that health depends on a balance of four humors: blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile, each linked to an organ, temperament, season, and element. Imbalance, caused by factors such as diet, geography, climate, social class, trauma, or the patient's mindset, produced disease. Treatment aimed to restore balance; the *miasma* theory attributed disease to bad air rather than to divine anger. Hippocrates and his students also produced the Hippocratic Oath, still used today, and coined medical terms such as acute, chronic, epidemic, exacerbation, and relapse.

## Women in Hippocratic medicine

Because the Corpus focused on pathology, its account of female anatomy was incomplete and reflected the patriarchal assumptions of its society. Hippocratic authors held that women's flesh was "spongy, loose and porous," unlike the firmer male body, and so absorbed excess blood that was then released through menstruation; breasts grew larger for the same reason. The womb was thought to be a detached organ that could wander through the body, causing symptoms when it pressed on other organs, and was said to be drawn toward pleasant smells and repelled by foul ones, which shaped treatments involving scented fumigations. These views framed female physiology as a less efficient version of a male norm.

## Aristotle, Herodicus, and Hellenistic anatomy

Aristotle brought systematic empiricism to the study of nature, classifying 540 animal species and dissecting at least 50. He treated male and female bodies as variants of a single biological system rather than opposites, and rejected the wandering-womb idea, though his theories still assumed male superiority. Some of his anatomical claims, such as men having more skull sutures and more teeth than women, were wrong.

Herodicus of the 5th century BC, considered the father of sports medicine, is credited with the first use of therapeutic exercise for treating disease and is believed to have been one of Hippocrates' tutors. He prescribed massage routines (slow gentle rubbing, then faster pressure, then gentle friction) along with good diet and herbal oils.

In Alexandria, Herophilus of Chalcedon, called the father of anatomy, and Erasistratus of Chios performed systematic human dissection, including vivisection of prisoners supplied by the Ptolemaic kings. Herophilus argued that intelligence resides in the brain, distinguished veins from arteries by their pulse, and categorised pulse differences for diagnosis. Erasistratus linked the brain's folded surface, compared with other animals, to superior intelligence and proposed that air enters the lungs, is converted in the heart to vital spirit, pumped by the arteries, and partly transformed in the brain into animal spirit distributed by the nerves.

## Galen and the Roman inheritance

Through contact and conquest, Rome adopted Hippocratic medicine. The most influential Roman-era Greek physician was Galen (d. c. 207 AD), who spent more than fifty years in Rome as physician to emperors. His work in anatomy, physiology, pathology, pharmacology, and neurology drew on humorism and on dissections of Barbary macaques and pigs, since human dissection was restricted. Galen demonstrated by nerve ligation experiments that the brain controls the muscles through the cranial and peripheral nervous systems, and he saw himself as both physician and philosopher.

Galen's anatomy remained authoritative until 1543, when Vesalius's *De humani corporis fabrica* corrected it, and his circulatory theory stood until William Harvey's 1628 *De motu cordis* showed that the heart pumps blood in a circuit. The first-century Greek physician Pedanius Dioscorides compiled *De Materia Medica*, describing about 600 plants and drugs from observation; it was never out of print and shaped Western pharmacopoeia into the 19th century.

## The Hippocratic-Galenic tradition

After the Western Empire's collapse, Hippocratic and Galenic texts survived in the Byzantine East and were translated into Arabic, Persian, and Andalusi languages after about 750 AD, with Avicenna the most influential Muslim commentator. From the late eleventh century they returned to Latin Europe through Arabic translations, and during the Renaissance directly from Greek. Although dissection began in the thirteenth century, findings were often forced into Galenic categories, and bloodletting, a core Hippocratic-Galenic therapy, persisted into the 19th century despite its ineffectiveness.
