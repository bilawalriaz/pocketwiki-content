# Byzantine–Ottoman wars

The Byzantine–Ottoman wars were a series of conflicts from the late 13th century to 1453 that destroyed the Byzantine Empire and made the Ottoman Empire the dominant power in the Eastern Mediterranean. The fall of Constantinople on 29 May 1453 marks the conventional transition from the Middle Ages to the early modern period.

## The starting positions

The Byzantine Empire re-established itself in 1261, when Greek forces recaptured Constantinople from the Latin Empire, a Crusader state created when the Fourth Crusade (1204) had sacked the Byzantine capital and partitioned its lands. The restored state was a shrunken realm. Anatolia had fragmented after the decay of the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum, and the Balkans were contested by Serbs and Bulgarians. The military elite was sustained through the pronoia system, land grants in exchange for service that functioned as a fief.

To the east, among the Turkish beyliks (small Anatolian principalities) that filled the Seljuk vacuum, the followers of Osman I began raiding Byzantine Anatolia from around 1265. These warriors, called ghazis, fought under religious and material incentives against non-Muslims. Decisive early engagements: Bapheus (1302), where the Byzantines were defeated and Nicaea was besieged; Bursa, captured in 1326 and made the Ottoman capital; Nicaea, lost in 1331 after the Byzantine relief army was beaten at Pelekanos (1329); Nicomedia in 1337. By 1390, with the fall of Philadelphia, no Byzantine territory remained in Anatolia.

## Civil war and territorial loss

The Byzantines spent most of the wars fighting themselves, and each internal crisis translated into Ottoman territorial gain.

- A civil war (1321–1328) coincided with the Ottoman seizure of Bursa.
- Andronikos III won that war but could not reverse the frontier. He recovered ground in Greece and the Aegean, yet Nicaea (1331) and Nicomedia (1337) still fell.
- A second civil war (1341–1347), triggered by Andronikos's death and fought between regents, allowed the Ottomans to cross into Europe. Gallipoli was taken in 1354.
- A third round, between John V and his sons, eroded imperial authority into the 1390s.

The Ottomans repeatedly exploited the moment a Byzantine faction sought outside help, and each Turkish or Serbian intervention left a permanent gain on the map.

## Balkan expansion and vassalage

The 1354 foothold at Gallipoli opened a second front the empire could not afford. The Ottomans defeated the Serbs at Maritsa (1371) and Kosovo (1389), subjugated Bulgaria, and by the 1390s had encircled Constantinople. Byzantium became an Ottoman vassal, a weaker state pledging tribute to a stronger one in exchange for being left alone, from 1371 to 1394.

War resumed in 1394, when Sultan Bayezid I besieged Constantinople. The siege was broken by Timur's invasion of Anatolia in 1402, which triggered an Ottoman interregnum (a disputed succession) and a temporary Byzantine recovery of some territory. That breathing space ended under Murad II, who reimposed tribute after a renewed siege in 1422.

## The fall of Constantinople (1453)

Sultan Mehmed II ("the Conqueror") and Emperor Constantine XI Palaiologos regarded each other as enemies. Mehmed built a fortress on the Bosporus to control ship movement into the city. The siege opened on 6 April 1453. The defenders, roughly 7,000 men including about 2,000 foreign mercenaries, faced an army more than ten times their number. The siege warfare the Ottomans had mastered, combined with the absence of relief, decided the contest. A formal union of the Catholic and Orthodox churches failed to bring the Western aid Byzantium had hoped would arrive. The city fell on 29 May 1453.

The last Byzantine enclaves followed: the Morea in 1460, Trebizond in 1461, and Epirus in 1479.

## Why the Ottomans won

Three structural advantages recur:

1. Unified command. The Ottomans spoke with one voice, especially after the interregnum, while Byzantium divided its forces across civil wars and Balkan fronts.
2. A vassalage system that conserved resources. Conquered polities paid tribute and provided troops instead of being occupied indefinitely.
3. Support from local populations. Heavy Byzantine taxation in Anatolia made Ottoman rule preferable to many peasants and frontier lords.

The Ottomans also outpaced the Byzantines in siege warfare, the decisive military form of the period. The Byzantines were crippled by multi-front commitments against Latins, Serbs, Bulgarians, and Turks simultaneously, and by the fragmentation of the Eastern Mediterranean following the Fourth Crusade.

## What remains uncertain

Historians still debate how much weight to give the Byzantine civil wars of 1341–1371 relative to Ottoman military superiority and Latin interference, and how much weight to give the Anatolian peasantry's apparent preference for Ottoman rule in explaining the rapid early expansion. The absence of substantial Western aid in 1453 is variably attributed to political disunity, the religious schism between Rome and Constantinople, and competing priorities such as the Hundred Years' War.
