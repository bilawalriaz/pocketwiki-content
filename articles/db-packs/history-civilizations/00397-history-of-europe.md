# History of Europe

Europe's history is the record of how a peripheral peninsula of Eurasia repeatedly reorganised itself through migration, religion, empire, revolution, and war, until it became the centre of global power and then had to share that position again.

## Foundations in Migration and Antiquity

The continent's deep history is a layered story of population movement. The first anatomically modern humans with ancestry recognisable in present-day Europeans appear around 37,000 years ago, during the Paleolithic, when everyone lived by hunting and gathering. The Neolithic revolution reached Europe between 7000 and 2000 BC, carried by Early European Farmers from Anatolia who brought agriculture and permanent settlements. A later wave of steppe pastoralists called the Yamnaya, expanding from the Pontic-Caspian steppe after about 4200 BC, spread Indo-European languages, the family that now includes English, Spanish, Russian, and Hindi, across the continent.

The first literate European civilisations were the Minoans on Crete and the Mycenaeans on the Greek mainland. Classical antiquity, from about 800 BC, was defined by the Greek city-states, which pioneered democracy and philosophy, and then by the Roman Republic and Empire. Rome unified the Mediterranean basin, and Christianity, emerging in the 1st century AD, became the Roman state religion in the 4th century, embedding a single religious framework across the region. When the Western Roman Empire fell in AD 476, the Latin Church became the main institution preserving literacy, law, and administration in the West.

## The Middle Ages (c. AD 500–1500)

Western Europe fragmented into Germanic successor kingdoms, while the Byzantine Empire, the Eastern Roman state with its capital at Constantinople, preserved Greek and Roman learning and Christian theology in the East. A milestone of Western recovery came in 800, when the Pope crowned Charlemagne "Emperor of the Romans," reviving the idea of a unified Christian empire. In the 8th century the Umayyad conquest of Hispania created Al-Andalus, a Muslim-ruled zone that transmitted Greek science, philosophy, and mathematics into Christian Europe. The year 1054 saw the East–West Schism divide Latin Christianity from Eastern Orthodoxy, a split that has never healed.

The High Middle Ages brought the Crusades, the rise of towns, a population boom, and the growth of universities; the 12th-century foundation of the University of Bologna is the oldest university still in continuous operation. This expansion ended with the 14th-century crises: the Great Famine, the Black Death, a bubonic plague pandemic that killed roughly a third of Europe's population, and the Western Schism. Recovery took the form of the Renaissance, a revival of classical learning centred on Italy.

## Early Modern Transformation (c. 1500–1800)

Three forces reshaped Europe. The printing press, movable type perfected in the mid-15th century, slashed the cost of books and made ideas portable. The Age of Discovery put Europeans in contact with the Americas, Africa, and Asia, generating colonies, wealth, and the Columbian exchange, the transfer of crops, peoples, and diseases between hemispheres. In 1517 Martin Luther's Ninety-five Theses ignited the Protestant Reformation, shattering religious unity and producing over a century of wars that culminated in the Thirty Years' War. The same era saw the Scientific Revolution, led by Galileo and Newton, establish an empirical method based on observation and mathematics, and the Enlightenment, an 18th-century movement championing reason and individual rights. By 1800 the great powers were France, Britain, and Russia.

## The Long 19th Century (1789–1914)

The Industrial Revolution, beginning in Britain, shifted economies from agriculture and crafts to factory production powered by fossil fuels. The French Revolution and Napoleonic Wars spread nationalist and liberal ideals across the continent. The Concert of Europe attempted to keep the peace after 1815, but nationalism drove the unification of Italy (1861) and Germany (1871). Revolutions in 1848 challenged old regimes across the map. The period closed with imperial rivalries, the Scramble for Africa, and the formation of two alliance blocs whose confrontation would ignite World War I.

## The World Wars and Cold War (1914–1991)

World War I (1914–1918) destroyed four empires, Ottoman, Russian, Austro-Hungarian, and German, and redrew the map into new nation-states. The interwar years saw the rise of fascism and communism, and ended with World War II (1939–1945), the deadliest conflict in history, marked by the Nazi Holocaust. Post-war Europe split into a U.S.-led Western bloc organised through NATO and a Soviet-led Eastern bloc organised through the Warsaw Pact. Western Europe integrated economically, beginning the path that produced the European Union in 1993. The Cold War ended with the Revolutions of 1989 and the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991.

## Contemporary Europe

The post-Cold War era saw the European Union and NATO expand eastward into the former communist bloc. Challenges have included the Euro area crisis, Brexit, and renewed large-scale war after Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022, which prompted Finland and Sweden to abandon long-standing neutrality and join NATO.
