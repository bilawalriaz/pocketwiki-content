# Gravity

Gravity is a fundamental interaction that attracts every object with mass toward every other object. It dominates the large-scale structure of the universe, governing the motion of planets, stars, and galaxies, yet is negligible at subatomic scales. A key assumption is that inertial mass, the *m* in Newton's second law F=ma, is identical to gravitational mass, which determines how strongly an object is pulled. This equivalence has been tested to better than one part in a trillion.

## The Newtonian Model

In 1687, Isaac Newton's *Principia Mathematica* unified terrestrial and celestial mechanics under a single law. The force between two masses is proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them, an inverse-square law. Newton's theory predicted Neptune, discovered in 1846, and remains accurate for nearly all practical purposes, but it required the unsettling idea of instantaneous action at a distance and failed to explain a small drift in Mercury's orbit.

## Einstein's General Relativity

In 1915, Albert Einstein published the general theory of relativity, which describes gravity not as a pull but as the curvature of spacetime produced by mass and energy. Objects follow geodesics, the straightest possible paths through this curved geometry, which is why they appear to accelerate toward massive bodies.

Three confirmations followed:

- **1919**: Arthur Eddington's eclipse expedition showed that the Sun bends starlight, a phenomenon called gravitational lensing.
- **1959**: The Pound-Rebka experiment measured gravitational time dilation, confirming that clocks deeper in a gravitational well run slower.
- **2015**: LIGO directly detected gravitational waves from merging black holes, ripples in spacetime predicted by relativity.

General relativity also predicts black holes, regions inside an event horizon from which nothing, not even light, can escape. The first strong candidate, Cygnus X-1, was identified in 1971, and a binary pulsar observed in 1973 provided indirect evidence for gravitational radiation before LIGO's direct detection.

## Gravity on Earth and in the Cosmos

On Earth's surface, gravitational acceleration varies from about 9.780 m/s² at the equator to 9.832 m/s² at the poles, shaped by the planet's rotation and its slightly flattened shape. Tides, the fall of objects, and the weight we feel all arise from this local field.

At cosmic scales, gravity shapes structure. Dark matter halos, vast invisible scaffolding inferred from gravitational effects, attracted hydrogen gas that collapsed into the first stars and galaxies. The accelerating expansion of the universe points to a countervailing influence called dark energy, whose nature remains unknown. Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) attempts to explain galaxy rotation curves without invoking dark matter, though most cosmological evidence still favors unseen mass.

## Open Problems

General relativity is incompatible with quantum mechanics. Physicists continue to seek a theory of quantum gravity that would unify gravity with the strong, weak, and electromagnetic interactions. The speed of gravity, confirmed in 2017 to equal the speed of light, constrains any such theory. Whether dark matter is composed of unknown particles, whether dark energy is a fixed cosmological constant, and how gravity behaves at the Planck scale remain the deepest unresolved questions in fundamental physics.
