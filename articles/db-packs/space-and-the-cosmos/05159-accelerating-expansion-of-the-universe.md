# Accelerating expansion of the universe

The expansion of the universe is accelerating, meaning the recession velocity of a distant galaxy from an observer is continuously increasing with time. The acceleration is described by the cosmic scale factor $a(t)$, a number proportional to the size of the universe. Acceleration means the second derivative $\ddot{a} > 0$, equivalent to a negative deceleration parameter $q \approx -0.55$. Because $H(t) = \dot{a}(t)/a(t)$ and $dH/dt = -H^2(1+q)$, the Hubble parameter is still decreasing: each galaxy's recession velocity grows while its velocity-to-distance ratio declines.

## The 1998 discovery

Two independent teams, the Supernova Cosmology Project and the High-Z Supernova Search Team, used Type Ia supernovae, exploding white dwarfs that all reach nearly the same peak luminosity, as standard candles. A standard candle is an object whose true brightness is known, so its apparent dimness reveals its distance. Their spectra gave the redshift $z$, related to the scale factor at explosion by $a(t) = 1/(1+z)$. The result was that high-redshift supernovae were 10 to 15 percent farther than expected for a decelerating universe with $\Omega_M = 0.2$, a universe containing only matter, with the matter density set to 20 percent of the critical density. Three team members later received Nobel Prizes.

## Why gravity alone predicts deceleration

Within general relativity, the Friedmann equation governs expansion:

$$H^2 = \left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho - \frac{\kappa c^2}{a^2}$$

The critical density is $\rho_c = 3H^2/(8\pi G)$ and the density parameter is $\Omega = \rho/\rho_c$, giving

$$H(a) = H_0\sqrt{\Omega_k a^{-2} + \Omega_m a^{-3} + \Omega_r a^{-4} + \Omega_{DE} a^{-3(1+w)}}$$

The curvature, matter, and radiation terms all dilute as the universe expands, so a universe containing only those components must decelerate. The acceleration equation

$$\frac{\ddot{a}}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left(\rho + \frac{3P}{c^2}\right)$$

shows that acceleration requires negative pressure with $w < -1/3$, where $P = w c^2 \rho$ and $w$ is the equation-of-state parameter relating pressure to energy density.

## Dark energy and the cosmological constant

The accepted explanation is a positive cosmological constant $\Lambda$, equivalent to a constant vacuum energy density called dark energy, with $w = -1$. This gives the Lambda-CDM model, the standard cosmological model since 2003. The acceleration began roughly 5 billion years ago when the universe entered its dark-energy-dominated era. As the scale factor doubles, matter density falls by a factor of 8 while dark energy density stays nearly constant, so dark energy comes to dominate. Confirmatory evidence comes from baryon acoustic oscillations, which are sound waves frozen into the galaxy distribution at recombination, from the growth of galaxy clusters, and from the age of the universe derived by integrating the Friedmann equation.

## Relation to inflation

Cosmic inflation was a far shorter, quasi-exponential accelerated expansion in the very early universe. The mechanism is different, but the defining feature, $\ddot{a} > 0$, is shared.

## Alternatives and open questions

A 2019 reanalysis using ten times as many Type Ia supernovae as the 1998 studies found little evidence for an isotropic monopole acceleration, that is, a uniform acceleration in all directions. Alternatives include quintessence, a dark energy whose density slowly decreases; phantom energy with $w < -1$, which would tear the universe apart in a Big Rip; the backreaction conjecture, in which local underdensities mimic acceleration; and modified gravity, though the gravitational-wave event GW170817 ruled out many modified-gravity models by constraining the speed of gravity to equal the speed of light.

## Consequences

A cosmological-constant-dominated universe asymptotically approaches a de Sitter universe, one expanding exponentially forever. The cosmic microwave background is steadily redshifted to longer wavelengths until it is absorbed by the interstellar medium when the universe is less than 50 times its present age, after which distant objects become unobservable. In the far future, structures disintegrate into isolated stable particles, a scenario called heat death. Alternatives for the ultimate fate include the Big Rip, a Big Bounce, or a Big Crunch.
