# Age of the universe

The age of the universe is the time elapsed since the Big Bang, the point at which the universe's scale factor extrapolates back to zero. The expansion we observe can be run backwards and integrated forward to yield a finite age. The best current estimate, 13.8 billion years, comes from the Planck Collaboration's 2018 analysis of the cosmic microwave background (CMB), the faint relic radiation left over from the early hot, dense state. This value is consistent with the ages of the oldest known stars, which set a lower bound the universe must exceed.

## Two routes to the same number

Astronomers determine the age using two different methods. The first is the Lambda-CDM model, a standard cosmological model containing ordinary matter, cold dark matter, radiation, and a cosmological constant representing dark energy, matched to early-universe observations, especially the CMB. The second builds a distance ladder from nearby stars and galaxies, then measures how fast they recede, depending on the local present-day expansion rate.

Both routes produce the Hubble constant, the present expansion rate, which feeds into the Friedmann equation from general relativity to calculate age. The two methods give slightly different values, a tension that is one of the most actively studied problems in cosmology. The accepted value also lies within the range set by the oldest observed star.

## The mathematics in plain terms

The expansion rate at any time is the Hubble parameter H(t). Its present value H₀ is the Hubble constant, with units of inverse time. A rough age estimate is the Hubble time, 1/H₀. With H₀ near 69 km/s/Mpc (kilometres per second per megaparsec, a unit of cosmic distance), this works out to about 14.5 billion years.

A more accurate figure comes from the Friedmann equation:

t₀ = (1/H₀) · F(Ω_r, Ω_m, Ω_Λ)

where Ω_m, Ω_r, and Ω_Λ are fractional density parameters for matter, radiation, and dark energy. The correction factor F adjusts the Hubble time for the universe's actual composition. For a flat universe without a cosmological constant, F = 2/3, giving a younger universe. For Planck's measured values (Ω_m, Ω_Λ) = (0.3086, 0.6914), F ≈ 0.956, producing the 13.8 billion-year result.

Dark energy matters because the matter-only model gave a universe younger than the oldest globular clusters, densely packed ancient star clusters in the Milky Way, an obvious contradiction. Adding the cosmological constant raises the calculated age and resolves the conflict.

## Lower limits from old objects

Any age of the universe must exceed the age of the oldest things inside it. Two stellar measurements anchor the lower bound. The coolest white dwarfs, the slowly cooling remnants of dead stars, provide a clock because they cool predictably. The dimmest main-sequence turnoff point, the faintest stars still burning hydrogen in their cores, in star clusters sets another, because lower-mass stars spend longer on the main sequence, so the lowest-mass stars just leaving it mark the cluster's minimum age.

## The historical road to a finite, expanding universe

Through the early 20th century, most scientists assumed the universe was eternal and unchanging. The first crack came from thermodynamics: in an infinitely old closed system, entropy would have driven everything to the same temperature, leaving no stars or life. No explanation for this contradiction was available at the time.

In 1915 Einstein published general relativity, and in 1917 he built a cosmological model. To keep the universe static he added a cosmological constant. Eddington later showed this static model was unstable.

The first observational evidence for expansion came when Vesto Slipher measured galaxy recession velocities and Edwin Hubble, in 1929, combined these with galaxy distances to show that more distant galaxies recede faster. Hubble's initial age was far too low because he assumed galaxies were nearer than they are. Allan Sandage's 1958 measurement of the Hubble constant was the first reasonably accurate one; he initially distrusted his own result.

The discovery of the CMB in 1965 by Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, alongside theoretical work by Robert Dicke, Jim Peebles, and David Wilkinson, effectively ended debate about whether the universe was expanding. The CMB is the afterglow of recombination, the era roughly 380,000 years after the Big Bang when atoms formed and the universe became transparent, and its existence matched a prediction of Big Bang theory.

## Refining the number with space missions

The Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe (WMAP), launched in 2001, used the first acoustic peak in the CMB power spectrum, a pattern of temperature variations across the sky, to determine the size of the universe at recombination. Light-travel time to that surface gives a reliable age. WMAP's 2012 data release produced 13.772 ± 0.059 billion years. Planck, launched in 2009, refined this to 13.813 ± 0.038 billion years in 2015 and 13.8 billion years in 2018, slightly higher than WMAP but within its error margins. Both probes measured CMB features independently of galaxy distances, removing the largest source of error in earlier work.

## Lookback time

Light from distant objects was emitted when the universe was younger. The lookback time, t_L(z) = t₀ − t_e(z), is the difference between the universe's present age and its age when the light left a source at redshift z, a measure of how much the light has been stretched by expansion. It depends on the same cosmological parameters as the age itself, so it grows with distance and approaches t₀ for the most distant observable sources.

The reported precision of 13.8 billion years assumes the underlying model is correct. Adding plausible variations, such as extra relativistic particles, can widen the error bars by an order of magnitude, showing that the published precision is conditional on the model assumptions.
