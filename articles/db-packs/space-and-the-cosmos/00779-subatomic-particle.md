# Subatomic particle

A subatomic particle is any particle smaller than an atom, the basic units of matter and force described by quantum mechanics. They divide into two kinds: elementary particles, which are not made of anything smaller, and composite particles, which are bound collections of elementary ones. Their behaviour is governed by quantum mechanics and quantum field theory, so every subatomic particle also behaves like a wave.

## The Standard Model roster

The Standard Model of particle physics lists twelve elementary fermions and a small set of bosons. The fermions split into six quarks (up, down, strange, charm, bottom, top) and six leptons (electron, electron neutrino, muon, muon neutrino, tau, tau neutrino). The quarks carry a property called color charge and feel the strong force; the leptons do not. Every elementary fermion has spin 1/2, a quantum measure of intrinsic angular momentum whose value of 1/2 is an odd half-integer.

The elementary bosons carry forces. The photon carries electromagnetism, the W and Z bosons carry the weak force, and eight gluons carry the strong force, each with spin 1. The Higgs boson, spin 0, endows other particles with rest mass. The photon and the gluons are massless; W and Z are massive. The remaining elementary particles predicted by theories beyond the Standard Model, including a graviton of spin 2 that would carry gravity, remain unobserved. The top quark was confirmed in 1995, the tau neutrino in 2000, and the Higgs boson in 2012.

## Composite particles: hadrons

A hadron is any composite particle built from quarks, a term introduced in 1962 by Lev Okun. Quarks bind by exchanging gluons, and a property called color confinement keeps them permanently trapped inside hadrons; isolated quarks have never been observed.

Hadrons fall into two families. Baryons contain an odd number of quarks, almost always three. The proton, two up quarks and one down quark, and the neutron, two down quarks and one up quark, are the only stable baryons and the only stable hadrons. Mesons contain an even number of quarks, usually one quark paired with one antiquark; the pions and kaons are the best-known examples. Because baryons combine three spin-1/2 quarks, their total spin is 1/2 or 3/2, making them fermions. Mesons, built from a quark and an antiquark, have combined spin 0 or 1, making them bosons. Almost every other hadron is unstable, decaying in microseconds or less. Exotic exceptions such as positronium (an electron and positron) and muonium (a muon and an antimuon) contain no quarks at all.

## Statistics: bosons versus fermions

Every quantum particle is either a boson (integer spin) or a fermion (odd half-integer spin), and the split governs collective behaviour. The elementary fermions in the Standard Model are the quarks and leptons, all with spin 1/2. The elementary bosons are the gauge bosons of spin 1 plus the Higgs boson of spin 0. Composite particles inherit their statistical class from how their constituents' spins add.

## Mass and decay

Mass and energy are interchangeable, expressed by E = mc². A particle with a frame in which it rests has positive rest mass and is called massive; all composite particles are massive. The names reflect early mass categories: baryons meant "heavy," mesons "intermediate," leptons "lightweight." After the quark model was accepted in the 1970s, these labels were redefined by structure: baryons as three-quark composites, mesons as quark–antiquark pairs, and leptons as elementary fermions without color charge. Baryons are usually heavier than mesons, which are usually heavier than most leptons, yet the heaviest lepton, the tau, is more massive than a proton or neutron. Every electrically charged particle is massive; the only massless particles are the photon and the gluon, and gluons cannot be isolated.

Decay pathways differ by particle. Quarks, most leptons, and unstable baryons decay through the strong or weak forces. The proton has never been observed to decay, though some Grand Unified Theories require it to. The muon and tau decay by the weak force. The electron, the lightest charged particle, is theoretically stable because no lighter charged particle exists to carry away its charge. Neutrinos are likewise stable, but a separate quantum effect, neutrino oscillation, lets them change flavour even through a vacuum.

## Charge, waves, and conservation

Every observable charge is an integer multiple of the elementary charge e, the magnitude of the electron's charge. Quarks carry charges that are multiples of one third of e, but because they are confined, only whole-charge combinations, like the proton's +e or the neutron's 0, appear in isolation. Einstein, Bose, and de Broglie established that every particle also has a wave nature, verified from electrons up to entire atoms and molecules; for macroscopic objects the wavelengths are too small to detect.

Two conservation laws dominate particle interactions: conservation of energy and conservation of momentum. They allow quantitative predictions on every scale, from collisions inside stars to scattering events at particle accelerators. The electron's mass is about 1/1836 that of a hydrogen atom, with the rest carried by the proton. An element's atomic number is its count of protons, while its isotopes share that count but differ in neutron number, measured together as the mass number. Chemistry is shaped by the electron, proton, and neutron, with nuclear physics tracking how protons and neutrons pack into nuclei.

## Open questions

Particle physicists still disagree on a single definition of a particle, describing it variously as a collapsed wave function, an excitation of a quantum field, an irreducible representation of the Poincaré group (the mathematical symmetry of spacetime), or simply an observed phenomenon. The proton's stability is experimentally undecided, the graviton plus the extra particles predicted by extensions of the Standard Model remain undetected, and current data cannot fully separate a truly stable neutrino from one that is merely extremely long-lived.
