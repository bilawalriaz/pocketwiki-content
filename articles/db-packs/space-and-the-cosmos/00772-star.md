# Star

A star is a self-gravitating ball of plasma whose energy comes from hydrogen fusing into helium in its core. The core is hot and dense enough that hydrogen nuclei overcome their electrical repulsion and collide, releasing energy according to E=mc². Outward radiation pressure balances gravity's inward pull, so a stable star exists in hydrostatic equilibrium. The energy slowly leaks to the surface as light.

## How stars form

Stars form when a region inside a cold molecular cloud becomes so dense that its own gravity overpowers the gas pressure trying to push it apart. This threshold is the Jeans instability: once the cloud crosses it, the region collapses. A spinning fragment flattens into a protostar wrapped in a protoplanetary disk of gas and dust, the same kind of disk that later builds planets. As the protostar's core contracts, gravitational energy heats it until hydrogen fusion ignites. In low-mass protostars (the T Tauri phase) and their higher-mass counterparts (Herbig Ae/Be stars), magnetic jets shoot outward and help blow away leftover gas.

## Main sequence and mass-driven evolution

Once fusion starts, the star settles onto the main sequence, the hydrogen-burning phase that consumes roughly 90% of a star's life. What happens next depends almost entirely on initial mass.

- Below 0.5 M☉, a star is fully convective, mixes its fuel completely, and eventually dies as a helium white dwarf.
- From 0.5 to 2.25 M☉, the star swells into a red giant, undergoes a brief helium flash as its core ignites, then sheds its outer layers as a planetary nebula, leaving a white dwarf behind.
- From 2.25 to 8 M☉, helium ignites smoothly without a flash, then follows the same white-dwarf path.
- Above 8 M☉, the star fuses heavier elements in successive burning stages: helium via the triple-alpha process, then carbon, neon, oxygen, and silicon, building up layer by layer until the core is mostly iron-56, which cannot release energy by further fusion. The core collapses, the outer layers rebound, and the star explodes as a supernova, scattering heavy elements into space and leaving a neutron star or black hole.

Mass also decides which fusion pathway dominates. Sun-like stars run the proton-proton chain, where hydrogen nuclei stepwise combine into helium. More massive stars, hotter at the core, use the carbon-nitrogen-oxygen (CNO) cycle, where carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen act as catalysts for the same net reaction.

## Stellar structure

A star's structure mirrors how it moves energy outward. The core is the fusion engine. Above it, in Sun-like stars, sits a radiation zone where photons slowly diffuse through dense plasma, then a convection zone where hot plasma rises and cooler plasma sinks. The visible surface, the photosphere, sits atop these layers. Above the photosphere, the chromosphere, transition region, and corona are zones of increasingly superheated, tenuous plasma; their existence depends on the churning of a convective envelope below.

## Classification and observation

Astronomers sort stars by their spectra, the pattern of light and dark lines produced when starlight passes through a star's outer layers. Spectral types O, B, A, F, G, K, M run from hottest (O, tens of thousands of kelvin, blue) to coolest (M, around 3000 K, red). Within each type, luminosity classes I–VII mark size, from supergiants down to white dwarfs. The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram plots luminosity against temperature: most stars lie on a diagonal main-sequence band, while red giants, supergiants, and white dwarfs occupy off-band regions. Apparent magnitude is how bright a star looks from Earth; absolute magnitude is how bright it would look from 10 parsecs, a distance that reflects the star's true power output. Stellar masses, ages, metal content, distances, and motions are extracted from spectra, brightness measurements, and precise positions.

## Companions and clusters

Most stars belong to binary or multiple systems, in which two stars orbit a common center. When one star swells and overflows its Roche lobe, the gravitational boundary of its share of the system, matter spills onto its companion in a process called mass transfer. Such interactions drive Type Ia supernovae, when a white dwarf accretes enough material to explode, and may explain luminous blue variables and Wolf–Rayet stars. Star clusters, both loose open clusters and dense ancient globular clusters, hold coeval populations whose shared age makes them natural laboratories for testing stellar theory. Stellar motions through the galaxy, measured as radial velocity and proper motion, trace how stars are born and how the Milky Way is structured. After the Sun, the nearest star is Proxima Centauri at 4.25 light-years.

## Why stars matter chemically

Stars are the main factories of elements heavier than lithium. Carbon, oxygen, and iron in the Solar System were forged inside earlier generations of stars, either by steady fusion or in supernovae. When massive stars die, they seed galaxies with these elements, so successive generations of stars and planets form from richer material. Stellar metallicity, the abundance of elements heavier than helium, both marks this chemical evolution and influences whether new planets can form.
