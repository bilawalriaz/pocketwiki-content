# Astronomy

Astronomy is the natural science that studies celestial objects and the phenomena they produce. Its subject matter includes planets, moons, stars, nebulae, galaxies, asteroids, comets, supernovae, pulsars, and the cosmic microwave background. It applies mathematics, physics, and chemistry to explain their origin and evolution, with cosmology as the branch that studies the universe as a whole. The name comes from Greek *astron* ("star") and *nomos* ("law" or "rule"). Astronomy is distinct from astrology, which holds that human affairs correlate with celestial positions; the two share an origin but diverged, with astronomy grounded in physics. "Astronomy" and "astrophysics" are largely synonymous, though "astrophysics" typically denotes physics-oriented work. It is one of the oldest sciences, evolving from calendar-making and navigation into a physics-driven discipline, and remains one of the few sciences where amateurs contribute directly to discoveries.

## Historical Development

Early astronomy served practical needs such as agricultural calendars and navigation. Prehistoric sites including Stonehenge, the Nebra sky disc, and Nabta Playa (aligned with the heliacal rising of Sirius) show sustained interest in the sky. Babylonians began mathematical astronomy and discovered that lunar eclipses recur in a saros cycle of 223 synodic months. Greek astronomers sought rational explanations: Heracleides Ponticus proposed Earth's rotation in the 4th century BC, Aristarchus of Samos proposed a heliocentric model and estimated lunar and solar sizes and distances, and Hipparchus calculated the Moon's size and distance, invented the astrolabe, and cataloged 1020 stars. The Antikythera mechanism (c. 150–80 BC) was an analog computer for predicting celestial positions, a level of mechanical complexity not matched in Europe until the 14th century.

After the Greeks, the geocentric Ptolemaic system from Ptolemy's *Almagest* dominated for over a thousand years, offering the most accurate planetary predictions available at the time. Islamic astronomy flourished from the 9th century: al-Sufi described the Andromeda Galaxy in 964, Ali ibn Ridwan observed the SN 1006 supernova, and al-Biruni showed that the Sun's apogee is mobile rather than fixed. Copernicus proposed a heliocentric model that retained circular orbits, and Galileo confirmed it in 1610 by observing the phases of Venus, which only a Sun-centered system produces. Kepler replaced circular orbits with elliptical motion, and Newton supplied the theoretical basis through gravitation and celestial mechanics, also inventing the reflecting telescope. Spectroscopy advanced with Fraunhofer's catalog of 574 dark solar spectral lines (1814–15) and Kirchhoff's attribution of those lines to elements (1859), giving astronomy a way to identify the composition of distant objects. Hubble proved in 1922–23 that Andromeda and Triangulum lie beyond the Milky Way, then published Hubble's law in 1929, showing that galaxies recede at velocities proportional to distance, evidence that the universe is expanding. The 1965 discovery of the cosmic microwave background confirmed the Big Bang, in which the universe began dense and hot and has expanded over 13.8 billion years, and LIGO detected gravitational waves from a binary black hole merger in 2015.

## Observational Methods

Observational astronomy divides by wavelength. Radio astronomy (1 mm to 15 m) detects neutral hydrogen via its 21 cm line, along with pulsars, supernovae, and active galactic nuclei. Infrared astronomy studies cool objects such as planets and dust-shrouded star-forming regions, and most infrared wavelengths are absorbed by the atmosphere, so observatories sit on high, dry sites or in space; the James Webb Space Telescope uses infrared to observe highly redshifted distant galaxies. Optical astronomy (roughly 380–700 nm) is the oldest branch and progressed from hand drawings to photography to CCD detectors. Ultraviolet, X-ray, and gamma-ray astronomy require satellites or high-altitude platforms; gamma-ray bursts, the brightest known phenomena, are detected by satellites and by Cherenkov telescopes that catch visible flashes from gamma rays striking the atmosphere. Non-electromagnetic methods include neutrino detectors (SAGE, GALLEX, Kamioka), which recorded 24 neutrinos from supernova 1987A, and gravitational-wave detectors. Combining different messengers is called multi-messenger astronomy. Astrometry, one of the oldest fields, measures precise celestial positions for navigation, calendars, and stellar parallax, the baseline method for measuring cosmic distances.

## Subfields by Scale

Physical cosmology studies the large-scale structure of the universe within the Big Bang framework, with dark matter and dark energy thought to make up about 96% of its mass. Extragalactic astronomy studies galaxy formation, evolution, and clusters. Galactic astronomy examines the Milky Way, a barred spiral galaxy whose mass is dominated by a dark matter halo of unknown nature. Stellar astronomy covers star formation in giant molecular clouds, main-sequence nuclear fusion, and stellar evolution ending in white dwarfs, neutron stars, or black holes (formed when a collapsing star is at least roughly three times the Sun's mass). Solar astronomy studies the Sun, a G2 V main-sequence dwarf about 4.6 billion years old, including its sunspot cycle and layered structure from core to corona. Planetary science covers Solar System bodies and exoplanets, examining differentiation, magnetic fields, and internal heat sources such as radioactive decay, collisions, and tidal heating that drive volcanism and tectonics.

## Interdisciplinary Work and Amateurs

Astrochemistry studies molecular abundances and reactions in the Solar System and interstellar medium. Astrobiology investigates the origin of life beyond Earth and the biosignatures that would reveal it, drawing on astronomy, biochemistry, geology, and microbiology. Related fields include archaeoastronomy, which studies ancient astronomies in cultural context; astrostatistics, which applies statistical methods to astrophysical data; and forensic astronomy, which applies astronomical methods to art history and legal questions. Amateur astronomers observe with consumer or homemade equipment, work through the Messier (110 objects) and Herschel 400 catalogues, discover comets, measure stellar occultations to refine minor planet orbits, and advance astrophotography; Karl Jansky's discovery of a radio source at the Milky Way's center grew out of amateur radio astronomy.

## Open Questions

Unresolved questions include the nature of dark matter and dark energy, the ultimate fate of the universe, why observed lithium abundance is roughly four times lower than standard Big Bang nucleosynthesis predicts, the origin of the stellar mass spectrum, how the first galaxies and supermassive black holes formed, the source of ultra-high-energy cosmic rays, and whether other life exists.

## Key Terms

- **Heliocentric model**: A Sun-centered planetary system, proposed by Aristarchus and later by Copernicus.
- **Geocentric model**: The Earth-centered Ptolemaic system that dominated for over a millennium.
- **Cepheid variables**: Pulsing stars whose period-luminosity relation allows distance measurement.
- **Hubble's law**: Galaxies recede at velocities proportional to distance, indicating an expanding universe.
- **Cosmic microwave background**: Faint radio radiation from the early universe, discovered in 1965.
- **Astrometry**: Measurement of precise celestial positions.
- **Multi-messenger astronomy**: Combining electromagnetic, neutrino, and gravitational-wave observations.
