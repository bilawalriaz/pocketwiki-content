# Particle physics

Particle physics, also called high-energy physics, studies the smallest pieces of matter and the forces between them. The field's organizing framework is the Standard Model, a theory that classifies every known elementary particle and describes three of the four fundamental forces. Quantum field theory is the mathematical language used to express it, and a central goal of the discipline is to extend the framework so it also includes gravity, which the Standard Model does not yet handle.

The modern picture took shape in stages. In the 19th century, John Dalton concluded that each chemical element is composed of unique atoms. In 1939 Lise Meitner explained nuclear fission from experiments by Otto Hahn, and Hans Bethe explained nuclear fusion. By 1947 Bethe's calculation of the Lamb shift is credited with opening the modern era of particle physics. The 1950s and 1960s produced so many new particles that physicists called the collection the "particle zoo". The Standard Model was formulated in the 1970s and gained acceptance once experiments confirmed quarks. In 2008 the Large Hadron Collider at CERN circulated its first beam, and in 2012 researchers there announced a new particle behaving like the long-predicted Higgs boson.

The Standard Model divides elementary particles into fermions and bosons. Fermions are the matter particles; they have half-integer spin and obey the Pauli exclusion principle, which prevents two identical fermions from sharing a quantum state. Bosons are the force carriers; they have integer spin and can occupy the same state. Fermions come in three generations, but ordinary matter uses only the first: the up and down quarks, the electron, and the electron neutrino. The other two generations contain heavier copies, found in cosmic rays and accelerators but absent from stable matter.

Three forces are carried by gauge bosons. Photons mediate electromagnetism, the force between electric charges. W and Z bosons mediate the weak interaction, which drives radioactive beta decay. Eight gluons mediate the strong interaction, which binds quarks together. The Higgs boson is different: it is not a force carrier but the particle manifestation of the Higgs field, the process by which that pervasive field gives mass to the W and Z bosons.

Quarks are the building blocks of composite particles called hadrons. They carry fractional electric charge, either -1/3 or +2/3, and a separate "color" charge that comes in three varieties, red, green, and blue, with gluons carrying eight color combinations. A property called color confinement means that the energy needed to pull a quark away from its neighbors grows so quickly that a new quark-antiquark pair is produced instead, so isolated quarks are never observed. Baryons are hadrons made of an odd number of quarks; protons and neutrons each contain three quarks and make up atomic nuclei. Mesons are hadrons made of a quark paired with an antiquark; they are short-lived and appear in cosmic ray collisions and accelerator experiments. Exotic hadrons such as tetraquarks and pentaquarks extend this family.

Leptons, including the electron and the electron neutrino, are fermions that do not feel the strong force. Quarks have fractional electric charge, while leptons have whole-number charges, either 0 or -1. Every charged particle has a corresponding antiparticle with opposite charge, and some neutral particles, like the photon, are their own antiparticle. When a particle meets its antiparticle, they annihilate, converting their mass into other particles or pure energy.

The Standard Model contains 61 elementary particles and has agreed with nearly all experimental tests, yet physicists consider it incomplete. Neutrinos were assumed massless because the model predicts none, but experiments have shown they have mass, the first confirmed deviation. Other gaps include the hierarchy problem, the strong CP problem, and the absence of any particle that explains dark matter and dark energy. Gravity also remains outside the model; the graviton, a hypothetical boson that would carry the gravitational force, has not been detected. Proposed extensions include supersymmetric particles, axions, extra spatial dimensions, and string theory, which tries to merge quantum mechanics with general relativity into a single theory of everything.

Testing these ideas requires enormous machines that slam particles together at high energy. Major laboratories include CERN with the Large Hadron Collider, Fermilab with its former Tevatron, and Brookhaven with the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider. These facilities have produced technologies used in medicine, computing, and industry. Searches for physics beyond the Standard Model, including the Deep Underground Neutrino Experiment and the proposed Future Circular Collider, continue.
