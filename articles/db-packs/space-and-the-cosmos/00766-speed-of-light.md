# Speed of light

The speed of light in vacuum, written c, is exactly 299,792,458 metres per second. It is the maximum speed at which energy, matter, or information can travel, and the central constant of Einstein's theory of relativity. Since 1983, c has been exact by definition: the metre is the distance light covers in 1/299,792,458 of a second, and the second is set by the caesium-133 hyperfine transition at 9,192,631,770 cycles. Before the redefinition, techniques such as cavity resonance and laser interferometry had already pushed fractional uncertainty below 10⁻⁹.

## Why c is a universal speed limit

Einstein's 1905 special relativity treats c as a property of spacetime, not just of light. The theory links space and time through c, producing time dilation, length contraction, and the mass–energy equivalence E = mc². The Lorentz factor γ = (1 − v²/c²)⁻¹/² governs all of these effects; it is near 1 at ordinary speeds and diverges as v approaches c. The limit is causal: a signal faster than light would let an effect precede its cause in some frames, breaking the order physics depends on. Gravitational waves and every massless particle travel at c, so the limit applies to anything without rest mass, not only to photons.

## Light in materials

In transparent media such as glass or water, light slows. The refractive index n = c/v captures this; glass has n ≈ 1.5 and air n ≈ 1.0003. A light wave in a medium has several characteristic speeds. The phase velocity is the speed of individual crests; the group velocity is the speed of the pulse envelope; the front velocity, the speed of the first disturbance, always equals c. Under exotic conditions group velocity can exceed c or turn negative, but these superluminal features carry no new signal, since the pulse front, and therefore any information, still travels at c. When a charged particle moves through a medium faster than the local phase velocity of light, it emits an electromagnetic shock wave called Cherenkov radiation.

## How the value was established

Rømer made the first quantitative estimate of c in 1676 from the timing of Io's eclipses at Jupiter. Bradley refined it in 1729 using stellar aberration, the apparent drift of a star's position caused by Earth's motion through incoming light. In 1856 Weber and Kohlrausch found that an electromagnetic ratio matched the known speed of light, which led Maxwell in 1865 to identify light as an electromagnetic wave. The 1887 Michelson–Morley experiment then failed to detect any motion of the Earth through a luminiferous aether, preparing the ground for Einstein's postulate that c is the same in every inertial frame. Fizeau's cogwheel (1849) and Foucault's rotating mirror (1850) provided early Earth-based measurements, and by 1972 the NBS had reached a fractional uncertainty of about 3.5 × 10⁻⁹.

## Practical consequences

Signals in a wire travel at roughly 30 cm per nanosecond, so the layout of a processor and the length of its interconnects cap how fast a computer can run. A signal around the Earth's circumference takes at least about 67 ms, setting a hard floor on global communications. Spacecraft are worse, with roughly 3 seconds to the Moon and 4 to 24 minutes to Mars depending on orbital geometry. The same delay works in reverse for astronomy: the farther an object is, the longer its light has been in flight, so the Hubble Deep Field shows galaxies as they appeared roughly 13 billion years ago.

## Open questions

Only the two-way speed of light can be measured without a clock-synchronisation convention, and Einstein's convention fixes the one-way speed at c by definition. Some theories propose that c has varied over cosmic history or depends on photon energy at Planck-scale energies, but gamma-ray-burst observations such as GRB 090510 have found no such variation. The Scharnhorst effect predicts that light might travel faster than c between closely spaced conducting plates, though any arrangement that carried a real signal would run into causality.
