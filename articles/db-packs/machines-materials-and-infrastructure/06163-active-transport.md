# Active transport

In cellular biology, active transport is the movement of molecules or ions across a cell membrane from lower to higher concentration, against the concentration gradient. The phospholipid bilayer blocks most solutes, and moving "uphill" defies natural diffusion, so the cell must spend energy and use specialised transmembrane proteins that recognise and carry each substance. This distinguishes active transport from passive transport, in which molecules diffuse down their gradient using only kinetic energy and entropy.

The function of the process is accumulation. Cells use it to take in nutrients such as glucose and amino acids, to maintain internal ion balances that drive nerve signals and muscle contraction, and to secrete hormones. When active transport fails, the consequences are concrete: a malfunctioning chloride channel causes cystic fibrosis, and defective glucose uptake is central to diabetes.

## Two forms of energy coupling

Active transport splits into two forms based on the energy source.

**Primary active transport** uses metabolic energy directly. Most enzymes that perform it are transmembrane ATPases, proteins that split ATP and use the released energy to change shape and pump a solute across the membrane. Phosphorylation of the carrier, binding of the cargo, a conformational change, then release of the cargo and hydrolysis of the bound phosphate reset the cycle. The defining example is the sodium–potassium pump, which moves three Na⁺ out of the cell for every two K⁺ in, maintaining the membrane potential that every animal cell depends on. Other primary ATPases handle Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, and H⁺. The energy source need not be ATP: the mitochondrial electron transport chain uses redox energy from NADH to pump protons, and photosynthetic proteins in thylakoid membranes use light energy to build a proton gradient. The major families of primary transporters are P-type ATPases (Na⁺/K⁺, Ca²⁺, H⁺ pumps), F- and V-ATPases, and ABC transporters, a diverse family that drives import or export of many substrates, including the CFTR chloride channel defective in cystic fibrosis. In plants, ABC transporters such as PhABCG1 export volatile compounds from petals, and NtPDR1 exports antimicrobial diterpenes from root and trichome cells.

**Secondary active transport**, also called cotransport, uses no ATP directly. It exploits the electrochemical gradient built up by primary pumps, most often the inward Na⁺ gradient in animals or the inward H⁺ gradient in bacteria and yeasts. Letting one ion flow down its gradient releases free energy, and the carrier uses that energy to drag a second solute uphill against its own gradient. In a symporter both solutes cross in the same direction; in an antiporter they cross in opposite directions. The classic symporter is SGLT1, which imports one glucose molecule per two Na⁺ ions and is the mechanism Robert K. Crane first proposed in Prague in 1960 to explain intestinal glucose absorption. SGLT1 also underlies oral rehydration therapy, since pulling sugar in osmotically drags water with it. A related symporter, SGLT2, reabsorbs glucose in the kidney proximal tubule, and defects in it cause familial renal glucosuria; SGLT2 inhibitors are now a major class of diabetes drugs. The classic antiporter is the sodium–calcium exchanger, which lets three Na⁺ in to push one Ca²⁺ out of cardiac muscle cells at roughly 2000 ions per second, fast enough to clear the Ca²⁺ spikes that follow each heartbeat, while the slower calcium ATPase (about 30 ions per second) sets the resting baseline.

## Vesicular transport

A second mode of active transport moves material in bulk, inside membrane-bound vesicles rather than through individual carriers. In endocytosis the plasma membrane folds inward, enclosing extracellular material in a vesicle inside the cytoplasm, where lysosomal enzymes often digest the contents. Cells use this to take up hormones, growth factors, and proteins, and viruses exploit a related fusion step to deliver their DNA into host cells. Pinocytosis refers to uptake of fluid and dissolved solutes (for example, fat droplets in the small intestine), and phagocytosis refers to engulfment of solid particles. Exocytosis is the reverse: a vesicle inside the cell fuses with the plasma membrane and releases its contents to the outside, which is how neurons release neurotransmitters across a synapse.

The history of the concept tracks the slow recognition that cells do work at their boundaries. Emil du Bois-Reymond suggested active transport in 1848; Dennis Robert Hoagland showed in 1926 that plant roots absorb salts against a gradient using metabolic energy; Rosenberg formalised the idea on thermodynamic grounds in 1948; and Jens Christian Skou received the 1997 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the sodium–potassium pump, the first molecular machine of active transport to be understood in detail.
