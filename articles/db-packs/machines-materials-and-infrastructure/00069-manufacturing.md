# Manufacturing

Manufacturing is the industrial-scale transformation of raw materials into finished goods using equipment, labor, machines, tools, and chemical or biological processing. It is the defining activity of the **secondary sector** of the economy, which sits between the **primary sector** (agriculture, mining, forestry) and the **tertiary sector** (wholesale, retail, services). A steel mill turns iron ore, coal, and limestone into beams; an assembly line turns stamped panels, engines, and electronics into a car. Manufacturing engineering plans the whole chain, from materials specification and product design to machine layout and work flow.

The word *manufacture* comes from Latin *manū* ("hand") and French *facture* ("making"); its earliest English use, in the mid-16th century, meant "made by human hands." Every later shift in manufacturing is a change in where energy and precision come from: at first the human hand and muscle, then animals and water, then steam, then electricity, then information.

## From stone to bronze to iron

Human ancestors manufactured stone tools long before *Homo sapiens* existed. The earliest direct evidence, found in Ethiopia's Great Rift Valley, dates to about 2.5 million years ago. The **Oldowan industry** of striking a stone core with a hammerstone to produce sharp choppers and scrapers dates to at least 2.3 million years ago. By roughly 300,000 years ago the Middle Paleolithic introduced the **prepared-core technique**, in which a knapper shaped one stone so a single blow detached a ready blade. By about 40,000 years ago the Upper Paleolithic added **pressure flaking**, pressing a bone or antler punch against an edge to pop off tiny flakes for controlled shaping impossible by hard percussion alone. Each step replaced brute force with deliberate preparation, raising output per unit of effort.

The leap from stone to metal came when pottery kilns grew hot enough to smelt ore. Copper smelting probably began this way, and smelting arsenic-rich ores gave an early work-hardenable alloy. True **tin bronze** arrived much later because tin deposits are globally rare. Bronze outperformed stone in strength, ductility, and castability, allowing hulls held by bronze nails to survive open-sea voyages where lashings failed. The **Iron Age** is defined by the spread of iron and steel tools and weapons, requiring hot-working and purpose-built furnaces. Where and when iron smelting began is still unknown, partly because nickel-bearing ores are hard to distinguish from meteoritic iron that was simply hot-worked.

## Medieval and early modern shifts

Papermaking, invented in 2nd-century China, reached the Middle East when Chinese papermakers were captured in the 8th century, then entered Europe with the Umayyad conquest of Hispania; a paper mill was running in Sicily by the 12th century. The **blast furnace** spread through 15th-century France because cannon foundries needed it (China had used one since the 4th century BC). The 1598 **stocking frame** showed early productivity scaling: one knitter's output jumped from 100 to 1000 knots per minute, a tenfold gain from mechanising a single hand motion.

## The Industrial Revolutions

The **First Industrial Revolution (1760 to the 1830s)** moved Europe and the United States from hand production to machines, powered mainly by steam and water. Textiles dominated because mechanised spinning in 1780s Britain produced thread far faster than hand spinners, and steam engines freed factories from riverside locations. After a recession in the late 1830s to early 1840s, growth resumed sharply after 1870 in the **Second Industrial Revolution**, adding cheap steel, mass production, assembly lines, electrical grids, and more capable machine tools. The practical incandescent bulb of the late 1870s enabled second and third shifts, roughly doubling the productive hours of the same factory.

Electrification was the most important enabler of modern mass production. The 1890s brought practical AC and DC motors, and 1900 to 1930 was the fastest period of factory electrification, driven by central power stations and falling electricity prices. Electric motors were flexible in placement and low-maintenance compared with the old line shafts and belts, and many factories saw output gains of about 30% from switching alone. Henry Ford's Ford Motor Company in the late 1910s to 1920s combined electric motors with sequential, chain-driven production, using special-purpose machine tools such as multi-spindle drill presses and a multi-head milling machine that cut 15 engine blocks at once. Model T production eventually used 32,000 machine tools, the capital density that defines a modern factory.

**Lean manufacturing (just-in-time)** was developed in Japan in the 1930s to shorten both production time and supplier or customer response time. It reached Toyota through British Motor Corporation's Victoria Park plant in Sydney in the 1950s. Western industry became aware of it in 1977, when two English-language articles appeared, one naming it the "Ohno system" after Taiichi Ohno. Implementation spread across US industry from 1980 onward. The core idea is to make only what the next station needs, when it needs it, so that inventory, defects, and delays surface immediately rather than hiding in stock.

## Strategy and control

Classical manufacturing strategy, associated with Wickham Skinner ("the father of manufacturing strategy"), evaluates performance on five dimensions: cost, quality, dependability, flexibility, and innovation. Skinner's "focus" or "trade-offs" argument holds that a business cannot excel on all five and must pick one or two competitive priorities; the opposite view became somewhat fashionable by 1991, though Skinner in 1992 reported renewed enthusiasm. Two main production-control strategies sit beneath strategy: **push manufacturing**, where output is driven by forecast and emphasises batch processing, and **pull manufacturing**, where each stage replenishes only what the next stage has actually consumed, the basis of lean.

## Industrial policy, costs, and scale

Manufacturing carries social and environmental costs such as hazardous waste, worker exposure to harmful chemicals, and pollution. Developed countries address them with labor and environmental laws, pollution taxes, and product-liability rules; these protections are often weaker in developing economies, encouraging relocation. In 2009 GE CEO Jeff Immelt called for raising manufacturing to 20% of US employment to counter excessive outsourcing; the UK's EEF has made the analogous call to rebalance away from financial services.

UNIDO reports that in 2023 China was the world's top manufacturer at 28.7% of global output, followed by the US, Germany, Japan, and India. UNIDO's **Competitive Industrial Performance (CIP) Index** combines gross output, high-tech capability, and world economic impact; Germany topped the 2020 index. In 2023 US manufacturing was 10.70% of national output and employed 8.41% of the workforce, with $2.5 trillion in output; Germany's output reached $844.93 billion, employing about 5.5 million people, or 20.8% of its workforce. Germany's economy still leans on manufacturing in a way the US and UK no longer do.
