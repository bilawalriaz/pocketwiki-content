# Belt (mechanical)

A belt is a loop of flexible material that links two or more rotating shafts to transmit power, change speed, or track relative motion. The belt wraps around pulleys mounted on the shafts. Different pulley diameters change the speed ratio, because angular speed is inversely proportional to pulley diameter. An open belt, with no twist, drives both pulleys the same way; a crossed belt reverses the driven shaft's direction. If the belt is twisted between pulleys, the shafts need not be parallel. A conveyor belt is the same loop principle, adapted to carry a load between two points instead of driving a shaft.

## How power gets through

Power transmission depends on friction between belt and pulley. Transmitted power equals the difference between tight-side tension T₁ and slack-side tension T₂, multiplied by belt velocity:

P = (T₁ − T₂) v

The tension ratio is fixed by friction and the wrap angle α, measured in radians of belt-pulley contact:

T₁ / T₂ = e^(μα)

where μ is the coefficient of friction. A larger wrap angle or higher friction raises this ratio, so the belt transmits more power before slipping.

Belt drives typically run at 90–98% efficiency (usually about 95%), quieter than chains or gears, and absorb shock loads that would damage motors or bearings. They tolerate misalignment, need no lubrication, and cost less than geared drives, especially when shafts are far apart. Trade-offs: they transmit less peak power than gears or chains, the speed ratio drifts slightly from slip and stretch, and they wear. Working temperatures span roughly −35 °C to 85 °C. Centre distance must be adjustable, or an idler pulley added, to take up stretch.

## A short history

A belt drive is recorded in a text by the Han Dynasty scholar Yang Xiong in 15 BC, winding silk onto quilling bobbins. The belt drive became essential to the spinning wheel and, by the 1st century AD, was applied to hydraulic bellows. The endless rubber V-belt was developed in 1917 by Charles C. Gates of the Gates Rubber Company; the multiple-V arrangement was patented by Walter Geist of Allis-Chalmers in 1925 (US patent 1,662,511) and sold as "Texrope".

## Belt types

**Flat belts**, traditionally leather, later rubber or fabric, deliver high power at high speed (up to 373 kW at 51 m/s) but only over wide, long layouts. Pulleys were given a slightly crowned (convex) face so the belt would self-centre. Joining methods include leather thonging, steel comb fasteners, gluing, and welding. A half-twist Möbius assembly equalises wear on both sides.

**Rope drives**, developed by mid-19th-century British millwrights, used cotton, hemp, manila, or flax ropes in multi-groove pulleys and could carry power over long distances with intermediate support sheaves.

**Round belts** have a circular cross-section running in a 60° V-groove. Wedging raises friction, but the cross-section limits them to low-torque drives such as sewing machines.

**V-belts** are trapezoidal in cross-section and seat in a matching pulley groove. The wedge locks the belt in place and increases grip as load rises, so a V-belt transmits more torque than a flat belt of the same width and tension. Optimal speed is 1,000–7,000 ft/min (300–2,130 m/min). For high power, several V-belts run side by side in a multi-groove sheave.

**Multi-groove (V-ribbed) belts** carry several V-ribs side by side. They are thinner and more flexible than single V-belts, run cooler, and can be bent by external idlers into a serpentine path that drives many accessories from one belt, as in modern car engines. A "740K6" automotive belt is 74 inches long with 6 ribs at the K-series pitch.

**Timing (toothed) belts** have teeth that mesh with a toothed pulley, eliminating slip and giving a fixed speed ratio. They handle up to about 200 hp (150 kW) at 16,000 ft/min, need the least tension of any belt, and drive camshafts, printers, and stepper motors. Helical-offset-tooth designs engage progressively and run quieter.

**Ribbed belts** spread tension evenly across the pulley and reach about 600 kW. **Film belts**, very thin plastic strips, handle low-power (under 10 W), high-speed jobs such as printers and tape recorders at up to 98% efficiency. **Spring belts**, helical steel coils, appear on toy steam engines and tolerate misalignment better than rubber, at the cost of lower friction.

## Selection and wear

For high speed ratios or short centre distances, the belt may wrap less than 180° around a pulley, reducing traction; published power ratings are then adjusted upward before selection. Fatigue, rather than abrasion, ends most belt life, accelerated by high tension, slip, heat, and shock loads. A loose or worn belt can be retensioned or treated with a belt dressing, a sticky or solvent-like coating that boosts friction, but replacement is eventually unavoidable. Selection balances power, speed, shaft distance, environment, and shock load against the smallest pulley diameter that keeps outer-fibre bending stress within the belt's limit, because undersized pulleys sharply shorten life.

In 19th- and early 20th-century factories, flat leather belts driven by overhead line shafts powered groups of machines from a single steam or water engine. Today most factory machines carry their own electric motors, and flat belts have largely given way to V-belts and timing belts.
