# Cable transport

Cable transport is a family of transport systems that move people or goods along, or suspended from, cables. The cable can be powered or passive, and loads are moved by pulling, sliding, or by an onboard drive. Pulleys and the balancing of opposing loads are recurring design elements, and cable systems are common where large elevation changes make roads or rail impractical.

## Main variants

Aerial systems suspend cars from cables strung between towers. The group includes aerial tramways (two-station back-and-forth cabins), chairlifts and gondola lifts (continuous loops of many carriers), ski tows and surface lifts, and zip lines. Cable railways run cars on rails using a haulage cable, typically up steep ground-level gradients; the main types are cable cars (on-street grip systems) and funiculars (rail cars permanently attached to the cable). Other cable-hauled modes include cable ferries, which use a cable to hold a boat against a river current while crossing, and elevators.

## How gravity balance works

A defining engineering principle is load balancing. On a funicular or a two-cabin aerial tramway, a heavy descending car helps pull a lighter ascending one, with motors or brakes only making up the difference. Gravity inclines, the earliest cable railways, worked the same way: loaded wagons pulled empties up a single rope around a braked drum. This balance is why cable systems are efficient in mountainous terrain, where one car is always going downhill.

## History

Rope-drawn transport is illustrated in South China around 250 BC. The first recorded mechanical ropeway is attributed to Fausto Veranzio in 1616; the first operational system is credited to Adam Wybe in 1644. Wire rope took over during the nineteenth century, with disputed early use by Peter Cooper in Baltimore around 1832 and a documented iron-ore tramway at Ringwood, New Jersey in 1853. The first funicular opened in Lyon in 1862, and Andrew Hallidie's Clay Street Hill Railroad in San Francisco made the on-street cable car commercially successful using a steel cable and a hand-operated grip. By 1890 the United States had more than 500 miles of cable track carrying over a million passengers a year, before electric trams displaced most urban cable lines.

For skiing, Robert Winterhalder built the first surface lift in 1908 in Germany. The J-bar and T-bar were invented in 1934 and first installed in Davos. The first chairlift was developed in 1936 for Sun Valley, Idaho, adapting a cable hoist originally designed for loading bananas.

## Urban cable cars

Cities have begun using aerial cable cars as public transport, starting with Medellín's Metrocable around 2004. By 2023, 33 aerial cable car transit lines operated across Latin America and the Caribbean, most opened after 2010. La Paz's network is the world's longest and highest urban cable car system; one of its lines is about 16 km long and carries up to 65,000 people per day at roughly $0.42 per ride. Typical urban systems run 10–20 km/h and move up to 2,000 passengers per hour in each direction.

Cited advantages are lower cost and faster construction than rail, smaller ground footprint, low noise, and lower energy use per passenger. Cable lines can cross obstacles like rivers, motorways, and steep hillsides, and Medellín's Metrocable is reported to have helped lower crime by connecting hillside neighbourhoods to the city centre. Disadvantages include sensitivity to weather and modest speeds.

## Safety and accidents

A TÜV SÜD-cited study reports about 25 deaths per 100 million hours of travel from car accidents, 16 from plane accidents, and 2 from cable car accidents, with most cable fatalities linked to passenger behaviour rather than system failure. The worst aerial lift disaster on record is the 1976 Cavalese, Italy cable car crash, which killed 43 people when a car fell about 200 m. A US Marine aircraft cut a cable at Cavalese in 1998, killing 20. The 2000 Kaprun funicular tunnel fire in Austria killed 155 people and remains one of the deadliest cable transport accidents.
