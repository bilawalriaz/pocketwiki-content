# Carbon steel

Carbon steel is steel whose only deliberate alloying element is carbon, with the other elements present only as residuals. The American Iron and Steel Institute (AISI) defines the boundary: a steel counts as carbon steel (called non-alloy steel in Europe) as long as no minimum is specified for chromium, cobalt, molybdenum, nickel, niobium, titanium, tungsten, vanadium, or zirconium, the minimum for copper does not exceed 0.40%, and the maxima for manganese (1.65%), silicon (0.60%), and copper (0.60%) are not exceeded. Exceed those limits and the material is classed as alloy steel.

Carbon content runs from about 0.05% to 2.1% by weight. That single percentage controls most of the behavior: hardness, strength, ductility, weldability, melting point, and the response to heat treatment. As carbon rises, steel can be hardened and strengthened by heat treatment, but ductility and weldability fall, and the melting point drops.

## Classes by carbon content

The AISI/SAE system splits carbon steel into four classes:

| Class | Carbon (% by weight) | Character | Typical uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low-carbon | 0.05–0.15 | Soft, ductile, easily welded and machined; low tensile strength | Car body panels, pipes, construction, food cans, structural shapes |
| Medium-carbon | ~0.3–0.5 | Balances ductility and strength; good wear resistance; responds to heat treatment | Forgings, large machine parts, automotive components |
| High-carbon | ~0.6–1.0 | Very strong and hard, but brittle; poor weldability | Springs, cutting tools, high-strength wires, chisels, axes |
| Ultra-high-carbon | ~1.25–2.0 | Tempered to extreme hardness; very low ductility; expensive | Knives, punches, axles, specialty tools |

Mild steel is the most common form of steel, cheap and workable, with carbon in the broader range of about 0.05–0.30%. Its density is 7.85 g/cm³ and its Young's modulus is 200 GPa. A peculiarity of low-carbon steel is yield-point runout: the material shows two yield points, and stressing it between them produces visible Lüder bands on the surface. Surface hardness can be raised by carburizing.

The European standard EN 10020 divides non-alloy steels into quality steels (broader tolerances, general structural use; examples are S235, S275, S355) and special steels (tighter composition control, intended for heat treatment to precise properties; examples are steels for gears and machine parts).

## The role of other elements

Manganese is routinely added to improve hardenability, the depth to which steel hardens on quenching, up to 1.65% without crossing into alloy-steel status. Trace sulfur makes steel red-short, brittle and crumbly at hot-working temperatures; low-alloy structural grades such as A36 carry about 0.05% sulfur and melt near 1,426–1,538 °C. When substantial chromium, nickel, molybdenum, vanadium, or silicon is added to push strength, toughness, or high-temperature performance further, the material becomes an alloy steel; the hardness-versus-malleability trade-off remains but is much milder.

Carbon steel has no built-in corrosion resistance. In moist or salty environments it rusts unless coated (paint, varnish, plating) or replaced with a stainless, chromium-bearing alloy.

## Heat treatment

All heat treatments of steel trade ductility for strength; Young's modulus is essentially unchanged, and electrical and thermal conductivity shift only slightly. The processes exploit the iron–carbon phase diagram. Iron dissolves much more carbon in the face-centered cubic austenite phase than in the body-centered cubic ferrite phase, so treatments start by heating into the austenite region and then control cooling to place the carbon.

The eutectoid point sits at about 0.77% carbon and 727 °C. Steels with less carbon are hypoeutectoid; those with more are hypereutectoid. The cooling rate through 727 °C sets the microstructure:

- Slow cooling lets carbon diffuse out of austenite, forming layers of iron carbide (cementite) interleaved with ferrite in a lamellar structure called pearlite. Slower cooling produces coarser pearlite, which is softer.
- Fast cooling (quenching) traps carbon inside the iron lattice, distorting the cubic cell into a body-centered tetragonal (BCT) structure called martensite. Martensite is extremely hard but brittle, and the internal stresses can crack the surface. Quenched steel is roughly three times harder than normalized steel of the same composition, and about four times harder at higher carbon.

Common treatments:

- Spheroidizing: heat to about 700 °C for over 30 hours, allowing cementite to coalesce into spheroids in a ferrite matrix. This is the softest, most ductile state, used so high-carbon steels can be machined or shaped.
- Full annealing: heat just above the austenite region (A₃ for hypoeutectoid, A₁ for hypereutectoid) and cool very slowly, about 20 °C per hour, usually by turning the furnace off. Produces coarse pearlite, soft and stress-free, the standard pre-machining condition. Only spheroidized steel is softer.
- Process annealing: heat cold-worked low-carbon steel to 550–650 °C for about an hour to relieve internal stress.
- Normalizing: heat into the austenite region and cool in still air. Refines grain size, raises strength above annealed levels, and homogenizes the structure.
- Quenching: austenitize steel with at least ~0.4% carbon, then rapidly cool in water, brine, or oil to form martensite. The temperature at which martensite starts to form drops as carbon content rises.
- Martempering (marquenching): quench into a salt bath just above the martensite-start temperature, hold until the part is uniformly cool, then air-cool. Reduces cracking and distortion, and allows some bainite to form from retained austenite.
- Austempering: a similar interrupted quench held at 205–540 °C, producing bainite, an acicular (needle-like) microstructure that is slightly weaker than martensite but tougher, more ductile, and less distorted. Practical only on thin sections and limited batch sizes because of the salt bath.
- Tempering: reheat quenched steel to a controlled temperature below 727 °C. Tiny amounts of spheroidite form, partially relieving stress and trading some hardness back for useful ductility. Final properties are set precisely by the chosen time and temperature.

## Case hardening

Carbon steel has low hardenability, so thick sections cannot be through-hardened. The shallow hardening of carbon steel is useful when only the surface needs to be wear-resistant: a hard outer case of martensite can be produced on a tough, shock-absorbing ductile core.
