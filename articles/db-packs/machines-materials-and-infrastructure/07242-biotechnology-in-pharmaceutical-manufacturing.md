# Biotechnology in pharmaceutical manufacturing

Biotechnology in pharmaceutical manufacturing is the use of biological systems, living organisms, or their components to develop and produce medicines. The central technique is recombinant DNA, in which a gene coding for a useful human protein is inserted into a host cell, which then manufactures that protein. The host is usually a microbe such as the bacterium *Escherichia coli* or yeast, or, for proteins that need more elaborate processing, mammalian cells such as Chinese hamster ovary cells. A plasmid, a small ring of DNA that replicates inside a host cell, is the standard vehicle for moving a gene into bacteria.

## Recombinant DNA in practice

Among the earliest uses of biotechnology in pharmaceutical manufacturing was recombinant DNA to make human insulin, performed at Genentech in 1978. Before that, insulin was extracted from the pancreases of cattle and pigs. Animal insulin works, but its molecular structure differs slightly from human insulin, and the differences can trigger allergic reactions. To make human insulin, Genentech built artificial genes for the A and B protein chains of insulin, inserted them into plasmids next to genes activated by lactose, and introduced the plasmids into *E. coli*. When the bacteria were exposed to lactose, each cell produced roughly 100,000 molecules of one chain; the two chains were then combined into complete insulin.

A similar strategy solved a different shortage for human growth hormone. Previously, the hormone had to be extracted from the pituitary glands of human cadavers, because animal growth hormones have no therapeutic value in humans, and a single year's supply required up to fifty glands, so production was severely limited. In 1979, Genentech produced the hormone in *E. coli*. Researchers started with mRNA from human pituitary glands and used reverse transcription, copying RNA back into DNA, to generate complementary DNA (cDNA). A fully synthetic gene for the long growth-hormone protein would have been prohibitively laborious, so they cut the cDNA with HaeIII, a restriction enzyme (a protein that cuts DNA at a specific short sequence), to isolate a 551 base-pair fragment covering amino acids 24–191, then attached a short synthetic DNA "adaptor" carrying the ATG start codon and the codons for amino acids 1–23. The combined "synthetic-natural hybrid" gene was inserted into a plasmid and put into *E. coli*, which then produced the hormone. Inserting the cDNA directly, without this hybrid approach, would have forced bacteria to translate regions of the gene that human cells normally ignore, yielding a faulty pre-hormone with 26 extra amino acids.

## Blood clotting factors and HIV

Recombinant methods also changed the safety of treatments for hemophilia. Before the mid-1980s, human blood clotting factors came from donated blood that was inadequately screened for HIV. Between 1979 and 1984, roughly 60 to 80 percent of hemophilia patients who received Factor VIII concentrates became HIV-positive, and by May 1988 more than 659 hemophilia patients had developed AIDS.

Factor IX became the first clotting factor made in meaningful quantities by recombinant DNA, in 1986, using transgenic Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells. Lacking a map of the human genome, researchers first microsequenced the purified protein to learn a stretch of its amino acids, then built a short synthetic DNA probe matching the mRNA. That probe located the gene in a human liver DNA library, and the same probe then found the rest of the gene in a human X-chromosome library. The complete 35 kilobase gene was assembled from five overlapping recombinant phage. To make the cells actually use the gene, the researchers co-transfected a plasmid carrying a methotrexate-resistance gene. Transfection, the insertion of DNA into a eukaryotic cell, usually does not integrate the new DNA into the host genome, so growing the cells in stronger and stronger methotrexate selected for the rare cells that had stably integrated the Factor IX gene into their own chromosomes. The resulting recombinant Factor IX had a specific activity of 75 units/mg, about half that of plasma-derived Factor IX at 150 units/mg, but it avoided the HIV risk. In 1992, the FDA approved recombinant Factor VIII, the first clotting factor produced this way to reach patients.

## Other hosts and products

Recombinant techniques extend beyond microbes and cell cultures. Transgenic pigs have been engineered to produce human hemoglobin, which can be purified and used as a blood substitute, even though whole pig blood cannot be transfused into people. Bristol-Myers Squibb is reported to manufacture the cancer drug paclitaxel (Taxol) using *Penicillium raistrickii* and plant cell fermentation, though the source flags this as uncited. Transgenic yeast now produce artemisinin, a key antimalarial, along with several insulin analogs.

These examples share a common logic: a useful protein is scarce, dangerous to extract, or hard to obtain in pure form from its original source, so the gene that codes for it is moved into a host that can be grown cheaply and indefinitely, replacing extraction with fermentation or cell culture.
