# Transistor radio

A transistor radio is a small portable radio receiver built around transistors, the solid-state semiconductor devices invented at Bell Laboratories on 23 December 1947 by William Shockley, Walter Brattain, and John Bardeen. A transistor amplifies a signal and acts as an electronic switch. Replacing vacuum tubes with transistors made radios dramatically smaller, lighter, cheaper to run, and more rugged, because transistors drew far less current, needed no warm-up, and were not fragile glass enclosures.

## Why transistors mattered

Before transistors, portable radios used vacuum tubes. A typical 1950s tube portable was the size and weight of a lunchbox, needed two batteries (a low-voltage "A" battery to heat the tube filaments and a 45 to 90 volt "B" battery for the signal circuits), and the tubes were inefficient, short-lived, and easily broken. As transistor manufacturing matured, the radios shrank until they fit in a shirt pocket, weighed half a pound or less, and ran on a single small battery.

## Key chronology

- 1947: Bell Labs demonstrates the first transistor (23 December).
- 1952: RCA builds a prototype transistor radio.
- 1953: Intermetall demonstrates a working all-transistor radio at the Düsseldorf Radio Fair, using four hand-made germanium point-contact transistors; never commercially produced.
- 1954: Texas Instruments (Dallas) and Industrial Development Engineering Associates (Indianapolis) release the Regency TR-1 in October, the first commercial transistor radio, on sale from November. Four transistors; poor sound; about 150,000 sold at $49.95.
- 1955: Raytheon introduces the 8-TP-1, with eight transistors and much better sound. Chrysler and Philco offer the Mopar 914HR, the first all-transistor car radio, as a $150 option on 1956 models. Sony (then Tokyo Telecommunications Engineering Corporation) sells the TR-55 in Japan.
- 1957: Sony TR-63 reaches the United States in December, slightly smaller than the TR-1 and the first radio to popularise the now-standard 9 V battery. About 100,000 imported in 1957; eventually seven million sold worldwide.
- Late 1950s onward: Japanese firms (Sony, Toshiba, Sharp) dominate on price and style; by 1964 no US-made transistor radio remained.

Sony did not invent the transistor radio. Intermetall demonstrated a working all-transistor set in August 1953, Texas Instruments showed an all-transistor AM radio in May 1954, and RCA had a prototype in 1952, but none of these reached the market before Regency.

## The 9 volt battery

Early transistor radios needed a 22.5 V brick battery. The TR-63 needed a smaller cell to fit its case, and Sony chose the compact 9 V flat battery. This format, the small rectangular 9 V cell still common today, became the standard for handheld transistor radios and remained so for decades.

## Cultural and economic impact

The pocket-sized radio put music into the listener's hand anywhere, breaking the family living-room radio as the centre of home audio. Three social forces amplified its reach: the post-war baby boom, widespread disposable income, and the rise of rock 'n' roll. Cheap battery-powered radios also brought regular broadcasting to rural and impoverished areas for the first time; music broadcast from New Orleans and received in Jamaica inspired the development of ska and reggae. Inexpensive Japanese manufacturing, with labor costs far below US levels, drove prices down from $39.95 for the TR-63 in 1957 to as low as $15 by 1962, forcing American makers to match or exit. Around 1980, transistor radios began to be displaced by the boombox, the Sony Walkman, portable CD players, MP3 players, and eventually smartphones, most of which still contain an FM receiver.

By the mid-1960s the transistor radio was the most popular electronic communication device in the world, and billions had been sold worldwide by about 2012.
