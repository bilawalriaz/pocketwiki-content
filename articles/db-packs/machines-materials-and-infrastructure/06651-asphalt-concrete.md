# Asphalt concrete

Asphalt concrete is a composite paving material: mineral aggregate (crushed stone, gravel, sand) bound by bitumen, a sticky, viscoelastic petroleum derivative also called asphalt cement. The mix is laid in layers and compacted while workable; as it cools, the bitumen hardens into a continuous matrix that locks the aggregate in place. Engineers define "concrete" as any mineral-aggregate-plus-binder composite, so asphalt concrete sits beside Portland cement concrete in the concrete family. In North America the surface is called asphalt, blacktop, or pavement; in the UK and Ireland the older terms tarmac, bitmac, and bitumen macadam remain common. The word "asphalt" is ambiguous, meaning both the binder and the finished pavement.

The material's working property is temperature-dependent viscosity. Cold bitumen is nearly solid; warmed, it flows and wets aggregate; cooled, it bonds them. This lets crews mix, haul, lay, and compact the material as a fluid, then collect a load-bearing surface within hours. The same behavior also explains why asphalt softens in summer heat and cracks in winter cold.

## History

Natural asphalt was used for waterproofing in antiquity across Mesopotamia, Egypt, Babylon, Greece, and Rome, including the c. 625 BC Procession Street of Nabopolassar in Babylon. Modern road paving began in 19th-century France and England: compressed natural-rock asphalt from the 1820s, then tar-bound macadam. The decisive shift came in 1902–1903, when Edgar Purnell Hooley patented a steam-heated process for mixing hot petroleum bitumen with aggregate and laying it as a continuous surface, founding a company whose product name "tarmac" became a generic word for any paved area, even those not built with tarmacadam.

## Mixture types

Most modern asphalt is hot-mix asphalt (HMA). Aggregate is dried and the bitumen heated to roughly 150 °C (300 °F) for virgin binder or 170 °C (340 °F) for polymer-modified binder, with the asphalt cement around 93 °C (200 °F), so the mix pours and compacts. Paving is seasonal in cold climates because a frozen base chills the mix before it reaches density. HMA is standard for highways, racetracks, and airfields, and is also used as an impermeable liner for landfills and reservoirs.

Warm-mix asphalt (WMA) adds zeolites, waxes, emulsions, or small amounts of water to lower mixing and placement temperatures, cutting fuel use and emissions while enabling longer hauls and cooler-weather paving. By 2012 about 25% of US asphalt production was warm mix.

Cold-mix asphalt emulsifies the bitumen in water so it can be mixed and placed without heat; the emulsion breaks as the water evaporates, and the cured product approaches HMA performance. It is mainly used for patching and low-traffic roads. Cut-back asphalt, the older solvent-thinned equivalent, has been largely phased out because the evaporating petroleum fractions release volatile organic compounds.

Two specialty variants serve heavy duty. Mastic (sheet) asphalt is cooked from hard, partly oxidized blown bitumen and laid in dense layers about 20–30 mm thick for roads, around 10 mm for flooring and roofing. High-modulus asphalt concrete (French: enrobé à module élevé, EME) uses a very hard 10/20 penetration bitumen at about 6% by weight of aggregate plus 8–10% mineral powder, reaching an elastic modulus near 13,000 MPa and allowing the base course to be thinned by up to 25%.

Polymers and antistripping agents are routinely blended in to stiffen the binder, improve flexibility, or keep bitumen bonded to wet aggregate. Stone-matrix asphalt is a tough wearing course; porous asphalt is a permeable surface that drains stormwater through the pavement.

## Performance and failure modes

Asphalt is cheaper than Portland cement concrete and easier to patch, which is why most local roads and many highways use it. It is also quieter under traffic because the slightly viscous surface absorbs tire-noise energy that concrete reflects as sound.

Asphalt fails in ways concrete does not. Heat softens the binder so heavy axles press ruts into it. Sunlight and oxidation age the bitumen into a stiffer, brittle phase that then cracks under winter contraction. Water penetrating the pavement softens the subbase, and in freezing climates it expands as ice, prying cracks open. In spring, thaw proceeds from the top down, so meltwater is trapped between still-frozen soil and the impermeable asphalt; the saturated subgrade yields under traffic, forming potholes. Some jurisdictions impose frost laws that reduce legal truck weights during this season. Pavement damage scales roughly with axle load raised to the fourth power, so doubling an axle's weight multiplies road damage by about 16.

Common distress modes include crocodile (fatigue) cracking from repeated flexing, raveling as binder-aggregate bonds fail, bleeding when excess bitumen rises, rutting from plastic flow, and shoving from braking or turning loads.

## Design, maintenance, and repair

Design starts with traffic estimates, especially heavy trucks, and subsoil testing. Pavement and subbase thickness are calculated to spread wheel loads, sometimes with geogrids for reinforcement; ditches, storm drains, and underdrains keep water out of the subbase. Sealcoating sheds water and petroleum products, and sealing small cracks prevents frost weathering. As damage accumulates, repairs step from chip seals to thin overlays, multi-course overlays, milling plus overlay, in-place recycling, and full-depth reconstruction. Because preventive maintenance costs far less than later reconstruction, most agencies run pavement management systems that rank repairs by network condition rather than worst-first.

## Recycling

Asphalt concrete is the most recycled material in the United States by weight, mostly as reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) milled from old roads, with reclaimed asphalt shingles (RAS) as a second stream. In 2019 US mixes averaged 21.1% RAP and 0.2% RAS, and plants accepted about 97.0 million tons of RAP and 1.1 million tons of RAS.

The bitumen in aged binder, about 5–6% of a typical mix, hardens through oxidation, evaporation of light fractions, and physical stiffening, which makes recycled mixes stiffer and more prone to fatigue cracking. The fix is soft virgin binder (effective at low recycled content) or chemical rejuvenators, sometimes derived from waste engine oil, vegetable oil, or grease, that restore the aged binder. Conventional plant mixing tolerates up to about 50% RAP without rejuvenators; with optimal rejuvenator dosing, mixes of 100% recycled binder and aggregate can meet conventional performance. Crumb rubber from recycled tires improves fatigue resistance, and California mandates its use; steel slag, blast furnace slag, and cellulose fibers are also reused at scale, while research has tested waste plastics, glass, brick, ceramic, quarry fines, and discarded face masks as partial aggregate or binder replacements. In-place methods such as hot in-place recycling, cold in-place recycling, rubblizing, and full-depth reclamation let crews reclaim, remix, and repave on the same alignment without hauling material to a plant.
