# Chemical engineer

A chemical engineer is a professional who combines knowledge of chemistry and other basic sciences with engineering skills to convert raw materials into products, principally by designing, constructing, and operating industrial plants. The role extends beyond the chemical industry into the wider process industries, where matter undergoes a change of state or composition, and into materials, energy, and biomedical engineering.

## What the work involves

Chemical engineering historically split into two complementary areas: chemical reaction engineering, which designs reactors where raw materials are transformed, and separation processes, which purify the products. Modern practice has expanded well beyond these. Chemical engineers now work in four broad areas:

- **Process engineering**, the traditional core, covers designing, manufacturing, and operating plants and machinery for industrial chemical and related processes, sometimes called chemical process engineering.
- **Chemical product engineering** develops or adapts substances for products such as foods, cosmetics, cleaners, and pharmaceutical ingredients.
- **New technology and adjacent fields** include fuel cells, hydrogen power, and nanotechnology, as well as fields derived from chemical engineering such as materials science, polymer engineering, and biomedical engineering.
- **Biology and biomedical projects** include studying biopolymers such as proteins, mapping the human genome, and producing bio-compatible materials, gels, pharmaceuticals, and films for optoelectronic devices.

Across all of these, a chemical engineer manages complex physical and chemical processes at manufacturing scale, whether the output is a commodity chemical, an aerospace composite, or a drug.

## A short history

The title is contested. A 1951 presidential address to the Institution of Chemical Engineers proposed Edward Charles Howard (1774–1816) as the first chemical engineer of any eminence, while others credit Johann Rudolf Glauber (1604–1670) for developing processes to manufacture the major industrial acids. The Oxford English Dictionary records the noun "chemical engineer" in 1838, and in 1839 the term appeared in print, where context suggests a mechanical engineer working in the chemical industry.

A defining 1880 statement by George E. Davis described a chemical engineer as someone who "possesses chemical and mechanical knowledge, and who applies that knowledge to the utilisation, on a manufacturing scale, of chemical action." Davis proposed a Society of Chemical Engineers; what emerged in 1882 was the Society of Chemical Industry, with about 15 of 300 initial members calling themselves chemical engineers. A Chemical Engineering Group within the Society attracted roughly 400 members by 1918. The American Institute of Chemical Engineers (AIChE) was established in 1908, and in 1924 the UK Institution of Chemical Engineers adopted a definition still recognisable today: a professional experienced in the design, construction, and operation of plant and works in which matter undergoes a change of state and composition.

## Employment, pay, and demographics

The AIChE salary survey reported a US median annual salary of about $127,000 in 2015, falling to $124,000 in 2017, a drop attributed in part to a change of survey firm. The 2015 figures ranged from $70,450 for engineers with under three years of experience to $156,000 for those with more than 40 years. The UK's IChemE 2016 Salary Survey reported a median of about £57,000, with a graduate starting salary averaging £28,350. By 2023 figures, Bayes Business School graduates led UK universities with an average of £51,921 within five years of graduation, followed by Oxford at £49,086 and Warwick at £47,446.

Chemical engineering is male-dominated, but less so than most engineering fields. In the US, women were about 35% of chemical engineering students as of 2024, compared with roughly 20% across engineering overall. The UK started from a lower base: in 2014, 25% of all UK students beginning degrees were women versus 15% in engineering. A 2015 AIChE survey found 18.8% of US graduate respondents were women. Women in Chemical Engineering (WIC), a sub-group of AIChE, works to improve representation, and IUPAC has given an annual Distinguished Women in Chemistry or Chemical Engineering award since 2011.

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics projected 3% growth for chemical engineers between 2024 and 2034.
