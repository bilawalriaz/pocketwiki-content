# Aqueduct (water supply)

An aqueduct is any structure that moves water from a source to a point of use. The term comes from Latin *aqua* (water) and *ductus* (led). In everyday use it often means the stone arch-bridge that carries a water channel, but in engineering it covers ditches, canals, tunnels, and pipes. Across history aqueducts have done two jobs: irrigating crops in dry regions and supplying drinking water to large cities.

## Gravity and gradient

Every aqueduct depends on gravity: the destination must sit lower than the source. The channel therefore needs a steady downward slope, steep enough to keep water moving but gentle enough to avoid erosion. Roman channels typically used a gradient near 1 in 4800, about 20 centimetres per kilometre. A higher gradient lets a smaller channel carry the same flow, but it stresses the structure; a lower gradient needs a larger channel to deliver the same water.

The water also has to be contained. Early channels were cut straight into earth; later builders lined them with stone, then concrete, and modern projects use concrete, polymers, or impermeable soil, since unlined channels lose large fractions of their flow to seepage.

## Ancient systems

Aqueducts long predate Rome. The Minoans on Crete used irrigation channels around 2000 BC. By the 7th century BC the Assyrians had built an 80 km limestone aqueduct, including a 10 m high section crossing a 300 m valley, to supply Nineveh. In India, channels near Hampi once ran about 24 km to feed royal baths. In Peru, the Nazca *puquios*, some possibly built around AD 540–552 in response to drought, still carry water. Costa Rica's Guayabo site holds stone aqueducts of unknown origin that still function.

The most influential ancient design is the qanat, developed in Persia around 3000 years ago and copied as Oman's *falaj* system. A qanat taps an underground water source by digging a gently sloping tunnel from an upland aquifer down to the surface, with vertical shafts at intervals for excavation and maintenance. Because the water never sees sunlight, almost none is lost to evaporation, which makes qanats well suited to hot, dry climates. Three Omani variants exist: *Daudi* (pure underground tunnels), *Ghaili* (fed by a dam), and *Aini* (fed by a spring).

Greek engineers cut the Tunnel of Eupalinos on Samos between 538 and 522 BC, an underground aqueduct that served Pythagoreion for about a thousand years. In the Levant, engineers progressed from open earth ditches, as at Qumran, to stone channels sealed with waterproof plaster. From the Hellenistic period onward they used inverted siphons, closed pipes that exploited water's tendency to find its original level, to push supplies across deep valleys and up to hilltop fortresses. Sri Lanka's Yoda Ela, built in the 5th century AD, ran 87 km between two reservoirs with a gradient of only 10–20 cm per kilometre, a precision the builders could not repeat after the civilisation fell in the 13th century.

## Roman aqueducts

Roman aqueducts set a standard unmatched for more than a thousand years. The city of Rome alone had over 415 km of them, supplying public baths and drinking water. The Romans perfected the multi-arch stone bridge to carry water across valleys, which is why "aqueduct" still conjures that image, and they built the system so reliably that some Roman channels still supply Rome. The Pont du Gard in Gaul carried water to Nimes while its lower tiers carried a road, an example of an aqueduct bridge doing two jobs at once.

## Modern aqueducts

Modern systems follow the same gravity principle at a larger scale, and increasingly use pipelines when water must cross hills or where evaporation, freezing, or pollution make open channels poor choices. The United States hosts some of the largest: the 190 km Catskill Aqueduct to New York City, the 389 km Colorado River Aqueduct feeding Los Angeles, the 1,129 km California Aqueduct from the Sacramento–San Joaquin Delta to Lake Perris, and the 336 km Central Arizona Project, the country's most expensive, carrying water from Parker to Phoenix and Tucson. Spain's Tagus–Segura system, opened in 1979, moves water 286 km. China's South–North Water Transfer Project links the Yangtze basin to Beijing, partly reusing the Grand Canal.

Where an old aqueduct cannot be shut down during construction, engineers build a replacement alongside it and switch over only when complete. As cities grow and water grows scarcer, the choice between open channels, which are cheaper and easier to maintain, and sealed pipes, which lose less water and resist contamination, increasingly favours pipes, so today's "aqueduct" is as likely to be a buried pipeline as a line of stone arches.
