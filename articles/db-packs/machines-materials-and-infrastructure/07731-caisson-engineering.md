# Caisson (engineering)

A caisson is a watertight retaining structure that lets builders work in a dry environment below water level. The name comes from the French for "box," in turn from the Italian *cassone*. Caissons are used for bridge piers, concrete dams, ship repair, and any foundation that must be built through water or waterlogged soil. The core idea: sink a sealed enclosure into place, then pump the water out so workers can reach the foundation.

The principle is ancient: Roman and medieval engineers used it for harbor works. Modern pneumatic caisson technology, in which compressed air holds back the water, was developed in the 19th century. The Royal Albert Bridge (1859), the Eads Bridge (1874), and the Brooklyn Bridge (1883) are prominent early examples.

## Placement

A caisson is brought down through soft mud until it meets a firm layer. Bedrock is preferred, but a stable hard mud works when bedrock is too deep. If the soil at the bottom still cannot support the load, friction piles — piles that grip the surrounding earth along their length — can be driven first and tied together by a foundation pad on which the pier is built.

Four main types exist. A **box caisson** is a prefabricated box with sides and a bottom, open at the top. It is set on a prepared base and filled with ballast to become part of the structure, such as a bridge pier foundation. Because hollow concrete is less dense than water, box caissons must be anchored until ballasted, sometimes with elaborate systems in tidal zones.

An **open caisson** is like a box but has no bottom face. It suits soft clays and is also used for deep manholes, pump stations, and pits for microtunnelling and pipe jacking. It sinks under its own weight, or with concrete, water, or hydraulic jacks pressing down. The leading edge, called the cutting shoe, is sloped outward in steel to bite vertically and is usually wider than the caisson walls to reduce friction. Pressurised bentonite slurry, a clay that swells when wet, is sometimes injected at the edge to fill voids and steady the descent. A clamshell bucket on a crane excavates from above. If the soil at the bottom is too weak, a reinforced concrete plug is poured underwater by tremie placement — pouring through a pipe whose lower end stays buried in the fresh concrete so the mix never falls through water. Once the caisson is drained, that plug acts as a pile cap.

A **monolithic caisson** is a very large open caisson, often used in quay walls where resistance to ship impact is needed.

## Working under pressure

A **pneumatic caisson** is a bottomless box sealed at the top and pressurised with compressed air to keep water and mud out at depth. Workers, called sandhogs, enter and leave through an airlock. They shovel loosened mud and rock (called muck) into a water-filled pit connected to the surface by a muck tube. A crane lifts the muck out with a clamshell bucket. The water column in the tube balances the air pressure, with excess air bubbling up the same tube. Airflow must stay constant: it refreshes the workers' breathing air and prevents mud or water from surging in at the base. When the caisson reaches bedrock, the sandhogs exit through the airlock and the chamber is filled with concrete to form a solid pier.

The drawback is that workers breathe air at higher than normal pressure, which dissolves extra nitrogen into their blood and tissues. They must decompress slowly so the dissolved gas releases without forming bubbles. Decompressing too fast causes decompression sickness, a condition first identified in caisson workers and originally called "caisson disease." Construction of the Brooklyn Bridge's pressurised caissons killed or permanently injured many workers this way. Other pressure-related risks include barotrauma, injury from pressure imbalance, to the ears, sinuses, and lungs, and dysbaric osteonecrosis, long-term bone damage from repeated pressure exposure.

## Other applications

During the 1944 Allied invasion of Normandy, large caissons codenamed Phoenix were an integral part of the Mulberry harbours, the artificial ports floated across the English Channel. The word "caisson" is also used for the water-filled trough that carries boats in canal locks and lifts, for reinforced concrete piers cast in cylindrical formwork below ground, and for airtight housings around ventilation filters in facilities that handle hazardous materials.
