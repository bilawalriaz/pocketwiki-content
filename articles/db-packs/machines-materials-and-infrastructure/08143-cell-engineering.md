# Cell engineering

Cell engineering is the purposeful addition, deletion, or modification of genetic sequences in living cells to achieve biological engineering goals: altering what a cell produces, changing its growth requirements, adding or removing functions, or tuning its physical properties. The field sits between broad organ engineering and the more specific disciplines of tissue, protein, and genetic engineering. It relies on recombinant DNA technology (cutting and recombining DNA from different sources) and overlaps with tissue engineering. Publication volume rose from a few thousand papers in the early 2000s to nearly 40,000 in 2020.

## Core strategies

Most cell engineering work uses a small set of recurring approaches.

**Boosting or repressing native metabolism** changes how much of a substance a cell naturally makes. Overexpressing transporter genes in Chinese hamster ovary cells has raised monoclonal antibody (an identical antibody population produced by cloned immune cells) yield.

**Introducing foreign genes** reroutes a cell's chemistry. *Escherichia coli* that produces ethanol was given genes from *Zymomonas mobilis*, redirecting fermentation so ethanol became the main product rather than a side reaction.

**Changing growth substrates** lowers cost. Foreign enzymes inserted into an industrial yeast strain let it grow on cheaper feedstocks than conventional glucose.

**Programming cells to manufacture new products** is the strategy behind recombinant insulin. *E. coli* was transformed to transcribe and translate a human insulin precursor, reducing the cost of production. The same approach was applied in 1979 to make human growth hormone for treating pituitary dwarfism, and later to produce antigens (molecules that provoke an immune response) for recombinant vaccines.

**Adjusting inherent cell properties** changes how cells tolerate stress. In 1988, researchers expressed a *Vitreoscilla* hemoglobin gene in *E. coli*, producing a strain more tolerant of the low-oxygen conditions found in dense industrial bioreactors.

**Directing stem cell differentiation** steers unspecialised cells toward a chosen fate. Partial differentiation of stem cells into myocytes (muscle precursor cells) has been used to generate pro-myogenic factors (signals that promote muscle growth) for treating sarcopenia and disuse atrophy.

## Stem cells as a platform

Stem cells matter because they can differentiate into many cell types, making them a starting point for both therapeutic production and further engineering. Cardiac and muscle-loss therapies draw on the same modification strategies used elsewhere in the field.

## History and boundaries

The phrase "cell engineering" first appeared in print in 1968, describing improvements to fuel cells rather than biology. The term was gradually adopted for biological work once "fuel-cell engineering" became standard for the original sense. The first biological use came in 1971, in a paper on grafting reproductive caps between algae cells. Boundaries between cell engineering and adjacent disciplines such as tissue and protein engineering remain unclear.

## Representative applications

- **Therapeutic T cell engineering:** altering T cells (immune cells that kill infected or cancerous targets) so they recognise cancer-related antigens.
- **Monoclonal antibody production:** using engineered cells to improve antibody yield.
- **In vivo cell factories:** engineering patient cells to produce therapeutics inside the body.
- **Directed stem cell differentiation:** using physical and chemical cues to guide stem cell fate.
- **Antibody–drug conjugates:** engineering linkages between an antibody and a cytotoxic drug to target diseased cells.

The economic motivation behind most of these strategies is reducing the cost of producing biological molecules and the infrastructure required for large-scale culture.
