# Alkaline battery

An alkaline battery is a single-use (primary) cell whose electricity comes from the reaction between zinc powder and manganese dioxide (MnO₂) in a water-based electrolyte of potassium hydroxide (KOH). The "alkaline" name refers to this electrolyte, whose pH is above 7, distinguishing it from the older zinc–carbon cell that uses the acidic electrolyte ammonium chloride or zinc chloride. Because the KOH is not consumed, only the zinc and MnO₂ are used up as the cell delivers current.

## Chemistry

The negative electrode (anode) is metallic zinc powder dispersed in a gel that holds the KOH. The positive electrode (cathode) is a compressed paste of MnO₂ mixed with carbon powder for conductivity. A porous separator between them keeps the electrodes from touching while letting ions pass.

During discharge, zinc is oxidised from oxidation state 0 to +2, and manganese is reduced from +4 to +3. The half-reactions are:

- Anode (oxidation): Zn(s) + 2OH⁻(aq) → ZnO(s) + H₂O(l) + 2e⁻ (E°ₒₓ = +1.28 V)
- Cathode (reduction): 2MnO₂(s) + 2H₂O(l) + 2e⁻ → 2MnO(OH)(s) + 2OH⁻(aq)

The overall reaction is Zn(s) + 2MnO₂(s) + H₂O(l) ⇌ ZnO(s) + 2MnO(OH)(s), giving a nominal cell voltage of 1.5 V.

## Voltage and capacity

A new alkaline cell measures between 1.50 and 1.65 V on an open circuit, depending on the purity of the MnO₂ and how much zinc oxide is in the electrolyte. Voltage sags as current draw rises and as the cell drains, and a cell is treated as empty when voltage falls to about 0.9 V.

Usable capacity falls steeply with heavier loads. An AA alkaline cell that delivers about 3000 mAh at low drain may deliver only around 700 mAh at the 1 A draw typical of a digital camera, because at higher currents much of the zinc is not accessed before the cutoff voltage is reached. Connecting cells in series adds their voltages, which is how a 9 V PP3 battery is built from six small 1.5 V cells.

## Current and construction

The current a cell can deliver scales with its physical size because larger electrodes have more internal surface area, lowering internal resistance. As a rule of thumb, an AA cell can deliver about 700 mA without significant heating; higher-current devices require C or D cells. Lithium-ion and NiMH cells of the same AA size can comfortably deliver around 2 A.

A cylindrical cell is housed in a drawn steel can that doubles as the cathode contact. The MnO₂–carbon paste is pressed against the can wall, and a porous cellulose or polymer separator lines the hollow centre. Powdered zinc in KOH gel fills the centre, with a plastic gasket sealing the end. In AAA, AA, C and D cells the flat end is negative and the raised button is positive; in coin and button cells the polarity is reversed.

## Recharging

Standard alkaline cells are not designed to be recharged. Forcing current backwards can rupture the cell or push corrosive liquid out. A small number of commercial "rechargeable alkaline" products exist, but independent testing found capacity dropping to about 10% of original after only two cycles. Laboratory work in 2017 showed that cells using copper-intercalated MnO₂ can in principle be recharged thousands of times and could approach the energy density of lithium-ion (around 250 Wh/L) if zinc utilisation were improved, though this remains a research result.

## Leaks

As an alkaline cell discharges, whether in use or through slow self-discharge, hydrogen gas is produced and internal pressure rises until seals or the steel can fail, releasing KOH. Potassium hydroxide is caustic and irritates skin, eyes and airways; it also attacks aluminium, which is why a leaking cell can ruin a flashlight. Once exposed, the KOH absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and forms feathery crystals of potassium carbonate that grow along metal traces, corroding copper circuits and contacts. Mixing cells of different age or chemistry in one device, or attempting to recharge disposable cells, accelerates outgassing. Pre-1996 cells additionally contained mercury to suppress the reaction and tended to leak less.

## Disposal and recycling

Alkaline cells made before 1996 are still classified as hazardous waste because of mercury; modern cells contain only zinc, manganese and potassium compounds, all of moderate toxicity. The EU banned alkaline batteries from landfill in 2004, set a recycling target of 45% by 2016, and in 2023 raised the target to 63% by 2027 and 73% by 2036; EU retailers that sell batteries must take back old ones. In the United States only California classifies alkaline batteries as hazardous, while Vermont runs a statewide alkaline collection program. Recycling shreds the cells, mechanically separates the steel, and chemically treats the "black mass" to recover zinc, MnO₂ and KOH for reuse. Batteries left in unused devices should be removed, because every cell eventually self-discharges to the point of leaking.

## History

Waldemar Jungner patented an alkaline electrolyte battery in 1899 and Thomas Edison developed his nickel–iron cell, also using KOH, in 1901. The modern zinc/MnO₂ alkaline dry cell was invented in the 1950s by Canadian engineer Lewis Urry, who later worked at Union Carbide's Eveready division in Cleveland; he, Karl Kordesch and P. A. Marsal filed US patent 2,960,558 on 9 October 1957, granted in 1960. The cell reached consumers in the late 1960s and, by 2011, accounted for about 80% of manufactured primary batteries in the United States and more than 10 billion units produced worldwide each year.
