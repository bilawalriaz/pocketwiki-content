# Diesel engine

A diesel engine is an internal combustion engine that ignites its fuel purely by the heat of compressed air, with no spark plug. This compression ignition drives most of the engine's other properties: high efficiency, throttleless air supply, the characteristic knocking sound, fuel flexibility, and dominance in heavy transport, ships, and locomotives.

## How it works

During the intake stroke, only air (often mixed with a small amount of recycled exhaust gas) enters the cylinder. The piston compresses this air to a ratio typically between 15:1 and 23:1, raising its temperature well above the auto-ignition point of diesel fuel. Near the top of the compression stroke, an injector sprays atomised fuel into the hot air. The fuel vaporises, ignites after a brief delay that causes the familiar "diesel clatter," and burns at roughly constant pressure, forcing the piston down.

Because only air is compressed, the diesel can use much higher ratios than a petrol engine without premature detonation, the main reason for its superior thermal efficiency. Real engines convert 43–45% of fuel energy into work in trucks and cars; slow-moving two-stroke marine engines reach 55%. The theoretical maximum is about 75%.

Torque is controlled by the amount of fuel injected, not by a throttle restricting the air. The air supply is maximised at all times, and the engine runs with a large excess of air (a "lean" mixture) at low loads. This is why diesels are far more efficient than petrol engines at part load, and why they need no electrical ignition system, an advantage in environments sensitive to radio interference, including parts of the U.S. National Radio Quiet Zone.

## The diesel cycle

The idealised pressure–volume diagram traces four events: adiabatic compression of air (1→2), constant-pressure combustion as fuel burns in the hot air (2→3), adiabatic expansion doing work on the piston (3→4), and an abrupt pressure drop when the exhaust valve opens. The enclosed area is the net work per cycle. In a real engine, combustion is a slightly noisy, two-stage process: a short ignition delay, then rapid pressure rise as accumulated vapour burns.

## Combustion cycles and speed classes

Diesel engines are built as either four-stroke or two-stroke machines. Most road vehicles use the four-stroke design for its broader useful speed range. Two-stroke diesels combine intake and compression into one stroke and power and exhaust into another, mechanically simpler and more efficient, but they require a scavenge blower or turbocharger to push fresh air in. The Wärtsilä-Sulzer RTA96-C, a two-stroke marine unit rated at about 80 MW, is among the largest reciprocating engines ever built.

Engines are classified by rotational speed: high-speed (over 1,000 rpm) for cars, trucks, and tractors; medium-speed (300–1,000 rpm) for locomotives, generators, and large ships; and slow-speed (under 300 rpm) for the largest merchant vessels, where size is unconstrained and 55% efficiency is achievable.

## Fuel injection

The injection system is the heart of a modern diesel. Early engines used air-blast injection, where compressed air atomised the fuel, but it was heavy and sluggish. Direct mechanical injection, in which a high-pressure pump sends fuel through a separate line to each cylinder, became standard for trucks and ships. Electronic unit injectors, which combine pump and nozzle in one component above each cylinder, reach pressures of about 220 MPa. Since the late 1990s, common-rail systems have dominated passenger-car diesels: a single high-pressure rail (modern pressures reach 270 MPa) feeds solenoid- or piezoelectric-actuated injectors that the engine's electronic control unit can trigger multiple times per stroke, reducing noise and emissions.

In direct injection (DI), fuel sprays straight into the main combustion chamber, usually into a bowl in the piston. In indirect injection (IDI), fuel first enters a small pre-chamber or swirl chamber, where partial combustion drives the burning mixture into the main chamber through a narrow passage. IDI is smoother and quieter but loses 5–10% efficiency to heat absorbed by the pre-chamber walls.

## Fuels

Diesel fuel is graded by cetane number, a measure of how readily it ignites under compression, rather than by octane rating, which matters only for spark-ignition engines. Diesel engines tolerate kerosene, vegetable oil, biodiesel, and heavy fuel oil because the compressed air provides ample heat for ignition. Low volatility also makes diesel fuel much less flammable than petrol, with a flash point around 55 °C.

## Efficiency and emissions

The combination of high compression, lean operation, and no throttling losses gives the diesel the highest thermal efficiency of any practical combustion engine. The trade-off is emissions: incomplete combustion produces carbon monoxide, hydrocarbons, soot-like particulate matter, and nitrogen oxides. Diesel particulate matter is classified as an IARC Group 1 carcinogen, linked to lung and bladder cancer. Modern diesels use diesel particulate filters, selective catalytic reduction (which injects urea to convert NOx into nitrogen and water), and exhaust gas recirculation, which together can remove about 90% of pollutants. Road diesel fuel has been essentially sulfur-free since 2003, eliminating sulfur dioxide emissions from vehicles.

In 2015, Volkswagen was found to have programmed its TDI engines to defeat laboratory emissions tests, triggering global scrutiny of diesel regulation.

## Brief history

The German engineer Rudolf Diesel conceived a "rational heat motor" in the early 1890s, aiming to surpass the 6–10% efficiency of contemporary steam engines. He was inspired by Carl von Linde's lectures on the Carnot cycle and by a fire piston that ignites tinder through rapid adiabatic compression. His 1893 patent described a constant-temperature cycle that proved unworkable; by mid-1893 he had adopted the constant-pressure cycle, which is the basis of the modern engine. The first successful prototype ran on 17 February 1894, but achieved only 16.6% efficiency and was troublesome. A third prototype, tested from February 1897, reached 13.1 kW at 26.2% effective efficiency, making Diesel a millionaire by 1898.

Commercial diesel engines entered service in submarines and ships from about 1910, in locomotives from 1912, in trucks from 1924, and in passenger cars from 1936 (the Mercedes-Benz 260 D). The 1970s oil crisis spurred diesel adoption in small European cars, and by the early 2010s roughly 47% of new European passenger cars were diesels. The14-cylinder, two-stroke marine units now in service on supertankers produce nearly 100 MW each.

Aviation was an early adopter, and the airship Hindenburg flew on four diesel engines in 1936, but weight and cold-starting difficulty kept diesels out of aircraft for decades. From the late 1990s onward, diesel engines designed to run on Jet-A fuel have re-entered general aviation, with over 5,000 such engines delivered worldwide between 2002 and 2018, and the technology is now standard in some light aircraft and unmanned aerial vehicles.
