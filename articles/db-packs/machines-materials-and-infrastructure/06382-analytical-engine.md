# Analytical engine

The analytical engine was a design for a fully programmable, general-purpose digital computer, drawn up by the English mathematician Charles Babbage in 1837 as the successor to his simpler difference engine, which had been a special-purpose calculator for tabulating polynomials. The analytical engine was never built in Babbage's lifetime, but its structure matched the architecture that later dominated electronic computing: a memory ("the Store"), a processing unit ("the Mill"), and input and output mechanisms. Because the proposed instruction set included conditional branching and loops, the design would have been Turing-complete, meaning it could in principle execute any computation a modern computer can.

The machine was to read programs and data from punched cards, a technique Babbage borrowed from Jacquard looms, and to output results through a printer, a curve plotter, and a bell. It also had provision for punching results onto cards for later re-use. The Store was specified to hold 1,000 numbers of 40 decimal digits each, roughly 16.6 kilobytes, and the Mill could perform addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division, together with comparisons and optionally square roots. Arithmetic was fixed-point base-10. Internally the Mill relied on pegs inserted into rotating barrels, a mechanical cousin of the microcode that drives modern central processing units. Three kinds of punched cards controlled the machine: operation cards for arithmetic and control instructions, variable cards for data, and load/store cards for moving numbers between the Store and the Mill, each read by its own card reader. The user-level programming language resembled a modern assembly language, and Babbage produced about two dozen example programs between 1837 and 1840, handling polynomials, iterative formulas, Gaussian elimination, and Bernoulli numbers.

In 1842, the Italian mathematician Luigi Federico Menabrea published a French description of the engine based on lectures Babbage had given in Turin two years earlier. Ada Lovelace translated the paper into English in 1843 and added extensive notes, including a detailed procedure for computing Bernoulli numbers, widely regarded as the first published computer program. Lovelace is often called the first computer programmer, though historians continue to debate the title.

Construction never reached a working whole. Babbage fell out with his chief engineer, Joseph Clement, lost British government funding, and assembled only a small fragment before his death in 1871. From 1880 to 1910, his son Henry Prevost Babbage built a portion of the Mill and the printing apparatus, which in 1910 produced a faulty table of multiples of pi; this was not programmable and lacked a Store, and photographs of it are sometimes mislabelled as the complete engine. In 1878 a committee of the British Association for the Advancement of Science called the design "a marvel of mechanical ingenuity" but declined to recommend building it, citing uncertain cost and unverified function. In 1991 the London Science Museum built and ran Babbage's later Difference Engine No. 2, demonstrating that the precision engineering he required was within the manufacturing capability of his era. A "Plan 28" effort launched in 2010 to build the analytical engine from Babbage's digitised drawings had, by 2017, made limited progress because the surviving design notes, especially for the indexed variables Lovelace's Bernoulli program would have required, remained difficult to interpret.

The analytical engine anticipated concepts that later became central to computer science. Babbage himself posed the question, in *Passages from the Life of a Philosopher*, of how the machine could obtain results "in the shortest time," an early statement of algorithmic efficiency. Later engineers largely worked without knowledge of his plans. Percy Ludgate published a smaller analytical engine design in 1909, Leonardo Torres Quevedo demonstrated an electromechanical arithmometer in 1920, and Konrad Zuse built the first working general-purpose computer, the Z3, in 1941, more than a century after Babbage's proposal. Howard Aiken, who built the Harvard Mark I between 1937 and 1945, later said his visit to the surviving portion of Babbage's engine was "the greatest disappointment of my life," and the Mark I, lacking conditional branching, was not Turing-complete. Menabrea reported that Babbage estimated his engine could multiply two 20-digit numbers in about three minutes, roughly thirty times slower than the electromechanical Harvard Mark I and about a hundred billion times slower than a contemporary central processing unit.
