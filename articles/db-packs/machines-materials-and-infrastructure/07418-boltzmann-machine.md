# Boltzmann machine

A Boltzmann machine is a network of binary units whose joint behaviour is governed by an energy function borrowed from statistical physics. Each unit is on (1) or off (0), and symmetric weights w_ij encode how strongly unit j pushes unit i toward agreement (positive weight) or disagreement (negative weight). The network has a global energy E that depends on every weight, every bias, and every unit state. At thermal equilibrium the probability of any global configuration follows the Boltzmann distribution: low-energy states are exponentially more likely than high-energy ones, with a temperature T controlling how sharply that preference is enforced. The probability that unit i is on depends only on the energy change that flipping it would cause, giving the logistic function used in many variants.

Units split into two groups. Visible units receive data from the environment; hidden units are internal and never directly observed. After enough random updates the network settles into a Boltzmann distribution over global states, and a simulation that starts at high temperature and slowly cools drifts toward low-energy configurations through simulated annealing.

The training objective is to make the distribution the network produces over its visible units, P⁻(V), match the distribution P⁺(V) of a training set. The match is measured by Kullback–Leibler divergence G. Minimising G maximises the log-likelihood of the data, and gradient descent on G yields a local rule: each weight w_ij is updated by the difference between how often units i and j are both on when the network is clamped to a training example (the positive phase) and how often they are both on when the network runs freely (the negative phase). Because each weight update depends only on the two units it connects, the rule is Hebbian and biologically plausible. Biases θ_i use the same idea applied to a single unit's activity.

General Boltzmann machines have a scaling problem. The time needed to gather reliable equilibrium statistics grows exponentially with network size and connection strength, and weights with intermediate activation probabilities are more plastic, so noise pushes connection strengths on a random walk until activities saturate. General Boltzmann machines are therefore largely of theoretical interest.

Two constrained variants escape the trap. A restricted Boltzmann machine (RBM) forbids connections within the visible layer and within the hidden layer, leaving only visible-to-hidden links. That bipartite structure makes sampling and learning efficient, and RBMs can be stacked so the hidden activities of one RBM become the training data for the next, a common deep learning strategy. Speech recognition is one practical application. Extensions handle real-valued inputs; spike-and-slab RBMs model continuous data by giving each hidden unit both a binary spike (a discrete mass at zero) and a real-valued slab (a density over a continuous range).

A deep Boltzmann machine (DBM) generalises stacking to multiple hidden layers, with every layer pair symmetrically and undirectedly coupled, unlike a deep belief network whose lower layers are directed. DBMs propagate information both bottom-up and top-down, but exact maximum likelihood learning is intractable, so they rely on approximate methods such as mean-field inference and Markov chain Monte Carlo. That approximate inference runs roughly 25 to 50 times slower than a single bottom-up pass, which limits DBMs in practice.

The Boltzmann machine sits inside a family: the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick spin glass is the underlying physics model, the Hopfield network shares the same energy form, and the Gibbs measure and log-linear model are the same Boltzmann distribution expressed in different fields. The machine is named after Ludwig Boltzmann's distribution and was popularised in cognitive science by Geoffrey Hinton, Terry Sejnowski, and Yann LeCun as part of the energy-based models programme. Hinton designed its learning rule in early 1983 while preparing a talk on simulated annealing, building on Hopfield's 1982 application of spin-glass methods to associative memory and on Sherrington and Kirkpatrick's 1975 solvable spin-glass model. In 2024 Hopfield and Hinton shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for foundational contributions to machine learning that include the Boltzmann machine.
