# Electronics engineering

Electronics engineering is a sub-discipline of electrical engineering that emerged in the early 20th century. It differs from classical electrical engineering by the additional use of active components, chiefly semiconductor devices, to amplify and control current flow. Where older electrical work used only passive parts such as resistors, inductors, capacitors, and mechanical switches, electronics adds devices that can switch, amplify, or rectify signals.

The discipline covers analog electronics, digital electronics, consumer electronics, embedded systems, and power electronics, and overlaps with solid-state physics, radio engineering, telecommunications, control systems, signal processing, computer engineering, instrumentation, photonics, and robotics.

## History

The profession traces to Karl Ferdinand Braun's 1874 crystal detector, the first semiconductor device, and to the 1897 identification of the electron, which enabled the vacuum tube. Practical applications followed with Fleming's diode and De Forest's triode in the early 1900s, allowing non-mechanical detection of small voltages such as radio antenna signals. Commercial broadcasting, long-distance telephony, and recording amplified electronics growth through the 1920s. World War II systems such as radar and sonar expanded the field, and Shockley, Bardeen, and Brattain's transistor opened the consumer era that followed.

## Subfields

Signal processing analyzes and manipulates signals, which are analog (continuous) or digital (discrete values). Analog processing amplifies and filters audio and modulates and demodulates radio carriers; digital processing compresses data and checks for errors.

Telecommunications engineering transmits information across coaxial cable, optical fiber, or free space. Free-space transmission encodes information onto a carrier wave by modulation, commonly amplitude modulation or frequency modulation. Engineers design transmitters and receivers, sometimes combined as transceivers, with transmitter power consumption tied to signal strength, since weak signals are corrupted by noise.

Control engineering applies feedback systems to aircraft flight and propulsion, automotive cruise control, and industrial automation.

Instrumentation engineering designs devices that measure physical quantities such as pressure, flow, and temperature, often by exploiting physical effects, for example radar guns using the Doppler effect for vehicle speed and thermocouples using the Seebeck effect for temperature difference. Instrumentation typically feeds sensors into larger control systems.

Computer engineering designs computers and computer systems, including new hardware, embedded systems for specific tasks, and microcontrollers. Software-intensive work often belongs to software engineering rather than computer science.

VLSI (very large-scale integration) design engineering covers IC fabrication. Engineers first build schematics describing components and connections, then translate them into physical layouts mapping the layers of conductors and semiconductors.

## EducationElectronics engineers usually hold a three- or four-year bachelor's degree designated Bachelor of Engineering, Bachelor of Science, Bachelor of Applied Science, or Bachelor of Technology, often ending with a capstone project. Many UK universities also offer an MEng. Postgraduate options include Master of Science, PhD, or Engineering Doctorate. A bachelor's degree from an accredited program is the first step toward professional certification as a Professional Engineer (US, Canada, South Africa), Chartered or Incorporated Engineer (UK, Ireland, India, Zimbabwe), Chartered Professional Engineer (Australia, New Zealand), or European Engineer.

Degrees cover physics, chemistry, mathematics, project management, and electrical engineering topics. Quantum mechanics and solid-state physics matter for VLSI work but are largely irrelevant to embedded systems practitioners.

## LabsHands-on labs use breadboarding with LEDs, diodes, and resistors; microcontroller programming on Arduino-class boards; and soldering components onto printed circuit boards. Renewable-energy labs cover photovoltaic panel simulators, wind turbine aerodynamics, water turbine flow, and smart-grid simulation.

## Supporting knowledge

Foundational supporting areas include electromagnetics (Maxwell's equations, plane waves, transmission lines, waveguides, antennas), network analysis (graph matrices, nodal and mesh methods, superposition, Thevenin and Norton theorems), and steady-state AC analysis using phasors, Laplace transforms, and two-port parameters. Electronic-device study covers energy bands and carrier transport in silicon, p-n and Zener diodes, BJTs, JFETs, MOSFETs, LEDs, photodiodes, lasers, and CMOS fabrication. Analog-circuit work includes diode and transistor biasing, single- and multi-stage amplifiers, op-amp circuits, filters, and oscillators. Digital-circuit work covers Boolean functions, logic-gate families, combinational and sequential circuits, ADCs and DACs, semiconductor memories, and the 8086 microprocessor. Signals and systems covers Laplace, Fourier, and z-transforms, sampling, LTI system properties, and noise analysis.

## Professional practice

Professional bodies include IEEE in the US and IET in the UK; the IEC publishes relevant standards. Senior IEEE membership is a recognized US designation. Most engineers spend time on proposals, budgets, scheduling, and managing technicians or other engineers, so project management and written communication matter. Workplaces range from fabrication labs and consulting offices to research labs, and supervisors often coordinate scientists, electricians, programmers, and engineers. Skill obsolescence is acute in fast-moving areas such as consumer electronics, making continued learning essential. Common tools include LTSpice for circuit simulation and Eagle for PCB design.
