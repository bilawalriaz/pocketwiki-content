# Earthworks (engineering)

Earthworks are engineering works that reshape the earth's surface by moving quantities of soil or unformed rock. The work covers both civil projects, such as roads and dams, and military fortifications, but the underlying task is the same: cut material from where it is unwanted, place it where it is needed, and hold the ground in place while doing it.

## Shoring

When a slope or excavation cannot stand on its own, engineers add shoring, temporary or permanent structures that retain the ground. Common options include retaining walls, slurry walls, mechanically stabilized earth, soil nailing, tiebacks, ground freezing, gabions, caissons, dams, grout curtains, earth anchors, trench shoring, and cliff stabilization. Shoring matters because soil is weak in tension, so an unsupported excavation collapses and endangers workers and neighbouring structures.

## Excavation

Excavation is classified by material and by purpose. The material categories are topsoil, earth, rock, muck (waterlogged unsuitable soil), and unclassified (any mix). The purpose categories include stripping, roadway, drainage or structure, bridge, channel, footing, borrow, dredge, and underground excavation. The classification matters because cost, equipment choice, and disposal rules depend on which category the work falls into.

## Civil and military uses

Typical civil earthworks include road construction, railway beds, causeways, dams, levees, canals, and berms, plus land grading to reconfigure topography or stabilize slopes. Geofoam, a lightweight fill, has been used to build a bridge overpass on weak soil near Montreal. In military engineering, earthworks are soil-built fortifications such as moats, sod walls, motte-and-bailey castles, hill forts, trenches, and berms. Soil is individually weak but cheap enough that huge volumes form formidable structures.

## Equipment

The volumes involved, often millions of cubic metres, require heavy equipment. The Fresno scraper, loader, dump truck, grader, bulldozer, backhoe, and dragline excavator transformed earthwork construction from manual labour into a mechanised industry.

## Mass haul planning

The core engineering problem is balance. Engineers must match the volume taken from cuts with the volume needed in fills, while minimising haul distance. Earthworks cost equals hauled amount times hauled distance, so mass haul planning sets the quantities and mass haul optimisation minimises one or both. During the Panama Canal's Culebra Cut, excavation removed over 76 million cubic metres, of which about 23 million cubic metres was extra because of landslides. Past calculations were done by hand with slide rules and methods such as Simpson's rule; today they use computer software that can optimise on haul cost rather than haul distance alone, because cost is not proportional to distance.

## Calculation software

Earthwork software is usually a CAD add-on, often an extension to a package such as AutoCAD. Its main job is to calculate cut and fill volumes, producing material and time estimates. Most products extract terrain elevations from plans using contour lines and spot heights, draw shaded cut and fill maps, generate cross sections, and render terrain in 3D. Different products use different volume algorithms: triangulated irregular networks and triangular prisms are common, but high-density grids and cross-section methods are also used, so the same input data can produce different results. Some programs focus on transport optimisation and construction planning rather than volume calculation.
