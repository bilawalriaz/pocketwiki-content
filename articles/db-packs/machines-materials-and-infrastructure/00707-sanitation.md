# Sanitation

Sanitation is the safe management of human waste and the conditions that keep it from causing disease. The World Health Organization defines it as facilities and services for safely disposing of human waste, together with garbage collection, wastewater handling, and other practices that maintain hygienic environments. The UN General Assembly recognised access to water and sanitation as a human right in 2010.

## The problem sanitation solves

The disease sanitation prevents is the fecal–oral route: pathogens travel from one person's feces into another person's mouth. Sanitation blocks that route wherever feces could escape into water, food, soil, or hands and reach a new host. Health effects are concrete: lower rates of diarrhoea, worm infections, cholera, and childhood stunting. Indicator organisms such as *E. coli* and helminth eggs are measured in water, soil, and sludge because their presence signals that fecal contamination has reached places it should not.

The economic case is large. Every US$1 invested in sanitation returns about $5.50 in reduced healthcare costs, lost productivity, and tourism losses. Inadequate sanitation was estimated to cost India 6.4% of GDP. Household toilets also improve women's school attendance and labour-force participation, since girls without a private latrine often drop out at puberty.

## How systems work

Two ideas organise the field. The "sanitation value chain" describes the full journey of waste: capture, storage, transport, treatment, and final reuse or disposal. Each link must function, because a failure at any step lets pathogens back into the environment. The "F-diagram" is a teaching model that names the six transmission routes sanitation has to interrupt: **f**eces, **f**ingers, **f**lies, **f**ields, **f**luids, and **f**ood. Blocking one route is not enough; a system that safely buries feces but lets untreated sewage run onto vegetable fields still spreads disease.

Sanitation work divides into four overlapping systems: excreta management (human feces and urine), wastewater treatment, solid waste management, and stormwater drainage. "Environmental sanitation" widens the scope to industrial waste and noise. Some organisations in the WASH sector (Water, Sanitation and Hygiene) use "sanitation" more narrowly to mean only excreta management; the Sphere Handbook, a widely used humanitarian standard, adds hygiene promotion and vector control.

## Technologies

Sanitation technologies fall into two broad camps. Centralised systems use sewer networks that carry waste to a treatment plant. On-site systems, also called non-sewered sanitation systems (NSSS), treat waste where it is produced. Pit latrines and septic tanks are the familiar on-site examples, and they account for roughly half of all sanitation provision worldwide. Centralised sewers handle large volumes reliably but are costly; on-site systems are cheaper but require planning for emptying, sludge transport, and treatment of the accumulated sludge, a process known as fecal sludge management (FSM). Other approaches include container-based sanitation, where sealed barrels are collected and replaced, and ecological sanitation, which recycles nutrients from human waste into agriculture. Community-led total sanitation (CLTS) is different: it triggers behaviour change, especially the end of open defecation, without subsidising hardware.

The "sanitation ladder" ranks service levels from worst to best: open defecation, limited service (shared improved facilities), basic service (improved and not shared), and safely managed, the top rung. Safely managed sanitation, defined by the Joint Monitoring Programme in 2016, means an improved, non-shared facility whose waste is treated on-site or transported safely for treatment elsewhere.

## Global state

Sanitation is Target 6.2 of the UN Sustainable Development Goal 6, which aims for universal access to safely managed water and sanitation by 2030. The Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP), run by WHO and UNICEF, tracks country progress; in 2017 it reported 4.5 billion people without safely managed sanitation, and by 2024 the UN estimated the figure at 3.4 billion. The COVID-19 pandemic pushed handwashing and household sanitation up the political agenda. The remaining gap is concentrated in informal urban settlements and rural areas where finance and land tenure are weakest.

## Environment and climate

Sanitation choices carry environmental costs. On-site systems release methane as sludge decomposes, estimated at 377 million tonnes of CO₂-equivalent in 2020. Properly designed landfills and wastewater treatment prevent leachate, the liquid that drains from waste, and runoff from polluting groundwater and surface water. Reusing treated wastewater for irrigation or recovering nutrients and energy from sludge is increasingly common, but each reuse path has to be weighed against the public-health risk of recycling material that may still carry pathogens.

## Open debates

Scope remains contested: should sanitation officially include solid waste, stormwater, and industrial waste, or stay focused on excreta? Approach is also debated: CLTS shows that communities can sometimes build their own toilets once they recognise the contamination problem, while subsidised hardware often goes unused, so the relative weight of infrastructure versus behaviour change is unsettled. Finally, reuse of treated waste (nutrients, water, energy) has to be balanced against the public-health and environmental risks of recycling material that may still contain pathogens.
