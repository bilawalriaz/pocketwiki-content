# Chemical reaction engineering

Chemical reaction engineering (also called reaction engineering or reactor engineering) is a specialty in chemical engineering or industrial chemistry that deals with chemical reactors. The term is often used more narrowly for catalytic reaction systems, whether the catalyst is homogeneous (dissolved in the same phase as the reactants) or heterogeneous (a separate solid phase). A reactor is not always a standalone vessel; it can be embedded in a process such as a reactive distillation column, a retort, certain fuel cells, or a photocatalytic surface, and solvent effects on kinetics are treated as part of the discipline.

## Origins

The discipline took shape in the early 1950s, driven by researchers at the Shell Amsterdam research center and the University of Delft. The name "chemical reaction engineering" was coined by J.C. Vlugter while organising the 1st European Symposium on Chemical Reaction Engineering, held in Amsterdam in 1957.

## What the discipline does

Chemical reaction engineering studies and optimises chemical reactions in order to define the best reactor design. Four interacting phenomena control reactor performance: flow behaviour of the reacting mixture, mass transfer (movement of reactants and products to and from the reaction site), heat transfer (in or out of the reactor), and reaction kinetics (the intrinsic rate at which molecules react). Reactor performance is the result of these four factors combined, expressed as a function of feed composition and operating conditions such as temperature, pressure, and residence time.

The field began in the petroleum and petrochemical industries, where a hydrocarbon feedstock, a catalyst, and a reactor defined the core technology. The same methodology, however, is general: by joining reaction chemistry with chemical engineering analysis, it applies to any system where reactions must be modelled, designed, or improved, including new processes and incremental upgrades to existing plants.

## Community

The international community is anchored by the International Symposia on Chemical Reaction Engineering (ISCRE). These three-day conferences are held every two years and rotate through North America, Europe, and the Asia–Pacific region on a six-year cycle, bringing together academic researchers, industrial practitioners, and students.

The ISCRE Board administers two main awards. The Neal R. Amundson Award, established in 1996 and given every three years, recognises a pioneer whose work has shaped the field through originality and novelty; it is supported by ExxonMobil and carries a plaque and US $5,000. The Rutherford Aris Young Investigator Award, first given in 2016 and supported by UOP, recognises researchers under 40 for outstanding contributions in experimental or theoretical reaction engineering, with a plaque, US $3,000, and travel funds to present at an ISCRE meeting.
