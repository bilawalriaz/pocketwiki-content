# Steel

Steel is iron with a small, controlled amount of carbon (up to 2.14% by weight). That tiny carbon addition does almost all of the work: it makes the metal harder and stronger than pure iron, while keeping iron's useful stiffness and low cost. Adding other elements in small amounts lets engineers tune steel for specific jobs, including corrosion resistance, extra strength, and easier forming. More than 1.6 billion tonnes are produced each year.

## What separates steel from nearby alloys

Carbon content is the dividing line. Steel holds up to 2.14% carbon; cast iron, with more than about 2.1%, is hard but brittle and not formable; wrought iron, with under 0.1% carbon plus slag fibres, is soft and tough but no longer made in volume. Alloying elements adjust behaviour. Chromium (at least 11%) gives stainless steel its corrosion resistance by forming a thin, self-healing oxide skin. Manganese, nickel, molybdenum, and vanadium appear in other grades to improve toughness, strength, or wear resistance. Phosphorus, sulphur, and lead are impurities because they embrittle the metal, so they are stripped out during refining.

## How steel is made

Production is a two-step process. Iron ore is smelted in a blast furnace with coke or charcoal, producing *pig iron* that is too high in carbon to be useful steel. That pig iron is refined by blowing oxygen through the molten metal, which burns off excess carbon and impurities. This step, basic oxygen steelmaking (BOS), was introduced in 1952 and now dominates production. The refined steel is shaped by continuous casting (about 96% of output) into slabs, blooms, or billets, which are rolled or forged into final products. Electric arc furnaces (EAFs) melt scrap steel using roughly 440 kWh per tonne, and they are increasingly important because recycling sidesteps the blast furnace. Globally, more than 60% of steel is recycled.

## Why carbon changes the metal: microstructure

Steel’s properties come from which crystal lattice iron adopts and how carbon sits inside it. *Ferrite* (alpha iron) is a body-centred cubic arrangement stable below 910 °C, holds almost no carbon (max 0.021%), and is therefore soft and ductile. *Austenite* (gamma iron) is a face-centred cubic arrangement stable above 910 °C, and carbon dissolves in it much more easily, up to 2.1%, which is why high-temperature processing is central to steelmaking.

When austenite with about 0.8% carbon cools slowly, the carbon precipitates as *cementite* (iron carbide, Fe₃C) alongside ferrite, producing a layered structure called **pearlite**, which is strong and reasonably tough. When austenite is quenched, carbon atoms are trapped in a distorted, body-centred tetragonal lattice called **martensite**, which is very hard but brittle. Reheating quenched steel (*tempering*) lets some carbon escape and trades a little hardness for much more toughness. The slow-cool, quench, and temper cycle is the toolkit that turns one composition into many different steels.

## A short history of scale

The earliest known steel comes from around 1800 BC in Anatolia, and high-carbon *Wootz* steel from India and Sri Lanka appears by the first millennium BC, while the Chinese were quench-hardening steel during the Warring States period (403–221 BC). What changed the world was price and volume. The Bessemer process, introduced in England in 1855, blew air through molten pig iron to cut its carbon cheaply, dropped the cost of steel dramatically, and let mild steel replace wrought iron in bridges, rails, and buildings. Basic oxygen steelmaking in 1952 then replaced Bessemer for most uses, and electric arc furnaces later made scrap recycling practical. By 2005 China was the leading producer with about a third of world output, and by 2023 it produced 54% of the world's steel.

## Steel today

Around 90% of steel made is plain carbon steel, used for beams, pipes, panels, and rebar. Specialty grades handle harder jobs: stainless steel for kitchens and medical equipment, weathering steels such as Cor-ten for bridges and facades, and tool steels for cutting and shaping other materials. In cars, Advanced High Strength Steels (AHSS), including dual-phase and TRIP steels, let designers thin body panels for lighter weight while meeting crash safety targets. The legendary sharpness of ancient Damascus blades has been speculated to involve carbon nanotubes, though the exact composition and processing of those wootz-derived swords is still debated. Steelmaking is responsible for roughly 8% of global carbon dioxide emissions, and is a focus of work on hydrogen-based reduction and carbon capture alongside continued growth in electric arc furnace recycling.
