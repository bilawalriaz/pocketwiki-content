# Cable-stayed bridge

A cable-stayed bridge carries its deck with straight cables that run directly from one or more towers down to the deck, usually in a fan or a row of near-parallel lines. This direct connection is the defining feature and the main contrast with a suspension bridge, where the deck hangs from vertical suspenders that drop from a draped main cable strung between the towers and anchored at each end.

Cable-stayed spans occupy the range where cantilever bridges become uneconomically heavy and suspension bridges become wasteful: medium to long. The towers carry the load down to the ground, so the form does not need the massive end anchorages a suspension bridge requires when ground conditions are poor. The trade-off is that the diagonal cables pull sideways as well as upward, so the deck must resist horizontal compression, which a suspension deck does not. In a symmetrical layout the sideways pulls on opposite sides of a tower cancel, leaving the tower to handle mainly vertical load plus the horizontal forces of live traffic. Cables are typically steel rope, the deck may be steel or a post-tensioned concrete box girder, and the towers (pylons) may be steel or concrete. Construction usually proceeds by cantilevering deck segments outward from each tower, with the stays acting as both temporary and permanent supports, so no falsework under the span is normally needed.

The form existed in concept from 1595, when Fausto Veranzio published a chain-stayed design in *Machinae Novae*, but it could not compete with pure suspension for the longest crossings in the early twentieth century, and shorter crossings were often built in reinforced concrete. It returned in the 1950s once new materials, larger construction equipment, and bridge replacement programs made it competitive again. Albert Caquot's 1952 concrete-decked bridge at Pierrelatte, France, is often called the first of the modern type, but Franz Dischinger's 1955 Strömsund Bridge in Sweden, a steel-decked design, is more frequently credited because it shaped what followed. Early modern bridges used only a few stays per tower, which raised erection costs; later designs use many more cables so that each one is smaller, cheaper, and easier to install.

Four cable arrangements dominate. In the **harp** (or parallel) layout the cables are nearly parallel, so their tower-attachment height is proportional to their distance from the tower along the deck. In the **fan** layout every cable passes through or terminates near the top of the tower; the fan is the most efficient because it puts a minimum bending moment on the tower, but engineers usually prefer a **modified fan** (semi-fan) that spaces the anchorages apart near the top to make them easier to terminate, protect, and maintain. The **star** arrangement spaces the cables along the tower as in a harp but groups their deck attachments to one point or a small region. A **mono** layout, a single cable per tower, is rare.

The towers themselves come in seven principal arrangements. **Single** uses one column, usually rising through the centre of the deck, as in the Millau Viaduct in France. **Double** uses a pair of columns, one on each side of the deck, as in the Øresund Bridge between Denmark and Sweden. **Portal** adds a cross-member at the top to form a door shape, giving extra resistance to transverse loads. **A-shaped** achieves the same goal by leaning the two columns toward each other to meet at the top, as in the Arthur Ravenel Jr. Bridge in South Carolina. **H-shaped** stacks a portal on top of a double column. **Inverted Y** combines an A-shaped base with a single column above, as in the Pont de Normandie in France. **M-shaped** places two A-frames side by side, used in very wide decks such as the Fred Hartman Bridge in Texas. Columns may be vertical, angled, or curved.

Several variations extend the form. A **side-spar** bridge rests its central tower on only one side of the deck, letting the deck curve. The **cantilever spar** type, exemplified by Santiago Calatrava's 1992 Puente del Alamillo in Seville, has cables on only one side of a single tower, so the tower must resist a large overturning moment at its foundation and bending in its own spar. **Multiple-span** cable-stayed bridges, with more than three spans, are harder to design than two- or three-span layouts because end spans can no longer anchor the main spans, and engineers compensate with extra cross-bracing stays (Ting Kau Bridge, Hong Kong), twin-legged towers (Millau Viaduct), or stiff multi-legged frame towers (General Rafael Urdaneta Bridge, Venezuela). An **extradosed** bridge is a hybrid: a stiffer, deeper deck allows the cables near the tower to be omitted and the towers to be shorter than the span would normally demand; the Ganter and Sunniberg Bridges in Switzerland are early examples, and the United States' first extradosed bridge, the Pearl Harbor Memorial Bridge in New Haven, opened in 2012. A **cradle-system** bridge, used in the 2006 Penobscot Narrows Bridge in Maine and the 2007 Veterans' Glass City Skyway in Ohio, runs each epoxy-coated steel strand continuously from one side of the deck to the other inside a steel tube, eliminating anchorages in the pylons and allowing any single strand to be removed and replaced.

A **self-anchored suspension bridge** resembles a cable-stayed bridge in that the deck itself resists the horizontal pull of the cables, but it keeps the draped main cable and vertical suspenders of a true suspension bridge; it also requires temporary falsework during construction, which raises cost.

The Changtai Yangtze River Bridge in Jiangsu, China, opened in 2025 with a 1,176 m main span, the world's longest cable-stayed span as of 2026; the Russky Bridge in Vladivostok, Russia, holds the second-longest span at 1,104 m, and the Sutong Yangtze River Bridge in China, completed in 2008, is third at 1,088 m. The Jiaxing-Shaoxing Sea Bridge in Zhejiang, China, opened in 2013 and is 10,100 m end to end, the longest cable-stayed bridge in total length. The Queensferry Crossing in Scotland, completed in 2017, is the longest triple-tower cable-stayed bridge at 2,700 m. The Gordie Howe International Bridge between Detroit and Windsor, opened in 2026, has the longest cable-stayed main span in the Western Hemisphere at 853 m. The Rod El Farag Axis Bridge in Cairo, at 67.3 m wide, holds the Guinness record as the world's widest cable-stayed bridge. The Millau Viaduct's piers reach 341 m, the tallest of any bridge, and its roadway sits 266 m above the river Tarn.
