# Automotive engineering

Automotive engineering is a branch of vehicle engineering that applies mechanical, electrical, electronic, software, and safety engineering to the design, manufacture, and operation of motorcycles, automobiles, and trucks and their subsystems. The field also covers vehicle modification. It is research-intensive, uses mathematical models directly, and organises work around three functions: production, development, and manufacturing.

## Core disciplines

Each discipline addresses a slice of how a vehicle behaves, lasts, and is built.

**Safety engineering** simulates and tests crash scenarios against strict regulations, covering seat belt and airbag function, front and side impact, and rollover resistance. Methods include finite element crash simulation, crash-test dummies, sled tests, and full-vehicle crashes. **Fuel economy and emissions** measures efficiency in miles per gallon or kilometres per litre, and tests for hydrocarbons, NOx, CO, CO₂, and evaporative emissions. **NVH (noise, vibration, and harshness)** characterises customer-perceived sound and feel across powertrain, road, wind, component, and squeak-and-rattle sources, then either removes undesirable NVH or reshapes it (for example, tuning an exhaust tone). **Vehicle electronics** now spans dozens of systems controlling throttle, brake, and steering plus HVAC, infotainment, and lighting, and modern safety and fuel-economy targets cannot be met without them.

**Performance** is quantified by quarter-mile times, 0–60 mph runs, top speed, 70–0 mph braking distance, g-force capability, lap times, cornering speed, brake fade, and behaviour in snow, ice, and rain. **Shift quality** is the driver's tactile and audible perception of automatic transmission events, shaped by the powertrain, driveline, suspension, and engine mounts. **Durability and corrosion** verification uses mileage accumulation, severe-condition driving, and salt baths. **Drivability** covers cold starts, stalls, idle dips, launch hesitations, and stumbles.

A vehicle program budget splits into variable per-vehicle cost plus up-front tooling and fixed costs, with warranty and marketing on top, and schedules align to market windows and plant plans. **Design for manufacturability (DFM)** shapes components to be feasible, cost-efficient, and within tolerance, requiring close coordination between design and assembly. **Quality management** follows ISO/TS 16949 from the International Automotive Task Force (IATF), which merges ISO 9001 with regional standards such as AVSQ (Italy), EAQF (France), VDA6 (Germany), and QS-9000 (USA); functional safety per ISO/IEC 17025 covers electric and electronic systems. Total quality management has been applied in automotive production since the 1950s at firms including Ford, Motorola, and Toyota.

## Job functions

A **development engineer** owns the engineering attributes of a complete vehicle, balancing manufacturer targets, regulation, and customer expectations. Like a systems engineer, they manage how every subsystem interacts: brakes must deliver stopping power, pedal feel, low noise, and ABS compatibility at once. The role centres on trade-offs, such as a 1.4 L engine favouring economy and a 5.4 L engine favouring performance, with weight, drag, gearing, emissions hardware, handling, ride, and tyres all interacting. The development engineer also runs vehicle-level testing, validation, and certification, since individual component tests cannot expose issues like radio interference with other electronics, heat dissipation, control ergonomics, or cabin-wide sound quality.

A **manufacturing engineer** owns safe, efficient production. The team includes process engineers, logistics coordinators, tooling engineers, robotics engineers, and assembly planners. Manufacturers now join the R&D stage to keep designs buildable; once a design is frozen, manufacturing engineers specify machinery, tooling, and mass-production methods, raise plant efficiency, and apply lean techniques such as Six Sigma and Kaizen. Other roles include aerodynamics engineers advising the styling studio on drag, body engineers judging panel feasibility, change-control engineers tracking design and manufacturing changes, and NVH engineers running sound and vibration testing.

## Modern process and the V-Model

A large share of a modern vehicle's value, and most of its innovation, comes from intelligent systems, which forces the engineering process to absorb mechatronics, the combination of mechanical, electronic, control, and software design. Configuration, optimisation, integration, control, and validation at component, subsystem, and system levels have to sit alongside structural, vibro-acoustic (sound and vibration), and kinematic (motion) design, making the workflow heavily simulation-driven.

The industry has used the V-Model for systems development for over twenty years to manage this. System-level requirements cascade down the V through subsystems to components, and performance is validated back up at each integration level. Mechatronic systems need two parallel V-cycles: one for multi-physics engineering (mechanical, electrical, sensors, actuators, as in electrically powered steering) and one for controls engineering (logic, software, control hardware, and embedded software).
