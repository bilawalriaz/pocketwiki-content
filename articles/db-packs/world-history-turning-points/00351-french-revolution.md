# French Revolution

The French Revolution (1789–1799) dismantled the *Ancien Régime*—the pre-1789 system of absolute monarchy, feudal privilege, and a society legally divided into clergy, nobility, and commoners—and replaced it with republican governments grounded in civic equality. A fiscal crisis, war debt, and elite resistance to reform forced the calling of the Estates-General in 1789. The Revolution progressed through a constitutional monarchy, the abolition of feudalism, and the First Republic (1792), then collapsed into the Reign of Terror (1793–94) before a general named Napoleon seized power in 1799.

## Why the old system broke

By 1788, half of state revenue serviced debt, much left over from the 1778–83 war with Britain. The tax system was the structural flaw: rates varied by region, nobles and clergy were largely exempt, and collection was outsourced to private *tax farmers*. Reform ministers Calonne and Brienne proposed new taxes, but the *parlements*—regional high courts dominated by nobles—blocked them, insisting only the Estates-General had such authority. Bankruptcy forced Louis XVI to summon it for May 1789, the first such meeting since 1614. Population had grown from 21 to 28 million between 1715 and 1789, expanding a propertied middle class while immiserating wage labourers and tenant peasants; failed harvests in 1787–88 spiked food prices and unemployment. Enlightenment critiques of privilege and scandals like the Diamond Necklace Affair had eroded the monarchy's moral standing.

## From Estates-General to Republic (1789–1792)

The Estates-General met at Versailles on 5 May 1789. The Third Estate—mostly lawyers—demanded voting by head rather than by estate, which would let them outvote clergy and nobility combined. When Louis XVI refused, the Third Estate unilaterally declared itself the **National Assembly** on 17 June. Locked out of their hall on 20 June, deputies swore the **Tennis Court Oath** not to disperse until a constitution was written. Under pressure from popular demonstrations and French Guards mutinies, Louis ordered the privileged orders to join on 27 June, surrendering the old regime's corporate structure.

The dismissal of the popular minister Necker on 11 July triggered the Storming of the Bastille on 14 July, a medieval royal fortress whose fall symbolised the collapse of arbitrary authority. The **August Decrees** abolished feudal dues, tithes, and noble privileges. The **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen** (26 August) defined liberty, property, security, and resistance to oppression as natural rights and established popular sovereignty and equality before the law.

The Revolution then turned on the Church. The Assembly nationalised church lands—roughly 10% of France—to back a new paper currency called *assignats*. The **Civil Constitution of the Clergy** (July 1790) made priests and bishops elected state employees required to swear loyalty. Only 24% complied, splitting clergy into "juring" supporters and "refractory" opponents and alienating devout regions like the Vendée, which later fed counter-revolutionary war.

The 1791 Constitution created a constitutional monarchy with a unicameral Legislative Assembly and a suspensive royal veto, but restricted suffrage to "active citizens"—men over 25 paying taxes equal to three days' labour. The **Le Chapelier Law** (June 1791) banned guilds and worker associations. Louis XVI's attempted flight to Varennes in June 1791 shattered trust in the monarchy. In April 1792, the Assembly declared war on Austria. Military reverses and the Brunswick Manifesto's threat to Paris triggered the **Insurrection of 10 August 1792**, in which volunteers and the Paris National Guard stormed the royal palace; the Assembly suspended the king and called a National Convention elected by universal male suffrage.

## The Republic, regicide, and the Terror (1792–1794)

The Convention abolished the monarchy on 22 September 1792 and proclaimed Year One of the Republic. Louis XVI was tried and executed on 21 January 1793, a break with Christian Europe's principle of sacred kingship that pushed Britain, Spain, and the Dutch Republic into the **War of the First Coalition**. Facing invasion, federalist revolts, and civil war in the Vendée, the Convention created the **Committee of Public Safety** in April 1793. After the moderate Girondins were purged on 2 June, the Committee—dominated by Robespierre—became the de facto government. The **Law of Suspects** and the **Law of 22 Prairial** suspended due process. The Revolutionary Tribunal issued roughly 16,600 death sentences; thousands more died in prison or in summary killings such as the *noyades*—mass drownings—at Nantes. Price controls (*Maximum*) and *assignat* hyperinflation wrecked the economy.

On 9 Thermidor Year II (27 July 1794), the Convention turned on Robespierre; he and 19 allies were executed the next day. This **Thermidorian Reaction** ended the Terror.

## Directory and coup (1794–1799)

The Thermidorians drafted the Constitution of Year III, ratified August 1795, which created a bicameral legislature and a five-man executive called the **Directory**. The Directory survived royalist uprisings, Jacobin resurgence, the **Conspiracy of the Equals** led by Babeuf, and chronic inflation. The regime annulled elections it disliked using the army, which made generals political kingmakers. Director Sieyès allied with Napoleon—recently returned from Egypt. On 18 Brumaire (9 November 1799), troops dispersed the Councils; the Directory was replaced by the three-man **Consulate**, ending the revolutionary period.

## What the Revolution changed

War and state-building fused. The **levée en masse** of August 1793 mobilised 750,000–800,000 men by 1794, dwarfing coalition armies. War aims shifted from dynastic disputes to ideological expansion: "natural frontiers" on the Rhine, Alps, and Pyrenees, and "sister republics" in the Netherlands, northern Italy, and Switzerland. French armies lived off occupied territories, tying generals' political power to continued fighting.

In the colonies, the record was contradictory. Saint-Domingue (Haiti) held 500,000 of the empire's 700,000 enslaved people. A revolt erupted in 1791; commissioners abolished slavery locally in 1793 to secure Black military allegiance against British and Spanish invasion. The Convention ratified **universal abolition on 4 February 1794**, the first general emancipation by a major power. Yet "emancipated" workers were often compelled to stay on plantations for wages, and Napoleon reinstated slavery in 1802.

Women participated actively—marching on Versailles in October 1789, running the Society of Revolutionary Republican Women, and writing pamphlets like Olympe de Gouges' 1791 *Declaration of the Rights of Woman*—but both constitutions denied them political rights, and in October 1793 the government banned all women's clubs.

The Revolution permanently ended feudalism, noble privilege, and venal office in France, established equality before the law, secularised the state through civil marriage and divorce, and created a nationalist citizen army. The *Code Napoléon* (1804) codified these gains; the privileges of 1789 were never restored, despite the Bourbon restoration of 1815 and the Vichy regime of 1940. Across Europe, French armies exported abolition of serfdom, dissolution of guilds, emancipation of Jewish ghettos, and the Napoleonic Code, changes that correlated with faster urbanisation after 1850.
