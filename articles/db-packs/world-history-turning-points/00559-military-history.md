# Military history

Military history is the study of armed conflict and its wider effects, examining how wars begin, how they are fought, and how they reshape societies, economies, and the relations between states. It treats warfare as a system of interacting variables: doctrine and leadership, logistics, technology, strategy, and tactics. Because each changes unevenly over time, the discipline is dynamic, and because they matter operationally, it is taught inside military academies under the motto "Past is Prologue," so that commanders can recognise historical parallels and avoid repeating old mistakes.

The field rests on a written record that begins early. Cuneiform tablets mention a confrontation between Upper and Lower Egypt around 3150 BC and a conflict between Sumer and Elam around 2700 BC. The Battle of Megiddo in the 15th century BC, recorded by the Egyptian scribe Tjaneni, is the first battle preserved in detail that historians treat as relatively reliable. Greek writing later established the critical standards of the discipline. Herodotus, the "father of history," gathered eyewitness accounts; Thucydides wrote an impartial, document-based narrative of the Peloponnesian War and set the template for evidence-driven military narrative; Xenophon centred the leader's perspective in the *Anabasis*; Polybius analysed causation. Roman historians (Sallust, Livy, Appian, Cassius Dio) chronicled Rome's rise, and Julius Caesar's commentaries on the Gallic and Civil Wars let modern readers compare campaigns from inside command. Comparable strategic codifications appeared in the East, including India's *Arthashastra* and China's *Art of War*, *Book of Lord Shang*, and *Guanzi*, alongside Sima Qian's *Records of the Grand Historian* on the Warring States and Qin unification.

Technology shows a clear long-run pattern: each major step changed the dominant battlefield arm, and each appeared first or most fully in one region before spreading. In the ancient world, chariots (around 2000 BC) gave elite forces mobile missile platforms, and infantry became the core of agrarian states. The Greeks and Romans built rigid phalanges; the Macedonians lengthened pikes into the sarissa; the Romans later switched to the more flexible maniple, decisive at Cannae in 216 BC. Cavalry decided battles of its own, especially the horse-archery of the Parthians, Scythians, and later the Mongols, and the heavy cataphracts of Parthia, Sassanid Persia, Byzantium, and Han China. War elephants, originating in India, were adopted by Persia and Carthage (most famously by Hannibal). At sea, Cretan galleys gave way to Greek triremes at Salamis in 480 BC, and the Romans invented the *corvus* boarding bridge in the First Punic War.

Two inventions mark the medieval transition. The stirrup enabled the shock cavalry that dominated European battlefields for centuries. The crossbow, developed in China around 500 BC and reintroduced in Europe as the longbow in the 12th century, broke mounted armour at Crécy and Agincourt. Gunpowder evolved more slowly, from Chinese alchemical experiments in the 4th century to weapons by the 10th and siege incendiaries by the 13th, with transmission to the Islamic world and Europe still historically unclear. Cannons appeared in Europe in the early 14th century, and the 1453 Ottoman breach of Constantinople's walls with 800-pound shots, achieved in 55 days, ended the era of unbreakable stone walls. The same arms race produced a stream of incremental innovations: the matchlock arquebus in the mid-15th century, flintlock mechanisms by the early 17th century, howitzers, bayonets, naval mines, submarines (Drebbel in 1624, Bushnell's *Turtle* in the American Revolution, Fulton's *Nautilus*), and observation balloons in 1783.

The most consequential tactical change of this period was volley fire, the drill in which infantry ranks alternate loading and firing to keep a continuous stream of shot. East Asia got there before Europe: Korea's King Sejong ordered "fire barrels" fired in squads of five in 1447, and Oda Nobunaga implemented volleying and countermarch drills in Japan in 1575, about two decades before Lord William Louis of Nassau did so in the Netherlands. During the Imjin War of 1592–1598, Japanese firepower overwhelmed Korean arrow volleys, and Korean reforms after the war, drawing on Ming General Qi Jiguang's manuals, absorbed volley techniques along with small-group tactics and the close drilling that turned specialised sub-units into cohesive formations. Michael Roberts later framed the European version as a "Military Revolution," though the East Asian evidence shows the tactical core was not uniquely European.

The 19th and 20th centuries compressed these changes into a single accelerating sequence. Conscription, pioneered in the French Revolution, replaced reliance on mercenaries and produced the mass armies of Napoleon, and the 18th-century shift from *Kabinettskriege* (limited "princes' wars" waged by cabinets with hired troops and shifting alliances, 1648–1789) to total war, in which an enemy's whole economy and infrastructure become targets, was theorised by Napoleon and realised in Sherman's March, Sheridan's burning of the Shenandoah Valley, and the strategic bombing of the Second World War. Firearms evolved from musket and rifle (Winchester repeaters, Springfields) to machine guns in the late 19th century and automatic rifles in the early 20th. The First World War introduced tanks (British and French superiority against few German A7Vs), large-scale chemical warfare (chlorine gas at the Second Battle of Ypres, April 1915, after a non-lethal attempt at Bolimov in January), and combat aviation, which had begun with the Wright Brothers in 1903. The Second World War added carrier warfare, decided at Midway; radar; and the atomic bomb in 1945. The first nuclear submarine in 1955 then made possible quiet underwater missile platforms, completing the nuclear triad of land-based missiles, strategic bombers, and submarine-launched missiles.

The "Revolution in Military Affairs" (RMA) labels this pattern of short, technology-driven bursts of rapid change followed by longer periods of stability, applying equally to gunpowder, the tank, and the information age. The field also carries unresolved methodological tension: leaders' own battle narratives tend to minimise failure and exaggerate success; classified archives (for instance, those of Operations Enduring Freedom and Iraqi Freedom) and lost secrets (such as Greek fire's composition) leave permanent gaps; and Jeremy Black has catalogued inherited biases, including Eurocentricity, technological determinism, focus on dominant powers, the artificial separation of land, sea, and air, attention to state-on-state conflict at the expense of insurgency, and neglect of political "tasking," meaning how forces are actually directed. William H. McNeill in 1982 famously called the discipline an "intellectual ghetto" split between popular writing and work produced inside the military, a divide that persists even though courses remain popular with students, and a Jeffrey Kimball study from the early 1980s found that historians' own political views moderately correlated with their explanations of 20th-century war causes.
