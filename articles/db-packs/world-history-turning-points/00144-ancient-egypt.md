# Ancient Egypt

Ancient Egypt was a civilization concentrated along the lower Nile River in northeastern Africa, built on the river's predictable annual flood. Between June and September, the Nile overflowed and deposited mineral-rich silt. This dependable fertility produced surplus crops that supported dense populations, a centralized bureaucracy, monumental construction, and long-distance trade.

## The agricultural year

Farming followed three seasons tied to the river. Akhet (June–September) was the flood. Peret (October–February) was the growing season, as receding waters were channeled through ditches onto fields. Shemu (March–May) was the harvest. Staples were emmer wheat and barley, made into bread and beer, flax for linen, and papyrus. Cattle were the most valued livestock, supplemented by sheep, goats, pigs, poultry, and fish; horses arrived with the Hyksos around 1785 BC.

## Unification and the three kingdoms

By about 3150 BC, rulers based at Abydos extended control north into the delta, traditionally under a king called Menes, whom most Egyptologists identify with Narmer on the Narmer Palette. The capital was set at Memphis, where the Nile enters the delta, giving the king control of delta labor, agriculture, and Levant-bound trade. Egyptian history then followed a repeating rhythm: long stable kingdoms separated by shorter turbulent "Intermediate Periods."

The Old Kingdom (2686–2181 BC) was the age of the Giza pyramids and Great Sphinx. A vizier directed a bureaucracy that collected taxes, coordinated irrigation, and drafted peasants for royal tombs. Over five centuries kings rewarded officials with land and mortuary estates, steadily transferring wealth out of the crown. Severe droughts between about 2200 and 2150 BC finished the collapse, and for roughly 140 years regional nomarchs ruled locally. Order returned when the Theban Mentuhotep II defeated the Herakleopolitan rulers around 2055 BC.

The Middle Kingdom (c. 2055–1785 BC) restored central government. Amenemhat I moved the capital to Itjtawy in the Faiyum around 1985 BC and extended irrigation. The army retook Nubia for its gold and quarries. Stability ended when the Hyksos, a Western Asian people, seized the delta around 1785 BC and made Avaris their capital, pushing native rule south to Thebes. After more than thirty years of fighting, Ahmose I expelled the Hyksos by about 1555 BC, founded the Eighteenth Dynasty, and made the military a permanent priority.

The New Kingdom (1549–1069 BC) was Egypt at its most powerful. Thutmose I and Thutmose III extended rule to its largest extent; Hatshepsut ruled as pharaoh and sent expeditions to Punt. Around 1350 BC Amenhotep IV renamed himself Akhenaten, elevated the sun-disk Aten, and moved the capital to Akhetaten; after his death the old cult was restored and his memory attacked. Ramesses II fought the Hittites to a stalemate at Kadesh and signed the earliest known peace treaty, around 1258 BC. Pressure from Libyan Berbers and the Sea Peoples, combined with internal unrest, ended the dynasty.

## Decline and foreign rule

After Ramesses XI died in 1078 BC, the country split between a northern dynasty at Tanis and the High Priests of Amun at Thebes. Libyan and then Kushite rulers took power (the Twenty-Fifth Dynasty, from about 727 BC); Taharqa built an empire close to the New Kingdom's size. The Assyrians invaded between 671 and 667 BC; Saite kings backed by Greek mercenaries expelled them by 653 BC. The Persians under Cambyses II conquered Egypt in 525 BC; after a brief native revival, Alexander the Great was handed the country in 332 BC without a fight.

The Macedonian Ptolemies ruled from Alexandria, whose Library and Mouseion made it the Mediterranean intellectual center. They built Egyptian-style temples, adopted pharaonic titles, and merged Greek and Egyptian gods into composites such as Serapis. Internal revolt and Roman demand for Egyptian grain led to annexation in 30 BC, after Octavian defeated Mark Antony and Cleopatra VII. Christianity spread from the mid-first century AD; Theodosius I closed pagan temples in 391, and the reading of hieroglyphs faded as temple priesthoods disappeared.

## Government, economy, and society

The pharaoh was an absolute monarch responsible for upholding Ma'at, the principle of right order. A vizier ran civil administration over up to 42 nomes governed by nomarchs. There was no coinage until the Late Period; the economy ran on barter using grain sacks and the deben, about 91 grams of copper or silver. Temples acted as banks and granaries. Farmers bore a labor tax and were drafted for irrigation and construction; scribes and officials formed a privileged class marked by a white kilt. Men and women had nearly equal legal rights, including owning property, contracting, marrying, divorcing, and litigating.

## Writing, science, and technology

The Egyptian language belongs to the Afro-Asiatic family and has the longest continuous written record of any tongue, from about 3200 BC into the Middle Ages. Hieroglyphs, in use by about 3000 BC, used hundreds of signs for whole words, sounds, or silent determinatives; scribes wrote daily in the cursive hieratic script, later replaced by Demotic. The Coptic alphabet added Demotic signs to Greek around the first century AD. Thomas Young and Jean-François Champollion deciphered the system in the 1820s using the Rosetta Stone.

Mathematics used a decimal system with signs for each power of ten; Egyptians knew an empirical form of the Pythagorean rule and estimated a circle's area as roughly 3.16r². Medicine was specialized by ailment; physicians treated wounds with honey, opium, and thyme and performed surgery. Shipbuilders were constructing planked vessels by about 3000 BC using mortise-and-tenon joints; the Khufu ship measured 43.6 meters. Egyptians developed faience, a non-clay ceramic, and Egyptian blue pigment, and were among the earliest glassworkers. Mummification, refined in the New Kingdom, took about 70 days using natron to dry the body, with ushabti statues entombed to do labor in the afterlife.

## People and legacy

Population estimates run from 1–1.5 million in the third millennium BC to 2–3 million by the first. A 2025 whole-genome study of an Old Kingdom man (c. 2855–2570 BC) traced most of his ancestry to North African Neolithic farmers, with about 20% from the eastern Fertile Crescent, providing direct evidence of long-standing migration from Mesopotamia. Earlier samples show continuity with modern Egyptians and ties to both Middle Eastern and East African groups; Upper Egyptians leaned toward Sudanese and southern African populations, Lower Egyptians toward the Levant and Mediterranean. Scholars describe ancient Egypt as a heterogeneous civilization whose biology and culture stretched across Africa and Eurasia. Egyptian religion, art, and writing spread into Nubia and the Kingdom of Kush; the cult of Isis became one of the last pagan faiths to flourish across the Roman Empire.
