# Human history

Human history is the written and archaeological record of humankind from biological origins to the present. Modern humans (*Homo sapiens*) evolved in Africa around 300,000 years ago, spread to every habitable continent by the end of the Last Ice Age about 12,000 years ago, and then passed through three transformative transitions: the Neolithic adoption of agriculture, the rise of states and civilizations, and industrialization. Each transition reorganized population, technology, environment, and social structure, producing accelerating change that defines the present era.

## Prehistory and the Peopling of the World

Hominins (the human lineage) arose 7–5 million years ago, with bipedalism and stone tools documented by 3.3 million years ago. Brain size increased, *Homo erectus* left Africa by 2 million years ago, and controlled fire was likely in use by 1.5 million years ago and certain by 400,000 years ago. Anatomically modern *Homo sapiens* appeared by 125,000 years ago. Migrations out of Africa occurred in waves, but genetic evidence indicates all non-African populations descend primarily from a single dispersal 70,000–50,000 years ago, reaching Australia by 65,000, Europe by 45,000, and the Americas by 21,000 years ago. This expansion coincided with the Quaternary extinctions of large animals and Neanderthals, attributed to climate change, human hunting pressure, or both.

## The Neolithic Revolution

Beginning around 10,000 BCE, agriculture and animal herding emerged independently in at least 11 regions, including wheat and barley in Mesopotamia, rice in China's Yangtze Valley, and squash and potatoes in the Americas. Food surpluses allowed dense populations, division of labor, and permanent settlements, setting the foundations for cities, states, and warfare. The causes remain debated: population growth may have driven farming or resulted from it, with climate change, resource scarcity, and ideology also proposed. By 4500 BCE bronze (a copper-tin alloy) was in use, and by the 3rd millennium BCE it was widespread, linking distant regions through the tin trade.

## Civilizations, Empires, and the Axial Age

Civilizations arose near major rivers by 3300–2200 BCE, in Mesopotamia, Egypt, Norte Chico (Peru), the Indus Valley, and China, sharing central government, record-keeping, and inventions such as the wheel, mathematics, sailing craft, monumental building, and writing. Writing emerged in Mesopotamia (cuneiform, 3300 BCE), Egypt (c. 3250 BCE), China (1200 BCE), and Mesoamerica (by 650 BCE).

The Axial Age (800–200 BCE) produced transformative philosophical and religious ideas largely independently, including Confucianism, Buddhism, Jainism, Greek philosophy (Plato, Aristotle), and Jewish monotheism, traditions that shaped later history. Between 500 BCE and 500 CE, regional empires of unprecedented size relied on professional armies, bureaucracies, and unifying ideologies: Achaemenid Iran, Rome, Maurya and Gupta India, and Han China. Rome's Christianization was legalized in 313 CE and made sole state religion in 380 CE.

## Decline and the Post-Classical Era (c. 500–1500)

The Western Roman Empire fell in 476 CE under pressure from overextension and steppe nomads. The post-classical era saw Islam rise in the 7th century, with the Abbasid Caliphate presiding over an Islamic Golden Age. Europe experienced feudalism, the 1054 Great Schism, the Crusades, universities, and the Black Death (1347–1350), which killed 75–200 million people. In Asia, China's Tang and Song dynasties flourished, and the Mongol Empire under Genghis Khan (united 1206) became history's largest contiguous empire, splitting after 1259. Chinese inventions in gunpowder, firearms, and printing later transformed warfare and communication worldwide.

## Early Modern and Modern Periods

Between 1450 and 1500, Constantinople fell (1453), printing spread, and European voyages initiated a maritime global order. The 1492 Columbian exchange transferred crops, animals, diseases, and peoples across hemispheres, decimating the Americas: 60–90 million people died of disease by 1600, a 90–95% population decline. Europe's Renaissance, Reformation, Scientific Revolution, and Enlightenment coincided with the rise of absolute monarchies and colonial empires in the Americas, Africa, and Asia.

The Industrial Revolution, beginning around 1770 in Britain, was the greatest economic transformation since the Neolithic, producing factories, mass production, and modern globalization through railroads and steamships. The two World Wars (1914–1918, 1939–1945) killed 10–22.5 million and 55–80 million respectively, ending with nuclear weapons. After 1945, the Cold War between the United States and the Soviet Union dominated geopolitics until 1991. Over the 20th century, population quadrupled to six billion, life expectancy rose from about 31 to over 66 years, and extreme poverty fell from 75% (1820) to about 20% (2001), though inequality grew. The 21st century has seen smartphones, an early-2020s AI boom, the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic, and accelerating environmental change.

## Key Terms

- **Neolithic Revolution**: Transition beginning c. 10,000 BCE from hunting-gathering to agriculture.
- **Axial Age** (800–200 BCE): Era of largely independent philosophical and religious innovation.
- **Columbian exchange**: Transfer of plants, animals, diseases, and peoples between hemispheres after 1492.
- **Feudal system**: Medieval structure of manorialism and vassalage.
- **Globalization**: Increasing worldwide economic, political, and cultural interconnection.

## Open Debates

Historians continue to debate the causes of the Neolithic transition, the Quaternary extinctions, Europe's "Great Divergence," and the appropriate periodization of history, with late-20th-century scholarship challenging Eurocentric frameworks.
