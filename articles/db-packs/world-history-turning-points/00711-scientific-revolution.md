# Scientific Revolution

The Scientific Revolution (16th–17th centuries) was a transformation in how Europeans investigated nature. It replaced textual authority with observation, experiment, and mathematical reasoning, and produced a mechanistic picture of the universe whose parts obey quantifiable laws. Newton's *Principia* (1687) was the high point, deriving planetary motion from universal gravitation and showing that one physics governs the heavens and the Earth.

## The Aristotelian Framework

By the 16th century the dominant framework was Aristotelian cosmology: a geocentric universe in which four terrestrial elements (earth, water, air, fire) each seek their natural place, surrounded by an unchanging celestial realm of aether in perfect circular motion. Ptolemy's *Almagest* supplied the mathematics for predicting planetary positions within this scheme. The model assumed a sharp qualitative boundary between Earth and the heavens.

Medieval translations of Greek, Arabic, and Byzantine sources into Latin, combined with the rise of universities, made the Aristotelian corpus available and contestable. Gutenberg's printing press in the 1440s spread ideas rapidly, and improved optical glass later enabled telescopic observation.

## Astronomy as the Testing Ground

Astronomy became the first modern science because the heavens were where Aristotelian physics was most clearly testable. In 1572 Tycho Brahe observed a new star (SN 1572) showing no detectable daily parallax, the apparent shift in a nearby object's position as an observer rotates with the Earth, meaning it lay beyond the Moon, yet it did not move like a planet. A new celestial object contradicted the doctrine that the heavens were unchanging. Brahe published the finding in *De Nova Stella* (1573). Copernicus's *De Revolutionibus* (1543) had earlier proposed a Sun-centred solar system. Using Brahe's precise observations, Kepler showed in *Astronomia Nova* (1609) that planetary orbits are ellipses, not the circles Aristotelian cosmology required.

Galileo's 1609 telescopic observations supplied visible evidence. Jupiter's moons showed that celestial bodies can orbit something other than Earth. The phases of Venus matched the Copernican model rather than Ptolemy's. Lunar mountains and sunspots showed that the heavens are not perfect or unchanging. Newton's *Principia* then derived Kepler's elliptical laws from a single inverse-square law of gravitation, every mass attracts every other with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them, unifying celestial and terrestrial mechanics.

## A New Method

The new thinkers replaced Aristotelian deduction, drawing conclusions from first principles, with induction, drawing general laws from particular observations, and with mathematical demonstration. Bacon's *Novum Organum* (1620) called for systematic experimentation and a process of elimination. Galileo wrote that "philosophy is written in the language of mathematics," meaning that natural laws are best expressed as quantitative relationships testable against measurements. A related philosophy, empiricism, developed by Locke, Berkeley, and Hume, treated the mind at birth as a *tabula rasa*, a blank tablet, with all knowledge drawn from sensory experience.

## Mechanism and Mathematics

The shift was the rejection of Aristotelian final causes, the idea that objects move because of the purposes they serve, in favour of a mechanical philosophy that explained nature through matter in motion, contact action, and physical causation. Newton's gravitational attraction seemed to revive an invisible influence, yet he framed it as a mathematical principle from which motions could be derived rather than as an occult quality.

New mathematical tools made this possible. Viète's algebraic notation (1591) and Descartes's *La Géométrie* (1637) made geometry tractable as algebra, and Newton's calculus handled continuously varying quantities such as velocity and acceleration.

## New Sciences and Instruments

The revolution extended beyond astronomy. Vesalius's *De humani corporis fabrica* (1543) reconstructed human anatomy from direct dissection. Harvey's *De Motu Cordis* (1628) demonstrated that blood circulates in a closed system driven by the heart. Gilbert's *De Magnete* (1600) founded the study of magnetism and electricity by systematically testing materials. Boyle's *Sceptical Chymist* (1661) rejected the four-element theory in favour of corpuscles, minute particles, and his 1662 gas law made chemistry quantitative.

Telescopes (Galileo 1609, Newton's reflector 1668) and microscopes (van Leeuwenhoek) opened the very small and the very distant. The barometer (Torricelli, 1643) made air pressure measurable. Napier's logarithms and the slide rule (Oughtred, 1622) extended calculation.

## Institutions and Debates

Science became a social institution. The Royal Society of London received its charter in 1662, and the French Academy of Sciences was founded in 1666. The Royal Society's *Philosophical Transactions* (1665) introduced peer review and the practice of claiming priority through publication.

Historians still debate how revolutionary the period really was. The continuity thesis (Duhem, Randall, Crombie) treats the transformation as gradual, while others note that much of what was recovered, including Greek mathematics, Arabic optics, Indian numerals, and Chinese technology, came from non-Western sources, a point emphasised by Arun Bala. Recent scholarship has also highlighted the contributions of women working outside formal institutions, challenging the older narrative of a purely male achievement.
