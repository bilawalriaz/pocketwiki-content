# Civilization

A **civilization** is a complex society characterized by the state, urbanization, and standardized symbolic communication such as writing. Civilizations concentrate power, extend human control over nature, and rely on intensive agriculture, social hierarchy, division of labour, taxation, currency, and technological advancement. The word descends from French *civilisé* and Latin *civilis* (*civis* = citizen; *civitas* = city). The abstract noun entered French in 1757 (Mirabeau) and English with Adam Ferguson's 1767 *Essay on the History of Civil Society*. During the French Revolution it was used only in the singular, meaning humanity's collective progress; the countable plural "civilizations" became common only in the late 20th century.

The concept emerged in the 18th-century Enlightenment and was long opposed to "barbarism." Rousseau saw civilization as discordant with human nature, while German thinkers (Herder, Kierkegaard, Nietzsche) treated cultures as natural organisms with a pre-rational "folk spirit," making civilization an unnatural, corrupting force. Leo Strauss argued this view underlay Nazism. Albert Schweitzer (*The Philosophy of Civilization*, 1923) blamed civilizational crisis on the loss of civilization's ethical idea. The equation of "civilization" with "culture" remains controversial and is generally rejected.

V. Gordon Childe identified subsistence, settlement patterns, government, stratification, and literacy as defining traits. Writing was first developed in Sumer; the Inca substituted knotted-string **quipus**. All civilizations depended on agriculture, mostly cereals. The traditional **surplus model** holds that cereal farming produced storable surpluses enabling specialization. Research in the *Journal of Political Economy* contests this: horticulture was more productive, but cereal harvests were **appropriable** (taxable), allowing elites to extract surplus and fund urban growth. Highly productive roots and tubers were a "curse of plenty" preventing state formation.

Politically, Morton Fried and Elman Service classified societies from egalitarian hunter-gatherer bands through chiefdoms to civilizations with complex hierarchies. Money emerged by the early Iron Age to coordinate obligations among strangers; monetary monopolies have benefited elites since the earliest monetarized civilizations. Andrew Nikiforuk argues slavery powered pre-modern civilizations. Complexity did not always raise living standards: medieval males were taller than Roman-era males, and 19th-century Plains Indians taller than their "civilized" counterparts. Secondary markers include transportation systems, standardized measurement, legal systems, metallurgy, mathematics, and organized religion.

Civilization also denotes a society's broader cultural sphere, often spanning nations, its widest cultural identity, with neighbors assimilated (Chinese civilization influencing Korea, Japan, and Vietnam). The UN and UNESCO protect cultural heritage in war; Karl von Habsburg notes cultural destruction targets identity and memory. Samuel Huntington defined civilization as the highest cultural grouping short of species-level identity.

Systems theorists see civilizations as networks of interacting cities. Trade networks historically exceeded political boundaries: Silk Road and Indian Ocean routes linked Rome, Persia, India, and China roughly 2,000 years ago. David Wilkinson dates a **Central Civilization** to c. 1500 BCE via Mesopotamian–Egyptian integration, expanding globally by the 19th century. Many theorists see globalization producing a single world system; others date its first step to the Crusades.

Civilizations end by absorption into another (Egypt into Hellenistic then Roman) or collapse into simpler living ("Dark Ages"). Explanations include Ibn Khaldun's nomadic invasions, Gibbon's "immoderate greatness," Spengler's organic cycle, Toynbee's failing "creative minority," Tainter's diminishing returns to complexity, Diamond's environmental damage and climate change, Turchin and Korotayev's fiscal-demographic cycles, and Ward-Perkins' archaeological evidence of collapsed Roman living standards.

Huntington predicts 21st-century conflict between civilizations; Amartya Sen questions dividing people by religion and culture, while Inglehart and Norris locate the Muslim–Western clash in sexual values rather than ideology. The **Kardashev scale** ranks hypothetical civilizations by energy harnessed, though an alternative envisions minimalism and quantum-scale engineering with negligible energy use. The **Silurian hypothesis** asks whether an industrial civilization could be detected in the geological record, while astronomers search for alien technosignatures using Drake-equation variants.
