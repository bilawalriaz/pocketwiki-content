# History of the United Kingdom

The United Kingdom is a sovereign state assembled in stages: the 1707 union of England (with Wales) and Scotland, the 1800 addition of Ireland, and the 1922 partition that left only Northern Ireland in the union. Its history is shaped by the rise and decline of a global empire, the gradual widening of political rights, two twentieth-century world wars, and a recurring tension between British national identity and closer ties with Europe.

## Formation and the 18th-Century State

The 1707 Acts of Union merged England and Scotland into the Kingdom of Great Britain, creating a single Parliament at Westminster while keeping separate legal systems and established churches. The union produced two recurring pressures: Scottish demands to keep a distinct identity, and English political and economic dominance.

The new state's early decades were marked by Jacobite risings, attempts to restore the exiled Stuart dynasty, which ended with defeat at the Battle of Culloden in 1746. The post-1688 constitutional settlement had already made Parliament sovereign over the monarch, and during this period the role of Prime Minister emerged informally. Robert Walpole (in office 1721–42) is usually considered the first, governing through Parliament rather than the king.

Overseas, victory in the Seven Years' War (1756–63) confirmed Britain as a leading European and colonial power, adding territory in North America and India. The loss of the Thirteen Colonies in 1783 ended the "First British Empire" and pushed British expansion toward Asia and Africa, producing a "Second British Empire."

## Industrialisation, Reform, and the 19th Century

The Acts of Union 1800, passed by both the Westminster and Irish parliaments, incorporated Ireland and created the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland. The century that followed combined industrial growth, imperial expansion, and a steady widening of political rights.

The Reform Act 1832 abolished "rotten boroughs" (depopulated constituencies that still returned MPs) and extended the vote to much of the urban middle class, beginning a long process of franchise expansion. The Victorian era (1837–1901) saw Britain at the height of its economic power, but also the rise of Irish nationalism, which made "Home Rule," a devolved Irish parliament within the UK, a central political question.

## World Wars and the Post-War Settlement

The First World War (1914–18) mobilised British society, and the post-war settlement led to the 1922 creation of the Irish Free State. Six counties in the north remained in the UK, and in 1927 the state formally became the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland. The interwar years combined mass unemployment with the policy of appeasement toward Nazi Germany, which ended when war broke out in 1939.

Victory in 1945 came at heavy cost. The post-war Labour government (1945–51) founded the National Health Service, nationalised major industries, and established the welfare state, a system of universal provision for health, pensions, and unemployment support. At the same time, decolonisation gathered pace: independence for India and Pakistan in 1947 marked the start, and most of the empire had gone by the 1960s. The UK also joined NATO in 1949, anchoring its foreign policy to the United States and Western Europe.

## Late 20th Century to the Present

Post-war Britain combined economic difficulty with a loss of great-power status, symbolised by the 1956 Suez Crisis, when a joint British-French intervention in Egypt collapsed under American pressure. Joining the European Economic Community in 1973 tied Britain more closely to continental Europe, though membership remained politically contentious.

The late 1970s brought industrial unrest and economic stagnation. Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher (1979–90) responded with privatisation and deregulation, policies associated with Thatcherism. From the 1990s, devolution created elected legislatures in Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland with powers over domestic policy, while Westminster kept authority over foreign affairs, defence, and the constitution.

A referendum in 2016 produced a vote to leave the European Union, and Brexit was completed in 2020. The years since have featured political instability, the COVID-19 pandemic, and renewed debate over the union itself, driven by Scottish nationalism and the special status of Northern Ireland within both the UK and the EU's trading area.

Britain's history shows a repeated pattern of union and fragmentation: the 1707 and 1800 unions built the state, the 1922 Irish partition and ongoing Scottish debate show the same pressures still at work, and the twentieth century left the UK smaller in empire but still managing the tensions between its constituent nations and its relationship with Europe.
