# Neolithic Revolution

About 11,700 years ago, at the end of the last Ice Age, most human societies shifted from hunting and gathering to farming and permanent settlement. This change, the Neolithic Revolution, occurred independently in several regions over thousands of years and made dense populations, towns, and eventually states possible. It also narrowed diets, spread new diseases, and altered daily life so completely that the era before it is treated as a separate age.

## How Domestication Worked

Domestication is the genetic modification of a wild species through generations of selective breeding, so that it depends on humans and serves human needs. Early farmers favoured plants whose seeds stayed on the stalk rather than shattering off, and whose seeds were large, easy to harvest, and easy to germinate. Repeated sowing and reaping of these plants slowly produced crop varieties that could not survive on their own.

In the Fertile Crescent, archaeologist Daniel Zohary identified eight founder crops that formed the basis of early agriculture: three cereals (emmer wheat, einkorn wheat, barley), a pseudo-cereal (flax), and four pulses (peas, chickpeas, bitter vetch, lentils). Rye was an early failure in Anatolia, abandoned and later successfully domesticated in Europe. Animals followed a similar path: dogs were first, roughly 15,000 years ago in East Asia, then sheep, goats, cattle, and pigs, with West Asia as a major source for later livestock. Granaries developed to store surplus grain, freeing some people from food production and allowing craft specialisation, trade, and social hierarchy.

## Where and When It Started

Agriculture emerged in several independent centres, each working with locally wild species. The east–west axis of Eurasia let crops and livestock move between similar climates, accelerating spread at roughly 1 km per year. The north–south axis of Africa crossed climate zones and slowed diffusion. When Middle Eastern conditions dried, farmers migrated along the favourable east–west corridor, carrying crops and animals with them. Carbon-14 dating and mitochondrial DNA show that the arrival of farming in Europe, between roughly 8,500 and 6,000 years ago, was accompanied by significant human migration, much of it by sea via the Aegean and Cyprus; no European animals were independently domesticated.

## Why It Happened

No single cause is accepted. The Oasis Theory (Pumpelly, Childe) blamed climate drying that forced people and animals together near water. The Hilly Flanks Hypothesis (Braidwood) pointed to the well-watered Taurus and Zagros mountains rich in wild cereals. The Feasting Model (Hayden) argued that ambitious elites invented farming to host large displays of food and power. Demographic theories (Sauer, Binford, Flannery) treated population growth as the pressure that forced food production. Rindos framed agriculture as a gradual mutual adaptation of humans and plants. Richerson, Boyd, and Bettinger emphasised the unusually stable Holocene climate as a prerequisite, and Moore and Hole saw it as a long, millennia-scale evolution in the Levant beginning in the Epipaleolithic. These explanations are not mutually exclusive; most archaeologists now treat the transition as multicausal and locally contingent.

## What Changed for Society

Surplus food let some people stop farming and become potters, metalworkers, soldiers, scribes, or priests. Ruling elites emerged who stored and redistributed grain, defended territory, and organised labour. Permanent villages grew into towns, and towns into the first states. Religion, art, and gender roles shifted: the female figurines of the Upper Palaeolithic, such as the Venus of Lespugue, suggest reverence, while later mythological figures like Circe and Charybdis reflect female power recast as dangerous. Gender inequality likely intensified, though the evidence is indirect.

A second wave followed, the Secondary Products Revolution proposed by Andrew Sherratt, in which domesticated animals began supplying milk, wool, hides, manure, and traction, not just meat. This change enabled intensive plough agriculture, long-distance nomadic pastoralism, and the camel-based desert trade; it also brought overgrazing and contributed to the desertification of marginal lands.

## What Changed for Bodies

The cost was biological. Neolithic diets were dominated by starchy cereals, lower in fibre, protein, and micronutrients than the varied hunter-gatherer diet. Dental caries, slower childhood growth, higher body fat, and shorter stature followed. Europeans lost roughly 13 cm of average height after the transition, a loss not recovered until the twentieth century. Living close to livestock and to other humans in permanent settlements created ideal conditions for infectious disease; zoonoses such as influenza, smallpox, and Salmonella jumped from animals to people, and natural selection in farming populations favoured genetic resistance, a legacy that still shapes disease patterns across the world.
