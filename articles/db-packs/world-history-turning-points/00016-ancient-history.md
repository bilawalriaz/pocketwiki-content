# Ancient history

Ancient history spans roughly 5,000 years from the invention of writing in Sumer to the expansion of Islam in AD 500. Historians divide it into the Stone, Bronze, and Iron Ages, with recorded history usually beginning in the Bronze Age a few centuries before 3000 BC. The world population grew about 100-fold in this span, from an estimated 2 million in 10,000 BC to 209 million by AD 500, driven by the Neolithic Revolution and the spread of agriculture, writing, metallurgy, and complex states.

## The Neolithic foundation

Agriculture began around 9000 BC in the Fertile Crescent and spread along with animal domestication. Sheep and goats were herded by 9000 BC, cattle and water buffalo by 7000 BC, and horses, donkeys, and camels by 4000 BC; dogs were domesticated at least 15,000 years ago. Independent agricultural centres emerged: millet, rice, and legumes in China and taro in New Guinea by 7000 BC, squash in Mesoamerica around the same time, sorghum and millet along the Nile by 8000 BC. The plough, in use by 6000 BC, raised yields, and surplus food allowed denser settlement, division of labour, and record-keeping. Copper smelting began about 6000 BC; bronze alloying emerged around 3500 BC in Mesopotamia and independently in China by 2000 BC. Writing arose separately in Mesopotamia (cuneiform, 3400 BC), Egypt (hieroglyphs, 3200 BC), the Indus Valley (undeciphered script, 2800 BC), China (oracle bone script, 1600–1100 BC), and Mesoamerica (Zapotec, 600 BC).

## West Asia and Egypt

Mesopotamia is considered the cradle of civilisation, with cities in the Uruk period (4000–3100 BC) and the first writing. The Akkadian Empire under Sargon (2330–2150 BC) spread Akkadian as a diplomatic language, used across the region by 1450 BC. Babylonia rose under Hammurabi, and the Neo-Babylonian Empire under Nebuchadnezzar II conquered Jerusalem in 586 BC. The Hittites, in Anatolia from about 1900 BC, clashed with Egypt at Kadesh in 1274 BC before their capital Hattusa fell in 1207 BC. Assyria ruled successive empires (Old, Middle, and Neo-Assyrian), conquering Israel in 722 BC. Cyrus the Great founded the Achaemenid Empire by 539 BC, controlling Media, Lydia, and Babylon; it fell to Alexander the Great by 330 BC. Egypt was unified around 3100 BC, traditionally under Menes, and developed through the Old, Middle (from c. 2000 BC), and New (from c. 1550 BC) Kingdoms, separated by intermediate periods of fragmentation.

## South and East Asia

The Indus Valley (Harappan) Civilisation developed around 3000 BC, trading widely before its cities were abandoned by about 1600 BC for unknown reasons. Indo-European speakers arrived about 1500 BC, beginning the Vedic period with its varna caste system. Siddhartha Gautama (born c. 560 BC) founded Buddhism, and the Magadha kingdom peaked under Ashoka Maurya, who sent missionaries to Sri Lanka. The Austronesian expansion from Taiwan (c. 3000–1500 BC) spread peoples across the Pacific and Indian Oceans, reaching Madagascar between the 1st and 6th–7th centuries AD and connecting by the 2nd century BC to form the Maritime Silk Road. In China, the Shang dynasty (traditionally 1766–1122 BC) developed bronze and oracle bone writing; the Zhou introduced the Mandate of Heaven to legitimise rule; Ying Zheng unified China as Qin Shi Huangdi by 213 BC, standardising writing and measurements. The Han dynasty promoted iron tools, paper, and silk before the Yellow Turban Rebellion led to division for almost 400 years.

## The Americas, Europe, and Africa

Andean civilisation began with fishing communities from 3500 BC; the Chavín culture (c. 1000 BC) built large temples, and later cities like Huari and Tiahuanaco arose after about 200 BC. In Mesoamerica, maize was cultivated by 4000 BC; the Olmec built stone centres from about 1200 BC, and Maya culture emerged around AD 300, using a writing system, astronomy, and the concept of zero before declining from about AD 800. In Greece, Minoan and Mycenaean civilisations (writing Linear A and Linear B) collapsed around 1200 BC; the Classical period culminated in the Peloponnesian War, and the Hellenistic age (Alexander's death to 30 BC) spread Greek culture through successor kingdoms. Rome grew from the 8th century BC, destroyed Carthage in 146 BC, and saw its western empire break into independent kingdoms in the 5th century AD, while the eastern empire continued as Byzantium. In sub-Saharan Africa, the Nok culture (c. 1000 BC–AD 200) produced terracotta and possibly independent iron smelting; Djenné-Djenno in Mali was occupied from 250 BC to AD 900; and the Kingdom of Aksum (c. AD 100–940) extended across the Red Sea into Arabia.

## Ideas, religions, and technology

Around the 6th century BC, new philosophies and religions arose: Buddhism and Jainism in India, Zoroastrianism in Persia, and Greek philosophy through Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle. Judaism (c. 1700 BC) became the origin of the Abrahamic religions, and Christianity spread through the Roman world. In China, Confucianism, Taoism, and Legalism dominated thought. Civilisation brought institutional polytheistic religion, often tied to city or state, with sacrifices. Technological gains included metal alloys, Egyptian machines (ramp, lever), Babylonian and Egyptian astronomy, qanats for water management, the Hindu–Arabic numeral system with zero developed in India, and paper invented in China in the 1st century AD.
