# Prehistory

Prehistory is the roughly 3.3-million-year stretch of human history before writing. It runs from the first known stone tools (c. 3.3 million years ago, at Lomekwi, Kenya) to the invention of writing systems (proto-cuneiform pictographs c. 3,700 BCE in Sumer, cuneiform proper by c. 3,200 BCE). Because writing spread slowly, prehistory ended at very different times in different places: in Egypt around 3100 BCE, in Europe across the Iron Age with Roman expansion, and in New Guinea only in the 1870s, when an outsider first wrote about its people. Unlike written history, which deals with named individuals and nations, prehistory is known only through material evidence and studies archaeological cultures rather than states. Where a society is described by literate neighbours but has not developed its own writing, historians call the interval its protohistory (the Celts and Etruscans in classical times are the textbook examples). Because the term depends on a technology (writing) that spread unevenly, it is a useful distinction but a fuzzy one.

## How we know about it

Prehistory leaves no written traces, so evidence comes from excavation, survey, and scientific analysis. Contributing disciplines include archaeology, physical anthropology, comparative linguistics, molecular genetics, geology, paleontology, and palynology (pollen analysis). Dating is the central technical problem, and radiocarbon dating is the most common method. Genetic analysis of bones reveals kinship and physical traits, while comparative linguistics attempts to reconstruct ancient spoken languages.

## The long arc: stone, copper, bronze, iron

Historians divide the Old World past into the three-age system: Stone, Bronze, and Iron, with a transitional Chalcolithic (Copper Age). The system does not apply cleanly to places where metalworking arrived suddenly through contact, such as Oceania, much of Sub-Saharan Africa, and parts of the Americas. The Stone Age alone covers roughly 99% of human history.

The Palaeolithic ("Old Stone Age") runs from the Lomekwi tools to about 11,650 years ago, the end of the Pleistocene. The earliest tools predate our own genus and were probably used by *Kenyanthropus*; the genus *Homo* appears around 2.8 million years ago. Control of fire is uncertain until c. 790,000–690,000 BP at Bnot Ya'akov Bridge, Israel, with the first definitive evidence at c. 180,000 BP in Zambia. Anatomically modern *Homo sapiens* appear in Africa around 300,000 BP. The Upper Palaeolithic (50,000–12,000 years ago) saw the first organised settlements and a flowering of art, with humans living mostly as small, nomadic, egalitarian hunter-gatherers, though some groups became sedentary and stratified, and long-distance cultural contact existed (the Australian "songlines" being a striking example).

The Mesolithic ("Middle Stone Age") begins with the retreat of glaciers around 10,000 BP and ends when farming arrives, which happened at very different times. Where farming came early, the Mesolithic is short and poorly defined; scholars use "Epipalaeolithic" in areas with limited glacial impact. The period is defined by small composite flint tools called microliths, plus bows, canoes, fishing tackle, and stone adzes, many of which first appear in Africa and spread via the Iberomaurusian and Kebaran cultures. Because stone does not survive well in marshy ground, much of what remains is refuse heaps, or middens.

The Neolithic ("New Stone Age") began around 10,200 BCE in the Middle East with the Neolithic Revolution, the transition to farming and permanent settlement. Early domesticates included einkorn wheat, millet, spelt, dogs, sheep, and goats, with cattle, pigs, pottery, and mudbrick houses following by c. 6,900–6,400 BCE. Settlement triggered new social forms, including chiefdoms and ancestor cults, and a textile shift from animal skins toward wool and linen. Only *Homo sapiens sapiens* remained; other hominins had disappeared. Trial plant cultivation appears as early as c. 23,000 BP at Ohalo II, Israel, and figs were cultivated at Gilgal I c. 9,400–9,200 BCE, possibly the first full-scale agriculture. The Vinča culture may have produced the earliest known writing, though this is debated.

The Chalcolithic, or Copper Age, is a transition in which early copper metallurgy appears while stone tools still dominate. A Serbian site now contains the oldest securely dated high-temperature copper making, around 7,500 years ago, pushing the record back about 800 years and suggesting that copper smelting may have been invented independently in Asia and Europe rather than spreading from a single source. Timna Valley shows copper mining 7,000 years ago.

The Bronze Age begins when smelted copper is alloyed with tin, a rare metal that often travelled long trade routes. Tin bronzes appear in Western Asia only after 3000 BCE. Bronze was used for weapons long before farm tools and was hoarded by elites, so the metal shaped politics as much as craft. Large literate empires arose in Egypt, Mesopotamia, Anatolia (the Hittites), and China during this period. The Bronze Age is prehistoric only for regions without writing; in Sumer, the Indus Valley, and Egypt it marks the end of prehistory.

The Iron Age arrives where ironworking does. Iron requires more heat than copper or bronze to smelt, but once solved, iron's abundance let armies be armed at a scale bronze could not match. Most remaining civilisations adopted writing during the Iron Age, often through conquest by literate empires, with Rome's expansion replacing the Iron Age label in much of Europe. The period coincides with the "Axial Age" in philosophy, a time of broad religious and intellectual change.

## Key dates

- c. 3.3 million BP: earliest stone tools, Lomekwi, Kenya.
- c. 300,000 BP: anatomically modern humans appear in Africa.
- c. 80,000–50,000 BP: *Homo sapiens* leave Africa as a single population.
- c. 26,500 BP: Last Glacial Maximum, after which humans re-enter Western Europe and reach North America.
- c. 12,500–9,500 BCE: Natufian sedentary hunter-gatherers in the Levant.
- c. 9,400–9,200 BCE: fig cultivation at Gilgal I.
- c. 3,700 BCE: proto-cuneiform pictographs in Sumer.
- c. 3,000 BCE: Stonehenge begun; Yamnaya steppe expansions.
- 1788: conventional end of Australian prehistory.

## Mental model to keep

Prehistory is a category defined by an absence, the lack of writing, bounded at one end by the first stone tools and at the other by the uneven arrival of literacy. The three-age system organises the long stretch between those bookends by the dominant tool material, with most of that stretch being stone, and with agriculture, metallurgy, and writing arriving in a compressed sequence toward the end. Because the boundaries depend on a technology that spread across millennia, every date in prehistory is regional and provisional, and the same person can stand at the start of written history in one culture and deep in protohistory in another.
