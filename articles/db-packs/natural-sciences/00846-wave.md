# Wave

A wave is a propagating disturbance in a physical field that carries energy from place to place without permanently displacing the medium it travels through. Physically, a wave is a change in some property (pressure, displacement, electric field strength) at one point, caused by changes in neighbouring points that respond with a small delay. That neighbour-to-neighbour coupling lets a local disturbance spread outward at a finite speed. Waves are described by a function F(x, t) giving the field value at each point in space x and time t, defined over some spatial domain D.

The two great families are mechanical and electromagnetic. Mechanical waves (sound, seismic, waves on strings) need a medium with both elasticity and inertia. Electromagnetic waves (light, radio, X-rays) are coupled oscillations of electric and magnetic fields and need no material medium; in vacuum they all travel at c, the speed of light, with electric and magnetic components perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel. Maxwell showed in the 19th century that light is an electromagnetic wave, and Hertz generated and detected radio waves in the 1880s, confirming the prediction.

A few basic quantities describe any wave. Amplitude is the peak size of the disturbance; wavelength λ is the distance over which the pattern repeats; period T is the time for one cycle; frequency f = 1/T counts cycles per second (hertz). Phase velocity v_p = ω/k is the speed at which a crest moves, while group velocity v_g = ∂ω/∂k is the speed at which the overall envelope (and energy) moves. In a non-dispersive medium the two velocities are equal; in a dispersive medium they differ, so a wave packet gradually spreads.

A central property of waves is superposition: when two waves meet, their amplitudes add point by point. This single rule produces an enormous range of behaviour.

Interference occurs when coherent waves combine constructively or destructively. At a boundary, angle of incidence equals angle of reflection, and Snell's law governs bending when speed changes across the boundary. Diffraction is the bending of waves around obstacles and through openings. In transverse waves, polarization fixes the orientation of oscillation. Absorption transfers wave energy to the medium, gradually damping the wave. The Doppler effect shifts the observed frequency when source and observer move relative to each other.

Mechanical waves split into transverse, where the disturbance is perpendicular to travel (a string, v = √(T/μ), with T tension and μ mass per length), and longitudinal, where it is parallel (sound, v = √(B/ρ₀), with B bulk modulus and ρ₀ density). Seismic waves include body waves (P-waves and S-waves) and surface waves (Rayleigh and Love). A shock wave forms when a wave outruns the local sound speed, producing a near-instantaneous pressure jump.

When two waves of the same frequency travel in opposite directions they superpose into a standing wave, with fixed nodes of zero amplitude and antinodes of maximum amplitude. The sound pressure inside a recorder playing a pure note is a standing wave of this kind, modelled by F(A, L, n, c; x, t) with wavelength λ = 4L/(2n−1) and frequency f = c/λ, where n is a positive integer (the mode number) and A, L, c are amplitude, tube length, and wave speed. A soliton is a self-reinforcing wave packet that keeps its shape because nonlinearity and dispersion exactly cancel.

Electromagnetic waves are unified by Maxwell's equations. The spectrum divides by frequency into radio, microwave, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-ray, and gamma radiation, all the same physics at different wavelengths. In vacuum v_p = v_g = c, so these waves are non-dispersive.

In quantum mechanics the wave concept reappears as the wavefunction governed by the Schrödinger equation. De Broglie proposed that every particle has a wavelength λ = h/p, with h Planck's constant and p momentum, so electrons, atoms, and large molecules all interfere like light. A wave packet, often Gaussian, localises a particle in space; the uncertainty principle is the mathematical statement that a narrow packet contains a broad range of frequencies. The Dirac equation extends this to relativistic spin-½ particles and predicts antimatter.

Waves are constrained by differential equations. The heat equation ∂F/∂t = α∇²F + βQ governs diffusion, where Q is a source term. The wave equation ∂²F/∂t² = α∇²F + βP governs mechanical vibrations and electromagnetic fields, where P is a driving term; its solutions define the behaviours waves can exhibit, and the same equation underpins sound, light, and gravitational waves, the ripples in spacetime predicted by general relativity and first observed on 11 February 2016.

No single definition captures every kind of wave, and whether crowd waves or traffic patterns count as physical waves is debated, but in every case the disturbance spreads by neighbour-to-neighbour coupling, at finite speed, in a medium or field whose response to local change produces the propagation.
