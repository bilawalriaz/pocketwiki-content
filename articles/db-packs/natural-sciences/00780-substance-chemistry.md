# Substance (chemistry)

A chemical substance is matter with a fixed composition and characteristic properties, existing as either an element (one kind of atom) or a compound (two or more elements joined by chemical bonds). The concept was solidified in the late 18th century by Joseph Proust's law of constant composition, which showed that every sample of a given compound contains the same elements in the same mass proportions. Proust demonstrated this with basic copper carbonate, and the principle still defines what counts as a single substance.

Substances change phase between solid, liquid, and gas without losing their identity, because phase changes do not alter composition. They can also undergo chemical reactions to form new substances, each with its own fixed composition. Substances that resist such reactions under normal conditions are inert.

## Elements

An element is a substance made of one type of atom and cannot be broken down by chemical means, though nuclear transmutation can change one element into another. As of 2025, 118 elements are recognised, of which roughly 80 are stable. Many elements exhibit allotropy, meaning the same atoms arrange into different structural forms with distinct properties, as with dioxygen (O₂) and ozone (O₃). Elements are broadly grouped as metals (lustrous, conductive, malleable), non-metals (high electronegativity, tending to form negative ions), and metalloids (intermediate properties).

## Compounds

Compounds form when elements combine through chemical bonds: covalent bonds share electrons between atoms, ionic bonds arise from the electrostatic attraction of oppositely charged ions, and coordination complexes involve dative bonds in which a ligand donates both electrons to a central metal or non-metal atom, as in the deep-blue tetraamminecopper(II) complex. Some ligands bind through several atoms at once and are called chelates. Compounds containing carbon–hydrogen frameworks are organic; the rest are inorganic.

Two compounds can share identical elemental composition yet differ because their atoms are arranged differently. Such compounds are isomers; when they interconvert spontaneously, they are tautomers; glucose, for example, exists as both a ring and an open chain in equilibrium.

## Substances, mixtures, and edge cases

The defining feature of a substance is constant composition. Mixtures contain two or more substances with no fixed ratio (butter, soil, gasoline), and they separate by physical means such as chromatography or magnetism. A compound forms only through a chemical reaction that locks elements into fixed proportions, and once formed it shows properties not found in its elements; iron and sulphur, for instance, react to give iron(II) sulphide, which cannot be separated back mechanically.

Several cases complicate the picture. Alloys are mixtures of metals that the Chemical Abstracts Service registers as if they were single substances. Non-stoichiometric compounds such as palladium hydride tolerate variable ratios of their constituent elements. Polymers are almost always mixtures of chains of differing lengths and are defined by their precursor, their reaction, and their molar mass distribution; polyethylene appears as low-density, high-density, and ultra-high-molecular-weight grades depending on how it is made. Minerals in geology are natural inorganic solids of uniform composition, rocks are aggregates of minerals, and many mineral families are solid solutions in which one atom substitutes for another, as sodium and potassium do in feldspars such as anorthoclase.

Legal frameworks such as the EU's REACH regulation stretch the everyday meaning of "substance" further, classifying materials as monoconstituent, multiconstituent, or of unknown or variable composition, and sometimes relying on the manufacturing process rather than exact chemical identity to define a material, as with charcoal.

## Identifying and naming substances

Every substance has a systematic IUPAC name and a common name; glucose carries the IUPAC designation 6-(hydroxymethyl)oxane-2,3,4,5-tetrol. The Chemical Abstracts Service assigns a unique registry number to each indexed substance, and as of February 2021 its literature contained roughly 177 million organic and inorganic entries. Computer-readable formats such as SMILES and InChI let software exchange and search structural information reliably.

In industry, "chemical" is used loosely to include mixtures, and pure substances are sorted into bulk (large scale, low cost), fine (smaller scale, higher value), and research (laboratory scale, most expensive) categories, with grades such as technical versus pharmaceutical/USP reflecting how tightly purity is controlled.
