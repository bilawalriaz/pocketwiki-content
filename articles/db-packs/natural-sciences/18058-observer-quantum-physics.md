# Observer (quantum physics)

In quantum mechanics, an "observer" is any physical process that records the outcome of a measurement, whether a photographic plate, a detector, or a person. Some interpretations of quantum mechanics give this recording step a central role, tying it to the long-running puzzle of how a quantum system settles into a single, definite result.

## The observer effect and the word "observable"

Any measurement is an interaction. A detector must exchange energy or momentum with a quantum object to learn its properties, and that exchange inevitably disturbs the system. This disturbance is called the observer effect. The same idea, recast in formal language, gave "observable" a technical meaning: it now denotes a self-adjoint operator, the mathematical object whose possible measurement results are its eigenvalues.

## Collapse of the wave function

A quantum system before measurement is described by a wave function ψ written as a superposition of eigenstates, ψ = Σₙ aₙ ψₙ, with the coefficients aₙ giving the amplitude for each outcome. When a measurement is performed, ψ collapses into one of the eigenstates ψₙ associated with the observable being measured, with probabilities fixed by the amplitudes. The collapse is discontinuous and not time-reversible: once the system is in one eigenstate, it has decohered from the others, and no future strong quantum interference between them is possible. The kind of measurement performed therefore determines the end-state.

The quantum Zeno effect shows this experimentally. A quantum state that would decay if left alone can be frozen by continuous observation, because each observation restarts the decay clock. The dynamics of a system under continuous observation follow a stochastic master equation known as the Belavkin equation. Delayed choice quantum eraser experiments go further: observing the detection records after a photon has already been produced can collapse the wave function and fix a definite back-history.

## The wave function is not a physical object

A common misconception treats ψ as a real thing like an atom. That view forces one to invent an external mechanism, outside the usual time evolution, that forces ψ to "jump" on measurement. The cleaner picture is that ψ is an abstract mathematical function encoding all the statistical information an observer can obtain from measurements. Because ψ only summarises what measurements can reveal, there is no separate physical mystery in its abrupt change after a measurement; the mathematics simply updates to reflect the new information.

Entanglement sharpens this issue. Bell's theorem shows that a measurement on one particle of an entangled pair can appear to have a nonlocal effect on the other. Modelling the observer itself as a quantum system then runs into decoherence, the loss of coherent superposition through interaction with an environment.

## What "observer" means in the Copenhagen interpretation

The Copenhagen interpretation is the most widely accepted view among physicists. In it, an observer is just a physical process. Werner Heisenberg stressed that the observer has only the function of registering decisions, processes in space and time, and that registration, the transition from "possible" to "actual," cannot be omitted from the interpretation. Niels Bohr made the same point by appealing to permanent marks such as a spot on a photographic plate caused by an electron impact; for Bohr, these irreversible amplification effects are what observation really is, and the description is objective, requiring no reference to any particular observer. Asher Peres drew a parallel with special relativity: an "observer" there is a fictitious physicist who may as well be an inanimate automaton sending and receiving signals. Nothing in the formalism requires a conscious mind.

Critics note that if observers can themselves be observed, paradoxes like Wigner's friend appear, and it becomes unclear how much consciousness is required. John Bell pressed the point: was the wave function waiting to jump for thousands of millions of years until a single-celled creature appeared, or did it need a PhD?

## Anthropocentric readings

The early language of quantum theory has fed a recurring philosophical disquiet and a market of new-age and spiritual claims that grant the observer a special, sometimes conscious, role. Fritjof Capra, for example, declared that the human observer is necessary even to define the properties of an atomic object. No peer-reviewed research supports such claims.

## Confusion with the uncertainty principle

The observer effect is often conflated with the uncertainty principle, even, on the evidence, by Heisenberg himself. In its standard form, the uncertainty principle sets an upper bound on the precision with which position and momentum can be measured simultaneously: improving one requires worsening the other. An alternative formulation does treat the disturbance caused by measurement and the resulting error, but this is not the sense in which "uncertainty principle" is normally used.
