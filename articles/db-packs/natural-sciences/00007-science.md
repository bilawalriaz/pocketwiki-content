# Science

Science is a systematic discipline that builds knowledge as testable explanations of nature and society. Its core tool is the scientific method, an empirical cycle of observation, hypothesis, testing, and conclusion. Modern science divides into three branches. Natural sciences (physics, chemistry, biology, astronomy, earth science) study the physical world through observation and experiment. Social sciences (anthropology, economics, psychology, sociology) study human behaviour using historical methods, case studies, and statistics, since controlled experiments are often impractical. Formal sciences (logic, mathematics, theoretical computer science) use deductive reasoning to derive theorems from axioms rather than the scientific method; whether they count as science is disputed, though they underpin empirical fields. Applied sciences such as engineering and medicine pursue practical goals. Computational science increasingly relies on simulation, machine learning, and AI, which require human guidance and can introduce bias.

## History

Scientific thinking emerged gradually over tens of thousands of years, with no single origin. Bronze Age Egypt and Mesopotamia (c. 3000–1200 BCE) produced the earliest written records of mathematics, astronomy, and medicine. In the 6th–5th centuries BCE, the Milesian school (Thales, Anaximander, Anaximenes) first explained natural phenomena without supernatural causes, and pre-Socratics distinguished "nature" from "convention." Pythagoreans advanced mathematical science; Leucippus and Democritus developed atomism; Hippocrates founded systematic medical science. Aristotle built a teleological philosophy. Aristarchus's 3rd-century-BCE heliocentrism was rejected as physically impossible, so Ptolemy's geocentric model held until the Renaissance.

After Rome's collapse, Latin encyclopaedists like Isidore of Seville preserved knowledge while Byzantium improved it. John Philoponus challenged Aristotle's physics with an impetus theory later cited by Galileo. Under the Abbasids, Nestorian and Miaphysite translators built Baghdad's House of Wisdom. Ibn al-Haytham used controlled experiments in optics, and Avicenna's Canon of Medicine remained standard until the 18th century. The University of Bologna (1088) drove demand for Latin translations.

Movable-type printing spread from Mainz around 1440, reaching 270-plus cities and 20 million volumes by 1500. Copernicus's heliocentric model eliminated Ptolemy's equant, and Galileo's 1610 telescopic observations ended geocentric orthodoxy. Brahe's data let Kepler show planetary orbits are elliptical, making astronomy the first modern science and proving phenomena follow physical laws.

The 19th century brought professionalisation, scientific journals, Dalton's atomic theory, thermodynamics, electromagnetic theory (Ørsted to Hertz), radioactivity (Becquerel, Marie Curie), the electron, evolution by natural selection (Darwin, Wallace), and Mendelian genetics. The 20th and 21st centuries added the modern synthesis uniting evolution and genetics, relativity and quantum mechanics, integrated circuits, satellites, the internet, and milestones including the Human Genome Project (2003), the Higgs boson (2013), gravitational waves (2015), and the first black hole image (2019).

## Method

The scientific method assumes an objective reality governed by discoverable natural laws. Hypotheses, chosen for parsimony and consilience, yield falsifiable predictions tested by experiment, or by predicted observation in fields like astronomy and geology. Hypotheses that survive may join a scientific theory, a self-consistent framework binding many hypotheses. Bias is reduced through transparency, careful design, peer review, replication, and intersubjective verifiability. Basic research seeks knowledge; applied research seeks solutions. The first scientific journals (Journal des sçavans, Philosophical Transactions) appeared in 1665.

## Philosophy

Empiricism holds that knowledge comes from observation and includes inductivism, Bayesianism, and the hypothetico-deductive method. Rationalism (Descartes) locates knowledge in intellect. Popper's critical rationalism replaces verification with falsifiability, treating one method of criticism as universal. Instrumentalism treats theories as predictive tools; constructive empiricism requires only truth about observables. Kuhn described paradigms, consistent world-portraits within which normal puzzle-solving occurs, with paradigm shifts driven by accumulating anomalies; paradigm choice is social as well as logical, though not relativist. Methodological naturalism restricts science to natural explanations, and the is-ought problem shows facts alone cannot dictate action.

## Community

Peer review maintains quality. Scientists work in academia, industry, government, and nonprofits; women historically faced discrimination, with uneven progress since the late 20th century. Learned societies emerged in the Renaissance: Accademia dei Lincei (1603), Royal Society (1660), French Academy (1666), US National Academy (1863). Research funding totals 1.5–3% of GDP in developed countries, with industry performing about two-thirds of OECD R&D, universities 20%, and government 10%. Major challenges include anti-science attitudes, the replication crisis (most severe in social and behavioural sciences), misconduct, pseudoscience, censorship, and political bias.

## Key terms

- **Hypothesis**: a tentative explanatory claim yielding falsifiable predictions.
- **Scientific theory**: a self-consistent framework binding many hypotheses.
- **Falsifiability** (Popper): the requirement that theories can conflict with observation.
- **Paradigm shift** (Kuhn): replacement of a guiding world-portrait when anomalies accumulate.
- **Replication crisis**: a systemic problem of unreproducible study results.
