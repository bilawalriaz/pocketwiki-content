# Alcohol (chemistry)

An alcohol is an organic compound whose molecule carries at least one hydroxyl group (–OH) bonded to a saturated carbon atom (a carbon whose remaining bonds go to hydrogen or to other carbons, never to a doubly-bonded partner such as those in an aromatic ring). The general form is R₃C–OH, where R is a carbon substituent or a hydrogen. The simplest members are methanol (CH₃OH) and ethanol (C₂H₅OH); the class extends to large natural products such as sugar alcohols and cholesterol. The –OH is called a functional group because it is the part of the molecule that drives most of the chemistry: substitution at that site, and the polarity the group brings, explain nearly every property below.

## Why the hydroxyl group matters

Replacing one hydrogen of a hydrocarbon (a compound of only carbon and hydrogen) with –OH changes the molecule's behaviour in two ways. First, oxygen carries a partial negative charge and its hydrogen a partial positive one, so the group is polar and forms hydrogen bonds with water and with other alcohol molecules. Short-chain alcohols (methanol, ethanol, propanol) are fully miscible with water; 1-butanol is only moderately soluble; longer-chain alcohols behave like hydrocarbons again. Hydrogen bonding also raises boiling points: ethanol boils at 78.29 °C, against 69 °C for the similarly sized hexane and 34.6 °C for diethyl ether. Second, the –OH is a reactive site, so alcohols can be deprotonated, oxidised, dehydrated, esterified, or converted into alkyl halides, ethers, and alkenes.

## Names and classification

The word "alcohol" comes from the Arabic *al-kuḥl*, originally a fine powder (antimony sulfide) used as eyeliner; the meaning drifted through "distilled spirit," then "spirit of wine," before being generalised to the whole chemical class after 1850. The IUPAC (International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry) name is built from the parent alkane: drop the terminal *-e* and add *-ol* (ethane → ethanol). The position of –OH is inserted as a number when needed (propan-1-ol, propan-2-ol). Compounds with more than one –OH are called polyols and use suffixes such as *-diol* and *-triol*; glycerol is propane-1,2,3-triol. When a higher-priority group such as a carboxylic acid is present, the IUPAC name uses the prefix *hydroxy-* instead (1-hydroxy-2-propanone).

By structure, the carbon bearing the –OH can carry one, two, or three other carbons, giving primary (1°), secondary (2°), and tertiary (3°) alcohols. Methanol is the simplest primary alcohol; propan-2-ol is secondary; *tert*-butanol is tertiary. Phenols, where –OH sits on an aromatic ring, are not classified as alcohols because their chemistry is distinct.

## Making alcohols

The most important biological and many industrial routes rely on hydroxylation: installing –OH using oxygen or a related oxidant, often catalysed by enzymes called hydroxylases. The body uses hydroxylation to convert fat-soluble toxins into water-soluble forms for excretion. Specific industrial processes include the Ziegler process (linear alcohols from ethylene, triethylaluminium, then oxidation and hydrolysis), hydroformylation of alkenes (alkene + CO + H₂, then hydrogenation, to give fatty alcohols for detergents), acid-catalysed hydration of alkenes (adding water across the double bond, used for ethanol, isopropanol, and *tert*-butanol), and fermentation (yeast, or the bacterium *Clostridium acetobutylicum*, turns sugars into ethanol or butanol with CO₂ as a by-product). In the laboratory, alcohols are commonly made by reducing aldehydes or ketones with sodium borohydride or lithium aluminium hydride, and by reacting Grignard reagents (organomagnesium compounds, RMgX) with carbonyl compounds.

## How alcohols react

Deprotonation. With pKₐ around 16–19 in water (a logarithmic measure of acid strength: lower means stronger), alcohols are slightly weaker acids than water. Strong bases such as sodium hydride or metallic sodium remove the –OH proton to give alkoxides (RO⁻), useful as bases and as nucleophiles (electron-rich species that attack electron-poor centres).

Substitution. The –OH can be replaced by a halide. Tertiary alcohols react with HCl directly; primary and secondary alcohols usually need thionyl chloride or phosphorus tribromide. The Barton–McCombie deoxygenation removes the oxygen entirely to give an alkane.

Dehydration. Heating an alcohol with a strong acid such as sulfuric acid removes water to form an alkene. Tertiary alcohols dehydrate near room temperature; primary alcohols need higher temperatures. The dominant product is the most substituted alkene, following Zaitsev's rule.

Esterification. Alcohols combine with carboxylic acids under acid catalysis in the Fischer esterification to give esters, releasing water; the same logic is used to make tosylate esters for synthesis.

Oxidation. Primary alcohols oxidise first to aldehydes and then, via a transient aldehyde hydrate, to carboxylic acids; secondary alcohols stop at ketones; tertiary alcohols resist oxidation. Common reagents include the Collins reagent, Dess–Martin periodinane, potassium permanganate, and the Jones reagent.

## Toxicity

Simple alcohols have low acute toxicity, with LD₅₀ (the dose lethal to 50 % of test animals) values of 2–5 g/kg for pentanols, hexanols, and octanols in rats, and all are mild skin irritants. Methanol and ethylene glycol are the dangerous exceptions: their metabolites (products of breakdown in the body) attack the optic nerve and the kidneys, respectively. Ethanol binds more tightly than they do to the liver enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase, so giving ethanol in a poisoning case lets the body excrete methanol or ethylene glycol unchanged.

## Where alcohols come from in nature

Alcohols are pervasive: sugars, cellulose, and lignin all carry –OH groups, and biomass fixes roughly 180 billion tons of such carbohydrate per year (as of 2014). Simple alcohols such as methanol, ethanol, and propanol also occur in small natural quantities, but world supply is dominated by industrial synthesis. The most important industrial alcohols are methanol (about 12 million tons per year as of 1980, used for formaldehyde and as a fuel additive), ethanol (beverages, fuel, solvent, sterilising), C₄–C₁₁ alcohols (solvents and plasticisers for PVC), and C₁₂–C₁₈ fatty alcohols (precursors to detergents).
