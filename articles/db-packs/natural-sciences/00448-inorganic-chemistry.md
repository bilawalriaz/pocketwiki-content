# Inorganic chemistry

Inorganic chemistry is the study of the synthesis, structure, and behaviour of chemical compounds that are not primarily carbon-based, including the large subdiscipline of organometallic chemistry, in which metals bond to carbon. The boundary with organic chemistry is not absolute. A compound's classification can be practical rather than philosophical: fullerenes and binary carbon oxides contain carbon but are usually treated as inorganic.

## Composition and bonding

Inorganic compounds include the minerals that make up most of the Earth's crust, such as iron sulfide (pyrite) and calcium sulfate (gypsum). They also operate inside living things. Sodium chloride is an electrolyte in body fluids, magnesium sits at the centre of ATP, the molecule that stores and releases cellular energy, and the polyphosphate backbone of DNA is an inorganic framework, which is one reason carbon-based life depends heavily on non-carbon chemistry.

Bonding in this field spans the full spectrum. Sodium chloride (NaCl) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) are largely ionic, while sulfur dioxide (SO₂) and iron pentacarbonyl (Fe(CO)₅) are strongly covalent. Most real compounds sit between these extremes as polar covalent. Acid–base behaviour is described by Lewis theory, which defines an acid as an electron-pair acceptor and a base as an electron-pair donor. A metal cation such as Na⁺ acts as a Lewis acid by accepting a lone pair from a coordinating water molecule, and the water is the Lewis base that donates it.

## Subdivisions

The field divides by structural family. Organometallic chemistry covers metal–carbon bonds and underpins much of industrial catalysis. Cluster chemistry studies metal–metal bonded units, bridging molecular compounds and extended solids. Bioinorganic chemistry examines how metals function in biology. Solid-state chemistry deals with extended solids whose collective properties depend on the whole crystal rather than individual molecules.

## Coordination chemistry

A coordination compound centres a metal atom or ion surrounded by ligands: ions or molecules such as water, ammonia, or cyanide that bind through a lone electron pair. The resulting three-dimensional arrangements can be chiral. In 1893 Alfred Werner resolved a coordination compound into mirror-image forms, or enantiomers, proving that chirality is not exclusive to carbon-based molecules. Main-group compounds, drawn from periodic-table groups I–VII and 0 (excluding hydrogen), range from silica (SiO₂) in quartz to nitrous oxide (N₂O).

## Theoretical tools

Qualitative models let chemists predict structure and properties without solving the full quantum equations. VSEPR (valence-shell electron-pair repulsion) explains why water bends and methane forms a tetrahedron: bonded atoms and lone pairs arrange to minimise repulsion, producing shapes predictable from the number of electron domains. Crystal field theory explains the colours and magnetism of transition-metal compounds by treating ligands as negative charges that split the energies of the metal's d-orbitals. Group theory supplies the mathematical symmetry language that underpins spectroscopy and orbital analysis. For transition metals, d-orbital occupancy also governs how fast one ligand can swap for another, with rates that vary by roughly twenty orders of magnitude across the periodic table.

## Industrial applications

A nation's industrial output was once gauged by its production of sulfuric acid, the workhorse acid of fertilizers, petroleum refining, and mineral processing. The Haber–Bosch process, developed by Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch in the early 1900s, fixes atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia over an iron catalyst. The ammonia feeds fertiliser production. Vanadium pentoxide (V₂O₅) catalyses the oxidation of sulfur dioxide to sulfur trioxide in the same sulfuric acid chain. Portland cement, the binder of concrete, is another fundamentally inorganic product. Reaction mechanisms in this field often involve redox steps, either direct electron transfer or atom transfer, and a coordinated ligand frequently reacts differently from the same ligand free in solution, which is the core principle behind homogeneous catalysis.

## Characterisation and synthesis

Determining structure relies on X-ray crystallography for precise three-dimensional atomic positions, and on UV-visible, NMR, infrared, and electron-spin resonance spectroscopies to identify bonding and oxidation states. Electrochemistry probes redox behaviour. Synthesis routes are chosen by the physical nature of the reactants: air-sensitive compounds demand Schlenk lines or glove boxes under inert gas, volatile species are manipulated on vacuum manifolds, and refractory solids are grown in sealed tubes inside furnaces.

## Origins and current frontier

Humans handled inorganic compounds long before the field had a name. Elemental sulfur was in use around 3000 BC, and in the 1770s Lavoisier and Priestley identified oxygen (O₂), establishing the stoichiometric laws that govern every quantitative reaction since. Modern research concentrates on nanoscience, where the distinction between very large molecular clusters and bulk solids grows indistinct, on catalysts that selectively break or form specific bonds, and on bioinorganic systems where metals drive processes such as photosynthesis and nitrogen fixation. The Haber–Bosch catalyst itself is still a target of active research because replacing the iron with more efficient or sustainable alternatives would reshape global fertiliser supply.
