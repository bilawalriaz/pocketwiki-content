# Artificial gravity

Artificial gravity is an inertial force that mimics gravity, almost always produced by spinning a spacecraft. In a rotating habitat the hull pushes inward as centripetal force and the occupant feels an outward push, the centrifugal force of the rotating frame. The two have equal magnitude by Newton's third law, so a chosen spin rate and radius can in principle reproduce any desired "g." Linear acceleration produces the same effect: a constantly accelerating rocket pushes the floor into the occupant's feet, indistinguishable from gravity by the equivalence principle.

## Why bother

Long spaceflight in weightlessness is medically costly. Within 24 hours blood volume drops about 10%, and white blood cell count and muscle mass decline. Over longer missions, brain swelling and eyesight degradation appear. On return, fluids pool in the legs, heart rate rises, and blood pressure falls. Artificial gravity would remove weightlessness itself rather than treat its symptoms, which makes it attractive for a roughly six-month Mars trip, where exercise, diet, and Pingvin suits only partially help. Soviet planners in 1964 feared a 14-day limit; missions have since run up to 437 consecutive days, but the underlying health risks remain.

## How rotation differs from real gravity

Three differences appear with increasing severity at small radius.

1. Force scales with distance from the spin axis, a = ω²r. A standing person's feet feel noticeably more than the head, so a small radius produces uncomfortable gradients.
2. Moving toward or away from the axis introduces the Coriolis effect, a sideways apparent force that stimulates the inner ear and causes dizziness. Spinning at 2 rpm or less is generally considered free of Coriolis problems, though people have adapted to as much as 23 rpm.
3. Shifting the spin axis, whether by people moving around or by changing rotation, can set the whole station wobbling. Pumping water ballast between sections is one proposed stabilizer.

## What has actually been tried

- Gemini 11 (1966) tethered its capsule to the Agena Target Vehicle with a 36-meter line and fired side thrusters to rotate the pair, producing about 0.00015 g, unfelt by the crew but enough that loose objects drifted toward the "floor."
- Bion 3 (1975) and Bion 4 (1977) carried centrifuges for biological specimens.
- Kosmos 936 (1977) showed centrifuged rats returning with higher bone density than uncentrifuged ones, but still below Earth-normal levels.
- The Mars Gravity Biosatellite was designed to give 15 mice 0.38 g (Mars surface level) at 32 rpm with a ~30 cm radius for five weeks in orbit, before cancellation in June 2009 for lack of funding.
- The Multi-Mission Space Exploration Vehicle and ISS Centrifuge Demo (both 2011 NASA proposals) outlined torus centrifuges 30–40 feet across delivering 0.08–0.69 g for crews of up to six.
- China reported a 60 cm magnetic levitation chamber in January 2022 for simulating lunar gravity, inspired in part by the magnetically levitated frog of Andre Geim and Michael Berry (Ig Nobel in Physics, 2000).

## Why it isn't flying

The relation a = ω²r fixes the central tradeoff. A small radius needs high spin, which worsens the head-to-foot gradient and Coriolis sickness. A large radius produces comfortable, near-uniform gravity but needs a large, expensive structure. No craft massive enough to provide Earth-like g comfortably exists, and the cost of building and launching one is large. For typical short flights, current symptom-by-symptom countermeasures (exercise, diet, suits) are considered acceptable, and funding for spinning habitats has stayed sporadic.

## Linear acceleration as an alternative

A rocket accelerating at 1 g (9.8 m/s²) produces uniform "gravity" toward the engines throughout the ship, with no moving parts. At 1 g sustained, a ship could reach Mars in days, or approach relativistic speeds in a year. The catch is propellant: chemical rockets can only fire for minutes, and any coast phase or mid-course flip means weightlessness. Electric thrusters such as Hall effect thrusters and VASIMR have very high specific impulse but low thrust, so they yield only milli-g levels, still useful for long cargo transfers and for settling cryogenic propellant in microgravity, where as little as 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻⁵ g of acceleration makes fluids behave in a tank the way they do on Earth.

Apart from rotation and acceleration, every proposed shortcut, from Podkletnov's spinning superconductor (a 2006 ESA-funded group reported 0.0001 g) to so-called "paragravity" devices, remains unconfirmed.
