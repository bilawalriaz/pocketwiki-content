# Chemical element

A chemical element is a species of atom defined by its number of protons, called its atomic number. The term also refers to a pure chemical substance made of a single element. Every atom with 6 protons is carbon, every atom with 79 protons is gold, and so on. As of November 2016, the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) recognized 118 elements: the first 94 occur naturally on Earth, and the remaining 24 are synthetic, made only in laboratories.

## Two Meanings

The word has two related senses: a kind of atom, the element as a component of compounds, and a free chemical substance, the free element. Water (H₂O) contains the elements hydrogen and oxygen, but it does not contain the substances H₂ or O₂. French distinguishes *élément chimique* (atom kind) from *corps simple* (substance), and Russian makes a parallel distinction.

## Atomic Structure and Isotopes

An atom has a dense nucleus of positively charged protons and neutral neutrons, surrounded by a cloud of electrons. The strong nuclear force, a short-range attraction, holds the nucleus together against electrical repulsion between protons. Stability depends on the neutron-to-proton ratio: as proton count grows, more neutrons are needed, so lead (82 protons) requires about 3 neutrons per 2 protons.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different neutron counts. Carbon has three natural isotopes: carbon-12 (≈98.9%), carbon-13 (≈1.1%), and carbon-14 (≈1 atom per trillion). Because neutron number has little effect on chemistry, atomic number, not mass number, identifies an element. The mass number (A) is the total nucleon count; the relative atomic mass of an element is the abundance-weighted average of its isotope masses. Chlorine's standard atomic weight of 35.453 u reflects about 76% chlorine-35 and 24% chlorine-37.

Elements 1 through 82 each have at least one stable isotope, except technetium (43) and promethium (61). Elements 83 through 94 are radioactive but long-lived enough to occur naturally. Bismuth-209 has the longest known alpha-decay half-life at 2×10¹⁹ years; tellurium-128 undergoes double-beta decay with a half-life of 2.25×10²⁴ years. The 24 heaviest elements have half-lives so short they must be synthesized.

## Chemical and Physical Properties

Quantum mechanics arranges electrons into shells, each holding a fixed maximum of 2n² electrons (2, 8, 18, 32…). This shell structure governs chemical behavior and produces the periodic patterns of the periodic table. Elements are classed as metals, nonmetals, or metalloids, which are intermediate and often semiconducting. Specialized groups include alkali metals, alkaline earths, transition metals, halogens, noble gases, lanthanides, and actinides.

At standard temperature and pressure, only bromine and mercury are liquid; caesium melts at 28.4 °C and gallium at 29.8 °C. Allotropy is the existence of multiple structural forms; carbon appears as diamond, graphite, graphene, fullerenes, and nanotubes. Density generally rises with atomic number; noble gases and alkali metals are lightest.

## Origin of the Elements

Hydrogen and helium formed in the first 20 minutes after the Big Bang, in roughly 3:1 mass ratio, with traces of lithium and beryllium. Heavier natural elements came from stellar nucleosynthesis (carbon through iron), explosive nucleosynthesis in supernovae and neutron-star mergers (heavier than iron), and cosmic-ray spallation (lithium, beryllium, boron). On Earth, carbon-14 is still produced from nitrogen by cosmic rays, and argon-40 from decay of potassium-40. Earth's crust differs from solar composition because light and volatile elements were lost; oxygen is the most abundant element by mass in the bulk Earth. Life depends almost entirely on six elements, abbreviated CHNOPS: carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, and sulfur.

## Nomenclature

Since 1947, IUPAC has set official names and symbols. The letter-based notation derived from Latin names was introduced by Jöns Jacob Berzelius in 1814. Eleven common elements have symbols detached from their English names: Fe (iron), Hg (mercury), Sn (tin), Au (gold), Ag (silver), Pb (lead), Cu (copper), Sb (antimony), Na (sodium), K (potassium), and W (tungsten). Element names are lowercase in English; symbols are always capitalized, as in ¹²C or ²³⁵U. Hydrogen's isotopes have special symbols: H (protium), D (deuterium), T (tritium). New elements are named for properties, minerals, places, mythical subjects, astronomical objects, or scientists.

## Historical Development

Plato formalized the classical elements earth, water, air, and fire around 360 BCE; Aristotle later added aether. Robert Boyle's 1661 *The Sceptical Chymist* argued that matter consists of irreducible particles, a view called corpuscularism. Hennig Brand discovered phosphorus in 1669, the first new element identified. Antoine Lavoisier's 1789 *Elements of Chemistry* listed 33 elements, and Dmitri Mendeleev's 1869 periodic table arranged 63. Henry Moseley's 1913 discovery that nuclear charge defines atomic number established the modern criterion: an element exists if it has isotopes with lifetimes above 10⁻¹⁴ seconds. The first transuranium element, neptunium, was made in 1940, and by January 2016, all 118 elements had been confirmed.

## Key Terms

- **Atomic number**: number of protons, defining the element.
- **Mass number (A)**: total protons plus neutrons.
- **Isotope**: atoms of the same element with different neutron counts.
- **Nuclide**: atoms of a specific proton number, neutron number, and nuclear state.
- **Relative atomic mass**: abundance-weighted average of isotope masses.
- **Allotropy**: multiple structural forms of one element.
- **Nucleosynthesis**: creation of new atomic nuclei.
- **Metalloid**: element with properties between metals and nonmetals, often a semiconductor.
- **Transuranic element**: element with atomic number greater than 92.
