# Magnetic flux quantum

Magnetic flux is the magnetic field multiplied by the area it passes through. In a superconductor, that flux can only take fixed, discrete values: integer multiples of a constant called the magnetic flux quantum, Φ₀. This restriction is magnetic flux quantization.

## Where the quantum comes from

A superconductor is described by a complex wave function, the order parameter, whose phase θ appears as Ψ = Ψ₀ e^(iθ). A complex wave function is single-valued only if its phase returns to itself after any closed loop. A magnetic field shifts that phase, so the field can only be present in amounts that make the total shift a whole multiple of 2π. That condition locks the flux into discrete values and sets the size of one quantum.

The supercurrent is carried by Cooper pairs, bound electron pairs with charge q = 2e. Using minimal coupling and setting the current density to zero in the superconductor's interior gives a phase gradient of (q/ℏ)A. Integrating around the hole and applying Stokes' theorem yields Φ_B = (ℏ/q)(2π) = h/(2e). One flux quantum is therefore

Φ₀ = h/(2e) ≈ 2.067 833 848… × 10⁻¹⁵ Wb.

Because h and e are fundamental constants, Φ₀ is the same for every superconductor. Fritz London predicted it in 1948 from the phenomenological London equations; Deaver and Fairbank, and independently Doll and Näbauer, observed it experimentally in 1961.

## The Dirac version

Substituting q = e, the charge of a single electron, into the same derivation gives a quantum twice as large, h/e. This is the Dirac magnetic flux quantum, named for Paul Dirac's 1931 work on magnetic monopoles. It applies wherever the circulating charge is one electron rather than a Cooper pair.

## Geometry and vortices

Inside a bulk superconductor the magnetic induction B is zero. Screening currents flowing within a London penetration depth λ_L, typically around 100 nm of the surface, expel the field. Flux quantization holds only when the integration path lies several λ_L inside the superconductor, away from those screening currents. In thin wires or shells where no such path exists, the effective flux quantum differs from Φ₀.

In type II superconductors placed in a field between the first and second critical fields, the field penetrates as Abrikosov vortices: tubes of normal material of diameter set by the coherence length ξ, each surrounded by circulating supercurrents. Every vortex carries exactly one Φ₀.

## Measuring and using flux quanta

The Josephson effect relates the voltage across a Josephson junction to a frequency, with the Josephson constant K_J = 1/Φ₀ = 2e/h = 483 597.848 416 98… GHz/V as the constant of proportionality. Before the 2019 SI revision, flux quantization and the quantum Hall effect were used to measure h with extreme precision. Since then, h and e have fixed values, so K_J and the von Klitzing constant R_K = h/e² are fixed too; the Josephson and quantum Hall effects now serve as the practical realisation of electrical units. SQUIDs, among the most sensitive magnetometers available, sense the discrete jumps in flux that occur when a superconducting loop crosses from one Φ₀ to the next.
