# Visible-light astronomy

Visible-light astronomy, also called optical astronomy, is astronomical observation carried out with telescopes sensitive to visible light. It uses the narrow band of the electromagnetic spectrum that human eyes can see, roughly 380 to 750 nanometres in wavelength, sitting between infrared and ultraviolet. Other branches use radio, infrared, ultraviolet, X-ray, or gamma-ray wavelengths, each requiring different instruments because Earth's atmosphere blocks or distorts them differently.

Because visible light needs no special detector, naked-eye observation counts as visible-light astronomy too. That makes it the oldest and the most widely practised kind of astronomy, and the only one humans pursued for most of history. The constellations used today, mostly Greek names, are a record of that long pre-telescopic era.

The optical telescope changed what could be resolved. Hans Lippershey, a German-Dutch spectacle-maker, was the first person recorded to apply for a telescope patent, though it is unclear whether he actually built the first one. In the following year, Galileo Galilei built a telescope of about 3× magnification from Lippershey's uncertain description and later improved versions reached about 30×. His early telescopes were simple terrestrial spyglasses that produced upright, magnified images. On 25 August 1609 he demonstrated one of about 8 or 9× to Venetian lawmakers, and in March 1610 he published his first telescopic sky observations in *Sidereus Nuncius* (Starry Messenger). The human eye remained the only astronomical image sensor until photographic plates arrived in the 19th century.

Modern visible-light astronomy spans amateurs, who can now buy capable telescopes cheaply, and large government programmes such as NASA's. The sharpest results come from space telescopes placed above Earth's atmosphere, because air blurs and absorbs light; in space a telescope is also free of weather and daylight, so it can observe at any time. The Hubble Space Telescope, launched into low Earth orbit in 1990, is still operating; its four main instruments cover the near-ultraviolet, visible, and near-infrared ranges, and its images helped pin down the rate of cosmic expansion.

Three designs dominate optical telescopes. Refracting telescopes use lenses and are cheap and simple, so amateurs favour them for bright targets like the Moon and planets. Reflecting telescopes use mirrors and are the workhorse of scientific observatories. Catadioptric telescopes combine lenses and mirrors. Each design has its own aberration: refractors show chromatic aberration, where lens dispersion focuses colours at slightly different points and coloured fringes appear on bright–dark boundaries; reflectors suffer off-axis aberrations near the edges of the field of view; catadioptric designs vary because there are many of them. Aberrations are why professional astronomy moved from lenses to mirrors, and ultimately above the atmosphere.

Seeing conditions matter as much as the instrument. Light pollution from artificial lighting and the natural brightening caused by a full Moon both wash out faint objects. Filters and shielded fixtures reduce the problem, but professional and serious amateur observers still travel to dark sites far from cities. Putting telescopes in space eliminates light pollution, atmospheric blurring, and weather in one step.

Most observed targets need no telescope at all. The Moon is the brightest and largest night-sky object and the basis of many calendars. Meteors, often called shooting stars, are easiest to watch during showers such as the Perseids and Leonids, when many appear in a short window. Venus is bright enough to see in daylight and is the easiest planet without aid; Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn are also visible to the naked eye, though binoculars or a telescope show more detail. Constellations and bright stars were historically used for navigation at sea; the Big Dipper, part of Ursa Major, is among the most recognisable, and the patterns also serve as a coordinate system for locating fainter objects nearby.
