# Conservation of energy

The law of conservation of energy states that the total energy of an isolated system remains constant over time. In a closed system, energy changes only when it enters or leaves across the boundary. Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it is transformed or transferred between forms. When dynamite explodes, its chemical energy becomes the kinetic and potential energy of fragments, plus heat and sound, and summing these exactly recovers the chemical energy lost.

Classical physics treated mass and energy as separately conserved. Special relativity merged them through E = mc², so the conserved quantity is mass-energy. This matters in extreme conditions: nuclear reactions release binding energy from a measured mass defect, and it governed the early universe and processes such as black hole Hawking radiation.

The first law of thermodynamics expresses conservation for closed thermodynamic systems:

dU = δQ − δW,

where δQ is the energy added by heating, δW is the energy lost as the system does work, and dU is the change in internal energy. The δ symbols mark that heat and work describe modes of energy transfer across the boundary, not stored forms. Internal energy is a state function, so its changes are fully accounted for by these transfers.

Noether's theorem gives the deeper reason. Every continuous symmetry of a physical theory has a corresponding conserved quantity, and the symmetry of time translation (the laws of physics do not depend on time) implies energy conservation. A system that is not itself time-invariant still conserves energy once the external system it exchanges energy with is included.

In special relativity, energy is one component of a conserved energy-momentum four-vector. A massive particle's energy includes its rest energy, E = mc², plus kinetic energy. The invariant mass, the Minkowski norm of the four-vector, is the same for all observers, even when they disagree on the energy. An electron and a positron can annihilate into photons that have no rest mass; the photons still carry energy, and the system's total energy and invariant mass are unchanged. The continuous beta-decay spectrum that initially seemed to violate energy conservation was resolved in 1933 when Fermi proposed the antineutrino, which carries the missing energy.

In quantum mechanics, the energy observable is the Hamiltonian operator. When the Hamiltonian is time-independent, the expectation value of energy is conserved under unitary evolution, and quantum Noether's theorem enforces local conservation. The Born rule can yield individual measurement outcomes above or below the expectation value, but no confirmed experiment has demonstrated a genuine violation.

General relativity complicates global energy bookkeeping. In expanding universes, photons redshift, tethers gain tension, and vacuum energy grows with volume. Energy-momentum is represented by a pseudotensor that depends on coordinates, so in cosmological spacetimes governed by the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric, global energy conservation is not well defined. In asymptotically flat or static spacetimes, including good approximations of Earth and the Solar System, energy is exactly conserved.

A consequence is that a perpetual motion machine of the first kind is impossible: no system without an external energy supply can deliver unlimited energy. Nuclear-physics experiments have verified the law to about one part in 10¹⁵. Any future theory, including quantum gravity, must explain why energy appears exactly conserved in every terrestrial test.
