# Chemical compound

A chemical compound is a substance made of identical molecules that contain atoms from two or more different chemical elements, held together by chemical bonds in a fixed stoichiometric ratio, a set whole-number proportion such as the 2:1 of hydrogen to oxygen in H₂O. This fixed ratio, written as a chemical formula, is what separates a compound from a pure element. Hydrogen gas (H₂) and sulfur (S₈) contain many atoms but only one element each, so they are not compounds. Over 350,000 compounds are registered globally, each assigned a unique CAS number by the Chemical Abstracts Service.

Compounds differ from mixtures because their atoms are chemically bonded rather than blended, and their properties are distinct from the elements that form them. Sodium, a reactive metal, and chlorine, a toxic gas, combine to give sodium chloride (NaCl), a stable edible crystal with neither of the originals' hazards.

## Historical development

The modern idea of a compound began in 1661 when Robert Boyle published *The Sceptical Chymist*, the first work to use "compound" in roughly its current sense. Boyle distinguished compounds from elements by recovering mercury unchanged after reacting it with nitric acid and sulfur, showing components keep their identity inside the compound. He proposed that atoms of different elements have specific sizes and shapes, allowing them to combine in many proportions to produce an enormous variety of distinct substances. In 1724, Isaac Watts formalised the split in *Logick*: elements are simple substances that cannot be broken down into other kinds, while compounds are made of two or more such substances. Watts rejected the Aristotelian four elements and the Paracelsian three principles (salt, sulfur, mercury) in favour of definitions grounded in experiment.

## Types of compounds

Compounds are classified by the type of bonding that holds them together.

**Molecular compounds** are neutral groups of atoms called molecules, joined by covalent bonds (shared pairs of electrons). They are heteronuclear, meaning they contain different elements, unlike homonuclear elemental molecules like O₂. A single molecule is the smallest piece that still has the substance's properties.

**Ionic compounds** are built from cations (positive ions) and anions (negative ions) held by electrostatic attraction. They form continuous three-dimensional crystal lattices rather than separate molecules. Sodium chloride (Na⁺Cl⁻) and ammonium carbonate (NH₄⁺CO₃²⁻) are standard examples. They tend to be hard, brittle, high-melting solids that do not conduct electricity as solids, because their ions are fixed in place, but conduct well when melted or dissolved, because the ions can move. Those containing hydroxide (OH⁻) or oxide (O²⁻) ions are bases; most others are salts.

**Intermetallic compounds** are ordered alloys of two or more metals, held by metallic bonding. They are typically hard and brittle and retain their strength at high temperatures.

**Coordination complexes** have a central metal atom, the coordination centre, surrounded by ligands (ions or molecules) attached through coordinate covalent bonds, in which the ligand donates both electrons of the shared pair. Many transition-metal compounds belong to this class.

## The bonds themselves

Each bond type arises from how atoms handle their outermost electrons. Covalent bonds form when atoms of similar electronegativity (their power to attract electrons) share electrons, usually between nonmetals near each other on the periodic table, letting both atoms complete a stable octet (eight-electron outer shell). Ionic bonds form when a metal transfers one or more valence (outer-shell) electrons to a nonmetal, producing a cation and an anion that attract each other. Metallic bonds, found in intermetallics, involve a delocalised "sea" of electrons shared across all the metal atoms.

Beyond the bonds within a molecule, intermolecular forces govern how molecules attract each other. Hydrogen bonding is a strong dipole-dipole attraction (between molecules with permanent positive and negative ends) that occurs when a hydrogen atom already bonded to a highly electronegative atom (nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine) is pulled toward another electronegative atom nearby. London dispersion forces are much weaker, arising from temporary dipoles created by fleeting shifts in electron density; they are the only force holding together nonpolar substances such as noble gases or hydrocarbons at low temperatures.

## Chemical reactions

A chemical reaction rearranges the atoms in compounds by breaking some bonds and forming new ones, producing different compounds with their own fixed ratios. A simple double-displacement reaction shows the pattern: AB + CD → AD + CB. The bonds within AB and CD break, the partners swap, and the new compounds AD and CB form.

## Exceptions to fixed ratios

Not every solid fits the fixed-ratio rule. Non-stoichiometric compounds, such as palladium hydride (PdHₓ, where x ranges between 0.02 and 0.58), have reproducible but non-integer atomic ratios. Many crystalline minerals once classified this way are now thought to be stoichiometric compounds with foreign atoms trapped in defects of the crystal lattice rather than truly variable bonding, and such minerals make up most of Earth's crust and mantle. Whether these materials count as true compounds or only as chemical substances remains an open debate, as does the broader inconsistency in how chemists use the two terms for solids that lack simple formulas.
