# Electricity

Electricity is the set of physical phenomena tied to the presence and motion of matter carrying an electric charge. It is part of electromagnetism, the unified behaviour of electric and magnetic fields, described by Maxwell's equations. Electricity powers modern lighting, transport, communications, and computation, and harnessing it drove the Second Industrial Revolution.

## Historical development

People knew of electric shocks from fish long before theory: Egyptian texts from c. 2750 BCE described electric fish, and around 600 BCE Thales of Miletus recorded static attraction from rubbed amber. In 1600, William Gilbert's *De Magnete* separated lodestone magnetism from static electricity and coined *electricus*, from Greek *elektron* ("amber"), giving English "electric" and "electricity" (first in print in 1646). In 1752, Franklin's kite experiment showed lightning is electrical. Volta's 1800 battery provided the first reliable source of electrical energy. Between 1819 and 1820, Ørsted and Ampère recognised electromagnetism; Faraday built the first electric motor in 1821 and discovered electromagnetic induction in 1831, which underlies the generator. Maxwell linked electricity, magnetism, and light in 1861–1862. Hertz discovered the photoelectric effect in 1887, and Einstein's 1905 quantum explanation of it helped launch quantum theory. The first working transistor, built in 1947 at Bell Labs, launched the electronics age.

## Electric charge

By convention, electrons carry negative charge and protons positive, definitions introduced by Franklin. A proton's charge, 1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹ coulombs, is the elementary charge; all observable charges are integer multiples of it. Like charges repel and opposite charges attract, in a force that falls off as the inverse square of the distance and is described by Coulomb's law. The electromagnetic interaction is the second strongest of the four fundamental forces, about 10⁴² times stronger than gravity between two electrons, and acts at all distances. Net charge is conserved: it cannot be created or destroyed in an isolated system.

## Current and the electric field

Electric current is the flow of charge, measured in amperes. By convention, current runs from the most positive to the most negative point of a circuit, opposite to the motion of electrons in a metal. In metals, conduction is by electrons; in liquids and plasmas, by ions. Although individual charges drift slowly, the electric field that drives them propagates near light speed.

A charge creates an electric field in the surrounding space, defined as the force per unit positive test charge. The field is a vector that follows an inverse-square law. Field lines begin on positive charges, end on negative ones, meet conductors at right angles, and never cross. A hollow conductor carries all excess charge on its outer surface, with zero field inside, the principle of the Faraday cage. When the field exceeds a medium's breakdown strength, it conducts: in air, arcs form at about 30 kV/cm across small gaps, which is how lightning begins.

Electric potential, measured in volts, is the energy per unit charge needed to bring a positive test charge from infinity to a point; one volt is one joule per coulomb. The electric field is the local gradient of potential, in volts per metre. Earth is a convenient reference, called ground. Equipotential lines cross field lines at right angles and lie parallel to conductor surfaces.

## Electromagnetism and circuits

Ørsted's 1821 discovery that a current-carrying wire is surrounded by a magnetic field showed electricity and magnetism to be linked, with the force on a nearby charge at right angles to the wire. Ampère found that parallel wires attract when currents run the same way and repel when they run opposite, a result that became the basis of the international definition of the ampere. Faraday's 1831 discovery of electromagnetic induction, that a changing magnetic flux induces a proportional potential difference, made the generator possible.

An electric circuit is a closed conducting path carrying components such as resistors, capacitors, inductors, switches, and transformers. A resistor opposes current and dissipates energy as heat; Ohm's law states that current is proportional to the voltage across an ideal resistor. A capacitor stores energy in an electric field between conducting plates separated by a dielectric and blocks steady current. An inductor, usually a coil, stores energy in a magnetic field and opposes rapid current changes.

Electric power equals current times voltage (P = IV), in watts. Maxwell's 1864 equations showed that changing electric and magnetic fields reinforce each other as electromagnetic waves that travel at light speed in vacuum, identifying light itself as electromagnetic radiation. Electronics exploits the nonlinear behaviour of transistors, diodes, and integrated circuits for switching and amplification; a modern chip can hold billions of transistors on a few square centimetres.

## Generation, transmission, and the natural world

Modern electricity comes mostly from electromechanical generators driven by steam from fossil or nuclear heat, by wind or water turbines directly, or by photovoltaic panels. The late-19th-century transformer enabled efficient high-voltage, low-current transmission, which made centralised power stations practical. Because electricity is hard to store at scale, generation must continually match demand, a challenge sharpened by variable wind and solar. Demand has grown rapidly with industrialisation, at roughly 12% per year in the early-20th-century United States, a rate now seen in China and India.

Lightning is atmospheric electrical breakdown, with a large storm cloud reaching potentials of about 100 MV. The Earth's magnetic field comes from circulating currents in the molten core, and piezoelectricity, discovered in 1880 by Pierre and Jacques Curie, generates a voltage when certain crystals are squeezed. All animals transmit signals along cell membranes as action potentials, and some organisms, such as electric eels, generate substantial voltages using modified muscle cells called electrocytes.
