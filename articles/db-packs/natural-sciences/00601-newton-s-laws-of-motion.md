# Newton's laws of motion

Isaac Newton's three laws of motion, published in 1687 in the *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, describe how forces change an object's motion. They form the foundation of classical mechanics, covering everything from falling apples to planetary orbits. The laws break down at atomic scales, where quantum mechanics takes over, and near the speed of light, where relativity replaces them.

## The three laws

The first law says a body remains at rest or moves in a straight line at constant speed unless a net external force acts on it. This property, inertia, is why a book on a table stays put and why a puck slides forever on frictionless ice without slowing. All observers moving at constant velocity relative to each other see the same physics, so no single inertial frame is privileged. Newton believed in absolute space and time, yet only relative measurements can be tested.

The second law quantifies how a force changes motion. A net force equals the rate of change of momentum: F = dp/dt. For a body whose mass stays constant, this reduces to F = ma. Force and momentum are vectors, meaning they have both magnitude and direction, and the net force is the sum of all forces. When the net force is zero, a body is in mechanical equilibrium. For systems whose mass changes, like a rocket burning fuel, the full momentum form must be used because F = ma alone gives wrong results.

The third law says that when one body pushes another, the second pushes back with equal force in the opposite direction. These two forces always act on different bodies, which is why they do not cancel each other out. The law guarantees conservation of momentum: in any isolated system, the total momentum stays constant because every internal push is matched by an equal and opposite push.

## How the laws apply

Near Earth's surface, gravity pulls every object downward with the same acceleration, g ≈ 9.8 m/s², regardless of mass, which is why a feather and a hammer fall at the same rate in a vacuum. In circular motion, an inward centripetal force produces acceleration a = v²/r aimed at the center; planetary orbits approximate this under gravity. A mass on a spring oscillates because the restoring force F = -kx is proportional to displacement, producing oscillation at frequency ω = √(k/m).

A rigid body moves as a combination of translation of its center of mass and rotation about that center. The rotational analogues of force and momentum are torque τ = r × F and angular momentum L = r × p, and angular momentum is conserved whenever net torque vanishes.

## Energy, gravity, and limits

Energy concepts fit naturally into the framework. Kinetic energy comes from motion, potential energy from position, and the work done by a force equals the change in kinetic energy. Conservative forces, such as gravity, derive from a scalar potential (F = -∇U), while friction dissipates energy and is non-conservative.

Newton's law of universal gravitation explains orbits as conic sections, ellipses for planets, parabolas and hyperbolas for fly-by trajectories, though the three-body problem has no general solution and must be solved numerically. Some Newtonian systems, including the three-body problem and a double pendulum, are chaotic: tiny differences in starting conditions grow exponentially, making long-term prediction impossible even though the underlying equations are deterministic.

Later reformulations restate the same physics in more powerful mathematical forms. Lagrangian mechanics writes motion in terms of kinetic minus potential energy, Hamiltonian mechanics in terms of position and momentum, and the Hamilton-Jacobi equation draws an analogy with wave optics. Fluid flow follows from Newton's laws through the Euler and Navier-Stokes equations, whose possible finite-time blowup remains an open mathematical problem.

## Where the laws fail

Special relativity modifies momentum to p = mγv at high speed and breaks the third law's assumption of instant action at a distance. General relativity replaces gravity with curved spacetime, recovering Newton's law only when gravity is weak. Quantum mechanics replaces definite positions and momenta with operators, though Ehrenfest's theorem shows that quantum averages still follow Newton's second law. Outside these regimes, the three laws describe motion exactly.
