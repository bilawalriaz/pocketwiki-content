# Thermodynamics

Thermodynamics is the branch of physics that studies heat, work, temperature, and energy, and how they relate to matter and radiation. Four laws give it a quantitative framework; statistical mechanics later explained those laws by linking them to the behaviour of atoms.

## Origins in the steam engine

The subject grew out of a practical problem: making steam engines do more useful work per unit of fuel. Otto von Guericke built the first vacuum pump in 1650, showing that a vacuum could be physically produced. Robert Boyle and Robert Hooke followed in 1656 with an improved air pump, and Boyle formulated his inverse pressure–volume law. Denis Papin's 1679 steam digester demonstrated the power of pressurised steam; Thomas Savery (1697) and Thomas Newcomen (1712) turned it into working engines. In the 1760s, Joseph Black introduced heat capacity and latent heat (the energy absorbed or released during a phase change at constant temperature), while James Watt separately improved engine efficiency. Sadi Carnot's 1824 *Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire* gave the field its theoretical foundation by analysing the ideal heat engine. Rudolf Clausius stated the second law in 1850, introduced entropy in 1865, and Lord Kelvin offered a concise definition in 1854. William Rankine published the first textbook in 1859, and J. Willard Gibbs's papers of 1873–76 extended thermodynamics to chemistry.

## The four laws

The **zeroth law** says two systems each in thermal equilibrium with a third are in equilibrium with each other. This makes temperature a well-defined, measurable quantity.

The **first law** is conservation of energy: the change in a system's internal energy equals the heat added to it minus the work it does, ΔU = Q − W.

The **second law** introduces entropy, a measure of how many microscopic arrangements a macroscopic state corresponds to. It sets the direction of natural processes: heat flows spontaneously from hot to cold, and engines cannot convert heat into work with perfect efficiency. The formal statement is that the total entropy of an isolated system never decreases.

The **third law** says entropy approaches a minimum, taken as zero, as temperature approaches absolute zero (0 K, or −273.15 °C). This gives an absolute reference point for entropy.

## Branches

Classical thermodynamics treats systems at equilibrium using only macroscopic quantities like pressure, volume, and temperature. Statistical mechanics derives those macroscopic laws from the statistics of large numbers of particles. Chemical thermodynamics applies the framework to reactions and phase changes. Equilibrium and non-equilibrium thermodynamics divide the field by how far a system sits from balance. Non-equilibrium thermodynamics lacks general principles for predicting the rate at which far-from-equilibrium systems relax.

## Systems, states, and processes

A thermodynamic system is a region of the universe bounded off from its surroundings; the boundary may be fixed or moving, real or imaginary. Systems are *open* (matter and energy cross the boundary), *closed* (only energy crosses), or *isolated* (neither crosses). Their condition is specified by *state variables*, which are either *intensive* (independent of system size, such as temperature and pressure) or *extensive* (proportional to size, such as volume and total internal energy). Equations of state, the ideal gas law being the simplest example, relate these variables.

A *process* is how a system moves from one state to another. Standard named processes are adiabatic (no heat exchanged), isothermal (constant temperature), isobaric (constant pressure), and isochoric (constant volume). Reversible processes are idealisations in which the system passes through a continuous sequence of equilibrium states; real processes are irreversible.

## Potentials and conjugate variables

A thermodynamic potential stores the energy available for work under particular constraints. The main ones are internal energy U, enthalpy H (convenient at constant pressure), Helmholtz free energy F (at constant temperature and volume), and Gibbs free energy G (at constant temperature and pressure). They are related by Legendre transformations, a mathematical switch from one set of variables to another that preserves the underlying physics. Each potential pairs with a *conjugate variable*: pressure with volume, temperature with entropy, chemical potential with particle number. One member of each pair acts as a generalised "force" and the other as the corresponding "displacement."

## Axiomatic formulation and open questions

Constantin Carathéodory's 1909 axiomatic approach rebuilt thermodynamics from rigorous mathematical axioms based on which states are adiabatically accessible from which others, without appealing to engines. Modern applications reach across physics, chemistry, engineering, biology, materials science, meteorology, and economics. Quantum thermodynamics investigates how these classical laws hold at atomic scales. The meaning of entropy, whether as disorder, missing information, or a statistical count, remains debated, as does who coined the word "thermodynamics" itself: it appears linked to William Thomson and William Rankine around 1854, though James Joule used the phrase "perfect thermo-dynamic engine" in 1858.
