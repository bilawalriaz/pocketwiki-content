# History of physics

Physics is the science of matter and energy, the forces that act on them, and the laws that govern their behavior. Its history traces a shift from philosophical speculation to a mathematical, experimental discipline, with two 20th‑century revolutions (relativity and quantum mechanics) redefining space, time, and the fundamental constituents of the universe.

## Mental model

Physics developed through three layered stages, each preserving the previous one as a special case:

- Classical physics: macroscopic objects, everyday speeds, energies dominated by mechanics, heat, and electromagnetism.
- Modern physics: very small scales, very high speeds, and strong gravity, where classical laws break down.
- Contemporary physics: the subatomic and cosmic frontier, organized around the Standard Model and open questions about the universe as a whole.

## Ancient and medieval foundations

Early physics was intertwined with astronomy, optics, and mechanics, studied through geometry. Babylonian and Egyptian cultures tracked celestial bodies for calendar and predictive purposes. In Greece, rational explanation began with the Pre‑Socratics, including Thales. Aristotle (384–322 BCE) built a system of "physics" based on four elements and qualitative explanations of motion, an account that dominated Western thought for nearly two thousand years. Archimedes (c. 287–212 BCE) gave mathematical treatments of buoyancy, the lever, and hydrostatics, demonstrating that quantitative reasoning could solve physical problems. Aristarchus of Samos (c. 310–230 BCE) proposed a heliocentric model of the Solar System, a claim that anticipated Copernicus by roughly eighteen centuries. Parallel traditions of atomism and astronomy existed in ancient India and China, though their influence on later European physics was indirect.

## The Scientific Revolution (16th–17th centuries)

The decisive break with Aristotelian physics began with Nicolaus Copernicus's 1543 heliocentric model in *De revolutionibus*. Johannes Kepler refined it with three laws of planetary motion, showing that orbits are ellipses, not combinations of circles. Galileo Galilei championed the experimental method, used the telescope to discover Jovian moons and Venus's phases, and formulated the laws of falling bodies and projectile motion. Isaac Newton's *Principia Mathematica* (1687) unified terrestrial and celestial mechanics through the three laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation, the same gravitational law that accounts for both a falling apple and orbital motion. The period also produced foundational work in fluid dynamics (Torricelli) and optics (Huygens).

## 18th and 19th century developments

The 18th century extended Newtonian mechanics into a full mathematical formalism through Euler, Lagrange, and others. The study of heat shifted from the caloric theory (heat as a fluid) to the mechanical theory, in which heat is molecular motion; Count Rumford's boring‑of‑cannon experiments provided early evidence. The 19th century was defined by James Clerk Maxwell's unification of electricity, magnetism, and light into a single theory of electromagnetism (1865). The laws of thermodynamics, especially the conservation of energy and the increase of entropy, were established experimentally, and the kinetic theory of gases linked heat to molecular motion, giving rise to statistical mechanics. J. J. Thomson's 1897 discovery of the electron and the study of radioactivity showed that the atom is not indivisible, opening the door to a physics of the subatomic realm.

## 20th century: modern physics

By 1900 classical physics failed to explain several phenomena. Blackbody radiation (the light emitted by a hot object) and the photoelectric effect (electrons ejected from a metal struck by light) could not be reconciled with continuous energy. Max Planck (1900) introduced the quantum hypothesis, that energy is emitted and absorbed in discrete packets. Albert Einstein's 1905 theory of special relativity revised the concepts of space and time, showing that the speed of light is the same for all observers and that mass and energy are equivalent (E = mc²). His 1915 general theory of relativity reinterpreted gravity as the curvature of spacetime produced by mass and energy, replacing Newton's gravitational force. Quantum mechanics, developed by Heisenberg, Schrödinger, Bohr, and others in 1925–26, described atoms and subatomic particles with wave‑particle duality and probabilistic outcomes, formalized in Heisenberg's uncertainty principle. These advances split physics into "classical" and "modern" domains, a split that remains in use.

## Contemporary physics

After World War II, particle physics advanced rapidly. The Standard Model classifies the known fundamental particles and three of the four fundamental forces (electromagnetic, weak, and strong), with gravity excluded. The 2012 discovery of the Higgs boson at CERN confirmed the mechanism by which particles acquire mass. In cosmology, the Big Bang theory, supported by the 1965 discovery of cosmic microwave background radiation, became the standard account of the universe's origin and evolution. Major open questions include the identities of dark matter and dark energy, which together account for most of the universe's mass‑energy content, the arrow of time, and the unification of general relativity with quantum mechanics into a theory of quantum gravity.

## Key concepts

- **Classical physics**: the physics of macroscopic phenomena, based on Newtonian mechanics, thermodynamics, and Maxwell's electromagnetism, valid at everyday scales and speeds.
- **Modern physics**: physics based on relativity and quantum mechanics, describing phenomena at atomic and subatomic scales or at speeds approaching the speed of light.
- **Quantum mechanics**: the theory describing matter and energy at the atomic and subatomic levels, where outcomes are probabilistic and particles exhibit wave‑like properties.
- **General relativity**: Einstein's theory describing gravity as the curvature of spacetime caused by mass and energy, not as a conventional force.
- **Standard Model**: the framework classifying fundamental particles and the electromagnetic, weak, and strong forces, leaving gravity outside.
- **Entropy**: a measure of the disorder of a system; the second law of thermodynamics states that entropy in an isolated system tends to increase.
- **Higgs boson**: a particle associated with the Higgs field, which gives mass to other elementary particles.
