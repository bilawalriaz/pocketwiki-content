# Generation (particle physics)

In particle physics, a **generation** (also called a **family**) is a division of elementary particles. Members of different generations differ in flavour quantum numbers and mass, but share identical electric and strong interactions. The Standard Model recognises three generations, each containing two leptons and two quarks. The leptons split into one negatively charged particle (electron-like) and one neutral particle (neutrino); the quarks split into one with charge −1⁄3 (down-type) and one with charge +2⁄3 (up-type).

## The three generations

| Type | Subtype | First | Second | Third |
|------|---------|-------|--------|--------|
| Quarks (carry colour) | down-type | down | strange | bottom |
| Quarks | up-type | up | charm | top |
| Leptons (colour-free) | charged | electron | muon | tau |
| Leptons | neutral | electron neutrino | muon neutrino | tau neutrino |

Each member of a higher generation has greater mass than the corresponding particle in the previous generation, with the possible exception of neutrinos, whose small but non-zero masses have not been accurately determined. The electron weighs 0.511 MeV/c², the muon 106 MeV/c², and the tau 1777 MeV/c², roughly twice the proton's mass. This mass hierarchy causes higher-generation particles to decay into first-generation ones, which is why everyday atoms contain only up and down quarks and electrons. Second- and third-generation charged particles appear only in extreme environments such as cosmic rays or particle accelerators. Neutrinos of every generation permeate the universe but interact so rarely that they barely register. The term "generation" was introduced by Haim Harari at the 1976 Les Houches Summer School.

## The fourth-generation question

Most theoretical physicists consider a fourth generation unlikely. Extra generations would subtly alter precision electroweak observables, and measurements strongly disfavour such shifts; collider searches have found no evidence. Hypothetical fourth-generation quarks are denoted with a prime, such as b′ and t′. A fourth-generation neutrino lighter than about 45 GeV/c² was ruled out by Z-boson decay-width measurements at CERN's Large Electron–Positron Collider (LEP) as early as 1989. As of 2010, the lower bound for a fourth-generation neutrino mass stood at roughly 60 GeV, millions of times larger than the upper bounds on the other three neutrino masses. By 2024, neutrino-oscillation studies had also failed to detect one, and LHC data from 2019 placed the lower bound for fourth-generation quark masses at about 1.4 TeV. In 2012, the lower bound for a fourth-generation charged lepton was 100 GeV, with a proposed unitarity upper bound of 1.2 TeV. The Koide formula, if it holds, predicts a fourth-generation charged lepton mass of 44 GeV (already excluded), with b′ at 3.6 TeV and t′ at 84 TeV, both beyond the LHC's roughly 6 TeV proton-energy reach.

## Why three generations?

The origin of multiple generations, and the specific count of three, remains an unsolved problem in physics. String theory offers a mechanism: multiple generations arise from the compactification of D-brane intersections, though the exact number depends on those geometric details. Certain E₈ grand unified theories compactified from ten dimensions down to four on particular orbifolds naturally produce three generations, a feature shared by many heterotic string models. In standard quantum field theory, a single fermion field can, under certain assumptions, generate multiple fermion states with mass ratios near e^π ≈ 23 and e^(2π) ≈ 535, values that could account for the large gaps between successive generations. A derivation from first principles, connected to gravity, has at least tentatively produced exactly three generations with the correct structure, implying gauge unification into SU(5). The associated masses, however, depend on Yukawa couplings and remain unexplained within that framework.
