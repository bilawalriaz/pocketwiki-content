# Light

Light is electromagnetic radiation the human eye can detect, occupying the visible band of roughly 400–700 nanometres. Physics uses "light" more broadly for all electromagnetic radiation, from radio waves to gamma rays. Its behaviour combines wave and particle ideas, and its vacuum speed, *c* = 299,792,458 m/s, is a fundamental constant from which the metre is now defined.

## Wave and particle together

Light propagates as a spreading wave but delivers and absorbs energy in discrete packets. The wave picture explains interference (overlapping beams producing bright and dark bands) and diffraction (bending around edges); the particle picture explains how detectors register energy in indivisible units called **photons**. Photons are massless, carry energy *E = hν* and momentum, and behave as spin-1 bosons. Neither picture alone is complete: low-frequency radiation acts more wave-like, high-frequency radiation acts more particle-like, and visible light displays both.

## The spectrum

Electromagnetic radiation spans radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, and gamma-ray regions, distinguished by wavelength. Only photons energetic enough to drive electronic transitions in retinal molecules trigger human vision, which fixes the visible band where it sits. Infrared photons are too weak for vision, though some animals (pit vipers) sense them as heat via molecular vibrations. Ultraviolet photons carry enough energy to damage tissue, which is why the cornea and lens filter wavelengths below about 400 nm. Under good conditions humans can detect light from roughly 310 nm to about 1,050 nm.

## Speed and refraction

In vacuum every electromagnetic wave travels at *c*, regardless of frequency. In transparent media light slows, to about three-quarters of *c* in water, and this slowing governs refraction.

When light crosses a boundary between transparent media it bends. The angles obey **Snell's Law**, *n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂*, where *n* is the **refractive index**, the ratio *n = c/v*. Frequency stays constant across the boundary; wavelength changes, forcing a direction change when the light hits at an angle. Lenses exploit refraction to magnify, in spectacles, microscopes, and telescopes.

**Optics** is the study of light–matter interaction. **Geometrical optics** treats light as straight rays and works when wavelength is far smaller than the objects involved; it governs lenses, mirrors, cameras, eyes, and fibre-optic cables. **Physical optics** adds interference and diffraction. **Quantum optics** describes individual photon interactions.

## Sources

**Thermal (black-body) emission** comes from anything hot enough to radiate. The Sun's surface, near 6,000 K, peaks in the visible; an incandescent bulb dumps roughly 90% of its energy into infrared. As temperature rises the spectrum's peak shifts to shorter wavelengths, red at moderate heat, white at higher temperatures, blue-white at extremes (Wien's displacement law). Stars reach blue-white thermally; blue in flames or welding usually comes from molecular emission, not heat.

**Atomic and molecular emission** produces sharp spectral lines, the basis of LEDs, gas-discharge lamps, and the yellow sodium glow. Stimulated emission of these lines powers lasers and masers. Other mechanisms include Cherenkov radiation (the blue glow when charged particles exceed light's local speed in a dense medium), bioluminescence (fireflies, deep-sea squid), fluorescence and phosphorescence (immediate versus delayed re-emission), and high-energy processes such as radioactive decay and matter–antimatter annihilation.

## Measurement

**Radiometry** measures raw optical power in watts. **Photometry** weights that power by the standard human brightness response, which peaks near 555 nm, giving units like the lumen, lux, and candela that match perceived brightness. Ordinary photodetectors also respond to infrared and ultraviolet, so they sometimes record light people cannot see.

## Light pressure

Because photons carry momentum, a beam exerts a force equal to its power divided by *c*. A 1 mW laser pointer pushes with about 3.3 piconewtons, far too small to notice, but the effect drives nanoscale machines, spins small asteroids over geological time via the YORP effect, and forms the basis of solar sails. Crookes radiometers spin for a different reason, thermal transpiration in a partial vacuum, while Nichols's more delicate apparatus confirmed light pressure directly. Moving objects experience a velocity-dependent drag, since more photons reflect off the leading face than the trailing one.

## History of the idea

Greek thinkers offered the earliest sustained theories: Empedocles imagined rays leaving the eye, Euclid formalised straight-line propagation and the law of reflection, and Lucretius anticipated a particle view. In 1637 Descartes recast refraction as a wave process but wrongly assumed light travels faster in denser media.

Newton's **Opticks** (1704) made the corpuscular theory dominant for a century, because it explained reflection and the apparent bending of refraction. Huygens developed a mathematical wave theory in a hypothetical "luminiferous aether," and Young's interference experiments in 1801–1802 demonstrated wavelength and tied it to colour. Fresnel and Arago proved that light waves are transverse, needed to explain polarisation (the restriction of wave vibration to one direction). Foucault's 1850 measurement of slower light in water finally killed the classical particle theory.

The next unification was electromagnetic. Faraday's 1845 discovery that a magnetic field rotates the polarisation plane of light, now called **Faraday rotation**, was the first evidence linking light to electricity and magnetism. Maxwell's equations (1862–1873) showed electromagnetic waves travel at *c*, identifying light as one form of such radiation; Hertz confirmed this in 1887 by generating and detecting radio waves. The Michelson–Morley experiment (1887) ruled out the aether and was reframed by Einstein's special relativity.

The quantum era added the missing particle side. Planck's energy quanta (1900) explained black-body spectra; Einstein (1905) tied light quanta to the photoelectric effect; Compton scattering (1923) showed X-rays transfer momentum like particles; Lewis named the quantum "photon" in 1926; Dirac's quantised electromagnetic field (1927) led, by the 1940s, to quantum electrodynamics (QED), the relativistic quantum field theory describing light–matter interaction. Quantum optics since the 1950s has explored single photons, coherent laser light, squeezed states, and optical tweezers, and now underpins quantum information science.

Sunlight drives photosynthesis, the foundation of nearly all biological energy, while a few species generate their own light through bioluminescence, from fireflies signalling mates to deep-sea squid using counter-illumination to hide their silhouettes. The same body of theory underlies fibre-optic communication, medical imaging, laser manufacturing, and quantum technologies moving from laboratory experiments into working devices.
