# Chemistry

Chemistry is the scientific study of matter: its composition, structure, properties, and the changes it undergoes in reactions. It is called the central science because it underpins pharmacology, geology, forensics, and ecology, and it drives the global chemical industry.

## Etymology and definitions

The word comes from a Renaissance modification of *alchemy*, from the Arabic *al-kīmīā*, possibly from Egyptian *Kemet* (Greek *χημία*) or from *χημεία*, "cast together." Definitions have shifted: Boyle (1661) called it the material principles of mixed bodies; Stahl (1730) the art of resolving and reconstituting them; Dumas (1837) the science of molecular forces; by 1947, the science of substances' structure, properties, and reactions. Chang broadened it in 1998 to the study of matter and its changes.

## Matter, atoms, and elements

Matter is anything with rest mass and volume, made of particles. The atom is chemistry's basic unit: a dense nucleus of positively charged protons and uncharged neutrons, surrounded by a cloud of negatively charged electrons. A nucleon's mass is about 1,836 times an electron's, while an atom's radius is about 10,000 times its nucleus's. The atom is the smallest entity that keeps an element's chemical properties.

A chemical element is a pure substance of one atom type, defined by its atomic number Z, the proton count. Atoms of one element with different mass numbers are isotopes. The periodic table, developed in the 1860s by Mendeleev and others, arranges elements by Z into groups (columns) and periods (rows), revealing periodic trends.

## Compounds, molecules, and mixtures

A compound is a pure substance of more than one element whose properties differ from those of its constituents. Nomenclature is set by IUPAC; the Chemical Abstracts Service assigns each substance a unique registry number. A molecule is the smallest indivisible unit retaining a substance's chemical identity, typically neutral, with atoms held by covalent bonds and paired valence electrons. Charged species are polyatomic ions; molecules with unpaired electrons are radicals, usually reactive (nitric oxide, NO, is a stable exception). Noble gases exist as lone atoms; ionic compounds and network solids are described by formula units or unit cells. The mole, the unit of amount of substance, contains exactly 6.02214076×10²³ particles (the Avogadro constant). A mixture is a collection of substances, such as air or an alloy.

## Phase and bonding

A phase is a set of states with similar bulk structural properties. Familiar phases are solid, liquid, and gas; many substances have multiple solid phases (iron has three). Plasmas, Bose–Einstein condensates, and fermionic condensates are less common. A supercritical state has continuous phase boundaries; a triple point is where three phases coexist.

Chemical bonds include covalent (shared valence electrons), ionic (one atom loses electrons to another, e.g., NaCl), hydrogen, and Van der Waals. Covalent bonding follows the octet rule (eight electrons) or the duet rule (two, for hydrogen and lithium). For metal complexes, molecular orbital theory is used instead of valence bond theory.

## Energy, reactions, and equilibrium

Every transformation changes a system's energy. Reactions are exergonic when energy decreases, endergonic when it rises; exothermic reactions release heat, endothermic absorb it. All reactions must overcome an activation energy barrier, and their rates depend exponentially on temperature (Arrhenius equation). A reaction is feasible only if the change in Gibbs free energy is negative (ΔG ≤ 0); at ΔG = 0 the system is at dynamic equilibrium. Quantum mechanics requires quantized energy levels, so excited states are usually more reactive. Characteristic energy levels let spectroscopy identify substances, including those in distant stars.

IUPAC defines a chemical reaction as "a process that results in the interconversion of chemical species," written as a chemical equation with equal atoms on both sides. The mechanism is the proposed step sequence; rules such as the Woodward–Hoffmann rules help guide it.

Ions are charged atoms or molecules: cations (positive) and anions (negative), which can form crystalline salts. Plasma is fully ionized gas. Acid–base behavior is defined by Arrhenius (hydronium and hydroxide in water), Brønsted–Lowry (proton transfer), and Lewis (electron-pair transfer) theories; strength is measured by pH or the acid dissociation constant Kₐ. Redox (reduction–oxidation) reactions change oxidation state by electron transfer; an oxidizing agent removes electrons, a reducing agent donates them.

## History

Practical metallurgy, pottery, and dyes preceded theory. Aristotle proposed four elements; Greek atomism (c. 440 BC) remained philosophical. Alchemy spread through the Hellenistic world, Byzantine scholars such as Zosimos, and Arab scholars including Jabir ibn Hayyan, with al-Bīrūnī and Avicenna disputing it. Agricola's *De re metallica* (1556) advanced metallurgy. Boyle (1661) recast chemistry as an independent discipline and formulated Boyle's law. Black (1754) isolated carbon dioxide; Cavendish, hydrogen; Priestley and Scheele, oxygen. Lavoisier overturned phlogiston theory and established conservation of mass. Dalton proposed atomic theory; Davy, Berzelius, Frankland, Gibbs, and Arrhenius advanced electrochemistry, valence, and thermodynamics. Wöhler's synthesis of urea founded organic chemistry. Ramsay and Rayleigh discovered noble gases. Thomson (1897) found the electron; Rutherford, the nucleus and proton; Becquerel and the Curies, radioactivity. Bohr, Moseley, and Hahn (nuclear fission) refined atomic theory; Pauling and Lewis developed electronic bond theory. The UN declared 2011 the International Year of Chemistry.

## Subdisciplines and industry

Chemistry divides into pure (fundamental principles) and applied (real-world problems). Subdisciplines include analytical, biochemistry, inorganic, materials, organic, physical, and theoretical chemistry, with electrochemistry, photochemistry, polymer, and radiochemistry as others. Interdisciplinary areas include astrochemistry, chemical engineering, environmental chemistry, geochemistry, green chemistry, medicinal chemistry, and nanotechnology. In 2013 the global top 50 chemical producers reported US$980.5 billion in sales with a 10.3% profit margin.

## Open questions

The field's definition continues to evolve, and the organic–inorganic boundary is not absolute, especially in organometallic chemistry.
