# Magnetism

Magnetism is the set of physical effects produced by **magnetic fields**. Those fields are generated by three sources: electric currents, the intrinsic spin of elementary particles, and changing electric fields. Magnetism and electricity are not separate forces but two aspects of a single electromagnetic field. Einstein's special relativity makes this precise: a purely electric effect in one frame of reference appears as a mixture of electric and magnetic effects in another. The fundamental rule is the **Lorentz force**, **F = q(v × B)**, the perpendicular push a magnetic field *B* exerts on a charge *q* moving at velocity *v*. It is why currents push other currents, motors turn, and generators produce voltage.

All materials respond to a magnetic field, but the strength of that response spans many orders of magnitude. Atomic magnetic moments come almost entirely from orbiting and spinning electrons; nuclear moments are thousands of times smaller and negligible for bulk magnetization. In most atoms, electron moments cancel because electrons pair up in filled subshells, and even unpaired electrons usually point in random directions because of thermal motion. A material becomes magnetically significant only when a large fraction of its atomic moments line up.

How that alignment behaves defines the main classes of magnetic response. In **diamagnetism**, present in every material, an applied field slightly distorts electron orbits through the Lorentz force and produces a tiny moment opposite the field, a weak repulsion. The Bohr–Van Leeuwen theorem shows that classical physics cannot explain this effect; quantum mechanics is required. In **paramagnetism**, unpaired electron spins, as in aluminium and oxygen, partially align with an applied field and weakly reinforce it. In **ferromagnetism**, found in iron, cobalt, nickel, and some rare-earth alloys, a quantum-mechanical **exchange interaction** roughly a hundred to a thousand times stronger than ordinary magnetic dipole coupling forces neighbouring electron spins to point the same way. This produces strong spontaneous magnetization below a critical temperature, the **Curie temperature**, above which thermal agitation scrambles the spins and ferromagnetism disappears. Within a ferromagnet, **magnetic domains** are micrometre-scale regions of uniform alignment; an external field grows well-aligned domains at the expense of others, and removing the field can leave a permanent magnetization. Full alignment of every domain is **magnetic saturation**. **Antiferromagnetism** uses the same exchange interaction but favours anti-parallel neighbours, giving zero net moment, as in chromium and manganese. **Ferrimagnetism**, as in magnetite (Fe₃O₄), keeps the anti-parallel pattern with unequal moments on each sublattice, so a net magnetization survives. **Superparamagnetism** appears in nanoscale ferromagnets small enough to flip as a single unit under thermal agitation.

The quantum origin of all this comes from the indistinguishability of electrons. When two hydrogen atoms bond, the Pauli exclusion principle ties the spatial symmetry of the combined orbital to the spin symmetry. A symmetric, bonding spatial state forces an antisymmetric singlet spin state with anti-parallel spins, which is the path to diamagnetism and antiferromagnetism. An antisymmetric spatial state allows a symmetric triplet spin state with parallel spins, which is the path to ferromagnetism and paramagnetism. In iron, cobalt, and nickel, exchange wins and the metal is ferromagnetic; in most other metals, anti-parallel alignment wins and the metal is nonmagnetic or antiferromagnetic. Oxygen is a paramagnetic exception because of its π-orbitals.

The field inside matter is described by **B = μ₀(H + M)**, where *M* is the magnetization, the magnetic moment per unit volume. In vacuum, *M* is zero and this reduces to **B = μ₀H**. For diamagnets and paramagnets, *M* is proportional to *H*: **M = χH**, with a dimensionless **susceptibility χ**, giving **B = μᵣμ₀H = μH**. Ferromagnets break this proportionality: *M* depends on past field history (hysteresis) and stays non-zero when *H* returns to zero, the property that makes permanent magnets possible.

Every magnet observed in nature is a **dipole** with both a north and a south pole; cutting a bar magnet in half simply produces two smaller dipoles. A true **magnetic monopole**, an isolated north or south pole, would explain why electric charge comes in fixed-size quanta, as Dirac showed in 1931, and is predicted by grand unified theories. Early cosmological models predicted so many monopoles that they would have halted the expansion of the universe; inflation theory resolved this by diluting any primordial population to unobservably low density. Systematic searches since 1931 have found none.

Magnetic fields also act on living things. **Magnetoception** lets birds, bees, and certain bacteria sense the geomagnetic field for navigation. **Biomagnetism** covers fields produced by organisms, including the small amounts of magnetite found in human tissue and the magnetite chitons use to harden their teeth. Water is diamagnetic, so a strong enough field can levitate a small living organism, a direct demonstration that the "weak" magnetic response of ordinary matter is still physically real.

Most useful magnets in technology are **electromagnets**: wire coils, often wrapped around an iron core to concentrate magnetic flux, whose field switches on and off with the current. They power motors, generators, relays, solenoids, loudspeakers, hard-disk read heads, MRI scanners, and industrial lifting magnets, trading continuous power consumption for precise, rapid control over the field.
