# Organic chemistry

Organic chemistry is the study of carbon-containing compounds, their structure, properties, and reactions. Carbon's distinctive bonding, the ability to form four bonds (single, double, triple, or delocalised) and link into chains, rings, and lattices, produces a structural diversity far greater than that of any other element. This diversity is the molecular basis of all known life and of the majority of manufactured chemicals, including pharmaceuticals, polymers, fuels, dyes, and agrochemicals.

The field's central organising idea is the **functional group**: a recognisable atomic cluster such as –OH (alcohol) or –COOH (carboxylic acid) that dictates a molecule's reactivity wherever it appears. A hydroxyl group in methanol, ethanol, or a complex sugar behaves similarly because the local atomic environment is the same. Classifying compounds by functional group lets chemists predict reactions across millions of different molecules from a comparatively small toolkit of patterns.

The framework underlying this predictability rests on three linked ideas. First, the **tetravalent carbon model** proposed independently by Friedrich August Kekulé and Archibald Scott Couper in 1858: each carbon makes four bonds and can link to others, allowing structural formulas to be drawn from reaction data. Second, **aromaticity**, captured by Hückel's rule that planar, cyclic, conjugated π-systems with 4n + 2 π electrons (n = 0, 1, 2…) gain extra stability; benzene (n = 1, six electrons) is the prototype. Third, the concept of **mechanism**: reactions are explained by tracking electron movement with curved arrows, through reactive intermediates whose stability governs which pathway a molecule takes.

## How the field took shape

For most of history, "organic" meant "from living things," and the doctrine of **vitalism** held that such compounds could not be made without a life force. Michel Chevreul's 1816 work on fats and soaps showed that animal-derived substances could be transformed chemically without biology, and in 1828 Friedrich Wöhler synthesised **urea** (a normal constituent of urine) from the inorganic salts potassium cyanate and ammonium sulfate. The Wöhler synthesis is now regarded as the experiment that ended vitalism, demonstrating that the same chemical laws apply to compounds from living and non-living sources. Justus von Liebig then systematised organic analysis, turning the field into an empirical science.

Industrial growth followed rapidly. William Henry Perkin's 1856 accidental synthesis of **mauveine**, the first aniline dye, launched the synthetic dye industry. Adolf von Baeyer's route to synthetic indigo between 1897 and 1914 collapsed natural production from about 19,000 tons to 1,000 tons, illustrating how laboratory synthesis could displace agriculture for a major commodity. In the 1890s, Bayer began producing **acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin)**, and in 1910 Paul Ehrlich introduced **arsphenamine (Salvarsan)**, the first effective treatment for syphilis and the founding of chemotherapy. The rise of the petrochemical industry early in the 20th century supplied cheap feedstocks from petroleum, fuelling the development of plastics, synthetic rubber, and adhesives.

## Hydrocarbon frameworks and the functional-group hierarchy

Organic compounds divide first by their carbon skeleton and then by their functional groups.

| Class | Defining feature | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Aliphatic alkanes** | Single C–C bonds, open chains or non-aromatic rings | Branching alters physical properties (e.g. octane rating of fuels); cycloalkanes are most stable with 5–6 members. |
| **Alkenes / alkynes** | C=C double or C≡C triple bonds | Unsaturation enables addition reactions. |
| **Aromatic compounds** | Planar, cyclic, conjugated π-systems with 4n + 2 π electrons (Hückel) | Extra stability from electron delocalisation; 4n π-electron analogues are destabilised (**antiaromaticity**). |
| **Heterocyclics** | Rings containing O, N, or S as ring members | Includes aromatic pyridine and furan, and saturated piperidine and tetrahydrofuran; central to dyes, drugs, alkaloids, and nucleic acids. |

Functional groups modify the chemistry of any skeleton they decorate. **pKa** (a logarithmic measure of acid strength, lower means more acidic) sets a baseline: electron-withdrawing groups lower pKa and raise **electrophilicity** (susceptibility of an electron-poor site to attack by an electron-rich reagent), while electron-donating groups raise pKa and raise **nucleophilicity** (ability of an electron-rich reagent to attack). Acyl chlorides are more reactive than esters, which are more reactive than amides, a hierarchy that holds across most substrates. **Steric hindrance** (physical crowding around the reactive site) can override electronic effects.

## Reactions and synthesis

Organic reactions fall into recurring types: **addition** (atoms add across a multiple bond), **elimination** (a small molecule departs, often forming a double bond), **substitution** (one group replaces another, as in Nu⁻ + C–X → C–Nu + X⁻), **pericyclic** (concerted rearrangements through cyclic transition states), **rearrangement**, and **redox**. A single substrate can undergo many of these, and the practical aim is usually to convert a cheap starting material into a target with high selectivity.

Building a complex molecule from simple ones is called **total synthesis**. The modern planning method, E. J. Corey's **retrosynthetic analysis**, works backward: the chemist imagines disconnecting bonds in the target using reactions known to work in the forward direction, producing a tree of possible precursors. Practical success still depends on laboratory execution, and some classical mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

## How structures are determined

Because organic reactions rarely give a single product, **purity assessment** through distillation, crystallisation, extraction, or chromatography (HPLC, gas chromatography) comes first. Structure is then assigned mainly by instrumentation: **¹H and ¹³C NMR spectroscopy** reveals which atoms are connected and in what spatial arrangement (**stereochemistry**), including mirror-image (**chiral**) configurations; **mass spectrometry** gives molecular weight and, at high resolution, an exact molecular formula from fragmentation patterns; and **X-ray crystallography** provides precise three-dimensional geometry when suitable single crystals can be grown. Infrared, UV/Vis, and optical rotation supply supporting evidence on functional groups and chirality.

## Polymers, biomolecules, and special classes

Long chains of covalently linked **monomers** form **polymers**. Synthetic polymers (polyethylene, synthetic rubber) dominate materials; **biopolymers** are proteins, nucleic acids, and polysaccharides such as starch and cellulose. Other recurring classes include **isoprenoids** (cholesterol, steroids, rubber) and petroleum itself, a fossilised mixture of biomolecules. In pharmacology, **small molecules** (organic compounds under roughly 1000 g/mol) account for most drugs. Distinct subfields cover carbon bonded to metals (**organometallics**) and exotic carbon allotropes such as C₆₀ buckminsterfullerene, discovered in 1985 by Kroto, Smalley, and Curl and recognised with the 1996 Nobel Prize.

Structures are drawn in shorthand as **line-angle (skeletal) formulas**, where each vertex or terminus is a carbon and hydrogens are implied by tetravalency; machine-readable equivalents such as **SMILES** and **InChI** encode the same information for databases. The 1892 Geneva Rules were the first international standard for naming, and today's IUPAC system remains rigorous but cumbersome for large natural products, which is why trivial names like aspirin persist.
