# Energy

Energy is the quantitative property transferred to a body or physical system, recognizable as the capacity to do work and as heat and light. It is a conserved quantity: energy changes form but cannot be created or destroyed. Its SI unit is the joule (J). Energy underpins every physical, chemical, biological, and cosmological process, from cellular metabolism to stellar fusion.

## History of the concept

The word derives from Ancient Greek *energeia* ("activity, operation"), possibly first used in Aristotle's 4th-century BC work as a qualitative idea broad enough to include happiness and pleasure. In the late 17th century Gottfried Leibniz proposed *vis viva* (mass × velocity²), which differs from modern kinetic energy only by a factor of two, and guessed that thermal energy consisted of microscopic motion. In the early 18th century Émilie du Châtelet, in marginalia to her translation of Newton's *Principia*, first formulated a conserved measurable quantity distinct from momentum. Thomas Young may have been the first to use "energy" in its modern sense in 1807; Gustave-Gaspard Coriolis named "kinetic energy" in 1829; William Rankine coined "potential energy" in 1853.

In 1845 James Prescott Joule established the link between mechanical work and heat, ending a long debate over whether heat was a substance (caloric) or a quantity, and founding thermodynamics. Clausius formulated entropy; Stefan introduced laws of radiant energy. Einstein's 1905 special relativity merged mass and energy conservation into one law, expressing rest energy as E₀ = m₀c². Bohr's 1913 theory introduced quantized atomic energy levels, which Schrödinger and Heisenberg explained with quantum mechanics in 1926. Noether's 1918 theorem showed that energy conservation follows from time-translation symmetry, the fact that physical laws do not change from moment to moment.

## Forms and units

Total energy divides into potential energy (set by position in a field) and kinetic energy (set by motion), categories that overlap. Mechanical energy sums translational and rotational kinetic and potential energy; nuclear energy refers to combined potentials inside an atomic nucleus. Other forms include elastic, chemical, radiant, internal, and rest energy.

Energy's base units are M L² T⁻². The joule is the work done applying one newton through one meter; power is the watt, one joule per second; one kilowatt-hour equals 3.6 million joules. Other units include the CGS erg, electronvolt, food calorie, and BTU.

## Energy in science

In classical mechanics, work is the line integral of force along a path, and total energy is the Hamiltonian, a function that summarizes a system's total energy and from which its equations of motion can be derived. The Lagrangian, kinetic energy minus potential energy, is often more convenient for deriving those equations but requires a system without dissipation, so Noether's theorem does not extend to dissipative systems. In chemistry, reactions are exothermic or exergonic if products are lower in energy, endothermic if higher, and typically must surmount an activation energy barrier whose rate depends on temperature through the Arrhenius equation.

In biology, green plants capture radiant energy from the Sun; animals consume chemical energy. Photosynthesis combines carbon dioxide and water into carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and oxygen; cellular respiration releases stored energy. The basal metabolism rate measures food-energy use at rest. Because concentrated energy tends to spread as heat, organisms are physically inefficient, producing the ecological pyramid of biomass. Mitochondria generate most cellular chemical energy, with ATP the primary transporter.

In Earth science, continental drift, mountains, volcanoes, and earthquakes record energy transformations within the planet; weather reflects solar energy processed in the atmosphere. Radioactive decay of uranium, potassium, and thorium supplies more than half of Earth's internal heat, driving plate tectonics. Landslides and earthquakes release gravitational or elastic potential energy traceable to heavy atoms made in supernovae.

In cosmology, stars, supernovae, quasars, and gamma-ray bursts are the highest-output energy transformations, powered by gravitational collapse or nuclear fusion. Hydrogen left unfused after the Big Bang is stored potential energy released by stellar fusion. The Penrose process would extract energy from a rotating black hole; Hawking radiation causes steady mass loss.

In quantum mechanics, energy is defined through the Hamiltonian operator acting on the wave function, producing discrete energy levels (quanta) for bound systems. For electromagnetic waves, E = hν, where h is Planck's constant and ν the frequency; the corresponding states are photons. In relativity, rest energy E₀ = m₀c² attaches energy to every massive body at rest; electron–positron annihilation converts rest mass into photon radiant energy. The stress–energy tensor sources the gravitational field in general relativity. Energy is a scalar, the canonical conjugate to time (paired with it the way position pairs with momentum), invariant under spatial rotations but not boosts.

## Transformation and conservation

Energy transforms between forms through transducers such as batteries, dams, and heat engines. Carnot's theorem and the second law limit heat-to-work conversion in cyclic processes; entropy directs change, making concentration of energy statistically improbable. A pendulum reversibly exchanges potential and kinetic energy. In a gravitational field, mass and energy both contribute to weight, and E = mc² gives their equivalence: 1 kg of rest mass equals 9×10¹⁶ joules, or 21.5 megatonnes of TNT. Irreversible dissipation drives the hypothetical heat death of the universe, in which available energy for work continually decreases.

The first law of thermodynamics states that a closed system's energy is constant unless transferred as work or heat. Most energy forms obey strict local conservation, gravitational energy excepted. The energy–time uncertainty principle (ΔEΔt ≥ ℏ/2) limits how precisely energy can be defined over short intervals, permitting virtual particles that carry momentum and mediate fundamental forces. Internal energy sums all microscopic energy forms; thermodynamics tracks changes, not absolute values. The equipartition principle shares total energy equally among degrees of freedom on average. The second law holds cleanly only near equilibrium; the principle of maximum entropy production is a guiding idea for non-equilibrium systems.

## Key terms

- **Joule (J):** SI unit of energy, the work of one newton through one meter.
- **Vis viva:** Leibniz's "living force," mass × velocity².
- **Activation energy:** Barrier a chemical reaction must overcome.
- **ATP:** Primary energy transporter in living cells.
- **Hamiltonian:** A function (or operator, in quantum mechanics) giving a system's total energy.
- **Lagrangian:** Kinetic energy minus potential energy, used to derive equations of motion.
