# Astronomical interferometer

An astronomical interferometer is a group of separate telescopes, mirror segments, or radio antennas that operate together as one instrument. By combining their signals through interferometry, the array achieves the angular resolution (the smallest angle two points can be told apart) of a single telescope whose aperture (the diameter of the light-collecting area) equals the distance between the components, a separation called the baseline. The technique produces the sharpest astronomical images of any telescope type, reaching a few microarcseconds at radio wavelengths and hundreds of microarcseconds in the visible and infrared. One arcsecond is 1/3600 of a degree.

## The trade-off

Resolution is gained by sacrificing light-gathering area. Because the individual elements are small, the array collects far less light than a single mirror the size of the baseline, a limitation called the thinned-array curse. Interferometers are therefore most useful for fine angular detail on bright sources, such as resolving close binary stars or imaging the surfaces of nearby giant stars, not for finding faint objects. The maximum angular size of a source an array can image is also limited by the smallest gap between its elements.

## Aperture synthesis

The optical path length from the source to the beam combiner (the device that merges the light) must match what a full mirror would produce. A signal-processing technique called aperture synthesis then uses these matched signals to reconstruct an image as detailed as a giant telescope would give. The equivalence holds because the same image can be obtained either by spreading small telescopes across a large area or by placing an aperture mask over a single big telescope and taking many exposures as the holes are moved. Radio interferometers exploit this by physically relocating antennas; at optical wavelengths the mask-and-move scheme is often used instead. Software such as BSMEM and MIRA converts the measured visibility amplitudes and closure phases (combinations of phase measurements that cancel atmospheric noise) into the final image.

## Radio versus optical and infrared

Interferometry is easiest at radio wavelengths, where signals from antennas hundreds or thousands of kilometres apart can be recorded separately and later combined. Very Long Baseline Interferometry (VLBI) links radio telescopes across continents, simulating a single dish thousands of kilometres across. The technique is much harder at optical and infrared wavelengths because the light must be kept coherent, meaning its waves must stay in fixed phase relationship, to within a fraction of a wavelength over long optical paths, demanding extremely precise mirrors.

## Key facilities

The Very Large Telescope Interferometer (VLTI) at ESO combines four 8.2-metre unit telescopes and four movable 1.8-metre auxiliary telescopes whose light paths must be equal to within about 1/1000 mm, giving an equivalent mirror diameter of up to 200 m and roughly 25 times the resolution of a single VLT unit. The Atacama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA), fully operational since March 2013, uses 66 antennas spread across 150 m to 16 km on the Chajnantor plateau in Chile, with resolution up to ten times better than the Hubble Space Telescope. Other operational optical arrays include the Navy Precision Optical Interferometer (NPOI), with a 437 m baseline, and the CHARA array. Radio facilities such as the Very Large Array and MERLIN use baselines of typically 10–100 km.

## History and applications

Optical interferometry began when Michelson's stellar interferometer on Mount Wilson measured the diameter of Betelgeuse on 13 December 1920. Radio interferometry took off in the 1940s and dominated the field for three decades. Johnson, Betz, and Townes extended separated-telescope interferometry to the infrared in 1974, and Labeyrie to visible light in 1975. Late-1970s computer improvements enabled fringe-tracking interferometers that adjust fast enough to follow atmospheric seeing and reach the diffraction limit. In 1995, separated optical telescopes were first used together to image stellar surfaces.

Interferometers image stellar surfaces, search for extrasolar planets by astrometric wobble, by nulling the star's light, or by direct imaging, and measure Cepheid variable stars and young stellar objects with high precision. Their limited light-gathering area and atmospheric sensitivity confine most optical interferometers to bright stars and the cores of nearby active galactic nuclei.
