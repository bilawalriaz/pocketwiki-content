# Agricultural chemistry

Agricultural chemistry is the application of chemistry — especially organic chemistry and biochemistry — to agriculture. It studies the molecular structures and reactions behind producing, protecting, and using crops and livestock, with the practical goal of raising yields and improving quality.

## The two main branches

Agricultural chemistry divides into two strands. Agricultural and environmental chemistry covers the molecular processes inside farming systems and the unintended harms farming can cause. Biotechnology uses biological catalysts and engineered organisms to make food, fuel, and other products.

## Plant biochemistry

Plant biochemistry studies the chemical reactions inside plants, with the aim of turning molecular knowledge into technologies for providing food. A focus is the biochemical differences between plants and other organisms, and the differences within the plant kingdom: dicotyledons versus monocotyledons, gymnosperms versus angiosperms, and C3- versus C4-fixers (plants using different carbon-fixation pathways in photosynthesis).

## Pesticides

Pesticides are chemical materials that assist in producing food, feed, and fibre. The main classes are herbicides, insecticides, and fungicides. They raise crop yield and reduce losses by keeping pests away from crops, regulating insects, diseases, and other animals. Disadvantages include contamination of soil and water — a class of compounds known as persistent organic pollutants — and toxicity to non-target species such as birds, fish, pollinators, and farmworkers.

## Soil chemistry and fertilisers

Soil chemistry aims to preserve or increase soil fertility to maintain or improve yield and crop quality. Soils are analysed in two parts: inorganic matter (minerals), which makes up most of the mass of dry soil, and organic matter, consisting of living organisms, their degradation products, humic acids, and fulvic acids. Fertilisers are central. Organic fertilisers are traditional, but their use has largely been displaced by synthetic chemicals produced from phosphate rock mining and the Haber–Bosch process (an industrial method for synthesising ammonia from atmospheric nitrogen). These materials dramatically increased the rate of crop production, which is what supports the growing human population. Common fertilisers include urea, ammonium sulphate, diammonium phosphate, and calcium ammonium phosphate.

## Biofuels and bio-derived materials

Agricultural chemistry also covers feedstocks for fuels and materials. Ethanol fuel comes from fermentation of sugars. Biodiesel is derived from fats, both animal and plant. Methane is recovered from manure and other agricultural wastes by microbial action. Lignocellulose, the structural material of plant cell walls, is a promising precursor to new materials.

## Biotechnology and biocatalysis

Biocatalysis (using enzymes or whole cells to drive chemical reactions) produces many food products. More than five billion tons of high-fructose corn syrup are produced annually by the action of the immobilised enzyme glucose isomerase on corn-derived glucose. Emerging uses include enzymes for clarifying or debittering fruit juices. Engineered plants yield a variety of useful chemicals, and bioremediation offers a route to biodegradation. Proteomics (large-scale study of proteins) is relevant because protein content guides much of agriculture.

## GMOs

Genetically modified organisms (GMOs) are plants or other living things whose genomes have been altered to improve their characteristics. Practical outcomes include new vaccines for humans, increased nutrient supplies, and unique plastics; some GMOs grow in climates unsuitable for the original organism. Examples are virus-resistant tobacco and squash, delayed-ripening tomatoes, and herbicide-resistant soybeans. Market interest in biotechnology grew during the 1970s, bringing more infrastructure, lower costs, and more research; genetically modified crops have been incorporated since the early 1980s, driven by the need to feed a growing population. Concerns include potential antibiotic resistance from eating GMOs and uncertainty about long-term effects on the human body, since many GMOs were developed only recently. In the United States, foods containing GMOs must be labelled as such.
