# Flavour (particle physics)

In the Standard Model, every elementary fermion belongs to a species called a flavour. There are six flavours of quarks (up, down, charm, strange, top, bottom) and six of leptons (electron, muon, tau, plus their three neutrinos), giving twelve in total. Particles of the same flavour share a fixed set of conserved labels. Only the weak interaction can change a particle's flavour; the strong and electromagnetic forces cannot.

## Flavour quantum numbers

A quantum number is a discrete label that classifies a particle, much as the principal quantum number labels an electron's shell in an atom. A flavour quantum number counts how many quarks or leptons of a given type a state contains, with antiquarks counted as negative. Adding these counts for every flavour reconstructs a hadron's identity from its valence quarks.

| Symbol | Flavour | Value for the named quark |
|--------|---------|---------------------------|
| I₃ | Isospin (up vs. down) | u = +½, d = −½ |
| S | Strangeness | s = −1 |
| C | Charm | c = +1 |
| B′ | Bottomness (or "beauty") | b = −1 |
| T | Topness (or "truth") | t = +1 |

Leptons carry a parallel set: electron number, muon number, tau number, and matching numbers for the three neutrinos. These per-generation lepton numbers are only approximately conserved, since neutrinos of different flavours mix.

## Relation to electric charge

The Gell-Mann–Nishijima formula ties the flavour labels to electric charge:

Q = I₃ + ½Y, with Y = B + S + C + B′ + T.

Once you know a hadron's baryon number and flavour counts, its charge follows. Antiquarks reverse every flavour number in sign, so an anti-s quark has strangeness +1. Weak hypercharge Y_W is a separate quantity used in the electroweak theory.

## Conservation and violation

Strong and electromagnetic interactions conserve every flavour number individually, so they cannot turn a strange quark into a down quark. The weak interaction can change flavour, with a rough selection rule: for a first-order weak decay each flavour number changes by at most one unit, so ΔC = ±1, ΔS = ±1, ΔB′ = ±1. The quantities conserved even by weak processes are electric charge Q, weak isospin T₃, baryon number B, and lepton number L. Some Grand Unified Theories relax B and L but keep the difference B − L.

## Generations and weak doublets

Each charged lepton pairs with one neutrino to form a generation, and each up-type quark (u, c, t) pairs with a down-type quark (d, s, b) to form another. Members of a pair have opposite weak isospin T₃: +½ for up-type quarks and neutrinos, −½ for down-type quarks and the charged leptons. Three such doublets exist for quarks and three for leptons; together they account for all twelve flavours.

## Flavour mixing

A weak eigenstate is not the same as a mass eigenstate. The Cabibbo–Kobayashi–Maskawa (CKM) matrix expresses the relation for quarks, and the Pontecorvo–Maki–Nakagawa–Sakata (PMNS) matrix does the same for neutrinos. A quark produced by the weak force is a superposition of mass eigenstates, so it can decay into any down-type partner with a probability given by a CKM entry. The CKM matrix allows CP violation in the Standard Model and requires at least three quark generations. Neutrino mixing, described by the PMNS matrix, has been observed: an electron neutrino produced in the Sun can arrive at Earth as a muon or tau neutrino.

## Flavour symmetry in QCD

In quantum chromodynamics, flavour is an approximate global symmetry. When the up, down, and strange quark masses are small compared with the QCD scale of about 250 MeV, transformations that rotate these three flavours into one another are nearly exact. For massless quarks the symmetry splits into independent copies acting on the left- and right-handed parts of the quark field, described by the chiral group SU(N_f)_L × SU(N_f)_R. Below the QCD scale this symmetry is spontaneously broken, which is why quarks acquire a valence (constituent) mass much larger than their bare current mass. The top quark is an extreme outlier: with a predicted lifetime of only about 5×10⁻²⁵ s, it decays before it can bind into a hadron.

## History

Isospin was introduced in 1932 by Werner Heisenberg to treat the nearly degenerate proton and neutron as two states of one particle. The discovery of strange particles in the 1950s added strangeness, and Kazuhiko Nishijima's 1953 formula tied strangeness, isospin, and charge together. Murray Gell-Mann's Eightfold Way then organised the hadrons into representations of SU(3) and motivated the quark model. The 1970 GIM mechanism predicted the charm quark to suppress flavour-changing neutral currents, and the J/ψ meson was found in 1974. Makoto Kobayashi and Toshihide Maskawa predicted the bottom and top quarks in 1973 as the simplest way to accommodate CP violation, a scheme that required the CKM matrix and a third generation.

## The flavour problem

The Standard Model leaves the fermion masses and the CKM and PMNS mixing angles as free parameters with no explanation for their values. The electron is about 350,000 times lighter than the top quark, the muon is roughly 200 times heavier than the electron, and the CKM entries vary by orders of magnitude. Why three generations exist, why the masses spread so widely, and why the mixing angles take the values measured in experiment remain open questions collectively called the flavour puzzle.
