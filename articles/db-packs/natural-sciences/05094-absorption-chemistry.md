# Absorption (chemistry)

Absorption is a process in which atoms, molecules, or ions move into the bulk phase of a liquid or solid, distributing themselves through the volume of the absorbing material rather than staying at its surface. The surface-only counterpart is adsorption, and the umbrella term covering absorption, adsorption, and ion exchange is sorption.

The bulk-versus-surface distinction is the central idea: an absorbent pulls material into its interior, while an adsorbent holds material on its exterior. This contrast fixes what each process depends on, how it scales, and how it responds to the structure of the absorbing medium.

## Absorption versus adsorption

| Property | Absorption | Adsorption |
|---|---|---|
| Where the species resides | Throughout the bulk (volume) of the material | On the surface only |
| Locus of uptake | Interior of the absorbing phase | Exterior of the absorbing phase |

## Mechanisms

Absorption can be physical, chemical, or a mixture of both. The type determines the equations that describe equilibrium.

### Physical absorption

When no chemical reaction accompanies the transfer, the distribution of a solute between two immiscible bulk phases at equilibrium follows the Nernst distribution law. The ratio of the concentrations of a given species in the two phases is constant at a fixed temperature:

[x]₁ / [x]₂ = K_N(x,12)

The constant K_N is the partition coefficient, and its value depends on temperature. The relation holds when concentrations are not too high and the species does not change its chemical form between the phases. If the species associates or dissociates in one phase, the equation still governs the equilibrium of the unreacted form, while every other form must be tracked through the additional equilibria that produce it. For gases, concentration can be obtained from partial pressure using the ideal gas law, c = p/RT, or replaced by partial pressures directly.

A purely physical transfer between two liquid phases is the basis of liquid–liquid extraction, in which a solute moves from one liquid into another immiscible liquid without reacting. Noble gases and osmium tetroxide are textbook solutes that transfer this way.

### Chemical (reactive) absorption

Reactive absorption combines physical uptake with a stoichiometric reaction between the absorbed and absorbing substances. Uptake then depends on reaction stoichiometry and reactant concentrations, not just on a partition coefficient. Acid–base reactions behave this way and do not follow the Nernst partition law, which is why they can pull a gas such as carbon dioxide out of a gas stream far more effectively than physical solubility alone. The reaction of carbon dioxide with sodium hydroxide is the standard example. Industrial reactive absorption is commonly carried out in plate or packed columns that provide controlled contact between gas and liquid phases.

## Water in a solid

Hydrophilic solids, including many of biological origin, absorb water readily. Polar interactions between water and the solid's functional groups favor partitioning of water into the bulk, so a dry hydrophilic material can pick up measurable water from air even at modest humidity. A fiber exposed to the atmosphere usually contains some water even when it feels dry; heating in an oven drives that water off as a measurable loss in weight, and the mass returns when the fiber is brought back to a normal atmosphere. In the textile industry the proportion of a material's weight made up by water under standard atmospheric conditions is called the moisture regain, a quantity that affects the weight, feel, and processing of every natural and synthetic fabric.
