# Amalgam (chemistry)

An amalgam is an alloy of mercury with another metal. The product may be a liquid, a soft paste, or a solid, depending on the proportion of mercury present. Alloys form through metallic bonding, in which the electrostatic attraction of shared conduction electrons binds positively charged metal ions into a crystal lattice. Many metals dissolve readily into mercury at room temperature, and solubility increases with temperature. The most soluble elements are indium, thallium, cadmium, and cesium. These reactions are typically exothermic.

Heavier metals amalgamate more readily than lighter ones, and notable exceptions include iron, platinum, tungsten, tantalum, and aluminium. Iron is among the least soluble, which is why iron flasks were historically used to transport mercury. Mercury has high electronegativity, which helps drive the formation of these metallic mixtures.

## Where mercury comes from

Mercury is relatively abundant in the Earth's crust but is most often found as cinnabar (mercury sulfide), with an estimated 600,000 tonnes of cinnabar deposits worldwide. Several natural silver amalgams exist, including schachnerite, paraschachnerite, moschellandsbergite, arquerite, and eugenite. Gold amalgams include aurihydrargyrumite and weishanite.

## Specific amalgams

| Amalgam | Notable feature or use |
|---|---|
| Sodium (NaHg₂) | Byproduct of the chloralkali process; reducing agent; decomposes in water to NaOH, H₂, and Hg |
| Potassium (KHg, KHg₂) | Gold and silver intermetallics, melting at 178 °C and 278 °C; air- and water-sensitive |
| Aluminium | Reagent for reducing imines to amines; destroys the protective oxide layer, so even trace mercury seriously corrodes aluminium, which is why mercury is banned on aircraft |
| Tin (with mercury) | Mirror coating developed in 16th-century Murano, roughly 75% Sn and 25% Hg; production ceased around 1900 |
| Zinc | Used in the Clemmensen reduction and Jones reductor; once amalgamated onto dry-battery plates to prevent deterioration |
| Thallium (8.5%) | Eutectic freezes at −60 °C, lower than mercury's −38.8 °C, suited to low-temperature thermometers |
| Gold (AuHg₂ to Au₈Hg) | Forms readily when clean gold contacts mercury; basis of gold extraction from ore |
| Ammonium (NH₄Hgₙ) | Spongy mass from electrolytic reduction; stable near −40 °C, decomposes at room temperature or in water or alcohol |

## Dental amalgam

Dentistry has long used mercury alloys containing silver, copper, indium, tin, and zinc. The mixture stays workable briefly so it can be packed into irregular cavities, then hardens. The recipe appears in the Chinese *materia medica* of Su Kung in 659 AD, was recommended in 1528 by the German physician Johannes Stockerus, and was formally developed as a filling by Louis Nicolas Regnart in 1818. The first American dental society dissolved in 1856 over amalgam-related controversy.

Amalgam is inexpensive, long-lasting, and considered generally safe for most patients, though some studies have been challenged. Environmental and health concerns have driven restrictions: in July 2018 the EU prohibited amalgam for children under 15 and for pregnant or breastfeeding women, and in 2020 the US FDA recommended against its use in similar high-risk groups.

## Gold and silver mining

Mercury's affinity for precious metals made it central to historical mining. The patio process, developed in Mexico in 1554, was the first amalgamation method applied to silver ores. In placer mining, mercury was added to riffle boxes to capture fine gold; in stamp mills, crushed ore was washed over mercury-coated copper plates. The resulting amalgam was scraped off and heated, evaporating the mercury (recovered by condenser for reuse) and leaving the gold behind. Mercury amalgamation has been largely replaced in developed nations because of toxic waste, but small-scale placer miners, often illegally in developing countries, still use it. The legacy of 19th-century California placer mining continues to pollute river and estuary environments.

## Historical and practical notes

Before electroplating existed, amalgam gilding was a common technique for coating metal surfaces, used in China as early as the third to first centuries BC and still practised in the Morimoto workshop in Kyoto. Mercury and its amalgams require special handling and disposal, and waste from reactions such as aluminium-amalgam reductions contains mercury that must be captured.

## Detection of mercury ions

Mercury salts are far more water-soluble and toxic than the metal itself. A simple probe uses the readiness of Hg²⁺ to form an amalgam with copper foil: a nitric-acid solution of the unknown salt is applied to copper, and any mercury ions deposit as silvery spots of amalgam. Silver ions leave similar marks, but the silver spots wash away, allowing the two to be distinguished. The underlying reaction is Hg²⁺ + Cu → Hg + Cu²⁺.
