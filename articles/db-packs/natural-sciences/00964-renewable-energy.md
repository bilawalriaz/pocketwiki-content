# Renewable energy

Renewable energy is electricity, heat, or fuel drawn from natural processes that refill faster than humans consume them: sunlight, wind, moving water, Earth's internal heat, and plant matter. Burning fossil fuels is the largest source of human greenhouse gas emissions, so replacing them with renewables is the central lever for limiting climate change, and it also cuts the air pollution that kills millions each year. The categories are not the same: nuclear power emits little carbon but consumes mined uranium, so it is low-carbon rather than renewable; biomass can be carbon-intensive if forests are not replanted. Sustainable energy goes further, weighing total impact on future generations.

## Scale and trajectory

Renewables supplied 28% of global electricity in 2021, up from 20% in 2011, and exceeded 30% by 2025. In 2025 they made up 85.6% of all new power capacity added worldwide and 49.4% of total installed capacity. Solar PV crossed 1 terawatt of global capacity in 2024. The International Energy Agency estimates that reaching net-zero emissions by 2050 requires about 90% of electricity to come from renewables. At COP28 in 2023, roughly three-quarters of countries pledged to triple renewable capacity by 2030; the European Union hit 47% renewable electricity by 2025 against a 40% target.

## Why costs collapsed

After four decades of investment, solar and wind are the cheapest way to add new electricity in most countries. In 2022 about 86% of new renewable capacity (187 GW) undercut new fossil-fuel plants. Global investment reached $499 billion that year, and installation costs fell 69% for solar PV, 33% for onshore wind, and 45% for offshore wind between 2013 and 2022. Roughly 60% of solar potential and 15% of wind potential are already economically competitive. Renewables employed about 12 million people in 2020, led by 4 million in solar PV; clean-energy jobs reached 35 million by 2022.

## The main technologies

Solar photovoltaic (PV) cells convert light directly into electricity using the photoelectric effect, accounting for about two-thirds of solar capacity and growing fastest. Output grew from 230 GW (2015) to 890 GW (2021), with China adding 560 GW over 2016–2021. Variants include floatovoltaics (floating panels, slightly more efficient) and agrivoltaics (dual farming-and-energy land use). Concentrated solar thermal uses mirrors to heat water that can be stored or drive turbines, but is rarer because it costs more than PV.

Wind power scales with the cube of wind speed, so offshore sites, where winds average about 90% stronger than onshore, produce far more per turbine. Global capacity grew by 557 GW between 2013 and 2021; in 2015 wind met 4% of world electricity, over 40% in Denmark, and around 20% in Ireland, Portugal, and Spain.

Hydropower is the oldest large-scale renewable. Water is roughly 800 times denser than air, which is why turbines convert over 90% of the moving water's energy, the highest efficiency of any renewable. Global capacity reached 1,360 GW in 2021, but only about a third of the estimated 14,000 TWh/year potential is developed. Forms range from giants like Three Gorges (2003) and Itaipu (1984) to small plants under 50 MW and run-of-the-river designs that need no large reservoir. Pumped-storage hydropower, pumping water uphill to release it later, provides over 85% of all grid storage.

Bioenergy burns plant material for heat or ferments sugars from sugarcane, maize, rapeseed, or palm oil into liquid transport fuels. Biofuels supplied 3.5% of transport energy in 2022. Burning biomass emits about 39 g of CO₂ per MJ against 75 g for fossil fuels, but demand for palm oil has driven deforestation in Brazil and Indonesia, and some feedstocks are unsustainable at current rates of harvest.

Geothermal energy taps heat from radioactive decay and the planet's original formation, concentrated near tectonic plate edges. The United States leads with 2.7 GW (2023); global capacity is about 15 GW. Iceland runs almost entirely on renewables (geothermal plus hydro), and Kenya draws 43% of its electricity from geothermal. Shallow heat pumps work almost anywhere; generating electricity needs rock above about 150 °C.

## The intermittency problem

Sun and wind are variable, so their capacity factor, the share of nameplate output actually delivered over a year, is lower than that of dispatchable fossil, nuclear, or hydro plants. Grids manage this with gas plants on standby today, and over the long term with batteries, pumped storage, smart grids that shift demand, transmission between regions, and sector coupling: linking electricity with transport (EV charging and vehicle-to-grid), industry (green hydrogen from electrolysis), and buildings (thermal storage). Building more panels than needed guarantees output on cloudy, still days but forces curtailment when the weather is good.

## Emerging options

Enhanced geothermal systems drill about 10 km down and fracture hot rock to extract heat anywhere, still in costly demonstration. Marine energy from waves, tides, and ocean currents remains small; the Sihwa Lake tidal barrage in South Korea is the largest at roughly 550 GWh per year. Space-based solar, artificial photosynthesis, algae fuels, and piezoelectricity are at research or pilot scale; a 2024 NASA study judged space-based solar economically uncompetitive with terrestrial renewables for the foreseeable future.

## Risks and trade-offs

China centralises about 90% of the photovoltaic supply chain and dominates battery and mineral processing, while lithium, cobalt, nickel, copper, and rare earths are concentrated in Chile, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Indonesia, Australia, and China. Mining in the Salar de Atacama already uses 65% of the region's water, and demand for lithium could grow fortyfold by 2040. Climate change itself is starting to bite: Sudan and Namibia saw hydropower output roughly halve in 2023, heatwaves and clouds cut solar yield, and droughts constrain nuclear cooling. Renewables also need up to ten times more land than fossil plants for the same energy, and over 2,000 facilities overlap with environmentally important areas, while distributed inverters raise cyberattack risks that the EU's NIS2 directive now covers. Current expansion, although record-breaking, still falls well short of the pace the IEA says is needed for net zero, held back by fossil-fuel subsidies, incumbent lobbying, and local land opposition.
