# Physical chemistry

Physical chemistry uses physics to explain and predict chemical behavior, from a single bond to a reactor full of material. It applies thermodynamics, quantum mechanics, statistical mechanics, and kinetics to connect what atoms and electrons do with what a measurable sample does: whether a reaction proceeds on its own, how fast it runs, what phases appear, and how molecules are shaped and bonded.

## Core branches

**Quantum chemistry** applies quantum mechanics to electrons in molecules. It predicts bond strength, geometry, vibrational motion, and the wavelengths of light a substance absorbs or emits. These predictions need the positions of nuclei and the distribution of electrons.

**Chemical thermodynamics** sets the limits. It tells how far a reaction can go, how much energy it can release or absorb, and whether a proposed process is feasible at constant temperature and pressure. Gibbs energy indicates spontaneity; the phase rule relates the number of phases, components, and degrees of freedom in an equilibrium system; chemical potential measures the energy change when a mole of a substance is added to a system.

**Chemical kinetics** addresses how fast reactions proceed. It studies reaction rates, the high-energy transition state that reactants pass through to form products, and how catalysts lower that barrier.

**Statistical mechanics** is the bridge between scales. A sample contains on the order of 6×10²³ particles, yet its state is captured by a few variables (pressure, temperature, concentration) derived from molecular behavior, without empirical correlations. Pressure, phase behavior, and equilibrium constants can be predicted from molecular models rather than measured particle by particle. The same logic underlies *colligative properties*, such as boiling point elevation and freezing point depression, which depend only on the number of solute particles, not their identity.

**Spectroscopy** probes structure by measuring how matter interacts with electromagnetic radiation, since each molecule's electronic and vibrational states produce characteristic absorption and emission patterns.

## History in brief

Mikhail Lomonosov coined the term "physical chemistry" in 1752. The modern discipline took shape in the 1860s–1880s as researchers applied thermodynamics, electrolyte theory, and rate laws to chemistry. J. Willard Gibbs's 1876 paper *On the Equilibrium of Heterogeneous Substances* introduced Gibbs energy, chemical potential, and the phase rule. The 1887 founding of *Zeitschrift für Physikalische Chemie* by Ostwald and van 't Hoff, and Nobel Prizes for Ostwald, van 't Hoff, and Arrhenius between 1901 and 1909, established the field as a profession. Quantum mechanics matured into quantum chemistry under Linus Pauling in the 1930s; the 1940s–1950s added nuclear chemistry, including isotope separation during World War II.

## Boundaries and open questions

Physical chemistry is sometimes confused with chemical physics. The distinction is one of emphasis: physical chemistry focuses on supra-molecular, bulk phenomena such as chemical equilibrium and colloids, while chemical physics leans toward molecular and atomic-level problems. A related question is how far macroscopic behavior can be predicted from molecular structure alone. Statistical mechanics and quantum chemistry offer theoretical routes, but real systems often need empirical input. Non-equilibrium and quasi-equilibrium thermodynamics extend the classical equilibrium-based framework to dynamic processes such as living cells, reactors, and atmospheric chemistry, and remain active research areas.
