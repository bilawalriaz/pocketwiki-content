# Matrix element (physics)

A matrix element in quantum mechanics is the complex number obtained by sandwiching an operator between two quantum states in Dirac (bra-ket) notation. The most commonly studied case is the matrix element of a Hamiltonian, written $\langle f|\hat{H}|i\rangle$, which encodes how strongly $\hat{H}$ drives a transition from an initial state $|i\rangle$ to a final state $|f\rangle$. Its squared modulus $|\langle f|\hat{H}|i\rangle|^{2}$ is the transition probability amplitude. Matrix elements of this kind are central tools in atomic, nuclear, and particle physics, where they appear in processes such as beta decay, neutrinoless double beta decay, and double beta decay.

The notation $\langle f|\hat{H}|i\rangle$ is an inner product, not an entry of a matrix in the linear-algebra sense. A "ket" $|i\rangle$ denotes a state vector and a "bra" $\langle f|$ its dual. $\hat{H}$ is the Hamiltonian, the observable for total energy; a linear operator acts on states and distributes over superpositions. Perturbation theory is the approximation scheme in which a small extra interaction is added to a solved Hamiltonian so its effects can be computed order by order.

In the unperturbed system, the Hamiltonian $\hat{H}^{(0)} = -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2} + V$ has eigenstates $|\psi_{n}^{0}\rangle$ satisfying $\hat{H}^{(0)}|\psi_{n}^{0}\rangle = E_{n}^{0}|\psi_{n}^{0}\rangle$, where $V$ is the potential energy and $m$ the particle's mass. Because the eigenstates are orthonormal, $\langle \psi_{m}^{0}|\psi_{n}^{0}\rangle = \delta_{nm}$, the matrix elements $\langle \psi_{m}^{0}|\hat{H}^{(0)}|\psi_{n}^{0}\rangle = E_{m}^{0}\delta_{nm}$ vanish unless $m=n$. The unperturbed Hamiltonian therefore leaves eigenstates uncoupled and cannot induce transitions between them.

A perturbation is introduced by writing the full Hamiltonian as $\hat{H} = \hat{H}^{(0)} + \lambda \hat{H}^{\text{int}}$, where $\lambda$ (running from 0 to 1) scales the interaction strength. The interaction term generally produces off-diagonal matrix elements $\langle \psi_{m}^{0}|\hat{H}^{\text{int}}|\psi_{n}^{0}\rangle = V' \neq 0$ for $m \neq n$, and these non-zero off-diagonal elements are what give a non-zero probability of transitions between eigenstates. The superscript on $E_{n}^{0}$ marks the perturbation order: 0 is the unperturbed value and integers $n \geq 1$ label successive corrections.

The hydrogen-to-helium comparison illustrates this. The hydrogen Hamiltonian contains only the proton–electron Coulomb potential $V$. Adding a second electron introduces an electron–electron repulsion written as $\hat{H}^{\text{int}} = V'$, a perturbation whose matrix elements couple the previously independent hydrogen eigenstates. Extending the procedure to heavier atoms and nuclides means adding interaction terms for each extra electron, proton, and neutron, and the many-body problem grows rapidly in complexity as those matrix elements accumulate.
