# Scientific method

The scientific method builds reliable knowledge about the world by observing carefully, forming testable guesses, and letting evidence decide whether those guesses survive. A *hypothesis* is any proposed explanation; it is only useful if some conceivable observation could prove it wrong. This requirement, called *falsifiability*, separates a scientific claim from a belief that no result could disturb. The method is often taught as a tidy sequence: ask, hypothesise, test, conclude. In practice it is a set of habits applied with judgement: observing, modelling, predicting, experimenting, and revising over years or centuries.

## A working cycle

A pragmatic version of the cycle, used in most working research, runs:

1. Define a question.
2. Gather information and observe.
3. Form a hypothesis.
4. Test it reproducibly.
5. Analyse the data.
6. Interpret the results, then publish.
7. Retest, often looping back to step 3.

Four components recur in every version: *characterisations* (observations, definitions, measurements), hypotheses, predictions, and experiments. Each is subject to *peer review*, the anonymous expert evaluation that checks whether the work is sound rather than whether its conclusions are correct.

A confirming result raises the credibility of a hypothesis but never proves it; a contradicting result can sink it. This asymmetry is the heart of the method. Eddington's 1919 eclipse observations supported Einstein's general relativity over Newton's gravitation by matching a prediction Newtonian physics could not produce. Mercury's perihelion anomaly, a small drift in the planet's orbit, played the same role decades earlier, prompting a relativistic test.

Measurements always carry uncertainty, so scientists attach error estimates and use statistics carefully. Bayes' theorem gives the formal rule for updating a belief's probability in light of new evidence. A 2005 analysis by Ioannidis warned that much published research, especially in biomedicine, fails to replicate because its statistical foundation is weak.

## Reasoning styles

Three styles of reasoning feed the cycle. *Deduction* draws specific conclusions that must follow from general premises. *Induction* generalises from observations, as Kepler did when he distilled centuries of planetary positions into laws. *Abduction*, Peirce's term for inferring the most plausible explanation, is the creative leap that generates a hypothesis in the first place, which Peirce linked to the "irritation of doubt." Science uses all three: induction and abduction propose, deduction predicts, and experiment judges.

The *hypothetico-deductive model* tests the *consequences* of a theory rather than the theory itself. A failed prediction falsifies the hypothesis; a successful one only corroborates it. *Induction* fell out of favour for a time, dismissed as a "fishing expedition," but has been revived through data-mining and high-throughput measurement, though its conclusions are always probabilistic. *Mathematical modelling* builds abstract structures, tests them computationally, and translates results back to the world via *correspondence rules*. Monte Carlo simulations are one example.

## Principles that keep the cycle honest

Falsifiability, Popper's demarcation criterion, draws the line between science and claims like astrology that nothing could refute. Two further principles guard against self-deception. *Theory-ladenness*, named by Hanson in 1958, holds that no observation is theory-free: Kepler and Brahe watched the same sunrise and drew different conclusions, and Neptune was located mathematically before observers knew what they were seeing. *Confirmation bias* makes people register evidence that fits their expectations; Muybridge's stop-action photographs disproved the long-held belief that a galloping horse extends its legs forward and back together in a splayed posture. *Strong inference*, the practice of entertaining several rival hypotheses at once, counters this bias directly.

Scientists also use heuristics, knowing they are imperfect guides rather than rules. A good theory is accurate, internally consistent, explanatory beyond the data it was built on, unifying, and fruitful. *Parsimony* (Occam's razor) prefers the simplest explanation that fits. *Elegance* is a contested aesthetic that can either illuminate or stall a field. After Einstein's relativity, *invariance* (explanations that hold regardless of the observer's perspective) gained weight, and David Deutsch called the search for "hard-to-vary explanations" the origin of all scientific progress.

## Measurement, instruments, and replication

Science advances with its instruments. Thermometers, spectroscopes, particle accelerators, and space telescopes extend what can be measured, and the largest facilities, including the LHC, LIGO, the National Ignition Facility, the ISS, and the James Webb Space Telescope, cost billions over decades. Careful record-keeping goes back to Hipparchus around 190–120 BCE. Controlled experiments are documented for al-Battani and Alhazen in the 9th and 10th centuries, with Alhazen's *Book of Optics* (1027) disproving the prevailing theory of vision by experiment and leading him to deduce that space is rarer than air. In 1079, Ibn Mu'adh inferred a roughly 50-mile-thick atmosphere from astronomical data.

A single confirming experiment is not enough; results must be reproducible by others. Journals such as *Nature* and *Science*, and funders such as the NSF, now require raw data to be archived so claims can be checked. The *metascience* movement, which studies science itself, was largely founded by Ioannidis's 2005 paper exposing how poorly many findings hold up under replication.

## Empiricism, realism, and the role of theory

Empiricism holds that knowledge comes from observation, in contrast to the stricter rationalism of Descartes. Peirce reframed inquiry as a movement from real doubt to a belief one can act on, rejecting Descartes' "hyperbolic doubt." Hawking's 2010 idea of *model-dependent realism* extends this: accept any model that makes useful predictions, without claiming it is literal truth. Underlying these views is the recognition that there is no pure observation; every measurement is interpreted through prior theory.

## Is there one method?

A long-running debate asks whether a universal method exists. Unificationists say yes; pluralists reply that science is too varied. Feyerabend's 1975 *Against Method* argued that any proposed rule has historical counterexamples, concluding "anything goes," though he still accepted some rules justified by a meta-methodology. Kuhn replaced the image of science as homogeneous with one of *thought collectives*: groups of researchers sharing largely invisible assumptions, pursuing local problems with local tools. In 2013, the US NGSS standards dropped the singular "scientific method," and Smolin's essay of the same year argued that two ethical principles (honesty and respect for evidence) do more work than any rulebook.

The realism debate runs alongside this: realists hold that science uncovers how the world really is, while antirealists see theories as useful instruments without deep truth. Related open questions include how much progress is driven by chance (estimates put a third to half of discoveries in the stumbled-upon category, leading Taleb to call the method "anti-fragile") and why mathematics describes the physical world at all, the puzzle Wigner called the "unreasonable effectiveness" of mathematics, which some now attribute to selection in the questions mathematicians choose to study.
