# Theory of relativity

The theory of relativity is a framework, developed by Albert Einstein, that replaced Newton's classical mechanics by treating space and time as a single four-dimensional entity called spacetime. It comes in two parts: special relativity (1905), which describes motion without gravity, and general relativity (1915), which adds gravity by curving spacetime.

## Core postulates

Special relativity rests on two postulates. First, the principle of relativity: the laws of physics are identical in all inertial frames (coordinate systems where force-free objects move at constant velocity). Second, the constancy of light speed: every observer measures the same speed of light, regardless of their own motion. These postulates contradict Newtonian intuition but align with experiment.

From them, the old Galilean transformations give way to Lorentz transformations, which mix space and time. Observable consequences follow: relativity of simultaneity (events simultaneous in one frame need not be in another), time dilation (moving clocks run slower), length contraction (moving objects shorten along the direction of motion), a universal speed limit set by light, and mass–energy equivalence E = mc².

## Adding gravity

General relativity begins with the equivalence principle: accelerated motion and a uniform gravitational field are locally indistinguishable. A corollary is that free fall is inertial motion along curved spacetime, not a force pulling objects downward. Einstein worked with Marcel Grossmann using Riemannian geometry, the mathematics of curved multidimensional spaces. The result was the 1915 Einstein field equations, which relate spacetime curvature to the mass, energy, and momentum it contains. Solutions are metric tensors, objects that define distances, geometry, and the paths of inertial motion.

Observable consequences include gravitational time dilation (clocks deeper in a gravitational well run slower), orbital precession (the slow rotation of elliptical orbits, observed in Mercury and binary pulsars), bending of light by gravity, frame-dragging (the twisting of spacetime near rotating masses), and cosmic expansion.

## Acceptance and evidence

Special relativity drew on earlier work by Michelson, Lorentz, and Poincaré. Max Planck and Hermann Minkowski added refinements, and the name "theory of relativity" itself derives from Planck's 1906 term "Relativtheorie." By the 1920s the theory was widely accepted and embedded in atomic, nuclear, and quantum physics.

General relativity, completed between 1907 and 1915, was harder to absorb. It offered only small corrections to Newtonian gravity, and its mathematics was accessible to only a few specialists. Acceptance came mainly after roughly 1960, when new mathematical tools joined astronomical discoveries: quasars (1963), the 3 K cosmic microwave background (1965), pulsars (1967), and the first credible black hole candidates. Each provided fresh tests, and the predictions held.

Three experiments anchored special relativity: the Michelson–Morley experiment (1881, 1887), which detected no ether wind and showed light's speed to be isotropic; the Kennedy–Thorndike experiment (1932), which confirmed light-speed constancy between inertial frames in relative motion; and the Ives–Stilwell experiment (1938, 1941), which observed time dilation directly through the transverse Doppler effect. General relativity was confirmed through Mercury's orbital precession, the bending of starlight by the Sun, gravitational redshift, equivalence-principle tests, and frame-dragging measurements. Einstein classified relativity as a "principle-theory," derived from empirical regularities rather than from a model of underlying mechanisms.

## Practical applications and a historical caveat

GPS, GLONASS, and Galileo satellite navigation systems must correct for both special and general relativistic effects, because their satellites move faster and sit in a weaker gravitational potential than Earth-bound receivers. High-precision timekeeping, electron microscopes, and particle accelerators likewise depend on relativistic corrections to function.

The Michelson–Morley null result showed that the speed of light is isotropic, but it could not by itself rule out every aether model. The ad hoc FitzGerald–Lorentz contraction hypothesis attempted to preserve aether theory by assuming matter shrinks in motion, though it had no deeper justification. The same caution applied to the Kennedy–Thorndike result, which was hard to explain by appealing to the solar system's motion through space. Einstein's preference for principle-theories over constructive theories was a deliberate response to this kind of patchwork reasoning.
