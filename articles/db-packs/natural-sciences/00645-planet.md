# Planet

A planet is a large, rounded astronomical body orbiting a star, a stellar remnant, or a brown dwarf. The word comes from Greek *planētai*, "wanderers," because these bodies moved against the fixed stars. The Solar System has eight planets: four terrestrial (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars) and four giant planets (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune). Beyond it, over 6,400 exoplanets have been confirmed, including hot Jupiters, super-Earths, and mini-Neptunes.

## How Planets Form

Planets form inside protoplanetary disks, the rotating gas-and-dust rings around young stars. Dust grains collide and stick, growing into kilometer-scale planetesimals. Continued collisions and gravity build these into protoplanets, and once a body is massive enough, its own gravity overcomes rigid material strength and pulls it into a round shape, a state called hydrostatic equilibrium.

Composition depends on distance from the star. Beyond the frost line, where water, ammonia, and methane can condense into ice, giant planets accrete large amounts of ices and gas. Inside the frost line, only rock and metal condense, producing smaller, denser terrestrial planets. Solar wind and photoevaporation from the young star clear the remaining gas and dust. Internal heat from impacts and radioactive decay drives differentiation, separating each planet into a metallic core, a rocky mantle, and a thin crust.

## Planets of the Solar System

The terrestrial planets share rocky compositions, solid surfaces, and thin atmospheres. Mercury, the smallest planet at 0.055 Earth masses, has almost no atmosphere. Venus matches Earth in size but has a dense CO₂ atmosphere. Earth supports liquid water and life. Mars is smaller, cold, and lost most of its atmosphere to space.

Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants dominated by hydrogen and helium. Jupiter is 318 Earth masses, the most massive planet in the Solar System. Uranus and Neptune are ice giants, similar in size to the gas giants but with a larger fraction of water, ammonia, and methane ices. Neptune was discovered in 1846 by predicting its position from gravitational effects on Uranus, the first planet found by prediction.

Beyond the eight, dwarf planets such as Ceres in the asteroid belt and Pluto and Eris in the Kuiper belt share the same rounded shape but have not cleared their orbits of other debris. Pluto was reclassified in 2006 because it shares its orbit with Kuiper Belt objects; Eris, discovered in 2005 and 27% more massive than Pluto, belongs to the same category. At least 19 Solar System moons reach planetary mass, including Ganymede, larger than Mercury, and Titan, the only moon with a thick atmosphere.

## Exoplanets and Diversity

The first exoplanets were found in 1992 around pulsar PSR B1257+12, and the first around a Sun-like star, 51 Pegasi b, was confirmed in 1995. Hot Jupiters orbit close to their stars with surface temperatures of thousands of degrees; most are tidally locked, the same face permanently turned toward the star. Super-Earths (1–10 Earth masses) and mini-Neptunes fill the range between Earth and Neptune, and roughly one in five Sun-like stars hosts an Earth-sized planet in its habitable zone, the orbital range where liquid water can exist on the surface. Rogue planets drifting through interstellar space without a host star and planets orbiting brown dwarfs extend the category's reach.

## Defining a Planet

The 2006 International Astronomical Union (IAU) definition requires an object to (1) orbit a star or stellar remnant, (2) be in hydrostatic equilibrium so its own gravity makes it round, and (3) have cleared its orbital neighborhood of other large bodies. The clearing criterion is the most contested part, because it ties planethood to orbital location rather than to the body's intrinsic properties. Geophysical definitions based purely on roundness would restore Pluto's planetary status but have not been adopted; Jean-Luc Margot has proposed a quantitative threshold, the π parameter, for orbital clearing. The 2018 IAU working definition for exoplanets sets the upper bound at 13 Jupiter masses, where deuterium fusion becomes possible, though many catalogs include more massive objects, and the boundary between the largest planets and brown dwarfs remains unresolved.

## Planetary Properties

Orbits are ellipses. Eccentricity measures how stretched the orbit is, the semi-major axis sets its average size, and inclination is its tilt relative to a reference plane. Axial tilt drives seasons. Rotation periods range from under 10 hours (Jupiter) to 243 days (Venus), and Venus rotates backward relative to its orbital motion. A magnetic field requires a convecting, electrically conducting fluid in the interior; Earth, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune generate fields through motion in molten iron or metallic hydrogen.
