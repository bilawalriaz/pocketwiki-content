# Atom

An atom is the smallest unit of a chemical element that still behaves like that element. Every solid, liquid, gas, and living thing is made of atoms. Almost all of an atom's mass sits in a tiny central nucleus, while a cloud of much lighter electrons occupies a volume roughly 100,000 times larger around it. The number of protons in the nucleus, called the atomic number, fixes which element the atom is. The number of neutrons can vary without changing the element, and atoms of the same element with different neutron counts are called isotopes.

A mechanical picture: a dense nucleus of positively charged protons and electrically neutral neutrons, surrounded by negatively charged electrons held by the electromagnetic force. Inside the nucleus, protons and neutrons (collectively nucleons) are themselves made of quarks held together by gluons, and they stay bound against their mutual electrical repulsion through the residual strong force. The nucleus spans only 2–7 femtometers, while the whole atom extends to about 100,000 femtometers, so an atom is almost entirely empty space, with nearly all its mass packed into a region too small to resolve with an ordinary optical microscope.

Electrons do not orbit like planets. Quantum mechanics, developed by Werner Heisenberg and Erwin Schrödinger in 1925–1927, describes each electron as a three-dimensional standing wave called an orbital, with quantized energy, shape, and orientation. The uncertainty principle forbids specifying both the exact position and momentum of a bound electron, so the "electron cloud" is a probability distribution rather than a definite path. Electrons fill orbitals in shells, and the outermost shell, the valence shell, governs an atom's chemistry. When atoms approach one another, they transfer or share valence electrons to reach more stable configurations, producing ions, molecules, or crystals. A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons; a cation has fewer electrons, an anion more. Properties such as atomic radius, ionization energy, and magnetic moment follow from the electron configuration and the spin of the nucleus.

Nuclear stability depends on the ratio of neutrons to protons and on magic numbers, proton or neutron counts that fill closed quantum shells and give extra binding. Unstable nuclei release excess energy through radioactive decay, emitting alpha particles, beta particles, gamma rays, or splitting through fission, and each radioactive isotope has a characteristic half-life.

## How the model was built

The atomic concept is ancient: Greek philosophers around 3000 BC proposed indivisible particles called *atomos*, but had no experimental evidence. In 1803–1808, John Dalton gave the idea scientific form by analyzing chemical compounds and formulating the law of multiple proportions, the observation that when two elements form several different compounds, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in small whole-number ratios. For example, tin oxides contain oxygen and tin in a 1:2 mass ratio, iron oxides in a 2:3 ratio, and nitrogen oxides in a 1:2:4 ratio. Dalton concluded that each element consists of identical atoms with a characteristic weight, and that chemical reactions are rearrangements of those atoms in whole-number combinations.

In 1897, J. J. Thomson discovered the electron, proving atoms were not indivisible, and proposed the "plum pudding" model, in which negative electrons sat inside a diffuse sphere of positive charge. That picture collapsed in 1908–1913, when Ernest Rutherford, Hans Geiger, and Ernest Marsden fired alpha particles at thin metal foils and saw a small fraction deflecting at angles greater than 90°, something a diffuse positive sphere could not produce. Rutherford proposed a nuclear model instead: nearly all the mass and all the positive charge concentrated in a tiny central nucleus, with electrons occupying the surrounding space.

A planetary version of that model broke classical physics, because an orbiting electron should radiate energy and spiral inward. Niels Bohr solved the puzzle in 1913 by restricting electrons to stable, quantized orbits that absorb or emit photons of specific energies when electrons jump between them, accounting for the discrete spectral lines of hydrogen. Bohr's model worked for hydrogen but not for heavier atoms, and the deeper theory came from Heisenberg's matrix mechanics and Schrödinger's wave equation, which together replaced fixed orbits with orbitals.

In 1919–1920 Rutherford isolated and named the proton as the hydrogen nucleus and predicted a neutral partner to account for the missing atomic mass. James Chadwick confirmed the existence of that particle, the neutron, in 1932, completing the picture of the nucleus. Since the 1930s the Standard Model, quantum electrodynamics, and the study of quarks and gauge bosons have refined the description of what nucleons are made of and what holds them together. Open questions include the island of stability (predicted long-lived superheavy isotopes around Z = 110–114), baryogenesis (why matter dominates over antimatter), and the nature of dark matter.

## Working with atoms

Atoms reveal themselves through spectroscopy: because each element absorbs and emits light at characteristic wavelengths, the spectrum of a glowing gas, a flame, or a distant star identifies which elements it contains, and this is how the composition of stars is measured. Mass spectrometry sorts atoms by mass, separating isotopes; scanning tunneling microscopes image individual atoms on surfaces; and X-ray spectroscopy probes inner-shell electrons.

The atoms that make up Earth, and almost all the atoms in living things, were forged in the Big Bang (hydrogen, most helium, and trace lithium), inside earlier generations of stars that fused lighter elements into heavier ones up to iron, or in supernovae and neutron-star mergers that built the rest by rapid neutron capture. The first neutral atoms appeared about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, during recombination, when electrons combined with nuclei. The atoms around us today are mostly primordial or stellar in origin, with small contributions from the ongoing radioactive decay of unstable isotopes and, in laboratories, from synthetically produced elements.
