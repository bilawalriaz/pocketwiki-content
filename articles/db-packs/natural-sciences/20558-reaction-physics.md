# Reaction (physics)

Newton's third law of motion states that forces always come in pairs: whenever one object exerts a force on a second object, the second object simultaneously exerts an equal and opposite force on the first. Newton phrased this as, "To every action there is always opposed an equal reaction: or the mutual actions of two bodies upon each other are always equal, and directed to contrary parts." The two forces are simultaneous; neither causes the other. Labeling one the "action" and the other the "reaction" is a matter of convention, and either assignment is valid.

## Interaction with the ground

When a person is about to jump, they push down on the ground with a force greater than their own weight. The ground pushes back up with a force of the same magnitude. When these forces are perpendicular to the ground, physicists call them normal forces. The upward reaction exceeds the person's weight, so the net force is upward and the person accelerates off the ground. In biomechanics, the ground's upward push is called ground reaction force.

A moving vehicle shows the same pattern with friction. A spinning tyre tries to slide backward across the road; the road exerts a forward friction force on the tyre equal in magnitude to the backward force the tyre exerts on the road. That reaction propels the vehicle forward.

## Gravitational forces

The Earth orbits the Sun because the Sun's gravitational pull supplies the centripetal force that bends Earth's path. By the third law, Earth pulls back on the Sun with an equal and opposite force. Because the Sun's mass is far larger than Earth's, the Sun's response is barely visible, and the motion appears to involve only Earth. A more precise description of the two-body system is that Sun and Earth both orbit their common center of mass, the barycenter, which lies inside the Sun but not at its exact center.

## A mass on a support

A hanging, resting, or floating object involves four forces of equal magnitude:

| Symbol | Force |
|---|---|
| F1 | Earth pulls the object downward (its weight) |
| F2 | Object pulls Earth upward |
| F3 | Support pushes the object upward (tension, normal force, or buoyancy) |
| F4 | Object pulls or pushes the support downward |

Newton's third law guarantees F1 = F2 and F3 = F4 because each pair acts on different bodies. The equality F1 = F3 holds only because the object is in equilibrium, which follows from Newton's second law applied to a single body, not from the third law. If a mass hanging from a spring is given a small push, it oscillates because F1 and F3 become unequal during the motion, while F1 = F2 and F3 = F4 continue to hold throughout.

## "Equal and opposite" on the same object

A common student error calls any equal-and-opposite pair acting on the same object an action-reaction pair. A book resting on a table feels a downward gravitational pull from Earth and an upward normal push from the table. Those two are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, yet they both act on the book. Because they share one body, they are not a third-law pair; their equality comes from Newton's second law, since the book is not accelerating. The genuine third-law pairs are the book's weight paired with the book's pull on Earth, and the book's push on the table paired with the table's push on the book.

When a third force is applied, or the table is tilted, or the system is in an accelerating elevator, the two forces on the book are no longer equal. Newton's second law, summing all forces on each body, still governs.

## Causal reading

"Action" and "reaction" suggest that one force produces the other after a delay. They do not. When a soccer player kicks a ball, the player's decision explains why foot and ball come into contact, but the contact itself produces both forces at the same instant through the same underlying electric repulsion between atoms. Physically the situation is symmetric; only the language assigns a cause.

## Centripetal and centrifugal forces

Another common error claims that the centrifugal force a body feels is the reaction to the centripetal force on it. If both acted on the same body, they would cancel and circular motion would be impossible. The centripetal force is real and acts on the moving body, supplied by something external such as a string or a gravitational source. The centrifugal force is a fictitious force, also called a pseudo force, that appears only in a rotating, non-inertial reference frame, and it acts on that frame's contents rather than on the body following the circular path. Confusing centrifugal with a reaction to centripetal mixes a bookkeeping artifact of a non-inertial frame with a real force between two bodies.

A book sliding across a table to a stop makes the distinction concrete. The friction from the table on the book and the friction from the book on the table form a third-law pair acting on two different bodies. The book's weight and the table's normal push on the book are equal and opposite yet both act on the book, so they are not a third-law pair at all.
