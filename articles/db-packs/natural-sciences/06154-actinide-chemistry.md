# Actinide chemistry

Actinide chemistry is a branch of nuclear chemistry concerned with the actinides, the 15 metallic chemical elements from actinium (atomic number 89) to lawrencium (103). The series is named after actinium, the group 3 element that precedes it. Fourteen of the fifteen are f-block elements whose 5f electron shell fills progressively; lawrencium is technically a d-block element but is counted as an actinide by convention. The general symbol "An" stands in for any actinide in equations and general discussion.

The key distinction from the lanthanides (the 4f series directly above) is much greater variability in valence. The lanthanides behave as a relatively uniform +3 family; the actinides access a wider range of oxidation states, which underpins both their chemistry and their technological uses.

## Subfields

**Organoactinide chemistry** studies compounds in which an actinide binds directly to carbon. Although transition-metal organometallic chemistry began in 1955, the actinide equivalent developed largely only in the past few decades, revealing a rich set of compounds with parallels to and sharp differences from d-block organometallics. Carbon binds to actinides through covalent bonds, and the metals also coordinate organic functional groups.

**Actinide thermodynamics** concerns the enthalpies and related quantities needed to predict compound stability. Systematic measurement began with the Manhattan Project, when accurate thermodynamic data became a prerequisite for exploiting nuclear energy. Both fundamental and applied work has continued since the end of the Second World War.

**Nanotechnology and supramolecular chemistry** of the actinides draws on their optical, magnetic, and electronic properties. Compounds with phthalocyanines, porphyrins, and naphthalocyanines show linear and nonlinear optical behaviour, and thermodynamically stable nanoparticles of LnₓMᵧ composition can be formed by d elements in the second halves of the transition series. The source material on proposed molecular-scale devices (storage engines, photoconversion engines based on Ln(II) and Ln(III), electrochemical engines in silicate and phosphate glasses, phase-transition engines) is framed around lanthanides and is treated as a parallel rather than an actinide-specific programme.

## Biological and environmental behaviour

The chemical form of an actinide governs how it moves through the body and the environment, often more than its radioactivity does.

Ingested insoluble compounds, such as high-fired uranium dioxide or mixed oxide (MOX) fuel, pass through the digestive system largely untouched because they cannot dissolve and be absorbed. Inhaled insoluble compounds are more hazardous: they lodge in lung tissue and deliver a continuous radiation dose. Soluble forms such as nitrates, and low-fired oxides, can be absorbed into the bloodstream from the gut; if inhaled, they can dissolve out of the lungs, lowering the local dose.

Thorium deserves separate mention. Monazite, a rare-earth thorium phosphate mineral, is the primary commercial source of thorium. In India, large placer deposits along the Tamil Nadu coast expose residents to natural radiation doses about ten times the worldwide average. Thorium has been linked to liver cancer. Thorium dioxide (thoria) was once used as an X-ray contrast agent under the name Thorotrast, but that medical use has been discontinued.

Uranium is roughly as abundant in the crust as arsenic or molybdenum and is recovered commercially from phosphate rock, lignite, and monazite sands. Seawater contains about 3.3 parts per billion of uranium by weight, held in solution as a uranyl carbonate complex; extraction from seawater has been investigated as a long-term resource. Because uranium's specific activity is very low, its chemical toxicity can outweigh the effects of its radioactivity in many environmental contexts.

Plutonium in the environment takes the form of a plutonyl core, PuO₂, which readily complexes with carbonate and other oxygen-bearing ligands (OH⁻, NO₂⁻, NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻). The resulting charged species are mobile in water and only weakly held by soil.

Radon and radium are often mentioned alongside actinides because they are radioactive, but they are not actinides. Both are daughters produced when uranium decays, and their environmental chemistry is treated separately.

## Nuclear reactions and the discovery of fission

Early evidence for nuclear fission came from neutron irradiation of uranium, which produced short-lived barium isotopes. ¹³⁹Ba (half-life 83 minutes) and ¹⁴⁰Ba (half-life 12.8 days) are major fission products. At the time, standard radiochemical practice used a barium sulphate carrier to isolate what was thought to be a new radium isotope, until the product's chemistry showed it behaved as barium, not radium, and the complementary heavier fission products were identified.

## PUREX reprocessing

The PUREX process is the standard liquid–liquid extraction method for reprocessing spent nuclear fuel. Dissolved fuel in nitric acid is contacted with 30% tributyl phosphate (TBP) in a hydrocarbon diluent such as kerosene. Both uranium, as UO₂(NO₃)₂·2TBP, and plutonium, as a similar complex, move into the organic phase, while most fission products and the transuranium elements americium and curium remain in the aqueous nitric acid.

The extraction of plutonium by an extractant S proceeds by a solvation mechanism:

Pu⁴⁺(aq) + 4 NO₃⁻(aq) + 2 S(org) → [Pu(NO₃)₄S₂](org)

A complex forms between the metal cation, the nitrate ions, and the tributyl phosphate. A model dioxouranium(VI) complex with two nitrates and two triethyl phosphates has been characterised by X-ray crystallography. Insoluble fine solids are filtered out before extraction, since they can stabilise emulsions (called "third phases" in the solvent-extraction community) at the liquid–liquid interface.

Plutonium is separated from uranium by reducing it to the +3 state with aqueous ferrous sulphamate, which sends it back into the aqueous phase. Uranium is then stripped from the kerosene by back-extraction into dilute nitric acid at about 0.2 mol dm⁻³. The minor actinides americium and curium stay with the aqueous raffinate throughout.
