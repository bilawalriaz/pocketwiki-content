# Quantum chemistry

Quantum chemistry is the branch of physical chemistry that applies quantum mechanics to atoms, molecules, and materials to predict their structures, spectra, energies, and reaction behaviour. In practice it is solving the Schrödinger equation for a chemical system, almost always with approximations that make the calculation affordable.

The central object is the electronic wave function: a mathematical description of where electrons are likely to be in a molecule. From it chemists extract bond lengths, vibrational frequencies, reaction rates, and spectroscopic transitions. Because the Schrödinger equation can be solved exactly only for the hydrogen atom (and for the bound states of H₂⁺ within the Born–Oppenheimer approximation), every other system requires approximations or numerical methods, which together form computational chemistry.

The Born–Oppenheimer approximation is the foundational simplification. Nuclei are thousands of times heavier than electrons, so the electronic wave function is calculated for fixed nuclear positions, then nuclei move on the resulting potential energy surface. Treating motion on that single surface is adiabatic dynamics; transitions between coupled surfaces are non-adiabatic dynamics, which govern spin-forbidden reactions and Landau–Zener transitions.

Two traditions describe bonding. Valence bond (VB) theory, beginning with Heitler and London's 1927 treatment of H₂ and formalised by Slater and Pauling, pictures bonds as overlaps of atomic orbitals, with hybridisation and resonance explaining geometry. A covalent bond forms when half-filled orbitals from two atoms overlap, producing a shared electron pair; the bond length is where attraction and repulsion balance. Head-on overlap gives a sigma (σ) bond, side-by-side overlap of in-phase p-orbitals gives a pi (π) bond. Molecular orbital (MO) theory, developed by Hund and Mulliken in 1929, instead describes electrons with wave functions delocalised across the whole molecule, constructed as linear combinations of atomic orbitals. MO theory is less intuitive but predicts spectroscopic properties more reliably and underpins the Hartree–Fock family.

Density functional theory (DFT) replaces the wave function with electron density as the fundamental quantity. Its modern form, the Kohn–Sham scheme, splits the energy into a kinetic term, an external potential, the Hartree (classical electron–electron) energy, and an exchange–correlation term that absorbs all quantum corrections. DFT scales roughly as n³ with the number of basis functions, far cheaper than post-Hartree–Fock methods, yet often matches their accuracy, which is why it is the workhorse of computational chemistry.

Chemical dynamics methods form a spectrum of approximation: full quantum dynamics (solving the time-dependent Schrödinger equation), semiclassical dynamics, classical molecular dynamics, mixed quantum–classical dynamics, and Feynman path-integral molecular dynamics, which adds quantum corrections such as nuclear tunnelling.

Quantum chemistry began when Heitler and London applied the Schrödinger equation to H₂ in 1927, explaining the covalent bond itself. Lewis's 1916 valence-electron model provided earlier conceptual scaffolding, and contributions by Sugiura, Wang, and Slater fed into Pauling's 1930s synthesis. Pauling's 1939 book *The Nature of the Chemical Bond* made the theory accessible to chemists and became a standard text. Hellmann published the first dedicated textbooks in Russian and German in 1937. Parallel work by Mulliken, Born, Hartree, Fock, Hückel, Lennard-Jones, Mayer, and Langmuir extended the theory to structure, reactivity, and bonding, while RRKM theory (Marcus, 1952) and the Landau–Zener formula gave tools to estimate reaction rates and non-adiabatic transition probabilities.

The two persistent challenges are accuracy for small systems and size for large ones: computation time grows as a power of the number of atoms, so progress depends on better exchange–correlation functionals, cheaper post-Hartree–Fock methods, and machine-learning-augmented electronic structure models.
