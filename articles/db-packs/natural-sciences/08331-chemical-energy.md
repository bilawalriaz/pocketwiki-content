# Chemical energy

Chemical energy is the energy stored in chemical substances that is released when they react and rearrange into different substances. Batteries, food, and gasoline are familiar storage media; oxygen gas is another, because its relatively weak double bond makes it energy-rich and indispensable for releasing that energy in combustion.

## Why bonds store energy

Breaking and re-making chemical bonds always involves an energy exchange with the surroundings. When reactants with relatively weak electron-pair bonds rearrange into more strongly bonded products, the surplus energy is released. The general rule follows: the more weakly bonded and less stable a molecule, the more chemical energy it stores.

## How the released energy is measured

The energy released or absorbed by a reaction equals the difference between the energy content of the products and the reactants, provided the initial and final temperature are the same. In practice this change can be estimated from bond energies, or calculated from the internal energies of formation of the reactant and product molecules.

The measured heat depends on the conditions. In a closed, constant-volume container such as a bomb calorimeter, the heat exchanged equals the change in internal energy. In an open vessel at constant pressure, however, the measured heat also includes pressure–volume work, so it equals the enthalpy change — the enthalpy of reaction — at equal initial and final temperatures. A closely related quantity, the heat of combustion, is the energy released by a combustion reaction, drawn largely from breaking oxygen's weak double bond, and is widely used to characterise fuels.

## Energy and food

Food behaves like a hydrocarbon or carbohydrate fuel. When the body oxidises it to carbon dioxide and water, the energy released is analogous to the heat of combustion, though it is measured through food-energy conventions rather than fuel chemistry.

## Conversion into other forms

Chemical energy is a form of potential energy tied to the structural arrangement of atoms, whether through bonds inside a molecule or interactions between molecules. A chemical reaction converts it into other forms: burning a fuel turns the chemical energy of the fuel and oxygen into heat; photosynthesis in green plants converts solar energy into chemical energy, mostly stored in oxygen; and electrochemical reactions allow chemical energy to be turned into electrical energy and back again.

## Chemical energy versus chemical potential

Chemical potential is sometimes confused with chemical energy, but the two are distinct. Chemical potential is not itself a form of potential energy; it is more closely related to free energy, and it expresses a substance's tendency to undergo a change of configuration — through reaction, spatial transport, or particle exchange with a reservoir. The confusion arises because in low-entropy physical systems every bit of potential energy is available to do useful work, so no separate "free" energy term is needed. In high-entropy chemical systems, the total conserved energy (from the first law of thermodynamics) is divided into a portion that can spontaneously drive the system forward as global entropy increases, in line with the second law, and a portion that cannot. Chemical potential belongs to the free-energy portion.
