# Project 8 (physics experiment)

Project 8 is an international neutrino-physics experiment, active since 2009, whose goal is to measure the absolute mass of the electron antineutrino. Its method, Cyclotron Radiation Emission Spectroscopy (CRES), was proposed in 2009 by Benjamin Monreal and Joseph A. Formaggio and first demonstrated by the collaboration in 2015.

## Why neutrino mass matters

Neutrino oscillation experiments have shown that neutrinos have non-zero mass, an observation beyond the original Standard Model. Oscillations are sensitive only to the differences between the squares of the three neutrino masses, not to the absolute scale. Cosmological observations can in principle give the absolute scale but depend on model assumptions. Direct laboratory measurement therefore requires a kinematic, model-independent approach: examining the beta-decay spectrum of tritium near its endpoint, the maximum kinetic energy an emitted electron can carry. If the neutrino has mass, the endpoint is slightly lower and the spectrum's shape near it is distorted in a predictable way.

The electron neutrino produced in tritium beta decay is a quantum-mechanical mixture of three mass states. For convenience, the effective mass of that mixture is denoted $m_\beta$. Neutrino oscillations set a lower bound on $m_\beta$ that depends on the unknown ordering of the three masses: roughly 0.0085 eV/$c^2$ for the normal ordering and 0.048 eV/$c^2$ for the inverted ordering. Project 8 targets a sensitivity of about 0.04 eV/$c^2$, just below the inverted-ordering bound; failing to detect a mass above this level would disfavor the inverted ordering.

## Cyclotron Radiation Emission Spectroscopy

In tritium beta decay a nucleus converts one neutron into a proton, ejecting an electron and an electron antineutrino. CRES measures the electron's kinetic energy from the frequency of its cyclotron radiation rather than from a direct energy sensor.

When an electron spirals in a magnetic field, it emits electromagnetic radiation whose frequency depends on the magnetic field strength and on the electron's relativistic energy, the sum of its rest mass and its kinetic energy divided by $c^2$. Because the rest mass is fixed, the kinetic energy follows from the frequency. Each individual electron shows up in a time–frequency spectrogram as a slowly rising track, a "chirp," as it radiatively loses energy while trapped.

## Phases

Phase I (CRES Demonstrator). The goal was to prove CRES works on single electrons. Monoenergetic conversion electrons from $^{83m}$Kr decay (lines near 17.8 keV and 30 keV) were confined in a roughly 1 T field from a superconducting solenoid. Microwave radiation near the K band was guided through a WR42 rectangular waveguide in its fundamental TE$_{10}$ mode. Magnetic trapping lengthened each electron's observation time. Phase I produced the first direct detection of cyclotron radiation from a single electron and confirmed frequency-based energy reconstruction.

Phase II (Molecular tritium, first CRES neutrino-mass limit). The technique was extended to molecular tritium (T$_2$) in a cryogenic gas cell at 85 K inside a 0.96 T solenoid. Tritium was held at $10^{-6}$ mbar to balance count rate against scattering. Radiation near 26 GHz was coupled into the TE$_{11}$ mode of a circular waveguide (10.03 mm inner diameter, 132 mm long), amplified at 30 K, and processed into real-time spectrograms. Two trap depths were used: a shallow trap (~0.08 mT) giving 1.66 ± 0.19 eV FWHM resolution after subtracting the natural krypton linewidth, and a deep trap (~1.4 mT) accepting ~40 times more events at ~54 eV resolution. $^{83m}$Kr electrons at 17.8 keV provided in-situ calibration. Over 82 live days in three frequency windows spanning 16.2–19.8 keV, 3,770 tritium beta-decay events were recorded, yielding the first neutrino-mass bound ever set by cyclotron-frequency spectroscopy:

$$m_\beta < 155 \text{ eV}/c^2 \text{ (90\% Bayesian credible interval)}$$
$$m_\beta < 152 \text{ eV}/c^2 \text{ (90\% frequentist confidence level)}$$

Although far above KATRIN's 0.45 eV/$c^2$ bound, this result validated CRES on tritium with low backgrounds.

Phase III (Cavity-based CRES, scaling R&D). Electrons excite standing-wave modes inside resonant microwave cavities rather than coupling to open waveguides. Operating at lower magnetic fields, and thus lower cyclotron frequencies, lets the detection volume grow while preserving frequency resolution. Mode-filtered cavity designs control electromagnetic mode crowding as the apparatus scales up.

Planned Phase IV. The aim is to push sensitivity toward ≲ 40 meV by combining a much larger atomic-tritium source with cavity CRES. Atomic tritium replaces T$_2$ to remove the rotational and vibrational excitations of the molecule, which broaden the spectrum near the endpoint. Producing, cooling, and trapping atomic tritium, exploiting its electron magnetic moment, is itself an active R&D program.

## Where Project 8 stands

KATRIN currently leads with an upper limit of $m_\beta < 0.45$ eV/$c^2$ at 90% confidence, using a large electrostatic spectrometer on tritium beta decay. Going further with that design would require much larger spectrometers. Project 8's frequency-based approach is precise because frequency can be measured with high accuracy, statistically scalable because extended source volumes operate as differential spectrometers, and systematically clean because atomic tritium removes molecular broadening. Because CRES samples the beta spectrum differentially over a wide energy range, it is also sensitive to hypothetical sterile neutrinos, which would imprint small kinks below the endpoint.
