# Asterism (astronomy)

An asterism is a recognisable pattern of stars in the night sky. The term covers anything from a shape of two or three stars to a sprawling figure spanning many constellations. Asterisms are a broader and more casual concept than the 88 official constellations, which are defined regions of sky with strict boundaries set by the International Astronomical Union (IAU) in 1928. Every constellation is built around an asterism, but an asterism can also cross constellation borders, sit inside one, or form a sub-part of a traditional figure.

The stars in an asterism are not necessarily physically related. The Summer Triangle (Deneb, Altair, Vega) is a line-of-sight pattern: its stars lie at very different distances and have no gravitational link. By contrast, the three stars of Orion's Belt all belong to the Orion OB1 association, a real co-moving group, and five of the seven Big Dipper stars are members of the Ursa Major Moving Group. Physical clusters such as the Hyades and the Pleiades can act as asterisms on their own and also form parts of larger figures. Stars in an asterism are usually of similar brightness, because mismatched magnitudes would distort the shape.

## How asterisms relate to constellations

In early civilisations, including ancient India and Babylon, star patterns were imagined as stick figures, and the terms "constellation" and "asterism" were used interchangeably. Pliny the Elder listed 72 asterisms in his *Naturalis Historia*. A general list of about 48 constellations began to take shape with the Greek astronomer Hipparchus around 190–120 BCE. As European explorers reached the southern hemisphere, Johann Bayer and Nicolas Louis de Lacaille added new southern figures between the 16th and 18th centuries; some were accepted into the modern list, while others remain asterisms. The IAU's 1928 division of the entire sky into 88 bounded regions officially separated the two terms, though informal use still blurs them.

## First-magnitude asterisms

The most useful asterisms for learning the sky are built from first-magnitude stars, the brightest class. The Summer Triangle dominates northern summer skies and sits in the Milky Way band toward the Galactic Center. The Winter Triangle (Betelgeuse, Sirius, Procyon) is its winter counterpart. The larger Winter Hexagon is a six-star ring including Pollux, Capella, Aldebaran, Rigel, Sirius, and Procyon, with Castor on its edge; adding Betelgeuse at its centre turns it into the Heavenly G. The Spring Triangle (Arcturus, Regulus, Spica) marks northern spring, and the Southern Cross pairs Acrux and Mimosa with the first-magnitude Pointers Alpha and Beta Centauri.

## Constellation-based asterisms

Many asterisms take their names from the constellations they inhabit. The Big Dipper (also The Plough or Charles's Wain) is the seven brightest stars of Ursa Major. Orion's Belt (Alnitak, Alnilam, Mintaka) and Orion's Sword, which contains the Orion Nebula, are sub-figures inside Orion. Cassiopeia forms a W, the Great Square of Pegasus is a quadrilateral inside Pegasus, and the Teapot in Sagittarius uses the Milky Way as "steam" rising from its spout. Smaller asterisms pick out parts of figures: the Water Jar of Aquarius, the Crab Breast of Cancer enclosing the Beehive Cluster, the Snake Head of Hydra, and the Bull's Face of Taurus, where the Hyades forms a V with the red eye Aldebaran.

Other single-constellation asterisms ignore the traditional figure: the Keystone in Hercules, the Sickle in Leo, Job's Coffin in Delphinus, and the Saucepan, which is Orion's Belt and Sword viewed differently.

## Asterisms across multiple constellations

Some asterisms cut across constellation borders. The Egyptian X, built around the Winter Triangle and a southern triangle through Sirius, straddles the celestial equator and is best seen from south of the Mediterranean. The Lozenge mixes three stars from Draco with one from Hercules. The False Cross in Vela and Carina is sometimes mistaken for the Southern Cross, a recognised hazard for astronavigation. The Northern Y (Arcturus, Seginus, Izar, Alphecca) stays visible even in light-polluted skies where fainter stars vanish. The Lightning Bolt, running from Pegasus through Aquarius into Capricornus, is bright enough to serve as a guide between three constellations.

## Finding the poles with pointers

Two bright pairs act as directional signposts. Dubhe and Merak, the two stars at the bowl of the Big Dipper, are called the Pointers: extending a line from Merak through Dubhe by about five times their separation lands on Polaris, the North Star. In the south, Rigil Kentaurus and Hadar, the Southern Pointers, lead the eye toward the Southern Cross.

## Telescopic asterisms

Asterisms range far beyond the naked eye. The "37" or "LE" inside NGC 2169 in Orion is visible in binoculars. The Engagement Ring in Ursa Minor uses Polaris as a diamond set in a faint circle of stars, and the Broken Engagement Ring sits near Merak in Ursa Major. The Christmas Tree Cluster in Monoceros, the Coathanger (Brocchi's Cluster) in Vulpecula, Kemble's Cascade ending in NGC 1502 in Camelopardalis, and Napoleon's Hat south of Arcturus are faint patterns rewarding binoculars or a small telescope. Messier 73 in Aquarius, once catalogued as a cluster, was shown by 2002 analysis to be an asterism of four unrelated stars.
