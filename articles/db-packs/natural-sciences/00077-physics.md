# Physics

Physics is the science of matter, energy, force, and motion. It is called the fundamental science because every other natural science operates within its laws. Its working method is to explain diverse phenomena with a small number of underlying causes, then keep unifying those causes. Two and a half millennia of this search have produced two great frameworks: classical physics, valid for ordinary objects and slow motion, and modern physics, required when things are very small, very fast, very massive, or very cold.

## Scale decides which theory applies

The most useful idea in physics is that scale dictates the rules. Classical mechanics, thermodynamics, and electromagnetism describe everyday objects moving far slower than light. Quantum mechanics describes atomic and subatomic systems, where energy and momentum come in discrete packets. Special relativity describes motion near the speed of light. General relativity describes gravity as the curvature of spacetime. Each theory is exact within its domain. An electron orbiting a nucleus needs quantum mechanics; a planet orbiting a star needs classical mechanics and general relativity; the two pictures of space, time, and matter differ fundamentally.

## How physics got here

Physics grew out of astronomy. Sumerian, Egyptian, and Indus Valley cultures before 3000 BCE could predict the motions of the Sun, Moon, and stars, though their explanations were not scientific. Greek thinkers from about 650 BCE onward, especially Thales, Leucippus, and Democritus, began to seek natural causes. Aristotle's 4th-century BCE treatise *Physics* built a system around four elements seeking their natural place, in which heavier objects fall faster and motion requires a sustained force. Aristotelian physics dominated for roughly two thousand years, reinforced by the idea of a prime mover as the source of all motion.

By the 6th century CE, John Philoponus had begun challenging Aristotle. Islamic scholars, notably Ibn al-Haytham in his *Book of Optics*, advanced the experimental study of light, treating rays as physical and deriving laws of reflection and refraction through controlled experiment. The Scientific Revolution of the 16th and 17th centuries replaced the geocentric model with Copernicus's heliocentric one, codified planetary motion through Kepler's laws of 1609–1619, and was sealed by Newton's unification of motion and universal gravitation. Newton and Leibniz independently invented calculus to handle these problems mathematically. By the late 19th century, mechanics, thermodynamics, and electromagnetism together formed "classical physics," matching nearly all known observations.

Three experiments then refused to fit. The luminiferous aether could not be detected, blackbody radiation emitted the wrong spectrum of light, and illuminated metals released electrons whose energies did not depend on brightness. Planck's quantum hypothesis, Einstein's special relativity, and the quantum mechanics built by Heisenberg, Schrödinger, and Dirac dissolved all three anomalies. Quantum theory treats energy and momentum as discrete; relativity holds the speed of light constant for every observer. These two theories are the pillars of modern physics and the source of the Standard Model of particle physics.

## Core theories

| Theory | Domain | Key idea |
|---|---|---|
| Classical mechanics | Ordinary objects, slow motion | Forces change motion predictably; gravity acts at a distance |
| Thermodynamics and statistical mechanics | Heat, gases, bulk matter | Energy is conserved; entropy tends to increase |
| Electromagnetism | Charges, currents, light | Electric and magnetic fields form a single entity; light is an electromagnetic wave |
| Special relativity | Motion near light speed | Speed of light is invariant; time and space are observer-dependent |
| Quantum mechanics | Atoms and subatomic particles | Observable quantities come in discrete steps; particles and waves are complementary |
| General relativity | Gravity, cosmology | Mass curves spacetime; objects follow the curvature |

## Branches

Classical physics splits into statics, kinematics, dynamics, fluid mechanics, acoustics, optics, heat, and electricity and magnetism. Modern physics studies extremes of scale: atomic, molecular, and optical (AMO) physics; nuclear physics, with applications in medicine, MRI, and radiocarbon dating; condensed matter physics, the largest subfield, studying solids, liquids, superfluids, and Bose–Einstein condensates; astrophysics, which uses nuclear physics to trace stellar evolution and cosmology; and particle physics, which probes the most basic constituents of matter.

The Standard Model accounts for twelve known particles of matter, six quarks and six leptons, interacting through gauge bosons that carry the strong, weak, and electromagnetic forces. Its most famous recent triumph was CERN's 2012 detection of a particle consistent with the Higgs boson.

## Research method and open frontiers

Physics advances by the scientific method: theorists build mathematical models, experimentalists test predictions, and phenomenologists connect messy data to underlying theory. Active frontiers include high-temperature superconductivity, evidence for physics beyond the Standard Model (notably that neutrinos have mass), and the nature of dark matter and dark energy. Many familiar phenomena, including sandpile formation, water droplet shapes, and turbulent flow, remain poorly understood despite seeming tractable, and have drawn growing attention since the 1970s through new mathematics and computing.
