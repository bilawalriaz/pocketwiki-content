# Materials science

Materials science studies how a material's internal structure, from atomic arrangement to features visible under a microscope, controls its mechanical, electrical, thermal, and optical behavior, and how that knowledge is used to design materials for specific uses. The field is organized around the **processing-structure-properties-performance** paradigm: processing determines structure, structure determines properties, and properties determine how a material performs in service. This framework links every topic in the discipline, from steel alloys to computer chips.

Modern materials science grew out of metallurgy, which grew out of the use of fire and the craft of working metals and ceramics. A turning point came in the late 19th century when Josiah Willard Gibbs showed that the thermodynamic properties of atomic-scale phases could be linked to bulk physical properties. The 20th century absorbed contributions from the Space Race (refractory alloys and silica- and carbon-based reentry materials), the rise of polymers and semiconductors, and the founding of dedicated university departments. In the United States, the Advanced Research Projects Agency funded university-hosted materials laboratories in the early 1960s to expand basic research and training. The discipline is now interdisciplinary, drawing on physics, chemistry, and engineering, and increasingly on computer simulation.

## Structure across length scales

Structure is studied at several length scales, and each one contributes to the final properties:

- **Atomic structure (angstroms):** the bonding and arrangement of atoms. Most electrical, magnetic, and chemical behavior originates here.
- **Crystallography:** the geometry of crystals. The **unit cell** is the smallest repeating block of a crystal lattice. Most engineering solids are **polycrystalline**, aggregates of many small crystals (grains) with different orientations, and powder diffraction of these aggregates is the standard way to identify structures. Many polymers are partly or wholly **amorphous** (non-crystalline, with no long-range atomic order), as are glasses and some ceramics.
- **Nanostructure (1 to 100 nm):** features such as nanotubes, nanoparticles, and surface textures, with one, two, or three dimensions in the nanoscale. These features often produce unusual optical, electronic, or mechanical behavior.
- **Microstructure (100 nm to a few cm):** grains, phase boundaries, precipitates, and defects such as dislocations, vacancies, and interstitials. Microstructure largely controls strength, toughness, ductility, hardness, and wear. Real crystals always contain such defects; the Hall-Petch relationship, for example, ties grain size to strength.
- **Macrostructure (mm to m):** features visible to the naked eye, the result of how the part was shaped.

A perfect crystal is physically impossible; what makes a material useful is often which defects it contains and how they are arranged.

## Properties and processing

The properties engineers care about, mechanical, chemical, electrical, thermal, optical, and magnetic, are determined by structure and can be tuned through processing. Heat treatment of steel (quenching and tempering) is a classic example: the same composition can be made soft or hard by controlling microstructure. Diffusion is the dominant mechanism by which a material's structure changes over time, which is why **kinetics** (the rates at which a material moves toward equilibrium) matters as much as thermodynamics, which sets where equilibrium lies. **Phase diagrams** are the central thermodynamic tool for predicting which phases are stable at a given temperature and composition.

## The main classes of materials

Most solids fall into three broad classes, with composites as a fourth engineered combination:

- **Metals and alloys:** Iron-carbon alloys dominate by quantity and value. Steel contains 0.01 to 2.00% carbon by weight, with carbon raising hardness and strength but lowering ductility. Cast iron has 2.00 to 6.67% carbon. Stainless steel is regular steel alloyed with more than 10% chromium, often with nickel and molybdenum added. Alloys of aluminum, titanium, and magnesium are valued for their high strength-to-weight ratios and are widely used in aerospace.
- **Ceramics and glasses:** typically the most brittle of the industrial classes, often held together by covalent or ionic-covalent bonds with silica (SiO2) as a building block. Glasses are amorphous: a viscous liquid that freezes into a disordered state on cooling, used for windowpanes, eyeglasses, and optical fibers. Engineering ceramics such as alumina, silicon carbide, and tungsten carbide are made by sintering powders and can be toughened by adding second-phase particles to deflect cracks.
- **Polymers:** long chains of repeating units, used as the raw materials for plastics and rubber. Common plastics include polyethylene, polypropylene, PVC, polystyrene, nylons, and polycarbonates. They are classified as commodity, specialty, or engineering plastics based on performance, with additives and plasticizers extending their range.
- **Composites:** two or more combined phases, ranging from steel-reinforced concrete to the reinforced carbon-carbon used on Space Shuttle wing leading edges, which withstands reentry temperatures up to 1,510 degrees C.

**Semiconductors** form a distinct functional class. Their conductivity sits between that of conductors and insulators and is extremely sensitive to small amounts of impurities, a sensitivity exploited by **doping** to engineer electronic behavior. Silicon is the dominant semiconductor, used in wafers and integrated circuits. Gallium arsenide is preferred for high-speed electronics such as mobile phones and radar because of its higher electron mobility.

## How the field works

Characterization draws on X-ray, electron, and neutron diffraction, electron microscopy, Raman and energy-dispersive spectroscopy, and thermal analysis to determine structure at each length scale. **Thermodynamics** describes the equilibrium states a material can occupy; **kinetics** describes how it gets there, including through diffusion-controlled microstructural changes during heat treatment. **Computational materials science** spans density functional theory at the atomic scale, molecular dynamics, phase-field and dislocation-dynamics models at the mesoscale, and finite-element methods at the continuum scale, and is increasingly integrated with experiments to accelerate materials design.

## Active research areas

Research areas include **nanomaterials** (fullerenes, carbon nanotubes, nanocrystals, graphene), **biomaterials** (synthetic and natural substances used in implants, drug delivery, and tissue engineering, including hydroxylapatite-coated hip implants), and electronic, optical, and magnetic materials for superconducting, spintronic, and metamaterial applications. Forensic materials engineering applies the same structure-property reasoning to explain why components fail, helping prevent accidents in aviation and other safety-critical industries.
