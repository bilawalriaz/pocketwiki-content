# Electric light

An electric light converts electricity into visible light. Three physical principles have powered its history: heating a material until it glows, sustaining an electric arc through a gas, and pushing electrons across a semiconductor boundary. Efficiency has climbed at every step, from roughly 2–5% of input power becoming light in incandescent bulbs to several times that in modern LEDs, and that shift now reshapes electricity demand itself.

## How the three families work

**Incandescent lamps** force current through a thin tungsten filament inside a sealed glass envelope, usually filled with argon or evacuated. Operating temperatures sit between 2,000 and 3,330 K, hot enough that thermal radiation spans the visible spectrum and produces a continuous, warm-toned light. The drawback is waste: 95–98% of the energy leaves as heat. Halogen lamps are a higher-temperature variant using fused silica envelopes; they last longer, hold output more steadily, and pack small filaments for precise optical control, but they run above 200 °C and shatter if skin oils contaminate the glass.

**Gas-discharge lamps** pass current through a gas between two electrodes, sustaining an arc or plasma. Fluorescent tubes are the best-known example: low-pressure mercury vapour emits ultraviolet photons that a phosphor coating on the inner wall converts into visible light. They reach 50–100 lumens per watt, several times incandescent efficiency, so a 15 W tube can replace a 60 W bulb. A ballast is required because the arc's resistance collapses once it strikes; without regulation the lamp would draw runaway current. Compact fluorescents (CFLs) package the same idea in an Edison-screw base, but their mercury content forces hazardous-waste recycling. Other discharge variants use neon, sodium, xenon, or metal halides. Low-pressure sodium lamps are the most efficient artificial source of all, but emit a near-monochromatic orange-yellow light, useful to astronomers because it can be filtered out, yet unsuitable for homes because colours look wrong.

**LEDs (light-emitting diodes)** are solid-state. An electron crossing the band gap, the energy step inside a semiconductor, releases a photon whose wavelength is set by the material. Red LEDs appeared in 1962 and spent decades as indicator lights. The breakthrough was Shuji Nakamura's 1994 high-brightness blue LED, because blue plus a yellow phosphor yields white light. LEDs now dominate general illumination: they use little power, run cool, switch instantly, hold their colour, and can last up to 100,000 hours in indicator service, less in lighting because the diodes are driven harder. Lifespan is highly temperature-dependent, so thermal management is a central design problem.

|---|---|
| 1799–1800 | Volta's battery enables heating wires to incandescence. |
| 1802 | Petrov sustains the first persistent electric arc. |
| 1806 | Davy demonstrates a practical arc light. |
| 1840 | de la Rue builds a platinum-filament vacuum bulb, too costly to sell. |
| Late 1870s–1880s | Swan (UK) and Edison (US) commercialise incandescent lamps and later merge. |
| 1910 | Claude introduces neon lighting. |
| 1912 | Coolidge patents the tungsten filament, setting the incandescent standard. |
| 1934 | Inman's team at GE builds the first fluorescent prototype. |
| 1962 | First practical red LED demonstrated. |
| 1994 | Nakamura's blue LED enables white LEDs via phosphor. |
| 2009 | Philips ships LED lamps to replace 60 W incandescents. |
| 2019 | US electricity demand falls for a fifth straight year, driven largely by LEDs. |

Carbon arc lamps, two carbon rods struck together in open air, were the first practical electric light. They outshone early filaments and lit streets and projectors from the 1870s until well into the 20th century, but constant rod adjustment, strong UV output, and the rise of enclosed tungsten bulbs ended their era.

## How lamps are compared

Lamps are labelled by shape and socket codes such as A19/A60 E26/E27 for ordinary Edison-screw bulbs. Performance is judged on luminous flux (lumens), power (watts), luminous efficacy (lumens per watt), colour temperature (kelvins, lower being warmer), and the colour rendering index (CRI), which scores how faithfully colours appear. Rated life is the median running time to 50% failure, though for LEDs the industry now defines it as the time until half the units drop to 70% of initial output, a different rule that makes consumer comparison awkward. In the 1920s–30s the Phoebus cartel deliberately shortened bulb life as a form of planned obsolescence. Frequent switching shortens life, especially for CFLs.

## Beyond illumination

Incandescent filaments act as heat sources in incubators, infrared heaters, and toys such as the Easy-Bake Oven. Phototherapy uses bright or blue light to treat seasonal affective disorder, skin conditions, and neonatal jaundice. Horticultural grow lights drive indoor hydroponics. Lasers, a related solid-state technology, supply tightly focused beams where ordinary lamps cannot.

Outdoor lighting grew 3–6% per year in the late 20th century, and an estimated 80% of the world's population now lives under skies brightened by light pollution, which disrupts wildlife and obscures the stars. Regulations in many countries now phase out inefficient incandescents; economic analyses project tens of billions of euros in savings and billions of tonnes of avoided CO₂, while critics note higher upfront costs, light-quality trade-offs, and the recycling burden of mercury-bearing fluorescents.
