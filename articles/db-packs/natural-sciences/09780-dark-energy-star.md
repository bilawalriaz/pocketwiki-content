# Dark-energy star

A dark-energy star is a hypothetical compact object proposed by physicist George Chapline as an alternative to black holes. In the standard picture, a black hole's interior ends in a singularity where gravity crushes matter to infinite density and information is destroyed. A dark-energy star replaces that singularity with a region of vacuum energy. Infalling matter is converted into dark energy as it crosses the event horizon, and the resulting negative pressure pushes back against gravity, preventing collapse to a point. The result is a compact body that looks like a black hole from the outside but has no central singularity and no information loss.

Chapline first argued in March 2005 that quantum mechanics makes it a "near certainty" that black holes do not exist and are instead dark-energy stars, a view held by a minority of physicists. A dark-energy star is distinct from a gravastar, another speculative compact object, though both attempt to avoid singularities.

The motivation is a tension between quantum mechanics and general relativity at the event horizon. Quantum physics treats time as absolute, but general relativity predicts that an object falling toward a black hole appears, to a distant observer, to freeze at the event horizon, with its time slowing infinitely. The infalling object itself experiences normal time. To reconcile these views, Chapline proposed that a phase transition occurs in the structure of space at the event horizon, drawing an analogy from superfluid physics. In a superfluid column, increasing density slows sound until the speed would reach zero. Before that happens, quantum effects force the sound waves to dissipate their energy into the superfluid, so the zero-speed condition is never reached. The dark-energy star applies the same logic to space at the horizon: rather than letting the classical description produce a singular collapse, a quantum phase transition intervenes.

The hypothesis has several testable consequences. As infalling matter approaches the event horizon, it decays into successively lighter particles. Near the horizon, environmental effects are predicted to accelerate proton decay, which may explain observed high-energy cosmic-ray sources and positron sources in the sky. When matter crosses the event horizon, its mass-energy is converted into dark energy, producing negative pressure that halts further collapse. The dark-energy contribution inside the star corresponds to a very large value for the cosmological constant in that region.

Primordial dark-energy stars could form from fluctuations of spacetime itself, analogous to liquid droplets condensing out of a cooling gas. If such objects formed after the Big Bang, the framework would alter the interpretation of black hole candidates and offer a single mechanism that could simultaneously account for dark energy and dark matter, two of cosmology's largest unexplained components.
