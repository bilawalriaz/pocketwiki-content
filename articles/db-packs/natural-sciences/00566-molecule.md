# Molecule

A molecule is a stable group of two or more atoms bound together by chemical bonds, usually covalent. It is the smallest piece of a substance that keeps that substance's chemical identity, and the basic unit from which gases, liquids, and living matter are built. Molecules differ from the extended solids that make up rocks, metals, and salts, because those materials have no clear molecular boundary; their atoms run on in continuous networks rather than stopping at a discrete cluster.

The idea is older than its proof. Greek philosophers Leucippus, Democritus, and Empedocles proposed around 450 BC that matter is made of indivisible atoms moved by attraction and repulsion. Robert Boyle restated this in 1661 as a corpuscular picture, where chemical change is clusters of particles rearranging. The modern word arrived in 1811 when Amedeo Avogadro, in his *Essay on Determining the Relative Masses of the Elementary Molecules of Bodies*, argued that gas particles are usually clusters of atoms, not single atoms. In 1926 Jean Perrin won the Nobel Prize in Physics after measuring the Avogadro constant by three independent methods (Brownian motion, emulsions, and Einstein's rotation theory), converting the molecule from a hypothesis into a measured object.

The physical explanation came a year later. In 1927 Fritz London and Walter Heitler applied the Schrödinger equation (the quantum rule that governs how electrons behave around nuclei) to H₂ and showed that the bond arises because the shared electron's quantum state lowers the system's energy. This effect is saturable, directional, and far more precise than the "hook-and-eye" picture Dalton had proposed. Linus Pauling built on this in 1931 with *The Nature of the Chemical Bond*, introducing **hybridization**: atomic orbitals on a single atom mix into new shapes, so that one 2s and three 2p orbitals blend into four equivalent sp³ hybrids, and those hybrids form bonds at fixed angles. This is why methane (CH₄) is tetrahedral with four identical C–H bonds rather than a flat, lopsided cluster.

Bonds come in two main kinds. A **covalent bond** holds a molecule together by sharing a pair of electrons between two nuclei, balancing their attraction against electron-electron and nucleus-nucleus repulsion. An **ionic bond** forms when one atom transfers an electron to another, producing a positive *cation* and a negative *anion* that stick together electrostatically. Ionic bonding almost always builds extended crystal lattices such as table salt, giving solids rather than isolated molecules at ordinary temperatures, though vaporising a salt can free individual ion pairs into the gas phase. Metallic bonding is sometimes called a "giant molecule," but metals conduct and deform in ways that discrete molecules do not.

Molecules are classified by composition. A **homonuclear** molecule is built from one element, like O₂ or N₂. A **heteronuclear** molecule combines two or more elements, like H₂O or CO₂, and is what chemists usually call a compound. The strict definition excludes anything held together only by weaker forces such as hydrogen bonds or by ionic contacts, though **polyatomic ions** like NH₄⁺ are routinely treated as molecules in quantum physics and biochemistry despite their charge.

Three kinds of formula describe a molecule. An **empirical formula** gives the smallest whole-number ratio of its atoms (CH for acetylene, C₂H₂). A **molecular formula** gives the exact count of each atom. A **structural formula** shows how those atoms connect in three dimensions, and that shape, the fixed bond lengths and bond angles called **molecular geometry**, governs how the molecule reacts. **Isomers** share a molecular formula but differ in connectivity, so they behave as different substances; **stereoisomers** share even physical properties but can act very differently in biology, which is why two molecules with the same atoms can be a medicine and a poison.

Sizes vary enormously. The H–H bond in H₂ is 0.74 Å long, near the small end; proteins and DNA stretch into the macroscopic, even though every link in the chain is still a small repeating molecular unit. Molecules are studied by **spectroscopy**: microwaves probe rotation, infrared probes vibration, ultraviolet and visible light probe electrons, and NMR probes the magnetic environment of nuclei. These methods identify molecules in space, in cells, and in reaction mixtures. The simplest molecule-like system is H₂⁺, a single electron bound to two protons, which the Schrödinger equation solves exactly; everything larger is approximated by computational chemistry. IUPAC's working definition treats a molecule as any potential-energy well deep enough to trap at least one vibrational state, a criterion that includes weakly bound pairs such as the helium dimer He₂.
