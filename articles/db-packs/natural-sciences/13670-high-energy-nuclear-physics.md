# High-energy nuclear physics

High-energy nuclear physics studies nuclear matter under the extreme conditions created when heavy ions collide at relativistic speeds. The central goal is to produce and characterise the quark–gluon plasma (QGP), a state of free quarks and gluons that filled the universe microseconds after the Big Bang, before matter condensed into atoms.

## Why heavy ions

Colliding two heavy nuclei concentrates enough energy into a tiny volume to "melt" ordinary nuclear matter into its quark–gluon constituents. Lighter collision systems cannot reach the same energy density, so heavy-ion collisions probe regimes inaccessible to electron–positron or proton–proton colliders, including electromagnetic production of leptons and mesons at far higher luminosities.

Quarks and gluons carry colour charge and are normally confined inside hadrons (protons, neutrons, and related particles). Above a threshold temperature called the Hagedorn temperature, confinement lifts and quarks and gluons move freely, forming the QGP.

## How the experiments work

Fixed-target experiments accelerate a bunch of roughly 10⁶–10⁸ ions to about 0.999c and smash them into a stationary target. Energies progressed from about 1 GeV/nucleon at JINR and the LBNL Bevalac up to 158 GeV/nucleon at CERN's SPS. Collider experiments steer two beams of ions toward each other: RHIC at Brookhaven began colliding ions in 1999, and the LHC at CERN began heavy-ion collisions in 2010 at an order of magnitude higher energy. Late-1990s fixed-target work at the SPS and the BNL AGS focused on symmetric systems, gold on gold and uranium on uranium.

At RHIC, gold–gold collisions reach a centre-of-mass energy of 200 GeV/nucleon, while proton–proton collisions reach 500 GeV/nucleon. The LHC's ALICE detector studies Pb–Pb collisions at 2.76 TeV per nucleon pair, with ATLAS, CMS, and LHCb also participating. For about one month per year the LHC runs in nuclear-collision mode, stripping lead nuclei of their electron cloud and colliding about 1250 valence quarks to generate a hot quark–gluon soup.

## Achieved conditions

ALICE announced in August 2012, at the Quark Matter 2012 conference in Washington, D.C., that its collisions produced QGP at about 5.5 trillion kelvins, roughly 38% higher than the 4 trillion kelvin record set at Brookhaven in 2010. The energy density reached is about 10 GeV/fm³ (≈1.8 × 10¹⁶ g/cm³), the temperature exceeds the Sun's core by more than 100,000 times, and the resulting relativistic pressure is about 0.52 × 10³¹ bar. A typical QGP temperature is T ≈ 300 MeV/k_B ≈ 3.3 × 10¹² K.

## Research objectives

The programme targets three linked goals: to form and characterise the QGP that filled the universe in its first 30 microseconds; to study the transition at the Hagedorn temperature from the confining vacuum, where quarks are locked inside hadrons, to a state where they move freely; and to investigate how hadrons acquire most of their mass, which theorists link to confinement and the structure of the vacuum.

## Origins of the field

Theoretical foundations came from Enrico Fermi in the US and Lev Landau in the USSR, whose work on multiparticle production led in the early 1960s to a thermal description and Rolf Hagedorn's statistical bootstrap model. The first modestly relativistic heavy-ion collisions were performed at LBNL's Bevalac (using ions from the HILAC accelerator) and at JINR in Dubna, at about 1–2 GeV per nucleon, enough to compress nuclear matter to a few times its normal density. That demonstration motivated successive generations of higher-energy fixed-target and collider experiments.
