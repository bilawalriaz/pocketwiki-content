# Anomaly (physics)

In quantum physics, an anomaly is the failure of a symmetry of a theory's classical action to survive quantisation. The classical equations respect the symmetry, but no regularisation of the full quantum theory can preserve it. A symmetry is anomalous when it leaves the action invariant but not the path integral measure, so the partition function as a whole does not respect it.

The first anomaly discovered was the Adler–Bell–Jackiw (chiral) anomaly: the axial vector current is conserved in classical electrodynamics but is broken once the theory is quantised. The same anomaly reappears in the Atiyah–Singer index theorem, which counts zero modes using the same mathematical structure.

In classical physics, a classical anomaly is a symmetry that is not restored when the parameter breaking it is sent to zero. The earliest known example is the dissipative anomaly in turbulence: time-reversibility stays broken, and the energy dissipation rate stays finite, even as viscosity vanishes.

## Local versus global anomalies

Anomalies split into two kinds, depending on whether perturbation theory can detect them.

Local (perturbative) anomalies appear in Feynman diagrams. In gauge theories they are fixed at one loop, because multi-loop diagrams contain internal boson propagators and admit a Pauli–Villars regularisation that preserves the symmetry; only one-loop chiral diagrams generate gauge anomalies. A local gauge anomaly is fatal: gauge symmetry is required to cancel unphysical negative-norm states, so the theory is inconsistent.

Global (non-perturbative) anomalies cannot be seen in any number of loops. They come from large gauge transformations that approach the identity at infinity and so cannot be fixed by boundary conditions on the path integral. These transformations must be summed over, and their phases form a subgroup of U(1). When the anomaly is present the phases are not all equal, the sum over every non-identity subgroup vanishes, and every path integral is zero: the theory does not exist. A consistent truncation is possible only when the configuration space is disconnected, so that one can integrate over a single connected component and the large transformations never act.

A special case is the scale anomaly, the most common anomaly in practice. Since regulators introduce a distance scale, classically scale-invariant theories are forced by quantum corrections to run under the renormalisation group. The strong nuclear force is the standard example: a theory weakly coupled at short distances flows to strong coupling at long distances.

## The Witten SU(2) anomaly

In SU(2) gauge theory on four-dimensional Minkowski space the full gauge group is connected, but the subgroup of gauge transformations that vanish at infinity is not. Collapsing the 3-sphere at infinity to a point turns Minkowski space into a 4-sphere, and the gauge transformations vanishing at infinity are then the maps from the 4-sphere to the 3-sphere (the group manifold of SU(2)). The connected components of this map space are classified by the fourth homotopy group of the 3-sphere, Z₂, with one identity component and one disconnected component.

When the theory contains an odd number of flavours of spin-1/2 Weyl fermions, the disconnected component acts on every physical state with an extra minus sign relative to the identity component. Contributions to the path integral cancel pairwise, and the theory does not exist. This is the Witten SU(2) anomaly.

In 2018, Wang, Wen, and Witten found a related global SU(2) anomaly when the theory is coupled instead to an odd number of spin-3/2 Weyl fermions; it is detectable only on certain non-spin manifolds. Both anomalies are Z₂ classes with analogues in 4 and 5 dimensions, and their linear combinations classify all global SU(2) anomalies for half-integer spin representations. The new anomaly constrains SO(10) grand unification built on a Spin(10) gauge group with chiral fermions in the 16-dimensional spinor representation, defined on non-spin manifolds.

## Constraints in the Standard Model

Cancelling gauge anomalies is a hard requirement on any chiral gauge theory, and it shaped the Standard Model. The mixed anomaly of two SU(2) generators and one U(1) hypercharge must vanish, which forces the electric charges of all particles in a fermion generation to sum to zero. For one generation,

$$-1 + 3 \times \tfrac{2-1}{3} = 0,$$

so the proton charge plus the electron charge is zero, and quark and lepton charges are commensurate. The same cancellation logic predicted the top quark before its discovery.

't Hooft's anomaly matching condition says that a chiral anomaly computed with high-energy (UV) degrees of freedom must match the result with low-energy (IR) degrees of freedom, so an anomalous symmetry cannot be removed by a UV completion: an anomalous symmetry is not a symmetry, even when it appears to be classically.

## Cobordism classification

In the modern cobordism description, perturbative local anomalies correspond to integer (free) classes, while non-perturbative global anomalies correspond to torsion classes, finite cyclic groups Z_n. The Standard Model is known to be free of perturbative local anomalies, and recent work uses cobordism together with the Atiyah–Patodi–Singer eta invariant to search for and rule out further non-perturbative anomalies in chiral gauge theories.
