# Chemical bond

A chemical bond is the association of atoms or ions that builds molecules, crystals, and extended solids, and so fixes the structure and properties of nearly all matter. Every bond is a balance of electrostatic forces: negatively charged electrons are pulled toward positively charged nuclei, while electron clouds repel each other and like charges repel. The bond settles at a stable **bond distance** where attraction and repulsion cancel. Quantum mechanics describes bonding fully, but chemists still rely on simpler models, the octet rule and VSEPR theory, to predict geometry and polarity quickly.

Bonds divide into **strong (primary) bonds** that hold atoms together inside a molecule or crystal, and **weak (secondary) intermolecular forces** that act between molecules and govern bulk properties such as melting and boiling points. The three primary classes are ionic, covalent, and metallic.

## Ionic bonding

An ionic bond forms when the electronegativity difference between two atoms is large (typically greater than 1.7) and one atom transfers one or more electrons to the other. The resulting ions carry charges usually between −3e and +3e and stick together through isotropic Coulomb attraction. Because the force has no direction, ions in a crystal are each surrounded by oppositely charged neighbors at equal distances, with no specific pairs. Ionic crystals have high melting points but are brittle: shifting one layer brings like charges into contact and the crystal shatters. In salts like sodium cyanide, ionic bonds hold Na⁺ to CN⁻ while a covalent triple bond persists inside the cyanide ion; melting or dissolving the salt breaks only the ionic links, freeing the intact CN⁻ ions.

## Covalent bonding

A covalent bond forms when two atoms share valence electrons. When the electronegativity difference is small (0–0.3), the bond is **non-polar** and the electrons are shared equally. Between about 0.3 and 1.7, the bond is **polar covalent**: the more electronegative atom pulls electron density toward itself and gains a partial negative charge, leaving the other end partially positive. The number of shared electron pairs sets the **bond order**: one pair is a single bond, two pairs a double bond, three a triple bond. Quadruple bonds exist but only between a few transition metals.

Covalent bonds are directional, which is why they produce discrete molecules like CH₄ as well as network solids like diamond and quartz, both with high strength and melting points. A special case is the **coordinate (dative) bond**, in which both electrons of the shared pair come from a single atom called a Lewis base, donated to an empty orbital on a Lewis acid; this is common in transition metal complexes such as Ag(NH₃)₂⁺.

The energy released when a covalent bond forms comes mainly from a drop in electron kinetic energy. Once two nuclei share electrons, the electrons occupy a more spatially extended orbital with a longer de Broglie wavelength, which lowers their kinetic energy more than it raises potential energy.

## Metallic bonding

In a metal, each atom releases its valence electrons into a delocalized "sea" that permeates the entire lattice, leaving behind a regular array of positive metal cations. The cations are held together by their attraction to this mobile electron cloud. The delocalization is an extreme of electron sharing across a vast network, and it explains the classical properties of metals: high electrical and thermal conductivity from the mobile electrons, metallic luster from the cloud's response to light, and ductility and malleability because non-directional bonding lets planes of atoms slide past one another instead of fracturing.

## Intermolecular forces

Weak forces act between molecules and tune physical properties such as boiling point. **Van der Waals forces** is the umbrella name, including Coulombic attraction between partial charges and Pauli repulsion between filled shells at very short range. **Keesom forces** are the dipole-dipole attraction between permanent dipoles of polar molecules. **London dispersion forces** arise from transient dipoles induced in non-polar molecules and are present in all matter. **Hydrogen bonds** form when a hydrogen atom already covalently bound to a strongly electronegative atom (N, O, or F) carries a partial positive charge and is attracted to a lone pair on a neighboring electronegative atom; they explain the unusually high boiling points of water and ammonia. **Halogen bonds** are analogous interactions in which a halogen atom sits between two electronegative atoms.

## Theories of bonding

The pure ionic model treats electrons as fully transferred and uses classical electrostatics, Coulomb's law, to describe the resulting attraction. **Valence bond (VB) theory** describes a bond as a localized pair of electrons in the overlap region of two atomic orbitals, and is extended with **orbital hybridization** to match observed geometries and with **resonance** to handle delocalization across several structures. It is spatially localized and intuitive for reasoning about reaction mechanisms. **Molecular orbital (MO) theory** instead builds delocalized orbitals that span the whole molecule by linearly combining atomic orbitals (LCAO), and assigns each orbital an energy that can be read directly from photoelectron spectra; it is closer to the underlying quantum description. The two frameworks are mathematically equivalent at full accuracy but diverge in their approximations, and chemists choose whichever is more convenient for the problem. **Density functional theory (DFT)** is a third, increasingly popular approach for quantitative calculation. A useful symmetry classification: a **sigma (σ) bond** is symmetric around the internuclear axis, while a **pi (π) bond** has a nodal plane containing that axis.

## A continuous spectrum

Bonds do not sort cleanly into boxes. Ionic, covalent, and metallic bonding merge into each other, and most real bonds are best described as polar covalent, partway between pure sharing and full electron transfer. Choosing a bond type is a way to predict behavior, not a statement that the categories are sharp. The single unifying picture is quantum mechanical: the electron distribution around two or more nuclei lowers the total energy below that of separated atoms, and that energy reduction is what a chemical bond is.
