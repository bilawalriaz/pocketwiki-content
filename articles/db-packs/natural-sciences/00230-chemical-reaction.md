# Chemical reaction

A chemical reaction rearranges atoms into new combinations by breaking some chemical bonds and forming others, without changing the atomic nuclei themselves. Reactants become products, and the process is described by a balanced chemical equation. The two governing questions are whether a reaction can happen, set by thermodynamics, and how fast it happens, set by kinetics.

## What actually changes: bonds and energy

Electrons shift: bonds break, bonds form, atoms end up partnered differently. Because bonds store chemical energy, the rearrangement releases or absorbs energy as ΔH (enthalpy change). Exothermic reactions release heat (ΔH < 0); endothermic reactions absorb it (ΔH > 0). Thermodynamics combines enthalpy with entropy (ΔS, the dispersal of energy and matter) into the Gibbs free energy:

ΔG = ΔH − TΔS

If ΔG < 0 the reaction is exergonic and proceeds spontaneously at constant temperature and pressure. If ΔG > 0 it does not. ΔG tells you nothing about timing.

## Kinetics: why reactions take time

Reactants must collide with enough energy and the right orientation to reach the transition state, the high-energy arrangement of atoms between reactants and products. The minimum energy required is the activation energy, Eₐ. Only collisions exceeding Eₐ can react.

Collision theory says higher concentration and higher temperature raise collision frequency and the fraction energetic enough to react. The Arrhenius equation captures temperature dependence:

k = k₀ exp(−Eₐ / k_BT)

Rate laws express concentration dependence; for a first-order reaction, v = k[A]. A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with lower Eₐ, accelerating the reaction without being consumed. The reaction mechanism, the actual sequence of elementary steps, can be one step or many.

## Equilibrium and how to shift it

Most reactions are reversible. At chemical equilibrium, forward and reverse rates are equal, concentrations stabilise, and ΔG = 0. Le Chatelier's principle predicts responses to disturbance: increasing pressure favours the side with fewer gas molecules; removing product pulls the reaction forward to replace it; heating shifts the equilibrium in the endothermic direction.

## Classifying reactions

Reactions sort into families that share a defining event.

- Synthesis (A + B → AB) and decomposition (AB → A + B) are opposites.
- Single displacement (A + BC → AC + B) and double displacement (AB + CD → AD + CB) swap partners.
- Redox reactions transfer electrons. Oxidation is electron loss, reduction is electron gain. Combustion of hydrocarbons in O₂ is the canonical example, releasing heat and light. Electrolytic cells drive otherwise nonspontaneous redox reactions using electrical energy; batteries run redox in the opposite direction, producing that energy.
- Acid–base reactions are proton transfers (Brønsted–Lowry) or electron-pair donations (Lewis). Neutralisation yields a salt.
- Precipitation forms an insoluble solid once its solubility limit is exceeded.
- Photochemical reactions are initiated by photon absorption, as in photosynthesis and vision.

## Organic mechanisms: how carbon reactions proceed

Organic chemistry concerns covalent bonds of carbon, often with heteroatoms (non-carbon atoms such as O, N, S, halogens). A handful of mechanism types cover most cases.

- Substitution replaces a group. SN1 proceeds stepwise through a carbocation (a positively charged carbon intermediate); SN2 occurs in a single concerted step, with inversion at carbon. Electrophilic substitution dominates aromatic rings; radical substitution runs chain reactions.
- Addition and elimination change how many substituents a carbon carries. Electrophilic, nucleophilic, and radical additions build complexity; E1 and E2 eliminations remove it. Markovnikov's rule predicts which end of an unsymmetrical alkene gets the proton.
- Pericyclic reactions reorganise bonds through cyclic transition states, governed by orbital-symmetry rules (Woodward–Hoffmann). The Diels–Alder [4+2] cycloaddition is the textbook case.

## Reactions in living systems

Metabolism is the network of enzyme-catalysed reactions sustaining life. Enzymes are protein catalysts that bind substrates at an active site, often using induced fit, acid/base catalysis, or proximity effects. Anabolism builds macromolecules such as proteins and carbohydrates, powered by ATP. Catabolism breaks organic molecules down. The ATP comes from oxidising glucose or, in photosynthetic organisms, from capturing sunlight.

## A short history of the model

Phlogiston theory (Becher, 1667) held that combustion released a fire-like substance; Lavoisier disproved it in 1785 by showing combustion is reaction with oxygen. Alchemists had already produced mineral acids by ~1300, and the lead chamber process made sulfuric acid industrial by 1746. Gay-Lussac showed in 1808 that gases react in fixed volume ratios, a step toward balanced equations. Wöhler's 1828 synthesis of urea from inorganic precursors ended vitalism, the belief that organic compounds required a living source. By 1909–1910 the Haber process had made industrial ammonia synthesis feasible, proving catalysis could be central to chemistry at scale.
