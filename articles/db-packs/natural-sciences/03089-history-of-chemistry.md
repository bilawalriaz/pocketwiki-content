# History of chemistry

Chemistry began as craft. Metalworkers, potters, brewers, and dyers transformed matter through technique long before the work had a name. Its history is the record of how those techniques were catalogued by alchemy, then turned into a quantitative science, and finally joined to physics at the atomic scale.

## From craft to classical idea

The earliest chemically significant milestone is the **Bronze Age**, beginning c. 3500 BC with the smelting of copper-tin alloys. It was followed c. 1200 BC by the **Iron Age**, when Hittite smiths worked a harder, more abundant metal. Egyptians had used meteoric iron for weapons and practised gold metallurgy by c. 3000 BC. These were metallurgies, not theory, but they showed that matter could be systematically transformed.

Greek philosophers offered the first explanatory frameworks. **Empedocles** proposed that everything is composed of four elements (earth, fire, air, water), while **Democritus** argued for indivisible particles he called *atomos*. Both schemes were speculative, but they posed the questions chemistry would later answer: what are substances made of, and how do they change?

## Alchemy: a protoscience

Alchemy ran from Greco-Roman Egypt through the Islamic Golden Age and into early modern Europe. It blended mysticism, including the search for the philosopher's stone and attempts to transmute base metals into gold, with laboratory technique. **Jābir ibn Hayyān** classified substances and developed apparatus such as the alembic, a still for distillation. **Paracelsus** introduced the *tria prima*: sulfur, mercury, and salt as the three principles of matter.

Alchemy lacked a shared nomenclature and reproducible methods, which invited fraud and scepticism. It left behind laboratory apparatus, written procedures, and a habit of careful description that later chemists inherited.

## The making of modern chemistry (17th–18th centuries)

**Robert Boyle's** *The Sceptical Chymist* (1661) rejected the classical four elements and insisted on experimental rigour. **Georg Stahl's** phlogiston theory (1702) explained combustion by proposing that burning materials release a substance called phlogiston. The model worked until careful weighing contradicted it.

Two gas discoveries sharpened the crisis: **Joseph Black** isolated carbon dioxide in 1754, and **Henry Cavendish** isolated hydrogen in 1766. **Antoine Lavoisier** then showed that combustion is the combination of a substance with a part of the air (oxygen), disproving phlogiston. His 1789 *Traité Élémentaire de Chimie* established two lasting pillars: the **conservation of mass** (matter is neither created nor destroyed in a reaction) and a chemical nomenclature still used today. **Alessandro Volta's** voltaic pile (1800) opened electrochemistry.

## The 19th century: atoms, order, and synthesis

**John Dalton's** atomic theory (1803) gave chemistry its central object: each element consists of indivisible atoms with characteristic weights, and compounds form in fixed numerical ratios. **Jöns Jacob Berzelius** followed with a systematic set of chemical symbols and accurate atomic weights. **Humphry Davy** used electrolysis, the use of electric current to drive chemical reactions, to isolate alkali metals such as sodium and potassium.

In 1828, **Friedrich Wöhler** synthesised urea from inorganic starting materials, disproving **vitalism**, the belief that organic compounds require a special "vital force," and opening organic chemistry to synthesis. The discipline's organising chart came in 1869, when **Dmitri Mendeleev** published his periodic table, arranging elements by atomic weight and recurring properties. Gaps left for elements not yet discovered were the table's strongest evidence, and several were filled in the 1890s when **William Ramsay** isolated the noble gases. **Marie and Pierre Curie** discovered radioactivity, isolating polonium and radium, and showed that atoms were not, after all, indivisible.

## The 20th century: quantum chemistry and beyond

**Ernest Rutherford's** nuclear model (1911) and **Niels Bohr's** quantised atom (1913) replaced the picture of atoms as solid units with a structured, mostly empty one. Quantum mechanics, formalised in **Schrödinger's** wave equation (1926) and **Heisenberg's** uncertainty principle (1927), explained why atoms have stable structures and how they bond. **Gilbert Lewis's** electron-pair theory (1916) translated this into the picture of covalent bonds as shared electron pairs, and **Linus Pauling** applied quantum principles to molecular geometry.

The century also widened chemistry's practical reach. **Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch's** ammonia synthesis made industrial fertiliser possible, and **Otto Hahn's** discovery of nuclear fission (1938) made nuclear energy possible. Polymer science, the study of large chain molecules such as plastics, and biotechnological methods such as **Kary Mullis's** polymerase chain reaction (PCR, 1983), which amplifies specific DNA sequences through repeated heating and cooling, extended chemistry into materials and biology.
