# Motion

Motion is the continuous change in position or configuration of a physical system relative to a reference frame over time. Because no absolute frame of reference exists, all motion is relative, and everything in the universe, from subatomic particles to galaxies, is in constant motion. The branch of physics that studies motion without reference to its causes is kinematics, while dynamics studies the forces that cause motion.

## Describing motion mathematically

Three vector quantities capture motion. Displacement is the change in position, with both distance and direction. Velocity is the rate of change of displacement over time, that is, speed with a direction. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity over time. An object is "at rest" only relative to a specific frame; another observer, moving differently, will usually see it as moving.

## Classical mechanics

For macroscopic objects moving far slower than light, classical mechanics applies. It rests on Isaac Newton's three laws, published in the *Principia* on 5 July 1687:

1. Inertia: a body remains at rest or in uniform motion unless acted on by an external force.
2. Force: $\vec{F} = m\vec{a}$ for constant mass, or more generally, force equals the rate of change of momentum.
3. Action–reaction: every force exerted by body A on body B is matched by an equal and opposite force from B on A.

These laws unified celestial and terrestrial motion and remain accurate for engineering and orbital calculations within their domain.

## Relativistic mechanics

At velocities approaching the speed of light, classical mechanics fails. In special relativity, velocity is replaced by rapidity, a hyperbolic angle $\varphi$ where $\tanh \varphi = v/c$, and acceleration acts on rapidity through Lorentz transformations, the rules that relate space and time coordinates between moving observers. General relativity extends this by describing gravity as the curvature of spacetime, using differential geometry, the mathematics of curved spaces, developed by W. K. Clifford and Albert Einstein. In this picture, gravity is not a force at all but the geometry of spacetime itself.

## Quantum mechanics

At atomic and subatomic scales, quantum mechanics takes over. It introduces wave–particle duality: matter and radiation behave as both waves and particles. The Heisenberg uncertainty principle forbids knowing a particle's position and velocity exactly at the same time. Quantum effects, though rooted in the very small, also produce macroscopic phenomena such as superfluidity, superconductivity, and aspects of biological function including smell receptors and protein structures.

## The universal speed limit

Light in vacuum travels at exactly 299,792,458 m/s, denoted $c$. This value is the upper limit for energy, matter, information, and causation, and it is invariant: every observer, regardless of their own motion, measures the same $c$. Since the 2019 SI redefinition, the metre is defined by fixing this exact value. Apparent faster-than-light motion seen in relativistic jets from black holes or in light echoes is a geometric projection effect caused by finite light travel time, not a real violation of the limit.

## Motion across all scales

Motion occurs at every scale, often imperceptible because no local reference frame reveals it or because it is simply too small to notice. The cosmos itself expands, stretching all distances, as Edwin Hubble demonstrated in 1929 by showing galaxies receding from Earth (Hubble's law). The Milky Way moves at roughly 600 km/s relative to nearby galaxies, about 582 km/s relative to the cosmic microwave background. The Sun orbits the galactic centre at 210–240 km/s, carrying the planets with it. Earth rotates at 0.465 km/s at the equator and orbits the Sun at about 30 km/s. Continents drift through plate tectonics at rates ranging from roughly 21 mm/year (Eurasian Plate) to 75 mm/year (Cocos Plate). Inside the body, blood flows at roughly 0.33 m/s in major vessels, peristalsis moves food at about 3.48 km/h, and lymph creeps at about 0.0000097 m/s. Within cells, motor proteins powered by ATP hydrolysis, the chemical release of energy from ATP, carry vesicles at about 0.00000152 m/s. All matter above absolute zero undergoes random thermal motion, perceived as temperature. Below the atomic level, electrons occupy probability clouds rather than classical orbits, and protons and neutrons move within the nucleus under competing electrical repulsion and angular momentum.

## Classifying motion

Motion types are distinguished by trajectory and the forces involved. Simple harmonic motion oscillates with a restoring force proportional to negative displacement, as in a pendulum. Linear motion follows a straight line. Circular or rotatory motion traces a path around a fixed point or axis. Curvilinear motion follows a curved path, either planar or in three dimensions. Rolling motion combines rotation and translation, as in a wheel. Brownian motion is the random wandering of microscopic particles. Projectile motion decomposes into uniform horizontal velocity combined with vertically accelerated fall under gravity.

## Open frontiers

The two great frameworks of modern physics remain unreconciled. General relativity describes gravity as curved spacetime, while quantum mechanics describes matter through probabilistic fields; a working theory of quantum gravity does not yet exist. Observed cosmic motions, including galaxy rotation and the expansion of the universe, point to unseen mass and energy that the Standard Model cannot explain, the problems of dark matter and dark energy. Within quantum mechanics itself, the question of how a probabilistic wavefunction, the mathematical description of a particle's range of possible states, yields a single definite measured outcome, known as the measurement problem, has no consensus answer. And although Newton's absolute space has been rejected, philosophers still debate whether spacetime is a real substance or merely a relation between events.
