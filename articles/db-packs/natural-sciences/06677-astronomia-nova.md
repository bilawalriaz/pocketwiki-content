# Astronomia nova

Johannes Kepler's *Astronomia nova* ("New Astronomy"), published in 1609, is a 650-page, 70-chapter account of his ten-year investigation of Mars, based on the naked-eye observations of his late mentor Tycho Brahe, who died in 1601. Its subtitle, *ΑΙΤΙΟΛΟΓΗΤΟΣ*, combines Greek roots for "cause" and "reason," signalling Kepler's aim to find the physical causes of planetary motion, not only to predict positions. He worked through his reasoning step by step, rejecting the Ptolemaic, Copernican, and Tychonic systems, and replaced circular orbits with ellipses and rotating spheres with planets moving freely through space.

## The dead end: the equant and the vicarious hypothesis

Ptolemy explained a planet's non-uniform motion with an *equant*, a point offset from the circle's center such that the planet appears to move at a constant angular rate when viewed from the equant, even though it is closer to the Sun at perihelion and farther at aphelion. The Sun sits on the line joining equant and circle center, the *line of apsides*. Copernicus reproduced nearly the same effect with two combined epicycles. Kepler showed the two constructions are mathematically equivalent, then proposed a physical alternative: the planet's speed varies inversely with its distance from the Sun.

To work from data, Kepler needed a precise geometric model. He used *oppositions*, moments when the Sun and Mars lie 180° apart in ecliptic longitude, when Mars is closest and its observed position most directly reveals its true direction. His first model, the *vicarious hypothesis*, generalized the equant: the circle's center B can sit anywhere on the line of apsides between the Sun A and the equant C, not exactly halfway. Using four of Tycho's twelve Mars oppositions, Kepler iteratively adjusted the longitude of aphelion and the time of aphelion until all four positions lay on a single circle whose center B fell on the line of apsides. He found the eccentricity of the circle (AB/radius) to be 0.11332 and that of the equant (CB/radius) to be 0.07232.

The vicarious hypothesis predicted the remaining eight oppositions to within Tycho's accuracy of 2 arc-minutes, yet independent distance measurements gave Mars an eccentricity of only about 0.09, between the two values, so the model could not be right. Kepler then tested the *bisected eccentricity* rule, placing B exactly halfway between A and C, as Ptolemy had, and the error jumped to 8 arc-minutes, four times the observational noise. "These eight minutes alone will lead us along a path to the reform of the whole of Astronomy," he wrote.

## The Earth's orbit: bisecting the eccentricity

To rescue Mars, Kepler first had to fix the Earth. Using pairs of Mars observations 687 days apart, one full Martian orbital period, after which Mars returns to the same point in space, he showed the distance from Earth to the equant is not constant, ruling out uniform circular motion about any fixed point. The real eccentricity of Earth's orbit, he found, is about 0.018, exactly half of Tycho's 0.03584. The same "bisection" rule already used for Jupiter, Saturn, and the Moon, and implicit in Copernicus's treatment of Venus and Mercury, applies to every body orbiting the Sun.

Kepler proposed a physical cause: a magnetic force from the rotating Sun, weakening with distance, pushes each planet along, while each planet has its own resistance, a notion resembling inertia, and its own magnetic axis that perturbs the path. The same magnetism, he suggested, makes the Moon orbit the Earth, since the Earth also rotates and carries a magnetic field.

## The area law

From the bisected Earth model, Kepler derived the *area law*: a line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times. The mathematical link between time and position is the *Kepler equation*, M = E + e·sin(E), where M is the *mean anomaly* (time since aphelion expressed as an angle), E is the *eccentric anomaly* (angle measured from the circle's center), and e is the eccentricity.

Kepler stated the area law in two forms. The "distance law" of Chapter 32 says the planet's speed is inversely proportional to its distance from the Sun, equivalent to equal areas in equal times only on a circle. The "area law" of Chapter 59 restates it directly for the ellipse. He arrived at the area law before he arrived at the ellipse, and presented only the Mars case in 1609; he extended the elliptical result to the other planets later.

## The ellipse

With the Earth's orbit in hand, Kepler plotted Mars accurately: a line from the Sun in the heliocentric longitude given by the vicarious hypothesis intersected with a line from the Earth in the observed direction, corrected for Mars being slightly out of the ecliptic, the plane of Earth's orbit. The resulting path was flatter at the sides than the best-fit circle, an oval, not a circle.

His first physical attempt produced an egg-shaped orbit, wider at perihelion than at aphelion, wrong by 8 arc-minutes, with the errors on the opposite side of the true position from the bisected-eccentricity circle, so the real path lay halfway between the two. After several false starts, Kepler found that the radius from the Sun to Mars can be written r = 1 − e·cos(E), the polar equation of an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. After briefly re-deriving it and doubting himself, he wrote, "Ah, what a foolish bird I have been!"

## Latitude and inclination

In the final part, Kepler determined Mars's ascending node at 46° 32½′ and descending node at 226° 32½′, exactly 180° apart once measured from the true Sun, with an orbital inclination of 1° 50′. He proposed that the Sun's rotation defines an invariable plane and that each planet's magnetic axis is attracted toward a fixed direction slightly below that plane, tilting the orbit.

## Gravitation, in passing

In the introduction, Kepler sketched a related idea: a mutual attractive force between "kindred bodies," capable of reaching from the Earth to the Moon, which he used to explain the tides. He rejected the Aristotelian notion of "lightness" as a quality, reframing buoyancy in terms of relative density. This was an early qualitative hint of universal gravitation, and scholars have suggested Newton likely knew the passage. Kepler did not, however, formulate a quantitative inverse-square law; his "gravity" was limited in range and acted only between similar matter, more a step toward establishing heliocentrism than toward Newtonian gravity.
