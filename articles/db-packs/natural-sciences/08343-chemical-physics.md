# Chemical physics

Chemical physics is a subdiscipline of physics that studies chemical processes using physical principles. It investigates physicochemical phenomena through techniques drawn from atomic and molecular physics and condensed matter physics.

The United States Department of Education defines the field as combining physical chemistry with atomic and molecular physics, with instruction in heterogeneous structures, surface phenomena, quantum theory, mathematical physics, statistical and classical mechanics, chemical kinetics, and laser physics.

## Distinction from physical chemistry

Chemical physics and physical chemistry both sit at the interface of physics and chemistry, but they emphasize different methods. Chemical physics relies on quantum mechanics, statistical mechanics, and molecular dynamics to explain chemical behavior at the microscopic level, such as how bonds form and break and how energy moves through molecules. Physical chemistry applies a broader set of tools, including thermodynamics, kinetics, and spectroscopy, to connect macroscopic measurements with microscopic chemistry. The boundary is not sharp: many researchers work in both, and journals such as Physical Chemistry Chemical Physics publish across the two areas.

## Origins

The term "chemical physics" in its modern sense was introduced by A. Eucken, who published *A Course in Chemical Physics* in 1930. Earlier, in 1927, V. N. Kondrat'ev, N. N. Semenov, and Iu. B. Khariton had used the title "Electronic Chemistry" to signal a similar scope. The Institute of Chemical Physics of the USSR Academy of Sciences was established in 1931, and *The Journal of Chemical Physics* began publication in the United States in 1933. The Irving Langmuir Award in Chemical Physics, established in 1964, recognizes contributions that use physical principles to understand chemical phenomena.

## What the field studies

Chemical physicists investigate the structure and dynamics of ions, free radicals, polymers, clusters, and molecules. Experiments use spectroscopic methods to study hydrogen bonding, electron transfer, bond formation and dissolution, reactions, and nanoparticle formation. Theoretical work targets how structures and reactions behave at the quantum level, how ions and radicals react in the gas phase, and approximations that make computing these systems tractable.

Representative open questions include whether quantum mechanical predictions of molecular vibrations and rotations can be tested, whether electronic structure can be computed more accurately, and whether reactions can be understood from first principles. Other questions ask why quantum dots blink after absorbing photons, how solvation proceeds for a single molecule or an ensemble, whether negative ions can reveal molecular structure or reaction dynamics, and why soft x-rays can strip enough electrons from a xenon cluster to cause it to explode.
