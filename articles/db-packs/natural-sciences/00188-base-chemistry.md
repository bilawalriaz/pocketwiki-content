# Base (chemistry)

A base is a substance that reacts with an acid. Three modern definitions capture this idea at increasing breadth, and chemists use all three depending on the problem.

## The three definitions

An **Arrhenius base** (1884, Svante Arrhenius) dissociates in water to release hydroxide ions (OH⁻). Familiar examples are metal hydroxides such as NaOH and Ca(OH)₂. Their aqueous solutions feel slippery, taste bitter, and have pH above 7. Arrhenius's view is the everyday one, but it cannot describe what ammonia does, because NH₃ carries no OH⁻ to release.

A **Brønsted–Lowry base** (1923, independently by Brønsted and Lowry) is anything that accepts a proton (H⁺). This includes ammonia and amines, which take a proton from water and so generate OH⁻ indirectly:

NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻

Every Arrhenius base is a Brønsted base, but not the reverse. In non-aqueous solvents the Brønsted view also covers species like the amide ion NH₂⁻ dissolved in liquid ammonia.

A **Lewis base** (1923, G. N. Lewis) is an electron-pair donor. It shares a lone pair with a Lewis acid, an electron-pair acceptor such as H⁺, BF₃, or a high-oxidation-state metal ion (Fe³⁺, Mn⁷⁺, Ag²⁺). The resulting adduct with a metal ion is a coordination complex. Lewis's view is the most general: it explains acid–base behaviour in reactions where no proton moves at all, which neither Arrhenius nor Brønsted can do.

The progression Arrhenius → Brønsted → Lewis is a widening circle, each definition containing the previous one.

## Strength and the leveling effect

A **strong base** is one that is quantitatively protonated by water. No base can act stronger than OH⁻ in water because of the **leveling effect**: water protonates anything more basic than OH⁻ completely, collapsing its effective strength to that of OH⁻ itself. NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂, and tetraalkylammonium hydroxides are strong bases; alkali and alkaline-earth hydroxides are the everyday examples.

Weak bases such as NH₃ only partially accept protons. Their strength is given by the base-dissociation constant:

Kb = [BH⁺][OH⁻] / [B]

For ammonia at 25 °C, Kb ≈ 1.8 × 10⁻⁵, small because ionisation is incomplete. A lower Kb means a weaker base.

## Superbases

Anything stronger than OH⁻ is a **superbase** and cannot exist in aqueous solution; it reacts with water instantly. Examples are butyllithium, lithium diisopropylamide (LDA), sodium amide (NaNH₂), and sodium hydride (NaH). Their conjugate acids are unusually weak: stable hydrocarbons, amines, and H₂. The current record holders for intrinsic basicity are gas-phase diethynylbenzene dianions (ortho stronger than meta stronger than para), surpassing the previous record, LiO⁻. Synthesis and measurement of such species remain a research frontier.

Superbases matter in the laboratory because, under anhydrous conditions, they deprotonate weakly acidic C–H bonds that no aqueous base can reach.

## Alkalinity without OH⁻

Many useful bases contain no hydroxide group. Sodium carbonate makes water basic by accepting a proton through its carbonate ion:

Na₂CO₃ + H₂O → 2 Na⁺ + HCO₃⁻ + OH⁻

Carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, and occasionally fluorine or heavier noble gases can carry the basic site. Without resonance stabilisation of the resulting anion, such compounds tend to be superbases; with resonance stabilisation, as in carboxylates, the anion is a weaker base.

## Neutralisation

In water, a strong base and a strong acid combine through the net reaction:

H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2 H₂O

Equal molar amounts of a strong base such as NaOH and a strong acid such as HCl give a neutral salt solution, sodium chloride here. The reaction is exothermic, so mixing concentrated strong acids and bases is hazardous. Weak bases (baking soda, egg white) are safer for neutralising acid spills.

## Acidity of a base: mono, di, triprotic

The number of ionisable OH⁻ ions per formula unit is the base's **acidity**.

| Class | OH⁻ per unit | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Monoprotic | 1 | NaOH, KOH, NH₄OH |
| Diprotic | 2 | Ca(OH)₂, Mg(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂, Zn(OH)₂ |
| Triprotic | 3 | Al(OH)₃, Fe(OH)₃ |

Low-solubility hydroxides like Al(OH)₃ and Mg(OH)₂ work as antacids: they neutralise stomach acid while releasing OH⁻ only slowly, protecting surrounding tissue.

## Solid bases and catalysis

Insoluble **solid bases** expose basic sites on their surfaces and act as heterogeneous catalysts. Examples include oxide mixtures such as MgO/SiO₂ and CaO/SiO₂, KF on alumina, basic zeolites, and activated charcoal treated at high temperature or with N₂O, NH₃, or ZnCl₂–NH₄Cl–CO₂. Their strength is reported as basic sites per unit surface area, measured by benzoic-acid titration or by adsorption of a gaseous acid such as NO followed by CO₂. They catalyse hydrogenation, double-bond migration, the Meerwein–Ponndorf–Verley reduction, and Michael additions; CaO and BaO become especially active when strongly heated.

## Everyday uses

Sodium hydroxide underpins soap, paper, and rayon. Calcium hydroxide (slaked lime) bleaches and scrubs SO₂ from flue gas. Magnesium hydroxide is an antacid. Sodium carbonate (washing soda) softens water. Sodium bicarbonate (baking soda) leavens, neutralises acid, and powers soda-acid fire extinguishers. Ammonium hydroxide removes grease stains.

## Origin of the term

Louis Lémery first used "base" in 1717 as a synonym for the Paracelsian "matrix," the earthy substrate an acid was thought to impregnate to grow salts. Guillaume-François Rouelle sharpened the meaning in 1754 to the substance that neutralises an acid into a solid salt, the sense chemists still use.
