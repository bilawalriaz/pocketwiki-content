# High Energy and Particle Physics Prize

The High Energy and Particle Physics Prize is a biennial award established in 1989 by the European Physical Society's High Energy and Particle Physics Division (EPS-HEPP). It recognises outstanding contributions to high-energy physics in experimental, theoretical, or technological areas. The prize is presented at the EPS conference on High Energy Physics, alongside the Cocconi Prize, the Gribov Medal, the Young Experimental Physicist Prize, and the Outreach Prize. Nominations are handled by the EPS-HEPP board through an open call.

Until 2003 the prize honoured only European physicists; from that point the nominee pool opened globally. By 2023, seventeen awards had been given to thirty-four individuals and six collaborations, and twelve individual winners have also received the Nobel Prize in Physics.

## The strong interaction

Quantum chromodynamics (QCD) is the quantum field theory that describes the strong force binding quarks into protons, neutrons, and other hadrons. Its central feature is asymptotic freedom: the couplings become weak at large momentum transfers, allowing perturbative calculation, while at low energies the force is so strong that quarks cannot be pulled apart.

The 2003 prize recognised David Gross, David Politzer, and Frank Wilczek for demonstrating asymptotic freedom and establishing QCD as the correct theory of strong interactions. In 2015, James D. Bjorken received the prize for predicting scaling behaviour in proton structure, and Guido Altarelli, Yury L. Dokshitzer, Lev Lipatov, and Giorgio Parisi received it for building a probabilistic field-theory framework that turns QCD's equations into quantitative predictions for hadron collisions.

When electrons and positrons collide, they can briefly convert into a quark and an antiquark that radiate additional quarks and gluons before forming ordinary hadrons. The 1995 prize went to Paul Söding, Bjørn Wiik, Günter Wolf, and Sau Lan Wu for the first evidence of three-jet events at the PETRA collider, three separate sprays of particles showing that a hard gluon had been radiated alongside the quark and antiquark, as QCD predicts.

On the non-perturbative side, Jürg Gasser, Heinrich Leutwyler, and Martin Lüscher received the 2025 prize for new theoretical methods that make the low-energy, confined regime of the strong interaction calculable. The 2021 prize went to Torbjörn Sjöstrand and Bryan Webber for parton-shower Monte Carlo simulations, software that models the cascade of quarks and gluons from a high-energy collision by sampling many random outcomes.

## The electroweak theory and the Higgs mechanism

The electroweak theory unifies the weak force, responsible for radioactive beta decay, with electromagnetism. It predicts that the W and Z bosons, the carriers of the weak force, must be massive, and that their mass is generated by the Higgs field, whose quantum is the Higgs boson.

The 1997 prize went to Robert Brout, François Englert, and Peter Higgs for formulating a self-consistent theory of these charged massive vector bosons, the foundation of the electroweak theory. Martinus Veltman received the 1993 prize for showing that massive Yang-Mills theories, non-abelian gauge theories whose force-carrying particles themselves carry the charge being transmitted, play the central role in weak interactions. Gerard 't Hooft received the 1999 prize for proving that such non-abelian gauge theories can be renormalised, meaning infinities in the calculations can be systematically removed to yield finite predictions, including their non-perturbative aspects.

The 2009 prize honoured the Gargamelle collaboration for the first observation of weak neutral currents, scattering events that the electroweak theory predicted. The 2013 prize went to the ATLAS and CMS collaborations for the discovery of a Higgs boson, and to Michel Della Negra, Peter Jenni, and Tejinder Virdee for their leadership in building those experiments.

## Quarks, neutrinos, and CP violation

CP violation is the subtle asymmetry in which nature treats matter and antimatter differently, and one possible ingredient in explaining why the observable universe contains far more matter than antimatter.

In 2007, Makoto Kobayashi and Toshihide Maskawa received the prize for proposing a mechanism that accommodates CP violation in the Standard Model by requiring at least three families of quarks. Nicola Cabibbo received the 1991 prize for the theory of quark mixing, the idea that one type of quark can transform into another through the weak interaction. In 2011, Sheldon Glashow, John Iliopoulos, and Luciano Maiani received the prize for their contribution to the theory of flavour, embedded in the Standard Model. The 2005 prize honoured Heinrich Wahl and the NA31 Collaboration for the first direct observation of CP violation in decays of neutral K mesons.

In neutrino mixing, one neutrino species oscillates into another during flight, governed by angles such as θ₁₃. In 2001, Don Perkins was honoured for using neutrinos as probes of the quark structure of the proton. In 2023, the Daya Bay and RENO collaborations received the prize for observing short-baseline reactor electron-antineutrino disappearance and measuring θ₁₃, opening the door to searches for CP violation involving leptons. Cecilia Jarlskog shared the 2023 prize for an invariant measure of CP violation that applies in both quark and lepton sectors. The 2019 prize went to the CDF and D0 collaborations for the discovery of the top quark and the detailed measurement of its properties.

## Instrumentation

In 1989, the inaugural prize went to Georges Charpak for multiwire proportional chambers and drift chambers, gaseous detectors that locate the trails left by charged particles. The 2017 prize honoured Erik H. M. Heijne, Robert Klanner, and Gerhard Lutz for silicon microstrip detectors, devices that record particle passages into fine silicon strips and deliver micrometre-scale position measurements needed to find short-lived decay vertices.
