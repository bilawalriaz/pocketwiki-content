# Universe

The universe is everything that exists: all matter, energy, space, and time. Modern cosmology places its origin in a hot, dense state called the Big Bang about 13.8 billion years ago, after which space itself has been expanding. Most of the contents is invisible: roughly 68% is dark energy driving accelerating expansion, 27% is dark matter holding galaxies together, and only about 5% is the ordinary matter that makes stars, planets, and people. Understanding the universe means understanding a cosmos dominated by two components we have never directly detected.

## Origin and early history

The first fraction of a second set the stage. The universe began extremely hot and dense, and a brief burst of cosmic inflation stretched it to enormous size and smoothed its geometry. As it cooled, the fundamental forces separated. Within about one minute, protons and neutrons began fusing into helium, deuterium, and traces of lithium during Big Bang nucleosynthesis, a process that lasted about 15 minutes. After roughly 377,000 years, the universe cooled enough for electrons to settle around nuclei, forming the first stable atoms and releasing a flood of light that still reaches us as the cosmic microwave background (CMB), a near-uniform glow at about 2.7 kelvins. Tiny temperature variations in the CMB are the seeds of every galaxy that now exists.

Around 100 to 300 million years after the Big Bang, the first stars (Population III) ignited in dark matter halos, reheated surrounding hydrogen, and began forging heavier elements. About 9.8 billion years after the Big Bang, dark energy came to dominate over matter, and expansion began to accelerate.

## Composition

| Component | Share of mass–energy | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| Dark energy | ~68% | Unknown energy permeating space; drives acceleration |
| Dark matter | ~27% | Invisible mass inferred from gravity; ~84.5% of all matter |
| Ordinary matter | ~5% | Atoms, stars, gas; under 10% is visible |
| Neutrinos | <0.3% | Nearly massless particles |
| Radiation | ~0.005% | Mostly the CMB |

A cubic meter of space holds the mass–energy of about five protons, though it is distributed with extreme unevenness. The universe also holds about ten billion times more matter than antimatter, a fact called baryon asymmetry whose cause is unknown.

Dark energy is thought to behave like a constant energy of empty space (a cosmological constant), about 7×10⁻³⁰ g/cm³, far thinner than matter inside a galaxy, yet dominant on cosmic scales because it does not thin out as space expands. Dark matter has never been observed directly; its existence is inferred from galactic rotation and the bending of light. Ordinary matter is built from quarks (protons have two up and one down; neutrons two down and one up) and leptons such as electrons, organized by the Standard Model of particle physics.

## Expansion and geometry

Expansion is not objects flying apart through space; space itself stretches. Evidence comes from the redshift of distant galaxies and from Type Ia supernovae, which act as standard candles. Before 1998, cosmologists expected gravity to slow expansion. Two teams instead measured the deceleration parameter at about −0.55, showing expansion has accelerated for the last 5 to 6 billion years. This made dark energy central to cosmology.

The observable universe is about 93 billion light-years in diameter, even though it is only 13.8 billion years old, because the edge and Earth have been moving apart the whole time. Whether the whole universe is finite or infinite is unknown, but measurements from the COBE, WMAP, and Planck satellites show space is very close to flat, a geometry consistent with infinite extent.

A shorthand called Omega (Ω) captures this: Ω = 1 means flat, less than 1 means open (saddle-shaped), greater than 1 means closed (spherical). A sufficiently dense, positively curved universe would end in a Big Crunch; a flat or open one expands forever, cooling toward a Big Freeze and heat death. A Big Rip, in which expansion tears apart galaxies, stars, and atoms, is one possible variant of accelerated expansion.

## The modern framework

General relativity, published by Einstein in 1915, describes gravity as the curvature of spacetime produced by energy. Applied to the whole cosmos under the cosmological principle, the assumption that the universe is homogeneous and isotropic on large scales, it yields the Friedmann equations, which describe how the scale factor changes over time. Their solutions, called FLRW models, are the mathematical backbone of modern cosmology.

Special relativity, published a decade earlier, established that observers in relative motion disagree on distances and time intervals but agree on the speed of light and on the spacetime interval (c²T² − D²). Together, the two theories treat events as points in four-dimensional spacetime: three spatial dimensions plus time.

## A brief intellectual history

Early written astronomy appeared in Egypt and Mesopotamia between roughly 3000 and 1200 BCE. Greek thinkers produced the first systematic geocentric models, notably Eudoxus with 27 celestial spheres. Aristarchus of Samos proposed a Sun-centered cosmos in the 3rd century BCE, but the geocentric view, defended by Aristotle, dominated for two millennia until Copernicus revived heliocentrism in the 16th century. Newton then showed that the same laws govern falling apples and orbiting planets.

Kant speculated that faint nebulae were separate galaxies; Hubble confirmed this in 1922–1923 using Cepheid variable stars in Andromeda and Triangulum, proving the Milky Way is one of many. Einstein's 1915 theory gave cosmology a physical foundation, and the 1998 supernova measurements transformed it again by revealing dark energy.

## Open questions

The nature of dark energy and dark matter is unknown. The cause of the universe's preference for matter over antimatter is unsolved. Whether anything preceded the Big Bang, and whether ours is one of many disconnected universes in a multiverse, are unsettled. The fine-tuning of physical constants for life is debated, and even its meaning is contested. Whether the total universe is finite or infinite has no answer.

The observable universe is built almost entirely from components we cannot see, shaped by a beginning we can only reconstruct from the faint glow of light released 13.8 billion years ago.
