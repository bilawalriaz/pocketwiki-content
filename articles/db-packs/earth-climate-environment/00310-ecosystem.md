# Ecosystem

An ecosystem is a dynamic system of interacting organisms and their physical environment, linked by nutrient cycles and energy flows. It is the basic unit ecologists use to study how life is organised and sustained on Earth, and the focus is on the two-way exchanges between living things and their non-living surroundings.

## Origin of the concept

The term was coined in 1935 by Arthur Roy Clapham at the request of ecologist Arthur Tansley, and first appeared in Tansley's publication that year. Tansley wanted a word that put transfers of materials between organisms and their environment at the centre, rather than a community of organisms alone. He treated ecosystems as "mental isolates": boundaries a researcher draws around a system for analysis, not natural fences. Later ecologists, notably G. Evelyn Hutchinson and the Odum brothers, extended the idea by treating ecosystems as measurable systems with quantifiable inputs, storages, and outputs of energy and matter. In 1942 Raymond Lindeman argued that energy flow is a primary driver of ecosystem behaviour. In 1963 the Hubbard Brook Ecosystem Study began, treating an entire watershed as one ecosystem; in 1972 its researchers identified acid rain in North America. The 2005 Millennium Ecosystem Assessment synthesised the state of Earth's ecosystems.

## What controls an ecosystem

Two kinds of factors govern behaviour. External factors, such as climate and parent material (the underlying geology that forms soils), set the overall structure and resource budget, and the ecosystem does not change them. Internal factors, such as which species are present, how fast decomposition runs, and the disturbance regime, determine how those inputs are used, and are themselves shaped by the ecosystem's own processes.

Because ecosystems are constantly perturbed, ecologists separate two recovery properties. Resistance is the tendency to stay near an equilibrium state when disturbed. Ecological resilience is the capacity to absorb disturbance and reorganise while still keeping the system's essential function, structure, and identity. An ecosystem that loses its defining features after a shock has undergone ecosystem collapse.

## Energy flow versus nutrient cycling

Ecosystems run on two parallel currents that behave very differently.

Energy enters almost entirely through primary production, the creation of organic matter by photosynthesis. Gross primary production (GPP) is the total energy captured. After plants use some for their own respiration, the remainder is net primary production (NPP), the energy budget available to every other living thing in the system. Energy then moves through trophic levels (feeding positions such as primary producer, herbivore, carnivore) along food chains embedded in more tangled food webs. At each step most energy is lost as heat, so ecosystems need a continuous input of solar energy and do not recycle energy the way they recycle matter.

Mineral nutrients (carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and others) behave the opposite way: they are mostly cycled within the ecosystem rather than lost. Decomposition breaks down dead organic matter and releases nutrients back into reusable form, also returning carbon to the atmosphere. Some cycles limit life more than others. Nitrogen is frequently the limiting nutrient, and phosphorus becomes increasingly limiting as landscapes age and weather.

## Biodiversity and function

Function depends on the traits of the species present and how abundant each one is, not just on how many species there are in total. Ecologically distinct species contribute more to function than redundant ones do. Two roles capture disproportionate effects. A keystone species has an effect far larger than its abundance would suggest, so removing it reshapes the system. An ecosystem engineer physically creates or modifies habitat; beavers flooding valleys into wetlands are the standard example.

## Humans in the system

Humans draw two kinds of value from ecosystems. Ecosystem goods are tangible products such as food, water, and fuel. Ecosystem services are the wider benefits: air purification, water purification, pollination, climate regulation. Pollution, habitat fragmentation, and climate change degrade these flows, and in extreme cases drive ecosystem collapse. Ecosystem management applies ecological principles to use resources sustainably; ecological restoration tries to recover systems that have already been degraded.

## Open questions

The boundary between a biome (a general ecosystem category such as "temperate forest") and a specific named ecosystem is not sharp. It is not settled how much function depends on biodiversity as a whole versus on the presence of particular functional species. Assigning an economic value to ecosystem services is hard for the same reason, and the attempt remains an active, contested part of environmental policy.
