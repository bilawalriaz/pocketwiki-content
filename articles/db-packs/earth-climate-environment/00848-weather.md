# Weather

Weather is the state of a planet's atmosphere at a specific place and time, described by temperature, humidity, cloud cover, precipitation, and wind. On Earth, it is the short-term expression of a global heat engine: uneven solar heating creates pressure and moisture differences, and the atmosphere constantly works to even them out.

## How weather is generated

The Sun heats the equator far more than the poles, because the tropics receive a more direct solar angle. This temperature contrast, combined with the moisture content of air, produces pressure differences. Air flows from high to low pressure as wind, and Earth's rotation deflects that flow through the Coriolis effect, curving it right in the Northern Hemisphere and left in the Southern.

Global circulation organises into three cells in each hemisphere. The Hadley cell lifts warm air near the equator, carries it poleward aloft, and sinks it in the subtropics. The Ferrel cell operates at mid-latitudes, and the polar cell covers the high latitudes. Where they meet, sharp temperature contrasts form the jet stream, a fast, narrow current of upper-level air. Instabilities in that flow, a property called baroclinity, spawn the extratropical cyclones that dominate mid-latitude weather. Tropical systems such as monsoons form differently, driven by intense surface heating rather than jet-stream instability.

Earth's axial tilt produces the seasons, while slow orbital shifts, the Milankovitch cycles, redistribute solar energy over tens of thousands of years and shape the long-term climate that weather plays out within.

## The atmospheric layer that matters

Almost all familiar weather happens in the troposphere, the lowest atmospheric layer. Air is warmed from below by the ground, so temperature generally falls with height at the adiabatic lapse rate. Sometimes the pattern inverts: a temperature inversion can make mountaintops warmer than valleys while trapping pollutants and suppressing thunderstorms. Local surfaces also matter. Oceans, forests, ice sheets, and cities differ in reflectivity, roughness, and moisture, so they heat and cool at different rates and shape the weather above them.

## Weather as a shaper

Weather is a major force sculpting Earth's surface. Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide to become slightly acidic, which accelerates the chemical breakdown of rock. Wind and water carry the resulting sediments away, and over geological time those materials are redeposited and lithified into new rock. Acid rain, the same chemistry amplified by industrial sulphur and nitrogen emissions, is the most visible modern case.

## Weather and human history

Extreme weather has repeatedly redirected human affairs. The 1281 Kamikaze winds reportedly wrecked a Mongol fleet bound for Japan. A 1565 hurricane destroyed a French fleet off Florida and ended French claims there. The Little Ice Age and a related cold episode, the Grindelwald Fluctuation, brought crop failures across Europe, including the 1696–1697 Finnish famine that killed roughly a third of the population and the 1690s famine in France, the country's worst since the Middle Ages. Hurricane Katrina displaced over a million people in 2005. Today, agriculture plans planting and harvesting around temperature and precipitation outlooks, utilities balance energy demand against expected heat or cold, and individuals plan their days from a forecast.

## Forecasting and its limits

The atmosphere is a chaotic system: small errors in the initial state grow into large errors in the predicted state, placing a theoretical ceiling of about two weeks on useful deterministic forecasts. Modern forecasting combines dense observations with numerical models, run as ensembles of slightly perturbed simulations whose spread indicates uncertainty. Human forecasters still choose which models to trust and how to communicate the result. Tropical forecasting is harder in some ways: weaker Coriolis influence near the equator makes convection and rain more spontaneous and harder to pin down, even though temperatures vary less.

## The record extremes

Two surface temperatures anchor the global record. A reading of 57.7 °C at ʽAziziya, Libya in 1922 long stood as the highest, but the World Meteorological Organization has since declared it illegitimate. The coldest confirmed surface temperature is −89.2 °C, recorded at Vostok Station in Antarctica on 21 July 1983.

## Changing the weather

Humans have long tried to steer the weather. Cloud seeding has been used to disperse fog, boost winter precipitation, suppress hail, and, in China's 2008 Beijing Olympics campaign, keep opening ceremonies dry. Military programs such as Operation Popeye tried to extend monsoon rain over Vietnam. Decades of study have produced no conclusive evidence that intentional modification works as intended. Unintentional modification is well documented: sulphur and nitrogen emissions cause acid rain, cities create urban heat islands, and greenhouse gases shift the climate, raising the frequency of heatwaves, heavy rainfall, and other extremes.

## Weather beyond Earth

The same physics operates elsewhere, but results depend on each world's atmosphere. Jupiter's Great Red Spot is an anticyclonic storm, a high-pressure system with descending air at its centre, that has persisted for at least 300 years. Neptune, which receives about 1/900 as much solar energy as Earth, hosts winds gusting to roughly 600 metres per second, a contrast that still puzzles planetary scientists. On Titan, the Cassini–Huygens mission found clouds of methane and ethane that rain liquid methane and other organic compounds onto the surface. Above the planets, space weather, driven by the solar wind and coronal mass ejections, generates aurorae and can disrupt satellites and power grids.
