# Climate commitment

The atmosphere has already been altered by long-lived greenhouse gases, and the climate system has not yet finished responding. Climate commitment is the unavoidable future climate change that results from this lag, including the warming locked in by past emissions, even if atmospheric composition were held steady.

## The basic idea

Earth's climate reacts to a climate forcing (an imposed change such as higher greenhouse gas levels or stronger sunlight) with a delay. The principal reason is the ocean's enormous heat capacity and thermal inertia, the tendency of a body with much mass to take a long time to change temperature.

Consider heating a thin metal plate: it warms quickly. Heating a thick metal block of the same surface area takes far longer to reach temperature equilibrium with the same heat source, because the block has more mass to warm. The ocean behaves like the thick block. Land only stores heat in the top few metres. Ocean water moves vertically through convection and stores heat throughout the depth. That is why the land surface warms more than the ocean in observations, and why a small surface warming commits the planet to centuries of further warming as heat slowly penetrates the deep ocean.

The same distinction runs through climate modelling. In a "transient" simulation, the planet's incoming and outgoing energy flows are substantially out of balance, and a shallow ocean representation may suffice. In an "equilibrium" simulation, those flows approach a new balance, and a full-depth ocean model is needed.

The commitment applies to more than temperature. Heat reaching the deep ocean on long mixing times commits the planet to centuries of sea level rise from thermal expansion of seawater. Once a threshold is crossed, it also commits to very slow melting of the Greenland ice sheet, on the order of 5 m of sea level rise over millennia.

## Definition and types

Climate commitment is the unavoidable future climate change that follows from inertia in the geophysical and socio-economic systems. The literature distinguishes three common cases. The constant composition commitment fixes atmospheric composition and asks how the climate continues to adjust. The constant emissions commitment holds emissions steady rather than concentrations. The zero emissions commitment asks what further change is still in the pipeline if emissions are set to zero. A standard illustrative constant is CO₂ doubling or quadrupling relative to the pre-industrial level; another is the present forcing.

## What models project

Recent model studies estimate that, even if greenhouse gases were stabilised at present levels, the Earth would warm by an additional 0.5 °C by 2100, a rise comparable to the entire warming of the twentieth century. By 2050, as much as 64% of that committed warming would still be attributable to past natural forcings, although that share diminishes over time as the human signal dominates. At 2005 greenhouse gas levels, the total committed warming could exceed 1 °C. Thermal expansion of the ocean would add roughly 10 cm of sea level rise over the same period. These figures omit ice caps and glaciers; including those feedback effects pushes the estimated temperature increase to 1–1.5 °C.

## Misuse of the concept

Climate commitment studies are not predictions of fate. They cover a range of emissions scenarios tied to past, present and future human choices. The concept is misused when worst cases are presented as inevitable regardless of social agency. Models indicate instead that additional surface warming can be halted almost simultaneously with rapid emissions reductions. The concept has appeared in successive IPCC assessment reports, including the Second and Third, and remains central to interpreting any stabilisation pathway.
