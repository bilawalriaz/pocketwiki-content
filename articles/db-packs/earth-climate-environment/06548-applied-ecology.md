# Applied ecology

Applied ecology is the branch of ecology that takes the science of ecology and uses it to solve real-world management problems, usually involving land, water, wildlife, or other natural resources. The core idea is that ecological knowledge, theories, and models become useful only when they are deliberately turned toward a human decision: which crop to plant, where to build a road, how to restore a damaged wetland, how to control an invasive species. Because those decisions involve trade-offs between people and nature, applied ecology integrates ecological, social, and biotechnological aspects of natural resource conservation and management.

A useful split is between outputs and inputs. Outputs are the goods and services a landscape provides, such as timber, fish, clean water, pollination, and other ecosystem services, together with the management of those resources. Inputs are the pressures and policies that act on the system, including pollution, land use, conservation policy, and direct human influence on biodiversity. Most applied projects address both sides at once, because protecting an output usually means controlling an input.

The discipline is broad by design. It covers agriculture, forestry, and fisheries, and also reaches into global change, urban development, and pollution. It draws on subjects that pure ecology does not usually depend on, including conservation biology, restoration ecology, ecotoxicology (the study of how toxic chemicals affect ecosystems and organisms), biomonitoring (tracking ecosystem health by measuring living organisms or their responses), biodiversity science, environmental policy, and economics. Restoration ecology is especially central, because rebuilding a damaged system is a common, concrete test of whether ecological theory holds up in practice.

The tools are the same ones ecologists use anywhere, but they are chosen to answer a decision. Three approaches dominate: observation of natural systems, controlled experimentation to identify causal relationships, and mathematical or computer models that extend findings beyond what can be measured directly. Simple statistical and analytical models, such as spatial models and matrix models that track how populations or habitats change in discrete stages, are common. Larger digital simulations forecast the consequences of a specific action and weigh biological outcomes against economic ones, a combination called bioeconomic analysis.

A wildlife preservation project shows how the three approaches fit together. Field observation describes what species are present and how they use the habitat. Experiments test what limits them, for example whether a predator, a disease, or a food shortage is the binding constraint. Models then project how the population will respond to a particular intervention, such as reconnecting a fragmented forest, controlling an invasive plant like yellow star thistle in Yosemite National Park, or changing a hunting rule. The result is a set of plausible management options that can be compared rather than a single prescription.

Applied ecology is therefore not just a technical exercise. It also requires judgments of relative value and goal, because ecology can predict the likely outcome of an action, but it cannot say which outcome a society should prefer. Managing an ecosystem well depends on ecological knowledge, but the decision to act, and the choice of which outcome to aim for, sits outside the science itself.

The practical reach is wide. Common application areas include agro-ecosystem management, biodiversity conservation, biotechnology, conservation biology, disturbance management, ecosystem restoration, environmental engineering, environmental technology, habitat management, invasive species management, landscape and development planning, protected-area management, rangeland management, restoration ecology, and wildlife management including game species. The work is intersectoral and interdisciplinary, which is why applied ecology is sometimes folded into national economic planning so that environmental effects are considered alongside other goals, especially as fluctuations in global food production feed through into prices and availability to consumers.

The main outlets for new findings are the Journal of Applied Ecology, Ecological Applications, and Applied Ecology and Environmental Research. The largest professional home is the Ecological Society of America, with the Society for Ecological Restoration, the Institute for Applied Ecology, the Kazakh Agency of Applied Ecology, and Germany's Öko-Institut among the bodies that coordinate work and set standards.
