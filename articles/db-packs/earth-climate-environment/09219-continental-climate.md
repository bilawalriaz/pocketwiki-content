# Continental climate

A continental climate is the weather pattern that develops over the interior of large continents in the middle latitudes, far enough from the ocean that land, not water, controls the seasonal temperature swing. The defining signature is a large gap between summer and winter: at least one month averages below 0 °C (32 °F) and at least one averages above 10 °C (50 °F). Because land heats and cools faster than water, these regions run hot in summer and cold in winter, while oceans on the same latitude stay milder year-round.

Continentality is the measure of this oceanic isolation. It is highest deep inside a continent, where prevailing winds travel over land rather than water, and lowest along coasts. Continental climates cluster in the central and eastern parts of the three northern continents (North America, Europe, and Asia), in a band roughly 40° to 55–60° N, and are largely absent from the Southern Hemisphere, which has too little land at those latitudes to produce the same effect.

In the Köppen climate classification, the umbrella letter for these climates is **D**. The D group is split by three criteria: how warm the summer gets, how cold the coldest month is, and how precipitation is distributed through the year.

**Subtypes by summer warmth and winter cold**

- **Dfa, Dfb, Dfc, Dfd** have no marked dry or wet season. Dfa is hot-summer humid continental, with at least one month above 22 °C (71.6 °F) and at least four months above 10 °C. Dfb is warm-summer, with all months below 22 °C but still four or more above 10 °C. Dfc is subarctic, with only one to three months above 10 °C. Dfd is extremely cold subarctic, with the coldest month averaging below −38 °C (−36.4 °F), and is found only in the deepest parts of Siberia.
- **Dwa, Dwb, Dwc, Dwd** repeat the same four temperature classes but are monsoon-influenced, because the wettest summer month receives at least ten times as much rain as the driest winter month. These cover much of eastern Asia, including northern and northeastern China, Korea, and parts of Mongolia, where summer rains from the East Asian monsoon dominate.
- **Dsa, Dsb, Dsc, Dsd** are Mediterranean-influenced, with the rainfall pattern reversed: the wettest winter month gets at least three times the precipitation of the driest summer month, and that driest summer month falls below 30 mm. These appear in Turkey, the Caucasus, Iran, and adjacent parts of Central Asia, where winter storms from the Mediterranean and Caspian reach inland before the summers dry out.

Many modern maps use a relaxed cold-month threshold of −3 °C (27 °F) instead of 0 °C, which shifts borderline oceanic and subtropical stations into or out of D. The 1991–2020 maps in the source use the −3 °C threshold. The Trewartha climate classification recognises the same climates as **Dc**.

**Seasons and precipitation**

Annual precipitation usually falls between 600 mm (24 in) and 1,200 mm (47 in). Most of it comes in the warmer months, except in the mountains of the Pacific Northwest of North America and in the Persian Plateau region (western Iran, northern Iraq, adjacent Turkey, Afghanistan, northern Pakistan, and Central Asia), where winters are wetter. A meaningful share of the yearly total arrives as snow, and snow cover lasting more than a month is typical. Summers bring warm to hot conditions and frequent thunderstorms, but summer weather is generally more stable than winter weather, when cold-air outbreaks sweep in as days shorten.

The timing of spring and autumn depends on latitude and elevation. In the Northern Hemisphere, spring can arrive in March in the south of the D zone or as late as May in the north. In the small Southern Hemisphere patches, the window runs from September in the north to November in the south.

**Why the swing happens**

Continental climates exist where cold air masses build in winter under short days and weak sunlight, then warm air masses form in summer under high sun and long days, and where the landscape is far enough from the ocean that no maritime air mass regularly tempers the extremes. The same latitude on a coast stays milder in winter and cooler in summer, which is why summer temperatures characteristic of the tropics can appear over Manitoba or Ukraine while western Europe at the same latitude stays cool.

**Neighbouring climates**

Köppen places D climates between milder and drier neighbours. Equator-ward, they grade into temperate climates where winters are less severe. Pole-ward and into dry interiors, they give way to semi-arid and arid climates where rainfall can no longer support tall-grass prairie and shrubland. In Europe, the western edge of the D zone transitions into oceanic (Cfb) or subpolar oceanic (Cfc) climates as the Atlantic influence grows. In central and eastern Asia and the central United States, the southern boundary shifts into humid subtropical (Cfa or Cwa), subtropical highland (Cwb), or Mediterranean (Csa or Csb) climates.

Because continentality is a continuous property, the boundaries between D and its neighbours are fuzzy on any real map, and small changes in the cold-month threshold can move a station from one category to the next.
