# Climate change in Europe

Europe is the fastest-warming continent. From pre-industrial times, European land temperatures have risen about 1.94–1.99 °C, compared to a global average of 1.11–1.14 °C, and the WMO reported that over the preceding 30 years Europe warmed at more than twice the global rate. Anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions are the cause, and without rapid cuts, dangerous warming could arrive before 2050.

## Greenhouse gas emissions

The EU-27 produces about 6.4% of global emissions. In 2019, EU emissions reached 3.3 Gt, with 80% from fossil fuels. Between 1990 and 2014, road transport (cars, vans, trucks) added 124 Mt of CO₂ and aviation added 93 Mt, an 82% rise, the largest absolute growth of any sector. Coal use has collapsed: European consumption fell from 26,060 PJ in 1985 to 9,400 PJ in 2020, and CO₂ from coal dropped from 3.31 Gt in 1987 to 1.36 Gt in 2019. Renewables overtook fossil fuels as the EU's main electricity source by 2020, and solar and wind together exceeded fossil fuels in 2025.

Agriculture causes about 10% of Europe's emissions and is the largest source of non-CO₂ greenhouse gases, mainly methane and nitrous oxide. Methane traps roughly 20 times more heat than CO₂ and nitrous oxide about 300 times. Road transport still emits around a fifth of EU greenhouse gases, and shipping emissions equal the carbon footprint of a quarter of passenger cars. Between the 1980s and 2010s, falling aerosol pollution, sometimes called "global dimming," reduced a previous cooling mask and increased net heating over Europe.

## Effects on the natural environment

Western European heatwaves have increased three to four times faster than in the rest of the northern midlatitudes over the past 42 years. Arctic sea ice has shrunk by about 33,000 km² per year in winter and 79,000 km² per year in summer between 1979 and 2020, and Baltic sea ice reached a record low in winter 2019–2020. A 2026 heatwave pushed temperatures above 40 °C across southern and central Europe, and the resulting drought was made about 80 times more likely in western Europe and 40 times more likely in eastern Europe by climate change. The 2003 heatwave, probably the hottest summer in at least 500 years, killed around 70,000 people. The 2010 Russian heat cut grain harvests by 25%, banned wheat exports, and cost about 1% of Russian GDP with roughly 55,000 deaths. From 2012 to 2022, extreme weather cost Europe more than €145 billion, with losses growing about 2% each year. Heat mortality rose by 17 deaths per 100,000 people between 2003–12 and 2013–22, with women more vulnerable than men. Warming has also allowed mosquito-borne diseases like Zika and dengue to appear in Europe.

## Mitigation and policy

In 2021 the European Parliament passed a climate law committing the EU to carbon neutrality by 2050, with an interim target of at least 55% net emission cuts by 2030 against 1990. A provisional 2025 deal added a 90% reduction target for 2040. In 2023 EU emissions fell 8.3%, the largest drop since the pandemic, leaving them 37% below 1990 while GDP grew 68%. The EU Emissions Trading System, launched in 2005 as the world's first large carbon market, sets a cap on emissions and lets companies trade allowances, and now covers power, heavy industry, and intra-European aviation. The European Green Deal bundles these efforts, with sectoral rules: a ban on new petrol and diesel car sales from 2035, renewable-energy and energy-efficiency targets, an EU carbon border adjustment to prevent leakage, and biodiversity measures such as planting 3 billion trees and restoring 25,000 km of rivers. In 2024 the European Court of Human Rights ruled in *Verein KlimaSeniorinnen Schweiz v. Switzerland* that inadequate climate action violates the European Convention on Human Rights, the first such international ruling.

## Society and culture

Around 90% of Europeans believe their children will experience climate change in daily life, and only 9% of Europeans doubt it is happening, compared with 18% in the United States. Greta Thunberg's school strikes, beginning in Sweden in 2018, and Extinction Rebellion, founded in the UK in 2018, both spread across the continent. National targets vary in ambition: Denmark has legally committed to a 70% emissions cut by 2030, Finland to carbon neutrality by 2035, and the Netherlands to a 49% cut by 2030, while Germany remains Europe's largest coal importer and only phased out nuclear power in 2023.
