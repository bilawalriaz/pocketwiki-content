# Forest ecology

Forest ecology studies how the living parts of a woodland—plants, animals, fungi, and microorganisms—interact with the non-living environment of soil, water, light, and climate within a single forest unit. Trees dominate forests because of their size and longevity, which produces layered canopies, large biomass stores, and extensive root networks not found in other terrestrial ecosystems. Research therefore focuses on populations, communities, and whole ecosystems rather than on individual organisms.

## Global importance

Forests cover roughly a third of Earth's land, host about 80% of terrestrial species, and hold around 606 gigatonnes of living biomass plus 59 gigatonnes of dead wood. They generate about 28% of atmospheric oxygen (most of the rest comes from ocean plankton), store large amounts of carbon, and recycle moisture inland through a biotic pump that draws ocean evaporation over continents. Loss of forest cover increases erosion and sediment delivery to waterways.

## Threats from human activity

Deforestation breaks continuous habitat into patches too small for species needing large territories, lowering biodiversity and disrupting functions such as pollination and nutrient cycling. Mining adds soil erosion, acidification, and heavy-metal contamination that reduces nectar quality and harms pollinators; liming and phytoremediation can partly restore soil microbial biomass. Urbanization cuts forests into fragments, while agriculture replaces them with monocultures and adds fertilizers and pesticides that alter nutrient cycles.

## Forest structure and biodiversity

Tree height creates vertical layering. Canopies produce steep gradients in light, temperature, humidity, and wind over short distances, and roots create equally varied conditions underground. This heterogeneity supports more species than simpler plant communities. Mixed-age stands with uneven canopy heights hold more wildlife than even-aged plantations; wild turkeys, for example, decline sharply under even-aged management. Silvicultural techniques that mimic natural disturbances, such as variable retention forestry, let beetle and plant communities recover quickly.

## Major forest types

Boreal forests (taiga) circle the high northern latitudes and are dominated by conifers such as fir, pine, and spruce. Wildfire is the strongest control on which species dominate, with composition shifting as time since the last burn increases. Birch, aspen, alder, and willow appear mostly at the southern edge or as successional species after fire.

Temperate forests make up about 25% of global forest cover and face periodic drought. Species cope using stomata that close rapidly in dry air and by adjusting leaf budding to yearly weather rather than to long-term averages. Temperate rainforests along the west coasts of the Americas and Australasia stay cool and wet year-round; in the northern hemisphere they are mostly coniferous and rich in lichens.

Tropical forests are the most species-rich terrestrial ecosystems. Competition for light drives extreme tree height, buttressed and stilt roots for support in shallow wet soil, and drip-tip leaves that shed water quickly to discourage algae. Tall canopies shade the forest floor, so epiphytes and vines grow on trunks and branches to reach light. Coniferous forests cope with cold, drought, wind, and snow through wax-coated resinous needles that decompose slowly into acidic litter, tapered crowns that shed snow, and evergreen habits that exploit short growing seasons. Diversity is lower than in tropical or temperate broadleaf forests, dominated by mammals such as moose, elk, bears, wolves, and by migratory birds.

## Ecological interactions

Trees and shrubs act as nurse plants, sheltering seedlings from temperature extremes and drought. Below ground, mycorrhizal fungi form networks whose fine hyphae extend past root tips and trade phosphorus and water for sugars; some conifers cannot survive without these partners. Benefits shrink when soils are already nutrient-rich, because the sugar cost outweighs the gain, and plants lacking mycorrhizal partners can be harmed by their presence.

Competition runs alongside cooperation. Intraspecific competition occurs within a species and interspecific competition between species. Invasive insects such as the hemlock woolly adelgid and emerald ash borer can dominate disturbed resources because their low intraspecific pressure lets them spread quickly.

Ecologists also score a species' ecological potential, its capacity to compete in a given region, using three parameter groups: site tolerance (cold, drought, frugality), specific qualities (shade tolerance, height growth, longevity, regeneration), and specific risks (late frost, wind, fire, pests), each rated 0–5. In central Europe, European beech scores 3.82 (high), ash 3.08 (intermediate), and walnut 2.92 (low).

## Matter and energy flows

Forests store large amounts of potential energy in biomass. Wildfires and treefalls release it suddenly and reset forest structure, while slower growth-driven changes alter local environment over decades. Trees regulate water at landscape scale: about 399 million hectares are now designated mainly for soil and water protection, up 119 million hectares since 1990. Leaf litter acts as a sponge whose removal by grazing or compaction causes erosion and dry-season water loss.

After a major disturbance, forests pass through four stages: a rapid establishment phase of seedlings, a thinning phase once the canopy closes and shaded seedlings die, a transition phase when the death of a canopy tree opens a light pocket for new growth, and a steady-state phase with trees of many ages and sizes. Coarse woody debris decays slowly because of lignin and cold or dry conditions, shaping wildlife habitat, fire behaviour, and regeneration for decades.
