# Climate change in Brazil

Brazil is both a major victim and a major driver of climate change. In 2023 its emissions reached about 2.38 billion tonnes of CO₂ equivalent, over 4% of the global total and the sixth-highest of any country. Per person, Brazilians emit around 11 tonnes, more than double the global average of about 5. Cumulatively since 1850, only the United States, China, and Russia have released more greenhouse gases.

## Where the emissions come from

In 2016, official sectoral shares were agriculture 33.2%, energy 28.9%, land-use change and forestry 27.1%, industry 6.4%, and waste 4.5%. Two mechanisms dominate the top three:

- **Deforestation.** Trees are cut mainly to clear land for cattle pasture and soy. The Amazon, once a major **carbon sink** (a system that absorbs more carbon than it releases), emitted more carbon than it absorbed through the 2010s because the biomass being burned holds large carbon stores. Deforestation peaked in 2004, declined through the early 2010s, then rose again through 2020.
- **Cattle and farming.** Brazil's roughly 205 million cattle (2012) are **ruminants** (animals whose stomachs ferment food), producing **methane** through **enteric fermentation** (digestive belching); manure adds **nitrous oxide**. In the energy sector, the largest single source is oil products burned in transport, with coal-fired power a smaller but still subsidised contributor.

## The Amazon as a tipping system

The Amazon regulates rainfall far beyond its borders. Around 30–50% of rainfall in the basin is recycled by the forest itself, as moisture evaporates from leaves, forms clouds, and falls again, and winds carry that humidity into central and southern Brazil. A feedback loop links forest cover to rainfall: less forest means less recycled moisture, which means less rain, which kills more forest. Researchers estimate that once about 40% of the basin is cleared, this self-sustaining rainfall collapses and large areas flip to savanna, with major losses in biodiversity and regional rainfall. About 19% of the original Amazon has already been lost, and recent studies place the system near that critical threshold. The Cerrado, Caatinga, Pantanal, Atlantic Forest, and Pampa face their own warming and drying pressures.

## How Brazil is changing

Brazil's First National Assessment on Climate Change projects a warmer, drier country with regional contrasts. Worst-affected are the Amazon (up to 6°C hotter, up to 45% less rain), the Cerrado that hosts most grain production (up to 5.5°C hotter, up to 45% drier), and the Semi-arid Northeast (up to 4.5°C hotter, up to 50% less rain, with desertification risk). Rainfall is also becoming more erratic: the South and Southeast see stronger, more frequent downpours and landslides, while the Northeast faces harsher droughts. Extreme weather already costs Brazil around R$13 billion (US$2.6 billion) a year, roughly 0.1% of 2022 GDP. Sea levels along the coast have risen about 30 cm in the past century, faster than the global mean, and are projected to rise another 20+ cm by mid-century.

## Cascading impacts

**Energy** is the most exposed sector because most Brazilian electricity comes from hydropower. In the worst case, flows at major plants such as Furnas, Itaipu, Sobradinho, Tucuruí, and Belo Monte could fall 38–57%, pushing the risk of electricity deficits toward 100% in some years and driving a shift to coal and gas that worsens emissions.

**Agriculture** faces shrinking suitable land. A 2008 Embrapa study projected losses up to R$14 billion by 2070, with soy hardest hit. A 2022 study found 28% of farmland already outside its climatic optimum, projected to reach 51% by 2030 and 74% by 2060. The 2012–13 drought killed 360,000 cattle in Paraíba alone.

**Cities and health** suffer because most Brazilians live on coasts or hillsides vulnerable to flooding, landslides, and storm surge. The 2011 Rio de Janeiro disasters killed over 900 people and displaced 30,000. Heat waves hit the elderly hardest, and diseases such as malaria, dengue, and leptospirosis are expanding into new ranges. Poor and indigenous communities, often in the most exposed rural areas, bear the heaviest burden.

## Policy response

Under the Paris Agreement, Brazil has pledged net-zero emissions by 2050. Its 2024 Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) targets a 59–67% emissions cut below 2005 levels by 2035, strengthening earlier pledges of 48% by 2025 and 53% by 2030. The 2019–2022 Bolsonaro government was widely criticised for rolling back enforcement while deforestation surged. The Lula administration has reactivated the Amazon Fund and announced that Belém will host the 2025 UN climate conference. Independent analyses estimate Brazil's pledges would still need to roughly triple to keep warming below 1.5°C with reasonable confidence, so reducing deforestation remains the single most powerful lever, because standing forest is both a carbon reservoir and the engine of regional rainfall.
