# Biodiversity hypothesis of health

The biodiversity hypothesis proposes that reduced contact between people and natural environments weakens the human commensal microbiota and its immunomodulatory capacity. It rests on two parallel trends: ongoing loss of biodiversity and a steady rise in chronic inflammatory, non-communicable diseases, especially in urbanised communities. Microbes from nature are taken to mean environmental bacteria, fungi, and other organisms, especially those that are not pathogens, though some parasites also help regulate immune responses.

Sufficient exposure to diverse natural environments, and the microbes they harbour, is essential for healthy development and ongoing regulation of the immune system. Through evolution the human body has in effect outsourced many physiological tasks to the microbiota, which in turn trains the immune system to tell threatening particles from harmless ones and to tolerate the body's own cells. Modern urban populations show signs of chronic inflammation consistent with weakened immune defence. The precise mechanism is not yet agreed.

In wealthy, highly sanitised countries the incidence of allergies, asthma, diabetes, and inflammatory bowel disease has climbed sharply. The hypothesis attributes part of this rise to habitat degradation, urbanisation, and reduced microbial exposure. The wider group of conditions plausibly tied to mild, persistent inflammation and immunological imbalance also includes metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, cancer, neurological disease, and mental disorders. Known risk factors explain only a fraction of these trends and have not accounted for the rising rate of allergies. A 2011 assessment put the societal cost of asthma and allergies in Finland at 1.3 to 1.6 billion euros, and Finland's national allergy programme later shifted emphasis from treating symptoms to prevention, including encouraging contact with natural environments; direct costs of allergic disease, including work disability, fell by roughly 15 percent in the 2000s.

People encounter environmental microbes mainly through the respiratory tract, digestive system, and skin, with habitat and diet shaping which microbes colonise them. Both the amount and the diversity of exposure matter. Northern Karelia illustrates the effect: Finnish and Russian adolescents living in adjacent but socially different regions have markedly different skin and nasal microbiotas, and only about one third as many Russian youth have allergies as Finnish youth. Comparable allergy gradients appear between Finnish children in urban versus rural settings.

Improved hygiene has reduced several infectious diseases, and traditional advice for allergies was to avoid the triggering substance. The hypothesis inverts that for benign environmental microbes: exposure is needed to develop a functional immune system, so unnecessary avoidance is counterproductive, while still recognising that exposure to genuine pathogens carries real risk.

A baby's microbiota is seeded by the mother during pregnancy, birth, and breastfeeding, so maternal nutrition, lifestyle, and birthing and feeding choices all shape early immune development, with vaginal birth and breastfeeding especially important. Because the immune system continues to mature through childhood, regular contact with biodiverse environments matters for children, and living far from forests and fields is associated with less balanced skin microbiota. Kindergartens and schools reach whole age groups and are efficient settings for exposure that does not depend on family habits. Body microbiotas shift throughout life, so exposure benefits all ages, including older people whose immune function naturally weakens.

Urban planning is therefore part of public health. Cities should conserve urban nature and design daily routes, yards, and nearby parks so that biodiverse environments are within easy reach. For young children and the elderly, whose mobility is more limited, the near environment is especially important, and features such as cultivation boxes, fruit trees, berry bushes, green roofs, and gardens in daycare, school, sheltered housing, and retirement settings can help. Leaving deadwood in forests and parks supports biodiversity and encourages movement; accessible benches support older residents. Practical steps for maintaining tolerance include supporting breastfeeding, introducing solid foods from 4 to 6 months, avoiding unnecessary avoidance of environmental exposures, strengthening immunity through contact with natural environments, using antibiotics thoughtfully because most microbes are beneficial, and considering probiotic bacteria from fermented foods.

The optimal amount, frequency, and route of exposure are not yet known, nor is it clear how environmental microbes move into the body or how different microbes interact. Despite these open questions, the hypothesis is already being used to argue that urban and regional planning should treat biodiverse, nature-rich cities as a public-health priority.
