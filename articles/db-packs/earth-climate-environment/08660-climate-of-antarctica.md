# Climate of Antarctica

Antarctica is the coldest and driest continent on Earth. Almost all of it has an ice-cap climate (Köppen classification **EF**), where nearly all precipitation falls as snow and temperatures stay far below freezing year-round. The continent averages about **166 mm (6.5 in)** of precipitation per year, and because snow rarely melts, it compacts into glacier ice that forms the ice sheet. Most of the interior qualifies as a polar desert under the standard 250 mm threshold.

## Temperature

Antarctic temperatures span an extraordinary range. The highest reliably recorded value on the continent is **19.8 °C (67.6 °F)** at Signy Research Station (Signy Island) on 30 January 1982. On the Antarctic mainland, the record is **18.3 °C (64.9 °F)** at Argentina's Esperanza Base on 6 February 2020. The Antarctic Peninsula, which has the mildest climate, occasionally reaches similar values, though summer temperatures there sit just below 0 °C most of the time.

At the opposite extreme, the lowest air temperature ever measured by thermometer was **−89.2 °C (−128.6 °F)** at Vostok Station on 21 July 1983, at an elevation of 3,488 m. That is roughly 10.7 °C colder than dry ice subliming at sea-level pressure. Satellite measurements on the cloud-free East Antarctic Plateau, between Dome A and Dome F, have since identified about 100 points reaching **−93.2 °C to −98 °C**; this is a *surface* temperature deduced from infrared radiance, not an air temperature, so the two records are not directly comparable.

Mean temperatures vary sharply with latitude, elevation, and distance from the ocean. The interior averages about **−43.5 °C**, with the elevated inland around Vostok near **−55 °C**. Coastal stations average about **−10 °C** in the mildest areas. Monthly means at McMurdo Station range from **−26 °C in August** to **−3 °C in January**. Even the South Pole, where the all-time maximum is just **−12.3 °C** (25 December 2011), follows this seasonal swing. East Antarctica is colder than West Antarctica because it sits at higher elevation. Katabatic winds, dense cold air draining down the high interior, deflect approaching weather fronts and keep the interior unusually stable.

## Precipitation and humidity

Precipitation is unevenly distributed. The Antarctic Peninsula receives **380–640 mm per year**, while the high interior may receive as little as **50 mm**. Almost all of it falls as snow; rain is rare and occurs mainly in summer along the coast and nearby islands. The quoted figures are water-equivalent depths, not actual snow depths. The cold air holds very little water vapour, so absolute humidity is extremely low.

## Ice cover and sea level

A continuous ice sheet covers nearly the whole continent, averaging at least **1,500 m thick** and holding roughly **90% of Earth's ice** and over **70% of its fresh water**. The total ice volume is about **30 × 10⁶ km³**. East Antarctica dominates, with an inland ice sheet of 9.86 million km² averaging 2,630 m thick, compared with West Antarctica's 1.81 million km² averaging 1,780 m. If all land ice melted, global sea level would rise by more than **60 m**, but Antarctic temperatures are so low that a few degrees of warming would still leave most ice below the melting point. A warmer atmosphere holds more moisture, producing more snowfall that could offset about one third of the sea-level rise expected from thermal expansion of the oceans.

About **75% of the coastline** is ice shelf, floating ice that fringes the land. Floating ice does not raise sea level when it melts, but ice shelves *buttress* the glaciers behind them, slowing their flow. When a shelf breaks up, the glaciers it held back accelerate into the sea. Between 1992 and 2003, East Antarctica thickened by about **1.8 cm per year** on average while West Antarctica thinned by **0.9 cm per year**.

Several Peninsula ice shelves have already collapsed: the Wordie Ice Shelf shrank between 1936 and 1989, ice in the Prince Gustav Channel disintegrated in 1995, Larsen A broke up in January 1995, and 3,250 km² of Larsen B disintegrated in February 2001. A 2015 study concluded the remaining Larsen B would disintegrate by the end of that decade. The George VI Ice Shelf, possibly already unstable, has only existed for roughly 8,000 years after an earlier collapse, likely triggered by warm ocean currents.

## Climate change

Despite its isolation, Antarctica has warmed and lost ice in recent decades because of greenhouse gas emissions. West Antarctica warmed by more than **0.1 °C per decade** between the 1950s and 2000s, and the exposed Antarctic Peninsula has warmed by about **3 °C since the mid-20th century**. The colder, more stable East Antarctic interior showed no clear warming until the 2000s. The surrounding Southern Ocean has absorbed more heat than any other ocean, with strong warming below 2,000 m depth, and waters around West Antarctica have warmed by about **1 °C since 1955**. That ocean warming has weakened or collapsed coastal ice shelves, causing coastal glaciers to retreat and thin. By 2100, net Antarctic ice loss is expected to add about **11 cm** to global sea level; if marine ice sheet instability is triggered in West Antarctica before 2100, it could add tens of centimetres more.
