# 100% renewable energy

100% renewable energy means sourcing all energy use—electricity, heating, cooling, and transport—from resources that replenish naturally: sun, wind, water, biomass, and geothermal heat. The goal is driven by climate change, pollution, energy security, and economics. It is stricter than carbon neutrality, which also accepts nuclear power or fossil fuels paired with carbon capture.

## Feasibility

A 2022 review of 181 peer-reviewed papers found that the great majority of publications conclude 100% renewable energy systems are technically feasible and economically viable across power, heat, transport, and industry. A 2022 review of 97 island studies reached the same conclusion. The IPCC's 2011 report found few fundamental technological limits to integrating renewable technologies at scale.

Stanford's Mark Z. Jacobson has argued that wind, water, and solar (WWS) could power the world by 2050, and a 2022 study by his group modelled paths for 145 countries, finding a WWS system could cut energy demand by 56% through efficiency, lower costs, and create more jobs than fossil-based systems. In 2017, 21 researchers published a rebuttal accusing his models of errors, including an assumption of expanding hydropower by the equivalent of 600 Hoover Dams. Jacobson disputed the critics point by point, and a separate 2017 study confirmed similar results for North America using more realistic storage assumptions. The main barriers, across the literature, are political and social rather than technological.

## Where it already exists

Several countries and regions already meet most or all of their electricity demand with renewables:

| Place | Renewable share | Main sources |
|---|---|---|
| Albania, Paraguay, Tajikistan | ~100% | Hydroelectric |
| Iceland | 100% | 72% hydro, 28% geothermal |
| Norway | 96% hydro, 4% other renewables | Hydro dominates |
| Costa Rica | 99% | 90% hydro, plus geothermal and wind |
| Scotland (UK) | 97% (2020) | Wind, hydro |
| Quebec (Canada) | 99% | Hydro |

In 2016, Portugal ran on 100% renewable electricity for four consecutive days. Germany occasionally meets nearly all demand with solar and wind alone. At least 30 nations have renewables supplying more than 20% of their energy, and over 120 countries have renewable policy targets.

## Brief history

Danish physicist Bent Sørensen proposed 100% renewable energy in a 1975 *Science* paper. Amory Lovins coined "soft energy path" in 1976. The first detailed 100% scenario was published in 2006; publications rose sharply after 2009. Iceland was the first country to propose 100% renewables in 1998. Denmark committed to 100% by 2050 in 2012.

## Intermittency and how systems handle it

The main technical challenge is that solar and wind are variable, and during dark, calm periods ("Dunkelflauten") output can be very low. Six categories of solutions recur in the literature:

- **Diversification** of wind, solar, hydro, and geothermal so their peaks and troughs offset.
- **Storage**: batteries for short-term gaps, pumped hydro for medium-term, and hydrogen or ammonia for seasonal storage.
- **Super grids** connecting regions with different weather patterns.
- **Demand flexibility** through smart grids that shift non-critical loads to high-supply periods.
- **Power-to-X**: converting surplus electricity into hydrogen, ammonia, or synthetic fuels.
- **Oversizing** capacity beyond average demand to meet peaks, accepting some curtailment.

Geothermal and hydroelectric power are considered "firm" or dispatchable renewables, available on demand. Critics including Vaclav Smil and a 2021 J.P. Morgan Asset Management report have argued that renewable forecasts since the 1970s underestimate intermittency, energy density, and infrastructure complexity. A 2018 study found that a renewable-only grid with batteries could raise electricity prices 11–163% depending on regional resource quality, unless firm low-carbon sources like nuclear or geothermal are included alongside variable renewables.

## Non-technical obstacles

A World Bank scenario for staying below 2 °C requires about 3 billion tonnes of metals by 2050, meaning supplies of copper, nickel, cobalt, and lithium must rise by up to 500%. Current cobalt and lithium reserves are insufficient for a full transition using today's battery chemistries. China produces roughly 95% of the world's polysilicon and solar wafers, a supply-chain chokepoint. Political barriers include climate denial, fossil fuel lobbying, political paralysis, outdated infrastructure, and financial constraints; both the IEA and IPCC have been criticised for under-representing 100% renewable scenarios.

In 2014, renewables provided 19% of global energy, with hydro at 16.6% and wind at 3.1% of electricity. By 2021, wind and solar still represented about 5% of global primary energy, though a much larger share of useful energy. As of 2019, renewable energy needed to grow six times faster to limit warming to 2 °C. Oil (31.2%), coal (27.2%), and natural gas (24.7%) still dominated world primary energy in 2020.

## Costs and policy momentum

McKinsey estimates that reaching net zero by 2050 will cost about 7.5% of global GDP cumulatively between 2021 and 2050; current spending is roughly half that. Jacobson argues a WWS system would be cost-competitive with existing arrangements. Hawaii, California (SB 100), and Washington State have enacted 100% clean electricity laws targeting 2045. By 2018, no G20 country had committed to a fully 100% renewable target, though most had set intermediate renewable goals under the Paris Agreement.
