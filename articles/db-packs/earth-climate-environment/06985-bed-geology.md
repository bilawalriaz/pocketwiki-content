# Bed (geology)

A bed is a single layer of sediment, sedimentary rock, or pyroclastic material (fragmented volcanic ejecta) bounded above and below by bedding planes, or by curved bedding surfaces. In a cross-section, the same contact is often called a bedding contact. In an undisturbed sequence, each bedding plane was the depositional surface on which younger sediment came to rest, so a bed usually records a single episode of accumulation under roughly steady conditions. Sediment is loose material such as sand, silt, or mud that has not yet been hardened into rock.

## How beds and laminae are distinguished

Sedimentologists use two definitions, and the choice depends on the study:

- Structural definition (Campbell; Reineck & Singh). A bed is any coherent layer bounded by bedding planes, regardless of thickness. Laminae are the smallest visible layers in the same hierarchy, treated as small beds within a larger bed.
- Thickness definition (Collinson & Mountney; Miall). A bed is a layer thicker than 1 cm; a lamina is 1 cm or thinner. This is the cut-off most field geologists apply in practice.

The structural definition describes layering relationships; the thickness definition sorts layers by a measurable property.

## What bedding planes mean

Bedding planes usually record a change in the rate or type of accumulating sediment: a pause in deposition, an erosional event, a shift in current or wind regime, or a sudden change in composition driven by a change in environment such as sea level, climate, or river behaviour. Some planes are not depositional at all, created or sharpened later by diagenesis (physical and chemical changes that turn sediment into rock after burial) or weathering. Distinguishing primary from post-depositional planes is a routine interpretive problem.

The shape of a bed follows the geometry of its bounding planes. Most beds are subparallel to parallel, producing tabular forms, but non-parallel planes give rise to wedge-shaped, lenticular, curved, and irregular geometries.

## Two important bed types

- Cross-beds (or cross-sets) are not horizontal. They form when sand or coarser material is deposited on the lee side of a ripple or dune, and the upper part of that inclined surface is later truncated by erosion, leaving internal layers inclined to the main bedding.
- Graded beds change grain size from one side to the other. Normal grading puts coarser grains on the older (bottom) side and finer grains on the younger (top) side, the signature of a waning current such as a turbidity current (a sediment-laden underwater flow). Inverse grading reverses the pattern and points to different mechanics, such as grain-flow processes on a steep dune face.

## Bed thickness

Thickness is one of the most basic bed properties, used to map stratigraphic units, interpret sedimentary facies (bodies of rock formed in a particular environment), recognise breaks in sedimentation, identify cyclic patterns, and detect gradual environmental change. The working assumption in many studies is that thicknesses follow a log-normal distribution: most beds cluster near a typical thickness with a long tail toward thicker beds. No single classification has been universally adopted, and engineering geology has its own standard nomenclatures in Australia, the EU, and the UK.

Two representative schemes bracket the range. McKee and Weir (1953) draw lines at 1 cm (bed versus lamina), 5 cm, 60 cm, and 120 cm. Tucker (1982) places the very-thick threshold above 1 m and uses 10 cm and 30 cm for medium and thick. Below the 1 cm bed–lamina boundary, lamination classes extend down to 2–3 mm.

## Beds in lithostratigraphy

Lithostratigraphy classifies rock layers by their physical characteristics rather than by age. Under both the North American Stratigraphic Code and the International Stratigraphic Guide, the bed is the smallest formal lithostratigraphic unit for sedimentary rocks. A formally named bed is called a key bed or marker bed, and only distinctive, regionally traceable beds usually receive proper names. The hierarchy below a member is the bed; above it sit members, then formations, then groups.

For volcanic rocks the equivalent unit is a flow, a discrete surface-erupted volcanic body distinguishable by texture, composition, order of superposition, paleomagnetism, or other objective criteria. A flow belongs to a member the way a sedimentary bed does.

## Engineering significance

In geotechnical engineering, a bedding plane is a mechanical discontinuity. Tunnels, foundations, and slopes can fail by sliding or separating along these planes, so their orientation, spacing, and shear strength are routinely mapped before construction.

## Four governing principles

Beds are the evidence base for the relative-dating principles that underpin stratigraphy:

- Law of Superposition: in an undisturbed sequence, younger beds lie above older ones, and the order is preserved unless tectonic activity overturns the sequence.
- Law of Original Horizontality: beds are deposited essentially horizontal under gravity, so tilted or folded beds record later deformation.
- Law of Lateral Continuity: a bed originally extends sideways in all directions until it thins to nothing or meets a barrier, so similar rocks on either side of a valley or fault were once joined.
- Law of Cross-Cutting Relationships: any feature that cuts across a bed, such as a fault or an igneous dike (a sheet of magma that intrudes existing rock), must be younger than the bed it cuts.

These four rules let geologists read the order of events in a rock sequence even when no absolute dates are available, which is why an outcrop of tilted beds in the French Alps is read as flat-lying sediments later deformed by mountain building.
