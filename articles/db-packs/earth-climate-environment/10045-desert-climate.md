# Desert climate

A desert climate is a dry climate in which evaporation far exceeds precipitation. The exposed rock, gravel, and sand that dominate these landscapes store almost no moisture, so what little rain falls runs off or evaporates quickly. In the Köppen classification these regions carry the code BW, and they cover roughly 14.2% of Earth's land area, more than any other climate type.

## How the Köppen system defines a desert

Köppen splits BW into two subtypes. **BWh** (hot desert) and **BWk** (cold desert) are separated by an isotherm: a mean annual temperature at or above 18 °C (64.4 °F) marks the climate as hot; below that line it is cold. Within BW, the boundary between full desert and semi-arid (BS) is set by a precipitation threshold computed from temperature and the seasonal timing of rainfall. The threshold in millimetres equals twenty times the annual mean temperature in °C, plus a seasonal bonus of 280, 140, or 0 depending on whether 70%+, 30–70%, or less than 30% of precipitation falls in the high-sun half of the year (April–September in the Northern Hemisphere, October–March in the Southern). A station with less than half of this threshold is classified as BW; 50–100% is semi-arid.

## How dry "desert dry" really is

Most arid regions receive between 25 and 200 mm (1–8 in) of rain per year. The Atacama in northern Chile averaged just 5 mm (0.20 in) a year over a 17-year span, the lowest measured figure for a long record. Kufra, in the Libyan Sahara, registers 0.86 mm (0.034 in) annually. Death Valley officially receives about 60 mm (2.4 in), but only 16 mm (0.63 in) fell across 40 months between 1931 and 1934. The classification is not strictly about rainfall totals, however: where potential evapotranspiration (the amount of water that would evaporate from soil and plants under given conditions) is extreme, areas with as much as 430 mm (17 in) a year, such as the Sahel, parts of central Australia, and the Guajira Peninsula, can still count as arid because so much water would be lost to the air.

## Hot desert climates (BWh)

Hot deserts sit between roughly 20° and 33° latitude in both hemispheres, beneath the subtropical ridge (a band of persistent high-pressure air that circles the globe around 30° latitude). Persistent high pressure and descending air suppress clouds, fronts, and storms, so skies stay clear, the air is dry from the surface upward, and sunshine is intense. They occur on every continent except Antarctica: the Sahara, Namib, and Kalahari in Africa; the Arabian, Syrian, Dasht-e Lut, Dasht-e Kavir, and Thar deserts in West and South Asia; the Mojave, Sonoran, and Chihuahuan in North America; the sertão of Brazil; the coasts of Peru and Chile; the Simpson and Great Victoria in Australia; and a sliver of southeastern Spain.

Summer is the dominant season. Mean hot-month temperatures usually run 29–35 °C (84–95 °F), and midday readings of 43–46 °C (109–115 °F) are routine. The world's extreme heat records belong to these regions, including the 56.7 °C (134.1 °F) measured in Death Valley, the highest air temperature reliably recorded on Earth. Some tropical deserts, such as Dallol in Ethiopia, stay hot year-round, with annual means above 30 °C (86 °F) and approaching 35 °C (95 °F). Clear skies allow strong radiative cooling after sunset, so winter nights can approach or slip just below freezing, though hard freezes are rare.

## Cold desert climates (BWk)

Cold deserts share the dryness but sit further from the equator, usually in the 30s and 40s latitude bands, often at higher elevation, and almost always in the rain shadow of mountain ranges that block the dominant moisture-bearing wind. The Andes keep the Patagonian Desert dry by intercepting the westerlies (winds that blow from west to east in the mid-latitudes); the mountains ringing Central Asia block the eastern monsoon, producing the Gobi, Kyzyl Kum, and Taklamakan. The Great Basin's cold deserts, including the Bighorn Basin and Columbia Plateau, lie leeward (on the sheltered, downwind side) of the Cascades and Sierra Nevada. The high-altitude Ladakh region around Leh, India, is another example. In Europe, only a few inland pockets of southeastern Spain, such as Lorca, qualify.

Summers are hot or warm but rarely as severe as in BWh regions. Winters are cold and dry, with freezing temperatures common and snowfall rare. The Gobi illustrates the pattern: summers are hot, but it shares the freezing winters of inland Asia. Frigid air holds almost no moisture, so very little precipitation falls in the McMurdo Dry Valleys of Antarctica, and the ground stays desert-like. Their summers are too cold (below 10 °C / 50 °F on average) to qualify as BW, so they are classified as polar climate even though they are functionally deserts.

## What the two subtypes share

BWh and BWk both result in a landscape where evaporation wins, vegetation is sparse, and life is organised around drought. BWh is the product of Hadley-cell subsidence (large-scale sinking air in the tropics and subtropics, which warms and dries as it descends) under the subtropical highs. BWk is mostly the product of continental interiors and rain shadows, where mountains strip moisture from air before it can reach the basin.
