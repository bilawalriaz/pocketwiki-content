# Agricultural pollution

Agricultural pollution is the contamination of air, water, and soil by farming byproducts. Sources range from a single discharge point (point source) to landscape-wide diffuse runoff (non-point source). Management practices largely determine how much pollution is produced; badly timed fertilizer or pesticide applications, poorly contained manure, overgrazing, and excessive plowing are common drivers. Once released, pollutants cause immediate harm such as killing wildlife or contaminating drinking water, and downstream harm such as coastal dead zones where nutrient-rich runoff accumulates. The main pollutant groups are sediments, nutrients (nitrogen and phosphorus), pathogens, pesticides, metals, salts, and plastics. Animal agriculture is an outsized contributor across most of these categories.

## Pesticides

Without pest control, roughly 40% of crops would typically be lost before harvest, but pesticide persistence creates its own problems. Compounds such as 2,4-D and atrazine can persist in soil for up to 20 years, and DDT, aldrin, dieldrin, endrin, heptachlor, and toxaphene are also long-lived. The danger is mainly to non-target organisms, since these compounds bioaccumulate up the food chain and harm soil organisms. Biopesticides derived from natural sources are often proposed as cleaner alternatives, but their use remains modest, they can share the same drawbacks as synthetics, and in the United States they face fewer environmental regulations while remaining permitted under USDA organic standards.

Leaching, the downward migration of dissolved pesticides, is a leading route into groundwater. It is worst for water-soluble compounds on sandy soils after heavy watering, especially when the chemical binds poorly to soil particles. Mixing areas, equipment-wash sites, and disposal zones are additional leaching hotspots.

## Fertilizers

### Nitrogen

The most common nitrogen fertilizers are nitrate (NO3−) and ammonium (NH4+). They have roughly quadrupled the productivity of farmland: at 1900 yields, the year 2000 harvest would have needed nearly four times more land and claimed about half of all ice-free continents instead of the under 15% used today. The cost is that crops do not take up everything applied. Because nitrate is highly soluble and repelled by negatively charged clay particles, it readily runs off into surface water and leaches into groundwater. Drinking-water nitrate above 10 mg/L (10 ppm) can cause blue-baby syndrome (methemoglobinemia) in infants and is linked to thyroid disease and certain cancers.

In aquatic systems, surplus nitrogen drives eutrophication: the explosive growth of algae and cyanobacteria, followed by oxygen depletion that creates hypoxic or anoxic dead zones. Sensitive invertebrates such as mayflies and stoneflies are replaced by tolerant species like molluscs, which alters how rivers cycle nutrients. Fertilizers also volatilize ammonia, which becomes NOx and acidifies ecosystems and damages human lungs, and they release nitrous oxide (N2O), a greenhouse gas that also destroys stratospheric ozone. In soil, excess nitrogen alters microbial communities, depletes organic matter, and can break mutualisms such as the legume–rhizobia partnership by selecting for less cooperative bacteria.

### Phosphorus

Phosphorus is usually applied as phosphate (PO43−) from synthetic salts, manure, or compost. Crops need only small amounts, so most applied phosphorus is surplus and washes into surface water. Even tiny additions matter because phosphorus is usually the nutrient limiting freshwater algae, so any extra can disrupt the natural biogeochemical cycle. Phosphorus is the largest contributor to freshwater eutrophication, and the resulting blooms release cyanotoxins harmful to humans, livestock, and aquatic life. Phosphate rock can contain high levels of cadmium, and the European Commission has considered caps on fertilizer cadmium because repeated use contaminates soils. It is also often high in fluoride, which rarely reaches humans through plants but can poison livestock that ingest soil and may harm soil microbes.

## Equipment, plastics, and land management

Farm machinery leaks oil, hydraulic fluid, and tire particles, and agriculture produces about 10 million tons of plastic waste per year, much of it from greenhouse sheeting, mulch, and feed or seed bags, with very low global collection rates. Soil erosion adds roughly 6 million hectares of irreversibly degraded farmland each year; eroded sediment clogs waterways, blocks light, and carries attached phosphorus and pesticides into rivers. Tillage also influences nitrous oxide emissions from soil microbes, which is one reason conservation agriculture, built on minimal disturbance, permanent soil cover (mulch or cover crops), and species diversification, reduces fertilizer needs, cuts ammonia and greenhouse-gas emissions, and stores more carbon.

## Biotic sources and livestock

Manures and biosolids spread on land can contain pharmaceuticals and personal care products (PPCPs) consumed by animals. Undiluted manure is about 100 times more concentrated than domestic sewage, can carry the parasite Cryptosporidium, and silage liquor from fermented grass is even stronger, with a low pH and very high biological oxygen demand that corrodes storage tanks. A dairy farm with 2,500 cows produces as much waste as a city of about 411,000 people, and a 2005 USDA report counted more than 335 million tons of dry-matter manure produced on U.S. farms each year, with nitrate concentrations in groundwater sometimes reaching 50 mg/L.

Livestock also drive climate impacts. The FAO has estimated livestock at 18% of anthropogenic greenhouse gases, more than transport, though the figure is contested on both sides; a PNAS model found that removing animals from U.S. agriculture would cut total U.S. emissions by only 2.6%, since manures would be replaced by synthetic fertilizers.

Mitigation options include composting, mechanical solid–liquid separation, and anaerobic digestion, which captures biogas. Methane is roughly 20 times more potent than CO2, so uncontrolled lagoon storage matters, while heated digesters and aerobic systems cut both odor and greenhouse-gas emissions at higher upfront cost.

## Introduced species and biological control

Globalised trade accidentally moves pests, weeds, and diseases; European bumblebees shipped to North America as commercial pollinators carried a parasite that has contributed to native bumblebee declines. Infested machinery, livestock, fodder, and seed spread weeds, and quarantines under WTO sanitary and phytosanitary rules, enforced nationally by agencies such as USDA/APHIS, are the main regulatory tool. Biological control using predators, parasitoids, and pathogens can replace pesticides, but releases are hard to reverse: a parasitoid introduced in North America to control gypsy and browntail moths later attacked native silk moths, causing their decline.

## Genetic engineering

GM crops can cross into wild relatives, creating superweeds or eroding native genetics, and herbicide-tolerant crops have driven more herbicide use, in some cases reducing milkweeds needed by monarch butterfly larvae. They can also cut pollution: the Enviropig, a Yorkshire pig engineered to express phytase in its saliva, breaks down phytic acid in grain so the animal absorbs 20–60% more phosphorus, reducing fecal phosphorus and the need for supplemented feed.
