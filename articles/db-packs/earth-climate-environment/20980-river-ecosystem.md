# River ecosystem

A river ecosystem is a flowing freshwater system in which living organisms interact with each other and with the moving water, its bed, and its dissolved chemistry. The word *lotic* describes flowing water (from Latin *lotus*, washed); *lentic* describes still water like lakes and ponds (from Latin *lenis*, calm). There is no sharp boundary between the two, and together they make up freshwater ecology.

What makes flowing water unique is the current itself. Flow is unidirectional, the channel changes constantly as water erodes and deposits material, and habitats vary sharply over short distances and times. Because turbulence mixes air into the water, fast, shallow reaches carry more dissolved oxygen and support more life than deep, slow pools. Streams gain volume and discharge as they join into larger rivers, and their general pattern of flow over time is the flow regime.

The water column carries four broad classes of material: dissolved ions and nutrients (calcium, sodium, nitrogen, phosphorus, silica), dissolved gases (oxygen, carbon dioxide, nitrogen), suspended inorganic sediment, and dissolved and particulate organic matter. Oxygen is the most critical gas. It enters by diffusion at the surface, dissolves less easily as temperature rises, and is depleted at night when plants respire instead of photosynthesising. Light intensity drops rapidly with depth and with the angle at which it strikes the surface, so shallow, turbulent reaches stay well lit while deep waters go dark. Temperature, set by air, shade, climate, and elevation, matters because most river animals have body temperatures that match the surrounding water.

The living part of the system runs on two energy sources. *Autochthonous* energy is made inside the river by algae and plants using sunlight. *Allochthonous* energy arrives from outside, mainly as leaf litter, twigs, and woody debris falling or blowing in from the surrounding land. The relative mix shifts from headwaters to mouth: shaded small streams depend on leaf litter, mid-sized open reaches depend on attached algae, and large turbid rivers depend on fine particles washed in from upstream and from floodplains. This downstream shift is the basis of the River Continuum Concept, a model linking stream size, light, and food source to the organisms found there, though its applicability to non-temperate or disturbed systems is limited.

The biotic community is layered. Bacteria, fungi, and diatoms colonise every surface and particle, often within biofilms, slimy matrices of microbes and algae glued to stones by an extracellular polymer that traps enzymes and shields cells from stress. Algae are the main primary producers, floating as phytoplankton in slow water or clinging as periphyton to rocks in fast water. Rooted plants like pondweeds and water hyacinths anchor in slack water and provide habitat as well as food. Insects dominate the invertebrate fauna, especially mayflies (Ephemeroptera), stoneflies (Plecoptera), and caddisflies (Trichoptera), with beetles, dragonflies, and flies also common, alongside snails, mussels, and crustaceans. Fish sit higher on the chain. Salmon are anadromous, meaning they are born in fresh water but mature at sea, while eels are catadromous, the reverse. Many other vertebrates, including amphibians, turtles, otters, and river dolphins, use rivers at least part of their lives.

Energy moves through these organisms as a food web of producers, consumers, and decomposers, with bacteria and fungi returning nutrients from dead matter so the cycle can start again. Different invertebrate feeding roles keep the system from collapsing into competition: shredders chew coarse leaves, collectors gather fine particles, grazers scrape algae, filter feeders strain the passing current, and predators consume the rest. Fish divide into plankton-eaters, bottom-grazers, surface-feeders, benthic insect-eaters, and top predators. Diversity is maintained by resource partitioning, where species separate by habitat, diet, or timing, and by the system's ability to return to a stable community after disturbance.

Three pressures now dominate human impact on rivers. Pollution arrives in many diffuse forms, including excess nutrients, sewage, plastics, pharmaceuticals, heavy metals, road salt, and heat, all of which lower oxygen, alter pH, and reduce species richness. Flow modification by dams, diversions, and channel straightening fragments habitats, traps sediment, alters temperature, and blocks migrations of fish that move between fresh and salt water. Invasive species, introduced intentionally or by accident, outcompete or prey on natives and are hard to remove once lotic connectivity lets them spread. Because every tributary, pollutant, and blockage upstream affects every stretch downstream, disturbances accumulate along the full length of a river.
