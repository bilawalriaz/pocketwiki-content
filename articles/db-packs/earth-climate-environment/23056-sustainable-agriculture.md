# Sustainable agriculture

Sustainable agriculture is farming that produces food and fibre while preserving the soil, water, and biodiversity on which production depends, so that the same land can keep yielding indefinitely. The U.S. Department of Agriculture gave the first formal definition in 1977, though communities had practised land-conserving methods long before the term existed.

Why it matters
--------------
Food systems cause roughly one third of human greenhouse gas emissions and drive water scarcity, water pollution, land degradation, and deforestation. The climate and soil changes farming creates feed back on harvests, so the industry is both driver and victim. Demand keeps rising as the population grows, putting pressure on a finite land base.

Core principles
---------------
Four ideas recur across definitions. First, work with ecological processes, including nutrient cycling, nitrogen fixation by legumes and their root bacteria (rhizobia), and pollination, rather than overriding them. Second, minimise non-renewable and harmful inputs such as synthetic fertilisers and pesticides. Third, build farmer knowledge, equity, and economic viability, because practices that ignore livelihoods do not last. Fourth, weigh short-term gains against long-term regeneration, since many benefits of sustainable methods, such as richer soil microbial life, appear only after years.

The main practice families
--------------------------
Crop rotations and polyculture break pest and disease cycles, and legumes in the rotation restore soil nitrogen. Cover crops and reduced or no tillage protect topsoil from wind and water erosion and keep soil carbon intact, since intensive tillage depletes soil carbon and accelerates climate change. Rotational grazing and mixed crop-livestock systems recycle nutrients and lower external input needs. Agroforestry and shade-grown systems imitate natural ecosystems: trees provide shade, fix nitrogen, stabilise soil, and yield timber or fruit alongside the main crop. Integrated systems such as rice-fish or rice-fish-duck culture, for example in South-East Asia where ducks eat weeds and their manure replaces fertiliser, add a second product and cut weeds and runoff. Organic farming prohibits most synthetic pesticides and fertilisers, while regenerative and ecological farming go further by rebuilding soil organic matter and biodiversity.

Land-use strategies
-------------------
Three approaches compete. Land sparing concentrates production on high-yield fields so that separate areas can revert to natural habitat; land sharing, also called wildlife-friendly farming, integrates biodiversity into working fields through lower-intensity methods; land blending combines the two, tailoring the mix to local conditions. A review of empirical studies found that land sparing was favoured for conserving tropical forest biodiversity in 67% of relevant studies, while land sharing was seen as better for farmer food security and livelihoods in 58% of studies on developing countries.

Soil, nutrients, and water
--------------------------
Four factors set a site's potential: climate, soil, nutrients, and water. Of these, soil and water are most open to human influence. Excessive tillage erodes soil, and irrigation without drainage salinises it. The IPCC estimates that current soil erosion on agricultural fields runs 10 to 100 times faster than soil formation, and about a quarter of Earth's ice-free land is degraded. Agriculture uses about 69% of global fresh water, and over-extraction from sources such as the Ogallala Aquifer turns groundwater into a de facto non-renewable resource.

Nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium each have sustainability ceilings. Nitrate fertiliser leaches into waterways; alternatives include recycling manure, growing legumes, making hydrogen for the Haber process by electrolysis with renewable electricity, and engineering nitrogen fixation into non-legume crops. Phosphate is mined from a non-renewable rock reserve and is often a limiting nutrient; peak phosphorus is projected within a few hundred years. Using phosphate-solubilising microbes, including mycorrhizal fungi, can free existing soil phosphorus. Potassium chloride, the dominant K source, brings chloride that harms soil organisms, so chloride-free fertilisers are an option. Crop residues left on the surface, biochar, and animal and green manures recycle nutrients back to the soil.

Pests, weeds, and energy
-----------------------
Options that reduce synthetic pesticide use include steam or solar soil sterilisation, biofumigant cover crops from the Brassicaceae family that release toxic isothiocyanates when ploughed in, biological control (in east Asian rice systems, nectar plants that support pest predators cut insecticide use by 70% while lifting yields 5%), and precision tools such as the false seedbed tiller, which removes small weeds while disturbing only the top centimetre of soil. Modern agriculture depends heavily on fossil energy for machinery, transport, and inputs such as synthetic fertiliser, so energy and food prices move together. Agrivoltaics (growing crops under solar panels) and renewable-powered systems decouple the two.

People and economics
-------------------
Externalities, the environmental and social costs not priced into food, are why purely market-driven systems under-deliver sustainability. A 2025 United Nations report noted that hunger has risen for six consecutive years and that investment in sustainable agriculture is roughly four times more cost-effective than direct food aid, yet receives only about 3% of humanitarian funding. Policies matter: the European Union's 2020 "Farm to Fork" strategy targets 25% organic farmland, 50% cuts in pesticide and antimicrobial use, 20% less fertiliser, and 50% less nutrient loss and food waste by 2030. The 2021 UN Climate Change Conference secured over 4 billion dollars for sustainable agriculture transitions. Standards such as organic certification, Rainforest Alliance, Fair Trade, UTZ Certified, GlobalGAP, Bird Friendly, and the Common Code for the Coffee Community (4C) translate these principles into verifiable labels.

History and outlook
-------------------
The phrase "sustainable agriculture" is attributed to Australian agronomist Gordon McClymont and entered common use in the late 1980s, though indigenous land-management traditions (North American intercropping such as the Three Sisters of corn, beans, and squash, the Anishinaabe "Honorable Harvest" rules of taking only what is needed, Amazonian dark-earth raised fields, and South-East Asian rice-fish culture) embodied its principles centuries earlier. By 2018, sustainable intensification practices were in use on 163 million farms across 100 countries, covering 453 million hectares, about 29% of farmland worldwide.
