# Ocean surface ecosystem

The ocean's surface is a thin sunlit boundary roughly one metre deep; nearly half of UV-B is absorbed in that first metre. Organisms living here are called neuston, from Greek *νέω*, meaning both to swim and to float. Ocean surfaces cover about 72% of Earth's surface, making this one of the planet's largest habitats, and among its least studied.

Neuston link distant ecosystems. In the North Pacific, 80% of the loggerhead turtle's diet is neuston prey, and nearly 30% of the Laysan albatross's diet is neuston. Commercially important fish such as Atlantic cod, salmon, and billfish use the surface as juveniles. Floating seaweed, drifting debris, and surface-dwelling animals move nutrients, shelter, and larvae between coral reefs, the deep sea, islands, and freshwater systems.

## Position at the surface

Three terms describe position at the air-water interface. *Pleuston* live floating at the surface itself. *Epineuston* sit on top, held by surface tension. *Hyponeuston* swim just beneath.

## Major groups

**Floating cnidarians** anchor the surface food web. *Velella* (by-the-wind sailor) and *Porpita* (blue button) are sister genera in the chondrophore clade of Hydrozoa; both host symbiotic dinoflagellates that may allow them to live in nutrient-poor water, like reef corals. *Velella* has a raised transparent sail on a blue oval disc, and because some sails lean left and others right, wind does not push the whole population one way. *Physalia* (Portuguese man-o-war) evolved a similar sail and float convergently and carries a powerful sting. The rare *Actinecta* floats using a gas bubble on its pedal disc.

**Bubble-rafting snails** (*Janthina*, *Recluzia*, *Glaucus*) trap air in mucus and ride the surface; *Glaucus* floats upside down using surface tension. Violet *Janthina* prey on *Velella*, *Porpita*, and sometimes *Physalia*, depending on passive contact.

**Buoy barnacles** (*Dosima fascicularis*) settle as larvae on feathers or debris, then secrete a soft, airy float instead of the usual hard cement and eventually drift free.

**Neustonic seaweeds** include *Sargassum fluitans* and *Sargassum natans*, whose gas-filled floats form the Sargasso Sea in the western North Atlantic Subtropical Gyre, an ecologically distinct region hosting endemic species and serving as nursery for commercially important fish. *Turbinaria ornata* in the Pacific detaches buoyant fronds that ride surface currents.

**Epineuston** in the open ocean are limited to the insect genus *Halobates* (sea skaters), the only truly oceanic insects. They prey on other neuston and lay eggs on floating feathers, wood, plastic, or pelagic mollusc shells.

**Hyponeuston** are dominated by copepods, especially *Pontellidae*, which have specialised attachment structures, blue astaxanthin-based pigmentation (likely UV protection or camouflage), and flight behaviour to escape predators. *Idotea metallica*, a surface isopod, floats upside down or clings to flotsam and to *Janthina* bubble rafts, with thermal tolerance around 13 to 16 °C. Many fish, including anchovy, mahi-mahi, marlins, swordfish, flying fish, halfbeaks, viperfish, lanternfish, and eels, use the surface only as juveniles; American and European eels converge in the Sargasso Sea to spawn.

## Sea surface microlayer

The sea surface microlayer (SML) is the air-sea interface itself, operationally 1 to 1000 μm thick (about 60 μm by pH change), covering about 70% of Earth's surface. Though thinner than a millimetre, it has measurably different chemistry and biology from the water below: enriched organic matter, surfactants, distinct microbial communities, and biofilm-like gel properties. Carbon accumulates there at roughly 200 Tg C per year, comparable to deep-sea sedimentation but with a very short residence time. The SML behaves as a global biochemical microreactor, central to gas exchange between ocean and atmosphere and to the ocean's carbon cycle.

## Surface slicks

Surface slicks are smooth meandering lines where organic surfactants accumulate and where subsurface internal waves drive surface convergence. They aggregate plankton, eggs, and larvae, becoming nursery habitat for over 100 fish species whose adults later occupy coral reefs, the epipelagic, or the deep sea, linking surface processes to reef function and fisheries yield.

## Human pressure and unknowns

The surface receives the bulk of climate change, oil, and plastic pollution. Pre-industrial oceans probably carried more wood and no plastic, so the surface ecosystem today likely differs sharply from centuries past. Basic questions, including how many neustonic species exist, how their populations connect, and how life histories shape distribution, remain largely unresolved.
