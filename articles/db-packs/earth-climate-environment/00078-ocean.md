# Ocean

The ocean is a single, interconnected body of salt water covering about 70.8% of Earth's surface and holding 97% of all water on the planet. It dominates Earth's heat balance, carbon cycle, and water cycle, forming the physical basis of climate and weather. It harbours most of Earth's animal and protist life, originated photosynthesis, and still supplies about half of the atmospheric oxygen.

## Origin

Water was likely present in the material that formed Earth, though the young planet was hot enough that water escaped gravity. Outgassing, volcanism, and meteorite impacts produced an early atmosphere of carbon dioxide, nitrogen, and water vapour. As the surface cooled, vapour condensed into a first ocean, probably hotter than today and greenish from dissolved iron. Pillow basalts in the Isua Greenstone Belt and rocks in the Nuvvuagittuq Greenstone Belt in Quebec, dated to 3.8 and possibly 4.28 billion years old, preserve direct evidence of that liquid water. A 2020 model argues the full ocean volume was present from the start, kept liquid by greenhouse gases even when the young Sun emitted only 70% of today's energy. Life evolved in the ocean about 3 billion years before colonising land.

## Shape of the seabed

The seabed is shaped by plate tectonics. At mid-ocean ridges, rising magma pushes plates apart. Where plates converge, one slides beneath another in subduction, forming deep trenches, earthquakes, and volcanic island chains. The mid-ocean ridge system is Earth's longest mountain range at 65,000 km. The deepest point, Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench, reaches about 10,935 m. Average depth is 3,688 m, and waters below 200 m cover roughly 66% of Earth's surface. As of 2024, just over 26% of the seafloor is mapped at higher resolution than satellite bathymetry.

The world ocean is conventionally divided into five named oceans by descending area: Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern, and Arctic. The Southern Ocean, defined by the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, gained international recognition in 1999–2000. Point Nemo in the South Pacific, the ocean's pole of inaccessibility, lies about 2,688 km from the nearest land.

## Light, temperature, and layering

Sunlight warms only the upper layer. The photic zone, where light intensity is at least about 1% of surface value and photosynthesis is possible, extends to roughly 200 m. Below lies the mesopelagic twilight zone (200–1,000 m), then the permanently dark aphotic zone, subdivided into bathypelagic (10–4 °C, roughly 700–4,000 m), abyssopelagic (to about 6,000 m), and hadalpelagic (6,000–11,000 m, the trenches). Boundaries between water masses are clines: thermoclines (temperature), haloclines (salinity), chemoclines (chemistry), and pycnoclines (density). Because cold, dense deep water dominates by volume, the world ocean's average temperature is 3.9 °C. Surface temperature can exceed 30 °C in the tropics and stabilises near −2 °C where sea ice forms; deep water everywhere sits between −2 and 5 °C.

## Movement: tides and currents

Tides are driven mainly by the Moon's gravity, with the Sun and Earth's rotation modulating the pattern. A full cycle takes about 12.5 hours. When Sun and Moon align, spring tides produce the largest range; at right angles, neap tides produce the smallest. Open-ocean tides are usually under 1 m, but the Bay of Fundy and Ungava Bay reach 16 m.

Currents are continuous flows caused by wind, the Coriolis effect (the apparent deflection of moving objects caused by Earth's rotation), and density differences from temperature and salinity. Wind affects mostly the upper hundreds of metres. Below, thermohaline circulation, the deep overturning driven by dense, cold, salty water sinking near the poles, moves water that may be isolated from the surface for hundreds to thousands of years. The Atlantic branch, AMOC, carries tropical heat toward Europe. It has weakened since preindustrial times and is projected to weaken further during the 21st century.

## Chemistry: salt, gases, and acidity

Average salinity is about 34.7‰. Evaporation raises salinity, precipitation lowers it, and freezing sea ice squeezes salt out, raising residual salinity. Between 1950 and 2019, salty regions grew saltier and fresher regions fresher, with the Pacific and Southern Ocean freshening while the Atlantic became saltier.

Dissolved oxygen, carbon dioxide, and nitrogen exchange at the surface; colder water holds more of every gas. Photosynthesis in sunlit water adds oxygen and removes carbon dioxide; bacterial decay of sinking organic matter reverses both at depth. The ocean is a major carbon sink, and coastal vegetation such as mangroves and salt marshes stores especially large amounts (blue carbon). Some deep regions, oxygen minimum zones, naturally have very low oxygen, and this hypoxia is intensifying with climate change.

Surface pH is slightly alkaline, around 8.05–8.08 today, down from about 8.2 over the past 300 million years and from roughly 8.15 in 1950 to 8.05 in 2020. When seawater absorbs CO₂, it forms carbonic acid, releasing hydrogen ions and lowering pH. This is ocean acidification, distinct from alkalinity, the buffering capacity supplied mainly by carbonate and bicarbonate ions.

Elements persist in seawater for very different spans. Sodium and chloride, both highly soluble, have residence times of millions of years. Reactive, insoluble elements like iron and aluminium linger only hundreds of years. Nitrate, phosphate, and potassium have residence times of about 10,000, 69,000, and 12 million years; phosphate behaves as non-renewable on human timescales because runoff settles onto the ocean floor.

## Life and human use

Marine life includes most animal phyla, many found only in the sea: sponges, cnidarians, comb jellies, brachiopods, and echinoderms, plus fish, cephalopods, crustaceans, whales, seabirds, sea turtles, kelps, and microbes. Humans use the ocean for shipping, fishing, aquaculture, energy (tidal, wave, thermal, offshore wind), mining, drilling, and desalination. The largest commercial fisheries are anchovies, Alaska pollock, and tuna; a 2020 FAO report found 34% of stocks overfished in 2017, and fish provided about 17% of global animal protein. Navigation is governed by the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea; shipping safety by the International Maritime Organization.

## Threats and protection

Greenhouse gases warm the ocean and melt ice, expanding volume and raising sea level; during the last interglacial about 125,000 years ago, seas stood roughly 5.5 m higher than today. CO₂ lowers pH, overfishing depletes stocks, and pollution accumulates in coastal and open waters. Protection includes marine protected areas, pollution rules, sustainable seafood, and clean-up projects. The 2023 High Seas Treaty, adopted by 193 UN Member States, for the first time allows marine protected areas in international waters and supports a 30% protection target by 2030.
