# Soil

Soil is a three-phase mixture of minerals, organic matter, water, and air. Roughly half its volume is solids (about 45% mineral particles and 5% organic matter) and half is pore space, split between water and air. It supports most plant life, filters water, stores carbon, and hosts the majority of Earth's terrestrial biodiversity.

## How soil forms

Soil develops when five factors act together on weathered rock: climate, organisms, relief (topography), parent material, and time. The acronym CLORPT names this framework. Early in formation, lichens and cyanobacteria colonize broken rock, and organic matter accumulates from roots and microbes. Over time the profile differentiates into horizontal layers called horizons, labeled from the surface down: O (organic), A (topsoil), E (eluviated, where minerals have been washed out), B (subsoil, where minerals accumulate), and C (weathered parent material). The A and B together, known as the solum, hold most biological activity. Modern classification traces to Vasily Dokuchaev's morphological approach around 1880, later codified in the USDA's *Soil Taxonomy* and the World Reference Base for Soil Resources.

## Texture, structure, and density

Mineral particles are sorted by size into sand (above 0.05 mm), silt (0.002–0.05 mm), and clay (below 0.002 mm). Clay and silt particles bind with organic compounds into aggregates called peds, which give soil its structure. Texture controls how much water the soil holds, how quickly it drains, and how much air its pores contain. Typical bulk density is 1.1–1.6 g/cm³, while the particles themselves have a particle density of 2.6–2.7 g/cm³. Compaction squeezes pore space, restricting root growth and gas exchange.

## Chemical behavior

Clay minerals and humus are colloids, microscopic particles (1 nm–1 μm) with high surface area. They carry negative charges that attract and hold positively charged nutrients (cations) such as calcium, magnesium, and potassium. This storage is cation exchange capacity (CEC), measured in cmolc/kg. Soils rich in clay or organic matter have the highest CEC and the strongest buffering against pH swings. The fraction of CEC occupied by the main base cations is the base saturation, which correlates with pH: acidic soils (below pH 6.5) can release toxic aluminum and manganese, while alkaline soils (above pH 7.5) lock up micronutrients. Redox reactions also shape nutrient availability; under oxygen-poor conditions, microbes convert nitrates into nitrogen gases through denitrification.

## Life in the soil

A single gram of soil can harbor billions of organisms from thousands of species. Living biomass is roughly 70% microorganisms (bacteria and fungi), 22% larger fauna such as earthworms and insects, and 8% roots. These organisms form a food web: bacteria and fungi consume dead organic matter, protozoa and nematodes graze on them, and earthworms and arthropods break down the resulting humus. Their collective respiration returns large amounts of CO₂ to the atmosphere, while a fraction of organic carbon stabilizes as humus that persists for long periods. The same organisms drive nutrient cycling, decomposition, and the formation of soil structure.

## Water and air

Soil water passes through stages from fully saturated through field capacity, wilting point, and air-dry to oven-dry. The difference between field capacity and wilting point is the available water capacity, the moisture range plants can use. Capillary action can pull groundwater upward against gravity. Soil gas differs sharply from the atmosphere above it: root and microbial respiration deplete oxygen and concentrate CO₂, often to 10–100 times atmospheric levels. Diffusion rates depend on how open the pore network remains, which texture and moisture control. When pores fill with water and oxygen runs out, denitrification begins, releasing nitrous oxide (N₂O) and stripping the soil of nitrogen.

## Human dependence and degradation

Soil supports about 98.8% of human food production and is also essential to construction, mining, and landscaping. Its main threats are acidification from fertilizers and acid rain, contamination by heavy metals and microplastics, desertification driven by overgrazing and deforestation, erosion by water and wind, and salination when irrigation water evaporates in dry regions. Reclamation adds gypsum, organic amendments, biochar, or clay; bentonite trials in Thailand raised yields by 73%. Peatlands are especially delicate: fertile when drained, but they release greenhouse gases once disturbed, and rewetting them does not reliably cut emissions.
