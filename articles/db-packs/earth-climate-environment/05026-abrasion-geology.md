# Abrasion (geology)

Abrasion is a form of physical weathering in which a moving agent scrapes, scuffs, or rubs particles against a surface, gradually wearing it away. Unlike chemical weathering, which alters rock by reaction, abrasion is mechanical: contact plus motion removes material. The rate of wear depends on four particle properties — hardness, concentration, velocity, and mass — and on the force driving the agent, whether gravity-driven ice, flowing water, breaking waves, or wind.

## The four natural settings

The same mechanical process recurs in four environments, each defined by its transporting agent.

- Glaciers carry rock fragments frozen into the ice and drag them across the bedrock beneath, polishing the surface and leaving fine dust called rock flour. The debris that does this work comes from plucking, the other major glacial erosion process, in which ice freezes onto blocks and tears them loose. In soft rock with widely spaced joints, abrasion can match or exceed plucking in total erosion. Glacial striations scratched into polished bedrock record the direction of ice flow.
- Rivers and streams carry a bedload of larger clasts that roll, slide, and saltate (bounce) along the channel, plus a suspended load of silt, clay, and fine sand lifted into the flow. Grains polish and scour the bed and banks whenever they make abrasive contact, with the threshold velocities for movement and deposition shown on the Hjulström curve. The classic signature is a U-shaped valley cut by a former glacier, and a steepened, undercut channel where a stream still grinds.
- Coastlines experience wave-driven abrasion as breaking waves throw sand and shingle against cliffs. Hydraulic action from wave impact compounds the effect, undercutting the cliff base and eventually collapsing the overhanging rock above. The result is an abrasion platform exposed at low tide; if the platform sits permanently above the high-water mark it is a raised beach, formed by uplift or falling sea level rather than by abrasion. Coastal engineers respond with sea walls, but rising sea levels from climate change are eroding their effectiveness in many locations.
- Wind carries sand and small stones through arid and coastal landscapes, sandblasting exposed rock. The same process operates on other planets. Recent evidence suggests wind-driven abrasion can deepen bedrock canyons — landforms once attributed only to rivers — by up to an order of magnitude beyond the rate fluvial abrasion alone could produce.

## Distinguishing abrasion from attrition

The geomorphology community often uses abrasion and wear interchangeably, but a stricter definition separates two related terms.

- **Abrasion** is two surfaces rubbing against each other. One or both are worn down, producing gradual surface-level destruction over time.
- **Attrition** is particles colliding with each other and breaking into smaller pieces. It is faster and produces more total change because each impact fragments the particles themselves, generating fresh abrasive material.

The distinction matters for sediment budgets: abrasion removes material from a fixed surface such as bedrock, a cliff, or a streambed, whereas attrition reduces clast size in transit and feeds the sediment supply downstream.

## Geological signatures

Three landforms and one small-scale mark record abrasion in the field. Glaciated bedrock is smooth and polished, often scratched by parallel glacial striations that reveal the direction of ice flow. A U-shaped valley marks the cross-section of a former glacial channel. An abrasion platform on a coast is a near-horizontal bench cut into solid rock at the modern tide line. In deserts, ventifacts — rocks faceted and polished by windblown sand — are the small-scale equivalent. Each feature identifies the agent responsible and, from its orientation and extent, the path the agent travelled.
