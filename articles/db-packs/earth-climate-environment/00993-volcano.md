# Volcano

A volcano is a vent or fissure in a planet's crust where molten rock, ash, and gas escape from a subsurface reservoir called a **magma chamber** (molten or semi-molten rock beneath the surface). When this material reaches the surface it is called **lava**. The escape can be gentle or violently explosive, and the difference is driven mainly by how runny or sticky the magma is and how much dissolved gas it carries.

## Where volcanoes form

Most of Earth's volcanism tracks the movement of its tectonic plates. At **divergent boundaries**, such as mid-ocean ridges, plates pull apart and hot mantle rock rises to fill the gap. As pressure drops, the rock melts without gaining heat, a process called **decompression melting**, and produces fluid, gas-poor lava that builds broad, gently sloping **shield volcanoes** like those in Hawaii.

At **convergent boundaries** (subduction zones), an oceanic plate sinks beneath another, carrying water down with it. The water lowers the melting point of the overlying mantle wedge, a process called **flux melting**. The resulting magma is rich in silica, making it thick and gas-rich, which produces the steep, layered **stratovolcanoes** (also called composite volcanoes) such as Mount Fuji that line subduction zones like the Pacific **Ring of Fire**. These are the volcanoes responsible for the most violent eruptions.

Two other settings sit away from plate boundaries. **Hotspots** are thought to be plumes of hot mantle rising from the core-mantle boundary; as a plate drifts over a fixed plume, it leaves a chain of volcanoes, the classic example being the Hawaiian Islands. **Continental rifts** form where upwelling mantle stretches and thins continental crust, as in the East African Rift, sometimes opening the way for plate breakup.

## Shapes volcanoes take

A volcano needs three pieces: a magma chamber, a **conduit** (the pipe feeding the surface), and a **vent** (the opening). Build enough eruptive material and the resulting landform reflects how the lava behaved.

Shield volcanoes have broad, low slopes made of fluid basaltic lava, as in Hawaii. Stratovolcanoes are steep cones built of alternating lava and **tephra** (fragmental ejecta; ash is under 2 mm), with high-silica magma, as at Mount Fuji. Cinder cones are small, steep cones from explosive bursts of lava fragments, as at Parícutin in Mexico. Lava domes are bulges of slow, viscous felsic lava, like the dome that rebuilt inside Mount St. Helens after 1980. Fissure vents are long cracks erupting basaltic lava, often feeding shield volcanoes. A **caldera** is a large collapse depression left when a chamber empties, and supervolcanoes are extreme calderas whose eruptions exceed 1,000 km³, as at Yellowstone.

## How eruptions behave

Magma composition controls style. **Basaltic** lava is low in silica and flows easily, producing **effusive** eruptions. As silica rises toward felsic (above 63%), the melt becomes stickier, traps gas, and erupts explosively as Plinian or Vulcanian columns. Two other styles add water: **phreatomagmatic** explosions happen when rising magma meets groundwater, and **phreatic** explosions occur when groundwater flashes to steam with no fresh magma involved.

Erupted material is lava, tephra, and volcanic gases (steam, CO₂, SO₂). Gases can matter as much as rock: sulfur dioxide injected into the stratosphere forms aerosols that reflect sunlight and cool the climate, an effect seen after the 1991 Mount Pinatubo eruption.

## Active, dormant, or extinct

Volcanoes are classed as **active** (erupting or showing unrest such as earthquakes or gas emissions), **dormant** (quiet for now but capable of erupting again), or **extinct** (no remaining magma supply). The labels overlap and are unreliable predictors: Chile's Chaitén volcano in 2008 erupted after being thought dormant. Monitoring relies on seismic data, ground deformation, and gas measurements, and warning times before an eruption can range from days to virtually none.

## Hazards and benefits

The main hazards are **pyroclastic flows** (fast avalanches of hot gas, ash, and rock), **lahars** (volcanic mudflows), ashfall, acid rain, and **volcanic winter** from stratospheric aerosols. The 1815 Mount Tambora eruption triggered the "Year Without a Summer" and crop failures across the Northern Hemisphere. The **Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI)** runs from 0 to 8 and communicates magnitude. The 1991 Pinatubo evacuation, which saved roughly 20,000 lives, illustrates what monitoring and planning can achieve. Despite the risks, volcanic soils are exceptionally fertile and geothermal energy drawn from volcanic systems is a significant renewable resource.

## Beyond Earth

Volcanism is not unique to Earth. Venus shows vast lava plains and possible recent activity. Mars hosts enormous shield volcanoes, including Olympus Mons, the largest known. Jupiter's moon Io is the most volcanically active body in the Solar System, erupting sulfur and silicates. Icy moons such as Europa, Enceladus, and Triton instead display **cryovolcanism**, erupting water, ammonia, or methane at temperatures far below rock melting. Whether Venus and Mars remain volcanically active today is still debated, with conflicting satellite and atmospheric evidence.
