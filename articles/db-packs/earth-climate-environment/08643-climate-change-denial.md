# Climate change denial

Climate change denial is the rejection, dismissal, or false doubt of the scientific evidence and expert agreement that the global climate is warming largely because of human activity, especially the burning of fossil fuels. It is a form of *science denial* because it disputes conclusions on which researchers broadly agree. The tactic relies less on counter-argument than on rhetorical devices that create the *appearance* of an open controversy where one does not exist: cherry-picking data, citing fake or unqualified experts, exaggerating uncertainty, alleging conspiracies, and demanding impossible standards of proof.

## What is being denied

Scientific consensus, in this context, means the shared judgment of working climate researchers that warming is happening, that human greenhouse-gas emissions are the dominant cause, and that the risks are serious. Studies between 2019 and 2021 put the level of agreement at 98.7–100%. Four long-running global temperature records correlate with each other by at least 99.29% since 1880. "Skeptic" is therefore a misleading label for those who reject this consensus: scientific skepticism is the routine testing of claims, not the wholesale rejection of an established body of evidence. "Denier" is the term most commonly used in academic and journalistic description, though outlets such as the Associated Press prefer "climate change doubters" or "those who reject mainstream climate science".

Denial can be *explicit* (the science is wrong) or *implicit* (accepting the science but refusing to act on it, sometimes called soft denial). Stefan Rahmstorf grouped explicit deniers into four moving targets: trend deniers (warming is not happening), attribution deniers (it is natural, not human-caused), impact deniers (it is harmless or beneficial), and consensus deniers (scientists are not really in agreement). Michael Mann's "six stages of denial" describe how, as each claim collapses, the goalposts shift to the next one down the list. Common counter-arguments all have well-rebutted scientific responses: that CO₂ is only a trace gas (it is, but small amounts of greenhouse gases have a large warming effect when their concentration changes); that water vapor matters more (it does, but it is a fast feedback, while CO₂ is the long-lived forcing); that warming has paused (short-term fluctuations are being confused with the long-term trend); and that future technology will solve the problem without changing today's energy system.

## Who promotes it, and why

The fossil-fuel industry, especially in the United States, has run a multi-decade campaign to delay regulation. U.S. coal and oil companies were warned by their own scientists as early as the 1960s and 1970s that burning fossil fuels would warm the planet, yet publicly funded doubt for decades. A 1966 coal-industry report projected melting ice caps and coastal flooding. A 2008 study found that 92% of "environmentally skeptical" literature came from conservative think tanks, and more than 90% of skeptical papers originate from right-wing ones.

The campaign mirrors the tobacco industry's playbook. An internal tobacco-industry memo read, "Doubt is our product." The same public-relations firm (APCO) and the same front groups that earlier defended tobacco later took oil money, including the Advancement of Sound Science Center, run by Steven Milloy, which recast public-health concerns about smoking and climate as "junk science". Robert Brulle's analysis of 91 counter-movement organizations counted roughly $900 million in annual income between 2002 and 2010, much of it channelled through opaque donor funds such as Donors Trust and Donors Capital Fund. ExxonMobil, the Koch family foundations, and the American Petroleum Institute are documented as major funders; between 1998 and 2004, ExxonMobil gave $16 million to advocacy groups disputing global warming.

The denial effort is not only industrial. Surveys show climate-change denial in the U.S. maps strongly onto political identity: voters of the Republican Party are much more likely than Democrats to reject the science, and the gap has widened since the late 2010s. Denial rose in 2023 among supporters of the far right, where climate action can conflict with nationalist, free-market, or fossil-fuel-aligned worldviews. Comparable but smaller denial networks exist in the United Kingdom, Australia, Brazil under Bolsonaro, and Russia. Countries with state-run energy systems, such as France (around 80% nuclear) and Brazil (around 80% hydroelectric), show far lower public-denial rates, because domestic fossil-fuel lobbying is weaker.

Notable promoters have included Senator Jim Inhofe, who in 2015 tossed a snowball on the Senate floor to "prove" the planet was not warming; Donald Trump, who in 2012 tweeted that global warming was invented by China to hurt U.S. manufacturing and who in 2025 called climate predictions "the greatest con job ever perpetrated on the world"; the Heartland Institute, which publishes school material framing climate science as controversial; and a small circle of contrarian scientists such as Richard Lindzen, Roy Spencer, John Christy, and Willie Soon, who received more than $1.25 million from fossil-fuel interests while not always disclosing the funding.

## What it has done

Manufactured doubt has measurably shifted public opinion. Around 2017, about 40% of Americans believed climate change was a hoax, even as studies put the scientific consensus on human-caused warming at 98.7–100%. People in many countries substantially underestimate how much scientific agreement exists; Americans likewise underestimate how many of their fellow citizens support climate policy, a pattern called "pluralistic ignorance". Eight investigations of the 2009 "Climategate" affair found no evidence of fraud, but the initial leak was used to imply the opposite. During coverage of an IPCC report between August and October 2013, Fox News gave contrarian guests 69% of slots against a 97% expert consensus. By 2015, the climate-denial industry was most powerful in the U.S., and it had been a key factor in the U.S. Senate's 1997 rejection of the Kyoto Protocol.

Over time, outright "warming is a hoax" claims have grown harder to defend, and visible denial has partly shifted to softer forms: "neoskepticism" that concedes warming but stresses the costs of action, "lukewarmism" that urges cautious delay, and "adaptation only" arguments that push mitigation aside. Each still delays the rapid emissions cuts that the underlying climate science implies are needed.
