# Climate change in Japan

Japan is the world's fifth-largest carbon emitter, producing 2.6% of global greenhouse gases (GHGs). Per-person CO₂ emissions of 9.31 tonnes in 2017 are nearly double the global average. Total GHG emissions fell about 23% from 2013 levels to 1.085 billion tonnes of CO₂-equivalent in the fiscal year ending March 2023, yet fossil fuels still supply about 70% of electricity and 88% of primary energy (oil 38%, coal 27%, gas 23%). Coal alone generates over 30% of electricity, and 114 of 140 coal-fired plants are classed as old and inefficient.

Japan has pledged carbon neutrality by 2050 and a 46% emissions cut by 2030 from 2013 levels. In February 2025 it added 60% by 2035 and 73% by 2040, plus 50% renewable electricity and a nuclear share rising from under 10% to 22% by 2040. Climate Action Tracker and equivalent analyses judge these pledges insufficient: a 50% chance of holding warming below 2°C would require commitments 49% deeper, and a 50% chance of staying below 1.5°C would require 229% deeper. Offshore wind, targeted at 10 GW by 2030, is projected to reach only 4.4 GW.

Emissions concentrate in energy supply (about 433 Mt CO₂ in 2019), industry (279 Mt, dominated by iron and steel at roughly 112 Mt), and transport (199 Mt, about 20% of the national total and still almost entirely oil-powered). The industrial sector's high energy use reflects continued steel and cement production. After the 2011 Fukushima disaster shut most reactors, nuclear's share of primary energy fell from 15% in 2010 to 4% in 2019, locking in greater fossil dependence. Renewables supply about 8% of primary energy, double the 1990 share. A carbon tax of ¥289 (about US$2.63) per tonne has applied to petroleum, coal, and natural gas since 2012, and Tokyo has run a local emissions-trading system since 2010. In 2018 Japan adopted a National Plan for Adaptation covering agriculture, water, ecosystems, health, and infrastructure.

Climate impacts already visible in Japan include rising temperatures, heavier extreme rainfall, coral bleaching, disease-vector spread, and coastal flooding. Mean temperature is rising faster than the global average. Projections for 2071–2100 versus 1971–2000 show daily mean warming of 3.0°C under a low-emissions scenario (B1) or 4.2°C under a higher one (A1B), with daily maximums 3.1°C or 4.4°C higher. A worst-case pathway could bring nearly 6°C of winter warming by 2100; deep cuts would limit it to about 2°C. Annual precipitation ranges 1,000–2,500 mm, with extreme rainfall events intensifying even as totals trend downward.

Heat is the deadliest direct consequence. In summer 2018, 95,137 people required emergency care for heatstroke and 160 died, half of them over 65. With about 38% of the population projected to be over 65 by 2035, heat-related mortality could roughly double in eastern and northern regions. Sekisei Lagoon in Okinawa lost about 70% of its coral by 2017 after mass bleaching in 2015–2016 killed roughly 90% of the lagoon. Tiger mosquitoes, dengue vectors, now appear further north than historically recorded. Sea-level rise and stronger typhoons threaten southern and eastern coasts, where Honshu holds 91.8% of Japan's urban population (rising to about 95% by 2050). In 2018 torrential rains caused over 200 deaths, 2.3 million evacuations, and US$7 billion in damage; Tokyo flood depth could grow 170% by 2050, with real-estate and infrastructure damage rising 220–240%. Typhoons Faxai and Hagibis in 2019 cut power to 10 million households.

Agriculture shows the same pressure: rice yields fall in hot years because of direct temperature sensitivity, and crop zones are shifting uphill. Water resources are tightening, with shrinking snowpack reducing dry-season river flow, while heavier runoff increases soil erosion and flood risk. By 2060 outdoor air pollution could cause 779 deaths per million people annually, up from 468 in 2010, and climate change could shrink Japan's labour force by 0.88% under low emissions or 2.2% under medium emissions.
