# Earth science

Earth science, also called geoscience, is the study of how Earth's physical, chemical, and biological systems work and interact. Practitioners divide the planet into four "spheres": the lithosphere (rock), hydrosphere (water), atmosphere (air), and biosphere (life), with some adding a cryosphere (frozen water) and pedosphere (soil). Nearly every phenomenon on the planet, from earthquakes to climate, is the product of exchanges between these spheres. The same physics extends to other rocky worlds, so the field generalises into planetary science.

## Earth's restless interior

Earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain building, and the drift of continents all come from processes deep inside the planet. Beneath the crust lies the mantle, heated partly by the radioactive decay of heavy elements. The mantle is not fully solid; rock there behaves as a very viscous fluid, convecting in slow loops over geologic time. This convection drags the cooler, rigid lithosphere above it, fracturing it into plates that move, collide, and slide.

The theory that describes this is plate tectonics, and three boundary types explain most of the action. At divergent boundaries, plates pull apart and magma rises to create new crust, a process called seafloor spreading. At convergent boundaries, one plate dives beneath another, returning crust into the mantle through subduction. At transform boundaries, plates grind sideways past each other with no new crust made or destroyed. Earthquakes cluster along these boundaries, especially where subduction forces rock downward. Volcanoes form largely when subducted crust descends into the asthenosphere, the hot, ductile layer just below the lithosphere, partially melts, and the lighter magma rises. Tectonics is a recycling system: new crust forms, old crust sinks, and the surface is continuously resurfaced over hundreds of millions of years.

## The atmosphere

Earth's atmosphere is a thin gas envelope organised into five layers from the surface upward: troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere, and exosphere, reaching roughly 1000 km altitude. The lowest layer, the troposphere, holds about 75% of the atmosphere's mass and is where weather occurs. The air is roughly 78% nitrogen, 20.9% oxygen, 0.92% argon, with trace carbon dioxide and variable water vapor.

Two properties of this envelope are essential. First, water vapor and CO2 absorb outgoing infrared radiation and reradiate some of it back toward the surface. This is the greenhouse effect, and without it the surface would be too cold for liquid water or life. Second, Earth's magnetic field, generated by motions in the liquid outer core, produces the magnetosphere, which deflects the solar wind. Earth is roughly 4.5 billion years old; without this shield it would long since have lost its atmosphere to that wind.

Atmospheric science began in the late 19th century as meteorology for weather forecasting, expanded in the 20th century into atmospheric chemistry to measure pollution, and broadened further in the 1970s when acid rain became a public concern. Climatology studies climate and its long-term change.

## Water, ice, and life

Hydrology covers all water on the planet but focuses most on freshwater because of human use. Applied hydrology treats water as engineering: dams, aqueducts, flood control, and supply. Subdisciplines track different parts of the cycle. Oceanography studies the oceans. Hydrogeology maps groundwater, traces contaminants, and supplies drinking water; ecohydrology examines the mutual effects of water and living systems. Glaciology studies ice: glaciers, ice sheets, and snow cover, all of which act as frozen reservoirs and are sensitive to climate change.

Ecology is the biological wing of Earth science. It studies how organisms draw resources (oxygen, water, nutrients) from the other spheres, how species interact, and how human activity reshapes natural systems. Earth is the only known life-bearing planet in the Solar System, so the biosphere is treated as a defining feature of our world rather than a routine planetary ingredient.

## Branches and methods

Earth science subdivides into fields that mostly pick one sphere or one method. Geology focuses on the lithosphere: mineralogy and petrology classify minerals and rocks; geomorphology studies how landscapes form; structural geology analyses rock deformation; paleontology and stratigraphy read the fossil and layer record of Earth's past; environmental geology examines how pollution alters soil and rock. Geophysics and geodesy measure physical properties such as gravity, magnetism, and seismic waves to infer what lies beneath the surface and to locate minerals, petroleum, and earthquake risk. Geochemistry tracks the abundance and cycling of elements and isotopes. Soil science treats the pedosphere. Physical geography integrates all spheres at the surface, drawing on climate science, geomorphology, biogeography, and soil studies, and is distinguished from human geography by its focus on physical systems.

Methodologically, Earth science is observational, experimental, and theoretical. Researchers combine fieldwork in places like Antarctica or volcanic hot-spot chains with laboratory work and numerical simulations. A guiding principle is uniformitarianism: the slow processes visible today, including erosion, sedimentation, volcanism, and plate motion, have operated throughout geologic time, so reading them in the present lets us reconstruct the deep past.
