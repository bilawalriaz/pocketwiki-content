# Climate change in Antarctica

Antarctica is warming and losing ice overall, while its surrounding Southern Ocean absorbs most of the excess heat and reshapes global climate. The continent's responses vary by region, are amplified by feedback loops, and unfold on timescales from years to millennia.

## Regional warming is uneven

Antarctica does not warm uniformly. The Antarctic Peninsula has warmed about 3 °C since the mid-20th century, one of the fastest rates on Earth. West Antarctica warmed by more than 0.1 °C per decade from the 1950s through the 2000s; a 2022 analysis narrowed the central West Antarctic ice sheet's warming to 0.31 °C per decade between 1959 and 2000 and attributed it to human-caused greenhouse gases. East Antarctica, colder and more stable, showed no clear warming until the 2000s, when the South Pole began warming at roughly three times the global average.

Early data were sparse, with fewer than twenty permanent weather stations continent-wide and only two inland. Several early-2000s studies therefore reported net cooling, and a 2002 paper led by Peter Doran found strong cooling in the McMurdo Dry Valleys, but its data was limited and the paper still found warming across 42% of the continent. Media outlets and Michael Crichton's 2004 novel *State of Fear* misrepresented the work as contradicting global warming. By 2009, combined historical and satellite records showed continent-wide warming above 0.05 °C per decade since 1957.

## Ice loss, shelf collapse, and instability

Ice shelves are floating platforms that buttress land-bound glaciers; once they weaken or collapse, the glaciers behind them accelerate toward the sea. Around West Antarctica, ocean warming of about 1 °C since 1955 has driven shelf thinning and glacier retreat. From 1992 to 2017, Antarctica lost roughly 2,720 ± 1,390 gigatonnes of ice, averaging about 109 gigatonnes per year and contributing about 7.6 mm to global sea level. East Antarctica's interior has continued to gain mass in some periods because warmer air holds more moisture, and increased snowfall can offset melt at high elevation.

Two physical mechanisms threaten larger losses. Marine ice sheet instability lets warm ocean water slip beneath an ice sheet once the ice thins enough to lose contact with the seafloor. Marine ice cliff instability can cause ice cliffs taller than 100 m to collapse under their own weight once buttressing shelves disappear; this process has not been observed and appears only in some models.

The West Antarctic ice sheet, grounded deep below sea level, is highly vulnerable. It likely collapsed during the Eemian period about 125,000 years ago at temperatures similar to today. A full loss now would raise sea level by about 3.3 m, or 4.3 m if mountain ice caps also melt, and reversing it would require lowering global temperature to 2 °C below 2020 levels. Full loss would take 500 to 13,000 years. East Antarctica is far more stable, but vulnerable subglacial basins like Wilkes and Aurora could collapse over roughly 2,000 years at about 3 °C of warming, adding 1.4 to 6.4 m.

## Sea level projections

By 2100, net Antarctic ice loss is expected to add roughly 11 cm to global sea level under central estimates. Adding the two instabilities raises that to 41 cm under low emissions and 57 cm under high emissions, with some estimates reaching or exceeding 2 m if high warming persists. Under the Paris Agreement's 2 °C goal, Antarctic loss continues at today's rate through the century; at 3 °C, loss accelerates after 2060 and adds 0.5 cm per year by 2100.

## The Southern Ocean and overturning circulation

The Southern Ocean absorbs more heat and carbon dioxide than any other ocean; between 1971 and 2018, over 90% of global heating's thermal energy entered the oceans, and after 2005 the Southern Ocean alone took in 67% to 98% of it. The Southern Ocean overturning circulation (SOOC) is the southern half of the global thermohaline system, the ocean "conveyor belt" driven by differences in water temperature and salinity. It has two cells: a smaller upper cell driven by winds and precipitation, and a larger lower cell defined by cold, salty Antarctic bottom water that sinks and spreads northward. Since the 1970s, the upper cell has strengthened by 50% to 60% while the lower cell weakened by 10% to 20%, partly because fresh meltwater from West Antarctica dilutes the dense bottom water.

If global warming reaches 1.7 to 3 °C, some research suggests the SOOC could collapse, though the estimate is less certain than for most other climate tipping points, and any collapse unfolds over centuries. Likely consequences include less rainfall in the Southern Hemisphere, more in the Northern, declining Southern Ocean fisheries, and disruption of marine ecosystems.

## Ecosystems

Marine life dominates Antarctic biodiversity; by 2015, 8,354 species had been formally described, only 57 of them non-marine. Antarctic krill, the keystone of the food web, have declined in parts of the Southwest Atlantic since the 1970s, slowing the recovery of baleen whales from past whaling. Under 3 °C of warming, Antarctic species diversity may fall by about 17% and suitable climate area by roughly half.

Penguins illustrate both winners and losers. Gentoo penguins, ice-intolerant and moss-nesting, have spread and grown more numerous. Adélie, chinstrap, emperor, and king penguins are declining; king penguins lose about 9% of their population for every 0.26 °C of Southern Ocean warming, and without mitigation roughly 80% of emperor penguin colonies are projected to be at risk of extinction by 2100, falling to 31% under a 2 °C goal and 19% under 1.5 °C.

On land, Antarctica has only two native vascular plants, hairgrass and pearlwort, plus lichens, mosses, and algae in coastal areas. Warming has boosted photosynthesis and range, but drying has turned green mosses red or brown in some East Antarctic sites. Human activity adds pressure: each additional person at a research station disturbs on average an area close to 1,000 football fields, and 16 non-native species are considered capable of establishing themselves soon.

## Contaminants from thawing permafrost

Antarctic permafrost is far less extensive than the Arctic's, but stores persistent organic pollutants such as DDT, hexachlorobenzene, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, along with heavy metals including mercury, lead, and cadmium. Thawing releases them into surface water, where bioaccumulation and biomagnification spread them through the food web.
