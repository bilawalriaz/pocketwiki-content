# Forest

A forest is a tree-dominated terrestrial ecosystem covering roughly 4.14 billion hectares, about 31 percent of Earth's land area. Forests are the largest terrestrial ecosystem by area, hold most of the planet's plant biomass, and produce about 75 percent of terrestrial gross primary production, the total carbon fixed by photosynthesis.

Definitions of forest vary. The FAO defines it as land over 0.5 hectares with trees taller than 5 meters and canopy cover above 10 percent, excluding agricultural and urban land. Other definitions emphasize legal designation (administrative) or primary use (land-use). These differences matter: a logged area may still count as forest under one definition and not another, which complicates global accounting.

Forests are classified by biome: boreal, temperate, tropical, and subtropical, distinguished by latitude, precipitation, and temperature. They are further grouped by leaf longevity (evergreen or deciduous), leaf type (broadleaf, coniferous, mixed), and disturbance history (old-growth versus second-growth). Old-growth forests retain natural biodiversity patterns with minimal human alteration. Secondary forests regrow after harvest or other major disturbance and may contain non-native species. The UNEP-WCMC system consolidates these into six categories: temperate needleleaf, temperate broadleaf and mixed, tropical moist, tropical dry, sparse trees and savanna, and plantations.

Vertical structure divides a forest into layers. The forest floor holds decomposing litter, detritivores (organisms that break down dead material), and mycorrhizal networks, the symbiotic fungal threads linking tree roots and moving carbon and nutrients between plants. The understory contains shade-tolerant shrubs and young trees. The canopy, where mature crowns interlock, is the zone of peak photosynthesis. In tropical rainforests, an emergent layer of scattered giant trees rises above the canopy. This three-dimensional complexity increases with lower disturbance and higher tree diversity.

Biodiversity concentrates in tropical moist forests such as the Amazon, Congo Basin, and Southeast Asian dipterocarp forests, and in montane regions. Temperate forests host fewer species, but each species tends to occupy a wider range. Human population density and agricultural expansion correlate strongly with biodiversity loss.

Forests store large amounts of carbon. Living biomass holds about 606 gigatonnes (Gt) and dead wood about 59 Gt; soil carbon adds another large fraction. Roughly 46 percent of forest carbon sits in soil, 44 percent in living biomass, and 10 percent in litter and dead wood. Forests also recycle water. In the Amazon, evapotranspiration from trees triggers the wet season two to three months early; if this recycling weakens, rainfall across the region falls.

Global forest area has been declining for millennia, though the rate has slowed. From1990 to 2020, net loss averaged about 7.8 million hectares per year in the 1990s, dropping to 5.2 million in the 2000s and 4.7 million in the 2010s. Gross deforestation was estimated at 10 million hectares per year from 2015 to 2020. The top five forested countries (Russia, Brazil, Canada, the United States, and China) hold 54 percent of the world's forest area; Europe holds the largest regional share at 25 percent. The main drivers of loss are commodity production: wood, beef, soy, and palm oil, especially in the tropics.

Several regions have undergone a forest transition, a shift from net loss to net gain, driven by commercial plantations, agroforestry, or natural regeneration on abandoned farmland. China banned logging in 1998 after erosion and flooding, and subsequent reforestation has contributed to the global slowdown. Protected forest area reached about 813 million hectares in 2025, roughly 20 percent of the total.

Forests supply ecosystem services: carbon sequestration, water purification, flood mitigation, timber, fuelwood, food, and recreation. About 22 percent of India's population depends on forests for subsistence. Indigenous communities manage lands with higher biodiversity intactness and lower deforestation than comparable lands without tenure recognition, though corporate land acquisition in lower-income countries often proceeds without meaningful consultation.

Sustainable forest management integrates timber harvest, biodiversity protection, and community needs. Logging, urban expansion, fire, invasive species, and acid deposition are the main pressures. Natural disturbances include fire, insects, disease, and weather. Plantations, even-aged stands of a single species grown for timber or pulp, expand forest area but generally support less biodiversity and store less carbon than old growth, so their weight in global targets remains contested.

Whether forests remain net carbon sinks depends on how disturbance, area, and diversity change. Some models project parts of the tropics becoming net carbon sources by the 2060s, and the Amazon may cross a threshold where rainfall recycling collapses and forest transitions to savanna. The 1.5 °C climate pathway requires adding forest area roughly the size of Canada by 2050.
