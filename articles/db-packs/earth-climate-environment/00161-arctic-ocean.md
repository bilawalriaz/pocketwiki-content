# Arctic Ocean

The Arctic Ocean is the smallest and shallowest of the world's five oceans, a roughly circular basin of about 14,060,000 km² centred on the North Pole. It is the coldest ocean, mostly covered by sea ice year-round, and the least salty, because evaporation is low, large rivers (Ob, Yenisei, Lena, Mackenzie) deliver freshwater, and its connection to saltier oceans is narrow. Its sea ice reflects sunlight that would otherwise warm the planet; its cold fresh surface waters and dense deep waters drive thermohaline circulation (currents driven by temperature and salinity differences), redistributing heat worldwide; and the central basin may hold a quarter or more of the world's undiscovered oil and gas.

## Shape, depth, and origin

The basin averages only about 1,038 m deep because much of it sits on continental shelves of Eurasia, North America, Greenland, and Iceland. The deepest point is Molloy Hole in the Fram Strait at about 5,550 m. The Lomonosov Ridge splits the deep basin into the Eurasian Basin (4,000–4,500 m) and the Amerasian Basin (about 4,000 m). The Siberian Shelf is the largest continental shelf on Earth and holds large oil and gas reserves.

Jurassic–Triassic subsidence created the rock formations that hold oil and gas; later rifting of Pangea opened the Arctic, and the Gakkel Ridge began spreading in the Paleocene–Eocene, rifting the Lomonosov Ridge away from Eurasia. Seafloor geology remains poorly explored due to ice and remoteness.

## Water structure and circulation

The top roughly 50 m is colder and fresher than the water below, fed by river inflow and meltwater, and floats on denser saltier water. Below it a halocline (a layer where salinity and temperature rise with depth) insulates the sea ice from warmer Atlantic water beneath.

Three water masses fill the ocean. Arctic Bottom Water, below 900 m, is the densest water in the world ocean, formed when cold salty shelf water sinks and mixes with Greenland Sea Deep Water. Atlantic Water, the warmest layer, sits between 150 and 900 m and is the largest inflow, entering through the Fram Strait at roughly ten times the Pacific inflow, which passes through the Bering Strait at about 0.8 Sv. Arctic Surface Water occupies the top 150–200 m. Outflow leaves mainly through the Fram Strait at about 9 Sv, deflected by the Coriolis force (rightward deflection in the Northern Hemisphere caused by Earth's rotation). Measurements from 2016–2018 show that ocean heat flux has overtaken atmospheric flux in the eastern Eurasian Basin, the halocline is weakening, and vertical mixing is increasing, so the insulating lid between ice and warm water is thinning.

## Ice and climate

Sea ice follows a strong seasonal cycle. Winter extent averages about 15,600,000 km²; summer extent is roughly 7,000,000 km² smaller. Maximum extent occurs in April, minimum in September, and the ocean is essentially ice-locked from October to June. Since 1980, ice extent has shrunk by about 12.85% per decade, reaching a record low in September 2012 of 49% below the 1979–2000 average. Surface water sits near −1.8 °C year-round. Winters bring polar night (continuous darkness); summers bring midnight sun (continuous daylight) with temperatures just above 0 °C. The Arctic is warming about twice as fast as the global average.

## Life and resources

Primary production is restricted to the spring and summer window of midnight sun. Ice algae and phytoplankton feed copepods and amphipods. The Barents Sea hosts herring, young cod, and capelin; the ice-covered central Arctic is dominated by polar cod, which feeds seals, whales, and polar bears. Walruses and several whale species are endangered.

The central Arctic may hold a quarter or more of the world's undiscovered oil and gas, and the Lomonosov Ridge is estimated to contain up to 10 billion barrels of oil.

## Human history

People crossed the Bering land bridge from Siberia to Alaska 17,000–50,000 years ago during the Wisconsin glaciation. Successive Paleo-Eskimo cultures (Pre-Dorset, Saqqaq, Independence I and II, Dorset) occupied the region before the Thule Tradition (c. 200 BC–AD 1600) migrated east from Alaska and gave rise to the modern Inuit.

European knowledge was largely speculative until modern times. In 325 BC, Pytheas of Massalia described "a congealed substance" on the sea, almost certainly loose sea ice. Cartographers long argued whether the pole was land or sea, and the myth of an "Open Polar Sea" persisted into the late 19th century before thick year-round ice disproved it. Key crossings fixed the true geography: Fridtjof Nansen's drift on the Fram (1893–1896), the USS Nautilus submarine transit (1958), Wally Herbert's dog-sled crossing from Alaska to Svalbard (1969), and the NS Arktika surface ship transit (1977). In July 2015, five nations signed a declaration to bar fishing vessels from a 1.1 million square mile central zone until science could guide management.

## Why the changes matter

Loss of bright sea ice lowers the surface albedo, so the ocean absorbs more sunlight and warms further, melting more ice. Fresh meltwater pouring into the North Atlantic can weaken the density-driven currents that redistribute heat globally. Warming can destabilise methane clathrates, frozen methane deposits that release a greenhouse gas far more potent than carbon dioxide over short timescales. Reduced ice has pushed polar bears into settlements, prompting a state of emergency on Novaya Zemlya in December 2018–February 2019.
