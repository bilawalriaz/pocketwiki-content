# Biomass (ecology)

Biomass is the total mass of living organisms in a given area or ecosystem at one point in time, whether counted for a single species, a community, or all life together. It can be reported as wet weight, dry weight, or, most commonly in ecology, the mass of organic carbon. The 2018 Bar-On estimate puts Earth's total live biomass at about **550 billion tonnes of carbon (Gt C)**, the overwhelming majority stored in plants on land.

A key distinction separates **standing stock** (mass present at one moment) from **net primary production** (new biomass created per year). These move independently: oceanic primary producers generate nearly half of global new biomass each year yet hold only about 0.2% of total standing stock, because marine producers are tiny, fast-growing cells consumed almost as quickly as they appear. Terrestrial producers are mostly large, slow-growing plants that accumulate carbon over decades.

**Biomass pyramids** show mass distribution across a food chain. Only about 10% of the energy entering one trophic level is converted into biomass at the next; the rest is lost to metabolism and heat. On land this produces a right-side-up pyramid: grassland has enormous grass biomass, modest grasshopper and vole mass, and far less hawk and wolf mass. In the ocean the pyramid is often inverted, with more consumer mass (krill, forage fish) than producer mass, because phytoplankton turn over so quickly that a small standing stock can support a larger one. Energy loss also caps food chains at roughly six levels.

**Kingdom breakdown of Earth's ~550 Gt C** (Bar-On 2018, with bacteria+archaea updated):

| Kingdom | Billion tonnes C |
|---|---|
| Plants | 450 |
| Bacteria + Archaea | 30 |
| Fungi | 12 |
| Protists | 4 |
| Animals | 2 |

Plants therefore hold about 82% of all living carbon, and the combined biomass of every animal is under 0.5%. Land plants outweigh land animals by roughly a thousand to one, and about 18% of that plant mass is eaten by animals; in the ocean, animals outweigh the autotrophs they consume. Roughly 70% of all prokaryotic biomass sits underground, in deep sediments and aquifers, a number revised sharply downward in the 2010s once better measurements arrived (an earlier 1998 estimate had placed prokaryotes near the mass of all plants).

**Animals** (about 2 Gt C total) are dominated by marine arthropods, especially copepods (~1 Gt C) and fish (~0.7 Gt C); about half of all fish biomass is mesopelagic lanternfish that spend the day in deep, dark water. On land the most striking figure is that humans and their livestock make up about 96% of all mammalian biomass, with wild mammals only 4%. The smallest but most numerous marine primary producer, the cyanobacterium *Prochlorococcus* (0.5–0.8 µm across), may be the most plentiful species on Earth at roughly 10²⁷ individuals, and is thought to generate about 20% of atmospheric oxygen.

**Global net primary production** runs at about **105 Gt C per year**, split nearly evenly between land (56.4) and ocean (48.5). Productivity per unit area tells a different story: swamps and marshes, tropical rainforests, and coral reefs each fix around 2,000 gC/m²/yr, while open ocean fixes only about 125, and deserts almost nothing. Shallow aquatic systems (wetlands, estuaries, coral reefs) can therefore be as productive per hectare as forests, even though open ocean dominates total production through sheer area.

By 2020 the mass of human-made materials (technomass) was projected to exceed all living biomass, with plastic alone heavier than every land and marine animal combined.
