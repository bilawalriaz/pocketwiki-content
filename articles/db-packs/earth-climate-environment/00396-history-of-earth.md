# History of Earth

Earth's 4.54-billion-year history is a sequence of planetary cooling, atmospheric oxygenation, and increasing biological complexity, punctuated by mass extinctions that reset evolution. The same causal chain links Earth's structure, atmosphere, and biosphere.

## A condensed timeline

| Date | Event |
| :--- | :--- |
| 4.54 Ga | Earth forms from the solar nebula by accretion. |

| 251.9 Ma | Permian–Triassic extinction ("Great Dying") ends the Paleozoic. |
| 66 Ma | Cretaceous–Paleogene asteroid impact ends the Mesozoic and the non-avian dinosaurs. |

| 8500–7000 BC | Agriculture begins in the Fertile Crescent. |

## Formation and the Hadean

Earth grew from the solar nebula, a rotating cloud of gas and dust, by accretion, the gravitational accumulation of matter into a planet. Heat from impacts and radioactive decay melted the interior, letting dense iron sink to form the core (the iron catastrophe) while lighter silicates rose into the mantle. A Mars-sized body, Theia, then struck the young Earth; the ejected debris reassembled into the Moon between 4.53 and 4.48 Ga. The Hadean eon that followed was dominated by volcanism and meteorite bombardment, yet liquid water likely existed by 4.4 Ga, a prerequisite for the life that would follow.

## First life and the Archean

The earliest undisputed fossils, stromatolites (layered mats built by microbes, usually cyanobacteria), appear around 3.5 Ga. How non-living chemicals first produced life is still debated, with leading hypotheses including an "RNA world" of self-replicating molecules, a "metabolism-first" origin at hydrothermal vents, and a role for lipid membranes or mineral surfaces. All modern organisms descend from a last universal common ancestor (LUCA), probably a prokaryote (a single-celled organism without a nucleus), in place by 3.5 Ga.

## The Proterozoic and the oxygen revolution

During the Proterozoic eon (2.5–0.5388 Ga), two transformations reshaped the planet. First, eukaryotic cells (cells with a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles) evolved, likely through endosymbiosis, in which one cell engulfed another and the two became permanent partners, producing mitochondria and later chloroplasts. Second, photosynthetic cyanobacteria released oxygen as a waste gas. At first this oxygen reacted with dissolved iron, creating the banded iron formations still mined today, and only later accumulated in the atmosphere around 2.4 Ga. The result was an ozone layer that protected land life from ultraviolet radiation, but it was also toxic to most existing anaerobic organisms, an "oxygen catastrophe." The same period saw severe "Snowball Earth" glaciations, when ice may have covered the globe, possibly triggered by falling greenhouse gas levels.

## Phanerozoic: explosion, colonization, and collapse

The Phanerozoic eon began 538.8 million years ago with the Cambrian Explosion, a rapid diversification that produced most modern animal body plans. Life then invaded the land: plants and fungi by 480–460 Ma, arthropods by about 450 Ma, and tetrapods (four-limbed vertebrates) by 380–375 Ma. This lineage split into amphibians and amniotes (reptiles, birds, and mammals).

Two mass extinctions bracketed the Mesozoic. The Permian–Triassic extinction at 251.9 Ma, the largest in Earth's history, killed roughly 83% of genera and ended the Paleozoic. The Cretaceous–Paleogene impact 66 Ma later killed 75% of species, including the non-avian dinosaurs, allowing mammals to diversify in the Cenozoic, the "Age of Mammals," as grasslands expanded and Pleistocene ice ages reshaped migrations. *Homo sapiens* appeared about 200,000 years ago in Africa, and agriculture in the Fertile Crescent around 8500–7000 BC launched civilizations that now alter the planet on a geological scale, prompting a debated proposal to name a new "Anthropocene" epoch.

## What the timeline teaches

Three patterns recur. Planetary change and biological change are coupled: the Moon-stabilized tilt, the oxygenated atmosphere, and the breakup of supercontinents each altered the environment in which evolution unfolded. Oxygen was both a poison and a prerequisite, killing much of the existing biosphere while enabling larger, more energetic cells. Mass extinctions, whether triggered by asteroid impacts, volcanic eruptions, or climate shifts, repeatedly pruned diversity and allowed surviving lineages to expand into emptied niches, which is why mammals, not dinosaurs, dominate land vertebrates today.
