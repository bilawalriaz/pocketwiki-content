# Geology

Geology is the natural science of the Earth and other astronomical bodies, their rocky composition, and the processes that change them over time. The name comes from Ancient Greek *gê* ("earth") and *-logía* ("study of"). Geology describes Earth's structure, surface and interior, and the processes shaping it. It overlaps with hydrology, integrates with Earth system and planetary science, and is applied to mineral and hydrocarbon exploration, water-resource evaluation, natural-hazard assessment, environmental remediation, and reconstruction of past climate.

## Geological material

Most geological data come from solid Earth materials. **Minerals** are naturally occurring elements and compounds with definite chemical composition and ordered atomic arrangement; amorphous equivalents are mineraloids. They are identified by color, streak, hardness, fracture versus cleavage, luster, specific gravity, effervescence in hydrochloric acid, magnetism, and taste. A **rock** is any solid mass or aggregate of minerals or mineraloids. Three major types exist: igneous (solidified from melt), sedimentary (weathered, eroded, redeposited, lithified), and metamorphic (altered by heat and pressure), linked by the rock cycle. Sedimentary rocks divide into sandstone, shale, carbonate, and evaporite; coal, oil, and gas occur mainly in organic-rich sedimentary rocks. Unlithified deposits above bedrock form Quaternary geology.

## Whole-Earth structure

In the 1960s geologists discovered that Earth's **lithosphere** (crust and rigid uppermost mantle) divides into **tectonic plates** moving over the plastically deforming **asthenosphere**, the ductile solid upper mantle. Evidence includes seafloor spreading and the distribution of mountain belts and seismicity. Plate motion couples to mantle convection: oceanic lithosphere is the rigid upper thermal boundary layer of the convecting mantle. This "grand unifying theory" explains mid-ocean ridges (divergent boundaries), volcanic and seismic arcs (convergent boundaries with subduction), and transform boundaries such as the San Andreas Fault, and supplied a mechanism for Wegener's continental drift.

Seismology, computer modeling, and high-pressure mineralogy revealed a liquid outer core, where shear waves cannot propagate, and a dense solid inner core, giving a layered model: lithosphere, mantle (with discontinuities at 410 and 660 km), outer core, inner core. Since the 1970s, seismic full-waveform inversion has produced CT-scan-like images, replacing this simple picture with a more dynamic one.

## Geological time

The geological time scale begins with Solar System material at 4.567 Ga and Earth's formation at 4.54 Ga (Hadean start) and runs to the present Holocene. Key milestones: photosynthesis c. 3.5 Ga; transition from stagnant lid to plate tectonics 3.2–2.3 Ga; supercontinents including Rodinia (1,100–750 Ma) and Pangaea (300–180 Ma); second snowball Earth 730–635 Ma; first vertebrate land animals c. 380 Ma; Himalayas forming 45–35 Ma; first *Australopithecus* 3.9 Ma. Lunar epochs are named after defining impacts (e.g., Imbrian for Mare Imbrium).

## Dating methods

**Relative dating** applies uniformitarianism (Hutton's "the present is the key to the past"), intrusive relationships, cross-cutting relationships, inclusions being older than their host, original horizontality, superposition, and faunal succession, which William Smith laid out nearly a century before Darwin. **Absolute dating** uses radioactive isotopes, measuring ratios in minerals to find the time since the rock passed its **closure temperature**, when isotopes stop diffusing. Common systems: uranium–lead, rubidium–strontium, potassium–argon, and uranium–thorium for calcium carbonate. Lava and volcanic ash calibrate relative timescales. Optically stimulated luminescence, cosmogenic radionuclide dating, dendrochronology, and radiocarbon dating extend coverage to surfaces, erosion rates, and young organic materials.

## Geological development of an area

Rock units are emplaced by deposition or intrusion. Deformation arises from shortening, extension, or strike-slip at convergent, divergent, and transform boundaries. Compression shortens and thickens rock through thrust faulting and folding (antiforms up, synforms down). Higher pressures cause metamorphism, producing foliation. Extension lengthens and thins units through normal faulting and ductile stretching, which can form boudins (lens-shaped pinches). Strike-slip faults at the surface become shear zones at depth. Deformation often pairs with volcanism and dike swarms, and may occur in stages, as at the Grand Canyon, where lower units were metamorphosed and deformed before undeformed upper units were deposited.

## Investigative methods

Geologists combine field, laboratory, and numerical work. Fieldwork includes mapping, surveying, erosion and deposition studies, subsurface mapping via shallow seismic surveys, ground-penetrating radar, aeromagnetic surveys, and electrical resistivity tomography, plus well drilling and logging, biogeochemistry, paleontology, and glaciology. In the lab, petrologists use optical microscopy to identify minerals by birefringence, pleochroism, and twinning, and electron microprobes for chemistry. Structural geologists use oriented thin sections, plot structures on stereonets (stereographic projections used to display fault and fold orientations), and run analog and numerical experiments on orogenic wedges (mountain-building zones at convergent boundaries). Stratigraphers analyze drill cores and well logs; biostratigraphers date cores with fossils; magnetic stratigraphers track reversals; stable-isotope studies reconstruct past climate.

## Planetary and applied geology

Planetary geology (astrogeology) applies Earth's principles to terrestrial planets, icy moons, asteroids, comets, and meteorites, with some work on giant planets and exoplanets. Subfields include selenology (Moon), areology (Mars), and hermesology (Mercury). A major focus is searching for past or present life; the Phoenix lander analyzed Martian polar soil for water and mineral traces of biological processes.

Applied subfields include economic geology (profitably extracted minerals: petroleum, coal, iron, copper, uranium), mining geology, petroleum geology in sedimentary basins, engineering geology for safe construction, hydrogeology for groundwater and contaminant tracking, and paleoclimatology using ice and sediment cores, the main source of climate information beyond the instrumental record. Hazard geology supports building codes and warning systems.

## History

Early observations appear in ancient Greece: Theophrastus wrote *Peri Lithon* (372–287 BCE); Aristotle noted geological slowness. Al-Biruni (973–1048) and Ibn Sina (981–1037) explained mountains and earthquakes; Shen Kuo (1031–1095) inferred land formation from fossil shells. Agricola's 1546 *De Natura Fossilium* founded geology as a discipline. Steno (1638–1686) formulated superposition, original horizontality, and lateral continuity. Smith drew early geological maps ordering strata by fossils. Hutton's 1785 *Theory of the Earth* argued for a far older Earth; his Plutonists opposed Werner's Neptunists. Lyell's 1830 *Principles of Geology* promoted uniformitarianism against catastrophism. By the early 20th century radiometric dating gave an age near two billion years; plate tectonics in the 1960s was the century's key advance, and Earth is now known to be approximately 4.5 billion years old.

## Key terms

- **Lithosphere**: crust and rigid uppermost mantle, divided into tectonic plates.
- **Asthenosphere**: plastically deforming solid upper mantle beneath the plates.
- **Uniformitarianism**: present processes explain past geology.
- **Closure temperature**: the temperature at which radiometric isotopes stop diffusing, starting the radiometric clock.
- **Metamorphism**: mineralogical and textural change of rock under high pressure and temperature.
- **Boudins**: lens-shaped pinches from ductile stretching.
- **Stereonet**: stereographic projection used to plot structures.
- **Orogenic wedge**: mountain-building zone at a convergent plate boundary.
