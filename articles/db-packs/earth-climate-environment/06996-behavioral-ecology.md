# Behavioral ecology

Behavioral ecology studies the evolutionary basis of animal behavior under ecological pressure. The field grew from ethology after Niko Tinbergen proposed four questions for any behavior: its proximate causes, its development (ontogeny), its survival value, and its evolutionary history (phylogeny). A behavior persists when it is adaptive, raising gene copies passed to future generations, and disappears when it is maladaptive.

## Competition, strategies, and the ESS

Resources (food, territory, mates) are always limited, so individuals are in conflict with rivals, predators, mates, and offspring. When a population settles into a stable mix of tactics, no alternative tactic can invade. John Maynard Smith (1982) borrowed the Nash equilibrium from game theory to name this outcome the evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS). Because the payoff to any tactic depends on how common the others are, behavior is frequency-dependent, shaped by both the physical environment and social interactions. In three-spined sticklebacks, food dropped twice as fast at one end of a tank pulls six fish into a 4:2 split, equalising the average feeding rate.

A territory is worth defending only when the energetic benefit exceeds the cost (Jerram Brown, 1964). Golden-winged sunbirds defend flowers only when doing so yields a net energetic profit. The ideal free distribution formalises habitat choice: informed, free-moving individuals fill high-quality patches until crowding drops the per-capita benefit to that of lower-quality patches, then alternate between them.

## Mating, sexual selection, and sexual conflict

Female reproductive success is usually limited by access to resources needed to raise young, so females tend to be choosy. Male success is limited by access to females, so males compete for mates or for resources females need. When females choose for resources (nest sites, food, protection, as in sedge warblers), males fight over those resources. When males offer only sperm, females choose on genetic quality, and three hypotheses dominate: the good-genes view, including Zahavi's handicap principle (costly ornaments signal survival ability) and Hamilton and Zuk's parasite-resistance idea; Fisher's runaway selection, where preference and trait co-evolve until the trait signals only attractiveness; and sensory bias, where a pre-existing perceptual preference (guppies attracted to orange objects, originally useful for finding rare fruit) is exploited by mates (male guppies with large orange spots).

Males and females do not always want the same outcome. Sexual conflict arises whenever their preferred mating outcome differs, producing antagonistic co-evolution. Males use mate guarding, nuptial gifts, forced copulation, copulatory plugs, and anti-aphrodisiacs (Heliconius melpomene). Females retaliate through cryptic choice, ejecting unwanted sperm (feral fowl eject subordinate-male sperm via cloacal contractions).

## Parental care and family conflict

Parental investment (Robert Trivers, 1972) is anything a parent does for one offspring that reduces its ability to invest in others. Three conflicts follow. (1) Sexual conflict: each parent prefers the other to do more; great tit pairs match each other's provisioning. (2) Parent–offspring conflict: an offspring is twice as related to itself as to a sibling, so it begs for more than its fair share, while parents balance current and future broods; the cost of exaggerated begging enforces honesty. (3) Sibling conflict: in Galápagos fur seals and blue-footed boobies, larger chicks may kill smaller ones, especially when food is scarce or hatching is asynchronous.

Some species outsource care. Brood parasites such as the common cuckoo lay eggs in host nests, and the cuckoo chick ejects host young. Larvae of Phengaris butterflies chemically mimic ant larvae and are raised inside ant colonies.

## Social behavior, kin selection, and eusociality

Cooperation evolves when the actor benefits, directly or through relatives. Hamilton's rule, rB − C > 0, predicts helping whenever the benefit to the recipient (B), weighted by relatedness (r), exceeds the actor's cost (C). Inclusive fitness is an individual's own reproduction plus its effects on relatives' reproduction. Kin are recognized through genetic cues (variable lag genes in social amoebae, cuticular hydrocarbons in stingless bees), self-referencing (Belding's ground squirrels), or simple rules such as "anyone in my nest is kin" (reed warblers feeding cuckoo chicks) and imprinting (Lorenz).

Eusocial insects (honey bees, ants, wasps) show extreme cooperation. In Hymenoptera, haplodiploidy makes workers related to sisters by 0.75 but to brothers by only 0.25, so workers favor a 3:1 female-biased sex ratio while the queen favors 1:1, generating a tug-of-war. The monogamy hypothesis holds that high relatedness from a single, lifelong mating is ancestral to eusociality; multiple mating came later to boost disease resistance and division of labor.

## Communication and signaling

Signals are traits that evolved to convey information; cues are incidentally informative. Honest signals are kept honest by indices (unfakeable traits like body size), handicaps (costly ornaments only high-quality bearers can afford), or shared interests. Dishonest signaling persists where receivers benefit more on average by accepting than rejecting, as in the anglerfish's glowing lure or Batesian mimics such as Papilio polyxenes imitating the toxic Battus philenor.
