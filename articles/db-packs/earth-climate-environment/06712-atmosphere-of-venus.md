# Atmosphere of Venus

Venus is wrapped in a CO₂ atmosphere about 93 times more massive than Earth's, with a surface pressure of 92 bar and a surface temperature of 740 K (467 °C) — hotter than Mercury despite Venus receiving only 25% of the solar energy per unit area that Mercury does. The air is 96.5% carbon dioxide and 3.5% nitrogen, with trace sulfur dioxide (150 ppm), water vapor (20 ppm), argon (70 ppm), and smaller amounts of carbon monoxide, helium, neon, hydrogen chloride, and hydrogen fluoride. Because the air is so dense, CO₂ at the surface exists as a supercritical fluid (above its critical temperature and pressure, where liquid and gas phases merge) rather than a true gas, forming a planet-wide layer about 6.5% as dense as water that efficiently buffers day–night temperature swings.

The dense CO₂, along with water vapor and sulfur dioxide, creates a runaway greenhouse effect. Venus may once have had Earth-like surface conditions with liquid water oceans, but as the Sun brightened, evaporation released greenhouse gases and the atmosphere became permanently overheated, with no plate tectonics to recycle CO₂ back into rock.

Sulfuric acid clouds sit between roughly 48 and 70 km altitude, composed of 75–96% H₂SO₄ droplets. They form when ultraviolet light splits CO₂; the released oxygen attacks sulfur dioxide, and the resulting sulfur trioxide combines with trace water vapor. These clouds reflect about 75% of incoming sunlight, the highest geometric albedo (the fraction of light reflected back toward the source) of any planet, leaving the surface dim at roughly 14,000 lux, comparable to an overcast Earth day. Surface humidity is below 0.1%, and sulfuric acid rain evaporates before reaching the ground, a phenomenon called virga.

The wind structure is the atmosphere's most striking dynamical feature: super-rotation. The upper troposphere circles the planet in only about four Earth days, while Venus's own sidereal rotation period (the time to rotate once relative to the stars) is 243 Earth days, so the atmosphere moves up to 60 times faster than the solid planet. Cloud-top winds reach 100 m/s (360 km/h) at low latitudes, dropping to 35–50 m/s at the edges of the polar vortices and to under 2.8 m/s at the surface, where the dense air still moves dust and small stones. Circulation follows a cyclostrophic balance (centrifugal force balancing the pressure gradient in fast-curving flow) rather than Earth's geostrophic balance (where the Coriolis effect balances the pressure gradient). A single planet-wide Hadley cell (a large-scale tropical-to-polar overturning circulation) carries heat from equator to ±60° latitude, where descending air forms cold polar collars 30–40 K cooler than the surrounding upper troposphere. Inside each collar sits a hurricane-like polar vortex with two "eyes" connected by an S-shaped cloud structure, rotating with a period of about three days. Akatsuki imagery also revealed an equatorial jet stream between 45 and 60 km altitude.

The mesosphere (65–120 km) and thermosphere (above 120 km) behave differently. At about 95 km, the dayside mesopause is the coldest part of the sunlit atmosphere at 165 K (−108 °C), while the nightside thermosphere, the coldest place on the planet, drops to about 100 K (−173 °C). Between 90 and 150 km, air flows from day to night side, where downwelling compresses and heats it to a warm layer near 230 K. On the night side the cloud deck thickens into haze reaching 90–105 km, well above the day-side cloud top near 65 km. A thin ozone layer sits at about 100 km.

Because Venus has no intrinsic magnetic field, the solar wind interacts directly with the ionosphere, the upper atmospheric layer where solar ultraviolet radiation strips electrons from atoms and molecules. The ionosphere, between 120 and 300 km with peak electron density of 3 × 10¹¹ m⁻³, deflects the solar wind's magnetic field to form an induced magnetosphere (a cavity carved out of the solar wind by the planet's conducting ionosphere) with a bow shock, magnetosheath, magnetopause, and magnetotail. At solar minimum the bow shock stands about 1900 km above the surface and the magnetopause near 300 km. Hydrogen, deuterium-enriched water, and other light gases escape through the magnetotail. The present D/H ratio of 0.015–0.025, 100–150 times Earth's value, is a fingerprint of long-term water loss.

At roughly 50–55 km altitude, pressure and temperature (about 293–310 K) match Earth's surface, making the upper cloud layer the most Earth-like environment in the Solar System. Because breathable air (21% O₂, 78% N₂) is less dense than the surrounding CO₂, it acts as a lifting gas, and a habitat would float like a dirigible at that altitude, the basis for proposed crewed missions like HAVOC.

Several open questions keep Venus scientifically active. The "unknown UV absorber" creates dark patches in cloud imagery that Carl Sagan flagged in 1963 as a possible microbial signature; disulfur dioxide (S₂O) is now a leading non-biological candidate. The 2020 phosphine (PH₃) detection at 53–61 km proved controversial: reanalyses reduced the signal to roughly 1–20 ppb and attributed most of it to sulfur dioxide contamination, though a 2024 JCMT-Venus reanalysis tentatively reported 300 ppb at 55 km, leaving the question open. Venus Express detected whistler waves (low-frequency radio bursts from lightning) consistent with lightning at roughly half Earth's rate, but Akatsuki recorded a much lower flash rate and a Parker Solar Probe flyby suggested some of those whistlers originate outside the planet, so Venus lightning's existence, frequency, and chemistry remain disputed.
