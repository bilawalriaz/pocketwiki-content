# Soil ecology

Soil ecology studies how soil organisms interact with their physical and chemical environment, with particular focus on nutrient cycling, aggregate formation, and biodiversity. Soils are heterogeneous mixtures of minerals, organic matter, water, air, and living organisms. This heterogeneity operates across scales, from microscopic pores to landscapes, and from hours to geological time, and it is the primary reason soils support so much life and drive so much ecosystem function.

## The abiotic foundation

Pedogenesis (soil formation) is described by Hans Jenny's equation as a function of five factors: climate, organisms, relief, parent material, and time (*s = f(cl, o, r, p, t)*).

Soil structure develops as clay, silt, and sand bind with organic matter into aggregates. Aggregates create the porosity that lets water, air, and roots move through soil, and they protect organic matter from rapid decomposition. Tillage disrupts aggregates and the bacterial communities that depend on them.

Nutrients cycle through biogeochemical processes that set fertility. Plants need nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium (NPK); microbes additionally use calcium, magnesium, sulfur, and micronutrients such as iron, manganese, zinc, copper, boron, and molybdenum. Synthetic fertilizer overuse and runoff degrade soil health and drive eutrophication in adjacent freshwaters.

Microclimate imposes hard limits. Soil organisms constantly face dehydration, and microbial community composition shifts with wetting-drying cycles; rewetting dry soil triggers a pulse of carbon loss called the Birch effect. Air is needed for respiration, and wind, atmospheric pressure, convection, and diffusion all drive soil aeration. Temperature varies with depth: surface layers track air temperature, deeper layers are buffered, and temperature governs microbial activity, enzyme function, and decomposition rates.

## Soil fauna

Soil animals drive formation, litter decomposition, nutrient cycling, and plant growth, yet remain underrepresented in ecological models, partly because below-ground diversity has long been treated as redundant. Spatial patterns are shaped by abiotic filters (temperature, moisture, pH) and by patchy resource distribution. Soils are also vertically stratified: pore size, water films, texture, and resources differ by horizon, so communities change with depth. Microbes occupy micropores; larger fauna need macropores or water films.

Two spatial scales behave differently. At local scales, microhabitat diversity creates intense hot spots within a single gram of soil. At global scales, soil animals lack the clear latitudinal diversity gradient seen in plants; a study of 17,516 18S rRNA sequences across 20 phyla found below-ground animal diversity may be *inversely* related to above-ground plant diversity. Fungal richness correlates with climate and edaphic factors rather than plant diversity, and salinity is the dominant global determinant of bacterial community composition. Whether soil biodiversity loss reduces multiple ecosystem functions, or whether above-ground plant diversity predicts them better, remains debated.

## The soil microbiome

Soils contain bacteria, archaea, protists, fungi, and viruses, most uncultured. Environmental DNA and omics tools (metagenomics, metatranscriptomics, proteomics) now reach them without cultivation, and metaphenomics links this molecular data to environmental conditions.

A defining feature is spatial separation. Microbes cluster in hot spots such as the rhizosphere (around roots) and the detritusphere (in decaying litter), where moisture, carbon, and nitrogen concentrate. These patches change in time, producing hot moments of intense activity. Organisms communicate through soluble metabolites, volatile compounds, and extracellular polysaccharides. Bacterial peptidoglycans stimulate *Candida albicans* growth, while farnesol from *C. albicans* modulates bacterial quorum sensing (cell-density-dependent gene regulation). Ectomycorrhizal fungi release extracellular enzymes that liberate nutrients used by bacteria; bacteria in return release organic acids that promote fungal growth. Such metabolite dependencies shape community assembly as much as competition does.

## The soil food web and its services

The food web runs from one-celled bacteria, algae, fungi, and protozoa through nematodes and micro-arthropods to earthworms, insects, small vertebrates, and plants. As they feed and move, these organisms deliver ecosystem services: they decompose organic compounds including manure, plant residues, and pesticides, preventing water pollution; they sequester and fix nitrogen; they build aggregates and pores that increase infiltration and reduce runoff; and they prey on crop pests while feeding above-ground animals. Functional dissimilarity (differences in what species do rather than how many exist) can matter more than raw species richness for these processes, and non-trophic interactions such as phoresy (passive transport of one species by another) also shape outcomes.
