# Carbon

Carbon is element six: six protons, symbol C, group 14 of the periodic table, the 15th most abundant element in Earth's crust and the second most abundant in the human body by mass (about 18.5%, after oxygen). Each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds and bonds readily to other carbon atoms in long chains, a property called catenation. Four bonds plus strong C–C links let one element form millions of compounds, including the sugars, fats, proteins, and DNA that every living cell is built from.

## Why Carbon Is Special

Carbon is nonmetallic and tetravalent. Its four bonds can be single, double, or triple, connecting to hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, or other carbons. Because C–C bonds are strong, carbon builds chains, branches, and rings of almost any size.

Pure carbon is unusual: at normal pressure it does not melt into a liquid but sublimates (transitions directly from solid to gas), and it has the highest sublimation point of any element.

## Allotropes

An allotrope is a different structural form of the same element. Carbon has several, and they differ because the atoms are arranged differently.

| Allotrope | Structure | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Graphite | Stacked sheets of hexagonal carbon layers | Soft, black, electrically conductive; thermodynamically stable at room conditions |
| Diamond | Each carbon bonded to four others in a rigid 3D lattice | Extremely hard, transparent, electrically insulating |
| Graphene | A single graphite layer, one atom thick | Exceptionally strong and conductive |
| Fullerenes | Hollow cages or tubes (buckyballs, nanotubes) | Used in materials science and nanotechnology |
| Amorphous carbon | Disordered arrangement (soot, carbon black) | Powders and chars |
| Lonsdaleite, Q-carbon | Rare or newly reported forms | Properties still being studied |

## Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. Carbon has three natural ones:

- **Carbon-12**: stable, 98.93% of natural carbon.
- **Carbon-13**: stable, 1.07%.
- **Carbon-14**: radioactive, half-life 5,700 years. Trace amounts form in the upper atmosphere, enter living things, and stop exchanging after death. Radiocarbon dating, invented in 1949, measures how much C-14 remains in organic material to estimate its age.

In 1961, IUPAC adopted carbon-12 as exactly 12 atomic mass units, the reference on which all other atomic weights are set.

## Where Carbon Comes From

Carbon is the fourth most abundant element in the universe by mass. Stars build it through the triple-alpha process: three helium nuclei fuse in hot stellar cores into a single carbon-12 nucleus. Once carbon exists, stars burn hydrogen more efficiently through the CNO cycle, a hydrogen-fusion chain in which carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen act as catalysts (substances that speed reactions without being consumed).

On Earth, carbon sits in four main reservoirs: the atmosphere (mostly CO₂), the oceans (dissolved CO₂ and carbonates), the biosphere, and the lithosphere (fossil fuels such as coal, oil, and natural gas, plus carbonate rocks like limestone). The carbon cycle is the exchange of carbon between these reservoirs.

## A Short Human History

Soot, charcoal, and diamond have been used since antiquity. In 1722, Réaumur showed that iron absorbs carbon from carbon-rich material and becomes steel. In 1772, Lavoisier proved diamond is a form of carbon by burning weighed samples and capturing the same CO₂ charcoal produces. In 1779, Scheele showed graphite is carbon contaminated with iron; in 1786, Berthollet, Monge, and Vandermonde confirmed it and proposed the name "carbone." Lavoisier listed carbon as an element in his 1789 textbook.

Buckminsterfullerene (C₆₀, the buckyball) was discovered in 1985; the 1996 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to its discoverers. Q-carbon was announced in 2015. The relative stability of diamond versus graphite at very low temperatures remains debated: recent work suggests graphite is more stable than diamond below about 400 K.

## Carbon Compounds

Organic compounds are built around carbon–carbon and carbon–hydrogen backbones, decorated with functional groups that carry oxygen, nitrogen, or sulfur and give each molecule its specific behaviour. Hydrocarbons, containing only carbon and hydrogen, are the main component of fossil fuels. Sugars, fats, proteins, and DNA follow the same pattern.

Inorganic carbon compounds are simpler: CO₂ and CO are the gaseous oxides, carbonates form the rocks (limestone is calcium carbonate, CaCO₃), and carbides bond carbon to metals or silicon. Organometallic compounds contain a direct carbon-metal bond.

## Applications

Fossil-fuel hydrocarbons power most vehicles and power plants. Graphite is the lead in pencils, a dry lubricant, and an electrode material. Diamond serves as a gemstone and an industrial cutting tool; synthetic diamonds are grown by high-pressure, high-temperature (HPHT) or chemical-vapour-deposition (CVD) methods. Carbon fibre makes stiff, lightweight composites for aircraft and sporting goods. Activated charcoal's high surface area adsorbs impurities, which is why it filters water and air.
