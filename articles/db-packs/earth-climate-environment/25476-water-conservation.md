# Water conservation

Water conservation is the deliberate reduction of water loss, use, and waste, together with the protection of water quality, to sustain fresh water for people and ecosystems. It differs from water efficiency, which refers to technologies and practices that deliver the same service with less water, such as a 2.5 gallon-per-minute showerhead replacing a 5–10 gallon-per-minute one. Climate change, population growth, and rising affluence have made conservation urgent, especially in manufacturing and in agricultural irrigation, which alone uses about 70% of the world's fresh water.

## Strategies

Three strategies matter most. Rainwater harvesting collects precipitation for non-drinking uses such as toilet flushing, garden irrigation, and small-scale farming. Groundwater protection prevents contamination from septic systems, storage tanks, landfills, and road salts, because contaminated groundwater can take years to replenish. Sustainable groundwater use avoids over-pumping, which lowers water tables, shrinks rivers and lakes, and, in coastal zones, pulls saltwater into freshwater aquifers.

Education and outreach sit alongside these strategies, because conservation depends on people understanding how water systems work. World Water Day, held on 22 March, marks this public role.

## Households and cities

Conservation is usually run locally, by municipal utilities or regional governments, using tiered water rates, restrictions on outdoor use, and incentives for xeriscaping, which is landscaping that needs little or no irrigation. Universal metering is foundational: the US EPA estimates metering alone cuts consumption by 20 to 40%, while in the UK less than 30% of households are metered. Smart meters add real-time feedback; a Valencia, Spain study found they increased household savings.

Replacing toilets and retrofitting washers saves more than shorter showers, according to two US end-use studies. Recycling shower systems recapture both water and heat.

## Industry and commerce

High-income countries use roughly 59% of their water for industry, compared with about 8% in low-income countries. The highest-impact changes are routine: assess and maintain water systems, check for leaks at regular intervals, and install rain sensors that suspend irrigation during precipitation. Many home devices translate to business settings, including low-flush and composting toilets, faucet aerators, waterless urinals and car washes, foot-operated taps, cooling towers, and water-to-water heat exchangers.

## Agriculture

Blue water is liquid water in rivers, reservoirs, and aquifers; green water is rainfall stored in soil and used directly by rainfed crops. Only blue water is routinely measured. The aim is to minimise losses to evaporation, runoff, and subsurface drainage while maximising yield. Flood irrigation, the oldest method, distributes water unevenly. Overhead sprinklers, centre-pivot or lateral-move, are more uniform. Drip irrigation is the most precise and expensive, yet increasingly affordable, and can save up to 30,000 gallons per year compared with spraying in all directions. Cheap substitutes include soaking hoses that can be buried in the growing medium to eliminate evaporation. Gains also come from improving the existing system: chiselling compacted soils, building furrow dikes to prevent runoff, and using soil-moisture and rainfall sensors to schedule irrigation. Plastic mulch, a thin perforated sheet over the soil, reduces evaporation, though total savings are not yet well quantified.

## Reuse and waste

More than 40% of the world's population lives where water demand exceeds supply, making reuse essential. Methods include seawater desalination, which is energy-intensive but increasingly deployed, and sand filtration, which is effective against protozoa and bacteria but weak against viruses and requires large land areas. Treated wastewater must reduce pathogens to safe levels before it can irrigate food crops or enter drinking supplies. Surveys in 2014 and 2021 found people avoid recycled water out of fear of infection, with better-educated respondents more willing to accept it.

The US EPA estimates household leaks waste about 900 billion gallons (3.4 billion cubic metres) per year. Water returned through sewers remains within the hydrologic cycle, yet it was captured, treated, and transported at cost, so wasting it represents real environmental degradation in the source watercourse and riparian zone, the vegetated land beside it. Permanent restrictions in Palo Alto, California and Victoria, Australia, and temporary hosepipe bans in the UK, codify what counts as waste: leaks, runoff, irrigation during rain, and using potable water where non-potable water is available.
