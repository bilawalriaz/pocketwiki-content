# Alpine climate

Alpine climate is the weather regime found above the tree line on high mountains, where low temperatures prevent trees from growing. It is also called mountain or highland climate. Although it covers a tiny fraction of Earth's surface, the same conditions appear across mountain ranges from the equator to the Arctic.

## How it is defined

The two main classification systems agree on the core idea: the location is too cold for trees, and the mean temperature never reaches the threshold needed for warm-climate life.

In the Köppen classification, alpine climate belongs to group E, the same group as polar climates. A location qualifies when no month has a mean temperature above 10 °C (50 °F).

The Holdridge life zone system uses a quantity called biotemperature: the mean of all temperatures, but with any reading below 0 °C (32 °F) or above 30 °C (86 °F) clipped to 0 °C before averaging. Holdridge reasoned that plants stop photosynthesising outside that range, so readings outside it carry no biological meaning. Holdridge splits the tree-free mountain zone into two:

- Alpine climate, with biotemperature between 1.5 °C and 3 °C (34.7–37.4 °F), roughly matching the warmest Köppen tundra types (ET).
- Alvar climate, the coldest mountain band, with biotemperature between 0 °C and 1.5 °C, matching the coldest tundra types and the ice cap climate (EF). Biotemperature cannot fall below 0 °C because cold readings are clipped to zero.

The seasonal behaviour depends on latitude. On a tropical summit such as Mauna Loa, temperatures stay nearly constant all year. At a mid-latitude peak like Mount Washington in New Hampshire, temperatures swing seasonally but never become warm.

## Why it gets colder with height

Sunlight warms the ground, the ground warms the air above it, and the air radiates heat to space. If radiation alone transferred heat, the greenhouse effect would keep the surface near 333 K (60 °C) and temperature would decay exponentially with altitude.

In reality, warm air expands, becomes less dense, and rises, carrying heat upward. A rising parcel of air expands and cools without exchanging heat with its surroundings, because air conducts heat poorly. This is an adiabatic process, and its characteristic cooling rate is the dry adiabatic lapse rate, about 9.8 °C per kilometre (5.4 °F per 1,000 ft).

Water vapour changes the picture. Rising air eventually saturates; the vapour condenses into cloud droplets, releasing latent heat that partly offsets the adiabatic cooling. Above condensation, the cooling rate drops to the moist adiabatic lapse rate, roughly 5.5 °C per kilometre (3 °F per 1,000 ft). The real atmosphere, called the environmental lapse rate, fluctuates with weather, season, and region, but a typical mid-latitude value is 5.5 °C per 1,000 m (about 3.57 °F per 1,000 ft). The working rule: climbing 100 m (330 ft) in altitude is roughly equivalent to moving 80 km (50 miles, or 0.75° of latitude) toward the pole, though proximity to oceans and other local factors can shift the climate sharply. As elevation rises, precipitation turns to snow and wind strengthens, until the tropopause at about 11,000 m (36,000 ft), higher than any summit on Earth.

## Where it appears, and at what altitude

Because the tree line marks the boundary, the lowest altitude of alpine climate varies with latitude. In northern Sweden near 68°N, the tree line is as low as 650 m (2,130 ft). On Mount Kilimanjaro in equatorial Tanzania, the tree line sits at 3,950 m (12,960 ft), more than six times higher. The same climate band therefore appears near sea level in the Arctic and only near the top of tall tropical peaks, a consequence of warmer lowland temperatures at the equator.
