# Atmosphere

An atmosphere is a layer of gases held against an astronomical body by its gravity. A body keeps its atmosphere longest when gravity is high and temperature is low. Hot, fast molecules exceed escape velocity and drift into space, and the solar wind strips the outer gas unless a magnetic field deflects it.

## How atmospheres form

Planets acquire gas in two stages. A *primary atmosphere* is captured from the surrounding nebula and only survives where gravity can hold it against heating and collisions; the inner rocky planets lost these early envelopes. Their *secondary atmospheres* came later, when volcanic outgassing released trapped volatiles (substances such as water, carbon dioxide, and nitrogen) from the interior. The circumstellar disk dissipates on timescales of roughly 10⁷ years, and a solar-mass star spends about 3×10⁷ years contracting before hydrogen fusion ignites. After formation, photochemistry (reactions driven by sunlight) and surface chemistry reshape the air, with life being the main driver of Earth's current composition.

## What controls the composition

Mass and temperature decide which gases a body can keep. Gas giants (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune) are cold and massive enough to hold hydrogen and helium, so their atmospheres are reducing and dominated by H₂ and He. Smaller, warmer, rocky planets closer to the Sun lose these light gases and keep heavier ones: Venus and Mars are mostly CO₂ with nitrogen and argon, while Earth is 78% N₂ and 21% O₂, with oxygen sustained by photosynthesis.

Distance from the Sun also matters: farther out, solar-wind stripping weakens. Lighter molecules escape faster than heavier ones at the same temperature, so planets tend to lose hydrogen over geological time. On Venus and Mars, solar ultraviolet split water into hydrogen and oxygen, and the hydrogen escaped, removing most of the original water. Mars has lost an estimated 80–85% of its water; Venus kept almost none.

|---|---|---|
| Venus | 9,200 kPa (~90× Earth) | 96.5% CO₂, 3.5% N₂ |
| Earth | 101.3 kPa | 78% N₂, 21% O₂ |
| Mars | 0.6 kPa | 95.1% CO₂, 2.6% N₂ |
| Jupiter, Saturn | 100 kPa level | ~90–96% H₂, 3–10% He |
| Titan | 147 kPa | 98.4% N₂, 1.5% CH₄ |
| Pluto | 0.001 kPa | 99% N₂, 0.5% CH₄ |

Mercury, the Moon, Ceres, and most small moons hold only a tenuous exosphere (a thin outer layer where molecules rarely collide and many escape directly to space). Titan and Triton keep substantial nitrogen atmospheres despite low gravity because they are cold enough that nitrogen moves too slowly to escape. Pluto's nitrogen atmosphere collapses and freezes onto the surface when it is far from the Sun.

## Vertical structure of a terrestrial atmosphere

Earth, Venus, and Mars share a layered structure. The *troposphere* (ground to 17 km on Earth, 40 km on Mars, 65 km on Venus) holds 80–98% of the total mass and all the weather. Above it, the *stratosphere* contains the ozone layer on Earth, which absorbs UV and warms the air with altitude; Mars and Venus lack a stratosphere because they have no oxygen to make ozone. The *mesosphere* cools again with altitude, the *thermosphere* heats up by absorbing X-rays and extreme UV, and the *exosphere* at the top is so thin that fast molecules escape into space. On Earth the exosphere begins near 500 km, and the ionosphere (a layer where solar radiation strips electrons from atoms) extends toward the magnetosphere (the region around a planet dominated by its magnetic field).

## Gas giants

On Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune the atmosphere has no solid surface; it compresses smoothly into denser interior layers under hydrostatic equilibrium (downward weight of overlying gas balanced by upward pressure below). Hydrogen may turn into metallic hydrogen deep inside Jupiter and Saturn, while Uranus and Neptune are ice giants with interior layers of water, ammonia, and methane ices rather than metallic hydrogen. Visible cloud layers are arranged by condensation temperature: ammonia ice on top, ammonium hydrosulfide below, then water clouds. All four radiate more heat than they receive from the Sun, signalling strong internal heat sources. Hot Jupiters such as 51 Pegasi b, gas giants that migrated inward and became tidally locked, can lose their entire atmosphere to stellar irradiation.

## Circulation and weather

Circulation is driven by heat differences. On Earth, Mars, and Venus the dominant pattern is the Hadley cell, with rising warm air near the equator and descending air at higher latitudes. Earth's rotation twists these flows via the Coriolis effect, producing trade winds, westerlies, and polar easterlies. Jupiter and Saturn show alternating east–west zonal jets, with the equatorial jet reaching roughly 150 m/s on Jupiter and 300 m/s on Saturn.

## Escape and loss

Three processes shrink an atmosphere over time. Thermal escape leaks the high-velocity tail of the gas distribution whenever its speed exceeds escape velocity. Solar-wind stripping is accelerated where the magnetic field is weak. Cold trapping freezes gases onto the surface or into the regolith (the loose layer of dust and broken rock covering solid ground). A magnetic field deflects the solar wind and slows stripping, but polar funnels and charge exchange still let some ions leak; Earth may have lost roughly 2% of its atmospheric oxygen over 3 billion years through this polar leakage.

## Why atmospheres matter

An atmosphere redistributes heat from equator to pole, allows liquid water to exist at the surface, shields the surface from meteoroids and radiation, and drives weathering and erosion that resurface a planet. Without one, every impact leaves its crater and no liquid can persist. Atmospheric composition is the main remote signature of life: biosignatures such as Earth's oxygen-methane disequilibrium are what telescopes read off exoplanets using transit spectroscopy, a method that first detected sodium in the atmosphere of HD 209458 b in 2002.
