# Arctic ecology

Arctic ecology studies how living things interact with the physical environment north of the Arctic Circle (66°33′N). The region is defined less by temperature alone than by two biomes shaped by cold, light, and frozen ground: the treeless **tundra** and the coniferous **boreal forest (taiga)**. Underlying both is **permafrost**, substrate frozen for at least two years, whose surface thaws each summer into a thin, biologically critical active layer. The other defining habitat is sea ice, frozen seawater laced with salty brine channels that host algae, bacteria, and small animals in an environment most people wrongly assume is lifeless.

## The two biomes

**Tundra** sits north of about 70°N and at high elevations elsewhere. Mean annual temperature is roughly −34 °C, summer averages stay below 10 °C, and precipitation is only 20–30 cm per year. With permafrost close to the surface, trees cannot anchor; vegetation is low-stemmed, dominated by perennial forbs, dwarf shrubs, grasses, lichens, and mosses, with most living tissue below the soil.

**Boreal forest** has a longer, warmer growing season (3–4 months), 30–85 cm of annual precipitation, and supports taller, denser stands of evergreen conifers, mainly spruce, fir, pine, and tamarack, with some diffuse-porous hardwoods. Interior boreal stands grow on permafrost; coastal and southern ones often do not. Crown fires and ground fires are both natural drivers, and recent shifts in fire frequency and drought are degrading the biome's biodiversity.

## Adaptations to cold

Cold acts through three pressures: short growing seasons, long winters without sun, and tissues at risk of freezing.

**Animals** cope with body heat loss by growing larger than temperate relatives (Bergmann's rule: a smaller surface-area-to-volume ratio conserves heat), developing proportionally large feet that act as snowshoes (snowshoe hare, caribou), and insulating themselves with fat, fur, and plumage. Many species digest woody plants with microbial help when soft vegetation is buried under snow. Others avoid winter by hibernating or migrating south.

**Plants** face ice crystals forming inside cells, which ruptures tissue. They either avoid freezing, by hugging the ground, building snow-cover insulation, or supercooling liquid water down to about −38 °C, or tolerate it by allowing ice to form only in extracellular spaces, which dehydrates the cell and lets it survive below −38 °C. Dissolved solutes lower the nucleation point. A separate threat is cavitation in water-transport pores; ring-diffuse wood resists it but transports water less efficiently than ring-porous wood.

**Humans** rely on acclimatization and on metabolic, physical, and behavioural adaptations. Modern Inuit carry gene variants that affect fat distribution for thermal regulation, and indigenous Arctic populations show significantly higher basal metabolic rates than non-indigenous ones, with thyroid-hormone-linked mitochondrial responses that enhance metabolic heat production.

## The sea-ice ecosystem

Solid sea ice is not a desert. Briny channels and the ice underside support diatoms and other ice algae, which in the Arctic Ocean can account for close to half of summertime primary production. These algae feed krill, which graze the underside of the pack ice in spring, and through them the wider plankton, fish, and marine food web. Bacteria, fungi, flatworms, and crustaceans, including "ice worms," also live in this sympagic (ice-associated) habitat.

## Human history of the Arctic

Hominids have hunted in the Arctic for tens of thousands of years; a woolly-mammoth kill site dates presence to about 45,000 years ago, and a debated question is whether these earliest inhabitants were Neanderthals or early modern humans. *Homo sapiens sapiens* using "leaf point" tools occupied Siberia by about 13,000 years ago, and Paleo-arctic populations lived in northern Alaska between roughly 13,000 and 8,000 years ago, spanning the Pleistocene-to-Holocene transition. Paleo-Eskimos followed between 5,000 and 6,000 years ago, sharing ancestry with modern indigenous Arctic peoples, and the Arctic Small Tool tradition (including Saqqaq and Pre-Dorset cultures) gave rise to the Dorset culture (about 2700–1200 years ago). The Thule (early Inuit) replaced Paleo-Eskimo populations around 700–800 years ago and are ancestral to today's Inuit, one of more than 40 ethnic groups living in the Arctic. By the late 18th and early 19th centuries, European trade goods, including kettles, iron tools, tobacco, alcohol, and guns, were absorbed into indigenous communities, and the efficiency of firearms reduced local game, a pattern Garrett Hardin later called "the tragedy of the commons."

## Arctic amplification and its effects

The Arctic is warming roughly twice as fast as the global average, a pattern called Arctic amplification. Consequences cascade through physical and biological systems: sea ice retreats, water salinity drops, ocean currents and temperatures shift, precipitation increases, and melting permafrost releases long-stored carbon. Recent projections suggest most Arctic summer sea ice could disappear by 2050. Melting permafrost is already damaging infrastructure (producing "drunken tree" syndrome and ground subsidence that threaten long-inhabited settlements) and risks releasing hazardous chemicals once trapped in frozen ground.

These changes reshape the thermohaline circulation, the global system of deep ocean currents driven by differences in salinity and temperature, because freshwater input from melting ice alters seawater density. Models disagree on whether thermohaline circulation will weaken, strengthen, or hold steady, but its sensitivity matters globally because it shapes temperatures and precipitation far beyond the Arctic.

Biologically, sea-ice loss disrupts polar bears, Arctic foxes, seals, and seabirds that depend on ice for migration, hunting, and mating, and reduces the nutrients available to phytoplankton that anchor the marine food web. Warmer conditions, reduced ice cover, and increased shipping are letting non-indigenous species establish themselves in ecosystems once protected by cold and isolation, a shift described as a major emerging threat to Arctic biodiversity. Mitigations include antifouling hull coatings, though their biocides create their own marine pollution problem, and long-term monitoring adapted to local conditions rather than generalisations across the whole region.
