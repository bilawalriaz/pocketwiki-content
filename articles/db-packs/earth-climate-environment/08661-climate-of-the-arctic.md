# Climate of the Arctic

The Arctic is a frozen ocean surrounded by land, which shapes its climate at every scale. Seawater cannot fall below about −2 °C (28 °F), so the Arctic Ocean acts as a heat reservoir that keeps the North Pole from being the coldest place in the Northern Hemisphere and explains why Antarctica, a frozen continent surrounded by ocean, is far colder. Long cold winters and short cool summers define the region, with strong variation between the moderating coast and frigid continental interiors a few hundred kilometres inland.

## Defining the Arctic

Two climatic definitions are used: the area north of the Arctic Circle, where the sun does not set on the June solstice, and the area where the average summer temperature is below 10 °C (50 °F), which nearly coincides with the first over land. The climate divides into four regions: the ice-covered Arctic Basin around the North Pole, the Canadian Arctic Archipelago, Greenland (a permanent ice sheet ringed by ice-free coast), and the seasonally or permanently ice-free seas such as the Norwegian, Barents, and Bering Seas.

## The sun: extreme by latitude

The Arctic's defining feature is its huge seasonal swing in sunlight. North of the Arctic Circle the sun does not rise in midwinter or set in midsummer. On the June solstice the North Pole receives 36% more solar radiation over the day than the Equator, but from the September equinox to the March equinox it receives none. Because snow and ice reflect 70–90% of incoming sunlight, the little winter sunshine that arrives is mostly bounced back to space, leaving atmospheric and oceanic heat transport from the south as the main reason the Arctic does not cool continuously all winter.

## The annual cycle of energy

In spring the sun returns high in the sky, but bright snow cover still reflects most of it, so warming is slow. Once melting begins, a feedback takes hold: wet snow reflects only 50–60% of sunlight, dry snow reflects 70–80%, and bare ground or open water absorbs more than 80%. Each step exposes a darker surface that absorbs more energy and melts faster. By July and August, bare Arctic land absorbs over 80% of the sunlight reaching it, while the central Arctic Basin stays near 0 °C because extra energy goes into melting ice rather than warming the surface. Sea ice is mostly fresh water, because salt is rejected as it freezes, and melts at 0 °C, so the air above it stays close to freezing all summer. Frequent summer cloud cover over the Arctic Ocean (over 80% of the time) further reduces surface sunlight. In autumn the sun disappears, fresh snow restores high reflectivity, and winter sets in rapidly.

## Temperature by region

The Arctic Basin, centred on the North Pole, is not the coldest part of the Arctic. Winter heat leaking up through cracks in the ice from the −2 °C ocean keeps average winter temperatures around −30 to −35 °C, with minima near −50 °C. The Canadian Arctic Archipelago has similar winters but warmer summers because exposed land absorbs sunlight. Greenland is split: its ice sheet, covering about 80% of the island at an average elevation of 2,100 m and a maximum of 3,200 m, is the coldest part of the Arctic, with January averages of −45 to −30 °C and winter minima below −60 °C, while its ice-free coast is moderated by the ocean. The coldest verified temperature in the Northern Hemisphere is −69.6 °C, measured near the summit of the Greenland ice sheet on 22 December 1991 and confirmed by the WMO in 2020.

Ice-free seas such as the southern Barents and most of the Norwegian Sea have the smallest temperature ranges: winters hover near −2 °C, the freezing point of seawater, and summers stay below 10 °C. At Shemya Island in the southern Bering Sea, the coldest monthly average was −0.6 °C and the warmest 9.7 °C over a 46-year record.

## Precipitation

The Arctic is dry overall. The Arctic Basin qualifies as a polar desert (a cold region with very little precipitation), receiving under 250 mm per year, with monthly totals of about 15 mm in winter and 20–30 mm in summer. The interior of the Greenland ice sheet is even drier, with under 100–200 mm per year, all falling as snow. The wettest areas are southeast Greenland and the southern North Atlantic seas, which receive 1,200–2,200 mm annually as cyclones (large rotating storm systems) from the North Atlantic storm track force moist air up the coastal terrain. Most Arctic precipitation falls as snow, though summer rain occurs in lower latitudes.

## Sea ice and wind

Sea ice, generally less than 4 m thick, covers the Arctic Basin year-round and most Arctic seas in winter, reaching a March maximum of about 15 million km². It insulates the warm ocean from the cold atmosphere, reflects sunlight, and, because it rejects salt as it forms, drives ocean circulation. Winds are modest over the Basin (4–6 m/s on average) but reach 7–12 m/s in the stormy North Atlantic seas, with winter peaks near 50 m/s.

## Climate change

The Arctic is warming faster than the global average, a pattern called Arctic amplification. Five mechanisms drive it: the ice-albedo feedback, in which melting exposes dark surfaces that absorb more heat, the limited water vapour in cold air, which means absorbed energy goes to warming rather than evaporation, the shallow atmospheric boundary layer (the lower part of the atmosphere mixed by surface heat), so less air needs to warm, greater ocean-to-atmosphere heat transfer as ice retreats, and shifts in circulation that funnel more heat northward. Over the past century the annually averaged Arctic temperature has risen by almost twice the global mean. The IPCC projects 2.8 to 7.8 °C of additional Arctic warming by 2100, with winter warming reaching 4.3 to 11.4 °C.

Reconstructions from ice cores, tree rings, and lake sediments show that Arctic summer temperatures are now higher than at any point in the past 2,000 years. After roughly 1,900 years of slow cooling driven by orbital changes, culminating in the Little Ice Age of the 17th to 19th centuries, temperatures reversed sharply after 1950, and four of the five warmest decades of the past two millennia occurred between 1950 and 2000. Satellite records since 1979 show a steady decline in summer sea-ice extent, and some models project an essentially ice-free Arctic Ocean in late summer between 2050 and 2100.
