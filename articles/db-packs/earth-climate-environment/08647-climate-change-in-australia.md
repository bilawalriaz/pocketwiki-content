# Climate change in Australia

Australia is warming faster than the global average and is unusually exposed to the consequences. It is the driest populated continent, has extensive arid and semi-arid land, and concentrates most of its people along a vulnerable coastline. Greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from burning fossil fuels trap heat in the atmosphere. Australia contributes more than its share, with per capita emissions roughly three times the global average, and is both a cause and a casualty of the problem.

## Observed warming and shifting rainfall

Australian average annual temperatures have risen by more than 1.5 °C since the start of the 20th century, with warming over the past 50 years running at about twice the rate of the previous 50 years. Record-breaking years have clustered recently: 2009 was the second-warmest, 2014 the third-warmest, 2015 the fifth-warmest, and January 2019 the hottest single month ever recorded, with average temperatures exceeding 30 °C. Overnight winter minimums have risen markedly since the 1960s, and frost has become less frequent.

Rainfall patterns are diverging by region. The southwest has lost 10–20% of its rainfall since the 1970s, and the southeast has seen a moderate decline since the 1990s. Rainfall is becoming heavier but less frequent and is shifting from winter toward summer. Streamflow data from 1970 to 2024 show significant decreases at 28% of Australia's hydrological reference stations, almost all in the south, while only 4% increased, all in the north. A severe drought has historically occurred about once every 18 years.

## Bushfires

Fire activity has increased since 1950, driven by hotter, drier conditions, more dry lightning, and more dangerous fire-weather days. A 2006 study identified southeastern Australia as one of the three most fire-prone areas on Earth. Combined very-high and extreme fire-danger days are projected to rise 4–25% by 2020 and 15–70% by 2050. The 2019–20 "Black Summer" burned more than 4 million hectares in New South Wales alone, killed or displaced roughly 3 billion animals, and produced air pollution more than 11 times the hazardous threshold in some areas. Researchers found the climatic conditions behind those fires were made more than four times more likely by climate change, and that such a year could become average by 2040 if emissions continue to grow.

## Sea level rise and coasts

Global sea level is projected to rise 8–88 cm by 2100. A 1-metre rise, plausible within 30–60 years, could inundate around 700,000 Australian properties, including 80,000 buildings, collectively worth about A$155 billion. The federal government has flagged up to 247,600 houses at risk from a 1.1-metre rise. Western Australia has set a 100-year sea-level benchmark in its coastal planning policy.

## Ecosystems

Coral bleaching on the Great Barrier Reef has been unprecedented over the past two decades, and additional warming of just 1 °C is expected to cause substantial coral loss; a 2–3 °C rise could bleach 97% of the reef annually. The Bramble Cay melomys is recorded as the first mammal extinction attributed to climate change. Kakadu's freshwater wetlands could lose 80% of their area with a 30 cm sea-level rise. CSIRO projections for a 3–4 °C rise include a 32% chance of reduced wheat output and a 128% increase in tick-related cattle losses.

## People, economy, and Indigenous communities

A 1–2 °C rise is projected to cut inflows to the Murray-Darling basin by 12–25%, shrink Melbourne's water supply by 7–35%, double Australians exposed to dengue-fever risk from 170,000 to as many as 1.6 million, and double flood exposure. Air-conditioning use during heatwaves can double electricity demand, stressing the grid. The 2009 Victorian fires cost about A$4.4 billion, and the 2010–11 Queensland floods over A$5 billion. Under 2 °C of warming, Australia is projected to lose about A$115 billion over the next decade and A$350 billion over twenty years; under 3 °C, the figures rise to A$200 billion and A$600 billion by 2042. By 2070, people unaccustomed to the heat could face up to 45 days a year unsuitable for being outside, against 4–6 today.

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, who make up about 30% of the population in northern Australia, face compounded risk because remote locations, socio-economic disadvantage, and dependence on traditional land for food, culture, and health magnify the impact of cyclones, sea-level rise, and longer fire seasons. Many communities face a choice between adapting in place or relocating.

## Emissions and policy

Coal supplied 66% of Australia's grid electricity in 2020, and coal is the single largest emissions source. National emissions for the year to March 2021 were 494.2 million tonnes CO₂-equivalent, 20.8% below 2005, helped by COVID-related transport cuts. By 2024, total emissions had fallen to 378.73 million tonnes CO₂-equivalent, and most existing coal-fired stations are scheduled to close between 2022 and 2048 as renewables grow. Australia ratified the Paris Agreement in 2015 and updated its target in 2022 to a 43% reduction by 2030 and net zero by 2050; a 2035 target of 62–70% was announced in September 2025. The Climate Action Tracker rates Australia's overall commitment as "insufficient" and its climate finance as "critically insufficient". Australia has consistently ranked poorly in the Climate Change Performance Index, scoring last in the 2021 UN Sustainable Development Report. Successive governments have passed and repealed carbon pricing, with Australia becoming the first country to repeal such a scheme, and the Liberal and National parties both formally abandoned their net-zero commitment in November 2025.

## Politics, media, and protest

Climate policy has been a divisive issue since the 2000s, with conservative parties generally protecting coal interests and some media outlets, including Sky News Australia, identified as global sources of climate misinformation. Public pressure has been substantial: a 2009 Melbourne rally drew 40,000 people, the 2019 climate strikes drew an estimated 180,000–300,000 across the country, and ongoing Rising Tide blockades at the Newcastle coal port have resulted in mass arrests, including 156 in November 2025. In November 2023, Australia began offering permanent residency to 280 climate-displaced Tuvalu citizens per year under a bilateral deal.
