# Deforestation and climate change

Deforestation and climate change reinforce each other. Cutting forests releases stored carbon, and a warming, drying climate kills more forest. Roughly 11% of global greenhouse gas emissions come from deforestation. Tropical deforestation alone emitted 2.6 billion tonnes of CO₂ per year in 2010–2014, about 6.5% of global CO₂ emissions. Global tree cover loss has roughly doubled since 2001, approaching an area the size of Italy each year, and primary tropical forest loss rose 12% from 2019 to 2020.

## Why forests matter for climate

Forests are terrestrial carbon sinks: through photosynthesis, trees absorb CO₂ from the air and store it as biomass in roots, stems, branches, and leaves. Forests sequester about 25% of human CO₂ emissions each year, and Amazonia absorbs about 18% of fossil-fuel CO₂. Standing tropical forests also cool the planet through evapotranspiration and cloud formation; studies suggest they lower average global temperature by more than 1°C.

Carbon is held in living wood and in soil. The FAO reports the world's forests held about 668 gigatonnes of carbon in 1990 and 662 in 2020. In Canada's boreal forests, up to 80% of total carbon sits in soil as dead organic matter.

## The feedback loop

Climate change increases forest loss through more wildfires, insect outbreaks, invasive species, and extreme storms. Wildfires and higher temperatures then release more carbon, which causes more warming, a positive climate feedback. In 2019, forests took up about a third less carbon than in the 1990s, and national inventories from 1999–2020 show some forests approaching the threshold where they switch from carbon sink to carbon source. The typical tropical forest may become a net carbon source by the 2060s. 23% of tree cover loss is driven by wildfires, and climate change is increasing their frequency and intensity.

Boreal forests behave differently. Some are gaining resilience, possibly because warming and CO₂ fertilisation outweigh the damage from hotter, drier conditions, but warming also drives huge boreal wildfires that release stored carbon.

## The Amazon at risk

The Amazon illustrates the danger. It already absorbs 30% less CO₂ than in the 1990s, and nearly 20% of it has been clear-cut. Modelling identifies two thresholds: 4°C of warming and 40% deforestation, beyond which the forest could shift to a drier, grass-dominated state. Under unmitigated warming, 21% of the Amazon could be vulnerable to post-fire grass invasion by 2100, and the south-eastern Amazon is at highest risk today. The Amazon is also a "flying river": trees release moisture that becomes rainfall, so deforestation reduces regional precipitation. Researchers project the Congo will lose 8–10% of rainfall by 2100.

## Biophysical effects

Carbon is not the only climate-relevant change. Tropical deforestation generally produces warming, but large-scale deforestation north of 50°N can produce net cooling, because bare high-latitude ground reflects more sunlight (higher albedo) than dark forest canopy. The same hectare of forest cleared or planted has different climate effects depending on latitude. Tropical tree planting also forms clouds that reflect sunlight, adding to the cooling.

## Mitigation and trade-offs

Avoiding deforestation protects carbon faster than replanting can restore it, because new trees take decades to reach the storage capacity of mature forests. The main international framework is REDD+ (Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation in developing countries), a UN mechanism that pays developing countries to keep forests standing. Key UNFCCC decisions came between 2010 and 2015, including the Warsaw Framework in 2013. A 2024 multi-country evaluation found modest, often unsustained forest and welfare impacts. Critics point to problems with baselines, additionality, leakage, non-permanence, and the risk that some offset credits (an estimated ~90% of certain rainforest credits) are "phantom credits". Tree-planting projects have introduced fast-growing invasive species, displaced Indigenous communities, and, in boreal regions, can warm the climate by lowering albedo.

National efforts include Brazil's Amazon Fund, established in 2008, which by 2018 had received R$ 3.4 billion, mostly from Norway, with Germany and Petrobras as other major donors. The UN's Trillion Tree Campaign, launched in 2006, has planted more than 12 billion trees. Earth has room for about 0.9 billion additional hectares of tree canopy, though the area that genuinely cools the climate is 20–80% lower once albedo is accounted for. Tropical forests fix carbon faster than boreal ones because they grow year-round, and a 2023 study found African, Amazonian, and Asian tropical forests together absorb close to one-fifth of fossil-fuel emissions.
