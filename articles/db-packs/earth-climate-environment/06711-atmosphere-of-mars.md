# Atmosphere of Mars

Mars is wrapped in a thin, cold envelope of gas, almost entirely carbon dioxide, whose behaviour is dictated by its low pressure. The average surface pressure is about 610 pascals, or 0.6% of Earth's, with peak density near 0.020 kg/m³, roughly what Earth's atmosphere is like 35 km up. The effective temperature sits near 210 K (−63 °C), with a mean surface emission temperature of 215 K, comparable to inland Antarctica. Despite being 95% CO₂, the greenhouse warming is only about 5 °C, versus 33 °C on Earth, because the air is so rarefied that greenhouse gases absorb infrared weakly and water vapour is almost absent.

The atmosphere divides into three layers. The troposphere (0–40 km) holds nearly all weather, including the planet-encircling dust storms that occur on average every 3 Martian years (5.5 Earth years). Suspended dust particles absorb sunlight and heat the surrounding air, lowering the measured lapse rate to about 2.5 °C/km against a dry adiabatic value of 4.3 °C/km. The mesosphere (40–100 km) is the coldest layer, with CO₂ radiating heat efficiently; the mesopause near 100 km reaches 100–120 K and can spawn CO₂ ice clouds. The thermosphere (100–230 km) is heated by extreme UV to 175–390 K depending on season and solar activity, but it stays cooler than Earth's because CO₂ cools it radiatively and Mars lacks a strong magnetic field.

Seasonal swings are dramatic. In winter, CO₂ condenses into 1–2 m of dry ice at the poles, dropping atmospheric mass by about 25%, then sublimes back in summer. This cycle drives roughly 25% variation in surface pressure. Dust devils, tens of metres wide and kilometres tall, lift about 2.3 × 10¹¹ kg of dust each year, while local storms (~10³ km²) appear about 2000 times per Martian year, sometimes merging into planet-wide events such as the 2018 storm that ended the Opportunity rover's mission.

Atmospheric composition by mean volume ratio: CO₂ 95%, N₂ 2.8%, Ar 2%, O₂ 0.174%, CO 0.0747%, H₂O 0.03% (variable), plus trace noble gases. CO₂ is constantly broken down by solar UV via `CO₂ + hν (λ < 225 nm) → CO + O`, but hydroxyl radicals regenerate it through `CO + OH → CO₂ + H`, so its abundance stays roughly stable. Water vapour is sparse and variable,10–20 precipitable µm globally and up to 50–70 near the north pole in early summer. Ozone appears in a seasonal layer over the south pole and a persistent near-surface layer below 30 km, destroyed by odd-hydrogen catalysis in moist air.

Atmospheric escape has thinned Mars over billions of years. Hydrogen escapes by Jeans escape, the thermal evaporation of atoms with enough energy to escape gravity, at roughly 10⁷–10⁹ cm⁻² s⁻¹, modulated by dust storms. Carbon escapes mainly through dissociative recombination of CO⁺ and CO₂⁺ ions in the ionosphere, producing fast atoms that exceed Mars's escape velocity; the overall flux is about 0.6–2.2 × 10⁷ cm⁻² s⁻¹. Nitrogen escapes similarly at about 4.8 × 10⁵ cm⁻² s⁻¹. Solar wind interaction drives ionospheric outflow, though it is too small to explain total loss.

The isotopic record confirms this loss. Mars's atmosphere is enriched in deuterium, with D/H about 6 times Earth's, but depleted in ¹⁵N relative to Earth's ratio (about 0.64×), because lighter isotopes escape preferentially. ⁴⁰Ar/³⁶Ar is about 6 times Earth's because primordial ³⁶Ar was stripped by impact erosion. The C/⁸⁴Kr ratio is only 10% of Earth's, implying early Mars held at least ten times its present CO₂. Early hydrodynamic escape, driven by extreme UV from the young Sun, could have stripped about 1 bar of CO₂ in 1–10 million years, while sputtering has removed an estimated 65% of Mars's argon. Impact erosion during late heavy bombardment may have removed up to 99% of the primordial atmosphere, and 12–30 m of water equivalent has been lost to space via hydrogen escape.

The early atmosphere was thicker and wetter, but explaining liquid water under the faint young Sun requires a stronger greenhouse. Carl Sagan proposed that 1 bar of H₂ could warm early Mars, though photochemical models show such an atmosphere is hard to maintain. SO₂ has also been proposed, but its solubility and aerosol formation likely prevent long-term buildup.

A long-standing puzzle is methane. It should survive only about 400 years in Mars's oxidising atmosphere, so any persistent detection implies an active source. ESA's Trace Gas Orbiter found no methane to a limit of 0.05 ppb, but Curiosity measured seasonal variations and a 7.2 ppb spike in 2013–14. As of 2024, methane has only been found in Gale Crater. Candidate sources include water–rock reactions, radiolysis, and Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, with living methanogens as a possible but unsupported origin.

Human missions could exploit this air: the Sabatier reaction (`CO₂ + 4H₂ → CH₄ + 2H₂O`) can make rocket fuel, and in 2021 Perseverance demonstrated oxygen production by electrolysing compressed, heated CO₂ through a zirconia electrolyte, generating 122 g of O₂ total as of 2023.
