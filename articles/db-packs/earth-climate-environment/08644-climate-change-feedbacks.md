# Climate change feedbacks

A climate change feedback is a natural process that magnifies or dampens the warming caused by greenhouse gas emissions. Positive feedbacks amplify warming; negative feedbacks diminish it. The net of all feedbacks is negative, which is why warming is slower than it would be without them, but the net is becoming less negative as emissions continue, so warming will accelerate if they do not fall.

The strongest single feedback is the Planck response. As the surface warms, it emits more thermal radiation to space, roughly with the fourth power of absolute temperature (Stefan–Boltzmann law), giving a stabilizing effect near −3.8 W/m²·K. It is several times larger than any other feedback and the main reason human emissions alone cannot trigger a runaway greenhouse effect.

The most important amplifying feedback is water vapor. A warmer atmosphere holds more moisture, and because water vapor is itself a greenhouse gas, extra vapor traps more heat, which allows still more vapor: a self-reinforcing loop. Satellite and model estimates put its strength at about 1.8 W/m²·K, roughly doubling the warming that CO₂ alone would cause.

The surface albedo (ice–albedo) feedback is also positive. Ice and snow reflect most sunlight; when they melt, darker land or open ocean absorbs more solar energy, which melts more ice. Arctic sea ice has shrunk since 1979, and the Arctic is now warming nearly four times faster than the global average. Between 1979 and 2011, Arctic ice loss contributed about 0.21 W/m² of radiative forcing, roughly a quarter of CO₂'s effect over the same period. East Antarctica has warmed little because its high, stable ice sheet loses little area.

Cloud feedback is positive on the global average, because the mix of cloud types is expected to shift toward less cooling, but it has the widest uncertainty of any feedback: about 0.42 W/m²·K with a range from −0.10 to +0.94, since some marine clouds are poorly observed and aerosols from sulfur-rich fuels complicate the picture.

The lapse rate feedback is mostly negative, because warming reduces how fast temperature falls with altitude, weakening the greenhouse effect. It flips positive in the polar regions, where strong surface warming and temperature inversions trap heat near the ground, contributing to polar amplification.

Carbon cycle feedbacks are largely negative today. Land plants and the ocean together absorb more than half of annual CO₂ emissions, and about three-quarters of all fossil-fuel CO₂ will eventually dissolve in the ocean over centuries. But warming-induced droughts, wildfires, and permafrost thaw add positive feedbacks on the same system. Permafrost alone could release the equivalent of 14–175 billion tonnes of CO₂ per 1 °C of warming, against about 40 billion tonnes of annual human CO₂ emissions in 2019. The Amazon could shift toward savannah if warming reaches around 3.5 °C.

Some feedbacks act on very long timescales. Ice sheets respond over millennia in two stages: meltwater disrupting ocean circulation (a small negative feedback), followed by the ice-albedo feedback from their eventual extent (positive, adding 0.13 °C from full loss of Greenland and 0.6 °C from the East Antarctic sheet at 1.5 °C of warming). Methane hydrates in seafloor sediments react so slowly that no detectable effect is expected this century, though 0.4–0.5 °C of warming over millennia remains possible.

Modern climate models diagnose these processes with a linearized framework in which Earth's energy imbalance equals an external forcing plus the sum of individual feedback parameters (water vapor, clouds, albedo, carbon cycle, Planck, lapse rate) multiplied by the temperature change. Uncertainty in those parameters, especially clouds and the carbon cycle, now sets the main limit on how much warming a given amount of emissions will eventually produce.
