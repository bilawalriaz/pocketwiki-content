# Climate change in India

India sits at the centre of climate change in a specific way: it is the world's third-largest greenhouse gas emitter and one of the countries most affected by the warming its emissions contribute to. The tension between rapid development, high exposure to climate impacts, and low per-capita emissions defines nearly every policy choice the country makes.

## Where India's emissions come from

India emits about 3 gigatonnes of CO₂-equivalent per year, roughly 7% of the global total, while holding 17% of the world's population. Per person this is about 2.5 tonnes, near half the world average and far below China and the United States. A coal-heavy electricity system is the largest source: as of 2021, 51% of electricity came from coal, with fossil fuels supplying 60.2% overall and renewables 39.8%. The industrial sector (cement, iron, steel) accounts for about a quarter of emissions and was responsible for 42% of total energy use by 2014. Agriculture contributes a growing share, up 25% between 2005 and 2014, from livestock, fertilisers, and rice cultivation. Methane makes up 14% of emissions and nitrous oxide 5%.

Inequality runs through these numbers. A citizen in the top 1% by income produces about 4.5 times more CO₂ than a citizen in the bottom 38%.

## How the climate is changing

Average Indian temperatures rose 0.7 °C between 1901 and 2018. Heatwaves have become far more frequent: what would once have been a once-in-312-years event is now expected roughly every 3 years, and exposure to heatwaves is projected to rise eightfold between 2021 and 2050. In May 2022, temperatures reached 51 °C in parts of India and Pakistan. Rainfall patterns are shifting, with northern and north-western India projected to face more severe droughts. Himalayan glaciers are retreating, threatening the flow of the Ganges, Brahmaputra, Yamuna, and Indus. Sea level rise has already displaced millions: 3.6 million Indians between 2008 and 2018, and roughly 5.4 million internal displacements were recorded in 2024. Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai, and the Sundarbans face the highest flood risks.

## Who bears the cost

The social cost of carbon in India is estimated at US$86 per tonne, higher than the US ($50) or China ($26), meaning each additional tonne of CO₂ costs the Indian economy more in damages. An estimated 3–10% of GDP could be lost annually by 2100. Farmers are on the front line: 85% have low financial resilience, and crop yields for irrigated rice, wheat, and maize are projected to fall substantially by mid-to-late century. Air pollution, mostly from the same fossil fuels that drive warming, kills an estimated 2.5 million people per year, with 21 of the world's 30 most polluted cities located in India.

Climate impacts fall disproportionately on Dalits, Adivasis (Scheduled Tribes), the urban poor, and coastal communities, groups with less political voice, fewer resources, and livelihoods most exposed to weather extremes. Coal mining has displaced tens of thousands of tribal citizens, and Adivasi land is regularly appropriated for both mines and renewable energy projects. India has no comprehensive policy for climate-driven displacement.

## Policy and mitigation

India was the only G20 nation to meet its initial Paris Agreement targets, cutting emissions intensity of GDP by 24% between 2005 and 2016. A coal tax, raised to ₹400 (US$4.20) per tonne in 2020, exists in place of a carbon tax. At COP26 (2021), Prime Minister Modi announced "Panchamrit": net zero by 2070, 500 GW of non-fossil energy capacity by 2030, 45% reduction in carbon intensity by 2030, 50% of energy from renewables by 2030, and 1 billion tonnes of avoided emissions by 2030. A national carbon market is planned for 2026. In 2024, the Supreme Court declared the right to be free from climate change effects a fundamental right, though policies addressing slow-onset displacement remain limited. Calculations indicate India's commitments would need to roughly double for a 50% chance of holding warming to 1.5 °C.

Adaptation is already underway at the local level: salt-tolerant rice in the Sundarbans, ice stupas in Ladakh, floodwater vegetable cultivation in Odisha, and shifting crop and irrigation practices in the Mahanadi and Bengal deltas, all examples of communities responding to a warming that national pledges alone cannot yet prevent.
