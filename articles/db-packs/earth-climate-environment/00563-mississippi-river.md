# Mississippi River

The Mississippi is the primary river of the largest drainage basin in the United States, running 2,340 miles (3,766 km) from Lake Itasca in Minnesota to the Gulf of Mexico. Its watershed drains 32 U.S. states and two Canadian provinces, covering nearly 40% of the continental United States. The basin is the world's fourth-largest watershed at 1,245,000 square miles, reaching as far west as Mount Elbert in the Rockies.

## Geological Origins

The Mississippi Embayment formed when the North American plate passed over the Bermuda hotspot around 95 million years ago. The resulting uplift raised the Appalachians; as the plate drifted away, the crust subsided up to 2.6 km, creating a trough later filled with sediment. The Laurentide Ice Sheet then shaped the modern drainage: glacial deposits enriched basin soils, and meltwater carved oversized valleys now abandoned, since the Minnesota, James, and Milk Rivers once carried meltwater to the Gulf before drainage shifted toward Hudson Bay.

The lower river naturally undergoes **avulsion** (delta switching) roughly every 1,000 years. Silt clogs the active channel, raising the riverbed until the water breaks to a steeper, shorter path. Abandoned channels become bayous, and over 5,000 years this process advanced the Louisiana coastline 15–50 miles. The active **Birdfoot (Balize) Delta** dates from around 1000 AD.

The **New Madrid Seismic Zone**, a failed rift, produced four earthquakes of roughly magnitude 8 in 1811–1812, felt across the Midwest, and the shaking created Reelfoot Lake in Tennessee. Future avulsions threaten capture by the Atchafalaya River (steeper gradient) or a shorter route to Lake Pontchartrain.

## The Three Reaches

The river divides into three sections:

- **Upper Mississippi** (headwaters to St. Louis): 493 miles to St. Anthony Falls, then 664 miles of navigable channel maintained by 29 locks and dams built in the 1930s to ensure a 9-foot depth. Forty-three dams in total (14 above Minneapolis for power and recreation) moderate flow. Topography shifts from entrenched bedrock bluffs in the north to a multi-thread floodplain in the south.

- **Middle Mississippi** (St. Louis to Cairo, IL): 190 miles, relatively free-flowing, dropping 220 feet. Few major tributaries enter here.

- **Lower Mississippi** (Cairo to Gulf): about 1,000 miles. At Cairo, the Ohio River contributes roughly 281,500 cubic feet per second compared to the Mississippi's 208,200, making the Ohio the volumetric main stem. Near the Old River Control Structure in Louisiana, 30% of the combined Mississippi–Red River flow is diverted down the Atchafalaya distributary.

Average discharge ranges from 200,000 to 700,000 cubic feet per second, ranking 14th globally. Water travels from Lake Itasca to the Gulf in roughly 90 days.

## Human History

The basin was an independent center of plant domestication, with sunflower, goosefoot, marsh elder, and squash cultivated by the 4th millennium BC. The Mississippian culture (from around 800 AD) developed intensive maize agriculture that supported stratified chiefdoms and urban centers. Cahokia, near present-day St. Louis, peaked between 600 and 1400 AD with an estimated 8,000 to 40,000 inhabitants.

European exploration began with Alonso Álvarez de Pineda in 1519, followed by Hernando de Soto in 1541. René-Robert Cavelier, Sieur de La Salle, claimed the Mississippi Valley for France in 1682, naming it Louisiana. New Orleans was founded in 1718; the first levees were built in 1727 using enslaved African labor. The Treaty of Paris (1763) made the river the border between British and Spanish empires, and the 1783 treaty guaranteed free navigation. The Louisiana Purchase (1803) and Adams–Onís Treaty (1819) completed American control.

The steamboat era transformed the river into a commercial highway: the *New Orleans* made the first Lower Mississippi run in 1811, but the Upper Mississippi remained difficult until the 1840s. Railroads eclipsed steamboats by the early 1900s. During the Civil War, the river was central to the Union's Anaconda Plan; Federal forces captured Vicksburg on July 4, 1863, splitting the Confederacy.

## Engineering the River

The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, established in 1802, reshaped the Mississippi for navigation and flood control. The modern system began with the Rivers and Harbors Act of 1930, which authorized a 9-foot navigation channel and led to 23 new locks and dams in the 1930s. The lower river maintains a 45-foot channel to allow container ships to reach New Orleans.

The **1927 Great Flood** was the turning point. Levees failed in 145 places, inundating 27,000 square miles to depths of 30 feet, disproving the "levees-only" theory that confining the river would scour a deeper channel. Instead, the confined water simply rose higher. The Corps then adopted spillways, floodways, and intentional meander cutoffs. Three structures now manage flood risk: the **Old River Control Structure** (completed 1963) prevents the Mississippi from switching to the Atchafalaya; the **Morganza Spillway** can divert floodwater to the Atchafalaya; and the **Bonnet Carré Spillway** can divert water to Lake Pontchartrain. The Old River Control Structure nearly failed in 1973, requiring $300 million in upgrades.

## Environmental Consequences

Engineering solved navigation and flood control but created new problems. Sediment transport dropped by over 60%, from 440 million short tons per year pre-1900 to 160 million tons today, starving Louisiana's coastal wetlands. During droughts, a salt wedge from the Gulf intrudes up to 64 miles upstream (as in 2022), threatening freshwater supplies; underwater sills have been constructed in 1988, 1999, 2012, and 2022 to block it.

Agricultural runoff of nitrogen and phosphorus drives a seasonal Gulf of Mexico dead zone where oxygen collapses. A 2015 assessment gave the basin a "D+" grade for aging infrastructure and unresolved environmental problems. Invasive species compound the challenge: Asian carp threaten to enter the Great Lakes through the Chicago Area Waterway System, prompting closure of the Upper St. Anthony Falls Lock in 2015 as a barrier.

The basin supports roughly 375 fish species, the highest diversity in the temperate Northern Hemisphere except for the Yangtze, including relict species such as paddlefish, sturgeon, gar, and bowfin. About 40% of U.S. migratory birds (326 species) use the Mississippi corridor as a flyway, alongside alligators, turtles, and crayfish in the lower reaches.
