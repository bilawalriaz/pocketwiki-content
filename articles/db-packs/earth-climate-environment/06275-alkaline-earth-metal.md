# Alkaline earth metal

The alkaline earth metals are the six elements in group 2 of the periodic table: beryllium (Be), magnesium (Mg), calcium (Ca), strontium (Sr), barium (Ba), and radium (Ra). Each has two electrons in its outermost *s* orbital, which the atom readily loses to form cations with a +2 charge. Losing those two electrons leaves a full outer shell, the energetic driving force behind nearly all of the group's chemistry. The name comes from their oxides, historically called "alkaline earths" because they were basic (alkaline) when combined with water and were nonmetallic, infusible substances that early chemists called "earths."

## Periodic trends

The defining trend down the group is that atomic radius increases, ionization energy decreases, and reactivity grows. Beryllium is a hard, high-melting oddity whose compounds are largely covalent rather than ionic, because its small, high-charge Be²⁺ polarizes nearby electron clouds so strongly that beryllium chloride, bromide, iodide, and even beryllium fluoride behave as covalent substances. The rest of the group behaves as typical metals, forming ionic halides such as CaCl₂, basic oxides, and hydroxides.

With water, calcium, strontium, and barium react vigorously to give hydrogen gas and the metal hydroxide. Magnesium reacts only slowly, and beryllium resists water because an oxide coating passivates its surface. Heavier members react more vigorously than lighter ones, in line with their lower ionization energies. All members react with halogens and with oxygen.

All six are silver-colored, soft metals with low densities and modest melting points compared to most transition metals. Beryllium and magnesium do not color a flame because their excitation energies are too high for a Bunsen burner to reach; the visible flame colors of calcium, strontium, barium, and radium underpin qualitative identification and the use of strontium and barium salts in fireworks. In solution, Mg²⁺ precipitates as ammonium magnesium phosphate, Ca²⁺ as calcium oxalate, and Sr²⁺ as strontium sulfate.

## Nuclear stability

Beryllium, magnesium, calcium, strontium, and barium together have 19 stable (or observationally stable) isotopes, plus two primordial but radioactive ones: calcium-48 and barium-130, which decay by double-beta emission with half-lives of 5.6 × 10¹⁹ and 1.6 × 10²¹ years. Radium has no stable or primordial isotopes; its longest-lived isotope, radium-226, has a half-life of 1600 years and exists only as a decay product of uranium-238 and thorium. Strontium-90 (28.90 years) and barium-140 (12.7 days) are common fission products of uranium-235. Because the alkaline earths have even atomic numbers, their isotopes benefit from proton pairing, which is why the group as a whole has more stable isotopes than its neighbors.

A related nuclear oddity is beryllium-8, which splits into two alpha particles almost as soon as it forms. Inside stars, the triple-alpha process that fuses three helium nuclei into carbon-12 only proceeds because the third alpha particle is captured before beryllium-8 decays; this slow step limits how long main-sequence stars burn hydrogen.

## Occurrence and history

Magnesium and calcium are the fifth and eighth most abundant elements in the Earth's crust, both locked in minerals such as magnesite, dolomite, limestone, and gypsum. Strontium ranks 15th, mainly in celestite and strontianite. Barium is slightly rarer, concentrated in barite. Beryllium is scarce, around 2–6 ppm in the crust, much of it in the mineral beryl, whose green chromium-bearing variety is emerald. Radium occurs only as a trace decay product of uranium.

Lime and calcite have been used since prehistoric times, and beryl has been known since the Ptolemaic Kingdom. Antoine Lavoisier proposed in 1789 that the alkaline earths were metal oxides rather than elements, and in 1808 Humphry Davy confirmed this by isolating magnesium, calcium, strontium, and barium through electrolysis. Beryllium resisted electrolysis and was independently isolated in 1828 by Friedrich Wöhler and Antoine Bussy, who reduced beryllium chloride with potassium; large pure samples only became possible in 1898 when Paul Lebeau electrolyzed beryllium fluoride with sodium fluoride. Radium was discovered by Marie and Pierre Curie in pitchblende on 21 December 1898 and isolated as a pure metal by Marie Curie and André-Louis Debierne in 1910.

## Applications and biology

Beryllium's stiffness, light weight, and dimensional stability make it valuable in alloys and precision instruments; beryllium oxide is a strong electrical insulator and heat conductor. Magnesium is alloyed with aluminum to reduce weight and is a constituent of chlorophyll. Calcium is a structural element of bone and shell, a key cell-signaling ion, and is used industrially to reduce uranium from ore. Strontium compounds color fireworks red; radioactive strontium-90 mimics calcium, accumulates in bone, and has been used in some radioisotope thermal generators. Barium sulfate is a radiocontrast agent in X-ray imaging because of its density (4.5 g/cm³) and low solubility, and barium is used as a getter in vacuum tubes. Radium's former uses in luminous paints and patent medicines ended when its radioactivity sickened workers; today, shorter-lived isotopes such as iridium-192 have replaced it even where radioactivity is wanted.

Beyond calcium and magnesium, biological roles are limited. Strontium helps corals build exoskeletons and is used in desensitizing toothpastes. Barium is toxic, though some ciliates concentrate barium salts in organelles called Müller vesicles for gravity sensing. Beryllium and radium are both toxic, beryllium because of chronic lung inflammation (berylliosis) and radium because of its radioactivity, which causes bone damage, anemia, and cataracts. Radioactive alkaline earths behave chemically like calcium and concentrate in bone, a property exploited in targeted radiotherapy of bone cancers but also the reason strontium-90 is a long-term contaminant from nuclear fallout.
