# Climate change in the Middle East and North Africa

The Middle East and North Africa (MENA) is both a major fossil fuel producer and one of the regions most exposed to climate change. In 2018 it emitted 3.2 billion tonnes of CO₂, about 8.7% of the global total, while holding roughly 6% of the world's population. Ninety-seven percent of regional energy still comes from oil, gas, and coal, and MENA sits on about 60% of proven oil reserves and 45% of natural gas reserves. Saudi Arabia and Iran, the world's 7th and 9th largest CO₂ emitters, together account for 40% of regional emissions. Iran is also the world's largest fossil fuel subsidizer.

Models project the region warming faster than the global average, especially in summer, and losing rainfall. Under current trajectories, parts of MENA risk becoming uninhabitable before 2100.

## How the climate is changing

The 1998–2012 Middle Eastern drought was the worst in roughly 900 years, and a NASA study identified human-driven warming as a major amplifier. Max Planck Institute models show the number of very hot days in MENA has already doubled since the 1970s. Under current pathways, heatwaves could last 80 days per year by 2050 and 118 days by 2100. Average maximum temperatures on the hottest days, now around 43 °C, could approach 50 °C, and heat-related mortality could rise to roughly 20 times today's level by 2100.

Water stress is the most acute pressure. Twelve of the seventeen most water-stressed countries are in MENA, and over 60% of the population lives in high-stress zones, against a global average of 35%. Per-capita renewable water averages 1,274 m³ per year, below the 1,700 m³ World Bank threshold, and agriculture consumes 85% of it. A 30% decline in crop yields is projected by 2050, and 88% of North African crops are rainfed, with no irrigation buffer.

## Rising seas

About 60 million people lived in MENA coastal areas in 2010, projected to reach 100 million by 2030. Egypt is most exposed: one-third of the Nile Delta and much of Alexandria sit below mean sea level, protected by ageing sea walls. A 1-metre sea level rise would submerge 12–15% of Egypt's farmland, displace an estimated 6.7 million people, and erase over 90% of the region's coastal and freshwater wetlands. A 50 cm rise would still displace about 2 million and cause roughly US$35 billion in damages.

## Climate, drought, and conflict

The 2007–2010 Syrian drought has been linked by some researchers to the unrest that followed, but a 2017 study led by Jan Selby disputed the connection, and the evidence remains contested. Iraq shows a clearer pattern: Tigris–Euphrates flows, which supply 98% of Iraq's surface water, have fallen 30–40% over the last 40 years, partly from upstream damming. The UN ranks Iraq the world's 5th most climate-vulnerable country.

## Mitigation and policy

MENA has exceptional solar and wind potential, and renewables use far less water than fossil fuel extraction, yet they supply only about 1% of regional primary energy. At COP22 in Marrakech in 2016, 47 countries including Morocco, Tunisia, Yemen, Lebanon, and Palestine pledged 100% renewable energy by 2050. Morocco's Ouarzazate Solar Power Station, the world's largest concentrated solar plant, was expected to reach 2,000 MW by 2020. The World Bank's 2016 MENA Climate Action Plan committed 18–30% of regional lending, then about US$1.5 billion per year, to climate projects.

Eleven MENA states attended the Paris Agreement negotiations, but as of 2021 Eritrea, Iran, Iraq, Libya, and Yemen had not ratified it. The UAE has ratified but has been judged to have "insufficient" targets. Morocco has set a 17–42% emissions cut and a 52% renewable electricity share by 2050, reaching 28% renewables by 2018, and was ranked 7th in the 2023 Climate Change Performance Index. Iraq's oil and gas sectors produced an estimated 9% of global methane emissions in 2019, and after ratifying Paris in 2021 Iraq pledged a 15% emissions cut by 2035 focused on curbing that methane.
