# Climate change and infectious diseases

Climate change is reshaping where, when, and how infectious diseases spread. A 2022 review found that more than half of the 375 known human pathogenic diseases (218) have already been aggravated by climate change. Rising temperatures, shifting rainfall, and more frequent extreme weather alter the biology and geography of pathogens, their vectors (mosquitoes, ticks, and sandflies, which carry pathogens between hosts), and human hosts, while also worsening water and food safety.

## Why vectors drive the strongest signal

Mosquitoes and ticks cannot regulate their body temperature, so their survival, biting rate, and the speed at which parasites mature inside them all depend on local climate. Warmer conditions speed up larval development, lengthen transmission seasons, and push populations toward the poles and to higher elevations. Since 1970, malaria incidence has risen sharply in highland East Africa as warming has moved the altitudinal ceiling above cities like Nairobi and Harare. The *Aedes aegypti* mosquito that carries dengue has established populations in France and Italy, and reported dengue cases grew from about 505,000 in 2000 to 14.6 million in 2024, with true infections likely 100–400 million per year. Zika could expose more than 1.3 billion additional people by 2050 as *Aedes* habitat expands. Ticks carrying Lyme disease and tick-borne encephalitis are spreading north in Europe, Canada, and the northeastern United States, and warmer springs are lengthening their feeding season.

## Waterborne and foodborne illness

Warmer waters and heavier floods favour bacteria such as *Vibrio cholerae* (cholera) and other *Vibrio* species. The coastline suitable for *Vibrio* transmission expanded 35% in the Baltic, 25% in the Atlantic Northeast, and 4% in the Pacific Northwest between 2011 and 2021, and a 1°C warming in Zanzibar doubled confirmed cholera cases. Flooding overwhelms sanitation, contaminating drinking water and food, while drought concentrates pathogens in scarce supplies. These conditions simultaneously raise the risk of typhoid, hepatitis A, and diarrhoeal disease, the third leading cause of death in children under five, killing about 444,000 annually. Harmful algal blooms are also becoming more frequent as cyanobacteria thrive above 25°C, sometimes producing neurotoxins and hepatotoxins.

## Zoonotic spillover

Climate-driven ecosystem disruption, including deforestation, habitat fragmentation, and shifting wildlife ranges, increases contact between wildlife, livestock, and people. Bats, which host many viruses, are moving closer to populated areas as aridity increases. A 2021 study suggested that climate-driven shifts in bat ranges may have contributed to the conditions that enabled SARS-CoV-2 to emerge, though the WHO has stated there is no direct evidence linking climate change to COVID-19 transmission. The fungus *Candida auris*, which appeared on three continents around the same time, can tolerate mammalian body temperature, a trait researchers have linked to environmental adaptation to warming.

## Unequal vulnerability

Nearly one in three people globally lacks safe drinking water, a structural weakness that amplifies climate-driven outbreaks. Low-income countries with weak sanitation and health infrastructure bear the greatest burden, and the World Health Organization lists climate change among the top threats to global health. Beyond physical disease, the climate crisis raises chronic stress and cortisol levels, which suppress immune defence. Effective responses combine greenhouse-gas mitigation with stronger surveillance, vector control, vaccination, water and sanitation investment, and "One Health" programmes that link human, animal, and environmental health.
