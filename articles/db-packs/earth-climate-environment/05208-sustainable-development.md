# Sustainable development

Sustainable development is growth and human progress that meets today's needs without reducing the capacity of future generations to meet theirs. It tries to keep three goals in balance: a healthy environment, a functioning economy, and a fair society. This balance is often called the *Triple Bottom Line*, and its three parts are also called the dimensions, pillars, or domains of sustainability.

The idea predates the phrase. Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century European foresters, including John Evelyn in *Sylva* (1662) and Hans Carl von Carlowitz in *Sylvicultura oeconomica* (1713), argued that timber had to be harvested no faster than forests regrew. Their thinking fed later conservationists such as Gifford Pinchot and Aldo Leopold. Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* (1962) brought the wider environmental crisis into public view. Kenneth Boulding's 1966 essay "The Economics of the Coming Spaceship Earth" framed the economy as a subsystem of a finite ecosphere, and Garrett Hardin's 1968 article popularised the *tragedy of the commons*, the idea that shared resources get overused when no one owns them.

The modern definition came in 1987 from the UN World Commission on Environment and Development, chaired by Gro Harlem Brundtland, in *Our Common Future* (the *Brundtland Report*):

> Sustainable development is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

The report stresses two ideas inside that sentence: the essential needs of the world's poor, which should be given overriding priority; and the limits set by technology and social organisation on what the environment can provide. A useful distinction drawn by UNESCO: *sustainability* is the long-term goal of a world that endures, while *sustainable development* names the processes and pathways that may get us there.

Global policy carried the idea into action in two waves. The 1992 Earth Summit in Rio produced Agenda 21, which named public participation and integrated decision-making as prerequisites for sustainable development, and the Rio agreements, the first shared global sustainability agenda, though without binding targets. The Millennium Development Goals (2000–2015) focused on reducing poverty through growth and trade. Their successors, the 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), were adopted by the UN General Assembly in 2015 for the year 2030. The SDGs are broader and more people-centred, with concrete targets and indicators across poverty, climate, biodiversity, peace, and inequality. Of the 17 goals, 11 include targets on equity or inclusion, and SDG 10 is devoted entirely to inequality.

Economist Herman Daly stated three operational rules in 1990 that still anchor environmental sustainability: harvest renewable resources no faster than they regenerate; for non-renewables, develop renewable substitutes at the same pace; and emit no more waste than the environment can absorb. The related idea of a *circular economy* routes materials through reuse, repair, remanufacture, and recycling instead of a linear take-make-dump path; the European Commission's Circular Economy Action Plan (2020) pushes this approach in the EU. A 2019 summary for policymakers from the Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services argued that nothing less than transformative change in agriculture, consumption, fishing, and water management can keep humanity inside planetary limits. One projection warns that, on present trends, humanity could consume around 140 billion tonnes of minerals, ores, fossil fuels, and biomass per year by 2050, roughly three times today's amount, unless growth is decoupled from resource use.

The concept is widely used and widely criticised. Critics call it an oxymoron, arguing that growth-based development is inherently unsustainable, and note that *development* itself is never precisely defined. The Brundtland definition has been attacked as vague and easy to appropriate, and a "weak" reading that promises growth, social welfare, and a healthy environment all at once has been called indefensible. Natural capital, the stock of ecosystems, soil, water, air, and species, cannot always be swapped for built capital, and services such as ozone-layer protection or the climate-stabilising role of the Amazonian forest cannot be replaced at all. Digitalisation is often sold as a sustainability tool, but recent scholarship suggests that the energy and material footprint of digital systems can worsen environmental outcomes even as it helps the social and economic sides. Scholars have mapped four competing policy frames: mainstream sustainability (politically and economically conservative), progressive sustainability (economically conservative, politically reformist), a limits discourse (economically reformist, politically conservative), and radical sustainability (transformative, seeking to break with current structures).

One practical lever is Education for Sustainable Development (ESD), a UN term for teaching that builds the knowledge, skills, values, and attitudes needed for a sustainable and just society. Agenda 21 named education as essential, and SDG 12 target 12.8 commits the world by 2030 to ensuring that people everywhere have the information and awareness for sustainable lifestyles in harmony with nature. The 1997 Thessaloniki Declaration, drafted by UNESCO and the Greek government, linked sustainability not only to the natural environment but also to poverty, health, food security, democracy, human rights, and peace.

Sustainable development rests on a single trade-off: present welfare, future welfare, and the planetary systems that make both possible must be weighed against each other, and the weighing has to be done by the people whose welfare is at stake.
