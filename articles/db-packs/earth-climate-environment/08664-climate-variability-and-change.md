# Climate variability and change

Climate is the long-term average of weather in a region, distinct from day-to-day weather. **Climate variability** covers all fluctuations beyond individual weather events, from seasons to ice ages; **climate change** usually means shifts that persist for decades or longer.

The **climate system** runs on an energy budget: nearly all incoming energy comes from the Sun, and the balance between what arrives and what radiates back to space sets Earth's temperature. When more energy comes in than goes out, the planet warms; when more leaves, it cools. This balance is reshaped by two kinds of influences working together.

**External forcings** are changes imposed from outside the system's normal workings. They include variations in solar output, volcanic eruptions, slow shifts in Earth's orbit (Milankovitch cycles), continental drift, and human emissions of greenhouse gases and aerosols. **Internal variability** arises from processes within the system itself, especially the ocean and atmosphere redistributing heat. Examples include the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO), the Pacific Decadal Oscillation, and the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation. Together these internal modes can shift regional climates for years to decades without any change in external forcing.

The system also contains **feedbacks**, processes that amplify or dampen an initial push. Melting Arctic sea ice exposes dark ocean that absorbs more heat, a positive feedback. Increased cloud cover can reflect more sunlight, a negative feedback. Some feedbacks are fast (atmospheric cooling after a volcanic eruption); others are slow (thermal expansion of seawater, which takes thousands of years). When feedbacks push the system past a threshold, change can become rapid or irreversible.

The ocean dominates the system's memory. It holds hundreds of times more mass than the atmosphere and responds to changes far more slowly. Heat absorbed at the surface can be stored in the deep ocean for centuries, which is why past climate shifts, such as the abrupt circulation changes during the last ice age, could persist long after their trigger disappeared.

## Common oscillations

Several internal oscillations appear on different timescales. ENSO swings between warm El Niño and cool La Niña phases in the tropical Pacific every two to seven years, redistributing heat worldwide. The Madden–Julian Oscillation moves tropical rainfall eastward over 30–60 days. The North Atlantic Oscillation and Arctic Oscillation shift the path of storms across the Northern Hemisphere on weekly to monthly timescales. On longer scales, the Pacific Decadal Oscillation (decades) and Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation (55–70 years) shape regional rainfall and temperature. These are quasiperiodic, not perfectly regular, because random weather disturbances interact with the ocean's thermal inertia.

## Long-term natural drivers

Three external mechanisms shape climate over very long timescales. **Milankovitch cycles** (changes in Earth's eccentricity, axial tilt, and precession) alter the seasonal and geographic distribution of sunlight and are the main pacemaker of the glacial–interglacial cycles of the past 2.6 million years. **Solar output** has risen about 25% over Earth's history; the faint young Sun paradox asks how liquid water existed when the Sun was dimmer, with high greenhouse gas concentrations the leading explanation. **Plate tectonics** rearranges continents and ocean basins, changing circulation patterns: the closing of the Isthmus of Panama about 5 million years ago altered the Gulf Stream and may have triggered Northern Hemisphere glaciation.

**Volcanism** is a short-term but powerful forcing. Eruptions that inject more than about 100,000 tonnes of SO₂ into the stratosphere reflect sunlight and cool the planet for a few years. The 1991 Mount Pinatubo eruption lowered global temperatures by roughly 0.5 °C for up to three years; the 1815 Tambora eruption caused the "Year Without a Summer." Human CO₂ emissions now exceed volcanic output by a factor of 100–300.

## Life as a climate force

Living things actively regulate climate. The evolution of oxygenic photosynthesis drew down atmospheric CO₂ and triggered glaciation 2.3 billion years ago. Burial of plant matter over millions of years formed coal seams, locking carbon out of the atmosphere. Marine phytoplankton drew down CO₂ during the warm Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum 55 million years ago, and large Arctic *Azolla* blooms cooled the early Eocene about 49 million years ago. Grassland expansion over the past 40 million years increased weathering and helped drive long-term cooling.

## Evidence and measurement

Direct temperature records cover only the mid-19th century onward; satellite data begins in the 1970s. For earlier climates, scientists rely on **proxies**: tree rings, ice cores (which trap ancient air including CO₂), ocean and lake sediments, pollen, and beetle remains. These show that atmospheric CO₂ and temperature have tracked each other closely through at least the past 800,000 years.

## Modern warming

Since the Industrial Revolution, human activity has become a forcing comparable to the great natural ones. Burning fossil fuels, deforestation, livestock (cattle produce methane), and industrial aerosols have altered the composition of the atmosphere. The result is unambiguous: global average surface temperature has risen, the oceans have absorbed about 90% of the excess heat, land has warmed faster than sea, the Northern Hemisphere has warmed faster than the Southern, and the upper atmosphere has cooled while the lower atmosphere has warmed, the signature of greenhouse warming. Arctic sea ice has shrunk, the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have lost mass since 2002, and global sea level has risen through thermal expansion and meltwater.
