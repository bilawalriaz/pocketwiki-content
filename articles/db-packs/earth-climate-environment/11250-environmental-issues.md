# Environmental issues

An environmental issue is a disruption of ecosystem function so severe that recovery is uncertain, and catastrophic when collapse becomes certain. Most contemporary issues are anthropogenic, meaning caused by humans. They are driven by population growth, overconsumption, fossil-fuel combustion, and economic expansion. The term *Anthropocene*, introduced by atmospheric scientist Paul Crutzen in the mid-1970s, names the current geological epoch in which human activity is the dominant force shaping the planet.

## Major categories

The United Nations groups the most severe problems into a "triple planetary crisis": climate change, pollution, and biodiversity loss. Common forms of pollution include air, water, soil, plastic, noise, light, thermal, and radioactive contamination. Pollution is classed by source as point source (concentrated, such as a factory or mine) or nonpoint source (diffuse, such as agricultural runoff or microplastics).

Environmental degradation is the deterioration of air, water, soil, and ecosystems through resource depletion, habitat destruction, species extinction, and pollution. It can be direct, like deforestation, or indirect, like the slow accumulation of greenhouse gases crossing climate tipping points (thresholds beyond which a small change produces a large, often irreversible shift). Planetary boundaries are scientifically defined limits within which humanity can safely operate; a 2022 review found that chemical pollution has exceeded the boundary for novel entities, meaning synthetic chemical outputs have surpassed the safe range for the planet as a whole.

## Drivers

The wealthiest populations, through excessive consumption, produce the bulk of environmental impact. Livestock production is a major climate driver because methane and nitrous oxide from cattle and manure management have far higher global warming potentials than carbon dioxide. Burning fossil fuels for electricity, transport, industry, and heating, together with deforestation, is the primary cause of a heated atmosphere. Neoliberal economic policies that treat the economy and the ecosphere as separate systems accelerate ecological unraveling.

## Consequences

In 2019, pollution killed roughly nine million people worldwide, about one in six deaths that year, with air pollution causing around three-quarters of them. The 2023 IPCC report warned that every increment of warming intensifies heatwaves, extreme rainfall, and other hazards, and that nearly half the world's population lives in highly vulnerable regions. Annual costs in the United States from air pollution and climate-related illness exceed US$820 billion; global climate-related losses are projected at US$1.7 to US$3.1 trillion per year by 2050, and unpriced environmental externalities from agriculture, fossil fuels, forestry, infrastructure, fisheries, and mining total US$10.5 to US$22.6 trillion annually.

## Distributional effects

Environmental harm falls disproportionately on poor, marginalized, and politically weak communities. Polluting facilities, extractive projects, and toxic-waste sites cluster near low-income and racialized populations. This pattern drove the rise of the environmental justice movement, which began in the United States in the 1980s under the influence of the civil rights movement, then expanded globally as burdens shifted to the Global South through extractivism and the global waste trade. Indigenous and peasant communities, whose livelihoods depend directly on land or ocean, are frequent flashpoints, and environmental defenders face lethal threats when confronting multinational corporations.

## Governance and law

Responses operate at local, national, and international levels. Environmental law, which emerged prominently after the 1972 Stockholm Conference and the 1992 Rio Declaration, rests on principles including the precautionary principle (act to prevent harm even without full scientific certainty), the polluter pays principle (the polluting party bears cleanup costs), and intergenerational equity (current generations must not compromise future ones' environment). Key instruments include the 1992 Convention on Biological Diversity, the 1997 Kyoto Protocol, and the 2015 Paris Agreement. The United Nations Environment Programme, founded in 1972, is the largest international body, alongside groups like the International Union for Conservation of Nature, Greenpeace, Friends of the Earth, and WWF.

Environmental impact assessment (EIA) requires decision-makers to identify, predict, evaluate, and mitigate the biophysical and social effects of a proposed project, policy, or program before committing to it. EIAs do not mandate a predetermined environmental outcome; they require justification in light of environmental studies and public comment.

## Conflicts and the movement

Environmental conflicts are social disputes triggered by degradation or unequal distribution of environmental resources. The Environmental Justice Atlas documented 3,100 such conflicts worldwide by April 2020, with many more unrecorded. Typical flashpoints are resource extraction and hazardous-waste disposal, which can also worsen natural disasters and displace populations as environmental migrants. The contemporary environmental movement traces its origins to the Industrial Revolution, expanded through the 20th century around figures like Rachel Carson, and now addresses climate change, biodiversity loss, and pollution across a network of NGOs, scientists, religious groups, and grassroots organizations.

A 2022 review found that chemical pollution has exceeded the planetary boundary for novel entities, confirming that anthropogenic impact now surpasses the safe operating range for the planet as a whole.
