# Illustrative model of greenhouse effect on climate change

In the long run Earth radiates the same power it absorbs from sunlight. When something shifts this balance, the surface warms or cools until emission matches absorption again. Carbon dioxide is the main human-driven agent of this shift, because it changes how infrared light escapes the ground.

## Why CO₂ traps heat

CO₂ absorbs the ground's infrared radiation mainly between 13 and 17 micrometres (µm). Outside this window the atmosphere is nearly transparent, so adding more CO₂ changes little. Inside it, the absorption per molecule (the cross section) varies sharply with wavelength: optical depth is roughly 0.1 at 13 µm, climbs past 10 at 14 µm, exceeds 100 near 15 µm, then falls through 10 at 16 µm, 1 at 17 µm, and below 0.1 by 18 µm. Optical depth measures how much light a layer extinguishes along a path; doubling CO₂ doubles every optical depth in this band because optical depth is proportional to the number of CO₂ molecules above a given altitude (Beer–Lambert law).

The climate effect concentrates in two narrow windows where optical depth sits between about 0.5 and 9: a band near 17 µm and a narrower one near 13.5 µm. In those windows, adding CO₂ pushes the radiating layer upward to colder air, so less energy escapes and the surface warms until emission rises enough to restore balance.

## Where the CO₂ lives

About 88% of atmospheric CO₂ sits in the troposphere, the lower atmosphere extending from the ground to roughly 11 km. Ground-level air averages 288 K (15 °C); temperature falls about 6.5 K per km through the troposphere, reaching about 220 K (−53 °C) at its top. Above it lies the tropopause, about 9 km thick and roughly isothermal, holding most of the remaining CO₂. CO₂ density falls exponentially with altitude, with a scale height of 6.3 km in the troposphere (density drops to 37% there, 17% at 11 km) and a shorter scale of 4.2 km through the tropopause. A scale height is the altitude over which density falls by a factor of about 2.7.

## How much radiation CO₂ blocks

A one-layer model that treats the troposphere as a single slab shows that the change in outgoing infrared flux per e-fold change in CO₂ concentration sums contributions from the 13.5 µm and 17 µm windows to about 5 W/m². The result depends only logarithmically on CO₂ amount, so a doubling reduces outgoing flux by roughly 3.4 W/m² in this simple model. More elaborate treatments converge near 3.1 W/m² per doubling, the value used in the rest of this calculation.

This forcing arises because the troposphere accounts for about 88% of atmospheric CO₂, while the tropopause above it holds 12% and sets a ceiling on the effect: once the radiating layer reaches the tropopause, adding more CO₂ no longer raises the effective emission altitude, so the forcing saturates.

## Comparing to Earth's total flux

Earth absorbs a fraction (1 − a) of the solar constant S₀ = 1360 W/m² across its cross section and re-emits over its full surface. With Earth's albedo a ≈ 0.3, the mean outgoing flux is j = ¼ · (1 − a) · S₀ ≈ 240 W/m². A CO₂-doubling forcing of 3.1 W/m² is therefore about 1.3% of the total outgoing flux.

## From forcing to temperature

By the Stefan–Boltzmann law, emitted power per unit area scales as T⁴, so a relative change in flux splits into a change in effective emissivity plus four times the relative change in temperature. Holding the total emitted flux fixed, a 1.3% forcing produces only a quarter as large a relative temperature change:

ΔT ≈ ¼ · 288 K · 1.3% ≈ 0.9 K

So the direct warming from a CO₂ doubling, ignoring feedbacks, is roughly 1 K.

## Ice–albedo feedback

Warming melts ice, lowers Earth's reflectivity, and increases the sunlight absorbed, which amplifies the warming. Between 1979 and 2015 Northern Hemisphere sea ice shrank by about 2×10¹² m² on average, roughly 0.4% of Earth's 510×10¹² m² surface, while global temperature rose by about 0.6 °C. Scaling suggests about 2% of Earth's surface converts from ice to darker ocean or land per 1 °C of warming. Because ice and ocean differ in albedo by about two-thirds, and most of this change occurs near 60° latitude, the global albedo drops by roughly 0.7% per 1 °C, about half the 1.3% direct forcing. Each round of ice loss then adds half as much new forcing as the previous round, so the geometric series sums to a factor of 2: the feedback roughly doubles the 1 K direct warming to about 2 K in the short term, and to about 3 K once slower feedbacks are included.
