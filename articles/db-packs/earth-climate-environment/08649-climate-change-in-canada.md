# Climate change in Canada

Canada is warming roughly twice as fast as the global average, because human-caused greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions continue to rise. GHGs trap heat in the atmosphere; their accumulation is the basic mechanism behind the warming. Canada is a high per-person emitter and an early, visible victim of the change it helps drive.

## Emissions: scale, sources, and trajectory

Canada was the world's 11th largest carbon dioxide emitter in 2022 and 7th largest emitter of total GHGs in 2021. In 2020 only Saudi Arabia had higher per-person GHG emissions among G20 countries, and between 1950 and 2000 Canada had the highest per-person emissions of any first-world country. Canada ranks 10th in cumulative historical emissions, holding about 2.6% of the world total, split roughly evenly between fossil fuel combustion and deforestation or land use.

Total emissions reached 708 million tonnes of CO₂ equivalent (Mt CO₂eq) in 2022, up 1.3% from 2021 but still below 2019. This was 1.5% of the global total in 2020, down from 1.8% in 2005, though higher than the 602 Mt CO₂eq in 1990. Emissions have been roughly flat since the mid-2000s while the economy grew, a pattern of slow decline.

In 2022 transport, oil and gas extraction, and fugitive emissions together produced 82% of national emissions. The oil and gas sector alone emits about 195 Mt CO₂eq (27% of the total), driven by the energy-intensive processing of Alberta's tar sands. From 1990 to 2017 the sector's emissions grew 84%, and oil sands production emissions alone rose 467% by 2022. Transportation adds 24%, mostly road transport (144 Mt CO₂eq) from cars and long-haul trucks serving a vast, low-density country. Electricity is comparatively clean: 81% came from non-emitting sources in 2017, mainly hydro, nuclear, wind, and solar. Only five provinces still use coal, and remote communities such as all of Nunavut rely on diesel generators.

## Physical impacts

Environment and Climate Change Canada has concluded it is "extremely likely" that observed warming is driven by human GHG emissions, and that a 2 °C global rise implies a 3–4 °C rise for Canada. The national annual average temperature over land increased 1.7 °C between 1948 and 2016, with winter warming of 3.3 °C far outpacing summer warming of 1.5 °C. Northern Canada, the Prairies, and northern British Columbia warmed the most, up to 2.3 °C annually in the north, with some Arctic locations near 3.5 °C, about three times the global mean rate. The southern Maritimes warmed less than 1 °C.

Precipitation has generally increased since 1950, especially in the north and in winter, spring, and fall. Observed changes include longer growing seasons, more heatwaves, fewer cold spells, thawing permafrost, earlier river ice break-up, and earlier tree budding. Fire-spread days are projected to rise 35–400% by 2050, most severely in coastal and temperate forests. Sea levels could rise up to 1.6 m in some areas, with the greatest impact on southwestern and southeastern coasts and waters north of Yukon.

The 2023 wildfire season burned over 15 million hectares, more than seven times the 40-year average, releasing 480 Mt of carbon, about 23% of global wildfire CO₂ emissions that year. By 2024 fires in the northwest had become a year-round rather than seasonal phenomenon. Milder winters also enabled a mountain pine beetle epidemic from 1996 to 2015 that killed trees across 18 million hectares.

## Human and ecological consequences

Warmer winters have expanded blacklegged tick populations, and reported Lyme disease cases rose from 144 in 2009 to 2,025 in 2017. Drought repeatedly hits Prairie agriculture: during 2002 and 2009 droughts, Alberta ranchers sold herds because feed prices rose while cattle prices fell. Eight of Canada's ten costliest natural disasters have occurred since 2013, with the 2016 Fort McMurray wildfire the most expensive at $5.96 billion.

For the Inuit, warming and acidifying oceans are shifting whale and seal migration, threatening the traditional food system. Ringed seal populations have dropped sharply, undermining *ningiqtuq*, the Inuit cultural practice of sharing hunted meat that holds communities together. The Gwichʼin face parallel loss as caribou, central to their identity, decline with warming and ice loss. In the boreal forest, trees are migrating north and uphill, with conifers giving way to aspen in drier, fire-prone zones, and many species unable to keep pace.

## Policy history and the gap with targets

Canada signed the Kyoto Protocol but never met its 6% reduction target; 2009 emissions ran 17% above 1990 levels. Canada formally withdrew in December 2011, citing the risk of large financial penalties. Under the Paris Agreement, Canada pledged a 30% cut below 2005 levels by 2030, then in July 2021 raised this to 40–45% through the Canadian Net-Zero Emissions Accountability Act, with a net-zero goal by 2050. In 2019 the House of Commons declared a national climate emergency.

Carbon pricing is the central federal tool: a national carbon tax from 2018, starting at $20/tonne and rising to $50/tonne in 2022, with revenue returned through rebates. British Columbia introduced North America's first revenue-neutral carbon tax in 2008 at $10/tonne, rising to $50/tonne by 2022, but in 2018 admitted it would miss its 2020 target, having stalled at 6.5% below 2007 levels. Ontario eliminated coal-fired generation by 2014, cutting electricity emissions from 36.5 Mt to 4.2 Mt, but later rolled back climate programs. Alberta, the highest per-capita emitter at 67.12 t CO₂eq, set the first North American carbon price in 2007 and has cycled through multiple plans. The Climate Action Tracker rates Canada's overall action as "insufficient," and one 2021 analysis estimated Canada would need to increase its Paris commitment by 215% for a 50% chance of staying below 1.5 °C.
