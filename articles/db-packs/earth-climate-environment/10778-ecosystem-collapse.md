# Ecosystem collapse

An ecosystem is a community of organisms interacting with their physical environment. These systems are never static; they continually absorb small shocks and recover. **Ecosystem collapse** is what happens when disturbances push the system past a critical threshold, or tipping point, beyond which it can no longer return to its original state. The defining features of the original ecosystem are lost, including the services it provided to other species and to humans. A collapsed area rarely becomes lifeless. Instead, a different, often less useful ecosystem takes its place, and recovery is usually slow, expensive, or impossible.

## What collapse looks like

Collapse is a transition, not a single event. The IUCN Red List of Ecosystems, in development since 2008, grades ecosystems from Vulnerable through Endangered and Critically Endangered, with Collapsed as the endpoint. Its criteria include shrinking spatial extent, declining populations of key species, disrupted ecological processes, and the loss of the services the system once provided.

Two patterns recur. First, collapse is often abrupt once a threshold is crossed. A 2020 analysis found that once a "point of no return" is reached, breakdowns unfold rapidly rather than gradually. The Amazon could shift to a savannah-like mix of trees and grass within 50 years of crossing its threshold; Caribbean coral reefs could collapse within 15. Second, loss of resilience can be measured directly. Scientists track **critical slowing down**, the tendency of a system to recover more and more slowly from small perturbations, as an early warning that a tipping point is near. A 2022 study found that more than three-quarters of the Amazon has been losing resilience since the early 2000s, a signal that it is approaching a critical transition.

## Drivers

Natural disturbances such as fires, floods, disease, and invasive species have always caused collapses. The Carboniferous rainforest collapse, around 300 million years ago, was triggered by cooling and drying climate and wiped out the giant lycopsid forests and the giant arthropods and amphibians that depended on them, clearing the way for seed-bearing plants and early reptiles. Lakes Baikal and Hovsgol collapsed during the Last Glacial Maximum and later re-evolved with new species.

In the modern era, human activity has multiplied the frequency and intensity of these pressures. Estimates indicate that 87% of oceans and 77% of land surface have been altered by people, and 30% of global land area is now degraded. The main mechanisms are habitat destruction and fragmentation, overexploitation of species, pollution, and anthropogenic climate change. These drivers rarely act alone. The Rapa Nui forests of Easter Island collapsed under the combined weight of overexploitation, climate change, and introduced rats. The northern Benguela upwelling system off southwestern Africa shifted from a sardine-dominated fishery to one dominated by jellyfish and pelagic goby after overfishing coincided with two warm Benguela Niño events in 1974 and 1984. The Grand Banks cod fishery off Newfoundland collapsed in the early 1990s when overfishing reduced stocks to about 1% of historical levels.

## Case studies and current risk

The Aral Sea, once one of the world's largest lakes, shrank to 10% of its original size after Soviet-era irrigation projects diverted its feeder rivers. By 1997 it had split into hypersaline remnants surrounded by desert steppe. The Great Barrier Reef has lost roughly 50% of its coral as of 2023, with bleaching events in 1998 and a record spread in 2020, driven mainly by ocean warming and pollution.

Ecosystems currently on the brink include the Kimberley and Cape York Peninsula savannas in Australia, where fire-promoting weeds and grazing pressure are converting woodland to grassland, and the Great Salt Lake in Utah, which has lost over 73% of its water and 60% of its surface area since 1850. Updated 2022 estimates project that at 1.5 °C of warming only about 0.2% of the world's coral reefs would still survive marine heatwaves, falling to 0% at 2 °C.

## Can collapse be reversed?

Sometimes, but rarely easily. Where reversal has succeeded, it demands active intervention and long timescales, conditions far costlier than preventing collapse in the first place. Conservation must therefore act on early warning signals before visible crisis, the same logic that obliges biologists to protect a species before it falls below a viable population and an extinction debt is locked in. The IUCN Red List of Ecosystems is younger and less comprehensive than its species counterpart, and criteria for assessing collapse risk are still being refined.
