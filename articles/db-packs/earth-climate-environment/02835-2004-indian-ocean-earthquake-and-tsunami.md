# 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami

The 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami was a megathrust earthquake (an earthquake at a boundary where one tectonic plate is forced beneath another) and resulting ocean waves that struck on 26 December 2004. It killed an estimated 227,898 people across 14 countries and is one of the deadliest natural disasters in recorded history. The event was unusual in scale: the earthquake was one of the most powerful ever recorded, and the waves crossed the entire Indian Ocean to reach East Africa.

## The earthquake

The earthquake, known as the Sumatra–Andaman earthquake, began at 07:58:53 local time off the coast of Aceh, Indonesia. It occurred along the Sunda megathrust, where the Indian plate slides beneath the Burma plate at a subduction zone. The rupture extended 1,200–1,300 km along this boundary, making it the longest ever observed, and shaking lasted at least ten minutes.

Magnitude is reported as Mw 9.2–9.3 (Mw, or moment magnitude, measures the total energy released by an earthquake). This made the event the strongest earthquake in Asia on record and one of the strongest worldwide since 1900. The sudden vertical displacement of the seafloor by several metres moved an estimated 30 km³ of water, which is what generated the tsunami. The energy released was so large that the entire planet vibrated, and Earth's rotation was altered, shortening the day by 2.68 microseconds.

## The tsunami

In the deep ocean, tsunami waves travel at 500–1,000 km/h but are only about 600 mm high and pass unnoticed beneath ships. As the waves entered shallow coastal water, they slowed and the water piled upward into waves up to 30 m high. This process of wave growth in shallow water is why the same open-ocean disturbance becomes catastrophic at the shore.

Coastlines close to the rupture, in Indonesia, were struck within minutes. Distant coastlines in Somalia and South Africa were reached hours later. The Indian Ocean had no tsunami warning system, so almost no coastal community received advance notice. Damage was concentrated in Indonesia, Sri Lanka, India, and Thailand, with the waves causing destruction as far as East Africa. A tsunami that causes damage across an entire ocean basin is called a teletsunami, a category the 2004 event fits.

## Human impact

Indonesia suffered the most, with around 170,000 deaths. About one-third of the dead were children, and up to 9,000 foreign tourists also died. The waves contaminated freshwater supplies with salt and destroyed coastal ecosystems including mangroves and coral reefs, undermining local fishing and tourism economies for years.

A worldwide humanitarian response followed, with donations totalling over US$14 billion. The devastation also had a political effect in Aceh, where the Free Aceh Movement (GAM) declared a cease-fire on 28 December 2004. On 15 August 2005, the Indonesian government and GAM signed a peace agreement that explicitly cited the tsunami as a reason for ending the long-running conflict.

## Warnings and hazards after 2004

The lack of an Indian Ocean warning system was a major reason the death toll was so high. The Indian Ocean Tsunami Warning System was formed in early 2005 to fill that gap, and its coverage and effectiveness continue to be evaluated.

Before 2004, scientists thought that giant earthquakes required young, buoyant crust, fast plate convergence, and the absence of back-arc basins (basins formed behind a volcanic arc on the overriding plate). The Sumatra–Andaman event violated all three assumptions, forcing a reassessment of earthquake hazard in similar subduction zones. Sediment records suggest the region last produced a comparable tsunami around A.D. 1300–1450, a recurrence interval of several hundred years.
