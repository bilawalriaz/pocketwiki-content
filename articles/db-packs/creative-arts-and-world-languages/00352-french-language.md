# French language

French is a Romance language of the Indo-European family. It descends from the Vulgar Latin spoken in Roman Gaul, was reshaped by Celtic (Gaulish) and Germanic (Frankish) contact, and passed through Old French and Middle French before reaching its modern form. It is official or working in dozens of states and major bodies (UN, EU, NATO, IOC), with 321–396 million speakers (OIF, 2022–2025) and growth driven by sub-Saharan Africa, which holds 65–70% of Francophones.

## Origins

Latin arrived in Gaul with Roman conquest (1st c. BCE–5th c. CE) and spread from cities outward. Around 90% of the population stayed indigenous, so Gaulish, a Celtic tongue, persisted into the 6th century and left 150–240 loanwords (plants, animals, rural measures), some sound changes, and a syntactic calque that became *oui* (from *hoc ille*, "that, yes"). Gaulish literacy vanished by the 5th century.

Frankish invasions (5th c. onward) split the territory into two dialect groups, named for their word for "yes": the *langues d'oïl* in the north (ancestors of French) and the *langues d'oc* in the south (ancestors of Occitan). Frankish contributed about 15% of modern vocabulary, including *guerre*, *bleu*, and the pronoun *on*, and gave the language its name. Old French kept a Latin-style two-case system longer than most Romance tongues and produced diphthongs under heavy syllabic stress.

## Standardisation

The Francien dialect of the Paris basin became the prestige norm. During the Crusader Kingdom of Jerusalem (mid 13th c.), French-speaking nobles replaced Latin with an *Outremer* French koiné to write the Assizes of Jerusalem, then brought the administrative model back to France under Louis IX, professionalising the royal chancery. The Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts (1539) made French the sole language of law and administration in France, and the Académie française (1635) was founded to regulate it. Noun declensions dropped out during Middle French.

From the 17th century, French replaced Latin as the *lingua franca* of diplomacy, science, and literature. The Revolution and Third Republic turned schooling into a tool for eradicating regional languages (*patois*): Grégoire's 1794 report aimed to "annihilate the patois", and teachers shamed children out of Breton and Occitan (the *Vergonha* movement). The bilingual 1919 Treaty of Versailles marked the rise of English at French's expense. The 1992 Constitution enshrined French as the sole official language of the French state.

Colonialism carried French across the Americas, Africa, and Asia, producing creoles such as Haitian Creole, which derive most vocabulary from French but have independent grammars.

## Africa: the demographic engine

Africa is now the centre of gravity, with an estimated 167 million Francophones across 35 countries in 2023. French is mostly a second language and an urban lingua franca, but in Kinshasa, Abidjan, Douala, and Yaoundé it is becoming native. Youth sociolects like Camfranglais and Nouchi drive innovation feeding back into the global Francosphere through music and social media.

Politics have shifted against French in parts of the Sahel: Mali (2023), Burkina Faso (2024), and Niger (2025) demoted or removed French as an official language, framing the move as anti-colonial sovereignty. French still dominates higher education, media, and interethnic trade in Bamako and Ouagadougou. The Maghreb diverges: Algeria pushes Arabic and English in schools, while Morocco and Tunisia keep bilingual tracks. OIF projects 466–666 million speakers by 2050, with 9 of 10 new Francophones in Africa, driven by population growth, a youth bulge, urbanisation, and rising school enrolment.

## Other regions

French is a federal official language in Canada and Quebec's sole official one, with about 80% of Quebecers native French speakers; Bill 96 (2022) reinforces this. Louisiana French, Missouri French, and New England French are largely endangered. French is official in Haiti, the French West Indies, and French Guiana. In former Indochina it has faded (under 1% fluent in Vietnam by 2018) but persists in Lebanese education and business (about 40% Francophone). In the Pacific, it is official in Vanuatu, New Caledonia (97% literacy), French Polynesia (95%), and Wallis and Futuna (84%).

## Structure

*Phonology.* Up to 17 vowels, oral and nasal, though mergers reduce the inventory in France, while Quebec and Belgium preserve more distinctions. Voiced stops are fully voiced; voiceless stops are unaspirated. Liaison pronounces an otherwise silent final consonant before a vowel-initial word, with rules for when it is mandatory, optional, or forbidden.

*Orthography.* 26 Latin letters, four vowel diacritics (acute, grave, circumflex, diaeresis), and the cedilla. Spelling is morphophonemic: it preserves Latin etymology (*doigt* from *digitus*, *pied* from *pedem*) rather than sound, mapping about 130 graphemes to 36 phonemes. A 1990 reform, phased into schools from 2016, allows simplifications.

*Grammar.* Nouns have two genders and mark number, with the plural often silent. Adjectives agree in gender and number. Verbs inflect across four finite moods and three non-finite forms. Spoken French prefers *passé composé* over the literary *passé simple*. Basic word order is subject–verb–object, with pronoun objects before the verb and inversion in questions.

*Vocabulary.* Most words trace to Vulgar Latin and Greek, producing doublets such as *frère* / *fraternel*, *doigt* / *digital*, *foi* / *fidèle*, *œil* / *oculaire*, where the popular form descended naturally and the learned form was borrowed back later. About 12% of common words are non-Greco-Latin borrowings, led by English, then Italian, Germanic, Gallo-Romance regional, and Arabic. The 1994 Toubon Law requires French neologisms for foreign imports (*mercatique* for "marketing").

*Numerals.* French counts by ten except for 70, 80, and 90, which keep vigesimal relics: *soixante-dix*, *quatre-vingts*, *quatre-vingt-dix*. Belgium, Switzerland, the Aosta Valley, DR Congo, Rwanda, and Burundi use *septante*, *huitante* or *octante*, *nonante*. The decimal separator is a comma and the thousands separator a space.

## International standing

French is one of two UN Secretariat working languages, one of three EU procedural languages (and the sole working language of the EU Court of Justice), and a working language of NATO, the IOC, the WTO, and the Red Cross. Post-1995 and post-2004 EU enlargements favoured English inside EU institutions, and Brexit reopened the debate without overturning English's working dominance. OIF membership has grown beyond its Francophone core: Rwanda, Ethiopia, Ghana, Moldova, and the UAE have joined for diplomatic and economic access. About 65.5% of Francophones had internet access in 2025, so French ranks fourth online, but the digital corpus skews toward northern and urban usage, leaving rural African varieties underrepresented.
