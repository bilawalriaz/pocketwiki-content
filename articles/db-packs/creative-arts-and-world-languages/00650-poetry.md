# Poetry

Poetry is literature that uses the aesthetic and rhythmic qualities of language to convey meaning beyond literal expression, through devices such as meter, rhyme, alliteration, and symbolism, organized into structures that vary across cultures and historical periods.

## Origins and Early Written Works

Poetry likely predates literacy, originating in oral traditions like hunting songs and folk epics, where rhythm and repetition aided memorization. The earliest surviving written poems include the Epic of Gilgamesh (c. 2100 BCE) in Sumerian cuneiform and the Istanbul tablet #2461 (c. 2000 BCE), possibly the world's oldest love poem. Ancient civilizations produced diverse collections: Egyptian epics such as the Story of Sinuhe, the Indian Rigveda, the Hebrew Psalms, and the Chinese Shijing. These works often served religious, ceremonial, or mnemonic purposes.

## Western Theory and Historical Shifts

In the 4th century BCE, Aristotle's *Poetics* classified poetry into epic, tragic, and comic modes, establishing foundational principles of poetic theory. Roman and medieval scholars later distinguished poetry from prose by its emphasis on aesthetic form and emotional resonance. The Romantic era introduced "negative capability," the capacity to remain in uncertainty without reaching for fact, treating poetic form as distinct from logical narrative. Modernists challenged fixed boundaries between poetry and prose; free verse rejected regular meter, while other movements revived classical forms. Postmodernism shifted attention to reader interpretation and cultural context, complicating universal definitions. In the 21st century, digital tools and AI-generated poetry have raised questions about authorship and authenticity.

## Prosody: Rhythm, Meter, and Sound

Prosody studies the rhythmic and sonic elements of poetry. Meter is the structured pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables; rhythm is that pattern as actually sounded. Languages impose different rhythmic systems: mora-timed (Japanese), syllable-timed (French, Spanish), and stress-timed (English). Classical meters include iambic pentameter and dactylic hexameter, while modern poetry often relies on the natural cadence of free verse. Alliteration, assonance, and rhyme create sonic cohesion, with rhyme schemes varying across languages.

## Form and Structure

Poetic form covers lineation, stanza organization, and visual presentation. Stanzas, including couplets, quatrains, and tercets, group lines by convention. Some forms, like villanelles and ghazals, interlock through refrains or repeated lines, and a volta marks the thematic turn typical of sonnets. Visual elements such as acrostics and concrete poetry integrate spatial arrangement into meaning. Free verse, though apparently formless, still depends on subtle prosodic structures.

## Diction and Meaning

Poetic diction is deliberate word choice. Metaphor, simile, and allegory layer meaning; imagery evokes sensory experience; repetition and formulaic phrases such as Homer's "rosy-fingered dawn" shape tone and memorability. Poets also shift register, from colloquial speech to elevated language, to direct interpretation and emotional effect.

## Major Forms and Genres

Sonnets come in Italian (Petrarchan) and English (Shakespearean) varieties, both built on a volta and strict rhyme scheme. Shi is Classical Chinese poetry governed by tonal regulations. Tanka and haiku are Japanese forms emphasizing seasonal reference (kigo) and moraic structure. The ghazal is a Middle Eastern and Mughal form built from refrained couplets. Genres also include narrative (epic, ballad), lyric (personal expression), dramatic (verse drama), satirical, elegiac, and light verse, with contemporary additions like slam poetry, performance poetry, and prose poetry.

## Defining Disputes

Debates center on what counts as poetry. Some theorists hold that any linguistic act can be poetic; others insist on formal distinctions from prose. The role of meter and rhyme in modern poetry remains contested, as does the fidelity of translating poetic form across languages, since meter, sound patterns, and formal conventions rarely transfer directly between linguistic systems.
