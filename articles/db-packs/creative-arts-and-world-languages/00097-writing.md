# Writing

Writing is the act of producing a persistent, usually visual record of language on a surface, and a **writing system** is a particular **script** (set of symbols) together with the rules that map those symbols onto a spoken language. Literacy, the ability to read and write, is a major driver of social mobility and civic participation, and writing itself extends spoken language across space and time while letting people slow down, revise, and externalise thought.

## Why writing arose and what it does

Writing emerged to handle the bookkeeping and administration of increasingly complex societies: tracking stored grain and livestock, recording debts and treaties, codifying laws, and preserving knowledge. Individual motives range from offloading memory (lists, recipes, maps) to storytelling, correspondence, and creativity. In modern economies the work of business, finance, law, and government runs on documents, and financial institutions in particular depend on written records for integrity. Science publishes through peer-reviewed articles and monographs, where publication signals that work merits expert attention rather than that it is true; a result becomes established knowledge only through later citation and aggregation.

## How writing differs from speech and signing

Writing, speech, and signing are three distinct language modalities. Spoken and signed language are immediate, emotionally coloured, informal, and built around turn-taking. Writing is planned, revisable, permanent, and stripped of tone and gesture, so it compensates with more explicit description. In English, written prose leans on declarative sentences, third-person noun phrases, the passive voice, and topic-fronted structures such as "On the hill, there was a tree," and it packs more meaning words per clause than talk. Because written standards change slowly, they often retain archaic features and coexist with modern speech, producing **diglossia**, where a formal written "high" variety (e.g. Modern Standard Arabic) sits alongside regional spoken forms. **Digraphia** is the related case of one language written in more than one script, as Serbian uses Cyrillic or Latin and Hindustani is written in Devanagari or Urdu.

## Classification of writing systems

Systems are classified by which unit of language their symbols stand for. A **logography** uses one symbol per word or morpheme (Chinese characters, cuneiform, Maya). A **syllabary** uses one symbol per syllable and fits languages with simple syllable shapes, such as Japanese kana, Linear B, or Cherokee. An **alphabet** represents both consonants and vowels as separate letters. An **abjad** represents only consonants, leaving vowels unwritten or marked with optional diacritics; the earliest alphabets were abjads, typical of the Middle East. An **abugida** (alphasyllabary) writes consonants fully and marks vowels by altering the consonant or adding a diacritic, as in Devanagari and Ethiopic Geʽez.

## Origins and spread

Writing was invented independently in at least four places. In Mesopotamia, clay tokens used to count goods were sealed in bullae, then replaced by impressed marks on clay, which by about 3200 BC had become cuneiform, a script that turned from pictographs into phonetic wedge-shaped signs and was adapted for Akkadian, Elamite, Hittite, and other languages; the last known Akkadian cuneiform texts date to the 1st century AD. Egyptian hieroglyphs developed from pictographic proto-writing around the same era, with early labels linked to rulers such as "Scorpion I" and the Narmer Palette, expanding from about 800 signs in the pharaonic period to over 5,000 in Greco-Roman times, while literacy stayed confined to scribal elites. The earliest Chinese writing, oracle bone inscriptions for divination, dates to about 1200 BC. The Maya script, the only fully deciphered pre-Columbian Mesoamerican system, appears by the 3rd century BC and combines roughly 800 logograms and syllabograms.

The alphabet was invented only once. Canaanite miners in the Sinai adapted Egyptian hieroglyphs into Proto-Sinaitic around 1800 BC, assigning letters West Semitic sound values; their Phoenician descendants passed the system on, and around 800 BC the Greeks added vowels, producing the first true alphabet. Greek variants spawned Latin, Cyrillic, and related scripts, while the parallel Aramaic branch led to Square Hebrew, Arabic, and the Brahmic family used across South and Southeast Asia.

## Orthography and open questions

An **orthography** is the full set of conventions for rendering a language, including punctuation, capitalisation, and how graphemes map to sounds. **Shallow orthographies** such as Finnish and Serbo-Croatian approach a one-to-one sound–letter match; **deep orthographies** like English and French allow many spellings per sound, so /f/ can appear as ⟨f⟩, ⟨ph⟩, or ⟨gh⟩.

A few systems remain genuinely uncertain. The Indus script (c. 2600 BC) is undeciphered and may or may not count as true writing. Proto-Elamite and Proto-Sinaitic are documented but not fully readable. Whether notation systems for sign languages, such as SignWriting, are writing in the same sense is still disputed. Scholars also continue to debate how strongly the medium of writing itself shapes thought: McLuhan's claim in *The Gutenberg Galaxy* (1962) that print remade society around linear, individualist reading is criticised as too deterministic, but the broader point that persistent text changes how people remember, argue, and govern, from Hammurabi's code onward, is hard to deny.
