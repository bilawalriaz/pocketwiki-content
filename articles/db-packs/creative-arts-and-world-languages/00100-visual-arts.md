# Visual arts

The visual arts are art forms perceived primarily through sight: painting, drawing, printmaking, sculpture, ceramics, photography, video, filmmaking, design, crafts, and architecture, plus the applied arts (industrial design, graphic design, fashion design, interior design, and decorative art). Many hybrid fields (conceptual art, textiles, performing arts) also use visual methods. The boundary between "fine" and "applied" art is historical, not natural. Before the Arts and Crafts Movement at the turn of the 20th century, "artist" usually meant a painter, sculptor, or printmaker, and crafts were considered manual labour. Since then, fine art, applied art, and crafts sit under one term, though Western and East Asian traditions have continued to privilege painting, and to a lesser extent sculpture, over other forms.

## How the disciplines differ

Each major visual art works through a different combination of surface, tool, and reproduction method.

- **Drawing** produces flat images by applying pressure or moving a dry tool across a surface. Media include graphite, pen and ink, charcoal, pastel, crayon, and wax pencil. Chinese practice formalises the basics as the Four Treasures of the Study: brush, ink, inkstone, and paper. Drawing on paper expanded in the Middle East and Europe during the 14th century through trade from China, and was treated as an independent art by Raphael, Michelangelo, and Leonardo da Vinci.
- **Painting** applies pigment suspended in a binder to a surface. The earliest pigments were ochre, chalk, and charcoal rubbed on stone. Classical binders included tempera (egg yolk, animal glue, or milk) and encaustic (hot wax). Drying-oil binders allowed slow blending and the use of shading and glazing to produce chiaroscuro, which suggests volume through light and shadow. Wood panels gave way to stretched canvas for portability, and 19th-century collapsible paint tubes plus box easels enabled *plein air* painting outdoors. Synthetic acrylic binders in the 20th century offered a fast-drying, non-cracking alternative, and digital painting now manipulates pixels directly.
- **Printmaking** transfers an image from a reusable matrix to a flat surface. A monotype is the exception, with only one impression. Major techniques are woodcut, line engraving, etching, lithography, and screen printing. European woodcut on paper appeared around 1400, building on Byzantine and Islamic methods; Albrecht Dürer brought the Western woodcut to a peak never surpassed. In China, printmaking developed about 1,100 years ago as woodblock illustrations for text, with landscape cutting emerging in the Song dynasty. Japanese *mokuhanga* (Edo period, 1603–1867) uses water-based inks that allow vivid colour and transparency, and its *ukiyo-e* genre became internationally known. In the early 20th century, *shin-hanga* merged that tradition with Western painting.
- **Photography** records light patterns reflected or emitted from objects onto a sensitive medium or storage chip, through timed exposure in a camera. The word combines Greek *phos* ("light") and *graphê* ("drawing"), literally "drawing with light." Electronic capture has increasingly replaced the word "photograph" with the broader term "image."
- **Architecture** is the planning, designing, and constructing of buildings and structures. The Roman architect Vitruvius set the classical standard in the early 1st century AD in *De architectura* with three principles: *firmitas* (durability), *utilitas* (utility), and *venustas* (beauty). Architecture began as shelter, security, and worship expressed through available materials, and the name now applies to the most formalised versions of that craft.
- **Sculpture and the plastic arts** are three-dimensional works created by shaping or combining materials such as stone, clay, metal, glass, wood, concrete, and steel. The earliest undisputed sculptures come from the Aurignacian culture at the start of the Upper Paleolithic. Modern sculptors often use art fabricators and 3-D printing for larger, more complex works, and some monumental pieces, like the Litlington White Horse geoglyph in East Sussex, use the natural landscape as a medium by removing turf to reveal underlying chalk.
- **Filmmaking and computer art** extend the field into time-based and digital media. Filmmaking runs from conception and scriptwriting through shooting, animation, special effects, editing, sound, and distribution. Computers have been part of the visual arts since the 1960s, and computer art can take the form of an image, animation, video game, website, algorithm, performance, or installation. Digital tools now blur the lines between illustrators, photographers, 3-D modellers, and craftspeople.

## A short history

Visual marking is ancient. Tools for processing body-painting pigments appear around 200,000 years ago in regions like Zambia, and formal cave paintings using twig brushes, fingers, bird feathers, and animal hair date back at least 50,000 years. Figurative art of the Upper Paleolithic began at least 40,000 years ago, while non-figurative cave paintings are older still. Greek vase drawing developed into the human form with black-figure pottery in the 6th century BC, and Indian palm-leaf manuscript drawing dates to the 5th–6th centuries. Chinese printmaking matured during the Ming (1368–1644) and Qing (1616–1911) dynasties. After 1400, woodcut prints on paper spread in Europe, drawing on paper expanded in the 14th century via trade from China, and the 19th-century box easel and collapsible paint tube unlocked outdoor painting and synthetic colours. The 1960s brought computers into the visual arts, and the late 20th and early 21st centuries added digital painting, 3-D printing, and postdigital practices that treat the technology itself as a subject.

## Education and open questions

Training historically followed apprentice and workshop systems lasting 2 to 6 years, and still varies by region. European and North American academies became universities, often emphasising applied arts; several Sub-Saharan African systems have reduced art in primary curricula in favour of vocational or STEM subjects; Singapore has concentrated resources on digital visual arts as a creative-economy strategy; and some former colonial systems, notably in Mexico, have used contemporary art to negotiate between indigenous heritage and Western academic structures. The persistent bias that ranks painting above other arts, on the grounds that it depends most on imagination and least on manual labour, remains contested, as does the question of how to define computer art when the same digital tools now serve nearly every traditional discipline.
