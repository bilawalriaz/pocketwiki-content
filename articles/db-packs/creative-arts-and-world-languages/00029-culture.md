# Culture

## Central idea

Culture is the learned social behavior, institutions, and norms found in human societies, including knowledge, beliefs, arts, laws, customs, and habits. Humans acquire it through enculturation and socialization, learning processes that account for cross-societal diversity. Culture has both material expressions (technology, architecture, art) and immaterial aspects (social organization, mythology, philosophy, science), and it has served as the central concept of anthropology.

## Etymology and early meanings

The term derives from Cicero's *Tusculanae Disputationes* (45 BC), where he wrote of *cultura animi*, the "cultivation of the soul," adapting an agricultural metaphor to philosophy. The word traces through Middle English "place tilled" to Latin *colere* ("to inhabit, care for, till, worship"). Samuel von Pufendorf reused the metaphor without assuming philosophy was humanity's natural perfection. By 1870, E. B. Tylor defined culture as "that complex whole which includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of society."

## German and English Romanticism

Immanuel Kant defined enlightenment as "man's emergence from his self-incurred immaturity" and urged "Sapere Aude." Reacting, Johann Gottfried Herder proposed a collective *Bildung*, the totality of experiences giving a people coherent identity and common destiny, and insisted that human creativity rivals rationality. Romantic scholars developed *Weltanschauung* (worldview), treating each ethnic group's perspective as incommensurable. Adolf Bastian (1860) countered with "the psychic unity of mankind" and universal "elementary ideas" (*Elementargedanken*). Franz Boas, trained in this tradition, brought it to the United States and coined *Kulturbrille* ("culture glasses"), the lenses through which a person sees their own culture and which can blind insiders to what outsiders notice.

In 19th-century England, Matthew Arnold used "culture" for "the best that has been thought and said," comparable to *bildung*. This framed an elite ideal of art, classical music, and haute cuisine, classed as "civilization." Romantic interest in folklore distinguished high culture (ruling class) from low culture. Europeans contrasted "culture" with "the state of nature," ranking nations as more or less civilized, a framework underpinning Herbert Spencer's Social Darwinism and Lewis Henry Morgan's cultural evolution, with Tylor applying the logic to propose religion evolving from polytheistic to monotheistic forms. Critics countered that folk music and indigenous peoples expressed a more natural life, casting them as "noble savages."

## Anthropology and sociology

In 20th-century American anthropology, culture became the central concept, referring to the universal human capacity to classify and encode experiences symbolically. The discipline is organized into four fields: biological, linguistic, and cultural anthropology, and archaeology. For Georg Simmel, culture meant "the cultivation of individuals through the agency of external forms which have been objectified in the course of history." It can be non-material (values, beliefs, norms, language, institutions) or material (physical evidence in objects and architecture). Cultural sociology first emerged in Weimar Germany (1918–1933) with Alfred Weber's *Kultursoziologie* and was reinvented in the English-speaking world through the 1960s cultural turn. Cultural sociologists generally reject scientific methods in favor of hermeneutic interpretation, though quantitative sociologists have recently joined the field.

## Cultural studies

In the UK, scholars influenced by Marxism, among them Stuart Hall and Raymond Williams, identified culture with consumption goods and leisure activities, treating consumption patterns as shaped by relations of production. Richard Hoggart coined the term in 1964 when founding the Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies (CCCS). The field combines political economy, communication, sociology, literary theory, and media theory, treating films, photographs, fashion, and hairstyles as meaningful "texts." The British version was overtly political and left-wing, borrowing from the Frankfurt School; the American version, grounded in pragmatic liberal-pluralism, emphasized audience reactions and fandom. Marxist views hold that mass production concentrates power among producers; non-Marxist views stress that varied consumption reshapes meaning.

## Psychology and protection

From the 1990s, psychological research challenged universals assumed in general psychology. People from collectivistic cultures such as the Japanese suppress positive emotions more than Americans; East Asians respond more to avoiding failure, while Westerners respond more to success. Cognitive tools can function differently: people raised with an abacus develop distinctive reasoning. Robert Epstein noted that "American-style teen turmoil is absent in more than 100 cultures around the world," suggesting such mayhem is not biologically inevitable.

UNESCO and Blue Shield International coordinate protection. The Hague Convention for the Protection of Cultural Property in the Event of Armed Conflict and the UNESCO Convention on the Diversity of Cultural Expressions govern cultural defense. Article 27 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights guarantees participation in cultural life. Karl von Habsburg describes destruction of cultural assets as psychological warfare against identity and memory, and tourism increasingly impacts culture through physical damage and socio-cultural effects.

## Open questions

Scholars dispute whether culture can be objectively ranked: cultural relativism holds evaluation is situated within each culture's value system. Marxist and critical theorists see culture as a tool of elites producing false consciousness. The "civilized" versus "uncivilized" dichotomy has been criticized as expressing European colonial power. Researchers continue to debate whether adolescent turmoil is biologically inevitable, and whether the mind is independent from culture remains an open psychological question.

## Key terms

- **Enculturation/socialization**: learning processes acquiring culture
- **Material culture**: physical expressions (technology, architecture, art)
- **Weltanschauung**: "worldview," each ethnic group's distinct perspective
- **Bildung**: German formation/education giving coherent identity
- **Kulturbrille**: "culture glasses" shaping insider perception
- **Acculturation**: replacement of one culture's traits by another's
- **High culture/low culture**: elite versus popular or folk culture
