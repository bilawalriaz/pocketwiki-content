# Epic poetry

An epic is a long narrative poem that tells the heroic deeds of exceptional characters, often with gods or supernatural forces, and that helps a culture understand its own origins and values. Because epics began in preliterate societies, they were first composed and performed orally by bards, then eventually written down. The form shaped Western literature through Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, but equally ancient and independent traditions exist in Sumer, India, Japan, and West Africa.

The word "epic" traces from Latin *epicus* and Greek *epikos*, meaning "of or pertaining to a word, story, or poem." Originally *epikos* named any poetry in dactylic hexameter, a six-beat line of one long syllable followed by two shorts; later usage narrowed it to the heroic poem. Aristotle defined epic as a narrative in elevated style about superior characters, distinguished from tragedy chiefly by length and the absence of stage-time limits.

## How epics were built and remembered

Early epics were oral artefacts. Bards performed them, relying on metrical and rhetorical patterns to improvise and recall vast stretches of verse. In the 1930s Milman Parry and Albert Lord showed that Homeric epics are built from short, paratactic clauses (clauses placed side by side without subordination) of roughly equal weight, easy to memorise and recombine. They argued the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* are fundamentally oral poetry, likely fixed in writing by dictation from a performance rather than composed at a desk.

## Conventions of the form

Scholars Harmon and Holman describe an epic as a long, elevated poem about high-status characters whose adventures are central to a people's history. Standard features include:

- *In medias res*: opening in the middle of the action, with earlier events supplied by flashback.
- An invocation to a muse in the proem, the opening section.
- A vast setting that spans nations or worlds.
- Stock epithets, the repeated descriptive tags such as "rosy-fingered dawn".
- Epic catalogues, or *enumerationes*: long lists of warriors, ships, or places that anchor the story in a wider world.
- Formal speeches, divine intervention, and a hero who embodies communal values and often undergoes a quest or transformation.
- Stylistic habits such as heavy repetition and the epic simile, an extended comparison running over many lines.

## Verse forms across traditions

Each linguistic tradition finds its own metrical shape. Ancient Greek and Latin epics use dactylic hexameter. Old English and other Germanic epics such as *Beowulf* use alliterative verse, in which the initial consonant sounds of stressed words echo across the line. Italian and Spanish epics favour stanzaic forms: *terza rima* in Dante, *ottava rima* in Italian romance. Later English epics, like Milton's *Paradise Lost*, use blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter) or heroic couplets. The Sanskrit *Ramayana* uses the *shloka*, a flexible two-line verse, and the Finnish *Kalevala* uses trochaic tetrameter.

## Genres and global reach

The dominant mode is the heroic epic. Alongside it sit the didactic epic, which teaches through verse (Hesiod's *Works and Days*), the brief romantic *epyllion*, and the mock epic, which parodies heroic scale for comic effect. Outside Europe, folk epics are long communal poems tied to a people's identity: the Malian *Epic of Sundiata* and the Japanese *Tale of the Heike* are two major examples.

## A short chronology

| Period | Work and significance |
| :--- | :--- |
| c. 2500–1300 BCE | *Epic of Gilgamesh* recorded in ancient Sumer, the oldest recognised epic. |
| c. 8th century BC | Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey* composed, founding the Western tradition. |
| c. 3rd century BC–3rd century AD | The Sanskrit *Mahabharata*, the longest ancient epic, composed. |
| 19 BCE | Virgil's Latin *Aeneid* published. |
| c. 8th–10th century AD | Old English *Beowulf* composed in alliterative verse. |
| 1308–1321 | Dante completes the *Divine Comedy* in *terza rima*. |
| 1667 | Milton publishes *Paradise Lost* in blank verse. |
| 1930s | Parry and Lord demonstrate the oral-formulaic composition of Homer. |

## Unresolved questions

Scholars still debate how directly written Homer reflects oral performance versus later literary shaping, and whether the boundary between epic and novel is as firm as once assumed. Defining the genre is especially contested when extended to non-European folk epics, whose conventions only partly overlap with the Greco-Roman template.
