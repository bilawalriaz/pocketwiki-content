# History of literature

Literature is written work in prose or poetry meant to entertain, educate, or preserve human experience. Before writing, every culture had oral storytelling; literature begins when stories and their techniques are committed to a page. Writing was not invented for literature. It emerged independently in several regions for accounting and administration, and only later absorbed poetic and narrative forms.

The earliest known literature is Sumerian, written in Mesopotamia from about 3200 BCE for record-keeping, then extended with poetic structure, dialogue, and varied characters; the neighbouring Akkadian tradition adopted and expanded it. Egyptian writing moved from records into autobiography, into *wisdom literature* (didactic texts of maxims, such as the *Instructions*), and into entertainment. In China, writing appears around 1250 BCE; under the Zhou dynasty, literature was shaped by *Confucianism*, a system of ethics centred on community, and *Taoism*, the philosophy of harmony with the natural way, producing works like the *Classic of Poetry* and the historical *Zuo Zhuan*. The Hebrew Bible was compiled over centuries, its oldest texts dating to the 11th–10th centuries BCE. In Mesoamerica, writing begins around 650 BCE. These traditions arose independently, so early literary history runs in parallel, not along a single line.

Classical antiquity added *epic poetry* (long narrative poems about mythic heroes, codified in Homer), Greek tragedy and comedy in 6th-century BCE Athens, and the prose forms used by philosophers. In Rome, Latin literature reached a peak in works such as Virgil's *Aeneid*. This Greco-Roman core shaped the next two millennia of Western writing.

After the fall of Rome, European literature narrowed around Christian faith and was written mainly in Latin. Greek works, which the West had largely lost, were preserved and translated by Muslim scholars and returned to Europe through Arabic and Latin. Vernacular languages, the everyday tongues of a region rather than Latin, slowly re-entered literature: Old English produced *Beowulf*; Italian produced Dante's *Divine Comedy* around 1321. Outside Europe, the Islamic world produced *One Thousand and One Nights* and Persian epics such as Ferdowsi's *Shahnameh*; the Chinese Tang dynasty saw a high point in poetry, the Song dynasty developed lyric poetry and popular fiction, and the Yuan dynasty produced the first Chinese novels, including *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*; Japan diverged from Chinese models with works like *Tale of Genji*.

The printing press, introduced in mid-15th-century Europe, made written works reproducible and widened their audience. The novel, a long fictional prose narrative, then took shape: *Don Quixote* in the early 17th century is an early landmark, and Daniel Defoe's *Robinson Crusoe* (1719) is a foundational novel in English. Playwriting flourished in the same period, most visibly in Shakespeare. In East Asia, woodblock printing performed a similar widening role in Japan, while in China the Ming and Qing dynasties produced major novels such as *Dream of the Red Chamber*; in 1919, the New Literature Movement broke with the classical tradition in favour of vernacular writing.

The 18th-century Enlightenment and 19th-century Romanticism reshaped European literature around reason and then feeling; Realism and Naturalism countered Romantic excess with close depiction of ordinary life, and the novel became the dominant genre, with figures like Charles Dickens. The United States developed its own arc, from Transcendentalism through Mark Twain to the Modernism of F. Scott Fitzgerald, and Latin American literature emerged as a distinct tradition in the 19th century. After 1945, contemporary literature is marked by *postmodernism*, a movement roughly 1945–1980 that questions narrative certainty and embraces fragmentation, alongside the rise of popular genres such as science fiction and fantasy, which developed their own critical awards.

The dating of foundational texts, especially within the Hebrew Bible, remains debated, as does whether Apuleius's *The Golden Ass* deserves the title of "world's oldest novel." The broader question of how much literary traditions influenced one another, or developed in genuine isolation, is an open area of scholarship, though writing and literary form clearly appeared independently in several regions and began to interact through translation, trade, and conquest from the classical period onward.
