# Greek language

Greek is an Indo-European language with the longest documented history in its family, spanning at least 3,400 years of continuous written use. It has shaped Western literature, science, philosophy, and Christianity, and remains the first language of at least 13.5 million people across Greece, Cyprus, and a worldwide diaspora.

## A single language across 34 centuries

Greek is conventionally divided into stages: Proto-Greek (unrecorded), Mycenaean Greek (the earliest attested form), Ancient Greek, Koine Greek, Medieval Greek, and Modern Greek. Despite major sound and grammar changes, modern speakers treat Homer and the New Testament as part of their own heritage because core structure has stayed recognisable.

The earliest written evidence is the Linear B syllabary, a script in which each symbol stands for a syllable rather than a single sound, dated to roughly 1450–1350 BC in Mycenaean sites such as Messenia. The Greek alphabet followed, adapted from the Phoenician script around the 9th century BC; it was the first to assign its own letters to vowels rather than only consonants. The modern alphabet has 24 letters.

In the 4th century BC, the conquests of Alexander the Great spread a simplified common dialect, Koine Greek, across the Mediterranean and Near East, where it became the dominant shared language. Koine is also the language of the New Testament (1st century AD). From the 4th to the 15th century, Medieval (Byzantine) Greek served as the official language of the Byzantine Empire.

## From empire to nation-state

The Greek War of Independence (1821–1832) created the modern Greek state, which set up a long tension between two written standards: Katharevousa, an archaising 19th-century form used for official and literary purposes, and Dimotiki, the vernacular, the everyday spoken form. In 1976 Dimotiki became the sole official language of Greece, and in 1982 a reform replaced the traditional polytonic accent system (multiple accent and breathing marks) with a monotonic orthography that uses only one accent mark.

## How the language changed

The most consequential sound shifts occurred during the Koine period. The ancient pitch accent was replaced by a stress accent. Several vowels and diphthongs merged into /i/, a process called iotacism, and aspirated consonants like /pʰ/ shifted to fricatives such as /f/ (consonants produced by forcing air through a narrow gap, like "f" or "th").

Greek remains heavily inflected, but is simpler than its ancient ancestor. The dative case, the infinitive, and the synthetic future and perfect tenses have been lost and replaced by periphrastic constructions: helper-verb phrases such as "I will write" in place of a single future-tense verb form.

## Reach and influence

Modern Greek is the official language of Greece and Cyprus (alongside Turkish), of the British Overseas Territory of Akrotiri and Dhekelia, and one of the 24 official languages of the European Union. It is a recognised minority language in southern Albania and in parts of southern Italy (Apulia and Calabria), where Griko and Calabrian Greek are still spoken.

Modern Greek vocabulary is mostly inherited from Ancient Greek, with borrowings from Latin and Ottoman Turkish. Greek roots remain highly productive in international scientific and technical terminology, giving English words such as *democracy*, *philosophy*, and *theatre*, and suffixes like *-logy*.

## Open questions

The closest relative of Greek within Indo-European is unsettled; Phrygian is the current leading candidate, though Armenian and the Indo-Iranian languages have also been proposed. The status of Ancient Macedonian is similarly disputed, with most scholars treating it as a Greek dialect and others as a separate language. The exact triggers of large sound changes, such as the shift of aspirated plosives to fricatives, are still under investigation.
