# Bridge

A bridge is a structure that spans an obstacle such as a river, valley, roadway, or railway to carry people, vehicles, or utilities across it. Bridges connect transportation networks, enable settlement in difficult terrain, and frequently become cultural landmarks. Their evolution from log boardwalks to cable-stayed spans exceeding 2 kilometers tracks advances in materials, structural analysis, and construction methods.

## Historical Development

Early spans were simple logs or planks laid across gaps. Ancient builders advanced the craft: Mycenaean Greeks constructed corbel arch bridges around the 13th century BC, Romans perfected the semicircular stone arch and waterproof concrete, and Han Dynasty Chinese engineers developed cantilever and suspension rope bridges. After Rome's fall, European bridge-building declined until the High Middle Ages revived masonry construction. The Renaissance introduced scientific analysis through Galileo and Palladio, producing flatter arches and early truss forms. Industrialization replaced stone with cast iron, then wrought iron, and finally steel, enabling longer spans. The 20th century brought record-breaking suspension and cable-stayed designs.

## Structural Types

Bridges are classified by how they carry loads. In an **arch** bridge, a curved member in compression supports the deck. A **beam** bridge uses horizontal girders that bend under load. A **truss** uses a triangulated framework where forces divide among many members. A **cantilever** uses anchored projecting arms that meet at the center. A **suspension** bridge hangs the deck from curved cables strung between towers, while a **cable-stayed** bridge connects the deck directly to towers by straight cables. Hybrid forms combine types, as in the Brooklyn Bridge, which merges suspension and cable-stayed behavior. Specialized variants include **viaducts** (multi-span elevated roads), **trestles** (closely spaced short spans), **movable bridges** (bascule, swing, or lift), and **pontoon bridges** (floating decks).

## Design Process

Design starts with functional requirements: span length, load capacity, environmental exposure, clearance, and lifespan. Engineers apply **limit state design** or **Load and Resistance Factor Design (LRFD)** to ensure safety under **dead load** (self-weight), **live load** (traffic), and **environmental load** (wind, earthquake, flood). **Structural analysis** calculates internal stresses, including compression, tension, shear, and torsion, plus deflections. Modern analysis relies on the **finite element method**, which divides a structure into small elements whose behavior can be computed numerically. **Vibration** analysis prevents resonance failures, such as the 1940 Tacoma Narrows collapse. Aesthetic choices influence form and produce landmarks like Santiago Calatrava's designs.

## Materials

Wood is cheap but short-lived. Stone and masonry handle compression well but fail in tension. Iron was brittle and has been largely replaced by steel. **Steel** dominates long spans because it resists both tension and compression. **Reinforced and prestressed concrete** is economical for beam and box-girder bridges. Prestressed concrete contains embedded high-strength cables kept under tension so that the concrete experiences mainly compression under service loads. **Composite bridges** combine steel and concrete. Modern materials include **fiber-reinforced polymers** for corrosion resistance and **weathering steel**, which forms a protective rust layer that stops further corrosion.

## Construction Methods

Work proceeds from **substructure** (foundations, piers, abutments) to **superstructure** (deck, cables, towers). Water-based foundations rely on **caissons**, watertight chambers sunk to the riverbed, or **cofferdams**, temporary enclosures that hold back water. **Incremental launching** pushes prefabricated deck sections across the obstacle. Suspension cables are built by **cable spinning**, which draws wire strands across the span one at a time. **Accelerated bridge construction** uses prefabrication to limit traffic disruption.

## Maintenance and Management

**Bridge management systems** integrate inspection, monitoring, and life-cycle costing. Inspections use **nondestructive testing** (ultrasonic, radar, thermal imaging) and **structural health monitoring** sensors. Preventive tasks include painting, deck sealing, and bearing lubrication. **Scour protection** with riprap or cofferdams prevents foundation erosion. Corrosion control combines waterproofing, coatings, and **cathodic protection**, which uses a sacrificial anode to absorb corrosion.

## Societal Impact

Bridges reduce transport costs and connect regions, supporting economic activity, but also encourage urban sprawl and pollution. Signature spans such as the Golden Gate and Brooklyn Bridge function as civic symbols. Bridges also concentrate risks: they are used for suicide, which barriers partly mitigate, and they are targets in conflict, as in the 2023 Crimean Bridge strike. Financing increasingly relies on **public-private partnerships**, in which private firms build and toll the bridge before transferring ownership to the public sector.
