# Integrated circuit

An integrated circuit (IC), or microchip, is a compact assembly of electronic components—transistors, resistors, and capacitors—fabricated onto a single piece of semiconductor material, usually silicon. Putting whole circuits on one chip instead of wiring together separate parts made electronics far smaller, faster, cheaper, and more reliable.

The idea took shape in 1958, when Jack Kilby of Texas Instruments built the first working IC on a slab of germanium, joined by hand-soldered gold wires. About six months later, Robert Noyce at Fairchild Semiconductor produced the first *monolithic* IC, meaning the whole circuit sat on one continuous piece of silicon. Noyce's design used Jean Hoerni's planar process, a method for growing a protective silicon-dioxide layer on the silicon surface and patterning it so aluminium wires could be deposited directly on top. He also relied on Kurt Lehovec's p–n junction isolation—using reverse-biased junctions to electrically separate components on the same wafer—so that transistors, resistors, and capacitors on one chip would not interfere with each other. Because every component and wire lived on one flat silicon surface, the same manufacturing recipe could be repeated for millions of identical chips, which became the foundation of the modern industry.

Two transistor types powered the field. *Bipolar transistors* were paired into transistor–transistor logic (TTL), a circuit family in which transistors directly switch other transistors; James L. Buie developed TTL at TRW Inc. around 1961, and it dominated digital ICs from the 1970s into the early 1980s. The other type, the metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor (MOSFET), is a transistor whose current is controlled by an electric field across a thin oxide, not by a current at the input. MOSFETs were developed at Bell Labs between 1955 and 1960 and enabled denser chips because each MOSFET draws almost no current when idle. The first experimental MOS IC, a 16-transistor chip by Fred Heiman and Steven Hofstein at RCA, appeared in 1962. By 1964, MOS chips had surpassed bipolar chips in transistor density and cost; General Microelectronics sold the first commercial MOS IC that year, a 120-transistor shift register (a circuit that moves data in sequence through a chain of stages) by Robert Norman and Frank Wanlass.

A 1967 refinement at Bell Labs by Robert Kerwin, Donald Klein, and John Sarace produced the self-aligned silicon-gate MOSFET, in which the gate electrode itself serves as a precise mask for the source and drain, shrinking each transistor and boosting speed. Federico Faggin adapted this for production at Fairchild Semiconductor in 1968, creating the silicon-gate technology that underlies modern CMOS circuits. CMOS (complementary MOS) pairs n-type and p-type MOSFETs so current flows only during switching, cutting power use dramatically.

Gordon Moore noted in 1965 that component counts were doubling yearly, then revised the prediction in 1975 to roughly every two years—the cadence now called Moore's law. Robert Dennard's scaling rule explained why smaller transistors also used less power: as each transistor shrank, its power density stayed roughly constant, so per-transistor power fell in proportion to area. Feature sizes fell from tens of microns in the 1970s (a micron is a millionth of a metre) to 10 nanometres by 2017, a millionfold increase in transistors per unit area. One-megabit RAM chips arrived in 1986, and microprocessor chips passed a billion transistors in 2005; as of 2023, leading chips carry over 5.3 trillion transistors.

Engineers label integration levels by transistor count: Small-Scale (SSI, a few), Medium-Scale (MSI, hundreds), Large-Scale (LSI, tens of thousands by the mid-1970s), Very-Large-Scale (VLSI, hundreds of thousands in the 1980s), and Ultra-Large-Scale (ULSI, over a million). VLSI designs became so complex that they required electronic design automation (EDA) software. Fabrication still uses a planar process of photolithography (projecting circuit patterns onto light-sensitive coatings), deposition, etching, doping (introducing impurity atoms to change silicon's conductivity), and cleaning, repeated dozens of times. A modern fab can cost over $12 billion, and chips are built either by integrated device manufacturers (IDMs) that design and produce in-house, or by fabless firms that outsource to specialised foundries such as TSMC.

As transistor shrinkage hits physical and economic limits, the industry turns to advanced packaging: multi-chip modules, 2.5D and 3D packaging, and through-silicon vias stack dies vertically to add transistors without shrinking them. System-on-a-chip (SoC) designs put an entire computer system on one die, reducing power and latency. Experimental graphene transistors, carbon-nanotube FETs, and flexible electronics offer alternative paths, though it remains uncertain whether any of them, or advanced packaging alone, can sustain historical growth rates.
