# Telephone

A telephone is a device that converts sound, usually the human voice, into an electrical signal, sends that signal over wires, radio, or other channels to a distant point, and reproduces it as audible sound. The end-to-end chain is: sound → electrical signal → transmission medium → electrical signal → sound.

## How a call is made

At the sending end, a transmitter acts as a microphone. Sound waves vibrate a diaphragm; a transducer (a device that converts one form of energy into another) translates those vibrations into a changing electrical current that mirrors the waveform of the voice. The signal travels over a copper pair, a fibre link, a radio channel, or an internet packet path — a sequence of small data units routed across the network. At the far end, a receiver drives a small speaker diaphragm so the listener hears the original voice. The two transducers are normally mounted in a handset, the moulded unit held to the ear and mouth.

Early telephones were heterogeneous. Some used liquid transmitters, others a moving diaphragm, and from the late 1870s carbon-granule microphones (popularised by Edison and Berliner) gave much louder output. Most early sets needed a local battery or a hand-cranked magneto. Subscribers were originally wired in pairs, so one phone could call only its fixed partner. Manual switchboards, then automatic exchanges, broke that limitation: a network of exchanges could connect any two subscribers. Rotary dialling arrived in the 1930s. In 1963 AT&T introduced Touch-Tone, a push-button system that sends pairs of audio tones (dual-tone multi-frequency signalling) so the exchange can read the dialled digits.

## Inflection points

- 1876: Alexander Graham Bell received U.S. Patent 174,465 for the electric telephone and on 10 March 1876 transmitted the first clear speech ("Mr. Watson, come here, I want to see you") using a liquid transmitter.
- 1947: The invention of the transistor enabled stored-program exchanges and digital pulse-code modulation (PCM), which encodes each audio sample as a number, replacing electromechanical switching.
- 1963: Touch-Tone replaced rotary dialling.
- 1979: Analog cellular networks launched; digital GSM followed in the early 1990s, and handsets identified themselves with SIM cards storing an IMSI number.
- 1990s–2000s: Smartphones (dominated by Android and iOS) folded computing, internet access, cameras, and apps into the phone.
- By the end of 2009 there were roughly 4.6 billion mobile and 1.26 billion fixed-line subscribers worldwide, about 6 billion in total.

Digital handsets and IP phones must be powered locally, so the line-power and location assumptions of the old analog network do not transfer automatically to emergency services.

## Variants

A sound-powered telephone removes the battery. The caller's voice provides enough acoustic energy to drive a moving-coil microphone at one end and a similar transducer at the other, while a hand-cranked magneto rings a buzzer at the called station. The system is simple, rugged, and works over twisted-pair lines up to about 48 km, which is why it survives on ships, in mines, in aircraft, and in other places where mains power is unreliable.

Voice over IP (VoIP) sends voice as ordinary data packets across an IP network. Adoption reached roughly 10 % of subscribers in Japan and South Korea by 2005. Modern mobile networks carry voice the same way through VoLTE and Vo5G, using analog telephone adapters, hardware IP phones, or software "softphones" on a computer.

Satellite phones connect through orbiting satellites rather than ground-based towers, so they reach oceans, deserts, polar regions, and disaster zones where cellular coverage is absent. Throughput is limited to voice, SMS, and low-bandwidth data, and coverage ranges from regional to global depending on the constellation.

## Who invented it

The invention of the telephone is disputed. Charles Bourseul published a theoretical design for a make-and-break transmitter in 1854; Antonio Meucci built a working voice-communication device around 1849 and filed a patent caveat in 1871; Johann Philipp Reis demonstrated a voice-carrying device publicly in 1861 with the sentence "Das Pferd frisst keinen Gurkensalat"; Elisha Gray filed a caveat on the same day Bell filed his patent in 1876. Bell was the first to receive a U.S. patent and to transmit clear speech, and he is still the figure the legal and scientific consensus credits. The U.S. House of Representatives passed a resolution in 2002 recognising Meucci's earlier work, but the broader credit has not shifted.
