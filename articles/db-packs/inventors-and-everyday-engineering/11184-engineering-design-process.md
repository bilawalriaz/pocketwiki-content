# Engineering design process

The engineering design process is how engineers create and validate designs for products, processes, and systems, including their lifecycle: manufacture, maintenance, and end-of-life choices such as recycling, remanufacture, or disposal. No single standard form exists; engineers and companies describe it differently, though many elements recur across models. A core feature is that the process is iterative. Activities and decisions get revisited as new information appears, and what gets repeated, and how often, depends on the situation.

Because the process is complex and context-specific, no single perspective covers it. It can be viewed as a progression through stages, a collaborative social activity among many participants, or a decision-making process that applies engineering sciences, basic sciences, and mathematics to meet stated objectives. The fundamental elements shared across nearly every model are the establishment of objectives and criteria, synthesis, analysis, construction, testing, and evaluation.

The term *engineering design process* refers to how engineering work unfolds over time through stages and activities. The related terms *engineering method* and *engineering mindset* describe the broader systematic approach engineers use to tackle problems, an approach that also applies to complex non-engineering problems such as business decisions.

## Common stages

Different models use different framings and terminology, so the boundaries between steps vary. A detailed American model lists research, conceptualization, feasibility assessment, establishing design requirements, preliminary design, detailed design, production planning and tool design, and production. Simpler versions group work into problem definition, conceptual design, preliminary design, detailed design, and design communication. European engineering design literature uses clarification of the task, conceptual design, embodiment design, and detail design. Across all three, activities such as concept evaluation and prototyping sit inside one or more of the named stages rather than standing alone.

## Research

Locating information can occupy a significant share of design time, often before formal design begins. Engineers review applicable literature, the problems and successes of existing solutions, costs, and marketplace needs. Reverse engineering a competitor's product on the market is often effective. Other sources include the internet, libraries, government documents, trade journals, vendor catalogues, and individual experts.

## Design requirements

Establishing design requirements, sometimes called requirement analysis or problem definition, is one of the most important elements of the process in many industries and often happens alongside feasibility analysis. Requirements control the design throughout its life and include functions, attributes, specifications, hardware and software parameters, maintainability, availability, and testability, all determined after assessing user needs.

## Feasibility

A feasibility study evaluates whether a proposed project should proceed. It analyses alternative methods for achieving the desired outcome and narrows the project's scope. The decision rests on two criteria: the idea must be achievable, and it must fit within cost constraints. A feasibility report is produced, followed by a Post Feasibility Review, then schedules, resource plans, and estimates for the next phase.

## Concept generation

Concept generation, also called conceptualization or conceptual design, produces ideas and weighs their pros and cons to minimise error, manage cost, assess risk, and evaluate likely success. Synthesis, arranging the elements of a concept in the right way, is the creative core of this step. Widely used techniques include:

- Trigger word: stating a word or phrase associated with the issue and letting further associations follow.
- Morphological analysis: listing independent design characteristics in a chart and proposing engineering solutions for each, accompanied by a preliminary sketch and short report.
- Synectics: imagining oneself as the item and asking, "What would I do if I were the system?"
- Brainstorming: thinking up different ideas, typically in a small group, and adopting some as candidate solutions.

Generated ideas then go through concept evaluation, which uses tools to compare and contrast the relative strengths and weaknesses of alternatives.

## Preliminary design

Preliminary design, also called high-level design, FEED (front-end engineering design), or Basic design, bridges the gap between conceptual ideas and detailed design when conceptualization alone is not enough to evaluate. The overall system configuration is defined here, producing schematics, diagrams, and layouts. This stage sets the general framework; parameters shift during later detailed design and optimisation.

## Detailed design

Following FEED, detailed design elaborates every aspect of the product or system through complete descriptions, solid modelling, drawings, and specifications, and may include procurement of materials. Computer-aided design (CAD) has made this phase more efficient: CAD can optimise geometry to reduce volume without harming quality, and calculate stress and displacement using the finite element method (a numerical technique that divides a part into small elements and solves for stresses across them) to map stresses throughout a part.

## Production planning

Production planning and tool design covers how to mass-produce the product and which tools to use. Tasks include selecting materials, choosing production processes, determining the sequence of operations, and specifying tools such as jigs, fixtures, and metal-cutting or metal- and plastics-forming tools. Additional prototype testing iterations confirm that the mass-produced version meets qualification testing standards.

## Comparison with the scientific method

The engineering design process resembles the scientific method. Both start from existing knowledge and gradually become more specific in the search for knowledge (in basic science) or a solution (in applied science such as engineering). The difference is orientation: engineering applies systematic thinking to leverage technology in service of human needs, while science emphasises explanation, prediction, and discovery. Scientific discoveries may later feed into engineering solutions.

The engineering design process and its methods are taught in universities worldwide, including those with ABET or Washington Accord accredited engineering programs.
