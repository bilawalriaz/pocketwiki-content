# Mechanical engineering

Mechanical engineering is the discipline that designs, analyses, manufactures, and maintains physical machines and mechanisms, systems that convert force into movement. It draws on engineering physics, mathematics, and materials science, and is one of the oldest and broadest branches of engineering, formally named during the 18th-century Industrial Revolution but built on mechanical ideas that reach back thousands of years. Today it underpins robotics, medical devices, power generation, aerospace, and emerging fields such as nanotechnology, mechatronics, and biomechanics.

## Core subject matter

At its heart mechanical engineering is the applied study of mechanics, the behaviour of matter under force. That study splits into recognisable branches:

- Statics, which analyses forces on bodies at rest, the basis for designing structures and stationary components.
- Dynamics, which covers moving bodies and divides into kinematics (describing motion) and kinetics (relating motion to its causes).
- Mechanics of materials, which predicts how components deform or fail under load, including fatigue from repeated cycling.
- Fluid mechanics, which describes how liquids and gases move and exert force, essential for pipes, pumps, aircraft, and turbines.
- Thermodynamics, governing engines, refrigerators, and power plants by tracking energy, heat, and work.
- Structural analysis, which investigates failure modes and uses numerical methods such as finite element analysis (FEA) to predict stress and deformation in real geometries.
- Thermal engineering, which couples heat transfer and combustion to heat exchangers and engine design.
- Mechatronics and robotics, which merge mechanical hardware with electronics and software to produce automated systems such as CD-ROM drives and industrial robots.

These branches are connected: a car engine involves dynamics, thermodynamics, fluid mechanics, materials, and increasingly mechatronic control.

## A short history of the field

Mechanical ideas are ancient. The six classic simple machines, lever, wheel and axle, pulley, inclined plane, wedge, and screw, were known in the ancient Near East, with the wedge and inclined plane predating written records. The wheel appeared in Mesopotamia around 3000 BC, though some sources argue for independent invention in Eastern Europe, a debate still open. Levers appear around the same time in balance scales and Egyptian construction, and pulleys were used in Mesopotamia by the early 2nd millennium BC.

Water-powered machines followed: the water wheel and watermill emerged in the Persian Empire by the 4th century BC, and the Saqiyah, an animal-driven water lift, was developed in the Kingdom of Kush around the same period. Greek contributions included Archimedes' work on mechanics and the Antikythera mechanism, a geared analog computer from the 2nd century BC. Chinese engineers added Zhang Heng's seismometer, improved water clocks, and Ma Jun's differential-gear chariot, and in the 11th century Su Song integrated an escapement mechanism into an astronomical clock tower. The Islamic Golden Age is represented by Al-Jazari's 1206 *Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices*, a landmark catalogue of mechanisms.

The modern discipline crystallised in 17th-century Europe with Christiaan Huygens' pendulum clock (1657) and Newton's laws of motion and calculus, which gave mechanical reasoning a rigorous mathematical language. During the 1700s, mechanical engineering emerged as a profession alongside steam engines and factory machinery. The Institution of Mechanical Engineers formed in Britain in 1847 and the American Society of Mechanical Engineers in 1880, formalising the field. Friction stir welding, a solid-state joining technique discovered in 1991 by The Welding Institute (TWI), is now widely used in aerospace.

## Education, licensing, and work

Mechanical engineering degrees typically take four to five years, awarded as B.Eng., B.Sc., or B.Tech., with U.S. programmes accredited by ABET, Canadian programmes by CEAB, and Australian programmes through Engineers Australia under the Washington Accord. India's routes include a B.Tech. or B.E., or an ITI trade certificate with the All India Trade Test. Standard coursework covers calculus, differential equations, linear algebra, physics, chemistry, statics and dynamics, materials science, thermodynamics, fluid mechanics, heat transfer, machine design, manufacturing processes, and control systems, with electives in robotics, biomechanics, cryogenics, or automotive engineering. Practical internships and co-op placements are common, especially in the U.S.

Licensing rules vary. In the U.S. an engineer passes the Fundamentals of Engineering (FE) exam, works four years as an Engineer-in-Training (EIT), then passes the Professional Engineer (PE) exam. The UK grants chartered status through a master's degree and a peer-reviewed project, and in Australia registration as a Professional Engineer is mandatory in some states. These credentials authorise engineers to approve designs for critical infrastructure, a public-safety responsibility.

Mechanical engineers research, design, develop, build, and test mechanical and thermal devices: engines, tools, HVAC systems, medical devices, batteries, power plants, and vehicles. Their work spans problem analysis, CAD drafting, prototyping, testing, design refinement, manufacturing oversight, and team management.

## Modern tools and frontiers

Computer-aided design (CAD) and computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) translate engineering intent into detailed drawings and machine instructions. Computer-aided engineering (CAE) extends this into virtual prototyping and simulation. Two numerical methods dominate: FEA, which estimates stress, strain, and deformation in complex structures, and computational fluid dynamics (CFD), which simulates fluid flow and heat transfer, including difficult cases such as viscoelastic flow and turbulence. On-demand platforms now provide remote access to specialist simulation expertise.

Active research frontiers include micro-electro-mechanical systems (MEMS) for sensors and biomedical implants; friction stir welding for joining advanced alloys; composite materials with high strength-to-weight ratios; nanotechnology, where molecular assemblers remain speculative; biomechanics, applying mechanical principles to prosthetics and tissue engineering; and acoustical engineering, managing noise and sound quality in consumer and industrial products.
