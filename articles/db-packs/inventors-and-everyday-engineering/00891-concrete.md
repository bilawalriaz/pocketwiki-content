# Concrete

Concrete is an artificial stone made by gluing sand and gravel together with a cement paste that hardens on contact with water. It is the most-used building material on Earth, second only to water by mass consumed. The material is strong in compression, weak in tension, and can be poured into almost any shape before it sets. Its production, mainly Portland cement, drives roughly 8 percent of global CO₂ emissions.

## How it works

A typical mix combines Portland cement, water, fine sand, and coarse gravel. When water meets the cement powder, an irreversible reaction called **hydration** begins. Calcium silicates in the cement dissolve and re-precipitate as a dense, gel-like solid called C-S-H (calcium-silicate-hydrate), along with calcium hydroxide. C-S-H grows around each aggregate grain and interlocks them into a stonelike mass. The reaction releases heat, continues for years, and reaches about 90 percent of final strength in 28 days.

The most important design variable is the **water-to-cement ratio** (Abrams' law). Less water means less leftover pore space once hydration finishes, so the hardened paste is denser and stronger. More water makes the fresh mix easier to pour but leaves a weaker, more porous solid. Superplasticizers increase flow without adding water.

Small doses of other chemicals tune the mix: accelerators such as calcium nitrate speed setting for cold weather; retarders such as sugar or citric acid delay it; air-entraining agents create tiny bubbles that resist freeze-thaw damage; corrosion inhibitors protect embedded steel. **Supplementary cementitious materials** (SCMs) such as fly ash, ground granulated blast-furnace slag, silica fume, and metakaolin can replace 60 to 80 percent of the cement, lowering carbon emissions and improving durability, though they slow early strength.

## Structural weaknesses and fixes

Plain concrete carries compressive loads well but fails at roughly one-tenth that stress in tension. Three engineering solutions address this.

**Reinforced concrete** embeds steel bars (rebar) in the concrete. Concrete carries compression; steel carries tension. A minimum cover of about 50 mm of concrete around each bar protects the steel from corrosion, and the rough rebar bonds to the surrounding paste so the two act as one composite.

**Prestressed concrete** pre-compresses the member before service loads arrive, so tensile stresses only cancel the pre-compression rather than crack the concrete. In pre-tensioning, steel tendons are stretched before the concrete is cast around them; in post-tensioning, ducts are cast in, the tendons are stressed afterward, and the ducts are grouted. Prestressing enables long, slender bridges and lighter floor slabs.

**Mass concrete** for dams and thick foundations faces a different problem: the heat of hydration can crack the structure as it cools. Engineers cool the mix before placement, pipe chilled water through the hardening mass, or use roller-compacted concrete, a dry, low-cement mix placed in layers and compacted by rollers.

## A short history

Hydraulic binders that set underwater were known to the Nabateans in the fourth century BC, who built waterproof cisterns in the desert. The Romans scaled this into *opus caementicium* between 300 BC and AD 476, blending quicklime with volcanic ash (pozzolana) and pumice. Roman concrete reached about 20 MPa in compression, comparable to many modern mixes, and proved especially durable in seawater, where long-term crystallization of strätlingite and Al-substituted tobermorite continues to fill pores; the mechanism is still under investigation.

Quality declined across medieval Europe and gradually recovered. In 1756, John Smeaton used hydraulic-lime concrete for the Eddystone Lighthouse, proving the material for marine work. Joseph Aspdin patented Portland cement in 1824, standardizing a calcined limestone-and-clay binder. Joseph Monier introduced reinforced concrete in 1849, and Eugène Freyssinet patented prestressing in 1928. By 2006, the world was producing about 7.5 billion cubic meters of concrete per year.

## How fresh concrete becomes a structure

Batch plants weigh out cement, aggregates, and water. Central-mix plants add water at the plant for better control; ready-mix trucks add it en route. Engineers specify either precise performance targets (design mix) or simple volume ratios such as 1:2:4 (cement:sand:aggregate). The fresh mix's **workability** is measured with a slump test: a standard cone is filled and lifted, and the resulting slump ranges from 25–51 mm for a stiff mix up to 200 mm for a fluid one. Concrete is placed by chute, pump, skip, or underwater by tremie pipe or fabric bags to avoid washing out the cement. If a pour is interrupted long enough, a **cold joint** (a plane of weakness) forms between the layers. After placement, the concrete must stay moist and warm enough to hydrate; below about 4.5 °C the reaction slows dangerously, and fresh concrete must be protected from freezing until it reaches roughly 3.4–7 MPa.

## Beyond Portland cement

A few alternative binders and mix designs address specific limits. **Geopolymer concrete** reacts an aluminosilicate powder with an alkaline solution instead of cement, cutting carbon sharply. **Pervious concrete** omits the fine aggregate to leave 15–25 percent interconnected voids, letting stormwater drain through pavements. **Recycled-aggregate concrete** substitutes crushed construction waste for new gravel, but the old cement paste clinging to the recycled particles raises water absorption and reduces strength with each reuse cycle. **Microbial concrete** embeds *Bacillus* bacteria that precipitate calcium carbonate to seal cracks, a laboratory-proven approach not yet proven in the field.

## Durability, fire, and seismic behavior

Hardened concrete is non-combustible, and its low thermal conductivity protects embedded steel during fires. Over decades, atmospheric CO₂ reacts with calcium hydroxide in the paste to form calcium carbonate. This **carbonation** densifies the surface but lowers its pH, which can let rebar corrode if moisture and chlorides reach it; air entrainment, low water-to-cement ratios, adequate cover, and SCMs all extend service life. In earthquakes, unreinforced masonry walls tend to fail in shear, while reinforced concrete with ductile detailing can flex and absorb energy; retrofitting older unreinforced stocks, such as Istanbul's schools, is a major open problem.

## The climate cost

Cement-making produces roughly 8 percent of global CO₂ emissions, about one tonne of CO₂ per tonne of cement. About 60 percent comes from calcining limestone to release the lime; the rest comes from kiln fuel. Each tonne of finished concrete carries on the order of 100–200 kg of CO₂. Mitigations include clinker substitution with fly ash or slag, leaner mixes enabled by superplasticizers, and eventually kiln-scale carbon capture or alternative binders such as geopolymers and calcium sulfoaluminate cements. The embodied energy of concrete, roughly 1–1.5 MJ per kilogram, is far lower than steel. The fly ash and slag that make Portland-cement blends effective will themselves grow scarce as coal-fired power and steelmaking decarbonize, forcing a re-engineering of the binder the modern world is built on.
