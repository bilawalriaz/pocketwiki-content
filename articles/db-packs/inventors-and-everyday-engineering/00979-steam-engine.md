# Steam engine

A steam engine is a heat engine that turns heat into mechanical work by using steam to push a piston inside a cylinder. It is an *external* combustion engine, meaning the fuel burns outside the working fluid rather than inside it, and it operates on a thermodynamic loop called the Rankine cycle: water is boiled, the steam expands and does work, then it is condensed back to liquid and pumped back to the boiler to repeat. Because combustion products never mix with the steam, the same water can be reused indefinitely and the cylinder stays clean.

The core mechanism is pressure to motion. Steam at higher pressure than the atmosphere pushes the piston, the piston drives a crank and flywheel through a connecting rod, and the flywheel's inertia carries the piston through its return stroke. The Rankine cycle captures the practical reason this works: heat in, work out, heat rejected, fluid returned. Anything that raises the temperature difference across the cycle, raises steam pressure, or recycles otherwise wasted heat raises efficiency, the share of fuel energy converted into work.

The invention arc runs from curiosity to industrial power. Around the 1st century AD, Hero of Alexandria described the aeolipile, a spinning sphere driven by steam jets, but it produced no useful work. In 1606 Jerónimo de Ayanz patented a steam-powered mine pump. Thomas Savery's 1698 pump used steam pressure directly on water but was limited in lift height and was dangerous. Thomas Newcomen's 1712 atmospheric engine added a piston and a vacuum, becoming the first to deliver continuous mechanical power, though it burned huge quantities of coal. James Watt's 1764 insight, condensing spent steam in a separate chamber rather than inside the cylinder, dramatically cut fuel use. Rotary output came separately: James Pickard patented a flywheel and crankshaft in 1780, and Watt added a centrifugal governor in 1788 to throttle steam when speed rose, though early governors tended to hunt and oscillate. By 1800, when Watt's patent expired, the basic efficient reciprocating steam engine was mature.

Two refinements then expanded what steam could do. First, high-pressure steam let engines shrink while growing more powerful, making locomotives and portable engines practical. Richard Trevithick and Oliver Evans both built high-pressure engines in 1801, with Trevithick patenting his in 1802. Trevithick ran the first full-scale railway locomotive in 1804, and George Stephenson's *Rocket* won the 1829 Rainhill Trials and proved steam railways for mass transit. Second, compound and multiple-expansion engines let the same steam do work across two, three, or four cylinders of increasing size, smoothing the pressure drop and cutting losses. Arthur Woolf patented a high-pressure compound engine in 1805, and the Cornish engine of the 1810s applied the same idea to pumping. By the late 19th century, triple- and quadruple-expansion engines powered large ocean liners. Corliss valves, introduced in 1849, gave operators a variable steam cutoff that cut steam consumption by roughly 30% and made engines smooth enough to drive textile mills directly.

Reciprocating engines then gave way to a fundamentally different prime mover. Steam turbines replace pistons with bladed wheels: high-velocity steam jets strike the blades and spin a shaft directly. They are more efficient, smoother, and better matched to the continuous rotation that generators need, so from the 1880s through the early 1900s they took over electricity generation and large ship propulsion. Reciprocating steam engines survived longest where high torque at low speed and mechanical simplicity still paid off; as of 2023, large reciprocating piston steam engines were still being manufactured in Germany. A Stirling-type miniature steam engine demonstrated at the University of Stuttgart in 2011 is the smallest known working device of its kind.

A *compound engine* expands steam step-by-step across multiple cylinders; a *condensing engine* recovers latent heat by turning spent steam back to water in a separate condenser; *superheated steam* is steam heated past its boiling point to carry more energy; a *uniflow engine* lets steam flow in one direction through the cylinder, reducing heat loss to the walls; and a *governor* is a spinning weight that throttles steam when speed rises. *Duty* is an old efficiency unit, foot-pounds of work per bushel of coal, by which 19th-century engineers judged their engines. The Rankine cycle sets the thermodynamic ceiling; by 1928 a steam plant demonstrated 91% of that ideal, with an overall thermal efficiency near 31%.

Scholars still debate how much of Hero's aeolipile was a true engine versus a philosophical toy, whether Watt's separate condenser was the single most pivotal improvement, how compound engines really compared with simple ones at sea versus on rails, and whether 19th-century road-traffic laws slowed steam carriages as much as some accounts claim.
