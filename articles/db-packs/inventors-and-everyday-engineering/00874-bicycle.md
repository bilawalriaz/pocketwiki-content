# Bicycle

A bicycle is a human-powered, pedal-driven vehicle with two wheels mounted one behind the other on a frame. By the early 21st century more than 1 billion were in use, making it one of the most widely produced machines in history. Its combination of efficiency, low cost, and personal mobility has shaped transport, industry, and urban life since the 19th century.

## Origin and the safety bicycle

The word *bicycle* first appeared in French in 1828, describing a two-wheeled carriage, and entered English print in 1868 in *The Daily News*. The form recognised today, the **safety bicycle**, appeared around 1885. Its defining features, equal-sized front and rear wheels and a chain drive from the pedals to the rear wheel, replaced the dangerous high-front-wheel penny-farthing layout and remain the basis of virtually every modern bicycle. The 1890s brought a "bicycle craze" in Europe and North America as the new design spread rapidly.

## How a bicycle stays upright and moves

A moving bicycle is kept upright by active steering: the rider, or the geometry of the machine itself, continually turns the front wheel so that the combined pull of gravity and the outward centrifugal force in a turn acts through the wheel contact patches. To turn, the rider first countersteers, briefly pushing the handlebars the opposite way to tip the bike into the lean needed for the curve, then steers into it. A short-wheelbase or tall bicycle can lift its rear wheel under hard front braking; this trick is called a stoppie.

A bicycle is the most efficient human-powered vehicle at low to medium speeds of 16–24 km/h, where up to about 99% of the energy delivered to the pedals reaches the wheels. Above that range, air resistance, a drag force that grows with the square of speed, dominates. Streamlined and recumbent bicycles exploit this: in 2016 a streamlined recumbent set the hour record at over 92 km/h.

## Structure: frame, drivetrain, and components

The standard **diamond frame** is a **truss**, a rigid framework of triangulated members, formed of two triangles (front and rear) that efficiently carry the rider's weight between the wheels. Frames have moved from steel to aluminium, titanium, and carbon fibre; carbon fibre now dominates professional racing because of its strength-to-weight advantage. The drivetrain transmits pedal force through cranks and a chain (or, less commonly, a belt) to the rear wheel. **Derailleur** systems, with front and rear mechanisms that shift the chain across different-sized sprockets, are the most common way to vary gear ratio, while **hub gears** enclose 3 to 14 ratios inside the rear hub. Riders use lower gears, trading speed for **torque** (rotational turning force), to reduce effort on hills and under load. A **freewheel** in the rear hub lets the wheel spin faster than the pedals so the rider can coast.

Handlebars (upright, drop, or riser) match riding posture; saddles are wide and cushioned for upright bikes and narrow for racing. Brakes act on the rim, the hub, or a disc, except on **track bicycles**, which are fixed-gear with no brakes and rely on pedalling resistance to slow down. Suspension smooths rough terrain on mountain and hybrid bikes, and tyres range from narrow high-pressure road versions to wide, treaded off-road patterns.

Recumbent bicycles, in which the rider lies back, are more aerodynamic than upright designs. The Union Cycliste Internationale has excluded them from conventional racing since 1934, a rule still debated by enthusiasts.

## Uses

Bicycles serve transport, commuting, recreation, fitness, sport (road, mountain, BMX, track, and artistic cycling), and work, including mail and courier delivery, police patrol, and emergency response. Cycling the same distance takes about one-fifth the energy of walking. The 1968 Vienna Convention on Road Traffic classifies bicycles as vehicles, so riders must obey traffic rules and, in most jurisdictions, use lights at night. Bicycle theft is common; serial numbers help recovery but are rarely reported.

## Social and economic impact

Around 1900 bicycles made suburban commuting practical, eased urban crowding, and cut dependence on horses, prompting cities to plan for cycling. Modern bicycle-sharing systems descend from Amsterdam's 1965 White Bicycle plan, the first of its kind, and Dutch train stations still provide free or low-cost cycle parking, often beyond capacity.

The safety bicycle also gave women independent mobility. Susan B. Anthony called it a "freedom machine," and Frances Willard promoted it for health and political reasons; it helped end the corset-and-long-skirt norm by popularising bloomers. In 1985 Georgena Terry founded the first women-specific bicycle company, addressing long-standing fit problems. The traditional step-through frame, which made mounting easier in long skirts, survives in many women's designs but at a small cost in stiffness.

Bicycle manufacturing pioneered mass production. Firms in the late 19th century used vertical integration, large-scale advertising, and annual model changes, practices later adopted by Ford and General Motors. The Wright brothers and motor firms such as Rover and Morris started out in bicycles. More than 60% of bicycles are now made in China; most Western brands design but do not manufacture their frames.

## Environmental footprint

Manufacturing a bicycle emits roughly 70–250 kg of CO₂-equivalent, depending on material and location. Steel, aluminium, and carbon fibre are largely recyclable, and tyre wear adds less rubber pollution than car tyres. Electric bicycles have higher production emissions than ordinary bikes but remain far cleaner over their life than motor vehicles. In 2011 Italians bought 1.75 million bicycles, more than new cars sold that year.
