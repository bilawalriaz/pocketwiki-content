# Transistor

A transistor is a semiconductor device, usually silicon, with at least three terminals. A small electrical signal applied to one pair of terminals controls a much larger signal at another pair. Because the output power can exceed the input power, a transistor can amplify a signal; used as a switch, the same property lets it turn a large current on or off with a tiny one. The metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor (MOSFET) accounts for 99.9% of all transistors made.

## How it works

A semiconductor, typically silicon, has conductivity controlled by adding tiny amounts of impurities called dopants. Doping creates n-type regions (extra electrons as mobile charge carriers) and p-type regions (missing electrons whose absences act as positive holes). The transistor exploits the boundaries between these regions.

The two main families differ in how the control signal is applied:

- A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) has three layers (n–p–n or p–n–p) with terminals called base, collector, and emitter. A small current at the base controls a much larger current between collector and emitter. BJTs use both electrons and holes.
- A field-effect transistor (FET) has terminals called gate, source, and drain. A voltage on the gate produces an electric field that controls current between source and drain. Most FETs use one kind of charge carrier, making them unipolar. The MOSFET, the most common type, has a thin insulating oxide layer under the gate, so almost no current flows into the gate, giving very high input impedance and very low power consumption.

Both families act as amplifiers (small input change producing proportional large output change) or as switches (input driven hard enough to make the device either fully conducting, called saturation, or fully off, called cut-off). A logic gate uses a few transistors; an advanced microprocessor contains tens of billions on a single silicon die.

## Brief history

The thermionic triode, a vacuum tube invented in 1907, enabled amplified radio and long-distance telephony, but it was fragile, bulky, and power-hungry. Physicist Julius Edgar Lilienfeld patented a field-effect transistor concept in 1925, but the semiconductor materials of the era were too impure to build it.

From November 17 to December 23, 1947, John Bardeen and Walter Brattain at Bell Labs built the first working point-contact transistor on a germanium crystal. The term transistor was coined by John R. Pierce as a contraction of transresistance. William Shockley, their group leader, invented the bipolar junction transistor, patented on June 26, 1948. The three shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics. The point-contact transistor was independently invented in 1948 in Paris by Herbert Mataré and Heinrich Welker.

Morris Tanenbaum built the first working silicon transistor at Bell Labs on January 26, 1954, and Texas Instruments announced the first commercial silicon transistor that May. The Regency TR-1 of October 1954 was the first production pocket transistor radio, and Sony's TR-63 of 1957 was the first mass-produced transistor radio, with seven million sold by the mid-1960s, when transistors became the dominant electronic technology.

In 1955 Carl Frosch and Lincoln Derick accidentally grew a layer of silicon dioxide over a silicon wafer. Building on that, Mohamed Atalla and Dawon Kahng at Bell Labs proposed a silicon MOSFET in 1959 and demonstrated a working device in 1960. Its scalability, low power use, and high density made possible integrated circuits with more than 10,000 transistors per chip. Bardeen and Brattain's 1948 inversion-layer concept underpins CMOS, invented in 1963 by Chih-Tang Sah and Frank Wanlass, which now backs nearly all digital logic. Later refinements include the self-aligned silicon-gate MOS transistor of 1967, the double-gate MOSFET of 1984, and the FinFET of 1989. The 1947 point-contact transistor, the 1948 junction transistor, and the 1959 MOSFET are IEEE Milestones.

## Why it matters

Transistors replaced vacuum tubes because they are far smaller, need no heater, run on low voltages compatible with small batteries, are mechanically rugged, cost almost nothing per device, and can be mass-produced by the billions in integrated circuits. Their limitations matter only in narrow niches: vacuum tubes still serve in some very-high-frequency and high-power applications like travelling-wave tubes, and solid-state devices are vulnerable to electrostatic discharge, radiation, and certain transient events. By 2018, more than 13 sextillion MOSFETs had been manufactured, more than any other artificial object, and a single chip can now hold tens of billions of transistors, a density that underpins the digital age.
