# Transport

Transport is the deliberate movement of people, animals, and goods from one place to another. Every system, whether a person walking, a container ship crossing an ocean, or a satellite in orbit, is built from the same three pieces: infrastructure (fixed installations like roads, rails, ports, and airports), vehicles (the moving carriers), and operations (the rules and organisations that run services). Trade, cities, and civilisation rest on this stack. The price is heavy energy use, large land footprints, and significant safety and environmental costs.

## A brief timeline of what changed

For most of human history movement was slow: walking, riding animals, and floating cargo on rivers and seas. The wheel and the sled made animal hauling more efficient, and most trading cities grew on water. Two revolutions broke the old limits. The 19th century brought steam engines on rails and ships, plus the optical telegraph, which decoupled communication from physical movement. Around 1900 the combustion engine and automobile put road transport back in competition with rail. The 1903 Wright flyer added controllable flight, the 1960s commercialisation of the jet engine made international air travel routine, and the 1964 Shinkansen in Japan launched high-speed rail.

Two later shifts reshaped freight. Containerisation, the use of standardised ISO boxes that swap cleanly between ships, trains, and trucks, took off in the 1950s and made global trade cheap enough to drive globalisation. Scientific spaceflight opened in the same decade.

## Modes and what each is good at

A mode is a distinct way of moving: human-powered, animal-powered, air, land (rail or road), water, pipeline, cable, or space. Real trips usually combine modes; a flight plus a taxi plus walking is intermodal. The mix is chosen on cost, speed, and what the cargo or route demands.

Human-powered and animal-powered travel (walking, cycling, working animals) is cheap and needs no infrastructure, so it remains common for short trips, leisure, and rough terrain where nothing else works. Road is the most flexible mode because vehicles can change direction, route, and timing on the fly, giving door-to-door service. That flexibility is why there were about 950 million passenger cars worldwide in 2015, projected to reach 2.5 billion by 2050. The cost is land use, congestion, noise, and air pollution.

Rail moves wheeled vehicles along steel rails on a ballast foundation. Steel-on-steel friction is so low that trains are very energy-efficient. Conventional high-speed rail reaches 350 km/h; Shanghai's commercial maglev hits 460 km/h. Container trains have dominated general freight since the 1980s.

Air is the fastest practical mode (commercial jets about 955 km/h, second only to rockets). Aeroplanes range from small trainers to wide-body airliners; helicopters and drones add vertical lift. Aircraft only need infrastructure at terminals, so they link distant points cheaply, but high-altitude emissions trap extra heat.

Water is the slowest mode but the most efficient for huge volumes. Around 35,000 commercial vessels carried 7.4 billion tons of cargo in 2007, mostly on bunker-fuel engines, with some nuclear propulsion. Pipelines move liquids, gases, and slurries continuously; cable systems carry aerial tramways, funiculars, and ski lifts.

## Functions: people, medicine, freight

Passenger transport splits into public services on fixed routes and private travel. Every trip begins and ends with walking, so all passenger transport is intermodal. Buses are cheapest, taxis most flexible, and demand-responsive services sit in between. Medical transport covers road ambulances plus air ambulances (helicopters and fixed-wing aircraft) for aeromedical evacuation.

Freight anchors manufacturing supply chains. Containerisation cut handling costs and made global trade economical. Bulk transport ships rough-handled cargo like ore, coal, grain, and petroleum in huge volumes. Air freight carries under 1% of world tonnage but around 40% of its value, suiting high-value, low-weight goods such as electronics.

## Impact: the bills that come due

Transport is the largest single user of energy and the fastest-growing source of emissions. The IEA puts transport at over a third of global CO2 emissions in the early 2020s, with 2022 alone adding about 250 million tonnes to reach roughly 8 Gt, and road transport the largest slice. Per passenger mile, city buses emit about 0.3 kg CO2, commuter trains about 0.17 kg, and long-distance trains about 0.19 kg, so shifting passengers from road to rail cuts emissions sharply. Aircraft emissions trap extra heat at altitude; the IPCC estimates a jet flight has 2–4 times the climate effect of the same CO2 released at ground level. Cleaner engines have cut emissions per vehicle, but more vehicles and more travel have mostly cancelled the gain.

Safety is the other large cost. Road traffic crashes kill or injure about 1.35 million people a year worldwide. Transport also reshapes land: pavement can exceed 20% of a city's area, and road and air links have damaged ecosystems and, in places, displaced indigenous communities.

Policy responds by ordering what is naturally chaotic. Many cities now pair public transport, cycling, and walking with congestion charges and 20-minute neighbourhoods where daily needs sit within a short walk. Financing is usually public because transport infrastructure behaves like a natural monopoly, where a single provider is most efficient, funded by taxes and user fees such as tolls and landing charges, though a privatisation movement in the U.S. has gained ground since 2010.

## Open questions

Three debates remain unsettled. First, how heavily to restrict aviation and hypermobility (frequent long-distance flying) given aviation's amplified climate effect. Second, how to stop infrastructure projects from over-promising: poor forecasting of passenger numbers has repeatedly left roads, rails, and airports underused. Third, whether connected and autonomous vehicles, marketed as "future mobility" since 2016, will deliver safer, cleaner transport or simply add new cybersecurity and safety risks.
