# Internet

The Internet is a global system of interconnected computer networks that communicate using a shared rulebook called the TCP/IP protocol suite. Every email, video stream, web page, and voice call between two machines is really many small packets of data hopping through routers, cables, and wireless links, with TCP and IP providing the addressing and delivery rules that let them reassemble in the right order at the right destination. Because no single body owns or runs the whole thing, the Internet works as a decentralised "network of networks" stitched together by common standards rather than by any central switchboard.

The idea came from Cold War-era U.S. Defense research. In the 1960s, J. C. R. Licklider imagined a universal network for sharing computer time, while Paul Baran at RAND and Donald Davies at Britain's National Physical Laboratory independently worked out packet switching, the breakthrough of chopping a message into small, independently routed chunks so a network could survive damage and still deliver it. ARPANET, funded by the Defense Department's ARPA, went live in 1969 with one link between UCLA and Stanford, grew to 15 nodes by 1971, and reached Norway and University College London in 1973. The decisive leap came in 1974 when Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn published the TCP/IP design, letting many different networks interconnect. TCP/IP was standardised in 1982, NSFNet linked U.S. supercomputer centres in 1986, and when NSFNet was decommissioned in 1995 the Internet was fully commercialised. Tim Berners-Lee's World Wide Web followed in 1990, turning a technical network into a medium most people could use.

The four-layer TCP/IP model organises how data moves. The link layer handles the physical hop between a machine and its local network; the internet layer (IP) gives every device an address and routes packets toward it; the transport layer (TCP) splits messages into packets on the sending side and reassembles them on the receiving side, guaranteeing nothing is lost or duplicated; the application layer is where familiar protocols live, such as HTTP for the Web, SMTP for email, and DNS for translating names like `example.com` into numeric addresses. IPv4's 32-bit addresses cap the network at about 4.3 billion hosts, a limit formally hit in 2011 when the global allocation pool ran out; IPv6's 128-bit addresses exist to push that ceiling back. Routers forward packets using routing tables, and BGP, the Border Gateway Protocol, is what allows the tens of thousands of separate networks that make up the Internet to agree on paths to one another.

No one organisation governs the Internet. The Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF), an open volunteer body, writes the core protocols in documents called RFCs. ICANN coordinates the global address space and the Domain Name System, the phonebook that maps human-readable names to IP addresses, while five Regional Internet Registries hand out address blocks to local providers. The Internet Society, founded in 1992, supports these technical communities, and the UN's Internet Governance Forum, set up in 2005, provides a policy discussion space. ISPs operate in tiers: Tier 1 carriers peer with each other for free, while lower tiers pay for transit through them. Content delivery networks (CDNs) and colocation data centres cache popular content close to users, which is why a video stored in one country can stream smoothly in another. In 2016, stewardship of the DNS root zone passed out of U.S. government hands via the IANA transition, a landmark in the Internet's move from a U.S. research project into a global commons.

The applications built on this infrastructure shape daily life. The World Wide Web, navigated by browsers like Chrome and Firefox using HTTP, HTML, and URIs, is the dominant service but only one of many: email, Voice over IP telephony, peer-to-peer file sharing, streaming platforms such as Netflix and YouTube, social networks, and e-commerce all ride the same packet-switching backbone. By 2013, e-commerce generated around $16 trillion in transactions, and the broader digital economy reached $20.4 trillion, roughly 13.8% of global sales, while online advertising overtook cable TV revenues by 2011. Mobile networks, which by 2018 covered 80% of the world's population with 4G, drove the next wave of growth, and the International Telecommunication Union estimated in 2025 that 6 billion people, about 74% of humanity, were online.

The same reach that delivers opportunity also delivers harm. Malware such as viruses, worms, ransomware, botnets, and spyware drives projected cybercrime costs of around $6 trillion a year, and surveillance programs intercept traffic under legal mandates like the U.S. CALEA law. Governments filter content, impose blackouts, and build national firewalls, and critics warn of fragmentation, with zero-rating deals and regional networks pulling users away from a single global commons. Energy use, especially from streaming and data centres, raises a separate question about the carbon cost of digital life, and the ongoing transition from IPv4 to IPv6 is a reminder that even the plumbing is still being rebuilt.
