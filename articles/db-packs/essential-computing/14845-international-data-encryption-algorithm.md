# International Data Encryption Algorithm

The International Data Encryption Algorithm (IDEA) is a symmetric-key block cipher designed by James Massey and Xuejia Lai, first described in 1991 as a replacement for the Data Encryption Standard (DES). Originally called Improved Proposed Encryption Standard (IPES), it was a minor revision of an earlier cipher, the Proposed Encryption Standard (PES). IDEA processes 64-bit blocks under a 128-bit key.

Its security rests on mixing three operations on 16-bit quantities that come from algebraically incompatible groups: bitwise XOR, addition modulo 2¹⁶, and multiplication modulo 2¹⁶+1. Modular addition and multiplication wrap results around a fixed range. For IDEA's multiplication, the word 0x0000 is treated as 2¹⁶ and an output of 2¹⁶ is written back as 0x0000, so every 16-bit value stays invertible. Because the three operations resist expression as a single linear or algebraic system, the cipher is hard to attack algebraically.

The overall structure follows the Lai–Massey scheme, in which the block is split, combined through a round function, and recombined. To work with four 16-bit sub-blocks, IDEA runs two Lai–Massey instances in parallel and interweaves them, swapping two sub-blocks after each round to spread changes across the block.

IDEA applies eight identical rounds plus a final "half-round" output transformation, giving 8.5 rounds. The half-round exists to cancel a swap performed at the end of the eighth round so the cipher's inputs and outputs align.

Each round uses six 16-bit sub-keys and the half-round uses four, totaling 52. The first eight sub-keys are taken directly from the 128-bit key. The remaining 44 come from rotating the main key left by 25 bits between each group of eight sub-keys, producing only six rotations over the whole schedule, much less than once per round. This compact schedule kept the cipher efficient but exposed it to attacks that exploit key structure.

Decryption reuses the same machinery: round keys are applied in reverse order, and the sub-keys used in odd rounds are replaced by their inverses under the relevant group operation. For example, encryption sub-keys K1 through K4 become the inverses of K49 through K52 for decryption, while K5 and K6 are replaced by K47 and K48.

The designers analyzed IDEA against differential cryptanalysis and concluded it was immune under reasonable assumptions. As of 2007, the best published attack reduced IDEA to 6 of its 8.5 rounds and required about 2⁶⁴ known plaintexts and 2¹²⁶·⁸ operations; by convention any attack needing fewer than 2¹²⁸ operations is a theoretical break. In 2011 the full 8.5-round cipher was broken with a meet-in-the-middle attack, and in 2012 independently with a narrow-bicliques attack of complexity 2¹²⁶·¹, only marginally faster than exhaustive search of the 128-bit key. Both attacks remain computationally infeasible, so IDEA is still considered secure for practical use. Bruce Schneier called it the best publicly available block cipher in 1996, but by 1999 he no longer recommended it because faster algorithms existed and patents still applied.

Because the key schedule is simple, some keys with long runs of 0 bits produce weak encryption, and larger weak-key classes were identified in 2002. These keys are rare enough that random generation rarely hits them. XORing every sub-key with a constant such as 0x0DAE fixes most cases, though a full key-schedule redesign may be needed to cover them all.

IDEA was patented first in Switzerland in 1990 and internationally in 1991, with patents granted in Austria, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, the United Kingdom, the United States, and Japan. The last patents expired in 2011 and 2012, and IDEA is now free for all uses. It replaced the insecure BassOmatic cipher in Pretty Good Privacy (PGP) v2.0 and remains an optional algorithm in the OpenPGP standard. A successor called IDEA NXT, previously known as FOX, was released by MediaCrypt AG in 2005.
