# History of computing hardware

Computing hardware began as hand-operated aids to arithmetic and grew through repeated reinventions of the same idea: representing a problem, solving it by manipulating that representation automatically, and shrinking the machinery that does the work. Each breakthrough did the same trick at a smaller scale, on a more reliable physical medium, and at lower cost, until a general-purpose computer fit in a pocket.

## From fingers and gears to programmable machines

For most of recorded history, calculation was a manual skill. The abacus (Babylonia, c. 2700–2300 BC) shifted the burden from memory to a physical arrangement of beads, and the slide rule (1620s) encoded multiplication and division as lengths on a sliding scale. These were *analog* devices: numbers were represented by continuous physical quantities such as position or length, not by digits.

The first step toward true programmability came from the textile industry. Joseph Marie Jacquard's 1804 loom read patterns from punched cards, and Charles Babbage's 1833 Analytical Engine was the first design for a general-purpose programmable computer, though it was never built. A working machine that embodied the same idea arrived in 1941: Konrad Zuse's Z3, the first programmable, fully automatic digital computer, implemented with electromechanical relays rather than electronics. Because it represented numbers as discrete digits, it counted as *digital*; because it switched with relays, not vacuum tubes, it was not yet electronic. The British Colossus (1944) and the American ENIAC (1945) followed, both using vacuum tubes and both requiring physical rewiring to change programs.

A second foundational idea appeared in 1936, when Alan Turing described a theoretical "universal machine" that could simulate any other computer by reading its instructions as data. Turing's paper showed that one general-purpose device, given the right program, could perform every computation a special-purpose one could.

## Memory that holds its own program

The decisive architectural change came in 1945 with the "von Neumann architecture," which proposed storing program instructions in the same memory used for data. Without this, a computer had to be physically rewired to run a new task; with it, switching programs meant loading new instructions into memory, the way a modern computer boots. The Manchester Baby (1948) was the first working stored-program computer, followed by EDSAC (1949) and the first commercial machines, the Ferranti Mark 1 and UNIVAC I (both 1951).

## Smaller, faster, cheaper: transistors, ICs, and the microprocessor

Every later advance has been a matter of scale. The bipolar transistor, invented in 1947, replaced fragile, heat-generating vacuum tubes and gave rise to the smaller, more reliable "second-generation" computers of the 1950s, such as the IBM 1401. The integrated circuit, demonstrated by Jack Kilby in 1958, packed many transistors onto a single piece of semiconductor, producing "third-generation" machines. The MOSFET (1959) became the standard switch and also made semiconductor memory practical, replacing magnetic cores.

The endpoint of that scaling was the microprocessor: a complete central processing unit (CPU) on one chip. Intel's 4004 (1971) was the first commercial single-chip microprocessor, and the 1975 Altair 8800 kit showed that a usable computer could now be sold to individuals, beginning the personal computer revolution of the 1980s. The same trajectory of integration continues today in the smartphones in most pockets, whose processing power vastly exceeds the room-sized machines of the 1940s.

## Open attribution

The narrative contains genuine ambiguity at the edges. Mechanical calculation is sometimes credited to Wilhelm Schickard (1623) rather than Pascal. The label "first microprocessor" is contested among the Four-Phase Systems AL-1 (1969), the Garrett AiResearch MP944 (1970), and the Intel 4004 (1971). The "first microcomputer" is similarly disputed between the R2E Micral N (1973) and the Altair 8800 (1975). These disputes usually hinge on definitions rather than facts: what counts as programmable, electronic, or "on a single chip" decides which machine wins.
