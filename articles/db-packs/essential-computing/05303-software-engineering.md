# Software engineering

Software engineering is the branch of engineering that applies systematic, disciplined methods to the design, development, testing, and maintenance of software. A software engineer works through a development process that defines what to build, implements it, tests it, and keeps it working over time. Practitioners describe it as "programming integrated over time": not just writing code, but managing the tools and processes an organisation uses to build and maintain that code across its life.

## Why it became a separate field

In the 1960s, software projects frequently ran over budget, missed deadlines, accumulated bugs, and failed to meet user needs. The 1968 NATO conference, organised by Friedrich L. Bauer, was the first dedicated meeting on the problem and helped formalise guidelines for reliable, maintainable software. Margaret Hamilton used the label "software engineering" during the Apollo missions to give that work professional legitimacy during a perceived "software crisis."

Key institutions followed. The Software Engineering Institute (SEI) opened in 1984 as a federally funded centre at Carnegie Mellon University, where Watts Humphrey developed the Capability Maturity Model Integration (CMMI) for evaluating software teams. The IEEE Computer Society and ACM published the Software Engineering Body of Knowledge (SWEBOK), standardised as ISO/IEC TR 1979:2005 and now in version 4, which defines what a practising software engineer should know.

## Defining the discipline

Definitions agree on the core idea but emphasise different parts. The IEEE Standard Glossary calls it a systematic, disciplined, quantifiable approach to developing, operating, and maintaining software. Ian Sommerville defines it as an engineering discipline concerned with all aspects of software production. Fritz Bauer describes sound engineering principles to economically obtain software that is reliable and works on real machines. Merriam-Webster treats it as a branch of computer science dealing with the design, implementation, and maintenance of complex programs.

Commentators disagree on whether software engineering is genuinely engineering. David Parnas says it is. Steve McConnell says it is not yet, but should be. Donald Knuth treats programming as both an art and a science. Edsger Dijkstra, until his death in 2002, rejected the term entirely, calling the field "The Doomed Discipline" because its goal, of teaching people to program well when they cannot, is self-contradictory.

## The software development process

Software engineering organises work into recognisable stages.

**Requirements analysis** elicits, analyses, specifies, and validates what the software must do. Functional requirements describe expected behaviour and outputs. Non-functional requirements cover portability, security, maintainability, reliability, scalability, performance, and reusability, and include interface, performance, operating, life-cycle, and economic constraints. Domain requirements reflect the characteristics of a particular application area.

**Design** produces high-level plans. Interface design defines how the system interacts with its environment and with its own internals. Architectural design identifies the major components, their responsibilities, and the interfaces between them. Detailed design specifies internal elements: algorithms, data structures, and relationships.

**Construction** is the implementation stage: programming, unit testing, integration testing, and debugging. Testing is empirical and risk-based; when separated from construction it is run by test engineers or quality assurance at the system level to inform stakeholders about quality and to prioritise the most serious risks.

**Program analysis** examines software with respect to properties such as performance, robustness, and security.

**Maintenance** begins after release and typically consumes 40–80% of total project cost. It includes fixing errors, optimising performance, removing obsolete features, and enhancing existing ones.

In small teams one person may fill several of these roles; in large projects, analysts, architects, developers, testers, project managers, and product managers each specialise.

## Education and credentials

A working knowledge of computer programming is the prerequisite. Imperial College London introduced the first three-year software engineering bachelor's degree in 1987; the Rochester Institute of Technology established the first US programme in 1996, with ABET accreditation in 2003. A standard international undergraduate curriculum, SE2004, was defined by a joint IEEE Computer Society and ACM steering committee between 2001 and 2004. The first master's degree was at Seattle University in 1979.

Licensing varies by country. The UK has no legal requirement to hold the title. Canadian provinces including Alberta, British Columbia, Ontario, and Quebec license software engineers as Professional Engineers (P.Eng), though Professional Engineers Ontario does not recognise online degrees and does not treat computer science as equivalent to software engineering, which keeps P.Eng numbers low. In the US, NCEES offered a Professional Engineer exam for software from 2013 until April 2019, when it was dropped for lack of candidates. The IEEE and ACM jointly decided in the 1990s that formal licensing was inappropriate for industrial software practice.

The SEI offers certifications in security, process improvement, and software architecture. The IEEE had certified over 575 professionals as Certified Software Development Professionals by 2006 and added the entry-level Certified Software Development Associate in 2008. In the UK, the British Computer Society awards Chartered IT Professional status and routes to Chartered Engineer through the Institution of Engineering and Technology.

## Work, globalisation, and current pressures

An estimated 26.9 million professional software engineers worked worldwide in 2022, up from 21 million in 2016. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics counted 1,365,500 software developers in 2018 and projected 15% growth from 2024 to 2034, a figure revised downward several times from a 30% estimate for 2010–2020, partly because of offshoring and uncertainty about artificial intelligence's effect on demand.

Outsourcing shifted much routine development to India, China, and other countries, enabling follow-the-sun workflows in which teams in North America, Asia, and Europe hand work to each other across time zones for continuous oversight. The practice also introduced problems of geographical, temporal, cultural, and linguistic distance, and contributed in some regions to extended schedules such as the 996 and "007" work systems. Statistics do not show a clear threat to software engineering as a profession, but the related occupation of computer programming has declined in some projections, and the ratio of women in many software fields has fallen relative to other engineering disciplines.
