# Digital literacy

Digital literacy is the ability to find, evaluate, create, and communicate information across digital platforms, combining technical skill with the critical thinking needed to judge credibility, meaning, and social impact. It extends two related literacies: **information literacy** (locating and assessing information in any format) and **media literacy** (analyzing media messages). Digital literacy adds the practical, participatory layer of using digital tools to produce content and engage online.

The term was defined in 1997 by Paul Glister as understanding information in multiple digital formats. It has since expanded from computer skills to a wider view that includes the social, ethical, and political dimensions of digital interaction. As the internet and social media shifted the dominant platform from desktop computers to mobile devices, the concept now covers phones, apps, and networked services as much as PCs.

A widely used model lists six component skills: **photo-visual literacy** (reading meaning from images), **reproduction literacy** (creating or remixing digital work), **branching literacy** (navigating non-linear, hyperlinked spaces), **information literacy** (searching and evaluating sources), **socio-emotional literacy** (managing social presence online), and **real-time thinking** (processing many simultaneous digital inputs).

**Digital divide** refers to unequal access to information and communication technology, driven by economic and social stratification rather than ownership alone. A 2012 US study found 94–98% of youth report being online, so the gap increasingly shows as a *civic opportunity gap* and what media theorist Henry Jenkins called a **participation gap**: learners with sustained home access and adult guidance develop deeper skills, while those limited to school or library computers fall behind. Marc Prensky's **digital natives** (raised with technology) versus **digital immigrants** (who adopted it later) is widely used, though contested: some studies found students over 30 showed stronger digital-native traits, and many young people use technology mainly to consume content rather than create it.

**Digital rights** extend free expression and privacy online, and **digital citizenship** is the practice of participating responsibly in online society, including awareness of cyberbullying, copyright, and ethical behavior.

**AI literacy** has emerged as a related sub-skill as artificial intelligence becomes a new medium for receiving and producing information. Common frameworks cover four elements: knowing what AI is and how to use it, applying it in real scenarios, evaluating and creating with AI through higher-order thinking, and weighing ethical issues such as fairness, accountability, transparency, and safety.

Digital literacy is now treated as workforce infrastructure. The US 2014 Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act defines it as a workforce preparation activity, and EU labor market studies link higher digital literacy to higher employment. The COVID-19 pandemic exposed the cost of low digital literacy: an estimated 84% of the global student body was affected by closures, and a Croatian pilot that gave 920 instructors and over 6,000 pupils devices and training shifted online within two days. Frameworks like the EU's DigCompEdu and the Digital Education Action Plan (2021–2027) now structure national efforts. The United Nations included digital literacy in Sustainable Development Goal 4 (indicator 4.4.2) for 2030, and bodies such as UNESCO and the DQ Institute's Coalition for Digital Intelligence publish global standards to measure and improve it.
