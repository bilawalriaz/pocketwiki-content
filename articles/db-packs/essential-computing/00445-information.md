# Information

Information is meaning extracted from a pattern. It is not knowledge itself, but the raw material from which knowledge is built through interpretation. A non-random pattern, in DNA, a spoken sentence, or a voltage on a wire, carries information, because some observer or downstream process can read it as a signal rather than noise. Data is a representation; information is what that representation means once interpreted. Processed iteratively, data at one stage becomes information for the next, and the chain eventually terminates in knowledge.

## A short history of the word

The English word "information" appears in the 14th century as "formation or molding of the mind or character," descending from Latin *informatio*, meaning conception or teaching. During the Middle Ages it described an internal shaping of the mind; in modern usage it shifted to mean something communicated externally. In the 1920s Harry Nyquist and Ralph Hartley began quantifying information, and in the 1940s Claude Shannon's work established information theory as a formal discipline. By 2002, global digital storage capacity had overtaken analogue capacity, and by 2007 an estimated 97% of technologically stored information was digital bits, with global data creation forecast to reach 64.2 zettabytes by 2020. Technological storage capacity grew from 2.6 exabytes in 1986 to 295 exabytes in 2007.

## Information theory

Information theory, founded by Shannon, is the science of quantifying, storing, and communicating information. Its central concept is **entropy**, a measure of uncertainty. The more uncertain an outcome, the more information is needed to resolve it. The standard unit is the **bit**, defined as the amount of information that halves uncertainty. The same mathematics underlies data compression (such as ZIP files), error correction (such as DSL), and the design of the internet and mobile phones.

A central tension runs through the field. Shannon treated information as an objective, measurable quantity, but his framework also assumes the recipient already knows which messages are likely, a subjective choice. Critics argue the theory conflates two incompatible metaphors: information as something flowing like water in a pipe, and information as something an observer selects and interprets.

## Pattern, context, and meaning

A broader view defines information as any pattern that influences the formation or transformation of other patterns, with no conscious mind required. DNA guides organism development this way. Gregory Bateson captured the intuition by calling information "a difference that makes a difference." The Danish Dictionary of Information Terms offers a pragmatic gloss: information is an answer to a specific question. A stone in a field illustrates the point: to a geologist it carries one set of information, to an archaeologist quite another, showing that information is not an intrinsic property of the stone but depends on the observer's context and prior knowledge.

## Signs and signals

Semiotics, the study of signs, analyses information at four nested levels: **pragmatics** (the purpose behind a message), **semantics** (its meaning), **syntax** (its formal structure), and **empirics** (the physical signal that carries it). A spoken and a written sentence share syntax and semantics but differ in empirics, while a scrambled sentence keeps the same empiric signal yet loses meaning.

## Information in physics

Physics treats information as what reduces uncertainty about a system's state. Classical mechanics assumes a fully deterministic universe in which information about the present, in principle, determines the future. Quantum mechanics replaces this with a probabilistic **wave function**, a mathematical description from which the probabilities of measurement outcomes can be computed. The **black hole information paradox** asks whether information that falls into a black hole is destroyed when the hole evaporates through Hawking radiation. If it is, both classical determinism and quantum unitarity are violated; if it is not, the information must be encoded in the radiation, a result that has not been fully established.

## Records

A **record** is information deliberately retained as evidence of an activity, for legal, fiscal, or accountability purposes. Standards such as ISO 15489 govern how records are kept so their integrity can be trusted. Records differ from ordinary information in being preserved with the explicit intent that future readers can treat them as authentic traces of past events.

## The unresolved debate

Whether information is an objective quantity or an observer-dependent phenomenon remains open. Scholars including Bateson, Yovits, Brier, Buckland, Goguen, and Hjørland argue for the subjective side; Shannon's mathematics gives the field its most powerful objective tools but does not settle the philosophical question. The disagreement shapes how fields from archival science to quantum gravity describe what happens when a pattern is read, stored, or lost.
