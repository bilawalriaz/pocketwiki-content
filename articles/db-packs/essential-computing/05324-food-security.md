# Food security

Food security means that all people, at all times, have reliable access to enough safe, nutritious, and affordable food for an active, healthy life. Food insecurity is the opposite: limited or uncertain access to adequate food, which can mean skipping meals, eating poorly, or going hungry. The condition is broader than hunger, since someone can be food insecure without feeling hungry today, yet still face anxiety, poor nutrition, or risk of future shortages.

## The six dimensions

Food security rests on six dimensions. Availability is the supply of food through farming, imports, storage, and distribution. Access is whether people can afford or otherwise obtain that food; poverty, not scarcity, is the most common barrier. Utilization is how well the body uses the food eaten, which depends on diet quality, clean water, sanitation, and healthcare. Stability is whether the other three persist over time without disruption from drought, price spikes, or conflict. Agency, added in 2020, is the ability of people to choose what they eat and shape food systems. Sustainability, also added in 2020, is whether food systems can endure without wrecking the environmental and economic bases future generations will need. The first four were endorsed at the 2009 World Summit on Food Security; the original 1974 definition covered only supply, and the 1996 World Food Summit added access and nutrition.

## Causes

Food insecurity arises when one or more dimensions break down. Common drivers include high and volatile food prices, war and conflict, climate extremes such as drought and floods, pandemics, and land or water degradation. The Green Revolution lifted yields by roughly 250% between 1950 and 1984, but its dependence on synthetic nitrogen fertilizer, made from natural gas via the Haber process, and on pesticides tied food output to fossil fuels. Soil degradation already affects about 40% of the world's agricultural land. Around 300 million of Africa's 800 million people live in water-stressed conditions, and climate modelling projects that, even accounting for farmer adaptation, global yields of the six major staple crops could fall 24% by 2100 under a high-emissions scenario. Compounded shocks, such as the 2020 East Africa combination of COVID-19, flooding, and locusts, raise acute food insecurity by tens of percent at a stroke.

## Measurement

The Food and Agriculture Organization's State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World (SOFI) report, produced with WFP, IFAD, WHO, and UNICEF, is the main global tracker. Two indicators anchor it: the Prevalence of Undernourishment (PoU) and the Number of Undernourished (NoU). Since 2014 the report has also used the Food Insecurity Experience Scale (FIES), an experience-based survey that lets countries produce comparable estimates of moderate or severe food insecurity and feeds directly into Sustainable Development Goal 2 (Zero Hunger). Other widely used tools include the Household Dietary Diversity Scale, the Household Hunger Scale, and the Coping Strategies Index.

## Who is affected and how

In 2024 about 2.28 billion people, 28.0% of the world, experienced moderate or severe food insecurity, with the global rate gradually declining since 2021. Severe food insecurity affected roughly 10.7% of the global population in 2023, around 864 million people. Africa carries the heaviest burden: 58.0% prevalence in 2023, nearly double the global average, and still rising, while Latin America and the Caribbean are improving. Food insecurity is more common in rural areas than cities, and more common among women than men in every region; the gender gap widened from 1.7 percentage points in 2019 to 4.3 in 2021 during the pandemic, narrowed for two years, then widened again by 2024. Girls and women make up an estimated 60% of the world's chronically hungry. In the United States, 2024 data show stark racial disparities: households headed by American Indian and Alaska Native individuals face 23.3% food insecurity versus 5.4% for Asian-headed households, with Black, Hispanic, and multiracial households also well above the 11.1% national average.

Chronic childhood malnutrition causes stunting, an irreversible reduction in physical and cognitive development once a child is past about two years old. Worldwide stunting affected 21.3% of under-fives in 2019 (around 144 million children), with Africa and Asia accounting for more than 90% of cases. Food insecurity is also a social determinant of mental health: reviews covering more than 50 studies find roughly a threefold increase in depression and anxiety among food-insecure adults, and higher rates of suicidal ideation among adolescents. The Holodomor famine of the 1930s, which killed an estimated 7 to 10 million people, illustrates economist Amartya Sen's claim that no famine is purely natural; government action or inaction determines whether a drought becomes a catastrophe.

## Responses

The FAO's "twin track" approach combines long-term development, including rural infrastructure, agricultural research, and gender-equal policies, with short-term relief such as cash transfers, vouchers, school feeding, and emergency food aid. Resilience-building prepares food systems for shocks through diversified production and imports, robust transport, and guaranteed social safety nets. Digital tools such as precision agriculture, controlled-environment agriculture, and blockchain traceability can raise yields, cut waste, and shorten supply chains, while alternative protein sources like insects, algae, and mycoprotein offer compact, low-input options. Bangladesh met the Millennium hunger target by combining irrigation expansion, agricultural productivity growth, and macroeconomic stability through the 1990s, and the FAO credits that combination for the rapid reduction in undernourishment.
