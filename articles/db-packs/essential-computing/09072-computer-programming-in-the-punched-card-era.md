# Computer programming in the punched card era

From the invention of computer programming languages up to the mid-1970s, most programmers wrote their programs one line at a time on punched cards rather than typing into a screen. A punched card is a stiff paper card holding up to 80 characters as rectangular holes; a stack of such cards, called a deck, was both the program and, often, its data. A whole program therefore had a physical identity you could drop, sort, shuffle, or hand to an operator.

The medium shaped the work. Programmers usually drafted each line on a coding sheet, paying close attention to characters that look alike: the digit 0 versus the letter O, 1 versus I, 8 versus B, 2 versus Z. A common convention was the slashed zero, a 0 written with a diagonal stroke, so an O and a 0 could be told apart at a glance. A keypunch operator, or the programmer in smaller shops, then typed the line on a keypunch machine such as the IBM 026 or, later, the IBM 029, which punched the holes. A typing error ruined a whole card, so a second operator re-typed the same sheet on a verifier such as the IBM 059, which checked that the first deck had been punched correctly. Editing a program meant pulling out the offending card and slipping in a replacement; backing it up meant duplicating the deck or writing it to magnetic tape.

The room layout followed the workflow. A typical corporate or university installation had three spaces: a large, air-conditioned, restricted room housing the mainframe, a smaller adjacent room for submitting jobs, and a nearby room full of keypunch machines. An IBM 407 Accounting Machine printed newly created or edited programs onto fan-fold paper for proofreading, and an IBM 519 reproduced decks for backup or stamped sequence numbers in columns 73 to 80. In "closed shops," programmers handed their deck, often followed by data cards, to an operator behind a counter; at busy times they stood in line to submit. The deck was read and the program was run in batch mode, meaning the computer processed jobs in queues rather than interactively, and the output, printed on fan-fold paper or punched onto new cards, was returned later to alphabetically labelled cubby holes keyed by the programmer's surname. Turnaround could stretch to many hours, so dedicated programmers stayed past midnight to squeeze in a few runs. Smaller machines such as the IBM 1620, the IBM 1130, and minicomputers like the PDP-11 were cheaper and were often run as "open shops," where a programmer reserved a block of time and ran jobs directly. Many of those smaller machines read punched paper tape rather than cards.

Two tricks made life with cards bearable. First, only the first 72 columns of an 80-column card held program text; the remaining eight were reserved for the programmer. Languages such as FORTRAN, COBOL, and the IBM assemblers used only those 72 columns because the IBM 711 card reader on the IBM 704, 709, 7090, and 7094 series could read only 72 columns per pass. The IBM 704, the first mass-produced computer with hardware floating-point arithmetic, set the convention. Columns 73 to 80 were ignored by the compiler, so programmers stamped them with a sequence number. If the deck was dropped, a card sorter could reshuffle it into the original order; the same columns also held an identification label so anyone could tell whose deck it was. Second, the first column of the program text doubled as a switch. In FORTRAN, punching a C in column 1 turned the whole line into a comment, which let a programmer leave debug print statements in the deck but disable them. Reversing the card end-for-end moved the C to column 80, where the compiler ignored it, re-enabling the line without removing the card from the deck. A cruder but common technique was to draw a diagonal stripe across the top edge of every card in a deck so a dropped stack could be re-stacked by eye. An opposite-corner-cut card, often a JCL "JOB" card, acted as a job separator, letting the operator stack several decks in the card reader at once and pull them apart afterwards; on busy systems the cards were first transferred to magnetic tape by a smaller offline machine such as the IBM 1401 and only then read by the main computer.

In later years, the sequence numbers took on a second life. Once card data was converted to magnetic tape, those numbers became indices tied to time, so a card that had recorded a river's water level at 09:00 could be matched to a temperature log from the same hour, and whole vaults of card decks shrank into a few racks of nine-track tape. The term "closed shop" here means a site where only operators run the computer and programmers submit jobs; it is not a union-only workplace, and most programmers of this era were salaried staff.
