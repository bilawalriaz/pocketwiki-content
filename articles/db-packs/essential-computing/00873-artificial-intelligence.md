# Artificial intelligence

Artificial intelligence (AI) is the study and engineering of computational systems that perform tasks usually associated with human intelligence: learning, reasoning, perception, and decision-making. Its techniques now underpin search engines, scientific tools, medical diagnostics, and consumer products, while raising questions about bias, labor, and long-term risk.

## Origins and cycles

AI took shape in the mid-twentieth century. In 1943 McCulloch and Pitts introduced artificial neurons, simplified mathematical models of biological neurons. Alan Turing's 1950 paper *Computing Machinery and Intelligence* proposed the Turing test, in which a human interrogator must decide whether a text conversation partner is a person or a machine. The field was formally founded at the 1956 Dartmouth workshop, where the Logic Theorist demonstrated machine reasoning.

Funding has moved in cycles. A critical 1974 Lighthill Report triggered the first "AI winter" of reduced support. Expert systems, programs encoding human expertise as explicit rules, revived investment in the 1980s before a second winter set in late in the decade. The 2012 success of deep learning on GPUs started a sustained boom. Public milestones marked the shift: IBM's Deep Blue defeating chess champion Garry Kasparov in 1997, AlphaGo defeating Go champion Lee Sedol in 2016, and GPT-3 in 2020 followed by ChatGPT in 2022, which accelerated mass adoption.

## Subfields

Researchers decompose intelligence into subproblems. Reasoning uses formal logic and probabilistic models to handle uncertainty. Knowledge representation stores facts as symbols, ontologies (formal specifications of concepts and their relations), and knowledge bases; large language models (LLMs) instead learn such patterns implicitly from text. Planning concerns agents, entities that perceive their environment and act, choosing actions to maximize expected utility, a numerical score of an outcome's desirability. Learning spans supervised (from labeled examples), unsupervised (finding structure in unlabeled data), reinforcement (from rewards and penalties), transfer (reusing knowledge across tasks), and deep learning. Natural language processing covers reading, writing, translation, and dialogue. Perception uses sensors for vision, speech, and robotics. Social intelligence studies affective computing, systems that detect or simulate emotion. Artificial general intelligence (AGI) is the still-hypothetical goal of human-level versatility across tasks.

## Techniques

Search and optimization find good solutions among many possibilities, through state space search, gradient descent, and evolutionary algorithms. Logic supports propositional, predicate, fuzzy, and non-monotonic reasoning. Probabilistic methods, including Bayesian networks (graphs that encode conditional dependencies between variables) and Markov decision processes (frameworks for sequential decisions under uncertainty), quantify uncertainty. Statistical learning supplies classifiers such as decision trees, support vector machines, and naive Bayes. Neural networks connect layers of simple numeric units; deep networks stack many layers. Convolutional networks specialize in images, recurrent networks and LSTMs in sequences, and transformers, which use attention mechanisms that let a model weigh which parts of its input matter most, now dominate language. Training relies on backpropagation, an algorithm that propagates prediction errors backward to adjust connection weights, and on GPUs and TPUs that make large-scale computation practical.

## Applications, risks, and open questions

AI now powers search engines, recommendation systems, autonomous vehicles, medical diagnostics such as AlphaFold's protein structure predictions, finance, military systems, and generative content creation. These capabilities create ethical risks. Bias arises when skewed training data produces discriminatory outcomes, as when Google Photos labeled Black users as "gorillas." Deep networks are often opaque, prompting explainability tools like SHAP and LIME that estimate each input's contribution to a prediction. Privacy concerns follow from mass data collection, environmental costs grow with compute demands, and recommendation algorithms that optimize engagement can amplify misinformation. LLMs additionally hallucinate, producing confident but false or nonsensical outputs, because they generate plausible text rather than verify facts.

Whether AGI is achievable or desirable, how to align a possible superintelligence with human values, and how much weight to give existential risk warnings versus techno-optimism all remain contested. Policy responses are still forming: the EU AI Act and global summits attempt governance, while regulators, researchers, and the public disagree about job displacement, the relative merits of symbolic versus neural approaches, and the balance between innovation and safety.
