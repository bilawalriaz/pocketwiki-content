# Computer

A computer is a programmable machine that automatically carries out sequences of arithmetic or logical operations. Modern digital electronic computers execute generic sets of instructions called programs, so the same hardware performs a wide range of tasks. The defining feature is programmability: behavior is set by instructions, not by physical construction.

## Pre-20th-Century Computing

Computation aids stretch back millennia. Tally sticks preceded clay calculi sealed in containers in the Fertile Crescent, and the Roman abacus developed from Babylonian devices by about 2400 BCE. Around 100 BCE, the Antikythera mechanism was the earliest known mechanical analog computer, used to calculate astronomical positions; comparable mechanical complexity did not reappear until the 14th century. William Oughtred invented the slide rule in the 1620s–1630s, and Sir William Thomson's 1872 tide-predicting machine aided navigation by modeling tides mechanically. Vannevar Bush's differential analyzers from the 1930s solved differential equations the same way.

## Conceptual Foundations

Charles Babbage, the "father of the computer," conceptualized the analytical engine in 1833. It incorporated an arithmetic logic unit, conditional branching, loops, and integrated memory, making it the first design describable as Turing-complete, that is, capable in principle of running any computation. The project dissolved when parts had to be hand-made and funding ran out. In 1914, Leonardo Torres Quevedo published a design for a read-only-programmed machine with conditional branching and floating-point arithmetic.

## Electromechanical and Early Digital Machines

Claude Shannon's 1937 master's thesis applied Boolean algebra, the algebra of true and false, to switching circuits, laying foundations for digital logic. Konrad Zuse's Z3 (1941) was the first working electromechanical programmable, fully automatic digital computer, using 2,000 relays (electrically switched mechanical switches), 22-bit words, and floating-point numbers. The Z4 (1950) became the first commercial computer.

Electronic circuits then replaced relays. A vacuum tube is an electronic switch that controls current; vacuum tubes replaced mechanical relays for faster operation. Tommy Flowers's Colossus, delivered in 1944 to Bletchley Park, was the first electronic digital programmable computer, but was not Turing-complete. ENIAC (1945) was the first electronic programmable computer in the U.S., was Turing-complete, and could add or subtract 5,000 times per second. It weighed 30 tons, drew 200 kilowatts, and contained over 18,000 vacuum tubes.

## Stored Programs

Alan Turing's 1936 paper proposed the universal Turing machine; von Neumann acknowledged it as the source of the stored-program concept, in which all instructions live in memory alongside data. The Manchester Baby (1948) was the first stored-program computer, built to test the Williams tube (an early form of memory using a cathode-ray screen). It led to the Manchester Mark 1 and the Ferranti Mark 1 (1951), the first commercially available general-purpose computer.

## Transistors and Integrated Circuits

The first working transistor was built at Bell Labs in 1947 by Bardeen and Brattain. A transistor is a small electronic switch with no moving parts, far smaller and more reliable than a vacuum tube. From 1955, transistors replaced vacuum tubes, yielding "second generation" computers. The MOSFET, invented at Bell Labs between 1955 and 1960, was the first truly compact transistor that could be miniaturized and mass-produced, becoming the fundamental building block of digital electronics.

The integrated circuit (IC) places many transistors on one small chip of semiconductor. Jack Kilby demonstrated the first working IC in 1958, and Robert Noyce built the first true monolithic silicon IC. The first commercial MOS IC appeared in 1964, and MOS scaling led directly to the microprocessor: the Intel 4004, designed by Federico Faggin and colleagues, is largely undisputed as the first single-chip microprocessor. Modern systems on a chip (SoCs) integrate billions of transistors into coin-sized computers consuming only a few watts.

## Hardware and Software

A general-purpose computer has four main components: an arithmetic logic unit (ALU), a control unit, memory, and input/output devices, connected by buses. Bits are represented in logic gates. The control unit reads and decodes instructions, with a program counter tracking the next location; the ALU performs arithmetic and logic (AND, OR, XOR, NOT). Memory stores binary numbers in eight-bit bytes. RAM is read/write, ROM is read-only, and registers are fast on-CPU cells. Multitasking uses interrupts to switch between programs; multiprocessing distributes work across multiple CPUs.

Software includes programs, libraries, and data, divided into system and application software, with firmware controlling hardware. Machine code assigns each instruction a numeric opcode; assembly uses mnemonics like ADD or JUMP, and high-level languages are translated by compilers or interpreters. Program errors are called "bugs."

## Key Terms

- **ALU**: Component performing arithmetic and logic operations.
- **Stored program**: Instructions held in memory rather than wired in.
- **Turing-complete**: Equivalent in capability to a universal Turing machine.
- **MOSFET**: The compact transistor that became the basis of digital electronics.
