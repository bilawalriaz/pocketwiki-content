# Free verse

Free verse is poetry that does not use a regular meter or rhyme scheme. Instead, it follows the rhythm of natural speech. Despite having no fixed pattern, it is not formless: poets still work with line breaks, rhythm, stanza-like units, and the musical qualities of language. As T. S. Eliot put it, "No verse is free for the man who wants to do a good job."

## What makes it verse

The defining feature of free verse is flexibility. Unlike sonnets or ballads, it imposes no predetermined structure. Yet most poets and critics insist this freedom is limited. Charles Allen wrote that cadenced verse gains only "a limited freedom from the tight demands of the metered line." Donald Hall argued that the form of free verse is "as binding and as liberating as the form of a rondeau." William Carlos Williams insisted that verse cannot be free in the sense of having no guiding principles at all.

Free verse still depends on the poetic line, rhythm, strophic patterns, and cadence, the rise and fall of spoken sound. What changes is the basis of organization: instead of counting syllables or stresses, the poet shapes lines around the contours of thought, the rhythms of spoken language, and the need to breathe. Yvor Winters observed that the best free verse, by Williams, Marianne Moore, Wallace Stevens, and Ezra Pound, is "in its peculiar fashion, the antithesis of free."

## Vers libre and its origins

Modern free verse traces back to late 19th-century France. In 1886, a group of French poets associated with the weekly journal *La Vogue* developed **vers libre** (literally "free verse"). Its leading figures, Paul Verlaine, Arthur Rimbaud, Stéphane Mallarmé, Jules Laforgue, and Tristan Corbière, practiced what they called "Counter-Romanticism." Gustave Kahn was credited with inventing the term and became its first theorist.

Vers libre did not abandon pattern entirely. Robert de Souza argued that it could preserve the essential qualities of classical French verse while freeing it from rules that usage had made seem indispensable. Kahn defined it by denying a regular syllable count as the basis for versification. Lines grow long and short, oscillating with the poet's images and thoughts.

In vers libre, the fundamental unit is not the metrical foot or syllable count but the **strophe**, a stanza-like block that forms a complete rhythmic circle, whether it spans the whole poem or only part of it. The rhythm is "organic," built on the speaking voice rather than on a strict metrical grid.

French vers libre drew on three older traditions: **vers libéré** (late 19th-century French verse that kept regular rhyme and equal-syllable lines but loosened other rules), **vers libre classique** (a minor 17th- and 18th-century genre combining lines of irregular length), and **vers populaire** (versification drawn from popular song). Remy de Gourmont, in *Livre des Masques*, gave the movement its clearest early definition, insisting that a full, complete line must appear at regular intervals to reassure the ear and guide the rhythm.

## Arrival in English

T. E. Hulme and F. S. Flint introduced vers libre to the London Poets' Club in 1909. Flint's advocacy helped make it the heart of the **Imagist** movement, which became the main current of literary Modernism in English. T. S. Eliot called this shift "the point de repère usually taken as the starting point of modern poetry."

Important English and American antecedents existed, however. Walt Whitman, often called the American father of free verse, built the long lines of *Leaves of Grass* on the phrasing of the King James Bible. Heinrich Heine's *Die Nordsee* cycles contributed early free verse poems. The cadencing recognizable in free verse also appears in the Hebrew psalms.

## The paradox of freedom

Free verse sits between poetry and prose. John Livingston Lowes observed that "Free verse may be written as very beautiful prose; prose may be written as very beautiful free verse. Which is which?" Robert Frost likened writing free verse to "playing tennis with the net down." Carl Sandburg replied that some poets could play with unseen rackets over an insubstantial net.

The Imagists gave free verse discipline and status as a legitimate form, but Herbert Read noted that Ezra Pound "gave free verse its musical structure to an extent that paradoxically it was no longer free." By the 21st century, most published poems are free verse, and it is widely considered the norm. Poets have more freedom to shape expression, but the best practitioners work within self-imposed constraints of sound, line, and rhythm that make the writing verse rather than chopped-up prose.

In Welsh poetry, "free verse" means the opposite: it refers to traditional forms like the sonnet and ode that obey strict English rules, while the native Welsh strict-metre tradition follows complex rules laid down 600 years ago.
