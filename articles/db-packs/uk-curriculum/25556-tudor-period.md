# Tudor period

The Tudor period (1485–1603) transformed England from a feudal kingdom recovering from civil war into a centralized Protestant nation-state with a permanent navy, a bureaucratic government, and global reach. Five monarchs—Henry VII, Henry VIII, Edward VI, Mary I, and Elizabeth I—ruled sequentially, each reshaping the crown’s relationship with the church, the nobility, and the emerging state apparatus.

## Founding and fiscal foundation

Henry VII seized the throne at Bosworth Field (1485), ending the Wars of the Roses. He stabilized royal finances by enforcing existing laws against nobles and clergy through the Court of Star Chamber, reclaiming dispersed crown lands, and bringing the Duchies of Cornwall and Lancaster under direct control. Crown income tripled; a balanced budget emerged. Henry avoided foreign wars, preferring a defensive, anglocentric diplomacy sealed by marrying his son Arthur to Catherine of Aragon. His fiscal discipline left a full treasury for his heir.

## The Reformation engine

Henry VIII (1509–1547) broke with Rome after Pope Clement VII refused to annul his marriage to Catherine. The **Act of Supremacy (1534)** declared the king “only supreme head on earth of the Church of England,” transferring papal authority to the crown and making dissent treason. Henry dissolved the monasteries (1536–1545), seizing lands worth £1.3 million. Rents flowed to the crown; lands sold cheaply to the gentry created a wealthy, loyal class. Theology remained largely Catholic; Henry executed both Protestant “heretics” and Catholic recusants like Thomas More and Bishop Fisher.

Edward VI (1547–1553), a minor, was ruled by Protestant regents. The **Book of Common Prayer (1549)** imposed English-language services; the Mass, prayers for the dead, and veneration of relics were abolished. Some 2,374 **chantries**—endowments funding priests to say masses for souls in purgatory—were closed, their assets largely diverted to courtiers. The **Forty-Two Articles (1553)** codified a Calvinist theology.

Mary I (1553–1558) restored papal obedience, married Philip II of Spain, and burned nearly 300 Protestants. Her reign was brief, childless, and ended with the loss of Calais.

Elizabeth I (1558–1603) settled the church via the **Elizabethan Settlement**: the monarch as Supreme Governor, the Book of Common Prayer (revised) as law, Catholic ritual retained, Calvinist doctrine prevailing. She executed Catholic priests and Puritan extremists alike, enforcing outward conformity while avoiding doctrinal extremes.

## State building: from household to bureaucracy

Thomas Cromwell, Henry VIII’s chief minister (1532–1540), engineered a “revolution in government.” He separated the king’s household from the state, creating specialized departments—the Court of Augmentations for monastic revenues, the Court of First Fruits and Tenths—staffed by salaried officials rather than household servants. Parliament became a regular legislative partner. After Cromwell’s fall, William Paulet, Lord Treasurer, consolidated these courts into the Exchequer. The **Privy Council** emerged as the standing executive body, its decisions recorded and binding.

Local government relied on **Justices of the Peace (JPs)**—dozens per county—who handled administration, policing, and petty justice. **Lord Lieutenants**, a new Henry VIII creation, represented the crown in each county, mobilizing the militia and mediating gentry factions. Sheriffs, appointed annually, managed courts and elections. This structure held through rebellion and invasion scares.

## Economy, society, and rebellion

England’s population doubled from roughly 2.3 million (1520) to 4 million (1600). Post-Black Death labor scarcity had raised wages; now population pressure drove wages down and rents up. **Enclosure**—lords fencing open fields for sheep pasture—displaced peasants and fueled the wool trade, enriching merchants and gentry. London exploded as a commercial hub. Inflation, fed by New World gold and coinage debasement (Edward VI’s regime profited £1.2 million from debasement 1547–1551), widened inequality.

Rebellions punctuated the period: the **Pilgrimage of Grace (1536)** against dissolution and Cromwell’s policies; the **Prayer Book Rebellion (1549)** in Devon and Cornwall against Protestant liturgy; **Kett’s Rebellion (1549)** in Norfolk against enclosure; **Wyatt’s Rebellion (1554)** against Mary’s Spanish marriage; the **Northern Rising (1569–70)** by Catholic nobles for Mary, Queen of Scots. All were suppressed, but each revealed the fault lines of religion, land, and centralization.

## Navy, foreign policy, and the Scottish succession

Henry VIII founded the Royal Navy as a permanent, tax-funded force with dockyards, a navigation school, and purpose-built warships like the 1,600-ton *Henry Grace à Dieu*. Elizabeth inherited this asset. The **Spanish Armada (1588)**—130 ships sent by Philip II—was defeated by superior English gunnery, seamanship, and weather. The victory cemented naval prestige and Protestant nationalism.

Scotland was the persistent northern problem. Mary, Queen of Scots, a Catholic with a strong claim to the English throne, was driven from Scotland by Protestant nobles (the Lords of the Congregation) aided by Elizabeth. The **Treaty of Edinburgh (1560)** expelled French troops, secured the Scottish Reformation, and began a century of Anglo-French peace. Mary fled to England in 1568; after 19 years’ imprisonment and the Babington Plot, Elizabeth executed her in 1587. Mary’s son, James VI of Scotland, succeeded Elizabeth in 1603, uniting the crowns and opening the Stuart era.

## Cultural and scientific flowering

The period produced the English Madrigal School (Byrd, Dowland). Portraiture flourished under Holbein and Hilliard. Practical science advanced through craftsmen and explorers: Drake’s circumnavigation (1577–1580), Frobisher and Gilbert’s Northwest Passage attempts, and the mathematical work of Thomas Digges and Thomas Harriot. Francis Bacon articulated the empirical method; John Dee blended mathematics, navigation, and alchemy.

The Tudor myth—later historiography casting the 15th century as anarchic and the 16th as golden—obscures the period’s violence, fiscal strain, and religious coercion. Yet by 1603 England possessed a sovereign church, a professional bureaucracy, a navy capable of oceanic projection, and a demographic and commercial momentum that would drive the next century’s expansion.
