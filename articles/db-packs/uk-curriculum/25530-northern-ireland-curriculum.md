# Northern Ireland Curriculum

The Northern Ireland Curriculum defines the minimum skills each subject must develop and the activities through which pupils acquire and apply them. It replaced a system of total school autonomy with a nationally prescribed framework, then evolved toward a phased, skills-based structure that balances breadth with flexibility.

## From Autonomy to Phased Reform

Before 1988, schools devised their own curricula. The Education Reform Act 1988 imposed a common curriculum across England, Wales, and Northern Ireland. Teachers opposed the prescription. The original 1988 model rigidly defined both content and pedagogy by subject, allowed no teacher input, included no testing, and omitted cross-curricular work and personal development. It proved over-ambitious and unmanageable.

England's Dearing Report trimmed content but kept the subject-based structure. Northern Ireland pursued a more thorough, phase-by-phase review between 2000 and 2004, with a further review in 2010. The result is a curriculum organised around *areas of learning* and explicit *skills*, not just subject content.

## Curriculum Structure by Stage

### Foundation Stage (ages 4–6)
Learning is organised into six areas, each with defined strands:
- **Language and Literacy**: Talking and Listening, Reading, Writing
- **Mathematics and Numeracy**: Number, Measures, Shape and Space, Sorting, Patterns and Relationships
- **The Arts**: Art and Design, Music, Drama
- **The World Around Us** (integrating science, history, geography)
- **Personal Development and Mutual Understanding**: Personal Understanding and Health, Mutual Understanding in the Local and Wider Community
- **Physical Development and Movement**

### Key Stages 1–3 (ages 6–14)
The curriculum specifies eight **Areas of Learning**:
1. Language and Literacy
2. Mathematics and Numeracy
3. Modern Languages
4. The Arts
5. Environment and Society
6. Science and Technology
7. Learning for Life and Work
8. Physical Education

Three **Cross-Curricular Skills** must be developed across all areas:
- Communication
- Using ICT
- Using Mathematics

Three **Other Skills** are similarly embedded:
- Problem Solving
- Working with Others
- Self-Management

**Key Stage 3** (ages 11–14, Years 8–10) is the first post-primary phase. Pupils study all eight areas of learning while developing the cross-curricular and other skills.

## Key Stage 4: The Entitlement Framework

At **Key Stage 4** (ages 14–16, Years 11–12), pupils work toward GCSEs or equivalent qualifications. The **Entitlement Framework** guarantees breadth by requiring every school to offer a minimum number of courses:
- Originally 24 courses at KS4 (27 post-16), with at least one-third *general* (academic) and one-third *applied* (vocational).
- Subsequently reduced to **21 courses**, maintaining the one-third general / one-third applied split.

To meet this requirement, schools collaborate through **Area Learning Communities (ALCs)**. All secondary schools belong to an ALC, where they cooperate to deliver "shared education." A school may run a joint course with a neighbouring school; extra funding supports this arrangement. GCSE courses map to the eight areas of learning to ensure a balanced offer.

## Skills as the Organising Principle

The curriculum's defining shift is the move from prescribing *what to teach* to specifying *what skills pupils must acquire*. Areas of learning provide the contexts; cross-curricular and other skills provide the transferable capabilities. The Entitlement Framework then ensures that at 14–16, the qualification offer reflects both academic and vocational pathways, delivered through a cooperative network rather than by each school alone.
