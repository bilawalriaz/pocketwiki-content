# Spectrum

A **spectrum** is a set of related properties, objects, or conditions whose features overlap to form a continuum. The concept originated in optics: Isaac Newton introduced the term in the 17th century to describe the continuous band of colors produced when white light disperses through a prism. In this optical spectrum, wavelengths blend smoothly into one another. The idea then expanded to the entire **electromagnetic spectrum**—all radiation ordered by frequency or wavelength, most of it invisible to human eyes.

In physical sciences, a spectrum describes any continuous range of frequency, wavelength, energy, or mass-to-charge values. A spectrum plots intensity against one of these independent variables. **Fourier analysis**—a mathematical method that decomposes a complex wave into its constituent sine-wave frequencies—generalized the concept to signal theory, where a signal can be graphed as a function of frequency and information can occupy selected bands. Practical instruments include the **spectrometer**, which records spectra, and **spectroscopy**, which uses them for chemical analysis. Examples range from noise and sea-wave spectra to electron energy spectra in electron spectroscopy and mass spectra in mass spectrometry.

The term migrated by analogy to domains where values lack precise numerical definitions. A **broad-spectrum antibiotic** (e.g., ampicillin) acts against many bacteria; a **narrow-spectrum antibiotic** (e.g., dicloxacillin) targets specific families such as beta-lactamase-producing *Staphylococcus aureus*. In psychiatry, the **spectrum approach** classifies linked conditions—most notably the **autism spectrum** of neurodevelopmental disorders—sometimes including isolated symptoms and traits. A **spectrum of activity** for a drug similarly groups a range of effects under one title for ease of discussion.

These analogical uses carry a caveat: they may mislead by implying a simple linear continuum where reality is multidimensional. A single left–right **political spectrum** fails to capture the full structure of political belief; political scientists prefer biaxial or multiaxial models. An **economic spectrum** arranges social class along a wealth or income indicator but reduces multidimensional reality to one dimension. In most modern usages a unifying theme connects the extremes, though this was not always true in older usage.

In mathematics, the term has distinct technical meanings. The **spectrum of a matrix** is the multiset of its **eigenvalues**—the scalars by which a linear transformation stretches certain vectors. In functional analysis, the **spectrum of a bounded operator** generalizes this eigenvalue concept to infinite-dimensional spaces. In algebraic topology, a **spectrum** represents a **generalized cohomology theory**—a tool for assigning algebraic invariants to topological spaces.

Etymologically, Latin *spectrum* meant "image" or "apparition"—a ghost. **Spectral evidence**, testimony about actions of absent persons' spectres, convicted people of witchcraft at Salem, Massachusetts, in the late 17th century. Goethe in *Theory of Colors* and Schopenhauer in *On Vision and Colors* used *Spektrum* strictly for a ghostly optical afterimage. The prefix **spectro-** forms terms for spectrum-related instruments and methods: spectrometer, spectroscopy, spectrograph.
