# Morality

Morality is a phenomenon among social animals through which communities and individuals evaluate actions, character, and social arrangements using standards that vary across cultures, religions, classes, and traditions. These evaluations cover rightness, virtues, honesty, honor, reciprocity, purity, and relationship propriety.

Philosophy distinguishes descriptive morality—values and codes actually accepted by a society, making no objective claims—from normative morality—what is actually right or wrong independent of cultural beliefs. Meta-ethics divides into moral realism, which holds that true moral statements report objective facts, and moral anti-realism, which denies this. Anti-realism includes error theory (moral claims are false), ethical subjectivism (claims report attitudes), and non-cognitivism (sentences express emotions or commands). Some anti-realist forms, such as universal prescriptivism or divine command theory, nonetheless support moral universalism—the view that certain principles apply universally. Moral relativism opposes this, holding that moral virtues are right or wrong only within a specific cultural standpoint.

Evolutionary biology treats morality as a product of sociocultural evolution. Moral behaviors—empathy, reciprocity, altruism, cooperation, fairness—evolved because they provided survival and reproductive benefits, encouraging cooperation and restraining excessive individualism that would undermine group cohesion. Reciprocity ensures reliable resource supply in unpredictable environments. The maternal bond improves offspring survival; the Westermarck effect, where early proximity reduces sexual attraction, underpins incest taboos by decreasing inbreeding risk. Morality appears as a suite of other-regarding behaviors shared by mammals in complex social groups—wolves, elephants, dolphins, chimpanzees. Chimpanzees show empathy, deception, and reputation management. Moral complexity increased in hominids to avoid disputes after moving to open savanna and developing weapons, correlating with larger groups, brains, and theory of mind. Twin studies confirm heritability of moral foundations and dilemma responses.

Cultural anthropology reveals both universals and variation. Six major virtues appear across cultures: wisdom, courage, humanity, justice, temperance, transcendence. Analysis of 60 societies found seven universal moral rules: help your family, help your group, return favors, be brave, defer to superiors, divide resources fairly, respect others' property. Yet cross-national studies show wide divergence on divorce, homosexuality, abortion, and premarital sex. Territorial morality protects property and dependents while remaining permissive otherwise, aiming at universal rules; tribal morality imposes collective norms that are arbitrary and flexible. In-group/out-group discrimination enhances group survival; computational models confirm it produces in-group cooperation and out-group hostility. Nationalism and patriotism function as in-group/out-group boundaries; in-group loyalty is a moral foundation used substantially by conservatives, far less by liberals.

Moral psychology examines development and cognition. Kohlberg and Piaget proposed cognitive-developmental stages; Gilligan's ethics of care emphasizes development within interdependent relationships. Haidt distinguishes moral intuition—fast, automatic, affective feelings—from moral reasoning—conscious, controlled thought. Humans typically reason to support initial intuitions, but can override them through conscious reasoning, reframing, or discussion; social interaction drives most moral change. Recent work shows moral judgment concurrently evaluates three components: the agent's character (virtue ethics), the deed (deontology), and consequences (consequentialism). The widespread perception of moral decline across nations and decades is an illusion produced by distorted exposure to negative information and memory; peer evaluations have not actually declined.

Neuroscience reveals no single "moral module." The neural network for moral decisions overlaps with theory of mind (representing intentions) and empathy (representing emotional states), suggesting domain-global processing dissociable into cognitive and affective subsystems. The salience network detects morally salient content. The ventromedial prefrontal cortex activates during explicit right/wrong judgments; damage impairs consideration of intent, shifting judgment toward outcomes. The temporoparietal junction activates for intuitive reactions to implicit moral issues and understanding beliefs; disrupting it reduces reliance on belief information. Jean Decety argues the capacity to vicariously experience another's state was key to evolving social behavior and morality; psychopathy, defined partly by empathy deficit, supports this view.

Sociologically, morality maintains societal health and order. Public morality builds trust beyond kinship, enabling cooperation. Moral systems shift when productive forces, technology, or material conditions change. The condom enabled planned reproduction, altering attitudes toward marriage and sexuality. Gender equality brought contested debates over premarital sex, abortion, and artificial insemination. In low-productivity conditions, waste is immoral; in surplus, its moral weight diminishes. Stable moral rules often codify into law or religion; in Islamic law regions, religious doctrine is a codified moral system that ensured group survival in low-agricultural-productivity areas but now tensions with contemporary values. Marxism views morality as social ideology shaped by class and era, not innate, aligning with relativism.

Politics addresses "how ought we to live" at the social level. Haidt's Moral Foundations Theory identifies five foundations: care, fairness, loyalty, authority, purity. American liberals weight care and fairness higher; conservatives weight loyalty, authority, and purity higher, with both giving care highest overall weight. Haidt traces this to geo-historical factors: conservatism in homogeneous communities, liberalism in diverse port cities. Group morality, codified from shared concepts, regulates behavior; widespread conformity sustains groups, while inability to adjust codes can cause demise. Chomsky's principle of universality—if an action is right for others it is right for us—is, he argues, the elementary core of any moral code worth considering.

Religion and morality are not synonymous. Monotheistic religions define right and wrong by scriptural laws interpreted by leaders. Buddhism weighs intention and circumstances via merit. Hinduism decides by social rank, kinship, and life stage. Religions differ on dilemmas: Hinduism lacks absolute prohibition on killing; monotheistic traditions view abortion or divorce more absolutely. Hume noted great crimes are compatible with religious devotion; Blackburn and Anderson argue religious texts justify massacres, misogyny, slavery, and contain morally inconsistent teachings. Christian apologists counter that Jewish laws evolved toward protecting the vulnerable. Empirical studies on religion and crime are inconclusive: Denmark and Sweden, among the least religious nations, have the lowest violent crime and corruption; higher creator-belief correlates with higher homicide, mortality, STD, teen pregnancy, and abortion rates in prosperous democracies; some religiosity dimensions encourage homicide, others discourage it. Non-religious people are more motivated by compassion for pro-social acts; religious people more by moral obligation.
