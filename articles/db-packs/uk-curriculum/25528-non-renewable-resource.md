# Non-renewable resource

A non-renewable resource is a natural resource that cannot be replenished by natural processes at a rate comparable to human consumption. On a human timescale, the stock is finite. The elements themselves are conserved, except in nuclear reactions, radioactive decay, or atmospheric escape, but the concentrated, extractable forms require geological timescales to form. This distinguishes them from renewable resources such as sustainably harvested timber or wind, which regenerate within a human lifespan.

## Fossil fuels

Coal, petroleum, and natural gas originate from organic matter transformed by heat and pressure over thousands to millions of years. They supply the majority of global primary energy. Since the advent of the internal combustion engine, infrastructure and transport systems have locked in dependence on combustion. This economy is criticized for two linked reasons: physical depletion and climate change driven by carbon emissions.

An alternative hypothesis notes that methane hydrates on the sea floor contain more carbon than all conventional fossil fuels combined. However, their formation rate is unknown, and extraction at economically viable cost and scale remains unproven.

## Minerals and metal ores

Metals exist in vast quantities in Earth's crust, but economic extraction requires natural concentration into localized deposits. Plate tectonics, subsidence, crustal recycling, weathering, and organic activity achieve this over tens of thousands to millions of years. The resulting near-surface ore bodies are non-renewable in human timeframes. Rare earth elements, critical for electronics manufacturing, are among the most scarce and exhaustible.

## Nuclear fuels

Nuclear fission relies on naturally occurring radioactive material, primarily uranium-235. Uranium is mined in 19 countries at relatively low concentrations. Pilot programs have extracted only a few kilograms from seawater, though research suggests large-scale seawater extraction could become economically competitive for light-water reactors.

Conventional fission reactors consume uranium in a "once-through" cycle, producing approximately 200,000 metric tons of low- and intermediate-level waste and 10,000 metric tons of high-level waste (including spent fuel) annually worldwide. Unused natural uranium in this waste decays with a half-life of 4.5 billion years. The 2008 UNSCEAR estimate attributes 0.01 millisievert of average annual human radiation exposure to the legacy of atmospheric testing, the Chernobyl disaster, and the nuclear fuel cycle, against 2.0 mSv from natural radioisotopes and 0.4 mSv from cosmic rays.

Breeder reactors, which produce more fissile fuel than they consume, were classified as renewable by the World Commission on Environment and Development. The American Petroleum Institute concurs only for breeder fuel, not conventional fission, citing the centuries-long storage requirement for radioactive waste. Studies of the 2-billion-year-old natural fission reactor at Oklo, Gabon, inform long-term waste containment strategies.

Nuclear power provides about 6% of world energy and 13–14% of world electricity.

## Groundwater

Groundwater in certain aquifers is non-renewable when extraction exceeds natural recharge rates, which can span thousands of years.

## Land surface

Land occupies a unique category: it can be reused, but new land cannot be created on demand. Economically, its supply is perfectly inelastic, fixed regardless of price.

## Economic principles

In economics, a non-renewable resource is a good whose greater consumption today implies less consumption tomorrow. Hotelling's 1931 rule models efficient exploitation: under stable conditions, the net price (Hotelling rent) rises annually at the rate of interest, reflecting increasing scarcity. Hartwick's rule addresses intergenerational equity, showing that sustaining welfare requires investing resource rents in reproducible capital.

## Current trajectory

The fossil fuel economy remains dominant but faces converging pressures: physical depletion, rising extraction costs, climate policy, and competition from renewable energy. Investment capital has shifted toward solar, wind, hydro, geothermal, and biomass, driving commercialization and infrastructure conversion. As non-renewable stocks decline, their marginal cost rises, making alternatives economically inevitable.
