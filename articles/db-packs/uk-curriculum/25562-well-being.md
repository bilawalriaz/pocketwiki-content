# Well-being

Well-being is what is intrinsically good for a person—a measure of how well their life is going for them. Also called welfare or quality of life, it covers the balance of positive and negative aspects broadly, or only positive degrees narrowly, contrasting with ill-being. It is a person-specific value, distinct from impersonal value (good for the world at large), and an intrinsic value, good in itself rather than as a means like money. Researchers distinguish subjective well-being (how a person feels about and evaluates their life) from objective well-being (externally assessable factors such as health, income, education, and security). Individual well-being concerns a single person; community well-being applies to groups. Domain-specific forms include physical, psychological, emotional, social, economic, hedonic (pleasure-rich), and eudaimonic (fulfillment through actualizing potential) well-being.

Three major theoretical families identify well-being's essential features. **Hedonism** holds that well-being consists solely in the balance of pleasure over pain. Epicurus advocated a moderate version emphasizing a tranquil mind. Quantitative hedonism values pleasure by intensity and duration; qualitative hedonism argues some pleasures are intrinsically superior. Critics note sadistic pleasures seem bad and that Robert Nozick's experience machine—a simulation of perfect pleasure—lacks authenticity, suggesting pleasure alone is insufficient. **Desire theories** claim well-being derives from desire satisfaction, allowing diverse paths matching individual preferences. Critics object that people often desire harmful things, leading to modified versions requiring well-informed desires. **Objective list theories** assert well-being depends on multiple objective goods—health, friendship, achievement, knowledge, autonomy—regardless of subjective valuation. Critics charge these lists are incoherent or alienating. Related categorizations contrast subjectivist, objectivist, and hybrid theories; monist versus pluralist views; and perfectionism or eudaimonism, identifying well-being with excellence in fulfilling human nature through rationality, virtue, and actualizing potential. Variabilism argues well-being's nature differs across individuals or species.

Despite theoretical disagreements, wide agreement exists on contributing factors. Feelings, emotions, and life satisfaction form the subjective core. Pleasure and pain, tied to the brain's reward system, shape motivation; negative emotions serve adaptive functions by signaling problems. Moods last longer and color perception subtly. Life satisfaction is a global, long-term judgment about one's life as a whole. Achievements contribute based on significance—curing cancer outweighs counting cookie crumbs—though obsession can increase anxiety. Meaning in life organizes activity and motivates persistence; subjectivists see it as self-created, objectivists as grounded in objective values like truth or moral goodness. Friendship and social relationships provide intrinsic joy, support, trust, and belonging. Strength (time, trust, emotional intensity) and number matter, with diminishing returns. Health enables activity and reduces pain, yet severe illness does not preclude high subjective well-being. Thought patterns influence well-being; cognitive behavioral therapy reframes negative thoughts, while traditions like Buddhism recommend meditation and mindfulness. Knowledge and epistemic goods (intelligence, creativity, wisdom) help navigate the world. Autonomy and freedom allow life alignment with values but can backfire without maturity. Character traits and virtues (wisdom, courage, kindness, justice, temperance, gratitude) are central to eudaimonic conceptions, though virtue may conflict with well-being. Genetics explains up to one-third of subjective well-being differences; identical twins' life satisfaction correlates more strongly than non-identical twins'. Neurotransmitters mediate positive feelings. External conditions—work, government, physical environment, digital technology—also affect well-being, with mixed effects. Well-being tends to decline from childhood until around age 40 globally; thereafter trajectories diverge by region. Gender differences are minimal in most countries.

Several models structure components for measurement. Ed Diener's **tripartite model** defines subjective well-being as frequent positive affect, infrequent negative affect, and high life satisfaction. Carol Ryff's **six-factor model** comprises self-acceptance, personal growth, purpose, environmental mastery, autonomy, and positive relations. Corey Keyes' **social well-being model** uses integration, contribution, coherence, actualization, and acceptance. Martin Seligman's **PERMA theory** identifies positive emotions, engagement, relationships, meaning, and accomplishment. Michael Bishop's network model treats components as a mutually reinforcing causal network.

Well-being drives inquiry across fields. Positive psychology empirically studies optimal functioning, examining positive emotions, cognitive strengths, personality traits (via the VIA model's six virtues), self-concept (self-esteem, authenticity), social circumstances, resilience, and interventions (gratitude, kindness, meditation). The set-point theory challenges lasting improvement, proposing a stable, largely hereditary baseline. Philosophy develops theories, investigates scientific objectivity for a subjective phenomenon, and debates welfarism (well-being as the only basic value), the virtue-well-being link, death's harm, and animal well-being. Welfare economics evaluates policies using income distribution, GDP, consumer surplus, and compensating variation; the **Easterlin paradox** shows richer individuals are happier within a country, but rising average income does not raise average happiness. Policy increasingly adopts well-being indices—World Happiness Report, OECD Better Life Index, Bhutan's Gross National Happiness, UK Measures of National Well-being—and metrics like the **WELLBY** (one point on a 10-point life-satisfaction scale for one year). Law, sociology, and anthropology examine well-being's relation to legislation, social structures, and cultural norms, with anthropology comparing conceptions without ranking them. Religious traditions offer diverse paths: Hinduism emphasizes experiential self-knowledge via meditation; Buddhism targets suffering's elimination by removing craving and ignorance; Confucianism centers on virtuous activity and harmonious relationships; Taoism advocates effortless action aligned with the natural order; Christianity prioritizes connection to God; Islam centers on worshiping Allah. Biology and neuroscience trace well-being to genetic composition, neurotransmitters, and neural correlates via fMRI, while evolutionary theory views subjective well-being as a fitness-regulating tool shaped by natural selection. Medicine aims to restore and enhance patient well-being, balancing it against patient autonomy when they conflict.

The study originated in antiquity. Socrates rejected wealth and hedonism for wisdom and virtue. Aristotle developed eudaimonism: well-being as life's purpose achieved through virtues, distinguishing goods of the soul, body, and external circumstances. Hellenistic Epicureans sought tranquil pleasure through moderation; Stoics emphasized rational virtue. In ancient India, Hindu texts stressed duty and self-knowledge; Buddhism identified craving as suffering's root. Confucius highlighted benevolence; Laozi taught harmony with the Tao. Medieval Christian thinkers (Augustine, Aquinas) tied well-being to divine grace and the afterlife; Islamic tradition emphasized dedication to Allah. The Enlightenment shifted focus to earthly happiness via reason. Jeremy Bentham's **felicific calculus** quantified pleasure and pain for utilitarian "greatest happiness" policy; John Stuart Mill distinguished higher (mental) from lower (bodily) pleasures. Psychology emerged empirically; William James studied religious experience, Freud centered pleasure-seeking, Jung pursued individuation. Post-WWII surveys produced the Easterlin paradox. Late 20th-century models (Diener, Ryff, Seligman) and Derek Parfit's theoretical taxonomy structured modern research. Positive psychology's rise in the 1990s redirected focus from illness to flourishing. The 21st century integrates well-being into economics, policy, and global measurement.
