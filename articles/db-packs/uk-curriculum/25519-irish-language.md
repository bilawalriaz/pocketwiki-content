# Irish language

Irish (*Gaeilge*) is a Goidelic Celtic language of the Indo-European family, indigenous to Ireland. It was the majority first language until the 19th century, when English replaced it through state administration, socioeconomic pressure, and mass emigration. Today it survives as a community vernacular in rural *Gaeltacht* districts and as a revived urban language, with constitutional status in the Republic of Ireland and official recognition in Northern Ireland and the European Union.

## History

Written Irish begins with 4th-century *Ogham* inscriptions (*Primitive Irish*). By the 6th century *Old Irish* appears in Latin manuscript marginalia, absorbing Latin ecclesiastical vocabulary (*easpag* ‘bishop’ from *episcopus*). *Middle Irish* (10th–12th centuries) spread across Ireland, Scotland, and the Isle of Man, giving rise to Scottish Gaelic and Manx. *Early Modern Irish* (13th–17th centuries) served as a shared literary standard for Ireland and Gaelic Scotland; *Modern Irish* emerges in the 17th century.

The collapse accelerated from the late 18th century: English became the language of advancement, the Catholic Church preferred English, and the Great Famine (1845–52) devastated Irish-speaking districts. Monoglot speakers fell from ~800,000 (1800) to 320,000 (post-Famine) to under 17,000 (1911). The *Gaelic Revival* (1893 onward), led by the Gaelic League (*Conradh na Gaeilge*), reintroduced Irish into schools and created modern literature, but few adult learners achieved fluency.

## Current speakers and status

The 2022 Republic census records 1,873,997 people (40% of those aged 3+) claiming some ability, but only 71,968 (4% of adults) use it daily outside education. In the *Gaeltacht*—officially designated Irish-speaking regions—daily speakers dropped from 23,175 (2011) to 20,261 (2022); only one in four households is fluent. Northern Ireland’s 2021 census shows 228,600 with some ability (12.5%), including 43,557 daily speakers. Combined, roughly 30% of the island’s population reports some command.

Irish is the national and first official language of the Republic (Constitution, 1937), though government business operates mainly in English. The Official Languages Act (2003) mandates bilingual services; *An Coimisinéir Teanga* monitors compliance. In Northern Ireland, the 2022 Identity and Language Act granted official status after decades of political negotiation. Irish became an EU official language in 2007, fully operational from 2022.

Urban Irish is growing: *Gaelscoileanna* (Irish-medium schools) enroll thousands, creating a cohort of *nuachainteoirí* (new speakers) with distinct urban varieties. Some linguists warn these may diverge into a creole, as the phonological distinction between broad and slender consonants—fundamental to Irish grammar—is not consistently maintained.

## Dialects and the written standard

Three major traditional dialects correspond roughly to the provinces: **Connacht** (west), **Munster** (southwest), and **Ulster** (northwest, chiefly Donegal). Leinster died out in the 20th century; its last native speaker died in 1960. *Newfoundland Irish*, derived from 18th-century Munster, survived until the early 1900s.

Key differences: Connacht and Ulster pronounce final *-bh, -mh* as [w]; Munster uses [vˠ]. Munster retains synthetic verb forms (*caithfead* ‘I must’, *bhíos* ‘I was’) where others use analytic forms (*caithfidh mé*, *bhí mé*). Ulster uses the negative particle *cha(n)* instead of *ní*. Stress patterns differ: Munster stresses the second syllable when the first is short and the second long (*Ciarán* [ciəˈɾˠaːn̪ˠ]); Connacht and Ulster stress the first.

*An Caighdeán Oifigiúil* (1953, updated 2012, 2017) standardizes spelling and grammar for official and educational use. It selects single forms across dialects (e.g., *Gaeilge* for the language name, replacing *Gaedhilge*, *Gaoidhealg*, *Gaolainn*), but its spellings often reflect only South Connacht pronunciation, obscuring Munster and Ulster realizations. Schools outside the *Gaeltacht* teach the *Caighdeán*; *Gaeltacht* schools use local dialect.

## Core linguistic architecture

Irish is a **VSO, fusional, nominative-accusative** language. Its most distinctive feature is the **broad/slender consonant contrast**: every consonant (except /h/) occurs in a velarized “broad” and a palatalized “slender” pair, and this distinction carries grammatical meaning (e.g., *bó* [bˠoː] ‘cow’ vs. *beo* [bʲoː] ‘alive’). Broad consonants neighbor *a, o, u*; slender consonants neighbor *e, i*.

**Initial mutations** alter word-initial consonants to mark grammatical relationships:
- **Lenition** (*séimhiú*): stops become fricatives (*caith* ‘throw’ → *chaith mé* ‘I threw’; *Seán* → *a Sheáin* ‘John!’ vocative). Written with a following *h* (historically an overdot).
- **Eclipsis** (*urú*): voiceless stops voice, voiced stops nasalize (*Athair* ‘Father’ → *ár nAthair* ‘our Father’; *Gaillimh* → *i nGaillimh* ‘in Galway’).

Mutations are often the **only** marker distinguishing possessors: *a bhróg* (his shoe, lenition), *a mbróg* (their shoe, eclipsis), *a bróg* (her shoe, unchanged).

**Nouns** decline for three numbers (singular, dual with *dhá* ‘two’, plural), two genders (masculine, feminine), and four cases (nominative-accusative, vocative, genitive, prepositional-locative). Adjectives agree in number, gender, and case, usually following the noun.

**Verbs** conjugate for three tenses (past, present, future), two aspects (perfective, imperfective), four moods (indicative, subjunctive, conditional, imperative), and two relative forms. They employ a mixed **analytic/synthetic** system: present tense has synthetic forms only in 1st person (*molaim* ‘I praise’, *molaimid* ‘we praise’, *moltar* ‘is praised’) but analytic elsewhere (*molann sé* ‘he praises’). The conditional and past habitual favor synthetic forms.

**Two copulas** express “to be”:
- *Is/ba* for inherent identity/classification (*Is múinteoir mé* ‘I am a teacher’).
- *Tá/bhí/beidh* for transient states/location (*Tá mé anseo* ‘I am here’).

**No verb “to have”** exists. Possession uses the preposition *ag* ‘at’ with the transient *be* verb: *Tá leabhar agam* ‘I have a book’ (literally ‘is book at-me’). Prepositions inflect for person and number (*agam, agat, aige, aici, againn, agaibh, acu*).

**Numerals** operate dual decimal/vigesimal systems: 30 = *tríocha* (decimal) or *a deich is fiche* ‘10 and 20’ (vigesimal); 40 = *ceathracha* or *daichead/dá fhichead*. Numbers 2–10 use special nominal forms for people (*beirt* ‘two persons’, *triúr* ‘three persons’).

## Writing system

Ogham (4th century+) gave way to Latin script (5th century+). The traditional Irish alphabet uses 18 letters (*a b c d e f g h i l m n o p r s t u*); *j k q v w x y z* appear only in loanwords. Vowel length is marked by the acute accent (*fada*): *e* /ɛ/ vs. *é* /eː/. Lenition is written by adding *h* (*bh, ch, dh, fh, gh, mh, ph, sh, th*), replacing the historic overdot (*ḃ, ċ, ḋ, ḟ, ġ, ṁ, ṗ, ṡ, ṫ*). Gaelic type with overdots survives only in ceremonial contexts (e.g., the Defence Forces cap badge).
