# History of Northern Ireland

Northern Ireland was created on 3 May 1921 under the Government of Ireland Act 1920, partitioning six northeastern counties from the rest of Ireland. The border followed county lines rather than demographic logic: four counties (Antrim, Armagh, Down, Londonderry) had unionist majorities; two (Fermanagh, Tyrone) had slight nationalist majorities. Three predominantly nationalist Ulster counties (Cavan, Donegal, Monaghan) were excluded. This arrangement ensured a unionist majority but embedded a large Catholic minority—roughly one-third of the population—in a state designed to preserve Protestant and British identity.

From 1921 to 1972, the Ulster Unionist Party governed without interruption. The system institutionalised discrimination. Local government ward boundaries were gerrymandered to concentrate Catholic voters into fewer districts, guaranteeing unionist majorities on councils even where nationalists outnumbered them. The franchise restricted votes to property owners and their spouses and granted extra votes to business owners—a system abolished in Britain in the 1940s but retained in Northern Ireland until 1969. Public-sector employment, especially in shipbuilding and engineering, favoured Protestants. Catholics emigrated disproportionately, reinforcing the Protestant demographic advantage. By the 1960s, the Catholic birth rate was higher, yet their share of the population had fallen. Nationalist politics turned fatalist; the Nationalist Party boycotted the Stormont parliament for years, and Sinn Féin was banned.

Violence marked the state's birth. Between July 1920 and July 1922, 636 people died, 460 in Belfast. Catholics, under a quarter of the city's population, suffered 258 deaths against 159 Protestants. The new Royal Ulster Constabulary recruited few Catholics; nationalists viewed it as sectarian. A brief IRA border campaign (1956–62) failed for lack of nationalist support.

In the 1960s, Prime Minister Terence O'Neill attempted modest reforms. Opposition from hardline unionists like Ian Paisley and the rise of the Northern Ireland Civil Rights Association—demanding "one man, one vote," fair housing, and an end to gerrymandering—provoked loyalist counter-mobilisation. On 4 January 1969, a unionist mob including police reservists attacked a civil rights march at Burntollet Bridge. On 12 August, an Apprentice Boys parade forced through Derry's Catholic Bogside sparked the Battle of the Bogside; the RUC fired over 1,000 CS gas canisters in three days. Riots spread to Belfast. The British Army deployed on 14 August 1969, initially welcomed by nationalists as protection against loyalist attacks the IRA had not prevented.

The relationship soured rapidly. The Falls Curfew (July 1970) saw 3,000 troops seal off a nationalist area for three days. Internment without trial, introduced 9 August 1971, targeted almost exclusively nationalists; the SDLP withdrew from Stormont and launched civil disobedience. On 30 January 1972, the Parachute Regiment shot 14 unarmed civilians in Derry—Bloody Sunday. In March, London prorogued the Northern Ireland Parliament and imposed direct rule.

1972 was the deadliest year (479 deaths). The Provisional IRA, split from the Official IRA in 1970, pursued armed campaign against British rule; loyalist paramilitaries (Ulster Defence Association, Ulster Volunteer Force) killed Catholics indiscriminately. The Sunningdale Agreement (December 1973) established a power-sharing executive and a cross-border Council of Ireland. The Ulster Workers' Council strike (May 1974), enforced by loyalist paramilitaries shutting down power stations, collapsed the executive. London refused to intervene.

Violence declined after 1976 (under 150 deaths annually, under 100 after 1988), but the conflict deepened. The 1981 hunger strikes, in which 10 republican prisoners died, propelled Sinn Féin into electoral politics—Bobby Sands won a Westminster seat. The "Armalite and ballot box" strategy emerged. The 1985 Anglo-Irish Agreement gave Dublin a consultative role in Northern Ireland affairs, enraging unionists but surviving mass protests that had toppled the Sunningdale deal. It proved the British would not withdraw and that paramilitary intransigence could be politically counterproductive.

By the 1990s, military stalemate and Sinn Féin's electoral growth convinced republicans that negotiation could advance their goals further than violence. New leaders in London (John Major) and Dublin (Albert Reynolds) enabled the peace process. The 1993 Downing Street Declaration and 1994 IRA ceasefire led to the 1998 Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement, approved by referendums north and south. It created a power-sharing Assembly where ministerial posts are allocated by party strength, a North-South Ministerial Council, and a British-Irish Council. The Republic amended its constitution to replace its territorial claim with an aspiration to unity by consent. The IRA decommissioned arms in 2005; the British Army ended Operation Banner in 2007.

Devolution returned in May 2007 with Ian Paisley (DUP) and Martin McGuinness (Sinn Féin) as First and Deputy First Ministers—a symbolic breakthrough. The Assembly collapsed again in January 2017 over a renewable heating scandal (RHI), triggering an election that ended the unionist majority for the first time since partition. Sinn Féin refused to re-enter without an Irish Language Act; the DUP blocked it. The stalemate lasted 590 days, a world record, during which Westminster passed budgets and liberalised abortion and same-sex marriage (October 2019). The "New Decade, New Approach" deal restored Stormont in January 2020.

In February 2022, the DUP collapsed the Executive again, protesting the post-Brexit Northern Ireland Protocol's Irish Sea border. After two years, the DUP accepted a UK legislative package guaranteeing Northern Ireland's place in the UK internal market. On 3 February 2024, the Assembly reconvened with Michelle O'Neill (Sinn Féin) as First Minister and Emma Little-Pengelly (DUP) as Deputy First Minister—the first nationalist to hold the top post, reflecting a demographic and political shift unimaginable at partition.
