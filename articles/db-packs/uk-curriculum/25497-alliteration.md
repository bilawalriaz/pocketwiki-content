# Alliteration

Alliteration is the repetition of syllable-initial consonant sounds between nearby words, or of syllable-initial vowels when the syllable does not start with a consonant. It creates rhythm, emphasizes words, and shapes mood.

The word derives from Latin *littera* ("letter of the alphabet") and was coined by Italian humanist Giovanni Pontano in the 15th century, though he used it more broadly to include repeated letters that did not begin words or stressed syllables.

## How It Works

**Stressed syllables matter.** The repeated consonant sound must fall on a stressed syllable or an upbeat (a syllable with lighter stress that counts in the meter) in poetic meter. In James Thomson's line "Come . . . dragging the lazy languid line along," the final syllable of "along" alliterates with the preceding words because it carries metrical weight.

**Vowel alliteration works differently.** Any syllable-initial vowel can alliterate with any other, regardless of which vowel sounds they contain. John Milton used vowel alliteration in about 6.2% of *Paradise Lost* (670 lines), though same-vowel repetition was rare: only ten instances in the entire work. Two competing theories explain why vowel alliteration works: sonority (a shared acoustic quality across vowels) or the glottal stop that precedes vowel pronunciation.

**Consonant clusters can alliterate.** In "Bring a blessed boon," the clusters *br-*, *bl-*, and plain *b-* all alliterate together. A disputed claim holds that *sp-*, *st-*, and *sk-* cannot alliterate with simple *s-*, but examples like Barack Obama's "same high standards of strength and sacrifice" show *same* alliterating with *sacrifice* and *standards* with *strength*.

**Similar but non-identical consonants may alliterate**, often reflecting historical sound mergers. Middle English poets paired *z* with *s* (both originally *s*) and hard *g* with soft fricative *g* (sometimes written as yogh, ȝ).

## Related Devices

**Consonance** is the broader repetition of consonant sounds anywhere in a word (e.g., "coming home, hot foot"). Alliteration is a special case where the repeated sound opens the stressed syllable.

**Head rhyme** (or initial rhyme) requires each word to start with the same letter, as in "picture perfect" or "humble house."

**Symmetrical alliteration** uses parallelism or chiasmus (a reversed ABBA structure). With chiasmus, outer word pairs share sounds as they move toward the center: "rust brown blazers rule." With parallelism, matching sounds structure parallel clauses: "what in earlier days had been drafts of volunteers were now droves of victims."

## Historical and Modern Use

Alliteration structured entire poetic traditions: Old English (*Beowulf*), Middle English (*Sir Gawain and the Green Knight*), Old Norse (*Poetic Edda*), Old High German, Old Saxon, and Old Irish. Classical Latin, Greek, and Sanskrit poets used it as an ornament to suggest connections between ideas.

Today it appears across languages including German, Welsh, Mongolian, Hungarian, Somali, Finnish, and Icelandic, as well as in American Sign Language. Beyond poetry, it surfaces in music lyrics, magazine headlines, advertisements, business names, comic strips, video games, and character naming.

## Effects in Practice

Poets use alliteration to direct attention to specific words and create rhythmic patterns. Sound choice shapes mood: soft sounds like *s*, *l*, and *f* produce calm or hushed effects, as in E. E. Cummings's "Softer be they than slippered sleep the lean lithe deer the fleet flown deer." Harsh sounds generate tension or excitement.

In rhetoric, alliteration intensifies emotional appeals. John F. Kennedy used it 21 times in his Inaugural Address. S sounds can imply danger or deception; other sounds trigger positive or negative responses. The device appears across speeches from Cicero's "Patent portae; proficiscere!" to Martin Luther King Jr.'s "content of their character."

Translation often destroys alliteration. In Luke 10:41, Jesus doubles Martha's name with an alliterative Greek phrase (Μάρθα Μάρθα μεριμνᾷς) that English versions render flatly as "you are worried and distracted."
