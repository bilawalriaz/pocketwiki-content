# Roman Britain

Roman Britain was the province of **Britannia**, covering most of Great Britain from the Claudian invasion of **43 AD** until imperial withdrawal around **410 AD**. The occupation imposed Roman law, urban life, a money economy, and a professional army on an Iron Age tribal landscape, creating a hybrid **Romano-British culture** that persisted after the legions left.

## Conquest and Consolidation

Julius Caesar’s expeditions in **55** and **54 BC** established diplomatic clients and trade but left no garrison. A century later, **Claudius** invaded with four legions to restore the exiled Atrebatian king **Verica**. The Catuvellauni were defeated on the Medway and Thames; their capital **Camulodunum (Colchester)** became the first provincial capital. By **47 AD** Rome controlled the southeast up to the **Fosse Way**.

Resistance in Wales and the north prolonged the conquest. The Silures and Ordovices fought a guerrilla war under **Caratacus** until his capture in **51 AD**. In **60/61 AD**, the Iceni and Trinovantes revolted under **Boudica**, destroying Camulodunum, Londinium, and Verulamium and killing perhaps 70,000–80,000 people. Governor **Suetonius Paulinus** crushed the rebellion at the **Battle of Watling Street** despite being outnumbered twenty to one.

Governor **Gnaeus Julius Agricola** (77–84 AD) pushed into Caledonia (Scotland), winning a decisive victory at **Mons Graupius** (84 AD). Tacitus claims 10,000 Caledonian dead against 360 Roman losses. Agricola’s recall and strategic retrenchment fixed the northern frontier at the **Stanegate** road by **87 AD**.

## The Northern Frontier

Two walls defined the military zone. **Hadrian’s Wall** (begun **122 AD**), a stone barrier 73 miles long from the Tyne to the Solway, marked the permanent northern limit of Roman administration. The **Antonine Wall** (built **142 AD**), a turf rampart 39 miles across the Forth–Clyde isthmus, was held for only a generation before the frontier reverted to Hadrian’s Wall in **163/164 AD**. The zone between the walls remained a militarized buffer garrisoned by forts and outposts.

Three legions plus a larger auxiliary force were stationed in Britain for most of the 2nd century—a massive concentration that made the province a power base for usurpers. **Septimius Severus** divided it into **Britannia Superior** (south, capital Londinium) and **Britannia Inferior** (north, capital Eboracum/York) around **197 AD** to dilute that threat. His Caledonian campaigns (208–211 AD) achieved little beyond temporary treaties.

## Administration and Society

By the early 4th century, the **Diocese of the Britains** comprised four (later five) provinces under a civilian **vicarius** at Londinium, answerable to the praetorian prefect at **Trier**. Military command was separated: a **Dux Britanniarum** controlled the northern frontier troops. Local government ran through **civitates**—self-governing tribal cantons centred on chartered towns (*coloniae* like York, Lincoln, Gloucester; *municipia* like Verulamium). Each *civitas* sent delegates to an annual provincial council.

Population grew from perhaps 2–3 million in the 2nd century to **3.6 million** by the late 4th century, including **125,000** soldiers and dependants. **Londinium** held ~60,000 people and was ethnically diverse, with inhabitants from North Africa, Syria, and the Rhineland. Urban life peaked in the 4th century: towns acquired baths, forums, temples, and town houses with mosaics and hypocausts. Villas—rural estates with Romanized architecture—proliferated in the lowlands, especially 300–350 AD, reflecting agricultural prosperity.

## Economy

Britain was a net importer in the 1st–2nd centuries, consuming Gaulish **samian ware**, Spanish olive oil and wine, Mediterranean fish sauce, and Rhenish quern-stones, paid for by state bullion shipments to feed the army. Exports—silver, gold, lead, iron, tin, grain, oysters, salt—are archaeologically fainter. The **Dolaucothi** gold mines (Wales) used hydraulic prospecting and fire-setting under army engineers; Mendip lead/silver and Cornish tin were leased to private contractors.

From the mid-3rd century the trade balance reversed. The garrison shrank; British grain fed Rhine garrisons; local pottery industries (e.g., **Durobrivae**) replaced imports. Coin circulation peaked c. **165 AD**, then declined sharply after the **Antonine Plague** and **Marcomannic Wars**. By **402 AD** copper coinage vanished; by **430 AD** coinage as a medium of exchange had likely ceased.

## Religion

Native deities (**Ancasta, Rigonemetos, Sulis**) were syncretized with Roman counterparts (**Mars, Minerva**). The **druids** were suppressed by Claudius; their Anglesey stronghold was destroyed in **61 AD**. Emperor worship flourished at military sites. Eastern mystery cults—especially **Mithraism**—spread among soldiers (temples at London, Rudchester, Caernarfon).

Christianity arrived by **c. 200 AD** (Tertullian). The **Council of Arles (314 AD)** records three British bishops (York, London, Lincoln). **Saint Alban** (Verulamium) and **Saints Julius and Aaron** (Caerleon) were early martyrs. After **Constantine I** legalized Christianity (313 AD) and **Theodosius I** made it the state religion (391 AD), it became established. A distinct British heresy, **Pelagianism** (denying original sin), originated with the monk **Pelagius** (c. 354–420/440).

## Collapse and Transition

The 4th century brought escalating pressure: **Picts** and **Scoti** raided by land and sea; **Saxons** attacked the eastern coast. The **Saxon Shore forts** (built from c. 280 AD) proved insufficient. In **367 AD** the **Great Conspiracy**—coordinated attacks by Picts, Scoti, Attacotti, and Saxon raiders, compounded by garrison mutiny—overwhelmed the north and west. **Theodosius the Elder** restored order (368–369 AD), creating a fifth province, **Valentia**, likely in the far north.

Usurpers drained troops: **Magnus Maximus** (383 AD) took garrisons to Gaul; **Constantine III** (407 AD) did the same. By **401 AD** Stilicho withdrew more troops for the war against Alaric. A Saxon incursion in **408 AD** was repulsed by Britons themselves. In **409 AD** the native population expelled the Roman civilian administration (Zosimus). **Honorius’s** letter of **410 AD**—traditionally read as rejecting a British appeal for help—may have been addressed to Italy.

With imperial military and civil structures gone, **municipal authorities** and local **warlords** assumed power, maintaining Romano-British ideals. Germanic **foederati** invited to fight Picts (traditionally by **Vortigern**) eventually seized control of the lowlands by **c. 600 AD**. Many Britons migrated to **Brittany** and **Galicia**. The **Groans of the Britons** (446 AD), an unanswered plea to **Aetius**, and the **Battle of Deorham** (577 AD), which severed the West Britons from the southwest, mark the end of sub-Roman cohesion.

## Legacy

The Romans built **~8,000 miles of engineered roads**, many still underlying modern routes. They founded **Londinium, Mamucium (Manchester), Eboracum (York),** and dozens of *civitates* capitals. Water supply, sanitation, and hypocaust heating introduced urban infrastructure unknown before.

Unlike Gaul or Spain, Britain did not adopt a Romance language. **Common Brittonic** survived, later splitting into **Welsh, Cornish, Cumbric,** and **Breton**, absorbing ~800 Latin loanwords. **English** derives from the Germanic dialects of the 5th-century migrants. Place names (Chester, Lincoln, Winchester—from *castra*; *strat* from *strata*, road) and the **Roman road network** remain the most visible physical inheritance.
