# Election

An election is a group decision-making process where a population votes to choose individuals for public office or positions of responsibility. Since the 17th century, elections have been the primary mechanism of representative democracy, filling legislative, executive, and judicial offices at national and local levels. The same process operates in corporations, voluntary associations, and other organizations. The term "elect" means "to select," so referendums are sometimes called elections in the United States. Psephology is the study of election results and statistics, especially for predicting outcomes. Electoral reform describes efforts to introduce or improve fair electoral systems.

## History

Elections date to ancient Greece and Rome, where they selected rulers such as the Holy Roman Emperor and the pope. However, the democratic archetype—ancient Athens—viewed elections as oligarchic and instead used sortition (selection by lot) to fill most offices. The Spartan Ephors were elected by voice voting in 754 BC, predating Athenian democratic reforms by roughly 180 years. Other early systems existed: the Pala King Gopala (c. 750s–770s CE) was elected by feudal chieftains in Bengal, and the Chola Empire used the Kudavolai system around 920 CE, drawing palm leaves from a pot to select village committee members.

Suffrage has been the dominant historical question. Early elections in the UK and US were restricted to landed or ruling-class males. By 1920, universal adult male suffrage was standard across Western Europe and North America (except Switzerland), though women's suffrage remained contested. In Australia, Aboriginal people gained voting rights only in 1962. Some countries enforce compulsory voting—Western Australia fines first-time non-voters $20, rising to $50 for repeat offenders. The electorate excludes entire populations in practice: every jurisdiction sets a minimum age, and suffrage is typically limited to citizens to maintain "one man, one vote" equality, though EU citizens can vote in municipal elections regardless of nationality in their country of residence. The Roman Republic reached an electorate of 910,000 by 90 BC through the Lex Julia, though turnout rarely exceeded 10%. By contrast, Great Britain had only 214,000 eligible voters in 1780—3% of the population.

## Electoral Systems

Systems convert votes into political decisions through ballots, tallying, and seat allocation. Most systems fall into three categories: plurality (winner needs more votes than any other candidate, not necessarily a majority), majority (winner needs more than half), and proportional representation (seats allocated by vote share).

**First-past-the-post (FPTP)** elects the candidate with the most votes in single-winner districts. A winner commonly has less than half the vote—Toronto recorded an 18% winner in 2014. This creates vote splitting: when multiple candidates split a majority preference, a minority candidate can win. FPTP tends toward two-party systems and political polarization because voters abandon minor candidates who cannot win.

**Instant-runoff voting (IRV)** requires a majority. If no candidate reaches 50%, the last-place candidate is eliminated and their votes redistribute by preference until someone achieves majority support.

**Single transferable vote (STV)** uses ranked voting in multi-member constituencies for proportional results. A candidate reaching the quota (e.g., 20% plus one vote in a four-seat district under the Droop quota) is elected; surplus votes transfer to other candidates by preference.

**Approval voting** lets voters support as many candidates as they approve of, reducing vote splitting.

**Mixed systems** combine proportional and majoritarian elements. Mixed-member proportional systems produce results closer to pure proportionality, while parallel voting can be as disproportional as FPTP.

Switzerland holds elections and referendums about four times per year, with every citizen receiving ballot papers by mail without registration.

## Campaigns and Timing

Campaigns are organized efforts to influence voters through advertising and direct engagement. The most expensive campaigns were the 2012 US presidential election ($7 billion) and the 2014 Indian general election ($5 billion).

Democratic constitutions fix election terms to ensure accountability. Presidential terms vary: seven years in Ireland, six in Russia and Finland, five in France, four in the United States. Fixed dates improve fairness but lengthen campaigns and complicate emergency dissolutions. Some countries like the UK set only maximum terms, letting governments choose election dates strategically—a practice linked to democratic backsliding when abused. Rolling elections spread voting over time, as in US presidential primaries and European Parliament elections. Snap elections occur before the full term ends.

## Undemocratic and Sham Elections

In weak rule-of-law states, incumbent governments commonly interfere using police, censorship, and electoral manipulation. Non-governmental actors also use force, intimidation, or fraud. Gerrymandering manipulates district boundaries; electoral thresholds and winner-take-all rules waste votes. Foreign intervention is common: the US interfered in 81 elections between 1946 and 2000; Russia or the Soviet Union in 36.

Sham elections occur purely for show, with published results typically claiming 80–100% turnout and support. Ballots may offer only one "yes" option, and security forces may persecute those who refuse. The 1990 Myanmar general election famously backfired: the government-sponsored National Unity Party lost in a landslide to the opposition National League for Democracy, and the results were annulled.

## The Aristocratic Critique

Scholars argue that elections in liberal democracies function as aristocratic selection mechanisms that deny citizens equal chance of holding office. Bernard Manin identified four factors: voters treat candidates unequally, choosing requires distinguishing candidates, salience confers cognitive advantage, and information dissemination costs create barriers. These factors produce candidate pools selected by subjective standards—appearance, wealth, social prominence—resulting in representatives who are objectively unlike the voters they represent. Sortition, used in Western Europe before the 18th century, allowed regular citizens to exercise power. The Enlightenment shifted legitimacy toward consent, elevating elections as the mechanism for popular consent while sortition fell out of favor. Scholars who favor this view argue modern elections were never meant to give ordinary citizens power—merely the right to consent to rulers—and propose hybrid systems combining elections with sortition-based selection.
