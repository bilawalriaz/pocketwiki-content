# Magna Carta

Magna Carta is a royal charter of rights sealed by King John of England at Runnymede on 15 June 1215. Drafted by Archbishop Stephen Langton to end a rebellion by barons who demanded confirmation of Henry I's Charter of Liberties, it promised protection of church rights, protection from illegal imprisonment, access to swift justice, and limits on feudal payments to the Crown, especially requiring baronial consent for certain taxes. A council of 25 barons (clause 61) was empowered to seize the King's lands if he violated the terms. Neither side honored the agreement; Pope Innocent III annulled the charter within months, triggering the First Barons' War.

The 1215 charter failed as a peace treaty but survived as a political instrument. After John's death in 1216, the regency for his nine-year-old son Henry III reissued a shortened version, then again in 1217 alongside the new Charter of the Forest, at which point the larger document acquired the name Magna Carta ("Great Charter") to distinguish it. Henry III reissued it in 1225 in exchange for taxation, declaring it his "spontaneous and free will" and sealing it, giving it greater authority. Edward I confirmed the 1225 version in 1297 as statute law (Confirmatio Cartarum), establishing the principle that taxation required consent, though the mechanism was unspecified. This 1297 text remains on the statute book today, though nearly all its clauses have since been repealed.

In medieval practice, Magna Carta was invoked against royal officers—sheriffs and judges—rather than the King directly. It governed feudal reliefs (inheritance fees), wardship, debt collection, and forest law. Clause 29 (originally clause 39 in 1215) demanded due process: "no free man shall be seized except by the lawful judgement of his peers or by the law of the land." By 1350 half the clauses had fallen into disuse, superseded by legislation.

From the late 16th century, lawyers and antiquarians constructed a myth that Magna Carta restored an ancient Anglo-Saxon constitution overthrown by the Norman Conquest. Sir Edward Coke, working from the 1225 text, invoked it extensively against the Stuart kings' claims of divine right, arguing it made the King subject to common law, guaranteed trial by jury and habeas corpus, and founded Parliament. James I and Charles I suppressed discussion of the charter; Coke was arrested and his book banned. The Petition of Right (1628) cited Magna Carta in its preamble. After the Glorious Revolution (1688), Whig historians cemented the charter as the cornerstone of a "social contract" constitution. This interpretation shaped the American colonists, who believed Magna Carta guaranteed their rights as Englishmen. The U.S. Constitution's Fifth Amendment ("due process of law") and Suspension Clause (habeas corpus) derive phrasing from Magna Carta via Coke's glosses.

Victorian historians—Frederic Maitland, Edward Jenks, Albert Pollard—demonstrated that the 1215 charter concerned baronial privileges, not universal liberties. The "ancient constitution" narrative was a 17th-century invention. Clause 26 was the first repealed (1829); the Statute Law (Repeals) Act 1969 removed all but three clauses from UK law: clause 1 (church freedom), clause 9 (London's liberties), and clause 29 (due process for "no man"). Clause 29 also remains in force in New Zealand.

Today Magna Carta holds little direct legal weight in Britain; rights it named are secured by other statutes, but it retains immense symbolic authority. Politicians cite it in debates on detention limits; the Master of the Rolls dismissed its relevance to Occupy London evictions in 2012, noting the surviving clauses protect the Church and the City of London. Four 1215 exemplifications survive (two at the British Library, one each at Lincoln Castle and Salisbury Cathedral), inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register. As historian W. L. Warren observed, "many who knew little and cared less about the content of the Charter have, in nearly all ages, invoked its name, and with good cause, for it meant more than it said."
