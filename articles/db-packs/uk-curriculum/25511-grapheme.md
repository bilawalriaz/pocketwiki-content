# Grapheme

A grapheme is the smallest functional unit of a writing system. The term derives from Ancient Greek *gráphō* ("write") and the suffix *-eme* (by analogy with *phoneme*). The study of graphemes is **graphemics**. The concept is abstract, similar to a *character* in computing; a specific geometric shape representing a grapheme in a given typeface is a **glyph**. In notation, a glyph is enclosed in angle brackets: ⟨a⟩.

## Two competing concepts

Linguists define graphemes in two opposing ways. In the **referential conception**, graphemes are the smallest units of writing that correspond with phonemes. Here the *sh* in *shake* is a grapheme because it represents the phoneme /ʃ/. This view aligns with the dependency hypothesis — the claim that writing merely depicts speech.

The **analogical concept** defines graphemes via written minimal pairs such as *shake* versus *snake*. Here *h* and *n* are graphemes because they distinguish words. This view aligns with the autonomy hypothesis: writing is a system in its own right, studied independently from speech. Both concepts have weaknesses. Some models include two units: a "phonological-fit grapheme" (*sh* in *shake*) for the referential view, and a "graphemic grapheme" (*h* in *shake*) for the analogical view. Newer semiotic approaches define the grapheme as a minimal unit that is both lexically distinctive and correspondent to a linguistic unit — phoneme, syllable, or morpheme.

## Graphemes, glyphs, and allographs

The surface forms of graphemes are glyphs (also called graphs). Different glyphs representing the same grapheme are **allographs**. A grapheme is an abstraction over functionally equivalent glyphs. In English, the lowercase "a" appears in serif, sans-serif, italic, and bold forms — all allographs of ⟨a⟩ because substitution does not change word meaning. The Arabic numeral zero (U+0030) similarly varies in circularity or as a slashed zero.

Whether capital and lowercase letters are allographs or distinct graphemes is disputed. Capitals generally appear in triggering contexts that do not change meaning — proper names, sentence beginnings, headlines. But capitalization can determine meaning: *Polish* (the language) versus *polish* (for shining shoes).

Some linguists consider digraphs like ⟨sh⟩ in *ship* distinct graphemes, though they are generally analyzed as grapheme sequences. Non-stylistic ligatures such as ⟨æ⟩ are distinct graphemes, as are letters with distinctive diacritics such as ⟨ç⟩.

Identical glyphs may represent different graphemes. The characters ⟨A⟩, ⟨А⟩, and ⟨Α⟩ look alike but are Latin capital A (U+0041), Cyrillic capital A (U+0410), and Greek capital Alpha (U+0391), each with its own Unicode code point.

## Types of grapheme

Principal types are **logograms** (more accurately morphograms), representing words or morphemes — Chinese characters, the ampersand "&" for *and*, Arabic numerals; **syllabic** characters, representing syllables, as in Japanese *kana*; and **alphabetic** letters, corresponding roughly to phonemes. Additional graphemic components include punctuation marks, mathematical symbols, word dividers such as spaces, and other typographic symbols. Ancient logographic scripts often used silent determinatives to disambiguate neighboring words.

## Relationship with phonemes

In alphabetic systems, graphemes stand in principle for phonemes. In practice, orthographies deviate from exact correspondence. A phoneme may be represented by a **multigraph** — a sequence of graphemes — as the digraph *sh* represents a single sound in English. A single grapheme may represent multiple phonemes, as with Russian *я* or Spanish *c*. Some graphemes represent no sound, like the *b* in English *debt* or the *h* in Spanish words.

Correspondence rules become complex or irregular when historical sound changes are not reflected in spelling. "Shallow" orthographies such as Spanish and Finnish have relatively regular grapheme–phoneme correspondence. French and English have much less regular correspondence and are **deep orthographies**.

Multigraphs representing a single phoneme are normally treated as separate letters, not distinct graphemes. However, some languages treat a multigraph as a single unit for collation — in a Czech dictionary, words starting with ⟨ch⟩ follow those starting with ⟨h⟩.
