# Outline of ancient China

Ancient China spans the Xia, Shang, and Zhou dynasties, from roughly 2070 BC to 256 BC, when the Zhou fell. This era established the core political ideas, writing system, and philosophical traditions of Chinese civilization.

## Chronology and Dynasties

The period unfolds across three successive dynasties. The Xia dynasty (c. 2070–c. 1600 BC) is traditionally regarded as China's first hereditary dynasty, founded by Yu the Great. The Shang dynasty (c. 1600–c. 1046 BC) followed, leaving the earliest confirmed evidence of Chinese writing. The Zhou dynasty (c. 1046–256 BC) was the longest-lasting and splits into two phases: the Western Zhou (1046–771 BC), when the Zhou held unified authority from their western capital, and the Eastern Zhou (771–256 BC), when the court relocated eastward and real power fragmented among regional states.

The Eastern Zhou divides further into the Spring and Autumn period (c. 770–c. 476 BC), named after a chronicle of the state of Lu, and the Warring States period (475–221 BC), an era of escalating interstate warfare. The Warring States ended with Qin's unification of China in 221 BC, which marks the transition from ancient to imperial China.

## Political Thought

The Zhou justified their overthrow of the Shang with the Mandate of Heaven — the claim that heaven grants the right to rule based on the sovereign's virtue and withdraws it from the corrupt. This idea became the foundational legitimizing principle for every subsequent Chinese dynasty and the standard explanation for dynastic collapse.

Before the dynasties, tradition records the Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors, a sequence of mythic sage-rulers including the Yellow Emperor, Shaohao, Zhuanxu, Yao, and Shun, who were said to have established the basic institutions of civilization.

## Geography

Early Chinese geographic thought organized the world into the Nine Provinces: Ji, Jing, Liang, Qing, Xu, Yan, Yang, Yong, and Yu. This scheme, associated with the Xia and Shang periods, served as an idealized map of the known world and influenced Chinese administrative geography for centuries.

## Writing and Language

The earliest confirmed Chinese writing is the oracle bone script of the late Shang dynasty (c. 1200–1050 BC), inscribed on turtle shells and animal bones used for divination. This script evolved through bronze inscriptions on ritual vessels and later into the seal script, which the Qin dynasty standardized after unifying China. The spoken language of the period is reconstructed as Old Chinese; the written literary standard later scholars used was Classical Chinese.

## Philosophy and Military Thought

The political fragmentation of the Eastern Zhou, especially the Warring States period, produced an explosion of intellectual activity known as the Hundred Schools of Thought. Thinkers debated ethics, governance, language, and logic, laying the groundwork for Confucianism, Daoism, and Legalism.

The same era generated China's most enduring military texts. Of the Seven Military Classics, four were written during the Warring States period: *The Art of War*, attributed to Sun Tzu, a study of strategy emphasizing deception, intelligence, and winning without fighting; *Sun Bin's Art of War*, attributed to a later descendant of Sun Tzu; the *Wuzi*, by the commander Wu Qi; and the *Wei Liaozi*, of uncertain authorship. The *Six Secret Teachings*, attributed to Lü Shang (Jiang Ziya), the Zhou dynasty's founding general, predates this cluster and covers civil-military relations and grand strategy.

## Key Sources

Later historians relied on works like the *Records of the Grand Historian* and the *Twenty-Four Histories* to reconstruct this era, though these were written centuries after the events they describe.
