# Relationship quality

Relationship quality is the perceived positivity or negativity of a close relationship—romantic, friendship, or family—based on members' self-reported evaluations. It reflects overall feelings toward the relationship rather than objective behavior counts. Scholars measure it through self-report scales that either treat quality as a single dimension (unidimensional) or aggregate scores across hypothesized components such as satisfaction, commitment, trust, closeness, intimacy, passion, independence, and relationship flourishing (multidimensional). Responses typically use Likert scales; averages represent an individual's perception. No universal agreement exists on which components belong in the construct, even within a single relationship type.

## Romantic relationships

Early research split between quantifying observable behaviors (conflict frequency, maintenance acts, time together) and assessing internal perceptions (commitment, passion, trust). Modern scales favor the perceptual approach.

**Common dimensions:** satisfaction, commitment, trust, closeness, intimacy, passion, independence, relationship flourishing.

**Key scales:**
- **Quality of Marriage Index (QMI):** 6-item unidimensional scale (e.g., "My relationship with my partner is very stable").
- **Perceived Relationship Quality Components (PRQC):** Multidimensional; measures satisfaction, commitment, intimacy, trust, passion, love.
- **Relationship Flourishing Scale (RFS):** 12 items assessing meaning, personal growth, relational giving, goal sharing—designed to capture growth beyond closeness or satisfaction.
- **Couple Satisfaction Index (CSI):** 32-item satisfaction measure with 16- and 4-item short forms (e.g., "Do you enjoy your partner's company?").

**Outcomes:** High-quality romantic relationships correlate with lower rates of generalized anxiety disorder, PTSD, major depressive disorder, and bipolar disorder; greater happiness, lower stress, and higher life satisfaction. Physiologically, they associate with better self-rated health, lower mortality, improved cardiovascular functioning, and faster wound healing.

## Friendships

Friendship quality mirrors romantic structure: emotional closeness, communication, and trust strengthen bonds; open sharing deepens connections; effective communication reduces misunderstanding and aids conflict resolution; trust enables reliance for emotional support. High-quality friendships feature high, roughly equal prosocial behavior and mutual comfort relying on each other for social support.

**Adolescent friendships:** The Friendship Qualities Scale uses five dimensions—companionship, conflict, help, security, closeness. Quality links to higher self-esteem, less loneliness, better peer adjustment, and improved stress coping. At age 12, those with high-quality best friends show healthier sympathetic nervous system reactions to social rejection. Longitudinally, quality predicts better mental health: increased psychological well-being and resilience, reduced anxiety and depressive symptoms.

**Adult friendships:** Research often adapts romantic scales. The **McGill Friendship Questionnaire (1999)** conceptualizes quality as fulfillment of six functions: stimulating companionship (fun, excitement), help (tangible and emotional support), intimacy (closeness, trust), reliable alliance (dependability), self-validation (boosted self-esteem), and emotional security (comfort in adversity). The **Friendship Network Satisfaction (FNS) Scale (2022)** measures satisfaction with one's entire friend network via two subscales: closeness (feeling understood, close) and socialization (spending free time together).

**Outcomes:** Friendship quality predicts psychological well-being, happiness, life satisfaction, reduced loneliness and depression. University students with high-quality friendships adjust better to campus and adulthood. Greater friendship network satisfaction correlates with higher life satisfaction, better romantic and family relationships, and greater psychological well-being.

## Family relationships

Family measurement spans overall family functioning, parent–child dyads, and sibling dyads. The **Family Assessment Measure (FAM, 1983)** uses three components completed by all members: a General Scale (family system health), a Dyadic Relationships Scale (specific pair bonds), and a Self-Rating Scale (individual functioning). Overall family quality influences members' psychological well-being: better relationships associate with less psychological distress, stronger resilience, more self-compassion, optimism, self-esteem, and greater overall satisfaction.

### Parent–child relationships

Uniquely, parent and child receive different scale items. "Parent" means any primary caregiver. Quality typically reflects the balance of exchanging and receiving support and resulting emotional outcomes.

**Scales:**
- **Parent-Child Relationship Questionnaire (PCRQ):** Five domains—warmth, personal relationship, disciplinary action, power assertion, possessiveness.
- **Parent-Child Relationship Inventory (PCRI):** Six domains—parental support, satisfaction with parenting, involvement, communication, limit setting, autonomy.
- **Parent-Child Interaction Questionnaire—revised (PACHIQ-R):** Separate child (25 items) and parent (21 items) versions assessing conflict resolution and acceptance perceptions.

**Outcomes:** Parents with higher relationship quality report more happiness, well-being, and life satisfaction. Poor quality links to children's unhealthy eating behaviors, increased psychopathology risk, and disrupted oxytocin and cortisol regulation. Low-quality effects on mental health persist into adulthood. Positive quality predicts children's better future romantic functioning, academic achievement, and psychological and social well-being in college. A 2020 study found parental warmth and open communication associated with improved adolescent emotional regulation, fewer behavioral problems, higher self-esteem, better coping, lower stress, and reduced psychological difficulties; low trust and limited communication linked to emotional distress and conflict.

### Sibling relationships

Core theoretical components: warmth (intimacy, closeness, companionship), conflict, and differential parental treatment. Unequal parental affection worsens sibling bonds.

**Scales:**
- **Sibling Relationship Questionnaire (SRQ):** Warmth/closeness, relative power/status, conflict, rivalry.
- **Sibling Relationship Inventory (SRI):** Affection, hostility, rivalry.

**Outcomes:** Low quality links to increased sibling conflict, higher depression risk, and greater drug involvement. High quality associates with greater social competence, fewer adolescent problem behaviors, reduced depressive symptoms after stress, and higher psychological well-being. Supportive sibling relationships foster empathy, communication, and companionship; conflict provides practice for emotion regulation and problem-solving in a familiar setting. Consistent emotional support and communication strengthen trust and family bonds over time, shaping long-term social competence and psychological well-being.
