# Blank verse

Blank verse is poetry written in regular metre but without rhyme, most often in iambic pentameter, a line of five iambic feet (an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed one). It has been called the most common and influential form English poetry has taken since the 16th century; the critic Paul Fussell estimated that about three quarters of all English poetry is in blank verse.

The first known use in English was by Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey, in his translation of Virgil's *Aeneid* (composed around 1540, published posthumously 1554–1557). Surrey may have been inspired by classical Latin verse, which did not use rhyme, or by Ancient Greek verse or the Italian *versi sciolti*, both also unrhymed. Thomas Norton and Thomas Sackville's *Gorboduc* (1561) was the first English play to use the form.

Christopher Marlowe was the first English author to achieve critical fame for blank verse, exploiting its capacity for powerful, sustained speech. Shakespeare then developed two further possibilities: abrupt, irregular speech (he sometimes broke a single line between two characters) and increasingly frequent enjambment, where a sentence runs over the line break without pause. In his last plays he also used feminine endings, lines ending in an unstressed syllable, making his later blank verse exceptionally varied. This freedom was imitated by contemporaries and led to metrical looseness among less skilled writers, though John Webster and Thomas Middleton used it with some success. Ben Jonson, by contrast, wrote a tighter blank verse with less enjambment in *Volpone* and *The Alchemist*.

A single blank verse line broken between two speakers in *King John* shows how the form could mimic natural speech:

> My lord?
> A grave.
> He shall not live.
> Enough.

The other major landmark is John Milton's *Paradise Lost*, which uses blank verse with great syntactic complexity and freedom, supporting long, winding sentences that cross line boundaries freely. Milton also wrote *Paradise Regained* and parts of *Samson Agonistes* in the form. In the century after Milton, blank verse became stiffer and more regular; the best examples are John Dryden's tragedy *All for Love* and James Thomson's *The Seasons*.

William Cowper renewed the form at the close of the 18th century with *The Task* (1784), a volume of wide-ranging meditations. Cowper became the main influence on the next generation of major blank-verse poets, the Lake Poets William Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge, who were teenagers when *The Task* appeared. Wordsworth used blank verse for many of the *Lyrical Ballads* (1798 and 1800) and for his long autobiographical poems *The Prelude* and *The Excursion*. His verse recovers some of Milton's freedom but is generally more regular. Coleridge's blank verse is more technically self-conscious; his "conversation poems" such as "The Eolian Harp" and "Frost at Midnight" are his best-known works in the form. Keats's blank verse in *Hyperion* is modelled on Milton but takes fewer liberties with the pentameter. Shelley's in *The Cenci* and *Prometheus Unbound* is closer to Elizabethan practice than to Milton's.

Among Victorian poets, Alfred, Lord Tennyson and Robert Browning are the most prominent. Tennyson's blank verse in "Ulysses" and "The Princess" is musical and regular; his lyric "Tears, Idle Tears" is probably the first important example of the blank verse stanzaic poem. Browning's blank verse, as in "Fra Lippo Lippi", is more abrupt and conversational. W. S. Gilbert used blank verse throughout his 1884 opera *Princess Ida* (based on Tennyson's "The Princess"), though the other thirteen Savoy operas use prose.

Throughout the 20th century, blank verse of varying regularity has remained a staple of lyric poetry and narrative translation. Robert Frost, Wallace Stevens, W. B. Yeats, W. H. Auden, and John Betjeman all wrote significant blank verse.

Blank verse is also common in German literature. Gotthold Ephraim Lessing used it in his 1779 tragedy *Nathan der Weise* (*Nathan the Wise*), where lines run to ten or eleven syllables.
