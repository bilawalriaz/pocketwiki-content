# Ordnance Survey

Ordnance Survey (OS) is the national mapping agency for Great Britain. Formed in 1791, it began as a military enterprise to map the Scottish Highlands after the 1745 Jacobite rising, when the army lacked reliable maps to pursue dissenters. The Board of Ordnance, a predecessor of the Ministry of Defence, launched a national survey of the south coast in 1790 under William Roy, whose earlier work on the Principal Triangulation of Great Britain (1783–1853) established the rigorous standards the agency still follows. Since April 2015, OS has operated as Ordnance Survey Ltd, a government-owned company accountable to the Secretary of State for Science, Innovation and Technology.

## Survey methods: from triangulation to satellites

The agency's original maps were built by **triangulation**: measuring a precise baseline (Roy used a 5-mile line on Hounslow Heath in 1791) and calculating positions by angles from its ends. This created a network of known points across the country. For the 1936–1953 **retriangulation**, OS erected thousands of concrete **triangulation pillars** ("trig points") on hilltops as permanent, precisely surveyed stations; each theodolite reading was repeated at least 32 times.

Modern surveying relies on **OS Net**, a network of continually operating Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) stations providing real-time centimetre-level positioning. Surveyors still visit sites obscured by vegetation or new construction, aiming to capture major changes within six months. Orthorectified aerial photography—corrected for perspective distortion—now supplies most map detail.

## Reference systems: National Grid and height datum

OS maps use the **Ordnance Survey National Grid** (OSGB36), a transverse Mercator projection with a false origin southwest of the Scilly Isles, giving every location a unique easting and northing in metres. This replaces latitude and longitude for domestic use. Heights reference **Ordnance Datum Newlyn**, mean sea level measured at Newlyn, Cornwall, between 1915 and 1921. A 2016 redefinition shifted the datum upward by ~25 mm, reclassifying some hills as mountains.

## Map scales and series

OS mapping splits into **large-scale** (detailed, for professional use) and **small-scale** (less detailed, for leisure).

**Large-scale** products cover urban areas at 1:2,500 and rural areas at 1:10,000. These originated as the County Series (1840s–1940s), mapping county by county at six inches to the mile (1:10,560) and 25 inches to the mile (1:2,500). The 1:1,250 and 1:500 scales serve engineering and land registration. Since the 1980s, large-scale maps exist only as digital data.

**Small-scale** leisure maps include:
- **OS Explorer** (1:25,000, orange covers): 403 sheets showing all footpaths, field boundaries, and terrain detail for walkers and cyclists.
- **OS Landranger** (1:50,000, pink covers): 204 sheets covering Great Britain and the Isle of Man, general-purpose with fewer details.
- **OS Tour** (~1:100,000): regional sheets for touring.
- **Road maps** (1:250,000): eight sheets for motorists.

Paper maps now generate only ~5% of revenue.

## OS MasterMap: the digital core

Launched in November 2001, **OS MasterMap** is a single, seamless, topologically structured database of every fixed feature larger than a few metres. Each feature receives a **TOID** (Topographical Identifier), a persistent, meaningless key linking to a polygon in National Grid coordinates. MasterMap is delivered in themed layers:
- **Topography**: buildings, roads, vegetation, boundaries.
- **Highways Network**: routable link-and-node network with height restrictions, one-way streets, and road classifications.
- **Water Network**: rivers, lakes, flow direction.
- **Greenspace**: parks, allotments, sports pitches.
- **Imagery**: orthorectified aerial photography.

MasterMap is updated continuously; OS claims no feature is more than six months out of date. By 2011 it held hundreds of millions of TOIDs. Licensing costs depend on area, layers, TOID count, and duration.

## Data access: from trading fund to open data

From 1999 to 2015 OS operated as a **trading fund**, required to cover costs through data sales while remaining the public geographic supplier. This monopoly position attracted criticism. In 2010, **OS OpenData** released a package of datasets (including 1:250,000 and 1:50,000 raster maps, boundary data, and a simplified gazetteer) under an attribution-only licence compatible with CC-BY. High-value leisure scales and MasterMap were withheld, arguing that free access would require £30 million annual public funding.

In 2018, following the Geospatial Commission's recommendations, OS began releasing parts of MasterMap—property extents, TOIDs, building heights, highways, water network, detailed paths, and greenspace—free at the point of use via the **OS Data Hub** APIs, with transaction thresholds for larger consumers. Historical mapping over 50 years old falls under Crown Copyright and is free via the National Library of Scotland and SABRE Maps.

## Place names and persistent errors

OS established systematic place-name collection during the 1824–1846 Ireland survey. Surveyors recorded local spellings in "Name Books" with authorities cited. This process cemented some errors: the Pilgrims' Way on the North Downs follows a wrong route, and *Scafell Pike* retains a misspelling from an early Principal Triangle corner despite *Scawfell* being the local form. Once published, names persist because they become embedded in legal documents, gazetteers, and public usage.

## Organisational evolution

Headquarters moved from the Tower of London to Southampton after an 1841 fire. Blitz bombing in 1940 destroyed the city-centre offices; staff dispersed to temporary sites producing invasion maps of Europe. A purpose-built HQ opened at Maybush in 1969; the current "Explorer House" in Adanac Park opened in 2011. In-house printing ended in 2010; OS acquired full ownership of its printer, Dennis Maps, in 2025.

Governance shifted from the Board of Ordnance (1791–1855) to the War Office (1855–1870), then the Office of Works and later ministries (1870–1990). It became an Executive Agency in 1990 (operational independence), a Trading Fund in 1999 (commercial self-funding), and a government-owned company in 2015 (100% public ownership with commercial freedom).

## Research and derivatives

OS maintains a research department in geographical information science: spatial cognition, map generalisation, spatial data modelling, remote sensing, and semantic ontologies. It supports UK universities via the Digimap agreement and collaborates on MSc/PhD projects.

Licensed partners repackage OS data: Bing Maps uses it as a UK layer; Philip's publishes road atlases; Cassini reprints 19th-century County Series maps on the modern Landranger projection with 1 km gridlines, allowing direct historical comparison. OS also provides free Explorer maps to 11-year-olds (6 million distributed by 2010) and runs MapZone, an educational site for GIS awareness.
