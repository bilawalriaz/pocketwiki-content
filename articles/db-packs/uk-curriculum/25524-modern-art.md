# Modern art

Modern art designates the styles and philosophies of artistic work produced roughly from the 1860s to the 1970s. Its central tendency is a break with past traditions in a spirit of experimentation: artists tested new ways of seeing, rethought the nature of materials, and questioned the functions of art. A defining trajectory moves away from narrative representation toward abstraction.

## Origins: Enlightenment to Manet

The intellectual roots reach back to the Enlightenment. Critic Clement Greenberg called Immanuel Kant "the first real Modernist" because Modernism criticizes art from within its own methods, whereas the Enlightenment criticized from outside. The French Revolution (1789) uprooted fixed institutions, habituating the public to debate and choice—art historian Ernst Gombrich likened the result to selecting "the style of their building as one selects the pattern of a wallpaper."

Francisco Goya is often named the father of modern painting, though he was not a Modernist himself; later modernists claimed him as an influence. The most commonly cited birth date for the movement is 1863, when Édouard Manet exhibited *Le déjeuner sur l'herbe* in the Paris *Salon des Refusés*, a show for works rejected by the official Academy. Earlier markers exist—Gustave Courbet's *The Artist's Studio* (1855) or Jacques-Louis David's *Oath of the Horatii* (1784)—but as H. Harvard Arnason noted, "A gradual metamorphosis took place in the course of a hundred years."

## Nineteenth-century foundations

Three pioneering groups—Romantics, Realists, and Impressionists—established patterns repeated by later movements: a working method integral to the art, a visible active core of support, and international adoption.

**Impressionism** (1870s–1880s) argued that people see only reflected light, not objects. Painters worked *en plein air* (outdoors) to capture fleeting light effects. They formed a cooperative to mount independent exhibitions, bypassing state-sponsored salons.

**Post-Impressionism** (late 1880s–1900s) retained vivid color but rejected Impressionism's naturalism. Vincent van Gogh, Paul Cézanne, Paul Gauguin, Georges Seurat, and Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec each pursued structural or expressive ends. Cézanne's reduction of nature to "cube, sphere and cone" became the scaffold for Cubism. Seurat systematized color through Pointillism; Gauguin and Van Gogh intensified color for emotional force; Toulouse-Lautrec captured urban spectacle.

**Symbolism** and **Art Nouveau** ran parallel, emphasizing subjective vision and decorative integration across media.

## Early twentieth century: the great rupture

Between 1905 and 1914, Paris became the laboratory for radical new languages.

**Fauvism** (1905–1908), led by Henri Matisse and André Derain, liberated color from description. Matisse's *The Dance* (1909–1910) used intense warm figures against a cool blue-green ground to convey emotional liberation, influenced by his study of non-Western art.

**Cubism** began with Pablo Picasso's *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon* (1907), a brothel scene whose five women fracture into angular planes reminiscent of African tribal masks. Picasso and Georges Braque jointly developed **Analytic Cubism** (1908–1912), dissecting objects into interlocking facets. **Synthetic Cubism** (1912–1920s) reintroduced legibility through collage, *papier collé*, and mixed textures, merging subject matter with the physical surface.

**German Expressionism** formed in two collectives: *Die Brücke* (Dresden, 1905) with Ernst Ludwig Kirchner, and *Der Blaue Reiter* (Munich, 1911) around Wassily Kandinsky and Franz Marc. They distorted form and heightened color to externalize inner states. Kandinsky pushed toward pure abstraction by 1911.

**Futurism** launched in Italy with Filippo Tommaso Marinetti's 1909 manifesto, glorifying speed, technology, and violence. Benedetta Cappa Marinetti later expanded its ideology to include intuition and mysticism.

## War, anti-art, and the systematic avant-garde

World War I shattered the pre-war avant-garde and spawned **Dada** (Zürich, 1916; Berlin, 1918). Artists like Hugo Ball, Emmy Hennings, Hannah Höch, and Marcel Duchamp used chance, readymades (ordinary objects designated as art), and photomontage to reject aesthetic and cultural logic. Duchamp's *Fountain* (1917), a signed urinal, relocated artistic authority from making to choosing.

**Surrealism**, founded by André Breton in 1924, grew from Dada's ruins but sought to unlock the unconscious through automatic drawing, dream imagery, and juxtaposition. Giorgio de Chirico's metaphysical paintings (1911–1914) prefigured the style; Salvador Dalí, Max Ernst, and Joan Miró became its main practitioners.

Simultaneously, **De Stijl** (Netherlands, 1917) and the **Bauhaus** (Germany, 1919–1933) pursued total design reform. Piet Mondrian reduced painting to orthogonal grids and primary colors; the Bauhaus, under Walter Gropius and later Mies van der Rohe, unified craft, fine art, and architecture, with teachers including Kandinsky, Paul Klee, and László Moholy-Nagy.

The 1913 **Armory Show** in New York introduced European modernism to the United States. European artists fleeing World War I deepened the transplant.

## Post-World War II: New York takes the lead

Only after 1945 did the U.S. become the focal point of new movements. The 1950s and 1960s saw an explosion of mutually contesting tendencies:

- **Abstract Expressionism**: Jackson Pollock's poured paintings, Willem de Kooning's gestural figuration, Mark Rothko's color fields. Scale and physical action became content.
- **Color Field painting**: Barnett Newman, Helen Frankenthaler, Morris Louis stained unprimed canvas, emphasizing opticality over gesture.
- **Pop Art**: Andy Warhol, Roy Lichtenstein, Claes Oldenburg appropriated mass-media imagery and commercial techniques, collapsing high/low distinctions.
- **Minimalism**: Donald Judd, Dan Flavin, Carl Andre, Agnes Martin presented industrial objects in serial arrangements, rejecting illusion, metaphor, and the artist's hand.
- **Conceptual Art**: *Art & Language*, Sol LeWitt, Lawrence Weiner, Joseph Kosuth privileged the idea over the object; the work could exist as instruction, text, or documentation.
- **Performance, Happening, Fluxus**: Allan Kaprow, Yoko Ono, Nam June Paik, Joseph Beuys blurred art and life, using the body, time, and audience participation.
- **Land Art**: Robert Smithson, Michael Heizer, Richard Long, Ana Mendieta moved production into the landscape, often beyond gallery walls.
- **Video Art**: Nam June Paik and Wolf Vostell adopted television and video as sculptural and temporal media.

By the late 1960s, curators and critics favored these new forms over traditional media. Douglas Crimp's 1981 essay "The End of Painting" captured the mood, though painting revived in the 1980s through neo-expressionism (Georg Baselitz, Anselm Kiefer, Jean-Michel Basquiat) and figurative returns.

## The 1970s threshold

The period closes as artists and architects begin questioning "the modern" itself, producing works later labeled **Postmodern**: pluralistic, ironic, historically self-conscious, and skeptical of modernism's grand narratives of progress and purity. The center of gravity had shifted from Paris to New York; the definition of art had expanded from object to idea, action, and site; and the modernist drive toward medium-specific purity had fractured into a field of contested practices.
