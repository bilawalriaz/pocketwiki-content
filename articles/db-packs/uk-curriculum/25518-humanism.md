# Humanism

Humanism is a philosophical stance that centers human beings—their agency, dignity, well-being, and capacity for reason—as the starting point for moral and philosophical inquiry. In its contemporary form, it is a non-theistic, naturalistic life stance that relies on science, reason, and free inquiry rather than revelation or supernatural authority. Humanists affirm that humans have the right and responsibility to give meaning and shape to their own lives, and they advocate for human rights, democracy, secularism, and social progress.

## Historical Development

The term derives from Latin *humanitas*, used by Cicero for values of liberal education. Ancient Greek philosophy provides deeper roots: Pre-Socratics explained the world through reason and natural law; Protagoras expressed agnosticism about gods; Socrates turned philosophy toward human self-knowledge and ethics; Aristotle grounded ethics in human nature; Epicurus built a materialist philosophy centered on *eudaimonia* (flourishing through friendship, moderation, and freedom from pain). Medieval Islamic scholars in the Abbasid Caliphate preserved and extended Greek rationalism, developing ideas of individualism, skepticism, and free inquiry.

During the Italian Renaissance (14th–15th centuries), scholars like Petrarch revived classical texts, establishing the *studia humanitatis*—rhetoric, grammar, poetry, history, and moral philosophy—as an educational curriculum that dominated European pre-university education for centuries. The Enlightenment secularized these values: thinkers like Spinoza, Diderot, and d’Holbach argued rationality and naturalism could replace deism; utilitarianism made human happiness the moral metric. Darwin’s theory of natural selection naturalized humanity as a species among others, undermining theological exceptionalism.

Organized humanism coalesced in the 19th–20th centuries. Auguste Comte proposed a "religion of humanity"; ethical societies formed in Britain and the US; the *Humanist Manifesto I* (1933) declared a non-theistic, naturalistic philosophy. The American Humanist Association (1941) and British Humanist Association (1963) grew into mass-membership organizations. In 1952, Humanists International formed as a global umbrella, now advocating at the UN via UNESCO and UNICEF.

## Core Commitments

**Naturalism and reason.** The natural world is all that exists; science and rational inquiry are the reliable means to knowledge. Supernatural explanations are rejected as incoherent. Consciousness, creativity, and moral agency are emergent realities worthy of study and respect.

**Autonomy.** Beliefs and actions must result from one’s own reasoning. Kant provided the modern foundation: morality arises from human rational self-legislation, not fixed external codes. He proposed a universal *procedure* for ethics rather than universal *content*, allowing diverse societies to shape norms through reason.

**Universal human essence.** Human nature is shared across race, gender, and status. Collective identities are secondary to the individual, underwriting human rights and equal moral consideration.

## Morality Without Divinity

Humanist ethics is naturalistic, consequentialist, and grounded in human welfare. It rejects divine command theory: the Euthyphro dilemma asks whether God commands the good because it is good, or whether it is good because God commands it. If the former, goodness is independent of God and accessible to reason; if the latter, morality becomes arbitrary. Religious morality is always human-interpreted—scriptures yield contradictory readings.

Major frameworks include utilitarian approaches aiming at well-being and suffering reduction; moral naturalism viewing morality as an evolved, culturally shaped tool for social coordination; social contractual utilitarianism combining empathy, virtue, and idealism to pursue *eudaimonia* globally; and autonomy-centered ethics emphasizing personal moral autonomy and reason as the arbiter of moral judgment.

Critics claim "if God does not exist, everything is permitted." Humanists reply that acting from fear of punishment or hope of reward is heteronomy, not morality; true morality requires internal rational commitment. Secular societies with high social trust and low crime provide empirical counterevidence.

## Religion, Secularism, and Meaning

Humanism historically deconstructed religiously grounded authority and uncoupled human fate from divine will. Politically, humanists demand secularism: the state treats all citizens equally, religion remains private, and no faith holds privileged status—protecting religious freedom while preventing domination. Some humanists accommodate religious culture; the Humanist Society endorses humanist chaplains in universities and militaries. Copson argues that belief, practice, identity, and culture can coexist; one may subscribe to few religious doctrines yet identify as humanist. Most humanists favor religious tolerance over outrage.

On life’s meaning, humanists hold that meaning is created, not discovered. Camus’s *Myth of Sisyphus* illustrates the absurd hero who defies preset meanings yet affirms life’s value through self-determination. Personal meanings vary: pursuit of happiness without excess, contribution to human history, connection with loved ones and nature. Contributing factors include morally worthy purpose, positive self-evaluation, environmental understanding, mutual recognition, emotional connection, and the desire for meaning itself.

## Politics, Psychology, and Demographics

Contemporary humanism splits into individualistic (philosophical, leans libertarian, ethics informed by scientism) and collectivist (applied, leans socialist, ethics humanitarian) trajectories. Both oppose nationalism and totalitarianism. Humanism informed both 20th-century liberalism and humanistic Marxism focused on early Marx’s critique of alienation. Humanists International publishes the annual *Freedom of Thought Report* rating countries on rights for non-religious people.

Humanistic psychology emerged against psychoanalysis’s pessimism, emphasizing self-actualization, creativity, and the client’s worldview. Humanist counseling uses dialogue, critical thinking, and existential engagement, originating in post-WWII Netherlands and underpinning modern humanist pastoral care in the UK and Netherlands.

US data shows most humanists identify as atheist or agnostic; many were raised religious; most are highly educated, male, and non-Hispanic white.

## Criticisms and Consolidation

Criticisms fall into several camps: Western/neo-colonial (human rights discourse serves Western moral dominance; humanism secularizes Christian theology); bias (shaped by white, heterosexual males); vagueness ("semantic cloud"); materialism (denies spiritual needs, breeds greed—humanists reply this misreads materialism); and antihumanism (humanism is a pre-scientific ideology; objective truth is an anthropomorphic illusion; reason replaces one religion with another).

Historical labels—religious humanism, scientific humanism, secular humanism, Marxist humanism, Christian humanism, digital humanism—largely describe the same naturalistic, secular phenomenon. "Secular humanism" began as a pejorative but was embraced. "Christian humanism" is contested; critics argue it muddies the waters and is better described as "humanistic Christianity." In 1988, Harold Blackham urged dropping qualifying adjectives: the global movement shares core beliefs and values. Today, "humanism" unmodified suffices—by definition naturalistic, scientific, and secular.

## Organizations

Humanists International coordinates globally. Largest national organizations: Norwegian Humanist Association, Humanists UK, American Humanist Association, Humanistisch Verbond (Netherlands). They conduct non-religious ceremonies, advocate secularism, and promote humanist education and pastoral care.
