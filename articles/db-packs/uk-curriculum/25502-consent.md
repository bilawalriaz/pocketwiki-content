# Consent

Consent is voluntary agreement to another's proposal. Its meaning shifts by context: a person may willingly agree yet still fail to meet the legal threshold for consent.

## Types of consent

**Express consent** is unmistakably stated rather than inferred. It can be given in writing (a contract), orally, or through a clear gesture such as a nod. Unrecorded and unwitnessed, it is easily disputed.

**Implied consent** is inferred from actions or circumstances. A hockey player consents to contact by stepping onto the ice; a boxer consents to being punched. The test is whether the contact is ordinarily incidental to the activity.

**Informed consent** requires understanding the facts, implications, and consequences of an action. In medicine this means understanding a procedure before agreeing. In research it means understanding study procedures. In sexual contexts it means knowing any positive STI status one might be exposed to.

**Unanimous consent** means every group member agrees. **Substituted consent** lets a decision maker establish what an incompetent person would have decided. **Advance consent** is generally invalid, with narrow exceptions for healthcare directives and commercial contracts.

## Consent in law

In tort law (the law of civil wrongs causing harm), consent operates as a defence against liability. The Latin maxim *volenti non fit injuria* ("to a willing person, injury is not done") protects those who willingly accept known risks. Businesses often secure express consent through liability waivers.

Consent fails as a defence in cases of serious injury or death. English law rejected the "rough-sex defence" in accidental deaths during sexual bondage.

In international law, consent binds states rather than individuals. All relevant parties must agree for new rules to be binding, though some scholars argue consensus among states may suffice.

## Medicine and research

A medical practitioner must explain significant risks, those that might change a patient's mind, before consent is binding. Failing to disclose a material risk that then occurs is negligent. Material risks include the lost chance of a better result from a more experienced surgeon.

The UK Supreme Court modernized consent law with a patient-focused test: patients, not professionals, decide what level of risk to accept given full information, replacing medical paternalism.

In social science research, institutional review boards oversee studies involving human subjects. Informed consent means explaining the study's purpose and obtaining affirmation. Some studies permit deception but require immediate debriefing. Children, pregnant women, incarcerated persons, and those with mental disabilities receive special protections; children cannot provide informed consent.

## Digital consent

Under the GDPR, end-user consent must be freely given, specific, informed, and active. It is only one lawful basis for processing personal data. A 2020 study found that Google, Amazon, Facebook, Apple, and Microsoft use dark patterns, interfaces that manipulate users into consenting, raising doubts about whether their consent is lawful.

## Sexual consent

Sexual activity without consent from all parties is rape. Consent must be voluntary and can be revoked at any moment.

**Affirmative consent** ("enthusiastic yes") requires clear communication of willingness. It cannot be given by someone intoxicated, unconscious, or asleep. Its three pillars are: knowing what you are agreeing to, expressing intent to participate, and deciding freely and voluntarily.

Affirmative consent replaced the "no means no" model. Institutions describe it as ongoing: grinding on a dance floor is not consent for further sexual activity, regardless of prior history or relationship.

Some people cannot give consent. Minors below the jurisdiction's age of consent cannot validly agree to sexual acts. Persons with Alzheimer's disease or similar disabilities may be unable to consent even with a spouse.

Critics argue affirmative consent assumes people are rational, independent actors who can articulate boundaries at any moment. This neoliberal contractualism, treating consent as a rational contract between autonomous individuals, struggles with ambiguity, vulnerability, and unequal power. If marginalized groups are not free to say no, they may not be free to say yes. Others note the tension between legal definitions and socio-cultural change: law protects against violence, but transforming attitudes operates on a different plane.

Legal and moral understandings of consent do not always align. Adult siblings may voluntarily enter a relationship the law criminalizes as incest. A child below the age of consent may willingly choose a sexual relationship the law does not recognize. An age of consent is necessary but cannot account for varying levels of maturity and awareness.
