# Weight

In science and engineering, **weight** is the gravitational force on an object. It is a vector (has magnitude and direction toward the attracting body) with magnitude *W* = *mg*, where *m* is mass and *g* is local gravitational acceleration. The SI unit is the newton (N); a 1 kg mass weighs about 9.8 N on Earth and 1.6 N on the Moon. **Mass** is an intrinsic property of matter (constant everywhere); **weight** is a force that depends on gravity. Everyday language often uses "weight" for mass, and the kilogram (a mass unit) as the unit of "weight."

Three main definitions coexist. The **gravitational definition** (CGPM 1901) defines weight as the product of mass and acceleration due to gravity, a vector. Some textbooks treat weight as a scalar (magnitude only). The **operational definition** defines weight as the force an object exerts on its support—the reading of a spring scale. In free fall this force is zero, so the object is "weightless" despite unchanged gravitational pull; this definition is often refined by requiring the object to be at rest. The **ISO 80000‑4 definition** (*F*<sub>g</sub> = *mg*) includes the centrifugal force from Earth's rotation (an outward force due to rotation) but excludes buoyancy (upward force from a fluid); it is frame‑dependent (depends on the observer's reference frame) and matches the operational definition when the frame moves with the object. **Apparent weight** is any measured value that deviates from the chosen ideal definition, commonly due to buoyancy, acceleration, or mechanical suspension.

Because mass and weight are proportional in a uniform gravitational field, mass can be inferred from weight. On Earth, *g* varies by about 0.5 % with latitude (9.78 m/s² at the equator, 9.83 m/s² at the poles) due to rotation and oblateness. Spring scales measure local force and must be calibrated at their location for legal commerce. Balances compare the weight of an unknown object to standard masses, so they give the same mass reading anywhere gravity acts equally on both pans; they fail without gravity but work on the Moon.

Newton separated weight from mass, identifying mass with inertia and weight with the gravitational force relative to another body. He distinguished true weight (gravity alone) from apparent weight corrupted by buoyancy. Relativity made weight frame‑dependent: Einstein's equivalence principle (gravity and acceleration are locally indistinguishable) shows a scale in an accelerating elevator cannot be distinguished from one in a gravitational field. This led to debate over whether weight is fundamental or a context‑dependent quantity, but it remains essential in teaching and engineering.

Commercial usage retains **gross weight** (product plus packaging), **net weight** (product alone), and **tare weight** (packaging alone). Common non‑SI units include the pound‑force (lbf), kilogram‑force (9.80665 N exactly), poundal, and slug. The sensation of weight comes from forces in the vestibular system and is properly the sensation of *g*‑force (acceleration relative to free fall), whether from gravity, acceleration, or centrifugal effects.
