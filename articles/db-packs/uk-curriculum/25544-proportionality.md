# Proportionality

Proportionality describes a relationship where two quantities vary such that their ratio remains constant. In mathematics, this appears as direct proportion: *y = kx*, where *k* is the constant of proportionality. If *x* doubles, *y* doubles. Inverse proportion follows *y = k/x*: as *x* increases, *y* decreases such that their product stays fixed. These relations are expressed through ratios (comparing two quantities), fractions (a part relative to a whole), percentages (ratios scaled to 100), and aspect ratios (width-to-height proportions in geometry and media). Proportional division applies the principle to fair allocation—dividing a resource so each party receives a share proportional to their claim.

In control systems, proportional control produces an output signal linearly proportional to the error signal (the difference between a desired setpoint and a measured process variable). The controller gain *Kₚ* determines the response magnitude. This forms the *P* component of PID controllers, providing immediate correction but typically leaving a steady-state offset unless combined with integral action.

Scheduling algorithms use proportionally fair allocation to distribute resources among competing users. A scheduler assigns each user a share proportional to their weight or priority, maximizing the sum of logarithmic utilities. This balances efficiency with fairness, preventing starvation while adapting to demand fluctuations.

Typography distinguishes proportional fonts—where character widths vary naturally (e.g., *i* is narrower than *w*)—from monospaced fonts where every glyph occupies identical width. Proportional spacing improves readability and text density by respecting the visual weight of each letterform.

Legal systems adopt proportionality as a structural principle. In constitutional and administrative law, it requires that government measures be suitable to achieve a legitimate aim, necessary (no less restrictive alternative exists), and balanced (benefits outweigh harms). In international humanitarian law, proportionality governs attacks: anticipated civilian harm must not be excessive relative to the concrete and direct military advantage expected. This does not forbid civilian casualties but constrains them within a relational calculus.

Architecture and art treat proportion as a compositional logic. Classical orders define column heights, intercolumniation, and entablature depths through fixed ratios. Renaissance theorists such as Alberti and Palladio derived spatial harmonies from musical intervals and human dimensions. Body proportions in figure drawing relate part to whole—head height as a module for total stature, limb lengths relative to torso—enabling coherent representation at any scale.

Across these domains, proportionality operates as a scaling logic: a constant relational measure linking part to whole, input to output, claim to allocation, or means to ends. The mathematical core—*ratio as invariant*—reappears in feedback gains, scheduling weights, type metrics, legal tests, and compositional canons, each domain adapting the same structural idea to its own constraints.
