# Historical source

A historical source is any evidence humans have left of their past activities: written documents, oral testimony, the shape of landscapes, material artifacts, fine arts, photography, and film. Despite this breadth, historical study has nearly always relied on what historians can read in documents or hear from informants.

Historians classify sources by their proximity to the events described. The three main categories are primary, secondary, and tertiary.

## Primary sources

A primary source is a first-hand account created during the period under investigation by someone who lived through the events. Contemporaneity is the defining feature. A 1914 diary, an 1850 census, a protest photograph, a tool excavated from a settlement — all are primary sources for their respective periods. Format does not determine category; a memoir written decades later is not a primary source for the events it recalls, though it becomes one for the period in which it was written.

## Secondary sources

A secondary source discusses, analyzes, or evaluates information from primary sources. Produced after the period under investigation, often by non-witnesses, it selects, modifies, and arranges primary material while adding generalization, analysis, interpretation, or evaluation. A historian's monograph on the 1914 diary, a textbook chapter synthesizing census data, a documentary analyzing protest photographs — these are secondary sources, one step removed from the events.

## Tertiary sources

A tertiary source consolidates and indexes primary and secondary sources without adding new interpretation. It compiles established knowledge: encyclopedias, bibliographies, chronologies, handbooks. Its function is to help researchers locate seminal sources, identify key terms, and survey common knowledge or mainstream consensus. The boundary between secondary and tertiary varies by academic field.

## Why the distinction matters

The classification is not a hierarchy of reliability. A primary source can be biased, incomplete, or mistaken; a secondary source can be rigorous and illuminating. The categories describe the relationship between source and event: primary sources are the raw material; secondary sources are the first layer of scholarly processing; tertiary sources are the reference map. Historians move among all three, but their arguments must ultimately rest on primary evidence interpreted through secondary scholarship.
