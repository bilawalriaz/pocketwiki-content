# Elizabethan era

The Elizabethan era (1558–1603) spans the reign of Queen Elizabeth I, the last Tudor monarch. Historians often call it England's "golden age": a period of internal peace, growing prosperity, and extraordinary cultural achievement, especially in theatre. It sits between the religious violence of earlier Tudor reigns and the civil wars that would tear apart the seventeenth century.

## Why it worked

Elizabeth inherited a bankrupt state and a kingdom torn between Protestants and Catholics. Her response shaped everything that followed. She pursued fiscal frugality, clearing the Crown's debts by 1574 and building a surplus of £300,000 within a decade. She avoided the total war that bankrupted her neighbours: France was consumed by its Wars of Religion (1562–1598), Spain overextended itself, and England, by staying largely at peace on the continent, kept its taxes lower than other European states. Sir Thomas Gresham founded the Royal Exchange in 1565, England's first stock exchange, and trans-Atlantic trade and privateering, state-sanctioned raiding of Spanish ships, along with the slave trade, brought new wealth into the country, though it was distributed with wild unevenness.

## The religious settlement

Elizabeth's most delicate achievement was the Elizabethan Religious Settlement of 1559. She was Protestant but undogmatic, and she designed a church that deliberately blurred the sharpest doctrinal lines. The Book of Common Prayer (1559) reinstated a modified version of the 1552 prayer book, affirming a spiritual "Real Presence" in communion without defining exactly how, leaving it a mystery. She kept bishops, maintained apostolic succession, and preserved 98% of the existing clergy in their posts. The result was a Protestant church that still carried the ghost of Catholic tradition, a compromise that satisfied neither hardline Calvinists nor Roman Catholics but held for decades.

The settlement frayed under pressure. Pope Pius V declared Elizabeth a heretic in 1570, releasing her subjects from obedience. Jesuit missionaries entered England secretly. After repeated assassination plots, Ridolfi (1570), Throckmorton (1584), Babington (1586), Catholic priests were treated as traitors, not heretics, and hunted accordingly. Mary, Queen of Scots, the Catholic figurehead at the centre of these plots, was executed in 1587.

## Spain and the Armada

England's great rival was Habsburg Spain. The undeclared Anglo-Spanish War (1585–1604) ran through the second half of Elizabeth's reign. The crisis came in 1588, when Philip II sent the Spanish Armada to invade England. It failed. English ships were faster and more manoeuvrable, with heavier guns that could fire broadsides from a distance rather than closing to board. Spain's cannons reloaded slowly, and its strategy demanded impossible coordination between the fleet and an army waiting in Flanders. The Armada was scattered at the Battle of Gravelines and forced to sail around Scotland and Ireland, losing ships to storms. The war dragged on until the Treaty of London in 1604.

## Exploration and empire

England was a latecomer to colonisation. John Cabot, commissioned by Henry VII, reached Newfoundland in 1497 searching for a Northwest Passage. Elizabeth sent "Sea Dogs", John Hawkins and Francis Drake, to raid Spanish and Portuguese shipping off West Africa and in the Americas. Drake circumnavigated the globe (1577–1580) and sacked Cádiz in 1587. Humphrey Gilbert claimed Newfoundland for England in 1583. Walter Raleigh's Roanoke Colony (1584) ended mysteriously; the settlers vanished. The East India Company was chartered in 1600, establishing trading posts that would later become British India.

## Society and daily life

England was a society of sharp hierarchies. About one-third of the population lived in poverty. The able-bodied poor who left their parishes seeking work were classed as vagabonds and could be whipped or put in stocks. The wealthy ate manchet (fine white bread), roast beef, and elaborate spiced meals; the poor ate coarse barley or rye bread, cheese, and beer. Child mortality was around 150 per 1,000 births, and a 15-year-old could expect another 40–50 years of life. London grew from roughly 100,000 to 200,000 people across the reign. Most people lived in thatched timber-framed houses with one or two rooms; the wealthy built mansions with many chimneys, large windows, and symmetrical "E" or "H" floor plans.

Women married on average at 25–26 (noblewomen earlier, around 19–21). Elizabeth herself never married, cultivating an image of virginity and maternal devotion to her kingdom that became central to her political authority. Girls were largely excluded from formal education beyond petty school; boys attended grammar schools six days a week from age seven, studying Latin, Greek, and catechism.

## High culture

The era's cultural achievement was the English Renaissance theatre. The Globe Theatre opened in 1599. By 1595, about 15,000 people a week were watching plays in London. William Shakespeare and Christopher Marlowe wrote alongside a generation of playwrights who broke new ground in tragedy, comedy, and history plays. Edmund Spenser, John Lyly, and Richard Hooker produced major literary and theological works. William Byrd, John Dowland, and Thomas Campion composed madrigals and ayres for church and court. Nicholas Hilliard painted the miniature portraits that defined Elizabeth's public image.

## The end

Elizabeth died in 1603. James VI of Scotland, whom she had subsidised for years through a secret correspondence, succeeded her as James I, uniting the English and Scottish crowns. This made the Elizabethan era the last period of English history before the two kingdoms shared a monarch. The peace she had maintained did not survive her: within a generation, England would descend into civil war.
