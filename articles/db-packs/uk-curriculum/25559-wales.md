# Wales

Wales (Cymru) is a country on the island of Great Britain, part of the United Kingdom. Bordered by England to the east and the Irish Sea, Bristol Channel, and Celtic Sea elsewhere, it covers 21,218 km² with 2,700 km of coastline. The terrain is largely mountainous, shaped by the Devensian glaciation; Snowdon (Yr Wyddfa) at 1,085 m is the highest peak. Three national parks (Snowdonia, Brecon Beacons, Pembrokeshire Coast) and five Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty protect much of the landscape. The climate is temperate maritime: cool, wet, and windy, with Crib Goch in Snowdonia receiving 4,473 mm of rain annually, the UK's wettest spot. Cardiff, the capital and largest city, anchors the south-east where two-thirds of the 3.18 million population (2024 estimate) live. The economy, once driven by coal and steel, is now post-industrial: services dominate, tourism leverages the landscape, and agriculture remains livestock-based on 78% of land. GVA was £81.5 billion in 2023 (£25,742 per capita).

**Historical formation.** Permanent settlement followed the last ice age. Bronze Age copper mining at Great Orme fed continental trade. Roman conquest (43–383 CE) exploited mineral wealth; the post-Roman Britons fragmented into kingdoms. Anglo-Saxon expansion isolated the western peninsula, whose inhabitants the newcomers called *Wealas* (foreigners/Welsh), giving the country its English name. The native term *Cymru* and *Cymry* derive from Brythonic *combrogi* ("fellow-countrymen"). Brief unification under Gruffudd ap Llywelyn (1057–1063) ended with Norman incursions creating the Marches (border lordships) distinct from *Pura Wallia*. Llywelyn ap Gruffudd secured recognition as Prince of Wales (1267), but Edward I conquered the remainder by 1283. The Statute of Rhuddlan (1284) imposed English criminal law; the Laws in Wales Acts (1535, 1542) completed legal annexation, abolished the Marches, defined the border, and granted parliamentary representation. Industrialisation from the 18th century, based on coal, iron, and slate, swelled the population from 587,000 (1801) to 2.4 million (1911), concentrated in the South Wales Coalfield. The Great Depression devastated this mono-economy. Post-war restructuring replaced heavy industry with light industry and services. Devolution referendums (1997, 2011) created the Senedd (Welsh Parliament) with primary law-making powers over health, education, transport, and the Welsh language.

**Government and law.** Wales is a devolved nation within a parliamentary constitutional monarchy. The monarch (Charles III) is head of state. The UK Parliament retains reserved powers (defence, foreign affairs, justice, fiscal policy); Wales sends 32 MPs to the House of Commons. The Senedd (60 members, elected by proportional system) holds devolved powers under a reserved-powers model: it legislates on all matters not reserved to Westminster. The Welsh Government, a Plaid Cymru minority administration led by First Minister Rhun ap Iorwerth, executes policy. Local government comprises 22 principal areas (since 1996). The legal system is English common law (since 1536), but the Senedd passes Acts of Senedd Cymru on devolved matters. The Supreme Court is the final appeal. Wales has four police forces and five prisons (no women's prison; female inmates go to England).

**Language and demography.** Welsh (Cymraeg) and English are official languages. The 2021 census recorded 17.8% (538,300) of people aged 3+ speaking Welsh, down from 19% (2011) but up from 18.7% (1991); estimates suggest 29.7% (2022). Industrialisation brought English-speaking migrants to the south, accelerating language shift there, while the mountainous north and west retained Welsh-speaking communities. English is universal. The Welsh Language (Wales) Measure 2011 gave Welsh official status; Welsh-medium education is compulsory for ages 5–16. Ethnicity: 93.8% White (2021), 2.9% Asian, 1.6% Mixed, 0.9% Black. National identity: 55.2% "Welsh only," 8.1% "Welsh and British" (2021). Religion: 46.5% no religion, 43.6% Christian (2021). Net migration has driven population growth since the 1980s.

**Culture and identity.** Wales self-identifies as a modern Celtic nation. The red dragon (Y Ddraig Goch), emblem since Cadwaladr (c. 655), appears on the flag (official 1959). The Prince of Wales's feathers (three white feathers, gold coronet, motto *Ich dien*) is used by Welsh regiments and sports teams. Saint David (Dewi Sant), patron saint, is celebrated 1 March (not a bank holiday); leeks and daffodils are worn. *Hen Wlad Fy Nhadau* is the de facto anthem. Four UNESCO World Heritage Sites include Edward I's castles and town walls in Gwynedd, the Pontcysyllte Aqueduct, and the industrial landscapes of Blaenavon and Northwest Wales. The National Eisteddfod (annual festival of music, literature, performance) and Llangollen International Eisteddfod sustain the "land of song" tradition: male voice choirs, harp, folk revival. Rugby union is a primary symbol of national consciousness; the national team competes in the Six Nations and Rugby World Cup. Football's national team reached the Euro 2016 semi-finals. At the Olympics, Welsh athletes compete as Great Britain.
