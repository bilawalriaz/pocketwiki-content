# Topographic map

A topographic map is a large-scale map that quantitatively represents terrain relief, primarily through contour lines connecting points of equal elevation, while also depicting natural and artificial features: drainage, forest cover, administrative boundaries, populated areas, transportation networks, and individual buildings. Unlike planimetric maps (which omit elevation), chorographic maps (which cover vast regions at small scale), or thematic maps (which focus on a single topic), a topographic map conveys the shape and pattern of a landscape in measurable detail.

## Core mechanism: contour lines

Each contour line is an isohypse: a curve joining contiguous points at the same altitude above a vertical datum, usually mean sea level. The vertical distance between adjacent lines—the contour interval—is constant across a sheet. Closely spaced lines indicate steep slopes; widely spaced lines indicate gentle terrain. Closed loops represent hills (values increasing inward) or depressions (values decreasing inward, often marked with hachures). V-shaped contours pointing uphill mark valleys with streams; V-shapes pointing downhill mark ridges. Reading these patterns lets a user infer landforms—cliffs, draws, spurs, saddles—without seeing the ground.

## Symbols and marginal data

Features are rendered with conventional signs: blue for water, green for vegetation, black or red for cultural features (roads, buildings, boundaries), brown for contours. Colors may also classify road types. Every map includes a marginal legend explaining its specific symbology, the contour interval, the map projection, coordinate system, ellipsoid, geodetic datum, and the national grid referencing system. In the United States, the primary national series uses a 7.5-minute latitude/longitude grid; each sheet is called a quadrangle or "quad."

## Production methods

Historically, surveyors used theodolites, chains, and plane tables. From the mid-20th century, photogrammetric interpretation of overlapping aerial photographs via stereoplotters became standard. Modern production adds airborne lidar and satellite radar (e.g., SRTM, ASTER) to generate digital elevation models (DEMs) directly. DEMs and vector feature databases now underlie both printed sheets and dynamic digital maps.

## Historical development

- 1789: *Carte géométrique de la France*—first multi-sheet national topographic series.
- 1802 onward: Great Trigonometric Survey of India—triangulated the subcontinent and measured Himalayan peaks from over 160 km away.
- 1791 onward: UK Ordnance Survey—military origin (artillery siting), later a civilian resource.
- 1879: US Geological Survey created, consolidating federal mapping.
- 1913: International Map of the World (1:1,000,000, 4°×6° sheets)—project failed but left a global sheet-indexing system still used.
- 1980s: TIGER (Topologically Integrated Geographic Encoding and Referencing) and DEM datasets released into the public domain, enabling geographic information systems (GIS), GPS navigation, and on-demand printing.

## Uses and access

Topographic maps support infrastructure planning, civil engineering, mining, earth sciences, land navigation, hiking, and orienteering. Military training emphasizes contour interpretation for tactical movement. Since the 2007 EU INSPIRE directive, European national mapping agencies must provide public search, view, and download services for their official series; many offer data under Creative Commons licenses. Commercial vendors also compile international series.
