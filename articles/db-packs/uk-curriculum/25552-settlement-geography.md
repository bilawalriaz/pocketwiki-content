# Settlement geography

Settlement geography is the branch of human geography that investigates the portions of Earth's surface occupied by humans. The United Nations' 1976 Vancouver Declaration on Human Settlements defines a human settlement as the totality of a human community — whether city, town, or village — together with all the social, material, organizational, spiritual, and cultural elements that sustain it. The field examines how people distribute themselves across the land, the patterns they create, and the relationships between those patterns and the environments that shape them.

Traditionally, the discipline sits within cultural geography and divides settlements into two broad categories: urban settlements (cities and towns) and rural settlements (villages and hamlets). In this framework, settlements are treated as elements of the cultural landscape that have developed gradually over time. The term "settlement geography" itself has largely disappeared from English-speaking academia since the 1990s — the last major English-language text was published by Cambridge University Press in that decade — yet it remains a standard, active branch in German (Siedlungsgeographie), French (Géographie de l'habitat), Italian (Geografia insediativa), and Polish (Geografia osadnictwa) traditions.

That binary classification is now eroding. Processes such as counter-urbanization, peri-urbanization, and post-suburbanization — collectively described as urban sprawl — have blurred the line between urban and rural, especially in industrialized and newly industrialized countries. Planning strategies like unified settlement planning already treat urban and rural settlements as a continuum rather than a dichotomy. Consequently, an integrative geography of settlements that views the two as a continuous spectrum is regaining the prominence it lost during the twentieth century. The approach also extends into prehistoric, historic, and contemporary research, demonstrating its relevance across temporal scales.

The field's conceptual core has shifted through three influential definitions. Stone (1960) framed settlement geography as the description and analysis of the distribution of buildings by which people attach themselves to the land. Buildings, in this view, are tangible expressions of human–land relationships, mappable at any scale from broad regional patterns to individual structures; there is no restriction to architectural detail. Buildings are simply one representation of the process of people living in an area, a mappable division of the landscape to which attention needs direction. Jordan (1966) broadened the focus beyond mere distribution to the structures, processes, and interactions between settlements and their environment — soil, geomorphology, economy, society — that produce those patterns. More recently, the discipline has evolved into a holistic study of the interaction between humans and the physical and ecological world, explicitly concerned with sustainability, understanding the present landscape, and planning the future.

In synthesis, settlement geography describes and explains a settlement's location, substance, form, structure, and the functions and processes that shaped it over time — its genesis, from the Greek *génnēsis* for origin or birth. As an applied science, it projects future settlement development and contributes to the sustainable management of human–environmental systems.
