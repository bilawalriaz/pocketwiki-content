# Pension

A pension is a financial arrangement that provides regular income after a person stops working. Money flows in during working years—from the worker, the employer, or both—and flows out as periodic payments in retirement. The central distinction lies in who bears the risk that the money runs out or falls short.

## The Two Core Designs

**Defined benefit (DB) plans** promise a specific payout, usually a formula based on years of service and final or average salary (for example, 1.5% × years × final salary). The employer or plan sponsor must contribute whatever is needed to meet that promise. Investment risk and longevity risk—the chance that retirees live longer than expected—sit with the sponsor. If assets fall short, the sponsor must make up the difference. In the United States, corporate DB plans are governed by the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 (ERISA); public plans often operate under looser accounting rules, which has contributed to widespread underfunding.

**Defined contribution (DC) plans** specify only the input: a percentage of pay goes into an individual account invested in markets. The output depends entirely on contributions plus investment returns minus fees. The worker bears all investment and longevity risk. The account balance is portable when changing jobs, and the worker often chooses among a menu of funds. Common examples include the U.S. 401(k) and IRA, the U.K.’s personal pensions, Australia’s Superannuation, and New Zealand’s KiwiSaver.

Hybrid designs exist. Cash balance plans express a DB promise as a notional account balance, blending the guarantee of DB with the portability of DC. Notional defined contribution (NDC) systems—used in Italy, Latvia, Poland, and Sweden—track contributions in notional accounts that earn a rate tied to wage growth or GDP, then convert the balance to an annuity (a guaranteed income stream for life) at retirement.

## How the Money Is Financed

**Capital-funded (pre-funded) plans** invest contributions in assets—stocks, bonds, real estate—to grow a pool that pays future benefits. DB plans use actuaries to value assets against liabilities regularly; DC plans simply reflect market value. Most occupational pensions in common-law countries (U.S., U.K., Canada, Australia) operate this way, holding over 80% of global private pension assets ($50.7 trillion in 2019, with $32.2 trillion in the U.S. alone).

**Pay-as-you-go (PAYGO) plans** pay current retirees directly from current workers’ contributions and taxes. No large asset pool is built. Germany, France, Italy, and Spain rely heavily on this model for state pensions. Sustainability depends on the dependency ratio—workers per retiree. As fertility falls and life expectancy rises, that ratio drops: fewer contributors support more beneficiaries. PAYGO functions as a mandatory intergenerational transfer: working adults fund their parents’ pensions, expecting their children to fund theirs.

Many systems mix both. The U.S. Social Security trust fund holds special Treasury bonds; Canada’s CPP is partially funded with assets managed by the CPP Investment Board; Spain and France maintain reserve funds.

## The Multi-Pillar Framework

The World Bank’s “Averting the Old Age Crisis” (1994) organizes national systems into pillars:

- **Pillar 0:** Non-contributory, tax-financed social pensions to prevent elderly poverty (e.g., New Zealand Superannuation, U.S. Supplemental Security Income).
- **Pillar 1:** Mandatory, publicly managed, redistributive. Provides a minimum income and replaces a portion of pre-retirement earnings, often via PAYGO (e.g., U.S. Social Security, U.K. State Pension).
- **Pillar 2:** Mandatory, privately managed, fully funded. Occupational or personal plans, DB or DC, aimed at consumption smoothing (e.g., U.S. 401(k) with auto-enrolment, Dutch industry-wide funds).
- **Pillar 3:** Voluntary, fully funded personal savings (e.g., IRAs, extra employer contributions).
- **Pillar 4:** Informal support—family, home ownership, health care, other assets.

Most countries combine pillars. The taxonomy is prescriptive; in practice, public earnings-related schemes often sit in Pillar 1 despite being funded.

## Key Risks and Distortions

**Underfunding** plagues DB plans when promised benefits outgrow assets. The 2008 crisis turned an $86 billion surplus in the 100 largest U.S. corporate plans into a $217 billion deficit. Public plans face chronic shortfalls; in 2009 most U.S. states had unfunded liabilities exceeding reported state debt. The Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation (PBGC) insures private DB plans, but its own resources are limited.

**Demographic pressure** hits PAYGO hardest. Falling fertility, rising longevity, emigration of workers, and immigration of older adults all worsen the dependency ratio. Germany’s long-term care insurance charges childless adults higher contributions to offset this. Some proposals link pension contributions to the number of children a worker raises, internalizing the intergenerational transfer.

**Gender gaps** persist. OECD data (2013–2018) show gaps from 3% (Estonia) to 47% (Japan). Drivers include pay gaps, part-time work, career breaks for care, and longer female life expectancy (22.8 vs. 18.4 expected retirement years). Women often need higher contributions to buy the same annuity.

**Inflation** erodes fixed pensions. U.K. registered plans must index benefits to the Retail Prices Index (capped at 5% annually). Many DB plans offer limited or ad hoc cost-of-living adjustments.

**Portability**—keeping actuarial value when moving jobs or countries—is legally similar for DB and DC but practically easier for DC because no actuary is needed to calculate a lump-sum equivalent. Non-contributory benefits are often non-exportable.

## Historical Arc

The earliest recognizable scheme: Augustus Caesar (13 BC) paid Roman legionnaires 3,000 denarii after 20 years’ service, funded first from general revenue, then from a dedicated *aerarium militare*. Modern state systems began with Bismarck’s 1889 Old Age and Disability Insurance in Germany—PAYGO, financed by a worker tax, initially paying at age 70 (lowered to 65 in 1916). The U.K. introduced a means-tested old-age pension in 1908 (5 shillings/week at 70), then a contributory state pension in 1946. The U.S. created Social Security in 1935; federal civilian pensions date to 1920 (CSRS), replaced by FERS in 1987. Corporate DB plans boomed during World War II wage freezes; since the 1980s, DC plans have displaced DB in the private sector across the West.

## The Enduring Tension

Every pension system balances three functions: saving (consumption smoothing), redistribution (poverty prevention), and insurance (longevity and disability). No single design solves all three without trade-offs. DB plans insure longevity but concentrate risk on sponsors who may underfund. DC plans individualize risk but leave workers exposed to market crashes and cognitive decline. PAYGO avoids market risk but bets on demographic stability that is vanishing. The “pensions crisis” is fundamentally a mismatch between long-dated promises and short political horizons—a problem Augustus tried to solve with a dedicated fund, and one that still drives reform debates from Berlin to Sacramento.
