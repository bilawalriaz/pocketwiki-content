# Literary device

A literary device is any deliberate strategy of language use that a writer or speaker employs to focus attention, make language memorable, or evoke a specific emotional, rational, or aesthetic response. The terms *literary device*, *literary technique*, *figure of speech*, *rhetorical device*, *stylistic device*, and *trope* overlap heavily; in technical usage they carry subtle distinctions. A *trope* originally meant a figurative (non-literal) turn of phrase, but now also denotes recurring motifs or clichés. A *figure of speech* broadly synonymizes with literary device, but narrowly splits into *tropes* (deviations from literal word meaning) and *schemes* (deviations from expected word order). *Rhetorical device* emphasizes persuasive or argumentative purpose.

## Narrative devices
These shape storytelling. **Allegory** encodes a second, implicit meaning throughout a narrative (e.g., *Animal Farm* maps the Russian Revolution onto farm animals). **Flashback** inserts earlier events to supply context. **Foreshadowing** plants clues that build suspense. **Mood** is the audience’s emotional atmosphere; **tone** is the author’s attitude toward the subject. A **motif** repeats a word, image, or idea to develop theme or symbolism. A **plot device** advances the action. A **frame story** nests one narrative inside another (e.g., *Frankenstein* uses a sea captain’s letters to frame Victor’s tale).

## Sound-based devices
Poetry especially exploits auditory effects. **Alliteration** repeats initial consonant sounds (“Small showers last long”). **Assonance** repeats internal vowel sounds (“Flow slowly, you rosy glowing ocean”). **Consonance** repeats consonants anywhere in words (“streaks of light / flecked darkness”). **Cacophony** clusters harsh sounds for discord; **euphony** arranges pleasant sounds. **Onomatopoeia** mimics real-world sounds (*bang, plop*). **Rhyming** repeats terminal sounds to increase memorability.

## Rhetorical and argumentative devices
These persuade by appealing to emotion (*pathos*), logic (*logos*), or credibility (*ethos*). **Amplification** restates a point with added detail for emphasis; **pleonasm** uses more words than necessary. **Antanagoge** pairs a negative with a positive to soften criticism. **Apophasis** raises a topic by denying it should be raised. **Aporia** feigns doubt. **Enthymeme** is a syllogism with an unstated premise the audience supplies. **Gish gallop** floods a debate with weak arguments too numerous to rebut. **Hypophora** asks and immediately answers a question. **Innuendo** implies an accusation indirectly. **Metanoia** self-corrects for force. **Procatalepsis** anticipates and rebuts an objection. A **rhetorical question** provokes reflection without expecting an answer.

## Figurative language (tropes)
Figurative language departs from literal wording to heighten feeling, compress complex meaning, or forge unexpected connections. Kenneth Burke identified metaphor, metonymy, synecdoche, and irony as the “four master tropes” for their pervasiveness.

**Allusion** implicitly references a known cultural, historical, or literary work. **Apostrophe** addresses an absent, dead, or inanimate entity as if personified. **Analogy** compares two unlike things to illustrate a larger point. **Metaphor** fuses two unlike things without explicit comparison markers, often via a form of *be* (“That boy is a machine”). I. A. Richards distinguished the **vehicle** (the literal image) from the **tenor** (the implied meaning). Extended metaphors sustain the comparison across passages. **Simile** makes the comparison explicit with *like* or *as* (“humans look like ants”). **Hyperbole** deliberately exaggerates for effect.

**Metonymy** substitutes a related attribute for the thing itself (“the crown” for the monarchy). **Synecdoche**, a metonymic subtype, uses a part for the whole (“hands” for workers). **Personification** grants human traits to non-human things (“The moon smiled”). **Symbolism** invests a concrete element with abstract meaning (the risen sun representing hope). **Imagery** stacks vivid sensory details to “paint a picture.”

**Understatement** (or *meiosis*) minimizes significance; **litotes** achieves it through negation (“Heatwaves are not rare”). **Irony** inverts expectations. **Verbal irony** says the opposite of what is meant; **sarcasm** adds a biting tone. **Situational irony** produces outcomes opposite to what characters or readers expect. **Dramatic irony** gives the audience knowledge characters lack, as when Romeo’s suicide gains power because we know Juliet lives.

## Schemes (syntactic devices)
Schemes manipulate word order and repetition for rhythm, emphasis, or structural elegance.

**Anaphora** repeats opening words (“With mine own tears… With mine own hands…”). **Epistrophe** repeats closing words. **Anadiplosis** repeats the last word of a clause at the start of the next (“To die, to sleep; / To sleep, perchance to dream”). **Antimetabole** reverses word order exactly (“Ask not what your country can do for you; ask what you can do for your country”). **Antithesis** juxtaposes opposing ideas in parallel structure (“Some rise by sin, and some by virtue fall”). **Chiasmus** reverses parallel structure without identical words (“Who dotes, yet doubts; suspects, yet strongly loves”). **Asyndeton** omits conjunctions for speed (“Accursed, unhappy, wretched, hateful day”). **Polysyndeton** multiplies conjunctions for weight (“And pray, and sing, and tell old tales, and laugh”). **Epizeuxis** repeats a word immediately (“Horror! Horror! Horror!”). **Oxymoron** compresses a paradox into two words (“light and heavy”). **Zeugma** yokes two nouns to a single verb that must be read differently for each (“He caught the train and a bad cold”).

## Linguistic choices
**Diction** selects words for both denotation and connotation (stubborn versus tenacious, thrifty versus stingy), revealing tone. **Syntax** arranges sentences: length, voice (active/passive), structure (simple, compound, complex), and embedding (appositives, subordinate clauses). Wiesel’s “The ghetto was ruled by neither German nor Jew; it was ruled by delusion” uses parallel passive clauses to subvert expectation. **Verb choices** (tense: past, present, future; aspect: simple, perfect, progressive; mood: indicative, imperative, interrogative, subjunctive, conditional) encode temporal perspective and modal force, shaping how actions and states are presented.
