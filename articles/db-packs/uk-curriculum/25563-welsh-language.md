# Welsh language

Welsh (*Cymraeg*) is a Celtic language of the Brittonic subgroup, native to Wales and spoken in England, Argentina (Chubut Province), and diaspora communities. It holds *de jure* official status in Wales alongside English, regulated by the Welsh Language Commissioner since 2012.

## Origins and history

Welsh evolved from **Common Brittonic**, spoken across Britain south of the Firth of Forth. As Germanic and Gaelic expansion fragmented Brittonic territories, Welsh diverged from Cumbric (extinct) and Cornish (revived). Four periods with indistinct boundaries:

- **Primitive Welsh** (to c. 800 CE): earliest poetry; loss of final syllables (*bardos* → *bardd*, *abona* → *afon*)
- **Old Welsh** (9th–11th c.): poetry preserved in Wales and Scotland
- **Middle Welsh** (12th–14th c.): language of the *Mabinogion* and Welsh law; reasonably intelligible to modern speakers
- **Modern Welsh** (from 14th c.): standardized by William Morgan’s 1588 Bible translation, cementing **Literary Welsh** as a formal register

*Welsh* derives from Old English *wealh* (from Celtic tribal name *Volcae*); *Cymraeg* from Brythonic *combrogi* ‘compatriots’.

## Structure: sounds, grammar, writing

**Phonology** includes sounds rare in Europe: voiceless alveolar lateral fricative [ɬ] (spelled *ll*), voiceless nasals [m̥ n̥ ŋ̊], and voiceless alveolar trill [r̥]. Stress falls on the penultimate syllable; final unstressed syllables carry higher pitch.

**Orthography** uses a 29-letter Latin alphabet with eight digraphs (*ch, dd, ff, ng, ll, ph, rh, th*) treated as single letters. *W* and *Y* function as vowels alongside *a, e, i, o, u*. The circumflex (*to bach*) marks long vowels, often distinguishing homographs (*man* ‘place’ vs *mân* ‘fine’).

**Grammar** is moderately inflecting with VSO word order. Nouns have two genders (masculine/feminine) marked via **initial consonant mutations** (soft, nasal, aspirate) rather than case endings. Verbs inflect for person, number, and mood. A defining feature is **conjugated prepositions**—prepositions fused with personal pronouns (*i mi* ‘to me’, *wrthot ti* ‘by you’).

**Registers** differ sharply. **Literary Welsh** (standardized by the 1588 Bible) uses inflected verbs, pro-drop, distinct subjunctives, and archaic mutations. **Colloquial Welsh** relies on periphrastic constructions with auxiliaries (*bod* ‘be’ + *yn* + verbnoun), omits subjunctives outside fixed idioms, and readily adopts English loans. Gareth King notes they “are almost two different languages in practice.”

**Vocabulary** combines core Brittonic (*wy* ‘egg’, *carreg* ‘stone’) with extensive Latin loans (*ffenestr* < *fenestra*, *gwin* < *vinum*) and modern English loans (*silff*, *giât*). The traditional **vigesimal (base-20) counting system** persists for time, ages, and dates (*pedwar ar bymtheg ar hugain* = 39), alongside a decimal system from Patagonian Welsh (*tri deg naw* = 39).

## Speakers and revival

The 2021 census recorded 538,300 Welsh speakers in Wales (17.8% aged 3+); the Annual Population Survey estimated 843,700 (27.3%) for March 2026. Surveys consistently show higher figures than censuses. UNESCO classifies Welsh as **Vulnerable** but the least endangered Celtic language. The Welsh Government targets one million speakers and doubled daily use by 2050.

Decline accelerated during 19th-century industrialization (English immigration to South Wales Valleys) and the **Welsh Not** era, when children were punished for speaking Welsh. The 1847 *Brad y Llyfrau Gleision* report disparaged the language, embedding an inferiority complex.

Reversal began with **Welsh-medium education**: first primary school (1939), first secondary (1956). Since 2000, Welsh has been compulsory to age 16 in all Welsh schools. In 2014/15, 101,345 pupils received Welsh-medium education (22% of total). Adult learning centres operate across six regions.

## Legal status and public life

The **Welsh Language (Wales) Measure 2011** confirmed official status, created the Commissioner with enforcement powers, and imposed service-delivery duties on public bodies and some private companies. Road signs display Welsh first (since 2016). The *Senedd* operates bilingually. Welsh appears on UK coinage (*Pleidiol wyf i’m gwlad*), in courts, and at the EU (first used 2008). *S4C* (1982) and *BBC Radio Cymru* (1977) provide broadcast media.

## Global presence

**Patagonian Welsh** (1,500–5,000 speakers) originated with the 1865 *Y Wladfa* settlement in Chubut Province, Argentina; it incorporates Spanish loans and a decimal counting system later adopted in Wales. Communities persist in England (~110,000), the US (2,003), Canada (1,130 mother-tongue), Australia (1,737), and New Zealand (1,083). A Welsh greeting—*Iechyd da i chwi yn awr ac yn oesoedd* (“Good health to you now and forever”)—travels aboard the Voyager Golden Record.
