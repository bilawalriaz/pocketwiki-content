# History of Scotland

Scotland's recorded history begins with the Roman Empire's arrival in the 1st century AD. Rome established Britannia up to the Antonine Wall (Forth–Clyde) but soon retreated to Hadrian's Wall (Tyne–Solway). North lay Caledonia, inhabited by the Picti (Picts). As Rome withdrew, the Scoti, a Gaelic tribe from Ireland, colonised the west coast, founding Dál Riata in the 6th century. Irish missionaries, notably Columba, converted the Picts to Celtic Christianity. By the 9th century, Viking raids forced Picts and Gaels to unite into the Kingdom of Alba under the House of Alpin; the term *Scotia* gradually replaced *Alba*.

The kingdom consolidated under the House of Dunkeld. David I introduced feudal land tenure, royal burghs, sheriffs, justiciars, and reformed monasticism on Continental lines, creating medieval Scotland's institutional skeleton. Alexander III annexed the Western Isles by the 1266 Treaty of Perth.

Alexander's death in 1286 and his granddaughter Margaret's in 1290 triggered a succession crisis. Edward I of England arbitrated, extracting feudal overlordship, and installed John Balliol. Edward then systematically undermined Balliol, provoking the Wars of Independence. William Wallace and Andrew de Moray won at Stirling Bridge but lost at Falkirk. Robert the Bruce murdered his rival John Comyn, was crowned, and after years of guerrilla war defeated Edward II at Bannockburn. The 1320 Declaration of Arbroath asserted sovereignty to the Pope; the 1328 Treaty of Edinburgh–Northampton secured English recognition.

The Stewart dynasty began in 1371. James IV married Margaret Tudor (1503), linking the crowns. His death at Flodden (1513) left an infant heir. Mary, Queen of Scots, raised Catholic in France, returned to a Protestant kingdom. The 1560 Reformation Parliament, led by John Knox, abolished papal authority and the Mass, establishing a Presbyterian Kirk governed by elders rather than bishops. Mary's turbulent reign ended in abdication (1567) and execution in England (1587). Her son, James VI, inherited the English throne in 1603 (Union of the Crowns), moving to London.

The 17th century saw religious and constitutional conflict. Charles I's attempt to impose an Anglican prayer book sparked the 1638 National Covenant and the Bishops' Wars. The ensuing Wars of the Three Kingdoms drew Scotland into civil war; Covenanters allied with English Parliamentarians, then split over the Engagement with Charles I. Cromwell invaded, defeating the Scots at Dunbar and Worcester, imposing a short-lived parliamentary union (1652–1660). The Restoration (1660) brought back episcopacy, provoking the Killing Time of Presbyterian resistance. James VII's Catholicism and a male heir (1688) triggered the Glorious Revolution; the Scottish Estates declared he had forfeited the crown, offering it to William and Mary with a Claim of Right limiting royal power. Presbyterianism was restored, but Jacobitism—loyalty to the exiled Stuarts—persisted, especially in the Highlands.

Economic desperation in the 1690s (failed harvests, the disastrous Darien scheme) made union with England attractive. The 1707 Treaty of Union created Great Britain: the Scottish Parliament dissolved, 45 MPs joined Westminster, but Scots law and the Kirk remained separate. Jacobite risings in 1715 and 1745 failed; the 1746 Battle of Culloden ended the military threat. Post-Culloden, the Heritable Jurisdictions Act stripped clan chiefs of judicial power, accelerating their transformation into commercial landlords. The Highland Clearances evicted tenants for sheep, then deer forests; the 1846 potato famine drove mass assisted emigration.

Union opened imperial markets. The Scottish Enlightenment (Hume, Smith, Black, Hutton) flourished in Edinburgh and Glasgow, pioneering moral philosophy, economics, chemistry, and geology. Linen, then cotton, coal, iron, and steel drove industrialisation. The hot blast (1828) revolutionised iron; Clydeside became the world's shipbuilding centre. Railways (from 1831) integrated markets. Population quadrupled (1.6M in 1801 to 4.5M in 1901); Glasgow became the "Second City of Empire." Urban overcrowding spawned public health crises and a distinctive working-class culture.

Culturally, Walter Scott's Waverley novels and the 1822 royal visit romanticised Highland dress, fixing tartan and the kilt as national symbols. The 1886 Crofters' Act secured tenure for Highland smallholders. In the 20th century, heavy industry declined sharply after WWII. A 1997 referendum approved devolution; the Scottish Parliament reconvened in 1999. The 2014 independence referendum resulted in a 55% vote to remain in the UK.
