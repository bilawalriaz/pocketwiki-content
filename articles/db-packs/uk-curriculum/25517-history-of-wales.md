# History of Wales

Wales is a Celtic nation that preserved its language and identity through millennia of invasion, industrial transformation, and political integration, achieving devolved self-government within the United Kingdom.

## Prehistoric and Roman Foundations

Neanderthals occupied the region 230,000 years ago; modern humans settled continuously after the last ice age (c. 9000 BC). By the Iron Age (c. 800 BC) the inhabitants spoke **Brittonic**, ancestor of Welsh, Cornish, and Breton, and organised into tribes—Ordovices, Deceangli, Silures, Demetae—recorded by Rome. Over 600 hillforts reflect a society geared to local defence, not centralised statehood.

Rome conquered Wales in a thirty-year campaign (AD 48–78), meeting fierce resistance from the Silures and Ordovices. The occupation was military: a network of forts and roads exploiting gold, copper, and lead. Only the southern coastal zone saw significant Romanisation; the Demetae there uniquely retained their name and territory. Christianity took root in the 4th century. In 383 the general Magnus Maximus stripped Britain of troops for his imperial bid, transferring authority to local leaders. Medieval Welsh dynasties later claimed descent from him (Macsen Wledig), anchoring their legitimacy in a Roman past.

## Early Medieval Kingdoms (383–1066)

After the Roman withdrawal (c. 410), independent Brittonic kingdoms emerged: Gwynedd, Powys, Dyfed, Ceredigion, Glywysing, Gwent. They shared law, language, and a monastic church (David, Illtud, Teilo), but partible inheritance—dividing land among all sons—prevented lasting unity. Eastern kingdoms lost ground to Mercia; **Offa's Dyke** (late 8th century) marked a negotiated frontier. By 800 Wales was the largest of three surviving Brittonic zones, alongside Cornwall and the "Old North" (southern Scotland/northern England).

Dynastic marriages let **Rhodri Mawr** (r. 844–877) rule Gwynedd, Powys, and Ceredigion. His grandson **Hywel Dda** (r. 900–950) created Deheubarth, codified Welsh law at Whitland ("Laws of Hywel"), and pursued peace with England. Viking raids (950–1000) disrupted coasts but also prompted alliances. Only **Gruffydd ap Llywelyn** (r. 1055–1063) ever united all Wales—a seven-year achievement with "neither precedent nor successor."

## Conquest and Incorporation (1066–1542)

Within four years of Hastings, Norman lords established **Marcher lordships** along the ill-defined border. These semi-sovereign magnates, subject to neither English nor Welsh law, pushed westward. By 1093 the south seemed conquered, but a general revolt in 1094 began a century of flux. **Owain Gwynedd** (d. 1170) expanded Gwynedd during England's civil war; **Rhys ap Gruffydd** (Lord Rhys) rebuilt Deheubarth and held the first recorded **eisteddfod** (1176).

From this turmoil rose **Llywelyn ab Iorwerth** (Llywelyn the Great, d. 1240). By 1216 he received the fealty of other Welsh lords at Aberdyfi, becoming *de facto* Prince of Wales, confirmed by the Treaty of Worcester (1218). His grandson **Llywelyn ap Gruffudd** secured the title *Prince of Wales* from Henry III in the Treaty of Montgomery (1267). Disputes with Marcher lords gave **Edward I** pretext for invasion (1276). Llywelyn submitted under the Treaty of Aberconwy (1277), losing most gains. A final revolt led by his brother Dafydd (1282) ended with Llywelyn killed at Cilmeri and Dafydd executed. The **Statute of Rhuddlan (1284)** annexed the Principality of Wales to the English Crown, dividing it into shires under English law. Edward built the "iron ring" of castles—Caernarfon, Conwy, Harlech, Beaumaris—and bestowed the title Prince of Wales on his heir (1301), a tradition continuing today.

Wales became legally "annexed and united" to England, though its people spoke Welsh and kept distinct customs. The **Penal Laws (1402)**, passed after **Owain Glyndŵr's** rebellion (1400–1415)—which briefly controlled most of Wales, held a parliament at Machynlleth, and planned universities—barred Welshmen from office, fortified towns, and bearing arms. Glyndŵr's defeat entrenched second-class status.

**Henry Tudor** (Henry VII), born in Pembroke of Welsh descent, won Bosworth (1485) with Welsh support. His son **Henry VIII** passed the **Laws in Wales Acts (1535, 1542)**: Welsh law was abolished, English law imposed, the Wales–England border fixed, and Welsh MPs sent to Westminster. The Welsh language was banned from official use. Yet the **Welsh Bible (1588)** by William Morgan gave the language literary and liturgical authority, ensuring its survival as a spoken vernacular when elite patronage vanished.

## Nonconformity, Industry, and Labour (1542–1914)

The 18th-century **Welsh Methodist Revival** (Daniel Rowland, Howell Harris, William Williams Pantycelyn) was Calvinist, not Arminian like its English counterpart. It broke from the Anglican Church (1811), forming the **Presbyterian Church of Wales (1823)**. By 1850 Wales was predominantly Nonconformist; chapels became the social spine, and Welsh-language Sunday schools created mass literacy in Welsh.

Simultaneously, geology drove an economic revolution. **Copper** smelting at Swansea (Cornish ore, local coal) led the world. **Iron** at Merthyr Tydfil (Cyfarthfa, Dowlais) produced 40% of British pig iron by the 1820s. **Slate** quarrying (Penrhyn, Dinorwic) roofed the 19th-century world. But **coal** defined modern Wales. The South Wales Coalfield (Cynon, Rhondda valleys) fuelled the British Empire; Cardiff, Barry, and Penarth became global coal ports. Output peaked at nearly 60 million tonnes (1913). Population doubled 1801–1851 (to 1 million) and doubled again by 1911 (2.4 million), swelled by English, Irish, Italian, and Jewish migrants. The Welsh-speaking proportion in the Rhondda fell from 64% (1901) to 55% (1911).

Industrial conflict forged radical politics. The **Merthyr Rising (1831)**, **Chartism**, and the **1904–05 Revival** (100,000+ converts) coexisted. **Keir Hardie** won Merthyr Tydfil (1900) as Labour's first MP. By 1922 Labour held half of Wales's Westminster seats, a dominance unbroken since.

## Wars, Decline, and Devolution (1914–Present)

**WWI**: 272,924 Welshmen served (21.5% of males); ~35,000 died, notably at Mametz Wood and Passchendaele. **David Lloyd George** became the only Welsh Prime Minister (1916). The post-war coal slump brought mass unemployment; population declined for the first time in centuries. **WWII** saw bombing of Swansea, Cardiff, and Pembroke docks, and 10% of conscripts directed to mines as **Bevin Boys**.

Post-1945, the welfare state raised living standards, but coal and steel collapsed. By the 1990s one deep pit remained. The **Tryweryn valley flooding (1965)** to supply Liverpool—opposed by 35 of 36 Welsh MPs—galvanised nationalism. **Plaid Cymru** won its first MP (Gwynfor Evans, Carmarthen, 1966). The **Welsh Language Act (1967)** gave Welsh legal recognition; the **Welsh Language Measure (2011)** made it the only *de jure* official language in any part of Great Britain.

A 1979 devolution referendum failed (80% no). A second in 1997 passed narrowly (50.3%). The **National Assembly for Wales** convened in 1999 (Government of Wales Act 1998), gaining primary law-making powers after a 2011 referendum. In 2020 it was renamed **Senedd Cymru / Welsh Parliament**, reflecting its legislative maturity. Today Wales navigates a post-industrial, service-based economy within a devolved UK, its language spoken by roughly 20% of the population—up from a 1981 nadir of 18.9%—and its distinct civic institutions a testament to a history of resistance, adaptation, and survival.
