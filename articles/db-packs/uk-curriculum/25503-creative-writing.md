# Creative writing

Creative writing is any writing that goes beyond normal professional, journalistic, academic, or technical forms, emphasizing craft and technique through narrative structure, character development, literary tropes, genre, and poetics. It includes both fictional and non-fictional works such as novels, biographies, short stories, poems, and some forms of journalism. Screenwriting and playwriting fit under this category as well, though often taught separately.

## Definition and scope

Creative writing is essentially a contemporary, process-oriented name for what was traditionally called literature. Mary Lee Marksberry, referencing Paul Witty and Lou LaBrant, defines it as writing primarily in service of: keeping records of significant experience, sharing experience with an interested group, and free individual expression that contributes to mental and physical health.

Unlike academic writing classes that teach rules of language, creative writing focuses on self-expression. It is considered by some academics (mostly in the US) to be an extension of English studies, though in the UK, Australia, and increasingly elsewhere, it is treated as a discipline in its own right.

## Academic programs

Creative writing programs run from high school through graduate school and adult education, traditionally housed in English departments but increasingly forming their own departments. Undergraduate degrees are typically BA or BFA, with some BSc options. Postgraduate offerings include MA, MFA, and Master of Studies, with Ph.D. programs becoming more prevalent.

The first formal creative writing program was established as an MA at the University of East Anglia. Following post-war university restructuring, these courses gained increasing prominence.

Students typically emphasize either fiction or poetry, starting with short stories or poems, then building schedules around reading assignments, regular writing tasks, and workshops. Screenwriting and playwriting may be housed in film and theatre departments as well.

## Teaching approach

Creative writing is usually taught in workshop format, where students submit original work for peer critique. Students develop a writing process through practice and re-writing, while courses teach editing, narrative structure, literary techniques, genres, idea generation, and overcoming writer's block.

Research shows creative writing can increase academic performance and psychological resilience. Completing small goals consistently releases dopamine and increases motivation. Students build resilience by documenting and analyzing experiences, gaining new perspectives, and sorting emotions. It also increases compassion and creates community in otherwise isolating classrooms.

Gregory Stephens suggests that focusing heavily on academic writing prevents students from developing their own unique writing style and voice, which creative writing cultivates.

## Broader applications

**International students**: Creative writing provides a platform for sharing heritage, experiences, and values. A two-year case study of a Korean international student found that creative writing classes made her a more confident writer in English and other languages, and encouraged her to connect her Korean heritage with her English writing.

**STEM education**: Creative writing pedagogy helps STEM students develop empathy, self-awareness, and a narrative voice. Self-characterization and storytelling exercises build skills transferable to professional settings.

**Prison programs**: Following the prisoner rights movement, American prisons implemented creative writing programs. PEN established the Prison Writing Committee to advocate for these programs, improving prison libraries and founding annual literary competitions. These programs build self-esteem, create healthy social connections, and ease prisoner reentry. Studies found that art education increased emotional control and decreased disciplinary reports.

## Debates

Some argue creative writing cannot be taught. Kay Boyle, director of San Francisco State University's creative writing program for sixteen years, said all such programs ought to be abolished by law.

Critics also raise accessibility concerns, noting that methods like daily writing requirements or location-based writing discriminate against people with chronic illnesses, physical or mental health barriers, and neurodivergency. Scholars like Caleb González argue that the Western literary canon and writing pedagogy are historically rooted in exclusion and structural racism.

## Core elements

Creative writing combines elements such as character, plot, setting, point of view, theme, and voice. These elements interact: characters act within a setting to drive a plot, filtered through a chosen point of view, with voice and style shaping how the story is told.
