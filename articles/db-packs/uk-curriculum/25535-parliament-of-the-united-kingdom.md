# Parliament of the United Kingdom

The Parliament of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland is the supreme legislative body of the state. Established 1 January 1801 by the union of Great Britain and Ireland, it meets at the Palace of Westminster in London. Parliament possesses legislative supremacy: ultimate power over all other political bodies in the UK and the Overseas Territories. Since 1999, varying degrees of power have been devolved to the Scottish Parliament, the Senedd (Wales), and the Northern Ireland Assembly, but the UK Parliament retains authority over reserved matters including defence, currency, and broadcasting.

Parliament is formally tricameral, comprising the Sovereign, the House of Lords, and the House of Commons. The three acting together constitute the **King-in-Parliament**. In practice, the Monarch’s role is ceremonial: royal assent has not been refused since 1708. The House of Commons is the dominant chamber.

**House of Commons** — 650 Members of Parliament (MPs) elected from single-member constituencies by first-past-the-post voting. Elections occur at least every five years. The Speaker, elected by MPs, renounces party affiliation. By constitutional convention, all government ministers, including the Prime Minister, are drawn from the Commons (or less commonly the Lords) and are accountable to it. The Commons holds the exclusive right to initiate taxation and supply bills.

**House of Lords** — An unelected upper chamber of approximately 800 members (not fixed). It comprises **Lords Spiritual** (26 bishops of the Church of England) and **Lords Temporal** (life peers appointed by the Monarch on the Prime Minister’s advice, plus 92 hereditary peers elected by their peers under the 1999 reform). The Lords scrutinises legislation and government through questions and select committees but cannot block money bills or ultimately prevent other bills from becoming law.

**Legislative process** — A bill must pass both Houses and receive royal assent to become law. The **Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949** define the Lords’ subordinate role: money bills (solely national taxation or public funds, certified by the Speaker) become law if the Lords fail to pass them within one month; other public bills can be presented for assent after the Commons passes them in two successive sessions and the Lords rejects them twice, following a delay of two parliamentary sessions. The Lords retains an absolute veto on bills prolonging a Parliament beyond five years.

**Government relationship** — The Government must retain the confidence of the House of Commons. The Monarch appoints as Prime Minister the person most likely to command a majority—usually the leader of the largest party. The PM and most Cabinet ministers sit in the Commons. The Government controls the legislative timetable and, with a working majority, can usually pass its programme, a dominance Lord Hailsham termed an “elective dictatorship.” Parliament scrutinises the executive through daily Question Time (including Prime Minister’s Questions), cross-party select committees, and confidence motions. Defeat on a major financial bill or a confidence motion obliges the PM to resign or seek dissolution. The House of Lords cannot bring down the Government. The Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022 restored the PM’s power to call early elections while retaining a five-year maximum term.

**Parliamentary sovereignty** — Parliament can make or unmake any law, and no body can override its legislation. Three practical consequences follow: (1) **No entrenchment**: no Act can bind a future Parliament; the 1800 Act of Union declared the union with Ireland “forever,” yet Parliament permitted the Irish Free State to secede in 1922. (2) **Self-limitation**: Parliament has voluntarily constrained itself—devolving powers to Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland; accepting EU law supremacy 1973–2020; granting legislative autonomy to the Church of Scotland and the Church of England’s General Synod. Each constraint was enacted by Act of Parliament and can be repealed by one. (3) **Territorial limits**: the Australia Acts 1986 and Canada Act 1982 ended UK legislative competence over those nations; a UK Act reversing them would have no legal effect there. The **West Lothian question** highlights an asymmetry: Scottish, Welsh, and Northern Irish MPs vote on English-only matters at Westminster, while English MPs have no equivalent say over devolved matters in those nations.

**Historical evolution** — Key milestones: 1707 Treaty of Union creates Parliament of Great Britain; 1801 Acts of Union add Irish representation; 1832–1928 Reform Acts extend franchise, abolish rotten boroughs, introduce secret ballots, pay MPs, equalise constituencies, and grant universal suffrage (age 21 in 1928, lowered to 18 in 1969); 1911/1949 Parliament Acts curb Lords’ veto; 1922 Irish Free State leaves, 1927 Act renames Parliament; 1958 Life Peerages Act enables regular life peers; 1999 House of Lords Act removes most hereditary peers; 2005/2009 Constitutional Reform Act transfers judicial functions to the new Supreme Court; 2020 UK leaves EU, restating Parliament’s sovereignty.

**Privileges and emblem** — Each House guards ancient privileges. The foremost is **freedom of speech** in debate: nothing said in either House may be questioned in any court. Members also enjoy **freedom from arrest** in civil proceedings (covering all offences) during a session and 40 days before and after. Both Houses may punish contempt (e.g., disobeying a committee subpoena); the Lords may imprison for a fixed term, the Commons only until prorogation. These punishments cannot be challenged in court. The crowned portcullis, adopted from Tudor royal badges and formalised in 1996, serves as Parliament’s emblem. Proceedings are broadcast live on parliamentlive.tv, BBC Parliament, and YouTube.
