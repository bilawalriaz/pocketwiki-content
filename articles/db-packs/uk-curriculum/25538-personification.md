# Personification

Personification represents a thing, being, or abstraction as a person or with person-like qualities. It differs from anthropomorphism, which attributes human traits or emotions to non-human entities; personification creates a distinct figure that *embodies* the concept, often with standardized attributes. The device operates across literature, visual art, religion, and political symbolism.

## Classical Foundations

In ancient Greece and Rome, personifications functioned as minor deities or *daemons*. Every Athenian administrative district had a personified deity; *Demos* (the assembly) and *Boule* (the council) appeared in art. Roman coinage systematized the repertoire: virtually every province and virtue received a personified image, often labeled, creating a visual vocabulary that survived into the Renaissance. The predominance of female figures stems partly from Latin grammar, which assigns feminine gender to abstract nouns (*virtus, fortuna, libertas*). Major rivers were traditionally male, as was "Water" among the Four Elements. James J. Paxson notes that before the sixth century AD, virtually all personification figures were female, though river gods are a documented early exception.

Personifications generally lack independent narrative myths, though classical myth often gave them Olympian parents. The *tyches* (city fortunes) and tutelary deities survived Christianization as stripped, symbolic figures. Victoria/Nike, the winged goddess of victory, transitioned into the Christian angel.

## Medieval and Renaissance Systematization

Late antique works cemented personification as a literary engine. Prudentius’s *Psychomachia* (early 5th century) staged battles between personified Virtues and Vices. Boethius’s *Consolation of Philosophy* (c. 524) framed a dialogue between the author and Lady Philosophy, popularizing *Fortuna* and her Wheel. The medieval *Four Daughters of God* (Truth, Justice, Mercy, Peace) and the Seven Virtues (four cardinal, three theological) versus the Seven Deadly Sins became standard sets.

Rhetorical theory formalized the device. Demetrius of Phalerum (c. 300 BC) first described *prosopopoeia*—giving voice to an absent or imagined person. Quintilian’s *Institutio Oratoria* provided a taxonomy unmatched until the Renaissance. Erasmus and Petrus Mosellanus revived these classifications in the 16th century.

The *emblem book* standardized visual attributes. Cesare Ripa’s *Iconologia* (first illustrated 1603) fixed the iconography for hundreds of personifications—what each figure holds, wears, or stands beside—governing artistic practice until the 19th century. Karel van Mander’s *Schilder-boeck* (1604) extended theoretical treatment to painting.

## Literary Evolution and Decline

Andrew Escobedo defines literary personification as marshalling "inanimate things, such as passions, abstract ideas, and rivers, and makes them perform actions in the landscape of the narrative." He dates its literary dominance roughly from the fifth to the seventeenth century.

Middle English works like *Piers Plowman* (c. 1370–90) and Chaucer’s *House of Fame* populated narratives with personified characters. Petrarch’s *Triomphi* (1374) staged processions of personifications on triumphal cars, influencing court festivals and illustration. Spenser’s *Faerie Queene* used renamed personifications for virtues. Bunyan’s *Pilgrim’s Progress* (1678) marked the last great English personification allegory.

By the 18th century, critics attacked the device as hollow. Romantic literature largely abandoned personification, except in William Blake. Escobedo reports a modern scholarly consensus viewing personification as a "frozen or hollow version of literal characters" that "depletes the fiction."

## Visual Arts: Cathedral Programs to Melancholia

Medieval cathedrals (Chartres, Amiens) deployed virtues and vices in sculptural programs. Giotto’s Scrovegni Chapel (c. 1305) painted them along the lowest wall zone. Ambrogio Lorenzetti’s *Allegory of Good and Bad Government* (1338–39) in Siena made personifications the primary actors: Tyranny enthroned with Avarice, Pride, Vainglory; Cruelty, Deceit, Fraud beside him; Justice bound below.

The *Mantegna Tarocchi* (c. 1465–75) used fifty cards to teach personifications of social classes, planets, and virtues. *Ecclesia* and *Synagoga* became a standard portal pair. Death as a skeleton with scythe and hourglass emerged after the Black Death but rarely appeared in funerary art before the Counter-Reformation.

Botticelli’s *Calumny of Apelles* (c. 1494–95), following Lucian’s description of a lost Apelles painting, arranged eight personifications (Hope, Repentance, Perfidy, Calumny, Fraud, Rancour, Ignorance, Suspicion) in a complex judicial scene. Dürer’s *Melencolia I* (1514) remains an unusually potent single figure. In such works, identities and meanings remain contested despite centuries of scholarship.

## National Personifications

*Libertas*, Roman goddess of liberty, appeared on coins of Caesar’s assassins. With modern nationalism, liberty became central to national symbols, culminating in the *Statue of Liberty* (1886). James Thomson’s poem *Liberty* (1734) voiced a "Goddess of Liberty" traversing history to the Glorious Revolution; his *Rule, Britannia* merged Britannia and Liberty into "British Liberty," honored with a monument at Gibside (1750s).

A parallel, satirical lineage produced *John Bull* (1712) and *Uncle Sam* (c. 1812). Both began in literature, rose through political cartoons, and became visual shorthand for their nations. *Marianne* (official in France from 1792) blends classical form with a woman of the Paris streets. The *Dutch Maiden*, visual from the 16th century, repulsed Spanish advances in popular prints. These figures often carry classical attributes—Phrygian cap, shield, lion—but operate in mass media, not high art.

## Innovation and Modern Persistence

New concepts demanded new personifications. The 16th century added the *personification of the Americas*, completing a Four Continents set (Europe, Asia, Africa, America) that suited decorative schemes better than three. Australia’s discovery did not immediately add a fifth; the Albert Memorial (1860s) retains four continents at its corners alongside three-figure groups for Agriculture, Commerce, Engineering, and Manufacturing.

Technological change generated its own figures. Movable type printing introduced *Dame Imprimerie* ("Lady Printing Press") and *Typosine*, a muse of printing, in Lyon pageants. AT&T commissioned Evelyn Beatrice Longman’s gilt-bronze statue for their New York headquarters (1916), retitled over decades: *Genius of Telegraphy*, *Genius of Electricity*, *Spirit of Communication*. Eric Gill adopted Shakespeare’s *Ariel* as the personification of broadcasting for sculptures on Broadcasting House, London (1932).

By the late 20th century, personification seemed obsolete in serious art. Yet the 21st century saw semi-personificatory *superhero* figures—embodiments of ideals like Justice, Patriotism, Vengeance—dominate global cinema through franchise films. The device that once crowded cathedral porches and medieval dream visions now anchors blockbuster mythologies.
