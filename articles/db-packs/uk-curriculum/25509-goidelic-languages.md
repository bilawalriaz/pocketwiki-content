# Goidelic languages

The Goidelic (or Gaelic) languages form one of the two branches of Insular Celtic (the Celtic languages of Britain and Ireland), alongside the Brittonic branch (Welsh, Cornish, Breton). Three modern languages survive: Irish (Gaeilge), Scottish Gaelic (Gàidhlig), and Manx (Gaelg). They historically formed a dialect continuum stretching from Ireland through the Isle of Man to Scotland, meaning neighbouring varieties were mutually intelligible even if distant ones were not.

## Classification and origins

The family descends through several documented stages:

- **Primitive Irish** (from ~4th century): attested in Ogham inscriptions; forms closely resemble contemporary Gaulish
- **Old Irish** (6th–10th century): found in glosses (annotations) on Latin manuscripts
- **Middle Irish** (10th–12th century): predecessor of all three modern languages; much early Irish law literature survives in this form
- **Classical Gaelic** (13th–18th century): a shared literary standard used in both Ireland and Scotland

The names Gaeilge, Gàidhlig, and Gaelg (the endonyms, or names speakers use for their own languages) all derive from Old Irish *Goídelc*, itself from Old Welsh *Guoidel* meaning "wild men, savages."

The word "Gaelic" alone is ambiguous—in Scotland it typically means Scottish Gaelic, since "Gaelic" distinguishes it from the Germanic language called Scots. Irish and Manx never need the qualifier, since those names unambiguously denote the Goidelic languages. Scottish "Gaelic" is pronounced /ˈɡælɪk/, while Irish and Manx "Gaelic" is /ˈɡeɪlɪk/.

## Historical spread

Goidelic was historically restricted to Ireland and possibly Scotland's west coast. The kingdom of Dál Riata emerged in western Scotland during the 6th century—mainstream scholarship attributes its founding to Irish migrants, though archaeologist Ewan Campbell notes there is no archaeological evidence for invasion and suggests strong sea links maintained a pre-existing Gaelic culture across the North Channel. Gaelic language and culture were eventually adopted by the neighbouring Picts throughout Scotland.

## Irish

Irish is one of the Republic of Ireland's two official languages. The legally defined Irish-speaking areas are called the **Gaeltacht**, now primarily in Counties Cork, Donegal, Mayo, Galway, Kerry, and to a lesser extent Waterford and Meath. Of the Republic's population aged three and over, 1,774,437 (41.4%) claim some ability to speak Irish, but only 77,185 (1.8%) speak it daily outside school. Irish gained official and working language status in the European Union in 2007, the 23rd language to do so.

Irish was spoken by the majority of Ireland's population until the later 18th century. The Great Famine of the 1840s disproportionately affected Irish-speaking communities, and combined with emigration, precipitated a steep decline that has only recently begun to reverse. Irish also has some legal status in Northern Ireland under the 1998 Good Friday Agreement and became an official language there in 2022.

## Scottish Gaelic

Approximately 60,000 native speakers remain in Scotland, plus around 1,000 speakers of Canadian Gaelic in Nova Scotia. Its historical range was far larger—it was the everyday language of most of the Scottish Highlands until little more than a century ago. Galloway once spoke Galwegian Gaelic, now extinct for roughly three centuries, which was transitional between Scottish Gaelic and the other Goidelic languages.

Scotland's English name derives from Latin *Scotus/Scoti* meaning "Gael." Originally "Scotland" meant "Land of the Gaels" in a cultural sense; in early Old English texts it referred to Ireland. Until the late 15th century, *Scottis* in Scottish English referred only to Gaelic. As the ruling elite shifted to Scots Inglis (Early Scots), *Scottis* became associated with the land rather than the people, and the term *Erse* ("Irish") was applied to Gaelic as a culturo-political label implying it was foreign—despite Gaelic having been in Scotland at least as long as English.

Following the 1746 Jacobite Rebellion, Crown repression including the Highland Clearances caused further decline through enforced emigration. The Scottish Parliament has granted the language statutory status and "equal respect" with English, though not full legal equality.

## Manx

Manx declined sharply in the 19th century. The last monolingual speakers died around mid-century; by 1921 only 1.1% of the population spoke it. The last native speaker, Ned Maddrell, died in 1974. A revival began at the end of the 19th century through the Manx Language Society (*Yn Çheshaght Ghailckagh*), and linguists recorded the last native speakers throughout the 20th century. The 2011 UK census recorded 1,823 speakers (2.27% of the island's population). Today Manx is the sole medium at five pre-schools and one primary school (*Bunscoill Ghaelgagh*), taught as a second language at all other schools, and offered at University College Isle of Man.

Manx is closely akin to the Gaelic of the Hebrides, the Irish of northeast and eastern Ireland, and the extinct Galwegian Gaelic, with influence from Old Norse via Viking invasions. Its orthography (writing system), introduced in the 16th–17th centuries, was based loosely on English and Welsh, so it never formed part of the shared Classical Gaelic literary standard.

## Comparison

The three languages share clear cognates. Numbers illustrate both their common origin and divergence from Brittonic:

| No. | Old Irish | Irish | Scottish Gaelic | Manx | Welsh (Brittonic) |
|-----|-----------|-------|-----------------|------|-------------------|
| 1 | óen | aon | aon | un/un* | un |
| 2 | dá | dó | dà | daa/jees* | dau |
| 10 | deich | deich | deich | jeih | deg |
| 20 | fichi | fiche | fichead | feed | ugain |
| 100 | cét | céad | ceud | keead | cant |

*Manx counting uses suppletive (non-derived) forms *nane* and *jees* rather than the historic *un* and *daa*.

Common phrases show both similarity and divergence: *Fáilte/Fàilte/Failt* for "Welcome," *Sláinte/Slàinte/Slaynt* for "Health" (used as a toast).

## Influence beyond the family

Several non-Goidelic languages show Goidelic influence. **Shelta** is a cant (a secret language of a particular group) based on Irish grammar with English syntax. **Beurla Reagaird** is a Scottish travelling cant largely based on Scottish Gaelic. The **Bungi** dialect of Canadian Métis English was influenced by Scottish Gaelic among other languages. English, particularly Highland English, contains numerous words of both Scottish Gaelic and Irish origin.
