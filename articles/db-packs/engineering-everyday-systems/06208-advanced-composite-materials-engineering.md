# Advanced composite materials (engineering)

Advanced composite materials (ACMs) combine exceptionally strong, stiff, low-density fibres with a weaker surrounding matrix. The matrix is the bulk binder that holds fibres in place and transfers load into them; the fibres carry most of the mechanical load. Compared with everyday composites such as reinforced concrete, ACMs use higher-performance constituents and a much larger fibre volume fraction.

Because stiff, lightweight fibres carry load along their length, ACMs show high strength and high modulus (resistance to elastic stretching) in the fibre direction, together with low overall weight. They also resist temperature, chemicals, and dimensional change, and they mould into complex shapes more easily than metals. The trade-off is directionality: properties are best along the fibre axis.

ACMs are classified by matrix material:

- **Polymer matrix composites (PMCs):** resins such as polyester or epoxy reinforced with fibres; the most common type.
- **Ceramic matrix composites (CMCs):** ceramic matrices, used where temperature resistance matters.
- **Metal matrix composites (MMCs):** metal matrices that add stiffness and strength to a metallic base.

"Advanced" in these families also implies high longitudinal strength and stiffness, low weight, corrosion resistance, and sometimes special electrical behaviour.

Two industry segments share the same basic processes. *Industrial composites*, established in the US for over 40 years, use cheaper polyester or epoxy resins with glass-fibre reinforcement to make boats, piping, and car bodies. *Advanced composites* use expensive high-performance resins and high-strength, high-stiffness fibres, with aerospace as the dominant customer. Sporting goods (golf, tennis, fishing, archery), swimming-pool wall structures, and NASA's Advanced Space Transportation Program also use ACMs, and the sector is a multibillion-dollar industry. As automated manufacturing spreads, costs are expected to fall, broadening use into electronics, machinery, and surface transport.

ACMs face real limits. They are hard to inspect for hidden flaws, some absorb moisture, and fabrication is labour-intensive with costly equipment, so unit prices stay high. Aluminium, by contrast, has high fracture toughness (the ability to absorb damage before breaking) and can be hammered back into shape after a minor impact, while damaged ACMs usually must be replaced. Aluminium tolerates large plastic deformation (permanent reshaping without cracking) before failure, whereas composites fail in a more brittle manner. ACMs are therefore likely to remain more common in military aircraft, where constant maintenance is the norm, than in commercial aircraft, where low maintenance is essential. Aluminium persists as a structural material, and aluminium-lithium alloys continue to extend its usefulness.
