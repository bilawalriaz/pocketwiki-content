# Digital electronics

Digital electronics is the study of digital signals and devices that use or produce them. It handles the relationship between binary inputs and outputs by passing electrical signals through logic gates, transistors, resistors, capacitors, and amplifiers. The field contrasts with analog electronics, which works with continuously varying signals, but digital designs still depend on analog behaviour in their underlying components.

A digital circuit is built from logic gates, small transistor circuits that perform Boolean functions on their binary inputs and produce a single output voltage. Two kinds of assemblies exist. Combinational logic produces outputs that depend only on current inputs. Sequential logic feeds outputs back as inputs, so its outputs depend on past and present inputs, and stores bits in flip-flops, memory elements that capture an input voltage when triggered.

Sequential logic has two styles. Synchronous systems use a clock signal, a periodic voltage pulse that triggers every flip-flop on the same edge, so all state changes happen together; this is easier to design but caps speed at the slowest combinational calculation. Asynchronous systems change output whenever inputs change, running at the maximum speed of the gates, but every timing path must be analysed, because unintended mismatches can make the circuit unstable.

A circuit's behaviour is specified as a truth table mapping each input combination to an output, then translated into a network of gates. Electronic design automation tools minimise redundant logic using Karnaugh maps, the Quine–McCluskey algorithm, and binary decision diagrams, then output a description ready for fabrication.

The chief advantage of digital is noise immunity. A continuous waveform sent as a sequence of 1s and 0s is reconstructed without error as long as noise stays below the threshold that distinguishes a 1 from a 0; analog signals degrade at each stage. Precision is raised by adding more bits, each handled by identical hardware; analog precision needs fundamental improvements in linearity. Digital systems are upgraded through software without hardware changes, and stored data is retrieved without degradation if total noise stays below a tolerable level; parity bits and other redundancy detect and correct errors within that limit.

Trade-offs exist. Digital circuits often use more energy than analog ones for the same task and produce more heat, so battery devices keep a low-power analog front-end for radio reception while delegating complex processing to digital circuits. Converting between analog and digital introduces quantization error, governed by the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem, which sets how many samples per second are needed to capture a continuous signal faithfully. Digital systems also show a cliff effect: a small noise increase can cause sudden failure, and a single bit error in compressed data can corrupt large blocks.

Integrated circuits place thousands to billions of transistors on one silicon chip and are the cheapest way to build large numbers of interconnected gates. Chips mount on printed circuit boards connected by copper traces. Lookup tables, sold as programmable logic devices, replace fixed gate networks and can be reprogrammed without rewiring, which suits small-volume products. Complex systems are described in hardware description languages such as VHDL or Verilog, then compiled into a physical layout.

Testing every state of a large logic machine is impossible, because it has more states than microseconds since the universe began. Engineers break designs into sub-machines, isolate them with built-in test circuitry, and use boundary-scan schemes, formalised as the JTAG standard, that shift test data in and out through a few serial wires. Automatic test pattern generation targets specific potential faults, pushing fault coverage toward 100% in properly testable circuits.

The earliest digital logic used telephone relays: slow, reliable, and cheap. Vacuum tubes replaced relays in the 1940s, much faster but prone to burning out. The transistor, invented at Bell Labs in 1947–1948, launched second-generation computers by 1955: smaller, cooler, more reliable, and dense enough to pack tens of thousands of circuits in a compact space. The MOSFET, a fast, low-power switch that scales to high density, enabled chips with millions then billions of transistors by the 1980s. The integrated circuit appeared in 1958–1959, when Jack Kilby demonstrated the first working IC at Texas Instruments and Robert Noyce produced the silicon IC at Fairchild Semiconductor.

Each switching technology defines a logic family with characteristic speed, power, and fan-out, the number of gate inputs one output can drive. Resistor–transistor logic had a fan-out of 3. Diode–transistor logic raised it to about 7. Transistor–transistor logic pushed fan-out to 10 and later 20, with switching times as low as 20 nanoseconds. Emitter coupled logic was faster but consumed more power. CMOS dominates today: fast, dense, and low-power per gate, used even in large mainframes. Modern CMOS gates have mean time between failures exceeding 82 billion hours and routinely switch at 5 GHz, with laboratory systems exceeding 1 THz.
