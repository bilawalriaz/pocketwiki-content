# Manufacturing engineering

Manufacturing engineering (also called production engineering) is the branch of professional engineering concerned with turning raw material into a finished product in the most effective, efficient, and economic way possible. It overlaps with mechanical, chemical, electrical, and industrial engineering, and rests on industrial and mechanical engineering skills while drawing on mechatronics, commerce, economics, and business management.

The manufacturing engineer plans manufacturing practices, develops the tools, processes, machines, and equipment that shape a product, and integrates the facilities and systems that produce it at optimum cost and quality. The discipline is treated as a subdiscipline of industrial engineering and systems engineering, with strong overlap with mechanical engineering.

## Origins and history

Manufacturing engineering grew out of the tool-and-die trade in the early 20th century and expanded sharply from the 1960s, when factories added three innovations: numerical control machine tools and automated production systems; statistical quality control, pioneered by William Edwards Deming and first embraced in Japan, lifting its factories to world leadership in cost and quality; and industrial robots on the floor from the late 1970s, with computer-controlled welding arms and grippers attaching car doors twenty-four hours a day.

The factory predates the discipline. The Venice Arsenal, founded in 1104, mass-produced ships on assembly lines using manufactured parts, completing nearly one ship a day and employing 16,000 people at its height. Richard Arkwright's Cromford Mill (1771) was purpose-built around its equipment, and between 1820 and 1850 non-mechanized factories displaced artisan workshops as the dominant manufacturing institution. Henry Ford's early-20th-century moving assembly line, with specialized workers beside rolling ramps progressively building an automobile, drove production costs down across industry and enabled mass consumption.

## What manufacturing engineers do

Manufacturing engineers design, develop, and operate integrated production systems that may include material-handling equipment, machine tools, robots, and computer networks, aiming for high-quality, economically competitive output.

Modern practice leans on automation and robotics. Automated manufacturing raises consistency and quality, shortens lead times, simplifies flow, and reduces manual handling. Robotics applies mechatronics to preprogrammed, physically interactive machines that take over tasks that are dangerous, unpleasant, or repetitive; some factories run themselves on robot assembly lines, and the same technology drives bomb disposal and space exploration.

## Modern computer-aided tools

Most manufacturing firms in industrialized countries now run computer-aided engineering (CAE), including 2D and 3D solid-modelling CAD, which allows virtual assembly, easier tolerance and interface design, and full visualization before any physical part exists. Analysis tools such as finite element analysis (FEA), computational fluid dynamics (CFD), and computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) let teams iterate cheaply and model phenomena like viscoelasticity, complex contact between mating parts, and non-Newtonian flows. Multidisciplinary design optimization (MDO) wraps around CAE so iterative evaluation continues after the analyst leaves. Enterprise resource planning (ERP) overlaps with product life-cycle management (PLM) and shares master data with CAD.

## Subdisciplines and supporting mechanics

Key supporting areas include:

- **Mechanics**: the study of forces and their effects on matter, split into statics (non-moving bodies), dynamics (forces on moving bodies), mechanics of materials (deformation under stress), fluid mechanics, and continuum mechanics.
- **Kinematics**: the study of motion while ignoring its causes, used to design and analyze mechanisms such as cranes (open kinematic chains) or pistons (closed four-bar linkages).
- **Drafting (technical drawing)**: how manufacturers issue manufacturing instructions. CAD now makes this three-dimensional, and instructions feed machine tools manually, through CNC programs, or via CAD/CAM.
- **Machine tools and metal fabrication**: machines that constrain a workpiece and guide a cutting or shaping tool, plus the cutting, bending, and assembly processes that build metal structures.
- **Computer-integrated manufacturing (CIM)**: computers controlling the entire production process so previously separate stages exchange information and trigger each other, typically through closed-loop control based on real-time sensor input.
- **Mechatronics**: the convergence of electrical, mechanical, and manufacturing systems into electromechanical systems such as automated production lines and HVAC. Microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) are the small-scale version, already used in airbag triggers, digital projectors, and inkjet nozzles.
- **Advanced composite materials (ACMs)**: high-strength fibres in a weaker matrix, used heavily in aircraft, aerospace, and sports equipment.

Flexible manufacturing systems (FMS) combine machine flexibility (switching to new product types and reordering operations) and routing flexibility (using multiple machines for the same operation and absorbing changes in volume or capability). An FMS links automated CNC work machines through a material-handling system that optimizes parts flow to a central control computer. CIM is the broader computer-controlled production approach used in automotive, aviation, space, and shipbuilding industries.

Friction stir welding, discovered in 1991 by The Welding Institute, is a steady-state non-fusion technique that joins previously un-weldable aluminium alloys. Uses include the Space Shuttle external tank, the Orion Crew Vehicle test article, Boeing Delta II and IV rockets, SpaceX Falcon 1, amphibious-assault-ship armour, and Eclipse 500 aircraft panels; it is a candidate to replace rivets in future aircraft.

## Education and certification

Manufacturing engineers typically hold an associate's or bachelor's degree in engineering (BEng, BSc, B.TECH, or BASc, depending on country), followed by about five years of professional practice to qualify as a professional engineer. The curriculum covers mathematics (calculus, differential equations, statistics, linear algebra), mechanics (statics, dynamics, solid and fluid mechanics), materials science, hydraulics and pneumatics, HVAC, heat transfer, thermodynamics, instrumentation, engineering drawing and design, mechanism design, manufacturing processes, mechatronics, circuit analysis, lean manufacturing, automation, reverse engineering, quality control, CAD, CAM, and project management. Compared with mechanical engineering, the manufacturing degree substitutes more manufacturing-process depth for less product-design mathematics.

In North America, licensure as a Professional Engineer (P.E. in the U.S., P. Eng. in Canada) requires an ABET-recognized bachelor's degree, a state examination, and four years of work experience, which graduates can split by taking the Fundamentals of Engineering (FE) exam right after graduation and the Principles and Practice of Engineering exam four years later.

The Society of Manufacturing Engineers (SME), based in Dearborn, Michigan, runs non-degree certifications: Certified Manufacturing Technologist (CMfgT), Certified Manufacturing Engineer (CMfgE), and Certified Engineering Manager (CEM). The exams are multiple-choice, three to four hours long, and require between four and eight years of combined education and work experience.

## Where the discipline sits in the world

In the United States and the continental European Union the same work is commonly called industrial engineering; in the United Kingdom and Australia it is called manufacturing engineering. Major employers include General Motors, Ford, Chrysler, Boeing, Gates Corporation, and Pfizer in the U.S., and Airbus, Daimler, BMW, Fiat, Navistar International, and Michelin Tyre in Europe, across aerospace, automotive, chemical, pharmaceutical, food processing, garment, pulp and paper, toy, and systems industries.
