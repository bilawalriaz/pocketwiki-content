# Engine

An engine, or motor, is a machine that converts one form of energy into mechanical motion. The most common are heat engines, which turn thermal energy into work, and electric motors, which turn electrical energy into motion using magnetic fields. Other types draw on compressed air (pneumatic), pressurised fluid (hydraulic), or wound springs (clockwork). The same principle appears at the molecular scale: myosin and other motor proteins use chemical energy directly to produce movement inside living cells.

## How heat engines work

A heat engine burns fuel or absorbs heat to create a high-pressure gas, then lets that gas expand against a piston or turbine blade. The expansion does the work. In an internal combustion engine the fuel burns inside the sealed chamber that drives the piston, so heat and moving surface share one space. In an external combustion engine, such as a steam plant, a separate heat source boils or heats a working fluid through a wall or heat exchanger, and only the resulting vapour pushes the piston or turbine.

Air-breathing engines (cars, jets) draw oxygen from the atmosphere rather than carrying it. They gain specific impulse, thrust per unit of fuel, from the mass of air they can entrain, which is why they work in the atmosphere. Rockets carry their own oxidiser, so they work in space but burn more fuel per unit of thrust. Non-combustion heat engines include nuclear stations, where reactor heat boils water for a steam turbine, and thermoacoustic devices that turn temperature differences into sound waves.

## A short history

Humans first multiplied muscle through simple machines: levers, pulleys, capstans, treadmills, and water wheels. Hero of Alexandria described a small steam-reaction device, the Aeolipile, in the 1st century AD, but it produced no useful work. Medieval Islamic engineers, notably al-Jazari in 1206, added crank-and-connecting-rod mechanisms to water-raising machines, a building block later engines would reuse. The 16th and 17th centuries brought sketches of rudimentary steam turbines by Taqi al-Din (1551) and Giovanni Branca (1629).

The practical age opened with Thomas Newcomen's 1712 atmospheric steam engine, used to pump water from mines. James Watt's redesign (1763–1775) added a separate condenser, roughly doubling fuel efficiency and freeing steam power for factories and railways. Internal combustion followed: de Rivaz and the Niépce brothers tested gas-fuelled piston engines in 1807, Étienne Lenoir sold the first commercially successful gas engine in 1860, and Nikolaus Otto's 1877 four-stroke cycle delivered a power-to-weight ratio steam could not match. Karl Benz applied that lightweight Otto engine to his 1886 automobile. Rudolf Diesel's compression-ignition engine, commercialised soon after, traded refinement for higher thermal efficiency and became standard in trucks, ships, and trains.

## Engines on the road and at sea

Most petrol car engines follow the four-stroke Otto cycle: intake, compression, power, exhaust. Diesel engines compress air until it is hot enough to ignite injected fuel, skipping the spark plug. Both have been refined with turbocharging (forcing more air into the cylinder to raise power), electronic fuel injection, and variable valve timing. Cylinder counts range from one to sixteen; flat "boxer" layouts, where cylinders lie horizontally on either side of the crankshaft, keep the engine low and balanced. At the other extreme, the Wärtsilä-Sulzer RTA96-C, a 14-cylinder two-stroke turbocharged diesel, produces over 80 MW to drive the world's largest container ships.

## Electric and hybrid motors

An electric motor pushes current-carrying conductors through magnetic fields, or vice versa, so the rotor turns. Efficiency typically exceeds 90 percent, with little waste heat and almost no noise, which is why electric motors have replaced engines in appliances, industrial drives, and ship propulsion. Remaining losses sit mainly in stator and rotor iron and copper, magnetic material quality, and bearing friction. Hybrids pair a battery-fed electric motor with an internal combustion engine so the combustion part runs at its most efficient load while the electric part handles stop-start traffic and recovers braking energy.

## Measuring performance

Engine performance reduces to a few numbers. Speed, in revolutions per minute, sets how fast the shaft turns. Torque is the rotational force, equal to applied force times its distance from the axis. Power is the rate of doing work, the product of torque and rotational speed. Efficiency is output divided by input, and it sets fuel cost. A modern electric motor is more than 90 percent efficient; a petrol engine is closer to 30–40 percent thermally efficient, with the rest lost as exhaust heat and cooling. Thrust engines score fuel economy by specific impulse. Combustion engines roar because of rapid pressure pulses, and rockets are loudest because their supersonic exhaust plumes mix violently with surrounding air.

## Open trade-offs

No engine type is free of compromise. Diesel engines beat petrol engines on fuel economy by roughly 40 percent and emit less hydrocarbon and CO₂, but they produce more nitrogen oxides (NOx) and particulates. Electric motors cut local pollution to almost nothing, yet their global impact depends on how the electricity was generated. Exhaust from a spark-ignition engine is mostly nitrogen (70–75 percent by volume) and water vapour (10–12 percent), with carbon dioxide (10–13.5 percent), small fractions of carbon monoxide, unburnt hydrocarbons, oxygen, and trace NOx, sulphur dioxide, and particulates. Carbon monoxide, formed when combustion is incomplete, is the most acutely toxic of these, and catalytic converters cut the toxic fraction without removing it. The cumulative CO₂ output of the engine-powered industrial world remains a central driver of the greenhouse effect, and the next round of progress will be shaped as much by battery chemistry, fuel choice, and regulation as by the engines themselves.
