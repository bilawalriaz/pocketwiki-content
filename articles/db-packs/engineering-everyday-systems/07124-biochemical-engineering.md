# Biochemical engineering

Biochemical engineering, also called bioprocess engineering, is the discipline that takes reactions carried out by living cells or biological molecules and scales them into industrial manufacturing processes. It grew out of chemical engineering and biological engineering, and its working material is the **unit process**, a single defined step such as fermentation, filtration, drying, or purification, designed to be repeatable at factory scale.

The core job is translation. Biologists and chemists discover that an organism makes a useful product, or that an enzyme catalyses a useful reaction; the engineer then designs the vessel, feed, temperature, pH, aeration, and downstream recovery that turn that discovery into tonnes of product per day. Two pieces of hardware recur across the field. A **bioreactor** is a controlled tank in which cells grow or enzymes work under set conditions. **Tangential flow filtration** is a recovery step that circulates the broth across a membrane, letting the desired molecule pass while returning cells to the reactor so production continues.

The discipline has ancient roots. Humans have used microbial reactions for centuries to make foods and drinks. In the mid-1800s Louis Pasteur studied fermentation scientifically, which led to pasteurisation, the controlled heating that still preserves milk and other foods. By the early 1900s microorganisms were being used to make industrial chemicals, but biochemical engineering was not yet a named field. It coalesced in 1928 when Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin, the first antibiotic produced by a mould. Industrial demand forced engineers to grow *Penicillium* in large stirred tanks under sterile conditions, an effort that established the basic toolkit: sterile nutrient feed, controlled aeration, and reliable separation of product from cells.

**Pharmaceuticals.** Bioreactors and fermentation systems produce biopharmaceuticals such as monoclonal antibodies, vaccines, and therapeutic proteins. Recombinant proteins including insulin and erythropoietin are made by growing genetically modified cells in culture. Gene therapy adds another layer, since viral vectors that carry therapeutic genes into patients are also produced in cell culture and must be scaled up without losing potency or purity.

**Food industry.** Engineers design systems for production, processing, packaging, storage, and distribution. Foods such as wheat, fruit, and milk pass through milling, dehydration, and pasteurisation. Processing is usually described in three levels: primary turns crops into food ingredients, secondary combines those ingredients into products, and tertiary produces ready-to-eat meals. Preserving food by drying, pickling, salting, and fermenting works by stopping yeasts, moulds, and bacteria from growing, and modern packaging extends that protection against contamination, physical damage, and tampering. Biochemical engineers also work on nutritional quality, for example in golden rice, developed to address vitamin A deficiency.

**Biotechnology and medicine.** Biochemical engineering is treated as a sub-branch of biotechnology, with strong overlap in metabolic, enzyme, tissue, and cell-culture engineering. Cell culture is used to develop natural fuels, improve drug production, and support disease research, while genetic testing and pharmacogenomics depend on the same underlying tools.

The field is offered as a named degree at relatively few universities, more often appearing as a specialisation inside chemical engineering. Representative programmes include University College London's Department of Biochemical Engineering, McMaster University, the Technical University of Munich, the University of Queensland, and IIT Kharagpur, with bioprocess concentrations at places such as Keck Graduate Institute's Amgen Bioprocessing Center and the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign's Integrated Bioprocessing Research Laboratory.

Every application follows the same shape: a biological reaction is identified, moved from a flask to a sterilised, instrumented bioreactor whose conditions are tuned for yield, and coupled to a recovery train such as tangential flow filtration that turns the broth into a pure, storable product.
