# Semiconductor

A semiconductor is a solid whose electrical conductivity lies between a conductor's and an insulator's, and whose conductivity can be tuned by adding small amounts of impurity atoms ("doping"), applying an electric field, or shining light on it. That tunability underpins diodes, transistors, integrated circuits, solar cells, LEDs, and most modern electronics.

A *band gap* is a forbidden energy range inside a solid where no electron states exist. In a metal, the highest occupied electron energies sit inside an allowed band, so charge moves freely. In an insulator or pure ("intrinsic") semiconductor, the highest occupied level lies in the gap, so no electrons are available to carry current. The difference is only the gap's size: insulators have wide gaps, semiconductors have narrow ones of roughly one electron-volt, so thermal energy at room temperature lifts a small number of electrons from the full lower band (the *valence band*) into the empty upper one (the *conduction band*). A pure silicon crystal therefore conducts poorly on its own, and its conductivity rises with temperature, the opposite of a metal, where more lattice vibration scatters electrons and resistance grows.

Each promoted electron leaves behind a missing electron in the valence band called a *hole*, which behaves as a positively charged, mobile particle. Electron–hole pairs are constantly created by thermal energy or ionizing radiation, and destroyed when they meet, releasing a phonon (a quantized lattice vibration) or a photon. Doping multiplies this effect: replacing about one silicon atom in a hundred thousand with a pentavalent donor such as phosphorus donates a free electron, producing *n-type* material; substituting a trivalent acceptor such as boron leaves a missing bond that acts as a mobile hole, producing *p-type* material. 0.001% arsenic in germanium raises conductivity by a factor of about 10,000. Doped material is called *extrinsic*.

The *p–n junction* is the boundary where p- and n-type regions meet. Near the junction, electrons and holes diffuse across and recombine, leaving the fixed, charged donor and acceptor ions on either side. Those immobile ions create a built-in electric field that opposes further diffusion and permits current to flow easily in only one direction, the basis of the diode. Stacking and gating such junctions yields the transistor, and stacking transistors yields the integrated circuit.

Silicon dominates commercially because its native oxide, silicon dioxide, is an excellent insulator that can be grown thermally and patterned with photolithography. Fabrication starts with a near-perfect single-crystal ingot pulled from molten silicon by the Czochralski method, sliced into wafers, then processed through oxidation, photolithography, plasma etching, and high-temperature diffusion to build p–n junctions. Purity has been driven to the parts-per-trillion range because semiconductor behaviour is "structure-sensitive": trace defects and impurities change properties by orders of magnitude, which is why early experimental results disagreed so badly with theory.

The physics framework crystallised in 1928–31, when Felix Bloch described electrons moving through a periodic lattice and Alan Wilson extended band theory to insulators and semiconductors. Russell Ohl identified the first p–n junction in silicon in 1941. On 23 December 1947, John Bardeen, Walter Brattain, and William Shockley demonstrated the point-contact transistor at Bell Laboratories; a French group led by Herbert Mataré announced a similar "Transistron" shortly after. The first silicon junction transistor followed in 1954, and the integrated circuit appeared in 1958. Earlier empirical devices — the cat's-whisker detector (1904), Fritts' selenium solar cell (1883), Round's LED-like emission (1906), copper-oxide power rectifiers of the 1920s — relied on semiconductor behaviour without a working theory.

Some materials are not silicon. Germanium behaves similarly. Binary compounds such as gallium arsenide, silicon carbide, and gallium nitride, plus organic semiconductors, fill specialised roles. Gallium arsenide is a "semi-insulator" with a wide enough gap to serve as an insulating substrate for high-electron-mobility transistors once doped. Amorphous and liquid semiconductors (hydrogenated amorphous silicon for thin-film solar cells; arsenic–selenium–tellurium mixtures for phase-change memory) tolerate more disorder than crystalline silicon, which is why they work as inexpensive thin films.

Two caveats remain. No single accepted theory explains the electronic behaviour of amorphous semiconductors, where the regular Bloch picture breaks down. Historical credit is contested: Rosenschöld described rectification in 1835 but was ignored, and recent scholarship has questioned whether Jagadish Chandra Bose's 1904 lead-sulfide work really anticipated modern p- and n-type thinking. The narrower band gap, the substitutable lattice, and the usable native oxide are the three practical ingredients that made the silicon era possible.
