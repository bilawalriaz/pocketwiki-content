# Radio

Radio is the technology of sending information through *radio waves*, electromagnetic waves with frequencies between 3 Hz and 300 GHz. A transmitter drives an alternating current into an antenna, and accelerating charges in the metal radiate that energy into space as waves. A distant antenna intercepts a tiny fraction of the wave as a small voltage; a receiver amplifies it and decodes it back into sound, images, or data. Radio waves travel at the speed of light, pass through atmosphere and most building materials, and bend around obstacles, which is why radio links work where higher-frequency waves such as light and infrared are blocked or scattered.

## How information rides a wave

A radio signal by itself is just a steady oscillation, the *carrier wave*, produced by the transmitter's oscillator. To carry information, the transmitter *modulates* it, varying one of its properties so the variation encodes the signal. Amplitude modulation (AM) varies the carrier's strength; frequency modulation (FM) varies its frequency; frequency-shift keying (FSK) jumps between two discrete frequencies for digital data; and orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM) splits a data stream across many closely spaced carriers sent in parallel. OFDM underpins Wi-Fi, 4G and 5G cellular, digital television, and DAB radio because it resists fading and packs bits efficiently into limited spectrum.

At the receiver, a *tuned circuit* (an electrical resonator, analogous to an acoustic tuning fork) isolates the desired frequency and rejects others. Without this filtering, simultaneous transmissions on different frequencies would mix into noise.

## Propagation and range

Signal strength falls off with the square of distance, so range depends on transmitter power, antenna design, receiver sensitivity, and physical obstructions. An *omnidirectional antenna* radiates equally in all directions, suited to broadcasting. A *directional antenna* focuses energy into a beam, extending range in one direction at the cost of coverage elsewhere. The useful *bandwidth* of a signal, the difference between its highest and lowest frequency components, sets how much information it can carry. Video needs more bandwidth than audio, and a given signal-to-noise ratio carries the same data rate regardless of where the signal sits in the spectrum, because only the bandwidth matters.

Because usable spectrum is finite, digital modulation has largely replaced analog since the late 1990s. Digital methods offer better noise immunity, source compression, and higher *spectral efficiency* (more bits per hertz of bandwidth).

## A short history

James Clerk Maxwell predicted electromagnetic waves mathematically in 1873. Heinrich Hertz confirmed the prediction on 11 November 1886 by generating and detecting radio waves with a spark-gap apparatus, and later experiments by Jagadish Chandra Bose, Oliver Lodge, and others showed that radio waves reflect, refract, diffract, and polarize like light, differing only in frequency. Guglielmo Marconi built the first practical long-distance system in 1895 and transmitted the first transatlantic radio signal on 12 December 1901. He shared the 1909 Nobel Prize in Physics with Karl Ferdinand Braun for wireless telegraphy.

Early transmitters were spark-gap devices that produced only short pulses, so the *radiotelegraphy era* (roughly 1895–1920) was limited to Morse code text for shipping, diplomacy, and the military. Continuous-wave transmitters, developed around 1908, made amplitude modulation of audio practical, and Reginald Fessenden and others demonstrated AM radiotelephony. The first commercial broadcast aired on 2 November 1920, when KDKA in Pittsburgh transmitted U.S. presidential election results. The 1906 Berlin Radiotelegraphic Convention adopted "radio" as the universal term, replacing the older "wireless" label.

## Spectrum management

The International Telecommunication Union (ITU) divides the radio spectrum into 12 bands, each spanning a factor of ten in frequency and wavelength, from Extremely Low Frequency (3–30 Hz) to Extremely High Frequency (30–300 GHz). Each higher band contains ten times the bandwidth of the one below it, so the microwave and millimeter-wave bands hold vastly more capacity than the low-frequency bands. The spectrum is a shared, finite economic resource, and national regulators license transmitters, set power limits, and assign call signs to prevent interference. Some short-range, low-power uses, including Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, garage openers, and baby monitors, operate without individual licenses in *ISM bands* reserved internationally for that purpose, although the devices still require type approval.

Deliberate interference, called *radio jamming*, is used militarily and by censoring governments but is illegal for civilian sale or operation in the United States, including jammers targeting GPS, cellular, Wi-Fi, and radar.

## Applications

Radio underpins broadcasting to the public; two-way voice communication through mobile phones and walkie-talkies; wireless data links including satellite internet; radar, which detects objects by timing their reflected echoes; navigation systems such as GPS and VOR, which fix a receiver's position by measuring signal arrival times from beacons at known locations; and remote control of drones, keyless entry systems, and garage doors.
