# Chemical engineering

Chemical engineering is the discipline that designs and runs industrial processes which turn raw materials into useful products at scale. A chemical engineer applies chemistry, physics, mathematics, biology, and economics to find the cheapest safe way to convert a feedstock, transfer energy, and isolate a product, from a bench-scale flask to a city-block refinery.

## What the work actually involves

The output of chemical engineering is a *process*: a chain of physical and chemical steps running continuously inside a plant. Two organising ideas dominate.

A *unit operation* is a single physical step in that chain: crystallisation, filtration, drying, evaporation, distillation. The same handful of physical operations recurs across very different industries, because the underlying physics (boiling, settling, dissolving) does not depend on the chemistry being run. Engineers who specialise in these steps are process engineers.

A *unit process* is the chemical equivalent: one conversion step such as nitration, hydrogenation, or oxidation, where molecules are rearranged. Unit operations and unit processes together form the process flow of a plant, drawn as a Process Flow Diagram that lists equipment, sizes, interconnections, and materials of construction.

These ideas rest on *transport phenomena*, the unified study of momentum, heat, and mass transfer. Modelling transport at the macroscopic, microscopic, and molecular scales lets an engineer predict how a real reactor will behave before it is built, and forms the analytical backbone of modern chemical engineering alongside process design and process control.

## Origins and evolution

George E. Davis (1850–1907), an English consultant, is credited with coining the term and with trying, unsuccessfully, to found a Society of Chemical Engineering; the Society of Chemical Industry was established in 1881 instead, with Davis as its first secretary. The phrase "chemical engineer" was in common use in Britain and the United States by 1910.

From the early twentieth century until the 1960s, teaching and practice were organised almost entirely around unit operations. In the 1940s it became clear that unit operations alone could not design chemical reactors well, and transport phenomena began receiving greater emphasis. Together with process systems engineering, which brought control theory and synthesis into design, this shift became known as the "second paradigm."

Petrochemical demand drove much pre- and post-World War II development. Biochemical engineering advances in the 1940s enabled mass production of antibiotics including penicillin and streptomycin. Polymer science progress in the 1950s opened the "age of plastics." The Human Genome Project applied chemical engineering principles to produce DNA sequences in large quantities. Computing reshaped day-to-day work: simulators such as Aspen HYSYS now handle calculations and drawings that once had to be done by hand.

## Safety as a built-in discipline

Large-scale chemical plants can devastate surrounding communities when they fail, and the modern emphasis on safety grew directly from that experience. Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* (1962) alerted readers to the harmful effects of DDT. The 1974 Flixborough disaster in the United Kingdom killed 28 people and damaged a chemical plant and three nearby villages. The 1984 Bhopal disaster in India killed at least 4,000. In response, the Institution of Chemical Engineers required safety to be part of every accredited degree from 1982 onward, and national legislation and monitoring agencies were set up in the 1970s across France, Germany, the United States, and elsewhere. The systematic application of safety principles to chemical and other process plants became a recognised discipline, called process safety.

## Practice and outlook

Chemical engineers design new plants, run pilot plants, and modify existing ones. Design is constrained by funding, regulation, and safety standards, which together fix the choice of process, materials, and equipment. Construction is usually coordinated by project engineers and project managers; project engineering is one of the largest employment areas for the profession. Beyond design, chemical engineers troubleshoot operating plants, scale reactions up from the lab, manage waste, and work in research aimed at safer, cleaner, more resource-efficient production. Professional bodies such as the American Institute of Chemical Engineers (AIChE) and the Institution of Chemical Engineers (IChemE) accredit degrees and confer the title of Professional Engineer. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics projects 3% growth in employment for chemical engineers between 2024 and 2034.
