# Transportation engineering

Transportation engineering applies scientific and engineering principles to the full life cycle of transportation systems: planning, design, construction, maintenance, operation, and management of highways, railroads, pipelines, water routes, and airports. The stated goal is movement of people and goods that is safe, efficient, rapid, comfortable, convenient, economical, and environmentally compatible.

The discipline sits at the intersection of technical analysis and political decision making. Forecasting passenger demand, sizing a road, choosing pavement thickness, and timing a traffic signal are engineering problems. Deciding whether to build a rail line, what to tax, or which corridor to widen is policy. Transportation engineers translate between the two.

## Forecasting passenger demand

Planning for passenger movement runs through a four-step urban transportation planning model, where each step feeds the next:

1. Trip generation: how many trips originate in or are attracted to each zone.
2. Trip distribution: where those trips go, pairing origins with destinations.
3. Mode choice: which transport mode (car, bus, rail, walking) each traveler uses.
4. Route assignment: which specific links of the network each trip follows.

Advanced models add auto ownership, trip chaining (linking several errands into one tour), and land use forecasting (predicting where homes and businesses locate, which in turn drives future trips). Passenger trips drive planning because they create the peak loads that size the system.

## Design, operations, and human factors

Before any design begins, the engineer compiles an inventory of the study area: population, land use, economic activity, existing facilities, travel volumes, laws, finances, and community values. The inventory feeds business models that forecast future conditions.

Design then sets the physical shape of a facility: how many lanes or tracks it carries (capacity), the materials and thicknesses used in the pavement, and the vertical and horizontal alignment of the roadway or track.

Operations and management fall under traffic engineering, the discipline that keeps vehicles moving on the road or track. Traditional tools include signs, signals, markings, and tolling. Modern tools are intelligent transportation systems: variable message signs that update drivers in real time, ramp meters that release vehicles onto freeways one car at a time, and vehicle-infrastructure integration that lets cars and roads exchange data. Human factors work runs alongside, studying how drivers read signs, react to signals, and interact with vehicle controls.

## Subdisciplines

Highway engineering is the largest. Highway engineers plan, design, construct, and operate roads plus their pedestrian and bicycle realms. They estimate public demand, secure funding, locate high-crash and high-volume spots for safety fixes, and apply three design controls: the drivers, the vehicles, and the roadway.

Railroad engineering covers railroads and fixed-guideway mass transit such as light rail and monorails. Tasks include setting horizontal and vertical track alignment, choosing station locations, designing platforms and lines, and estimating construction cost. Train dispatching is a specialized branch that controls train movement on the network.

Port and harbor engineering handles ports, harbors, canals, and other maritime facilities. It is distinct from naval architecture, which covers hull design, and marine engineering, which covers propulsion and onboard ship systems.

Airport engineering designs and constructs airports. Engineers orient runways using prevailing wind analysis, size runway safety areas, set wingtip-to-wingtip clearances at gates, and define clear zones across the airfield. A civil and structural team within the department handles terminal buildings, cargo facilities, hangars, runways, and the airport master plan.
