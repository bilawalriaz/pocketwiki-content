# Aircraft

An aircraft is any device built to fly in the air. It stays aloft by one of three mechanisms: buoyancy from a gas lighter than air, aerodynamic lift from air flowing over wings or rotors, or direct engine thrust. Aviation, the human activity of designing, building, and flying these machines, has progressed from kites and lanterns to jets, drones, and hypersonic vehicles, and now shapes both daily travel and military power.

## Lighter-than-air and heavier-than-air

Lighter-than-air craft, called **aerostats**, float because the gas inside them (helium, hydrogen, or hot air) weighs less than the air it displaces. Balloons and rigid airships such as the Zeppelin craft that once crossed oceans belong to this family. Heavier-than-air craft, called **aerodynes**, are denser than the air around them and must generate lift by moving forward. Airplanes do this with fixed wings, helicopters with spinning rotors, and experimental lifting bodies with a shaped fuselage. The Airlander 10 is a 92 m hybrid blimp that combines both ideas. The distinction matters because aerostats can hover with no engine, while aerodynes must keep air moving across their lifting surfaces to stay up.

## Four forces of flight

Every heavier-than-air aircraft balances four forces identified in 1793 by Sir George Cayley: **lift** (up), **weight** (down), **thrust** (forward), and **drag** (back). Forward motion sends air faster over the curved top of a wing than under it, producing lower pressure above and higher pressure below. That pressure difference is lift. An aircraft cruises when lift equals weight and thrust equals drag. Sharp maneuvers multiply the load on the wings, expressed as a **load factor** (lift divided by weight); a 60° banked turn doubles the structural stress, at 2 g. A **flight envelope** describes the safe combinations of speed, altitude, and load factor an aircraft can handle.

## Controlling the aircraft

Pilots steer around three axes through the center of gravity. **Pitch** (nose up or down) is controlled by elevators on the tail. **Roll** (wing up or down) is controlled by ailerons on the trailing edge of the wings. **Yaw** (nose left or right) is controlled by the rudder. Helicopters do the equivalent by tilting their rotor blades cyclically and using a tail rotor to cancel the torque the main rotor would impose on the fuselage. Modern airliners often replace the mechanical cables with a **fly-by-wire** system, in which the pilot's inputs go to a computer that then moves the surfaces, letting unstable designs like flying wings remain controllable.

## Power

Gliders and sailplanes have no engine and stay aloft by riding **thermals**, columns of rising warm air, or by being towed aloft. Powered craft use piston engines, gas turbines, jet engines, rockets, ramjets, or electric motors. Propellers work well at lower speeds and altitudes; jet engines become more efficient above about 12,000 m, which is why long-haul airliners fly high. Some aircraft harvest energy in flight: solar panels on the wings, or sustained thermaling that can, in principle, let a glider stay up all day.

## Records and extremes

- **Largest by volume**: the British Airlander 10 hybrid blimp, 92 m long, 140 km/h, about two weeks aloft (as of 2016).
- **Heaviest**: the Soviet Antonov An-225 Mriya, 84 m long, maximum weight 550–700 t, destroyed during the Russo-Ukrainian War.
- **Widest wingspan**: the Hughes H-4 Hercules "Spruce Goose" at 94 m; it flew only one short hop in the late 1940s and never left ground effect.
- **Largest passenger airliner**: the Airbus A380.
- **Longest range airliner**: the Airbus A350-900ULR.
- **Fastest air-breathing powered flight**: the unmanned NASA X-43A at Mach 9.68 in 2004.
- **Fastest manned air-breathing airplane**: the Lockheed SR-71 Blackbird at 3,530 km/h (2,193 mph) in 1976.
- **Fastest manned airplane overall**: the North American X-15 at Mach 6.7 in 1967 (rocket-powered, briefly leaving the atmosphere).

A **Mach number** is the ratio of an aircraft's speed to the local speed of sound, which itself rises with air temperature.

## A short history

Chinese sky lanterns and kites achieved the earliest known man-made flights before the 3rd century BC. In 1783 the Montgolfier brothers launched the first manned hot-air balloon, and a decade later Cayley designed the first successful glider. Otto Lilienthal's careful glider flights in the late 19th century and Octave Chanute's tests taught engineers how wings behaved in practice. In 1903 the Wright brothers made the first controlled, powered heavier-than-air flight, and by 1909 they had settled on the cruciform tail (tailplane plus vertical fin) that most aircraft still use. Rigid airships dominated long routes until the 1940s, when land-based airplanes took over: first with more powerful propellers, then with jet engines. From the 1960s, digital electronics brought fly-by-wire controls, and in the 21st century cheap computers have made drones and other unstable designs practical.

## Uses

Aircraft split into military and civil roles. Military types include fighters, bombers, transports, and tankers. Civil aviation covers commercial airlines, aerial work such as crop-spraying and surveying, and **general aviation** (private and recreational flying). In the United States, general aviation carries around 166 million passengers a year, more than any single airline, and US airlines combined carried over 662 million passengers in 2014. **VTOL** (vertical takeoff and landing) aircraft, such as the Harrier jet and the V-22 Osprey tiltrotor, can hover and take off without a runway.

## Environmental impact

Aviation affects climate in two ways. Burning fuel emits CO₂ and nitrogen oxides, and the water vapour in the engine exhaust condenses into **contrails**, the white trails behind high-flying jets, which can spread into cirrus clouds. The combined effect, measured as **radiative forcing**, is about 1.4 times the warming from the CO₂ alone. In 2018, commercial aviation produced 2.4% of global CO₂ emissions, and per-passenger fuel use has fallen 47% since 1990; total emissions, however, have grown and are projected to triple by 2050. Noise from airports affects nearby sleep and health. Responses include more efficient airframes, optimised routes, biofuels, the international **CORSIA** emissions-trading scheme, and experimental hybrid-electric and hydrogen aircraft. IATA members pledged net-zero aviation by 2050 in 2021, with ICAO following in 2022.

Open questions remain about the true climate effect of contrails, whether biofuels can scale to replace jet fuel, how to integrate passenger drones into city airspace, whether supersonic airliners can return under new noise rules, and how to certify fully autonomous passenger aircraft as safe.
