# Engineered wood

Engineered wood is any wood product manufactured by binding together strands, particles, fibres, veneers, or boards with adhesives or other fixings. Also called mass timber, composite wood, or manufactured board, it is made to precise design specifications tested against national or international standards, giving uniformity and predictability that solid sawn lumber cannot. Panels can reach 64 by 8 feet (19.5 by 2.4 m), and cross-laminated timber (CLT) panels can exceed 16 inches (410 mm) thick. The two governing ideas behind every product are: break wood into small pieces, then reassemble them so the natural weakness of wood (its tendency to split along the grain) is neutralised by orientation, layering, or compression.

## Two structural strategies

Most engineered wood uses one of two strategies. **Cross-oriented panels** alternate the grain of veneers, strands, or boards between layers, balancing strength and stiffness in both directions. Plywood, OSB, and CLT work this way. **Parallel-aligned products** orient all fibres the same way to maximise strength along a single axis. Laminated veneer lumber (LVL), parallel-strand lumber (PSL), and laminated strand lumber (LSL) belong to this group. A smaller third category uses mechanical fixings instead of glue: dowel-laminated timber (DLT) locks softwood boards with hardwood dowels that swell as they absorb moisture, while nail-laminated timber (NLT) is nailed together.

## The main products

**Plywood**, the original engineered wood, is cross-laminated veneer bonded under heat and pressure. **Oriented strand board (OSB)** uses rectangular strands arranged in layers, with the outer layer aligned to the board's strongest direction; arrows on the panel mark that direction. Plywood and OSB, typically 560–640 kg/m³, are interchangeable for sheathing. **Fibreboard** (medium-density fibreboard, MDF; and hardboard, HDF) breaks wood into fibres, mixes them with wax and resin, and presses them into smooth panels for furniture and non-structural use. **Particle board** is pressed from chips, shavings, or sawdust with a synthetic resin binder; it is cheap and uniform but swells badly with moisture.

**Structural composite lumber (SCL)** includes LVL, PSL, LSL, and oriented strand lumber (OSL). PSL uses long veneer strands with a length-to-thickness ratio near300; LSL strands are about 1 foot (0.30 m) long with a ratio near 150; OSL strands have a ratio near 75. **I-joists** combine SCL flanges with a web of structural panel to carry heavy loads over long spans using less wood; by 2004 about 81% of wood-framed floors in North America used them.

## Mass timber

Mass timber is the class of large structural components (columns, beams, walls, floors) made by laminating lumber or veneers. Glued laminated timber (glulam) has existed for over a century. CLT, where every layer is placed perpendicular to its neighbours, is more recent and can be used for long-span floors, walls, and roofs. Products are prefabricated off-site to exact dimensions for fast on-site assembly. Mass timber has grown sharply from 2012 onward, driven by concerns about the carbon footprint of steel and concrete and the appeal of modular construction. The 2021 International Building Code permits mass timber buildings up to 18, 12, or 9 stories depending on construction type (IV-A, IV-B, IV-C). Mass timber performs well in fire because a char layer forms on the outside during a fire and insulates the inner wood.

## Adhesives

Adhesives are what let small pieces act as one large member. Urea-formaldehyde (UF) is cheapest but not waterproof and releases formaldehyde, which prolonged inhalation has been linked to cancer. Phenol-formaldehyde (PF) is yellow-brown, exterior-grade, and has not been found to pose significant health risks from formaldehyde emissions. Melamine-formaldehyde is white and water-resistant. Polymeric methylene diphenyl diisocyanate (pMDI) and polyurethane resins are the most waterproof and formaldehyde-free but are expensive and stick stubbornly to press platens.

## Modified wood

Laboratory treatments push wood beyond what adhesives alone can do. **Densified wood** is hot-pressed, sometimes after chemical removal of lignin and hemicellulose, to triple or more its density, yielding strength comparable to steel. A commercial version called SuperWood, developed by the Maryland startup InventWood, entered limited production in 2025, initially for building facades. **Delignified "white wood" (nanowood)** removes lignin entirely, producing a reflective material with average radiative cooling power of 53 W/m² and better thermal insulation than most commercial insulators; compressed, it gains up to 8.7× tensile strength and 10× toughness over natural wood. **Moldable wood** uses delignification plus water shock to allow shaping, with strengths comparable to some metal alloys. **Transparent wood composites**, still laboratory-scale as of 2020, replace lignin with a transparent polymer to combine see-through clarity with stiffness.

## Environmental significance

In 2014 steel and cement production together produced roughly 1320 and 1740 megatonnes of CO₂ respectively, about 9% of global CO₂ emissions. Engineered wood can replace both: one estimate puts potential savings at around 50 Mt CO₂-equivalent by 2050 through hybrid wood-steel construction, and mass timber buildings use up to 15% less energy to build, save 20–25% in construction time, and cut embodied carbon by about 20% compared with steel or concrete. Per cubic metre, laminated wood that is not incinerated absorbs roughly 582 kg of CO₂, while reinforced concrete emits 458 kg and steel emits 12,087 kg. Whether this carbon storage counts as sequestration depends on the forest source; ISO 21930 only credits biogenic carbon when the wood is from sustainably managed (typically FSC- or SFI-certified) forests. Engineered wood also makes efficient use of small trees, defective wood, and species such as poplar that would not yield structural lumber.

## Trade-offs

Engineered wood needs more primary energy to manufacture than solid lumber and relies on adhesives that can emit formaldehyde. It still rots when exposed to persistent moisture or termites, and mass timber buildings can sway uncomfortably in wind because of their lower stiffness. CLT and glulam cost more than solid wood; NLT is labour-intensive; DLT and SCL are limited in panel size.

By 2022, over 84 mass timber buildings of eight or more stories were completed or under construction worldwide. Ascent MKE in Milwaukee (2022, 25 stories, 87 m) is the tallest hybrid mass-timber high-rise. Stadthaus in London (2009, 9 stories, 30 m) was an early CLT residential tower. The Mistissini Bridge in Quebec (2014) spans 160 m using glulam beams and CLT panels. The largest engineered wood panels now reach sizes that allow entire wall and floor sections to be craned into place in a single lift.
