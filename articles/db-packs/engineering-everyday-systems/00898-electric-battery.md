# Electric battery

An electric battery is a source of electric power built from one or more electrochemical cells with external connections that drive current through a device. Each cell stores energy in chemical form and releases it through a redox reaction, in which one substance loses electrons (oxidation) while another gains them (reduction). The released energy equals the free-energy difference between higher-energy reactants and lower-energy products. During discharge, the negative terminal is the anode (the electron source) and the positive terminal is the cathode. Electrons travel through the external circuit from anode to cathode, while ions move through the electrolyte inside the cell, balancing charge and sustaining the reaction. A separator keeps the reactants from mixing directly, so charges must take the external path.

The driving force of a cell is its electromotive force (emf), measured in volts. Net emf equals the difference between the reduction potentials of the two half-reactions. Open-circuit voltage equals this emf; once current flows, internal resistance makes the terminal voltage drop below emf when discharging and rise above it when charging. A 1.5 V cell delivering one coulomb performs 1.5 J of work. Cell voltage depends on chemistry: zinc–carbon and alkaline cells deliver about 1.5 V, nickel-cadmium (NiCd) and nickel-metal hydride (NiMH) about 1.2 V, and lithium cells 3 V or higher because lithium undergoes unusually large electrochemical potential changes.

The history begins with Benjamin Franklin's 1749 use of the word "battery" to describe a row of linked Leyden jar capacitors. In 1800 Alessandro Volta built the first true electrochemical battery, the voltaic pile, layering copper and zinc plates separated by brine-soaked paper to produce a steady current. Volta believed the cells could supply power indefinitely; in 1834 Michael Faraday showed that electrode corrosion was an unavoidable consequence of the chemical reaction. Practical progress came in 1836 when John Frederic Daniell designed the Daniell cell, a copper pot holding copper sulfate solution, with an earthenware vessel of sulfuric acid and a zinc electrode inside. Its steady voltage made it the standard electricity source for the telegraph industry. Late in the 19th century, the dry cell replaced liquid electrolyte with a paste, eliminating fragile glass jars and leaking acid and making portable devices practical.

Batteries divide into primary and secondary types. Primary batteries, such as zinc–carbon and alkaline cells, are single-use because their reactions are effectively irreversible. They pack more energy per unit mass but perform poorly under heavy loads (below about 75 Ω of resistance). Secondary batteries, including lead–acid, NiCd, NiMH, and lithium-ion, can be recharged by applying a current that reverses the discharge reactions. Their active materials degrade, electrolyte is lost, and corrosion accumulates with each cycle. The lead–acid battery, the oldest rechargeable design, remains common in cars because it is cheap and can deliver surge currents of roughly 450 A. Its liquid sulfuric acid releases hydrogen gas during charging, so the cells need ventilation.

Battery performance is described by several linked quantities. Capacity, in ampere-hours, is the total charge a cell can deliver, rated for a 20-hour discharge at 20 °C. Higher discharge rates reduce usable capacity, a relationship Peukert's law captures for lead-acid cells as t = Q_P / I^k, where k is about 1.3. The C-rate expresses charge or discharge current as a multiple of the cell's one-hour nominal capacity; capacity is highest at low C-rates. Self-discharge drains charge even when no device is connected: disposable cells lose 8–20% per year at room temperature, and a fresh NiCd cell loses about 10% in its first 24 hours and roughly 10% per month after that. In 2024 a prototype electric-vehicle battery demonstrated a 10–80% charge in five minutes.

Lifespan has two dimensions. Endurance is the runtime per charge; cycle life is the number of charge-discharge cycles before failure. Low-capacity NiMH cells reach about 1 000 cycles, high-capacity versions about 500. NiCd cells also reach around 1 000 cycles but can show a "memory effect," in which repeated shallow discharges appear to reduce usable capacity. Automotive lead-acid batteries seldom last more than six years because vibration and sulfation degrade the plates; deep-cycle variants use thicker plates and must be kept above about 20% charge. Storing cells cool slows self-discharge and can extend alkaline shelf life by roughly 5%, though most manufacturers, including Duracell, advise against refrigerator storage.

Global production grew about 30% per year from 2010 to 2018, reaching 180 GWh, and is projected to reach 2 600 GWh by 2030, possibly 3 562 GWh if costs keep falling, driven by transport electrification and grid-scale storage for renewable energy. Distributed units in homes and electric vehicles can feed power back to the network (vehicle-to-grid), and EV batteries retired after five to eight years, once they drop below 80% capacity, are being reused for backup or renewable storage. Solid-state batteries, announced by John Goodenough's team at UT Austin in 2017, promise safer, faster-charging cells with roughly three times the energy density of earlier designs, using abundant materials such as sodium.

Hazards follow from the chemistry. Recharging a primary cell, short-circuiting, or overcharging can build up hydrogen and oxygen gases and cause an explosion. Jump-starting a car can release ignitable hydrogen. Over-discharge of a zinc-can cell can leak corrosive electrolyte. Button cells, if swallowed, can burn esophageal tissue within about six hours through electrical discharge and the generation of sodium hydroxide, a process called liquefaction necrosis. Roughly 179 000 tons of the nearly three billion batteries sold each year in the United States reach landfills, carrying lead, mercury, and cadmium into the e-waste stream. The 1996 US Mercury-Containing and Rechargeable Battery Management Act banned mercury batteries and required labelling, and the EU Battery Directive mandates recycling and the crossed-out wheeled-bin symbol. From 2026, EU appliances must use user-replaceable batteries.
