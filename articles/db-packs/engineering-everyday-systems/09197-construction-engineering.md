# Construction engineering

Construction engineering is a subdiscipline of civil engineering that handles the designing, planning, construction, and operations management of infrastructure such as roadways, tunnels, bridges, airports, railroads, facilities, buildings, dams, and utilities. It is also called construction operations.

Construction engineers learn design and project management techniques similar to those of civil engineers, but the priorities differ. Civil engineering students concentrate on analytical design and are trained to become design professionals. Construction engineering students focus on construction procedures, methods, costs, schedules, and personnel management, with a central concern of delivering a project on time, within budget, and at the desired quality.

## Education

A typical curriculum blends engineering mechanics, engineering design, construction management, and general science and mathematics, usually leading to a Bachelor of Science degree. Programmes should be accredited by the Accreditation Board for Engineering and Technology (ABET), which sets quality standards for the profession; the source notes 4,863 ABET-accredited institutions worldwide. Many graduates pursue further study in civil engineering, engineering management, or business administration.

## Licensing and career path

A design-track construction engineer typically needs a Professional Engineer (PE) license. The usual path is to sit the Engineer in Training (EIT) exam, also called the Fundamentals of Engineering Exam (FE), during college, then complete work experience before the PE exam: roughly five years post-graduate in most of the world and four years in the USA, though some US states allow eligible EITs to sit the PE exam as little as six months after the FE. A construction management position does not strictly require a PE license, but holding one makes the holder more marketable because it allows signing off on temporary structure designs.

## Work activities

New construction engineers usually start as project engineers or assistant project engineers. Day-to-day work includes preparing purchasing requisitions, processing change orders, preparing monthly budgeting reports, and handling meeting minutes. On larger projects they apply business, technical, and management skills to oversee bridges, buildings, and housing projects, taking charge of risk analysis, costing, and planning. Their work shapes infrastructure that must meet the unique demands of its environment, so understanding infrastructure life cycles is part of the role.

## Job prospects

Demand for construction engineers swings sharply with economic cycles because much of the work follows housing and commercial development. From 2008 through at least 2011, the collapse of housing bubbles in many parts of the world sharply reduced demand, pushing construction professionals toward infrastructure projects and increasing competition. The source reports that the early-2010 US construction industry unemployment rate was 27%, nearly three times the 9.7% national average and comparable to the 25% US unemployment rate of 1933, the lowest point of the Great Depression. Automation of some engineering tasks may further reduce demand.

## Remuneration

Salaries depend on sector, country, and experience. A 2010 UK survey of the construction and built environment industry put the average civil engineer salary at £29,582, rising from £23,813 at three to six years' experience to £38,214 at fourteen to twenty years. In the United States, the source gives a May 2013 national average of $85,640 for civil engineers, a closely related role.
