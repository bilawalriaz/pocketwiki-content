# Biological engineering

Biological engineering (also called bioengineering) applies the principles of biology and the tools of engineering to create usable, tangible, and economically viable products. A bioengineer either imitates a biological system to build something new, or modifies and controls a biological system to solve a problem. Working with doctors, clinicians, and researchers, they use traditional engineering ideas to replace, augment, sustain, or predict the chemical and mechanical processes of living things.

## How it sits among other fields

Biological engineering is a science-based discipline founded on the biological sciences in the same way that chemical, electrical, and mechanical engineering are founded on chemistry, electromagnetism, and classical mechanics. It overlaps heavily with biotechnology and the biomedical sciences, much as aerospace engineering overlaps with kinetics and astrophysics. The work splits into two directions:

- Engineering toward biology: design devices, materials, and processes used in or inspired by living systems, such as prosthetics, medical imaging, and tissue-engineered organs.
- Biology toward engineering: re-engineer living systems themselves, such as bacteria programmed to produce useful chemicals, or portable diagnostic devices that read biological signals.

Biomimetics illustrates the first direction: the imitation of natural designs. The hook-and-loop fastener Velcro arose when George de Mestral noticed how easily burs clung to a dog's hair.

## History

Before the Second World War, engineers and life scientists rarely collaborated, and engineers entering the field often lacked the underlying biology. After the war the discipline grew quickly. British scientist Heinz Wolff coined the term "bioengineering" in 1954 at the National Institute for Medical Research and in the same year became the first director of the Division of Biological Engineering at Oxford, the first time the subject was recognised as its own branch at a university. Early work leaned on electrical engineering because so much of it involved medical devices.

The first biological engineering program in the United States began at the University of California, San Diego in 1966, with later programs at MIT and Utah State University. Many older agricultural engineering departments have since rebranded as "agricultural and biological engineering" or "agricultural and biosystems engineering." Doug Lauffenburger of MIT has described the field's scope as spanning molecules (molecular biology, biochemistry, pharmacology, neuroscience), cells and tissues, whole organisms, and biomes and ecosystems.

## Education and sub-disciplines

A typical undergraduate program runs three to five years and awards a Bachelor of Engineering or B.S. in engineering. Core courses include thermodynamics, biomechanics, biology, genetic engineering, fluid and mechanical dynamics, chemical and enzyme kinetics, electronics, and materials properties.

Major sub-disciplines, which often overlap, include:

- Biomedical engineering, applying engineering to medicine and biology for healthcare.
- Tissue engineering and neural engineering, building or interfacing with tissues and the nervous system.
- Biochemical and bioprocess engineering, applying engineering to microscopic biological systems through fermentation, biocatalysis, and bioseparation to produce goods such as proteins from raw materials.
- Biological systems engineering, applying engineering to agriculture, food sciences, and ecosystems.
- Environmental health engineering, controlling the environment for human health, comfort, and safety, including life-support systems for space and ocean exploration.
- Human factors and ergonomics, optimising the human–machine relationship through physiology and psychology.
- Biomimetics, imitating natural models to solve human problems.
- Biomechanical engineering, linking mechanical engineering and biology to improve health.
- Bionics, integrating biomedical engineering with robotics and assistive technologies such as prosthetics.
- Bioprinting, using biomaterials to print cells, tissues, and organs.
- Biorobotics, combining electronics and sensors with prosthetics or biohybrid robots.
- Systems biology, studying molecules, cells, organs, and organisms through their interactions.

## Professional organisations

Several bodies support and shape the field. ABET, the U.S. accreditation board for engineering bachelor's programs, distinguishes biological engineering from biomedical engineering, though the two overlap substantially. The American Institute for Medical and Biological Engineering (AIMBE), with around 1,500 members, publicises the field's value and recognises achievement. The Institute of Biological Engineering (IBE) is a donation-funded non-profit that offers scholarships and promotes public engagement. The Society for Biological Engineering (SBE), part of the American Institute of Chemical Engineers, is a global community that hosts international conferences to advance the integration of biology with engineering. A bioengineer may tweak a single protein's behaviour one day and stabilise a riverbank or design a prosthetic the next, applying the same habit of treating living systems as something that can be measured, modelled, and redesigned.
