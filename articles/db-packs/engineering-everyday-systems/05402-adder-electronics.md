# Adder (electronics)

An adder is a digital circuit that adds numbers. Adders live inside the arithmetic logic units (ALUs) of processors, and they also compute addresses, table indices, and increment or decrement operations elsewhere on the chip. Adders can be built for many number formats, including binary-coded decimal and excess-3, but in practice they almost always operate on binary numbers. When negative values are stored in two's complement or ones' complement form, an adder can be reused as a subtractor with only a small amount of extra logic; other signed representations need more surrounding circuitry. George Stibitz built the first binary adder, the 2-bit Model K, in 1937.

A **half adder** takes two single binary inputs, A and B, and produces a sum bit S and a carry bit C. Together the two outputs encode 2C + S, so a half adder is a one-bit adder with a two-bit result. S is the XOR of A and B, and C is the AND of A and B. The carry is the overflow passed to the next more significant column of a multi-bit addition.

A **full adder** adds three one-bit inputs, A, B, and C_in (the carry in from the previous column), and produces a sum S and a carry-out C_out, with the result equal to 2C_out + S. The standard expressions are

S = A ⊕ B ⊕ C_in
C_out = (A · B) + (C_in · (A ⊕ B))

These come from simplifying a three-input truth table with a Karnaugh map. In the C_out expression, the second AND term is zero whenever A and B are both 1, so the final OR gate may be replaced by an XOR gate without changing the result. A full adder can also be built from two half adders and an OR gate, from nine NAND gates, or from nine NOR gates, the last two being useful when a chip provides only one gate type.

Adding N-bit numbers chains N one-bit adders. In a **ripple-carry adder**, each stage's C_in is wired to the previous stage's C_out, so the carry propagates from the least significant bit to the most significant one. The first stage can be a half adder because its C_in is permanently zero. The layout is simple, but the circuit is slow: each full adder introduces two gate delays on the carry path, so a 32-bit ripple-carry adder needs about 65 gate delays in the worst case. A design that alternates carry polarities and uses optimized AND-OR-Invert gates can be roughly twice as fast.

For wider or faster adders, the **carry-lookahead adder (CLA)**, introduced by Weinberger and Smith in 1958, computes carries in parallel. For each bit position it derives a propagate signal P, which is 1 when at least one input is 1 and the carry therefore passes through, and a generate signal G, which is 1 when both inputs are 1 and the carry is produced locally. P is the half-adder sum, and G is the half-adder carry. Once every stage has produced its P and G, the carries for all positions can be derived in a few logic levels. Variants of the same recurrence include the Brent–Kung and Kogge–Stone adders. Larger adders are built hierarchically: a 64-bit adder can combine four 16-bit CLAs with two levels of lookahead. Other block-based architectures include the carry-skip adder, which computes P and G for a whole block at once, and the carry-select adder, which pre-computes results for both possible carry inputs and uses a multiplexer to pick the correct one.

When three or more numbers must be summed, **carry-save adders** avoid propagating the carry after every stage. Each stage is a three-input adder that outputs a partial sum and a partial carry, both of which feed the next stage. A conventional adder then combines the final sum and carry. A full adder in this role is called a 3:2 compressor, because it maps three one-bit inputs to a two-bit output; a half adder is a 2:2 compressor. Stacks of compressors sum more than three numbers at once, with the two most common arrangements being the Dadda tree and the Wallace tree, also used in fast multiplier circuits.

Beyond ordinary digital logic, the same ideas appear in reversible and quantum computing. Using only Toffoli and CNOT gates, one can build half and full adders that work in classical reversible circuits and in quantum ones, and the quantum Fourier transform can also implement addition. Outside the digital domain, **analog adders** sum two input currents directly, and summing amplifiers exploit the same idea to reduce the transistor count of an addition circuit.
