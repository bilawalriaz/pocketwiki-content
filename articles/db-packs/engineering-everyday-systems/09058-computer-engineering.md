# Computer engineering

Computer engineering is the branch of engineering that designs computer hardware and the software that runs on it. It sits where electrical engineering meets computer science, which is why the same program is sometimes called Electrical and Computer Engineering or Computer Science and Engineering at some universities.

A computer engineer works on a system from the transistor up to the application. The work spans designing microcontrollers (small chips that run one device), microprocessors, personal computers, and supercomputers, then writing the firmware and software that make them useful, and finally integrating them into larger products like cars, phones, or spacecraft. A modern car contains separate embedded computers that manage engine timing, brakes, and airbags, and the engineer has to understand each subsystem and how the subsystems interact.

The field splits into two main practice areas. **Computer hardware engineering** focuses on the physical machine: processors, memory, boards, and circuit design. **Computer software engineering** focuses on the programs, from operating systems to applications.

## Specialty areas

Within those two areas, computer engineering breaks into focused specialties, each tied to a layer of the system.

**Processor design** picks an instruction set (the binary commands a chip understands) and an execution model such as RISC (Reduced Instruction Set Computing, a design that uses many simple instructions) or VLIW (Very Long Instruction Word, a design that bundles operations for parallel execution), then builds the microarchitecture, including datapaths (the circuits that move and transform data), control logic, register files (small fast on-chip memory), caches, and clock distribution, typically described in a hardware description language like VHDL or Verilog.

**Coding, cryptography, and information protection** develops methods to secure data, including digital watermarking and tamper resistance.

**Communications and wireless networks** advances modulation (encoding data onto carrier signals), error-control coding, high-speed network design, and fault-tolerant transmission.

**Compilers and operating systems** designs new OS architectures and program-analysis techniques, including post-link-time code transformation (rewriting programs after the linker has combined them).

**Computer networks, mobile computing, and distributed systems** builds integrated environments for shared-channel wireless networks, adaptive resource management, and quality of service.

**Computer architecture and parallel processing** designs CPUs, cache hierarchies, memory organization, and load balancing for reliable, high-performance systems.

**Computer vision and robotics** builds visual sensing, 3D environment representation, and manipulation, feeding into human-computer interfaces and specialized cameras.

**Embedded systems** designs dedicated computers built into other products, with current work in system-on-chip design (putting an entire computer on one chip), edge computing (processing data near where it is collected instead of in a distant cloud), and the Internet of Things.

**Integrated circuits and VLSI (Very-Large-Scale Integration) design** improves the speed, reliability, and energy efficiency of very-large-scale integrated circuits, for example by reducing power consumption in VLSI algorithms.

**Signal, image, and speech processing** advances human-computer interaction through speech recognition, medical imaging, and facial-feature recognition.

**Quantum computing** uses superposition (a particle existing in many states at once), interference, and entanglement (linked particles sharing a state) to run algorithms that would be impractical on classical machines, applied to quantum cryptography, physical simulation, and quantum algorithms.

## History

The discipline traces to 1939, when John Vincent Atanasoff and Clifford Berry began building the Atanasoff–Berry Computer (ABC), the first electronic digital computer. The ABC took five years to complete; the original was dismantled in the 1940s, and a working replica was built in 1997.

The modern computer emerged from a chain of semiconductor breakthroughs. Working transistors were demonstrated at Bell Labs in 1947. Silicon dioxide surface passivation (coating a silicon chip with an insulating layer to control its electrical behavior) followed in 1955, and planar silicon transistors (built as flat layers on a chip surface rather than as raised bumps) in 1957. Jean Hoerni's planar process and Robert Noyce's monolithic integrated circuit (a full circuit built on one piece of silicon) at Fairchild Semiconductor in 1959 followed. The metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor (MOSFET), the transistor type that now dominates all digital electronics, was demonstrated at Bell Labs in 1960, and the first single-chip microprocessor (an entire CPU on one piece of silicon), the Intel 4004, was created at Intel in 1971.

## Education

The first U.S. computer engineering degree program opened in 1971 at Case Western Reserve University. By 2015 there were 250 ABET-accredited computer engineering programs in the country. In Europe, accreditation is handled through the EQANIE network.

Most entry-level jobs require a bachelor's degree in computer engineering, electrical engineering, or computer science. Coursework typically covers calculus, linear algebra, differential equations, and computer science, plus analog and digital circuit design. A strong programming background is essential because hardware engineers routinely work alongside software systems. Some employers, especially for specialized work, require a master's degree. Because the full breadth of computer design exceeds what an undergraduate degree can cover, many schools let students concentrate in their junior and senior years, and computer engineers are expected to keep learning throughout their careers as technology evolves.
