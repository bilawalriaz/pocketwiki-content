# Aircraft engine

An aircraft engine is the power component of an aircraft propulsion system. Most are piston engines or gas turbines; a few have been rocket powered, and many small unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) now use electric motors.

## The market and its economics

As of 2025, five manufacturers dominate the global market: GE Aerospace, Pratt & Whitney, Safran, Rolls-Royce, and MTU Aero Engines. Jet engines have very high barriers to entry: they use advanced materials, take about eight years and billions of currency units to develop, and require exacting safety certification. The last successful new entrant in jet engines was General Electric in the 1970s. The engines themselves are typically sold at a loss; manufacturers make their profit from maintenance and parts over an engine's roughly thirty-year service life, following the razor and blades model. Because old engines need more service than new ones, this creates a perverse incentive to slow new production, contributing to engine shortages that have constrained Airbus and Boeing's output since the post-COVID demand surge after 2023.

## Shaft engines

Shaft engines deliver mechanical power to a propeller, rotor, or ducted fan.

### Piston engines

A piston engine burns fuel in cylinders, converting linear piston motion into crankshaft rotation. Common aircraft configurations include in-line, V-type, horizontally opposed, H-configuration, and radial. A radial engine places its cylinders in a circle around the crankshaft and is air-cooled, which is rugged and damage-tolerant but can lead to overheating.

A rotary engine keeps the cylinders in a circle but fixes the crankshaft to the airframe so the crankcase and cylinders spin with the propeller. This forces cooling air past the cylinders even at low airspeeds. The first practical example was the Gnome Omega, first flown in 1909.

The Wankel is a rotary design that replaces pistons with a rotor turning inside a housing. It is about half the weight and size of a four-stroke piston engine of equal power, and because the aluminium housing expands more than the steel rotor, it does not seize when overheated, an important safety factor. Postwar development was overshadowed by turbines, and the Wankel found more use in cars, notably by Mazda. It survives in motor gliders, where compactness and smoothness matter.

Diesel aircraft engines were attempted from the 1930s. Diesels are more reliable and better suited to long runs at medium power, but the lightweight alloys of that era could not handle the high compression ratios, so they had poor power-to-weight ratios; the Clerget 14F of 1939 matched a gasoline radial. Better automotive diesel technology and Europe's high taxation of AVGAS relative to Jet A1 have revived interest, with Thielert converting Mercedes automotive diesels for aircraft and Austro Engine later developing the AE300 turbodiesel.

### Power turbines

A turboprop uses a gas turbine core to drive a propeller through a reduction gearbox. Air enters the intake, is compressed, mixes with jet fuel in the combustor, and ignites. The expanding gases drive the turbine, which turns both the compressor and the propeller. Because nearly all of the turbine's power goes to the propeller, the exhaust contributes only a small share of total thrust.

A turboshaft is a gas turbine optimised for shaft output rather than jet thrust, and typically drives a helicopter rotor. It splits into a gas generator, comprising the compressor, combustor, and initial turbine stages, and a power section, with additional turbine stages, gear reduction, and the output shaft.

## Reaction engines

Reaction engines produce thrust by accelerating a mass rearward. The dominant family is the jet turbine. A turbojet has an axial compressor at the front, combustion chambers in the middle, and turbine stages at the rear, with most thrust coming from the high-velocity exhaust. A turbofan adds a large fan driven by a low-pressure turbine, so a significant fraction of thrust comes from air that bypasses the core. A rocket carries its own oxidiser, so it can operate beyond the atmosphere. A rocket turbine engine combines a liquid-propellant rocket with a turbine jet, giving a slightly higher power-to-weight ratio than a regular jet and the ability to work at higher altitudes.

## Electric propulsion

Electric motors have powered aircraft since the 1960s, mostly as military drones. In late 2007, a French light aircraft with an 18 kW electric motor and lithium polymer batteries flew more than 50 kilometres, the first electric airplane to receive a certificate of airworthiness. On 18 May 2020, the Pipistrel E-811 became the first electric aircraft engine awarded a type certificate by EASA, powering the Velis Electro, the first fully electric EASA type-certified aeroplane. Small multicopter UAVs are now almost always electric; larger gasoline-electric hybrids are under development.

## Notes: position numbering, fuel, hybrids

On multi-engine aircraft, engine positions are numbered from left to right as the pilot faces forward. For a four-engine Boeing 747, engine 1 is the left engine farthest from the fuselage and engine 3 is the right engine nearest it. For the unusual stacked English Electric Lightning, engine 1 sits below and ahead of engine 2.

Avgas is blended with tetraethyllead to reach high octane ratings, a practice no longer permitted for road fuel, and the shrinking supply of TEL has made finding replacements a priority for general aviation. Model aircraft use nitro engines, which burn a mixture of methanol, nitromethane, and lubricant.

A composite cycle engine concept presented at the 2018 ILA Berlin Air Show by Bauhaus Luftfahrt combined a 16-blade geared turbofan with a piston engine core: the fan was driven by a low-pressure turbine while a two-bank piston engine drove the high-pressure compressor, raising peak pressure to 30 MPa and overall efficiency to 48.2 percent. The 11,200 lbf design could power a 50-seat regional jet, with a 15 percent fuel burn reduction over comparable engines at the cost of 30 percent more engine weight.
