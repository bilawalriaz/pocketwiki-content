# Internal combustion engine

An internal combustion engine (ICE) is a heat engine that burns fuel inside a sealed working chamber, converting the chemical energy of combustion directly into mechanical motion. The expanding high-pressure gas pushes a piston, rotor, turbine blade, or rocket nozzle, and that motion turns a wheel, propeller, or generator.

The defining feature is that the same gas that burns is the gas that does the work. In an external combustion engine such as a steam engine, fuel heats a separate working fluid in a boiler, so the working fluid never mixes with the combustion products. Because the working fluid in an ICE is the hot combustion gas itself, peak temperatures are higher and the device is more compact, which is why ICEs dominate mobile vehicles such as cars, aircraft, boats, and most trucks.

## Classification

ICEs sort along three axes:

- **Combustion pattern.** Intermittent combustion burns fuel in discrete batches, one per cycle, as in the Otto cycle used in piston engines. Continuous combustion burns fuel in a steady flow, as in gas turbines, jet engines, and rocket engines.
- **Ignition method.** Spark-ignition (SI) engines compress a premixed air–fuel charge and ignite it with a spark plug. Compression-ignition (CI, or diesel) engines compress only air until it is hot enough to auto-ignite fuel injected into the cylinder.
- **Mechanical configuration.** Reciprocating pistons are most common; rotary Wankel engines and turbine engines are the main alternatives.

## Components of a reciprocating engine

The engine block, usually cast iron or aluminium, holds cylinder liners arranged in inline, V, or boxer layouts. Pistons seal the upper end of each cylinder and are driven down by combustion pressure; connecting rods and a crankshaft convert that linear push into rotation. Piston rings keep combustion gas from leaking past the piston and keep oil out of the combustion chamber. The cylinder head closes the top of the cylinder and houses the intake and exhaust valves (typically poppet valves), the spark plug on SI engines, or the fuel injector on CI engines. The crankcase encloses the crankshaft and holds the oil sump.

## Operating cycles

A spark-ignition piston engine typically runs on the four-stroke Otto cycle, two crank revolutions per cycle:

1. **Intake.** The intake valve opens and the descending piston draws in a fresh air–fuel mixture.
2. **Compression.** Both valves close and the rising piston squeezes the mixture; the ratio of maximum to minimum volume is the compression ratio.
3. **Power.** The spark plug fires, combustion raises the pressure sharply, and the expanding gas drives the piston down.
4. **Exhaust.** The exhaust valve opens and the rising piston expels the burned gases.

The two-stroke cycle completes the same four functions in one crank revolution by using ports in the cylinder walls that the piston uncovers as it moves, so fresh charge displaces exhaust without a separate valve event; this replacement of exhaust with fresh charge is called scavenging.

Continuous-combustion engines, including gas turbines, run on the Brayton cycle: a compressor, a combustion chamber, and a turbine operate simultaneously, with different portions of the working fluid in each stage at the same time. Jet and rocket engines are variants.

## Fuels and combustion

Most ICEs burn hydrocarbon fuels (gasoline, diesel, natural gas, ethanol, biodiesel) with atmospheric oxygen. SI engines mix fuel and air before compression; CI engines inject fuel into already-hot, highly compressed air. The air–fuel ratio, compression ratio, and ignition timing together govern how completely and efficiently the charge burns, and modern engines adjust all three continuously through an electronic control unit. Higher octane fuel resists knock, the uncontrolled self-ignition of part of the charge that creates damaging pressure spikes. Alternative fuels include hydrogen, which needs specialised high-pressure or cryogenic storage, wood gas, and biogas.

## Efficiency and performance

Thermal efficiency is the share of fuel energy that becomes useful work rather than heat. It is capped by the theoretical Carnot limit, which depends on the highest and lowest temperatures in the cycle, and by the temperature engine materials can survive. Ordinary road engines average roughly 18–20%; Formula One engines have exceeded 50%; a gas turbine paired with a steam bottoming cycle in a combined-cycle power plant reaches about 61%. Diesels run higher compression ratios than gasoline engines, so they extract more useful work per unit of fuel. Turbochargers and superchargers compress the intake air, packing more oxygen into each cylinder so more fuel burns per cycle. Parasitic losses from the oil pump, alternator, water pump, and cooling fan absorb part of the gross output, leaving less at the shaft.

## Environmental impact

Combustion of hydrocarbons produces carbon dioxide, water vapour, nitrogen oxides (NOx), carbon monoxide, unburned hydrocarbons, and particulate matter; incomplete combustion also yields toxic compounds such as benzene and formaldehyde. NOx drives ground-level ozone formation, and sulphur in fuel forms sulphur dioxide, a cause of acid rain. Catalytic converters, exhaust gas recirculation, precise fuel injection, low-sulphur fuels, and stop-start systems are the main ways emissions are reduced. In the United States the Clean Air Act sets the regulatory floor.

## History in one pass

John Barber sketched a gas turbine in 1791; Thomas Mead patented a gas engine and Robert Street a liquid-fuel ICE in 1794; in 1807 the Niépce brothers ran the Pyréolophore, and in 1808 François Isaac de Rivaz fitted a hydrogen-fuelled ICE with electric-spark ignition to a primitive vehicle, the first ICE-powered automobile. Étienne Lenoir sold the first commercial gas-fuelled ICE in 1860. The decisive breakthrough was Nicolaus Otto's 1876 patent of the four-cycle engine, developed with Gottlieb Daimler and Wilhelm Maybach. Karl Benz patented a reliable two-stroke gasoline engine in 1879, Rudolf Diesel built the first compression-ignition engine in 1892, and George Brayton had produced the first commercial liquid-fueled ICE in 1872. The same principles later extended to aviation: Robert Goddard's liquid-fueled rocket flew in 1926 and the Heinkel He 178 became the first jet aircraft in 1939.

## Open questions

The long-term role of the ICE is unsettled. Battery electric vehicles are gaining road market share, and hydrogen fuel cells beat hydrogen ICEs on efficiency, but batteries are heavy and hydrogen storage remains hard, so piston and turbine engines are likely to remain in heavy transport, aviation, and remote power. Advanced concepts such as homogeneous charge compression ignition (HCCI) and split-cycle engines have shown higher efficiency and lower emissions in prototypes but face commercialization barriers.
