# Architectural engineering

Architectural engineering, also called building engineering, is the discipline that applies engineering to the design and construction of buildings. It overlaps with architecture, civil engineering, mechanical engineering, and electrical engineering, but its focus is different: architects shape form, function, and spatial experience, while architectural engineers design the systems that make a building stand up, stay habitable, and remain safe, including structure, HVAC, plumbing, power, lighting, fire protection, acoustics, and the building envelope.

The discipline is relatively young. Formal architectural engineering education developed in the late 19th century and became widespread in the United States by the mid-20th century. Its identity as a separate profession was cemented when the NCEES created a dedicated Professional Engineering (PE) exam, first offered in April 2003.

## How it differs from architecture

Architects train in design theory, history, and aesthetics, and produce the concept and detailed plans that meet client needs and regulations. Architectural engineers blend that awareness with engineering science: structural integrity, mechanical systems, energy efficiency, and feasibility analysis. The engineer makes the building safe, efficient, sustainable, and buildable; the architect makes it coherent and visually resolved. On most projects the two work together from schematic design through construction.

## Core subdisciplines

- **Mechanical, electrical, and plumbing (MEP)**, called building services engineering in the UK, Canada, and Australia. Covers HVAC, plumbing, rainwater drainage, electrical power distribution, telecommunications, fire alarm, lightning protection, control systems, and lighting.
- **Structural engineering** for buildings: analysis and design of the frame, walls, and foundations to resist gravity loads, wind, rain, ice, and seismic events (earthquake engineering).
- **Sustainable engineering**, embedding energy efficiency and resource conservation from the outset.
- **Building envelope engineering**, the design of the outer shell (walls, roof, windows, doors) that controls heat, air, moisture, light, and noise between inside and outside.
- **Fire protection engineering**, modelling fire and smoke behaviour and integrating detection, suppression, and egress.
- **Acoustical engineering**, controlling sound transmission, reflection, and quality within and around the building.

## Licensing and professional practice

In most US jurisdictions, architectural engineering is a separately licensed profession. The typical path is graduation from an EAC/ABET-accredited program, experience under a licensed engineer, and the NCEES PE exam. About two-thirds of states also accept ETAC/ABET-accredited architectural engineering technology degrees for licensure, and PE licences can often be transferred between states by comity.

In most states an architectural engineer cannot legally offer architectural services without an architecture licence. A few states, including New Jersey, allow cross-credit between the PE and architectural registration exams, creating a formal dual-licence route. Structural engineering on high-risk projects such as hospitals, schools, or tall buildings in seismic zones often requires its own additional licence. Rules vary by state and city.

The UK model is similar in structure: an accredited MEng or MS plus further learning leads to registration as a Chartered Engineer through the Engineering Council.

## Variations across countries

The boundary between architect and architectural engineer is drawn differently around the world.

- In **Japan**, a first-class architect fills both roles, but buildings above a certain scale require a separately licensed structural designer.
- In **Germany, Austria, Iran**, and much of the Arab world, architecture graduates receive an engineering degree (Dipl.-Ing. or equivalent) and train across both design and engineering.
- In **Italy**, an *ingegnere edile* is legally entitled to practise architecture and is often called an architect.
- In **Spain**, the *arquitecto* holds a technical university degree with legal authority over building structure and facilities.
- In **Greece**, graduates of a Polytechnic architecture faculty earn a five-year Engineering Diploma (300 ECTS, equivalent to a Bologna master's) and gain full architect status after examinations by the Technical Chamber of Greece (TEE).
- In **Brazil**, architecture curricula still include structural, electrical, hydraulic, and mechanical training, and for small buildings an architect can be responsible for the whole project, except electrical systems above 30 kVA, which require an electrical engineer.
- In **Korea and Arabic-speaking countries**, the word translated as "architect" is literally "architectural engineer".
- In **Canada**, a CEAB-accredited engineering degree is the minimum academic requirement for P.Eng registration, and graduates of non-CEAB programs must demonstrate equivalence.
- In **Vietnam**, the engineering degree (*Bằng kỹ sư*) is awarded after five years of study.

## Education and integration

Architectural engineering education, like older engineering branches, normally culminates in a university degree. Some programs specialise in one system (structural, mechanical, or electrical); others offer a generalist building-engineering curriculum. The defining trait of the discipline is multi-disciplinary integration: the same student studies structural analysis, thermodynamics, electrical power, acoustics, and architectural design, so they can reason about how those systems interact within a single building rather than designing each in isolation.
