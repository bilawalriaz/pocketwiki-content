# Engineering economics

Engineering economics is the applied branch of microeconomics that helps engineers and managers decide among competing ways to spend limited money, time, and materials. It draws on economics for its logical framework and on mathematics and statistics for numerical analysis, and it is closely tied to cost accounting. Unlike pure microeconomics, it treats prices, competition, and demand as fixed inputs handed in from elsewhere, so analysts can compare alternatives rather than explain markets.

The core question is straightforward. Given a defined purpose, several alternatives exist, each with its own stream of costs and revenues spread over an analysis period, either a fixed number of years or the estimated life of the project. Engineering economics formulates those cash flows, estimates them, and evaluates which alternative best meets the decision maker's goals. Engineers weigh economic viability alongside technical performance and non-economic "attributes" such as appearance, style, or public image. Two alternatives always appear in any analysis: the "do nothing" option, which sets the baseline, and the opportunity cost of acting, which is the benefit foregone from the next-best alternative.

Because money received later is worth less than money received today, every future cash flow is discounted to a common point in time using an interest rate; only the simplest studies skip this step. The minimum attractive rate of return (MARR), set by the firm, acts as the cutoff for accepting a project. The guiding rule for funding is to allocate the firm's limited capital to projects in order of prospective return until the budget runs out, and the lowest return funded in that round becomes the MARR for the period, so every remaining candidate must beat it. Decision trees and real options analysis handle cases where later information changes the choice.

The practical toolkit combines several techniques. Value analysis logically simplifies product and process designs to cut cost and time. Linear programming uses mathematical methods, often represented as the best point inside a polygon of feasible solutions, to minimise or maximise an objective such as production cost. Critical path planning sequences labour, material, and capital so project duration and cost are controlled. Risk, uncertainty, and sensitivity analysis test how conclusions shift when assumptions move.

Interest and money-time relationships govern every comparison. Interest equals principal multiplied by time multiplied by rate; compound interest and annuities extend this to repeated payments. Engineers read factors from compound interest tables using three inputs, the time period, the MARR, and the capital value, and multiply the capital by the table factor to obtain present or future worth. For example, $5,000 borrowed for 5 years at 7% grows to $5,000 × 1.403 = $7,015 in a single payment at the end. A machine yielding $2,500 per year for 8 years at a 5% MARR has a present worth of $2,500 × 6.463 = $16,157.50 and a future worth of $2,500 × 9.549 = $23,872.50. Real projects must add costs, taxes, and inflation to these factors.

Assets lose value through three channels: normal depreciation from physical wear or obsolescence, price depreciation from market shifts, and depletion from exhausting a finite resource. Depreciation matters for two reasons: it estimates the recovery capital tied up in equipment, and it lets firms charge a non-cash cost against profits for tax purposes. Common calculation methods include straight line, declining balance, sum-of-the-years digits, and service output; in the United States, tax depreciation follows MACRS, which assigns each asset class a lifespan that fixes the annual deductible amount but does not force disposal when the schedule ends.

Cost minimisation drives many engineering-economic calculations. A representative total-cost model is C = ax + b/x + k, where x is the number of units produced and a, b, k are constants; this shape captures the trade-off between fixed and variable costs and lets the analyst find the production level that minimises C. Inflation, taxes, tax credits, accounting conventions, cost estimation accuracy, and the financing mix between equity capital (retained profits) and debt capital (borrowed funds that must be returned with interest) all feed back into the cash-flow comparison. In the United States, engineering economics is a required civil-engineering course in many undergraduate programs and appears on the Fundamentals of Engineering and the Principles and Practice of Engineering examinations, both part of the Professional Engineering registration process. In manufacturing-heavy economies, the discipline also shapes industrial economics, covering the growth and profitability of engineering firms, macro-level engineering trends, product-market demand, and the financing of new technologies.
