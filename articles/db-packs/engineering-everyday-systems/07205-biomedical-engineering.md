# Biomedical engineering

Biomedical engineering (BME), also called medical engineering, applies engineering principles and design to medicine and biology for healthcare, including diagnosis, monitoring, and therapy. BME grew out of cross-disciplinary work between established fields and has only recently been recognized as a discipline in its own right. Day-to-day work spans research, development, procurement, routine testing, and preventive maintenance of medical equipment, a role often filled by a Biomedical Equipment Technician (BMET) or clinical engineer.

Two constraints shape every BME problem. The device must work with biology rather than against it, so materials must be biocompatible, the body's response to an implant, and it must be safe enough to place inside a person. Everything that distinguishes biomedical engineering, from biomaterials to heavy regulation, follows from these two constraints.

## Subfields

BME is a cluster of overlapping specialties:

- **Bioinformatics** combines computer science, statistics, mathematics, and engineering to analyze biological data, especially in genomics, identifying candidate genes and SNPs, sequence patterns, and population differences.
- **Biomechanics** studies the structure and function of biological systems at every scale, from whole organisms down to cells and organelles, using mechanics. Crash-test data on human surrogates is a typical input.
- **Biomaterials** covers any matter, surface, or construct that interacts with living systems. A roughly fifty-year-old science, it draws on medicine, biology, chemistry, tissue engineering, and materials science to develop substances the body tolerates, such as titanium or silicone implants.
- **Biomedical optics** uses light to image and treat tissue. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) produces high-resolution 3D images of structures like the retina and coronary arteries. Fluorescence and confocal microscopy label specific molecules with dyes to visualize processes. Photodynamic therapy uses light to trigger treatment. Adaptive optics corrects tissue aberrations to push resolution higher.
- **Tissue engineering** aims to build artificial organs. Researchers have grown solid jawbones and tracheas from human stem cells and transplanted laboratory-grown urinary bladders into patients. Bioartificial devices combine synthetic scaffolds with living cells, as in hepatic assist devices using liver cells inside an artificial bioreactor.
- **Genetic engineering** directly manipulates an organism's genes, unlike traditional breeding. Medical applications include synthetic human insulin made by modified bacteria and erythropoietin produced in hamster ovary cells.
- **Neural engineering** applies engineering techniques to neural systems, with active work on cognitive neural prosthetics, implants that read neural signals and use them to operate assistive devices.
- **Pharmaceutical engineering** is the interdisciplinary design of drugs and their delivery, bridging chemical engineering and pharmacy.
- **Rehabilitation engineering** designs assistive technology for people with disabilities across mobility, communication, hearing, vision, and cognition, including walking aids that promote inclusion in everyday life.

## Medical devices, imaging, and implants

A medical device is any healthcare product that does not achieve its effects through chemical or biological means and does not act through metabolism. Devices range from tongue depressors and bedpans (Class I, minimal harm potential) through X-ray machines, infusion pumps, and surgical drapes (Class II, subject to special controls and postmarket surveillance) to replacement heart valves, silicone breast implants, and implantable pacemaker pulse generators (Class III, requiring premarket approval or 510(k) notification).

Imaging is the largest device category, using ultrasound, magnetism, and radiation to reveal structures invisible to the eye. Workhorse technologies include fluoroscopy, MRI, CT, PET, PET-CT, X-ray projection radiography, ultrasound, and optical and electron microscopy. Electromagnetic navigation systems track instruments in real time, such as feeding-tube placement systems that use an external field generator and passive sensors to follow a tube tip through the GI tract.

Implants are manufactured replacements for missing biological structures, distinct from transplants, which use donor tissue. Implant surfaces use biomaterials such as titanium, silicone, or apatite, and some implants contain electronics, as in pacemakers and cochlear implants. Drug-eluting stents and subcutaneous drug-delivery pills are bioactive implants.

## Clinical engineering

Clinical engineering implements and manages medical equipment in hospitals. Clinical engineers train and supervise BMETs, choose and roll out new technology, work with regulators on audits, and advise physicians and administrators, forming a bridge between device designers and end users. Staffing is roughly one engineer per eighty beds, and the role tends toward incremental redesign rather than long-horizon research.

## Regulation

Because devices can injure or kill, medical-device engineering is among the most heavily regulated fields. In the US, the FDA oversees devices, drugs, biologics, and combination products, with safety and effectiveness enforced through a quality system covering the entire device lifecycle. The EU uses a Conformity Assessment under Medical Device Regulation (EU) 2017/745; conformity for most classes is verified by accredited Notified Bodies, with a manufacturer's self-declaration sufficient for Class I, and a successful assessment allows a device to bear the CE mark. Both regimes converge on the same requirements: every manufactured item must be safe, effective, and consistent, proven through a risk management file, a technical file, and a quality management system built on ISO 13485.

Two other standards shape the work. RoHS 2 (Directive 2011/65/EU) restricts hazardous substances in electronics and extends to medical devices, shifting compliance duty onto importers and distributors. IEC 60601-1-11 (2010) defines safety requirements for electro-medical devices used in home and point-of-care settings.

## Education and careers

BME requires substantial knowledge of both engineering and biology. Entry typically requires a Bachelor's, Master's, or doctoral degree in BME or a related engineering field; an MD-PhD is also a recognized path. As of 2023, 155 undergraduate BME programs in the US are ABET-accredited, and the first Canadian undergraduate BME program was at the University of Guelph. Graduate training is effectively required for most research and industry positions. Outside the US, programs are growing in Canada, Australia, and Europe, with initiatives like BIOMEDEA working toward uniform standards. In the UK, Chartered Engineer status is available through the Institution of Mechanical Engineers and accreditation through the Institute of Physics and Engineering in Medicine. Australia regulates but typically only recommends registration.

The US field employed about 19,400 biomedical engineers in 2012 with 5% projected growth through 2022, and as of 2023 around 19,700 positions at an average pay of roughly $100,730, with a projected 7% job growth from 2023 to 2033. BME has the highest share of female engineers among common engineering professions.
