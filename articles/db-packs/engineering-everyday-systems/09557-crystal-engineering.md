# Crystal engineering

Crystal engineering is the design and synthesis of solid-state structures whose bulk properties are tuned by controlling how molecules pack together. It sits at the boundary of solid-state chemistry and supramolecular chemistry, the latter concerned with how molecules associate through non-covalent forces rather than full chemical bonds. The central idea is straightforward: if the arrangement of molecules in a crystal dictates its properties, then deliberately engineering that arrangement yields solids with predictable behaviour. The defining tools are weak, directional intermolecular interactions, especially hydrogen bonds (attractions between a hydrogen atom on one molecule and an electronegative atom on another), halogen bonds (analogous attractions involving iodine, bromine, or chlorine), and coordination bonds between metals and ligands.

## Origins and definition

The phrase "crystal engineering" was first used by R. Pepinsky in 1955, but the field's practical starting point is usually credited to Gerhard Schmidt, who studied photodimerization in crystalline cinnamic acids. The modern working definition comes from Gautam Desiraju, who in 1988 described crystal engineering as the understanding of intermolecular interactions in the context of crystal packing and the use of that understanding to design new solids with desired physical and chemical properties.

## Supramolecular synthons and building units

A supramolecular synthon is a recurring pair of molecules held together by a characteristic set of intermolecular interactions, useful as a design element because the same synthon appears across many different crystal structures. The same logic extends to secondary building units, larger metal–ligand clusters that propagate into extended coordination polymers. Early work relied almost entirely on organic hydrogen-bond networks; halogen bonds and metal–ligand coordination have since broadened the toolkit.

## Multi-component and pharmaceutical cocrystals

A cocrystal is a single crystalline solid containing two or more different molecules in a defined ratio. Pharmaceutical cocrystals pair an active pharmaceutical ingredient with a coformer deemed safe under World Health Organization guidelines. Adjusting which coformer is used lets chemists tune solubility, bioavailability, and permeability without changing the drug molecule itself.

## Polymorphism

Polymorphism is the existence of more than one crystal structure for the same compound. It matters commercially because distinct polymorphs can be patented separately, and a late-appearing polymorph can disrupt a drug's supply. The phenomenon reflects a contest between kinetics, where fast, long-range strong interactions trap a metastable form, and thermodynamics, where close molecular packing yields the most stable form. In organic molecules, three flavours dominate: packing polymorphism, conformational polymorphism, and the rarer synthon polymorphism. Designing a synthesis means choosing conditions that favour the desired member of this polymorphic family.

## Crystal structure prediction

Crystal structure prediction (CSP) asks whether, given only a molecular diagram, one can compute the most likely crystal forms in advance. The challenge is that many observed crystals are kinetic, not thermodynamic, products. A 2007 hybrid protocol, in which a tailor-made force field ranks candidate structures and dispersion-corrected density functional theory refines their lattice energies, marked a step change, as demonstrated in blind tests organised by the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre since 2002. Modern CSP also produces computed energy landscapes, showing that for many molecules several structures lie within a few kilojoules per mole of each other, a fact that explains why polymorphism is so common.

## From structure to properties

The same interactions that govern packing also govern function. Strong hydrogen bonds form load-bearing columns or layers, while weaker van der Waals contacts form slip planes. In acetaminophen, hydrogen-bonded chains stack through weaker contacts; those weak contacts define where the crystal will shear. Defects, including vacancies, dislocations, and grain boundaries, alter how a crystal responds to stress, so mechanical properties such as hardness, elasticity, plasticity, brittleness, and shear strength can be engineered by controlling the defect population. Nanoindentation is the standard way to quantify these properties, while energy frameworks visualise the slip-plane topology. Optical properties follow the same logic: non-linear optical materials with second harmonic generation are designed by aligning chromophores through hydrogen-bonded synthons.

## Characterisation

X-ray diffraction gives atomic positions from the diffraction pattern produced when X-rays scatter off a crystal's lattice. Microscopy reveals dislocations and grain boundaries. Calorimetric methods such as differential scanning calorimetry track the enthalpy and entropy changes of melting and fusion, from which lattice energy and percent crystallinity are extracted. Raman spectroscopy probes vibrational modes to identify which bonds and intermolecular contacts are present, and Hirshfeld surfaces offer a quantitative fingerprint of intermolecular contacts.
