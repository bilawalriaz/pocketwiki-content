# History of transport

Transport is the movement of people and goods, and its history follows a steady pattern: new technologies let societies move more, farther, and faster, and each shift reorganised trade and political reach. Four broad domains share this pattern: land, water, air, and space.

## Land: from footpaths to railways

Early routes were footpaths worn down by repeated use into sunken tracks called hollow ways. Around 4000 BC, animal-drawn wheeled vehicles probably appeared in the ancient Near East, multiplying the load a single animal could pull. The Romans tied their empire together with engineered road networks. During the Industrial Revolution, John Loudon McAdam's compacted aggregate surface created the modern highway; when motor vehicles arrived in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, ordinary surfaces wore away, producing the first tarmac road in 1902.

Railroads grew from horse-drawn coal wagons on wooden rails. Cast-iron plates replaced wood in the 1760s, and in 1802 Richard Trevithick built the first steam locomotive to run on smooth rails. George Stephenson's locomotives and the 1830 Liverpool and Manchester line, the first modern railroad, carried passengers and freight at a scale horse-drawn systems could not match. Electric and diesel locomotives followed, and by the early 20th century national networks in countries like Britain were consolidated and nationalised.

## Water: boats, canals, and global trade

Boats for fishing and river travel go back to the Stone Age. Galleys propelled by oars dominated Mediterranean warfare and trade from about 3000 BC until ocean-going sailing ships such as the caravel and man-of-war took over. The Industrial Revolution brought steamboats, then diesel power, and major canals (China's Grand Canal in the 7th century, English and American systems before the railway age) moved bulk cargoes inland. Ports that stored and re-exported goods, known as entrepôts, became multicultural hubs linking sea routes to inland caravans and waterways. International trade, especially from 1500 onward, was a primary driver of these advances and produced a worldwide division of labour.

## Air and space: escaping the surface

Human flight began with kites in China before 200 BC. In Europe, the 17th and 18th centuries brought the study of moving air, called aerodynamics, and hydrogen balloons. The first verified human flight was a hot-air balloon over Paris in 1783, and the Wright brothers achieved the first sustained, controlled, powered heavier-than-air flight in 1903. World War II accelerated aviation, and the post-war Jet Age made commercial flight routine. The 21st century emphasis is on unmanned aerial vehicles, or UAVs, aircraft without a human pilot on board.

Space transport rests on liquid-propellant rocket work by Konstantin Tsiolkovsky and Robert H. Goddard. Yuri Gagarin orbited Earth aboard Vostok 1 in 1961, Alan Shepard made the first US suborbital Mercury flight in Freedom 7 on 5 May 1961, and NASA's Apollo 11 crew landed on the Moon in 1969. Salyut and Mir space stations, with early docking by Kosmos 186 and Kosmos 188 in 1967, extended human presence beyond Earth.

## Navigation: finding the way

Seamen steered by landmarks and stars before the 13th century. The magnetic compass, adopted in that century, let navigators plot courses by magnetic north, sail in poor visibility, and take more direct routes, producing detailed portolan charts. The 1761 marine chronometer, a clock accurate enough to fix longitude at sea, completed the toolkit that made long ocean voyages routine.
