# Bearing (mechanical)

A bearing is a machine element that constrains two parts so only the desired relative motion is possible, while reducing the friction that opposes it. The name comes from "to bear": a bearing supports another part.

## Plain and rolling bearings

The simplest bearing is a plain bearing: a shaft spinning inside a hole, usually with a lubricant such as oil, grease, or graphite in the gap. Plain bearings are cheap, common, and tolerate misalignment and dirt.

A rolling-element bearing places balls or cylindrical, tapered, or spherical rollers between two rings called races. Each rolling element contacts the inner and outer race at a point or line, so the parts roll rather than rub. Because the rolling elements also touch each other, a cage, a small spacer holding each one in its own pocket, keeps them apart. Galileo described a caged design in the 17th century; John Harrison used a caged roller bearing in his H3 marine timekeeper in the 1740s. Philip Vaughan patented the first modern ball bearing in 1794, and Friedrich Fischer's 1883 ball-grinding machine produced roundness accurate enough for mass production. Sven Wingquist patented the self-aligning ball bearing at SKF in 1907, and Henry Timken patented the tapered roller bearing in 1898. These designs let industrial machinery run at the speeds and loads the Industrial Revolution required.

Steel-on-steel rolling friction is about 0.005, versus 0.05 to 0.35 for PTFE sliding; seals, grease, and preload (a small built-in load that removes internal slack) can push the real value toward 0.125.

## Non-contact bearings

Fluid and magnetic bearings keep the surfaces apart entirely. A fluid bearing forces gas or liquid through a gap, so the load rides on a pressurized film and the surfaces never touch. A magnetic bearing suspends the rotor in a magnetic field, either permanent, electrodynamic (induced by motion, needing no power), or active (electromagnets, needing continuous power). Both have near-zero friction at zero speed and very high speed limits: fluid bearings are limited by the speed of the sealing surface, magnetic bearings by centripetal force approaching the rotor's material strength. Fluid bearings can fail quickly if grit breaches the seal. Flexure bearings, in which a solid element bends elastically, are used in optical mounts and precision stages where backlash-free, smooth motion matters more than long travel.

## Classification by motion and load

By motion: radial rotation (a shaft in a hole), linear motion (a drawer slide), spherical rotation (a ball-and-socket joint), or hinge motion (a door or knee).

By load: radial (perpendicular to the axis), axial or thrust (along the axis), or bending moments about a perpendicular axis.

## Materials

Early bearings were wood, then bronze. Modern rolling-element bearings are usually through-hardened chrome steel (SAE 52100, also written 100Cr6) for general use, with case-hardened steel (SAE 4118) where a tough core matters. Stainless steel such as AISI 440C adds corrosion resistance at the cost of load capacity and fatigue life. Ceramics, especially silicon nitride, are lighter, run faster, survive higher temperatures, and resist corrosion, but they cost more, carry less load, and can crack from thermal shock. Soft white metals (Babbitt) line plain bearings because they embed grit and conform to small misalignments, so the shaft wears the bearing rather than the other way around. Plastics such as PTFE, nylon, and acetal are cheap, absorb vibration, and can run dry. Watch movements use jewel bearings, sapphire surfaces against metal, to reduce friction at very small scale.

## Lubrication

Lubrication is what lets a plain bearing survive. Splash lubrication flings oil from a partial pool onto bearing surfaces. Grease, a thick oil held by a soap thickener, is packed into the bearing and retained by rubber or plastic seals, so a "sealed for life" bearing needs no further attention. A ring oiler is a loose metal ring dipping into an oil bath; as the shaft turns, viscous adhesion drags oil up onto the shaft, and gravity returns the surplus. Large or fast machines use pressurised oil pumped through drilled passages, the same approach that keeps turbocharger bearings cool. Composite bearings wrap a thin PTFE liner around a metal backing, so the PTFE acts as its own lubricant for up to about 30 years without maintenance, at roughly one-tenth the weight of an equivalent rolling-element bearing.

## Performance trade-offs

Bearing speed is usually quoted as DN, the mean bearing diameter in millimetres times rotation rate in revolutions per minute. Plain bearings handle the lowest speeds, then rolling-element bearings, then fluid bearings, then magnetic bearings.

Rolling-element bearings carry higher loads for a given size than plain bearings; among rolling types, tapered roller bearings carry the largest combined radial and axial loads.

Stiffness is the distance the bearing deflects when the load changes, and is distinct from friction. A ball under load flattens slightly where it touches the race, so the bearing is not perfectly rigid; a correctly loaded fluid bearing is usually stiffer than a rolling bearing of the same size.

Play, also called slop, is the intentional gap between shaft and bore. A wheelbarrow tolerates several millimetres of play; a lathe positioning a tool must hold a ball lead screw to within 0.002 mm, so its bearings are preloaded and run with almost no play.

## Life and failure

Bearing life is statistical. Nominally identical bearings fail across a bell curve of lifetimes because microscopic steel structure and contamination differ part to part. The industry standard is L10 (also written B10) life: the running time at which 10 percent of a population has failed by classical fatigue. A separate rating, the static load rating C0, is a reference load, not a service interval.

Life depends on factors outside the bearing itself: mounting, temperature, lubricant cleanliness, environment, and stray electrical currents. Variable-frequency drives, motor controllers that switch rapidly on and off, can induce currents through a bearing; ferrite chokes on the motor cables suppress them. Storing a bearing where it vibrates, even under only its own weight, can break down the lubricant and cause false brinelling, wear marks that appear without any applied load. Fluid and magnetic bearings have no contact, so hydro-plant guide bearings have been in near-continuous service since around 1900 without measurable wear. Some bearings are designed to last only briefly: a documented rocket-motor oxygen-pump bearing ran for several hours when the mission needed only tens of minutes.
