# Simple machine

A simple machine is a basic mechanical device that changes the direction or magnitude of an applied force. It uses **mechanical advantage** to multiply the force a person applies, at the cost of distance moved. Simple machines are the fundamental building blocks of all more complex machines, from scissors to car engines.

## The Six Classical Types

Renaissance scholars formalised six classical simple machines, building on earlier work by Archimedes (c. 3rd century BC), who studied the lever, pulley, and screw, and by Heron of Alexandria (c. 10–75 AD), who described five of the six. The six are:

- **Lever** — a rigid bar rotating about a fixed pivot called a **fulcrum**.
- **Wheel and axle** — a larger wheel rigidly attached to a smaller cylinder, rotating together.
- **Pulley** — a wheel with a grooved rim carrying a rope or cable.
- **Inclined plane** — a flat surface tilted at an angle to the horizontal.
- **Wedge** — a portable inclined plane, typically driven into material to split or lift it.
- **Screw** — an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder; turning it converts rotation into linear motion.

Later work refined the theory: Simon Stevin (1586) derived the inclined plane's mechanical advantage, Galileo (1600) completed the dynamic theory in *Le Mecaniche*, and Amontons (1699) and Coulomb (1785) developed friction theory.

## Mechanical Advantage

Mechanical advantage (MA) is the ratio of output force to input force:

$$ MA = \frac{F_{out}}{F_{in}} $$

An **ideal** simple machine loses no energy to friction or deformation, so input power equals output power. Because power equals force times velocity, the ideal mechanical advantage also equals the **velocity ratio** — input velocity divided by output velocity. A longer lever arm, for example, gives a higher MA: it multiplies the output force, but the output end moves a proportionally shorter distance. The trade-off is fixed by geometry.

## Friction and Efficiency

Real machines lose energy to friction, which dissipates power as heat. **Efficiency** (η) measures how much input power becomes useful output:

$$ \eta = \frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}} $$

Efficiency is always between 0 and 1. Friction reduces the actual mechanical advantage:

$$ MA = \eta \times MA_{ideal} $$

A lever with an ideal MA of 10 and 80% efficiency delivers an effective MA of 8.

## Compound Machines

A **compound machine** joins two or more simple machines in series, so the output force of one becomes the input of the next. A bench vise combines a lever and a screw; gear trains connect wheels. The total mechanical advantage is the product of the individual advantages:

$$ MA_{compound} = MA_1 \times MA_2 \times \dots \times MA_N $$

The same rule applies to efficiency, so losses compound at every stage.

## Self-Locking Machines

Some machines are **self-locking**: friction is high enough that the load cannot push them backwards. This occurs in screws, inclined planes, and wedges when the coefficient of static friction and the geometry prevent **overhauling** — the backward motion that happens in a reversible machine when the load exceeds the input force. A machine is self-locking if its efficiency is below 50%, meaning friction absorbs more than half the input energy. Most levers and pulleys are reversible and will run backwards if the load is heavy enough.

## Modern Machine Theory

Modern mechanics treats machines as **kinematic chains** — systems of rigid links connected by joints called **kinematic pairs** that transmit forces and motion. Hinges are revolute joints (used in levers, wheels, pulleys); sliding surfaces form sliding joints (inclined planes, wedges); screws use helical joints. In the late 1800s, Franz Reuleaux classified over 800 machine elements and argued that the six classical categories oversimplify reality: levers, pulleys, and wheels share rotational principles, while inclined planes, wedges, and screws share sliding mechanics. **Kinematic synthesis** uses this framework to design linkages, cams, and gears that produce a desired motion or force.
