# Aerospace engineering

Aerospace engineering is the branch of engineering that designs, builds, and tests aircraft and spacecraft. The term "aerospace" was defined in 1958 to treat the atmosphere and outer space as a single realm, replacing the earlier term "aeronautical engineering" once flight technology extended beyond the air. The field splits into two overlapping branches: aeronautical engineering, focused on vehicles within the atmosphere, and astronautical engineering, focused on vehicles in space. Avionics engineering sits beside them, handling onboard electronic systems.

Flight vehicles must survive large swings in atmospheric pressure and temperature while carrying structural loads, so they depend on aerodynamics (the flow of air around bodies), propulsion, materials science, structural analysis, avionics, and manufacturing. Each discipline is specialised, so aerospace projects are carried out by large teams rather than individual engineers.

## Historical development

The intellectual roots reach back to Sir George Cayley in the late 18th and mid-19th centuries, the first person to separate lift from drag and to identify the four forces of flight: thrust, lift, drag, and weight. The Wright Brothers performed the first sustained, controlled, powered, heavier-than-air flight in 1903, lasting 12 seconds. World War I drove the systematic design of military aircraft, and Robert Goddard patented multi-stage rockets using solid and liquid fuel in 1914, ideas later central to space launch.

Governments then began organising the work. The U.S. Congress created the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA) in 1915, the first government-sponsored aviation research body, which later became NASA. Between the world wars, civil aviation expanded and the first operational jet-powered aircraft, the Messerschmitt Me 262, entered service in 1944. The Cold War accelerated the astronautics branch after the USSR launched Sputnik in 1957 and the United States responded with its first satellite in 1958; NASA was founded the same year. Apollo 11 landed on the Moon in 1969, with Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin on the surface and Michael Collins in lunar orbit.

In civil aviation, the Boeing 747 entered service in 1970 as the first "jumbo jet," carrying up to 480 passengers. The Concorde became the first passenger supersonic aircraft in 1976. The Antonov An-225 Mriya, which first flew in 1988, remains the heaviest aircraft ever built. The Airbus A380 entered service in 2007 as the first passenger plane to exceed the 747's capacity, carrying up to 853.

## Core technical elements

The discipline rests on a small set of foundations:

- Aerodynamics and fluid mechanics: the flow of air over wings, through engines, and around wind-tunnel models. Computational fluid dynamics now supplements physical testing.
- Astrodynamics: orbital mechanics, including prediction of orbital elements from a few variables, typically taught at graduate level.
- Propulsion: internal-combustion and jet engines for atmospheric flight, rockets for space, and electric or ion propulsion for efficiency in orbit.
- Structures and materials science: lightweight airframes that survive aerodynamic, thermal, and mechanical loads, aided by finite-element tools such as MSC Patran/Nastran.
- Aeroelasticity: the interaction of aerodynamic forces with structural flexibility, which can cause flutter, a rapid self-amplifying oscillation that destroys the airframe.
- Control engineering: mathematical modelling of dynamic behaviour with feedback, applied to aircraft, spacecraft, and engines to keep them stable.
- Avionics and software: onboard computers, flight software, and ground control systems.

These elements were once tested empirically using scale models in wind tunnels; computing now allows much of the behaviour to be simulated before hardware is built. Aerospace engineering also addresses the integration of subsystems such as power, thermal control, life support, and communications across a vehicle's full life cycle, from design through radiation, temperature, and lifetime limits.

## Education and the "rocket science" label

Aerospace engineering is taught at advanced diploma, bachelor's, master's, and doctoral levels, requiring a strong background in physics, chemistry, computer science, and mathematics. A few universities separate aeronautical and astronautical tracks.

The popular phrase "rocket science" is a misnomer. Aerospace engineers are not scientists, and most do not work on rockets; "rocket engineering" would be etymologically correct. The label persists as a synonym for extreme technical difficulty, which is why "It's not rocket science" means a task is straightforward.
