# Ecological engineering

Ecological engineering uses ecology and engineering to predict, design, build, restore, and manage ecosystems that integrate human society with the natural environment for the benefit of both. The field emerged in the early 1960s through the work of Howard Odum, who used natural energy sources as the main input to manipulate and control environmental systems, and who built ecological models to capture whole-system patterns of energy and material flow.

Mitsch and Jorgensen defined the field by five concepts: designs rely on the self-designing capacity of ecosystems, the work tests ecological theory, it uses systems approaches, it conserves non-renewable energy, and it supports biological conservation. Mitsch and Jorgensen also first defined ecological engineering as designing societal services so that they benefit society and nature, with design that is systems-based, sustainable, and integrates society with its natural environment. Bergen and colleagues added that the work draws on ecological science, applies to all ecosystem types, uses engineering design methods, and acknowledges a guiding value system. Barrett described the practice more literally as the design, construction, operation, and management of landscape and aquatic structures together with their plant and animal communities, to benefit humanity and often nature. Related terms include ecotechnology, soil bioengineering, and biotechnical engineering, but ecological engineering is distinct from cellular-level biotechnology and from bioengineering of artificial body parts.

Applications are grouped by spatial scale. Mesocosms span roughly 0.1 to a few hundred meters. Ecosystems span about one to tens of kilometers. Regional systems exceed tens of kilometers, and design complexity tends to grow with scale. Typical projects include restoring degraded wetlands, building multi-celled tubs and greenhouses that use microbial, fish, and plant communities to turn human wastewater into fertilizer, flowers, and drinking water, urban stormwater management done with landscape architects and urban planners, rural wetland treatment, and community reforestation guided by traditional ecological knowledge. Permaculture, shaped in part by David Holmgren's reading of Howard Odum, grew out of the same roots.

Design combines systems ecology with engineering design. Engineering design follows problem formulation, problem analysis, alternative solutions, decision, and specification. Matlock and colleagues added that solutions must be considered in ecological time and that ecological economics should enter design evaluation, with biological conservation as a guiding value. Odum's energy systems language, also called energy circuit language, is one way to model how energy and material move across system boundaries, which lets designers use renewable resources and raise sustainability. Mitsch and Jorgensen grouped designs into five functional classes: ecosystems that reduce pollution, imitated ecosystems that solve resource problems, ecosystems recovered after disturbance, ecosystems modified in ecologically sound ways, and ecosystems used for benefit without losing balance. Examples include phytoremediation, forest restoration, mine-land repair, selective timber harvest, biomanipulation, sustainable agro-ecosystems, and multi-layered agroforestry. They also listed nineteen design principles, with central ideas that ecosystems are self-designing, open, dissipative, hierarchical, edged, and shaped by forcing functions, that ecotones act as membranes between ecosystems, that coupling between ecosystems is useful, and that design should account for characteristic time and space scales and for the information stored in ecosystem structure. Before implementation, designers build a conceptual model, run a computer simulation of impacts and uncertainty, and then optimize the design.

Ecological engineering overlaps with environmental engineering and civil engineering in water resources, especially stormwater and wastewater. The fields differ in focus: ecological engineering works with natural infrastructure and the relationship between people and environment, civil engineering with built infrastructure and public works, and environmental engineering with public and environmental health through waste treatment. The first B.S. program in ecological engineering was formalized in 2009 at Oregon State University, and in 2024 ABET published accreditation criteria for the first time. Accredited programs cover mathematics through differential equations, probability and statistics, calculus-based physics, college-level chemistry, earth science, fluid mechanics, hydraulics, hydrology, biological and advanced ecological sciences, systems ecology, ecosystem services, ecological modeling, material and energy balances, fate and transport, thermodynamics of living systems, and design that accounts for climate, species diversity, self-organization, uncertainty, sustainability, resilience, and interactions between ecological and social systems.
