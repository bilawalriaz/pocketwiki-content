# Machine

A machine is a thermodynamic system that uses power to apply forces and control motion in order to do work. The word comes from Greek *mekhane* ("contrivance"), via Latin *machina*; the modern technical sense was fixed by John Harris in *Lexicon Technicum* (1704). The near-synonym *engine* derives from Latin *ingenium*, "ingenuity." The same idea scales from a hand axe to a steam turbine to a ribosome.

## The six simple machines

Every complex mechanism is built from a small set of elementary force-transformers. The classic six are:

- **Lever** — a rigid bar rotating about a pivot, trading distance for force.
- **Pulley** — a grooved wheel that redirects a rope, changing force direction and, when combined, multiplying force.
- **Wheel and axle** — a large wheel fixed to a smaller axle, so a force on the wheel produces a larger force on the axle.
- **Wedge** — two inclined planes joined back-to-back; a small push along its length yields a large push sideways. The stone hand axe, a wedge, is the earliest known force-transforming tool.
- **Inclined plane** — a sloping surface that lets a load be raised gradually rather than vertically.
- **Screw** — an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder, converting rotational motion into linear force.

**Mechanical advantage**, the ratio of output force to input force, is the property that links them. Archimedes (c. 3rd century BC) formalised this for the lever, pulley, and screw, though his analysis was confined to statics. Hero of Alexandria later listed five mechanisms that set loads in motion: lever, windlass, pulley, wedge, and screw.

## From simple machines to powered systems

A simple machine only redirects or multiplies the force a person or animal supplies. A powered machine adds an independent energy source and a controller, turning fuel, electricity, wind, or steam into sustained output.

Three shifts made this possible:

1. **Dynamics replaces statics.** Galileo, around 1600, established that machines do not create energy; they transform it. Leonardo da Vinci observed friction during the Renaissance, later quantified by Amontons and Coulomb.
2. **Programmable control appears.** In the 9th century the Islamic world built windmills and programmable automata; in 1551 Taqi ad-Din described a steam-powered roasting jack in Ottoman Egypt. James Watt's 1782 parallel-motion linkage allowed double-acting steam engines to drive locomotives, ships, and factories.
3. **Mechanised manufacturing spreads.** Between roughly 1750 and 1850 the Industrial Revolution replaced hand production with machine-based production in British textiles, iron, and coal.

## How a modern machine is organised

A modern machine is a layered system. At the top sit power sources: human or animal effort, wind and water wheels, external-combustion steam engines, internal-combustion and jet engines, steam turbines in fossil-fuel or nuclear power plants, electric motors, hydraulic and pneumatic systems, batteries, and solar cells. Their energy flows to **actuators** that convert stored energy into motion, and then to a **mechanism**, an assembly of machine elements such as frames, bearings, springs, gears, cams, linkages, and fasteners. A **controller** closes the loop, reading sensors and adjusting output; the flyball governor that regulates steam-engine speed and the programmable logic controller in a factory are two points on the same continuum.

A **joint** is any connection that permits movement (a hinge is *revolute*, a slider is *prismatic*). A **kinematic chain** is a sequence of such links. The number of independent motions a mechanism can perform is its **degrees of freedom**, and the related quantity **mobility** can be computed from the Chebychev–Grübler–Kutzbach criterion. A **cam and follower** uses a shaped surface to drive another part; a **flexure mechanism** uses the elastic bending of a material to produce precise, friction-free motion.

## Molecular and computing machines

The same blueprint reappears at smaller scales. Biological molecules such as myosin, kinesin, and dynein act as molecular motors, driving muscle contraction, intracellular transport, and the beating of cilia. ATP synthase and the ribosome are nanomachines that make ATP and assemble proteins. DNA-based nanostructures can now mimic mechanical linkages, so engineered components operate in the same regime as natural ones. Charles Babbage's 19th-century designs for mechanical calculators pointed the way toward computing machines, while Alan Turing's abstract machine extended the term to purely mathematical objects; whether a Turing machine counts as a machine in the engineering sense is still debated.

## Open questions

The traditional list of six simple machines is conventional rather than settled. Some scholars argue for joint-based categories rather than elemental devices, and the overlap between machines and living organisms grows closer as molecular biology reveals biological nanomachines that rival engineered ones in complexity.
