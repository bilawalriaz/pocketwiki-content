# Acoustical engineering

Acoustical engineering is the branch of engineering that applies acoustics, the science of sound and vibration, to design, analyse, and control sound. Its work divides into two aims: reducing unwanted sound, and using sound deliberately for useful ends.

## Noise control

Unwanted noise harms animal and human health, lowers school attainment, and causes hearing loss. Engineers attack it at three points: redesigning the source so it makes less sound, blocking the path with barriers, absorbers, suppressors, or buffer zones, and protecting the listener with earmuffs or earplugs. Working at the source is generally the most cost-effective option. In vehicles the same problem is known as noise, vibration, and harshness (NVH), tackled with vibration isolation, absorbent materials, and acoustic enclosures. Engineers also shape the sound a product makes, for example tuning the click of a car door, rather than only silencing it.

## Constructive uses

The other aim covers positive uses of sound: ultrasound imaging in medicine, programming digital synthesizers, designing concert halls to enhance orchestral sound, and specifying station announcement systems so speech remains intelligible.

## The profession

Acoustical engineers usually hold a bachelor's degree in acoustics, physics, or another engineering discipline, with strong scientific and mathematical content; some research and faculty roles require a PhD. In many Commonwealth countries a certified degree plus further requirements leads to the title Chartered Engineer. Practitioners work in acoustic consultancy, in fields such as architectural acoustics, environmental noise, or vibration control, or in industry, designing car audio, studying how people respond to urban soundscapes and domestic appliances, writing audio signal processing for mixing desks, or designing microphones and loudspeakers for mobile phones.

## Subdisciplines

- **Aeroacoustics** — noise generated by moving air through turbulence; central to aircraft, wind turbines, and wind instruments.
- **Audio signal processing** — electronic manipulation of audio signals, analog and digital, to enhance sound (for example by adding reverberation), remove unwanted noise such as echo in internet calls, compress signals using perceptual coding (MP3, Opus), or identify music tracks.
- **Architectural acoustics** — achieving good sound inside a building: speech intelligibility in theatres, restaurants, and stations, enhanced music in concert halls and studios, and suppressed noise in offices and homes; the work is usually done by acoustic consultants.
- **Bioacoustics** — sound production and hearing in animals, including communication, behaviour, evolution, neurophysiology, population monitoring, and the impact of man-made noise.
- **Electroacoustics** — design of headphones, microphones, loudspeakers, sound systems, and recording; the spread of portable playback devices has driven rapid growth in this area. The term also names electrokinetic effects in liquids under ultrasound.
- **Environmental noise** — control of traffic, aircraft, industrial, and recreational noise. Engineers measure or predict levels, set acceptable limits, and design controls. Recent work emphasises soundscapes, the positive use of sound such as fountains and bird song, and the preservation of tranquility.
- **Musical acoustics** — the physics and perception of music, covering instrument design including electronic synthesizers, the human voice, computer analysis and composition, music therapy, and the perception and cognition of music.
- **Psychoacoustics** — how humans respond to what they hear, from annoying noise to beautiful music. A human listener is usually the final judge of whether a design works, for example whether sound localisation succeeds in a surround-sound system.
- **Speech** — production, processing, and perception of speech, drawing on physics, physiology, psychology, audio signal processing, and linguistics. Applications include speech recognition, speech synthesis, and transmitting speech intelligibly in rooms, through public address systems, and over telephones.
- **Ultrasonics** — sound at frequencies above human hearing, used in medical ultrasonography, sonochemistry, nondestructive testing, material characterisation, and underwater acoustics (sonar).
- **Underwater acoustics** — sound in water, both natural and man-made: sonar to locate submarines, animal communication, observation of sea temperatures for climate monitoring, and marine biology.
- **Vibration and dynamics** — measurement, analysis, and control of mechanical motion and its interaction with the environment, including railway and construction ground vibrations, isolating recording studios, the effects of vibration on humans, protecting bridges from earthquakes, and modelling structure-borne sound through buildings.

## Fundamental science

A handful of ideal wave behaviours underlie all of this work. Absorption is energy loss when a sound wave meets a surface, either transmitted through or dissipated by the material. Reverberation is the persistence of sound from repeated boundary reflections after the source stops, especially important in enclosed spaces. Diffraction is the bending of sound around obstacles. Refraction is bending caused by changes in the medium, such as temperature gradients in air. Engineers combine these behaviours with mathematical analysis to control sound across every application above.
