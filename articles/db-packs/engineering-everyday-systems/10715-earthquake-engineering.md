# Earthquake engineering

Earthquake engineering is the interdisciplinary branch of engineering that designs buildings, bridges, and other structures to resist earthquakes. Its goal is not to make them indestructible but to keep them standing long enough to protect lives and limit damage to socially and economically acceptable levels. A well-designed structure survives minor shaking undamaged, survives a major event without collapse even if heavily damaged, and remains operational through more frequent, smaller shaking.

The working model rests on four properties a structure must combine: strength, resistance to applied forces; stiffness, resistance to deformation; ductility, ability to deform without breaking; and configuration, the geometric arrangement of members and loads. Getting these right lets the building absorb and dissipate earthquake energy without losing its gravity-load path, the vertical chain of beams, columns, and walls that carries the building's weight to the foundation.

## How earthquakes load structures

Seismic loading is the force an earthquake's ground motion applies to a structure, entering through its base, through contact with neighboring buildings, or along coasts through tsunami waves. Engineers estimate the expected loading at a site using engineering seismology, which links the local seismic hazard to the ground motion the design must resist. A structure's seismic performance is then judged against two standards: safety, meaning it does not endanger occupants by partially or fully collapsing, and serviceability, meaning it can still fulfill its intended function afterward.

## Measuring and predicting performance

Engineers assess expected performance in two ways. Experimental assessment places a scaled or full-scale model on a shake-table, a platform that reproduces recorded ground motions, and observes the damage. These tests are costly and mostly serve to validate computational models. Analytical or numerical assessment uses structural dynamics: the response spectrum method for elastic behavior, and step-by-step time-history integration or nonlinear static pushover analysis once damage pushes the structure past its elastic range. Common software includes SAP2000, PERFORM-3D, ABAQUS, ANSYS, and the research-oriented OpenSees.

In the United States, the National Science Foundation funds fundamental research and runs the George E. Brown Jr. Network for Earthquake Engineering Simulation, a system of shared laboratories for shake-table tests, tsunami wave-basin experiments, and field research. Japan's E-Defense shake table is the largest single facility in the world.

## Controlling earthquake energy

Once shaking enters a building, engineers apply three basic strategies: dissipate the wave energy inside the structure with dampers, disperse it across a wider frequency range, or absorb resonant oscillations with a mass damper tuned to the building's natural frequency. Taipei 101 uses a 660-tonne steel pendulum suspended between the 88th and 92nd floors to counteract typhoon and earthquake swaying. Tokyo Skytree uses a similar tuned mass damper.

A fundamentally different approach is base isolation, in which flexible pads under the building's main load-bearing elements decouple the superstructure from the shaking ground. The principle is ancient: the Mausoleum of Cyrus at Pasargadae, in 6th-century-BCE Persia, is the oldest known base-isolated structure, and the Inca dry-stone walls at Machu Picchu survive earthquakes because their unmortared blocks can shift and resettle. Modern variants include the lead rubber bearing, invented by New Zealander Bill Robinson, which combines rubber and a lead core for flexibility and damping, and friction pendulum bearings, which slide on a concave surface.

## Seismic design

Seismic design provides a structure with the strength, stiffness, ductility, and configuration to resist foreseeable failure modes. The field has shifted from force-based to displacement-based assessment, comparing the elastic displacement a ground motion demands with the inelastic displacement the structure can actually deliver, while guaranteeing ductility at every potential plastic hinge, a location where the member is designed to bend and absorb energy. For critical infrastructure such as nuclear plants, criteria are far more stringent, and the 2011 Tohoku earthquake prompted governments worldwide to re-evaluate their nuclear seismic standards after the Fukushima accidents.

Designers catalog and learn from typical failure modes: unreinforced masonry cracking from poor mortar and missing wall-to-roof ties; soft-story collapse when the ground floor lacks stiffness; soil liquefaction, in which saturated loose sand loses strength and buildings tilt or sink, as in 1964 Niigata; landslides triggered by shaking; pounding between adjacent buildings with different vibration periods; columns bursting when shear reinforcement is inadequate; and lateral spreading, where an unliquefied surface layer slides over a liquefied subsurface layer.

## Earthquake-resistant construction by material

Different materials pose different problems. Adobe, used by roughly thirty percent of the world's population, is highly vulnerable; improved performance comes from quality construction, a compact box-shaped layout, and reinforcement. Unreinforced limestone and sandstone are heavy and brittle, though modern base isolation has retrofitted historic landmarks such as the Salt Lake City and County Building. Light timber frames resist shaking through plywood shear walls and wood-panel diaphragms, provided connectors and fasteners are properly detailed.

Reinforced masonry became mandatory in California after the 1933 Long Beach earthquake exposed unreinforced masonry's fragility. Reinforced concrete frames must have ductile joints, and prestressed concrete, in which high-strength tendons pre-compress the member to counteract its weakness in tension, allows longer spans in beams, floors, and bridges. Steel frames are generally ductile but suffered unexpected brittle fractures in welded moment-resisting connections during the 1994 Northridge earthquake, prompting the AISC 358 standard requiring pre-qualified, cyclically tested connections.

## Estimating losses

Earthquake loss estimation expresses damage as a damage ratio, the repair cost divided by the building's replacement value, often called the probable maximum loss. Standardized since ASTM E2026 in 1999, seismic risk assessments combine the probability of various ground motions with the building's vulnerability under them, producing a loss expressed as a percentage of replacement value and used by insurers, owners, and governments to set retrofit priorities.
