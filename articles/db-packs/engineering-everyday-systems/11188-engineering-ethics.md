# Engineering ethics

Engineering ethics is the system of moral principles governing the practice of engineering. It defines the obligations of engineers to society, to clients, and to the profession, and it overlaps with business ethics, the philosophy of science, and the ethics of technology.

## Origins

Engineering became a distinct profession in the 19th century, and engineers split into two camps: independent professionals and technical employees of large industrial firms. In the United States, four founding societies crystallized this divide: the American Society of Civil Engineers (ASCE, 1851), the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME, 1880), the American Institute of Electrical Engineers (AIEE, 1884), and the American Institute of Mining Engineers (AIME, 1871). ASCE and AIEE represented the learned-professional identity; ASME leaned toward the technical-employee view; AIME aligned almost entirely with it. Ethics at this point was treated as a personal matter, not a professional one.

A series of structural failures forced the profession to confront both technical and ethical shortcomings. The Ashtabula River Railroad Disaster (1876), the Tay Bridge collapse in Scotland (1879, at least sixty dead), the Quebec Bridge collapse (1907), and the Boston Molasses Disaster (1919) pushed three of the four societies to adopt formal codes of ethics: AIEE in 1912, ASCE and ASME in 1914; AIME never did. These events also drove the movement for mandatory licensure. By the 1950s all Canadian provinces required licensing for engineers whose work posed risks to life, health, property, the public welfare, or the environment; US states took a narrower path, generally licensing only engineers offering services directly to the public.

The 1950 Association of German Engineers oath, "The Confession of the Engineers," responded to engineers' complicity in World War II atrocities. The Canadian Iron Ring and the American Order of the Engineer both trace back to the 1907 Quebec Bridge collapse and require members to swear an oath and wear a symbolic ring. In 1946 the National Society of Professional Engineers (NSPE) issued Canons of Ethics, which evolved into the current Code of Ethics adopted in 1964; its Board of Ethical Review, established in 1954, has issued nearly 500 advisory opinions. The 1990 Archimedean Oath, drafted by EPFL students, mirrors the Hippocratic Oath for engineers and technicians.

## General principles

Codes of ethics establish a precedence: the public comes first, then the client, then the employer, and finally the profession. The fundamental canon, shared across nearly every engineering society, is that engineers hold paramount the safety, health, and welfare of the public. ASCE, IEEE, ASME, AIChE, the Institution of Civil Engineers, Professional Engineers Ontario, and the American Nuclear Society all enshrine this hierarchy. ASCE's code adds requirements to act only within one's competence, issue truthful public statements, avoid conflicts of interest, compete fairly, reject bribery and corruption, support continuing professional development, and treat all persons equitably regardless of gender, race, religion, age, disability, or other status.

## Duty to report

An engineer's duty to report a risk created by a client or employer overrides the duty of confidentiality. If overruled by any authority, the engineer must put the objections and the consequences of deviation in writing. Where government fails to act, the engineer can discharge the duty only by going public. Courts have frequently sided with whistleblowing engineers, and engineers may face license revocation even when no one is harmed.

## Broader conduct

Beyond safety, codes address relationships with clients, consultants, competitors, and contractors; legal compliance; conflicts of interest; bribery, kickbacks, gifts, and entertainment; confidential and proprietary information; stewardship of employer assets; and outside employment. Environmental protection is increasingly treated as a standalone ethical question.

## Failures and their lessons

Many engineering failures involve management culture and process as much as technical miscalculation. The Challenger disaster (1986) is a standard case study in whistleblowing and groupthink. The Therac-25 accidents (1985–1987), Bhopal (1984), Chernobyl (1986), Deepwater Horizon (2010), the GM ignition-switch recalls (2014), and the Titan submersible implosion (2023) all combine ethical and technical breakdowns. Not every failure is ethical: the Tacoma Narrows collapse, Mars Polar Lander, and Mars Climate Orbiter were technical and process errors. Not every ethics case is a failure either: Northwestern instructor Sheldon Epstein cited the Holocaust as a breach of engineering ethics precisely because the engineers' work succeeded at its murderous purpose. Lysenkoism, in which political agendas override professional judgment, is a named form of ethical failure.
