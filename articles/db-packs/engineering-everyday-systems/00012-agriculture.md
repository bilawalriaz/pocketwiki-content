# Agriculture

Agriculture is the cultivation of soil and the planting, raising, and harvesting of food and non-food crops, together with livestock production. By producing food surpluses, agriculture enabled people to settle in cities, making sedentary civilization possible. Wild plants were independently domesticated in at least 11 world regions, and by the 20th century industrial agriculture based on large-scale monocultures dominated global output. Today agriculture employs roughly a quarter of the world's workforce, sustains rural economies, and is both a major driver of, and a sector sensitive to, environmental degradation.

## Origins and chronology

Humans gathered wild grains by at least 105,000 years ago, and cereal cultivation of emmer, barley, and oats appeared near the Sea of Galilee around 23,000 years ago. Sheep were domesticated in Mesopotamia by roughly 13,000–11,000 years ago; cattle descended from aurochs and pigs were domesticated in Eurasia, Turkey, and Pakistan by about 10,500 years ago; potatoes in the Andes by 10,000–7,000 years ago; and horses on the Eurasian Steppes by about 3500 BC. Ploughs appear in Sumerian pictographs by about 3000 BC, with seed-ploughs following around 2300 BC.

Specific centers of domestication include rice in China (11,500–6200 BC), sugarcane and root vegetables in New Guinea around 9,000 years ago, sorghum in the Sahel by 7,000 years ago, cotton in Peru by 5,600 years ago (also independently in Eurasia), and maize bred from wild teosinte in Mesoamerica between 10,000 and 6,000 years ago. Scholars continue to offer competing hypotheses for why agriculture arose where and when it did.

## Early civilizations and trade

From about 8000 BC the Sumerians farmed wheat, barley, lentils, dates, and other crops using Tigris–Euphrates irrigation. Egyptian farming depended on Nile flooding after 10,000 BC. India had domesticated wheat, barley, and jujube by 9000 BC, with an animal-drawn plough in the Indus Valley by 2500 BC. By the 5th century BC China operated nationwide granaries and silk farming, with water-powered mills by the 1st century BC and heavy iron ploughs by the late 2nd century. In the Americas, indigenous peoples developed raised-field canal systems, forest gardening, fire-stick farming, and the "Three Sisters" companion planting of squash, maize, and climbing beans. Indigenous Australians practiced systematic burning and eel farming from about 5,000 years ago.

The 1492 Columbian exchange moved New World crops (maize, potatoes, tomatoes) to Europe and Old World crops and livestock to the Americas. The British Agricultural Revolution from the 17th century introduced irrigation, crop rotation, and fertilizers, and the 20th century brought mechanization, Haber-Bosch synthetic fertilizer, and the Green Revolution, whose high-yielding hybrid varieties raised US corn from about 2.5 t/ha in 1900 to about 9.4 t/ha in 2001.

## Contemporary status

As of 2015 China led global agricultural output, followed by the EU, India, and the US. Agriculture employed 916 million people in 2023, down from 1,027 million (40% of the workforce) in 2000, and its share of global GDP has held near 4% since 2000. Despite rising production, 702–828 million people faced hunger in 2021. Small farms under 2 ha make up five-sixths of all farms but occupy only about 12% of farmland, while farms over 50 ha (1% of farms) hold more than 70%. Agricultural expansion drives nearly 90% of deforestation. Women make up large shares of the workforce (47% in sub-Saharan Africa) but face a 24% land productivity gap relative to male-managed farms and earn 18.4% less in wage employment. The ILO estimates at least 170,000 work-related agricultural deaths annually, about twice the rate of other sectors.

## Production systems

Cropping systems include shifting cultivation, multiple cropping, intercropping, and monoculture. Livestock systems are grassland-based, mixed, or landless; livestock used 30% of ice- and water-free land as of 2010 and employed about 1.3 billion people. Aquaculture grew roughly 9% per year from 1975 to 2007. Tillage raises productivity but increases erosion and CO₂ release. Integrated pest management combines chemical, biological, mechanical, and cultural methods, with pesticides as a last resort. Agriculture uses about 70% of freshwater withdrawals, and the FAO projects food production must rise roughly 50% by 2050 versus 2012.

## Environmental impact

Agriculture covers 4.8 billion ha (one-third of Earth's land surface) and produced about 13% of anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions (a 2011 UNEP figure). Livestock account for 18% of greenhouse gas emissions in CO₂-equivalent terms, and 70% of formerly forested Amazon land is now pasture. Nutrient runoff causes eutrophication: excessive nutrient enrichment of water bodies that triggers algal blooms and oxygen loss. Pesticide use has risen to 2.5 million short tons annually, and resistance creates a "pesticide treadmill" in which farmers apply more or stronger chemicals to maintain control. Plastic mulch leaves 50–260 kg/ha of residue in topsoil. Climate change is slowing productivity growth in mid and low latitudes and threatens up to about half of cropland in some regions under 1.5°C warming. Responses include conservation tillage, agroforestry, biochar, and payments for ecosystem services.

## Biotechnology and policy

Plant breeding advanced after Mendel's genetics work, and X-ray and UV mutagenesis in the 1950s produced modern grain varieties. GMOs (organisms whose genetic material is altered using recombinant DNA technology), including herbicide-tolerant and Bt insect-resistant crops, are widely used but restricted in many countries; the EU has required labeling since 1997 and the US since 2022. Global producer support totals about US$540 billion per year (2021) and is often inefficient and environmentally harmful. The discipline began systematically with Mayer's gypsum experiments in the 18th century and Lawes and Gilbert's Rothamsted trials in 1843, and was formally funded in the US by the 1887 Hatch Act.

## Key terms

- **Domestication**: gradual genetic adaptation of wild plants and animals through human selection.
- **Monoculture**: growing one crop across a large area, dominant in industrial agriculture.
- **Shifting cultivation (slash-and-burn)**: clearing forest by burning, farming a few years, then abandoning to regrow.
- **Subsistence farming**: farming to satisfy family or local needs only.
- **Intensive farming**: maximizing productivity with low fallow ratio and high inputs.
- **Green Revolution**: mid-20th-century spread of high-yielding hybrid varieties that sharply raised yields.
- **GMO**: organism whose genetic material is altered using recombinant DNA technology.
- **Eutrophication**: excessive nutrient enrichment of water bodies causing algal blooms and oxygen loss.
- **Integrated pest management (IPM)**: combining chemical, biological, mechanical, and cultural methods, with pesticides as a last resort.
- **Fire-stick farming**: controlled burning by indigenous peoples to sustain low-density agriculture.
