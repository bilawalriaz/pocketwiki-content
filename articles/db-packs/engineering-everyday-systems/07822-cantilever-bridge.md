# Cantilever bridge

A cantilever bridge is built from rigid arms that project horizontally into space and are supported on only one end. Small footbridges can use simple beams, but road and rail cantilevers use steel trusses or prestressed concrete box girders, the two structural forms that let a cantilever reach useful span lengths.

A single arm sticking out into space has nothing to counter the downward load at its tip, so it would tip over. The solution is two arms that meet in the middle of the obstacle, each acting as the anchor for the other. The arms reaching over the gap are the cantilever arms; the arms extending the opposite way are the anchor arms, which sit on solid ground or heavy counterweights. A bridge on two piers therefore contains four arms: two crossing the obstacle and two anchoring them. This arrangement is called a balanced cantilever, and most cantilever bridges take this form. Where a short central gap remains, a suspended span rests on the ends of the two opposing arms.

In a steel truss cantilever, the upper members carry tension and the lower members carry compression. The tension is fed back along the anchor arms to the outermost supports, while the compression travels down through the central piers. The famous illustration of the Forth Bridge makes the force paths visible: three engineers sit in chairs supporting a colleague, Kaichi Watanabe, in the middle. The wooden poles in their hands act in compression like the lower chord, their outstretched arms act in tension like the upper chord, and the bricks on their laps are the anchorages. Many truss cantilevers use pinned joints, which makes the structure statically determinate, meaning all forces can be calculated from statics alone.

The cantilever's central practical advantage is that it needs almost no falsework, the temporary scaffolding usually built across a gap to hold up a span during construction. A deep gorge, a busy shipping channel, or soft ground below can make falsework impossible or uneconomical. Cantilever construction builds outward from each pier, arm by arm, so nothing has to be placed beneath the growing span.

The first modern cantilever bridge, the Hassfurt Bridge over the Main river in Germany, was completed in 1867 with a central span of 124 feet (38 m). It followed an 1866 patent by Heinrich Gerber for a hinged girder, a beam with a hinge placed in or near the middle. A mid-span hinge had two benefits: the structure became statically determinate, and it could tolerate unequal settlement of the foundations, since a hinge breaks a continuous beam into segments that can rotate slightly without overstressing the rest.

The design spread quickly. C. Shaler Smith's High Bridge of Kentucky (1877) crossed a gorge 275 feet (84 m) deep and was the first to take full advantage of building without falsework. Charles Conrad Schneider's Niagara Cantilever Bridge (1883) and the Poughkeepsie Bridge (1889) followed. The Forth Bridge in Scotland, designed by Sir Benjamin Baker and Sir John Fowler and opened in 1890, held the world record for longest span for twenty-nine years, with two main spans of 1,710 feet (521 m). The Quebec Bridge in Canada took the record in 1919 at 1,800 feet (549 m) and still holds it.

A second construction method uses pure cantilever arms to build a steel arch from each side at once, with no falsework below and no temporary cables above. Each half-arch is anchored in rock that can resist the pulling force in the upper chord during construction, which limits this method to narrow canyons with suitable rock, such as the Navajo Bridge. When the two halves meet, a pin is driven in, the temporary anchorage is released, and the completed structure behaves as a tied or truss arch.

The longest cantilever spans remain steel trusses, but prestressed concrete balanced cantilevers built by segmental construction, in which the bridge is assembled from short factory-made pieces stressed together, have become the standard for medium and long road spans. They share the same shape as a steel cantilever and transfer internal forces through prestressed concrete box girders rather than steel trusses.

The Quebec Bridge at 1,800 feet (549 m), the Forth Bridge at 1,710 feet (521 m), the Minato Bridge in Osaka at 1,673 feet (510 m), the Commodore Barry Bridge in Pennsylvania at 1,644 feet (501 m), and the Howrah Bridge in Kolkata at 1,500 feet (457 m) are the five longest cantilever spans in the world, and the Quebec record has stood for more than a century.
