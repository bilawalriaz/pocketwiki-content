# Building

A building is an enclosed, permanent structure with a roof and walls that provides shelter, security, living space, privacy, and storage. It separates a controlled interior from the harsher exterior, and over time it has also served as a canvas for artistic expression and, more recently, a site for sustainable design practice. The first clear evidence of deliberate homebuilding appears around 18,000 BC, and buildings became common in the Neolithic period once settled agriculture was established.

## What counts as a building

The defining features are a roof, walls, and a more or less permanent location. In everyday use, fences and free-standing walls are excluded, even though the broadest reading of the term could include them. Height is measured from street level to the highest architectural detail, and whether spires and masts are included depends on the classification scheme in use, with antenna function sometimes treated as the deciding factor. The boundary between low-rise and high-rise is debated, though three stories or fewer is the general working threshold for low-rise.

## Types

Buildings are usually grouped by purpose:

- **Residential** ranges from single-family houses to apartment blocks, condominiums, and specialised housing such as nursing homes and prisons. The vocabulary used to describe them reflects several dimensions: function (holiday cottage, timeshare), size (cottage, great house), value (shack, mansion), construction method (log home, mobile home), architectural style (castle, Victorian), and geography (earth shelter, stilt house).
- **Commercial** buildings house businesses without residential occupancy, such as shops and restaurants.
- **Industrial** buildings support heavy activity like manufacturing, warehousing, and factory work.
- **Agricultural** buildings are farm outbuildings, with barns as the standard example.
- **Mixed-use** buildings combine functions, most often residential above commercial.
- **Complexes** are clusters of related buildings, such as a hospital campus or housing estate.

## How buildings get made

Designing and constructing a building is a collective effort. The typical team includes real estate developers, financial institutions, planning authorities, surveyors, construction managers, architects, several kinds of engineers (civil, structural, mechanical, heating/ventilation/air-conditioning, plumbing), landscape architects, interior designers, contractors, and facility managers. Their work is constrained by zoning ordinances, which govern land use in specific areas, and by building codes, fire codes, and life-safety regulations. For life-safety purposes, vehicles such as trailers, ships, and aircraft can be treated as buildings even though they are not permanent structures.

## Systems inside the walls

Most buildings hide substantial internal infrastructure. Heating, cooling, electrical power, telecommunications, water supply, and wastewater systems are routine, and in commercial buildings these networks can be intricate and space-intensive. People and goods move through conveying systems such as elevators, escalators, moving sidewalks, skyways, and underground connections, which can link a building to neighbouring ones.

## Risks

Buildings face damage from a predictable set of hazards: storms, explosions, ground subsidence, landslides, fire, flooding, poor maintenance, and improper alterations. Each of these modes is recognised in building codes and engineering practice, which is why surveyors and specialist engineers are part of the standard design team rather than optional additions.
