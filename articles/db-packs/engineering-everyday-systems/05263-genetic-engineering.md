# Genetic engineering

Genetic engineering is the direct, deliberate modification of an organism's DNA using laboratory techniques, rather than waiting for it to arise through breeding. New DNA is copied from another organism, synthesised chemically, or removed altogether. The altered organism is a genetically modified organism (GMO); if the new DNA comes from a different species it is transgenic, from the same or crossable species cisgenic, and if DNA is merely deleted it is a knockout. Compared to conventional breeding, a single identified gene can be moved across species boundaries, including between very different organisms, without the many unwanted genes that breeding drags along.

## How a GMO is built

The pipeline has three broad stages.

**1. Isolate or synthesise the gene.** The cell containing the desired gene is opened, its DNA is cut with restriction enzymes (proteins that cut DNA at specific sequences) and the fragments are separated by gel electrophoresis, a technique that sorts DNA by size using an electric field. The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) can amplify a known sequence from a tiny starting amount. If the sequence is known but no physical copy exists, the DNA is written chemically. The gene is spliced into a plasmid, a small ring of DNA that replicates inside bacteria, so unlimited copies can be grown. Promoter and terminator sequences (DNA switches that turn a gene on and off) and a selectable marker, usually an antibiotic-resistance gene, are added so transformed cells can be identified.

**2. Insert the DNA into the host.** Bacteria take up foreign DNA after heat or electric shock. Animal cells receive DNA by microinjection into the nucleus or via disabled viruses. Plant cells are transformed using *Agrobacterium tumefaciens*, a soil bacterium that naturally inserts its own DNA into plants, or by biolistics, shooting DNA-coated gold or tungsten particles into cells. Electroporation, a brief electric pulse that opens pores in the cell membrane, works for both.

**3. Regenerate and verify.** Because only one cell is altered, an entire plant or animal is often regrown from it: through tissue culture in plants, or from embryonic stem cells in animals. PCR, DNA sequencing, and protein-level tests such as Western blot, which detects specific proteins using antibodies, confirm the gene is present, in the right location, at the right copy number, and producing protein.

Two capabilities now dominate the field. *Gene targeting* uses homologous recombination, the cell's own DNA repair swapping in a matching sequence, to replace a specific existing gene, but it is inefficient. *Genome editing* (CRISPR/Cas9, TALENs, zinc-finger nucleases) uses an engineered nuclease, a programmable molecular scissors, to cut the genome at a chosen spot; the cell's repair then either disables the gene or inserts the new one. CRISPR, developed by Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier in 2012, is the most widely used.

## A short history

The term "genetic engineering" was coined in 1934, but the technology only became real in the 1970s. Paul Berg assembled the first recombinant DNA (DNA joined from two different sources) in 1972 by joining DNA from the monkey virus SV40 with that of lambda phage. Herbert Boyer and Stanley Cohen made the first transgenic bacterium in 1973, inserting antibiotic-resistance genes into *E. coli*. Rudolf Jaenisch produced the first transgenic animal, a mouse, in 1974. Fearing the new power, scientists met at Asilomar in 1975 and called for government oversight. Genentech, founded in 1976, produced human somatostatin in bacteria, announced genetically engineered human insulin in 1978, and brought it to market in 1982 after FDA approval. The first field trial of a transgenic plant used herbicide-resistant tobacco in France and the US in 1986, and the ice-minus bacterium was sprayed on a Californian strawberry field in 1987. The Flavr Savr tomato, engineered for longer shelf life, became the first commercialised GM food in 1994. By 2009, 25 countries grew 11 transgenic crops. In 2010, the J. Craig Venter Institute booted up a bacterium with a fully synthetic genome, and in 2014 a bacterium replicated DNA containing an unnatural base pair, the first organism with an expanded genetic alphabet.

## Where it is used

**Medicine.** Bacteria and cultured mammalian cells (especially Chinese hamster ovary, or CHO, cells) are used as factories for human proteins: insulin, growth hormone, monoclonal antibodies, vaccines including mRNA COVID-19 vaccines, and clotting factors. Gene therapy delivers functional copies of defective genes; Alipogene tiparvovec, approved in 2012, was the first such treatment. Germline editing, which would make changes heritable, is banned in 40 countries after He Jiankui's 2018 announcement of CCR5-edited embryos.

**Research.** Knockouts reveal what a gene does by removing it; gain-of-function studies add copies to test whether a gene is sufficient; tracking fusions, such as green fluorescent protein (GFP) attached to a protein of interest, reveal where a protein ends up; expression studies using reporter genes show when and where a gene is active. *E. coli* is the standard storehouse for engineered genes because it is cheap, fast-growing, and storable at −80 °C indefinitely.

**Industry.** Engineered microbes produce the enzyme chymosin for cheese, enzymes for laundry detergents, biofuels, and biodegradable battery components. Engineered bacteria also sense arsenic, break down oil spills, and extract metals in biomining.

**Agriculture.** Most commercialised GM crops carry an insect-resistance gene from *Bacillus thuringiensis* (Bt) or tolerance to glyphosate, the active ingredient in the herbicide Roundup. Other targets include virus resistance, drought tolerance, altered oil composition, and faster-growing salmon (approved in 2016). Animals can be engineered to express drugs in their milk, an approach called pharming.

## Regulation and controversy

The Asilomar recommendations of 1975 seeded a regulatory framework that now spans national agencies and the 2000 Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety, an international treaty covering the cross-border movement of GMOs, signed by 157 countries. The US regulates the *product*, using substantial equivalence (a food is safe if it is essentially the same as a conventional counterpart) and requires only voluntary labelling. The European Union regulates the *process*, treats all GM food as "new food" subject to case-by-case review, and mandates labelling above 0.9% GM content; 64 countries require some form of GM labelling. Scientific consensus holds that approved GM foods on the market are no riskier than conventional equivalents, though each new one must be tested individually. Objections split into ethical concerns (patenting life, "playing God", germline modification, possible use for enhancement), ecological ones (gene flow to wild relatives producing "superweeds", effects on non-target insects), and economic ones (corporate control of seed supply and farmer dependence). Public perception of GM food safety lags behind the scientific consensus.
