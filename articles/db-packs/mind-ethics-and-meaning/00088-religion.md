# Religion

Religion is a modern Western category, not a universal one. The word entered English in the 1200s, but the *concept* of a separate "religion" domain only formed in the 17th century, after the Protestant Reformation split Christendom and European contact with the wider world during the Age of Exploration required a vocabulary for sorting sacred from civil, Christian from non-Christian, and supernatural from natural. Many languages still lack a clean equivalent: Hebrew has no precise word, Arabic *din* and Sanskrit *dharma* both also mean "law," and Japan had no concept of religion until American warships arrived in 1853 and imposed treaties requiring freedom of worship. Calling something "a religion" is therefore a scholarly choice, not a neutral description.

## Why definition is hard

Scholars have failed to agree on a single definition, and the term is what philosophers call an *essentially contested concept*: it admits several legitimate uses that cannot be resolved by more evidence. Each famous formulation captures a different slice.

| Thinker | Year | Definition | What it captures |
|---------|------|------------|------------------|
| E. B. Tylor | 1871 | "Belief in spiritual beings" | Minimum threshold; Tylor argued narrowing would exclude many peoples from the category of religious |
| Émile Durkheim | 1912 | A "unified system of beliefs and practices relative to sacred things," uniting one moral community | Social function and the sacred/profane split |
| Antoine Vergote | 20th c. | Expressions, emotions, and actions referring to a supernatural being or beings | Explicit supernatural reference |

A community can count as "religious" by one definition and not by another, which is why the term resists closure.

## Shape of religious systems

Religious practice is recognisable by a recurring toolkit: rituals, prayer, meditation, sacrifice, festivals, initiations, weddings, funerals, music, art, dance, sermons, and ethical codes. A story important to a group is called a *myth* in sociology, neutral on whether it is factually true. Most systems draw a line between *sacred* (set apart, forbidden) and ordinary things, and most build community identity around shared symbols and obligations.

## How the field studies religion

Several disciplines work on religion: theology, philosophy of religion, comparative religion, anthropology of religion, psychology of religion, sociology of religion, and law and religion. Daniel L. Pals names eight classical theories: animism and magic (E. B. Tylor, J. G. Frazer), Sigmund Freud's psychoanalysis, Émile Durkheim, Karl Marx, Max Weber, Mircea Eliade, E. E. Evans-Pritchard, and Clifford Geertz. A modern strand, the *cognitive science of religion*, treats religious thought as a product of ordinary cognitive capacities, and *social constructionism* argues that "religion" is a Christian-rooted category misapplied to non-Western cultures. The origin of religious belief itself remains an open question, with proposed explanations including awareness of death, a sense of community, and dreams.

## Global picture

About 10,000 distinct religions exist worldwide, but four account for over 73% of the population: Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, and Buddhism. A 2015 global poll found 63% of people identified as religious, 22% as not religious, and 11% as convinced atheists. The two largest, Christianity (about 2.3 billion in 2015) and Islam (about 1.8 billion in 2015), are both *Abrahamic*, monotheistic and tracing spiritual lineage to Abraham.

## Three rough families

| Family | Core idea | Examples |
|--------|-----------|----------|
| Abrahamic | One God, descent from Abraham | Judaism (oldest; Torah), Christianity (Jesus, 1st c. CE; New Testament), Islam (Muhammad, 7th c. CE; Quran), Baháʼí Faith (19th-c. Iran) |
| Dharmic / Indian | *Dharma* (law, duty, order) | Hinduism, Jainism, Buddhism (Siddhartha Gautama, 5th c. BCE), Sikhism (Guru Nanak, 15th-c. Punjab) |
| East Asian, Iranian, Indigenous | *Tao* (the way), or local spirits and ancestors | Taoism, Confucianism, Chinese folk religion, Zoroastrianism (Zoroaster, 6th c. BCE), shamanic and animistic traditions |

## Etymology

The Latin root *religiō* did not originally mean belief in gods. In Roman usage it meant conscientiousness, moral obligation, or duty toward anything, including family, neighbors, rulers, or the gods, and even covered hesitation, caution, and fear. Cicero derived it from *relegere* ("go over again, consider carefully"); Augustine, following Lactantius, popularised *religare* ("bind, reconnect"). Both readings remain defended.

## Common confusions

- *Atheist* and *agnostic* are not the same as *irreligious*. Atheism is the lack of belief in gods; agnosticism holds that gods' existence is unknowable; *irreligion*, the absence of any religious commitment, is the true opposite and differs from active *antireligion*.
- Violence is not exclusive to religion. Critics such as Richard Dawkins argue religion is inherently violent; anthropologist Jack David Eller counters that "violence is neither essential to nor exclusive to religion."
