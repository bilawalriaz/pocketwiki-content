# Ethics of technology

Ethics of technology, also called *technoethics*, is the study of moral questions raised by the design, use, and impact of technological systems. It treats technology and ethics as interconnected and embedded in social life, asking who benefits, who is harmed, and who decides.

## Two kinds of problem

Technoethics splits into two recurring problem types. The first is the **morality of invention**: is creating a given technology right, wrong, or only conditionally acceptable? Nuclear weapons, energy-wasting processes, and genetically modified organisms are cases where the act of innovation itself is morally contested. The second is **power shifts caused by new tools**: technology magnifies or restricts what individuals, corporations, and states can do, intensifying older questions about privacy, autonomy, and harm. Bioethics has been reshaped by life-preserving and cloning technologies; law has been reshaped by surveillance and anonymity tools; the right to privacy has been eroded by RFID, biometrics, and genetic screening.

## Three foundational lenses

Technoethical reasoning typically draws on classical theories, plus a fourth suited to relationships:

- **Utilitarianism (Bentham):** judge actions by their consequences for overall happiness and suffering.
- **Duty ethics (Kant):** judge actions by their adherence to universal moral rules, independent of outcome.
- **Virtue ethics (Aristotle):** judge actions by the character of the person acting.
- **Relationship ethics:** ethical behaviour flows from care and consideration embedded in human communication.

These lenses often conflict. A utilitarian may approve a privacy-invading tool that prevents greater harm, while a Kantian may reject it for treating people as means rather than ends.

## Origins

Concerns about technology are old. Socrates' critique of writing in Plato's *Phaedrus* is an early example. The formal field is recent. Philosopher **Mario Bunge** coined "technoethics" in **1977**, arguing that technologists must be held morally, not just technically, responsible for both short- and long-term effects of what they design. Hans Jonas developed parallel arguments in *The Imperative of Responsibility* (1979). As computing, biotechnology, and the internet spread, separate disciplinary ethics (computer, engineering, bio-, environmental, nano-, military) converged under the technoethics umbrella. Spain's Instituto de Tecnoética was founded in 1998 under Josep Esquirol, and later reference works, including the two-volume *Handbook of Research on Technoethics* and the *Encyclopedia of Science, Technology, and Ethics*, helped establish technoethics as an interdisciplinary research area.

## Recurring issues

The literature groups technology-ethics questions into recurring categories: access rights, accountability, digital rights, environmental harm, existential risk, freedom, health and safety, human enhancement, human judgement versus automation, over-automation, the **precautionary principle**, privacy, security, self-replicating machines, transparency, and terms of service.

Concrete cases illustrate the stakes:

- **Artificial intelligence** systems can carry developer or data bias, producing discriminatory outcomes in hiring, facial recognition, and content moderation. AI design that affects human welfare is treated as requiring intelligibility, fairness, protection of data rights, and refusal of autonomous power to harm, destroy, or deceive.
- **Surveillance** technologies, including full-body airport scanners, GPS in phones and vehicles, and post-9/11 laws in the US, UK, and Germany, amplify old questions about privacy and state power; courts in the UK and Germany later ruled parts of these laws inconsistent with privacy protections.
- **Genetic engineering** raises ecological and health questions through GM crops and engineered animals such as the FDA-approved AquAdvantage salmon, which matures in roughly 18 months rather than three years, forcing regulators to weigh economic harm to traditional fisheries against benefits of cheaper, more plentiful food.
- **Data exploitation** turns user information into a commodity and a weapon. The Facebook–Cambridge Analytica affair, in which data on up to 87 million non-consenting users was harvested and used in political campaigns including the 2016 US presidential election, is a defining case. A later leak exposed data on over 533 million Facebook users from 106 countries.
- **Deepfakes**, synthetic video and audio produced through AI, enable fraud, political disinformation, and harassment. Their accessibility through consumer apps, combined with the many parties involved in producing a single deepfake, makes legal prosecution difficult.
- **Citizen journalism** lets non-professionals collect and broadcast news, accelerating events such as the 2011 Arab Spring, but lacks shared professional codes, raising questions about information quality and trust.
- **COVID-19 contact tracing** spread rapidly; by April 2020 over 43 tracing apps existed globally. The European Convention on Human Rights, the UN International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, and the Siracusa Principles permit restricting rights during infectious-disease emergencies only if restrictions are time-bound, necessary, proportionate, and scientifically valid.

## Why technoethics is hard

Technology advances faster than the social and legal frameworks meant to govern it, and users routinely adopt tools for purposes their designers did not intend. The rise of algorithmic decision-making has shifted the dominant view of technology from "inherently positive tool" to "inherently neutral," forcing explicit attention to where accountability for harm should lie between designers, deployers, and users.

Two formal responses have developed. A **Technoethical Assessment (TEA)** evaluates a technology's intended ends and side effects, weighs efficiency against fairness, considers all stakeholder perspectives, and examines effects across biological, physical, psychological, social, and environmental levels. **Technoethical Design (TED)** brings stakeholders into participatory design, performs a TEA, and optimises the system to meet their needs while making tacit relations visible. Both draw on systems theory, treating technologies as self-producing systems embedded in nature and society.

## Open tensions

The field cannot fully resolve several it identifies. Holding technologists responsible for long-term consequences presumes a capacity to foresee them that almost no one possesses. Debates such as mashup copyright, GMO regulation, and AI bias show that the same innovation can be called inhumane by some and life-changing by others, with no neutral ground for adjudication. Even when laws constrain surveillance or data exploitation, enforcement lags behind practice, as repeated data breaches demonstrate. Each generation of technology forces the field to renegotiate where acceptable innovation ends and unacceptable harm begins.
