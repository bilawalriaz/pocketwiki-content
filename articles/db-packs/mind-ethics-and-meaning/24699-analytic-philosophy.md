# Analytic philosophy

Analytic philosophy is a twentieth-century style of Western, especially anglophone, philosophy defined by method rather than doctrine. It emphasises clear prose, rigorous argument, formal logic, and engagement with mathematics and the natural sciences, and is often paired with the *linguistic turn*, the priority of analysing language as a route to philosophical problems. It is most often contrasted with "continental" philosophy, a loose label covering phenomenology, existentialism, and Hegelianism, though the boundary is contested and the contrast is sometimes cast as technical versus literary.

The tradition has two intellectual parents. In Germany, Gottlob Frege invented modern predicate logic in *Begriffsschrift* (1879) and argued that arithmetic reduces to pure logic, a project called *logicism*. In "On Sense and Reference" (1892) he showed that "the Morning Star" and "the Evening Star" both refer to the planet Venus but differ in cognitive significance, which requires distinguishing a term's *reference* (its object) from its *sense* (its mode of presentation). In Austria-Hungary, Franz Brentano introduced *intentionality*, the "aboutness" of thought, which became a central concern of the philosophy of mind. The movement's centre of gravity later shifted to Britain and the United States, which is why Michael Dummett preferred the label "Anglo-Austrian."

In Britain, Bertrand Russell and G. E. Moore rebelled against the dominant British idealism of F. H. Bradley and T. H. Green, whose Hegel-influenced monism they judged obscurantist. Against the idealists' *logical holism*, the view that the world can be known only as a whole, they advanced *logical atomism*: the world consists of independent facts, mirrored by atomic propositions combined with logical operators. Russell's 1905 essay "On Denoting" became a paradigm of the new method, analysing "the present King of France" as a quantified description rather than a name and showing how scope distinctions dissolve apparent contradictions. Russell and Whitehead's *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913) extended Frege's logicism with a *theory of types*, a hierarchy that blocks self-reference, to dodge the paradox Russell had discovered in Frege's system.

Russell's student Ludwig Wittgenstein pushed the picture further in the *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1921), arguing that the world is the totality of atomic facts, that propositions picture states of affairs, and that tautologies show the logical structure of language while saying nothing about the world. From the late 1920s, the Vienna Circle (Schlick, Carnap, Neurath) and Berlin Circle (Reichenbach, Hempel) developed Russell and Wittgenstein's insights into *logical positivism*, whose *verification principle* holds that every meaningful statement is either analytic, true by logic or definition, or empirically verifiable. Metaphysics, theology, and value claims were dismissed as cognitively meaningless. A. J. Ayer's *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936) carried these ideas into the anglophone mainstream.

After World War II the movement split. The later Wittgenstein, in *Philosophical Investigations* (1953), rejected the picture theory in favour of *meaning as use*: words acquire meaning from the *language-games* they belong to, connected by *family resemblance* rather than a single defining feature. The Oxford strain of ordinary language philosophy (Ryle, Austin, Strawson) argued that many traditional puzzles dissolve once we attend to what ordinary language already distinguishes. Ryle's *The Concept of Mind* (1949) attacked Cartesian dualism as the myth of the "ghost in the machine"; Austin's *How to Do Things with Words* (1962) developed speech-act theory, the idea that utterances can *do* things (as in "I promise") and not merely say things.

Logical positivism collapsed mid-century. W. V. O. Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) attacked the analytic–synthetic distinction; his "On What There Is" (1948) reformulated ontology with the slogan "to be is to be the value of a variable." Wilfrid Sellars critiqued the "Myth of the Given." Their work reopened metaphysics, which Saul Kripke then occupied with new tools. In *Naming and Necessity* (1980) Kripke argued that proper names are *rigid designators*, referring to the same individual in every possible world, separated metaphysical necessity from the epistemic notion of *a priori*, and supplied examples of necessary *a posteriori* truths, propositions knowable only by experience yet true in every possible world, such as water being H₂O and gold being atomic number 79. David Lewis extended this with *modal realism*, the view that other possible worlds are as concrete as the actual one, in *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986). This revival made metaphysics, philosophy of language, philosophy of mind, and philosophy of science the analytic mainstream.

Analytic philosophy also reshaped ethics. G. E. Moore's *Principia Ethica* (1903) defended *non-naturalist moral realism*, the view that moral goodness is a real but non-natural property, via the *open-question argument*, which holds that no analysis of "good" in purely natural terms can close the question "but is it good?" The positivists countered with *emotivism*, the view that value judgments express attitudes rather than report facts. After 1970, normative ethics revived and split into consequentialism, Kantian deontology (Rawls's *A Theory of Justice*, 1971), and virtue ethics (MacIntyre's *After Virtue*, 1981). In epistemology, Edmund Gettier's 1963 paper showed that justified true belief is not knowledge, generating decades of work on justification, closure, and the *KK thesis*, the claim that knowing *p* requires knowing that one knows *p*. In the philosophy of mind, behaviourism gave way to type-identity theory, the identification of mental states with brain states, then to functionalism, the view that mental states are defined by what they *do* rather than what they are made of. Thomas Nagel's "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974) and Frank Jackson's knowledge argument pressed the problem of qualia, the raw subjective qualities of experience, while David Chalmers named the "hard problem of consciousness."

By the second half of the twentieth century analytic philosophy was the dominant style in leading anglophone philosophy departments, distinguished by its piecemeal, problem-focused approach and its absorption of formal and scientific methods.
