# History of Islam

Islam began in the Arabian Peninsula in the 7th century CE with the mission of Prophet Muhammad. The faith spread rapidly through conquest, trade, and conversion, producing a civilization that stretched from West Africa to Southeast Asia and shaped global culture, science, and politics.

## Origins and the First Community

Muhammad was born in Mecca around 570 CE. In 610 CE he began receiving revelations that Muslims believe were the words of God; these were later collected as the Quran. Facing persecution in Mecca, he migrated to the city of Yathrib (later called Medina) in 622 CE. This migration, known as the Hijra, marks year one of the Islamic calendar. In Medina, Muhammad established the first Muslim community, a polity that bound together the early converts and the city's tribes under a shared religious and political order.

By Muhammad's death in 632 CE, most Arabian tribes had joined the community. The political and religious leadership of the Muslim community, the caliphate, passed to a series of successors called caliphs (khalīfa), starting with the Rashidun ("Rightly Guided") caliphs.

## Early Expansion

Under the Rashidun and then the Umayyad Caliphate (661–750), Muslim armies took Syria, Egypt, Persia, and much of North Africa from the Byzantine and Sasanian empires. The speed of this expansion rested on the prior weakness of those two empires and on support from local non-Muslim populations, who sometimes aided the Muslims in exchange for religious autonomy and lower taxes. Non-Muslims in conquered lands paid the jizya, a poll tax that granted protection and exemption from military service, while converts to Islam were expected to pay the standard Muslim alms tax.

The caliphate is a core concept here: it refers to the political-religious leadership of the global Muslim community. The Umayyads ruled from Damascus and extended the empire from al-Andalus (Islamic Spain) to the Indus Valley.

## Sectarian Split and the Abbasids

The succession of the caliphate produced a lasting division in Islam. After the First Fitna (the first civil war), a faction came to believe that leadership should have passed to Ali, Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law, and his descendants. They became known as Shi'a (from "Shi'at Ali," the party of Ali). The majority, who accepted the historical succession of the caliphs, became known as Sunni. The dispute was originally political, but over time it hardened into distinct theological and legal traditions.

In 750 CE the Abbasid Revolution overthrew the Umayyads. The Abbasid Caliphate, based in Baghdad, presided over the Islamic Golden Age, a flourishing of science, philosophy, medicine, and the arts that drew on Greek, Persian, and Indian knowledge. The Abbasid court also supported the spread of Sufism, the mystical tradition of Islam that emphasizes the inner, personal search for God. Over time, regional dynasties broke away, and a Shi'a Ismaili dynasty, the Fatimid Caliphate (909–1171), ruled Egypt and parts of North Africa.

## The Mongols and Regional Powers

In 1258 the Mongol armies sacked Baghdad, ending the Abbasid caliphate in the east. The Mamluks, a military caste of slave origin who ruled Egypt and Syria, halted the Mongol advance at the Battle of Ain Jalut in 1260. The Mongol successor states, the khanates that arose on Mongol imperial territory, gradually converted to Islam. In Central Asia the Timurids (descendants of Timur) sponsored a later cultural flowering, while in West Africa the empires of Mali and Songhai made Timbuktu a center of Islamic scholarship. Meanwhile, the Crusades (1095–1291) were a series of wars launched by Christian European powers to recover the Holy Land; they ultimately failed but left a lasting imprint on Muslim-European relations.

## Gunpowder Empires and Colonialism

From the 15th to 18th centuries, three "gunpowder empires," states whose power rested on firearms and artillery, dominated the Islamic world. The Ottoman Empire conquered Constantinople in 1453, took control of the Arab heartlands, and pushed into southeastern Europe. The Safavid Empire made Twelver Shi'ism, the branch of Shi'a Islam that follows twelve imams descended from Ali, the state religion of Iran, creating a durable Sunni-Shi'a frontier with the Ottoman lands. The Mughal Empire ruled most of the Indian subcontinent, blending Persian, Islamic, and local Indian traditions.

From the 19th century, European powers dismantled these empires. The Ottoman Empire collapsed after World War I, and the modern state system of the Middle East was drawn from its ruins. The British replaced the Mughals with direct rule over India. Colonial borders and the later struggles for independence, including the Arab-Israeli conflict, continue to shape the politics of Muslim-majority regions today.

## Open Debates

Several questions in early Islamic history remain unsettled. Scholars debate the reliability of the earliest Muslim sources, many of which were written down decades after the events they describe. The motives behind the early conquests are also disputed: religious conviction, economic pressure, and the political vacuum left by the Byzantine and Sasanian empires all played a role, and their relative weight is still argued. The Sunni-Shi'a split is similarly understood either as an immediate political rupture after Muhammad's death or as a slower process of theological divergence over the first few centuries.
