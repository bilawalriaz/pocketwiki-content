# Mind

A mind is the totality of mental phenomena—the conscious and unconscious processes through which an organism perceives, remembers, thinks, feels, attends, learns, and acts. Conscious processes include direct awareness of internal and external circumstances; unconscious processes influence thought and behavior without intention or awareness. Mental phenomena also include imagination, motivation, and emotion.

What a mind *is* remains disputed. The dominant view since the 20th century, physicalism, holds that minds are aspects of material objects, most obviously brains. The mind–body problem asks how matter and mind relate. Substance dualism treats them as independent substances; property dualism treats them as different properties of one thing. The hard problem of consciousness sharpens the puzzle: no account explains how physical brain states give rise to the felt quality of experience, such as the redness of red.

## Core vocabulary

A few terms carry most of the weight in any serious discussion. *Intentionality* is the aboutness of mental states—their power to refer to or represent things; only mental states have it originally, while words inherit a derivative version. *Qualia* are the qualitative characters of experience, such as colors, sounds, and pains. *Functionalism* defines mental states by what they do rather than what they are made of: pain is whatever plays the role of being caused by injury, producing distress, and motivating avoidance. Any system playing the right role—a brain, possibly a computer—could in principle have mental states. *Consciousness* splits into phenomenal consciousness (qualitative experience) and access consciousness (information available to other mental processes). States can also be dispositional (latent) or occurrent (actively in play).

## Evolution and development

Minds did not appear suddenly. Bacteria show minimal information processing 4–3.5 billion years ago. Nerve cells emerged with multicellular animals more than 600 million years ago, splitting into diffuse nerve nets and centralized nervous systems. Vertebrates evolved about 540 million years ago; the neocortex, the mammalian region supporting higher-order functions, developed about 200 million years ago. Primates appeared around 65 million years ago, hominins 7–5 million years ago, anatomically modern humans 300,000–200,000 years ago. *Behavioral modernity*—abstract thought and symbolic behavior—may have arisen 50,000–40,000 years ago, though the dating is uncertain.

Individual minds develop in stages. Jean Piaget described four cognitive stages: sensorimotor (birth–2 years), preoperational (until about 7), concrete operational (until about 11), and formal operational (abstract reasoning thereafter). Adolescence brings identity formation; adulthood brings gains in reasoning and later declines in learning speed and memory.

## The brain as substrate

The human brain contains roughly 86 billion neurons communicating at synapses. Higher mental functions cluster in the forebrain: the prefrontal cortex handles planning; the sensory cortex processes sensation; the hippocampus consolidates long-term memories; the amygdala drives emotion; the motor cortex governs voluntary movement; Broca's area produces speech. Neurotransmitters modulate these systems—dopamine shapes motivation, serotonin shapes mood. When the brain changes, the mind changes: psychoactive drugs shift brain chemistry temporarily, while neurodegenerative disease and injury (as in Phineas Gage, whose frontal lobe damage altered his personality) produce lasting effects.

## Minds beyond humans

Whether non-human animals have minds, and of what kind, is contested. Discontinuity views treat animal minds as fundamentally different from human minds; continuity views treat the difference as one of degree. Sentience, the capacity to suffer, is the central ethical question. For machines, functionalism leaves open the possibility of artificial minds. Alan Turing proposed a behavioral test: a computer passes if it can carry on a conversation indistinguishable from a human's. *Weak AI* solves specific tasks; *strong AI* would match the full range of human mental capacities. John Searle's Chinese Room argument and Hubert Dreyfus's phenomenological critique challenge whether running the right program is enough to constitute a mind.

## Mental health

Mental health can be defined positively as internal equilibrium and well-being, or negatively as the absence of disorder. Disorders are abnormal patterns that cause distress, though the norms themselves are culturally contested. Major categories include anxiety, mood, personality, and psychotic disorders, alongside dissociative and eating disorders. The biopsychosocial model attributes disorder to interacting biological, psychological, and environmental causes. Treatments divide into psychotherapies (psychoanalytic, cognitive-behavioral, behavioral, humanistic) and drug therapies (antidepressants, antipsychotics, mood stabilizers, anxiolytics).

## How minds are studied

Psychology, the experimental science of mind and behavior founded by Wilhelm Wundt in the late 19th century, supplies controlled experiments, correlational studies, and longitudinal designs. Neuroscience maps the nervous system using fMRI (blood flow), PET (metabolism), and EEG (electrical activity), alongside brain-damage studies. Philosophy of mind works by conceptual analysis and thought experiments, including Mary the color scientist, philosophical zombies, and brains in vats. Cognitive science, emerging in the mid-20th century, integrates these fields around a shared picture of the mind as an information processor, with explanations sought at abstract, algorithmic, and implementational levels.

## What remains open

Three questions still organize the field. What is the *mark of the mental*—is it private epistemic access, consciousness, or intentionality? Where does the mind *end*—does a notebook used as memory count, as the extended mind thesis claims, or does mind stop at the skull? How do physical states yield *subjective experience*, the hard problem no current theory has solved. The boundary between normal variation and disorder, the continuity of animal minds, and the possibility of artificial minds all remain live.
