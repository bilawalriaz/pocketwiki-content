# Knowledge

Knowledge is familiarity with facts, possession of a practical skill, or a true belief secured against error. The study of these questions is **epistemology**: what people know, how they come to know it, and what "knowing" amounts to.

## The standard account and its collapse

Since Plato, propositional knowledge (knowing *that* something is the case) has been characterised as **justified true belief**: a belief that is (1) true, (2) genuinely held, and (3) backed by adequate reason. Truth is uncontroversial; one cannot know something false. The belief condition rules out dormant or unconscious states. Justification is included because some true beliefs are not knowledge, for instance a correct superstition, a lucky guess, or a conclusion drawn from faulty reasoning.

This account was destabilised by Edmund Gettier's short counterexamples, showing that a belief can be true, believed, and justified, and still not be knowledge. The usual diagnosis is **epistemic luck**: the justification exists but is not connected in the right way to the truth. A driver in a countryside of painted barn facades stops at the one real barn by luck, forms a justified true belief that "there is a barn here," but cannot tell barns from fakes, so does not know. No consensus has emerged since. Some philosophers drop justification and tie knowledge to **reliability** (a generally truth-conducive process), some keep justification and add further conditions (no dependence on false beliefs, no hidden defeaters, counterfactual safety), and others locate knowledge in the exercise of **cognitive virtues** such as carefulness and open-mindedness. The debate remains open.

## The main kinds of knowledge

Propositional knowledge takes the form of *that*-clauses ("Akari knows that kangaroos hop") and is the paradigmatic type in analytic philosophy, plausibly restricted to minds capable of representing propositions, concepts, and general rules.

Two forms do not need a proposition. **Knowledge-how** is practical competence, knowing how to ride a bicycle or walk. An ant knows how to walk even though it presumably cannot represent walking. **Knowledge by acquaintance** is direct familiarity from prior experience, knowing what chocolate tastes like. Bertrand Russell, who introduced the term, treated acquaintance as more basic than propositional knowledge, since understanding a proposition requires being acquainted with its constituents.

A separate axis sorts knowledge by the role of experience. **A posteriori knowledge** depends on sensory input, such as seeing it is raining. **A priori knowledge** is possible without such support, illustrated by mathematics and logic. Plato held it is recollection of what the soul already knew; Descartes that it is innate; others posit a faculty of rational intuition. Empiricists deny it exists, and the dispute is unresolved.

Brief distinctions worth keeping: **self-knowledge** (awareness of one's own mental states, traditionally indubitable), **metaknowledge** (knowledge about what one knows), **common knowledge** (publicly shared within a group as a basis for coordination), **domain knowledge** (specialist expertise), **explicit versus tacit knowledge** (articulable facts versus hard-to-articulate skills like face recognition), and **situated knowledge**, the view that what counts as knowledge is shaped by historical, cultural, and linguistic context.

## Sources of knowledge

The standard sources are **perception** (the chief source of empirical knowledge, active and interpretive, prone to illusions like the Müller-Lyer lines), **introspection** (awareness of one's mental states, traditionally infallible but now contested), **memory** (reliable but sometimes deceptive), **inference** (reasoning from known premises), and **testimony** (learning from others, whose epistemic standing depends on the speaker's reliability). Some rationalists add **rational intuition** as a non-observational source. Science exemplifies empirical inquiry: precise problem formulation, controlled experiments and observational studies, and evidence that is public, reliable, and replicable.

## The structure of justification

When a belief is challenged, its defender may cite another belief, which may itself need defending, and so on. This is the **regress of reasons**. Three responses dominate. **Foundationalism** stops the regress by positing basic reasons justified independently of further beliefs, such as perceptual reports or self-evident truths. **Coherentism** denies the basic/non-basic contrast, holding that beliefs are justified by their mutual support within a web, though it must avoid circularity. **Infinitism** accepts that every reason needs another reason forever, and must show that minds can hold such a chain.

## The limits of knowledge

Kant argued that knowledge reaches appearances but not things in themselves. Physics imposes intrinsic limits: the uncertainty principle forbids simultaneously knowing the exact position and momentum of a particle, and chaos theory shows that some systems are so sensitive to initial conditions that long-term prediction is impossible. **Radical scepticism** holds that no knowledge exists, a position arguably self-undermining, since denying knowledge is itself a knowledge-claim. **Fallibilism**, the more modest view, holds that even the best-supported belief could in principle be wrong.

## The value of knowledge

Plato's *Meno* argues knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief because it is more stable and does not "run away" like an unsupported belief. Why justification adds value is unclear: a reliably formed true belief seems just as useful. Virtue epistemology answers that knowledge is the manifestation of cognitive virtues, and exercising those virtues is intrinsically worthwhile. Knowledge is also instrumentally valuable, which is why institutions in politics, business, the military, and education must decide deliberately what to seek and what to forgo.

## A short historical arc

Knowledge was transmitted orally until writing emerged around 3000 BCE in Mesopotamia, Egypt, India, and China. Ancient Greeks from the 6th century BCE formalised mathematics, astronomy, and medicine. The Islamic Golden Age from the 8th to 13th centuries preserved and extended this inheritance through institutions like Al-Qarawiyyin and Al-Azhar. The 15th-century printing press multiplied literacy, the 16th–17th-century Scientific Revolution shaped physics, chemistry, and biology, the 18th–19th-century Industrial Revolution applied that knowledge, and the 20th century added computers and the Internet. In epistemology, the 20th century is marked by Gettier, whose brief paper reopened a question Plato had thought settled two millennia earlier.
