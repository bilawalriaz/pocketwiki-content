# Philosophy of mind

Philosophy of mind is the branch of philosophy that studies the nature of the mind and its relation to the body and the external world. Its central question is the mind–body problem: how mental states (beliefs, pains, perceptions) relate to physical states of the brain and body. Around this core sit questions about consciousness, intentionality, free will, and personal identity.

## The mind–body problem

The puzzle begins with a striking contrast. A burnt finger feels like something; a surge of glutamate in the prefrontal cortex does not. Philosophers call the subjective "what it is like" aspect of experience **qualia** (singular: quale). The *hard problem of consciousness* is why certain physical processes are accompanied by any experience at all. A related issue is *intentionality*: mental states are about things (the thought "Herodotus was a historian" refers to Herodotus), yet brains contain only electrochemical activity. Both features make mental states hard to fit into a purely physical picture.

Two broad camps have dominated since the 17th century. **Dualism** holds that mind and body are distinct in kind. **Monism** holds they are not. Modern debate has largely shifted within monism toward *physicalism*, the view that only entities described by physics exist and that mental states must therefore be physical in nature.

## Dualist positions

*Substance dualism* was first clearly formulated by René Descartes in 1641. Descartes argued the mind is a non-physical *res cogitans* (thinking thing), lacking spatial extension, while the body is extended and non-thinking; because they have radically different properties, they cannot be identical. He also held that mental states causally interact with physical states, a view called *interactionist dualism*, and located the point of contact in the pineal gland.

Several other dualist variants avoid direct interaction. *Occasionalism* (Malebranche, al-Ghazali) holds that God intervenes on every mind–body occasion. *Pre-established harmony* (Leibniz) holds that mind and body run on parallel tracks, synchronised from the start by God. *Epiphenomenalism* (T. H. Huxley) holds that physical events cause mental events, but mental events cause nothing. *Property dualism* holds there is only one substance (the physical), but two kinds of properties: physical and mental. Mental properties *emerge* from, but are not reducible to, physical ones. Emergent materialism, non-reductive physicalism, and panpsychism are sub-varieties.

A persistent intuition drives dualism: conscious experience seems so unlike anything physical that they cannot be the same thing. Thomas Nagel pressed this with "What is it like to be a bat?" David Chalmers extended it with the zombie argument: a being physically identical to you but lacking consciousness seems conceivable, and if conceivable, possibly real. Frank Jackson added the knowledge argument (Mary, who knows every physical fact about colour vision, still learns something new when she sees red for the first time).

## Monist and physicalist positions

Baruch Spinoza (17th century) held a single substance with both mental and physical attributes, a *dual-aspect monism*. Neutral monists (Ernst Mach, William James) treated mental and physical as different descriptions of the same events.

In the 20th century, physicalism split into competing research programmes. *Behaviorism* (early-to-mid 1900s) treated mental states as dispositions to behave, eliminating inner mental life from scientific psychology; it declined after the rise of cognitivism. *Type-identity theory* (Smart, Place, 1950s) held that each mental state type is identical to a specific brain state type; token-identity versions allow looser correspondences. *Functionalism* (Putnam, Fodor, 1960s) defined mental states by their causal roles among inputs, other states, and outputs, making realisation substrate-independent. *Anomalous monism* (Donald Davidson) holds mental states *supervene* on (no change without physical change) physical states, but are not reducible to them, since strict psychophysical laws would conflict with the autonomy of psychology. *Non-reductive physicalism* keeps mental properties as a distinct ontological class dependent on, but irreducible to, the physical.

The harder problems remain qualia, intentionality, and the *explanatory gap* between third-person neuroscience and first-person experience. Chalmers treats the gap as ontological; Nagel and Colin McGinn treat it as epistemological, perhaps permanent.

## Sceptical and eliminative responses

*Eliminative materialism* (the Churchlands) predicts that folk psychology will be replaced by mature neuroscience, as Ptolemaic astronomy gave way to Copernican. *New mysterianism* (McGinn) argues human cognition is permanently closed to certain self-understanding, like an elephant trying to grasp quantum physics. Wittgensteinian critics (Peter Hacker) treat the mind–body problem itself as a *category error* arising from mismatched vocabularies.

## Mind, science, and related problems

Philosophy of mind intersects heavily with neuroscience, cognitive science, AI, and psychology. The *binding problem* asks how the brain combines features (colour, shape, motion, sound) into a unified conscious object; its inverse, the *boundary problem*, asks why some neural activity is included in a first-person perspective and other activity is not. Neuroimaging since the 1980s has sharpened but not settled these puzzles.

Strong AI aims at machines with human-like consciousness, often measured by the Turing test. John Searle's Chinese Room argument counters that manipulating symbols by rule does not give understanding: simulating cognition is not instantiating it.

Two practical issues follow. On free will, *compatibilists* (Hume, Dennett) hold that determinism and freedom are compatible if "free" means uncompelled rather than uncaused; *libertarians* insist on a stronger, indeterministic freedom. On the self, many physicalists (Dennett, Parfit) deny an enduring personal essence, treating the self as a distributed, shifting representational pattern rather than an immaterial soul; Buddhist anattā and Daniel Kolak's open individualism radicalise this into the claim that all conscious minds are numerically one.

Each of these positions carries a specific price: physicalism must explain how subjective experience arises from matter described without it; substance dualism must explain how a non-physical mind moves physical matter; eliminativism asks us to abandon the framework of belief and desire that organises our daily life.
