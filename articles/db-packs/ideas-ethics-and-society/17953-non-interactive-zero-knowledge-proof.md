# Non-interactive zero-knowledge proof

A non-interactive zero-knowledge proof (NIZK) is a single cryptographic object that lets a prover convince a verifier a statement is true without revealing anything beyond its validity, and without the two parties exchanging messages. In older interactive zero-knowledge proofs the prover and verifier must run several rounds of challenge and response; the single-object, no-back-and-forth form is what makes a proof non-interactive.

NIZKs work wherever the prover and verifier cannot talk in real time, which is why they are central to blockchains, where a distributed network of nodes checks transactions without a central authority.

Most NIZKs rely on structured public parameters agreed on in advance. The standard construction, introduced by Blum, Feldman, and Micali in 1988, is a common reference string (CRS): a shared string, often random group elements, fixed once and reused. A CRS is not information-theoretically random, because its structure is distinguishable from a uniform string, but it is fixed and public. Feige, Lapidot, and Shamir later generalised this to multi-theorem NIZKs, where one setup serves many statements. NIZKs can also be built in the random oracle model by applying the Fiat–Shamir heuristic, which replaces verifier challenges with hashes of the transcript.

Two caveats sharpen the model. Rafael Pass showed that CRS-model NIZKs do not preserve every property of interactive zero-knowledge proofs; deniability, for instance, can be lost. Shafi Goldwasser and Yael Tauman Kalai showed in 2003 that any one-shot identification scheme becomes an insecure digital signature scheme when instantiated with an arbitrary hash function.

The mechanism behind most short, verifiable NIZKs is pairing-based cryptography. The core idea is to hide the values fed into a pairing evaluation inside a commitment, so the prover can show a pairing equation holds without exposing the underlying secrets. Different commitment schemes yield proof systems under the sub-group hiding, decisional linear, or external Diffie–Hellman assumptions. These systems prove circuit satisfiability, and by the Cook–Levin theorem this covers membership proofs for every language in NP, the class of decision problems whose solutions can be verified in polynomial time. The CRS and proofs stay small, but converting a statement into a boolean circuit adds cost. Under strong knowledge assumptions one can build sublinear-length, computationally sound proofs for NP-complete problems using only a few bilinear group elements, where a bilinear group is a set of points on an elliptic curve with a pairing operation that lets one check multiplicative relations between group elements.

## Practical proof families

Two families dominate blockchain applications.

*zk-SNARK* (zero-knowledge Succinct Non-interactive ARgument of Knowledge) was first made practical by Alessandro Chiesa and collaborators in 2012. SNARKs produce short proofs that are cheap to verify, but most early schemes required a trusted setup: a one-time ceremony that produced the CRS under the assumption participants discarded secret randomness. Zcash first deployed SNARKs at scale in its Zerocash protocol, supporting private, shielding, deshielding, and public transactions, letting users decide how much data reached the public ledger. Ethereum zk-rollups use SNARKs to compress many transactions into one proof, increasing scalability.

*zk-STARK* (zero-knowledge Scalable Transparent ARgument of Knowledge) was introduced in 2018 by Eli Ben-Sasson, Iddo Bentov, Yinon Horesh, and Michael Riabzev. STARKs are transparent: they need no trusted setup and rely only on public randomness. They offer quasi-linear proving time and poly-logarithmic verification, so checking cost grows only with the logarithm of the computation size.

Bulletproofs (2017) let a prover show a committed value lies in a range using a logarithmic number of field and group elements, and were integrated into Mimblewimble (the basis of Grin, Beam, and Litecoin via extension blocks) and into Monero. In 2019 HALO recursive SNARKs removed the trusted setup, and Pickles, built on HALO, powers Mina, the first succinctly verifiable blockchain.

Systems in the field are commonly compared along three axes: transparent (no trusted setup, public randomness), universal (one setup works for any circuit rather than a per-circuit setup), and plausibly post-quantum (resistant to known quantum attacks). Older SNARKs such as Pinocchio, Geppetto, TinyRAM, and Buffet are neither transparent nor universal; PLONK, Marlin, Sonic, and vnTinyRAM add universality; SuperSonic, Halo, Hyrax, and Bulletproofs add transparency; Virgo, Ligero, Aurora, zk-STARK, and Zilch add all three. The trade-off between SNARKs and STARKs maps onto a long-running tension: succinct, cheap-to-verify proofs with a trusted setup versus transparent, scalable proofs with slightly larger proofs and no setup. Post-2019 work, including HALO, STARKs, and post-quantum systems, has been closing that gap by removing trusted setups and adding quantum resistance while keeping universality.
