# Functionalism (philosophy of mind)

Functionalism is the view that what makes a mental state the state it is — being a belief, a desire, pain, or hunger — is its functional role: the pattern of causal relations it has to sensory inputs, to other mental states, and to behavioral outputs. The same belief could in principle exist in a brain, a silicon circuit, or any system organised to play that role, because what matters is the role, not the material that implements it.

The view developed as a way between two earlier positions: type physicalism, which said each mental state just *is* a particular physical state ("pain = C-fiber firings"), and behaviorism, which defined mental states only as dispositions to behave. Functionalism accepts that mental states are realised by physical systems, but denies that any one physical type is essential.

The central supporting concept is **multiple realizability**. A valve can be plastic, brass, or steel and still control flow, because what makes it a valve is the function it performs, not its substance. By the same reasoning, functionalists claim that pain, belief, or perception could be realised by neurons, transistors, or alien biology, provided the relevant causal patterns are preserved. Hilary Putnam used this intuition in the 1960s, arguing that functionally identical silicon creatures would share our mental states.

Some theorists reject multiple realizability. Functional Specification Theories, developed by David Lewis and D.M. Armstrong, treat mental states as whatever brain process happens to realise a given role, so two realisers count as having different mental states even if they behave alike. Most functionalists disagree, holding that sameness of role is sameness of state.

Five main varieties of functionalism exist. **Machine-state functionalism** (Putnam) models mental states on Turing machine states: each state is defined entirely by what it does when it reads a symbol — print, move, halt, or transition to another state — so any system that cycles through such states counts. **Psycho-functionalism** (Fodor, Pylyshyn) treats mental-state terms as theoretical posits of cognitive psychology, like "heart" and "kidney" in biology; folk-psychological terms may refer to nothing, while cognitive science may discover states ordinary language never named. **Analytic functionalism** (Lewis) holds that the meanings of mental terms are fixed by the causal roles they occupy within folk-psychological theory, yielding necessary a priori identities between states and roles. **Homuncular functionalism** (Dennett, Lycan) responds to the China brain thought experiment by allowing a hierarchy of simpler "minds" inside a complex one, each implemented by less sophisticated agents below. **Mechanistic functionalism** (Piccinini, Gillett) requires that functional roles be explained by real physical mechanisms, not abstract input-output tables, bridging functionalism with neuroscience.

The main attacks on functionalism are thought experiments. In **Searle's Chinese room**, an English speaker shuffles Chinese symbols by rule and produces fluent replies without understanding Chinese; Searle concludes that symbol manipulation cannot yield semantics or intentionality (meaning or aboutness). In **Block's China brain**, an entire population wired up like neurons would, by functionalist lights, have a mind, which Block calls absurd. In the **inverted spectrum**, two people behave identically yet one might see red as the other sees blue, so functional identity leaves qualia (subjective feel) unexplained. **Twin Earth** argues that two functionally identical people could mean different things by "water" (H₂O versus XYZ), so functional role fails to fix mental content. David Chalmers responds with **organizational invariance**: any system with the same fine-grained functional organisation must have the same qualia, supported by his "dancing qualia" and "fading qualia" arguments.

A further complaint is that functionalism is either too narrow or too broad. If it demands strict identity with a brain type, it collapses into type physicalism and loses multiple realizability. If it accepts any system that maps inputs to outputs, it counts thermostats, economies, and rocks as minded, reducing to triviality or panpsychism (the view that mind is everywhere). Peter Godfrey-Smith argues these slides can be partly blocked by appealing to causal structure, but only at the cost of sliding toward behaviorism or identity theory.

In the 2020 PhilPapers survey, functionalism was the most popular theory of mind among philosophers: 33% accepted or leaned toward it, compared with 22% for dualism and 13% for identity theory. Its appeal is that it explains how creatures made of very different matter could nonetheless share beliefs, desires, and pains, by sharing the causal patterns those states consist in.
