# History of philosophy

The history of philosophy is the systematic study of how philosophical thought has developed as rational inquiry. It traces how core questions about reality, knowledge, ethics, and society have been explored across cultures, showing how ideas evolve, interact, and shape human understanding. The field has two sides: a historical side that contextualises ideas in their time, and a philosophical side that evaluates their truth and relevance. A key method is periodisation, the practice of dividing the long record into named eras so that patterns become visible. Modern scholarship extends this work beyond Europe to Arabic–Persian, Indian, Chinese, Japanese, African, and Latin American traditions.

## Western philosophy

Western philosophy begins in ancient Greece around the 6th century BCE. Presocratic thinkers searched for rational explanations of the cosmos, asking what underlying *arche* (first principle) generates the world. Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle (c. 469–322 BCE) then shifted attention to ethics, metaphysics, and logic, establishing the methods and topics that would dominate the tradition. The Hellenistic period (323 BCE – 31 BCE) saw schools such as Epicureanism, Stoicism, and Skepticism turn toward how to live well.

From the 4th to the 15th century, medieval Western philosophy centred on theology, working to reconcile Christian faith with Greek reason. *Scholasticism*, the dominant medieval method, used systematic dialectical inquiry and is epitomised by Thomas Aquinas's synthesis of Aristotelian philosophy with Christian doctrine.

The Renaissance (mid-14th to early 17th century) revived Greek learning and promoted humanism and scientific inquiry. The early modern period (17th–18th centuries) was shaped by the rivalry between *empiricism* (knowledge from sensory experience) and *rationalism* (knowledge from reason), which prepared the Enlightenment's challenge to traditional authority. The 19th century brought German idealism, utilitarianism, and pragmatism, and philosophy became a distinct academic discipline. In the 20th century the Western tradition split into two broad streams: *analytic philosophy*, which emphasises logic and language, and *continental philosophy*, which includes phenomenology, existentialism, and postmodernism.

## Arabic–Persian philosophy

Also called Islamic philosophy, this tradition peaked during the Islamic Golden Age (c. 9th–12th century CE). Building on Greek thought, thinkers such as Avicenna developed comprehensive systems synthesising Greek philosophy with Islamic faith, while Al-Ghazali's critique of the rationalists shifted later debate toward theology. The tradition preserved and transmitted Greek texts, directly influencing medieval Western philosophy.

## Indian philosophy

Indian philosophy is distinctive in joining metaphysics, epistemology, and the pursuit of spiritual liberation. Its roots lie in the Vedas, which generated *orthodox* (*āstika*) schools that accept Vedic authority, such as Vedanta, and *heterodox* (*nāstika*) schools that reject it, such as Buddhism. Central concepts include *Atman* (the eternal self), *Brahman* (ultimate reality), and *karma*. Classical schools developed sophisticated metaphysics and epistemology that ran in parallel with the Greek tradition; later thinkers have worked to harmonise Indian and Western approaches.

## Chinese philosophy

Early Chinese philosophy focused on ethics, social conduct, and self-cultivation. Confucianism emphasised moral virtues and social harmony, while Daoism focused on living in accord with the *Dao* (the Way) and nature, an ideal expressed through *wu wei*, or effortless action. Buddhism entered China and was later absorbed into *Neo-Confucianism*. The modern period brought sustained contact with Western thought, especially Marxism, producing new syntheses.

## A compact timeline

| Period | What happened |
| :--- | :--- |
| c. 6th c. BCE | Presocratics ask rational questions about the cosmos. |
| 469–322 BCE | Socrates, Plato, Aristotle found Western methods and topics. |
| 323–31 BCE | Hellenistic ethics-focused schools (Epicureanism, Stoicism, Skepticism). |
| 9th–12th c. CE | Arabic–Persian philosophy peaks; Greek texts preserved. |
| 4th–15th c. CE | Medieval Western philosophy: faith reconciled with reason. |
| Mid-14th – early 17th c. | Renaissance revives Greek learning; humanism and science rise. |
| 17th–18th c. | Empiricism vs. rationalism; the Enlightenment. |
| 19th c. | German idealism, utilitarianism, pragmatism; philosophy becomes academic. |
| 20th c. | Analytic and continental traditions diverge. |

## Live debates

Several questions remain unresolved. Neo-Kantians argue that philosophy is essentially historical, blurring the line between the history of philosophy and philosophy itself. Scholars dispute whether Arabic–Persian philosophy truly "declined" after its classical peak or simply shifted into theology. The boundary between orthodox and heterodox schools in Indian thought is itself contested, and traditional Western periodisations are increasingly seen as inadequate for a genuinely global picture of the discipline.
