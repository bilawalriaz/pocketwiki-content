# Sojourner Truth

Sojourner Truth (c. 1797–1883) was an American abolitionist whose activism linked the fight against slavery with the fight for women's rights. Born into slavery in New York, she escaped, reclaimed her son through the courts, and spent decades as a traveling preacher and orator arguing that race and gender together shaped the oppression of Black women.

## From slavery to freedom

Truth was born Isabella Bomefree in Swartekill, New York, to James and Elizabeth Bomefree, who had been captured in West Africa. Her first language was Dutch. After her father's death in 1806 she was sold repeatedly. Around 1815 the man she loved, a slave named Robert from a neighboring farm, was beaten for the relationship and soon died. She later married an enslaved man named Thomas and had five children.

New York began legislating slavery's abolition in 1799 and completed the process in 1827. Her owner, John Dumont, had promised to free her but reneged. In late 1826 she escaped with her infant daughter Sophia, leaving her other children behind because they were not yet legally free. The Van Wagenen family in New Paltz took her in. With their help she sued in 1828 for the return of her illegally sold son Peter and won, one of the first Black women to win a lawsuit against a white man.

## Public career

In 1843, feeling called by God to preach, she renamed herself Sojourner Truth and began traveling as a speaker against slavery. She joined the Northampton Association of Education and Industry, a utopian community in Massachusetts supporting abolition and women's rights, where she met William Lloyd Garrison and Frederick Douglass.

Her most famous moment came in 1851 at the Ohio Women's Rights Convention in Akron, where she delivered a short, improvised speech arguing that women deserved the same rights as men. A version, later titled "Ain't I a Woman?", was published in 1863 by Frances Gage. That text depicted Truth speaking in a Southern Black dialect and claimed she had borne thirteen children sold away. Both details are wrong: she had five children, and her first language was Dutch, so she would have spoken with a New York accent. An 1851 account by Marius Robinson, recorded closer to the event, is considered the more reliable record.

## Later years

During the Civil War, Truth helped recruit Black men for the Union Army and worked for the National Freedman's Relief Association, an organization that provided aid to formerly enslaved people. After the war she spent roughly seven years, from 1870 to 1877, lobbying the federal government for land grants for freed people, the idea behind the phrase "forty acres and a mule." She met President Abraham Lincoln in 1864 and later President Ulysses S. Grant, but Congress never acted. She continued advocating for women's suffrage and prison reform until her death in Battle Creek, Michigan, in 1883.

## Why she matters

Three threads connect her work. Her courtroom win over Peter showed that the law could be used against slaveholders, not only for them. Her speeches insisted that the rights of women and the rights of Black people could not be separated, a position later formalized as intersectionality, the idea that overlapping identities like race and gender create distinct forms of discrimination. Her failed lobbying campaign documented the federal government's retreat from the economic promises of emancipation, leaving formerly enslaved people without the land that might have secured their freedom in practice. In 2009 she became the first African American woman honored with a memorial bust in the U.S. Capitol Visitor Center.
