# Myth

A myth, to a scholar, is a sacred story a society treats as foundational. Whether the story is factually true is irrelevant: a narrative can be myth even if every word is invented, and a deliberate lie is not myth just because people believe it. The everyday English sense of "myth" as "falsehood" developed partly from labelling other cultures' religions as error and still creates friction when scholars apply the word to living faiths.

The term derives from Ancient Greek *mȳthos*, simply "speech" or "narrative." *Mythología* ("story-telling") entered Latin through Fulgentius's 5th-century *Mythologiæ* and reached English as "mythology" by c. 1425 in John Lydgate's *Troy Book*. The singular "myth" was first recorded in English only in 1830. Related terms: **mythography** (compiling and describing myths, as Ovid or Snorri Sturluson did), **mythos** (a body of interconnected myths, or an Aristotelian plot point), and **mythopoeia** (Tolkien's term for consciously inventing a mythology).

## What separates myth from legend and folktale

Myths typically feature gods, demigods, or other non-human protagonists and are set in a remote primordial past. They explain origins (cosmogony, the source of customs and institutions), sanctify rituals, and supply behavioural models. Legends usually centre on human heroes in a historical setting, though the border is soft: the *Iliad* and *Aeneid* shift between categories, and real historical kernels (the cycles known as the Matter of Britain or the Matter of France) can mythologise over centuries. Folktales, including fairy tales, are widely recognised as fiction even by their tellers.

## Major interpretive traditions

No single theory has won general acceptance. The main currents:

- **Euhemerism** (Euhemerus, c. 320 BCE): myths are distorted memories of real humans later deified. The wind-god Aeolus is read as a king who taught sailors to read the winds.
- **Allegory and nature mythology** (Max Müller, Edward Burnett Tylor, late 19th century): myths began as symbolic descriptions of natural forces (Apollo as sun) or ideas (Athena as wisdom), later literalised. Müller's "disease of language" and Tylor's animism have been largely abandoned for lack of evidence.
- **Ritualism** (William Robertson Smith, James George Frazer): myths arose to explain rituals whose original purpose was forgotten. Frazer framed a progression from magic through religion to science.
- **Functionalism** (Bronisław Malinowski, Mircea Eliade, Lauri Honko): what matters is what a myth does. Malinowski's "mythic charter" holds that origin stories legitimise present social norms; Eliade argued that ritual re-enactment returns participants to a sacred primordial age.
- **Structuralism** (Claude Lévi-Strauss, mid-20th century): myths encode fixed mental structures, especially binary oppositions such as life/death or nature/culture, recurring across cultures.
- **Psychoanalysis** (Sigmund Freud's Oedipus complex, 1899; Carl Jung's universal archetypes): myths express deep psychic contents.
- **Post-structuralism** (Roland Barthes, 1950s): "myth" is a mode of signification visible in modern popular culture, not only ancient religion.
- **Postmodernism and hylistics** (A. K. Ramanujan; Annette and Christian Zgoll, late 20th–21st century): myths are inherently plastic narrative material, the German *Erzählstoff*, with no single original version. The Zgolls' hylistics extracts minimal standardised plot units, called *hylemes*, so versions in different media can be compared formally.

The field remains divided between those seeking universal structures (Joseph Campbell, Eliade) and those insisting on cultural specificity, and between questions of origin and questions of function.

## Myth in modern and digital media

Mythological material is no longer confined to ancient texts. Blockbusters and franchise films reach global audiences with mythic archetypes packaged as cyberpunk and fantasy, and Disney "reinvents" childhood myths. Authors such as Rick Riordan transplant classical myth into modern settings. Fan-studies scholars, including Ika Willis and Henry Jenkins, treat fan fiction as contemporary myth-making: fans reclaim corporate-owned stories, critique dominant power structures, and build hyperconnected "universal story worlds." Sarah Iles Johnston calls this *hyperseriality*, meaning interwoven narrative universes where characters and plots from many sources cross over. Jenkins argues that fan fiction responds to a culture whose major myths are corporate property rather than folk property, and digital platforms have accelerated the shift from text to transmedial *Erzählstoff*.
