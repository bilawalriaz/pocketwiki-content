# Worldview

A worldview (German *Weltanschauung*, "wide world perception") is the fundamental cognitive orientation an individual or society uses to interpret reality. It blends knowledge, culture, point-of-view, values, and emotions into a single frame that shapes what its holder perceives, assumes, and concludes. When two people observe the same event but hold different worldviews, they can draw incompatible conclusions from the same evidence.

## The term and its history

*Weltanschauung* combines *Welt* ("world") and *Anschauung* ("perception" or "view"). The word is often wrongly credited to Wilhelm von Humboldt, whose key concept was actually *Weltansicht*, the overarching apprehension of reality shared by a linguistic community. Immanuel Kant introduced *Weltanschauung* and Hegel later popularized it, where it refers to philosophies, ideologies, and cultural or religious perspectives rather than to linguistic communities.

In 1911 Wilhelm Dilthey published "The Types of Weltanschauung and their Development in Metaphysics," in which he argued that worldviews integrate the cognitive, evaluative, and volitional aspects of human experience. He distinguished three recurring types: naturalism (perception and experiment determine what is real; contingency shapes evaluation), the idealism of freedom (reality is dualistic, and the freedom of the will and the mind's ordering activity are primary), and objective idealism (the ideal inheres in the actual, so reality is ultimately monistic and internally coherent). Dilthey treated these as useful schemas rather than systems that could be proved universally valid.

## What a worldview contains

Leo Apostel treated a worldview as a descriptive model, or ontology, of the world, and argued that it should contain six elements: an explanation of the world; a futurology; values and ethical answers; a praxeology, or theory of action; an epistemology, or theory of knowledge; and an etiology that accounts for the worldview's own origins and construction.

A worldview can also be understood as a set of basic beliefs that function like axioms of a logical theory. These axioms cannot be proved within the worldview itself, since they are argued *from* rather than argued *for*, though their coherence can be examined philosophically.

## Comparison and relativism

When two worldviews share enough common beliefs, constructive dialogue between them is possible. When they are treated as incommensurable and irreconcilable, the situation collapses into cultural relativism, which philosophical realists standardly criticize and many religious believers resist, since it reduces their beliefs to something "only true for them."

A third alternative treats the worldview approach as methodological relativism: suspending judgment about the truth of competing belief systems without denying that a global truth might exist. Ninian Smart called this "worldview analysis," the neutral, dispassionate study of religious and secular systems. Clément Vidal has proposed metaphilosophical criteria for comparing worldviews in three categories: objective consistency (scientific validity and scope), subjective consistency (personal utility and emotional satisfaction), and intersubjective consistency (collective utility and narrative coherence).

## How worldviews are shaped

Anthropologically, worldviews are the fundamental cognitive, affective, and evaluative presuppositions a group makes about the nature of things, and they are shaped by political borders and shared history, geography and climate, economic resources, socio-cultural systems, and language family. A map drawn on the basis of *Weltanschauung* would therefore cut across political borders.

Underhill proposed five subcategories for studying worldview: world-perceiving, world-conceiving, cultural mindset, personal world, and perspective.

## Terror management

In terror management theory, a worldview functions as a buffer against the anxiety produced by awareness of one's own mortality. Living up to its ideals produces self-esteem, which offers a sense of transcending human limits, whether literally (belief in immortality), symbolically (art or children), or through contributions to one's culture.

Experiments by Jeff Schimel and colleagues support this. Highly patriotic Canadians who read an essay attacking the dominant Canadian worldview showed higher death-thought accessibility than a control group who read a similar essay attacking Australian values, with no significant change in mood. A follow-up with creationists who read a pro-evolution essay produced the same effect. Goldenberg and colleagues found that highlighting humans' similarity to other animals, or focusing on the physical rather than meaningful qualities of sex, also raises death-thought accessibility.

## Religion and worldview

Religious practices tie closely to a religion's worldview. James W. Sire defines a worldview as a commitment, a fundamental orientation of the heart, expressed as a story or as a set of presuppositions about the basic construction of reality, held consciously or not, that provides the foundation on which people live. David Naugle argues that conceiving of Christianity as a worldview has been one of the most significant developments in the recent history of the church. David Bell has extended the question to designers of superintelligences, asking what religious worldviews imply for machines much smarter than humans.
