# Degrees of freedom (statistics)

In statistics, the **degrees of freedom** (d.f.) of an estimate is the number of independent pieces of information used in it, minus the number of parameters estimated as intermediate steps. To estimate a sample variance from N independent observations, you first compute the sample mean, which "uses up" one piece of information. That leaves N − 1 independent values free to vary, so the variance has N − 1 degrees of freedom.

Geometrically, degrees of freedom is the dimension of the subspace a random vector is allowed to occupy. A sample of N independent observations forms an N-dimensional vector that can point anywhere in N-dimensional space, so it has N d.f. Once the sample mean is fixed, the residuals (each observation minus the mean) must sum to zero and are confined to an (N − 1)-dimensional subspace. The sum of squared residuals has a scaled chi-squared distribution with N − 1 d.f., which is why the one-sample t-statistic follows Student's t with N − 1 d.f., and why the Bessel-corrected sample variance divides by N − 1.

The same logic extends to linear models. In simple regression, fitting an intercept and a slope consumes two parameters, leaving N − 2 d.f. for the residuals. In multiple regression with p estimated coefficients, the residuals have N − p d.f. In a one-way ANOVA comparing three groups, the total sum of squares splits into a model part with 2 d.f. (three group means constrained to sum to the grand mean) and a residual part with 3(N − 1) d.f., producing an F-statistic on 2 and 3N − 3 d.f. Under the null hypothesis of equal population means, these sums of squares have scaled chi-squared distributions, and their ratio follows the F-distribution with those d.f. The chi-squared, t, and F distributions all take d.f. as a parameter that records the dimension of the underlying vector subspace.

Degrees of freedom is denoted by the Greek letter ν (nu) in equations and "d.f." in text. R. A. Fisher used n for d.f., but modern usage reserves n for sample size. The concept was recognised by Carl Friedrich Gauss in 1821, developed by William Sealy Gosset ("Student") in his 1908 *Biometrika* paper on the t-distribution, and named by Ronald Fisher in 1922.

When ordinary least-squares projection is not available, as in ridge regression, smoothing splines, or k-nearest-neighbour smoothing, the geometric d.f. is no longer directly useful. The standard replacement is **effective degrees of freedom**, defined as the trace of the hat matrix H. Geometrically H is the projection matrix onto the fitted subspace; its trace is the sum of the leverage scores, the sensitivities of fitted values to observed values. For a k-nearest-neighbour smoother, each fitted value is the average of k observations, so tr(H) = N/k, giving N/k effective d.f. In structural equation modelling, d.f. equals the number of knowns (the d(d+1)/2 non-redundant elements of the sample covariance matrix) minus the number of free parameters. In complex survey sampling, the d.f. of the variance estimator is the total number of primary sampling units minus the number of strata.
