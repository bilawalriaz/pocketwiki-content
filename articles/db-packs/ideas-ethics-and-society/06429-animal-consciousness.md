# Animal consciousness

Animal consciousness is the capacity of non-human animals to have subjective experience: awareness, feeling, and a sense of self. The topic is scientifically unresolved and crosses philosophy, neuroscience, ethology, and ethics.

## Defining the terms

Consciousness has no single agreed definition. Roughly forty distinct uses appear in the literature, splitting into functions (attention, wakefulness, self-monitoring) and experiences (qualia, the raw feel of perception). In humans it is described as sentience, awareness, subjectivity, the ability to feel, wakefulness, a sense of selfhood, and the executive control system of the mind. Most philosophers accept a shared intuition about it despite the lack of a precise definition.

Two narrower terms recur. *Sentience* is the capacity to feel or perceive. *Self-awareness* is recognising oneself as an individual. An animal can feel pain without passing any test of self-recognition, so sentience and self-awareness are not the same thing.

## The problem of other minds

Because animals cannot use human language to report their inner states, the question of whether they are conscious poses the "problem of other minds" sharply. Thomas Nagel argued in *What Is It Like to Be a Bat?* that an organism is conscious if there is something it is like to be that organism, and that no amount of external observation of brain or behaviour lets us share its perspective. Critics such as Douglas Hofstadter call this argument incoherent. Walter Veit's 2023 review *A Philosophy for the Science of Animal Consciousness* compiles behavioural evidence that animals hold beliefs about things they cannot directly perceive.

## Historical positions

Descartes held Cartesian dualism: humans alone possess a non-physical mind, and animals are unconscious machines whose behaviour is purely mechanical. The view has been criticised for enabling animal mistreatment.

*Epiphenomenalism*, developed after Descartes by La Mettrie, T. H. Huxley, and others, holds that mental phenomena are caused by physical brain processes but cause nothing themselves. Huxley compared consciousness to the whistle on a steam locomotive, present but causally inert. William James and Karl Popper countered that if consciousness is a product of natural selection, it must have survival value, and so must be causally efficacious.

Behaviourism (Pavlov, Watson, Skinner) avoided inner states entirely, but gave way to the cognitive revolution by the 1960s.

Gerald Edelman's neural Darwinism distinguishes *primary consciousness*, the present-moment sensory awareness likely shared by many animals, from *secondary consciousness*, the awareness of one's own past and future, with self-reflection and language, seen in its richest form in humans.

## Scientific approaches

Researchers search for *neural correlates of consciousness*, the minimal brain events sufficient for a given conscious experience. Two findings have shifted the debate. The substrates of emotion and conscious states are not confined to the cortex; subcortical networks are centrally involved and are shared across vertebrates. Birds, despite lacking a neocortex, possess a pallium functionally analogous to the mammalian cortex and show REM-like sleep and other neural signatures once thought to require mammalian tissue.

A 2020 framework by Birch, Schnell, and Clayton proposes that consciousness varies along five dimensions rather than a single scale: perceptual richness, evaluative richness (the complexity of affective states), unity, temporality (integration across time through memory and anticipation), and selfhood.

## Behavioural evidence

The *mirror self-recognition test* marks an animal where it cannot see directly; directing grooming behaviour toward the mark in a mirror is taken as self-awareness. Passers include great apes, Asian elephants, bottlenose dolphins, killer whales, Eurasian magpies, and (in a 2019 study) cleaner wrasses. A 2015 "sniff test of self-recognition" suggests dogs meet a species-appropriate version of the criterion, since they rely on smell more than vision.

The boundary of pain-feeling is disputed. John Webster argues that suffering does not depend on intelligence or brain size. Peter Singer places the line somewhere between shrimp and oysters; Michel de Montaigne extended consciousness to insects and even plants.

Cognitive bias (optimistic or pessimistic judgements in ambiguous situations) has been documented in rats, dogs, rhesus macaques, sheep, chicks, starlings, and honeybees. Metacognition (monitoring one's own knowledge) has been shown in rhesus monkeys and apes, with rat evidence debated and bird evidence inconclusive. Tool use, play, deception, and observational learning in octopuses, corvids, and grey parrots indicate flexible, experience-based cognition.

## The two declarations

The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness (2012), signed by a group of neuroscientists, states that humans are not unique in possessing the neurological substrates of consciousness: all mammals, all birds, and many other creatures including octopuses share them, and near human-like levels of consciousness have been reported in grey parrots.

The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness (2024), signed by over 500 scientists, extends this. There is strong scientific support for consciousness in mammals and birds, and a realistic possibility in all vertebrates (including reptiles, amphibians, and fishes) and many invertebrates, including at minimum cephalopod mollusks, decapod crustaceans, and insects. It holds that when a realistic possibility of conscious experience exists, ignoring it in decisions affecting the animal is irresponsible.

## Distinctions that prevent confusion

Consciousness is not self-awareness: feeling pain does not require passing the mirror test. Consciousness is not language: extended consciousness does not require it. A neocortex is not required: birds and many invertebrates show relevant neural and behavioural markers. The mirror test is vision-biased, so species that rely on other senses need different tests.

The live empirical frontier is where conscious experience falls among invertebrates, fish, and reptiles. Recent declarations have shifted the burden of proof from "demonstrate that this animal is conscious" toward "consider the realistic possibility that it is."
