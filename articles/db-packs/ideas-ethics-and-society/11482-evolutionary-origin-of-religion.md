# Evolutionary origin of religion

Religion, in its modern sense, is a human trait. Its origins lie in three layered changes that made earlier humans uniquely capable of believing in unseen agents, holding collective rituals, and recording shared doctrine. Each layer built on the capacities that came before.

## Building blocks in other species and early humans

Chimpanzees and bonobos, our closest relatives, share a common ancestor with humans that lived six to eight million years ago. They exhibit the raw materials religion later needed: high intelligence, symbolic communication, a sense of social norms, and self-recognition. Barbara King argues these traits were prerequisites but never add up to actual religion in non-humans.

Elephants display mourning at death sites and later caress the remains of the dead. Elephants, dolphins, whales, and the great apes also share pre-moral sentiments with humans: attachment, cooperation, sympathy, reciprocity, conflict resolution, and awareness of group reputation. Frans de Waal concludes that morality grew out of primate sociality rather than appearing suddenly in humans.

## Cognitive prerequisites

Three neural and behavioral capacities set the stage.

Expanded brain and neocortex. The hominid brain tripled in size, peaking about 500,000 years ago. In chimpanzees the neocortex (the outer brain layer used for complex thought) occupies 50% of the brain; in modern humans it occupies 80%. Robin Dunbar's regression analysis places the threshold for processing language and religion at the speciation of archaic *Homo sapiens*, around 500,000 years ago. Dunbar's theory links neocortex size to maximum group size, which is why larger brains could support larger, more coordinated societies.

Tool use and causality. Lewis Wolpert argues that making a multi-part tool, such as a hand axe, forces the maker to imagine a finished object and understand cause and effect. Chimps also show some causal understanding (escaping multi-latched pens) but to a lesser degree tied to a smaller prefrontal cortex (the brain region behind planning and self-control).

Language as symbolic trust. Philip Lieberman holds that religious thought rests on a cognitive-linguistic base. Nicholas Wade concludes that, if religion requires modern articulate language, it must have emerged shortly before the dispersal from Africa about 50,000 years ago. The "ritual/speech coevolution" school argues that words are cheap signals, easy to fake, so they only become reliable inside a society that builds public trust through collective ritual. This is why anthropologists see ritual, not grammar alone, as the bedrock that let language and religion co-evolve.

## Evolutionary psychology of the religious mind

There are two main schools. One holds that religion evolved by natural selection because it boosts group survival. The other, advanced by Stephen Jay Gould, treats religion as an exaptation (a trait repurposed from an earlier function) or spandrel (a byproduct of other adaptations), specifically agent detection (the tendency to sense hidden intentional beings behind ambiguous events), causal storytelling, and theory of mind (understanding that others have their own beliefs and intentions). These three capacities let early humans posit purposeful agents behind thunder, disease, or death, and collective religion later standardized those agents as named deities.

A separate view, the triune brain model proposed by Paul D. MacLean, separates the reptilian brain (basic survival drives), the limbic system (emotions such as love, fear, and belonging), and the neocortex (reason). Collective religion draws on limbic emotions and social enforcement; individual belief draws more on neocortex-based reasoning.

## Prehistoric evidence

The oldest clear evidence of religious behavior is ritual burial. Most animals ignore their dead. Intentional burial signals awareness of death and, often, grave goods, items placed with the body, hint at belief in an afterlife.

Key sites:
- Atapuerca, Spain: bones of about 30 *Homo heidelbergensis* individuals in a pit.
- Shanidar (Iraq), Krapina (Croatia), Kebara Cave (Israel): Neanderthal burials with stone tools, animal bones, and (at Shanidar) medicinal plants.
- Qafzeh, Israel: earliest known modern-human burial, dated to 100,000 years ago, with red ocher staining and a wild-boar mandible placed in the skeleton's arms.

Symbolic capacity appears with the use of red ocher pigments in Africa from at least 100,000 years ago. Pigments have no obvious practical use, so they are read as symbolic or ritual. Upper Paleolithic cave art at Chauvet, depicting half-human, half-animal beings, is the clearest early evidence of religious imagination.

Matt Rossano singles out 80,000 to 60,000 years ago, after humans retreated from the Levant back into Africa, as a critical window for the evolution of religion.

## Organized religion and the Neolithic transition

Religion became organized when societies did. Agriculture, beginning about 11,000 years ago in the Near East, transformed small bands of related individuals into chiefdoms, and then states and empires with thousands of unrelated people. Jared Diamond argues that organized religion bonded these strangers together, since murder is the leading cause of death in hunter-gatherer societies. Moralizing gods, deities who reward cooperation and punish free-riders, helped unrelated individuals trust each other. Ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia were outright theocracies, with rulers doubling as political and spiritual leaders.

Writing, invented in Sumer or Egypt around 5,000 years ago, first for accounting and soon for myth, gave religion a stable medium. The Egyptian Pyramid Texts, dated 2400–2300 BCE, are among the oldest known religious writings. Writing let doctrines accumulate, survive across generations, and spread without distortion, turning oral tradition into sustained institutional religion.
