# Belief

A belief is a subjective mental attitude that treats a proposition as true, whether or not the person is actively thinking about it. Because beliefs are the primary bearers of truth-value, they are the building blocks of knowledge. The classical definition, from Plato's *Theaetetus* and canonised during the Enlightenment, is "justified true belief": you know *P* only if *P* is true, you believe it, and you have good reason to hold it.

## What a belief actually is

Philosophers disagree about what kind of thing a belief is, and each view changes what counts as a correct explanation of behavior.

Representationalists, following Jerry Fodor, treat beliefs as discrete mental sentences written in a private "language of thought," combining syntactically much like English sentences. The rival *map conception* keeps the representational idea but pictures the mind as a continuous, geometrically organised map rather than a string of sentences.

Functionalists ignore the inner format and define a belief by what it does: it is caused by perception and causes action. By this standard, an alien, a robot, or another human could share your beliefs, since the same causal role matters, not the physical substrate. A sharper version, *dispositionalism*, identifies a belief with a behavioral pattern: believing the pie is in the pantry disposes you to look there when hungry. Critics note that no single belief fixes a unique behavior; someone who knows the pie is poisoned will not walk toward it.

Interpretationists (Dennett, Davidson) treat belief as something ascribed rather than detected. Dennett's *intentional stance* treats any system whose behavior is predictably guided by goals and information as if it had beliefs and desires, because that is the most efficient way to forecast its actions. The stance can be realist (ascribed beliefs are causally real) or instrumentalist (ascription is merely useful).

*Eliminativism* (Paul and Patricia Churchland) denies that the folk concept of belief will survive. It is a prescientific placeholder, like *phlogiston*, that mature neuroscience will replace with a vocabulary of brain states.

## The shape of belief

*Occurrent* beliefs are actively in mind; *dispositional* beliefs are stored and activated when relevant. Most beliefs, such as that 57 is greater than 14, sit in storage until needed. The split is not conscious/unconscious: an occurrent belief can be unconscious and still drive reasoning.

*Full* beliefs are all-or-nothing. *Partial* beliefs, or *credences*, are degrees of confidence, formalised in Bayesian epistemology as probabilities between 0 and 1. The *Lockean thesis* reduces full belief to credence above a threshold such as 0.9; opponents reverse the reduction, treating a 90% credence as a full belief about a probability.

*Belief-that* is propositional and can be true or false. *Belief-in* is trust or commitment in a person, ideal, or institution, such as belief in God, and may not reduce cleanly to a claim about the world. *De dicto* belief is sensitive to wording (Lois believes Superman is strong but not Clark Kent, because she does not know they are identical), while *de re* belief is transparent to reference (Lois does believe of Clark Kent that he is strong, because he is Superman).

*Collective* beliefs are not the sum of individual beliefs. Margaret Gilbert analyses them as joint commitments to accept a proposition as a body, and Durkheim treated them as social facts that constrain members. Collective beliefs enforce group boundaries through accusations of heresy or deviation.

## What determines the content of a belief

*Atomism* says each belief's content is independent. *Holism* (Quine) says any belief's content depends on the entire web, so learning the concept of force requires the concept of mass. Holism explains why belief systems are hard to revise but threatens to make genuine disagreement impossible, since two people never inhabit the exact same web.

*Internalism* says a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of you would have all your beliefs, since content is fixed by inner states. *Externalism*, defended by Putnam's twin Earth thought experiment, says content depends on environment: "water is wet" refers to H₂O on Earth and to XYZ on twin Earth, even though the two inhabitants are internally identical. Critics counter that this external content produces no behavioral difference, so its explanatory value is unclear.

## Knowledge, justification, and Gettier

Edmund Gettier's 1963 paper broke the justified-true-belief account. Looking at a stopped clock at the exact moment it shows the right time gives a justified true belief that is only true by luck. Responses include Nozick's *tracking condition* (you would not still believe *P* if it were false) and *reliabilism* (the belief must be produced by a generally reliable process).

## Belief systems

Jonathan Glover pictures belief systems as boat-like networks, mutually supporting, where no plank holds up alone. Revising one belief requires adjusting others, and the system resists total change because enough must stay intact to remain afloat. This is why homeopathy, alien abduction theories, and political ideologies survive disconfirming evidence, and why Descartes, after doubting everything, rebuilt a recognisably 17th-century French worldview. People protect threatened beliefs with ad hoc hypotheses, and awareness that beliefs are contingent generally emerges around ages 12–15.

## Religion as a belief subsystem

Religious belief is a codified subset, often tied to identity, morality, and existential security. Common forms include *fundamentalism* (strict scriptural adherence), *orthodoxy* (adherence to a governing authority such as a Magisterium), and *modernism or reform* (integrating Enlightenment rationality, as in Reform Judaism). Attitudes toward other faiths range from *exclusivism* (one true faith) to *inclusivism* (truth in all), *pluralism* (each valid in its own culture), and *syncretism* (blending traditions). People leave religion for incompatible doctrines, moral objections, or religious violence.

## Belief in psychology

Mainstream psychology treats belief as a basic mental representation, the unit folk psychology uses to predict behavior. Lynne Rudder Baker sorted philosophical responses into four: *mental sentence theory* (Fodor: folk psychology is roughly right), *instrumentalism* (Stich: predictively useful but ultimately false), *eliminativism* (Churchlands: replace it with neuroscience), and *intentional stance* (Dennett, Baker: useful as a predictive level, not literally real).

Beliefs form through conditional inference, weighted combination of factors, and information processing at encoding. Influences include childhood internalisation, charismatic leaders, advertising's repetition and emotion, and physical trauma. Robert Wyer's subjective probability model treats belief ratings as probabilities and works well for single events. The DSM-5 defines *delusions* as fixed false beliefs resistant to conflicting evidence. A 2018 study estimated that belief in witchcraft ranges from 9% to 90% across nations, correlating with lower innovation, higher anxiety, and higher religiosity. Emotion is integral to forming and holding beliefs.
