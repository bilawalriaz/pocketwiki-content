# Normative ethics

Normative ethics is the branch of philosophical ethics that asks how one ought to act in a moral sense. It investigates standards for judging actions, rules, and character as right or wrong, rather than describing what people happen to believe is right.

## Distinguishing the field

Normative ethics sits between three neighbouring disciplines:

- **Meta-ethics** studies the meaning of moral language and the metaphysics of moral facts, not the standards themselves.
- **Applied ethics** addresses concrete issues such as whether abortion is acceptable; normative ethics works one level up, asking what kind of person one ought to be.
- **Descriptive ethics** empirically surveys people's actual moral beliefs.

For this reason normative ethics is sometimes called *prescriptive* ethics. Some moral realists, however, hold that moral facts are simultaneously descriptive and prescriptive at the same time.

## The three main approaches

Most classical moral theories rest on overarching principles that decide hard cases. Contemporary debate centres on three families, with hybrids between them.

**Virtue ethics** focuses on the inherent character of the actor rather than on specific acts, asking what kind of person one should be. Aristotle developed the classical version; Saint Thomas Aquinas extended it. After a long dormancy the approach was revived from the 1950s onward by G. E. M. Anscombe, Philippa Foot, Alasdair MacIntyre, and Rosalind Hursthouse.

**Deontological ethics** ties morality to duties and rights, judging the action itself rather than its consequences. Three influential forms:

- *Kantian deontology*: Immanuel Kant's categorical imperative roots morality in human rational capacity and asserts inviolable moral laws.
- *Contractualism*: John Rawls argues that moral acts are those everyone would agree to from behind a "veil of ignorance," a position in which no one knows their place in society.
- *Natural rights theories*: John Locke and Robert Nozick hold that human beings possess absolute, natural rights.

**Consequentialism** judges actions by their outcomes, asking which act produces the best result. Theories disagree about what outcome is valuable, a question studied under the name *axiology*. Major variants:

- *Utilitarianism*: an action is right if it produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number. Until G. E. M. Anscombe coined "consequentialism" in 1958, "utilitarianism" was the generic label for any outcome-based theory, covering all views that promoted maximising some form of utility.
- *State or Mohist consequentialism*: right actions promote order, material wealth, and population growth.
- *Situational ethics*: evaluates each act in its context; Christian versions identify the most loving result as the right one.
- *Intellectualism*: the best action most fosters knowledge.
- *Welfarism*: the best action most increases economic well-being.
- *Preference utilitarianism*: the best action maximises overall preference satisfaction.

## Other theories

- **Social contract theory**: morality rests on voluntary agreements whose terms bind while in force.
- **Ethics of care**, founded by feminist theorists, notably Carol Gilligan, grounds morality in empathy, compassion, and interdependence.
- **Pragmatic ethics**: moral norms evolve socially over time and improve through inquiry. Charles Sanders Peirce, William James, and John Dewey are its founders; James D. Wallace is a more recent proponent.
- **Role ethics**: morality is built from family roles.

## Whether morality can bind us

Beneath the theories sits a stubborn question: what does it mean to say a person "ought" to do something moral even when they do not want to? Anscombe warned that the moral "ought" had become "a word of mere mesmeric force." Philippa Foot argued that morality has no special binding power; people act morally only when other motivations, such as shame or love, make the demand feel inescapable. Kant maintained that proper reason alone always issues in moral behaviour; Foot countered that reason typically serves *hypothetical imperatives*, the practical means of getting what one wants, rather than moral ones.

John Stuart Mill added that moral behaviour rests on conscience, an internalised feeling shaped by upbringing and by social pressures such as the desire to please others. Humans must first reason about what is moral, then bring feeling into line with reason. Mill grounded this in the social feelings of mankind, the desire for unity with fellow creatures, which he thought civilisation strengthens. He conceded that some people, such as psychopaths, may lack these feelings entirely, so societies must nurture the moral sentiments through upbringing.
