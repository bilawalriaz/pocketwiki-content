# Western philosophy

Western philosophy is the tradition of systematic argument that began in ancient Greece and continues today. It asks what exists, what we can know, how we should reason, and how to live. The word "philosophy" comes from the Greek *philosophia*, "love of wisdom," and the tradition relies on reasoned argument rather than revealed authority or myth.

## The ancient Greek origin

Pre-Socratic thinkers (from c. 625 BC) asked what the *arche*, the one underlying principle of the cosmos, actually is. Thales answered water, Anaximander the boundless (*apeiron*), Anaximenes air, Pythagoras number and proportion, Heraclitus perpetual change, and Parmenides a single unchanging being. Zeno's paradoxes defended Parmenides by showing that the appearance of motion leads to contradiction. Democritus and Leucippus proposed atomism: reality consists of indivisible particles moving in empty space, an early materialist answer.

Socrates (executed 399 BC) turned philosophy inward, using persistent questioning, the Socratic method, to expose the gap between confident opinions and actual knowledge. His student Plato founded the Academy (387 BC) and argued that the visible world is a shadow of a higher realm of Forms. Aristotle, Plato's student, founded the Peripatetic school and built a more empirical system: logic, biology, politics, and a metaphysics built on four causes (material, formal, efficient, final) and an unmoved mover that sets the cosmos in motion without itself changing.

Hellenistic schools, including Stoicism, Epicureanism, Skepticism, and Cynicism, asked how a person should live. Their common target was *eudaimonia* (flourishing), typically reached through *ataraxia* (tranquility) and, for Stoics, *apatheia* (freedom from destructive passion). Pyrrhonian Skeptics pushed further: suspend judgment entirely and you escape distress. The Pyrrhonian method was influenced by Buddhist ideas encountered during Alexander's eastern campaigns.

## Medieval synthesis with Christianity

Philosophy in the West was largely reorganised around Christian theology. Augustine used Platonic ideas to address evil, time, and free will. The dominant method became Scholasticism, a rigorous dialectical argument by contradiction and resolution. Anselm of Canterbury tried to prove God exists from the concept of God alone (the ontological argument). Thomas Aquinas synthesised Aristotle with Christian doctrine, offering five ways to prove God's existence and treating the soul as the essence of a human being. Islamic philosophers Avicenna and Averroes, and the Jewish thinker Maimonides, kept Greek texts alive and extended them during this period. Renaissance humanism, centred on figures like Petrarch, redirected attention from metaphysics to ethics, literature, and the dignity of the individual, bridging the medieval and modern periods.

## The modern turn

Modern philosophy (roughly 1600–1900) loosened the grip of religious authority and joined the rise of science. Descartes began with universal doubt and arrived at *cogito ergo sum* ("I think, therefore I am") as the one indubitable starting point. Hobbes and Locke rebuilt political philosophy from a hypothetical "state of nature." British empiricists Locke, Berkeley, and Hume argued that all knowledge comes from sensory experience, undermining the rationalist claim that reason alone reveals the structure of reality. Spinoza identified God with nature as one substance; Leibniz built a rationalist system of monads.

Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) is the hinge of the modern period. He argued that we can know phenomena (things as they appear) but not noumena (things in themselves), because the mind structures experience using a priori categories such as causality. This gave rationalism and empiricism defined roles. Hegel's *Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807) then built reality out of thought, arguing that history is the unfolding of ideas through a dialectical process in which contradictions are resolved at higher levels. Marx turned Hegel's idealism upside down: material economic conditions, not ideas, drive history, and class struggle is the engine of change.

Late modern philosophy diversified: utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill) judged actions by their consequences for happiness; pragmatism (Peirce, James, Dewey) judged ideas by their practical effects; existentialism (Kierkegaard, Nietzsche) focused on individual choice, authenticity, and the absence of given meaning. Frege and Boole revolutionised logic, setting the stage for the 20th century.

## The 20th-century split

By 1900, with Husserl's *Logical Investigations* and Russell's *Principles of Mathematics*, philosophy fractured into two main traditions.

Analytic philosophy, dominant in the English-speaking world, prizes logical rigour and the analysis of language. The Vienna Circle's logical positivism held that only statements verifiable by experience or logic are meaningful. Wittgenstein, Austin, Quine, Kripke, and Davidson attacked and revised that picture, asking how words refer and how meaning is fixed.

Continental philosophy, rooted in German idealism and Husserl's phenomenology (the study of how things appear to conscious experience), includes Heidegger and Sartre on existence, Gadamer on interpretation, the Frankfurt School on power and ideology, and Foucault and Derrida on how knowledge and language shape social reality. It foregrounds subjectivity, power, and meaning in ways analytic philosophy often treats as outside its scope. Whitehead's process philosophy, a third strand, treats reality as fundamentally dynamic and relational rather than made of static substances.

## Enduring debates

Several unresolved questions run through the entire tradition. The mind-body problem asks how conscious experience relates to physical processes; physicalists, dualists, and panpsychists still disagree. Free will versus determinism pits moral responsibility against a causally closed universe. Epistemology debates internalism (justification is internal to the thinker) against externalism (it depends on reliable connection to the world). Moral philosophy cannot settle whether values are objective. Political philosophy continues to argue about justice, rights, and the legitimate role of the state. The analytic-continental divide still shapes which questions count as central and which methods are acceptable.
