# Neuroscience of free will

Neuroscience has challenged the intuition that conscious will causes action. Across experiments since the 1980s, brain activity that precedes a voluntary movement often begins before the person feels they have decided to move. The field studies two related topics: **volition**, how actions are initiated, and the **sense of agency**, the feeling of being the author of one's movements. Many researchers now argue that the conscious "I am deciding now" experience is a readout of brain processes already underway, not their cause.

## The readiness potential

The central finding is the **readiness potential** (German *Bereitschaftspotential*, BP), a slow rise in electrical activity over the motor cortex, discovered by Kornhuber and Deecke in 1965 and visible on EEG. It begins hundreds of milliseconds before any muscle movement, even a simple finger flick. In the 1980s Benjamin Libet had subjects watch a clock and report the moment they *felt* the urge to move. The BP started about **half a second** before that reported moment, suggesting the brain prepared the action unconsciously before the subject became aware of deciding.

Later work extended the gap. In 2011 Itzhak Fried recorded from individual neurons in epilepsy patients with implanted electrodes and found neurons firing **700 ms** before the reported "will" to act. A 2008 fMRI study by Soon, Brass, Heinze, and Haynes used machine learning to predict which of two buttons a subject would press up to **10 seconds** before the reported choice, at 60% accuracy. A 2013 follow-up predicted abstract decisions (add versus subtract) up to **4 seconds** in advance.

## How the interpretation has been challenged

Several objections have weakened the simple "unconscious decides first" reading:

- **Timing of the report.** Daniel Dennett argued that shifting attention from the felt intention to the clock distorts the reported time. The exact delay varies with attention, though the direction (brain activity precedes awareness) has held.
- **W is partly reconstructed.** Banks and Isham showed in 2009 that the reported moment W shifts with information received *after* the action, implying W is partly built in retrospect rather than read off an internal clock.
- **BP may not signal a decision.** Miller and Trevena (2010) found the same BP whether or not subjects actually moved, suggesting the signal marks attention or preparation. Schurger, Sitt, and Dehaene (2012) proposed the BP reflects spontaneous noise fluctuations in motor areas, and a 2019 study found readiness potentials absent for deliberate decisions and present only for arbitrary ones. A 2021 meta-analysis concluded the effect exists but rests on a small number of studies.
- **Self-knowledge is unreliable.** Kühn and Brass (2009) had subjects judge whether they had acted impulsively or after deliberation, then compared judgments to reaction times. Subjects could not reliably tell the difference, suggesting even the felt veto of an action may be unconscious.

## Can conscious will still matter?

Several findings preserve a narrow role for consciousness.

Libet himself argued for **"free won't"**: although the impulse to move builds unconsciously for about 500 ms, consciousness can **veto** the action at the last moment. A 2016 study by Schultze-Kraft and colleagues identified a **point of no return**: once the BP begins, a person can still cancel a self-initiated foot movement, but only if the cancel happens **at least 200 ms** before movement onset.

**Transcranial magnetic stimulation** (TMS, magnetic pulses that briefly activate or disrupt brain regions) applied to the pre-supplementary motor area or motor cortex can bias which hand a person "chooses," even while the subject reports a free choice. TMS to the pre-SMA **after** an action can shift the perceived onset of the intention **backward** in time, implying the feeling of "I just decided" is generated after the fact.

## The philosophical landscape

"Free will" is not one concept. Two main positions are tested against the neuroscience:

- **Libertarian free will** holds that actions are not fully caused by prior brain states. Most neuroscience findings weigh against this.
- **Compatibilist free will** holds that actions can be "free" if they are uncoerced, responsive to reasons, and not compulsive, even in a deterministic brain. Daniel Dennett, a compatibilist, argues this is what people actually want and that it survives the empirical evidence.

Sam Harris argues that intention does not initiate action, calling the illusion of initiation itself an illusion. Walter Freeman argues instead that unconscious systems, not awareness, are the real source of agency. A 2008 study by Vohs and Schooler found that reading a deterministic text increased cheating on a test, though later studies produced mixed results on whether belief in free will affects moral behavior. Because people associate belief in free will with their sense of being able to affect their lives, some philosophers worry that premature public announcements of free will's death carry social costs.

## Where action lives in the brain

The motor system divides labor across regions. The **supplementary motor area** (SMA and pre-SMA) generates the readiness potential and handles "when" decisions. The **anterior cingulate cortex** monitors conflict and supports "what" decisions. The **dorsal medial prefrontal cortex** supports "whether" decisions, and the **parietal-premotor circuit** links perception to action for object-guided movements. Brain disorders reinforce the picture: split-brain patients invent explanations for actions their other hemisphere initiated, alien hand syndrome produces movements against the patient's will, and removing the SMA in glioma patients impairs action execution but spares the *feeling* of having decided.

Conscious will appears late in the decision process, often cannot be cleanly separated from the action it supposedly causes, and is partly reconstructed after the fact, yet a narrow veto window remains open during which it can still block actions already underway.
