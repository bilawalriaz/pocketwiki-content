# Journalism ethics and standards

Journalistic ethics are the principles of good practice journalists apply when gathering, editing and disseminating news. About 400 codes exist worldwide, drawn from different cultural traditions, yet most share the same core values: truthfulness, accuracy, independence, objectivity, impartiality, fairness, respect for others and public accountability. Where these standards collide with state control, propaganda or corporate capture, they weaken, so ethics are shaped as much by law and ownership as by professional ideals.

Accuracy means reporting facts with appropriate attribution: a single eyewitness requires named sourcing, two or more independent eyewitnesses may be reported as fact, and controversial claims are always attributed. Corrections are issued when discovered. Defendants are described as having "allegedly" committed crimes until conviction, then outlets switch to "convicted of" or "pleaded guilty to". Opinion polls and statistics must state margin of error and methodological limits so audiences can judge their weight.

Libel law shapes accuracy because truth is a near-universal defence. In the United States, reporters on public figures receive qualified immunity if they reported without malice; in Canada no such immunity exists, so claims about public figures must be backed by verified facts. UK libel law is broader than the US version, and local standards adjust accordingly.

Harm limitation requires weighing the damage full disclosure can cause. Reporters typically withhold the names of minors, crime victims and juvenile suspects, and apply extra sensitivity when interviewing grieving or traumatised sources. The practice creates a recurring dilemma: not everything learned should be published, yet excessive sanitisation risks eroding public understanding, especially in war coverage.

## Self-regulation and oversight

Codes of ethics are voluntary. The Society of Professional Journalists frames the duty as seeking truth and providing a fair, comprehensive account, with public enlightenment as the foundation of democracy. The Radio Television Digital News Association centres its code on public trust, truthfulness and accountability. The International Federation of Journalists launched a global Ethical Journalism Initiative in 2008, and the Ethical Journalism Network followed in 2013. In 1993 the Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe approved Resolution 1003, urging respect for the presumption of innocence in cases still sub judice (under judicial consideration, not yet decided).

Beyond codes, outlets install ombudsmen (internal complaints reviewers) or public editors to audit performance. Industry-wide self-regulation exists in bodies such as the UK Press Complaints Commission, though critics call such bodies toothless. Newsroom culture, including peer fact-checking, scepticism and self-criticism, is what makes written codes operational.

## Objectivity, bias and variation

Objectivity is a stated goal in many US and UK outlets, especially the BBC, but its application varies by genre and country. Advocacy journalism rejects objectivity while keeping factual rigour; civic journalism facilitates public debate without endorsing a party; investigative journalism implies a position, that corruption or abuse is wrong, while demanding strict factual accuracy; gonzo and new journalism trade neutrality for immersive voice; tabloids often sacrifice accuracy and privacy to boost sales, as the 2011 News International phone hacking scandal illustrates.

Anonymous sourcing is the classic trade-off: whistleblowers can expose hidden wrongdoing, but unverifiable claims gain undeserved weight. The run-up to the 2003 invasion of Iraq saw the Washington press criticised for over-reliance on anonymous sources later proved unreliable.

## AI, algorithms and new pressures

Generative AI introduces fresh hazards: fabricated text, deepfakes (realistic but fake audio or video), biased training data and opaque "black box" decisions. The Associated Press Stylebook now requires AI-generated material to be clearly labelled. The BBC has published AI-use principles built on transparency, fairness and human oversight, though scholars warn that guidelines can amount to "ethics washing" when enforcement is weak. A 2025 Pressat survey of 2,000 journalists found 80 per cent concerned about bias in AI-generated news.

Algorithmic distribution on Facebook, Google News and publisher apps personalises feeds by engagement metrics, risking filter bubbles that hide opposing views and weaken democratic deliberation.
