# Descriptive ethics

Descriptive ethics is the empirical study of what people actually believe is right and wrong. It is also called comparative ethics. The field sits inside philosophy but borrows its methods from the social and behavioural sciences, because it observes moral beliefs rather than arguing for what morality should be.

Researchers in descriptive ethics treat moral attitudes as data. They record what individuals or groups think about values, which actions they judge right or wrong, what qualities they consider virtuous in a person, what ideals they hold up, and which behaviours a society rewards through law or politics. Because culture is generational and not static, each new generation arrives with its own set of morals, and descriptive ethics tracks how that landscape shifts over time.

Descriptive ethics is one of four branches that divide the study of morality:

- Descriptive ethics asks what people think is right.
- Meta-ethics asks what the word "right" even refers to.
- Normative, or prescriptive, ethics argues for how people ought to act.
- Applied ethics works out how to put moral knowledge into practice.

Descriptive ethics is the only one of these that is fundamentally empirical. Meta-ethics and normative ethics deal with concepts and arguments; descriptive ethics deals with observations. Value theory can be either normative or descriptive but is usually descriptive in practice.

The researchers who actually carry out descriptive ethics tend to be evolutionary biologists, psychologists, sociologists, or anthropologists, because they already have the tools to gather and analyse data about human behaviour. Philosophers, however, frequently draw on the findings, using empirical claims about how people reason about morality to support or challenge philosophical arguments about ethics.

Lawrence Kohlberg, a psychologist, is one example of descriptive ethics in action. He posed moral dilemmas to groups of boys and paid close attention not to which answer they picked but to the reasoning behind it. His best-known scenario is the Heinz dilemma: a man cannot afford a drug that would save his wife, and the question is whether he should steal it. Kohlberg used the patterns of reasoning he recorded to build a theory about the development of human moral reasoning that reflects the reasoning his participants actually carried out. Because he described how people do reason, his work counts as descriptive ethics. Had he instead set out to describe how humans ought to develop morally, the same project would have become prescriptive ethics.
