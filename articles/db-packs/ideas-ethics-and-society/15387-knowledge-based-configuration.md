# Knowledge-based configuration

Knowledge-based configuration is the use of artificial intelligence to assemble a customised product, service, or software bundle for a specific customer from a fixed catalogue of predefined components. It is also called product configuration or product customization and is one of the most commercially successful AI technologies, with established use in the automotive, telecommunication, computer, and power-transformer industries.

Configuration is narrower than general design. It is a special case of design in which the artefact is assembled from instances of a fixed, well-defined set of component types, subject to constraints that encode technical compatibility, economic or pricing rules, and conditions imposed by production. The output is a concrete configuration: a list of chosen component instances and sometimes the connections between them. A financial example is a loan paired with its corresponding risk-insurance product; a manufacturing example is a specific computer build ready for shipment.

AI is needed because configuration is combinatorial. Najmann and Stein's early axiomatic theory shows that typical optimisation goals, such as finding the cheapest valid build, are NP-complete, meaning worst-case hard for exact solvers. As the catalogue grows, the search space explodes, so heuristic configuration algorithms are the preferred approach for complex products and services.

Configuration systems, also called configurators or mass-customisation toolkits, are the software that performs this work. Early systems were rule-based; R1/XCON at Digital Equipment Corporation is the landmark example. Modern systems use model-based representations that strictly separate two kinds of knowledge: product domain knowledge (what components exist and how they fit together) and problem-solving knowledge (the algorithms that search for a valid configuration). The separation matters because catalogue changes do not require rewriting the search engine, and vice versa. Common problem-solving frameworks include the constraint satisfaction problem, Boolean satisfiability, and answer set programming. Two widely cited conceptualisations of configuration knowledge share the same core vocabulary: components, ports, resources, and functions.

Characteristic-based product configurators represent a product as a set of discrete variables that are binary or take one of several values, and together these variables enumerate every possible variation.

The configuration process has three core tasks: guiding the user through choices, checking that the user's requirements stay consistent with the knowledge base, and translating the final result into downstream artefacts such as a bill of materials. Knowledge bases are typically written in proprietary languages by knowledge engineers who elicit product, marketing, and sales information from domain experts, and they combine a formal description of product structure with constraints on which features and components may be combined.

Configurators also serve as open-innovation toolkits that help customers in the product-identification phase, treating their articulated requirements as a source of new product ideas. A documented downside is Mass Confusion, in which customers are overwhelmed by the number of possible alternatives. Personalised configuration environments that account for a customer's existing knowledge and preferences were developed specifically to counter this.

The same ideas extend to software configuration through two dominant modelling approaches: feature modelling, which organises the optional and mandatory features of a software product family, and the component-connectors view, which describes software as pieces wired together by explicit connections. The Kumbang domain ontology combines both traditions within the knowledge-based configuration lineage, applying the same separation of domain knowledge from problem-solving knowledge to software product families.
