# Ethics

Ethics, also called moral philosophy, is the philosophical study of moral phenomena. It investigates what people ought to do, which behavior is morally right, and how to lead a good life. Its main branches are normative ethics (general principles of right action), applied ethics (concrete moral problems), and metaethics (the nature and foundations of moral judgments). The term derives from Ancient Greek *êthos* ('character'); *morality* comes from Latin *moralis* ('manners'). Some philosophers distinguish them: morality focuses on obligations, while ethics is broader, covering ideas about the good life. Domain-specific codes (business, environment) are usually called ethics.

## Normative ethics

Normative ethics seeks general principles determining whether acts are right or wrong, distinct from descriptive ethics, which describes how people actually behave. The three most influential schools—consequentialism, deontology, and virtue ethics—often overlap.

**Consequentialism** holds that morality depends on consequences: an act is right if it brings the best future. Variants include act versus rule consequentialism, actual versus expected, and maximizing versus satisficing (best possible act versus one "good enough"). Mohism in 5th-century-BCE China is an early form. Utilitarianism, the best-known version, treats happiness as the only intrinsic value. Bentham introduced the hedonic calculus, weighing pleasure by intensity and duration; Mill distinguished higher intellectual pleasures from lower sensory ones.

**Deontology** assesses actions by norms like truth-telling and promise-keeping, independent of consequences. Kant's categorical imperative grounds morality in universal principles from practical reason, requiring that maxims be universalizable and that people be treated as ends, never merely as means. Divine command theory locates morality in God's will; contractualism (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Rawls) grounds it in a social contract; discourse ethics seeks agreement through communicative rationality.

**Virtue ethics** centers on character traits like courage and compassion, expressed through practical wisdom (*phronesis*). Aristotle linked virtue to flourishing, a view called eudaimonism, and defined each virtue as a golden mean between excess and deficiency. Stoicism taught rationality and self-mastery. The tradition revived in the late 20th century through Anscombe, Foot, MacIntyre, and Nussbaum. Religious traditions add Jewish law (613 commandments), Christian selflessness, Islamic Five Pillars, Buddhist compassion, Hindu duty, Confucian benevolence, and Daoist harmony with nature.

## Metaethics

Metaethics examines whether objective moral facts exist, what moral terms mean, and how moral knowledge is possible. **Moral realists** hold that objective moral facts exist independently of mind and culture; **relativists** see them as human inventions varying across cultures; **nihilists** deny them entirely. Among realists, naturalists identify moral properties with observable natural properties, while non-naturalists regard them as irreducible.

**Cognitivism** holds that moral statements are truth-apt (capable of being true or false); error theory combines this with nihilism, claiming all moral statements are false. **Non-cognitivism** denies truth value: emotivism treats moral statements as expressions of emotion; prescriptivism treats them as commands. On moral knowledge, foundationalism appeals to self-evident basic beliefs (as in ethical intuitionism); coherentism to mutual support among beliefs; skepticism denies moral knowledge is possible. Thought experiments like Foot's trolley problem and Thomson's violinist scenario probe moral intuitions. Motivational internalism holds that moral judgments directly motivate action; externalism denies this.

## Applied ethics

Applied ethics tests universal principles in concrete cases through top-down reasoning (principles to cases) or casuistry (cases to principles). **Bioethics** addresses the moral status of living entities, ranging from anthropocentrism (humans only) and sentientism (sentient beings) to biocentrism (all life) and ecocentrism (whole ecosystems), and divides into medical ethics (Hippocratic Oath, abortion, euthanasia, confidentiality), animal ethics (rights, factory farming, research), and environmental ethics (climate, sustainability, future generations). **Business and professional ethics** cover corporate social responsibility and the duties of engineers, lawyers, and journalists. Other subfields include ethics of technology (AI, human enhancement), just war theory, political ethics, educational ethics, and sexual ethics.

## Related fields

**Value theory (axiology)** distinguishes intrinsic from instrumental value; its relation to ethics is debated. **Moral psychology** empirically studies moral reasoning and development; Kohlberg identified stages from fixed rules to abstract principles, and evolutionary ethics explores natural selection's role. **Descriptive ethics** compares moral codes across societies without judging their correctness.

## History

Ethics arose in ancient Egypt (Maat: truth and balance), India (Vedas, Buddhism), China (Confucianism, Daoism), and Greece (Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, Stoicism, Epicureanism). Medieval ethics absorbed religious influence: Aquinas developed natural law, and Islamic philosophers synthesized Greek and Islamic thought. The modern period turned secular through Hobbes (self-interest, social contract), Hume (moral sentiments), Kant (reason), Hegel (social institutions), and Nietzsche's critique of Christian and Kantian morality. The 20th century saw metaethics emerge (Moore, Hare, Mackie, Parfit, Williams), virtue ethics revive through Foot, Rawls's Kantian political philosophy, and feminist ethics.
