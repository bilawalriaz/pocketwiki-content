# Social contract

The social contract is the idea that political authority is legitimate only because its subjects have consented to it. People trade some natural freedom for the security that a state provides. Without that agreement, in a hypothetical "state of nature" with no government, individuals act only on personal power and conscience.

The basic logic: because human life outside political order is dangerous, rational individuals give up certain freedoms and submit to a ruler, a majority, or a set of rules in exchange for protection of their remaining rights. Consent may be tacit, such as by living under a government's protection, rather than explicit. The theory holds that law and political order are human creations, justified only insofar as they serve the people who made them.

## Why the state of nature matters

Most contract theories begin by imagining life without government. Thomas Hobbes described this condition as "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short," where everyone has unlimited natural freedom, including the right to all things, producing a "war of all against all." The point is not history but a thought experiment: if you can show people would agree to leave such a state, you can justify the state they create.

John Locke used the same premise but reached a different conclusion. In the state of nature, people already have natural rights to life, liberty, and property, bound by a moral law of nature, yet without a neutral judge those rights are insecure. So they consent to form a government whose job is to act as that impartial judge, using collective force to enforce the law. A government that fails to protect these rights forfeits its legitimacy.

Rousseau went further. He argued that the social contract does not bind people to a ruler but to one another, producing a "general will" that belongs to the whole community. Each individual puts his person and all his power in common and receives back membership in an indivisible collective. Obeying laws made by this general will is, for Rousseau, true freedom, because the laws express the citizen's own will rather than the will of a superior. Anyone who refuses can be "forced to be free," meaning compelled to respect the collective decision he participated in making.

These three positions differ on one question: what does the individual surrender, and to whom? Hobbes surrenders rights to an absolute sovereign. Locke surrenders the right to be one's own judge, keeping natural rights that government must protect. Rousseau surrenders all rights to the collective in exchange for equal citizenship.

## A shared structure

Contract theories can be written in a common form. Choosers, imagined in an original position or state of nature, deliberate within a specific setting (fair conditions, ignorance of personal fortune, or self-interest) and choose rules or institutions. Those rules then give real people reason to follow them, provided the choosers' reasoning applies to the real world. Variation between Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau is mostly about the deliberative setting and what is given up.

## Classical and non-Western roots

The idea is older than the European Enlightenment. In the *Arthashastra*, Chanakya wrote that without enforced punishment "the strong devour the weak," a realist justification of state power. The Buddhist *Aggañña Sutta* tells of a "Great Elect" chosen by the people to keep order in return for a share of the harvest. Plato's *Republic* has Glaucon describe justice as a compromise: people give up the freedom to harm others without punishment in exchange for not being harmed themselves. Epicurus held that justice is a mutual agreement not to harm or be harmed.

## Criticisms

David Hume argued that consent of the governed is the best basis for government but has almost never actually existed; most states rest on conquest or habit. Lysander Spooner added that any contract imposed by a government willing to use force against dissenters is not voluntary and cannot bind anyone. Karl Widerquist and Grant McCall argue that not every individual is better off under a state than she could expect to be in a state of nature, undermining the rational-choice premise.

## Modern developments

John Rawls revived the tradition by placing choosers behind a "veil of ignorance," where they do not know their own class, talents, or beliefs, and would then choose fair principles of justice. David Gauthier modeled the contract using the prisoner's dilemma: two self-interested parties do best when each sticks to the agreed arrangement. Philip Pettit shifted the basis of legitimacy from explicit consent to the absence of effective rebellion.

The social contract's most visible political descendant is the United States Declaration of Independence (1776), which translated Locke's natural rights into "Life, Liberty, and the pursuit of Happiness" and asserted a right to "alter or to abolish" any government that violates them.
