# Robot ethics

Robot ethics, sometimes called "roboethics," examines two problems: how humans should design and use robots, and how humans should treat increasingly capable machines. The first sub-field, machine ethics, asks how to build robots that act ethically. Robot ethics sits inside the broader ethics of technology and draws on robotics, computer science, artificial intelligence, philosophy, law, cognitive science, and psychology.

## Origins and a founding thought experiment

Serious academic discussion began around 2000, but the conceptual seed is older. Isaac Asimov's 1942 short story "Runaround" introduced the **Three Laws of Robotics**, later expanded with a "zeroth" law. The laws were designed to surface contradictions: obeying humans, preventing harm, and self-preservation cannot all be satisfied at once, so simple written rules for intelligent machines produce paradoxes. Gianmarco Veruggio, who likely coined "roboethics," organised the First International Symposium on Roboethics in 2004.

## How ethics can be built into autonomous systems

Virginia Dignum's 2018 article in *Ethics and Information Technology* described three complementary strategies. **Ethics by Design** embeds ethical reasoning inside the algorithm itself, the technical core of machine ethics. **Ethics in Design** uses regulatory and engineering methods to evaluate how AI systems reshape social structures. **Ethics for Design** relies on codes of conduct, standards, and certification for the people who build and deploy AI. The three layers target three audiences: the system, the institution, and the individual developer.

## Killer robots and the question of human control

Lethal Autonomous Weapon Systems (LAWS) can select and engage targets without a human pulling the trigger. At 2014 Convention on Conventional Weapons meetings, states and NGOs divided along familiar lines. Deontological opponents argued that delegating killing to a machine violates human dignity, invoking the Martens Clause. Consequentialist critics argued that LAWS cannot satisfy international humanitarian law (IHL), because machines cannot reliably make the contextual judgements that proportionality and military necessity require. The International Committee of the Red Cross confirmed that IHL governs any future autonomous weapon. Bonnie Docherty's 2012 Human Rights Watch report defines the spectrum of human oversight:

| Control model | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Human-in-the-loop | A human must initiate each attack |
| Human-on-the-loop | A human may abort an attack in progress |
| Human-out-of-the-loop | No human involvement at any stage |

Out-of-the-loop weapons are the ones disarmament campaigns target.

## Sex robots

The 2015 Campaign Against Sex Robots argued these devices reinforce social inequalities and should be banned. A counter-analysis published by MIT Press called the specific claims "unpersuasive" and warned that opposing sex robots requires a generally conservative sexual ethics. Critics concede the harm is speculative and indirect, unlike the concrete harm attributed to autonomous weapons.

## Legal framing

Richards and Smart propose principles for regulating robots. First, define a robot as a non-biological autonomous agent, a category broad enough to anchor law. Second, borrow from cyber-law, where the metaphors used to describe a technology shape the rules that follow. Third, reject the "Android Fallacy," the assumption that robots are "just like people," which produces bad law by importing inappropriate rights and duties.

## How humans actually judge robot behaviour

Empirical studies find that people apply the same moral concepts to robots as to humans but with asymmetric expectations. Robots receive less credit for good actions and more blame for bad ones. Malevolent robot behaviour is judged more harshly than benevolent behaviour is rewarded, and is perceived as more intentional. When explaining a robot's choices, people fall back on simple heuristics such as advancing the self, doing what is right, advancing others, or doing what is logical.

## Cultural pressure on the field

In 2017 the humanoid robot Sophia was granted Saudi Arabian citizenship, prompting unresolved legal questions about voting, marriage, and whether switching her off could be treated as murder. Critics noted the contrast with restrictions on Saudi women. The same year the European Parliament passed a resolution on Civil Law Rules for Robotics. In a 2018 article, computer scientist Virginia Dignum argued that societal attitudes have shifted from viewing AI as a tool toward viewing it as an intelligent "team-mate" whose design, deployment, and treatment all raise separate ethical questions.
