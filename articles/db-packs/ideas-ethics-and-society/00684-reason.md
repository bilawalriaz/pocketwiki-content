# Reason

Reason is the human capacity to consciously apply logic, drawing valid conclusions from information. It underpins philosophy, science, mathematics, and language, and is often treated as the trait separating humans from other animals. The English word descends via French *raison* from Latin *ratio*, which translated Greek *logos* (speech, explanation, account, ratio). Bacon, Hobbes, and Locke treated the three terms as interchangeable.

Reasoning subdivides into three modes: **deductive** (conclusion follows necessarily from premises), **inductive** (probable generalisations from observations), and **abductive** (inference to the best explanation from incomplete data). The adjective is "rational," and a "reason" (count noun) is a consideration justifying a belief or action.

## From Cosmic Order to Subject-Centred Reason

For Plato and Aristotle, the world was **teleological**: everything had a built-in purpose, and reason (*logos*) was a cosmic principle linking the immortal human mind to the divine order. Plato ruled the soul (*psyche*) by reason over spiritedness (*thumos*) and passions. Aristotle defined humans as *rational animals* and located **eudaimonia** (flourishing) in a life lived by reason. Plotinus radicalised this into a single World Soul, the seat of all reason, that gives form to matter and draws souls back toward their source, a bridge to monotheism.

Church Fathers (Augustine, Basil, Gregory) adopted the Neoplatonic rational soul. Islamic philosophers (Averroes, Avicenna, Alhazen) preserved Aristotle. In the Latin West, **Scholasticism** fused them. Aquinas placed reason at the heart of **Natural Law**: because reason is a "spark of the divine," every human life is inviolable, equal, and intrinsically rights-bearing, a foundation later expanded by the School of Salamanca. Roger Bacon and Albertus Magnus, drawing on Alhazen's optics, treated reason as the ability to decode the created order, helping birth the scientific method.

The teleological worldview collapsed in the 17th century as nature came to seem mechanical. Descartes doubted everything except the thinking self (*cogito*) and unified reason, intellect, and understanding into one indivisible *res cogitans*, founding **subject-centred reason**: the knowing subject studies objects to master them. Hobbes reduced reason to "calculative" addition and subtraction. In the 18th century, Hume denied any necessary causal connection in experience and declared reason "the slave of the passions." Kant answered by positing a **transcendental self** as a condition of experience. Reason (*Vernunft*) legislates universal laws: **theoretical reason** supplies the laws of nature, and **practical reason** grounds moral autonomy in the **categorical imperative**, "act only according to that maxim whereby you can will that it should become a universal law."

## Modern Fragmentation and the Critiques

**Habermas** argued that the "substantive unity" of reason has dissolved, leaving only **formal, procedural** authority split into three spheres: **cognitive-instrumental** (sciences, prediction, control), **moral-practical** (universalisable deliberation), and **aesthetic** (art and literature as disclosure). Philosophy's task is to mediate between these expert spheres and the "lifeworld."

A counter-tradition questions reason's authority. Skeptics (Kierkegaard, Nietzsche) doubt universal, instrumental, or subject-centred reason. Hegel argued such reason obscures "spirit" or shared communal life. Foucault proposed multiple, conflicting rationalities with no super-rational arbiter. Habermas proposed **communicative reason**, cooperative and linguistically intersubjective, while Taylor, following Heidegger, treated reason as a faculty of "disclosure" in everyday sense-making. Foucault distinguished **private reason** (role-bound, "cog in a machine") from **public reason** (free and universal).

## Reason, Logic, and Symbolic Thought

Reason is broader than logic. Logic codifies formal rules inside a system; reason operates outside systems, skipping steps, changing rules, diagramming. Rationality requires **metacognition**, conscious control over inferences, which exceeds mere logicality. Animals associate causes and effects, but human reason demands an **explicable** mediating link, such as inferring fire from smoke.

Two capacities distinguish human reason. First, symbolic manipulation: for Hobbes and Peirce, strict reason requires symbol systems (language) whose elements have only habitual connections to referents. Second, representational detachment: Plato's **eikasia** (recognising an image as an image), Donald and Aristotle's **mimesis** (intentional representation, not mimicry), and **anamnēsis** (voluntary recollection, versus passive animal *mneme*) together mark the specifically human threshold.

## Cognitive Science and the Argumentative Theory

Psychology studies rationality through experiments such as the Wason selection task and conditional, counterfactual, and developmental reasoning (Piaget and neo-Piagetian theories). Neuroscience maps the brain structures involved, and clinical psychology works to alter unhelpful reasoning habits. Computer science applies **automated reasoning** (theorem proving, formal verification) and **meta-reasoning**, where a system reasons about its own operation by reflection.

Sperber and Mercier's **argumentative theory** treats reason as cognitively costly and biased, especially toward confirmation bias. On their account, reason evolved primarily for **collective** argumentation, for winning disputes and justifying actions to others, rather than for solitary truth-seeking. Group selection favours groups whose members argue and evaluate effectively.

## Political Reason and Its Rivals

Aristotle held humans are *political animals* because they possess *logos* (reasoned speech), which articulates what is just and unjust, advantageous and harmful. The *polis* is natural, and without virtue, reason-armed humans become "the most unholy and savage." Rousseau inverted this: in his *Second Discourse*, reason, language, and politics arose accidentally from cooperative habits, later hardened by defence needs; the "natural" state was ape-like, and human nature is malleable.

The Stoics treated emotions as false judgements; Hume made reason the slave of the passions. Damasio's **Somatic Marker Hypothesis** argues the opposite: emotional "gut feelings" are necessary inputs for rational choice, and what looks like reason is often **rationalisation**, desire dressed as logic. Faith and reason have been framed as "Athens versus Jerusalem." Plantinga and Ratzinger sought reconciliation, the latter arguing Christianity *is* the religion of the *Logos* and that Enlightenment rationality has Christian roots. Gould proposed **non-overlapping magisteria** (NOMA), with science and faith holding authority in separate domains. MacIntyre, Taylor, and Feyerabend countered that secular liberalism and science also suppress reasoning, a "tu quoque" against the partisans of reason alone.
