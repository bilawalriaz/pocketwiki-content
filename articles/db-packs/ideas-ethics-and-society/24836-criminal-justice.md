# Criminal justice

Criminal justice is the state's organised response to crime, carried out by linked agencies whose joint purpose is to enforce law, adjudicate guilt, and punish or rehabilitate offenders. Its stated aims are retribution, deterrence, reformation, victim support, and crime prevention.

## The three pillars

A criminal justice system has three core institutions that operate together to maintain the rule of law:

- **Police (law enforcement)**: investigate suspected crimes, arrest suspects, and maintain public order. The first contact a defendant has with the system is usually an arrest. When warranted, officers may use legal force.
- **Courts**: settle disputes and administer justice. The courtroom work group is the judge, prosecutor, and defence attorney. In the United States and many other countries, the adversarial system pits the two sides before an impartial judge or jury, which decides guilt "beyond a reasonable doubt." Most cases never reach trial; they end in plea agreements, where the accused pleads guilty or *nolo contendere* in exchange for a reduced charge or sentence. Where life or liberty is at stake, indigent defendants are entitled to a state-funded lawyer.
- **Corrections**: after conviction, offenders are transferred to prisons, probation, or parole. Early prisons merely sequestered criminals; the Quaker movement in America pushed the idea that prisons should reform inmates. Modern incarceration combines incapacitation (removing the offender from society) with rehabilitation through schooling, vocational training, and religious instruction.

## Punishment and its alternatives

In early civilisations lacking prison infrastructure, exile and execution were the principal sanctions. During the Middle Ages, *wergild* (payment to the victim or family) settled even violent offences; those who could not pay faced mutilation, branding, or flogging. Fines, probation, house arrest, and community service remain common substitutes for or supplements to prison. Capital punishment is still used worldwide, though its scope varies from political control to the most serious crimes; many states have abolished it as excessively cruel or irreversible.

## How the system thinks about justice

Three normative theories underlie criminal justice practice:

- **Retributive justice**: punishment proportional to the wrong done.
- **Utilitarian justice**: punishment justified by its future consequences, chiefly deterrence.
- **Restorative justice**: repair of harm done to victims and communities.

These operate through three mechanisms: **deterrence** (discouraging future crime), **rehabilitation** (changing the offender), and **incapacitation** (physically preventing reoffending). Systemic weaknesses undermine all three: low clearance rates allow many offenders to escape punishment (the "error of impunity"), while high recidivism shows that released prisoners often reoffend, eroding the system's political legitimacy.

## Origins and modern policing

The concept of criminal justice matured alongside the nation-state, which Max Weber defined as holding a monopoly on the legitimate use of force, a monopoly police embody. The first comparable police force was created in 1667 under Louis XIV in France, but modern police trace to the 1829 Metropolitan Police in London, founded by Robert Peel on Peelian principles that emphasised prevention and public consent. American police departments followed in Boston (1838) and New York (1844). The FBI was formed in 1908 to investigate specific federal crimes, a narrow slice of overall policing, whose dominant activities remain order maintenance and service provision.

## The academic field

Criminal justice emerged as a university discipline in the 1920s. Berkeley police chief August Vollmer started a program in 1916; his student O. W. Wilson professionalised policing. Federal funding expanded the field after the 1968 Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act, and by the 1970s there were 729 academic programs in the United States, enrolling over 100,000 students by 1975, combining practical police science with the sociology and psychology of deviance.

## A national contrast: France

France illustrates how the three-pillar model is arranged differently elsewhere. Investigation is split between the judicial police, which identifies perpetrators of criminal offences under Article 14 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, and the administrative police, which maintains public order. Criminal courts are tiered: the police tribunal handles minor *contraventions*, the correctional court hears *délits* (misdemeanours), and the Court of Assizes, three judges and six jurors sitting in each department, tries the most serious crimes. Appeals run through regional appeal courts to the criminal chamber of the Court of Cassation in Paris.

A 2023 Gallup poll found 58% of Americans thought the criminal justice system was not tough enough, while 14% considered it too strict, a gap that keeps punishment philosophy at the centre of political debate.
