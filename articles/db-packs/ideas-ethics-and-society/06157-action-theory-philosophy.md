# Action theory (philosophy)

Action theory is the branch of philosophy that asks what makes a bodily movement a *willful action* rather than a mere happening, and what must be true, mentally and causally, when you reach for a glass of water versus when your hand twitches.

The field traces to Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* and draws on epistemology, ethics, metaphysics, jurisprudence, and philosophy of mind. Since the rise of psychology and later neuroscience, many of its claims are now tested empirically.

## The belief–desire model

Basic action theory treats an action as intentional behaviour produced by an *agent* (the person acting) in a situation. A standard example: you want water, you believe the clear liquid in the cup is water, and these two mental states together cause you to reach for it.

- **Belief**: a representation of how the world is (the cup contains water).
- **Desire**: a representation of how the world should be (I want a drink).

When a belief and a desire pair in the right way, a bodily movement follows. This belief–desire model is the default starting point for most action theory.

## Disagreements inside the model

Donald Davidson's simple view holds that a belief and a desire *jointly cause* the action, with nothing more elaborate required. Michael Bratman objected that this is too thin, especially for complex, future-directed actions. He argued that *intention*, a settled commitment to act, must be taken as a basic category rather than reduced to a belief–desire pair, because intentions let us plan across time and coordinate sub-steps.

Aristotle's tradition requires both the *efficient cause* (the agent as the source of motion) and the *final cause* (the goal the agent aims at). Naming the agent without the goal, or the goal without the agent, leaves the explanation incomplete.

## Beyond desire-satisfaction

Some theories treat action as an attempt to *maximise desire-satisfaction*. This "prospective rationality" picture underlies much of economics and rational-choice social science, which explain behaviour by picking the best available means to a given end.

Other theorists reject this as too narrow. On their view, a moral belief (for example, that I ought to help) can directly cause action without first generating a desire to help. Rationality here means responding correctly to *reasons* the agent perceives, including moral ones, not just doing what the agent most wants. Acting for a reason is not the same as acting from desire.

## Free will and the language of cause

Action theorists generally write their explanations causally: beliefs and desires *cause* movements, intentions *cause* plans, agents *cause* outcomes. This causal vocabulary places action theory at the centre of debates about free will, since the meaning of "causal determination" is precisely what those debates contest. Whether an action can be both caused and free remains the live question.

## Open questions and confusions

Even the definition of "action" is unsettled. Should *thinking* count as an action when it is deliberate, or only bodily movements? When an action has many steps and many intended consequences, how should it be summarised or decomposed?

Philosophical action theory is not the sociological *theory of social action* developed by Talcott Parsons, nor *activity theory*, which addresses a different set of questions.
