# History of religion

Religious beliefs are the oldest documented layer of human culture, yet their written record begins only with the invention of writing around 3200 BCE, long after the practices themselves had appeared. Studying this history matters because religion has shaped laws, moral codes, social structures, and art across every major civilization. The very word "religion," used as a distinct category, is itself a recent Western concept with no clean equivalent in many other languages or cultures, ancient or modern.

Because the earliest evidence is archaeological rather than written, it remains conjectural. Some scholars read intentional burials from the Middle and Lower Paleolithic, hundreds of thousands of years old, as signs of belief in something beyond the visible world. Stronger, though still debated, evidence appears in Middle Stone Age African symbolic artifacts. A broader consensus accepts Upper Paleolithic objects from 50,000 to 13,000 BCE, such as the lion-man figurine and the Venus figurines, as representing religious ideas. The most striking pre-agricultural site is Göbekli Tepe, a complex of megalithic stone circles in southeastern Anatolia built around 9000 BCE, before the Neolithic Revolution. Its existence implies that a society capable of organizing large-scale religious building had already emerged among hunter-gatherers.

The first written religious texts are the Egyptian Pyramid Texts, composed between 2400 and 2300 BCE. Between roughly 1500 and 1200 BCE, the Vedas were composed in India during the Vedic period, recording the earliest layers of Indian religious thought. These are the oldest points from which the surviving textual tradition begins.

The most important conceptual turning point is the Axial Age, a term coined by the philosopher Karl Jaspers for 900 to 200 BCE. Across Eurasia, several major traditions took shape independently and roughly simultaneously: monotheistic ideas in Persia and Canaan, Platonism in Greece, Buddhism and Jainism in India, and Confucianism and Taoism in China. Jainism's historical roots in India reach back to the 9th century BCE with the figure of Parshvanatha, and Buddhism was later spread across Asia through the patronage of rulers such as the Indian emperor Ashoka. The Greek Septuagint translation of the Hebrew Tanakh, produced between about 300 and 200 BCE, marked a key moment in the transmission of monotheistic scripture.

The medieval period, beginning around 500 CE, was the age when these traditions became established across Eurasia. Christianity spread through the Christianization of the West, Buddhism reached East Asia through missionary activity, and Islam expanded rapidly after its founding. The same era saw recurring religious conflict, including Muslim campaigns against Zoroastrian Persia between 633 and 654, the Crusades launched in 1095, and the religious violence that accompanied the Mongol invasions. Within traditions, mystical movements such as Sufism in Islam and the Bhakti movement in India flourished. Across monotheistic and Hindu thought, the idea of a single ultimate reality took on definite doctrinal forms: Christian Christology, the Islamic concept of Tawhid, and Hindu Brahman.

From the 15th to 19th centuries, European colonization carried Christianity around the globe. The 15th-century invention of the printing press made scripture widely available in vernacular languages, which fed directly into the Protestant Reformation and the religious wars that followed, most devastatingly the Thirty Years' War from 1618 to 1648, which ravaged Central Europe. From the 18th century onward, secularization gathered pace, in which religion's social and cultural authority in Europe declined, accelerated by events like the French Revolution of 1789 and continuing into the late 20th century.

Three questions remain open. The interpretation of Paleolithic artifacts as evidence of religion is still debated. There is no consensus on religion's origin: 19th-century theories such as animism, which holds that religion began with the attribution of spirits to natural objects, and fetishism, the idea that religion grew from the worship of crafted objects, have been widely criticized, and the systematic study of religion as a socio-cultural phenomenon only began in late 19th-century Germany with the Religionsgeschichtliche Schule, a school of thought that depicted religion as evolving with human culture. And the very category of "religion" as a distinct sphere of life may be a Western projection that obscures how beliefs and rituals actually functioned in most other societies.
