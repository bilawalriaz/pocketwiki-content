# Comparative religion

Comparative religion is the branch of religious studies that systematically compares the doctrines, practices, themes, and impacts of the world's religions, including migration and contact. The aim is a deeper understanding of recurring philosophical concerns (ethics, metaphysics, salvation) and the origins and similarities religions share.

## How religions are grouped

Traditions are sorted into geographical families. Charles Joseph Adams, a standard reference, divides the main world religions as follows:

| Family | Core examples |
|---|---|
| Middle Eastern | Christianity, Islam, Judaism, Zoroastrianism, and ancient belief systems of the region |
| East Asian | Confucianism, Taoism, Mahayana Buddhism, Shintō (China, Japan, Korea, Vietnam) |
| Indian | Early Buddhism, Hinduism, Jainism, Sikhism, sometimes Theravada Buddhism |
| African | Indigenous belief systems of sub-Saharan Africa; ancient Egyptian religion is classed with the Middle East |
| American | Traditions of indigenous peoples across North and South America |
| Oceanic | Religions of Pacific Islands, Australia, New Zealand |
| Classical Hellenistic | Religions of ancient Greece, Rome, and their Hellenistic descendants |

This is a heuristic. Some traditions, like the Baháʼí Faith, sit across categories.

## The Abrahamic cluster

Within Middle Eastern religions, scholars single out the Abrahamic religions: Christianity, Islam, and Judaism, all claiming the patriarch Abraham (Hebrew *Avraham*, Arabic *Ibrahim*). The three share strict monotheism and trace their revelations to the same God.

- **Judaism** is the oldest. Jews see their faith as the covenant God made with the Children of Israel; the Torah is part of the larger Tanakh (Hebrew Bible), supplemented by the Midrash and Talmud.
- **Christianity** treats itself as the fulfillment of the Jewish scriptures. Christians hold that Jesus (Hebrew *Yeshua*) is the Messiah, and creeds affirm the incarnation, ministry, suffering, death, and resurrection of Jesus as the means of salvation.
- **Islam** teaches that prior Jewish and Christian scriptures were corrupted over time. Muslims regard the Quran as the final, complete revelation, given to Muhammad, the *Khatam an-Nabiyyin* ("seal of the prophets").

Of the three, Christianity and Judaism diverge most in theology and practice. Islam and Judaism share more in outlook, structure, jurisprudence, and ritual; many Islamic practices echo Hebrew Bible or post-biblical Jewish traditions, collectively called the *Isra'iliyat*. The Baháʼí Faith emerged from 19th-century Iran through the Bábí movement; after an 1860s split, the majority of Bábís followed Bahá'u'lláh and formed the Baháʼí Faith, a full religion in its own right with far fewer adherents than the three major Abrahamic faiths.

## Iranian religions and their echoes

Greater Iran produced several influential traditions, including Zoroastrianism, Manichaeism, Mithraism, Yazdanism, and Zurvanism. Zoroastrianism spread widely under the Achaemenid and Sasanian empires, declined after the Islamic conquests, and survives today chiefly among the Parsis of India and Pakistan.

Scholars have long debated whether Zoroastrianism shaped the Abrahamic faiths. Shared features include a cosmic duality of good versus evil (light versus darkness), belief in resurrection of the dead, and emphasis on free will and moral responsibility. Manichaeism, another Iranian faith, adopts a dualist cosmology and accepts the prophethood of Zoroaster, the Buddha, and Jesus.

## Indian religions

Indian religions include Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism, all rooted in South Asia. Gandhi's view that "I do not regard Jainism or Buddhism as separate from Hinduism" captures a common position: Jains, Buddhists, and Sikhs look back to Hinduism as a common mother tradition. Many Hindus regard the Buddha as an avatar of Vishnu and see Buddhism as a development within, rather than a break from, the Vedic fold. The 11th-century polymath Al-Biruni produced some of the earliest detailed descriptions of pre-11th-century Indian religions, and Adi Shankaracharya (c. 700–750 CE) consolidated the non-dualist philosophy of Advaita Vedanta.

## East Asian religions

In China, Japan, Korea, and Vietnam, Confucianism (centred on ritual propriety, *li*, and ancestor worship) coexisted with Mahayana Buddhism (centred on Dharma). Confucian patronage let indigenous polytheistic traditions such as Taoism in China and Shintō in Japan grow into organised religions; Taoism itself functioned more as a folk belief than a state religion, so the label "Taoic religions" is questionable. Taoism and Chan Buddhism influenced each other for centuries; Chan became Zen when it reached Japan.

Tang-dynasty China also saw Nestorian Christianity, whose *Jingjiao documents* borrowed Buddhist and Taoist vocabulary. Emperor Wuzong's 9th-century persecution of Buddhism swept Christianity along with it, because officials regarded it as a Buddhist heresy, an episode that helped end Nestorian Christianity in China.

## Where the field stumbles

Comparative religion developed inside Christian theology faculties, and Nicholas de Lange of Cambridge warned that this origin shapes its questions. Western categories assume a believer belongs to one religion and that religion concerns salvation and the afterlife. Buddhism and Chinese folk religion have not historically been mutually exclusive; they have blended, as in Pure Land Buddhism, where Chinese folk beliefs merged with Buddhist practice. The category of salvation itself is Western. Hinduism and Theravada Buddhism do not promise eternal life after death; both speak of escaping the cycle of reincarnation, with the self dissolving into nonexistence. Comparing them under a Christian-shaped "salvation" lens distorts what they actually claim.

## A historical note

Al-Biruni (973 – c. 1050) and Ibn Hazm (994–1064), both scholars of the Islamic Golden Age, are called the fathers of comparative religion. In 1655, the Sufi prince Dara Shikoh authored *Majma-ul-Bahrain*, a comparative study of Islam and Hinduism. From the 19th century, scholars such as Max Müller, Edward Tylor, William Robertson Smith, James Frazer, Émile Durkheim, Max Weber, and Rudolf Otto turned the study of religion, including "primitive" religion, into a social-science enterprise. Religions that aligned early with empires, including Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, and Buddhism, became the largest traditions, and this political alignment is itself a fact the comparative method must explain.
