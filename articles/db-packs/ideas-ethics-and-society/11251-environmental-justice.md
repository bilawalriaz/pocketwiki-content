# Environmental justice

Environmental justice is a social movement addressing the unfair distribution of environmental harms and benefits. It began in the United States in the early 1980s, when predominantly Black communities were found to bear a disproportionate share of toxic waste sites, pollution, and other burdens from which they received little benefit. The movement has since expanded globally, recognizing the same pattern worldwide: poor, racialized, Indigenous, and marginalized communities absorb the costs of industrial activity, resource extraction, and waste disposal, while wealthier populations capture the gains.

## Core definition

The US Environmental Protection Agency defines environmental justice as "the fair treatment and meaningful involvement of all people regardless of race, color, national origin, or income with respect to the development, implementation, and enforcement of environmental laws, regulations and policies." Fair treatment means no group should bear a disproportionate share of negative environmental consequences from industrial, municipal, or commercial operations. Three related ideas structure the field: distributive justice, the equitable sharing of environmental risks and benefits; procedural justice, fair and meaningful participation in the decisions that produce those distributions; and recognition justice, acknowledgment of the distinct histories and identities of affected communities, including Indigenous peoples.

## Origins in the United States

The 1982 protests in Warren County, North Carolina are the movement's founding moment. The state planned to site a landfill for polychlorinated biphenyl (PCB)-contaminated soil in Afton, the state's poorest county, in a predominantly Black community. Residents organized a six-week occupation that led to more than 500 arrests. Rev. Benjamin Chavis, jailed during the protests, coined the term "environmental racism."

Subsequent research by the United Church of Christ's Commission for Racial Justice found that race was the strongest predictor of where hazardous waste facilities were sited, more so than income. The federal government responded gradually: the EPA created the Environmental Equity Work Group in 1990, renamed its office the Office of Environmental Justice in 1994, and President Bill Clinton issued Executive Order 12898, which created the Interagency Working Group on Environmental Justice and remains the cornerstone of US environmental justice regulation.

## Expansion into a global movement

As wealthy countries tightened domestic regulations, hazardous waste, polluting industries, and resource extraction were increasingly displaced to poorer countries. Exports of toxic waste from the US escalated through the 1980s and 1990s. The 1986 Khian Sea incident, in which incinerator ash from Philadelphia was dumped on a beach in Haiti after other countries refused it, helped catalyze the Basel Convention, which regulates international shipment of hazardous waste.

Inside the movement, the 1991 First National People of Color Environmental Leadership Summit in Washington, DC adopted 17 Principles of Environmental Justice, which were carried to the 1992 Earth Summit in Rio. In 2002, a coalition meeting in Bali drafted the Bali Principles of Climate Justice, extending environmental justice thinking to climate change. By 2007, Global South and low-income Northern groups had formed the "Climate Justice Now!" coalition at COP13.

## Mechanisms of injustice

Four mechanisms reproduce environmental injustice. First, locally unwanted land uses, including landfills, incinerators, and refineries, are systematically sited near poor communities and communities of color, which have less political power to resist. Robert Bullard calls this a "triple vulnerability" of poverty, racial discrimination, and unincorporated status. Second, unequal protection: procedural barriers, English-only hearings, and remote locations weaken enforcement of environmental rules in marginalized areas. Third, burden shifting: hazardous waste exports, land grabs, and extractive industries relocate costs to the Global South through an unequal exchange in which Northern countries import resources and labor at low prices while exporting high-priced goods. Fourth, climate inequality: the richest 1% of the world emit more than twice as much as the poorest 50%, and the top 1% emit over 1,000 times more than the bottom 1%. Meeting the Paris Agreement's 1.5°C target would require the richest 1% to cut emissions by at least 30 times.

## Health and differential exposure

Communities that absorb environmental burdens face measurably worse health. Children metabolize contaminants differently from adults, and exposure begins in utero and accumulates through childhood, raising risks of respiratory, gastrointestinal, and developmental conditions. Workers in agriculture, mining, and textile production carry contaminants home on skin and clothing, exposing their families. Poor people account for more than 20% of human health impacts from industrial toxic air releases in the US, compared to 12.9% of the population.

## Intersecting dimensions

Environmental injustice compounds with other forms of marginalization. The EPA's official definition omits sex, and women remain underrepresented in institutionalized decision-making, though they often lead grassroots campaigns. LGBTQ+ communities face discrimination in housing, leading to higher exposure to air pollution and heat, and exclusion from disaster relief, as documented after Hurricane Katrina and the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami. Indigenous peoples face both historical contamination, such as uranium mining at Churchrock, New Mexico, which contaminated Navajo water supplies from 1954 to 1968, and ongoing displacement. Indigenous territories cover about 22% of the world's land surface but hold roughly 80% of its remaining biodiversity.

## Documented conflicts

The Environmental Justice Atlas recorded 3,100 environmental conflicts worldwide as of April 2020. Representative cases include the Ogoni people's resistance to Shell Oil in the Niger Delta, where 1.5 million tons of oil were spilled between 1956 and 2006, fifty times the volume of the Exxon Valdez spill, and the execution of nine Ogoni activists, including Ken Saro-Wiwa, in 1995; the Tiwi people's legal challenges to Santos's Barossa gas field off northern Australia; the assassination of Berta Caceres in Honduras in 2016 for opposing the Agua Zarca Dam; and Cancer Alley in Louisiana, an 85-mile stretch hosting 125 petrochemical plants with disproportionately high cancer rates among Black residents. In 2023, sixteen Montana youth aged 2 to 18 won *Held v. Montana*, the first US constitutional climate case to go to trial, upheld by the state supreme court on December 18, 2024.

The recognition that environmental harm is not randomly distributed has generated legal tools, global treaties, and grassroots organizations designed to give affected communities decision-making power over the activities that shape their lives.
