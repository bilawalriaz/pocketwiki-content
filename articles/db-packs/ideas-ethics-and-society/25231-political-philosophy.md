# Political philosophy

Political philosophy, also called political theory, is the normative study of how political life ought to be organised. It analyses the legitimacy, scope, and structure of states, the forms governments take, and the values, including justice, equality, and liberty, that should guide political action. Unlike political science, which describes how institutions actually behave, political philosophy asks what they should look like and why. Governments are not natural facts but human creations, and can be designed, justified, or criticised.

## Core concepts

**State and government.** A state is an organised political community exercising control over a territory and its citizens. Government is the institution that exercises that control. Many governments are constrained by constitutions, which institutionalise the rule of law and limit arbitrary power.

**Power, legitimacy, and authority.** Political power is the ability to produce intended effects, whether through consent or coercion. Legitimacy is the rightful exercise of that power, typically requiring that it be acquired by established rules and used for justifiable ends. Authority is the recognised right to rule; the two can diverge, and some theorists discuss illegitimate authority, where belief in legitimacy is mistaken. Max Weber distinguished three ideal types: charismatic, traditional, and rational-legal.

**Forms of government.** Power may rest with the people (democracy, direct or representative), a single ruler (autocracy, including monarchy and dictatorship), or a few (oligarchy). Authoritarian regimes reject pluralism; totalitarian variants such as fascism seek sweeping control over public and private life. Rule by elites defines aristocracy; meritocracy and technocracy select rulers by skill or expertise. Theocracies derive authority from religious law. Federalism and confederalism distribute authority across levels, contrasting with unitary states.

**Law and rights.** Laws are rules of social conduct. Natural law theory treats them as expressions of universal moral principles; legal positivism treats them as human conventions. Political obligation is the duty to obey the laws of one's community, though moral obligation may override unjust laws. Rights are legal entitlements protecting interests and freedom. Property, whether public, private, or collective, is foundational to most legal systems and a central concern of political philosophy.

**Justice, equality, liberty.** Justice means receiving what one deserves, expressed in distributive terms (fair allocation of goods) and retributive terms (proportional punishment). Equality before the law, political equality, and equal opportunity contrast with equality of outcome. Liberty has two complementary senses: positive liberty (the power to act) and negative liberty (freedom from interference). Autonomy is the capacity to govern oneself by informed choice. Welfare, well-being, and happiness are common standards for evaluating political arrangements.

## Major schools

**Anarchism** rejects centralised, coercive government as incompatible with liberty and equality, advocating voluntary association and self-governance. It splits into individualist strands (emphasising personal autonomy) and collectivist or socialist strands (emphasising communal ownership).

**Conservatism** values traditional institutions and gradual reform, remaining sceptical of radical change, which it sees as risking the accumulated wisdom of past generations. It typically defends family, religion, private property, and national identity.

**Liberalism** centres on individual rights, the rule of law, tolerance, and constitutional government. Classical liberalism favours a minimal state protecting negative liberty; modern liberalism accepts a more active state promoting positive liberty, social justice, and equal opportunity. Libertarianism limits state power to defence and enforcement of contracts.

**Socialism** holds that the means of production should be socially owned to overcome inequality, class hierarchy, and hereditary privilege. Marxism analyses capitalism as producing class conflict that ends in socialism and, eventually, communism, a classless society of collective ownership. Critics argue central planning reduces productivity or reproduces hierarchies.

**Other ideologies** include environmentalism (anthropocentric or ecocentric), realism (politics as power driven by self-interest), idealism (politics guided by moral principles), consequentialism and utilitarianism (judging actions by outcomes, especially happiness), nationalism, individualism and communitarianism (the individual-versus-community axis), republicanism (civic virtue and rule of law), feminism (gender equality), and postmodernism (scepticism toward universal truth-claims).

## Methodology

Political philosophy must justify normative claims that observation alone cannot settle. Particularists reason bottom-up from individual intuitions; foundationalists reason top-down from broad principles such as human nature or utility. Reflective equilibrium combines both. Foundationalist appeals to human nature face the is-ought problem: descriptive facts do not themselves entail normative conclusions. Universalism claims that basic political principles apply to all cultures; cultural relativism denies this. Thought experiments such as John Rawls's original position test theories by imagining fair-minded choosers behind a veil of ignorance.

## History

Political philosophy began in antiquity. Plato defended rule by philosopher-kings; Aristotle preferred practical, mixed constitutions. In ancient China, Confucianism grounded social order in benevolence, Taoism in harmony with nature, and Legalism in strict law. Indian thought, from the Rig Veda onward, debated caste and duty; Buddhist thought rejected caste in favour of universal equality. Medieval philosophy wove Greek thought with Christian and Islamic theology; Augustine and Aquinas developed natural law theory, while Al-Farabi, Al-Mawardi, and Ibn Khaldun theorised caliphates and statecraft.

The early modern period turned secular. Machiavelli grounded politics in power; Hobbes grounded it in a social contract to escape a war of all against all. Locke and Rousseau based legitimate authority on consent; Kant extended this to universal rights. Burke shaped conservatism; Bentham and Mill developed utilitarianism; Wollstonecraft pioneered feminist political thought; Hegel and Marx placed history and class conflict at the centre; Proudhon founded anarchism. The twentieth century saw the discipline eclipsed by political science, then revived by Arendt, Rawls, Nozick, and Foucault, while Gandhi reframed political struggle through nonviolence and Mao reinterpreted Marxism for peasant societies.
