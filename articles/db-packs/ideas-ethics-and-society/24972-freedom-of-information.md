# Freedom of information

Freedom of information is the right of individuals to publish and access information, grounded in Article 19 of the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights, which guarantees everyone the ability to "receive and impart information and ideas through any media and regardless of frontiers." The concept sits between two larger human rights: freedom of expression, which protects what people say and how they say it, and privacy, which protects what is said about them. Governments treat access to information as an extension of free speech, and freedom-of-information (FOI) laws translate that right into a tool citizens can use against the state. UNESCO frames the goal more broadly through inclusive Knowledge Societies, covering open standards, digital heritage preservation, multilingual content, and digital literacy.

## Barriers to access

Information scholar Michael Buckland identified six obstacles that must be cleared before access is real: finding the source, the source being available, the user being able to afford it, the provider being willing to supply it, the user being able to understand it, and the user being willing to use it. "Access to information," "right to information," and "right to know" are near-synonyms, though each term subtly shifts the emphasis. Most of these barriers map directly onto the digital divide.

## The digital divide

By 2024 roughly 5.35 billion people, about 66% of the world's population, were online, but unequal access to information and communication technologies persists, shaped by income, geography, race, gender, age, and disability.

- **Race and ethnicity.** In the United States, 81% of White and 83% of Asian households had home internet, versus 70% of Hispanic, 68% of Black, 72% of American Indian/Alaska Native, and 68% of Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander households; the gaps remain even at low incomes, pointing to structural discrimination.
- **Disability.** 54% of U.S. households including a person with a disability had home internet, versus 81% of those without. UK online disability hate crimes rose 33% in one year, often taking the form of accusations of "faking" disability.
- **Gender.** Wharton reported a 31% gender ICT gap in some developing countries and 12% globally in 2016, driven by lower pay, illiteracy, and local bans on women's mobile-phone use.
- **LGBTQIA.** Several states censor sexual-orientation and gender-identity content, and YouTube's 2017 decision to mark non-explicit LGBTQIA videos as "restricted" showed how platforms can narrow access.

## Censorship and surveillance

Reporters Without Borders lists Cuba, Iran, Maldives, Myanmar, North Korea, Syria, Tunisia, Uzbekistan, and Vietnam as "internet enemies." The Great Firewall of China blocks content at network gateways through IP filtering, proxy servers, and DNS poisoning. Digital-age security concerns have legitimised expanded state surveillance; the UN Human Rights Council responded in 2015 by creating a Special Rapporteur on the Right to Privacy and, in 2017, condemned unlawful surveillance as a threat to freedom of expression.

## Legal tensions

Three legal conflicts shape the current landscape. First, the 2014 Google Spain ruling by the European Court of Justice established a right to be forgotten, allowing EU citizens to demand de-listing of search results; the doctrine has since spread to Latin America. Recital 153 of the EU General Data Protection Regulation carves out journalism and artistic expression from data-protection rules. Second, mass surveillance and mandatory data retention put journalists' sources at risk; between 2007 and mid-2015, 84 of 121 countries surveyed revised source-protection laws. As of 2015, at least 60 nations had adopted some form of whistleblower protection, and 179 states had ratified the UN Convention against Corruption. Third, only four states had enshrined a general right to encryption in national law, while 31 had granted agencies power to intercept or decrypt communications; WhatsApp's end-to-end encryption and Apple's refusal to unlock a terrorist suspect's iPhone crystallised the dispute.

## How FOI laws work

FOI laws let citizens request government records. The first modern law passed in what is now Finland and Sweden 250 years ago. UNESCO monitors Sustainable Development Goal indicator 16.10.2, which tracks countries adopting FOI guarantees; 112 countries now have such legislation, up from roughly 70 in 2006, with Africa more than doubling its count since 2012. Implementation lags: of 109 countries with data, 43% lacked sufficient public outreach and 43% defined exceptions too broadly. Most laws cover government bodies, but only 19 countries extended coverage to private bodies, often only when those bodies receive public funds or perform public functions. The Open Government Partnership (launched 2011) and Open Data Charter (2015) push governments toward proactive disclosure rather than reactive request-based access. Top-rated laws tend to be younger, suggesting that implementation improves as legal frameworks mature.

Practical access to information depends less on whether a country has an FOI law than on whether the legal framework is enforced, whether digital divides are narrowed, and whether surveillance capacities are constrained.
