# Medical ethics

Medical ethics is the applied branch of ethics that analyzes the practice of clinical medicine and scientific research. It rests on four values systematized by Tom Beauchamp and James Childress in *Principles of Biomedical Ethics* (1978): respect for **autonomy** (the patient's right to choose or refuse treatment), **beneficence** (acting in the patient's best interest), **non-maleficence** ("first, do no harm," Latin *primum non nocere*), and **justice** (fair distribution of scarce resources, including who receives treatment). These are not ranked; conflicts among them require case-by-case judgment, and societies resolve them differently.

## How the four principles conflict

Beneficence can clash with non-maleficence when a risky procedure has uncertain odds; Young and Wagner argue beneficence historically takes priority over non-maleficence. Autonomy can clash with beneficence when a competent patient refuses life-saving treatment, requests euthanasia, or seeks unnecessary cosmetic surgery. Western medicine generally defers to competent patients; many other societies prioritize beneficence or family decision-making.

**Double effect** describes a single act that produces both a good and a harmful outcome, such as giving morphine to ease a dying patient's pain while potentially shortening life; such acts are often accepted as morally permissible when the intent is relief and the harm is foreseen rather than intended. **Involuntary treatment** is justified when patients lack decision-making capacity due to conditions such as dementia, delirium, or severe depression.

**Euthanasia** illustrates the legal split: physician-assisted death is permitted in several US jurisdictions but treated as a felony in Louisiana, Mississippi, and Nebraska.

## The shift from paternalism to autonomy

Until the mid-19th century, physician-patient relationships were familiar, with doctors holding paternalistic decision-making power. As that trust eroded through malpractice and changing social values, authority shifted toward patient self-determination. The growing emphasis on autonomy is now seen as a reaction against historic paternalism, though some scholars worry the shift has over-corrected, suppressing useful "soft paternalism." Loss of autonomy characterizes many terminal illnesses, particularly dementia.

## Historical foundations

The **Hippocratic Oath** dates to the 5th century BCE. The **Formula Comitis Archiatrorum** (5th century) was the first written code. Islamic scholars (al-Ruhawi's *Conduct of a Physician*, Avicenna's *Canon*, ar-Razi), Jewish thinkers (Maimonides), and Catholic scholastics (Thomas Aquinas, casuistry) shaped medieval practice. Thomas **Percival** coined "medical ethics" in 1803; the **American Medical Association** adopted its first code in 1847, based on Percival. The **Nuremberg Code** (1947) responded to Nazi war crimes; the **Declaration of Helsinki** (1964) expanded it for research. In the 1960s–1970s, medical ethics largely reconfigured itself into the broader field of **bioethics**, incorporating procedural justice and institutional review boards. Twentieth-century abuses documented in cases such as Tuskegee, Henrietta Lacks, and Willowbrook drove the expansion of ethics committees, IRBs, and ethics-integrated curricula.

## Core clinical concepts

**Informed consent** requires that patients be competent and given information about a treatment's nature, purpose, risks, benefits, and alternatives before consenting. Exceptions apply in emergencies and for patients who lack competence. The same standard governs research participation. Failure to obtain consent creates legal liability for battery.

**Confidentiality** is protected by patient-physician privilege and, in the US, HIPAA (1996). Exceptions include mandatory reporting of gunshot wounds, impaired drivers, and threats to identified third parties (the duty to warn).

**Conflicts of interest** distort judgment when financial or personal interests intrude. Self-referral increases test ordering; drug-company gifts influence prescribing, prompting bans at Stanford, Johns Hopkins, Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Yale. Fee-splitting is widely proscribed. The AMA advises physicians generally not to treat immediate family. Sexual contact with patients is prohibited, with psychiatrists and obstetrician-gynecologists showing higher rates of reported misconduct.

## Culture, values, and contemporary pressures

Different cultures implement the four principles differently. **Chinese** medicine, influenced by yin-yang philosophy and Confucian family-centered ethics, often favors family decision-making over individual consent. **Buddhist** ethics prohibits suicide and euthanasia; sedatives that cloud dying consciousness are discouraged because the dying state is thought to affect rebirth. **Islamic** medicine promotes beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice strongly, treats autonomy as important but more nuanced, and uses Islamic jurisprudence to adapt to new questions.

The 2019 **COVID-19** pandemic forced rationing of ventilators, PPE, and ICU beds, raised questions about the speed of open science versus safety, and intensified health disparities.

Enforcement varies: in the UK, breach of ethics can bar a physician from practice. Ethics committees, typically including clinicians, philosophers, lawyers, and lay community members, resolve complex cases. Medical ethics tends to demand a higher standard than law requires.
