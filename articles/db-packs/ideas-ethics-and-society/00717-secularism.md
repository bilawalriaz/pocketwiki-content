# Secularism

Secularism organises public life by naturalistic, material considerations rather than religious authority, most often expressed as the separation of religion from the state. It is not synonymous with atheism and need not oppose belief; instead it seeks to minimise religion's role in government and public institutions, ranging from outright hostility toward religion to neutrality to active accommodation of it.

## Core principles

Across its variants, secularism rests on a small set of commitments: legal equality regardless of belief or non-belief; separation of church and state, so that no religion is officially endorsed; rule of civil law over religious law in public affairs; freedom of thought and conscience, including freedom from religion; and non-discrimination among religious and non-religious groups.

## Historical development

The idea has ancient roots in the distinction between sacred and secular authority, but the modern form emerged in the early modern period. Philosophers such as Augustine, William of Ockham, Marsilius of Padua, Martin Luther, Roger Williams, John Locke, and Talleyrand gradually shifted the basis of legitimate authority from scripture to reason and contract. The English word was coined in 1851 by George Holyoake, a British agnostic, who used it to mean living by naturalistic considerations without necessarily rejecting religion; he distinguished secularism from atheism.

Major institutional milestones followed: the 1636 founding of Providence Plantations by Roger Williams with total religious freedom; the incorporation of Lockean ideas into the U.S. Constitution and Bill of Rights between 1787 and 1791; the 1790s French Revolution's replacement of Catholic influence with the Cult of Reason; France's 1905 law entrenching laïcité; Mustafa Kemal Atatürk's laiklik reforms in Turkey during the 1920s and 1930s; India's adoption of a secular constitution in 1947; and the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights, which protected freedom of religion globally.

## State frameworks

Modern secularism appears in four main shapes. Separationist secularism, exemplified by the United States, bars state support for any religion and enforces strict neutrality. Laïcité, used in France, asserts the legal supremacy of the state and restricts religious expression in public spaces; it was codified in the 1905 separation law. Kemalist laiklik adapts laïcité to Turkey. Accommodationism permits general support for religion without favouring any one sect and is the dominant model in India, with growing influence in parts of the United States. State atheism, associated with Marxist-Leninist regimes, bans religious practice altogether; whether it counts as secularism is itself debated, because it suppresses rather than tolerates belief.

Indian political discourse adds the pejorative term *pseudo-secularism*, used to attack policies that claim neutrality while allegedly favouring one religion.

## Contemporary challenges

Most modern democracies are considered secular because they protect religious freedom and limit clerical political power, yet surveys show public attitudes vary sharply: comfort with religion's public role is higher in the United States than in Europe. Secularism correlates with social progressivism and with urban, educated demographics, but remains a minority self-identity in many countries.

Two intellectual pressures have weakened secularism's framing in recent decades. Critics argue that secular ethics lack a firm moral foundation. Philosophers such as John Rawls have proposed *overlapping consensus*, in which groups holding incompatible worldviews agree on basic political principles without appealing to any one tradition. This raises the question of whether secularism, as a label, still does analytical work, or whether it has been absorbed into broader human-rights and democratic frameworks. The "post-secularism" debate extends the point: as religion re-enters public life, the older separationist model looks less like a neutral default and more like one contested position among several.
