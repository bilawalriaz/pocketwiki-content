# Altered level of consciousness

An altered level of consciousness (LOC) is any measurable departure from normal arousal and responsiveness to the environment. Prolonged unconsciousness is a medical emergency, and many clinicians treat LOC as a vital sign alongside pulse, blood pressure, and temperature, because a falling LOC correlates with higher morbidity and mortality.

## The graded spectrum

Consciousness exists on a scale from full alertness to coma. A normally alert person spontaneously states their name, location, and the time and is "oriented x3." Normal sleep, from which a person is easily awakened, also counts as a normal level. From there, the level can slip through:

- **Clouding of consciousness:** mild impairment of attention and wakefulness.
- **Confusion:** disorientation, slow thinking, difficulty following instructions.
- **Delirium:** disorientation with restlessness, agitation, and sometimes hallucinations or delusions.
- **Somnolence:** excessive drowsiness, with only incoherent mumbles or disorganised movements in response to stimuli.
- **Obtundation:** reduced interest in surroundings, slowed responses, persistent sleepiness.
- **Stupor:** a sleep-like state with little spontaneous activity; the person responds only by grimacing or pulling away from pain.
- **Coma:** no purposeful response at all, with loss of corneal, gag, and pupillary light reflexes.

The terms overlap across textbooks, but the underlying idea is consistent: as the brain becomes less able to process and react to stimuli, behaviour fades from alert conversation to silence.

## Measuring the level

The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is the standard bedside tool. It scores eye opening, verbal response, and motor response from 3 to 15, with lower scores meaning deeper depression of consciousness. Quicker alternatives include the AVPU scale (Alert, responsive to Voice, responsive to Pain, Unresponsive), the ACDU scale (Alert, Confused, Drowsy, Unresponsive), and the Grady Coma Scale, which grades from I (confusion) to V (coma).

## Why consciousness fails

Wakefulness depends on the **ascending reticular activating system (ARAS)**, a network in the reticular formation of the brain stem that receives sensory input and projects through the midbrain and thalamus to the cerebral cortex. Damage, pressure, or chemical disruption along this pathway, or to both cerebral hemispheres, lowers consciousness. Mass lesions in the brain stem usually produce stupor or coma because they directly affect the reticular formation. Lesions above the tentorium cerebelli only do so when very large or when they affect both hemispheres.

## Common causes

Anything that deprives the brain of oxygen, fuel, or electrical stability can depress LOC. Major categories include:

- **Oxygen or blood supply problems:** hypoxia, shock, ischemic stroke, brain bleeding, cardiac and pulmonary conditions.
- **Metabolic disturbances:** low or high blood glucose, abnormal sodium (hyponatremia or hypernatremia), dehydration, diabetic ketoacidosis, uremia, pH outside the brain's tolerable range, high ammonia in newborns (suggesting an inborn error of metabolism), vitamin B12 deficiency, and thyroid disease.
- **Drugs and toxins:** alcohol, prescription and non-prescription drugs, poisons.
- **Temperature extremes:** severe hypothermia or hyperthermia.
- **Pressure and structural injury:** raised intracranial pressure, traumatic brain injury including concussion, brain tumours, and brain herniation.
- **Seizure-related states:** epilepsy and the post-seizure period.
- **Infections of the central nervous system:** meningitis and encephalitis, with altered LOC the most common symptom of encephalitis.

A decreased LOC can also result from a combination of these factors.

## Diagnosis and first response

Assessment begins with observation: how quickly and accurately the person responds to questions, touch, and pain, and whether reflexes such as cough and gag are intact. In the emergency setting, the first investigations usually include pulse oximetry to rule out hypoxia, a blood glucose to rule out hypoglycaemia, a urine drug screen, and a CT head to look for bleeding. A lumbar puncture is added if meningitis is suspected.

Initial treatment targets the most easily reversible causes: intravenous dextrose for low blood sugar, oxygen for hypoxia, naloxone for opioid overdose, and thiamine, given with glucose to prevent Wernicke encephalopathy in malnourished patients. Subsequent care addresses the underlying cause revealed by the workup.
