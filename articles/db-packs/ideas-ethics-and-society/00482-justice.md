# Justice

Justice is the principle of treating people fairly. The Roman *Institutes of Justinian* (6th century) called it "the constant and perpetual will to render to each his due," and John Rawls called it the "first virtue of social institutions." It governs how goods are shared, how wrongdoing is answered, and how legal systems are built. Almost every serious disagreement in political philosophy is a different answer to one of three questions: what people are owed, how to respond to harm, and what gives justice its authority.

## What people are owed

Distributive justice asks what gets distributed (wealth, power, opportunity), among whom, and by what rule. The main answers each pick a different rule.

- **Equality**: the same share for everyone. Anatole France mocked the bare version: "the law, in its majestic equality, forbids rich and poor alike to sleep under bridges." *Equity* adjusts for context, treating equals equally and unequals unequally as the situation requires.
- **Merit**: goods follow talent and effort.
- **Need**: "from each according to ability, to each according to need."
- **Proportionality**: rewards and punishments match the gravity of the act, a principle with roots in Plato that now anchors criminal law and human rights.
- **Entitlement** (Nozick, 1974): a holding is just if it came by just acquisition (mixing labor with unowned resources) or just transfer (voluntary gift or sale). Anything else, including redistributive taxation, is theft, because justice is historical, not a pattern imposed on snapshots.
- **Fairness** (Rawls, 1971): two principles. First, everyone gets the maximum equal basic liberties. Second, social and economic inequalities are allowed only if they benefit the least advantaged (the *difference principle*) and attach to positions open to all under fair equality of opportunity. To model impartial choice, Rawls uses the *veil of ignorance*: parties who don't know their own race, class, or talents pick principles no one could bias toward themselves.

These rules collide. Strict equality clashes with merit; patterned distribution (Rawls, merit, need) clashes with historical entitlement (Nozick). Primate research (Emory, 2003) and neuroscience (UCLA, 2008) suggest the human sense of fairness is evolutionarily ancient and neurologically basic, older than any of these theories.

## How to respond to wrongdoing

Three families dominate, and they answer differently whether punishment can ever be unfair.

- **Retributive justice** holds that the guilty *deserve* punishment in proportion to the crime, independently of future consequences. It forbids punishing the innocent, even when doing so would prevent greater harm.
- **Restorative justice** focuses on repairing harm. Victims and offenders meet, the offender takes responsibility, and the victim's needs are addressed. Meta-analyses show high victim satisfaction but no reduction in repeat offending.
- **Utilitarian justice** (Mill, 1861) makes the right act whatever maximizes total or average welfare. Punishment is justified by deterrence, rehabilitation, or incapacitation. Because only outcomes matter, utilitarianism can in principle justify punishing the innocent or imposing disproportionate penalties if the consequences warrant it. Mill treated the *feeling* that justice has been done as a compound of revenge and sympathy, not evidence of a basic moral fact.

Mixed theories (von Hirsch) keep desert as a hard ceiling, punish worse crimes more, but allow utilitarian goals inside that bound. Modern sentencing aims at several targets at once: retribution, deterrence, rehabilitation, incapacitation, denunciation, and restitution. Civil cases add damages, injunctions, and *disgorgement* (stripping the wrongdoer's gains even when no one lost). Speed matters: "justice delayed is justice denied" is encoded in many constitutions as a right to a speedy trial.

## What makes justice authoritative

Four traditions ground it, and they disagree sharply.

- **Divine command** ties justice to God's will. The *Euthyphro* dilemma (Plato) presses the theory: is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good? The first horn makes morality arbitrary; the second makes it independent of God. Jewish, Christian, and Muslim traditions often pair justice with mercy. Buddhist ethics rejects the desert frame entirely: karma is the causal continuation of intentions, and justice means reducing suffering through compassion, interdependence (*pratītyasamutpāda*), and non-duality.
- **Natural law** says reason can discover universal moral principles. Aquinas grounded equal human dignity in reason as a "spark of the divine"; Locke used natural law to justify property and a right to revolution. It opposes *legal positivism* (law as human command) and divine-right monarchy.
- **Social contract** (Rousseau, 1762; Rawls) derives justice from mutual agreement, whether a real historical compact or a hypothetical fair bargain. Justice is what free, equal people would consent to under impartial conditions.
- **Consequentialism** (Mill) treats justice as derived from welfare rather than as a freestanding principle, which is what licenses the unsettling utilitarian conclusions about punishing the innocent.

These frameworks often conflict. A retributivist and a utilitarian can agree on the same punishment, but for opposite reasons, and they part company the moment punishing an innocent person would save lives.

## Critiques and live debates

A second wave of theories argues that distributing goods to individuals misses the point. **Social justice** focuses on the relationship between people and society, including privilege, mobility, and opportunity, distinct from *egalitarianism* (equal status) and *cosmopolitanism* (a single global community). Friedrich Hayek called "social justice" meaningless, treating justice as emergent from individual action in markets. **Relational justice** (Iris Marion Young, Young Kim) goes further: distribution matters, but so do power, group difference, and institutional structure, and a theory ignoring them cannot call itself just.

Psychology adds another wrinkle. Victims care about more than outcomes: they want respectful treatment, information, a voice, and a fair procedure. Simon Pemberton's "Big 2" model pins justice needs to *agency* (control) and *communion* (social belonging), and treats crime as a rupture of trust in a just world. Repair often means rebuilding agency and belonging more than extracting punishment. Evolutionary ethics warns that fairness intuitions were shaped for small ancestral groups and may misfire in modern, anonymous societies.

A live question runs through all of it: whether justice is a basic moral fact (Rawls, Aquinas, natural law) or a useful fiction built on deeper commitments like welfare (Mill), God's nature, or social agreement. The choice of foundation changes the verdicts: whether taxing the rich is theft or duty, whether punishing the innocent is ever rational, whether equality of outcome is a right or a mistake, and whether institutions that enforce power, from courts to historical slavery, can ever count as just.
