# Feminist philosophy

Feminist philosophy is an approach to philosophy that takes a feminist perspective and applies philosophical methods to feminist questions. It has two complementary tasks. The first is to reinterpret existing philosophical texts and methods so they serve feminist aims. The second is to criticise or re-evaluate traditional philosophy from within a feminist framework. The field is united by two commitments: a focus on gender, and some form of commitment to justice for women. Beyond those, it is internally diverse. Practitioners come from both the analytic (clarity-driven, logic-oriented) and continental (historically rooted, interpretive) traditions and span many varieties of feminism, and they study how race, sexuality, socioeconomic class, and other identity factors intersect with gender.

The field can be organised by three functions. It uses philosophical tools to theorise feminist concerns such as identity, equality, and oppression. It investigates sexism (bias against women) and androcentrism (centring of male experience) in the philosophical tradition, including by critiquing canonical texts for their treatment of women and recovering the work of overlooked female philosophers. And it contributes new questions and methods to philosophy itself, generated by reflecting on gender.

Feminist philosophy predates the twentieth century but acquired its name alongside second-wave feminism in the 1960s and 1970s, when many arguments targeted gender inequality in workplaces and education. Third-wave feminism then pushed the field to incorporate the experiences of women of different racial groups, classes, and global regions, broadening who counts as a subject of feminist thought.

The work is carried out in several subfields:

- **Feminist epistemology** challenges the traditional view that knowledge and rationality are objective, universal, or value-neutral. It argues that perspective, social situation, and values shape what gets known, including in the sciences.
- **Feminist ethics** argues that traditional moral thought's emphasis on objectivity, rationality, and universality excludes women's ethical realities. A major development is the ethics of care, which prizes empathy, responsibility, and non-violence, and recognises relations of care and dependency. Care ethics critiques ethics of justice as often rooted in patriarchal moral ideas, though some theorists warn that linking care to femaleness can reinforce the idea that moral development is essentially gendered.
- **Feminist phenomenology** (the study of lived experience) studies how cognitive faculties and social norms together shape lived reality. It centres personal and embodied experience, including experiences that resist articulation in language, and develops accounts of intersubjectivity (shared experience between subjects), relational life, and gendered embodiment. Recent work has extended this to temporality and how time shapes gendered existence.
- **Feminist aesthetics** examines gender and sexuality in art and aesthetic theory, including the subjectivity of creators, the reproduction of gendered norms, art's role in enculturation, and the representation of women. Since at least the Romantic era, "women" and "artist" have been treated as mutually exclusive categories, and the field intervenes against this patriarchal framing of aesthetics.
- **Feminist metaphysics** focuses on the ontology (the study of what exists and what categories things belong to) of gender and sex and the nature of social construction. Rather than treating the "real" and the "socially constructed" as a clean binary, it investigates how they interrelate and how systems of classification presented as natural often carry hidden political and moral biases. Some theorists question whether parts of metaphysics are inherently inhospitable to feminist critique, so the relationship between feminism and metaphysics remains contested.
- **Feminist philosophy of science** treats scientific practice as value-laden rather than value-free, arguing that ideologies, including those about gender, are embedded in the models and methods that produce scientific knowledge.

Running through these subfields is a recurring methodological move: take a category (knowledge, morality, perception, art, reality, science) that mainstream philosophy treats as neutral or universal, show how it is shaped by gender and related structures of power, and rebuild the account on that basis.
