# Freedom of the press

Freedom of the press is the principle that communication and expression through printed and electronic media should be exercised as a right, free of government censorship or prior restraint. It is usually protected by statute or constitution, and most countries couple it with freedom of speech, since the legal logic treats published and spoken expression the same. The right is not unlimited: governments may still enforce laws on classified material, state secrets, libel, copyright, privacy, and judicial gag orders. Where transparency is strong, freedom-of-information acts let citizens demand government records.

The concept often surprises novices because its legal meaning is narrower than its political one. Freedom of the press does not mean anyone can publish anything; it means the state may not stop publication in advance. A publisher also has the exclusive right to refuse content, so in practice the freedom belongs first to owners of outlets, not to authors. Twentieth-century American journalist A. J. Liebling summarized this with the line, "Freedom of the press is guaranteed only to those who own one." Authors who cannot reach a publishing deal must self-publish.

Three landmarks bracket the modern history. Britain formally ended press licensing when the Licensing of the Press Act lapsed in 1695, ending a system that had required government permission for every publication. Sweden went further in 1766 with the Freedom of the Press Act, the world's first constitutional guarantee of press freedom. Article 19 of the UN's 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights extended the idea globally, declaring that everyone has the right "to seek, receive, and impart information and ideas through any media regardless of frontiers." In Europe, Article 10 of the 1950 European Convention on Human Rights makes media freedom a binding right across member states, and the EU treats it as a key indicator of readiness to join.

Philosophical defenses of the principle developed alongside the laws. John Milton's 1644 pamphlet *Areopagitica* argued that individuals can use reason to judge good and bad ideas, so the state should not preemptively silence them; he helped originate the "marketplace of ideas," the view that open argument lets strong arguments prevail. John Stuart Mill made a utilitarian case in *On Liberty* (1859): silencing any opinion, even one held by a single dissenter, risks silencing truth, so free expression is necessary for social well-being. John Peter Zenger's 1735 acquittal in colonial New York established truth as a defense against libel in American practice.

In democratic theory, an independent press functions as a watchdog: it informs voters, raises political knowledge and turnout, and exposes both government and private abuse. In non-democratic systems, strict control of information is essential to regime survival, so states run official outlets, suppress dissent, and punish journalists through professional blacklisting, imprisonment, torture, kidnapping, and assassination. A small number of states dominate these abuses: in 2018 Turkey, China, Egypt, Eritrea, and Saudi Arabia accounted for roughly 70 percent of imprisoned journalists worldwide, and the global jailed total reached a record 262 in 2017. In 2023, nearly 75 percent of journalists killed worldwide were Palestinians who died in Israel's war in Gaza.

Three organizations dominate international monitoring. Reporters Without Borders publishes the annual Press Freedom Index from surveys of journalists, jurists, and activists, with Norway, Denmark, Sweden, and their neighbors typically at the top and North Korea, Eritrea, Iran, and Syria at the bottom. The Committee to Protect Journalists maintains a database of journalists killed and imprisoned and tracks impunity in murder cases. Freedom House issues a yearly Freedom of the Press report, scoring each country on a 100-point scale across political, legal, economic, and safety dimensions and labeling it "Free," "Partly Free," or "Not Free."

New technology continually shifts the boundary. Satellite television, internet publishing, encrypted voice over IP, anonymity tools like Tor, and secure drop systems for sources make journalism harder to suppress, because content can be produced cheaply, transmitted across borders, and shielded by cryptography. Governments respond with takedown orders, internet filtering, controls on internet service providers, and laws against "false information." Russia used this last tactic in March 2022, introducing up to 15-year sentences for publishing reports about the Russian military that the state deemed false, prompting at least 1,000 Russian journalists to flee and forcing independent outlets such as *Novaya Gazeta* to suspend print operations.
