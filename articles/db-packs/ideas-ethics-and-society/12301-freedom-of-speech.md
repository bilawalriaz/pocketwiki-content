# Freedom of speech

Freedom of speech is the right to express opinions and ideas without state retaliation, censorship, or legal sanction. It is recognised as a human right in Article 19 of the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR), which protects both holding opinions without interference and actively seeking, receiving, and imparting information through any medium. In legal usage, "freedom of expression" is the broader term, since speech is one medium among many.

The right is enshrined in binding instruments: Article 19 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR, 1966), Article 10 of the European Convention on Human Rights, Article 13 of the American Convention on Human Rights, and Article 9 of the African Charter. The ICCPR version adds a caveat: the right carries "special duties and responsibilities" and may be restricted for the rights or reputation of others, national security, public order, public health, or morals.

## Historical origins

The Athenian democratic principle of free speech emerged in the late 6th or early 5th century BC. In England, Edward Coke claimed freedom of speech as "an ancient custom of Parliament" in the 1590s, and the Bill of Rights 1689 established it as a constitutional right in parliamentary proceedings, a protection still operating today as parliamentary privilege.

One of the world's first freedom of the press acts passed in Sweden on 2 December 1766, championed by Anders Chydenius. It ended pre-publication censorship and introduced public access to official records. The 1789 French Declaration of the Rights of Man affirmed speech as inalienable in Article 11, and the First Amendment to the U.S. Constitution, ratified in 1791, became its most influential modern formulation.

John Milton's 1644 pamphlet *Areopagitica* argued against pre-publication licensing, insisting that truth emerges only through open contest with falsehood. The English Licensing Order was allowed to expire in 1695, ending state pre-censorship of the press. John Stuart Mill's *On Liberty* (1859) became the classic philosophical defence: because truth is not fixed but evolves, silencing even false views harms humanity. Mill accepted only one ground for restriction, preventing clear and direct harm to others.

## Structure of the right

Based on Milton's arguments, freedom of expression has four distinct components: the right to **hold** opinions without interference, **seek** information and ideas from others, **receive** information communicated by others, and **impart** information and ideas to others. The protection covers both the content of expression and the medium through which it travels, including print, art, and the internet.

## Limitations

Freedom of speech is not absolute. Recognised restrictions include libel, slander, obscenity, incitement, fighting words, hate speech, classified information, copyright violation, trade secrets, non-disclosure agreements, privacy, blasphemy, and perjury.

Two principles structure these limits:

- Harm principle (Mill): the only justification for restricting speech is preventing harm to others.
- Offence principle (Feinberg, 1985): serious offence to others can also justify restriction, considering extent, duration, social value, ease of avoidance, speaker's motives, and intensity.

In the United States, *Brandenburg v. Ohio* (1969) set the standard for political speech: advocacy of force is protected unless directed to inciting imminent lawless action and likely to cause it. Several European countries, including Germany, France, Austria, and Russia, criminalise Holocaust denial, and many restrict blasphemy. Hate speech is broadly protected under U.S. law but restricted elsewhere, especially where it threatens violence.

## A negative right

Freedom of speech is generally treated as a negative right: the government must refrain from punishing speakers for their views, but no one is required to provide a platform, listen, or agree. This distinction matters for media: freedom of the press does not guarantee freedom of speech if media owners suppress diverse voices, captured in the phrase "Freedom of the press is guaranteed only to those who own one."

## Speech and democracy

The link between free speech and democracy is central. Alexander Meiklejohn argued that self-government requires an informed electorate, which is impossible if those in power can withhold information or stifle criticism. Thomas Emerson argued free speech acts as a safety valve, releasing pressure that might otherwise build toward revolution, while letting the community maintain a balance between necessary consensus and healthy disagreement.

## Digital age

The internet extends freedom of expression to new mediums but brings new restrictions. Countries use internet censorship to block content: Reporters without Borders identifies China, Cuba, Iran, Myanmar, North Korea, Saudi Arabia, Syria, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, and Vietnam as pervasive censors. China's "Great Firewall" blocks IP routing and uses DNS poisoning at internet gateways.

In the United States, the Communications Decency Act of 1996 was partly struck down in *Reno v. ACLU* (1997), with Judge Dalzell calling the internet "a far more speech-enhancing medium than print, the village green, or the mails." Social media platforms also use content moderation, removing legal speech that violates their terms of service.

Tim Wu has argued that the internet reverses the original scarcity problem: when listeners' attention is scarce, "cheap speech" can be used to harass, drown out, and silence as much as to illuminate. On 4 March 2022, Russia introduced prison sentences of up to 15 years for spreading "fake news" about its military, with more than 4,000 prosecutions by December 2022, even though Article 29 of the 1993 Russian Constitution expressly prohibits censorship.
