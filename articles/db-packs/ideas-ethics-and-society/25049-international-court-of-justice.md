# International Court of Justice

The International Court of Justice (ICJ), also called the World Court, is the principal judicial organ of the United Nations. It settles legal disputes between states and issues advisory opinions on legal questions referred by UN bodies. It is the only international court that adjudicates general disputes between countries, and its rulings form primary sources of international law.

The ICJ was established by the UN Charter on 26 June 1945 and began work in April 1946, replacing the Permanent Court of International Justice (PCIJ), which the League of Nations created in 1920. The PCIJ itself grew out of the Permanent Court of Arbitration, established at the first Hague Peace Conference in 1899. The ICJ Statute, an integral part of the UN Charter, draws heavily on the PCIJ Statute. All 193 UN member states are automatically parties to the statute.

## Judges and composition

Fifteen judges serve nine-year terms, with five elected every three years for continuity. No two judges share a nationality. The bench must represent the "main forms of civilization and the principal legal systems of the world", interpreted to include common law, civil law, socialist law, and Islamic law. An informal allocation gives Western states five seats, Asian states three, African states three, Eastern European states two, and Latin American and Caribbean states two.

Candidates are nominated by national groups within the Permanent Court of Arbitration. Judges are elected independently by the UN General Assembly and Security Council, each requiring an absolute majority. In the General Assembly, 97 of 193 votes constitute an absolute majority; in the Security Council, eight votes suffice, with no distinction between permanent and non-permanent members. A candidate elected by both organs takes the seat.

A party to a contentious case that lacks a national judge may appoint one *ad hoc* judge for that case only. *Ad hoc* judges usually vote for the appointing state but often cancel each other out. In rare instances, the court sits as a chamber of three or five judges for particular disputes.

## Jurisdiction

Only states may be parties in contentious cases; individuals, corporations, and NGOs cannot. Jurisdiction depends on consent, given through four routes: special agreements; compromissory clauses in treaties (treaty provisions that refer future disputes to the court); optional declarations under Article 36(2), sometimes misleadingly called "compulsory" jurisdiction; and tacit consent (*forum prorogatum*), where a respondent accepts jurisdiction by pleading on the merits. As of 2018, seventy-four states held such declarations; among the five permanent Security Council members, only the United Kingdom had one.

Party status does not grant automatic jurisdiction. After the 1986 *Nicaragua v. United States* ruling that US covert action violated international law, Washington withdrew its declaration and vetoed Security Council enforcement. This exposed the court's central weakness: its judgments are binding and final, but unenforceable without Security Council action, which any permanent member can veto. Advisory opinions, requested only by specified UN organs, are non-binding but carry weight as authoritative statements of international law. The court also orders provisional measures to protect a party's rights pending judgment, requiring only prima facie jurisdiction (jurisdiction that appears valid on initial review).

## Law applied and procedure

Under Article 38, the court applies international conventions, international custom, general principles of law, judicial decisions, and academic writing. It is not formally bound by its own previous decisions under *stare decisis* (the legal doctrine that obligates courts to follow prior rulings), though it frequently cites them. The court has never decided a case *ex aequo et bono* (on the basis of general fairness rather than strict legal rules).

Cases follow written pleadings, oral hearings, then deliberation and judgment. Preliminary objections to jurisdiction or admissibility are resolved before merits are addressed. Judges may issue concurring or dissenting opinions. No appeal exists, though parties may seek clarification. As of September 2025, the court has entertained 201 cases since its first in 1947.

## Seat and working languages

The court sits in the Peace Palace in The Hague, Netherlands, the only principal UN organ not based in New York. Its official languages are English and French.
