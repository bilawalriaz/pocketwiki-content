# Xunzi (philosopher)

Xunzi (c. 310 – c. after 238 BCE), born Xun Kuang in the state of Zhao, was a major Confucian philosopher of the late Warring States period and the third great Confucian after Confucius and Mencius. His central claim, that human nature is bad and must be reshaped by education, ritual, and teachers, became a cornerstone synthesis that helped Confucianism dominate the Han dynasty and later East Asia.

The core idea is simple and pessimistic: people are born with selfish desires for profit and violence, and if nothing intervenes, those impulses produce chaos. Goodness is not natural. It is manufactured through *artifice*, the conscious, learned effort and cultural conditioning that turns raw nature into orderly, ethical conduct. Humans are utterly improvable, but only because external shaping, not inner goodness, does the work.

This puts him in direct opposition to Mencius, who held that human nature is innately good. For Xunzi, the Mohist rejection of music and the Legalist reliance on punishment both miss the same point: they either remove a needed tool for harmony or assume human nature can be deterred rather than transformed. Ritual (*li*, the system of social norms, ceremonies, and propriety that structures society and cultivates virtue) and music (*yue*, essential for human joy and social cohesion) are the channels through which transformation happens, and the morally cultivated "gentleman" (*junzi*, the Confucian ideal of a morally cultivated individual) is the model who should lead the state. Laws and punishments are not enough on their own; governance needs scholars who embody ritual refinement.

His biography reinforces the philosophy. Xunzi studied at the Jixia Academy in Qi, the most important philosophical center in ancient China, learned all major traditions, and later taught there with such renown that he was called "the most revered of teachers." Around 265 BCE he visited Qin hoping to convert its leaders; later, around 240 BCE, he became Magistrate of Lanling in Chu under Lord Chunshen, a post he lost in 238 BCE when Chunshen was assassinated. He then retired and probably died shortly after 219 BCE, possibly in his nineties, around the time his student Li Si rose to power.

His students are the clearest measure of his reach. Han Fei and Li Si, two of the most influential Legalists of the next generation, trained under him, along with scholars who produced important editions of the Confucian classics. That a thinker who argued for moral transformation taught the architects of China's first legalist empire is one of the sharper ironies of the period.

Two interpretive cautions matter. First, the Chinese term he uses for human nature (*xing*, innate inborn qualities) is usually rendered "evil," but translators have also used "bad," "odious," or "problematic"; the underlying claim is that nature, left alone, produces disorder, not that people are metaphysically wicked. Second, his reputation collapsed from the Tang dynasty onward as Neo-Confucianism favored Mencius, and only a 20th-century reevaluation restored his standing.

Xunzi's lasting contribution is a Confucianism that takes human self-interest seriously, treats ritual as a technology of self-cultivation rather than empty ceremony, and hands the state to cultivated scholars rather than to either innate saints or coercive laws.
