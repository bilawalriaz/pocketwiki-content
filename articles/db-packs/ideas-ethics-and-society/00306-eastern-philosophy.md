# Eastern philosophy

Eastern philosophy names the philosophical traditions that developed across East and South Asia—principally India, China, Japan, Korea, and Vietnam. These traditions evolved both independently and through long mutual exchange, and they address the same questions as Western thought (reality, knowledge, ethics, the self) from different starting assumptions. The label itself is a 19th-century Western academic invention; the traditions it groups never thought of themselves as one category.

## Two Civilizations, Two Converging Traditions

Almost everything grouped under "Eastern philosophy" reduces to two civilizational currents. The Indian tradition, called *dárśana* ("world views"), is organized around the question of how to escape an endless cycle of rebirth. The Chinese tradition is organized around how to live harmoniously in human society and with the natural order. Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese thought are largely offshoots or syntheses of these two, inflected with local concerns.

## Indian Philosophy: Liberation from Rebirth

Indian thought rests on three linked commitments: the soul (*ātman*) is real, actions (*karma*) shape future experience, and the self wanders through repeated lives (*samsara*). The shared goal of nearly every Indian school is *moksha*, escape from this cycle. *Dharma* names the cosmic and moral law that karma operates within; *ahimsa* (non-violence) is one of its most prominent expressions.

Schools divide by their stance toward the Vedas, the oldest revealed scriptures:

- **Orthodox (āstika)** accept the Vedas. There are six: Nyāya (logic and epistemology), Vaiśeṣika (atomism), Sāmkhya (a dualism of consciousness and matter), Yoga (meditation), Mīmāṃsā (ritual interpretation), and Vedānta (metaphysics grounded in the Upanishads). Vedānta, which identifies the self with the ultimate reality Brahman, became dominant from the post-medieval period onward.
- **Heterodox (nāstika)** reject Vedic authority. They include Jainism (non-violence and soul liberation), Buddhism (ending suffering), the Cārvāka materialists, the fatalist Ājīvika, and the radical skeptics of the Ājñana school. These traditions emerged from the Śramaṇa movement of the 6th–4th centuries BCE.

A central fault line runs through Indian thought on the self. Hinduism and Jainism hold that an eternal soul (*ātman*) persists through rebirth. Buddhism denies this, teaching *anatta* (no-self): what we call the self is a temporary bundle of processes. The disagreement determines whether practice aims to reunite the self with its true nature or to dismantle the illusion that any self exists.

## Chinese Philosophy: Harmony in Society and Nature

Chinese philosophy emerged during the "Hundred Schools of Thought" period (6th century–221 BCE) and converged on two organizing concerns: social order and the human relation to the natural way.

- **Confucianism**, founded by Kongzi (Confucius, 551–479 BCE), centers on *rén* (benevolence) and *lǐ* (ritual propriety). A well-ordered society depends on cultivated relationships, especially *xiao* (filial piety). Confucianism became the Han state ideology and was later revived as Neo-Confucianism, whose leading figure Zhu Xi lived 1130–1200.
- **Daoism (Taoism)** offers a complementary vision: align with the *Dao*, the nameless principle underlying reality, through *wu wei* (non-forcing, effortless action). Its key texts are the *Dao De Jing* and the *Zhuangzi*. An organized Daoist school (Tianshi) emerged in the 2nd century CE; the philosophical revival Xuanxue, or Neo-Daoism, ran from the 3rd to 6th centuries.
- **Legalism** argued that stable order requires strict law and pragmatic statecraft, not moral cultivation.
- **Mohism** advocated impartial care and meritocratic governance.
- **Buddhism** entered China via the Silk Road during the Han dynasty and developed the distinct Chan school, known in Japan as Zen.

## Modern Transformations

From the 19th century, Asian traditions engaged Western thought, often under colonial pressure. Hindu modernism (Neo-Vedanta) reinterpreted Vedānta for a global audience. Buddhist modernism produced a psychologically inflected, often secularized presentation of Buddhist teaching. New Confucianism tried to preserve classical ethics while engaging modern realities. The Kyoto School in Japan brought Western phenomenology into dialogue with Zen. Carl Jung engaged Eastern sources, notably the *I Ching*. In the 20th century, Marxist philosophy, in its Maoist form, became China's dominant political framework.

## How the Pieces Fit

Indian philosophy asks how to escape the cycle of rebirth, answering through analysis, devotion, meditation, or insight. Chinese philosophy asks how to live well in a human world, answering through ritual, virtue, or alignment with the natural order. The two streams merged, diverged, and modernized across more than two millennia, and their unresolved tensions—especially over the existence of the self and the proper role of ancient authority—still shape how these traditions are understood and practiced today.
