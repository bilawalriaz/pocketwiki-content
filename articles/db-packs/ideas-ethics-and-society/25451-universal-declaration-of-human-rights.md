# Universal Declaration of Human Rights

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR) is a 1948 United Nations General Assembly resolution that sets out, in 30 articles, the basic rights and freedoms owed to every human being. Eleanor Roosevelt chaired the drafting committee; Canadian John Peters Humphrey wrote the principal draft; French jurist René Cassin shaped its final architecture. The General Assembly adopted it as Resolution 217 on 10 December 1948 at the Palais de Chaillot in Paris. Of 58 UN members, 48 voted in favour, none against, 8 abstained, and 2 did not vote. Each year, 10 December is observed as International Human Rights Day.

## What it says

The text opens with a preamble, followed by articles organised as a Greek temple in Cassin's image. Articles 1–2 form the foundation, declaring that all humans are "born free and equal in dignity and rights" and entitled to these rights without distinction of nationality, place of residence, sex, origin, colour, religion, language, or any other status. Four "columns" then group the remaining substantive rights, with three closing articles acting as the pediment:

| Articles | Domain | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Foundation | Dignity, liberty, equality, non-discrimination |
| 3–11 | Individual rights | Life, freedom from slavery and torture, legal remedies |
| 12–17 | Civil rights in society | Movement, residence, property, nationality, asylum |
| 18–21 | Spiritual and political freedoms | Thought, conscience, religion, expression, assembly |
| 22–27 | Economic, social, cultural rights | Work, health, housing, education, adequate standard of living |
| 28–30 | Pediment | Social and international order enabling the rights; limits; duties to others |

The rights are described as "inherent, inalienable, and applicable to all human beings." The drafters avoided references to any particular culture, political system, or religion, which is why the document is treated as secular, apolitical, and universalist.

## Why it was written

The Allies' 1941 "Four Freedoms" speech (freedom of speech, religion, from want, and from fear) framed the war's moral aims. The 1945 UN Charter reaffirmed a commitment to human rights but did not enumerate them. After Nazi atrocities became fully apparent, UN members judged that a catalogue was needed. In June 1946 the Economic and Social Council created the Commission on Human Rights, which in February 1947 set up the Drafting Committee chaired by Roosevelt. Key members were P. C. Chang of the Republic of China, René Cassin of France, and Charles Malik of Lebanon. The Committee met in two sessions, in 1947 and May 1948, before forwarding the text to the General Assembly's Third Committee, which debated 168 proposed amendments across 81 meetings before adoption.

## Legal status

The UDHR is not a treaty and is not itself legally binding. Roosevelt preferred a declaration so it could shape global opinion the way the US Declaration of Independence had shaped American political culture. Its provisions have since been elaborated into binding instruments. The International Bill of Human Rights was completed in 1966 and entered into force in 1976, comprising the UDHR together with the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights. All 193 current UN member states have ratified at least one of the nine core international human rights treaties that descend from the Declaration, and most have ratified four or more.

Many UDHR provisions are widely regarded as part of customary international law, though some domestic courts remain restrictive. The 2004 US Supreme Court ruling in *Sosa v. Alvarez-Machain* held that the Declaration "does not of its own force impose obligations as a matter of international law," yet US, Belgian, Dutch, Indian, and Sri Lankan courts continue to use it as an interpretive aid. At least 90 national constitutions drafted after 1948 draw on its language; Portugal, Romania, São Tomé and Príncipe, and Spain explicitly require their courts to interpret domestic norms consistently with the UDHR. The document has been translated into more than 560 languages, more than any other, earning a Guinness record.

## Reception and criticism

Saudi Arabia, the sole Muslim-majority abstainer in 1948, objected to Article 18's protection of the right to change religion and to Article 16 on equal marriage rights. Pakistan, then an Islamic state, voted in favour and argued for religious freedom. The six communist abstainers cited insufficient condemnation of fascism; Roosevelt attributed their stance chiefly to Article 13, which protected the right to leave one's country. The American Anthropological Association warned during drafting that a universalist Western text could not fairly represent non-Western societies, an objection later largely abandoned by the discipline. In 1990 the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation adopted the Cairo Declaration on Human Rights in Islam, an alternative grounded in sharia, and the 1993 Bangkok Declaration stressed sovereignty and economic development alongside universality.
