# Music and mathematics

Music theory analyzes pitch, timing, and structure using mathematics. Sound itself can be described mathematically through acoustics, and musical scales show distinctive number patterns.

The Pythagoreans were the first known researchers to express musical scales as ratios of small integers, holding that "all nature consists of harmony arising out of numbers." From Plato onward, harmony was treated as a branch of physics, now called musical acoustics.

## Rhythm, meter, and form

Rhythm requires a regular pulse structure of accents, phrases, and durations. Meter and measure entered modern usage partly because music, alongside astronomy, drove early counting, arithmetic, and the measurement of periodicity.

Musical form is the plan that extends a short piece, comparable to an architectural plan. The composer balances function, available means, economy, repetition, and order. Binary and ternary form depend on the small integers 2 and 3, and many hypermetric structures are built from powers of 2 and 3.

## Frequency, pitch, and the octave

A musical scale is a discrete set of pitches. Each pitch corresponds to a frequency in hertz (Hz). The most important Western scale is the diatonic scale.

The octave is the interval of repetition: a pitch one octave above another has exactly twice the frequency. Superoctaves are at 4×, 8×, 16×, and so on; suboctaves at½×, ¼×, ⅛×. Because octaves share the same name in all systems (doh, A, or Sa), a single pitch class covers every octave it falls in.

The octave is exponential. The interval from A₂ (110 Hz) to A₃ (220 Hz) spans 110 Hz; A₃ to A₄ spans 220 Hz; A₄ to A₅ spans 440 Hz. Each octave covers twice the frequency range of the previous one.

Because ratios between pitches (intervals) describe a scale more usefully than absolute pitches, scale tones are given as ratios from a tonic fixed at 1/1. Cents measure interval size.

## Tuning systems

There are two main families of tuning.

Equal temperament divides the octave into equal parts on a logarithmic scale. With 12 parts, each semitone equals the twelfth root of two, and twelve semitones sum to exactly one octave. Ratios between adjacent pitches are irrational. This system lets fretted instruments place frets evenly across strings and dominates Western and much non-Western music.

Just tuning multiplies frequencies by rational numbers. Ratios are simple (3:2, 5:4), but the resulting scale divisions are uneven. Voices and unfixed-pitch instruments naturally gravitate toward just intonation, but a fixed-pitch instrument like a piano cannot: it produces two different whole tones (9:8 and 10:9) and cannot change key without retuning.

5-limit tuning, the most common form of just intonation, uses regular-number harmonics of a single fundamental. Kepler presented it in *Harmonices Mundi* (1619), Alexander Malcolm described it in 1721, and a form is still used in northern Indian music.

Western common-practice music usually cannot be played in just intonation, so it requires a tempered scale. Meantone temperament reduces the difference between 9:8 and 10:9 whole tones, treating their ratio 81:80 (the syntonic comma) as a unison.

Other equal temperaments exist. 19-tone equal temperament, proposed by Guillaume Costeley in the 16th century, gives better thirds at the cost of a flatter fifth. 24-tone equal temperament is widespread in Arabic music pedagogy, though Arabic intonation in practice uses rational ratios and includes a neutral second of variable size, which Habib Hassan Touma identifies as a defining flavor of the tradition. 53-tone equal temperament arises because 53 perfect fifths nearly equal 31 octaves, noted by Jing Fang and Nicholas Mercator.

## Mathematical connections

Musical set theory applies mathematical set theory to organize musical objects. A piece is analyzed as a set of tones (motives or chords), and operations such as transposition and inversion are isometries because they preserve intervals.

Abstract algebra goes further. The pitch classes of the 12-tone equal-tempered octave form an abelian group with 12 elements, and just intonation can be described using a free abelian group. David Lewin's transformational theory emphasizes transformations between musical objects rather than the objects themselves. The theory of regular temperaments has been developed mathematically by associating each temperament with a rational point on a Grassmannian.

Some composers have used the golden ratio and Fibonacci numbers in their work. Guerino Mazzola has built a music theory on category theory and topos theory, using topology for rhythm and motives, and differential geometry for phrasing, tempo, and intonation.

Many musicians have trained in mathematics, including Philip Glass, who studied mathematics and philosophy at the University of Chicago; Donald Knuth, an organist and composer; Tom Lehrer, who studied mathematics at Harvard; and Manjul Bhargava, a tabla player who won the Fields Medal in 2014.
