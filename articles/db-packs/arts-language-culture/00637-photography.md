# Photography

Photography is the practice of making images by recording light, either electronically with a sensor or chemically with a light-reactive material such as film. Every camera combines two parts: an optical chamber that gathers light into an image, and a recording surface that holds it.

**Forming the image.** The optical principle behind every camera is the *camera obscura*, a darkened enclosure with a small hole that projects an inverted image of the outside scene onto a surface inside. Greek writers, including Aristotle and Euclid, described this effect around the 5th century BCE, and Ibn al-Haytham gave the first correct geometric analysis in the 10th century. Painters used the obscura as a drawing aid for centuries. The leap into photography was putting a surface at the back that could fix the image permanently.

**Fixing the image: the chemical era.** Early experimenters knew that silver compounds darken in light. Nicéphore Niépce coated a metal plate with light-sensitive bitumen and, in 1826, produced *View from the Window at Le Gras*, the earliest surviving photograph; the exposure took days. Partnering briefly with Louis Daguerre, Niépce pushed sensitivity higher before his death in 1833. Daguerre then turned to silver halides and announced the *daguerreotype* in January 1839, a direct-positive image on a silver-plated copper sheet with exposures of only minutes. Almost simultaneously, William Fox Talbot developed the *calotype*, a paper-negative process whose key innovation was reproducibility: from one negative, many positive prints could be made, the principle on which nearly all later photography depends.

Gains then came from faster, more convenient coatings. Frederick Scott Archer's wet collodion process of 1851 cut exposures to seconds and dominated for two decades until dry gelatin plates replaced it in the 1870s. George Eastman's flexible roll film, sold under the Kodak name from 1885, put a loaded camera in amateur hands. Nitrate film, highly flammable, was phased out in favour of cellulose acetate, marketed as safety film, beginning in 1908.

**Color.** Color photography rests on James Clerk Maxwell's 1861 demonstration that any color can be reconstructed from three black-and-white images taken through red, green, and blue filters. Gabriel Lippmann recorded color directly by light interference in 1891 and received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1908, though his method never became practical. The Lumière brothers' Autochrome plate of 1907 was the first commercially viable color process, using a mosaic of colored starch grains. Kodak's Kodachrome of 1935 introduced the modern three-layer emulsion, in which red-, green-, and blue-sensitive layers are stacked on a single film, and remained the standard for decades. Polaroid brought instant color film to consumers in 1963.

**The digital era.** A *digital sensor*, typically a CCD or CMOS chip, replaces film with an array of light-sensitive pixels that convert photons into electrical signals stored as numbers. Sony's Mavica of 1981 used a CCD but recorded analog video rather than still image files. Fujifilm's DS-1P of 1988 was the first fully digital still camera, and Kodak's DCS 100 of 1991, a digital SLR, opened the medium to professionals. Because a digital image is data rather than a one-off physical object, it can be copied losslessly, transmitted instantly, and altered at will, which is why digital photography has reshaped news, science, and daily life. By the early 21st century more than 99% of photographs were taken digitally, the great majority with smartphones, and chemical film survives mainly as a niche medium.

The shift from the camera obscura to the digital sensor did not change the physics of image formation; it changed only the surface that holds the result, and with it the speed, cost, and mutability of every image made.
