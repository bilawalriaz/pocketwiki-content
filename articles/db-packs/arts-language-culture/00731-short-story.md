# Short story

A short story is a brief, crafted prose narrative, usually readable in one sitting, that focuses on a single incident to evoke a unified effect or mood. The form has no fixed length, no compulsory structure, and no settled definition, which is why it has survived and adapted across cultures for centuries.

## What sets the form apart

A short story uses the same ingredients as a novel: plot, character, setting, and resonance. What distinguishes it is compression. Edgar Allan Poe, in his 1846 essay "The Philosophy of Composition," argued that every element must serve one pre-calculated emotional impact, a principle he called the "single effect." H.G. Wells gave the practical test: a short story takes "fifteen to fifty minutes to read aloud."

These definitions do not fully agree. Somerset Maugham and Hugh Walpole insisted on strict structure with a clear beginning, middle, and end. Anton Chekhov rejected that, treating the short story as a "slice of life" without obligatory openings or resolutions. The tension between engineered form and open observation has shaped the genre since the 19th century.

## Length

No word count is fixed. Typical short stories run 1,000 to 4,000 words, occasionally up to 15,000. Stories under 1,000 words are called "flash fiction." The Science Fiction and Fantasy Writers of America caps Nebula-eligible short stories at 7,500 words; anything longer becomes a novelette or novella. Length usually reflects what magazines will print or prizes will accept.

## Origins and chronology

The form's roots lie in oral storytelling: epics such as the *Ramayana*, the *Mahabharata*, Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, and moral collections like the Indian *Panchatantra* and *Hitopadesha*, the Buddhist *Jataka* tales, and the Roman *Gesta Romanorum*. The 14th century brought written short narratives organized as frames: Boccaccio's *Decameron* and Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales* nest many tales inside a larger setting. In the late 16th century, Matteo Bandello's tragic *novelle* spread across Europe, and Charles Perrault's fairy tales and Antoine Galland's *Arabian Nights* translation added another layer in the late 17th and early 18th centuries.

The first true short-story collections appeared between 1810 and 1830. In Britain, gothic tales like "The Poisoner of Montremos" (1791) helped clear the path. In the United States, Washington Irving and Poe established a native tradition, with Poe's "single effect" as its theoretical core. German, French, and Russian writers soon joined.

From 1850 to 1900, print magazines drove demand. Periodicals such as *The Strand Magazine* in Britain created steady work for Maupassant in France, Chekhov in Russia, Tagore and Premchand in India, and Machado de Assis in Latin America. Between 1900 and 1945, *The New Yorker* and *Harper's* raised the form's prestige, while Joyce, Mansfield, Woolf, Hemingway, and Fitzgerald pushed modernist experimentation; Kafka and Akutagawa broadened the palette.

Since 1945, the form has fragmented and expanded. Borges, García Márquez, and Lispector brought magical realism, a blend of realistic narrative with surreal elements, into the mainstream. Carver and Beattie revived minimalism, a mode built on extreme simplicity. Munro, Morrison, and Chughtai opened space for feminist and minority voices. Digital platforms have renewed readership in the 21st century.

## Mechanics of compression

Most short stories move through a compressed arc of exposition, rising action, climax, and resolution, though two techniques dominate. Many begin *in medias res*, mid-action, trusting the reader to reconstruct what came before, and endings are often left ambiguous, with meaning carried by implication. Protagonists tend to be outsiders or marginalized figures, and themes of alienation, identity, and social critique recur.

## Vocabulary

A *frame story* embeds several tales within a main narrative. *In medias res* means starting mid-action. A *slice of life* presents ordinary experience without dramatic resolution. *Minimalism* and *postmodernism*, the latter marked by fragmentation, irony, and self-reflexivity, are the dominant late-20th-century modes. *Magical realism* fuses the everyday with the impossible.

## Reach

In the United Kingdom, short-story sales rose 45% in 2017. Awards such as the Sunday Times Short Story Award and the Nobel Prize in Literature, awarded to Alice Munro in 2013, confer prestige usually reserved for novels. Adaptations for radio, television, and film, from *The Twilight Zone* to *The Shawshank Redemption*, show how durable a single, well-made effect can be when lifted from the page.

The boundary between a long short story and a novella is set by publishers and prize committees rather than by any internal feature, and digital publishing continues to reshape both the economics of short fiction and the forms it can take.
