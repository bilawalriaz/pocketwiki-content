# Langmuir–Blodgett film

A Langmuir–Blodgett (LB) film is an ultrathin coating built by transferring floating molecular monolayers, one layer per dip, onto a solid surface. Each immersion or emersion of a substrate through a Langmuir monolayer (a single packed layer of molecules resting on water) deposits one monolayer homogeneously, so the final film thickness is just the number of dips multiplied by the known thickness of one monolayer. Monolayers are usually made of amphiphilic molecules, meaning molecules with a water-loving (hydrophilic) head and a water-hating (hydrophobic) tail such as fatty acids, though inorganic nanoparticles can serve the same role. LB films are classed as an emerging family of 2D materials useful for stacking into heterostructures for nanotechnology.

## Why a monolayer floatsSpreading surfactants (surface-acting molecules with hydrophobic tails and hydrophilic heads) on water lowers the system's surface energy. The hydrophobic tails prefer air, the hydrophilic heads prefer water, so the molecules stand upright at the air–water interface with heads down and tails up. The reduction in surface tension is called surface pressure Π, defined as Π = γ₀ − γ, where γ₀ is the surface tension of clean water and γ is the surface tension with the monolayer present.

For very dilute layers, the floating molecules move around like an ideal gas in two dimensions, and the relationship ΠA = RT is the 2D analogue of the ideal gas law PV = RT. As movable barriers compress the available area, density and pressure rise, then the film passes through transitions reminiscent of gas → liquid → solid: a gaseous phase, a liquid-expanded phase, a liquid-condensed phase (a denser sub-state of the liquid), and finally a tightly packed condensed phase above roughly 15 mN/m (often operated near 30 mN/m). Squeezing further destroys the monolayer by stacking into polylayer structures. The pressure–area (Π–A) isotherm that records this journey is the central diagnostic of LB work; it is measured with a Wilhelmy plate, a thin sensor whose wetting force changes in proportion to surface pressure.

## How a film gets onto a solid

A Langmuir–Blodgett trough holds the water subphase and movable barriers that compress the monolayer to the condensed state. A solid substrate is then drawn vertically through the interface. On each pass one monolayer adheres to the surface, allowing controlled-thickness organic or nanoparticle stacks to be assembled layer by layer. Irving Langmuir developed the trough (building on Agnes Pockels's barrier experiments at her kitchen sink); Katharine B. Blodgett, his assistant at General Electric, discovered the dip-transfer step. Langmuir received the 1932 Nobel Prize for the underlying surface chemistry, and Blodgett used the method to make 99% transparent anti-reflective glass by coating it with fluorinated organic compounds.

## History

In 1773 Benjamin Franklin dropped about a teaspoon of oil on a pond, calming the waves over roughly half an acre without realising he had made a monolayer. Over a century later Lord Rayleigh, knowing the oil volume (oleic acid) and the area covered, calculated a film thickness of 1.6 nm. In 1891 Pockels showed that barriers control film area and that surface pressure only changes once the area per molecule drops to about 0.2 nm²; Rayleigh helped her publish in *Nature*. Langmuir confirmed her findings and built the trough; Blodgett then added the transfer step in the 1920s after completing her PhD at Cambridge in 1926.

## What LB films are good for

Their defining features are molecular-scale thickness and high structural order, which give distinctive optical, electrical, and biological behaviour. Practical uses include anti-reflective coatings (successive fluorinated organic layers, Blodgett's original demonstration); nanoparticle coatings for functional films, sensor surfaces, and silicon wafers; passive insulator layers in metal–insulator–semiconductor devices that admit gases better than silicon oxide; glucose biosensors built from poly(3-hexylthiophene) LB films carrying glucose oxidase on indium-tin-oxide glass; half-membrane mimics for studying drug action, permeability, and chain reactions; field-effect devices for monitoring antibody–antigen and enzyme–substrate reactions; UV resists from poly(N-alkylmethacrylamides); and large-scale deposition of emerging two-dimensional layered materials.

The technique is limited by the monolayer collapse pressure: once lateral compression exceeds it, the film buckles into polylayers and loses its precise thickness.
