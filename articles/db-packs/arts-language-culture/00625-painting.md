# Painting

Painting is the act and the result of applying pigment suspended in a binder onto a solid surface called the support. Brushes are the default tool; knives, sponges, airbrushes, and fingers also count. The support can be a cave wall, canvas, wood, paper, glass, metal, or concrete, and the finished object is itself called a painting. What distinguishes one painting from another, more than subject or style, is the binder: oil, water, wax, egg yolk, acrylic polymer. The binder fixes drying time, gloss, flexibility, and how the pigment grips the surface.

The medium predates writing. The oldest securely dated figurative paintings are the anthropomorphic pig-hunters at Leang Karampuang in Sulawesi, Indonesia, around 51,200 years ago, with a related hunting scene at Maros-Pangkep dated to about 43,900 years ago; hand stencils and simple geometric marks are older still. Painted caves survive across France, Spain, China, Australia, and the Americas, so the impulse is not a single regional invention.

For most of its history, painting existed to record, narrate, or worship. Religious subjects dominated both East and West: Buddhist lives, Hindu myth, the Sistine Chapel, Orthodox icons. Regional materials shaped regional media: ink and color-ink on silk and paper in China and Japan, water-based media in India, encaustic (pigment in hot beeswax) for Greek and Roman panels, egg tempera and then oil in Europe. Islamic and Indian traditions centered on the miniature, often paired with calligraphy.

A photograph by Niépce in 1829, and the steady improvement of photography over the following decades, stripped painting of its job as the most accurate recorder of the visible world. That shock, more than any new pigment, produced modern art. Impressionism, rejected by the Paris Salon and given a parallel stage at the 1863 Salon des Refusés, turned the subject into light. Post-Impressionism, Fauvism, Expressionism, Cubism, and Dada dismantled Renaissance perspective in quick succession. In the 1910s Kandinsky and Mondrian pushed toward pure abstraction, partly because music offered a model of an art that did not have to depict anything. After 1945 the center of gravity moved to New York, where Abstract Expressionism and its gestural branch, Action Painting, treated the physical act of painting as the subject. Photorealism appeared in the late 1960s in the United States as a counter, painting from photographs at near-mechanical fidelity, and Hyperrealism in the early 2000s extended that logic with higher-resolution sources. The current period is pluralistic, with no consensus style.

The standard binders each produce a recognizable behavior. Encaustic fuses hot beeswax with pigment and is worked with heated tools. Watercolor, pigment in a water-soluble vehicle on paper, is the dominant East Asian medium, including Chinese finger-painting. Gouache is the opaque cousin, with a chalk filler. Tempera, the egg-yolk binder, dries fast and is nearly permanent; it was the main European medium before about 1500, when oil, pigment ground in linseed or poppyseed oil, took over. Oil's slow drying and blendable texture made Renaissance depth possible. Fresco is a wall technique: buon fresco paints onto wet lime plaster (intonaco) so pigment bonds chemically as the plaster cures, while a secco, on dry plaster, needs an added binder and is less durable. Acrylic, pigment in a synthetic polymer emulsion, was developed in 1946–49 as Magna (a mineral-spirit acrylic) and reached studios as the familiar water-based medium with Cryla in 1963; it dries fast, cleans up with water, and can mimic watercolor or oil. Pastel is nearly pure dry pigment in stick form, which is why unfixed pastels are fragile. Enamel is powdered colored glass fused to a metal support at 750–850°C, used historically for Limoges enamels and 18th-century portrait miniatures. Spray paint in pressurized aerosol cans became a graffiti medium in the late 1970s. Digital painting uses software like Photoshop or Corel Painter, with the artist driving the technique rather than writing the algorithm.

The traditional genres are stable across cultures. Portrait painting records a specific person, with face and expression carrying the likeness. Landscape, a coherent wide view in which sky and weather often dominate, developed only after figure traditions were mature, and runs as two roughly parallel thousand-year streams, Western and Chinese. Still life gives the artist full control of arrangement, and before about 1700 the objects usually carried religious or allegorical meaning. Figure painting, the human body clothed or nude, has been continuous since the Stone Age. Allegory uses symbols to point beyond the literal image. The veduta, a topographically accurate cityscape, originated in 16th-century Flanders and was popular with Grand Tour buyers. Illustration, the painted image made to accompany a text, ranges from medieval manuscript illuminations to N.C. Wyeth and Howard Pyle, and now to the game and comic industries of Korea, Japan, and the United States.

Color and rhythm structure how a painting is read. Color has three working dimensions: hue, saturation, and value. Its psychological effects are real but culturally variable, with black marking mourning in the West and white playing that role in much of the East, so color symbolism must be read locally. Kandinsky, who experienced synaesthesia, mapped colors to instruments and notes and titled paintings "Improvisations" and "Compositions" to mark the parallel with music. Rhythm in painting, in Metzinger's phrase, is a "pause incorporated into a sequence"; Mondrian argued that free flow of visual energy is what makes a composition feel alive, and Pollock's 1950 canvas *Autumn Rhythm (Number 30)* is the canonical image of that idea.

Philosophical disputes are old. Plato dismissed painting as the imitation of an imitation, twice removed from the Forms; Aristotle treated it as a knowledge-making activity; Kant framed the opposition as Beauty versus the Sublime, a distinction Turner and Friedrich painted. Leonardo's counter-thesis, that painting is "a thing of the mind," set the terms for painter-theorists including Kandinsky and Paul Klee. Maurice Denis's 1890 declaration, that a painting is "essentially a flat surface covered with colors assembled in a certain order," announced the modern focus on the means themselves over the depicted scene. Iconography, in Panofsky's sense, reads what is shown before how it is shown, then asks what the image meant to its first viewers and what it means in the wider culture. Open questions remain: the "oldest painting" record keeps moving as dating improves, and each new technology, photography in the 19th century, digital tools now, briefly reopens the question of whether painting has a future, and each time the medium has absorbed the challenge by shifting from documentation toward concept.
