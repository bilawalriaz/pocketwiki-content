# Film

Film creates the illusion of continuous motion by rapidly projecting a sequence of still images, synchronised with sound since the late 1920s. It sits at the intersection of art and commerce, functioning as a universal medium that can entertain, educate, and shape culture.

## How film works

A **frame** is one still image; **frame rate** measures frames per second (fps). Silent-era speeds varied (16–23 fps shot, 18+ projected). Sound required a constant rate, and **24 fps**, the slowest speed that still gave acceptable quality, was standardised by *The Jazz Singer* (1927). Modern video uses 24, 25, 30, 48, 50, or 60 fps depending on standard. The brain fuses a rapid stream of still images into continuous motion through *persistence of vision* and the *phi phenomenon* (≥16 fps). Early projection used glass plates; celluloid roll film and the single-lens motion picture camera (late 1880s) let entire scenes be stored on one reel, the foundation of practical cinema.

## Origins (1830s–1890s)

Film grew from optical toys, photography, and chronophotography (sequential photos of motion). The stroboscopic *phénakisticope* (1833) showed that sequential drawings could seem to move, and led to the zoetrope (1866), flip book (1868), and praxinoscope (1877). Eadweard Muybridge's 1878 photographs of a running horse, later projected by his zoopraxiscope, proved photography could freeze and replay motion. Ottomar Anschütz's Electrotachyscope (1886) projected 24 glass plates at ~30 fps; coin-operated models sold internationally by 1891. Émile Reynaud's Théâtre Optique projected hand-painted *Pantomimes Lumineuses* at the Musée Grévin (1892–1900). The first public ticketed screenings took place in 1895 (Latham, Skladanowsky brothers, Lumière brothers' *Workers Leaving the Lumière Factory*). The Oberammergau Passion Play (1898) became the first commercial motion picture.

## The silent era (1890s–1920s)

Early films were single static shots under a minute. Around 1900, filmmakers began stringing scenes together and discovered that juxtaposing shots creates narrative meaning in the viewer's mind, the basis of **film language**. Live music, from a single pianist to a full orchestra, accompanied screenings; by the early 1920s major productions had prepared scores. D. W. Griffith advanced narrative technique in *The Birth of a Nation* (1915) and *Intolerance* (1916), just as World War I (1914–1918) devastated European production and let Hollywood dominate. The Moscow Film School, founded in 1919, remains the world's oldest film school.

## The sound revolution (late 1920s)

Electronic recording made synchronised sound practical. *Don Juan* (1926, Vitaphone) and *The Jazz Singer* (1927, Warner Bros.) triggered a rapid switch to talkies; by 1931 silent film was effectively extinct in the U.S. American systems used sound-on-disc; Europe standardised on sound-on-film (Tobis-Klangfilm, Tri-Ergon). Later audio innovations, Cinerama's seven-channel surround sound, Dolby A noise reduction (1966), and Dolby Stereo (1977), standardised theatre acoustics. Television (1940s–50s) forced theatres to compete on spectacle: widescreen, 3D, stereophonic sound, and color became standard draws.

## Color

Manual hand-coloring gave way to photographic natural color. Three-strip Technicolor debuted in animation in 1932, reached shorts and sequences, and produced the first full live-action feature *Becky Sharp* (1935). *The Wizard of Oz* (1939) made color mainstream. By the mid-1960s all Hollywood studio productions were color, with rare exceptions by auteurs like Hitchcock (*Psycho*, 1960).

## Film language

Audiences read visual conventions built up since the 1900s. Three of the most important:

- **180-degree rule**: cameras stay on one side of an imaginary line through the actors so screen-left and screen-right relationships stay consistent, placing the viewer inside the scene.
- **Shot/reverse shot**: alternating views of a character looking off-screen and what they see, usually another character, imply conversation.
- **Flashback**: a zoom onto a reflecting face cutting to a younger lookalike signals memory or time shift.

## Montage

Montage assembles separate shots into a sequence to condense time, create emotional impact, or build meaning through juxtaposition. Soviet filmmakers Kuleshov and Eisenstein pioneered it in the 1920s; Eisenstein's *Battleship Potemkin* (1925) is the classic example. The French New Wave (Godard, Truffaut) used montage to break classical rules. Today it appears as rapid cutting, music-video stylisation, cross-cutting parallel action for tension (*Inception*, *Dunkirk*), and thematic patterning.

## Modern cinema (1960s–present)

The 1960s decline of the studio system birthed global New Wave movements (French, German, Japanese, Indian, New Hollywood, Egyptian) and the independent "Film School Generation" of auteur directors. Digital technology reshaped production: non-linear editing replaced razor-and-tape cutting, digital 3D projection (revived by *Avatar* in 2009) replaced the problem-prone 1950s and 1980s systems, and large-format 35mm/70mm screens (IMAX) spread in the late 2010s. By the mid-2010s theatres began adding 4D effects (wind, smells, vibration, smoke); the Korean 4DX format grew popular by the mid-2020s. Digital cameras allow instant review, though as of 2005 most major films still shot on photochemical film. Independent film flourished as camcorders (1985), digital video (1990s), and home-computer post-production (2000s) lowered costs, though financing and distribution remain the hard part.

## Film theory

Film theory seeks systematic principles for studying film as art, beginning with Canudo's *The Birth of the Sixth Art* (1911). Two early positions still anchor the field: **formalist theory** (Arnheim, Balázs, Kracauer) held that film's artistry lies in its difference from reality; **realist theory** (André Bazin) countered that film's essence is its mechanical reproduction of reality. Later frameworks include psychoanalytic (Lacan), structuralist (Saussure), and feminist (Mulvey's "male gaze") approaches.

## Industry

Film was profit-driven from the start: the Lumière brothers toured Europe opening Cinématographe theatres in 1896, and Chaplin earned $1M/year by 1917. Hollywood anchors the U.S. industry; Bollywood (Mumbai) produces the most films globally. High costs concentrate production under studios, but affordable digital gear enabled an independent sector that relies on film festivals and online platforms for distribution. The Academy Awards recognise artistic merit. Box office remains volatile and blockbuster-dependent.

## Animation

Animation produces each frame individually through computer graphics, hand-drawn cels, stop-motion, or direct painting on film stock (Norman McLaren, Stan Brakhage). The phi phenomenon fuses separate frames into continuous motion. Limited animation (UPA, Hanna-Barbera) trades detailed movement for cheaper, faster production.

## Open debates

Whether professional reviews move box office is contested: they help obscure releases but rarely shift blockbuster or genre-fan audiences; a 2004 survey found TV ads and word of mouth each influenced 70% of viewers, with reviews (33%) and online ratings (28%) trailing. Long-term digital preservation is also uncertain: digital formats face rapid obsolescence, making photochemical safety stock and Technicolor imbibition prints more durable for archives. Streaming and home viewing continue to reshape theatrical exhibition and the industry's employment base.
