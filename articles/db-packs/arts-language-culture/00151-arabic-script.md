# Arabic script

The Arabic script is a right-to-left, cursive, unicase writing system that originated for Arabic and is now the second-most widely used alphabetic script in the world, after Latin. It functions primarily as an abjad, transcribing consonants plus only a few vowels, though some adopting languages make it a true alphabet or abugida. Communities from West Africa to Southeast Asia have bent it to dozens of unrelated languages by adding or modifying letters, much as Latin was reshaped for Vietnamese or Turkish.

In its default form, the script writes consonants and a few vowels, so it is technically an abjad. Sorani Kurdish, Kashmiri, Uyghur, and Serbo-Croatian use it as a full alphabet with mandatory vowels, and Xiao'erjing uses it as an abugida for Mandarin, marking vowels with diacritics.

## Origins and derivation

The script descends from Phoenician, the ancestor of Greek, Latin, and Cyrillic, through the path Phoenician → Aramaic → Nabataean → Arabic. In the 6th–5th centuries BCE, northern Arab tribes (the Nabataeans) founded a kingdom at Petra in modern Jordan. They spoke a form of Arabic but wrote in Aramaic, the trade language of the region. Their Aramaic-based script split into a monumental form for inscriptions and a cursive, joined form for papyrus. Over centuries the cursive form influenced the monumental one, and the result became the Arabic script. A minority view argues for a more direct descent from Syriac, but the Nabataean route is standard.

## Script characteristics

Writing flows right to left. Most letters change shape by position: isolated, initial, medial, or final within a word. Because letters join, the script is intrinsically cursive, and there are no capital forms.

New sounds are handled by adding dots, diacritics, or new shapes onto existing Arabic letters. A standard example is the sound [p], a voiceless bilabial plosive absent in Arabic, which Urdu, Persian, and other languages each represent with their own chosen letter. Adaptations often cluster by region: Indian and Turkic languages use the Perso-Arabic form, while Indonesian languages use Jawi. West African adaptations for Hausa, Wolof, Fula, Mandinka, and Yoruba are called ʻAjamī, from the Arabic root for "foreign," and tend to follow Maghreb letter forms and dot positions.

## Geographic reach and adaptations

Perso-Arabic covers Persian, Urdu, Pashto, Sindhi, Kashmiri, and Punjabi in its Shahmukhi form. Jawi, the Malay adaptation, remains co-official in Brunei and in the Malaysian states of Kelantan, Kedah, Pahang, and Terengganu, and is used in parts of Indonesia, southern Thailand, and among Muslim communities in the Philippines and Cambodia. The script also serves Azerbaijani, Baluchi, Brahui, Uyghur, Berber languages, Swahili's predecessor, and many others. In the Americas, Arabic-script texts survive from enslaved Africans, including the Bilali Document and the autobiography of Omar Ibn Said (1770–1864). Xiao'erjing writes Chinese for some Hui, and Pegon serves Javanese, Madurese, and Sundanese, mainly in Islamic schools.

## Key terms

- **Abjad**: a script that writes consonants plus only a few vowels.
- **Abugida**: a script where vowels are written as diacritics attached to consonants.
- **Nabataean alphabet**: the Aramaic-derived script of the Nabataeans, ancestor of the Arabic script.
- **Perso-Arabic**: the modified Arabic script first developed for Persian.
- **Jawi**: the Arabic-based script for Malay.
- **Pegon**: the Arabic-based script for Javanese, Madurese, and Sundanese.
- **ʻAjamí**: any Arabic-based orthography of an African language.
- **Garshuni**: Arabic written in Syriac letters, originating c. 7th century CE.
- **Xiao'erjing**: the Arabic-derived script for Chinese (and Dungan) among Hui people.

## Retreats and replacements

The 20th century saw large-scale replacement. Turkey switched to Latin in 1928 under Atatürk, making Ottoman Turkish a distinct historical language. Northern Kurdish shifted to a Latin-based alphabet in 1932, introduced earlier by Jaladat Ali Badirkhan in Syria. Across the Soviet Union, Cyrillic was imposed after a brief Latinisation drive; Uyghur moved to Latin in 1969 and back to a simplified, fully voweled Arabic script in 1983. After 1991, several ex-Soviet Turkic languages again attempted Latin scripts, with limited renewed Arabic use in Tajikistan. Swahili moved to Latin in the 19th century and Somali in 1972. The general pattern was a 20th-century shift from Arabic to Latin in the Balkans, Sub-Saharan Africa, and Southeast Asia, and from Arabic to Cyrillic in the former Soviet Union.

## Digital encoding

The script is encoded across several Unicode blocks: Arabic (0600–06FF), Arabic Supplement (0750–077F), Arabic Extended-A (08A0–08FF), Arabic Extended-B (0870–089F), Arabic Extended-C (10EC0–10EFF), Presentation Forms-A (FB50–FDFF) and Presentation Forms-B (FE70–FEFF) for legacy joined forms, Arabic Mathematical Alphabetic Symbols (1EE00–1EEFF), Rumi Numeral Symbols (10E60–10E7F), Indic Siyaq Numbers (1EC70–1ECBF), and Ottoman Siyaq Numbers (1ED00–1ED4F).

## Why the pattern matters

A single morphologically flexible script has served as a template for dozens of unrelated languages by adjusting how many vowels it writes, which letters it adds, and which joined form it prefers. What changes between uses is the phonology the script is forced to carry; what stays constant is the right-to-left, cursive, joined-letter core.
