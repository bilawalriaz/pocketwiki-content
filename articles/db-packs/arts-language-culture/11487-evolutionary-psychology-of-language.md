# Evolutionary psychology of language

Evolutionary psychology of language treats language as a psychological faculty whose origins Darwinian processes can explain. The central question is whether language arose because natural selection built it for communication, because it piggy-backed on other adaptations, because an old trait was repurposed, or through some combination of these. Three positions dominate the debate.

An **adaptation** is a trait shaped by natural selection for its current function. Steven Pinker and Paul Bloom argue that language is an adaptation: like the eye or the hand, it is a complex organ whose design is hard to explain except as the product of selection. Children acquire any human language with no explicit instruction, which suggests that grammar is largely innate. Pinker largely agrees with Noam Chomsky on innateness but insists that the organic, organ-like character of the faculty points to an adaptive origin.

A **by-product**, or spandrel, is a trait that exists as a side effect of something else that was selected. Chomsky argues that language rides on the back of a generally enlarged and complex brain. The brain itself is the adaptation; language is what spilled out of it. The evolutionary biologist W. Tecumseh Fitch, following Stephen J. Gould, criticises "pan-adaptationism" and treats language as the probable convergence of many separate adaptations into one complex system. Terrence Deacon, in *The Symbolic Species*, argues that language's features co-evolved with the mind and that symbolic communication is woven into cognition as a whole.

An **exaptation** is a trait whose original purpose changed as the species evolved. Gould's classic example is the low-lying human **larynx**, originally useful for other functions but later co-opted for speech. Several other mammals share a descended larynx without having language, so the exaptationist reads this as a repurposed structure rather than a purpose-built speech organ.

Genes constrain how language can change. The **FOXP2** gene was first flagged in the 1980s by the psycholinguist Myrna Gopnik, who studied the British **KE family**, many of whose members carry a FOXP2 mutation and suffer from speech and language disorder, including **developmental verbal dyspraxia**. Humans carry a unique **allele** that appeared between roughly 100,000 and 200,000 years ago, after the hominin line split from chimpanzees, and is now nearly universal. Because FOXP2 is otherwise conserved across mammals, its recent change fits the idea that speech evolved late in human evolution. The **Baldwin effect** offers a route from learned behaviour to genetic encoding: faster trait acquisition gives a selective advantage, and over generations the same trait requires less environmental input until it is genetically encoded.

Around 7,000 human languages exist, and their diversity is usually explained by four forces: **founder effects**, **drift**, **hybridisation**, and adaptation to local environment. Groups that split off and lost contact let their languages drift apart; contact between groups produced hybrid vocabularies; new activities, such as bronze-making, forced new vocabulary. Quentin Atkinson has suggested that the greater phonetic diversity of African languages supports an African origin for language, since the longest-established populations tend to show the most variation.

Language is not uniquely human in every component. Apes, dolphins, and songbirds show elements of it. Non-human primates can discriminate between two spoken languages, and songbirds acquire species-specific songs by imitation, a capacity that depends on FoxP2 in their brains. Because non-human primates lack a descended larynx and therefore lack vocal imitation, the famous ape language projects, **Koko** the gorilla, who mastered about 1,000 signs, and **Nim Chimpsky** the chimpanzee, who mastered around 150, taught sign language rather than speech.
