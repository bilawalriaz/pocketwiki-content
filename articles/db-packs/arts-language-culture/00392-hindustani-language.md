# Hindustani language

Hindustani is a pluricentric Indo-Aryan language and the primary lingua franca of North India and Pakistan. It is not a single standard language but a spoken continuum with two major literary registers: Hindi (Sanskritised, written in Devanagari) and Urdu (Persianised, written in Perso-Arabic). Their shared grammar and core vocabulary make spoken Hindi and Urdu mutually intelligible; their formal vocabularies diverge.

## Origins and historical development

Hindustani emerged as a contact language in the Ganges-Yamuna Doab (the Delhi region) from the 7th to 13th centuries, growing from Middle Indo-Aryan Apabhraṃśa vernaculars. Its base is the Western Hindi dialect Khariboli, classed as Old Hindi. During the Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526), it absorbed extensive loanwords from Persian, Arabic, and Turkic, and the 13th-century poet Amir Khusrau used it under the name Hindavi. Under the Mughal Empire (1526–1858), Persian remained the state language, but Hindustani spread as a lingua franca across northern India, producing a syncretic "Ganga-Jamuni tehzeeb" that blended Hindu and Muslim traditions. Early names for the language include Hindavi, Rekhta ("mixed"), and Hindustani.

## Divergence into Hindi and Urdu

In the 18th century, the Persianised register of military and courtly settings was standardised as "Urdu," from *Zabān-i-Ordū*, "language of the camp." In the 19th century, a Sanskritised literary register written in Devanagari was promoted as "Hindi," partly in a political effort to displace Urdu's official status. The British Raj used the terms interchangeably before helping formalise the distinction. After the 1947 Partition, India and Pakistan officially recognised Hindi and Urdu respectively, causing "Hindustani" to decline in official use.

## Registers and modern status

At the spoken level, Hindi and Urdu are registers of a single language, sharing core grammar and vocabulary derived from Prakrit. They differ chiefly in formal vocabulary: Hindi draws from Sanskrit, Urdu from Persian and Arabic. Between them lies a colloquial spectrum, often called "Bazaar Hindustani," that avoids high-register words from either tradition and is prominent in Bollywood films. A third variety, "Hinglish" or "Urdish," incorporates significant English loanwords.

Hindi is the official language of the Indian Union, written in Devanagari. Urdu is the national language of Pakistan, written in the Nastaliq style of the Perso-Arabic script. Both are among the 22 scheduled languages of India. Hindustani is spoken by diaspora communities worldwide, including Fiji Hindi, and by the Deccani-speaking community in South India, with influence extending to regions such as Afghanistan.

## Phonology, vocabulary, and writing

Hindustani has a symmetrical ten-vowel system and a four-way distinction among plosives. Its core vocabulary is Indic, drawn from Prakrit and Sanskrit, with roughly 5,500 words of Persian, Turkic, and Arabic origin. Historically written in Devanagari, Perso-Arabic, or Kaithi scripts, it is now primarily written in Devanagari (for Hindi) and the Urdu alphabet (for Urdu), with the Latin script used for Romanised forms.

## Unresolved questions

Scholars debate whether Hindi and Urdu are truly separate languages or registers of one language, a distinction often driven by political and religious identity rather than linguistic criteria. There is ongoing discussion about the utility of "Hindustani" as a neutral, unifying label; Mahatma Gandhi endorsed it as a language that could transcend communal divisions. The choice of script (Devanagari versus Perso-Arabic) remains a major marker of cultural and political identity, making a single script-neutral standard difficult. Conversion between Hindi and Urdu is usually achieved by transliteration, with translation required mainly for religious and literary texts. As of 2025, Hindi and Urdu together rank as the third-most-spoken language globally, with 855 million speakers.
