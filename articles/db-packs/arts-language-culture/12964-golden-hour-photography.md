# Golden hour (photography)

In photography, the **golden hour** is the period of daytime shortly after sunrise or before sunset, when daylight is redder and softer than when the sun sits higher in the sky. Cinematographers and photographers also call it the **magic hour**. A closely related phase is the **blue hour**, the period just before the golden hour at sunrise or just after it at sunset, when the sun sits well below the horizon and residual indirect sunlight takes on a predominantly blue shade with no sharp shadows.

## Why the light looks the way it does

The character of the light is set by the sun's **altitude** (its angle above the horizon), not by the clock. When the sun is low, its rays travel a longer path through the atmosphere, which scatters more of the blue light and reduces the intensity of direct light. Illumination comes increasingly from indirect light from the sky, a form of diffusion that lowers the lighting ratio (the contrast between lit and shaded areas). With less direct light and more red wavelengths reaching the subject, shadows grow long but stay soft, and highlights are less likely to be overexposed.

The same scattering explains the changing **color temperature** of daylight, measured in **kelvin** (K). Color temperature sits around **2,000 K** shortly after sunrise or before sunset, climbs to about **3,500 K** during the golden hour, and reaches roughly **5,550 K** at midday. Altitude, latitude, season, and weather shift these numbers by changing how much atmosphere the light has passed through.

## How long the golden hour actually lasts

The word "hour" is figurative; the effect has no fixed duration. In Los Angeles, one hour after sunrise or one hour before sunset puts the sun at roughly **10–12°** above the horizon, the typical working altitude for the look. Closer to the equator, the sun climbs to that altitude in less than an hour, so the golden hour is shorter. Farther from the equator, it takes longer, and near the poles in certain seasons the sun may never reach 10°, so the golden-hour character can persist for the whole day.

## Why photographers and filmmakers favour it

At midday the overhead sun creates harsh highlights and deep shadows. Overexposure becomes a real risk because film and digital sensors each have a limited **dynamic range** (the span of brightness from darkest shadow to brightest highlight that the medium can record at once). The diffuse, warm, low-contrast light of the golden hour sidesteps those problems: a softer light wraps around subjects, fills in shadows, and renders skin and landscapes more evenly.

Portrait photographers often still use a **fill flash**, a burst of artificial light that brightens the shadows on a subject's face or body, to balance any remaining contrast. Landscape photographers usually welcome the warm tint, using it to enhance the colours of foliage, rock, and water. The look has shaped cinema too: the 1978 film *Days of Heaven* was filmed almost entirely during the golden hour and was acclaimed for its cinematography.
