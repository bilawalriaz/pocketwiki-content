# Ancient Roman architecture

Roman architecture began by borrowing Etruscan and Greek forms, then broke from them through one decisive material: **opus caementicium**, Roman concrete (lime mortar, aggregate, and volcanic *pozzolana* ash). That concrete could be poured into any shape and set underwater, which freed builders from the post-and-lintel limits of stone and made large-scale arches, vaults, and domes practical for the first time. The shift is called the "Roman architectural revolution" — a move from load-bearing stone to a skeletal use of arches and concrete shells, producing interior spaces larger and more varied than anything that preceded them.

The chronological frame runs from the Republic (c. 509 BC) through the 4th century AD, with a peak from c. 40 BC to 230 AD. The first known use of concrete is at Cosa around 273 BC. Under Augustus (27 BC–14 AD) a building boom spread marble facing, Imperial fora, and standardised monumentality across the empire. Two monuments mark the material's reach: the Colosseum (1st century AD) used arches springing directly from engaged columns, and the Pantheon's dome (c. 126 AD) remained the world's largest until the 19th century.

Three consequences follow from the material. First, columns were retained from Greek architecture but stopped being the main load-bearers; the Tuscan (a simplified Doric) and Composite (Ionic scrolls with Corinthian foliage) orders were added to the Greek canon, and all five orders became surface decoration on concrete walls. Second, because the shell carried the load, the floor plan could be whatever the function required. Third, pozzolana reacted with lime to form a strong, water-resistant binder, which is why Roman concrete structures survived aqueducts, harbours, and domes for two millennia.

The building types grew directly from this plasticity:

- **Amphitheatre** (oval, tiered seating) — the Colosseum, used for gladiatorial games; *vomitoria* allowed rapid crowd movement.
- **Basilica** — a large public hall with a central nave and aisles for courts and commerce, later adopted for Christian churches.
- **Thermae** (public baths) — elaborate complexes whose *hypocaust* underfloor heating circulated furnace air beneath a raised floor and through wall cavities.
- **Temples** — placed on a raised podium with a deep front portico, often Corinthian, the deity's statue inside the inner *cella*.
- **Insulae** — multi-storey apartment blocks housing most urban residents; the elite lived in a *domus* (townhouse) or *villa* (country estate).

Civil infrastructure scaled the same ideas. A road network served military and commercial movement. Aqueducts used gravity over long distances to supply baths, fountains, and homes. Stone-arched bridges, many with concrete, crossed rivers; defensive walls such as Hadrian's Wall (begun 122 AD) and the Aurelian Walls ringed cities and frontiers.

Triumphal **arches** (the last great one, of Constantine, 312 AD) commemorated victories with sculpture and inscription; **victory columns** such as Trajan's wrapped narrative relief around a single shaft. Floors and walls were paved with **mosaics** of small stone, glass, or ceramic **tesserae**, while gardens drew on Greek and Persian precedents in both public and private settings.

Vitruvius's idealised proportions describe how Romans *should* have built, not always how they did; practice was variable. The word *insula* ambiguously named both the apartment block and the city block it stood on, complicating social interpretation. The triumphal arch's origin is itself uncertain, with plausible Etruscan and Greek precursors rather than a single inventor.
