# Scientific literature

Scientific literature is the body of published research across the natural and social sciences. Within any field, researchers refer to it simply as "the literature." It enters the record mainly through *peer-reviewed* journals, where independent experts vet submissions for quality, validity, and reliability before publication.

## Primary, secondary, and tertiary sources

The literature is conventionally divided by how close a work is to original data. *Primary* sources contain original empirical research, theoretical contributions, minor results, or engineering work: journal articles, patents, technical reports, and software documentation. *Secondary* sources synthesise existing studies and include review articles, monographs, and edited volumes. A review article carries no new data; it highlights progress and open questions. *Tertiary* sources, such as encyclopedias, are written for general readers.

*Grey literature* sits outside traditional publishing channels and is usually not indexed by major databases. It includes theses, dissertations, technical manuals, working papers, newsletters, and bulletins. Its standing varies by field, typically higher in applied sciences, and has risen in mathematics and high-energy physics with the acceptance of preprints.

## Types of scientific papers

Papers fall into eight goal-specific categories plus two variable ones.

The goal-specific ones are: *original articles* (new findings supported by evidence, embodying the scientific method), *case reports* (descriptions of unique events), *technical notes* (modifications to a technique or piece of equipment), *pictorial essays* (teaching image collections), *reviews* (analyses of recent work, with subtypes such as narrative, descriptive, scoping, and meta-analysis), *commentaries* (short personal summaries), *editorials* (short critiques of an original article), and *letters to the editor* (questions and constructive criticism).

The variable categories cover historical articles, speeches, and other forms that do not fit the eight.

## Structure of a journal article

Most journal articles follow the **IMRaD** structure: Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion. The format, sometimes called the wineglass model, is nearly universal in the empirical sciences but applies less strictly in mathematics, where replicable procedure is not the central concern.

The title attracts readers and may be declarative (stating the conclusion), descriptive (summarising content), or interrogative (posing the paper's question). Authors and affiliations appear next, and after high-profile misconduct cases most publishers require every co-author to approve the final manuscript. An *abstract* summarises the work for bibliographic databases and indexing.

The Introduction cites prior literature to situate the new contribution. Materials and Methods must describe procedures in enough detail that a knowledgeable researcher in the same field could repeat them. Results are presented in tables, figures, and diagrams with captions. The Discussion interprets the results against established knowledge, with the explicit rule that conclusions should rest on evidence rather than on the author's authority, rhetoric, or appeals to belief. A References section lists every cited source.

## Peer review and indexing

Inclusion in selective indexing services now functions as a proxy for acceptance. Commercial databases such as Chemical Abstracts Service, Web of Science, and Scopus curate the most visible journals, while open alternatives such as DOAB, DOAJ, and Unpaywall serve open-access work.

The first recorded editorial peer review took place in 1665, when Henry Oldenburg, founding editor of the *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society*, vetted submissions before publication. René Descartes had earlier circulated texts among colleagues for opinion, an informal prototype. The phrase "peer review" entered common use only in the early 1970s.

## Ethics and common problems

Several recurring issues shape how the literature is produced. *Authorship* and author order follow guidelines from bodies such as NIH and the Council of Science Editors, with non-author contributors relegated to acknowledgements. *Conflicts of interest* can bias results whether acknowledged or not. *Redundant publication* (republishing material already in print without disclosure) is rejected because it inflates the apparent weight of findings. *Data fabrication* is the deliberate alteration of data to favour a desired conclusion.

*Human-subject research* descends from the Nuremberg Code and is governed by three principles from the Belmont Report: informed consent, a favourable risk-benefit ratio, and fair selection and distribution of benefits. *Animal welfare* in research is regulated in the United States under federal law enforced by DHHS, with guidelines maintained by bodies such as the APS.

## Language and access

Because career advancement in the hard and applied sciences usually depends on publishing in English-language high-impact journals, researchers with weaker English writing face a structural disadvantage regardless of the quality of their work. Many now hire specialist ESL copy editors to bring manuscripts up to journal standards.

## History in brief

The intellectual foundation of the modern literature is usually traced to Francis Bacon's 1620 *Novum Organum*, which first described the experimental method. Within a generation, the *Philosophical Transactions* (1665) established the journal as the standard vehicle for vetted research, and the institutional habits of peer review, indexing, and structured articles grew from that template.
