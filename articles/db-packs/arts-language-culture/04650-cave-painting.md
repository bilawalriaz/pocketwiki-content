# Cave painting

Cave paintings are prehistoric images made on the rock walls, ceilings, and floors of caves and rock shelters, a category scholars call parietal art. They are among the oldest unambiguous evidence that early humans thought symbolically and communicated in pictures. Modern dating has pushed the tradition far deeper into prehistory than 20th-century scholars accepted and has shown that Neanderthals, not only *Homo sapiens*, were among the first painters.

## How old the paintings are

Earlier radiocarbon dates were unreliable because charcoal mixed into the paint was easily contaminated. Modern techniques date the pigment itself, charcoal from torch marks on the wall, or thin carbonate crusts that grew over finished images. The result is a chronology reaching back more than 70,000 years.

| Date (years ago) | What was made |
| :--- | :--- |
| c. 73,000 | Oldest known rock painting: a hand-drawn line on a flake from Blombos Cave, South Africa |
| c. 67,800 | Oldest confirmed cave painting: a hand stencil on Muna Island, Indonesia |
| c. 64,000 | Non-figurative marks (lines, dots, hand stencils) in Maltravieso, Ardales, and La Pasiega, Spain, attributed to Neanderthals because modern humans had not yet reached Europe |
| c. 51,200 | Oldest known figurative painting: a pig-hunting scene in Sulawesi, Indonesia |
| c. 40,000 | Earliest figurative European art, at El Castillo, Spain |
| c. 35,000 and c. 30,000 | Two painting phases in Chauvet Cave, France |
| c. 17,000 | Magdalenian-style animal paintings at Lascaux, France, and Altamira, Spain |
| c. 10,000–5,500 | Iberian Mediterranean Basin style, with less detailed figures arranged in groups |

Figurative art depicts recognisable animals, people, or objects; non-figurative art uses abstract marks, lines, dots, and hand stencils. Figurative painting is younger than the earliest non-figurative marks in nearly every region, suggesting abstract symbol-use preceded image-making.

## What the paintings show

Across continents the dominant subjects are large game animals such as bison, horses, and deer, plus hand stencils and geometric patterns. Human figures are rare and usually schematic. Techniques included finger tracing in soft cave surfaces, engraved lines, and brushes or pads applying red and yellow ochre (iron-oxide earth pigments) and charcoal. Hand stencils were made by placing a hand against the wall and blowing pigment around it; some show missing fingers, with explanations ranging from frostbite to ritual mutilation. A 2012 analysis of animal figures in French and Spanish caves concluded that prehistoric artists recorded the gaits of cattle, horses, and deer with an accuracy matched only by 19th-century animal painters, evidence of the close observation hunter-gatherers paid to the prey they depended on.

## Who made them and why

For most of the 20th century, cave art was attributed solely to *Homo sapiens*. Non-figurative markings in three Spanish caves predate modern human arrival in Europe, so they are almost certainly Neanderthal work. Denisovan authorship of some Asian sites is plausible but unproven.

Interpretations of meaning have shifted across generations of scholars:

- Hunting magic: an early reading in which paintings were thought to ensure successful hunts and abundant prey.
- Shamanic trance: David Lewis-Williams proposed that the geometric patterns and composite animal-human figures record visions from altered states of consciousness.
- Adolescent authors: R. Dale Guthrie argued from hand size and injury patterns that many painters were young males.
- Female contributors: Dean Snow's measurements of European hand stencils suggest women made a substantial share, complicating the male-hunter stereotype.
- Proto-writing: a 2022 study proposed that the dots and lines accompanying animal figures track mating seasons via a lunar calendar.

No single theory explains every site, and the same cave often contains art remade across thousands of years, which is one reason cruder images sometimes overlie more sophisticated ones.

## Where the art is found

Europe holds the most famous sites (Chauvet, Lascaux, Altamira, El Castillo) but is not the origin. Indonesia has produced the world's oldest figurative art, in Sulawesi, with additional sites in Borneo, and Paleolithic paintings are documented in Mongolia, India, and Thailand. Africa's record begins with the 73,000-year-old Blombos flake and continues through the roughly 5,000-year-old Laas Geel cattle paintings in Somaliland and the San rock art of southern Africa. Australia's oldest reliably dated painting is a 17,300-year-old kangaroo in the Kimberley, though some ochre images in Arnhem Land may depict now-extinct megafauna; their age is inferred from the animals' estimated extinction rather than measured directly. The Americas yield paintings of comparable antiquity, including the 7,500-year-old Great Murals of Baja California and the 10,000-year-old hunting scenes of Cueva de las Manos in Argentina.

Two open problems shape the field. The first is authorship: as dating improves, more art is likely to be reassigned to Neanderthals, Denisovans, or unknown populations. The second is purpose: the same geometric vocabulary of dots, lines, and hand stencils fits trance-related, astronomical, and proto-linguistic interpretations, and the evidence is too thin to decide among them. In Arnhem Land, where megafauna depictions cannot be directly dated, and in Spanish caves where Neanderthal markings sit beneath later human additions, the surviving pigment is the oldest material record of deliberate symbolic marking on stone.
