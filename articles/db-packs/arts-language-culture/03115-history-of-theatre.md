# History of theatre

Theatre, in the autonomous sense this history tracks, is staged drama separated from ritual and from ordinary entertainment. That separation is the thread connecting 2,500 years of practice. The earliest documented large-scale events, such as the Abydos Passion Play in ancient Egypt (c. 2500–550 BCE), were religious spectacles reenacting myths like the death and resurrection of Osiris, sometimes staged at a pharaoh's 30th-year jubilee to symbolise renewal of divine power. Greek tragedy at the City Dionysia in Athens marks the point where performance moved onto civic, not sacred, ground. Official Athenian records, the *didaskaliai*, begin in 501 BC, the year the satyr play was introduced alongside tragedy.

A working definition: **theatre** is the autonomous art of performing scripted or improvised drama before an audience in a dedicated space, the Greek *theatron*, named from *theama*, "vision." Aristotle separated it from religious mystery by arguing that watching alone could produce the emotional purification (*catharsis*) that ritual participation once required.

## The Western arc

Greek theatre fixed the basic categories still in use: **tragedy**, **comedy**, and the **satyr play**, packaged as a **tetralogy** at festivals. Roman drama borrowed these forms from the 3rd century BCE, and the first major works of Roman literature are the tragedies and comedies of Livius Andronicus (240 BCE). Rome also added female performers and the *fabula palliata*, comedies set on a Greek street and built around eavesdropping and mistaken identity.

After the Western Roman collapse, theatre survived inside the church. The earliest **liturgical drama** is the *Quem-Quaeritis* Easter trope (c. 925), a short dialogued exchange grafted onto the Mass. It gradually moved outdoors and into the vernacular, becoming the **Mystery plays** performed by trade guilds. The Renaissance then rediscovered classical texts and built new physical theatres. The first Italian comedy, *La Cassaria* by Ludovico Ariosto, was performed at the court of Ferrara in 1508, and the first Italian actress known by name, Lucrezia Di Siena, is documented in 1564. In the same period, **Commedia dell'arte** (16th–18th centuries) professionalised improvisation around masked stock characters.

Three 17th–18th century national styles then dominated. The Spanish Golden Age (1590–1681) centred on Lope de Vega and Calderón de la Barca. French classical theatre imposed neoclassical rules through Molière and Racine. English **Restoration comedy**, following the 1660 reopening of theatres under the restored monarchy, introduced explicit sexual content and the first professional English actresses. The 19th century moved toward realism, with Henrik Ibsen's *A Doll's House* (1879) as the watershed: a play about ordinary domestic life treated with the seriousness previously reserved for kings.

## Asian and African traditions

Independent traditions developed in parallel and were not derived from the West. Indian **Sanskrit theatre** is codified in the *Nātyaśāstra* (c. 200 BCE–200 CE), a treatise covering acting, dance, music, and stagecraft, and later evolved into folk forms such as Kathakali in 17th-century Kerala. Chinese theatre runs from Shang dynasty entertainments (c. 1500 BCE) through Han dynasty shadow puppetry to **Yuan dynasty** zaju plays and eventually **Beijing Opera**. Japanese theatre includes Noh (14th century), Bunraku puppet theatre (17th century), and Kabuki (late 16th century). In West Africa, Yoruba **Aláàrìnjó** travelling troupes (16th century onward) kept masquerade and performance fused, a lineage visible in the popular travelling theatre that dominated Nigerian stages from the 1950s to the 1980s.

## The 20th century and after

Modernism broke realism apart. Bertolt Brecht's **epic theatre** used non-realistic staging to make the audience think critically about the society shown on stage. Antonin Artaud's Theatre of Cruelty attacked the separation between actor and spectator, while Samuel Beckett's Theatre of the Absurd exposed the limits of rational dialogue. Luigi Pirandello's *Six Characters in Search of an Author* (1920s–1930s) made the constructed nature of performance itself the play's subject. After 1945, Japanese **Butoh** added a dance-theatre of slow, white-painted bodies. Contemporary practice continues to blend historical revival, political theatre, and new technology, while figures such as Wole Soyinka fuse Yoruba and Western forms in a single work.

## What remains genuinely uncertain

The claim that theatre evolved directly out of ritual is plausible but unproven; the similarities may be parallel responses to similar needs rather than descent. Whether early Italian theatre grew out of late Roman practice or out of medieval liturgical functions is still debated, and the **elegiac comedies** of late antiquity are of disputed status, possibly rhetorical exercises never meant to be staged.
