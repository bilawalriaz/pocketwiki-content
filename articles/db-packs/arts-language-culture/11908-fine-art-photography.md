# Fine-art photography

Fine-art photography is photography made to express the photographer's vision, idea, message, or emotion, rather than to document an event or sell a product. It is distinguished from photojournalism, which provides a literal visual record of subjects and events, and from commercial photography, whose primary purpose is to advertise goods or services. The category is defined by intent and reception: museums, galleries, and critics decide what counts as fine art, so the same image can be read as documentary or as art depending on context.

## How photography became a fine art

John Edwin Mayall exhibited daguerreotypes (one-of-a-kind photographs on silver-plated copper sheets) illustrating the Lord's Prayer in 1851, often cited as an early exponent. Mid-Victorian practitioners included Julia Margaret Cameron, Charles Lutwidge Dodgson, and Oscar Gustave Rejlander. In the United States, F. Holland Day, Alfred Stieglitz, and Edward Steichen pushed photography toward fine-art status; Stieglitz placed photographs in museum collections, and his *The Steerage* (1907) is regarded as a landmark of artistic modernism. As late as 1960 photography was not generally recognised as fine art in Britain; S. D. Jouhar, founding the Photographic Fine Art Association that year, argued it was still treated as a craft. Institutional confirmation in the United States came when the Museum of Modern Art established a photography department in 1940 and appointed Beaumont Newhall as its first curator.

## Genres

Principal genres are inherited from painting and overlap: a single body of work, such as the typological grids of Bernd and Hilla Becher, can be read as both landscape and conceptual photography.

*Landscape* is the oldest fine-art photographic subject. Natural landscape took its modern shape from Timothy H. O'Sullivan's nineteenth-century U.S. Government Survey work (1867–1879) and from Group f/64 (a 1932 collective advocating sharp-focus, large-format photography), especially Ansel Adams's photographs of Yosemite and the Sierra Nevada. Urban landscape runs from Eugène Atget's Paris documentation (1890s–1927), championed by the surrealists and Berenice Abbott, through the Bechers and their Düsseldorf students (Andreas Gursky, Thomas Struth, Candida Höfer, Thomas Ruff), to the 1975 New Topographics exhibition, which made human-altered landscape a critical fine-art position.

*Portrait* has been a sustained focus since the medium's invention. Cameron aimed "to ennoble photography and to secure it for the character and uses of high art," a stance shared by Félix Nadar. August Sander's *Menschen des 20. Jahrhunderts* became a foundational work of New Objectivity (a sharp-focused German documentary movement of the 1920s–30s), and Richard Avedon's white-background portraits were the subject of a 2002 Metropolitan Museum of Art retrospective.

*Nude* was a teaching aid for painters shortly after the medium's invention and became a fine-art mainstay through the pictorialist F. Holland Day and Edward Weston and Imogen Cunningham of Group f/64; later practitioners include Bill Brandt, Robert Mapplethorpe, Sally Mann, and John Coplans.

*Still life* has been a fine-art genre since the 1840s, drawing on the seventeenth-century Vanitas tradition (compositions symbolising mortality). William Henry Fox Talbot produced images that functioned simultaneously as document, aesthetic composition, and proto-advertisement; Albert Renger-Patzsch and Weston worked between fine-art still life and commercial advertising in the 1920s; Karl Blossfeldt's plant studies entered New Objectivity after the 1928 publication of *Urformen der Kunst*.

*Street photography* is distinct from photojournalism by its artistic rather than reportorial intent. Henri Cartier-Bresson's 1952 book *The Decisive Moment* articulated the decisive moment concept, and John Szarkowski's 1967 MoMA exhibition *New Documents* recast the documentary approach of Diane Arbus, Lee Friedlander, and Garry Winogrand toward "more personal ends."

*Conceptual and staged.* From the mid-1960s, Conceptual Art used photography as a vehicle for ideas; John Baldessari, Sol LeWitt, Bruce Nauman, and Ed Ruscha worked in this mode. From the late 1970s, staged tableaux such as Cindy Sherman's *Untitled Film Stills* (1977–80) constructed scenes rather than recording found subjects.

## Staging, technology, and the market

Since the 1970s there has been a shift toward careful staging and lighting rather than discovery of a ready-made scene; Gregory Crewdson and Jeff Wall are noted for elaborate tableaux. Digital tools have enabled full-spectrum photography using controlled filtering across ultraviolet, visible, and infrared bands. Collectors' interest in finely printed, limited-edition artist books has grown since around 1980 because short print runs mean books are rarely reprinted. In 2004, around 7,000 photographs were sold at auction, with a 7.6 percent annual price rise between 1994 and 2004 and roughly 80 percent of sales in the United States.

## Overlap with fashion and photojournalism

The boundary between fine-art and fashion photography blurred during the 1990s. MoMA's 2004 exhibition *Fashioning Fiction in Photography since 1990* described the exchange as a "particularly fruitful moment," and Richard Avedon, Irving Penn, Cecil Beaton, and Man Ray are routinely exhibited in fine-art contexts. Overlap with photojournalism dates to the late 1960s and 1970s, when news photographers began working with art photography; the International Center of Photography opened in 1974 with both emphases, and by 1987 magazine and newspaper images regularly appeared in museum and gallery frames.

## Framing, size, and politics

Until the mid-1950s it was considered vulgar to frame photographs for galleries; prints were pasted to blockboard or pinned at the corners, as in Steichen's *The Family of Man* and Bill Brandt's 1966 MoMA show. From the mid-1950s to about 2000 most gallery prints were shown behind glass; since about 2000 the trend has shifted back toward unglassed prints on boards, and throughout the twentieth century print sizes grew substantially.

Fine-art photography has had political effects as a byproduct of its aesthetic aims. Ansel Adams's images of Yosemite and Yellowstone helped build public support for protecting the Sierra Nevada, and work centred on the nude has intersected with censorship law and free-expression debates.
