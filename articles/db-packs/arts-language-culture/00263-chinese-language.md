# Chinese language

Chinese (汉语 Hànyǔ; 中文 Zhōngwén) is the umbrella term for almost all Sinitic languages except Macro-Bai, natively spoken by the Han Chinese and ethnic minorities in Greater China and overseas communities. With about 1.39 billion first-language speakers (roughly 17 percent of the global population), Chinese forms the largest branch of the Sino-Tibetan family. Its varieties are often mutually unintelligible, leading linguists to treat them as separate languages while the Chinese government classifies them as dialects of one language.

## Position in the family

All Chinese varieties belong to Sino-Tibetan, which also contains Burmese, Tibetan, and languages of the Himalayan and Southeast Asian Massif. The relationship was proposed in the early 19th century and is broadly accepted. Reconstruction is weaker than for Indo-European or Austroasiatic because the family is diverse, lacks inflection, has undergone heavy contact, and includes inaccessible mountainous speech areas. Proto-Sino-Tibetan has not been securely reconstructed, so the higher-level split between Chinese and Tibeto-Burman is assumed but not demonstrated.

## History

Old Chinese is attested from oracle bone inscriptions of about 1250 BCE. It lacked retroflex and palatal obstruents, allowed initial consonant clusters and voiceless nasals, was probably atonal with final clusters that later became tones, and expressed grammar through word order and particles rather than inflection.

Middle Chinese (6th to 10th centuries CE) split into several varieties after long geographic and political separation. Most scholars treat the Qieyun rhyme dictionary (601 CE) as a diasystem combining 6th-century northern and southern reading standards. After the Northern Song fell, Old Mandarin developed on the North China Plain under Jurchen Jin and Mongol Yuan rule. Ming and Qing administration used Guanhua ("language of officials"), a koiné based on Nanjing-area speech. By the mid-19th century the Beijing dialect dominated contact with the court. In 1932 the National Language Unification Commission adopted Beijing speech as the national standard, called Guóyǔ; the PRC later renamed it pǔtōnghuà.

Written Chinese crystallized into Classical or Literary Chinese, a prestige diglossic form used nearly universally until the late 19th century. Vernacular written Chinese spread after the May Fourth Movement beginning in 1919.

## Influence abroad

Chinese control of northern Vietnam from 111 BCE, the Four Commanderies in Korea from the 1st century BCE, and the spread of Buddhism (2nd to 5th centuries CE) carried Literary Chinese across East Asia as the language of administration and scholarship until the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Each country developed its own Sino-Xenic pronunciations. Sino-Korean, Sino-Japanese, and Sino-Vietnamese words make up over half of those lexicons and reshaped their phonologies: moraic structure in Japanese, disrupted vowel harmony in Korean. Late 19th- and early 20th-century coinages for Western concepts circulated freely among these languages and even re-entered Chinese, with their foreign origin hidden by shared characters. English loans include tea (from Hokkien tê), dim sum, and kumquat (from Cantonese).

## Varieties

Linguist Jerry Norman estimated hundreds of mutually unintelligible varieties forming a dialect continuum; mountainous South China is more diverse than the North China Plain. The conventional seven groups, based on the evolution of Middle Chinese voiced initials, are Mandarin (about 800 million speakers, including Standard Chinese, Sichuanese, and Dungan), Wu (74 million, including Shanghainese), Min (75 million, including Hokkien and Teochew), Yue (68 million, including Cantonese and Taishanese), plus Gan, Xiang, and Hakka. Li Rong's 1987 Language Atlas of China adds Jin, Huizhou, and Pinghua, leaving Danzhou dialect, Waxianghua, and Shaozhou Tuhua unclassified. Transitional zones allow limited intelligibility, as between New Xiang and Southwestern Mandarin. Calling major branches "dialects" understates their unintelligibility, but calling them "languages" also fails, since Wu itself contains mutually unintelligible varieties.

## Phonology and tones

A Chinese syllable has an optional onset, vowel nucleus, optional coda, and tone; in Cantonese, /m/ and /ŋ/ can stand alone as syllabic. The sound inventory has shrunk since Middle Chinese. Mandarin has only about a thousand distinct syllables including tone, roughly an eighth as many as English, and compensates with far more polysyllabic words. Tones distinguish words in every variety. Northern dialects may have as few as three; southern dialects up to six or twelve depending on counting; Shanghainese has collapsed to a two-tone pitch accent similar to Japanese. Standard Chinese has four tones plus a neutral one; Standard Cantonese has six, historically nine counting "checked tones" now considered duplicates.

## Grammar

Chinese morphemes are monosyllabic. Classical Chinese was about 90 percent single-character words, but in modern Mandarin most nouns, adjectives, and verbs are disyllabic, a shift driven by phonetic erosion that shrank the syllable inventory and raised homophony (shí "stone" became shítou). Words (cí) usually combine two characters, sometimes more. All varieties are analytic: no tenses, voices, or grammatical number; aspect and mood use particles such as le, hái, and yǐjīng. Word order is subject–verb–object with topic–comment constructions, classifiers, serial verb constructions, and pronoun or subject dropping.

## Vocabulary and loanwords

Over 50,000 characters exist historically; about 10,000 are in use and about 3,000 appear frequently in media. Characters correspond to morphemes, not words. Early borrowings include possible Indo-European words (honey mì, lion shī), Silk Road terms (grape pútáo, from Persian), Buddhist vocabulary from Sanskrit and Pali (Fó "Buddha"), and Altaic-origin nomadic words (pípá, lào). Modern neologisms use calques, phonetic transcription, or phono-semantic matching (bēnténg "Pentium"). English influence has produced transliterations such as fěnsī "fans" and hēikè "hacker", and zìmǔcí ("lettered words") spelled with English letters.

## Writing system

Characters denote morphemes independent of pronunciation: 一 is yī in Mandarin and yat1 in Cantonese. Xu Shen's 100 CE dictionary classified six character categories; 80 to 90 percent of characters are phonetic compounds; the Kangxi Dictionary standardized 214 radicals. Traditional characters remain standard in Taiwan, Hong Kong, Macau, Malaysia, Myanmar (Kokang), and overseas communities. The PRC introduced simplified characters in 1954 to promote mass literacy. A well-educated reader knows about 4,000 to 6,000 characters; 3,000 suffice for a mainland newspaper; PRC functional literacy is set at 2,000. Romanization systems include Hanyu Pinyin (PRC, 1956), Wade–Giles, Gwoyeu Romatzyh, EFEO, and Yale; Bopomofo (zhuyin) is still used in Taiwan, and Dungan is written in Cyrillic.

## Open issues

Whether Chinese varieties are one language or many remains contested: mutual intelligibility criteria cut both ways, and linguists may understate intelligibility problems when varieties align with a prestige center. Proto-Sino-Tibetan is not securely reconstructed, leaving the top-level Chinese versus Tibeto-Burman split undemonstrated, and the number of main groups is unsettled (estimates run from seven to thirteen). The origins of some Altaic-etymology borrowings, and whether certain shared terms were coined in Japan or China, are also open.
