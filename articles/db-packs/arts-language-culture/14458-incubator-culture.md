# Incubator (culture)

An incubator is an insulated, sealed chamber that holds temperature, humidity, and gas composition steady so that microbes or cultured cells grow under reproducible conditions. In cell biology, microbiology, and molecular biology, incubators are essential for working with both bacterial and eukaryotic cells.

## What an incubator controls

The simplest incubators are insulated boxes with a heater, typically adjustable up to 60–65 °C, with some reaching 100 °C. More advanced models add refrigeration, humidity control, CO₂ regulation, internal air circulation, or a shaking platform that mixes cultures while they grow. Many laboratory units include a backup power source so an outage does not ruin long experiments.

## Why 37 °C, and why CO₂ and humidity

Most bacteria and mammalian cells used in the lab grow fastest near 37 °C (99 °F), roughly human body temperature. *E. coli* and human cell lines both thrive there. Budding yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) prefers about 30 °C (86 °F).

For mammalian cells, temperature alone is not enough. Evaporation from the culture medium concentrates salts and stresses the cells, so relative humidity is kept above 80%. Cells also acidify as they metabolise, so a CO₂ level of around 5% dissolved in the medium buffers the pH into the slightly acidic range mammalian cells need. CO₂ incubators exist specifically to hold heat, humidity, and CO₂ at once.

## How the design has evolved

Thousands of years ago in ancient Egypt and China, large heated rooms kept chicken eggs warm so hens could keep laying. In the 16th century Jean Baptiste Porta redesigned the Egyptian egg incubator; René-Antoine Ferchault de Réaumur continued this work in the mid-17th century, warming his chamber with a wood stove and monitoring it with the Réaumur thermometer he had invented.

In the 19th century, the first cell-culture incubators were bell jars holding a single lit candle: cultures sat near the flame on the jar's underside, and the whole jar went into a dry heated oven. Around the same period, doctors at a Paris women's hospital used kerosene-heated chambers to keep premature infants warm; Julius H. Hess later designed an electric infant incubator closely resembling modern neonatal units.

The CO₂ incubator reached the market in the 1960s. It let clinicians grow pathogens sampled from patients' bodily fluids at 37 °C under controlled carbon dioxide and nitrogen, making diagnostic culture practical. The same hardware then enabled the first wave of genetic engineering, including production of proteins such as insulin and modification of fruits and vegetables at the molecular level.

## What laboratories use today

A modern lab incubator is chosen by which variables it controls. A bacteriological incubator regulates only temperature. A CO₂ incubator adds gas and humidity for mammalian cells. A shaking incubator adds motion for aerobic cultures that need continuous mixing. Larger facilities sometimes convert entire rooms, called warm rooms, into incubators for high-volume work.

Incubators range from tabletop units to warm rooms, and the common thread is the same: hold temperature, atmosphere, and sometimes motion steady while living cells replicate, so an experiment run today can be compared directly with one run next month.
