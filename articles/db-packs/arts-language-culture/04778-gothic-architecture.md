# Gothic architecture

Gothic architecture is a medieval European building style, active roughly 1140–1500, defined by three structural inventions working together: the pointed arch, the rib vault, and the flying buttress. Together they shifted a building's weight off the walls and onto isolated piers, freeing the wall to become a thin, tall membrane of stained glass. The result was churches higher, brighter, and structurally more daring than the round-arched Romanesque buildings they replaced.

A pointed arch differs from a round arch in that its geometry lets builders tune height and span independently, so thrust is no longer locked to width. A rib vault is a ceiling of stone ribs meeting at a central point with lighter webbing between them; the ribs channel the load straight down into specific piers rather than along the whole wall. A flying buttress is an external arched prop that catches the vault's outward thrust and transfers it to a separate pier outside the building, so the wall no longer has to resist that push. With thrust redirected externally and weight concentrated on piers, the wall can be reduced to a frame around stained glass.

The style originated in the Île-de-France region around 1140. The choir of the Abbey of Saint-Denis, reconstructed c. 1140–1144, is usually cited as the first building to combine these features. Sens Cathedral (c. 1135–1164) is among the first fully Gothic structures, and Notre-Dame de Paris, begun in the 1160s, is the early building most associated with flying buttresses.

The period divides into phases. Early Gothic (c. 1130–1240) was a period of structural experimentation, with six-part rib vaults and thick walls. High Gothic (c. 1194–1250) simplified the vault to four parts, raised the vaults higher, thinned the walls, and opened up the clerestory (the upper wall level) to vast windows, with Chartres, Reims, and Amiens as landmark cathedrals. Rayonnant (c. 1230–1350) pushed window area further, replacing plate tracery (continuous stone panels) with bar tracery (slender stone bars subdividing the glass) into intricate patterns; Sainte-Chapelle (c. 1241–1248) is the defining example. Late Gothic (c. 1350–1500) is mainly decorative, splitting into Flamboyant in France, with flame-like curving tracery, and Perpendicular in England, with strong vertical lines and fan vaults.

The period also records a structural limit. In 1284 the choir vault of Beauvais Cathedral, then the tallest vault ever attempted at roughly 48 metres, partially collapsed. Builders across Europe then pulled back from extreme heights and pursued other qualities, such as England's horizontal length and fan vaulting, or Germany's hall churches (nave and aisles of equal height) and northern brick Gothic.

Gothic was not uniform across Europe. France remained the heartland and the source of most innovation. In England, the Perpendicular style emphasised vertical mullions, rectilinear tracery, and fan vaults, with King's College Chapel, Cambridge, as a late example. Northern Germany developed *Backsteingotik*, a brick-built Gothic, since good building stone was scarce. Italy adopted Gothic sparingly, keeping thick walls because fresco painting needed large unbroken surfaces, as in Florence Cathedral. Iberia blended Gothic with surviving Moorish craft, producing hybrid cathedrals such as Toledo. Construction of new Gothic buildings continued in central Europe into the 15th century, including St. Vitus Cathedral in Prague under Peter Parler from 1344 and Milan Cathedral from 1387, an unusual Italian attempt at French Gothic.

The style spread partly through Cistercian monks, trade routes, and the Crusades, and scholars still debate how much its pointed arches owe to Islamic and Armenian precedents. Christopher Wren later argued for a "Saracen" origin, but European pointed arches predate the Crusades, supporting the case for independent evolution from late Romanesque. The name "Gothic" itself was a later insult, applied pejoratively from the 16th century by Renaissance writers such as Giorgio Vasari in *Lives of the Artists* (1550), who wanted to associate the medieval style with the "barbarous" Goths; the style was first called *opus Francigenum*, "French work". Renaissance architecture, beginning with Brunelleschi's dome for Florence Cathedral (1420–1436), replaced Gothic as the dominant style in Italy and then across Europe, though local Gothic traditions continued in parts of France, Germany, and England. A major Gothic Revival began in 18th-century England, starting with the western towers of Westminster Abbey (1722–1745).
