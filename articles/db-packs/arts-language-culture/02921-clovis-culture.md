# Clovis culture

The Clovis culture is a Paleoindian archaeological culture of North America dated to roughly 13,050–12,750 years Before Present (BP). Paleoindian refers to the earliest archaeological period in the Americas, defined by mobile hunter-gatherers who lived alongside now-extinct Ice Age animals. Clovis is identified above all by a distinctive fluted stone projectile point, a biface (a stone tool flaked on both faces) that is large, lance-shaped, and thinned at the base by a groove called a flute. The Clovis toolkit also includes blades struck from prepared cores, scrapers, gravers, and bone and ivory tools such as beveled rods and shaft wrenches. The combination of this toolkit, especially the fluted point made using a technique called overshot flaking, where a flake travels from one edge all the way across the face of a biface, defines the culture and lets archaeologists recognise Clovis sites across the continent.

Clovis people were highly mobile hunter-gatherers. Their economy centred on large game, especially Columbian mammoths, but also mastodons, bison, and horses, supplemented by smaller animals and plants. Isotope analysis of Anzick-1, the only known Clovis-associated human burial (an infant from Montana dated to c. 12,990–12,840 BP), suggests mammoths made up roughly 35–40% of his diet. Sites range from mass kill locations, such as a bison herd driven into an arroyo (a steep-sided gully) at Jake Bluff, to seasonal camps. People moved raw stone across remarkable distances, sometimes more than 900 km, and deliberately stockpiled tools in caches, purposeful deposits of artifacts hidden for later recovery, of which over 20 have been recorded.

The history of Clovis research mirrors the history of the field. In 1927, the Folsom site first confirmed that people had lived in the Americas during the Pleistocene, the last Ice Age. Two years later, Ridgely Whiteman discovered the Clovis type site at Blackwater Draw, New Mexico, where stone tools lay alongside mammoth bones, and major excavations followed under Edgar Howard and John L. Cotter between 1932 and 1937. For decades, the resulting "Clovis First" model held that Clovis represented the earliest human culture in the Americas. That model is now rejected: sites such as Monte Verde II in Chile (c. 14,500 BP) and Cooper's Ferry in Idaho (c. 15,800 BP) document human presence several centuries to millennia earlier. The origin of Clovis itself is still debated, with candidates including an in situ development south of the Laurentide Ice Sheet, the vast ice sheet that covered northern North America, possibly deriving from the Dyuktai lithic style of Siberia, and an early, contemporaneous Western Stemmed Tradition in western North America.

The culture ended around 12,750–12,600 BP, overlapping the onset of the Younger Dryas (an abrupt return to glacial conditions around 12,900 BP) and the regional loss of megafauna. It was succeeded by related but distinct traditions such as Folsom, and archaeologists generally treat the transition as ordinary cultural change rather than sudden collapse, though declining game populations and reduced mobility may have contributed. Genetic analysis of Anzick-1 shows ancestry shared with many modern Indigenous peoples of the Americas, especially in Central and South America, and confirms an Asian origin for the population. A long-running debate asks whether Clovis hunting helped drive Late Pleistocene megafauna to extinction through overkill, or whether climate change was the main cause; a 2012 survey found most archaeologists favour a combination of factors, with climate as a major contributor.
