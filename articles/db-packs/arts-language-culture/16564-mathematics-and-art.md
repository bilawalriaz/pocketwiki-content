# Mathematics and art

The relationship between mathematics and art runs in two directions: mathematics provides tools for making art (perspective, symmetry, polyhedra, fractal analysis), and art has driven new mathematics, most notably projective geometry, which emerged from Renaissance perspective drawing. The connection also runs to subject matter: artists use recursion, paradox, and higher-dimensional geometry as themes.

## Proportion and the human figure

Greek sculptor Polykleitos (c. 450–420 BC) wrote the *Canon*, a lost treatise prescribing ideal body proportions. He is thought to have used the ratio 1:√2, where each length is the diagonal of a square drawn on the previous one. The *Canon* applied ratio, proportion, and *symmetria* (harmonious proportions) to describe the human form through continuous geometric progressions, and influenced Classical, Roman, and Renaissance sculpture. The *golden ratio* (≈ 1.618), known to Euclid, has been popularly claimed to appear in ancient works such as the Parthenon and the Great Pyramid, but these claims are not supported by measurement. Most scholars think artists and architects were unaware of the ratio before Luca Pacioli's 1509 treatise *De divina proportione*, illustrated by Leonardo da Vinci.

## Linear perspective

Classical painters sized figures by importance rather than distance; reverse perspective appeared in the Middle Ages. The Muslim mathematician Alhazen described optics in 1021 but never applied it to art. Giotto (1266/67–1337) attempted perspective using algebra. In 1415, Filippo Brunelleschi and Leon Battista Alberti demonstrated a geometric method in Florence, using Euclid's similar triangles. Alberti explained in *De pictura* (1435) that light rays form a pyramid with the eye at its vertex; a painting is a cross-section of that pyramid. Piero della Francesca (c. 1415–1492), called by Vasari "the greatest geometer of his time, or perhaps of any time," wrote *De prospectiva pingendi* and related treatises. Paolo Uccello (1397–1475) aligned broken lances along perspective lines in *The Battle of San Romano*. Controversially, David Hockney argued in *Secret Knowledge* (2006) that artists from the 1420s used a camera lucida, and Philip Steadman argued that Vermeer used a camera obscura; both claims are disputed.

## Polyhedra and other mathematical forms

Polyhedra appear repeatedly in Western art: a small stellated dodecahedron mosaic by Uccello in San Marco, Venice; Leonardo's illustrations of regular solids for Pacioli (1509); a glass rhombicuboctahedron in Jacopo de Barbari's 1495 portrait of Pacioli; a truncated polyhedron and magic square in Albrecht Dürer's *Melencolia I* (1514); and a giant dodecahedron in Salvador Dalí's *The Sacrament of the Last Supper* (1955). Dürer's 1525 *Underweysung der Messung* first described polyhedral nets (unfoldings laid flat for printing). In Dalí's *Corpus Hypercubus* (1954), Christ hangs on the unfolded net of a tesseract (a four-dimensional hypercube), symbolising a divine fourth-dimensional perspective.

## Symmetry in textiles, tilings, and Islamic art

Planar symmetries have been exploited for millennia in carpets, lattices, and tilings. Anatolian kilims often have central fields laid out as wallpaper patterns (repeating symmetries such as *pmm*) and borders as frieze patterns (*pm11*, *pmm2*, *pma2*), with three or more borders being common. Chinese lattices span 14 of the 17 wallpaper groups. Islamic art uses *girih* tiles (a regular decagon, elongated hexagon, bow tie, rhombus, and pentagon, with sides of equal length and angles that are multiples of 36°) whose fivefold and tenfold symmetries, as physicists Peter Lu and Paul Steinhardt argued in 2007, resemble quasicrystalline Penrose tilings. Moroccan *zellige* tilework and *muqarnas* vaults, the latter designed in two dimensions from geometric cells, are also characteristic.

## Fractals, analysis, and modern movements

Traditional Javanese batiks have a measured fractal dimension (a number between 1 and 2 that quantifies how a pattern's detail scales with magnification) of 1.1 (Cirebon) to 1.7 (Lasem, Tasikmalaya). Jackson Pollock's drip paintings have similar dimensions: *Number 14* (1948) ≈ 1.45, *Blue Poles* ≈ 1.72. Max Ernst pioneered a semi-automatic drip technique in 1942 by swinging a perforated bucket of paint, producing Lissajous figures (the looping curves traced by two perpendicular oscillations), a method that may have influenced Pollock. Henri Poincaré's *Science and Hypothesis*, read by Picasso and Metzinger, opened non-Euclidean geometry and the idea of a fourth dimension to artists, contributing to Cubism. M. C. Escher (1898–1972), advised by the mathematician H. S. M. Coxeter, made intensive use of tessellation (covering a plane with shapes that fit without gaps or overlaps) and hyperbolic geometry. The De Stijl movement (Theo van Doesburg, Piet Mondrian) embraced elementary geometric forms explicitly. Mathematical objects such as the hyperbolic plane (crocheted by Daina Taimiņa in 2001) and the Lorenz manifold have been physically realised in fibre art.

## Illustrating mathematical ideas

Giotto's *Stefaneschi Triptych* (1320) illustrates recursion (a process whose output becomes its own input) by depicting the triptych inside itself. Escher's *Print Gallery* (1956) is an infinite loop of a gallery containing a painting of itself, which mathematicians Bart de Smit and Hendrik Lenstra showed could hold a Droste-effect copy (an image containing a smaller copy of itself). Magritte's *La condition humaine* (1933) and Rembrandt's *Holy Family with a Curtain* (1646) play on the confusion between a painting's frame and the world it depicts, a visual paradox. Dalí's last painting, *The Swallow's Tail* (1983), was inspired by René Thom's catastrophe theory, a framework for modelling sudden shifts in behaviour.

## Sacred geometry

Plutarch attributed to Plato the maxim that "God geometrizes continually," a Pythagorean-rooted idea that the world's geometry is sacred. A 13th-century illumination in the *Codex Vindobonensis* shows God drawing the universe with compasses. Kepler modelled planetary orbits as nested Platonic solids in *Mysterium Cosmographicum* (1596). Blake's *Ancient of Days* (1794) and *Newton* (c. 1800) use the compass to critique reason as narrow. Dalí's tesseract cross and dodecahedron supper extend the tradition into four dimensions.
