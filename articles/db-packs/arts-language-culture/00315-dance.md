# Dance

Dance is sequenced body movement with aesthetic or symbolic value, usually tied to rhythm. It spans theatrical spectacle built for an audience and participatory social activity done for the participants themselves, serving ritual, storytelling, courtship, exercise, and communal cohesion. Today it operates as a global industry of education, sport, entertainment, and therapy.

## The defining split

**Theatrical dance** is built to be watched: ballet, Bharatanatyam, modern dance, Kabuki. It cultivates virtuoso technique, costume, and narrative. **Participatory dance** is done for the participants: folk, social, rave, partner dance. Forms share identical steps in lines, circles, or squares, or improvise freely. A *corps de ballet* serves the spectacle; a social *pas de deux* serves the partners.

## Origins and deep history

The earliest evidence is paintings at the Rock Shelters of Bhimbetka in Madhya Pradesh, at least 10,000 years old, and Egyptian tomb paintings from c. 3300 BC. Both predate writing, so dance is plausibly the oldest method for oral storytelling and for inducing the trance states used in healing rituals. Early textual references appear in Greek philosophy (*choros* in Plato, Aristotle, Plutarch, Lucian), the Bible and Talmud (over 30 dance terms), Chinese Neolithic pottery showing line dances, and the *Lüshi Chunqiu*. In India, Bharata Muni's *Natya Shastra* (first millennium BCE) codified dance inside dramaturgy and seeded a continuous tradition running into modern Bollywood. In China, early dance was tied to sorcery and shamanism.

## Music and rhythm

Dance is usually accompanied by music, but not strictly: tap and gumboot generate their own percussion, and many traditional forms bind dance and music so tightly that one name covers both (jig, waltz, tango, salsa). Rhythm needs a regular pulse (the *tactus*) and a pattern of accents and rests (metre). Tango sits in 2/4 at roughly 66 BPM; where polyrhythm is dense, as in the Balkans, fixed melodic phrases lock to step sequences so dancers cannot lose the pattern.

A long-standing debate asks which came first. Ancient Egyptians credited the god Athotus with noticing that music made people move, and the Renaissance theorist Guglielmo Ebreo da Pesaro treated dance as the physical expression of spiritual harmony. Ted Shawn and Lincoln Kirstein argued the reverse: music grew out of bodily movement, so poetic "feet" and musical "measures" inherit patterns from alternating strong and weak, left and right steps. This explains why ballad measures and the carol (originally a circle dance) take their names from dance. Émile Jaques-Dalcroze's *Eurhythmics* and Rudolf Laban's "rhythm and shape" formalised the music-movement link for modern training.

## Theatrical traditions

**Ballet** evolved from 16th- and 17th-century French and Italian court spectacles. Louis XIV, himself a dancer, chartered the Académie Royale de Danse in 1661, shifting ballet from amateur court performance to licensed profession; women entered the Paris Opera Ballet troupe in 1681. The 19th-century *ballet blanc* (*Giselle*, *La Sylphide*) introduced plotless rhythmic interludes called *petit allegro* and opened the path to 20th-century abstract ballet.

**Indian classical dance**, rooted in the *Natya Shastra*, splits technique into *nritta* (abstract rhythmic movement) and *nritya* (expressive storytelling), both bound to the *tala* cyclic rhythmic framework and taught through *bol*, spoken syllabic mnemonics. Shared vocabulary includes *mudras* (hand gestures), *abhinaya* (facial expression), and ankle bells (*ghungroo*). **Japanese dance-theatre** (Kabuki, Noh) makes the same split between narrative and abstract pieces. **Modern and contemporary dance** emerged in the early 20th century through Fuller, Duncan, Wigman, and Ruth St. Denis, then matured with Graham, Horton, and Humphrey.

## Participatory forms

In African dance, feet and trunk mark the pulse while shoulders, knees, and head articulate independent cross-rhythms, producing polyrhythm inside a single body. African American vernacular dance (tap, jazz, swing, lindy hop, hip hop) developed outside studios in everyday spaces and now drives global popular dance. In Latin America, partner dances (samba, tango, salsa) and national genres (merengue, cumbia, bachata) unify African, European, and Indigenous roots; *Capoeira*, the Afro-Brazilian blend of dance, acrobatics, and martial arts, was historically banned and is now celebrated.

## Industry and competition

Dance studies run from BA to PhD, covering choreography, *ethnochoreology* (the anthropology of dance), kinesiology, notation such as Labanotation, and dance/movement therapy. US studio revenue reached roughly $4 billion in 2019 across about 32,000 studios. Dancers are typically contract-based, with high competition, low pay, and high injury risk; US unions (AGMA, SAG-AFTRA, Equity) set minimum standards.

Studio competitive dance runs 2–3 day events in contemporary, lyrical, jazz, tap, hip hop, and ballet, with roughly two-minute routines scored on choreography, technique, and overall impression. DanceSport (competitive ballroom) is standardised by the World Dance Council across 59 nations, with ten International Standard and Latin dances; couples rise through levels by placing in the top three at sanctioned events. Breaking debuted as an Olympic sport at the 2021 Summer Olympics, ending a campaign since the 1930s and reopening the question of which styles count as sport.

In the 1950s and 1960s the People's Republic of China used dance tours across East and South Asia to demonstrate anti-colonial solidarity with newly independent states such as Indonesia, India, and Burma.

## Health

Pointe shoes concentrate the dancer's weight on the toes, and toe pads and tape manage the resulting pain and injury. Dancers face roughly three times the general population's risk of eating disorders, mainly anorexia nervosa and EDNOS, driven by pressure for low body mass and expressed through laxative abuse, over-exercise, and food restriction. The same activity delivers cardiovascular health, stress reduction, flexibility, strength, balance, coordination, improved memory and cognition, lower dementia risk, higher self-esteem, and stronger social connection.
