# Science fiction film

A science fiction film is a movie built around speculative, science-based ideas that mainstream science has not fully accepted: extraterrestrial life, spacecraft, robots, cyborgs, mutants, interstellar travel, time travel, and other advanced technologies. The genre uses these premises to explore political and social issues and philosophical questions about the human condition.

Film theorist Vivian Sobchack defined the genre as one that "emphasizes actual, extrapolative, or speculative science and the empirical method, interacting in a social context with the lesser emphasized, but still present, transcendentalism (belief that knowledge can come from spiritual or supernatural sources, such as magic or religion) of magic and religion, in an attempt to reconcile man with the unknown." The boundary is porous: science fiction horror blends the two genres (*Frankenstein*, *Alien*), and Hollywood "science" is often pseudo-science chosen for atmosphere over accuracy. Eric R. Williams treats science fiction as one of eleven super-genres covering all feature-length narrative film, alongside action, crime, fantasy, horror, romance, slice of life, sports, thriller, war, and western.

## Visual style

Sobchack argues science fiction differs from fantasy by seeking belief in the images, while fantasy asks viewers to suspend disbelief. It places the alien inside the familiar: alien images become domesticated, familiar images become strange, or the two are juxtaposed. Scott Bukatman adds that the genre gives contemporary culture an expression of the sublime through exaggerated scale, apocalypse, or transcendence.

## Chronology

The genre begins with Georges Méliès's *Le Voyage dans la Lune* (1902), which used trick photography to show a spacecraft reaching the Moon. *Himmelskibet* (1918, 97 minutes) is generally considered the first feature-length science fiction film. Fritz Lang's *Metropolis* (1927), produced at Studio Babelsberg in Germany, pioneered dystopian social prediction. Early cinema merged science fiction with horror in *Frankenstein* (1910) and *Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde* (1920).

From the 1930s to the 1950s the genre consisted mainly of low-budget B movies (cheap second-feature films), with big-budget exceptions such as *King Kong* (1933), *Things to Come* (1936), and *Destination Moon* (1950). The 1950s, driven by public interest in space travel, are widely called the golden age, producing *The Day the Earth Stood Still* (1951), *The War of the Worlds* (1953), *Forbidden Planet* (1956), and *Invasion of the Body Snatchers* (1956). Monster movies ran in parallel, peaking with Toho's *Godzilla* (1954), directed by Ishirō Honda with effects by Eiji Tsuburaya, which launched the kaiju (giant-monster) tradition and influenced Stanley Kubrick.

Kubrick's *2001: A Space Odyssey* (1968), co-developed with Arthur C. Clarke, brought new realism and philosophical scope and marked the genre's acceptance as serious art. Andrei Tarkovsky's *Solaris* (1972) and *Stalker* (1979) extended that prestige. *Star Wars* (1977) and *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* (1977) launched the big-budget, effects-driven blockbuster model. The late 1970s and 1980s produced a darker wave (*Alien*, 1979; *Blade Runner*, 1982; *The Terminator*, 1984) alongside family-friendly entries (*E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial*, 1982; *Back to the Future*, 1985).

The 1990s tied the genre to cyberpunk (high-tech, low-life urban settings dominated by computer networks) and the emerging World Wide Web (*The Matrix*, 1999); the 2000s brought digital effects and political commentary (*Minority Report*, *District 9*, *Avatar*); the 2010s produced realistic science-fiction epics (*Gravity*, 2013; *Interstellar*, 2014; *The Martian*, 2015; *Arrival*, 2016), superhero franchises, and young-adult dystopian adaptations. As of 2019, science fiction held roughly 4.77% of the North American box-office market.

## Recurring subjects

**Alien lifeforms.** Aliens are usually anthropomorphic (human-shaped) so audiences can relate, and often function as displaced metaphors for real-world threats, from Cold War anxieties to contemporary foreign-policy fears. Friendly counterparts appear in *E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial*, *Close Encounters of the Third Kind*, and *Avatar*.

**Disaster and apocalypse.** Standard subtypes include alien invasion (*Independence Day*, 1996), environmental disaster (*Waterworld*, 1995; *2012*, 2009), technology supplanting humanity (*The Matrix*, the *Terminator* series), nuclear aftermath (*Mad Max*, 1979; *Mad Max: Fury Road*, 2015), and pandemic (*I Am Legend*, 2007).

**Mind and identity.** Questions about what makes a person human run from *Frankenstein* through *Blade Runner* and *RoboCop* to *Total Recall*, *Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind*, *The Matrix*, and *Inception*, exploring memory erasure, brain-computer interfaces, virtual reality, and the indistinguishability of simulated and real experience.

**Robots.** Karel Čapek coined the word in 1921. Roles range from sidekicks (C-3PO, R2-D2, WALL-E) to villains (HAL 9000, the Terminator) to protagonists (replicants in *Blade Runner*). A central question is whether robots will replace humans, develop conscience, or destroy their makers.

**Time travel.** Films achieve it through machines (*The Time Machine*, *Back to the Future*, *Terminator*), physics concepts such as relativistic time dilation and wormholes (*Planet of the Apes*, *Interstellar*), or inner powers (*Donnie Darko*, *The Butterfly Effect*). Recurring motifs include the self-fulfilling loop (*La jetée*, 1962; *12 Monkeys*, 1995) and altered pasts.

## Social commentary

Science fiction offers a remote vantage for discussing sensitive issues without direct controversy. Early films expressed fears of automation and dehumanization (*Metropolis*). 1950s monster films stood in for nuclear anxiety and the Cold War (*Godzilla*). 1970s films satirised contemporary mores (*A Clockwork Orange*, *Logan's Run*). Post-2001 films engaged with surveillance, civil liberties, immigration, and corporate power (*Minority Report*, *V for Vendetta*, *District 9*, *Elysium*, *Avatar*).

## Influence of science fiction authors

Jules Verne (1828-1905) was the first major author adapted to screen, beginning with Méliès's 1902 Moon film. H. G. Wells, Arthur C. Clarke, Ray Bradbury, Kurt Vonnegut, and especially Philip K. Dick have supplied source material; Dick's paranoid fiction fed *Blade Runner*, *Total Recall*, *Minority Report*, and *The Adjustment Bureau*. Clarke's third law, "any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic," captures why so much screen technology, from communicators as smartphones to gesture recognition and autonomous cars, was once fictional.
