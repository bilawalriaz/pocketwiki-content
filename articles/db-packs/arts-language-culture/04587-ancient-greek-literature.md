# Ancient Greek literature

Ancient Greek literature is the body of writing in the Greek language from the earliest surviving texts (c. 12th century BC) through the Byzantine Empire. Its epic, dramatic, philosophical, and historical works fixed the genres that later writers imitated.

## Epic and Hesiodic foundations

The earliest major works are Homer's epics, the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, set in an idealized Mycenaean past and composed in dactylic hexameter (six metrical feet per line) using the Homeric dialect, a literary blend of Ionic, Aeolic, and Attic Greek. Hesiod, a near-contemporary, supplied a creation narrative in the *Theogony* and a guide to rural life and moral instruction in *Works and Days*. These poems became the bedrock of Greek religion and literary education. The "Homeric Question," whether a single poet named Homer wrote both epics or whether they were compiled from older oral traditions, remains open.

## Lyric, elegiac, and iambic poetry

After the epics, poetry diversified into shorter forms. Elegiac and iambic poetry, both in Ionic dialect, emerged in the 7th century BC; the elegiac couplet pairs a hexameter line with a shorter pentameter line. Lyric poetry, named because it was sung to a lyre, reached its peak with the "Nine Lyric Poets," of whom Sappho (Aeolic dialect) and Pindar (choral lyric) are the most celebrated survivors.

## Athenian drama

Athenian drama grew from choral songs performed at festivals of Dionysus. Tragedy explored heroic myths and was shaped by three 5th-century playwrights: Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides. Comedy moved from the political satire of Old Comedy, represented by Aristophanes, to the domestic plots of New Comedy, represented by Menander. All surviving plays use Attic dialect, the speech of Athens.

## Prose: history, biography, philosophy

Prose writing was a later Greek invention. Herodotus opened the genre with the *Histories*, an inquiry into the Persian Wars. Thucydides then set a new standard: his *History of the Peloponnesian War* applied critical analysis to events and policy. Later historians, including Xenophon, Polybius, and Diodorus Siculus, broadened the scope. Biography (*bios*) was distinct from history, focusing on a single person's character for moral example; Plutarch's *Parallel Lives* is the genre's most influential surviving work.

Philosophy moved from the fragments of the pre-Socratics to Plato's dialogues and Aristotle's treatises on logic, politics, ethics, and poetics. Plotinus later founded Neoplatonism, a blend of Platonic and earlier ideas.

## Hellenistic and Roman-period literature

After Alexander the Great's conquests, Alexandria became the cultural capital. The Library of Alexandria supported scholarly editing and preservation of classical texts. Hellenistic poetry diversified: Theocritus pioneered bucolic poetry, depicting idealized rural shepherd life; Callimachus wrote short, learned poems that rejected epic length; Apollonius of Rhodes revived epic with *The Argonautica*. Under Roman rule, Greek literature continued. Lucian wrote prose satire, and prose fiction emerged, including the pastoral novel *Daphnis and Chloe* (attributed to Longus) and the Milesian tale, a short, often bawdy adventure genre originating with Aristides of Miletus. Heliodorus's *Aethiopica* set the template for the later Greek novel.

## Science, mathematics, and Christian writing

Greek scientific prose produced texts of long-lasting authority. Euclid's *Elements* systematized geometry. Archimedes' treatises advanced applied mathematics. Ptolemy's *Almagest*, a geocentric mathematical model of the heavens, dominated astronomy for over 1,300 years. The New Testament was composed in Koine Greek, the common dialect of the eastern Mediterranean after Alexander, and early Christian thinkers such as Origen engaged directly with Greek philosophy. The vast majority of ancient Greek literature is lost, and survival is heavily skewed by papyrus finds, with Homer dominating the record.
