# Islamic art

Islamic art is the visual culture produced from the 7th century CE onward across lands inhabited or ruled by Muslims, spanning more than forty nations and over a millennium. The label is a late 19th-century Western invention, but it survives because a recognisable set of preferences recurs across very different regions and periods: an emphasis on calligraphy, geometry, and stylised plant forms, a complex relationship with the human figure, and a willingness to absorb foreign techniques. The art is anchored in the written word of the Quran, treated as the literal word of God, and that priority shapes almost every choice a maker faces.

## The shared visual vocabulary

Three decorative languages dominate religious and luxury objects. Calligraphy is the most prestigious, because it carries scripture; scripts such as *kufic* (angular, early) and *naskh* (rounded, cursive) appear on buildings, coins, ceramics, and manuscript pages. Geometric patterns and arabesques (rhythmic, spiralling foliage and interlaced lines) cover the surfaces that calligraphy leaves bare. Together these modes fill walls, tiles, manuscripts, and metalwork with dense, non-figural ornament.

A persistent avoidance of images of living beings, called aniconism, grows from prohibitions on idolatry. In practice the rule was uneven: religious and public contexts often went figure-free, while private courts and secular objects freely showed people, animals, and narrative scenes. Umayyad desert castles (c. 660–750) preserve some of the earliest figurative paintings. Aniconism is the default, figurative painting is the exception, and both must be held at once.

## Media and innovations

Islamic makers were technical innovators whose inventions travelled. Potters in Basra developed the first opaque tin glazes in the 8th century, and Iraqi kilns soon produced stonepaste, a white quartz body that took fine painted decoration better than clay. Egyptian workshops invented lustreware, a metallic iridescent glaze later applied to glass. These techniques were partly inspired by imported Chinese porcelain. Glass from Egypt and Syria was among the most sophisticated in the medieval world, using lustre and enamel. Metalworkers in brass and bronze inlaid silver and gold into densely decorated vessels, typically leaving the bodies figure-free even when the same workshop produced illustrated books.

Tilework became a signature of architecture after the 9th century, when glazed tiling let builders sheathe whole façades in geometry and inscription. Muqarnas, the honeycomb or stalactite vaulting, turned structural transitions into crystalline ornament. Among portable arts, the pile carpet is the most widely recognised; major weaving centres in Persia, Egypt, and Turkey shifted from early geometric fields to flowing arabesques between the 14th and 17th centuries. Textiles in silk continued Byzantine and Sasanian figural traditions, and Indonesian batik was refined under Islamic influence into its abstract, wax-resist style.

## Painting and the book

Manuscript illustration is one of the great Islamic art forms. A continuous figurative tradition runs from the wall paintings of Umayyad desert castles through Persian, Ottoman, and Mughal miniatures. By the 13th century the Persian miniature had become dominant, and it reached its technical peak in the 16th–17th centuries. These small paintings, often bound into albums called muraqqa, typically illustrate poetry or historical chronicles and are recognisable by high viewpoints and flat, brilliant colour, with a Chinese influence absorbed through Mongol-era contacts. The Mughals also perfected parchin kari (pietra dura), inlaying semi-precious stones into marble on tomb and palace surfaces.

## A short chronology

- **7th c. CE**: rise of Islam; the artistic tradition begins.
- **c. 660–750**: Umayyad desert castles preserve some of the earliest figurative paintings.
- **8th c.**: first tin-opacified glazes in Basra; lustre painting in Egypt.
- **9th c.**: stonepaste originates in Iraq; glazed tiling becomes a major architectural medium.
- **9th–11th c.**: East Persian epigraphic ware, pottery covered in stylised inscription.
- **13th c.**: Persian miniature becomes the dominant painting tradition.
- **16th–early 17th c.**: peak of Persian carpet weaving and Ottoman İznik pottery.
- **16th–17th c.**: Persian miniature reaches its technical climax.
- **2001**: the Taliban destroyed figurative artworks in Afghanistan.

## Why the category is contested

Two debates run through the field. First, scholars ask whether "Islamic art" is a coherent object at all, given that Muslim societies have produced work as varied as Andalusian ivory caskets and Timurid turquoise tilework, with religion often only one motivation among many. Second, the degree of aniconism is genuinely disputed: strict readings ban living figures outright, yet courts from the Umayyads to the Mughals, including rulers personally devout, commissioned exactly the figures the rule seems to forbid. Aniconism sets a default for sacred and public art, and figurative work thrives wherever a secular, courtly, or narrative context absorbs the religious cost.

The term endures because, beneath the regional variety, the same toolkit of calligraphy, geometry, arabesque, and careful surface treatment recurs from Spain to Indonesia. A 9th-century Iraqi lustre bowl, a 15th-century Persian carpet, and a Mughal inlaid dagger hilt share a preference for pattern over figure that is rare in the contemporary Christian or Buddhist worlds they traded with.
