# History of art

The history of art is the record of how humans have made visual objects for aesthetic, spiritual, narrative, and functional purposes, shaped by the cultural, religious, and philosophical conditions of each civilization. It is not a single line of progress but a network of parallel traditions, each solving recurring problems of representation, expression, and meaning in different ways.

## The Deep Background

The oldest known artworks are containers for holding paints, found in South Africa and dated to roughly 100,000 BC. Between about 43,000 and 35,000 BC, the Swabian Jura region of Germany produced the earliest known non-stationary art, small carved figurines including the Venus of Hohle Fels and the Venus of Willendorf. From roughly 38,000 to 12,000 BC, Upper Paleolithic artists in Europe created the famous cave paintings at sites like Lascaux. These early works are widely interpreted as tied to ritual or symbolic life, though the exact purpose of cave paintings remains debated, with theories ranging from religious ceremony to hunting magic.

## The Rise of Civilizations

When cities and writing appeared in fourth-millennium BC Mesopotamia, art became tied to institutions: religion, kingship, and record-keeping. Mesopotamian art featured ziggurats and narrative stone reliefs, and gave rise to cuneiform, the earliest known writing system. In the Indus Valley (c. 2400–1900 BC), advanced urban centers supported distinct artistic traditions. In China, the Shang Dynasty (c. 1600–1050 BC) produced ritual bronze vessels decorated with the taotie, a stylized face whose meaning is still uncertain.

Around the same broad era, Egypt developed art focused on immortality and used hierarchical scale, a convention in which more important figures are shown larger than others. In Greece, the Classical period of the 5th and 4th centuries BC emphasized harmonious proportion and the idealized human form, laying the foundation for Western art. Roman art adapted Greek styles but added greater realism and structural innovations such as the arch and dome; the 79 AD eruption of Mount Vesuvius preserved Roman wall paintings at Pompeii.

## Asian, Islamic, and Indigenous Traditions

Chinese art is notable for continuity, moving from early ritual bronzes into painting, calligraphy, and ceramics. Indian art developed alongside Buddhism, Hinduism, and Jainism, producing sculpture, stupas, and rock-cut cave temples. Japanese art absorbed foreign influences, evolving from early pottery to sophisticated painting and woodblock prints. Hallstatt culture (c. 800–500 BCE) marks the early phase of Celtic art in Europe.

Islamic art, emerging in the 7th century AD, relies on geometric patterns, calligraphy, and stylized motifs rather than figurative imagery, with rich regional variation across a vast empire. In Mesoamerica, the Olmec, Maya, and Aztec created monumental architecture, sculpture, and intricate crafts without metal tools, while Andean cultures such as the Paracas and Inca produced complex textiles and metalwork. Sub-Saharan Africa developed diverse sculptural traditions, including the bronze castings of Benin and the terracottas of Nok, often serving spiritual and social functions.

## Medieval Europe and the Renaissance

In medieval Europe, art served the Church. Byzantine art used gold mosaics and iconic, formal imagery. Romanesque art featured robust stone architecture and frescoes; the Bayeux Tapestry, depicting the Norman conquest of England, is a celebrated narrative artifact from this milieu. Gothic art, developing in 12th-century Northern France, introduced ribbed vaults and stained glass. The Renaissance (c. 1400–1600), beginning in 15th-century Florence, revived Classical learning and achieved unprecedented lifelikeness through figures like Leonardo da Vinci and Michelangelo, while the Northern Renaissance developed distinct techniques such as oil painting.

## From Baroque to Modernism

Baroque art (c. 1580–1750) used drama, emotion, and grandeur to serve the Catholic Counter-Reformation and absolute monarchies. Rococo (c. 1720 onward) reacted with lighter, playful themes. Neoclassicism revived Classical ideals of order and restraint, inspired by Enlightenment rationality and archaeological discoveries. Romanticism (c. 1790–1880) swung back toward emotion, nature, and individualism; Realism (c. 1830–1890) depicted everyday life; and Impressionism (c. 1865–1885) broke from academic finish to capture light and momentary effects, often painting outdoors.

Post-Impressionism (c. 1885–1910) extended these concerns while exploring structure and emotion, seeding Symbolism and early modernism. The early 20th-century avant-garde accelerated the break with tradition: Art Nouveau integrated organic forms into design; Fauvism used bold, non-naturalistic color; Expressionism conveyed subjective emotion through groups like Die Brücke and Der Blaue Reiter; Cubism, partly inspired by non-Western art, fragmented form and rejected traditional perspective; and Surrealism explored the subconscious under the influence of Freud, using techniques like automatism, the creation of art without conscious control.

## The Global Twentieth Century

Abstract Expressionism (1940s–1950s) was the first major American movement, emphasizing spontaneous, non-representational expression through gestural brushwork and thickly applied paint. Pop Art (1950s–1960s) drew from mass culture and consumerism, and artists began incorporating found objects, everyday items that challenged traditional definitions of art. Minimalism (1960s) reduced art to essential forms, while Conceptual art prioritized ideas over visual aesthetics, dissolving the old hierarchy between "fine arts" and crafts. The classification of fine art versus craft, the role of art in society, and the influence of non-Western traditions on early 20th-century modernism remain active areas of scholarly debate.
