# Russian language

Russian is an East Slavic language of the Indo-European family, native to roughly 137.5 million people in Russia and 93.7 million more across the former Soviet Union. With over 210 million speakers worldwide, it is the most spoken native language in Europe, the most geographically widespread language in Eurasia, one of the six official languages of the United Nations, and one of two official languages aboard the International Space Station.

## Family and periodisation

Russian sits in the East Slavic group of the Balto-Slavic branch, alongside Belarusian, Rusyn, and Ukrainian; the three largest members retain high mutual intelligibility. Its history is conventionally split into Old Russian (11th–17th centuries), Middle Russian (14th–17th centuries), and Modern Russian (18th century to the present), though some scholars collapse the first two. The earliest dated manuscript showing Russian elements is the Ostromir Gospels of 1056–1057, written as the vernacular began pulling away from Old Church Slavonic, the liturgical language introduced around 1000 CE. From the start this produced a diglossia, a stable split where Church Slavonic served as the high literary register and the spoken vernacular as the low one.

## How the standard language formed

The Moscow dialect, reinforced by southern-dialect speakers migrating to the capital, became the basis of the standard. Peter the Great drove the first orthographic reform in 1708–1710 and pushed Russian into secular use against Church Slavonic. Mikhail Lomonosov's *Russian Grammar* (1755) codified three stylistic registers, high (Church Slavonic), middle (literary prose), and low (vernacular), with the modern standard sitting closest to the middle. By the 1830s, Alexander Pushkin's writing had locked in a contemporary, non-archaic literary norm, and the language's official name shifted from *rossiyskiy* (citizenship-based) to *russkiy* (ethnic-cultural). The last major alphabet reform came in 1917–1918 under the Bolsheviks.

## Sound and grammar

Russian is a fusional language: a single ending on a noun can encode case, number, and gender at once. Nouns, adjectives, and past-tense verbs carry three genders (masculine, feminine, neuter), and six cases handle the grammatical roles English expresses with prepositions: nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, instrumental, and prepositional. Verbs inflect for three tenses and, more importantly, for two aspects: perfective for completed actions and imperfective for ongoing, repeated, or uncompleted ones. This aspectual contrast carries meaning English speakers usually express with adverbs or context.

The defining phonological feature is a hard–soft consonant split, where palatalization (raising the tongue toward the hard palate during a consonant) creates a second series of "soft" consonants next to "hard" ones, written indirectly by the following vowel rather than a mark on the consonant itself. Unstressed vowels also reduce toward a centralised sound, and stress is mobile and usually unmarked in writing. The syllable structure tolerates consonant clusters of up to four sounds, producing the dense runs visible in words like *vzglyad* ("glance").

## Where Russian is used today

Russian is official in Russia, Belarus, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, and Tajikistan, and functions as a lingua franca across much of the former Soviet space, especially in business, higher education, and interethnic communication. Globally it had about 259.8 million speakers in 2010, most of them in Russia and the CIS/Baltic states. Since the Soviet Union dissolved in 1991, its institutional position has weakened in several successor states, particularly Ukraine, the Baltic countries, and parts of Central Asia, where titular-language policies have reduced Russian in schools and public life; mixed varieties such as surzhyk (Ukrainian-Russian) and trasianka (Belarusian-Russian) mark the contact zone. The U.S. Defense Language Institute rates Russian a Category III language for English speakers, requiring roughly 1,100 hours of immersion study to reach intermediate proficiency.
