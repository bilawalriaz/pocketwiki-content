# Chinese literature

Chinese literature is one of the world's oldest continuous literary traditions, stretching over three millennia from oracle bone inscriptions to contemporary online novels. Its defining arc is a shift from a single classical written language used by an educated elite to a vernacular literature read by the masses, shaped at every stage by the technology, politics, and philosophy of the dynasties that produced it.

## Language: the engine of change

Two written forms governed what Chinese literature could do. **Literary Chinese (wenyan)** was a compact, ancient prose style modelled on the pre-Qin classics and used for nearly all formal writing until the twentieth century. Its distance from the spoken language meant that even basic literacy required years of classical study. **Vernacular Chinese (baihua)** was the actual spoken language, used in folk songs, plays, and popular stories for centuries. The 1910s–1920s New Culture Movement made baihua the medium of serious literature, with Lu Xun as a pioneering stylist. Every other transformation in the tradition, including Tang poetry, Song lyrics, Ming and Qing novels, and modern realism, sits on top of this linguistic foundation.

## Classical foundations: philosophy, history, poetry

The earliest collections set the conventions that still define the tradition. The *Shijing* (Classic of Poetry), compiled between the 11th and 7th centuries BC, preserved folk and ceremonial songs and fixed the four-character line that influenced later verse. The *Chuci* (Songs of Chu) added emotional intensity and exotic imagery. During the **Hundred Schools of Thought (770–256 BC)**, competing philosophical schools produced the texts that became China's intellectual bedrock: the Confucian **Four Books and Five Classics**, which later formed the basis of the imperial examination system; the Daoist *Dao De Jing* and *Zhuangzi*; and Sun Tzu's *The Art of War*.

History writing became a parallel classical art. A tradition of accurate court records existed by 841 BC, and Sima Qian's *Shiji* (Records of the Grand Historian, c. 90 BC) established the biographical and chronological model used by every later dynastic history, eventually producing the **Twenty-Four Histories**. Encyclopedias pushed textual production to industrial scale: the Ming *Yongle Encyclopedia* held about 50 million characters and the Qing *Complete Classics Collection of Ancient China* exceeded 100 million.

Poetry was the prestige form for scholars. During the Han, the descriptive *fu* and Music Bureau (*yuefu*) poetry expanded the repertoire, and the five-character line became standard. The **Tang Dynasty (618–907)** is the recognised golden age: Li Bai and Du Fu perfected regulated verse under fixed tonal and structural rules. The **Song Dynasty (960–1279)** then developed *ci*, a lyric form written to fit the fixed metrical patterns of pre-existing musical tunes. In the same era, the neoclassical prose movement led by Han Yu called for a return to the simple, forceful style of the ancient philosophical texts.

## Fiction and the rise of the vernacular

Although the earliest stories circulated orally, full-length vernacular fiction grew from the Song Dynasty onward out of performance traditions such as *bianwen* (transformation tales) and *pingshu* (storytelling). The novel matured between the 14th and 18th centuries, peaking in the eighteenth-century *Dream of the Red Chamber*. Vernacular fiction carried ideas and language into households where classical texts were never read.

## Print, politics, and the modern era

Woodblock printing had circulated books for centuries, but **Bi Sheng's invention of movable type between 990 and 1051** during the Song Dynasty multiplied the speed and scale of literary production, broadening the reading public. The **late Qing (1895–1911)** introduced reformist fiction and Western translations; the **Republican Era (1912–1949)** produced a diverse scene of leftist, modernist, and women writers.

After 1949 the state imposed **socialist realism** as the official doctrine, requiring literature to depict revolutionary struggle realistically. Mao Zedong's 1942 *Talks at the Yan'an Forum* had set the guidelines. The **Cultural Revolution (1966–1976)** narrowed expression further, reducing most published work to political slogans. Post-1978 reforms opened space for "scar literature" about that trauma and for experimental writing. The contemporary scene is plural: realist novels, avant-garde poetry, and a vast **online literature (wangwen)** sector, serialised fiction in fantasy, romance, and other genres consumed on digital platforms. Gao Xingjian won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 2000.

## Living debates

Scholars dispute whether modern Chinese literature begins in the seventeenth century or the late Qing. The relative quality of Song and Tang poetry has been contested since the Song, and the authenticity of early historical texts such as the *Bamboo Annals* remains uncertain. The ethical and artistic value of AI-assisted translation for online fiction is a current dispute.
