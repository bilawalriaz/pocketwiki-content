# English language

English is a West Germanic language that took shape in early medieval England and now functions as the world's dominant lingua franca, a shared language between speakers of different native tongues. It has official or co-official status in 57 sovereign states, making it the most geographically widespread language, and the most spoken overall, though only the third-most spoken as a native language, after Mandarin Chinese and Spanish. What makes English unusual is the gap between its small, Germanic grammatical core and its enormous, multi-source vocabulary: the basic plumbing of the language stayed simple while the lexicon was rebuilt by contact with Old Norse, Norman French, and Latin.

## Origins and the shape of the language

English belongs to the West Germanic branch of the Indo-European family, alongside Dutch, German, and Frisian. It is named after the Angles, one of the Germanic peoples who crossed to Britain in the 5th century. Their speech, Old English, was highly inflected, meaning a word's ending signalled its grammatical role, much like modern German.

Between the 8th and 11th centuries, Norse colonisation of eastern and northern England brought prolonged contact with Old Norse. The result was a deep borrowing of everyday words such as *give*, *get*, and *sky*, and the replacement of several pronouns, including *they* and *them*. The 1066 Norman Conquest triggered a second wave. Norman French and Latin together account for roughly 28% each of Modern English vocabulary, concentrated in formal, legal, and intellectual registers. This is why the most common English words (*the*, *is*, *man*, *eat*) are Germanic, while domains like law, cuisine, and medicine lean heavily French and Latin.

Two consequences followed. English lost most of its Old English inflections and became moderately analytic, where word order and small helper words carry grammatical meaning instead of endings. And the long vowel system was reshaped by the Great Vowel Shift (c. 1350–1700), a coordinated change in how long vowels were pronounced. Because spelling was never reset, modern English inherits irregular letter-sound correspondences (the *a* in *name* versus *cat*) from that mismatch.

## Timeline of consolidation

- c. 450–1150: Old English period; West Germanic dialects form the language.
- 8th–11th centuries: Old Norse contact reshapes core vocabulary and pronouns.
- 1066: Norman Conquest begins the Middle English period.
- c. 1350–1700: The Great Vowel Shift reshapes pronunciation.
- 1476: Caxton introduces the printing press to England, aiding standardisation.
- 1611: The King James Bible anchors an Early Modern English standard.
- 1755: Johnson's *Dictionary* codifies British usage.
- 1828: Webster's *American Dictionary* establishes a distinct US norm.
- Late 18th century: The British Empire carries English worldwide.
- 20th century: US economic and cultural power accelerates its global reach.

## How English works as a system

English grammar relies on fixed subject-verb-object (SVO) word order and a small set of auxiliary verbs (helpers like *do*, *have*, and *be*) to express tense, aspect (whether an action is ongoing or completed), mood, and questions. A characteristic rule is do-support: to negate or question a main verb, English inserts *do* (*does she speak?*, *I do not know*). This scaffolding replaces the case endings Old English once used to mark who did what to whom.

The lexicon holds an estimated 170,000 core words and absorbs loanwords readily. New words form through compounding (*toothbrush*), conversion, which reuses one word type as another (*to google*), and acronyms (*NATO*).

Phonology varies by dialect but typically includes around 24 consonant sounds and a large, complex vowel system. A useful diagnostic is rhoticity, whether /r/ is pronounced after a vowel: General American pronounces the *r* in *car*; Received Pronunciation (RP), the prestige accent of southern England, does not.

## Global distribution and standardisation

English is a pluricentric language: no single authority sets its standard, and educated speakers across regions collectively maintain norms. The three-circle model sorts users by how they acquired the language.

| Circle | Relationship to English | Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Inner | Native language | UK, US, Australia, New Zealand |
| Outer | Institutional second language | India, Nigeria, Philippines, South Africa |
| Expanding | Foreign language of choice | China, Brazil, much of Europe |

Outer-circle countries adopted English for administration and education during colonisation, where it remains official or co-official. Expanding-circle countries learn it for access to science, commerce, and the internet rather than from colonial history.

Major native dialect groups include British English and North American English. North American English is often perceived as more uniform, but it contains distinct varieties such as Southern American English and African-American Vernacular English (AAVE). Australian, New Zealand, South African, and Indian English are major national varieties, each with their own phonology and lexicon. Many regions also host English-based creoles, stable mixed languages that arise when speakers of different languages must communicate.

Orthography uses the Latin alphabet and reflects the language's layered borrowing history, with overlapping Germanic, French, Latin, and Greek conventions. This layering is the main reason English spelling is irregular and why literacy acquisition is harder than for languages with tighter spelling-sound mappings.

## Open questions

English's relationship to Frisian is debated: they share unusual sound changes, but the changes occurred at different times, so whether they descend from a special common ancestor or merely converged is unclear. AAVE's origins are also disputed, with competing accounts tracing it to Southern American English dialects or to an English-based creole. English's future trajectory is similarly open: use continues to grow, but concerns about linguistic imperialism and language death persist, and the language keeps evolving through contact and internal change.
