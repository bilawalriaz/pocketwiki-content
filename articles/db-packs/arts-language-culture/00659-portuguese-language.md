# Portuguese language

Portuguese is a Western Romance language of the Indo-European family, written in the Latin script, with roughly 267 million native speakers. It is the fifth-most spoken native language on Earth and the most widely spoken in the Southern Hemisphere. It is the sole official language of eight sovereign states—Portugal, Brazil, Angola, Mozambique, Cape Verde, Guinea-Bissau, São Tomé and Príncipe, and East Timor—and a co-official language in Macau and Equatorial Guinea. Speakers are called Lusophone, and the Community of Portuguese Language Countries (CPLP), founded in 1996, links the Lusophone states.

## Origins and medieval development

Portuguese descends from Vulgar Latin, the spoken Latin of soldiers, settlers, and officials, not the literary Classical Latin. Roman control of the Iberian Peninsula began in 218 BC during the Second Punic War and was consolidated by 19 BC under Augustus, who organized provinces such as Lusitania and Gallaecia. In the northwest province of Gallaecia, Vulgar Latin lost intervocalic *l* and *n* (so Latin *luna* became Portuguese *lua*, "moon") and simplified geminate consonants, traits that set the region apart from neighboring Romance varieties. Celtic-speaking peoples such as the Gallaeci and Lusitanians had lived in the region before Rome, and their languages left a substratum in Portuguese, especially in words for nature, agriculture, and material objects.

After Germanic invasions in 409 AD and a Muslim conquest beginning in 711 AD, northern Christian kingdoms preserved Romance speech. From the 9th to 14th centuries, Galician–Portuguese functioned as a single linguistic system and served as the literary language of lyric poetry across the Iberian Peninsula. The *Cancioneiro da Ajuda* (late 13th century) preserves some of the earliest known verses, including the *Cantiga da Ribeirinha* (c. 1189/1198).

Galician and Portuguese began diverging from the 11th century, and by 1250 Portuguese used digraphs such as *lh* and *nh* absent from Galician. The Kingdom of Portugal gained independence in 1143. In the late 13th century King Denis switched the royal chancery from Latin to Portuguese, a move often read as a way to strengthen royal authority. After the 1385 Battle of Aljubarrota, the political center moved south to Lisbon and Coimbra, accelerating separation from Galician. Around 1420 marks the transition to Middle Portuguese, and the word *português* first appears in a 1430 letter by Peter, Duke of Coimbra.

## Overseas expansion and the rise of Brazilian Portuguese

Portuguese expansion began with the 1415 conquest of Ceuta and reached Brazil in 1500 under Pedro Álvares Cabral, a region then home to over 1,175 Indigenous languages, mostly Tupi–Guarani. Jesuits wrote grammars of several Indigenous languages, and the transatlantic slave trade brought 200–300 African languages into contact with Portuguese. Gold discoveries in Minas Gerais in the 1690s drew population into the interior and reinforced Portuguese, and the 1757 *Diretório dos Índios* made Portuguese the sole official language in Brazil. The 1808 transfer of the Portuguese court to Rio de Janeiro brought European norms and the printing press, anchoring European Portuguese in the colony. After Brazilian independence in 1822, intellectuals argued over whether to admit Indigenous and popular vocabulary or keep European standards. The first printed Portuguese grammar appeared in 1536 (Fernão de Oliveira), and Luís de Camões's epic *Os Lusíadas* (1572) is taken as the start of Modern Portuguese.

## Speaker distribution

Roughly 200 million speakers live in South America, 30 million in Africa, 15 million in Europe, 5 million in North America, and about 330,000 in Asia and Oceania. Portuguese is the native language of 95% of the populations of Portugal, Brazil, and São Tomé and Príncipe. In Lusophone African countries it functions as a lingua franca across many native languages. Equatorial Guinea, which made Portuguese its third official language in 2011, joined the CPLP in 2014.

## Linguistic features

Portuguese keeps more Latin verbal inflections than any other major Romance language. Two unusual verb forms survive: the future subjunctive, used in dependent clauses to express a contingent future, and the personal infinitive, an infinitive inflected for person and number. Portuguese also retains a synthetic pluperfect (e.g., *eu estivera*, "I had been"), still heard in formal or literary speech. Phonologically, it is known for nasal vowels, a wider range of consonant realizations than Spanish, and distinct *r* sounds in European and Brazilian varieties. European Portuguese is stress-timed, with unstressed vowels heavily reduced; Brazilian Portuguese is more syllable-timed and keeps unstressed vowels clearer.

Two standardized varieties dominate: European Portuguese, based on Coimbra and Lisbon dialects, and Brazilian Portuguese. Brazil has named regional dialects—Caipira, Cearense, Baiano, Fluminense, Gaúcho, Mineiro, Nordestino, Nortista, Paulistano, and Carioca—each with its own phonological profile. African varieties generally follow the European written standard with local sound and word changes. The pronoun *você* dominates both formal and casual speech across most of the Lusophone world, while *tu* is used in parts of Portugal and southern Brazil. Portuguese-based creoles such as Cape Verdean Creole and Papiamento are now treated as independent languages, with more than three million speakers between them.

## Vocabulary

The Portuguese lexicon has around 228,500 entries, the great majority from Latin. More than 3,000 words trace to pre-Roman Celtic languages. The Germanic invasions of the 5th century contributed roughly 500 words, often in warfare (*guerra*), animals (*suíno*), and emotion (*orgulho*). The 400–600 Arabic loanwords from the 9th–13th centuries usually start with *al-* (*aldeia* "village", *azeite* "olive oil"). Contact with Asia from the 15th century added *chá* ("tea") and *catana* ("cutlass"). The slave trade and Indigenous contact contributed *batata* (from a Taino word) and *cafune* (a head caress, from Kimbundu). Modern Portuguese also borrows heavily from French and English.

## Open questions

Scholar Ana Maria Martins argues that the *Notícia de Fiadores* (1175) is the earliest Portuguese document, while others reject it as too lightly Romance; the Will of Afonso II (1214) is the earliest widely accepted text. Whether Galician and Portuguese are two languages or co-dialects of one remains contested, though many linguists lean toward the co-dialect view. The roughly 1420 boundary between Middle and earlier Portuguese, set by scholars such as Luís Lindley Cintra and Serafim da Silva Neto, is a working convention rather than a sharp break. The depth of Portuguese influence on the Asian and African languages it encountered, and how far those contacts produced creoles, remains an active area of research.
