# African literature

African literature is the body of oral and written works in African and Afro-Asiatic languages, with a continuous record stretching from at least the fourth century AD. Its defining feature is the integration of beauty and social purpose: art is valued for the truths it carries and the community it builds, rather than treated as a separate domain.

The literature unfolds in two complementary streams. **Orature**, a term coined by the Ugandan scholar Pio Zirimu, covers prose (myths, historical tales, **trickster tales** built around a cunning figure such as Anansi the spider), poetry (praise songs, epics), and song. Storytelling frequently uses **call-and-response**, in which the audience replies to the narrator. In West Africa, the **griot** is a hereditary storyteller, praise singer, and oral historian who keeps a community's memory alive. Alongside this oral tradition, written literature flourished for centuries in African languages: the **Garima Gospels** in **Ge'ez** in Ethiopia around 500 AD, the *Kebra Negast* ("Book of Kings") compiled in Ge'ez in the 14th century, more than 300,000 manuscripts from Timbuktu in Arabic and local Sahel languages, the Swahili *Utendi wa Tambuka* ("The Story of Tambuka") dated 1728, and over 4,500 documented manuscripts from the Horn of Africa that reveal long-standing scholarly networks. This dual foundation resists the older view that pre-colonial Africa was purely oral.

The colonial period introduced European-language writing. Olaudah Equiano's 1789 *The Interesting Narrative* set the model for the **slave narrative**, an autobiographical account by an enslaved person aimed largely at Western readers. Joseph Ephraim Casely Hayford's *Ethiopia Unbound* (1911) is regarded as the first African novel in English, and H.I.E. Dhlomo's *The Girl Who Killed to Save* (1935) as the first English-language African play. As independence approached, literature turned to liberation. In francophone Africa the **négritude** movement, led by figures such as Léopold Sédar Senghor, who in 1948 published the first anthology of French-language African poetry, affirmed Black African culture and identity. Chinua Achebe's *Things Fall Apart* (1958) became the global touchstone for analysing colonialism, and Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o's *The Black Hermit* (1962) opened East African drama. Writers paid for this work: the Nigerian novelist Ken Saro-Wiwa, for example, was executed in 1995.

After the political independence of the 1950s and 1960s, the field expanded rapidly, exploring tensions between tradition and modernity, individual and community, and state and citizen. Women's voices gained prominence, and international recognition grew: Wole Soyinka won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1986, Naguib Mahfouz in 1988, Ben Okri the Booker Prize in 1991, and Abdulrazak Gurnah the Nobel in 2021. The Caine Prize for African Writing, founded in 2000, now spotlights new short fiction. Contemporary writers have stepped away from academic styles, while literary festivals and the internet have produced new digital outlets such as the publisher OkadaBooks and the journal *Brittle Paper*.

A central scholarly debate, present since the colonial era, asks whether African literature should be defined the European way, with art held apart from content, or the traditional African way, in which art serves a social and communicative purpose. New manuscript discoveries in Timbuktu and the Horn of Africa keep reshaping the older narrative that orality was Africa's only literary inheritance, pointing instead to a long, multilingual written tradition running in parallel with the spoken one.
