# Indian literature

Indian literature is the body of writing produced across the Indian subcontinent in many languages, from ancient oral Sanskrit hymns to contemporary fiction in regional languages and English. Its defining feature is multilingualism: the Indian Constitution recognises 22 official languages, and the Sahitya Akademi, India's national literary academy, recognises 24 literary languages. Because most traditions developed independently and in different scripts, no single canon represents the whole field.

The earliest literature was oral. Sanskrit writing begins in the Vedic period (c. 1500–1200 BCE) with the Rig Veda, a collection of hymns, and the Upanishads, philosophical texts. These were followed by two long Sanskrit epics, the Ramayana and the Mahabharata, codified by the end of the 2nd millennium BCE. Classical Sanskrit then produced dramatic and grammatical works, including Kālidāsa's play *Recognition of Shakuntala* and Pāṇini's *Ashtadhyayi*, a grammar that standardised the language. The Prakrit languages, a group of Middle Indo-Aryan tongues used alongside Sanskrit in ancient and medieval periods, form a less visible but persistent layer of the tradition.

Parallel to Sanskrit, several non-Sanskrit traditions arose. The Pāli Canon preserved the teachings of Theravada Buddhism. In the south, the Sangam period (c. 300 BCE–300 CE) produced a body of ancient Tamil literature centred on human emotion, love, and social life. Further east, ancient Meitei literature appeared in the 1st century CE with works such as the Ougri and Numit Kappa. The Buddhist Charyapadas (8th–12th centuries) count as early works in Assamese and Bengali.

From the 9th century onward, literature in modern regional languages began to take shape, including Kannada, Telugu, Marathi, and Bengali. The most influential medieval development was the Bhakti movement, a wave of devotional Hinduism that began around the 6th century CE in Tamilakam (the ancient Tamil-speaking south) and spread northward between the 14th and 18th centuries. Bhakti poets such as Kabir, Tulsidas, and Guru Nanak wrote in local dialects rather than classical Sanskrit, making religious and moral ideas accessible to ordinary speakers and permanently shaping the literary character of the modern languages. Medieval Odia literature began in the 13th century with Sarala Das's translation of the Mahabharata.

Modern Indian literature is distinguished by the simultaneous flourishing of these regional traditions. Bengali literature gained international visibility when Rabindranath Tagore became India's first Nobel laureate in 1913. Hindi evolved from medieval religious poetry into modern prose and poetry. Gujarati, Assamese, Odia, Punjabi, and others developed their own canons. The 20th century also saw the rise of Indian English literature, a literary idiom shaped by colonial history. Contemporary writing includes explicitly political movements such as Dalit literature, which addresses caste discrimination, and LGBTQ literature.

Two institutional features help readers navigate the field. The Sahitya Akademi recognises 24 literary languages, and the Jnanpith Award is one of the highest honours, given annually for outstanding work in Indian literature. Scholars continue to debate the dating of several traditions, such as the earliest evidence for Punjabi literature, and the status of contested languages like Konkani.
