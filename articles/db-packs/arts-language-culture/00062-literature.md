# Literature

Literature is written work considered as art, especially novels, plays, and poems. The term also covers any body of writing on a subject, and in modern usage includes transcribed oral traditions. The word comes from Latin *litteratura*, "writing formed with letters." Definitions have shifted: before the 18th century in Western Europe, "literature" meant all books and writing; the early 20th-century *Encyclopædia Britannica* defined it as "the best expression of the best thought reduced to writing"; modern cultural studies include popular and minority genres.

## Mental Model

Literature is a system with four interlocking parts: (1) the **texts** themselves, (2) the **traditions** that produce and transmit them, (3) the **institutions** that preserve, study, and award them, and (4) the **forms** (poetry, prose, drama) and **types** (literary fiction, genre fiction, non-fiction, electronic literature) that classify them. Changing one part reshapes the others. Print technology made texts cheap to distribute; cheap distribution created a reading public; the reading public made literature a university discipline and a market for prizes.

## Oral and Early Written Literature

Writing emerged in Mesopotamia around the 4th millennium BC, driven by trade and administration, and independently possibly in ancient Egypt and Mesoamerica. Before writing, knowledge, genealogy, and law were carried in oral tradition. Australian Aboriginal oral traditions stretch back tens of thousands of years; a 2020 study of the Budj Bim and Tower Hill eruptions (34,000–40,000 years ago) aligns with Gunditjmara accounts, among the oldest oral traditions on record. The earliest poetry was likely sung or recited to preserve memory. Ancient Greek literature, including Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey* (late 8th to early 7th century BC), was composed and transmitted orally.

The world's oldest written literatures are Egyptian and Sumerian. Egyptian primary genres, mostly in verse, include didactic texts, hymns, prayers, and tales. Ancient Chinese literature focused on philosophy, historiography, military science, agriculture, and poetry; the Eastern Zhou period (769–269 BC) produced Confucian, Daoist, Mohist, and Legalist classics, plus Sun Tzu's *The Art of War* and Sima Qian's *Records of the Grand Historian*. Ancient Indian literature includes the Vedas (1500–1000 BC) and the epics *Mahabharata* and *Ramayana* (6th–1st centuries BC, with later redactions).

The earliest Greek writings are Mycenaean records in Linear B (c. 1600–1100 BC), containing only prosaic trade entries. Hesiod's *Works and Days* and *Theogony* (c. 700 BC) shaped Greek didactic and mythological tradition. Classical Greek genres spanned philosophy (Plato, Aristotle), lyric poetry (Sappho, Pindar), historiography (Herodotus, Thucydides), and drama; only plays by Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides survive from hundreds of tragedies. Roman literature begins in 240 BC with a Latin adaptation of a Greek play. Medieval Europe added Latin theology plus vernacular works such as *Beowulf* and the Icelandic sagas.

## Printing and the University Discipline

Gutenberg's movable type (c. 1450) made books cheap and widely available. Earlier movable type existed: Bi Sheng's earthenware type in China (c. 1045) and Korean metal type (c. 1230), which spread to Europe in the late 14th and early 15th centuries. Early European prints before 1501 are called *incunables*. In late-1820s England, utilitarians and Benthamites pushed English literary study at the new London University, framing literature as a vehicle for humanist national culture. The novel, a long fictional prose narrative, crystallized in the 18th century, though the term appeared in Romance languages in the late 15th century meaning "news."

## Forms, Types, and Criticism

Poetry uses heightened sonic patterning (assonance, alliteration, rhyme, rhythm) and is set in lines and verses; before the 19th century, poetry meant metrical verse. Prose is a later classical Greek development; Latin, especially Cicero, shaped European prose. The novella runs 17,000–40,000 words. The graphic novel combines art, dialogue, and text. Electronic literature is created for digital media. Drama is written for performance; Greek drama grew from religious and civic festivals, while closet drama is written to be read, not staged. Twentieth-century scripts added radio, television, and film.

Literary fiction explores the human condition and is often treated as having more artistic merit than genre fiction, a view contested by the academic study of genre fiction. Literary theory's central question is "what is literature?"; many contemporary theorists argue the term cannot be defined or applies to any use of language. Psychologist Hogan argues literature unites communities by evoking universal emotions and offering access to other cultures.

## Religion, Law, and Awards

Religious works (Vedas, Torah, Bible, Qur'an) have shaped literary form and language. The King James Bible is widely regarded as a landmark of English literary style. Copyright protects original expression, not ideas; the UK has protected literary works since at least 1710, the US federally since 1790 (revised 1976), and Japan joined the Berne Convention in 1899. Censorship has shaped what is published: James Joyce's *Ulysses* was banned in the US from 1921 to 1933, then became a central literary text.

The Nobel Prize in Literature, established by Alfred Nobel's 1895 will, is awarded for an author's body of work. Of 117 Nobels awarded 1901–2020, only 16 went to women; Selma Lagerlöf was first, in 1909. Other international prizes include the Neustadt, Man Booker International, Pulitzer, and Hugo awards.

## Key Terms

- **Belles-lettres**: the tradition of fine writing
- **Oral tradition**: knowledge and art passed by speech, not writing
- **Incunables**: European books printed before 1501
- **Literary fiction**: fiction exploring the human condition
- **Novella**: a prose narrative of 17,000–40,000 words
- **Electronic literature**: works created for digital media
- **Copyright**: exclusive right to copy a creative work, usually for a limited time
