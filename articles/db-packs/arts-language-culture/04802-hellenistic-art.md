# Hellenistic art

Hellenistic art is the body of work made in the Greek-speaking world between the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BC and the Roman conquest of Ptolemaic Egypt in 30 BC. Over those three centuries, Greek culture spread from the eastern Mediterranean to Central Asia. Local traditions blended with Greek ones, royal courts replaced city-states as the main patrons, and art moved from the calm, idealised forms of Classical Greece toward greater emotion, realism, and variety. Roman art draws directly from this period.

## What changed and why

Classical Greek art had been produced mostly for city-states and governed by ideas of balance, harmony, and the idealised body. After Alexander, his empire was redrawn into kingdoms run by his former generals, the Diadochi: the Ptolemies in Egypt, the Seleucids in the Near East, and the Attalids at Pergamon. These courts competed for prestige by building on an unprecedented scale and commissioning works meant to impress rather than only honour a god. Art became more willing to show pain, age, and individual likeness.

## Architecture

Hellenistic architects abandoned the Doric temple almost entirely and worked in a more theatrical, oversized register. The Ionic order survived mainly in colossal projects, such as the Temple of Apollo at Didyma, whose Ionic columns towered above worshippers. The Corinthian capital, with its acanthus leaves, became standard for grand civic buildings.

Alexandria developed an ornate manner with broken pediments, curved entablatures, and rich Corinthian decoration, a style Roman builders later absorbed. Pergamon shows what Hellenistic planning could be: an acropolis spread across a steep hillside, with a monumental altar and a theatre seating 10,000. Architects began designing buildings that followed the lie of the land rather than imposing a flat classical grid. Excavations at Olynthus reveal houses organised around private courtyards and decorated with mosaics.

## Sculpture

Figures in sculpture show strain, suffering, surprise, and age. Portraiture of named individuals, including philosophers, poets, and ordinary people, replaced the idealised anonymous type. Pliny the Elder later noted a brief sculptural revival in 156–153 BC.

Scholars group the work into several currents. Hellenistic Baroque, especially at Pergamon, pushed drama to its limits: the Great Altar of Pergamon, built under Eumenes II around 200–180 BC, has a Gigantomachy frieze whose figures writhe and snarl under deep, undercut drapery. The Laocoön Group, a priest and his sons being strangled by serpents, is the classic multi-figure Baroque example. A lighter current, sometimes called Rococo, produced small playful subjects such as the *Invitation to the Dance*, a dancing satyr pulling a girl behind him. From the second century BC, a Neo-Attic reaction returned to calmer Classical models, often producing versions of older statues for Roman buyers.

## Painting, mosaics, and the lost panel tradition

No original Greek panel paintings survive, so the period's pictorial innovations have to be read through wall paintings, such as those at the royal tombs of Vergina, and through mosaics. Hellenistic painters developed receding landscape backgrounds, three-dimensional perspective, and chiaroscuro, the use of light and shade to model form. Sacro-idyllic scenes, in which sacred figures appear in dreamlike pastoral landscapes, became a popular new genre.

Mosaic technique evolved in parallel. The early pebble technique gave way to opus tessellatum, which used larger cut cubes of stone or glass called tesserae, and to opus vermiculatum, a painstaking method using very fine tesserae to imitate brushwork. The Alexander Mosaic, found at Pompeii, is a Roman copy of a lost Greek painting. Sosos of Pergamon specialised in trompe-l'œil, images that look like real objects left on a floor, including a famous "unswept floor" with food scraps. The mosaics of Delos mark the technical peak of the art. The transition from pebble to tessellated mosaic is not directly documented, and the relationship between the two techniques remains uncertain.

## Pottery, figurines, and the minor arts

Fine painted pottery declined as metalwork and glass took over. Surviving vases are mostly simpler black wares, such as West Slope ware with its painted slip, or mould-made relief vessels such as Megarian ware. Regional Italian traditions like Apulian and Centuripe ware held on longer, with Centuripe pieces painted in polychrome after firing.

Terracotta figurines became a mass market. Tanagra figures, named after the site in Boeotia, show elegant women in everyday dress; a parallel tradition produced "grotesques", small figures exaggerating deformity, whose function is still debated. The Derveni Krater, a volute krater from a Macedonian tomb, shows the era's metalwork at its most virtuosic, with repoussé (hammered relief) Dionysiac figures. The period also saw the invention of glass-blowing, probably in the eastern Mediterranean, which made glassware cheap, and a flowering of cameo carving in hard stones. Coinage shifted from generic deity heads to realistic royal portraits, especially in the Hellenistic East.

The label "decadent" applied to this period after a supposed Classical "Golden Age" dates from eighteenth-century taste, and modern scholarship treats it as a prejudice rather than a fact.
