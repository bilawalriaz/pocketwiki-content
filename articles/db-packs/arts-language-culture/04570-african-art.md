# African art

African art is the body of visual work made across the African continent within specific social, political, and religious settings. Its defining habit is to pursue conceptual and symbolic accuracy rather than physical likeness: a figure is shaped to express a spiritual role, an ancestor, a deity, or a social function, not to record a particular body. Because most traditions treat images as instruments for ritual, rank, and community life, African art tends to be functional rather than decorative.

The depth of the tradition is far older than written history. Paint-making processes are documented in Africa around 100,000 years ago, and the oldest known beads, made from *Nassarius* shells and worn as personal ornaments, date to about 72,000 years ago. The first large sculptural tradition for which named work survives is the Nok culture of Nigeria, known for terracotta heads and figures and emerging around 3000 BC. By the 10th century, lost-wax bronze casting was practiced at Igbo Ukwu and Ile-Ife in West Africa. From the 12th to 14th centuries, the royal court of Benin in present-day Nigeria produced the Benin Bronzes, over a thousand brass plaques and royal heads that once clad the palace. In the Kuba Kingdom of Central Africa (17th–19th centuries), court art was densely decorated with geometric and narrative motifs. In West Africa between 1400 and 1900, Akan goldweights, tiny cast-metal sculptures, condensed proverbs into portable objects.

The human figure is the central subject across most of these traditions, and masks are the second great art form. Masks are worn in ceremonies by initiated dancers to embody an ancestor or spirit so the community can communicate with that being or mark a transition. This is why masks and statues are often treated as sacred; the Dogon of Mali keep many sculptures hidden from public view because their meaning is reserved for initiates. The Bambara of West Africa carve masks and statuettes for specific societies such as N'tomo and Komo that structure community life. A patina, the crust that builds up from sacrificial libations such as blood, oils, or poured offerings, is not dirt but a visible record of ritual use. Materials vary by region and need. Wood is the most common medium for sculpture because it is workable and locally available; clay, ivory, bronze, brass, gold, and stone are also used. Textiles such as Ghanaian kente cloth, hand-woven silk and cotton whose patterns and colors carry specific meanings, carry symbolic weight. Jewellery made of stone, bead, and wood marks rank, lineage, or initiation. Many sculptures carry readable visual conventions: Mande-speaking peoples tend to carve broad, flat surfaces, while Central African figures often use heart-shaped faces marked with circles and dots.

Regional styles have continually absorbed outside influence without losing the functional core. The spread of Islam and Christianity introduced new subjects, patrons, and forms. Colonial contact brought distortion: Western audiences in the 19th century routinely dismissed African work as "primitive." In the early 20th century, European Modernists such as Picasso and Matisse reacted against academic realism and found in African abstraction a model for distortion, flatness, and emotional directness, which is why African masks and figures appear so often in the background of Cubist painting. The direction of influence now runs both ways: contemporary artists such as El Anatsui and William Kentridge exhibit globally, and some deliberately borrow from Western Modernism, a reversal that unsettles the older one-way story. Biennials in Dakar and Johannesburg and growing representation in Western galleries have made this scene more visible since the late 20th century. A distinct popular strand emerged in 1968 when Edward Said Tingatinga began the brightly coloured, animal-centred Tinga Tinga style in Tanzania, and the 1950s and 1960s in South Africa laid the institutional foundations for the continent's professional art world.

The field also has live disagreements. The original purpose of ancient works like the Nok terracottas is still unknown. The stylistic groupings used for peoples such as the Bwa, Gurunsi, and Mossi in Burkina Faso carry the confusion of colonial-era labels, which scholars continue to untangle. A pressing contemporary question is restitution: a 2018 French-commissioned report on returning African heritage reignited debate over the tens of thousands of objects held in Western museums, and activists such as Mwazulu Diyabanha have taken direct public action for returns. Scholars continue to expand the scope of "African art" itself, arguing that the category should include the visual cultures of every region and era, as well as work from the African diaspora, if the continent's full aesthetic history is to be understood.
