# Gothic art

Gothic art was the dominant medieval artistic style of Western Europe from the early 12th century to the 15th, closely tied to Gothic architecture. It shifted away from the flatter, more abstract Romanesque style toward greater naturalism, human emotion, and new media such as stained glass and panel painting. Over time it produced an elegant courtly phase known as International Gothic in the late 14th century, before being absorbed into the Renaissance in the 15th and 16th centuries, though it survived in parts of Germany into the 1500s.

## Origins and Chronology

Gothic art emerged in the early 12th century in the Île-de-France region, the same area where Gothic architecture arose. Its first form was monumental sculpture carved into cathedral walls, beginning at the Abbey Church of St Denis around 1140. By about 1145, the elongated figures on the Western Portal at Chartres Cathedral already showed an early Gothic look; by 1215–1220, sculpture on Chartres's south transept had become far more naturalistic, with convincing human proportions and emotion.

Painting lagged architecture by more than 50 years. Gothic painting only began to develop around 1200, in England and France, and the earliest fully Gothic illustrated manuscripts appeared in the mid-13th century. The movement then spread through Western and Central Europe, carried by monastic networks like the Cistercians, though it never fully displaced the more classical tradition in Italy, where Nicola Pisano instead developed a Proto-Renaissance style influenced by Roman models.

## Religious Themes and Devotion

Most Gothic art served the Church. A common approach was typological, pairing Old Testament scenes with New Testament events to show the earlier story as a prefiguration of the later one. Two devotional shifts shaped the imagery. The first was a surge in Marian devotion, which transformed the Virgin Mary from a formal Byzantine icon into a tender, human mother holding her child. The second was a new emotional emphasis on Christ's suffering, exemplified by the Pietà, which depicts Mary cradling the dead body of Jesus. Both changes reflected a broader theological turn toward personal piety and identification with the humanity of Christ and Mary.

## Secular Art and the Rise of Cities

As trade revived and cities grew, a wealthy bourgeois class commissioned secular art on a large scale. Rising literacy and the spread of vernacular literature brought in subjects from romance and everyday life. Artists joined guilds, which kept better records, so more names of medieval painters survive from this period. Surviving secular objects include ivory carvings, mirror-cases, and illuminated Books of Hours made for private devotion, though religious commissions still dominate what remains.

## Painting in Four Media

Gothic painters worked in four main materials, each with a different regional strength.

- Frescoes, water-based pigments on wet lime plaster, remained the chief medium for narrative wall painting in southern Europe, with well-preserved examples in Scandinavian churches.
- Stained glass flourished in northern Europe because Gothic architecture's pointed arches and ribbed vaults made huge window walls possible.
- Illuminated manuscripts, decorated with gold, silver, and miniature paintings, provide the most complete record of Gothic painting styles, including royal bibles, psalters, and the Books of Hours that became popular among the laity. The Parisian Psalter of Saint Louis, illuminated 1253–1270, contains 78 full-page paintings.
- Panel painting on wood became innovative in the 15th century, when Early Netherlandish artists such as Robert Campin and Jan van Eyck used oil paint to achieve minute detail, luminous color, and layered religious symbolism.

## Sculpture

Gothic sculpture moved from the elongated, wall-bound figures of early portals to freestanding, naturalistic forms. Northern Gothic sculpture later produced large, expressive carved wooden altarpieces, especially in Germany. A parallel industry of small portable sculpture, mostly devotional ivory figures, thrived in Paris and elsewhere, aimed largely at lay, often female buyers.

## Terminology and Its History

The label "Gothic" was coined by Renaissance critics, most notably Giorgio Vasari, who used it as a pejorative meaning "barbaric," associating the style with the Goths blamed for the fall of Rome. Its gradual acceptance as a neutral art-historical term came later. The exact origins of the style remain debated, with proposed roots in Lombard, Frankish, and Norse traditions, and the boundary between late Romanesque and early Gothic painting is blurry, with no clear break, so scholars treat the transition as gradual rather than a sharp starting date.
